Spatial analysis and health implications of micro rubber and vanadium in urban street dust in Northwest of Iran
Authors: Pourya Ayremloo, Saeed Hosseinpour, Reza Fouladi-Fard, Amir Mohammadi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Urbanization and increasing vehicular traffic have led to significant environmental challenges, with street dust being a major contributor to urban pollution. This study investigates the distribution and characteristics of micro-rubber (MRs) particles and vanadium contamination in the street dust of Urmia, Iran. Street dust samples were collected from 30 sites across the city, and analyzed for MRs particle counts and vanadium concentrations. Results showed a wide variation in MRs concentrations, with particle sizes predominantly under 100 μm. The concentration of vanadium ranged from 25 to 44 mg/kg, suggesting a geogenic origin with minimal anthropogenic enrichment. Spatial analysis revealed hotspots of pollution in areas with heavy traffic and near fueling stations. The study highlights the potential health risks associated with long-term exposure to MRs particles and vanadium, particularly in urban areas with high traffic densities. The study calls for enhanced monitoring, targeted interventions to reduce pollution in high-risk areas, and further research to refine exposure estimates and evaluate long-term health impacts.

Introduction

With the rapid growth of urban populations and infrastructure, managing environmental pollution has become a pressing concern in urban areas1,2. Street dust, a complex combination of solid particles, chemical and organic pollutants, and minerals, is a major contributor to urban environmental pollution3,4. Among its significant components are vanadium and micro-rubber (MRs) as tire particles, which have potential implications for both human health and ecosystems5–7. MRs particles, composed of nano- and micro-scale fragments generated by tire abrasion, are a major contributor to microplastic pollution in urban areas8,9. Their minute size and complex chemical makeup allow them to spread easily throughout the environment, leading to contamination of soil, surface water, and air10. Beyond their immediate environmental effects, MRs can also serve as carriers for toxic compounds like heavy metals, further intensifying pollution11,12.

Vanadium, a rare metal often linked to industrial processes and fossil fuel usage, is another key contaminant found in street dust. It can be introduced into urban settings through sources such as vehicle emissions, the burning of petroleum-based materials, and waste incineration. High levels of vanadium are known to have toxic impacts on both human health and the environment. Research suggests that this metal can enter the food chain via street dust13,14. Vanadium is widely distributed in the Earth’s crust with an average concentration of 100 ppm, and is found in minerals such as carnotite, roscoelite, and vanadinite15,16. It is also present in phosphate rocks, certain ores, and crude oils as organic complexes17,18. According to reputable sources, it is estimated that more than 60,000 tons of vanadium are released into the atmosphere annually due to anthropogenic activities, particularly the combustion of fossil fuels19. This extensive emission leads to ambient air concentrations ranging from 20 to 300 ng/m³ in major cities, with levels reaching up to 10 mg/m³ reported in urban areas such as New York City19–21. Additionally, Hope (1997) reported that approximately 2.3 × 10⁸ kg of vanadium are introduced into the environment each year through human activities, of which about 1.32 × 10⁸ kg accumulate in soils21. In contrast, only about 8.4 tons of vanadium are emitted annually from natural sources, including volcanic eruptions, continental dust, forest fires, sea salt spray, and biogenic processes19.

Vanadium is used in vehicle catalysts to reduce nitrogen oxides (NOx). These catalysts play a key role in reducing pollutants. However, particles resulting from the wear of these catalysts can be released into street dust, contributing to environmental pollution. These particles, containing vanadium, can spread through dust and contaminate soil, surface water, and air, leading to negative impacts on human health and ecosystems7,22.

In recent years, many playgrounds and sports fields in some countries have been fitted with rubber surfaces made from recycled tires, raising concerns among parents and lawmakers about potential health risks for children. Tires contain harmful substances such as sulfur, zinc oxide, heavy metals like lead and cadmium, and toxic plasticizers and fire retardants23. A 2014 study by Indian scientists found that exposure to MRs particles significantly reduced lung function in workers at a tire factory. The severity of the impact was linked to the duration of exposure, with those exposed for longer periods showing the worst results, suggesting that fine rubber particles can adversely affect lung health24,25. In Abu Dhabi, UAE, the concentration of MRs in road dust was reported as 44.4 ± 40.6 MRs per gram26. A study conducted in Kraków, Poland, identified MRs particle sizes ranging between 50 and 200 μm, with the number of fragments per kilogram of dry matter also documented in road dust samples27.

Urmia, a major metropolitan city in Iran, accommodates over 250,000 vehicles daily, raising concerns about air and soil pollution that affect the health of more than 800,000 residents. Gaining a thorough understanding of the characteristics and distribution of MRs fragment number and vanadium value in street dust is essential for improving pollutant management and shaping effective environmental and urban policies. This research explores the distribution and properties of MRs and vanadium in street dust and offers recommendations to minimize their adverse effects.

Materials and methods

Study area

Urmia, the capital of West Azerbaijan Province, is the second-largest city in northwestern Iran, spanning about 100 square kilometers with a population of 800,000 (2018 data). It has rich water resources, mild and pleasant Mediterranean climate and an average annual rainfall of 300 mm. Located at 37° 33’ N and 45° 4’ E, the city lies approximately 20 km from the dried Lake Urmia bed, as a one of saltiest lake in world similar Great salt lake in USA. Sampling and chemical analysis

Street dust samples were collected from both main and secondary streets in Urmia, chosen based on traffic volume, population density, and proximity to fuel stations. Sampling locations were carefully distributed to ensure full city coverage. A total of 60 samples were collected, with 30 designated for vanadium analysis and 30 for MRs particle examination. At each location, around 200 g of surface dust was collected from an area of approximately one square meter using a plastic brush for vanadium and a metal brush for MRs. The samples were stored in sealed, non-plastic containers, labeled appropriately, and transported to the laboratory for analysis.

Using this equation, a minimum of 56 samples was required, and we collected 60 samples to ensure adequate statistical power and spatial coverage. Additionally, the sampling locations were selected to represent different traffic densities and urban settings28–30.

The process for digestion and vanadium analysis, utilizing the ICP-AES device, was thoroughly outlined in our prior research28,31. The method employed for digestion involved acid digestion.

To extract MRs particles from 30 dust samples, organic materials were first removed to prevent errors in counting MRs. For this purpose, 15 g of each sample were mixed with 35 milliliters of 30% H2O2 for 7 days. The samples were then washed using a vacuum filter and deionized water, followed by drying at 60 °C. After adding ZnCl2 solution to the samples and shaking them, the particles were separated and subjected to centrifugation for settling and filtering. Blank controls were used to verify the accuracy of the results32. The physical characteristics of the MRs, such as their matte black color and elasticity, were examined using a microscope with 200x magnification (echoLAB) and manual tools like tweezers. The MRs were counted and measured using a binocular microscope, and the particles were classified into five size categories: 100 μm, 100–250 μm, 250–500 μm, 500–1000 μm, and 1000–5000 μm.

QA/QC

The quality assurance and control (QA/QC) procedures for the digestion and vanadium analysis were designed to ensure accuracy and reliability. Sample handling, reagent purity, and equipment calibration were strictly monitored to prevent contamination, with blank controls used throughout the process. For vanadium analysis, the acid digestion method was used with 35 mL of 30% H2O2 per 15 g of sample, a procedure validated in previous studies. The ICP-AES device was calibrated with certified reference materials, and the vanadium concentration was measured using standard calibration curves. Filtration and washing steps, using vacuum filters and deionized water, were carefully controlled, followed by drying at 60 °C. Also, based on the analytical method employed in this study, the LOD was determined to be 1 mg/kg and the LOQ was 3 mg/kg33. The physical characteristics of MRs particles were analyzed using a microscope at 200x magnification, and particle sizes were categorized into five ranges. Data integrity was ensured through repeated analysis and statistical validation of the results. To prevent potential contamination with MR during laboratory procedures, strict quality control measures were implemented. All reagents and distilled water were pre-filtered using specialized filters, and glassware was thoroughly rinsed with distilled water. Laboratory surfaces were cleaned with ethanol, and during all procedures, cotton lab coats, disposable latex gloves, and face masks were used. When necessary, samples were covered with aluminum foil to minimize airborne contamination.

To assess reliability, several randomly selected street dust samples were reanalyzed for microtire content. The re-counts showed minimal variation (ranging from 2 to 5 particles) compared to the original counts, suggesting that external contamination was negligible under the applied laboratory conditions and that the results are robust and reproducible34.

Enrichment factor

To assess the impact of human activities on wet deposition, the enrichment factor (EF) was utilized. The enrichment factor was calculated following the methodology described in previous research31. This approach allows for the comparison of the concentration of specific elements in the samples to their background levels, providing insight into the extent of anthropogenic influence35,36.

Spatial analysis

The spatial trend analysis of microrubber and vanadium was conducted using ArcGIS 10.1. The spatial trend analysis was conducted using the Kriging interpolation method and generating independent raster layers. We also compared the performance of Kriging with the IDW method and found that Kriging provided lower RMSE values, indicating better accuracy. Dark red colors were used to identify hot spots in the data.

The resulting HQ values were then compared to the threshold value of 1. HQ values above 1 indicate a potential health concern due to vanadium ingestion. This approach provides a conservative estimation of health risks and aligns with established USEPA risk assessment protocols37.

To reduce the uncertainty inherent in deterministic risk assessment methods, a sensitivity analysis based on Monte Carlo simulation was conducted. This simulation was performed using Crystal Ball software (version 11.1.2.4, Oracle Corporation, USA) with 1,000 iterations. The stochastic model was designed to provide a more realistic estimation of risk by incorporating variability in input parameters, rather than relying on single-point estimates. A key advantage of this approach lies in its ability to identify and quantify the influence of each input variable on the estimated Hazard Quotient (HQ) values. Parameters with higher sensitivity coefficients have a more significant impact on the final risk estimate38s.

The total number of MRs ranged from 15 to 248 per 15 g of street dust, with an average of 71 ± 56.46 and a median of 53 particles. The predominant MR particle size was < 100 μm, although some locations showed notable contributions from the 100–250 μm size range. While both studies identify traffic emissions as a primary source of contamination, the Bushehr study also includes resuspended soil particles as a contributing factor. The presence of MRs and MPs in both studies emphasizes urban traffic’s significant impact on street dust composition, although your study shows lower concentrations of MRs compared to the Bushehr study. The potential health risks from street dust, particularly related to heavy metals and MRs, are a concern in both studies, with elevated ecological risks from elements like mercury (Hg) and arsenic (As)39.

In comparison to the study in Kraków on microplastics from tyres, which reported 515 ± 20 fragments per kilogram in road dust, my results showed MRs concentrations ranging from 15 to 248 particles per 15 g of street dust. Both studies emphasize traffic as a significant source of tyre-derived emissions. While my results reflect lower MR concentrations, they align with the finding that road dust particles are less degraded than those in soil, indicating a similar pattern of emission and degradation processes. These studies highlight the underappreciated environmental impact of tyre wear27.

Vanadium concentrations in the street dust samples varied between 25 and 44 mg/kg, with an average of 31 ± 3.68 mg/kg and a median of 32 mg/kg. These values indicate relatively higher vanadium levels compared to vehicle-emitted particulate matter (PM) reported in the in Los Angeles. In vehicle emissions, vanadium concentrations from V-SCR systems reached 14.3 µg/g, 14 times higher than the baseline vehicle’s 0.98 µg/g 7. The enrichment factor (EF) values ranged from 0.9 to 1.2, classifying all sites within class 1 (EF ≤ 10: unenriched) according to the EF classification system. This suggests that vanadium levels in the sampled street dust are primarily influenced by natural background levels, with minimal to no significant anthropogenic enrichment31,40. The values of EF indicating that vanadium levels were primarily of natural origin with negligible anthropogenic enrichment. Conversely, the PM data suggest significant anthropogenic contributions, especially from vehicles with V-SCR systems, where vanadium levels increased up to 400-fold over baseline vehicles7,31. The results indicate that while vanadium in street dust is primarily geogenic with minimal anthropogenic enrichment, its absolute concentration is significantly higher than PM emitted by vehicles. This highlights the importance of addressing both cumulative environmental contamination and localized emission sources in urban pollution management. Further studies should evaluate the bioavailability and size-specific distribution of vanadium in street dust to better understand its ecological and health implications.

Sampling sites with relatively higher EF values (e.g., sites 2, 3, and 4, where EF values reached 1.2) might indicate localized contributions from urban activities such as vehicular emissions and industrial processes and gas stations. However, these values remain well below the threshold for moderate enrichment (EF ≥ 10). Although the EF values indicate a predominantly geogenic origin for vanadium, the possibility of localized anthropogenic sources cannot be entirely ruled out. Urmia is primarily an agricultural city with limited industrial activity; however, the presence of brick kilns in the outskirts and fuel stations within the city may contribute to slight increases in vanadium levels in street dust.

The Witbank study in Republic of South Africa showed much higher vanadium levels in street dust, reaching up to 4,410 mg/kg, primarily due to industrial activities like metal smelting and coal combustion. This highlights the significant difference in vanadium contamination, with higher levels associated with industrial emissions41. The findings reflect varying environmental impacts on street dust contamination in different regions. This study measured vanadium in urban street, reflecting localized pollution. In contrast, for North-West Europe reported 1569 tons/year of vanadium emissions, mainly from shipping and industrial fuel combustion near major port cities. Both studies highlight the environmental and health impacts of vanadium, emphasizing the importance of monitoring and mitigation efforts42.

Although vanadium concentrations in the street dust samples from Urmia (ranging from 25 to 44 mg/kg, with an average of 31 ± 3.68 mg/kg) were relatively high, they remain significantly lower than levels reported in heavily industrialized areas such as Witbank, South Africa. This difference may be attributed to Urmia’s predominantly agricultural nature and the absence of major industrial activities, mining operations, or fossil fuel-based power plants in the region. Furthermore, lower traffic density compared to large metropolitan cities like Los Angeles likely contributes to the reduced anthropogenic vanadium input. Local sources such as brick kilns and fuel stations may contribute slightly to vanadium levels, but not to an extent that results in substantial enrichment (EF > 1.2). Thus, the relatively lower vanadium levels in Urmia reflect the predominance of natural sources and limited anthropogenic influence in this urban environment.

The highest number of MRs was observed at sampling site 26, with 248 particles, predominantly < 100 μm in size, highlighting a potential hotspot for MRs pollution. Similarly, site 17 exhibited 218 particles, reinforcing the spatial variability of MRs across the study area. In contrast, site 11 recorded the lowest MR count (15 particles), suggesting relatively lower pollution levels in this region.

In the vanadium distribution map of street dust in Fig. 3, hotspots of vanadium are observed in the east and southern parts of the city. This high dispersion is likely linked to pollutant sources such as heavy traffic and fueling stations. These areas, with dense vehicle traffic, may experience higher levels of pollution from fuels and chemicals such as vanadium. Additionally, the proximity to fueling stations could lead to greater accumulation of vanadium in these regions. These findings highlight the need for more attention and serious measures to reduce chemical and metal pollution in street dust in these areas. The comparison between normal and acute exposure levels shows that for both adults and children, the daily exposure at many sites exceeds the normal exposure thresholds. For example, in sampling site 17, the exposure for both adults (1.5 mg/day) and children (3.2 mg/day) significantly surpasses the normal exposure limit for adults (100 mg/day) and children (200 mg/day)44,45. These levels suggest that acute exposure, especially at hotspot sites, may lead to potential health risks due to long-term exposure to MRs particles. In this study, the health risk assessment was limited to ingestion exposure, following the approach commonly used in previous research to ensure consistency and comparability of results. However, it is important to acknowledge that this represents a limitation, as inhalation and dermal exposure pathways can also contribute significantly to the overall risk, especially in urban environments where street dust is prevalent. Therefore, future studies should incorporate these additional exposure routes to provide a more comprehensive evaluation of potential health risks associated with micro-rubber particles.

The stochastic Monte Carlo risk analysis revealed differentiated health risks from vanadium exposure through street dust ingestion among children and adults. For children, the mean Hazard Quotient (HQ) was approximately 0.42, with the 5th and 95th percentiles at 0.24 and 0.64, respectively. These results indicate that while the average risk remains below the threshold level of concern (HQ < 1), certain high-exposure scenarios may pose potential health risks. For adults, HQ values remained consistently low across all percentiles, with a mean around 0.45, suggesting negligible health risks in this age group (Fig. 4, This simulation was performed using Crystal Ball software, version 11.1.2.4, Oracle Corporation, USA, with 1,000 iterations.).

These findings underscore that children are more vulnerable due to their lower body weight and higher rates of inadvertent dust ingestion. If HQ values exceed 1 under extreme exposure scenarios, chronic vanadium intake may result in adverse health effects such as gastrointestinal issues, abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, weight loss, chest pain, bronchitis, headache, and potential damage to the liver, kidneys, and nervous system, as reported in toxicological studies46.

According to Fig. 5, the sensitivity analysis identified the dust ingestion rate (IR), body weight (BW), and exposure duration (ED) as the most influential variables affecting HQ variability. Other parameters, including vanadium concentration (C) and exposure frequency (EF), also contributed to risk fluctuations but to a lesser extent. The high sensitivity of HQ to IR highlights the significant impact of behavioral factors—such as outdoor play and hand-to-mouth activity—on risk levels. Overall, the study indicates that while non-carcinogenic ingestion risk from vanadium is minimal for adults, children may face potential health risks in high-exposure environments.

Urban authorities are advised to implement mitigation strategies in high-traffic or industrial areas, including street cleaning, traffic regulation, and public education campaigns to reduce children’s exposure to contaminated dust.

The findings also highlight that areas with the highest levels of MRs exposure, such as site 17 and 26, may require more targeted interventions and monitoring to mitigate the health effects associated with chronic exposure, particularly in urban regions with heavy traffic. It is crucial to address these hotspots to reduce exposure levels for both adults and children, thereby preventing potential adverse health outcomes related to microplastic contamination from vehicle tires.

Finally, no meaningful statistical relationship was found between vanadium and MRs, which may be attributed to differences in their respective sources of origin. The lack of statistical correlation between vanadium and other metal(loid)s may be attributed to differences in their sources and deposition mechanisms. While many metal(loid)s in urban dust often originate from anthropogenic activities such as traffic and industrial emissions, vanadium is primarily geogenic in origin. Moreover, vanadium may be deposited through different pathways, such as fine particulate emissions from specific combustion processes, which may not coincide spatially or temporally with the deposition of other metals. This divergence could explain the observed lack of correlation.

Overall, the findings underscore the need for targeted mitigation strategies to address MR and vanadium contamination in urban street dust, particularly in hotspots with elevated concentrations. The spatial distribution and enrichment data provide valuable insights into pollution sources and the associated risks to human health and the environment.

Although the study acknowledges that exposure estimates may involve some degree of uncertainty, several specific factors could further contribute to this variability. These include the limited data on the bioavailability of vanadium in street dust, variations in particle size distribution which affect respiratory deposition and ingestion rates, and behavioral factors such as hand-to-mouth activity frequency in children or outdoor activity duration in different age groups. Future studies incorporating site-specific bioaccessibility data and personal exposure monitoring could significantly improve the accuracy of risk estimations.

Conclusion

This study provides comprehensive insights into the spatial distribution, concentration levels, and health risks of micro-rubber particles (MRs) and vanadium in urban street dust in Urmia. The findings reveal notable spatial variability in MRs and vanadium concentrations, highlighting the influence of local traffic intensity, proximity to fueling stations, and urban infrastructure. Although vanadium levels in most areas appear geogenic with minimal anthropogenic enrichment (EF ≤ 1.2), localized hotspots may indicate minor contributions from human activities, particularly in zones with higher vehicle density or industrial proximity.

The health risk assessment underscores that while non-carcinogenic risks from vanadium exposure remain low for adults, children may be more vulnerable, particularly in high-exposure areas. Similarly, MRs exposure through ingestion poses a more significant concern for children, especially in identified hotspots, with daily intakes exceeding normal thresholds at certain sites. These findings call for immediate attention to street dust pollution management, especially in urban zones with dense traffic.

This research contributes to the growing body of literature on emerging urban pollutants by combining spatial analysis with probabilistic health risk assessment, offering a model that can be adapted for other cities. Furthermore, it underlines the importance of incorporating multiple exposure pathways in future studies and encourages policymakers to adopt targeted mitigation strategies—such as urban dust control, traffic regulation, and community education—to reduce environmental and public health risks associated with tire-derived particles and vanadium in street dust.
