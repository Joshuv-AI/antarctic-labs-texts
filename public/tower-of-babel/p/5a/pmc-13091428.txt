Preventing Carotid Blowout: Planned Carotid Sacrifice and Cerebral Revascularization Enabling Second Reirradiation in Recurrent Nasopharyngeal Carcinoma
Authors: Norihisa Masai, Hideya Yamazaki, Takeo Nishida, Koji Masui, Masahiko Koizumi, Daisaku Tatsumi, Taku Yasumoto, Ryoong-Jin Oh
Published: 2026

Introduction

Local recurrence of head and neck cancer after radiation therapy remains a major therapeutic challenge.1 Salvage surgery or chemotherapy are standard options but often limited by anatomy or patient condition, leaving reirradiation as the only feasible choice in selected cases.2 However, reirradiation carries a substantial risk of carotid blowout syndrome (CBOS), occurring in fewer than 10% of cases but fatal in more than half.3, 4, 5 Accurate prediction and prevention of CBOS are crucial.

In nasopharyngeal carcinoma, CBOS often occurs in the setting of radiation-associated nasopharyngeal mucosal and soft-tissue necrosis and infection, conditions that may precede overt hemorrhage and substantially increase mortality. Recent large cohort studies have suggested that early or preventive vascular intervention in patients with threatened carotid involvement may reduce catastrophic bleeding and improve outcomes.6,7

Management of CBOS includes surgical carotid ligation or endovascular embolization, but either may cause cerebral ischemia when the circle of Willis is incomplete. To preserve hemostasis and cerebral perfusion, covered stents have been introduced, though they remain prone to rebleeding from endoluminal leakage, infection, or stent-induced wall erosion.4 In neurosurgery, parent artery occlusion is used for complex unruptured aneurysms unsuitable for coiling,8, 9, 10 with cerebral perfusion assessed beforehand and revascularization performed if significant hypoperfusion is anticipated.11 These principles illustrate that planned carotid sacrifice and cerebral revascularization may be deliberately integrated into the overall treatment process when extreme vascular risk is anticipated.

We report a patient with recurrent nasopharyngeal carcinoma who was treated with a third course of radiation therapy (second reirradiation) as part of an integrated, multidisciplinary strategy that included preplanned carotid sacrifice and cerebral revascularization to mitigate extreme vascular risk associated with reirradiation.

Methods

Patient history and initial therapy

A 36-year-old man was diagnosed with undifferentiated nasopharyngeal carcinoma (cT3N0M0) in March 2009. He received concurrent chemoradiotherapy (70 Gy in 35 fractions) with docetaxel and cisplatin (Fig. 1A), followed by 2 cycles of docetaxel, cisplatin, and 5-fluorouracil–based combination chemotherapy (TPF regimen). Posttreatment imaging confirmed a complete response.

(B) The dose distribution of the first reirradiation delivered with CyberKnife at 32.5 Gy in 5 fractions for the first recurrence. (C) Multiple recurrent tumors identified 8 y after the initial diagnosis. The lesion extended circumferentially from the left nasopharynx to the petrous apex, cavernous sinus, and sphenoid sinus (360° direction), encasing the left internal carotid artery with a suspected wall invasion (red arrow).

In January 2011 (15 months later), a recurrent lesion was detected, and the patient underwent CyberKnife reirradiation (32.5 Gy in 5 fractions) (Fig. 1B), after which residual disease persisted. He subsequently received 5 additional TPF cycles, 25 biweekly cycles of S-1, and 56 doses of docetaxel plus cetuximab, but the tumor remained. In December 2016, 8 years after diagnosis, progressive enlargement was observed.

The recurrent tumor extended from the left nasopharynx to the petrous apex, cavernous sinus, and sphenoid sinus. Imaging showed circumferential encasement of the left internal carotid artery (ICA) by the tumor, suggesting wall invasion and nasopharyngeal ulceration (Fig. 1C). Surgery was infeasible, and a third course of radiation therapy (second reirradiation) was considered.

Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for publication of the medical treatment and images. The Institutional Review Board of Miyakojima IGRT Clinic, Osaka, Japan, approved the treatment and publication of this report.

CBOS risk and preventive strategy

The risk of CBOS was high due to 3 factors: 360° encasement of the left ICA, nasopharyngeal ulceration, and the need for second reirradiation. Therefore, prophylactic embolization of the left ICA was planned despite the required carotid sacrifice.

Balloon test occlusion for cerebral perfusion assessment

To evaluate the feasibility of prophylactic embolization of the left ICA, a balloon test occlusion (BTO) was performed on December 22, 2016, with temporary occlusion of the left ICA using a balloon catheter (Fig. 2A). No neurologic symptoms occurred during the 20-minute occlusion, but distal ICA opacification via the circle of Willis was limited. The mean stump pressure was 40 mmHg, about 50% of systemic pressure, indicating insufficient cerebral blood flow. Consequently, prophylactic ICA embolization with low-flow superficial temporal artery–middle cerebral artery (STA–MCA) bypass was planned.

Reirradiation and surgical interventions

The second course of reirradiation was delivered using intensity modulated stereotactic radiation therapy (IMSRT), a technique that combines intensity modulation with stereotactic immobilization and localization to enhance dose conformality to the planning target volume while reducing dose to surrounding normal tissues and adjacent organs at risk.12,13 To avoid dose attenuation from embolization coils, intensity modulated stereotactic radiation therapy was performed before embolization between January 10 and February 10, 2017. The prescribed dose was 72 Gy in 24 fractions, corresponding to an equivalent dose in 2-Gy fractions (EQD2) of 78 Gy for an assumed α/β ratio of 10 (Fig. 2B). Given the absence of alternative curative options, treatment planning was intentionally tumor-control driven, prioritizing delivery of a definitive tumoricidal dose despite the recognized risks associated with repeat irradiation.

Given the patient’s extreme vascular risk profile, vascular reconstruction and parent artery occlusion were deliberately scheduled soon after reirradiation to reduce the risk of early catastrophic hemorrhage rather than delaying intervention to await overt necrotic changes. On March 9, 2017, STA–MCA bypass and coil embolization of the left ICA were performed on the same day. The embolized segment corresponded to the high-dose field (>80% of the prescribed dose), extending from the cervical (C1) through the petrous (C2) and cavernous (C4) segments of the ICA as defined by the Bouthillier classification (Fig. 2B, right panel).

Results

Tumor control and neurologic course

The tumor regressed and disappeared within a year, with complete local control maintained during follow-up (Fig. 3A).

Tumor control and normal tissue response. (A) Axial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) images at the level of the cavernous sinus. Approximately 1 y after the second reirradiation, the enhancing tumor gradually regressed and subsequently disappeared, with sustained control thereafter (green arrow). Ten months after reirradiation, a new contrast-enhancing lesion suggestive of radiation-induced necrosis appeared in the left temporal lobe, which evolved into a cyst by 3 y and 8 mo and then stabilized (red arrow). (B) The axial MRI images at the nasopharyngeal level. The enhancing tumor resolved (green arrow). Approximately 4 y after reirradiation, mucosal thickening with contrast enhancement appeared, but it remained radiologically stable. Gradual mucosal sloughing was first observed 6 y 6 mo after treatment and progressed to cavity formation, as demonstrated in the image obtained 7 y 4 mo posttreatment (red arrow).

Immediately after surgery, cerebral perfusion was maintained via low-flow bypass without neurologic deficits. Despite loss of antegrade ophthalmic flow, collateral external carotid circulation preserved vision.

At the time of the second reirradiation, the patient had mild-to-moderate deficits of cranial nerves V, VI, VII, VIII, and IX, causing dysphagia, dysphonia, and orofacial dysfunction. Intermittent left-sided diplopia, suggesting new impairment of the fourth cranial nerve (trochlear nerve), was noted but remained stable during follow-up.

Late complications and terminal decline

The patient was followed for 7 years and 9 months. Mild intermittent epistaxis occurred 5 years after the second reirradiation. Six months later, imaging showed persistent enhancement from the nasopharyngeal wall to the skull base without size increase. Dysphagia and dysphonia slightly worsened.

At 6.5 years posttreatment, partial sloughing of the nasopharyngeal mucosa and soft tissue created a cavity between the Rosenmüller fossa and parapharyngeal space (Fig. 3B). The condition temporarily stabilized. However, on November 28, 2024—7.8 years after the second reirradiation—the patient was hospitalized with fever, headache, and altered consciousness. Coil exposure through necrotic mucosa and bacterial meningitis were observed, followed by left hemiparesis, medial medullary infarction, and brainstem abscess, resulting in a bedridden state. After stabilization, the patient was transferred to a palliative care facility, where follow-up was concluded.

The patient’s condition deteriorated abruptly, although local tumor control was maintained until the final stage. For 7 years and 9 months after treatment, the patient remained functionally independent. He was able to ambulate independently, live at home for most of the follow-up period, and maintain oral intake with compensatory strategies despite persistent cranial nerve deficits affecting swallowing and phonation. Vision was preserved through collateral external carotid circulation, and cerebral perfusion remained stable without ischemic neurologic events. Notably, no CBOS occurred during the entire follow-up period.

Discussion

This case illustrates the feasibility of a second reirradiation in a patient with a second recurrence of head and neck cancer and extremely high risk of CBOS, through a preplanned strategy incorporating ICA sacrifice with pre-emptive modification of cerebral blood flow. Balloon occlusion testing predicted inadequate collateral circulation, prompting an STA–MCA bypass followed by ICA embolization. To our knowledge, this is the first report to describe the pre-emptive integration of planned carotid sacrifice with cerebral revascularization specifically to enable reirradiation in such an extremely high-risk clinical setting.

CBOS is a life-threatening complication of reirradiation. Yamazaki et al3 reported an 8.4% incidence, with a median onset of 5 months (range, 0-69) and a 68.8% mortality, most deaths occurring within 1 month.3 Furthermore, Yamazaki et al3 identified 3 major risk factors: tumor encasement of the carotid artery >180°, ulceration, and a history of prior neck irradiation. They demonstrated that the 12-month CBOS-free survival rates were 100%, 95%, and 84% among patients with 0, 1, or 2 risk factors, respectively, but decreased to 25% at 6 months in all three.14 Our patient exhibited all 3 risk factors, along with complete 360° ICA encasement and a history of reirradiation, making this a third radiation therapy course, further compounding the risk. These findings underscore the need for pre-emptive vascular risk mitigation in the present case.

Roh et al15 noted that despite the use of covered stents or embolization, delayed rebleeding, infection, and poor long-term outcomes remained major challenges. In contrast, Lan et al16 reported that pre-emptive embolization effectively prevented CBOS in patients with imaging evidence of CBOS risk before boron neutron capture therapy for recurrent head and neck cancer. However, they did not address strategies to maintain cerebral perfusion. Postonset management of CBOS—or even early intervention—may not ensure reliable outcomes. In neurosurgery, several studies have assessed the feasibility of carotid sacrifice by combining BTO with cerebral perfusion imaging.17 These studies showed that over 70% of patients have sufficient collateral circulation through the circle of Willis, permitting carotid ligation or embolization. However, temporary ICA occlusion during BTO has been associated with fatal cerebral infarction in 3% to 8% of patients, whereas classic hemodynamic studies have shown that up to 28% may develop significant hypoperfusion under hypotensive challenge.18 Taken together, these observations suggest that even when carotid sacrifice is technically feasible, a substantial subset of patients remains vulnerable to ischemic complications. This consideration is particularly relevant in patients at high risk of CBOS, for whom ICA sacrifice—while effective for hemostasis—may carry a high risk of fatal outcome or severe neurological sequelae because of compromised perfusion. Importantly, this vascular strategy carries inherent procedural risks. Cerebral revascularization has been associated with perioperative stroke rates of approximately 4% to 9% and morbidity around 5% to 6%,19 while parent artery occlusion carries an estimated 5% risk of permanent ischemic complications.10 Even BTO, although generally safe, has been linked to ischemic events in approximately 3% to 4% of patients.20 Accordingly, such an aggressive preventive approach should be reserved for carefully selected patients in whom the predicted risk of carotid blowout outweighs the procedural risks.

In the context of nasopharyngeal carcinoma, Zhao et al6 conducted a large management strategy. Their analysis demonstrated that patients classified as high-risk or threatened carotid involvement experienced poor outcomes when vascular intervention was deferred until overt hemorrhage, underscoring the importance of early risk identification and proactive management.6 Similarly, Liu et al7 evaluated prophylactic ICA management in patients with postradiation nasopharyngeal necrosis, a severe late complication frequently associated with carotid exposure and infection. They reported that early or preventive vascular intervention in carefully selected high-risk patients could reduce catastrophic hemorrhagic events, supporting the concept that vascular management should be considered before the onset of carotid rupture.7 Furthermore, Jiao et al21 highlighted that while carotid sacrifice can be effective for hemorrhage control, cerebral perfusion must be carefully evaluated, and cerebral revascularization should be considered in patients with insufficient collateral circulation to mitigate the risk of ischemic neurological complications.21 Together, these findings emphasize that in nasopharyngeal carcinoma with extreme vascular risk, vascular assessment and intervention should be integrated into comprehensive treatment planning rather than reserved as a reactive measure.

In our case, a pre-emptive BTO detected inadequate perfusion, followed by an STA–MCA bypass with ICA embolization. The patient remained asymptomatic for cerebral hypoperfusion immediately after treatment and throughout 7 years and 9 months of follow-up.

A particularly important determinant of long-term outcome in this case was progressive radiation-induced nasopharyngeal mucosal and soft-tissue necrosis complicated by infection. Radiation-associated nasopharyngeal necrosis is increasingly recognized as a severe and potentially life-threatening late toxicity after reirradiation, frequently associated with skull base complications and vascular exposure. Although adjunctive hyperbaric oxygen therapy may provide symptomatic or local benefit in selected patients, prior studies have shown that conservative management alone often fails to achieve durable control in advanced necrosis. In retrospect, earlier recognition of progressive necrosis as a high-risk condition may have warranted consideration of more aggressive multidisciplinary interventions, such as earlier surgical debridement with vascularized flap reconstruction, as suggested in recent studies on radiation-associated nasopharyngeal necrosis.22,23

Nevertheless, although demonstrated in only a single case, this carefully planned, multidisciplinary approach enabled durable local control of twice-recurrent nasopharyngeal carcinoma for 7 years and 9 months without the occurrence of CBOS, while preserving cerebral perfusion and neurological function. During this prolonged clinical course, despite chronic cranial nerve deficits and progressive local tissue complications, the patient was able to maintain independent living at home for most of the follow-up period, underscoring that meaningful functional independence may be preserved in highly selected patients undergoing aggressive salvage treatment. At the same time, the clinical course highlights the substantial burden of late toxicities associated with reirradiation and underscores the complexity of treatment decision-making in this setting. This case illustrates the feasibility of integrating pre-emptive vascular management into comprehensive treatment planning for patients with extreme carotid risk, while emphasizing the need for vigilant assessment of competing risks and long-term complications. Ultimately, it suggests that definitive treatment may be considered in carefully selected patients who would otherwise be regarded as unsuitable for further therapy, provided that potential benefits and risks are thoroughly weighed.

Conclusion

In selected patients with recurrent head and neck cancer at extreme risk of CBOS, reirradiation may be considered within a carefully planned, multidisciplinary framework that incorporates pre-emptive vascular assessment and management. This teaching case highlights both the potential role of planned carotid sacrifice with cerebral revascularization in mitigating catastrophic vascular events and the substantial competing risks of severe late toxicities, underscoring the need for individualized decision-making and vigilant long-term follow-up.
