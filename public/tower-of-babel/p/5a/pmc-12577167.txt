Toward global reproductive justice: a universal framework for evaluating equity and autonomy in in-vitro fertilization policies
Authors: Ruoran Murphy Qiu, Renee Muthakana, Wenhui Mao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEquitable and ethical access to in-vitro fertilization (IVF) remains challenging globally. Existing evaluation frameworks often overlook critical dimensions of reproductive justice. This study develops and validates a Universal IVF Justice Framework, designed to systematically evaluate national IVF policies regarding equity and reproductive autonomy.MethodsWe conducted a systematic literature review (PubMed, Scopus; January 1, 2010 to June 11, 2025) to identify key policy dimensions and indicators, followed by expert consultations and pilot testing to ensure reliability. The framework was validated through comprehensive document reviews and double-coding of IVF policies from 24 diverse countries. Visualization tools were created to illustrate outcomes.ResultsAnalysis revealed three overarching dimensions that shape national IVF policy outcomes: economic and structural conditions, cultural and social contexts, and healthcare system factors. Substantial variation emerged across countries. At the micro-level, country-specific analyses exposed targeted strengths and weaknesses, supporting localized reform efforts. Visualizations such as radar and scatter plots highlighted performance disparities. At the macro-level, high-income countries generally scored higher, driven by broader insurance coverage, inclusive legal protections, and greater geographic access. In contrast, low- and middle-income countries often faced challenges in affordability, rural service delivery, LGBTQ+ inclusivity, and psychosocial support.ConclusionsThe Universal IVF Justice Framework offers an empirically-grounded and ethically informed approach for evaluating IVF policies globally. By clearly identifying policy strengths and critical gaps, it supports evidence-based decision-making and targeted reforms aimed at enhancing equity and reproductive autonomy in IVF services worldwide.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12978-025-02172-1.Keywords: IVF policy, Reproductive justice, Equity, Autonomy, Global health, Policy evaluation, Women’s health

Introduction

Infertility, recognized by the World Health Organization (WHO) [1] as a disease of the reproductive system, affects approximately one in six individuals globally and has contributed to growing demand for assisted reproductive technologies (ART), particularly in-vitro fertilization (IVF). However, this demand has not translated into equitable access and IVF remains inaccessible to many due to economic, legal, geographic, and sociocultural barriers. In many low-income settings, fewer than 5% of women experiencing infertility can access IVF services [1], with barriers including limited public funding, urban-centered services, shortages of trained specialists, and exclusionary eligibility criteria for single individuals and non-normative families [2, 3]. Legal restrictions also compound inequities: IVF is banned or severely curtailed in several Middle Eastern and African countries due to religious or political constraints [4]. Even within high-income countries, disparities persist; for example, in the United States, only 27% of women under 44 have any form of insurance coverage for IVF, and utilization remains disproportionately concentrated among affluent, predominantly white populations [5].

National policies play a pivotal role in shaping who has access to IVF and under what conditions. For example, Israel offers publicly funded IVF for a broad margin of the population and permits multiple cycles, whereas countries like Bulgaria enforce strict eligibility requirements and provide minimal state support, resulting in high out-of-pocket costs [4]. These cross-national differences reflect various interpretations of reproductive rights and underscore global disparities in access to care.

Despite increasing attention to equity in reproductive health, there is currently no globally accepted framework for evaluating national IVF policies through the lens of reproductive justice. Existing frameworks offer valuable conceptual tools but are limited in scope or applicability, for example, the WHO Eastern Mediterranean Regional Office (EMRO) Framework [6] focuses on system-level reproductive health domains such as financing and regulation but does not address ARTs in depth. The Economic Social Support for Treatment Reimbursement (ESSTR) system [7] incorporates economic and social support dimensions relevant to ART but lacks a standardized scoring mechanism and has seen limited adoption beyond its original context. Patient-centered models [8] offer insights into individual experiences—such as emotional burden and perceived fairness—but are not designed for policy evaluation or cross-country comparison. Similarly, the American Society for Reproductive Medicine (ASRM) [5] promotes access and equity within the United States but does not provide a globally scalable assessment tool. Moreover, medical advances such as IVF have expanded fertility opportunities for LGBTQ+ and gender-nonconforming populations; however, even though the ASRM has issued inclusive national guidelines, many fertility providers remain unaware of systemic inequities faced by sexual minority groups, who continue to encounter institutional discrimination, legal barriers, poorer patient experiences, and reduced trust, particularly among those experiencing intersectional marginalization [9].

These frameworks tend to focus on narrow geographies, omit critical social and cultural reproductive justice concerns, or lack tools for comparative policy assessment. To address these limitations, this study introduces a Universal IVF Justice Framework—a multidimensional, normatively grounded rubric designed to evaluate national IVF policies in terms of both equity and reproductive autonomy. Equity in this context involves the proactive inclusion of diverse populations, such as single women and LGBTQ+ couples, ensuring equitable access irrespective of marital status, sexual orientation, gender identity, or socioeconomic status [10]. Within the context of IVF and other ARTs, reproductive autonomy holds ethical significance under the reproductive justice framework, which emphasizes that reproductive freedom requires addressing intersecting social and economic factors affecting an individual’s ability and resources to make reproductive choices [11]. Reproductive autonomy here extends beyond mere access, highlighting the importance of enabling individuals to pursue IVF under ethically acceptable conditions that balance personal freedom with the responsible use of IVF technologies.

In addition to providing a detailed discussion and standardization of popular factors likely affecting equity and autonomy, the Universal IVF Justice Framework integrates frequently overlooked dimensions—such as psychosocial support, inclusion of diverse gender and sexual identities, and embryo regulation—facilitating systematic, cross-country comparisons. However, it also recognizes the necessity of certain reasonable limitations, such as restrictions against embryo selection solely based on sex preference, and responsible limits on publicly funded IVF cycles to ensure equitable resource allocation. Serving as a replicable diagnostic tool, it aims to assist policymakers, clinicians, and researchers in identifying specific policy gaps, guiding targeted reforms, and advancing global reproductive justice in fertility care.

Methods

Scope and settings

Existing approaches to evaluating IVF policy are limited in scope: they frequently emphasize biomedical outcomes, lack systematic attention to social and cultural dimensions of reproductive justice, and do not provide standardized tools for cross-country comparison. These gaps highlight the need for a more comprehensive and globally applicable evaluative framework.

This study therefore aims to develop a Universal IVF Justice Framework, addressing the central research question: How can national in-vitro fertilization (IVF) policies be systematically assessed with respect to equity and/or reproductive autonomy? Within this framework, equity is defined as the fair and impartial access to reproductive healthcare services, ensuring that all individuals, regardless of socioeconomic status or demographic background, have comparable opportunities to utilize IVF. In contrast, reproductive autonomy refers to individuals’ ability to make fully informed and voluntary reproductive decisions—such as whether and when to pursue IVF—free from coercion or undue external influence. These two dimensions are interconnected but analytically distinct. Equitable access to IVF services is a fundamental precondition for enabling reproductive autonomy. At the same time, autonomy must be safeguarded even when access is guaranteed. Integrating equity as a distributive concern and reproductive autonomy as an agency concern, the framework bridges structural and individual reproductive justice aspects, providing a normative basis for comprehensive policy evaluation.

For the purposes of this study, “IVF” is used as an umbrella term that includes intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI), given its widespread integration into modern IVF protocols. Although ICSI was initially developed to address male-factor infertility, it is now commonly used across a range of IVF cycles. Therefore, unless otherwise specified, policy references to IVF in this manuscript are assumed to include ICSI. It is worth noting that, given the interconnected nature of IVF with other ARTs—such as donor conception and embryo testing—these related aspects are discussed only insofar as they directly influence IVF policy domains. We emphasize this distinction clearly, recognizing the additional ethical, equity, and autonomy complexities introduced by broader ART practices, while maintaining analytical rigor and specificity.

Data collection and screening

Search strategy

We performed systematic literature search in PubMed and Scopus databases to comprehensively identify scholarly articles, including empirical studies, conceptual analyses, policy reviews, ethical analyses, and qualitative and quantitative research, that addressed ethical, policy, and social dimensions of IVF. The search strategy was structured around three distinct conceptual clusters:

Topic of Interest (IVF): To target studies specifically centered on IVF, the following search terms were utilized: “in vitro fertilization,” “IVF,” and the MeSH term “Fertilization in Vitro” (PubMed). These terms were applied within the Title/Abstract fields in PubMed and TITLE-ABS fields in Scopus to ensure articles explicitly discussed IVF.

These terms were applied within the Title/Abstract fields in PubMed and TITLE-ABS fields in Scopus to ensure articles explicitly discussed IVF.

These three clusters were combined using Boolean operators (AND, OR, NOT) to ensure precision and thematic relevance. Specifically, articles had to satisfy all three search clusters simultaneously (IVF-related terms AND Ethics/Policy/Justice-related terms AND Access/Inequality-related terms) to be included in the initial screening.

Exclusion criteria and filters

Articles primarily focused on biomedical or clinical outcomes were explicitly excluded. This included, but was not limited to, publications categorized as “clinical trial,” “randomized controlled trial,” or those emphasizing endpoints such as “pregnancy rate,” “live birth,” “embryo quality,” or other laboratory/biological metrics. Studies limited to reporting technical protocols (e.g., stimulation regimens, lab embryology techniques) or drug efficacy trials were also excluded, as they did not engage with ethical, policy, or social dimensions. By contrast, studies were retained if they explicitly addressed questions of access, regulation, financing, inclusion, or patient decision-making, even when embedded within a broader biomedical discussion. The scoping stage used to construct the framework drew exclusively on peer-reviewed studies indexed in PubMed or Scopus; grey literature was consulted only as contextual background and was not included in the scoping screen. Searches were further restricted to English-language publications from January 1, 2010 to June 11, 2025.

Search outcomes

The structured search yielded approximately 789 articles from PubMed and 747 from Scopus(see Appendix A for complete searching terms). After removing duplicates through Zotero 7.0, a total of 1,087 unique articles remained. Rubric development

A five-point rating scale operationalized subthemes, established by dividing global policy performance levels identified from literature into empirically-informed intervals. This approach aimed to balance discriminative sensitivity—clearly distinguishing policy performance variations across countries—and inter-rater reliability, ensuring consistency and reproducibility of scoring between independent researchers. Pilot coding was independently performed by two researchers on policy documents from five representative country cases, demonstrating substantial inter-coder agreement (Cohen’s ). Inter-coder reliability was calculated using Cohen’s Kappa, reflecting agreement beyond chance.

The rubric underwent two rounds of external feedback and refinement, incorporating input from three scholars with expertise in public health policy, medical ethics, and global health. These experts—selected for their academic standing and domain knowledge—provided feedback on the clarity and practical relevance of scoring anchors, based on a draft rubric and sample cases. When coding discrepancies arose, both coders independently re-checked sources and, where ambiguity persisted, sought targeted input from an expert panel using a standardized query template; consensus was reached through documented discussion. The rubric then underwent two structured consultation rounds with three external scholars (public health policy, medical ethics, global health) selected a priori for domain expertise and independence from scoring. In Round 1, experts provided written feedback on a draft codebook and sample cases; in Round 2, a consensus meeting resolved remaining differences using a pre-specified majority-agreement rule. Expert input informed refinements to indicator definitions, scoring anchors, and boundary conditions, enhancing the rubric’s theoretical coherence and practical utility.

The IVF policy documents analyzed comprised (1) official government-issued materials—national reproductive health laws, IVF-specific legislation, regulatory guidelines, insurance coverage criteria, and clinical practice guidelines; (2) peer-reviewed publications on policy implementation and effectiveness (January 1, 2010 to June 11, 2025); and (3) grey literature—World Health Organization (WHO) reports, national health-ministry publications, policy databases, and resources from professional bodies such as the European Society of Human Reproduction and Embryology (ESHRE) and the American Society for Reproductive Medicine (ASRM)—also limited to January 1, 2010 to June 11, 2025. Two reviewers independently searched, screened, and quality-appraised these sources using the AACODS checklist (Authority, Accuracy, Coverage, Objectivity, Date, Significance), resolving discrepancies by consensus. When sources conflicted, we prioritized, in order: (i) current statutes/regulations and official ministry circulars; (ii) national clinical guidelines and peer-reviewed publications; grey sources were used primarily to corroborate enactment dates, coverage scope, and recent policy changes. Drawing on multiple document types enabled a comprehensive evaluation of the legal, clinical, and sociocultural factors shaping IVF access and reproductive autonomy. A complete list of primary data sources, organized by country, is provided in Appendix C.

Limitation

While this selection ensured broad global and institutional diversity, certain limitations should be noted. Some regions remain underrepresented, such as Central Asia and parts of the Middle East, and the reliance on English-language sources may have excluded relevant national policy documents published in other languages. Furthermore, although the sample captures variation across universal public systems, mixed models, and privatized structures, it does not account for subnational differences in institutional implementation (e.g., provincial or state-level variation within countries such as India, China, or the United States). These geographical and institutional constraints are further elaborated in the Discussion.

Two coders independently scored each country’s policy documents using the rubric. Discrepancies were resolved through iterative discussions, with persistent disagreements referred to domain experts. Radar plots and three-dimensional scatter plots (executed in Python 3.12.6 and Excel 2024) were used to visualize cross-national comparisons and identify targeted areas for policy reform.

Results

The development of IVF justice framework

The thematic synthesis was structured around three overarching dimensions—(a) Economic and Structural Factors, (b) Cultural and Social Factors, and (c) Healthcare Systematic Factors—which together reflect the multifaceted determinants of reproductive justice in in-vitro fertilization (IVF) policy. The following narrative synthesizes findings across dimensions, preserving source intent (e.g., [1, 13]).

Economic and structural factors

Economic and structural factors play a foundational role in shaping access to IVF by determining who can afford treatment, whether financial protections exist, and how geographically reachable services are. Across countries, the cost of treatment is one of the most significant barriers. Out-of-pocket expenses can lead to catastrophic health spending—exceeding 50% of disposable income in high-income countries and surpassing GDP per capita in many Low- and Middle-Income Countries (LMICs) [1, 13, 14]. Even in the United States, a single IVF cycle can cost between USD 12,000 and 25,000, making it inaccessible for many [15]. Cost was prioritized as a subtheme because it directly determines who can engage with fertility care in the first place.

Insurance coverage serves as a critical counterbalance to these financial burdens. In the U.S., fewer than 25% of infertile couples have adequate coverage, although states with mandated insurance show improved access [16, 17]. By contrast, countries like Sweden and Japan have included IVF in their public health systems to promote broader utilization [18, 19]. These differences are further magnified when comparing high- and low-income contexts: for example, Denmark and Australia provide state-subsidized or universal IVF coverage, ensuring multiple cycles are available at minimal personal cost, whereas in Nigeria and India, treatment is concentrated in private urban clinics and financed almost entirely out-of-pocket, creating prohibitive barriers for the majority of the population. This subtheme signals the degree to which governments or insurers actively reduce financial risk, which often determines the scale and equity of access.

Physical accessibility is another major determinant. Rural–urban disparities frequently leave residents outside major cities without reasonable access to services. Patients in rural areas face longer travel times, greater indirect costs, and reduced treatment success rates due to delays in care [20, 21]. While interventions like telemedicine and satellite clinics offer promise, their rollout has been uneven. This subtheme was included to capture the spatial dimension of structural inequality, recognizing that geography can function as a gatekeeper to reproductive care.

Taken together, these three areas—cost, coverage, and location—form the material scaffolding on which equitable IVF access rests.

Cultural and social factors

Access and reproductive autonomy are also shaped by cultural and social forces, which govern not only who is permitted to receive treatment, but under what conditions they do so. In many countries, religious and cultural norms directly influence the content and permissibility of IVF policies. Whereas Judaism, Hinduism, and Buddhism generally support IVF, Catholic and Orthodox Christian traditions tend to oppose third-party reproduction—especially gamete donation and embryo manipulation—leading to restrictive legislation in countries where these faiths hold sway [22]. This subtheme highlights how normative belief systems can set ethical and legal limits on reproductive technologies. The influence of family dynamics—understood here as culturally embedded gender roles, familial expectations, and power hierarchies within households—often manifests through social pressures and individual experience rather than formal policies. In some cultural settings, infertility is treated as a woman’s failure, and those affected may face stigma, coercion, or diminished status [23]. In parts of Africa, for example, infertility can result in polygamy, social exclusion, or loss of property rights [24, 25]. In China and other Confucian-influenced societies, reproductive pressure may stem from expectations to fulfill filial duties rather than personal desire [26, 27]. This subtheme was included to account for internal social structures that constrain individual reproductive freedom, regardless of formal access. Exclusion categorized under sexual identity is used broadly to reflect how reproductive policies include or exclude individuals based not only on sexual orientation, but also on marital status and gender identity, recognizing that policies may implicitly or explicitly marginalize or discriminate against individuals across these overlapping categories. For example, single women and transgender individuals may be denied access to IVF even when their exclusion is not directly related to sexual orientation. While some Western countries extend IVF access to single women and LGBTQ+ individuals [28], many others—such as Greece—restrict access to heterosexual, married couples [1]. Transgender individuals face additional barriers, including inadequate access to fertility preservation and gender-affirming reproductive care [29]. This subtheme captures the institutionalization of heteronormativity in reproductive health systems and its consequences for equitable inclusion [30, 31]. Another often-overlooked factor is education and reproductive literacy. When individuals lack accurate, accessible information, they are less able to evaluate risks, understand treatment processes, or advocate for themselves [32]. These barriers disproportionately affect those from marginalized or lower-income backgrounds. Including this subtheme ensures that the framework accounts not just for structural or legal barriers, but also for the cognitive and informational resources that enable genuine reproductive autonomy [33]. Collectively, these four subthemes illustrate how power, identity, and cultural expectation shape both who gets access to IVF and how freely that access can be exercised.

Healthcare system factors

Equity in IVF policy cannot be fully assessed without examining the healthcare system itself—specifically, how services are delivered and what ethical standards govern that delivery. One of the most overlooked areas is psychosocial support, which remains sparse despite clear evidence of its importance. IVF treatment frequently leads to anxiety, depression, and relationship strain [34, 35], effects that are often intensified by hormonal medications and emotional uncertainty [36]. This subtheme assesses whether emotional support is treated as an essential part of care or left to patients to navigate on their own. Another crucial component is informed consent, which is foundational to ethical medical practice but often inconsistently applied. Patients frequently report receiving inadequate explanations about treatment risks, success probabilities, or long-term implications [37]. Clinicians, constrained by time or training, may be unable to offer comprehensive counseling [38]. Concerns about future maternal and offspring health only add to the complexity of these decisions [39]. This subtheme evaluates whether systems facilitate meaningful, informed engagement or simply meet minimal procedural requirements [40]. Finally, embryo regulation presents some of the most ethically and legally complex challenges. Inconsistent or absent national policies on embryo creation, storage, and disposal have led to documented cases of mismanagement and legal disputes [41]. The phenomenon of “abandoned embryos”—those left in storage without patient directives—raises unresolved ethical dilemmas [42]. Where regulation is lacking, clinics often self-govern, increasing the risk of exploitation—for example, through the use of embryos in research or destruction without explicit consent—and inequity, such as offering preferential treatment to wealthier patients while limiting options for others [43]. This subtheme probes the presence and enforceability of policies that govern embryonic material, serving as a litmus test for how seriously systems treat ethical responsibility in reproductive care. These healthcare-focused subthemes highlight that trustworthy IVF systems depend on ethical safeguards, attention to emotional well-being, and clear procedures.

Several potentially relevant factors were excluded from the final framework due to insufficient empirical support or limited generalizability. For instance, factors such as political stability, media influence, and commercial dynamics—including pharmaceutical pricing and private-sector dominance—were identified in some studies [44–46] but lacked consistent documentation across contexts. Additionally, demographic aging, cross-border reproductive care, and migration-related barriers to access [47–49] were deemed highly context-specific and therefore not broadly applicable within this study’s comparative framework. These exclusions were intentionally made to maintain the framework’s comparability, clarity, and relevance; however, future updates may consider incorporating such factors as further evidence becomes available.

This framework aims to help policymakers, scholars, and stakeholders identify and address inequalities in IVF policy, improving both access and reproductive autonomy. The next section elaborates on its application across different policy environments. The framework is composed of three dimensions and a total of ten specific elements that encapsulate the critical factors identified. Each dimension contributed unique insights: the economic and structural findings underscored the necessity of affordability and resource allocation; the cultural and social findings illuminated the less tangible but powerful role of beliefs, norms, and rights; and the healthcare system findings highlighted the importance of service delivery models and quality.

Constructing rubrics for evaluation

The IVF Justice Rubric was developed as a standalone evaluative instrument designed to systematically assess national IVF policies across ten critical indicators related to access, inclusion, and reproductive autonomy. Importantly, this rubric was created independently of empirical country analyses or policy data to maintain normative independence and ensure its generalizability. Indicators were prioritized based on two primary criteria: first, their normative centrality as identified in the scholarly literature on reproductive justice, global health equity, and bioethics; and second, the practical feasibility of acquiring reliable, publicly available, and internationally comparable data. This rigorous approach resulted in a rubric that is both theoretically robust and empirically applicable.

Each indicator was chosen to capture a distinct and well-supported barrier or enabler to reproductive justice. IVF Cost and Affordability was selected due to overwhelming evidence that financial constraints are a leading cause of unmet fertility care need worldwide. For instance, in many Low- and Middle-Income Countries (LMICs), a single IVF cycle may cost more than the average annual household income [13, 14], effectively excluding the majority of the population. Insurance Coverage was included as a structural counterbalance to direct costs, signaling whether states offer financial risk protection. According to the WHO [1], countries that provide mandated IVF insurance or subsidies—such as Israel or parts of Western Europe—achieve substantially higher utilization rates and lower socioeconomic disparities in access [16].

The Rural–Urban Disparity indicator addresses geographic inequities, particularly the concentration of fertility services in major urban centers. Studies show that rural populations face up to five times greater travel distances and reduced IVF success rates due to delays in initiating treatment [20, 21]. Education and Literacy was prioritized for its foundational role in enabling informed decision-making. This indicator refers to broader reproductive health literacy, including but not limited to IVF-specific knowledge, encompassing individuals’ ability to understand fertility, treatment options, and associated risks. The WHO [1] identifies reproductive health literacy as a key determinant of treatment uptake and safety, and gaps in knowledge disproportionately affect women in marginalized communities [50].

Religious and Cultural Factors were selected to account for faith-based restrictions that shape legal access, ethical boundaries, and clinical guidelines. In several majority-Muslim and Catholic countries, embryo handling, gamete donation, and surrogacy are significantly restricted or outright banned due to religious doctrine [22, 51]. The Family Dynamics indicator addresses intrafamilial power structures and gender norms, which often limit women’s autonomy in reproductive decision-making. Anthropological literature has shown that in contexts where family honor or patriarchal authority dominate, women may be pressured into or excluded from IVF against their own preferences [52, 53].

Sexual Identity was included to capture the legal and practical exclusions faced by LGBTQ+ individuals. In many countries, IVF access is restricted to heterosexual married couples, leaving same-sex couples and single individuals without recourse to care. Human rights audits have documented these exclusions in at least 65 countries as of 2022 [54, 55], underscoring the systemic barriers to reproductive inclusion. Psychosocial Support was chosen because IVF is often associated with elevated rates of anxiety, depression, and relationship strain, yet only a subset of countries mandate access to fertility counseling. Meta-analyses have demonstrated that structured psychosocial support improves patient well-being and treatment adherence [56, 57].

Embryo Regulation was included to reflect the ethical and legal frameworks governing the creation, storage, and destruction of embryos—areas central to reproductive ethics. This indicator captures whether national policies permit key procedures such as embryo cryopreservation, limits on storage duration, disposal protocols, and preimplantation genetic testing (PGT). While emerging technologies like embryo editing were considered during rubric development, they were excluded from scoring due to their limited real-world implementation and lack of legal authorization in most countries. Regulatory differences—such as restrictions on embryo storage or embryo research—significantly influence patient options and clinical practice [58, 59]. Informed Consent was selected as a foundational element of ethical care. The quality and comprehensiveness of consent procedures directly impact patient reproductive autonomy, especially where complex interventions such as embryo transfer, donor use, or genetic testing are involved. International reviews consistently identify informed consent as a variable practice across fertility settings, with some contexts failing to meet minimum ethical standards [60, 61].

To effectively accommodate the diversity of IVF policy environments, the ten selected indicators were internally stratified based on careful consideration of their theoretical significance, practical feasibility, and observed variation in global policy performance. Additionally, the researchers also recognized the necessity of certain reasonable limitations in specific sociocultural contexts—such as restrictions on embryo selection based solely on sex preference and responsible limits on publicly funded IVF cycles to ensure equitable resource allocation. Finally, each indicator was structured on a standardized five-point ordinal scale, designed to reflect actual global variability rather than fixed universal benchmarks. Figure 1 shows the specific codebook used in the Universal IVF Justice Evaluation Rubric(see Appendix D for complete codebook). The rubric’s overall structure and scoring approach were further refined through iterative consultations with experts and five-countries pilot testing, ensuring its rigorous theoretical grounding and adaptability across diverse national contexts.

Application in practice

In practical application, the Universal IVF Justice Framework and its associated evaluation rubric serve as structured analytical instruments for systematically assessing reproductive healthcare policies at both micro-level (individual countries or paired country comparisons) and macro-level (cross-national analyses). The rubric was developed independently, grounded in extensive literature on reproductive justice, global health equity, and bioethics, thereby ensuring its utility as a generalizable and replicable evaluative tool unaffected by the specific countries subsequently selected for analysis. Evaluators utilize the rubric’s detailed codebook (see Appendix E for blank codebook) to assign scores across three primary dimensions, each comprising several clearly defined sub-indicators. Each sub-indicator is rated on a standardized five-point scale, ranging from 0 (most restrictive) to 5 (most inclusive). The comprehensive evaluation approach by systematically referencing country-specific policy documents, national healthcare reports, international reproductive rights audits, peer-reviewed literature, and expert clinical guidelines, encompassing both policy provisions and their practical implementation, allows us to assess not only the intended design of policies but also their actual outcomes and effectiveness in real-world contexts.

At the micro-level, the rubric supports detailed assessments of individual countries, enabling policymakers and researchers to pinpoint specific areas of policy strength and weakness and conduct insightful paired country comparisons. This approach facilitates context-sensitive evaluations and strategic benchmarking, enhancing targeted policy interventions. At the macro-level, the rubric was systematically applied to in-vitro fertilization (IVF) policy data from 24 countries across six continents. This broader application underscores the rubric’s capability to provide nuanced insights into individual national contexts while simultaneously facilitating robust cross-national comparisons. Consequently, it enables identification of global patterns, structural inequalities, and overarching ethical tensions within international reproductive healthcare systems, ultimately informing equitable, inclusive, and reproductive autonomy-promoting policy interventions worldwide.

Micro-level assessment: individual and paired country evaluation

At the micro-level, the Universal IVF Justice Framework offers individual countries a structured tool for systematically assessing their IVF policy landscape. This internal evaluation mechanism enables policymakers to clearly identify strengths, weaknesses, and areas needing reform across three key dimensions and ten subthemes. Aligning national policy components and their status as described in existing literature with the rubric’s evaluative criteria allows for precise identification of underperforming areas requiring strategic intervention.

To visually represent these micro-level rubric-based assessments, we developed the Radar Plot of IVF Policy Performance, providing a comprehensive yet intuitive illustration of each country’s performance. By plotting average dimension scores on a standardized five-point scale, the radar plot clearly reveals imbalances and highlights internal disparities, facilitating targeted policy reform and informed investment decisions. To aid in comparison, each rubric score (0–5) was converted into a percentage scale (e.g., a score of 4 = 80%) for visual plotting; these are qualitative, rubric-based scores derived from policy evaluation criteria, not survey-based population statistics. Case-selection was guided by maximum-variation logic: we sought a country (i) with rapid ART market growth; (ii) situated in the upper-middle-income stratum; (iii) governed by strong state population policy; and (iv) exhibiting pronounced urban–rural health gradients. China uniquely satisfies all four criteria and serves as an analytically rich test case. Figure 2 exemplifies the application of this rubric to China. In the radar plot, the shaded triangle represents aggregated dimension scores: Economic and Structural Factors (top apex), Cultural and Social Factors (right apex), and Healthcare Systematic Factors (left apex). Each dimension comprises three to four specific sub-indicators, represented respectively by red circles (economic), green squares (cultural), and orange diamonds (healthcare), with scores ranging from 0 (most restrictive) to 5 (most inclusive).

Radar plot of IVF policy performance in China

In China’s case, the highest overall dimension score was observed within the Cultural and Social dimension (score 3), primarily driven by the absence of restrictive religious policies (Religious Factors = 5), reflecting China’s minimal religious influence on reproductive policymaking due to its specific historical and cultural context. However, substantial internal disparities exist, notably evident in low scores for sexual orientation equality and gender equity within familial contexts. These variations underscore the necessity of examining both aggregate dimension scores and specific sub-indicator details within individual radar plots.

China was specifically chosen for its exceptional representativeness and analytical necessity. Representing one of the world’s largest populations, marked urban–rural disparities, and a rapidly expanding assisted reproductive technology (ART) market within a complex regulatory context, China offers an ideal case for examining structural tensions and culturally embedded barriers characteristic of many middle-income countries. Additionally, the unique combination of strong governmental involvement in reproductive governance and Confucian socio-cultural norms enables rigorous testing of the framework’s capability to distinguish between aspirational policy objectives and their practical implementation, making China an academically essential example to demonstrate and validate the Universal IVF Justice Framework’s evaluative precision and utility. Moreover, as a high middle-income country with extensive ART availability yet inconsistent regulatory frameworks and uneven geographical access, China clearly exemplifies the policy complexities that the rubric seeks to capture. Data for China’s rubric scoring was compiled through triangulated analysis of peer-reviewed academic literature, national legislation, government health agency publications, and world health organization (WHO) regional reports. Where discrepancies or data gaps emerged—such as around psychosocial services and informed consent—priority was given to the most recent legal statutes and cross-validated with secondary expert commentary and non-governmental organization (NGO) reports. Challenges included limited transparency of private sector practices and variation in provincial-level implementation, which required conservative scoring estimates in the absence of uniformly reported outcomes. The disaggregated radar plot further highlights specific strengths (e.g., Insurance Coverage = 4) and notable gaps (e.g., Psychosocial Support = 2, Rural Access = 2), demonstrating the rubric’s practical utility for detailed, reflective policy diagnosis and targeted reform.

In addition to assessing a single country’s policy profile, the rubric can be used for pairwise comparisons, enabling nuanced analysis between two countries on a case-by-case basis. This application is particularly beneficial for facilitating contextualized policy analysis and enabling strategic benchmarking across diverse policy environments. As an illustrative example, India and Denmark were selected due to their sharply contrasting healthcare systems, legal frameworks, and cultural orientations toward reproductive care. India, as a lower-middle-income country with a largely privatized fertility sector and strong familial norms, highlights barriers in access and reproductive autonomy. Denmark, a high-income welfare state with universal healthcare and inclusive reproductive policies, exemplifies a rights-based model.

Data for both countries were drawn from a combination of national health policy documents, peer-reviewed academic studies, legal codes governing assisted reproduction, international human rights audits, and reports by organizations such as WHO and European Society of Human Reproduction and Embryology (ESHRE). Sources for Denmark included detailed reports from the Danish Health Authority and LGBTQ+ advocacy groups documenting inclusive care practices. For India, key references included national ART legislation, policy reviews published in The Lancet and BMJ, and ethnographic studies on stigma and access constraints.

India’s policy landscape shows relative affordability of IVF treatments but substantial equity gaps, including restricted rural access, exclusive legal frameworks limiting access to heterosexual married couples, and significant cultural stigma regarding infertility. Denmark demonstrates a policy framework robustly supporting reproductive justice across all dimensions, including public funding for IVF, inclusive legislation accommodating LGBTQ+ and single individuals, nationwide geographic accessibility, and integrated psychosocial support services. Evaluating these cases served to verify whether the rubrics could effectively capture and reflect variations in IVF policy performance. For ease of visualization, each sub-indicator is abbreviated using short codes: E1–E4 represent the four economic factors (Cost, Coverage, Access, Education); C1–C3 represent cultural and social factors (Religion, Sexual Identity, Family Roles); and H1–H3 represent healthcare system factors (Psychosocial Support, Embryo Regulation, Informed Consent).

Contrasting IVF justice framework scores: India vs. Denmark

Notably, the radar plot comparison reveals that while Denmark consistently scores higher than India across all three major dimensions, the relative distribution of scores within each country follows a similar pattern. This proportional similarity suggests that the structural composition of equity and autonomy challenges may be comparable, even though the intensity of these challenges clearly differs. Such alignment underscores the potential for cross-contextual policy learning, indicating that Denmark’s more developed strategies could offer relevant structural insights for informing policy reform in India, provided careful contextual adaptation. However, it is crucial to acknowledge that differences exist between countries—including economic capacity, healthcare infrastructure, and sociocultural contexts—that cannot be fully captured or compared by these individual indicators alone. Additionally, data extraction was complicated by discrepancies between official policies and clinical implementation, particularly in India, due to substantial regional variations. In response, we triangulated official government sources with empirical literature to ensure accuracy and mitigate bias from policies that might not reflect actual practice.

Macro-level analysis: global comparative evaluation

At the macro level, the Universal IVF Justice Framework serves as a powerful instrument for cross-national comparison and collaborative policy learning. By standardizing the evaluation of IVF policy along clearly defined dimensions and elements, the framework enables countries to engage in meaningful international benchmarking. To demonstrate its utility, this paper analyzed IVF-related policy data from 24 countries across six continents, deliberately selected to capture geographic diversity, variation in income levels, and a range of policy models—thereby providing a representative basis for testing the framework’s applicability across different socio-political and healthcare contexts. The analysis drew on a broad array of sources, including national healthcare policy reports, international reproductive rights audits, peer-reviewed academic literature, and expert clinical guidelines. Beyond evaluating country-level performance, the purpose of this analysis was to illustrate how the framework can reveal structural patterns, highlight ethical tensions, and assess the extent to which existing IVF systems advance or constrain equitable, inclusive, and autonomous reproductive care.

Discussion

This study developed and validated the Universal in-vitro fertilization (IVF) Justice Framework, a multidimensional, empirically grounded tool designed for systematically evaluating national IVF policies from the dual perspectives of equity and reproductive autonomy. The framework integrates qualitative interpretation with quantitative assessment through narrative synthesis, rubric-based scoring, rigorous double-coding validation, and comparative visualization techniques. This interdisciplinary and methodologically comprehensive approach provides structured, context-sensitive insights suitable for both academic research and practical policy evaluations. One of the major innovations of the framework lies in its incorporation of traditionally under-examined reproductive justice-relevant indicators—including rural accessibility, LGBTQ+ inclusivity, cultural stigma, psychosocial support, and informed consent practices. Unlike previous evaluative tools that frequently neglect these critical domains, our structured and empirically informed rubric facilitates systematic and nuanced comparative assessments across diverse economic, structural, social, and healthcare system contexts. By offering a replicable diagnostic tool, the framework enables policymakers and researchers to clearly identify policy strengths, pinpoint specific weaknesses, and guide targeted reforms. Consequently, it makes a contribution to global dialogues aimed at achieving greater reproductive justice and equitable IVF access.

Our findings reinforce and expand upon existing scholarship on reproductive justice within the context of IVF, emphasizing not only the right to access reproductive technologies but also the justice of how such tools are offered—including whether they enable informed choice, respect reproductive autonomy, and are equitably distributed across diverse populations. The dimensions of equity and autonomy, as operationalized in this study, reflect a synthesis of distributive justice (fair access to resources) and agency-based justice (freedom of choice), both of which are central to the reproductive justice paradigm. Our framework distinctly captures reproductive autonomy through indicators addressing informed choice, legal protections, psychosocial support, and freedom from coercive social or familial influences. This approach explicitly recognizes reproductive autonomy as a multifaceted construct requiring both individual-level agency and supportive policy environments. By integrating indicators often neglected in traditional health equity models—such as psychosocial support, sexual identity protections, and cultural stigma—the Universal IVF Justice Framework aligns with broader calls to recognize how systemic and intersectional barriers shape reproductive freedoms. This broader lens enables a more holistic understanding of reproductive justice, ensuring that policies are not only available but affirmatively inclusive.

Key lessons from this analysis underscore the importance of normative clarity, localized context, and multidimensional evaluation when striving toward global reproductive justice. Achieving reproductive justice in IVF access cannot be achieved by funding alone; it requires legal inclusion, culturally competent care, informational transparency, and respect for diverse family structures. Our framework offers a step toward such a comprehensive evaluative model, while recognizing that sustained engagement with context-specific realities is essential to ensure policies are not just equitable in theory, but also in practice.

Application of the framework to 24 diverse countries revealed substantial disparities in IVF policy fairness. Higher-scoring nations typically demonstrated strong public investment, comprehensive insurance, inclusive legislation, and proactive regulation, especially regarding informed consent and embryo protection. In contrast, lower-resource settings often showed limited coverage, patchy geographic access, and weak safeguards for emotional and informational support. While these findings align with previous literature emphasizing financial and geographic barriers, our framework expands the analysis by explicitly incorporating under-examined dimensions such as cultural stigma, sexual identity, and patient education. The visual outputs (radar plots and three-dimensional scatter plots) allowed for intuitive interpretation of complex policy variation, offering clear entry points for stakeholders interested in targeted reform.

Medical tourism also emerged as an important phenomenon in this context. Individuals in restrictive policy environments—due to marital status, age limits, sexual orientation, or embryo-related laws—often seek IVF services abroad to circumvent domestic barriers. While this expands access for some, it also introduces legal ambiguity, variable quality of care, and ethical concerns. Critically, it privileges those with financial means and may further entrench inequities for marginalized populations unable to travel, highlighting the need for more harmonized and rights-based international reproductive health policies.

Pronatalist policies also raise important concerns. While they may increase state support for fertility services, they often do so with the goal of boosting birth rates, which can conflict with principles of reproductive justice. In such cases, individuals—especially women—may face familial, societal or institutional pressure to reproduce, limiting true reproductive autonomy. These policies can also reinforce narrow definitions of family or gender roles, marginalizing those who do not conform to normative expectations. A reproductive justice-centered approach to IVF policy must ensure that support for fertility care does not translate into reproductive obligation.

The Universal IVF Justice Framework is particularly useful in contexts where policymakers, researchers, or advocacy groups aim to systematically evaluate existing national IVF policies, conduct updates of reproductive health legislation, perform cross-national comparisons, or benchmark policies against regional or international best practices. Translating the rubric’s diagnostics into action, we outline a sequenced reform agenda mapped to the indicators: (i) cost/insurance—introduce income-graded subsidies, mandate baseline IVF coverage, and cap out-of-pocket payments; (ii) rural access—fund regional hubs, travel vouchers, and telehealth consultation; (iii) patient safeguards—standardize plain-language consent with routine audits and require pre-/post-treatment counseling. In addition, (iv) psychosocial support—implement routine mental-health screening, on-site counseling, referral pathways, and reimbursement for supportive care; and (v) embryo governance—codify evidence-based rules on embryo creation, storage, and disposition, supported by independent ethics oversight, national registries, and periodic review.

The framework centers on equity and autonomy—core values of reproductive justice—to ensure applicability across diverse national contexts. We find that these two concepts are analytically distinct yet empirically overlapping within IVF policy. Accordingly, we did not compute parallel equity and autonomy scores at the indicator level. Accordingly, we did not generate parallel, separate scores for equity and autonomy at the indicator level. Instead, we considered each indicator assignable to a primary attribution (equity vs. autonomy) and, where appropriate, a secondary linkage that guided interpretation without affecting the numeric score or weighting. For instance, cost and insurance are equity-primary; informed consent is autonomy-primary; legal eligibility is equity-primary with autonomy-secondary effects; and religious/cultural constraints are autonomy-primary, with equity-secondary implications where they legally restrict access. Overall, the framework furnishes a structured diagnostic approach that clearly highlights where national reproductive health systems may be succeeding or failing in achieving equitable, inclusive, and autonomous IVF access. However, several preconditions are crucial for its meaningful application, including comprehensive and publicly accessible national policy documentation, reliable demographic and reproductive health data, and active local stakeholder engagement to ensure context-sensitive interpretation.

Moreover, while the framework offers a replicable method for assessing policy variations, several practical risks warrant attention during its application. First, there is inherent subjectivity in rubric-based scoring, particularly in dimensions where formal written laws may significantly diverge from their practical implementation. For instance, policies related to informed consent or psychosocial support might be clearly articulated yet inconsistently applied in practice, potentially affecting the accuracy and reliability of scores. Capturing the gap between policy intentions and real-world implementation remains a significant methodological challenge. Additionally, although the framework is designed around the universally recognized principles within reproductive justice, it is critical to acknowledge that legal, cultural, and healthcare system variations may influence its universal applicability. Therefore, to ensure accurate and fair application, the rubric must be contextualized through local engagement and iterative validation. Mitigating this subjectivity requires careful triangulation of written policies with empirical data from field reports, stakeholder consultations, and expert insights.

These cautious recommendations also highlight the framework’s important limitations, including potential subjectivity and interpretive biases arising from inadequate consideration of local socio-cultural norms, governance structures, legal frameworks, or healthcare systems. Certain qualitative dimensions—such as cultural stigma, gender roles, and reproductive autonomy—resist complete quantification and may introduce subjective interpretation biases despite rigorous coding procedures. Indicators that appear objective—such as insurance coverage or informed consent protocols—may carry context-specific implications shaped significantly by how they are practically implemented within diverse national legal frameworks, cultural practices, or administrative capacities.

Another significant interpretive challenge involves incomplete or missing data, particularly in lower-resource countries or settings with limited transparency, critical information—such as detailed rural access rates, the operational quality of informed consent protocols, or the availability and utilization of psychosocial support—might be unavailable or inconsistently reported. Moreover, our analysis primarily relied on publicly available, predominantly English-language sources, which might overlook important policy features documented in grey literature or non-English sources. While some effort was made to identify relevant grey literature, its inclusion was limited by concerns over verifiability, inconsistent availability, and language constraints. This reliance could result in the inadvertent omission of local policy innovations, context-specific practices, or recent undocumented changes. Additionally, certain sensitive or informal policy aspects—such as implicit practices within healthcare institutions or informal cultural norms—may not be adequately captured in formal documentation, potentially affecting comprehensive evaluation. Such data gaps may inadvertently skew results, typically leading to underestimations of national policy performance or equity outcomes. This underscores the necessity for ongoing investment in comprehensive data collection infrastructure and rigorous documentation practices, especially in resource-limited contexts. Additionally, the framework currently does not account for intra-national disparities—such as those based on race and ethnicity—which may affect IVF access and outcomes within individual countries.

Future research should aim to enhance the global applicability of the framework through the incorporation of multilingual and region-specific sources. Furthermore, the Universal IVF Justice Framework holds significant potential for future evolution and updating, especially as IVF technologies and associated policies continue to rapidly develop. Future iterations of the framework could incorporate emerging reproductive technologies, changing ethical standards, and evolving legal landscapes. Mechanisms for real-time policy monitoring could enhance responsiveness and accuracy. Engaging diverse stakeholders, interdisciplinary experts, and scholars through participatory workshops and national consultations could further refine scoring criteria and enhance the contextual relevance of indicators. Additionally, longitudinal adaptation of the rubric would enable systematic tracking of policy developments and evaluation of legislative reforms over extended periods, thereby maintaining its ongoing relevance and utility.

Conclusion

This study proposes a universal framework and dynamic rubric for assessing reproductive justice in global in-vitro fertilization (IVF) policy, grounded in a literature-informed analysis of economic-structural, cultural-social, and healthcare system dimensions. The analysis reveals significant global variation: high-income, publicly funded systems generally perform better in affordability, access, and support mechanisms, while lower-resource contexts continue to face financial and geographic constraints. Secular and inclusive legal environments tend to promote greater reproductive autonomy by reducing religious and social barriers. To promote more equitable and just IVF access globally, policymakers are encouraged to expand public financing, invest in infrastructure, strengthen psychosocial and consent safeguards, and foster interdisciplinary and cross-national collaboration.

Looking ahead, the Universal IVF Justice Framework can serve as a foundation for continued monitoring and comparison of policies over time and across regions, as well as for extension into related areas of assisted reproductive technology. Broader adoption will also benefit from inclusive data sources and cross-cultural perspectives, ensuring that reproductive policies evolve in ways that are both ethically grounded and responsive to diverse social contexts. Ultimately, each country should aim to develop culturally sensitive and adaptable reproductive policies that reflect shared ethical principles while respecting local values.
