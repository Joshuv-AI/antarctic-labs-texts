Neural dynamics of temporal interference stimulation monitoring by soft liquid metal interfaces across neural systems
Authors: Enji Kim, Wonjung Park, Yeon-Mi Hong, Jungsoo Hong, Inhea Jeong, Jakyoung Lee, Sanghoon Lee, Hyunjin Lee, Hyun-Mun Kim, Noviana Wulansari, Dong-Youn Hwang, Jang-Ung Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Neuromodulation is widely employed to enhance neural activity and restore impaired circuitry, yet current methods suffer from invasiveness, limited depth, or poor spatial resolution. Temporal interference stimulation (TIS) has emerged as a non-invasive approach to modulate deep brain regions via interference of high-frequency electric fields, achieving spatial selectivity. Despite its potential, the physiological effects remain unexplored. Here, we present a customized liquid metal-based neural interface to record electrophysiological responses to TIS across diverse neural tissues, from brain organoids to mouse brains. The softness of the interface minimizes invasiveness while ensuring stable access to deep neural regions. Electrophysiological analyses reveal TIS-induced dynamics in neurodevelopment and functional connectivity. Furthermore, we investigated latent neural dynamics, presenting population-level neural activity, to compare responses across models of differing complexity and maturity. By combining soft neural interfaces with multi-scale analyses, this study uncovers how neuromodulation shapes neural dynamics and supports the development of future therapeutic strategies.

Introduction

With the rapid advancement of bioelectronics, neuromodulation techniques, along with health monitoring systems, have been progressively developed to establish effective therapeutic strategies for a broad range of conditions, from neurodegenerative diseases to psychiatric disorders1–6. For example, deep brain stimulation (DBS) has been clinically applied to patients with Parkinson’s disease to modulate the subthalamic nucleus (STN), thereby alleviating motor symptoms such as tremor and bradykinesia7,8. Transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) has been reported to produce antidepressant effects in patients with major depressive disorders who are resistant to medications9. In addition, transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS), based on magnetic field induction, and focused ultrasound stimulation (FUS), based on acoustic energy, have also been widely explored and utilized in various clinical conditions10,11.

Combined with their remarkable therapeutic potential, recent neuromodulation platforms have demonstrated promising clinical potential to be user-friendly, stable for long-term use, and adaptable for real-world applications12–14. For instance, skin-attachable and non-invasive patch-type electronics have been developed to deliver neuromodulation in a convenient and painless manner15,16. To address the invasiveness of DBS while maintaining its high spatial resolution, soft material-based electrodes have been introduced to minimize tissue damage and improve biocompatibility17,18. Beyond device-level improvements, system-level innovations have also been implemented. For example, closed-loop neuromodulation systems now enable real-time feedback-controlled stimulation, greatly enhancing therapeutic efficiency19,20.

Nevertheless, despite these advances, intrinsic limitations of current neuromodulation approaches remain21. Non-invasive techniques such as tDCS, TMS, and FUS suffer from low penetration depth and limited spatial resolution, making it challenging to selectively target deep brain regions and often leading to off-target effects. In contrast, DBS provides high spatial specificity but requires invasive surgery, which carries risks of infection and long-term complications. To overcome these limitations, temporal interference stimulation (TIS) has been proposed as a promising neuromodulation strategy22. TIS delivers two or more high-frequency electric fields that interfere within the brain, potentially generating a low-frequency envelope field at a targeted location. It has been hypothesized that, since neuronal membranes act as low-pass filters with a cutoff in the sub-kilohertz range, neurons are less responsive to carrier frequencies above ~1 kHz23. Instead, they may respond to the difference frequency of the interfering fields, enabling selective neuromodulation of deep brain regions with non-invasiveness and improved spatial specificity24. However, the precise biophysical mechanisms underlying TIS remain an active area of investigation and debate25,26. While this proposed low-frequency envelope-based stimulation mechanism provides a conceptual basis for selective neuromodulation, recent work has highlighted that this interpretation may not fully explain the observed neural responses26.

Several studies have attempted to verify the theoretical mechanism of TIS, reporting that it can elicit neural activity and modulate neural connectivity27–30. These findings suggest that TIS has the potential to overcome the limitations of existing neuromodulation techniques and to emerge as an effective therapeutic strategy. However, most of these studies have relied on electroencephalography (EEG) or imaging modalities such as functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), which are inherently limited by low spatiotemporal resolution. EEG does not directly capture activity from deep brain regions, while fMRI is based on hemodynamic signals with temporal delays relative to neural activity. Consequently, these approaches cannot provide direct evidence of selective neuronal activation at the single-neuron or circuit level. Other studies have used in vitro systems, but monolayer cultures fail to capture the excitatory-inhibitory balance, synaptic plasticity, and circuit dynamics that define the complexity of in vivo neural networks. Therefore, this system is inadequate to thoroughly evaluate the dynamics of neural responses to TIS as a therapeutic method.

These limitations highlight the need to investigate the effects of TIS at single-neuron resolution in neural systems that exhibit complex circuitry with different developmental stages and neuronal maturity. Brain organoids have recently emerged as promising models for such studies31. Derived from human stem cells, brain organoids recapitulate aspects of human brain cytoarchitecture and functionality in a three-dimensional in vitro setting32. They contain diverse neuronal cell types, providing opportunities to study cell–cell interactions not possible in conventional two-dimensional cultures33. While engineering brain organoids has advanced to replicate specific brain regions or pathological features, several limitations still remain34. For example, subtypes of glial cells are restricted compared to the in vivo brain since the major proportion of glial cells in the brain organoid is astrocyte, lacking other glial cells such as microglia35. Also, the intrinsic neural circuits are deficient with relatively less synapse density and premature neural network organization, thereby resembling those of the fetal brain rather than a mature human brain36. These discrepancies in neural complexity obstruct brain organoids to serve as complete surrogates for the in vivo brain.

In this study, we developed a customized soft neural interface based on liquid metal (LM) to enable stable, high-fidelity recordings of neuronal responses to TIS from diverse neural tissues, both brain organoids and in vivo mouse brains. This approach provided three major advantages. By minimizing mechanical mismatch between neural tissues and electrodes using LM, soft neural interfaces enable the capture of delicate electrophysiological responses arising from internal neural circuits. First, we utilized soft LM as an electrode material, which possesses an elastic modulus (~200 kPa) far lower than that of conventional rigid metals37. Its intrinsic softness allows adaptively conformal contact with neural tissue under physical dynamics, thus minimizing insertion-related damage and micromotion-induced stress and enabling reliable recording from internal regions of neural tissues. Second, by leveraging neural responses recorded using this interface, we evaluated the effects of TIS not only on neuronal excitability but also on network-level dynamics by employing various types of electrophysiological analysis. To investigate the effect of TIS at the neural circuit level, we analyzed synchronization-based connectivity mapping and neuronal avalanches, revealing the maturity and robustness of functional connectivity developed within neural tissues. Furthermore, we analyzed latent dynamics to capture population-level activity embedded within common space. In parallel, biological analysis to investigate the complexity and maturation of neural tissues complemented electrophysiological findings, further validating the modulatory effects of TIS. Lastly, we quantified how neural complexity and network maturity influence the electrophysiological responses to TIS by examining its effect across two models with different levels of neuronal maturity. Customized, soft LM-based neural interfaces facilitate high-fidelity neural recordings from diverse neural systems while preserving their intrinsic neural complexity and networking circuits, leading to a comprehensive investigation of the TIS effect on neural systems. By integrating advanced soft neural interfaces with multi-scale electrophysiological and biological analyses, our study provides reliable datasets and new insights into how neuromodulation alters neural dynamics, offering opportunities to advance future therapeutic strategies.

Results

Customized soft neural interfaces for reliable monitoring of neural responses to TIS

TIS has recently emerged as a novel neuromodulation strategy that can overcome the major limitations of conventional approaches, including invasiveness, low spatial resolution, and shallow penetration depth. TIS delivers two or more high-frequency electric fields whose interference generates a low-frequency envelope field within neural tissues (Fig. 1a, Supplementary Fig. 1)22,23. However, to thoroughly investigate the effects of TIS, it is essential to analyze neural responses at single-neuron resolution within systems that contain complex circuitry and cellular structures38. In this study, we therefore selected two experimental models with distinct levels of neural complexity and maturity: brain organoids and mouse brains. Brain organoids, derived from human stem cells, reproduce aspects of human brain cytoarchitecture and functionality in a three-dimensional in vitro environment, providing opportunities to study diverse cell compositions and interactions, which are not feasible in monolayer cell cultures. On the other hand, mouse brains possess mature, well-organized neural networks with long-range connectivity, which are not yet achievable with current brain organoid technologies.

Since TIS modulates neurons within neural tissues, neural interfaces must access internal regions to monitor electrophysiological responses induced by TIS. To achieve stable and minimally invasive recordings, we developed soft LM-based neural interfaces customized for each neural model. Unlike rigid metal-based electrodes with high elastic moduli, gallium-based LMs exhibit a much lower elastic modulus (~200 kPa), which minimizes mechanical mismatch and reduces tissue damage upon insertion, while maintaining conformal adaptability to micromotion in living tissues37,39. Moreover, various structures of LMs can be fabricated directly through 3D printing, as described in previous studies40,41. The 3D LM printing system comprises a 6-axis stage for holding substrates, a glass capillary nozzle with an LM reservoir, a compressor for applying pressure on the nozzle for LM extrusion, and charge couple device (CCD) camera for observation (Supplementary Fig. 2). The presence of gallium oxide stabilizes the printed structures, enabling the formation of 3D electrode architectures42–45. 3D LM printing system provides high structural flexibility and allows the fabrication of free-form electrodes without additional processing steps46,47. Using this fabrication strategy and employing the tissue-like softness of LM, previous studies demonstrated that LM-based neural interfaces reliably capture neural signals from organoids and in vivo models without disruptions of neural networks and physiological functions17,43,44,46–49. Leveraging these advantages of LM-based neural interfaces, we designed neural interfaces tailored to the specific environments of each model, which exhibit numerous advantages compared to other TIS studies using microelectrodes or rigid probes (Supplementary Table 1).

For brain organoids, we designed a multi-electrode array (MEA) with 3D pillar-shaped LM electrodes (Fig. 1c, Supplementary Fig. 3), overcoming limitations of conventional platforms that disregard three-dimensional architecture, rely on surface-only contact, or cause tissue invasiveness50–52. To fabricate this MEA, we prepared a glass substrate and patterned an Au layer as an interconnection using photolithography. Since gallium readily penetrates into many metals and forms intermetallic alloys, which can degrade the electrical conductivity of both materials, careful selection of electrode materials is essential to ensure stable electrical performance of the device. Thus, we chose platinum for electrode pad material, which is known for its chemical inertness and minimal reactivity with other metals, making it a widely adopted material for stable interfacing with gallium-based liquid metals. After fabricating an encapsulation layer using a 2-µm-thick parylene-C layer, we printed 3D LM electrodes. With applied pressure of 36 psi on 20 µm-inner diameter glass capillary nozzle, the pillar-shaped electrodes with a height of 250 µm and a diameter of 18 µm were printed with the printing velocity of 0.05 mm·s−1 (Fig. 1d, e, Supplementary Movie. 1). The printed 3D LM pillars were encapsulated with 1 µm of parylene-C layer, and only the electrode tips, which form the electrode-neuron interfaces, were electrically exposed using reactive ion etching. The fabricated MEA was integrated with two PDMS wells to guide the position of organoids (inner well) and to maintain culture medium (outer well), allowing stable insertion of 3D LM electrodes into organoids’ internal volumes. In parallel, we designed TIS electrodes to be positioned around the PDMS inner well, ensuring non-invasive stimulation at the core of organoids without any penetrating electrodes. Using flexible polyimide (PI) film, we patterned Au and Pt layer with an electrode design (diameter: 500 µm) to generate envelope fields at the center of the organoids (Supplementary Fig. 4). After attaching PDMS wells, Pt nanoclusters were electrodeposited selectively only at the electrode tips of 3D LM electrodes, leading to improving electrical properties of 3D LM MEA to delicately capture the subtle electrophysiological dynamics (Fig. 1f, “Methods” section). As a result, 3D LM MEA for brain organoids showed uniform site-to-site impedance (18.04 ± 0.098 kΩ at l kHz) across 60 electrodes, enabling reliable multi-site recordings within organoids (Supplementary Fig. 5). Brain organoids were integrated into this 3D LM MEA, including TIS electrodes by aspirating the medium, whereby the gradual lowering of the medium level facilitated the spontaneous insertion of the 3D LM electrodes into the organoids. Immediately after the insertion process, the medium was refilled, enabling subsequent TIS and recording of neural responses from brain organoids for reliable monitoring of electrophysiological dynamics evoked by TIS (Supplementary Fig. 6).

For mouse brains, we fabricated shank-type probes with printed LM electrodes, thereby enabling reliable intracortical recordings without disrupting neural circuits (Fig. 1g, Supplementary Fig. 7)46,47,49. The 1 µm-parylene-C layer was patterned into a shank-shaped probe structure as a bottom layer on a Si wafer, with a sacrificial layer (LOR 3A, MicroChem) used to facilitate subsequent release of the device from the wafer46. For high spatiotemporal neural recording, we selected 8-µm-width LM lines as electrodes. With identical printing system, 16 LM lines were printed on the patterned parylene-C layer using 10 µm-inner diameter glass capillary nozzle with applied pressure of 45 psi and printing velocity of 0.05 mm·s−1 (Fig. 1h). Top passivation layer of 2.5 µm-parylene-C layer was patterned to electrically open the terminal of lines with the size of 10 µm-diameter circles and Pt nanoclusters were deposited at the open area where neurons are in contact (Supplementary Fig. 8). The probes were lift-off from the wafer and sterilized using ethanol before implanting into mouse brain. The fabrication process and surgical procedure for implantation of LM-based neural probes are described in the “Methods” section. The soft LM-based neural probe exhibited uniform impedance of 14.60 ± 0.08103 kΩ at 1 kHz across 16 electrodes (Supplementary Fig. 9). When considering the difference in electrode opening area of each device (3D LM MEA: 78.5 µm2, LM-based neural probe: 100.48 µm2), the area-specific impedances at 1 kHz showed 1416.3 ± 7.7038 kΩ·µm2 for 3D LM MEA (n = 60 electrodes in a single device) and 1466.5 ± 8.1419 kΩ·µm2 for LM-based probes (n = 16 electrodes in a single device), indicating excellent electrical performance of LM-based electrodes to reliably capture delicate neural signals (Supplementary Table 2). As TIS electrodes for the mouse brain, we fabricated a cranium-attachable TIS patch based on LM to target the hippocampus (Fig. 1i, Supplementary Fig. 10). The deformability of LM enables this patch to conform to the arbitrary curvature of the cranium, leading to effective delivery of electric fields. Owing to the structural flexibility of the 3D LM printing system, we printed LM lines with a width of 80 µm with the design to target the hippocampus region of the mouse brain (AP: −2.70 mm, ML: +2.04 mm, DV: −1.30 mm). The LM electrodes in TIS patch exhibited an impedance of 0.285 ± 0.000257 kΩ (n = 8 electrodes), which corresponds to area-specific impedance of 1429.884 ± 12.8915 kΩ·µm2 (Supplementary Fig. 11). Furthermore, voltage transient measurements were conducted using biphasic current pulses (pulse width: 0.25 ms), which corresponds to a carrier frequency of 2000 Hz, to evaluate the stimulation performance (Fig. 1j). The maximum charge injection capacity (CIC) was determined to be 2.49 mC·cm−2 at a current of 500 µA, confirming that the LM electrodes provide sufficient charge delivery capability for TIS. According to the previous study, the LM-based electrodes should be positioned at AP: −1.94 mm, ML: +0.50 mm, −0.50 mm, −3.90 mm, −4.30 mm, which is reflected in the design of LM lines53. The fabrication process of the TIS patch is described in the “Methods” section.

Using these model-specific soft neural interfaces, we evaluated TIS effects across two complementary dimensions (Fig. 1k). At the neural circuit level, we investigated changes in functional connectivity. Alteration in firing rates is insufficient to demonstrate the circuit-level impact of TIS, which is crucial to drive functional modulation. To this end, we employed synchronization-based network mapping and neuronal avalanche analysis54,55. At the population level, we analyzed latent dynamics by embedding high-dimensional, multichannel neural recordings into a low-dimensional common space, thereby capturing embedded neural dynamics and uncovering population-level activity patterns56. Through these approaches, our study examined the responses of organoids and mouse brains, allowing us to quantitatively determine how neural complexity and network maturity shape the neuromodulatory effects of TIS.

Dynamic alterations in spiking activity and connectivity during repetitive TIS

Firstly, we monitored the neural activity of brain organoids upon TIS to examine the effects of repetitive TIS. We generated cortical organoids following a previously described protocol with modifications, as described in the “Methods” section (Supplementary Fig. 12)57. We conducted continuous neural recordings using a 3D LM MEA integrated with brain organoids (DIV 90) during 100 consecutive TIS trials. To set the condition of TIS, we evaluated combinations of envelope frequencies (20, 50, 80 Hz) and stimulation amplitudes (0.6, 1.2, 1.8 V peak-to-peak) in brain organoids (n = 5, biological replicates), based on previous studies (Supplementary Fig. 13)58,59. The neural activation following each stimulation conditions was quantified using changes in spiking rate before and after stimulation, and we set envelope frequency as 50 Hz, carrier frequencies as 2000 Hz, 2050 Hz, and peak-to-peak voltage as 1.2 V for TIS in this study, which produced the pronounced increase in spiking activity (Fig. 2a). TIS was delivered by connecting TIS electrodes to a stimulator (33500B, Keysight). Each TIS session was consecutively repeated 100 times, and each session consisted of a 30 s of sinusoidal stimulation followed by 30 s of recording (Fig. 2b). Single-unit potentials detected from each electrode channel were analyzed to quantify spiking activity and network-level synchronization. Representative single-unit potentials are shown in Fig. 2c. Neural connectivity was assessed based on temporal synchronization between electrode pairs, generating neural networking maps over successive stimulation cycles (Fig. 2d). Specifically, pairwise synchronization scores between electrodes were quantified on a 0–1 scale, and electrodes with scores above 0.5 were defined as connected edges. The calculation method of synchronization score is described in the “Methods” section. The strength of synchronization was represented by edge color, and the number of edges per electrode was reflected in node size. Quantitative analysis of spiking rate and number of edges in neural networking maps showed alternating increases and decreases over repeated TIS (Fig. 2e, f). This pattern suggests adaptive modulation of network excitability under repetitive TIS. Moreover, the decreases in both neural activity and connectivity may reflect synaptic depression, potentially arising from neurotransmitter depletion or receptor desensitization during repeated stimulation. Such dynamics are consistent with transient reductions in synaptic efficacy commonly observed under sustained or high-frequency stimulation60,61. While these similar effects have been described in clinical neuromodulation studies, evidence of such adaptive synaptic dynamics in human brain organoids has been limited62,63. Nevertheless, our observations demonstrate that brain organoids exhibit functional synaptic maturation and plasticity, which are modulated by TIS. This suggests that they can serve as a high-resolution, mechanistic platform to investigate cellular-level responses to stimulation. Assessment of TIS effects on neuronal activation and neurodevelopment in neural tissues

To investigate the effects of TIS on neural tissues, we first divided brain organoids into four experimental groups: a control group with no stimulation, a high-frequency stimulation (HFS) group receiving 2000 Hz fields, a low-frequency stimulation (LFS) group, and a TIS group (“Methods” section). The HFS group serves as a negative control to confirm that high frequencies alone do not elicit neuronal responses and structural alteration. The LFS group received uniform low-frequency stimulation across the entire organoid, which is non-focal and lacks spatial selectivity (Supplementary Fig. 14). On the other hand, the TIS group underwent low-frequency stimulation by envelope field, which selectively targets the interior of the organoid (Fig. 3a, Supplementary Fig. 15). The envelope waveforms exhibited symmetric positive and negative phases, with the baseline remaining close to 0 V. In addition, the envelope amplitude remained stable across cycles without a detectable DC offset, indicating the reliability of TIS in our platform. All groups were stimulated at identical amplitudes of 1.2 V (peak-to-peak), and neural signals were recorded before and after stimulation using the soft 3D LM MEAs. To optimize the stimulation protocol for maximizing the effects of TIS on brain organoids (DIV 90; n = 6, biological replicates), we assessed spiking rates across multiple days of TIS (envelope field frequency: 50 Hz, peak-to-peak amplitude: 1.2 V, duration: 30 min/day) by recording neural signals with soft 3D LM MEA. We selected a 30 min TIS duration per day based on previous studies demonstrating that sustained electrical stimulation within this timescale effectively promotes neural maturation, differentiation, and synaptic development in 3D neural tissues64,65. Considering the variability of spiking activity across organoids, we calculated the relative change in spiking rate with respect to that of no stimulation (before stimulation, DIV 90). As shown in Supplementary Fig. 16, spiking activity increased after the first stimulation, peaked on day 3 (DIV 92), and then reached saturation by days 4–5 (DIV 93, 94), reflecting adaptive neural responses to repeated TIS. Based on these observations, we established the stimulation protocol as a 30 min daily stimulation applied over 3 consecutive days. Using this protocol, we subsequently applied stimulation conditions with varying frequencies and spatial specificity and recorded the corresponding neural responses using 3D LM MEA to investigate their effects.

To assess neuronal responses at the single-neuron level, we quantified the spiking rates across electrodes of the MEA. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 17, each dot represents the position of an electrode exposed tip interfacing with neurons, and the color scale and the size of the dots reflect the spiking rate recorded at that site. Each stimulation condition was applied for three days, and neural signals recorded during the 20 s following the final stimulation (at DIV 92) were used for analysis (n = 6 for each group, biological replicates). The representative results of spiking rates for 4 groups are shown in Fig. 3b. As plotted in Fig. 3c, the HFS group showed spiking rates (0.457 ± 0.0081 Hz) similar to the control, consistent with the principle that high-frequency electric fields above 1 kHz do not directly elicit neural activity. In contrast, the TIS group exhibited a significantly higher spiking rate (0.511 ± 0.0047 Hz) compared to the control group (0.455 ± 0.012 Hz) and also exceeded the LFS group (0.490 ± 0.0062 Hz). These results demonstrated that TIS more effectively activated neuronal spiking within the interior regions of organoids compared to uniform low-frequency stimulation.

Since brain organoids form three-dimensional cytoarchitectures containing diverse cell types and intercellular communications, we investigated how TIS affected network-level organization. Therefore, we performed neuronal avalanche analysis, which clusters spikes occurring within short time windows (3 ms) to evaluate the efficiency of signal propagation across neural circuits. Avalanche size is defined as the number of simultaneously activated electrodes, and the probability distribution of avalanche sizes was fitted to estimate the slope (α), a parameter reflecting network robustness and efficiency55. Smaller α values indicate more frequent large avalanches, which correspond to stronger functional connections and more efficient circuit-level communication. As shown in Fig. 3d, e, the TIS group displayed a markedly higher α (−2.685 ± 0.0967) compared to the control (−3.145 ± 0.0269), HFS (−3.188 ± 0.0212), and LFS (−3.137 ± 0.0621) groups. This shift indicated that TIS enhanced functional connectivity within the organoid network, enabling more efficient signal transmission between neurons. Importantly, this effect was observed only in the TIS group and not in the other stimulation conditions. These findings support that TIS not only enhanced neuronal excitability but also reorganized network dynamics and strengthened functional connectivity at the circuit level.

Along with electrophysiological analysis, we performed biological analyses to investigate how TIS affects neuronal complexity and maturation (Fig. 3f). First, in order to verify the safety of stimulation conditions with varying frequencies and spatial specificity, we assessed cell viability using DAPI staining, which labels cell nuclei (n = 5 for each group, biological replicates) (Fig. 3g, h). The distribution and density of DAPI-positive cells were comparable across all groups, indicating that electrical stimulation did not induce apoptosis, cytolysis, or other deleterious side effects. Next, we evaluated the impact of TIS on neural complexity by immunostaining for SYN (n = 3 for each group, biological replicates), a presynaptic marker (Fig. 3i). The TIS group exhibited a significant increase in SYN expression (41.92 ± 0.531) compared to the control (32.97 ± 0.907), while the HFS group showed negligible changes (32.99 ± 0.834). These results indicate that 50 Hz electrical stimulation enhances synaptic connectivity and that the spatial specificity of TIS further intensifies this effect. Moreover, we examined the expression of TUJ1 (n = 5 for each group, biological replicates), a marker for neurofilament protein required for neural differentiation, to assess the effect of TIS on neurodevelopment (Fig. 3j). TIS group showed higher expression of TUJ1 (37.48 ± 1.358) compared to the control group (25.39 ± 1.771), demonstrating that TIS advances the neural differentiation in brain organoids (Fig. 3k). Furthermore, the observed increases in multiple markers suggest that neuromodulation using high-frequency stimulation does not induce any cellular destruction or death, while enhancing the physiological functions of neurons. The immunohistological analysis using various markers following TIS is presented in Supplementary Fig. 18. We examined neural progenitor markers (Nestin), mature neuronal markers (MAP2), immature neuronal markers (TUJ1), and a post-synaptic marker (PSD95). An additional marker was used to distinguish specific neuronal layers (TBR1 for cortical layer 6), enabling precise mapping of organoid development and structure. This result aligned with the electrophysiological changes induced by TIS, thereby supporting its neuromodulatory effects. Collectively, these findings suggest that TIS increases the complexity of synaptic networks and promotes neural differentiation, thereby advancing the functionality of organoids toward that observed in more mature neural networks.

Analysis of TIS-induced modulation of neural connectivity across neural tissues

Brain organoids have recently gained attention as substitutes for human brain tissue because they recapitulate aspects of human cytoarchitecture and functionality, providing opportunities to overcome the limited accessibility of the human brain. Thus, organoids have been increasingly utilized to study the effects of neuromodulation techniques as well as the impact of pharmacological interventions. However, discrepancies remain compared to mature in vivo brains. For instance, in vivo brains contain diverse interneuron subtypes and glial populations, and they maintain a fully developed excitatory-inhibitory balance, enabling rich cell–cell interactions and complex circuitry. In contrast, brain organoids typically contain only subsets of glial cells, mostly astrocytes, display lower cellular density, and exhibit immature synaptic networks with limited long-range connectivity and plasticity. These differences can result in distinct responses to TIS, highlighting the importance of investigating its modulatory impact in relation to the complexity and maturity of neural systems.

To investigate changes in functional connectivity by TIS, we analyzed the temporal synchronization of spiking activity of both models and visualized it as a neural networking map. Since the number of electrodes differed between model-specific neural interfaces, connectivity features were normalized per electrode. The representative neural networking maps before and after TIS for each model are presented in Fig. 4b, f. In brain organoids (n = 6, biological replicates), the number of synchronized edges per electrode increased after TIS from 1.672 ± 0.0831 to 2.333 ± 0.2196, and the mean synchronization score also increased from 0.547 ± 0.00481 to 0.571 ± 0.00578, indicating that more neurons engaged in strong, localized connections (Fig. 4c, d). In in vivo brains (n = 5, biological replicates), the number of synchronized edges likewise increased after TIS from 6.425 ± 1.535 to 9.175 ± 0.563, consistent with the formation of denser networks (Fig. 4g). This tendency is identical in both brain organoids and in vivo brain (Fig. 4i). This shift reflected enhanced network flexibility and adaptability, in line with the presence of long-range connectivity in mature brain tissue.

To further validate TIS-induced changes in functional connectivity, we performed surrogate-based analyses on neural recordings from both brain organoid and mouse brain to distinguish simple increases in neural activity from coordinate network-level changes. Specifically, we generated circular-shift surrogate datasets that preserved spike timing within each electrode while disrupting temporal coordination across electrodes. These surrogate datasets maintained identical spiking rates to the original recordings, thereby isolating activity-level changes while removing structured network interactions. For both brain organoid and mouse brain recordings, 500 random surrogate datasets were generated for each dataset and averaged to obtain representative surrogate values. Since spiking rates were preserved, differences between real and surrogate data reflect coordinated population dynamics rather than increased neural activity. When we calculated the degree of synchronization based on spike-distance between electrodes, real recordings showed substantially larger TIS-induced changes compared to surrogate data in both models. In brain organoids, the change in synchronization was 0.000886 ± 0.000238 in real data compared to 0.000273 ± 0.0000925 in surrogate data. In mouse brain recordings, synchronization changes were 0.515 ± 0.0410 in real data and 0.209 ± 0.00200 for surrogate data (Supplementary Fig. 22). These results indicate that TIS induces coordinated network-level modulation beyond simple increases in neural firing in both models.

Finally, neuronal avalanche analysis was employed to evaluate changes in signal propagation efficiency within neural tissues (Fig. 4k). In both organoids (n = 6, biological replicates) and mouse brains (n = 5, biological replicates), α values derived from avalanche size distributions increased following TIS, from −3.162 ± 0.0320 to −2.685 ± 0.0967 for brain organoids and from −1.22 ± 0.0685 to −0.468 ± 0.0206 for in vivo brains, respectively (Fig. 4l). Notably, the α values in mouse brains were higher than those in organoids, indicating more efficient and robust signal propagation in mature networks. Taken together, these results demonstrated that TIS strengthened functional connectivity in both organoids and in vivo brains, while the magnitude and nature of changes differed depending on tissue maturity. Organoids exhibited strong local connections, whereas mouse brains reorganized from localized to global networks, reflecting their greater complexity and capacity for adaptive reconfiguration.

The geometry of the common latent space reflects the intrinsic organization of neural activity, and the resulting trajectories reveal how neuronal responses evolve over time. To quantitatively compare the latent dynamics upon TIS within each model, trajectory length was calculated as the cumulative Euclidean distance between consecutive trajectory points in latent space. This measure captures the overall magnitude of population activity evolution, with longer trajectories indicating richer or more extensive neural responses over time. Also, the curvature of latent trajectories was quantified to evaluate the smoothness and directional consistency of neural population dynamics. A lower average curvature corresponds to smoother, more coherent trajectories, whereas higher curvature reflects more frequent directional changes in population activity. In brain organoids (n = 6, biological replicates), latent trajectories became elongated and smoother after TIS. Quantitative analysis showed that trajectory length increased from 2.461 ± 0.164 to 4.242 ± 0.476, while curvature decreased from 1.028 ± 0.112 to 0.672 ± 0.0507, suggesting that TIS-enriched population responses and drove the activity into more consistent and organized trajectories (Fig. 5d, e). These findings indicated that while both systems exhibited reorganized latent dynamics in response to TIS, the effects were amplified in mature neural circuits.

Instead, the results support TIS-induced modulation of population-level neural dynamics.

Organoids exhibited modest increases in trajectory complexity and reduced stability, whereas mouse brains displayed stronger restructuring of trajectories while maintaining stable temporal dynamics. These findings further support that soft LM-based neural interfaces enabled reliable neural recordings while preserving subtle circuit-level properties, revealing how intrinsic network maturity shapes electrophysiological responses to neuromodulation.

Discussion

In this study, we developed a soft LM-based neural interface to directly examine the effects of TIS within neural tissues of varying complexity. Owing to the intrinsic softness and structural flexibility of LMs, we customized the designs of neural interfaces to specific neural tissues to reliably monitor neural dynamics upon TIS from the internal region. This platform allowed us to evaluate TIS-induced modulation across brain organoids and in vivo mouse brains. Our results demonstrated that TIS modulates not only spiking activity but also higher-order network properties, including functional connectivity and latent neural dynamics. Importantly, the magnitude and characteristics of these effects differed across the two models, reflecting the degree of neuronal complexity and network maturity.

Compared to previous TIS studies, which primarily relied on EEG or fMRI and were thus limited by low spatiotemporal resolution, our approach achieved single-neuron-level recording with high fidelity29,30. This allowed us to identify subtle electrophysiological responses that cannot be captured by population-averaged or hemodynamic signals. Moreover, by applying a consistent experimental framework across both brain organoids and in vivo mouse models, we provided a perspective on how neural systems of different maturity respond to TIS, offering new insights into the applicability of this technique.

In brain organoids, TIS was associated with increased spiking activity and local connectivity. In contrast, in vivo mouse brains exhibited more extensive network restructuring, characterized by increased global connectivity and enhanced network flexibility, which may reflect modulation of mature and established neural circuits. However, these changes should be interpreted with caution, as TIS-induced neural differentiation observed in Fig. 3 may contribute to alterations in population-level activity by increasing the number of mature and active neurons. The present results do not allow clear separation between effects arising from increased neuronal maturity and those resulting from modulation of network dynamics. This limitation highlights the need for future studies to quantitatively assess neural composition and maturity to disentangle these contributions.

Taken together, these observations suggest that TIS influences neural activity at multiple organizational levels, motivating further consideration of its potential underlying mechanisms. Although the present study does not directly interrogate the cellular and circuit-level mechanisms underlying TIS, the observed electrophysiological signals suggest that TIS may influence neural activity through modulation at the network level, which was consistently reflected not only in spiking activity but also in higher-order features such as functional connectivity, neuronal avalanche, and latent dynamics. Importantly, surrogate-based analyses controlling for spiking rate indicated that these changes cannot be attributed solely to increased neural excitability or signal amplitude. Instead, the results suggest that TIS influences coordinated population activity, potentially through modulation of network-level synchronization. These observations are consistent with previous reports suggesting that weak electric fields can bias spike timing and alter the temporal coordination of neural populations67,68. Accordingly, TIS may influence neural dynamics by altering synchronization across neural populations rather than directly driving individual neurons.

Despite these advances, several limitations should be acknowledged. By integrating brain organoids and in vivo mouse models within an experimental framework, we provided a perspective on how neuronal complexity shapes responses to TIS. Nevertheless, brain organoids, while recapitulating aspects of human cytoarchitecture, lack key cellular components such as microglia and vasculature, which constrain their ability to fully mimic in vivo dynamics. In addition to these structural limitations, there are also functional constraints, as the neural activity remains immature and is comparable to that observed in the early fetal human brain33,36. Thus, future work integrating molecular profiling, imaging, or behavioral paradigms will be essential to establish a more comprehensive view of TIS-induced modulation. In addition, the present findings suggest that TIS-induced effects are primarily acute, as repeated stimulation led to saturation of spiking activity and, in some cases, reduced responsiveness, indicating activity-dependent adaptation rather than sustained enhancement. Accordingly, whether TIS can induce long-term plasticity or persistent functional changes remains an open question and warrants further investigation. In parallel, while identical stimulation parameters were applied to both models, the effective electrical conditions may differ due to variations in size, geometry, and tissue composition. To account for these differences, the number of stimulation days was adjusted based on the timepoint at which spiking activity reached its maximum in each model, allowing model-specific responses to be evaluated under comparable functional states. While FEM simulations and in situ measurements of stimulation waveforms partially validated stimulation delivery, more rigorous approaches will be required to establish quantitative comparability. Future studies should therefore focus on optimizing stimulation parameters based on model-specific properties to improve translational relevance.

Collectively, this study demonstrates how advances in bioelectronics and neuroengineering can directly inform the future of neuromodulation. By integrating soft LM-based neural interfaces with high-resolution electrophysiological analyses, we established a robust framework to evaluate the effects of TIS across neural systems of differing complexity. This approach not only highlights the capacity of bioelectronic innovation to achieve stable and minimally invasive access to neural dynamics but also provides critical insight into how network maturity shapes the modulatory outcomes of TIS. Bridging organoid and in vivo models, our work connects mechanistic neuroscience with translational potential, underscoring the promise of TIS as a non-invasive and spatially selective therapy. Ultimately, the convergence of advanced bioelectronic platforms with clinically oriented neuromodulation strategies offers a path toward adaptive, patient-specific interventions for neurological and psychiatric disorders.

Methods

Liquid metal (LM) printing system

3D LM printing system employs EGaIn (75.5% gallium, 24.5% indium alloy by weight; Changsha Santech Materials Co. Ltd.) as a LM ink. The printing system comprised a 6-axis stage (H-820 6-Axis Hexapod, Physik Instrumente), compressor, nozzle holder, charge-coupled device camera (QImaging Micropublisher 5.0 RTV, Teledyne Photometrics), and a computer for overall system control40,48.

We customized glass capillary nozzles to exhibit the desired inner diameter using a micropipette puller (SU-P2000F, World Precision Instruments). Before printing, an air pressure above 60 psi was applied to extrude the LM from the glass capillary nozzle. To fabricate 3D LM MEA for brain organoids, we printed a pillar-shaped 3D structure of LM using 20 µm-inner diameter glass capillary nozzle. By applying 36 psi on the LM-filled nozzle while printing at 0.05 mm·s−1, 3D pillar electrodes with a diameter of 18 µm and a height of 250 µm were printed on the substrate. To print LM lines of 8 µm-width for a soft neural probe, we used a 10-µm-inner-diameter glass capillary nozzle with an applied pressure of 45 psi and a printing velocity of 0.05 mm·s−1.

Fabrication of 3D LM MEA

The fabrication steps of the 3D LM MEA are as follows: (1) A slide glass was prepared in a size of 4.5 × 4.5 cm. (2) 10 nm Cr/100 nm Au was deposited as an interconnect layer of MEA using an e-beam evaporator (Korea Vacuum Tech Co. Ltd.). (3) The interconnect pattern was photolithographically formed using photoresist S1818 (MicroChem) and Cr, Au etchant (Transene). (4) Pt pads (10 nm Cr/60 nm Pt) were fabricated using LOR 3A layer (MicroChem)48. (5) A 2-µm-thick parylene-C layer was deposited as an encapsulation layer (NRPC-500, Nuritech Co. Ltd.), patterned using S1818 (MicroChem), and etched by O2 plasma using a reactive ion etching (RIE) system (LAT Co. Ltd., Republic of Korea). (6) 3D LM pillars were printed on each Pt pad, and 2-µm-thick parylene-C was deposited for passivation. (7) For electrode tip opening, parylene-C layer on the tip of the pillars was selectively dry-etched by RIE with the condition of 200 W, 240 s. (8) For preparing 25 mL of an electrodeposition solution, 25 mL of isopropyl alcohol, 5 mg of lead acetate trihydrate (Sigma-Aldrich), and 0.25 g of platinum tetrachloride (Sigma-Aldrich) were mixed at room temperature. (9) A cathode (the 3D LM electrode) and an anode (Pt electrode) were immersed in this electrodeposition solution, and each was connected to a source meter (Keithley 2400, Tektronix). The electroplating reaction occurred under an electrical voltage of 2 V and compliance current of 0.1 mA for 120 s. (10) After removing the electrodeposition solution, the 3D LM MEA was washed three times using isopropyl alcohol, followed by rinsing with DIW. This electroplating process is widely used in the fabrication of bioelectronic devices that interface with living tissues and has demonstrated reliable biocompatibility, indicating negligible toxicity from residual lead acetate46,48.

Fabrication of TIS electrodes for brain organoids

The fabrication steps of the TIS electrodes for brain organoids are as follows: (1) A Si wafer was prepared with a size of 5 × 7 cm. (2) After spin-coating PDMS at 4000 rpm, the polyimide (PI) film was attached to the Si wafer. (3) 10 nm Cr/100 nm Au was deposited as an interconnect layer of MEA using an e-beam evaporator (Korea Vacuum Tech Co. Ltd.). (4) The electrode pattern was photolithographically formed using photoresist S1818 (MicroChem) and Cr, Au etchant (Transene). (5) Pt pads (10 nm Cr/60 nm Pt) were fabricated using LOR 3 A layer (MicroChem). (6) A 2-µm-thick parylene-C layer was deposited and patterned as encapsulation. (7) PI film with TIS electrodes was released from the wafer.

Attachment of elastomer wells

Elastomer wells were fabricated with polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS). For the outer well for containing medium, uncured PDMS was poured into a square petri dish with a 2 cm thickness. Once PDMS was cured, a 19-mm-diameter hole was created with a punch. For the inner well, micromold was designed with 3D printer (Form3, Formlabs), and uncured PDMS was poured onto the mold. When the inner well was cured, TIS electrodes were attached using uncured PDMS as an adhesive material. The inner well with TIS electrodes and the outer well were attached on the 3D LM MEA using uncured PDMS. After attaching the wells on the 3D LM MEA, the wells were filled with 2 mL of 70% ethanol for sterilization and exposed to UV light for 1 h under sterile conditions, followed by rinsing with DIW. Subsequently, brain organoids were inserted.

Fabrication of soft neural probes

The fabrication procedure of the soft liquid-metal neural probe is as follows: (1) a Si wafer (Dasom RMS, Republic of Korea) was spin-coated by a LOR 3A lift-off resist (MicroChem) as a sacrificial layer. (2) A 1-µm-thick parylene-C layer was deposited as the bottom layer of a neural probe using a parylene coater (NRPC-500, Nuritech Co. Ltd., Republic of Korea). (3) 16 EGaIn lines with a width of 8 µm were printed onto the bottom parylene-C layer. (4) A 2.5-µm-thick parylene-C layer was deposited as the top encapsulation layer. (5) Photoresist S1818 (MicroChem) was spin-coated and then photolithographically patterned to define the probe shape. (6) Exposed regions of parylene-C were etched by O2 plasma using a reactive ion etching (RIE) system (LAT Co. Ltd., Republic of Korea), followed by removal of the S1818 photoresist using acetone. (7) The S1818 photoresist was spun again and then patterned to open the electrode tip and interconnect pads, and procedure (6) was repeated. (8) The resulting probes were released from the wafer by dissolving the LOR 3A layer using a remover PG solution (MicroChem). (9) The released neural probes were rinsed with deionized (DI) water. (10) Platinum (Pt) nanoclusters were electroplated onto the exposed electrode tips using an electroplating solution containing lead acetate trihydrate (Sigma-Aldrich) and platinum tetrachloride (Sigma-Aldrich). The electroplating reaction occurred under an electrical current of 0.1 mA for 60 s using a source meter (Keithley 2400, Tektronix). (11) The neural probes were sterilized by immersing them in 70% ethanol for 1 h, followed by rinsing them with DIW.

Fabrication of the TIS patch for the in vivo mouse brain

PEO (molecular weight: 1,000,000, Sigma-Aldrich), deionized water, and methyl alcohol (Samchun Pure Chemical Co. Ltd.) were prepared in a vial with a mass ratio of 1:25:5. Then, this mixture was stirred with a magnetic stirrer overnight. An aqueous solution containing PEO was evenly cast and then subjected to curing at 60 °C for 2 h. After the solvent had fully evaporated, the dried PEO film, with a submillimeter thickness (∼100 μm), was flipped over, and a 2-μm-thick parylene-C layer was deposited. It was then carefully transferred onto a glass slide and fixed using polyimide tape. LM lines were printed on PEO-parylene-C film with the design to position LM electrodes at AP: −1.94 mm, ML: +0.50 mm, −0.50 mm, −3.90 mm, −4.30 mm to target mouse hippocampus (AP: −2.70 mm, ML: +2.04 mm, DV: −1.30 mm)53. After the printing step, the entire 3D electrode was coated with 1-µm-thick parylene-C for passivation. The tip was then exposed by reactive ion etching, and the exposed surface area served as the active stimulation site.

Impedance spectroscopy

The impedance measurements of the 3D LM electrodes were conducted in a PBS solution (Sigma-Aldrich). A three-electrode configuration was employed, where the 3D LM electrode, an Ag/AgCl electrode (BASi Research Products), and a platinum wire (CH Instruments) served as the working, reference, and counter electrodes, respectively. All measurements were performed using a multichannel potentiostat (PMC-1000, AMETEK) over a frequency range of 0.01–100 kHz with an applied sinusoidal voltage amplitude of 10 mV (rms). The impedance values reported in this study were extracted at 1 kHz unless otherwise specified. For LM-based neural probes, impedance measurements were conducted under identical conditions by connecting the individual electrode sites of the neural probe as the working electrode.

Human PSC cultures

H9 hESCs were obtained from WiCell and maintained on Matrigel (Corning Life Sciences, 356231) coated dish in mTeSR1 medium (STEMCELL Technologies, 85850) with daily medium change. Mycoplasma contamination was tested every 2 weeks using e-Myco MycoPlasma PCR detection kit (LiliF, 25235). Experiments using H9 hESCs were approved by the International Review Board of CHA University (IRB No: 1044308-201603-LR-004-09).

Generation of human brain organoids

Cortical organoids were generated according to a previously published protocol (38) with slight modifications. Briefly, hESCs were dissociated using Accutase (STEMCELL Technologies, 07920) and seeded at 10,000 cells per well in 96-well ultra-low attachment round-bottom plates (Corning, 7007). Cells were allowed to self-aggregate to form uniform embryoid bodies (EBs) in mTeSR1 medium supplemented with 20 μM ROCK inhibitor (Y-27632; Tocris 1254). From day 0 to day 10, the medium was changed every 2 days with neural induction medium consisting of DMEM/F12 (Gibco 11330032) medium supplemented with 15% knockout serum replacement (KSR; Gibco, 10828028), 1% GlutaMAX (Gibco, 35050061), 1% minimum essential medium–nonessential amino acids (MEM-NEAA; Gibco, 11140050), 0.1% β-mercaptoethanol (Gibco, 21985023), 10 μM SB431542 (Stemgent, 04001005), 100 nM LDN193189 (Stemgent, 04007402), and 2 μM XAV939 (STEMCELL Technologies, 72672). 5% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, A5256701) was added during the first 2 days, and 10 μM Y-27632 was added during the first 4 days. On day 10, organoids exhibiting clear neuroepithelial structures were embedded in 30 μL Matrigel and transferred into ultra-low attachment multiple 6 well plates (Costar, 3471) containing neural expansion medium [DMEM/F12: Neurobasal (Gibco,21103049) medium (1:1) supplemented with 0.5% N2 supplement, 1% B27 supplement without vitamin A (Gibco, 12587-010), 1% GlutaMAX, 0.5% MEM-NEAA, 0.025% human insulin solution (Gibco, 12585014), 50 μM β-mercaptoethanol (Invitrogen), and 1% Penicillin/Streptomycin (Gibco,15140122)]. Organoids were cultured under orbital shaking conditions at 80 rpm, and the medium was replenished every other day. On day 15, organoid maturation was induced by replacing the medium with final differentiation medium [DMEM/F12:Neurobasal medium (1:1) supplemented with 0.5% N2 supplement, 1% B27 supplement (Gibco, 17504044), 1% GlutaMAX, 0.5% MEM-NEAA, 0.025% human insulin solution, 50 μM β-mercaptoethanol, and 1% Penicillin/Streptomycin, along with brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF; 10 ng/mL; PeproTech, #450-02), glial cell-derived neurotrophic factor (GDNF; 10 ng/mL; PeproTech, #450-10), 100 μM ascorbic acid (Sigma-Aldrich, A4544), and 125 μM dibutyryl cyclic adenosine 3′,5′-monophosphate (db-cAMP; Sigma-Aldrich, D0627)]. The medium was changed every 3 days. From day 40 onward, organoids were cultured in the final maturation medium without BDNF, GDNF, db-cAMP, and ascorbic acid.

Animal experiments

6-week-old male C57BL/6 N mice (The Central Laboratory Animal Inc., Republic of Korea) were housed in a SPF environment with 23 °C temperature and 50% humidity, with a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle. Sex was not considered in the study, and only male mice were used to control the variables of the experiment. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the guidelines in the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals by Yonsei University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC-A-202302-1650-02).

Electrical stimulation

Four electrodes arranged as two pairs were connected to the stimulator (33500B, Keysight). Stimulation parameters were configured according to each experimental condition and applied to the respective models. For all stimulation groups, the amplitude was set to 1.2 V (peak-to-peak). For LFS and HFS groups, sinusoidal stimulation of 50 and 2000 Hz, respectively, was applied in phase to both electrode pairs. For the TIS group, sinusoidal stimulation at 2000 and 2050 Hz was delivered in phase to each electrode pair, respectively.

Neural recording

Brain organoid recording using 3D LM MEA: The neural signals of brain organoids recorded by 3D LM MEA were acquired using an electrophysiological recording system, which consisted of an RZ2 amplifier processor, PZ5 Neurodigitizer, MZ60 MEA interface (Tucker-Davis Technologies Inc, USA), and a computer with Synapse program. A sampling rate of 24,414 Hz and a 60 Hz notch were used during recording. Mostly, a 0.1–300 Hz bandpass filter was used for recording LFPs, and a 300–3000 Hz bandpass filter was used for recording single-unit spikes.

In vivo mouse brain recording using soft neural probe: The neural signals of the mouse brain recorded by soft LM neural probes were acquired using an electrophysiological recording system, which consisted of an RZ2 amplifier processor, Subject Interface, digital head stage, and a computer with Synapse program. A sampling rate of 24,414 Hz and a 60 Hz notch were used during recording. Mostly, a 0.1–300 Hz bandpass filter was used for recording LFPs, and a 300–3000 Hz bandpass filter was used for recording single-unit spikes.

Neural analysis

Spiking rate

For each electrode channel, spike detection was conducted with a threshold of −4.5. The spiking rate was calculated by dividing the number of spikes by the duration of recording. To plot spiking rate mapping, electrode locations were provided a priori as integer grid coordinates (x, y) and presented as dots. Each spiking rate of electrodes was mapped to color using a continuous colormap (turbo). Also, the spiking rate was reflected in the size of the dots. The radius of dots was calculated by using the scale factor, which is multiplied by the spiking rate.

Neuronal avalanche

Neuronal avalanches were analyzed to evaluate the efficiency of signal propagation and the robustness of functional connectivity within neural tissues. Spiking events recorded from multichannel electrodes were first binned into discrete time windows of Δt = 3 ms, corresponding to the average inter-event interval across electrodes. Consecutive bins containing at least one spike were grouped as a single avalanche, bounded by silent bins with no spikes.

For each avalanche, the avalanche size was defined as the total number of active electrodes within the event, normalized by the mean firing rate. The distribution of avalanche sizes was then computed for each stimulation group. To assess the scaling properties of network activity, the avalanche size probability distribution was plotted on a log–log scale, and the scaling exponent (α) was estimated by linear regression of the distribution. Neural networking map: We quantified the synchronization level between two electrodes using the SPIKE-distance between the spike trains54. For the calculation of SPIKE-distance, we used the Python package Pyspike (version 0.7.0) on a Python environment (version 3.9.2)69. The SPIKE-distance was normalized to an average of 1000 randomly generated baseline values; each baseline value is the SPIKE-distance between two uniformly randomly generated spike trains. Then, the synchronization score was defined as SPIKE-distance values subtracted from 1. This score ranged from 0 to 1, where a higher score indicated a high level of synchrony.

In the analysis, we modeled the electrodes as nodes (e.g., circles in the network map), whose 3D positions represented the position of the actual electrode. We set the synchronized scores between every pair of active electrodes as edge (e.g., lines in the network map) weights between the corresponding nodes. Edges with synchronized scores less than 0.5 were filtered out. Synchronization scores were color-mapped, ranging from 0 (yellow) to 1 (red). In addition, the node size indicated the number of connected electrodes.

Latent dynamics

Latent dynamics analysis was performed to characterize population-level neural activity embedded within high-dimensional spiking signals. Multichannel spike trains recorded from soft liquid-metal–based neural interfaces were first binned (bin size: 1000 ms) and smoothed with a Gaussian kernel (σ = 3). The resulting neural activity matrices (neurons × time bins) were subjected to principal component analysis (PCA), which identified neural modes by capturing the covariance structure of the population activity. The top 3 principal components were used to construct a low-dimensional neural manifold for visualization. For quantitative latent dynamics features, the top 10 principal components were used for computation.

Latent dynamics features

To quantitatively characterize the properties of latent neural dynamics, we extracted multiple geometric and dimensional features from the neural trajectories obtained in a low-dimensional latent space.

The total trajectory length was computed as the cumulative Euclidean distance between consecutive trajectory points in latent space. This measure captures the overall magnitude of population activity evolution, with longer trajectories indicating richer or more extensive neural responses over time.

The curvature of latent trajectories was quantified to evaluate the smoothness and directional consistency of neural population dynamics. For each triplet of consecutive points, two displacement vectors were constructed, and the angle between them was determined. Local curvature was then defined as the turning angle normalized by the arc length between the points, and the mean curvature across the trajectory was calculated. A lower average curvature corresponds to smoother, more coherent trajectories, whereas higher curvature reflects more frequent directional changes in population activity.

RNA extraction and qRT-PCR

Total RNAs were isolated using TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen, 15596026) and reverse-transcribed using the PrimeScript RT Master Mix (Takara, RR036A) to create cDNA. Transcript abundance was determined by qRT-PCR using PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix qPCR (Applied Biosystems, A25742) and performed using the CFX Connect Real-Time System (Bio-Rad). The ΔΔCq method was used to normalize expression levels of each gene to that of glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH). All primers used in this study were purchased from Cosmogenetech, Korea. Primer sequences used are listed in Supplementary Table 3.

Histology and immunohistochemistry

For immunohistological analysis to investigate the biological effect of TIS, whole organoids were immediately fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for overnight at 4 °C and washed with PBS three times. Organoids were embedded in paraffin and sectioned at a thickness of 5 μm using a Leica HisoCore Nanocut microtome. After paraffin removal, sections were permeabilized in 100% MeOH for 5 min and incubated in 10 mM citrate (Sigma-Aldrich, pH 6.0) for 10 min at a temperature higher than 90 °C. Tissue was blocked with blocking buffer containing 3% normal horse serum, 3% BSA, and 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS for 1 h, and double immunohistochemical analysis of the tissue was carried out after incubation with conjugated primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. The following antibodies were used: MAP2-AF647 (1:300, Abcam, ab225315), Nestin-AF488 (1:300, Cell signaling, 35884), PSD95-AF555 (1:300, Abcam, ab302571), SYN-AF488 (1:300, Abcam, ab196379), TBR1 anti-rabbit (1:300, Abcam, ab31940), and TUJ1-AF647 (1:300, BioLegend, 801207). Nuclei were visualized using a fluorescence microscope with an antifade mounting medium containing DAPI (Vector Laboratories, CA). Fluorescence images were captured using an Axio Observer Z1 LSM780 confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss AG, Oberkochen, Germany).

For immunohistological analysis to characterize cortical organoids, the organoids were fixed in 4% PFA for 30–60 min. Incubated in 30% sucrose overnight at 4 °C until organoids sank down and subsequently embedded in O.C.T. compound Tissue-Tek (Sakura Finetek, 4583). Frozen organoids were cryosectioned at a thickness of 18 µm. Sections were blocked and permeabilized in 5% BSA and 0.6% Triton X-100 in PBS. Sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibody diluted in PBS containing 1% BSA and 0.3% Triton X-100. Secondary antibodies diluted in PBS were applied. All primary and secondary antibodies used are listed in Supplementary Table 4. Stained samples were mounted in VECTASHIELD Antifade Mounting Medium with DAPI (Vector Laboratories, H-1200-10). Images were taken using a confocal microscope (Leica TCS SP5).
