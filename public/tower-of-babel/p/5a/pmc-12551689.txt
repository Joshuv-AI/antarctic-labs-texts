Steroid Withdrawal Versus Maintenance in Simultaneous Pancreas–Kidney Transplantation: Long‐Term Outcomes in a Single‐Center Retrospective Cohort
Authors: Raphael P H Meier, Ali B Abbasi, Gerardo Gamino, Yvonne M Kelly, Mehdi Tavakol, Giulia Conti, Minnie Sarwal, Peter G Stock, Chris E Freise
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundSimultaneous pancreas and kidney (SPK) transplants using steroid‐free regimens are a frequently used immunosuppressive strategy in low immunologic risk recipients. The long‐term effect on recipient outcomes remains incompletely understood.MethodsWe analyzed post‐transplant outcomes in 307 SPK recipients from a single center, comparing early steroid withdrawal (after completion of Thymoglobulin) with maintenance steroids. Steroid withdrawal was primarily utilized in low immunologic risk patients.ResultsIn total, 170 SPK recipients underwent steroid withdrawal, and 137 received maintenance steroids. The withdrawal group had a lower cPRA and included fewer Black recipients. Biopsy‐proven rejection rates (kidney, pancreas, or both) were similar between groups (26% vs. 29%, p = 0.523), with worse kidney/pancreas graft survival among those with rejection. Rejection occurred earliest in recipients rejecting both the kidney and pancreas, followed by those rejecting the kidney alone, and then those rejecting the pancreas. Multivariate analysis showed that kidney and pancreas survival were strongly interdependent. Kidney rejection was the primary predictor of graft survival. The steroid regimen was not independently associated with survival of either organ. Obesity at follow‐up was associated with an increased risk of pancreas graft failure.ConclusionIn this single‐center cohort, steroid withdrawal was safe in appropriately selected SPK recipients, with rejection—not the steroid regimen—driving long‐term graft outcomes.Keywords: body mass index, diabetes, end‐stage renal disease, immunosuppression, outcomes, simultaneous pancreas and kidney transplantation, steroids

Introduction

Simultaneous pancreas and kidney (SPK) transplantation is a well‐accepted therapy for patients with renal failure due to type 1 diabetes [1, 2]. Graft and patient survival have gradually improved over the last three decades, with a marked benefit noted with the introduction of tacrolimus‐based protocols following induction with lymphocyte depleting agents [3]. Rejection rates have dropped to under 30% at most large centers, and the concept of sparing steroids has been adopted in a subset of recipients to avoid the long‐term side effects associated with chronic steroids and avoid beta‐cell toxicity [4]. Several studies have confirmed favorable outcomes for graft and patient survival with the addition of mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) and/or sirolimus to tacrolimus‐based regimens [5, 6, 7]. These studies are limited by short‐term follow‐up, generally less than 3 years, but results support the potential safety and efficacy for steroid‐free immunosuppression in SPK recipients. There continues to be debate as to the best immunosuppressive agents to combine with tacrolimus, and there has been no double‐blind randomized trial to evaluate MMF versus sirolimus. Therefore, conclusions about the superiority or equivalence of these two therapies have been mostly based on retrospective single‐center studies [5, 6, 7]. Since the early 2000s, the University of California, San Francisco (UCSF) has progressively adopted an early steroid withdrawal regimen in SPK recipients considered to be at low immunologic risk. The initial experience utilized both sirolimus and MMF in combination with tacrolimus immediately post‐transplant, with MMF discontinued at 6 months post‐transplant. Our more recent maintenance protocol, for the above‐mentioned low‐risk recipient population, utilizes tacrolimus and MMF only, with discontinuation of steroids after completion of induction, usually before discharge. This retrospective analysis will compare long‐term graft outcomes and complications in the population who received steroid‐free maintenance immunosuppression versus those who were on a steroid maintenance regimen.

Methods

Patients

All consecutive adult patients undergoing SPK between 2005 and 2023 at UCSF were included. Patients with no follow‐up data were excluded. Patient characteristics, demographic data, pancreas and kidney function, patient and overall death‐censored graft survival, and deceased donor data were collected from chart review, administrative databases, and the UNOS database. Indication was insulin dependent type 1 or type 2 diabetes with end‐stage renal disease. The median follow‐up was 7.3 years (interquartile range [IQR], 4–12 years). Pancreas donor risk index (PDRI) was calculated as previously described [8]. Delayed graft function (DGF) was defined as patients requiring hemodialysis in the first week after transplantation. Six‐month protocol kidney biopsy was introduced in 2008. Only for‐cause pancreas biopsies were performed. Kidney and/or pancreas rejection was determined by pathological examination, following Banff criteria classification [7, 9, 10, 11]. Kidney graft failure was defined according to OPTN/UNOS criteria as the return to or initiation of maintenance dialysis or kidney retransplantation. Pancreas graft failure was defined according to OPTN/UNOS criteria as pancreas graft removal, pancreas retransplantation, or sustained reintroduction of insulin or oral hypoglycemic therapy. BMI data were collected at the time of transplant and at the time of last follow‐up. The study was approved by the Committee for Human Research at the University of California San Francisco and met criteria for waiver of consent.

Immunosuppression and Group Definitions

All patients received induction consisting of steroids and thymoglobulin 6 mg/kg. Patients with lower immunological risk were taken off prednisone completely by the end of their hospital stay and thereafter constitute the early “steroid withdrawal” group. Low immunologic risk was defined as first transplant, low cPRA (generally ≤ 60%), and absence of donor‐specific antibodies. Although Black recipients were generally considered at higher immunological risk, a small number were included in the withdrawal group, reflecting individualized clinical decision‐making at the time of transplant. The other SPK recipients receiving long‐term steroids were placed in the “steroid maintenance” group. Analyses were performed primarily by intention‐to‐treat, assigning patients to steroid withdrawal or maintenance per the discharge immunosuppression plan, with a prespecified per‐protocol sensitivity that reclassified patients to maintenance at the time of any post‐transplant steroid resumption for Cox models of graft survival. Both groups received equivalent and standard tacrolimus dosing with targeted trough levels of 8–12 ng/mL, and MMF or mycophenolic acid dosed at 1 g or 720 mg bid.

Statistical Analyses

Results are presented as mean ± standard deviation and as count and percentage for categorical variables unless specified otherwise. Differences between groups were analyzed with the Student's t‐test for continuous variables and the Chi‐square test for binary and categorical variables. The Mann–Whitney test was used to compare variables with a non‐Gaussian distribution. Survival analyses were performed with the Kaplan–Meier method, and groups were compared using the log‐rank test. Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional‐hazards regression was used to compute hazard ratios (HR). Univariate and multivariate logistic regression was used to compute odds ratio (OR). Ninety‐five percent confidence intervals (95% CI) were reported, and an exact two‐sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical data and graphs were generated using SPSS version 29.0 (SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA) and Prism version 10 (GraphPad, San Diego, CA, USA).

Results

Baseline Characteristics

A total of 307 patients were assigned to either the steroid withdrawal group (n = 170) or the steroid maintenance group (n = 137). The proportion of steroid withdrawal versus maintenance recipients and the availability of kidney and pancreas graft biopsies over the study period are shown in Figure 1A,B, respectively. Baseline characteristics for patients in the withdrawal and maintenance groups are shown in Table 1. Consistent with their lower immunological risk, patients in the steroid withdrawal group had lower cPRA, were less likely to be Black, waited less time on the transplant list, and spent fewer years on dialysis. The pancreas cold ischemia time (CIT) was longer in the steroid withdrawal group. Other patient and donor characteristics did not differ significantly between groups.

Pancreas and Kidney Graft Rejection

The steroid withdrawal protocol was applied to a majority of patients in 2005 and a decline was seen in recent years (Figure 1A). About one‐third of the recipients in the steroid withdrawal group ultimately had to resume steroids due to concerns for rejection (n = 62) (Table 2) with most conversions occurring within the first 2 years (Figure 2A). Immunosuppression regimens including mTOR inhibitors or belatacept were more frequently used in the withdrawal group, and consequently tacrolimus use was lower in this group, although it was still used in 94% of recipients (Table 2). mTORs were used more frequently from 2005 to 2008 (Figure S1). The 1‐year biopsy‐proven rejection rate was 11.1%, comprising 34 events (29 kidney rejections, four pancreas rejections, and one combined kidney–pancreas rejection). Clinical and/or biopsy‐proven rejection of kidney and/or pancreas occurred in 27.1% of recipients in the steroid withdrawal group compared with 35.0% in the maintenance group (p = 0.137). Kidney and/or pancreas biopsies were performed more frequently in the maintenance group compared with the withdrawal group (84.7% vs. 72.9%, p = 0.018). Overall rates of biopsy‐proven rejection (kidney and/or pancreas) were similar between the steroid withdrawal and maintenance groups (25.9% vs. 29.2%, p = 0.523) (Table 2). Most of the initial rejection episodes occurred within the first‐year post‐transplant (Figure 2B). When analyzed separately, the frequency of biopsy‐proven kidney rejection was not statistically different between the withdrawal group and the maintenance group (16.5% vs. 24.1%, p = 0.114). The distribution of rejection types in the kidney showed no significant difference between groups. Biopsy‐proven pancreas rejection occurred at comparable rates (10.0% vs. 8.8%, p = 0.845), with rejection type distribution also showing no significant differences between groups. The time to rejection stratified by organ showed that the earliest rejection occurred when both kidney and pancreas were involved, followed by kidney, then pancreas (Figure 2C). Eighty‐five percent (79/94) of patients who experienced pancreas or kidney rejection remained on or resumed long‐term steroid therapy.

Transplant Outcomes

The duration of hospital stay was similar for both groups. DGF rate and creatinine at discharge were significantly lower in recipients in the steroid withdrawal group (Table 3). The difference remained statistically significant at 1 year but became non‐significant in the long term and DGF had no impact on long‐term kidney graft survival (Figure S2A,B). Five‐year kidney graft survival was higher in the steroid withdrawal group compared with the maintenance group (96.0 ± 1.6 vs. 93.4 ± 2.3, p = 0.023) (Figure 3A). Among patients who lost their kidney graft, rejection was the cause in 84.2% (16/19) of the cases within the steroid withdrawal group versus 75.0% (12/16) in the maintenance group (p = 0.677). Five‐year pancreas graft survival was similar between groups (88.4% ± 2.5 vs. 84.7% ± 3.5, p = 0.871) (Figure 3B). Accordingly, hemoglobin A1c at last follow‐up was similar between groups (5.8 ± 1.3% vs. 5.9 ± 1.8%, p = 0.641). Among patients who lost their pancreas graft, rejection was the cause in 44.1% (15/34) of the cases within the steroid withdrawal group versus 65.2% (15/23) in the maintenance group (p = 0.177). Kidney and pancreas graft survival in the presence or absence of either pancreas or kidney rejection episode is shown for both groups in Figure 4A,B. The presence of one or more episodes of rejection of the pancreas and/or the kidney had a negative impact on kidney and pancreas survival, regardless of the steroid protocol. Moreover, assignment to either steroid protocol had no impact on kidney or pancreas graft survival among patients who experienced rejection (data not shown). Patient survival at 5 years was also comparable for both groups (93.7% ± 1.9 vs. 95.9% ± 1.8, p = 0.752). The different eras did not show significant differences in kidney or pancreas survival (Figure S3A,B). The proportion of patients who died of an infection and the number of hospital readmissions were not statistically different between the steroid withdrawal versus maintenance groups (Table 3). Risk factors for kidney and pancreas graft loss are provided in Table 4. In the univariate analysis, factors associated with inferior kidney graft survival were younger age at transplant, higher cPRA, steroid maintenance (intention to treat and per protocol), higher creatinine and hemoglobin A1c at last follow‐up, pancreas graft failure, and kidney rejection. In the multivariate model, only kidney rejection and pancreas graft failure at last follow‐up remained independently associated with kidney graft loss. For pancreas grafts, univariate risk factors for inferior survival included younger age at transplant, history of previous transplant, longer initial hospital stay, higher creatinine and hemoglobin A1c at last follow‐up, kidney rejection, and kidney graft failure. In the multivariate analysis, only kidney graft failure at last follow‐up was independently associated with inferior pancreas graft survival.

Body Mass Index Evolution After SPK

At transplant, most recipients in withdrawal and maintenance groups were in the normal or overweight BMI classes, with smaller proportions in obesity class I and very few in classes II–III (Table 5). By last follow‐up, the distribution had shifted upward in both groups, with fewer patients remaining in the normal BMI category (withdrawal group: 45.9% vs. 54.1% at baseline; maintenance group: 38.0% vs. 53.3% at baseline) and more classified as overweight or obese. Notably, more patients moved out of the underweight category in the steroid withdrawal group (withdrawal group: 1.2% vs. 2.9% at baseline), while overall a higher proportion of patients were in obesity classes II–III in the steroid maintenance group at last follow‐up (maintenance group: 8.1% vs. 0.7% at baseline) (Table 5, Figure 5A). BMI increase was not statistically different in type I diabetes compared with type II diabetes (+1.7 ± 3.8 vs. +1.2 ± 2.6 kg/m2, p = 0.553). The detailed evolution of post‐transplant BMI and weight is shown in Figures 5B and S4, respectively. The difference between the two groups was not statistically significant, although trends showed that patients in the steroid withdrawal group did not increase their BMI until post‐transplant year 4, whereas patients in the steroid maintenance group increased their BMI earlier in the post‐transplant period. Sixty‐three patients were obese by the end of their follow‐up, reaching the BMI class I and above. Their characteristics and comparison with the rest of the cohort are presented in Table 6. The significant differences were lower tacrolimus and higher belatacept use, and a lower donor terminal creatinine among recipients who were obese at the end of the follow‐up. Patients who were obese at the end of follow‐up (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2) had a 1.8‐fold increased hazard of pancreas graft failure (95% CI: 1.0–3.1, p = 0.044) (Figure 5C). There were no statistically significant differences in steroid use, rejection rates, or kidney graft survival between the groups.

Discussion

In this single‐center, long‐term study, we compared SPK transplant recipients who received an immunosuppression regimen consisting of either steroid withdrawal or steroid maintenance to analyze the effect of the different regimens on rejection and post‐transplant outcomes. We confirm that steroid withdrawal after SPK was non‐inferior to a steroid maintenance protocol for pancreas and kidney graft survival after multivariable adjustment. Kidney graft loss was more prevalent in the maintenance group; however, in multivariable analysis only rejection and pancreas graft failure remained significant predictors. About one‐third of the patients had to be transitioned back to steroid maintenance due to rejection. We also provide a unique assessment of BMI evolution post‐transplant and show that patients who became obese were at increased risk of pancreas graft loss. Steroid protocols did not significantly influence the development of obesity in our SPK recipient population.

In the 2000s, some groups started to analyze the outcomes of steroid withdrawal in pancreas transplant recipients who were on tacrolimus maintenance [6, 12, 13, 14]. SPK and pancreas after kidney (PAK) recipients in these studies tolerated steroid withdrawal well, and patient and graft survival rates were comparable between patients on and off steroids. Some showed that patients off steroids had better glucose control, with; however, a higher risk of rejection [12]. We did not observe a significantly higher rate of rejection in our cohort with steroid withdrawal. However, this assessment is limited by a selection bias since patients in the steroid withdrawal group tend to be those with a lower immunological risk. Cantarovich et al. compared two steroid‐sparing immunosuppressive regimens for SPK recipients: one without any steroids and the other including withdrawal after 3 months post‐transplant [15]. The study found that both groups had a low incidence of acute rejection (4% at 1‐year, compared to 11% in our study) with no statistically significant differences in patient, kidney, or pancreas survival after 1‐year. The occurrence of adverse events, hospitalization length, infectious episodes, and metabolic parameters did not significantly differ between the two groups. The serum creatinine level at 1 year was higher in the steroid avoidance group which contrasts with our observations. Withdrawal of steroids has also been done in PAK transplantation patients [16]. Steroids could be safely withdrawn in recipients receiving lymphocyte‐depleting antibodies and already on maintenance prednisone, with no significant differences in renal function, the incidence of infections, and allograft and patient survival. In 2014, a Cochrane systematic review analyzed three randomized controlled trials and several cohort studies and did not show clear evidence of an impact on mortality, graft survival, or risk of rejection when comparing steroid avoidance or early withdrawal with late withdrawal. The cohort studies generally supported the findings of the RCTs, showing benefits in terms of lowering hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) (only marginally lower in our study), reducing infections, and improving blood pressure control. This systematic review, however, deemed the level of evidence insufficient to draw definitive conclusions, and some studies reported an increased incidence of pancreas or kidney acute rejection, which we did not observe. It is important to reemphasize that the steroid withdrawal group had a lower cPRA and a non‐significantly lower rejection rate compared to the maintenance group (27% vs. 35%), further suggesting no increased risk of rejection with steroid withdrawal. More recently, a study from a single center and from the International Pancreas Transplant Registry, confirmed that there was no difference in 10‐year overall patient survival between SPK patients receiving steroid versus withdrawal maintenance [17]. In that regard, our study confirmed the absence of long‐term differences in terms of patient, kidney, or pancreas survival between steroid withdrawal and maintenance groups. Rejection of pancreas and/or kidney negatively affected graft survival.

Given the potential impact of steroids on body mass, we analyzed the difference in BMI pre‐ and post‐transplant. Recipient's pre‐transplant BMIs were similar amongst groups and they generally increased their BMI post‐transplant, without major differences amongst those on steroid versus those who were not. A trend toward faster BMI increase post‐transplant in the steroid maintenance group was observed but non‐significant. Although large doses of steroids have an easily observable effect on BMI, a lower maintenance dose (usually 5 mg) might require a larger cohort to demonstrate statistical significance. Of note, we previously observed a similar trend in the development of sarcopenia in post‐SPK recipients [18]. A subset of recipients (21%) became obese after their SPK (class I to III obesity levels) reaching a BMI of 34 kg/m2 on average. We observed that patients who became obese post‐transplant had inferior pancreas survival. Whether obesity contributed to graft failure (e.g., by increasing insulin requirements and production) or was a consequence of pancreas dysfunction remains to be determined. Our results contrast with a recent study in which significant weight gain was not associated with inferior pancreas survival [19]. The discrepancy may be explained by center‐specific differences, variations in the definition of BMI/weight gain (becoming obese based on BMI in our study vs. any weight gain in theirs), and differences in the definition of pancreas graft failure. In any case, some of these patients may benefit from GLP‐1 agonists, given the recently demonstrated efficacy of these agents [20].

The present study has several limitations. First, we conducted a retrospective analysis on a single‐center cohort of patients, which does not allow to add more granular data nor draw definitive conclusions on causality and implies that clinical and mechanistic data were conditional on prior availability. Second, it is notable that our study spans nearly two decades, during which progressive changes in practice occurred (e.g., evolving steroid and non‐steroid immunosuppressive regimens), which may confound outcomes. However, this broad timeframe allowed us to include a sufficient number of pancreas transplants with a reasonable follow‐up period. Importantly, graft survival outcomes did not show major changes between eras. Nevertheless, our study remained underpowered to draw definitive conclusions in subgroup analyses such as type 2 diabetes and Black recipients. Third, some degree of selection bias was expected between groups, with the steroid regimen being dictated in part by the immunization status. Our observations suggest that the tailored approach allowed comparable outcomes in both steroid withdrawal and maintenance groups. Patients in the steroid maintenance group underwent more frequent biopsies than those in the withdrawal group (85% vs. 73%, p = 0.018), likely driven by a higher suspicion of rejection. Of note, this may have led to earlier and higher detection rates of kidney/pancreas rejection (including subclinical cases). Also, tacrolimus trough levels and MMF dosing were not uniformly available across the study period, limiting our ability to model their impact. Finally, we concede that a prospective, double‐blind, randomized trial directly comparing steroid withdrawal to maintenance would be required to draw definitive conclusions. The only prospective (open‐label) evidence to date, reported by Gruessner et al., similarly found no significant difference in outcomes between steroid withdrawal and maintenance, with potential benefits in cholesterol levels favoring withdrawal [6].

In conclusion, among appropriately selected, low‐immunologic‐risk SPK recipients, early steroid withdrawal was non‐inferior to steroid maintenance for kidney and pancreas graft survival after multivariable adjustment; in Cox models, long‐term outcomes were driven chiefly by rejection. However, it is noteworthy that approximately one‐third of the patients required transition back to steroid maintenance primarily due to episodes of rejection. The study confirmed that rejection and co‐organ survival were significant factors influencing pancreas and kidney graft survival. Finally, SPK recipients who develop obesity appear to be at increased risk of pancreas graft failure.
