Natural 2-phenyl chroman flavonoids against Streptococcus mutans: prospects of herbal remedies for dental caries
Authors: Priyabrata Pattanayak, Ananya Pradhan, Sudhansu Sekhar Rout, Gunjan Srivastava
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dental caries is a multi-factorial oral infectious disease worldwide. Among the 600 different types of bacteria inhabiting the oral cavity, Streptococcus mutans is considered the most prolific colonizer contributing to dental caries. Although fluorides and broad-spectrum antimicrobials such as chlorhexidine remain the primary approaches for caries treatment, natural flavonoids can serve as helpful alternatives. Although flavonoids are effective against different biofilm-forming pathogens, in the present review, attempts have been made to emphasize the antibiofilm properties of only such flavonoids that are effective against S. mutans mediated biofilm formation, along with their possible mode of action and their future prospective as anticaries agent. Different flavonoids decrease bacterial virulence in the oral cavity by different mechanisms: decreasing biofilm adhesivity and formation, inhibiting sortase A (SrtA), inhibiting glucosyltransferase, etc. Fluorides and chlorohexidine, which show anticaries effects altogether by a different mechanism, can effectively combine with flavonoids in combating dental caries. Although most natural products are devoid of acute toxicity, the toxicity profile following brief exposure of topically applied flavonoids in the oral cavity must be assessed before clinical studies. Furthermore, innovative drug delivery systems could greatly enhance the applicability and utility of these flavonoids. This virulence-selective study will lay the foundation for preclinical and clinical studies to further investigate the potential of these flavonoids as possible anticariogenic drugs.Graphical abstract A schematic representation illustrating the mechanism of formation of biofilms

Introduction

Over 600 different types of bacteria inhabit the oral cavity, among which Streptococcus mutans (S. mutans) is the most prolific colonizer contributing to dental caries. S. mutans action on dietary carbohydrates produces lactic acid, leading to a fall of pH in the surroundings, which subsequently inhibits the growth of other oral microbial species, making S.mutans the predominant cariogenic species. The fall in pH in the surrounding environment due to lactic acid also accelerates the demineralization of the tooth, ensuring tooth decay and biofilm formation [1, 2]. The extreme acid tolerance (aciduricity) ability of S. mutans allows it to thrive in the low pH environment [3]. The F1F0-ATPase is crucial for this survival as it acts as an ATP-dependent proton pump that expels excess protons (H+) from the cytoplasm, preventing the cell from becoming too acidic [4].

Dental caries is associated with biofilms, which are a three-dimensional matrix of polymeric material composed of exopolysaccharides, proteins, and microbial cells. These extracellular biofilms, if left untreated for long, can mature, leading to dental caries. Although several species contribute to the pathogenesis of dental caries, S. mutans is the predominant etiological agent. The adhesion of free-floating planktonic cells of S. mutans to the tooth surface is mediated through sucrose-dependent and sucrose-independent mechanisms. In the sucrose-dependent pathway, cariogenic bacteria initially attach reversibly to the tooth surface and interact with food materials. The three types of glucosyltransferase enzymes (GTFs), like GTF B, GTF C, and GTF D, produced by S. mutans, effectively utilize the dietary carbohydrates and convert them into sticky insoluble 1, 3-linked glucans (Fig. 1). The glucan-binding proteins (GBPs) and the GTFs accelerate the adhesion of interlinked glucans and bacterial cells on the tooth surface and subsequently build the three-dimensional biofilm structures by interacting with polymeric substances and forming an irreversible adhesion [5]. The sucrose-independent adhesions of S. mutans to collagen in the oral cavity are mediated by several S. mutans surface adhesins, like streptococcal protein antigen P (SpaP) and wall-associated protein A (WapA). These adhesins are attached to the bacterial cell by a transpeptidase, sortase A (SrtA) [6]. Thus, SrtA has a crucial role in the development of biofilm. Finally, the biofilm matures and disperses to other tooth surfaces to continue the same cycle. S. mutans can cling tenaciously to the tooth surface through biofilm development, preventing it from being eliminated. Biofilm development ultimately increases antibiotic resistance by limiting S. mutans response to drugs.Fig. 1A schematic representation illustrating the mechanism of formation of biofilms

It has been demonstrated that eradicating or reducing S. mutans can prevent or lessen the advancement of caries [7]. The use of natural compounds against S. mutans biofilm formation has been extensively studied. Brushing teeth using plant twigs/branches was used before modern toothpaste and toothbrushes. Some of such plants are Calendula officinalis, Streblus asper, Camellia sinensis, Jatropa gossipiifolia, Azadirachta indica, Mangifera indica L., Vachellia nilotica, Butea monosperma, Phyllanthus emblica L., Terminalia arjuna, Pongamia pinnata, Senegalia catechu, etc. These plants are sources of different phytoconstituents like terpinoids, alkaloids, flavonoids, etc. Several research articles have elaborated on the anticariogenic properties of flavonoids. Although fluorides and broad- spectrum antimicrobials like chlorohexidine and cetylpyridinium chloride remain the primary approaches in the treatment of caries, natural flavonoids can be useful alternatives. Although flavonoids are effective against different biofilm-forming pathogens, in the present review attempts have been made to emphasize the anti-cariogenic properties of only such flavonoids that are effective against S. mutans mediated biofilm formation along with their possible mode of action and their future prospective as anticaries.

Chemistry of flavonoids

Humans consume a significant number of flavonoids in their daily diet, which provides different beneficial effects to the body. Flavonoids, produced as secondary metabolites in plants, are widely distributed in different plant parts, including flowers, fruits, stems, nuts, and seeds. The polyphenolic flavonoids have a 15-carbon basic skeleton with a C6-C3-C6 configuration. The three-carbon linker between the two aromatic rings A and C is commonly cyclized with oxygen to form a benzopyrano (chromano) moiety (Fig. 2). These natural products can be categorized into three groups according to where the aromatic ring is linked to the benzopyrano (chromano) moiety. They are 2-phenylbenzopyrans (flavonoids), 3- phenylbenzopyrans (isoflavonoids), and 4-benzopyrans (neoflavonoids).Fig. 2Skeletal system of different benzopyrano moiety in flavonoids

Skeletal system of different benzopyrano moiety in flavonoids

Thus, flavonoids have a 2-phenylchroman heterocyclic moiety as their basic skeleton. Flavonoids most often remain conjugated with common sugars like galactose, D-glucose, L-rhamnose, and exist as O-glycosides, C-glycosides or as aglycones. The glyconic sugar part and the substituted –OH group on the aglyconic part impart hydrophilicity to the flavonoids, whereas the aromatic skeleton imparts lipophilicity. Depending upon the degree of unsaturation and oxidation of ring B, flavonoids have been further classified as shown in Fig. 3. Prospects of antibiofilm activity of flavonoid

Apigenin

Propolis, a non-toxic resinous bee hive product secreted by Apis mellifera, is reported to have an inhibitory effect on S. mutans and glucosyltransferases enzymes. In rats, topical application of propolis twice daily has reduced the development and accumulation of dental caries [8]. Apigenin is an important 2-phenylchroman flavonoid isolated from propolis. Apigenin is also ubiquitous in many parts of plants, especially in the Cranberry fruit extracts (Vaccinium macrocarpon). It showed a low minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of 1.3 ± 0.4 µg/mL against S. mutans, which was comparable to chlorhexidine (1.6 ± 0.2 µg/mL), indicating potent antibacterial activity. Apigenin significantly reduced biofilm thickness at 25 µg/mL and inhibited glucosyltransferase activity (particularly gtf C), thereby inhibiting insoluble glucan synthesis and disrupting biofilm architecture [9]. In another study, apigenin, at a concentration of up to 135 μg/mL (500 μM), showed an intense inhibitory action on GTFs from in solution, with an inhibition of 90.5 to 95% [10]. However, the effect on the surface-adsorbed enzymes was not as potent as above, with 30–60% inhibition at the same concentration. The IC50 of apigenin for GTFs in solution was 16–28 μg/ml. Although the IC50 of apigenin for the surface-adsorbed enzyme was relatively high (>128 μg/mL), it was even more effective than the currently available chlorohexidine mouthwash. Chlorhexidine, a commercially available anticaries agent, showed only 30–45% inhibition in solution and 10–20% inhibition on the surface adhesion. Apigenin was devoid of bactericidal effects against S. mutans biofilm. So, the anticaries effectiveness of apigenin may be attributed to its ability to inhibit the extracellular glucan synthesis by inhibiting the glucosyltransferase enzyme [11]. Although the precise mechanism of glucosyltransferase inhibition by apigenin is not clear, it is thought that the presence of a C2-C3 double bond in apigenin’s molecular structure may serve as a site for nucleophilic attack by the side-chain amino acid of glucosyltransferases, resulting in enzyme inhibition [10]. Further, the dextran-dependent aggregation of S. mutans is associated with the increased expression of the glucan-binding protein (GBPs) by some mutants of S. mutans, contributing to the adherence and aggregation of S. mutans to the tooth surface [12]. Apigenin significantly suppressed glucan-binding protein expression by 95.3% at 156.3 µg/mL, potentially affecting S. mutans adherence and biofilm formation. Fluoride is an effective anticaries agent that probably interferes with caries formation physicochemically by accelerating remineralization and reducing demineralization of tooth enamel [13]. It also has a bactericidal effect. Thus, apigenin, which shows anticaries effect by a different mechanism that interferes in the metabolism of cariogenic bacteria, can be an effective combination with fluoride in combating dental caries [14].

Artocarpesin, artocarpin, artocarpanone, cycloartocarpin, and cyanomaclurin

All these flavonoids have been isolated from the heartwood of Artocarpus heterophyllus Lam. Sato and coworkers [15] isolated flavones artocarpin and artocarpesin from the methanolic extract of the heartwood of flavonoid-rich Artocarpus heterophyllus Lam., having an antiplaque effect. These two flavones showed intensive activity against the growth of cariogenic and plaque-forming streptococcal species S. mutans and related species with MIC values of 6.25 µg/mL and 12.5 µg/mL, respectively. The polyhydroxy substituents on rings A and B, including 5-hydroxylation, and the aliphatic substituent on ring A are essential for antibacterial activity against S. mutans, as reported by the authors. The above finding was also supported by the SAR studies of flavonoids [16].

In another bioassay-guided isolation, Septama and Panichayupakaranant [17] isolated four flavonoids, including cycloartocarpin and cyanomaclurin, as well as artocarpin and artocarpanone. Among these flavonoids, artocarpin showed the highest antibacterial activity against S. mutans with an MIC of 4.4 µM, followed by cyanomaclurin (MIC 6.8 µM), artocarpanone (MIC 25.8 µM), and cycloartocarpin (MIC 35.9 µM). All four compounds exhibited MBC values ranging from 8.9 to 54.5 µM. The standard drug ampicillin showed greater activity than these flavonoids, with MIC and MBC values of 1.4 µM against S. mutans. The structure-activity studies of these flavonoids indicate that an isoprenyl group at the C-3 position on ring B, a 3-methyl-1-butenyl group at the C-6 position on ring A, and a hydroxyl group at the C-2 position on ring C are essential for the antibacterial activity of artocarpin. Cyclization of rings B and C using the C-2 hydroxy group on ring C and the isoprenyl group at C-3 on ring B have decreased the activity as in cycloartocarpin and cyanomaclurin against S. mutans. Further, the presence of an additional hydroxy group at the C-4 position, which contributes to the hydrophilicity of the compound, might be essential for the activity [16]. Saturation of the double bond between C2 and the C-3 position on ring B has decreased the activity against S. mutans. These flavones could be effective in the prevention of dental caries.

Astilbin

Astilbin, isolated from the rhizome of Rhizoma Smilacis Glabrae, is a flavanone commonly used in traditional Chinese medicine. In the study of the effect of astilbin on the catalytic activity of SrtA, it was observed that astilbin has a potent inhibitory action on the activity of SrtA in a dose-dependent manner with an IC50 value of 7.5 µg/mL [18]. The MIC of astilbin against S. mutans was above 1024 µg/mL, suggesting that astilbin decreases SrtA catalytic activity without affecting the proliferation of S. mutans. Further, the growth of S. mutans was similar when treated with different concentrations of astilbin as observed from the S. mutans growth curve, indicating that astilbin could be used as a potential biofilm inhibitor without the development of drug resistance.

Baicalein

Baicalein is a 5, 6, 7-trihydroxyflavone isolated from the roots of Scutellaria baicalensis and Scutellaria lateriflora. It was reported to inhibit biofilm formation by S. mutans in a dose-dependent manner, with 50 and 80% inhibition at 200 µM and 400 µM, respectively [19]. Baicalein reduced biofilm biomass by 70% and biofilm matrix thickness by 75% relative to the control. Baicalein also decreased cell surface hydrophobicity at the 5th and 7th hours of treatment, which is essential for the auto-aggregation of bacterial cells during biofilm formation. At the 9th hour, a significant decrease in the hydrophobicity was observed with a hydrophobicity index of 25% compared to the untreated control with a hydrophobicity index of > 70%. Baicalein decreased the auto-aggregation of bacterial cells at all the time points considered by at least 20% compared to the control. S. mutans activity on dietary carbohydrates produces lactic acid, an important virulence factor leading to a fall of pH in the oral cavity, accelerating demineralization and cariogenicity. Baicalein did not allow a pH drop below 5.2–5.5, which is considered ideal for tooth demineralization. Baicalein solution increased the pH from the 5th hour of incubation onward and maintained it until the 9th hour, whereas the control group showed a drop in pH to about 4. Baicalein reduces lactic acid production by more than 90% [20]. The biofilm formation retards the adsorption of fluoride on the tooth surface, which accelerates remineralization of the tooth surface and is currently employed as an effective anticaries agent. The combination of sodium fluoride and baicalein demonstrated a synergistic effect on S. mutans biofilm formation, with a combination index of < 1 relative to sodium fluoride treatment alone. In another study, baicalein reduced the MIC of penicillin from 0.25 μg/mL to 0.0625 μg/mL (4-fold), producing a synergistic effect as indicated by the Fractional inhibitory concentration (FIC) index of < 0.5 against S. mutans [21]. The MIC of gentamicin was reduced from 8 μg/mL to 2 μg/mL when combined with baicalein, yielding an FIC index of 0.25. The above studies corroborate the potential usefulness of baicalein against S. mutans mediated dental caries.

Catechins

Catechins are a group of flavonoids isolated from the leaves of Camellia sinensis. Depending upon the various ways of processing leaves (green, black, and oolong tea), six different flavonoids have been isolated from the leaves of Camellia sinensis. These flavonoids include catechin, epicatechin (EC), epicatechin gallate (ECG), gallocatechin, epigallocatechin (EGC), and epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG). The Japanese green tea extract from the leaves of Camellia sinensis, containing all six polyphenolic compounds (designated as sunphenon in Japan), effectively inhibited S. mutans attachments to the surface of the saliva-coated hydroxyapatite disc at a concentration greater than 25 μg/mL. Sunphenon inhibited GTFs by 67.5% at a concentration of 333.3 μg/mL [22]. Among these polyphenols, EGCG was most potent, exhibiting 90.6% inhibition at 166.7 μg/mL, followed by EGC with 81.5% inhibition at the same concentration. Catechin did not show any inhibitory effect on GTFs, and other polyphenols show moderate inhibitory activity between 30.2–40.3% at 166.7 μg/mL. In another study, it was demonstrated that EGCG, the most pharmacologically active and abundant catechins, inhibited sucrose-dependent S. mutans adherence in a dose-dependent manner (7.8–31.25 μg/mL) with 98.33% inhibition at two h relative to the control in the chamber slide model without inducing cellular aggregation at the test concentrations employed. The real-time PCR (polymerase chain reaction) demonstrated that at sub-MIC level (15.6 μg/mL), EGCG significantly suppressed the expression of gtf genes of S. mutans that encode GTFs by 77–90%, which is responsible for the formation of exopolysaccharide matrix during biofilm formation compared to the control [23].

Guaijaverin

The antimicrobial activity of the methanolic extract of leaves of Psidium guajava Linn. MIC of > 5 mg/ml led to the isolation of Guaijaverin or quercetin-3-O-α-L-arabinopyranoside from the leaves of Psidium guajava Linn. Guaijaverin demonstrated bacteriostatic activity with an MIC of 4 mg/mL against the S. mutans strain MTCC 1943 and 2 mg/mL against the strain CLSM001. At sub-MIC concentrations (0.0078–2 mg/mL), guaijaverin gradually decreased the acid production of both bacterial strains and the hydrophobicity of the bacterial strain [24]. So, it could prevent S. mutans from adhering to tooth surfaces, thereby reducing the incidence of caries.

Hesperidin

Sweet orange peel extracts are a rich source of flavonoids. It contains higher levels of total phytochemicals than other edible parts. The phytochemicals include flavonoids like narirutin, hesperidin, quercetin, sinensetin, nobiletin, and tangeretin. Hesperidin (hesperetin-7-O-rutinoside) and narirutin were the prominent flavonoids among all the phytocompounds. These two flavonoids were the least susceptible to S. mutans. Even hesperidine was inactive at 10 mg/ml against S. mutans [25]. The other isolated flavonoids, such as nobiletin, tangeretin, and sinensetin, showed more potent activity against S. mutans. However, hesperidine exhibited potential antibiofilm activity against methicillin-resistant S. aureus at 100 μg/mL by inhibiting auto-aggregation and down-regulating the genes that promote adhesion [26]. It also accelerates remineralization of the tooth without affecting bacterial growth [27]. These flavonoids, including hesperidine, have demonstrated potential antibiofilm properties against other microorganisms. In another study, it was demonstrated that hesperidin exhibited significant antimicrobial and protective effects when incorporated (2%) into a total-etch dentin adhesive. It showed concentration-dependent antibacterial activity against key cariogenic microorganisms, including Streptococcus mutans, Lactobacillus acidophilus, and Enterococcus faecalis, with minimal cytotoxicity. Hesperidin functions as a natural collagen cross-linking agent, enhancing the mechanical properties of dentin and improving bond stability. It also inhibits matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), thereby preventing collagen degradation within the hybrid layer and increasing the durability of adhesive restorations. Overall, hesperidin improves the antimicrobial efficacy, biocompatibility, and longevity of dentin adhesives without adversely affecting their adhesive performance [28].

In the study published by Saha and coworkers, the polymethoxylated flavones sinensetin and tangeretin exhibited the strongest antibacterial activity against Streptococcus mutans among the identified citrus flavonoids in the sweet orange peel extracts. Both compounds demonstrated minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) of 0.25 mg/mL and minimum bactericidal concentrations (MBCs) of 0.50 mg/mL against S. mutans, indicating potent bacteriostatic and bactericidal effects. The enhanced efficacy of sinensetin and tangeretin is likely attributable to their polymethoxylated flavone structure, which facilitates disruption of bacterial membrane integrity and interference with biofilm formation [29].

Kaempferol

Nidus Vespae, the honeycomb of the yellow oriental paper wasp (Polistes olivaceous), Parapolybia varia, and eusocial paper wasp (Polistes japonicas), harvested in the autumn and winter seasons, is used as a traditional medicine in China to treat toothaches [30]. Different solvent extracts of these honeycombs have displayed remarkable activity against cariogenic microorganisms, with GTF-inhibitory and antibiofilm activities [31, 32]. This led to the isolation of quercetin and kaempferol as the predominant flavonoids in the extracts. Kaempferol was found more effective than quercetin against cariogenic S.mutans with a MIC value of 1 μg/mL and MBC value of 8 μg/ml. At MIC, kaempferol exhibited bacteriostatic effects, but at MBC, it demonstrated bactericidal effects with 100% killing capacity. At sub-MIC concentration, kaempferol significantly attenuated the acid production in a concentration-dependent manner by 6.74%, 25.76%, and 45.57% at 1/8 MIC, ¼ MIC, and ½ MIC, respectively. It also suppressed the acidity of S. mutans at concentrations as low as 0.125 mg/mL. Further, at the sub-MIC level (1/2MIC), kaempferol suppressed the F-ATPase by 49.66%, using which S. mutans resists environmental acid stress [33]. Kaemferol was more potent than quercetin for S. mutans biofilm formation and reduction, with minimum biofilm inhibition concentration (MBIC50) and minimum biofilm reduction concentration (MBRC50) of 8 mg/mL and 16 mg/mL, respectively [34]. Kaemferol inhibited biofilm formation and glucan synthesis more effectively than 0.12% chlorhexidine.

Kurarinone

Sophora flavescens is a perennial shrub native to Northeast Asia. Kurarinone, a flavonoid isolated from the ethereal extract of S. flavescens, displayed bacteriostatic activity against six serotypes of S. mutans with MIC values between 6.25–12.5 μg/mL, whereas the standard drug tetracycline HCl showed MIC values between 0.19–1.56 μg/mL against these strains [35]. Another study reported the potent bioactivity of the extract of S. flavescens against S. mutans and resistant strains like methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) and vancomycin-resistant enterococci (VRE) [36]. The activity was attributed to kurarinone, which displayed a MIC value of 2 μg/mL against all these tested strains. The standard drug ampicillin had an MIC of 0.15 μg/mL against S. mutans and 250 μg/mL against the resistant strains.

Kuwanon G

The strong antibacterial activity of the methanolic extract of Morus alba root bark led to the isolation of kuwanon G [37]. The MIC of kuwanon G against cariogenic S. mutans was 8 μg/mL. At 20 μg/mL, it completely inactivated S. mutans and other cariogenic bacteria in 1 min. Kuwanon G at 8 μg/mL also caused morphological damage to the bacterial cell wall, with a disintegrated margin, as evidenced by transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Kuwanon G was fourfold more active than sanguinarine (MIC value of 32 μg/mL), a natural agent used as an industrial anticaries agent in mouthwashes [38]. It was also 32 and 125 times more active than the tea polyphenols like gallocatechin and epigallocatechin, with MIC values of 250 μg/mL and 1000 μg/mL, respectively [39].

Luteolin

Luteolin inhibits S. mutan biofilm formation in a dose-dependent manner at a concentration above 12.5 µg/mL. It prevented initial S. mutans attachments within the first 4 h of biofilm formation at a concentration of > 12.5 µg/mL (>75% of biofilm inhibition), while a concentration of > 50 µg/mL was required for mature 24 h biofilm. Further investigation revealed that at concentrations > 50 µg/mL, lutrolin significantly reduced S. mutans dispersal from the 4-h biofilm into the fresh medium. Above 100 µg/mL, the dispersal from the 24 h biofilm was reduced [40]. On the saliva-coated hydroxyapatite, which mimics the biofilm formation, luteolin inhibited S. mutans adhesion to the saliva-coated hydroxyapatite surfaces at 12.5 µg/mL and 50.0 µg/mL in 4 and 24 h, respectively. Luteolin also decreased the extracellular polysaccharides (EPS) biomass compared to the control. Biofilms treated with luteolin (25 μg/mL) for 24 h significantly suppressed the expression of gtf genes in S. mutans, which encode surface adhesion and EPS formation, compared to untreated biofilms. Luteolin at 12.5, 25.0, and 50.0 µg/mL inhibited the production of water-insoluble glucan in the biofilm by approximately > 29%, >45%, and > 54%, probably directly interfering with Gtfs. Further, the planktonic culture of S. mutans, when treated with 12.5 μg/mL of luteolin, markedly decreased the acidogenicity of S. mutans, contributing to reduced biofilm formation.

Malvin

Malvidin-3,5-diglucoside (malvin), isolated from the flowers of Alcea longipedicellata at a concentration of 0.1% killed 40% of growing and resting S. mutans within 1 h of incubation with an MIC of 0.16–0.22 mg/mL [41]. It also showed an immediate effect on the acid production up to 2 h post-rinsing in the salivary glycolytic assay. This may be due to the bactericidal effect of malvin on the salivary bacteria. The glass adherence assay of S. mutans is considered a model for studying the sucrose-dependent adherence ability of S. mutans [42]. In the sucrose-induced adherence study, malvin at 0.1% was 60% effective in preventing S. mutans adherence. Flavonoids were reported to have anti-enzymatic activity on glucosyltransferase, which is responsible for the dietary conversion of glucose to sticky glucans and promotes S. mutans adherence on the tooth surface. The enzyme-inhibitory activity of flavonoids may be responsible for preventing S. mutans adherence to the tooth surface. Thus, Malvin could successfully prevent plaque formation on the tooth surface, as claimed by the authors.

Morin

S. mutans sortase (srtA)-mediated release of surface adhesion protein Pac. is an adhesin that mediates the adhesion of cariogenic S. mutans to the tooth surface [43]. So, inhibition of S. mutans SrtA via an anti-adhesion mechanism could be an effective strategy for caries prevention. Morin inhibited S. mutans SrtA in vitro with an IC50 of 27.2 ± 2.6 μM [44]. Morin at 30 μM decreased the release of Pac protein, thereby reducing the colonization of cariogenic S. mutans. This significantly reduced the biofilm mass. At this concentration, morin did not show any toxicity on viable S. mutans, suggesting that the anti-biofilm activity of morin is related to the lack of Pac protein. Recently, de Farias and coworkers demonstrated the antimicrobial and anti-biofilm activity of morin released from gellan gum-based films and mucoadhesive tablets [45]. The acidogenicity of biofilm, the formation, and the biomass of EPS were also effectively controlled. In contrast to biofilms produced in the presence of morin-containing film, biofilms formed in contact with the tablet containing morin were less acidogenic. The decreased viability observed may be due to the percentage variation of morin release from the formulations over time, which is relatively higher in morin-containing tablets (90%) compared to morin-containing film (40%) after 20 h.

Myricetin

Gregoire and co-workers demonstrated moderate but significant anti-virulence activity of myricetin at 500 µmol/L without exhibiting bactericidal effects. Myricetin inhibited surface-adsorbed glucosyltransferases (GTF B and GTF C) by 30%, but showed enhanced activity of 45% inhibition in combination with procyanidin A2. In addition to inhibiting glucan synthesis, myricetin significantly reduced the activity of membrane-associated F-ATPase, an enzyme crucial for maintaining intracellular pH homeostasis in S. mutans, by 32.6% alone and 43% in combination with procyanidin A2. [46]. Myricetin also has reduced biofilm formation through a non-sucrose-dependent pathway via an anti-adhesion mechanism by inhibiting the S. aureus SrtA [47]. In another study, Hu and co-workers reported myricetin as a potent S. mutans Srt A enzyme inhibitor in a dose-dependent manner with an IC50 value of 48.66 ± 1.48 μM [48]. However, the MIC of myricetin against the cariogenic S. mutans was 512 μg/mL, which was far higher than the IC50 value. This suggested that the non-sucrose dependent anti-biofilm activity of myricetin is via the S. mutans SrtA-induced adhesion process without eliciting antibacterial activity. Additionally, myricetin at sub-MIC level (50 μM, 100 μM, 150 μM) reduced the initial adhesion and mature biofilm (16 h) formation of S. mutans. This may be due to decreased SrtA-induced surface Pac proteins, which are responsible for S. mutans surface adhesion.

Naringin

Naringenin inhibited S. mutans growth, with an MIC of 100–200 μg/mL. At 100 μg/mL, naringenin inhibited biofilm formation by approximately 70%, and at 200 μg/mL, it completely inhibited biofilm formation in 4 h and 24 h biofilms. Naringenin reduces biofilm thickness in a dose-dependent manner and reduces bacterial aggregation by increasing S. mutans surface hydrophobicity at these concentrations. Among the three types of GTFs synthesized by S. mutans, GTFB and GTFC play a vital role in S. mutans sucrose-dependent surface attachments. Naringenin downregulated the mRNA expression of the corresponding genes gtfB and gtfC at 100 and 200 μg/mL and downregulated the internal signal transduction in S. mutans, thereby preventing biofilm formation [49]. The biocompatibility and toxicity of naringenin to human dental pulp cells (HDPCs) evaluated by MTT test demonstrated that at 100 μg/mL, naringenin did not have a significant effect on the growth of HDPCs, but at 200 μg/mL, it slightly decreased HDPCs growth. This finding validates the acceptability of naringenin for dental clinical applications.

Narirutin and nobiletin

Nobiletin is present in the lowest concentration among the flavonoids in sweet orange peel extracts [29]. The citrus flavonoids vary significantly in their MICs and MBCs, with nobiletin, tangeretin, and sinensetin exhibiting greater activity than hesperidin and narirutin against S. mutans. Further, tangeretin and sinensetin were found to be the most potent, followed by nobiletin and quercetin. Although narirutin is a predominant component along with hesperidine, it was the least susceptible to S. mutans, with no bactericidal activity even at 2 mg/mL.

Pinocembrin

Propolis is an essential source of flavonoids like chrysin, tectochrysin, pinocembrin, kaempferol, apigenin, pinostrobin galangin, and quercetin. Among these flavonoids, pinocembrin is the most abundant. The research conducted by Veloz et al. showed that pinocembrin and apigenin were the most effective among the flavonoids inhibiting S. mutans growth [9]. Pinocemrin with a MIC value of 1.4 ± 0.4 µg/mL was found to be more active than other polyphenolic mixtures and chlorhexidine with a MIC of 1.6 ± 0.2 µg/mL. Pinocembrin at a concentration of 25 μg/mL potentially reduced the biofilm formation when applied to S. mutans culture compared to chlorhexidine, traditionally used in the treatment of dental caries. Like apigenin, pinocembrin also affected S. mutans biofilm architecture by reducing biofilm thickness and inhibiting enzymatic gtfC activity.

Procyanidin A2

Cranberry fruit is a rich source of bioactive proanthocyanidins (PAC), including other flavonoids. The cranberry PAC is a mixture of monomeric epicatechin and oligomeric procyanidin A2 (containing 2-epicatechin units) and high-molecular-weight oligomers (with 2–10 and even more monomeric epicatechin units) with at least one A-type double interflavan linkage. The A-type PACs are unique to Cranberry, found in high proportion, and biologically active. The study by Koo et al. showed that the 1-min topical exposure of PAC reduced the S. mutans biofilm mass (35–40%), acidogenicity of S. mutans without bactericidal activity at a dose of 1.5 mg/mL compared with the vehicle control (10%) [50]. The biological activity may be attributed to the effective inhibition of GTFs (40–70% inhibition) compared to the control. The effect of PAC fraction on acid production and acid tolerance by S. mutans has been attributed to the presence of the dimeric procyanidin A2. Furthermore, at 0.1 mg/mL, procyanidin A2 inhibited GtfB activity in solution by 15.8 ± 3.5% and reduced the activity of surface-adsorbed GtfB by 27.6 ± 6.3% compared with the vehicle control. Importantly, procyanidin A2 showed the strongest effect on bacterial acidogenecity among the low molecular-weight PACs. In glycolytic pH-drop assays, it produced a ΔpH ≥ 0.21 relative to control at 30–60 min after glucose challenge, indicating marked suppression of acid production [46]. This suggests interference with glycolytic pathways and partial inhibition of membrane-associated F-ATPase activity without antibacterial activity. Thus, quantitatively, procyanidin A2 exerts moderate inhibition of glucan synthesis (≈16–28%) and strong suppression of acid production (ΔpH ≥ 0.21), thereby reducing biofilm virulence rather than directly killing bacteria.

Rutin and quercetin

Rutin is quercetin-3-rutinoside which is metabolized in vivo to the aglycone part, quercetin [51, 52]. This natural flavonoid is widely distributed in buckwheat, grapes, plums, cherries, grapefruit, apricots, oranges, and eucalyptus leaves. The activity of rutin against cariogenic S. mutans has not been reported in the literature; however, its activity against other bacterial and fungal strains residing in dental plaque, like Staphylococcus aureus, Agreggatibacter actinomycetemcomitans, Escherichia coli, Actinomyces naeslundii, Actinomyces viscosus, Lactobacillus casei, Enterococcus faecalis, Streptococcus sanguinis, and Streptococcus oralis, as well as Candida albicans, has been reported. Rutin was found to be most effective against A. viscosus and A. naeslundii at 0.61 mg/mL and showed moderate to good activity against other strains [53].

Quercetin demonstrated moderate activity against S. mutans, with a MIC of 4.1 ± 0.8 µg/mL, compared with chlorhexidine, which had a lower MIC of 1.6 ± 0.2 µg/mL. It was found to be less potent than apigenin and pinocembrin, with MICs of 1.3 ± 0.4 and 1.4 ± 0.4 µg/mL, respectively. Although quercetin reduced biofilm formation and interfered with bacterial virulence factors, such as glucosyltransferase activity, its antibacterial and antibiofilm effects were weaker than those of chlorhexidine [9]. The study by Zeng and co-workers reported MIC and MBIC₅₀ (minimum biofilm inhibition concentration) of 16 mg/mL, and an MBRC₅₀ (minimum biofilm reduction concentration) of 32 mg/mL for quercetin against S. mutans. In terms of biofilm biomass, quercetin demonstrated stronger inhibition (4.55 ± 0.05 mg) compared to chlorohexidine (6.57 ± 0.87 mg). Quercetin also displayed acidogenicity comparable to chlorhexidine, increasing the in situ pH to 5.4 ± 0.22 with quercetin and 5.3 ± 0.32 with chlorhexidine [34]. In another study, Quercetin showed an antibacterial effect against cariogenic bacteria S. mutans, with MIC and MBC of 2 mg/mL and 8 mg/mL, respectively. At MIC, it exhibited bacteriostatic effects; at MBC, it exhibited bactericidal effects [33]. At sub-MIC concentration, quercetin significantly attenuated the acid production in a concentration-dependent manner by 11.43%, 36.62%, and 50.11% at 1/8 MIC, ¼ MIC, and ½ MIC, respectively. It also suppressed the acidurity of S. mutans at concentrations as low as 0.125 mg/mL. Further, at the sub-MIC level (1/2MIC), it suppressed the F1F0-ATPase activity by 47.37%.

Theaflavins

Tea has been a popularly consumed beverage for thousands of years. The literature on tea confirmed that tea polyphenols, such as ECG, EC, EGCG, and EGC, can reduce dental caries by inhibiting growth, acid production and acid tolerance, adhesion, and accumulation of cariogenic bacteria [54, 55]. Theaflavins are oligomeric polyphenolic compounds formed from monomeric tea polyphenol units by oxidative polymerization during heating and fermentation. Theaflavins are important bioactive components present in black tea, with reported bacteriostatic effects on S. mutans and inhibitory activity against glucosyltransferases (50% inhibition at 10 μM), which is higher than that of tea polyphenols (≤ 28% inhibition at concentrations > 500 μM). The antibacterial study of the four main theaflavins, theaflavin, theaflavinmonogallate A, theaflavinmonogallate B, and theaflavindigallate against cariogenic S. mutans indicated strongest activity for theaflavindigallate (TFD) with MIC and MBC value of 62.5 µg/mL and 125 µg/mL [56]. Theaflavin showed the lowest inhibitory effect with a MIC value of 500 µg/mL. A bactericidal effect was observed by increasing the concentration of TFD by twofold of MIC. The killing curve of theaflavindigallate against S. mutans suggested that the killing effect largely depends on high concentration and sufficient time. The ability of theaflavindigallate to reduce S. mutans biofilm biomass was investigated by growing biofilms with theaflavindigallate at a sub-MIC level. At ¼ MIC (15.6 µg/mL), theaflavindigallate reduced biofilm biomass by less than 50%, and at ½ MIC (31.25 µg/mL), the effect was less than 25% compared to the control. Further, TFD reduced acid production and acid tolerance in the S. mutans biofilm at this sub-MIC level by downregulating genes encoding enzymes involved in glycolysis. Moreover, TF3 at sub-bactericidal concentrations was found to diminish acid production and acid tolerance in S. mutans biofilms by downregulating enzymes involved in glycolysis. At this sub-MIC level, TFD significantly lowered the levels of dextran and eDNA which are the essential components of EPS matrixes. The RT-PCR results have revealed that TFD at 1 x MIC has effectively downregulated the expression of GTF genes (gtfB, gtfC, and gtfD) responsible for dextran formation from dietary sources. TFDs have also downregulated eDNA production by suppressing S. mutans cell autolysis and affecting the eDNA secretion system [57, 58]. The present study revealed the scientific basis for using TFD effectively as an anti-caries agent in the future.

Challenges and future prospects

Despite the use of fluoride formulations, dental caries remains a prevalent infectious disease worldwide. Over 600 different types of bacteria inhabit the oral cavity, among which S. mutans are considered the prosperous colonizer contributing to cariogenic biofilms. This is probably due to their ability to synthesize EPS-rich biofilm, adherence to glucan-coated dental surfaces, acid-producing, and acid-tolerant ability. Although flavonoids remain a natural source of innovation for an effective anticaries agent, their effectiveness remains largely unknown for several reasons. (i) They have broad-spectrum activity not specifically effective against any specific bacterium (ii) Oral microorganisms would never be subjected to a high concentration of an exogenously induced flavonoid over a prolonged period. (iii) The classical assays use suspensions of natural compounds; however, shreds of evidence suggest that S. mutans in the biofilm matrix are more resistant to the exposed compounds in suspension. Further, the antimicrobial and clinical antibiofilm activity may not correlate effectively for these flavonoids [59, 60]. The retention time of the flavonoids in the oral cavity, their stability in saliva, metabolism, and topical concentration need to be addressed, along with their toxicity, especially during repeated short-term exposure to oral tissues.

Preventing dental caries by targeting Gtfs is an attractive possibility. Gtfs present on the dental pellicle and the surfaces of bacteria play an important role in caries development. However, most research in this field on flavonoids has focused on activity in solution rather than at the plaque pellicle interface. It is important to note that surface-adsorbed Gtfs exhibit increased resistance to natural compounds, including commonly available antiplaque mouthwashes in the solution phase [61]. The present review indicates that fewer flavonoids displayed activity against GtfB and GtfC in solution, but few disrupted surface-adsorbed Gtfs and EPS in dental biofilm. Even fewer have been evaluated using rodent models and at the clinical level.

Despite ongoing research to identify selective natural anti-cariogenic scaffolds, we have explored the anti-biofilm potential of a small series of flavonoids against cariogenic S. mutans. Different flavonoids decrease bacterial virulence by different mechanisms that can be consolidated into a unified anti-virulence framework targeting four key stages of cariogenic pathogenesis: inhibition of bacterial adhesion (via SrtA and glucan-binding proteins), suppression of glucosyltransferase-mediated EPS synthesis, reduction of acid production and F₁F₀-ATPase-mediated acid tolerance, and modulation of virulence gene expression. Among the reviewed flavonoids, Apigenin, Pinocembrin, and Kaempferol emerge as the most promising candidates for future anticariogenic research due to their very low MIC values comparable to chlorhexidine, strong inhibition of glucosyltransferases (GTFs), and significant suppression of biofilm formation and acid production. Theaflavin digallate and Epigallocatechin gallate also demonstrate potent downregulation of gtf genes and reduction of EPS synthesis, supporting their role as multi-target biofilm inhibitors. In contrast, Myricetin and Procyanidin A2 are particularly effective as anti-virulence agents by inhibiting sortase A, F-ATPase activity, and acidogenicity without exerting strong bactericidal properties, thereby minimizing the risk of resistance. Fluoride is an effective anticaries agent that probably interferes with caries formation physiochemically by accelerating remineralization and reducing demineralization of tooth enamel [59]. It also has a bactericidal effect. Thus, flavonoids, which exhibit anticaries effects via a different mechanism, can effectively combine with fluoride to combat biofilm formation and dental caries. Although most natural products are devoid of acute toxicity, the toxicity profile following brief exposure to topically applied flavonoids in the oral cavity must be assessed before clinical studies. Further, innovative novel drug delivery systems could significantly enhance the applicability and utility of these flavonoids. These natural flavonoids, which show promising anticaries properties, could serve as a template in the semisynthetic design of newer cariostatic agents using in silico approaches. Hopefully, this virulence-selective study will lay the foundation for preclinical and clinical studies to further investigate the potential of these flavonoids as possible anti-cariogenic drugs.
