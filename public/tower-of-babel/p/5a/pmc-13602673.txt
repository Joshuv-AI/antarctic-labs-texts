A novel Au-Se nanoplatform for co-delivery of cGAMP and acriflavine synergizes with photothermal therapy to enhance anti-tumor immunity via STING activation and HIF-1α inhibition
Authors: Yuanyuan Cui, Yonghui Liang, Huixin Yang, Xudong Wang, Yanjiao Zhang, Guilong Zhang, Renhui Zhan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractActivating the stimulator of interferon genes (STING) pathway is a potent strategy for promoting tumor vascular normalization and enhancing cancer immunotherapy. However, the poor metabolic stability of classical STING agonists limits their clinical translation. Herein, we engineered a self-assembled Au-Se nanoplatform (AAC) for the co-delivery of the STING agonist cyclic 2’,3’-GMP-AMP (cGAMP) and the hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α) inhibitor acriflavine (Acf). Upon cellular internalization, the weakly acidic tumor microenvironment (TME) triggers the synchronized release of cGAMP and Acf agents. Next, cGAMP activates the STING pathway to induce type I interferon (IFN-I) production, while Acf disrupts HIF-1α/β dimerization. The activation of STING signal pathway and the inhibition of HIF-1α/β expression synergistically suppress the expression of VEGFA and remodels the TME into an immune-permissive milieu, which promotes vascular normalization and elicits tumor immune response. Combined with the photothermal therapy (PTT) of Au nanorods, AAC induces robust immunogenic cell death (ICD), facilitates dendritic cell (DC) maturation, and enhances cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) infiltration. Introduction

In recent years, cancer immunotherapies, including immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), cancer vaccines, and adoptive cell transfer (ACT), have revolutionized the landscape of cancer treatment, yielding remarkable therapeutic outcomes particularly in the management of advanced malignancies [1, 2]. Nevertheless, the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) induced by abnormal tumor vasculature restricts the clinical efficacy of immunotherapies and further contributes to immunotherapy resistance [3, 4]. Dysfunctional vascular structures impair the delivery of oxygen and nutrients while hindering metabolic waste clearance, leading to a hypoxic and acidic TME that favors tumor progression [5–7]. Additionally, high vascular permeability facilitates metastasis, while impaired perfusion inhibits immune cell recruitment and activation, fostering immune escape [8–10]. Consequently, remodeling dysfunctional vessels through anti-angiogenic strategies (known as vascular normalization) to reshape the TME into an immune-permissive environment holds significant potential for enhancing immunotherapy.

Anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (anti-VEGF) or anti-VEGF receptor (VEGFR) agents are currently the most widely used therapeutics for promoting vascular normalization, having demonstrated favorable efficacy in various cancer treatments [11–13]. However, the extent to which these vascular remodeling strategies enhance tumor cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) infiltration and activation remains unclear. Moreover, for many cancer types, these responses are incomplete and may be of limited duration, thus failing to achieve meaningful clinical benefits. This necessitates the development of novel strategies for reprogramming the tumor vasculature to enhance the infiltration, proliferation, and function of CTLs.

The innate immune stimulator of interferon genes (STING), a cytosolic pattern recognition receptor, plays a pivotal role in the spontaneous induction of anti-tumor immunity [14, 15]. Cyclic dinucleotides (CDNs), typified by cyclic guanosine diphosphate-adenosine (cyclic GMP-AMP, cGAMP), represent the endogenous high-affinity cognate ligands for STING [16]. Activation of the STING pathway induces type I interferons and pro-inflammatory cytokines, thereby substantially facilitating DC-mediated cross-priming of T cells to develop robust anti-tumor immunity [17, 18]. Further, extensive studies in multiple mouse tumor models have confirmed that intratumoral injection of CDN-based STING agonists can promote vascular normalization, as evidenced by improved vascular integrity, reduced tumor hypoxia, and upregulated the expression of T-cell adhesion molecules on endothelial cells, ultimately transforming “cold” tumors into “hot” ones [19, 20]. However, owing to their poor metabolic stability, CDNs are rapidly cleared from the circulation upon intravenous injection, leading to minimal tumor accumulation, insufficient cellular uptake, and negligible therapeutic effects. Thus, optimizing the delivery of STING agonists is indispensable to systemic application. To address this critical challenge, numerous research groups have developed NP-based delivery platforms to refine the pharmacological profiles of CDNs, thereby enabling their systemic administration and attaining substantial therapeutic effects. Collectively, these endeavors underscore the critical necessity of rational STING agonist carrier selection and strategic therapeutic formulation for effective STING signaling in solid tumors.

HIF-1 plays a pivotal role in tumor angiogenesis and immune escape [21–23]. Under hypoxic conditions, HIF-1α maintains stability and heterodimerizes with HIF-1β to induce the transcription of multiple target genes [24, 25]. This heterodimer upregulates the expression of pro-angiogenic factors (e.g., VEGF), leading to aberrant tumor vasculature and the formation of a tumor-permissive microenvironment [26]. Acriflavine (Acf), a clinically established topical antiseptic, emerges as a potent inhibitor of HIF-1α/β heterodimerization [27, 28]. Furthermore, recent research interest in Acf as a promising anti-tumor agent with the potential to improve cancer patient prognosis has grown exponentially. However, as a hydrophilic cationic molecule, Acf exhibits a short plasma half-life, with 90% of the administered dose eliminated within 5 min following intravenous injection, which necessitates high-dose administration to achieve therapeutically effective concentrations at the tumor site. Accordingly, developing a nanosystem incorporating Acf to optimize its pharmacokinetic profile represents a rational strategy for enhancing therapeutic index.

Building on these insights, we engineered a novel self-assembled Au-Se nanoplatform for the co-delivery of cGAMP and Acf, enabling synergistic targeting of STING activation and HIF-1 inhibition. In this system, polyethylene glycol (PEG) and cationic polyethyleneimine (PEI) were covalently conjugated onto the surface of gold nanorods (AuNRs) via stable Au-Se bonds, followed by electrostatic adsorption of the two therapeutic agents to form AuNRs@PEI@PEG@Acf@cGAMP (AAC) nanodrug (Scheme 1a). Upon cellular internalization, the weakly acidic tumor microenvironment (TME) breaks electrostatic adsorption and then triggers the release of Ac f and cGAMP from AAC nanodrug. cGAMP activates the STING pathway to promote the release of type I interferons (IFN-I), while Acf inhibits HIF-1α/β dimerization and downregulates the expression of angiogenesis-related cytokines (e.g., VEGF). These combined effects facilitate tumor vascular normalization and tumor immune microenvironment remodeling. When combined with AuNR-mediated photothermal therapy (PTT), AAC induces immunogenic cell death (ICD), enhances dendritic cell (DC) maturation, and boosts the infiltration and activation of CTLs, leading to robust suppression of both primary and distant tumors in murine breast cancer models (Scheme 1b). Taken together, these findings validate the potential of AAC as a versatile nanotherapeutic platform, offering a synergistic strategy for clinical anti-tumor immunotherapy.

Results and discussion

Preparation and characterization of AAC nanodrug

Gold nanorods (AuNRs) employed in this study were synthesized via the classical seed-mediated growth method. Gold nanoseeds were fabricated by reducing HAuCl4·3H2O, followed by regulating the anisotropic growth of nanorods in a growth solution containing cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB), AgNO3, and ascorbic acid (AA). This approach ultimately yielded AuNRs with uniform size and excellent dispersibility. As illustrated in the transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images (Fig. 1a and S1), the width and length of the AuNRs were approximately 10 nm and 35 nm, respectively, corresponding to an aspect ratio of ~ 3.5. Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy confirmed that CTAB on the AuNR surface was completely eliminated through multiple water washings to mitigate the cytotoxicity of the AuNR (Fig. S2). To enhance biocompatibility and drug-loading capacity, PEG20k-Se-Se-PEI1800 polymer was introduced into the AuNR suspension to form AuNRs@PEI@PEG nanoparticles. The stable Au-Se bonds enable resistance to glutathione interference under in vivo physiological conditions, thereby minimizing drug leakage during systemic circulation [29]. Subsequently, two therapeutic agents (cGAMP and Acf) were loaded onto the AuNRs@PEI@PEG nanoparticles via electrostatic adsorption with PEI following intermolecular hydrogen bonds formation, yielding AuNRs@PEI@PEG@Acf@cGAMP (AAC) nanodrug. After uranyl acetate negative staining, TEM images of AAC revealed distinct filamentous network structures on the AAC surface, verifying the successful modification of PEG20k-Se-Se-PEI1800 polymer (Fig. 1b and c). Elemental mapping images revealed that Se elements are mainly distributed on the surface of the nanorods, which further validated the formation of Au-Se linkages in AAC (Fig. 1d). UV-visible absorption spectroscopy showed a slight blue shift in the maximum absorption peak of AAC compared with that of pristine AuNRs (Fig. 1e). Meanwhile, dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis demonstrated a significant increase in the size of AAC following PEG20k-Se-Se-PEI1800 modification (Fig. S3). The zeta potential of AAC was lower than that of AuNRs but remained positive, facilitating electrostatic adsorption onto negatively charged cell membranes and enhancing phagocytic internalization (Fig. 1f). Furthermore, FTIR spectroscopy of AAC exhibited characteristic peaks corresponding to both loaded drugs, confirming successful drug loading (Fig. 1g). Meanwhile, full X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) spectra further confirmed the presence of Au, Se, C, O, and N elements in AAC (Fig. 1h). Moreover, the Au4f and Se3d XPS spectra clearly demonstrated the existence of Au atoms and the formation of Au-Se bonds in AAC (Fig. S4). Based on the aforementioned characterizations, it was concluded that AAC was successfully fabricated and suitable for subsequent functional evaluations.

The photothermal performance of AAC was first evaluated under 808 nm near-infrared (NIR) laser irradiation. As shown in Fig. 1i, the temperature of AAC solutions exhibits a distinct concentration-dependent increase. Additionally, the photothermal effect of the AAC solution was confirmed to be enhanced with increasing laser power (Fig. 1j). Thermal imaging revealed that at a laser power of 1 W/cm² and a concentration of 50 µg/mL, the temperature of the AAC solution reached 49 °C within 5 min (Fig. 1k). Statistical calculations revealed that the photothermal conversion efficiency was approximately 52% (Fig. S5). Furthermore, the temperature-change curve remained essentially unchanged over 5 heating-cooling cycles (Fig. S6). These photothermal test results demonstrate that AAC possesses excellent photothermal conversion capability, supporting its potential application as a photothermal agent for antitumor photothermal therapy. Subsequently, the drug loading content and encapsulation efficiency of both cGAMP and Acf in AAC were determined. The loading content and encapsulation efficiency of cGAMP were 0.6% and 59.3%, respectively, while those of Acf were 2.3% and 31.1%, indicating efficient co-encapsulation of both drugs within the nanoplatform. The pH-responsive drug release behavior of AAC was then evaluated. A drug release study conducted at 37 °C under different pH conditions showed that AAC was relatively stable in neutral solution (pH 7.4), with less than 10% drug release within 12 h. The drug release rate gradually increased as the pH decreased, and a burst release of both Acf and cGAMP from AAC was significantly promoted in pH 4.5 buffer solution (Fig. 1l and S7). This pH-dependent release behavior indicates that the therapeutic cargo in AAC can be specifically released at the acidic tumor site.

Cellular uptake and in vitro cytotoxicity of AAC

As efficient cellular uptake is crucial for the therapeutic efficacy of nanodrugs, we subsequently evaluated the cellular internalization of the AAC nanodrugs. Firstly, AAC nanodrug was labeled with fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) to achieve green fluorescence. Confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM) imaging results showed that within 4 h, the green fluorescence of FITC within 4T1 cells (a typical triple-negative breast cancer, TNBC cell line) gradually increased with prolonged incubation time, exhibiting a distinct time-dependent internalization manner (Fig. S8a). In addition, the intracellular fluorescence intensity of FITC was significantly positively correlated with the concentration of AAC (Fig. S9a). Similar internalization trends were observed via flow cytometry analysis, collectively confirming the efficient cellular uptake of AAC (Fig. S8b and S9b). Subsequently, the cytotoxicity of AAC was evaluated using the CCK-8 assay. After treatment with different concentrations of AAC for 48 h, the viabilities of L929 cells (a mouse fibroblast cell line) and THLE-3 cells (a normal human liver epithelial cell line) remained above 85% (Fig. S10). This indicates that AAC exhibited extremely low cytotoxicity toward normal cells and possessed excellent cellular biocompatibility. In contrast, AAC exerted significant cytotoxicity in 4T1 cells, which was attributed to that efficient release of therapeutic agents from AAC triggered anti-tumor effects. Notably, after 808 nm NIR laser irradiation, the AAC+Laser (AAC + L) group exerted the strongest cytotoxicity against 4T1 cells, demonstrating superior therapeutic efficacy of AAC-mediated combination therapy over monotherapy (Fig. 2a). The excellent antitumor efficacy of AAC-mediated combination therapy was then assessed by the live/dead staining. Similar to the CCK-8 results, AM-PI live/dead staining assay confirmed that the AAC + L combination group exhibited a higher cell death rate than other groups (Fig. 2b). A flow cytometry-based apoptosis assay further verified that the AAC + L group had the highest apoptotic rate, with nearly all cells in this group being in the apoptotic state (Fig. 2c and d).

In vitro antitumor mechanism of AAC

Subsequently, we further verified the antitumor mechanism of AAC. First, Western blotting results showed that AAC could significantly up-regulate the intracellular expression of TBK1 and IRF3, and enhance the phosphorylation levels of STING and TBK1 in 4T1 cells compared with other groups, confirming its excellent performance in activating the STING signaling pathway. Meanwhile, AAC could also significantly reduce the intracellular accumulation of HIF-1α, indicating its notable inhibitory effect on the HIF-1α signaling pathway (Fig. S11). Additionally, a tube formation assay using human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) demonstrated that AAC exerted a potent inhibitory effect on tube formation, further confirming that the regulation of STING and HIF-1 signaling pathways by AAC could suppress the disordered proliferation of tumor blood vessels (Fig. S12). Subsequently, mitochondrial damage induced by AAC-combined PTT was examined using a JC-1-based mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) assay kit. CLSM images showed that in both normoxic and hypoxic conditions, the AAC + L group exhibited the weakest red fluorescence (indicating aggregated JC-1) and the strongest green fluorescence (indicating monomeric JC-1) (Fig. 2e). This observation confirmed a significant decrease in MMP and the occurrence of mitochondrial dysfunction in this group. Flow cytometry analysis of MMP in 4T1 cells from each group similarly validated the above conclusion (Fig. 2f). Furthermore, mitochondrial morphology was assessed using PK Mito Orange, a mitochondria-specific dye. Structured illumination microscopy (SIM) images showed that compared with other groups, the mitochondrial morphology of 4T1 cells in the AAC + L group was significantly aberrant, characterized by swelling, condensation, and irregular cristae structure, further confirming the functional damage to tumor cell mitochondria induced by AAC-mediated combination therapy (Fig. S13).

Previous studies have demonstrated that mitochondrial dysfunction can induce immunogenic cell death (ICD) [30, 31], a type of cell death characterized by cell-surface translocation of calreticulin (CRT), extracellular release of high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1), and stimulation of type I interferon (IFN) responses. Therefore, we further verified AAC induced ICD effect in different experimental groups. Firstly, CLSM imaging results showed that 4T1 cells in the AAC + L group exhibited the strongest CRT fluorescence signal and the weakest HMGB1 fluorescence signal, indicating the most potent ICD-inducing effect on tumor cells (Fig. 2g). Subsequently, we detected the secretion of type I interferons in 4T1 cells using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). Experimental data showed that AAC treatment could markedly increase the secretion of type I interferons including IL-6, TNF-α, and IFN-β in 4T1 cells, with the highest secretion level observed in the AAC + L group (Fig. 2h, i and j). In summary, the above experimental results demonstrate that AAC-mediated combination therapy can promote tumor cell ICD and release type I interferons, exhibiting excellent antitumor efficacy in vitro.

In vivo biosafety and photothermal effect of AAC

Considering the potential of AAC in clinical use, the biocompatibility and metabolic distribution of AAC were evaluated. The hemolysis rate was determined to be consistently below 5% when the concentration of AAC ranged from 0 to 200 µg/mL, indicating that AAC possesses favorable hemocompatibility and high potential for safe intravenous administration (Fig. S14). In addition, no significant abnormalities were observed in the blood routine parameters of mice 48 h after intravenous injection of AAC, which confirmed that AAC didn’t induce significant systemic hematological toxicity, thereby underscoring its high biocompatibility for in vivo applications (Fig. S15). To validate the tumor-targeting capability of AAC, free Cy5.5 dye was administered at an equivalent dose and timing as a control. The fluorescence distribution of Cy5.5-labeled AAC showed that the fluorescence signals at the tumor site gradually increased, reaching maximum intensity at 8 h post-injection, followed by a slow decline. Notably, a strong fluorescence signal persisted for at least 24 h, verifying that AAC can achieve prolonged tumor accumulation via the enhanced permeation and retention (EPR) effect. In stark contrast, mice injected with free Cy5.5 exhibited negligible fluorescence accumulation in the tumor region, with the signal rapidly dissipating within hours (Fig. 3a and b, and S16). Subsequently, the mice were sacrificed and dissected at post-injection 72 h. IVIS images revealed that AAC accumulated most abundantly at the tumor site, except for the liver and kidneys (Fig. 3c and d). Furthermore, HE staining results of major organs including the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidneys showed that intravenous injection of AAC caused no obvious pathological damage to these organs, which further verified the safety of AAC in mice (Fig. S17). Subsequently, the in vivo photothermal effect of AAC was evaluated at post-injection 8 h. The results showed that at an AAC intravenous injection dose of 2 mg/kg and an 808 nm NIR laser power of 1.5 W/cm², the temperature at the tumor site exceeded 50 °C within 3 min, achieving a potent photothermal therapeutic effect. Collectively, the above experimental results demonstrate that AAC has excellent biosafety and favorable tumor accumulation, enabling it to exert an effective therapeutic effect at the tumor site.

In vivo antitumor efficacy of AAC-combined PTT

Based on the excellent in vitro antitumor activity and desirable biosafety, we further investigated the antitumor efficacy of AAC in mice. Firstly, 4T1 cells were inoculated into the left hind limb of female BALB/c mice for the establishment of subcutaneous 4T1 tumor models. When the tumor volume reached approximately 100 mm³, the mice were randomly assigned into six groups (n = 6) with the following treatments: PBS, PBS + L, AuNRs, AuNRs + L, AAC, and AAC + L. Each group received intravenous injections at a dose of 2 mg/kg every two days, totaling nine administrations. Mice in the “L” groups were exposed to 1.5 W/cm² laser irradiation for 5 min at 8 h post-injection. Relative tumor volume curves were plotted throughout the 18-day observation period. As presented in Fig. 4a-c, compared with the control group, the AuNRs + L and AAC groups exhibited moderate tumor growth inhibition, respectively, highlighting the inadequate antitumor efficacy of monotherapies. Notably, the tumor growth was most significantly suppressed in the AAC + L group, indicating that AAC-combined PTT possesses superior antitumor potency. Moreover, as shown in Fig. 4d and Fig. S18, hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining of tumor tissues revealed that the AAC + L group had the most severe tumor cell damage, characterized by extensive necrotic areas. Consistently, terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) staining showed the strongest green fluorescence in tumor tissues from the AAC + L group, which was indicative of severe DNA damage and extensive cellular apoptosis. Furthermore, immunofluorescence (IF) staining results demonstrated that, compared with other groups, the AAC + L group had the lowest expression of HMGB1 and the highest expression of CRT in tumor cells, confirming that AAC + L effectively activates tumor ICD. In addition, IF imaging data also showed that the expression levels of VEGF-A and CD31 (a vascular endothelial cell marker) in tumor tissues of the AAC + L group were both the lowest among all groups, suggesting that AAC-combined PTT can efficiently inhibit the disorganized proliferation of tumor blood vessels and promote tumor vascular normalization.

Systemic antitumor immune activation and distant tumor suppression of AAC-combined PTT

The excellent tumor suppressive effect exerted by the AAC + L group precluded the isolation of an adequate number of immune cells from the tiny tumors for functional analysis. Therefore, we further established a mouse bilateral tumor model to evaluate the systemic antitumor immune activation elicited by AAC-combined PTT. As shown in Fig. 5a, the inoculation of 4T1 cells and treatment regimens for primary tumors in mice were consistent with those described previously. Subsequently, a secondary tumor challenge was conducted by inoculating 4T1 cells into the right hind limb on day 18. The growth of both primary and distant tumors was monitored until day 28. Consistent with previous treatment outcomes, the growth of primary tumors in the AAC + L group was most significantly suppressed, as shown in Fig. 5b-d. In addition, statistical analyses of distant tumor growth revealed that compared with the control group, the AuNRs + L, AAC, and AAC + L groups all significantly suppressed distant tumor growth at different degrees, with the AAC + L group exhibiting the most potent tumor suppressive effect (Fig. 5e-g). This indicated that AAC-combined PTT could effectively elicit a systemic antitumor immune response. To further investigate the establishment of immunological memory underlying the abscopal effect, we performed immunofluorescence staining for CD44, a well-established marker of effector memory T cells, in primary tumor tissues. As shown in Fig. 5h, the AAC + L group exhibited the strongest fluorescence signals for CD8, CD4, and p-STING, as well as markedly enhanced CD44 expression. These results indicate robust activation of the STING pathway, potent infiltration of CD8⁺ and CD4⁺ T cells, and the generation of effector memory T cells within the tumor microenvironment.

Subsequently, the proportion of immune cells in the primary tumor was further analyzed via flow cytometry. As shown in Fig. 6a-f, the proportions of CD4⁺, CD8⁺ T cells, and dendritic cells (DCs) in the AAC + L group were significantly increased compared with the PBS control group. Notably, these proportions were also higher than those in the AuNRs + L and AAC groups, further confirming that AAC-combined PTT could promote the infiltration and activation of antitumor immune cells at the tumor site. In addition, similar results of CTLs proportion changes were also observed in the spleen (Fig. S19-20). Moreover, the detection results of type I interferons in mouse serum showed that compared with other groups, the expression levels of IFN-β, IL-6, and TNF-α in the serum of mice in the AAC + L group were significantly elevated, indicating its robust activation of the STING signaling pathway (Fig. 6g-i). Collectively, these results demonstrated that AAC-combined PTT exerted a potent systemic antitumor immune enhancement effect, with significant inhibitory effects on both primary and distant tumors.

Conclusion

In summary, this work developed a novel self-assembled Au-Se nanodrug (AAC) for the co-delivery of the STING agonist cGAMP and HIF-1α inhibitor acriflavine (Acf) to tumor sites. As designed, AAC possesses favorable tumor accumulation by passively targeting action and the acidic tumor microenvironment promotes the release of the cGAMP and Acf from AAC. cGAMP activates the STING signaling pathway, while Acf disrupts HIF-1α/β dimerization; these effects collectively promote tumor vascular normalization and remodel the TME into an immune-permissive milieu. Both in vitro and in vivo results demonstrated that when combined with the photothermal properties of AuNRs, AAC induces immunogenic cell death (ICD), promotes the release of type I interferons, enhances DC maturation, and boosts the infiltration and activation of CTLs, leading to robust suppression of both primary and distant tumors in murine breast cancer models. Collectively, the development of the AAC nanodrug provides a promising new candidate for improving the clinical therapeutic efficacy of TNBC.

Materials and methods

Materials

Tetrachloroauric acid trihydrate (HAuCl4·3H2O) was obtained from Source Leaf Biotechnology (China). PEI1800-Se-Se-PEG20k was purchased from Ruixibio (China). 2’,3’-cGAMP was supplied by Macklin (China). Acriflavine, cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB), L-ascorbic acid (AA), and sodium chloride (NaCl) were acquired from Sigma-Aldrich (Shanghai, China), Solarbio (China), Macklin (China), and Xilong Scientific (China), respectively. Sodium borohydride (NaBH4) was provided by Tianjin Yongda Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (China). The Mitochondrial Membrane Potential Assay Kit (JC-1), CCK-8 Cell Counting Kit, One Step TUNEL Apoptosis Assay Kit, and apoptotic inhibitor (Z-VAD-fmk) were purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (China). The Calcein-AM/PI Double Staining Kit (Solarbio, Beijing, China) and Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Kit (Apexbio, China) were also obtained commercially. Other chemical reagents, unless otherwise specified, were purchased from Sinopharm Group Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (China) and used as received.

Preparation of AuNRs

AuNRs were synthesized via a previously reported seed-mediated growth method. Briefly, the seed solution was prepared by adding HAuCl4·3H2O (74 µL, 0.02428 M) and deionized water (1.0458 mL) to a CTAB solution (2.5 mL, 0.2 M), followed by the addition of ice-cold NaBH4 aqueous solution (0.5 mL, 0.01 M) with rapid stirring for 2 min. The solution color changed from clear orange to brown, indicating successful formation of the gold nanoparticle (AuNP) seed solution. Subsequently, the growth solution was prepared by mixing CTAB (25 mL, 0.2 M), AgNO3 (1.4 mL, 0.004 M), HAuCl₄ (1.5 mL, 0.02428 M), and deionized water (20 mL). L-ascorbic acid (620 µL, 0.0788 M) was then added, and the solution gradually faded to colorless and transparent. The seed solution (500 µL) was injected into the growth solution under stirring, and the mixture was incubated undisturbed at 27 °C for 24 h to allow AuNR growth.

Synthesis of AuNRs@PEI@PEG

For functionalization of AuNRs with the copolymer, a stable aqueous solution of PEG-Se-Se-PEI (1 mg/mL) was first prepared by dissolving PEG-Se-Se-PEI in ultrapure water. Subsequently, 1 mL of this copolymer solution was added to the as-synthesized AuNR dispersion. The mixture was stirred continuously at moderate speed for 24 h at room temperature to ensure stable anchoring of PEG-Se-Se-PEI onto the AuNR surface via robust Au-Se coordination bonds. This process yielded the core-shell structured AuNRs@PEG-Se-Se-PEI nanoparticles.

Synthesis of AuNRs@PEI@PEG@acriflavine@2’,3’-cGAMP (AAC)

Acriflavine and 2’,3’-cGAMP were dissolved separately in ultrapure water to prepare 1 mM stock solutions. The two stock solutions were mixed and incubated at room temperature for 1 h to ensure complete homogenization. The resulting drug mixture was then added to the AuNRs@PEG-Se-Se-PEI dispersion, and the final mixture was stirred continuously in the dark at room temperature for 24 h. This step facilitated electrostatic adsorption and potential intercalation of drug molecules onto the positively charged PEI-modified AuNR surface, leading to formation of the final AuNRs@PEI@PEG@Acriflavine@cGAMP (AAC) NPs.

Evaluation of photothermal performance of AuNRs

AuNRs exhibited a prominent localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR) peak at 808 nm, indicating potential for efficient photothermal conversion. In vitro photothermal properties were evaluated as follows: (1) AuNRs (50 µg mL− 1) were irradiated continuously for 300 s with an 808 nm laser at different power densities (0.5, 1, 1.5, and 2 W cm− 2). Temperature changes were recorded every 50 s using real-time infrared thermal imaging. (2) AuNR solutions with different concentrations (25, 50, 100, and 200 µg mL− 1) were irradiated with an 808 nm laser at 1 W cm− 2 for 300 s, with temperature measurements taken every 50 s. (3) For photothermal stability assessment, AuNRs (50 µg mL− 1) were irradiated with an 808 nm laser at 1 W cm− 2 for 300 s, followed by laser cessation and cooling to room temperature. This heating-cooling cycle was repeated 5 times, with temperature changes monitored every 50 s. (4) Photothermal conversion efficiency was calculated using AuNRs (50 µg mL− 1) irradiated with an 808 nm laser at 0.5 W cm− 2 for 800 s, followed by natural cooling to room temperature. Drug release assay

The pH-responsive release behavior of Acf and cGAMP from AAC was evaluated using a dialysis method. Briefly, a predetermined amount of AAC was dispersed in 1 mL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at different pH values (7.4, 6.5, 5.5, and 4.5) and transferred into dialysis bags (MWCO: 100 kDa). The dialysis bags were then immersed in 20 mL of the corresponding release medium and incubated at 37 °C with gentle shaking (100 rpm). At predetermined time points (1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, and 12 h), 1 mL of the release medium was withdrawn for quantification and replaced with an equal volume of fresh medium to maintain sink conditions. The concentrations of Acf and cGAMP in the collected samples were determined using fluorescence spectrophotometry and HPLC, respectively. Cumulative release percentages were calculated and plotted as a function of time. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Cellular uptake of AAC

AAC was labeled with fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) for quantitative and qualitative evaluation of cellular internalization. 4T1 cells were seeded at 2 × 10⁵ cells per dish and cultured for 24 h to allow full adhesion. For uptake kinetics analysis, cells were treated with FITC-labeled AAC (30 µg/mL) for different time intervals (1, 2, and 4 h) or with varying AAC concentrations (7.5, 15, and 30 µg/mL) for 4 h. After incubation, cells were thoroughly rinsed with PBS to remove non-internalized nanoparticles, counterstained with DAPI for nuclear visualization, and analyzed by confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) and flow cytometry. A time- and dose-dependent increase in FITC fluorescence confirmed efficient cellular uptake of AAC.

To identify the endocytic pathways involved, inhibition experiments were performed: pre-adhered 4T1 cells were co-incubated with FITC-AAC and pharmacological inhibitors of key endocytic routes (amiloride, 10 µg/mL, for macropinocytosis; chlorpromazine (CPZ), 10 µM, for clathrin-mediated endocytosis; filipin, 10 µM, for caveolae-mediated endocytosis). Additional cells were incubated at 4 °C to suppress energy-dependent processes. After 4 h of treatment, cells were processed and analyzed as described above. A significant reduction in fluorescence intensity indicated a major role of the corresponding pathway in AAC internalization.

In vitro antitumor efficacy

The cytotoxicity of AAC against 4T1 cells was evaluated using the CCK-8 assay. Briefly, 4T1 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 1 × 104 cells per well and allowed to adhere for 24 h. Cells were then treated with different formulations (PBS, PBS + L, AuNRs, AuNRs + L, AAC, AAC + L) for an additional 24 h. After treatment, cells were gently rinsed with PBS, and CCK-8 solution was added to each well. Following 0.5 h of incubation, absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader to determine cell viability.

Apoptosis assay and live/dead cell staining

For apoptosis quantification, 4T1 cells were seeded in 6-well plates and treated with the designated formulations (PBS, PBS + L, AuNRs, AuNRs + L, AAC, AAC + L) for 24 h. Post-treatment, cells were harvested, washed with cold PBS, and resuspended in binding buffer. Cell suspensions were double-stained with Annexin V-FITC and propidium iodide (PI) for 15 min in the dark, followed by immediate flow cytometric analysis to quantify viable (Annexin V⁻/PI⁻), early apoptotic (Annexin V⁺/PI⁻), late apoptotic (Annexin V⁺/PI⁺), and necrotic (Annexin V⁻/PI⁺) cells.

For live/dead cell staining, 4T1 cells were plated and treated identically for 8 h. Culture medium was replaced with Calcein-AM/PI staining solution (per Solarbio kit instructions). Calcein-AM is metabolized by esterases in live cells to produce green fluorescence, while PI penetrates only dead cells with compromised membranes, emitting red fluorescence. After incubation, cells were imaged by CLSM.

Mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) assay and mitochondrial morphology analysis

MMP changes were assessed using the JC-1 probe for both quantitative (flow cytometry) and qualitative (CLSM) analysis. 4T1 cells were treated with the designated formulations for 24 h. In cells with intact MMP, JC-1 accumulates in mitochondria as aggregates emitting red fluorescence; in apoptotic cells with collapsed MMP, JC-1 remains as cytoplasm-localized monomers emitting green fluorescence. The red-to-green fluorescence ratio reflects MMP status. For CLSM imaging, stained cells were imaged immediately to capture the color shift from red to green, indicating MMP loss.

For mitochondrial morphology analysis, a 250 µM PK Mito Red stock solution was prepared in anhydrous DMSO. 4T1 cells seeded in chamber slides (2 × 10⁵ cells/chamber) for 24 h were stained with a 1:1000–1:5000 dilution of the stock in pre-warmed medium for 15 min, washed with warm medium, and subjected to fluorescence imaging.

Western blot analysis

4T1 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at 5 × 10⁵ cells per well and cultured for 24 h. Cells were then treated with the designated formulations (PBS, PBS + L, AuNRs, AuNRs + L, AAC, AAC + L) at 50 µg/mL for 8 h. Post-treatment, cells were washed three times with cold PBS, harvested, and lysed. Total protein was extracted using a commercial kit, and protein concentration was determined via the BCA assay. Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes. Membranes were probed with primary antibodies (1:1000 dilution unless specified): HIF-1α, STING, TBK1, IRF3, p-STING, p-TBK1, p-IRF3, and β-actin (1:2000 dilution). Protein bands were visualized using a chemiluminescence detection system.

In vivo optical imaging

Female BALB/c mice (4 weeks old) were obtained from GemPharmatech Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Nanjing, China). All animal experiments were approved by the Ethics Committee of Shandong Medical and Pharmaceutical University (Ethics Approval No.: 2023 − 216) and conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines for laboratory animal care and use. Subcutaneous tumor models were established by injecting 2 × 105 4T1 cells into the right hind limb of each mouse. When tumor volume reached ~ 500 mm³, Cy5.5-labeled AAC and free Cy5.5 was administered via tail vein injection. At predetermined time points post-injection (10 min,1, 3, 6, 8, 10, 12, and 72 h), mice were anesthetized with an isoflurane-oxygen mixture (3–4% for induction, 1–2% for maintenance) and placed in a prone position in an IVIS Spectrum imaging system. After the final imaging time point, mice were euthanized, and major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, kidneys) and tumors were harvested for ex vivo imaging to confirm tracer biodistribution.

In Vivo Photothermal Imaging

Murine breast cancer models were established by subcutaneous injection of 2 × 105 4T1 cells (in sterile PBS) into the right hind flank of BALB/c mice. When tumors reached a palpable volume of ~ 200 mm3, mice were randomly divided into treatment groups. Mice in designated groups received intravenous injection of AAC. After a predetermined incubation period to facilitate tumor accumulation, tumor regions were irradiated locally with an 808 nm near-infrared (NIR) laser at a specified power density for 10 min. Temperature changes in the tumor area were monitored and recorded in real time using an infrared thermal imaging camera.

In vivo antitumor efficacy

Subcutaneous tumor models were established by injecting 4 × 105 4T1 cells into the right hind limb of each mouse. When tumor volume reached ~ 100 mm3, mice were randomly assigned to six groups (n = 6) and received intravenous treatments at a gold concentration of 4 mg/kg: (1) PBS, (2) PBS + L, (3) AuNRs, (4) AuNRs + L, (5) AAC, (6) AAC + L. Tumor volume and body weight were monitored every 2 days. All mice were euthanized on day 18, and tumors and major organs were harvested for histological analysis. Sections of major organs were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and imaged using a fluorescence microscope.

Flow cytometric analysis of antitumor immunity

Subcutaneous tumor models were established by injecting 4 × 105 4T1 cells into the right hind limb of each mouse. When tumor volume reached ~ 100 mm3, mice were randomly assigned to six groups (n = 6) and received intravenous treatments at a gold concentration of 4 mg/kg (groups as described in Sect. 2.13). Tumor volume and body weight were monitored every 2 days. On day 18, a secondary tumor challenge was performed by inoculating 4 × 105 4T1 cells into the left hind limb; tumor volume and body weight monitoring continued every 2 days. On day 28, mice were euthanized, and blood was collected for serum extraction. Serum levels of TNF-α, IFN-β, and IL-6 were quantified by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). Tumor tissues were minced and filtered through a nylon mesh to prepare single-cell suspensions. For T cell characterization, cells were stained with fluorescently labeled antibodies against mouse CD45 (FITC-conjugated, 11-0451-85), CD4 (PE-conjugated, 12-0041-82), and CD8a (PercP-Cyanine 5.5-conjugated, 45-0081-82). For mature dendritic cell (DC) identification, cells were stained with antibodies against CD11c (PercP-conjugated, 48-0114-82), CD80 (PE-conjugated, 12-0801-82), and CD86 (APC-conjugated, 117-0862-82). All antibodies were purchased from eBioscience (USA). Flow cytometric data were analyzed using FlowJo 10 software. Sections of major organs were stained and imaged by fluorescence microscopy.

Data analysis and statistics

All statistical analyses were performed using FlowJo 10, ImageJ, GraphPad Prism 8, or Origin 2018 software. Quantitative data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from at least three independent experiments. Statistical comparisons between two groups were performed using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test, and comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Differences were considered statistically significant when p < 0.05. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ns indicates p > 0.05.
