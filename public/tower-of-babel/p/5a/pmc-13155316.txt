Historical trends in self‐reported US heroin initiation
Authors: Jonathan P. Caulkins, Bishu Giri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimsIllegal opioids create challenges for public health and safety. There is imperfect understanding of when use of illegally manufactured opioids increased. This paper examined data on self‐reported year of first heroin use in the United States.DesignSecondary analysis of general population survey data.SettingUnited States.Participants1 708 720 across 38 surveys from 1979 to 2023.MeasurementsThe U.S. National Survey on Drug Use and Health and its predecessors ask respondents about their year of first use for various substances. In any single survey, few report initiating heroin in a specific year, but combining multiple surveys improves precision, enabling the plotting of time trends. Those who initiate can be broken down by age and by whether they report having misused pain relievers before they first used heroin.FindingsSelf‐reported heroin initiation appears to have been rare before the late 1960s, although data for those years are sparse. It rose sharply to a peak in 1972, fell by almost half, and remained stable from 1978 to 1994. It rose by about 75% in the late 1990s and a further 85% by the early 2010s. The proportion reporting misuse of prescription opioids before first using heroin increased from about one‐third before 1990 to one‐half by 2000 and 80% by 2010. The proportion who were over age 40 at the time of first heroin use increased from nearly 0 before 1990 to 10% in 2012 and about 40% in 2020–2021.ConclusionsIn the United States, heroin initiation that is self‐reported to have occurred after 2000 differs in magnitude and character from that from the late 1970s through mid‐1990s. Changes began before dates commonly associated with restrictions on opioid prescribing. This appears consistent with a view that “trading down” from prescription opioid misuse to consumption of illegally manufactured opioids did not only occur after implementation of policies to reduce opioid prescribing.

INTRODUCTION

The production, distribution and consumption of illegal opioids create problems for public health and safety in many countries. Overdose rates are particularly high in Canada and the USA, where illegally manufactured fentanyl has replaced heroin in many opioid markets, beginning in the mid‐2010s.

In North America, there have been multiple waves of opioids associated with overdose deaths [1]. Heroin dominated misuse from the late 1960s through the late 1990s [2], with the situation having more or less stabilized by the late 1970s. This stasis was broken by increasing overdose deaths, first with prescription opioids, then heroin and later fentanyl and other synthetic opioids, often now in combination with cocaine or methamphetamine [1, 3].

This article uses general population survey (GPS) data to examine historical trends in self‐reported heroin initiation in the USA. Parallel analysis is not conducted for fentanyl because survey questions about fentanyl were added only recently, and customers do not always know whether the opioids they purchase include fentanyl.

The GPS data have limitations, and two warrant mention at the outset. First, some people may not respond to the survey, or under‐report their use if they do. Second, non‐response and under‐reporting may be particularly pronounced for people who develop the greatest problems with use. We think the trends here are more reflective of the subset of initiates who do not escalate to highly problematic use. However, initiation trends within that subset are of interest, and they may parallel trends in initiation that leads to problematic use.

METHODS

Description of surveys

The data are derived from public access web pages to the National Survey on Drug Use and Health (NSDUH; https://datatools.samhsa.gov) since 2002 and the National Household Survey on Drug Abuse (NHSDA) before then (https://www.icpsr.umich.edu/web/ICPSR/series/64).

Surveys have been run annually since 1990, and also in 1979, 1982, 1985 and 1988. The target population initially was the household population age 12 years and older in the coterminous USA. In 1991, this expanded to include residents of Alaska and Hawaii, those in non‐institutional group quarters (e.g. college dormitories, civilians on military bases) and homeless people in shelters [4].

In 1999 interviewing switched from paper‐and‐pencil to computer‐assisted interviewing, and sampling expanded to permit state‐specific estimates. A $30 incentive payment was added in 2002. In 2015 there was a substantial questionnaire redesign. In 2020 and 2021 there was web‐based as well as in‐person data collection because of Covid‐19.

Variables

The primary NSDUH variable is IRHERYFU, which stands for ‘imputed revised year of first heroin use’. NSDUH calculates IRHERYFU from respondents’ ages and answers to the question ‘How old were you the first time you used heroin?’ (for details and imputation methods, refer to the NSDUH codebooks). NHSDA surveys do not include this variable, so we instead computed the survey year minus respondent age plus age at first heroin use.

Questions about the misuse of pain relievers have changed. Recent surveys only ask about year of initiation for recent initiates, but the 2002–2014 surveys include the variable IRANLYFU, which is based on the question ‘How old were you the first time you used any prescription pain reliever that was not prescribed for you or that you took only for the experience or feeling it caused?’. Respondents are weighted by standard sample weights (e.g. variable ANALWT2 in recent years).

Accounting for mortality

We obtain weighted count point estimates (X t,y ) and associated standard errors (s t,y ) for initiation in year t based on surveys in year y for all t such that y – 40 ≤ t ≤ y – 2. A survey conducted in year y understates initiation in year y because it misses initiation later that same year. It also seems to systematically understate initiation in year y – 1 relative to what gets reported for that year by later surveys; perhaps respondents are particularly likely to under‐report recent initiation.

We do not use surveys to look back more than 40 years. Most initiation, particularly before the year 2000, occurs at ages 15–24 years. Forty years later, those individuals are aged 55–64 years. After that, mortality becomes appreciable. For example, the US census reports that there were 30 236 000 people aged 15–24 years in 1965, but 58 years later, in 2023, it records only 21 887 260 people from those same birth cohorts. There is some mortality before the age of 65 years, but it is offset by immigration. For those same birth cohorts at 40 not 58 years later, the US census finds 30 641 500 people aged 55–64 years in 2005, or 1% more than the number of 15–24‐year‐olds in 1965. Across the 19 initiation‐year, survey‐year pairs that differ by 40 years (i.e. 1965–2005, 1996–2006, … 1983–2023), there were on average 1.1% more people age 55–64 years in the survey year than there were 15–24 year‐olds 40 years earlier. Hence, one approach is not to adjust for mortality within the range of years included in this analysis.

However, to the extent that immigrants had lower rates of heroin initiation, the first approach may slightly underestimate initiation. Our second estimate adjusts for mortality based on survivorship data from the National Vital Statistics Reports [5]. We estimate the proportion of 15–24 year‐olds in year t who are still alive in year y, and multiply the survey report (X t,y ) by the reciprocal of that proportion (K t,y ), producing adjusted estimates and standard errors: Xt,y′=Kt,yXt,y,andst,y′=Kt,yst,y.

This adjustment might be correct if no immigrants tried heroin before immigrating. To the extent that some had, this second estimate may be too high, whereas the first estimate will tend to be too low. Both series are shown in Figure 1. It turns out that both estimates are very close to each other because most data used to estimate initiation in year t come from surveys conducted sooner than 40 years after year t. Hence, whether or how one adjusts for mortality matters little relative to other uncertainties in the analysis.

Combining data across survey years

To combine data from multiple surveys, we take a weighted average of those X t,y values, including (t,y) pairs for which X t,y is zero, weighting by the inverse of the average standard error of that survey, ( 1sy¯), with that average computed across all initiation years for which there is an estimate and, hence, a standard error. The standard error of that weighted sum is computed as the square root of the sum of the survey‐year specific squared errors, weighted by the square of those weights: Xt^=∑y1sy¯Xt,y′∑y1sy¯, sXt^=∑y1sy¯2st,y′2.

This gives greater weight to surveys that tend to have smaller standard errors. The alternate approach of weighting by the inverse of the standard error for that specific estimate (i.e. by 1st,y) has two limitations. First, it does not permit factoring in (y,t) pairs for which no respondent reported initiating, because there is no standard error. Second, the estimates (X t,y ) and the (s t,y ) are positively correlated because low estimates create low standard errors. As a result, weighting by 1st,y systematically over‐weights estimates from (y,t) pairs for which X t,y values are low, producing weighted averages that are too low.

RESULTS

Figure 1 shows the levels of GPS‐reported US heroin initiation since 1965. Note: this is initiation into any use, even once, not just regular or ongoing use. The initial estimates (blue line) and those adjusting for mortality (red line with plotting points) are very close. To avoid cluttering the diagram we only show variability (plus and minus two standard errors) for the series with the mortality adjustment.

Heroin use is generally understood to have been rare before an epidemic in the late 1960s and early 1970s, and Figure 1 shows a surge in initiation at that time. There follows an extended period of relative stability from about 1978 to 1994, after which the estimated initiation jumped by about 75%. There was another 85% jump starting in the late naughts (2006–2009). Because of population growth, the per capita increases were smaller, about 55% between 1985 and 1994 and 1996–2005 and 65% between 1996 and 2005 and 2011–2020.

Figure 2 uses the IRANLYFU variable from the 2002–2014 surveys to divide heroin initiators through 2012 into groups based on when they reported first misusing pain relievers (the plotting points are 5‐year moving averages). Among those reporting heroin initiation before 1994, the proportion saying that they had previously misused prescription pain relievers was generally below 40%. This proportion grew steadily to approach 80% for those reporting heroin initiation in 2010–2012.

Five‐year moving average of proportions of self‐reported heroin initiates who say they misused pain relievers before starting heroin use, plotted by year of first reported heroin use (data from 2002–2014 NSDUH surveys).

Among these respondents, the average age of reported heroin initiation was mostly below 25 years until 2010, but then rose steeply to a mean of around 38 years by 2020. The increase stems from a striking increase in the proportion of heroin initiates who are older. Figure 3 illustrates this by plotting trends in the proportion of heroin initiates who were aged over 30 years and over 40 years. Until 2000, heroin initiation was uncommon for anyone over the age of 30 years and was rare amongst those aged over 40 years, but now these age groups account for a sizable minority of heroin initiation.

DISCUSSION

The analysis was not pre‐registered and the results should be considered exploratory. General population surveys have limitations, particularly for stigmatized drugs like heroin [6, 7, 8]. The data are self‐reported, lack validation from biological samples and exclude subpopulations that may use at different rates than the rest of the population. Aggregating so many respondents (over 1.7 million) overcomes the problem of initiation being rare, but we still expect significant under‐reporting. The levels reported here are likely to be underestimates, and by a considerable margin (sample errors do not account for non‐sampling error). Furthermore, those whose heroin initiation escalated to problematic use may be particularly under‐represented, and large samples do not overcome age‐heaping at rounded ages or forward/backward‐telescoping of first year of use.

Nevertheless, these trends may be of interest for several reasons. First, initiation by those who did not escalate to the point of disappearing from a survey sampling frame still represents risky use of an illegal drug. Second, trends in initiation among those who survive to answer surveys may parallel trends in total initiation, in part because most people do not escalate to highly problematic use [9]. Third, fluctuations in initiation as recorded here may be suggestive of shifts in supply (e.g. the observed downturn after 1972 corresponds to the timing of the Turkish poppy cultivation ban, the breaking of the French Connection and US troops withdrawing from Vietnam, all of which could have contributed to reduced supply) [2].

CONCLUSION

Historical perspective is important, but a pressing issue today is the abundance of cheap synthetic opioids (notably fentanyl) and resulting overdose. Non‐experimental evidence can never be more than suggestive, but the trends here are consistent with two themes.

First, the fact that by 2010 fully 80% of people reporting heroin initiation say they had already misused prescription opioids underscores that the multiple waves of opioid deaths represent shifts in which opioid was being misused, not separate events involving different people.

Second, after nearly 20 years of stability, heroin initiation changed in the late 1990s. The three figures show that initiation increased sharply, became dominated by people who misused prescription opioids before they used heroin and included a marked increase in initiation by older individuals (heroin initiation by people aged over 40 years was rarely reported before the year 2000, but grew to 40% of initiation by the end of these data series). Those changes are consistent with the idea that expansions in opioid prescribing in the 1990s were already affecting heroin initiation before subsequent efforts to restrict that prescribing. No single event fully captures the retreat from liberal prescribing, but milestones include the 2010 abuse‐deterrent reformulation of OxyContin and the 2016 Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) guidelines for prescribing opioids [10]. Although there may be alternative explanations (e.g. supply shifts, secular trends), these findings may help to defend those restrictions against attacks blaming them for causing all or almost all of the shift from prescription opioid abuse to the abuse of illegal opioids.

There are many potential avenues for further research, including reframing the question to focus on individual rates or risks of heroin initiation and/or tracking patterns by birth cohort to tease out age–period–cohort effects. It might also be instructive to examine the shift in initiation ages in more detail and to correlate shifts in heroin initiation with indicators of illegal opioid supply, price and availability.
