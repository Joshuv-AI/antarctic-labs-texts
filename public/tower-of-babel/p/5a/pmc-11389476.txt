Invited Perspective: Challenges and Opportunities in Evaluating the Effect of Chemical Mixtures on Congenital Chromosomal Abnormalities
Authors: Zeyan Liew, Pengfei Guo
Published: 2024

Congenital chromosomal abnormalities occur when the number or structure of the chromosomes in all or part of the embryonic cells is altered. Fetuses affected by chromosomal abnormalities have high mortality rates, and affected liveborn infants face a shorter life expectancy and a variety of physical and developmental conditions over their lifespans. In past decades, prenatal screening has been advanced and expanded for early detection of chromosomal abnormalities,1 but this sometimes leads to controversy and debate over associated interventions, such as elective abortion.2

Chromosomal abnormalities occur because of errors in cell division during the formation of the egg or sperm or during early embryonic development.3 However, it remains unclear if these errors are solely due to chance or whether external environmental factors also contribute.4–6 The only established pregnancy factor associated with fetal chromosomal abnormalities is advanced maternal age at conception, the global average of which has been increasing rapidly.7 Certainly, research that sheds light on possible modifiable risks of chromosomal abnormalities has high public health importance.

In this issue of Environmental Health Perspectives, Hasegawa et al.8 explored whether maternal exposure during pregnancy to several per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS), alone and as a mixture, was associated with chromosomal abnormalities in their offspring. PFAS, also known as forever chemicals, are widespread pollutants that are extremely persistent in the environment and slow to be excreted biologically.9 Hasegawa et al.8 posited that recent in vitro studies10,11 showed that PFAS affected oocytes and increased risks for aneuploidy, supporting the rationale for their analysis. To our knowledge, three prior studies of PFAS and congenital malformations did not examine chromosomal abnormalities.12–14 In one study that evaluated communities affected by perfluorooctanoic acid in drinking water, there were no or only a few cases of congenital malformations in each exposure category, rendering the results inconclusive.15

The new work by Hasegawa et al.8 is part of the Japan Environment and Children’s Study (JECS). The JECS cohort is impressive, including over 24,000 singleton pregnancies recruited from 15 regional sites, with maternal plasma PFAS data characterized around gestational week 12. Overall, the new work showed that higher maternal plasma concentrations of perfluorooctanesulfonic acid and a mixture of PFAS were associated with increased odds for chromosomal abnormalities in offspring. These results, however, were based on only 44 confirmed cases of chromosomal abnormalities, and the findings were inconsistent when evaluating the most common subtype (trisomy 21), casting doubt on the replicability of the findings. Nevertheless, this study provides a basis for future research investigating the effects of PFAS on human embryonic development, including chromosomal abnormalities, which are not always considered in studies evaluating teratogenic effects of environmental exposures. Moreover, congenital defects of the brain12 and heart13,14 that were linked to PFAS exposure in earlier studies also need confirmation.

It is a tall order to address all sources of selection and outcome misclassification biases in studies of chromosomal abnormalities. Environmental exposures might reduce the chance of conception or survival through early pregnancy and, thus, lower the probability of being enrolled in a pregnancy cohort.16 Moreover, maternal socioeconomic and other health care factors may influence the use of prenatal screening of fetal genetic disorders, resulting in elective abortion (and thus, exclusion from the ongoing cohort) or the outcome diagnosis if the child is born. In addition, the malformation status for other unscreened pregnancy losses and deaths of newborns are not always reported or confirmed. Selection bias could occur if fetal and neonatal mortality were associated with congenital chromosomal abnormalities and environmental exposures.

Rightfully, the authors have given attention to these issues in their investigation. The primary case group studied was limited to offspring with confirmed diagnoses of chromosomal abnormalities, arguably a correct approach in prioritizing the specificity of outcome classification, because even a low sensitivity would not result in enough undiagnosed cases to skew the exposure levels in the comparison group. The JECS did not study the prenatal genetic screening data because of the low usage of screening in Japan. Meanwhile, live birth selection bias is difficult to assess. Although the authors included pregnancy records of miscarriage and stillbirth, these records are likely incomplete. The authors performed a quantitative bias analysis for selection, demonstrating that both positive or negative biases could occur, largely depending on the direction or magnitude of PFAS effects on selection. This echoed our work that showed the importance of PFAS-induced “live birth bias.”17,18

Realistically, we may never rule out all live birth bias owing to the complex environmentally related selection forces. Investigating and discussing this issue, like the authors did, is a step in the right direction. Ultimately, it may be sufficient to know whether such bias is severe enough to alter the conclusion of a study in a way that affects policy decisions. In theory, a strong live birth bias could occur, making a prenatal environmental factor appear to be protective even when the true effect on child health is null or even harmful.17,19

Ensuring statistical power to study chromosomal abnormalities is a challenge. Alternative study designs could be considered, such as a nested case–control approach studying the additional 152 confirmed cases without PFAS measurements in the JECS cohort that were not included in the analysis by Hasegawa et al.8 Even so, data on specific types of chromosomal abnormalities will still be sparse and may require efforts to perform data pooling using other comparable cohorts if available.

Finally, this study addresses a single chemical class. The joint effect of chemical mixtures, including but also beyond PFAS, remains open for investigation. We must collaborate to understand how early life cell division is impaired by a complex cocktail of chemical exposures. Pinning down the role of environmental factors in congenital abnormalities remains a daunting challenge, but it also presents opportunities to lay the groundwork and lead the way.
