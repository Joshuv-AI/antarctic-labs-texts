Spatial Dissociation of Atrophy and Hypophysiology in Alzheimer's Disease: Implications for Biomarkers
Authors: Annie Dang, Di Wang, Jonathan M Towne, Sudha Seshadri, Mohamad Habes, Peter T Fox
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe Amyloid/Tau/Neurodegeneration (A/T/N) biomarker framework is used for in vivo pathological profiling of Alzheimer's disease. A binary (+/−) label is assigned for each pathology (A, T, N) based on its presence or absence as determined by imaging or fluid biomarkers. Using imaging, neurodegeneration is confirmed by either structural MRI (indicating atrophy) or 18F‐FDG PET (indicating hypometabolism), implicitly assuming that both modalities identify equivalent pathology. Preliminary evidence suggests that atrophy and hypometabolism do not spatially co‐localize and are likely caused by distinct underlying pathologies. Further, while MRI is widely available, many sites lack access to PET. Recent evidence indicates that hemodynamic indices, identified via functional MRI or SPECT, may be spatially concordant with hypometabolism identified by 18F‐FDG‐PET and able to serve as reliable proxies. Coordinate‐based meta‐analyses of voxel‐based morphometry and voxel‐based physiology reports in Alzheimer's disease, collectively analyzing 139 articles (4218 individuals) were performed to test the following hypotheses: (1) that atrophy and hypometabolism in AD are spatially dissociated; (2) that hemodynamic indices exhibit the same spatial distributions as hypometabolism identified by PET; (3) that regions of atrophy and hypometabolism involve different functional systems and cognitive operations. Results confirmed all three hypotheses. Separation of atrophy and hypometabolism into two distinct subcategories of neurodegeneration as well as the development of regionally specific biomarkers for each are in order.

Introduction

Biomarkers are quantitative, objective measures of biological processes. Their use spans biomedical research, clinical trial criteria, clinical practice, and medical product development. According to the FDA‐NIH Biomarker Working Group, biomarker contexts of use include diagnostic, monitoring, predictive, prognostic, response, and susceptibility, among others (FDA‐NIH Biomarker Working Group 2016). Most common categories of biomarkers include blood‐based, CSF‐based, and imaging‐based biomarkers.

The Alzheimer's disease (AD) community has been particularly successful in biomarker development and adoption. Before 2007, AD was diagnosed only by post‐mortem pathological examination. Diagnosis in living patients was limited to a probable AD diagnosis based on clinical evaluation. With the increasing use of biomarkers between 2007 and 2016, AD antemortem diagnosis was based on a joint clinical and radiobiological criteria system. Since 2016, the AD field has largely transitioned to pure biological criteria for diagnosis of AD (Villain and Dubois 2019).

The formal Amyloid‐Tau‐Neurodegeneration (A/T/N) AD biomarker framework was first introduced in 2016 and adopted as a core component of the National Institute on Aging– Alzheimer's Association (NIA‐AA) Research Framework in 2018 (Jack et al. 2016, 2018). The goal was for each individual to have a full “biomarker profile,” with binary (+/−) labels for each category (A, T, N), reflecting their pathological state. The framework outlined imaging and CSF metrics to establish these profiles. “A” refers to β‐amyloid biomarkers (amyloid PET, CSF Aβ42, or CSF Aβ42 /Aβ40 ratio); “T” refers to aggregated tau (tau PET or CSF phosphorylated tau); “N” refers to neurodegeneration biomarkers, indicating neuronal injury or dysfunction (structural MRI, 18F‐FDG PET, or CSF total tau). Under the 2018 Research Framework, both CSF and PET measures translate into (−) normal and (+) abnormal binary labels based on determined cut points. For all three biomarker types, binarization ignores the rich topological (spatial) information available with imaging. For neurodegeneration, it assumes that structural MRI and 18F‐FDG PET measure the same underlying pathology, which may not be accurate.

Spatial dissociation between atrophy, as measured by structural MRI, and hypometabolism, as measured by 18F‐FDG PET, has been preliminarily described in AD. This was first explicitly stated in 2002 when it was noted that prior structural and functional studies had identified atrophy starting in the medial temporal structures whereas hypometabolism was seen in the posterior cingulate gyrus and precuneus (Matsuda et al. 2002). This discordance was explored by several small‐sample reports (< 30 subjects per study) following Matsuda's initial venture (Villain et al. 2008; Morbelli et al. 2010). This was made explicit in Clifford Jack's 2010 proposed biomarker model and included in the 2011 NIA‐AA diagnostic guidelines (Jack et al. 2010; Sperling et al. 2011). Jack's 2010 model stated that hypometabolism occurs early, starting in the posterior cingulate and spreading to the lateral temporal and frontal lobes. By contrast, atrophy occurs later and starts in the medial temporal lobe. This spatial and temporal distinction suggests that atrophy and hypometabolism are likely indexed by different underlying pathologies.

Hypophysiology is currently measured by 18F‐FDG PET which reports glucose metabolism (CMRglu), a measure closely correlated to brain blood flow and oxygen metabolism in the resting state (Fox and Raichle 1986; Fox et al. 1988; Hillman 2014). Therefore, measures based on blood flow and oxygen metabolism could be potential alternatives to 18F‐FDG PET. Brain blood flow can be quantified with arterial spin labeled (ASL) functional MRI (fMRI). Therefore, if hypoperfusion is spatially concordant with hypometabolism in Alzheimer's continuum disorders, ASL fMRI could be a more accessible alternative to 18F‐FDG PET. Blood oxygen level dependent (BOLD) fMRI is dependent on the ratio of oxyhemoglobin to deoxyhemoglobin. This measure is directly related to oxygen metabolism and blood flow. Several novel functional indices derived from BOLD fMRI—namely amplitude of low‐frequency fluctuations (ALFF) (Hoptman et al. 2010), fractional ALFF (fALFF) (Zou et al. 2008), and regional homogeneity (ReHo) (He et al. 2007; Zang et al. 2004)—have been proposed as candidate proxies for 18F‐FDG PET. ALFF, fALFF, and ReHo reflect neuronally driven phasic hemodynamic fluctuations and can each detect regional abnormalities in Alzheimer's continuum (Han et al. 2011; Li et al. 2002; Cai et al. 2018) and numerous other neurologic and psychiatric disorders (Gray et al. 2020; Sha et al. 2018; Drevets et al. 1992; Hou et al. 2014; Li et al. 2014; Bing et al. 2013). Our group has recently shown that ReHo and fALFF, in particular, are close proxies for CMRglu (Deng et al. 2022), extending prior reports (Han et al. 2011; Li et al. 2002, 2014; Gray et al. 2020; Hou et al. 2014; Bing et al. 2013).

A systematic comparison of the regional distributions of various hypophysiological indices with one another and with regional atrophy is in order. Given the quantity of published data available, coordinate‐based meta‐analyses (CBMA) of neuroimaging case–control contrasts in Alzheimer's continuum are the most robust approach. The most prevalent category of structural neuroimaging studies reported in Alzheimer's continuum is voxel‐based morphometry (VBM), which computes statistical parametric maps of gray‐matter atrophy from T1‐weighted MRI (Ashburner and Friston 2000). VBM is a mass‐univariate method (the same parameter calculated at each image voxel) performed as group‐average, case–control contrasts in standard space. Results are reported as x‐y‐z coordinates of the center of mass (or peak voxel) of significant clusters. The functional counterparts of VBM studies are voxel‐based physiological (VBP) studies, which are group‐average, coordinate‐reporting, case–control contrasts of metabolic rate (CMRglu or oxygen metabolism), hemodynamics (blood flow or blood volume), and neurovascular coupling (ReHo, fALFF, and ALFF). As task performance dramatically affects functional images, VBP studies are performed in the resting state. Jointly, the VBM and resting‐state VBP literature in Alzheimer's continuum (~140 studies) is sufficiently large to support well‐powered CBMA.

Functional roles can be assigned to brain regions in a data‐driven manner by seed‐based CBMA of the task‐activation literature. Behavioral domains and paradigm classes are two widely used interpretative measures. These measures are part of BrainMap taxonomy (Fox et al. 2005) and incorporated into the BrainMap database as meta‐data. In this context, behavioral domains refer to cognitive processes (e.g., action, cognition, perception) and paradigm classes (e.g., n‐back, odor detection, facial recognition) refer to experimental tasks. Experimental paradigms recruit cognitive processes (behavioral domains), which are captured by these meta‐data. Interpreting VBM/VBP results via task‐activation CBMA utilizing these taxonomies clarifies the functional roles of regions exhibiting pathology.

Assessing spatial dissociation of atrophy and hypometabolism as measures of neurodegeneration will be most effectively accomplished by performing CBMA of their respective literatures. Further, the functional roles of the implicated regions can be interpreted via the task‐activation literature. To accomplish these goals, a comprehensive literature search was performed to identify coordinate‐reporting, peer‐reviewed reports of VBM and VBP case–control contrasts of Alzheimer's continuum (AD and mild cognitive impairment) vs. healthy controls. Anatomical likelihood estimation (ALE) was used to identify above‐chance regional confluences, as this is the most thoroughly characterized (Eickhoff et al. 2012; Turkeltaub et al. 2012) and widely used (Acar et al. 2018; Salimi‐Khorshidi et al. 2009) CBMA approach. Datasets were filtered for subject redundancy and region‐of‐interest analyses, which violate the statistical assumptions of ALE. Three hypotheses were tested: First, that atrophy and hypometabolism for AD would be spatially dissociated. Second, that various hemodynamic indices would exhibit the same spatial distributions as CMRglu. Third, that regions of structural (atrophy) and functional (hypometabolism) change in AD would align with different behavioral domains and paradigm classes.

Materials and Methods

Literature Search

BrainMap databases (VBM; VBP), Google Scholar, PubMed, and Scopus were searched to identify articles investigating AD and MCI and reporting one or more of the following metrics: T1 gray‐matter volume; cerebral metabolic rate of glucose (CMRglu); 18F‐FDG PET; cerebral blood flow (CBF); arterial spin labeled MRI (ASL); single‐photon emission computed tomography (SPECT); Oxygen‐15 PET; blood oxygen level dependent (BOLD) signal; regional homogeneity (ReHo); amplitude of low‐frequency fluctuations (ALFF); fractional ALFF (fALFF). Reference tracking of previous meta‐analyses was also performed. This study was compliant with the PRISMA statement for reporting systematic reviews and meta‐analyses (Page et al. 2021) and followed the best‐practice guidelines for conducting CBMA of published neuroimaging data (Müller et al. 2018).

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

BrainMap articles had already been screened for the following criteria prior to inclusion in the database: (1) published in peer‐reviewed English language journals, (2) report three‐dimensional peak coordinates (x, y, z) in standard space (Talairach or MNI), (3) whole‐brain, voxel‐wise, and cross‐sectional analysis (i.e., no region‐of‐interest based methods). Google Scholar, PubMed, and Scopus articles were screened for these criteria. Surviving articles were coded into BrainMap for ease of proceeding analyses. Articles with article‐level duplicates were then excluded.

Subject and experiment‐level screening criteria were then applied to the articles. AD patients required a diagnosis by NINCDS‐ADRDA or DSM‐IV criteria for probable AD; MCI patients required diagnosis by criteria from Petersen et al. (1999), Lopez OL et al. (Lopez et al. 2006) or other similar criteria (Table S1a,b). Healthy controls for AD/MCI group comparison were all age‐matched with AD or MCI patients. We did not differentiate between AD and MCI subtypes (typical vs. atypical, severity level, converters vs. nonconverters, etc.). At the experiment level, only group comparisons (patients < age‐matched healthy controls) with decreased VBM or VBP results were included. Experiments reporting increased or preserved VBM and VBP results or within‐AD continuum comparison (i.e., AD < MCI) were excluded. For articles reporting both baseline and follow up group comparison experiments, only follow up experiments were included. For PET and SPECT experiments, if available, only the experiment with partial volume correction was included.

Data Redundancy

Meta‐analytic results can be biased when duplicate subject populations are included. Screening for data redundancy ensures that, within each individual meta‐analysis, no dataset is used more than once. Two types of duplicate data emerged throughout our study. We defined them as: article‐level duplicates and experiment‐level duplicates. Article‐level duplicates were identified within the meta‐analysis of each metric: gray‐matter volume, CMRglu, CBF and BOLD. The two types of article‐level duplicates were: (1) articles published by the same research group; (2) articles with pooled subjects from the same primary dataset. This study only included the article with the largest subject number. Two types of experiment‐level duplicates were also identified: (1) experiments applying different statistical thresholds to the same data within the same article; (2) experiments using pooled subjects from the same open‐source dataset. We included only the experiment with the greatest number of subjects. Further details on experiment‐level filtering are included in Supporting Information 3—Methods.

Coordinate‐Based Meta‐Analysis

Meta‐analytic groups used were all VBM, all VBP, and each VBP metric with sufficient data for meta‐analysis (at least 23 experiments) (Frahm et al. 2023). Coordinates reported in Talairach space were converted into MNI space by applying an icbm2tal algorithm (http://brainmap.org/icbm2tal/). GingerALE version 3.0.2 (http://www.brainmap.org/software.html#GingerALE) was used to perform coordinate‐based meta‐analyses on each group and determined above‐chance spatial convergence of reported coordinates. Specifically, GingerALE applies functional activation likelihood estimation (fALE) to functional VBP data and structural anatomical likelihood estimation (sALE) to structural VBM data. Both fALE and sALE treat each reported coordinate (foci) as a three‐dimensional Gaussian distribution with varied kernel size reflecting spatial uncertainty (larger sample size = less spatial uncertainty). Voxel‐wise fALE and sALE values were generated by the union of all probability distributions. These fALE and sALE values were tested against the null hypothesis that coordinates are randomly distributed and do not show convergence above chance. Thresholding was applied to generate final significant convergent clusters with peak locations and cluster size (Eickhoff et al. 2012, 2009; Turkeltaub et al. 2012). For our main analyses, we adopted the recommended CBMA fALE and sALE best practice (Müller et al. 2018) for neuroimaging with a cluster level inference of p < 0.05 and a cluster‐forming threshold of p < 0.001. To ensure cluster stability, we also performed two sets of sub‐analyses: (1) keeping the cluster‐level inference at p < 0.05, we adjusted the cluster‐forming threshold to be either more stringent (p < 0.0005) or more lenient (p < 0.005); (2) we compared VBM and VBP patterns within AD‐only and MCI‐only groups. To reduce spatial uncertainty between modalities, we applied an additional 6 mm full width at half maximum (FWHM) for every meta‐analysis. After thresholding, each resulting cluster was converted to a binary map. Mango (http://ric.uthscsa.edu/mango/) was used to visualize results and compute cluster overlap.

Fail‐Safe N Noise Simulation

Fail‐Safe N (FSN) algorithm (Acar et al. 2018) was used to identify the robustness of each significant cluster identified in the meta‐analyses. This algorithm is typically used to protect against “file drawer effect”, a type of publication bias where positive findings are more likely to be published than null findings. Effect‐location meta‐analyses, as performed here, are less affected by the file drawer effect than effect size meta‐analyses are. However, it is still good practice to assess the stability of our identified clusters. We used the FSN algorithm to generate “noisy” experiments, integrated the noisy experiments with the original data, repeated the meta‐analysis, and determined whether the original clusters were still statistically significant. All clusters were tested with two noise levels, 5% and 40% (Gray et al. 2020).

Behavioral and Paradigm Class Analysis

Regions of identified VBM convergence and VBP convergence between studies were separately analyzed for spatial loadings onto behavioral domains and paradigm classes. Behavioral domains refer to cognitive processes (e.g., action, cognition, perception). Paradigm classes refer to experimental tasks (e.g., n‐back, odor detection, facial recognition) that are used to elicit activation in certain brain regions. To stay consistent with existing analysis software, all coordinates were converted to Talairach space. Mango version 4.1 was used to visualize VBM and VBP patterns, and a region of interest (ROI) was created by shrink wrapping to encompass each VBM and VBP altered region. The Mango Behavioral Analysis and Mango Paradigm Analysis Plugins (updated May 2023) were utilized to determine spatial loadings onto behavioral domains and paradigm classes. These Plugins are based on BrainMap healthy task‐activation data (> 20,000 experiments), manually annotated for behavioral domains and paradigm classes. Loadings are expressed as z‐scores based on the representation of the behavioral domain or paradigm class in the ROI relative to chance. Further technical details are provided in Supporting Information 3—Methods.

Results

In total, 301 articles were identified from BrainMap and additional sources for further screening (Figure 1). Articles were excluded for general relevancy (N = 18), reporting in unknown coordinate spaces (N = 6), ROI‐based analyses (N = 9), longitudinal analyses (N = 5), and non‐voxel wise analyses (N = 3). Additionally, article‐level duplicates (N = 47) and studies of healthy brain aging (N = 74) were excluded. Experiment‐level exclusions for duplicates can be found in Table S2a,b. A set of 139 articles (201 experiments) remained, comprising 4218 individuals. These included measures of VBM (n = 79) and various VBP measures (n = 122). Of note, “N” refers to the number of articles, while “n” refers to the number of experiments. Each article contains one or more experiments. VBP measures included CMRglu VBP (n = 49), CBF VBP (n = 44), and BOLD VBP (n = 29). Groups used for meta‐analyses were VBM, all VBP, CMRglu VBP, CBF VBP, and BOLD VBP. The full list of articles used in this study is available in Supporting Information 4—Meta‐analytic Bibliography. All articles included in this study have been coded according to the BrainMap experimental taxonomy using Scribe and have been made available in the database (www.BrainMap.org).

VBM and VBP Patterns

VBM and VBP patterns were largely distinct, with minimal overlap (Figure 2). VBM meta‐analysis identified three clusters: bilateral parahippocampus and thalamus. VBP meta‐analysis identified five clusters: bilateral parietal lobes, bilateral temporal gyrus, and posterior cingulate/precuneus. There is a 330 mm3 overlap in the middle temporal gyrus, taking up 0.4% volume in both VBM and VBP. Detailed cluster information (e.g., size, maxima coordinates, brain regions) can be found in Table 1. Figure S1 shows a full layout with additional axial slices.

The AD‐only and MCI‐only sub‐analyses are shown in Figure S2a,b. We observe that the AD‐only results closely resemble the original AD/MCI findings, whereas the MCI‐only results show significantly less convergence. This indicates that the AD‐only group has a stronger spatial signal and supports that the VBM‐VBP dissociation persists in AD‐enriched samples.

VBM and VBP results from different levels of thresholding can be found in Figure S3a,b. There is little change from Figure 2, showing that the clusters identified in the primary analysis persist despite different levels of statistical thresholding.

VBP Patterns

Patterns of CMRglu VBP, CBF VBP, and BOLD VBP were well co‐localized. CMRglu VBP identified the largest volume of clusters—6 clusters totaling 82,160 mm3 as well as the single largest cluster (Figure 3). These clusters contained the cingulate gyrus, bilateral inferior parietal lobe, bilateral temporal gyrus, and precentral gyrus. CBF VBP identified 4 clusters containing the inferior parietal lobe, cingulate gyrus, inferior parietal lobe, and parahippocampal gyrus. BOLD VBP identified two clusters containing the cingulate gyrus and angular gyrus. CBF VBP clusters showed 59.3% (15,330 mm3) overlap with CMRglu VBP; BOLD VBP clusters showed 68.2% (7668 mm3) overlap with CMRglu VBP. CBF VBP and CMRglu VBP showed 4646 mm3 overlap, nearly all of which is also an overlap with CMRglu VBP (4598 mm3). Detailed cluster information (e.g., size, maxima coordinates, brain regions) can be found in Table 1. Figure S4 shows a full layout with additional axial slices. Results from different levels of thresholding can be found in Figure S5a,b. Clusters identified in the primary analysis (Figure 3) overwhelmingly persist at different levels of thresholding.

FailSafeN

All meta‐analyses were performed again with 5% added noise to test the robustness of resulting clusters. Surviving clusters were re‐tested with 40% added noise. FSN percentage for each cluster (Table 1) can be interpreted as the percentage of additional noise that is required to be added before there is a lack of convergence of previously identified clusters. Of the 8 clusters identified in the VBM and all VBP meta‐analyses, 5 of them exhibit a FSN > 40%, with the other 3 clusters exhibiting a FSN between 5% and 40%. Of the 12 clusters identified by CMRglu VBP, CBF VBP, and BOLD VBP, 8 exhibit a FSN > 40%, 3 between 5% and 40%, and 1 under 5%.

Behavior and Paradigm Class Analysis

VBM and VBP patterns were analyzed for loadings onto behavioral domains and paradigm classes. VBM most strongly loaded onto olfaction, fear, and explicit memory (Figure 4a). VBP most strongly loaded onto cognitive domains such as reasoning, social cognition, and explicit memory (Figure 4b). Paradigm classes most associated with VBM regions include face monitoring/discrimination, olfactory monitoring/discrimination, and affective pictures (Figure 5a). VBP most strongly loaded onto finger tapping/button press, theory of mind, and n‐back (Figure 5b).

Discussion

These findings support all 3 of our hypotheses: (1) that atrophy and hypometabolism in AD are spatially dissociated; (2) that various hemodynamic indices (CBF, BOLD) exhibit the same spatial distributions as CMRglu; (3) that regions of atrophy and hypometabolism align with different behavioral domains and paradigm classes.

Spatial Dissociation of Atrophy and Hypometablism: Hypothesis One

In the present A/T/N framework, neurodegeneration “N” is considered abnormal if either atrophy or hypometabolism is present. This implies pathological similarity between the two changes. In hypothesis one, we test whether atrophy and hypometabolic changes in AD/MCI are spatially dissociated, as suggested by early, small‐sample reports (Jack et al. 2016; Matsuda et al. 2002; Villain et al. 2008; Morbelli et al. 2010). Dissociation between VBM and VBP patterns was prominent, with only a 0.4% volume overlap. This dissociation persists when limited to AD‐only and MCI‐only samples as well as with different levels of thresholding.

VBM changes primarily affect the bilateral parahippocampus and thalamus, aligning with the distribution of tau pathology (Braak and Braak 1991) as well as the anterior default mode network (Andrews‐Hanna et al. 2010). VBP changes primarily affect the bilateral parietal lobes, bilateral temporal gyrus, and posterior cingulate/precuneus—regions targeted by amyloid pathology and aligning with the posterior default mode network. These spatially distinct signatures of structural and functional neurodegeneration in AD/MCI suggest that atrophy and hypometabolism are not interchangeable biomarkers for neurodegeneration.

Diaschesis—a “disconnection process” whereby structural damage (e.g., a stroke) in one brain region leads to hypophysiology (decreased blood flow or metabolism) in a remote, connected region (Feeney and Baron 1986)—has been postulated as the causative mechanism for the spatial dissociation of atrophy and hypophysiology in AD (Matsuda et al. 2002; Villain et al. 2008). Specifically, these authors postulated that atrophy in the hippocampus causes the remote hypometabolism of the posterior cingulate cortex via their connection through the cingulum bundle. In AD, however, hypometabolism precedes atrophy (Jack et al. 2010; Sperling 2011), which argues against the hypothesized causality. Thus, other mechanisms must be considered for these spatially and temporally dissociated processes.

One such mechanism is the difference in oxidative phosphorylation/aerobic glycolysis ratio between the regions. It is well known that oxidative phosphorylation is responsible for most energy production (85%–90%) in the brain (Sokoloff et al. 1977; Raichle et al. 1970; Boyle et al. 1994; Powers et al. 2007). While all parts of the brain predominantly rely on oxidative phosphorylation, elevated aerobic glycolysis in the resting state has been noted in the posterior default mode network—consistent with patterns of amyloid distribution and, consequently, VBP patterns (Vlassenko et al. 2010; Vaishnavi et al. 2010; Goyal et al. 2017). Aerobic glycolysis is also preferentially affected in normal human aging. While oxidative phosphorylation decreases slightly with age (< 10% from age 20–80), aerobic glycolysis decreases significantly (~30%) within the same time frame (Goyal et al. 2017). Decreased aerobic glycolysis has also been shown to be associated with cognitive impairment in amyloid positive individuals (Goyal et al. 2023). Further, amyloid positive individuals without cognitive impairment show preserved, and sometimes even heightened, aerobic glycolysis as compared to younger, healthy individuals (Goyal et al. 2023). Taken together, these studies support further exploration of differences in brain metabolic pathways and alterations of mitochondrial function in areas of VBP change/amyloid deposition.

Spatial Similarity of Hemodynamic Indices With Glucose Hypometabolism: Hypothesis Two

Glucose hypometabolism (CMRglu) is currently measured exclusively by PET, a modality with more limited access than MRI. In hypothesis two, we test whether hemodynamic indices measurable by fMRI—cerebral blood flow (CBF) as measured by ASL fMRI; BOLD fMRI derived measures such as ALFF, fALFF, ReHO—exhibit the same spatial distributions as CMRglu. In AD/MCI, 59.3% of CBF clusters (15,330 mm3) and 68.2% of BOLD clusters (7668mm3) were overlapped with CMRglu. These overlapping clusters persist with different levels of thresholding, suggesting stability of results. Given the co‐localization between hypoperfusion (CBF) and hypometabolism (CMRglu), ASL fMRI could be a more accessible alternative to 18F‐FDG PET. The overlap between BOLD clusters and CMRglu also supports our recent report that ReHo and fALFF are close proxies for CMRglu (Deng et al. 2022). Use of BOLD or blood‐flow based measures as an alternative to 18F‐FDG PET for measurement of hypofunction would significantly lower the barrier to entry for utilization of the A/T/N framework. However, future studies are needed for full validation of these measures as surrogates for FDG‐PET.

Atrophy and Hypometabolic Regions Align With Different Behavioral Domains and Paradigm Classes: Hypothesis Three

Loadings on the behavioral domain and paradigm class were assessed for the interpretation of VBM and VBP alterations. VBM regions loaded most strongly on the behavioral domains of olfaction, fear, and explicit memory. This is supported by paradigm class loadings which show VBM regions loading most strongly on face monitoring/discrimination, olfactory monitoring/discrimination, and affective pictures. Face monitoring/discrimination is a test widely adopted for episodic memory (Preston et al. 2010; Savaskan et al. 2018; Gobbini and Haxby 2007). Olfactory monitoring/discrimination tests the lowest threshold of a stimulus that an individual can perceive as well as the ability to distinguish one smell from another. It is unsurprising that olfaction showed up in both behavioral domain and paradigm class loadings as 85%–90% of patients with AD experience olfactory impairment (Woodward et al. 2017). The affective picture paradigm tests the influence of negative affective states on somatic symptoms experienced (Petzke et al. 2024).

VBP regions loaded most strongly on the behavioral domains of reasoning, social cognition, and explicit memory. Like VBM, VBP behavioral loadings are supported by their paradigm loadings. VBP regions loaded most strongly on the paradigms of finger tapping/button press, theory of mind, and n‐back. Finger tapping/button press corresponds to tests where participants are asked to perform either of those actions in a cued or non‐cued manner. Theory of mind is a core component of social cognition (Pinkham et al. 2014; Healey et al. 2016). N‐back is commonly used to test working memory (Kirchner 1958).

Current clinical assessments of AD predominantly focus on memory, problem solving, daily functioning, executive function, visuospatial skills, and mood. Tests of olfactory and social cognition are not yet commonly present in routine AD neuropsychiatric batteries. However, there has been increased traction in exploring the role of odor identification testing in AD (Igeta et al. 2024; Baek et al. 2020). Measures of social cognition as applied to neurocognitive disorders are only in preliminary testing and there is currently a lack of validated measures (Setién‐Suero et al. 2022; Kessels et al. 2021; Cosentino et al. 2014; Samtani et al. 2023). Based on the results of the present study, we strongly urge the validation and implementation of measures of olfaction and social cognition in the clinical diagnosis and monitoring of AD.

Implications for ATN Framework

The adoption of the A/T/N biomarker framework in 2018 has had a profound impact on the AD community, moving AD from a clinical and radiobiological diagnosis to a purely biological diagnosis. The authors of the A/T/N framework have been transparent from the beginning about the uses and limitations of the framework. As well, they have continually sought to improve upon the initial 2016 framework as new biomarkers emerge or as new information about existing biomarkers becomes available (Jack et al. 2018). This is clear from the 2024 updated A/T/N framework guidelines, which have added considerable nuance and detail to the 2018 framework. The 2024 framework not only includes blood‐based biomarkers, but also recognizes the differences between fluid and imaging biomarkers. As well, it incorporates tau topology as an important staging measure (Jack et al. 2024). As such, we greatly appreciate the flexibility and extensibility of the A/T/N framework and propose two additions based on the results of the present study.

Our proposed changes follow the 2018–2024 trend of increasing detail in biomarker profiling for more accurate diagnosis and monitoring. Specifically, we recommend (1) inclusion of spatial distribution in neurodegeneration imaging biomarkers, and (2) separation of atrophy and hypometabolism into their own subcategories under neurodegeneration.

Inclusion of Spatial Distribution in Neurodegeneration Imaging Biomarkers. The patterns of both structural and functional neurodegeneration are specific to AD. It is a common misconception that because the presence of neurodegeneration is not specific to AD, neurodegeneration as a category cannot be useful in the diagnosis or staging of AD. Clinically, disease imaging signatures are used by neuroradiologists to differentiate AD from other dementias that may present with similar symptoms. Further, both atrophy and hypometabolism patterns are crucial to the diagnosis and management of AD. For example, structural MRI has an 87% accuracy for AD diagnosis (Vemuri et al. 2011). Additionally, 44% of patients from a memory clinic had their diagnosis or management changed based on 18F‐FDG PET results (Elias et al. 2014). Inclusion of spatial distribution of MRI and 18F‐FDG PET imaging biomarkers would allow for neurodegeneration to play a more prominent role in AD diagnosis and staging.

Limitations

This paper presents meta‐analytic results comparing VBM vs. VBP patterns as well as CMRglu VBP vs. CBF VBP vs. BOLD VBP patterns. A potential limitation is the grouping of all three VBP metrics (CMRglu, CBF, BOLD) together in the VBM vs. VBP analysis. We acknowledge that although these three metrics are well spatially co‐localized, variations do exist and are not accounted for in this VBM vs. VBP component of this study. These spatial differences may be explicable by the posterior DMN's tendency for glycolytic metabolism (Vlassenko et al. 2010; Vaishnavi et al. 2010; Goyal et al. 2017). We suggest that these variations may be leveraged in future studies aiming to select the most effective VBP biomarker for staging/subdividing clinical trial subjects with Alzheimer's pathologic change (sole amyloid pathology). Another limitation of this study is analyzing MCI and AD data collectively. This obscures any effects that could result from disease progression. Future studies comparing the extent of VBM and VBP dissociation in MCI and AD may be needed. As this study is a meta‐analysis, we are limited to the data that is available. We largely focused on spatial data (atrophy, hypometabolic changes) of large groups (VBM, all VBP, CMRglu VBP, CBF VBP, BOLD VBP). Future studies utilizing primary data to look at the time course of VBM/VBP changes and associated symptomology/cognitive changes are warranted.

Conclusions

Atrophy and hypometabolism are distinct, regionally specific processes underlying neurodegeneration in AD. The strong dissociation of VBM and VBP in AD/MCI leads us to recommend separation of atrophy (VBM) and hypometabolism (VBP) into separate subcategories of neurodegeneration. There is a need to further explore metabolic pathway‐related pathophysiology as an underlying etiology for the spatial dissociation of atrophy and hypophysiology. Further, we urge exploration and inclusion of ASL fMRI and BOLD‐based fMRI measures as alternative VBP measures beyond 18F‐FDG PET. This would allow for both VBM and VBP to be determined via MRI. Lastly, VBM and VBP patterns represent different cognitive processes and have different implications for clinical progression. Notably, we recommend the development and addition of olfactory and social cognition measures for AD. Overall, further efforts to discriminate between cognitive impacts of VBM and VBP patterns as well as the development of regionally specific biomarkers of atrophy and hypophysiology are warranted.
