From Prediction to Horizon: Clinicians' Negotiations of AI‐Driven Personal Prognoses in Clinical Practice
Authors: Iben Mundbjerg Gjødsbøl, Mette Nordahl Svendsen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTComputational methods and tools under the label ‘artificial intelligence’ (AI) are increasingly promoted as solutions to the challenges of under‐resourced and understaffed healthcare systems, with predictive modelling positioned as a means to improve efficiency and individualise care. Yet little is known about how predictive algorithms are taken up in the everyday practices of clinical decision‐making. Drawing on ethnographic fieldwork with Danish cardiologists working with the CARDIAIHD algorithm, this article examines how AI‐driven personal prognoses intersect with clinical reasoning and organisational temporalities. We show, first, that complex algorithms introduce new forms of interpretive work; second, that their mathematical non‐linear reasoning often conflicts with clinicians' causal and chronological frameworks; and third, that actionable horizons do not simply emerge with a survival prognosis but must be situated within specific times, places and organisational demands. Conceptualising prognostication with AI as horizoning, we argue that algorithmic personal prognoses do not straightforwardly deliver actionable futures, but must be anchored within the temporal and organisational conditions of clinical work to guide decision‐making. Our findings advance sociological debates on the future by demonstrating that in algorithmically informed healthcare, the future is not a pre‐given horizon but a negotiated, relational and socially enacted terrain.Keywords: artificial intelligence, Denmark, ethnography, horizoning, predictive analytics, prognosis

Introduction

In health policy and biomedical research and innovation, computational methods and tools such as machine learning and deep learning, grouped under the term ‘artificial intelligence’ (AI), are increasingly promoted as solutions to challenges faced by under‐resourced and understaffed healthcare systems (Hossein et al. 2025; U.S. Department of Health and Human Services 2025). Investments in the development and application of AI‐based tools in healthcare have thus become a priority for both national and international governmental institutions. The European Commission (2025) depicts AI as ‘not just a tool but as a transformative force reshaping healthcare delivery’, expected to revolutionise healthcare by making it more effective, accessible and economically sustainable. The revolutionary potential of AI rests in its claimed superiority to human capacities in analysing, integrating and processing volumes of data relevant to diagnostic work and clinical decision‐making (Obermeyer and Lee 2017; Topol 2019). Specifically, predictive analytics in healthcare promise to provide more accurate risk predictions than clinicians by detecting what remains hidden from human view and perception (Ali et al. 2023; Dixon et al. 2024).

Although algorithmic risk prediction is not a novel phenomenon in medical practice, with the computational power of AI, risk predictions are no longer calculated as population aggregates attached to groups defined by characteristics such as genetic markers, gender, disease diagnosis or social class. By processing massive amounts and types of patient data (biological, behavioural, environmental and clinical), predictions made with machine learning algorithms promise to become accurate and unique to the individual in ways previously unseen (Chen et al. 2025; Esposito et al. 2024). The ‘personal prognoses’ delivered by predictive algorithms are expected to help health professionals tailor prevention, diagnosis and treatment to individual patients, and thus improve the quality of clinical decision‐making, and make healthcare delivery more efficient and ‘precise’ (Bhardwaj 2022; Davenport and Kalakota 2019; Johnson et al. 2021; Yu et al. 2018). This paper examines how the promises of personalised predictions play out when a predictive AI tool is introduced into everyday clinical practice. Drawing upon ethnographic fieldwork in a Danish cardiology department that implemented a machine learning algorithm, CARDIAIHD, for mortality risk prediction in patients with acute ischaemic heart disease, we investigate how cardiologists made sense of the algorithm's predictions and translated them into meaningful prognoses for patients in clinical decision‐making.

Social science studies of the practical use of complex predictive algorithms are few, challenged by the difficulty of locating, ‘seeing’ and thus studying the empirical object in dispersed sociotechnical systems (Seaver 2017, 2018). The few existing empirical studies that examine the everyday use of novel AI‐driven predictive tools in healthcare demonstrate a significant gap between their promised and their actual efficiency and accuracy when applied in practice (Carboni et al. 2025). Schwennesen (2019) has pointed to the continuous need for ‘repair work’ in rehabilitation supported by AI, whereas studies by Maiers (2017) and Lebovitz et al. (2022) have conceptualised how human experts perform ‘conditioned readings’ and ‘AI interrogation practices’ in attempts to understand and integrate AI‐driven predictions in clinical decision‐making. Moreover, Lebovitz et al. (2022) and Tyskbo and Nygren (2024) have demonstrated that algorithms might increase rather than reduce uncertainty in clinical settings. Inspired by the sociology of expectations (Borup et al. 2006; Brown and Michael 2003), a growing body of scholarship at the intersection of sociology of health and science and technology studies (STS) has directed attention to the role of promissory discourses and imaginaries of digital health, including AI, algorithms and big data, in driving innovation and shaping values, assumptions and commitments (Henwood and Marent 2019; Watson and Wozniak‐O’Connor 2025). However, we know less about how and with what implications the technologies—containing claims about the future and shaping that very future—enter healthcare organisations, their everyday practices and people's lives.

As argued by Henwood and Marent (2019), in their introduction to a special issue on digital health in this journal, what needs further interrogation is the new spatiotemporal dimensions of digital technologies in healthcare. With this term, they refer to the ways digital and data‐intensive tools reconfigure relations between patient histories, anticipated futures and present decision‐making within healthcare practice. With this article, we aim to address this knowledge gap. To do so, we draw inspiration from Adriana Petryna's (2018, 2022) concepts of ‘horizoning’ and ‘horizon work’ as analytical tools for examining how clinicians establish and use temporal reference points from which uncertain futures can be rendered intelligible and actionable. Petryna (2022) recounts how horizons have historically functioned as reference points for navigating complex physical environments and how horizontal lines are used architecturally to situate objects in three‐dimensional space. She develops the concept of ‘horizon work’ to theorise how communities of experts—studying and experimenting with various responses to wildfires and the broader phenomenon of uncontrolled climate change—seek to render an unknown, and ultimately unknowable, future intelligible in the present so that action becomes possible. Whereas Petryna's interlocutors engage in determining critical thresholds and struggle to gain a foothold in ‘stressed and flammable ecological conditions’ (Petryna 2018, 573), the clinicians in our study, among other things, trawl their patients' electronic health records (EHRs) for information about vital values and previous medical events, examine and talk to patients and exchange information during conferences about plans for and outcomes of medical treatments. They do so meticulously to make decisions that will provide their patients with the best possible outlook, thereby—akin to Petryna's interlocutors—‘grasping horizons and bringing them to bear on present operations, where knowledge can be made actionable and not obsolete’ (Petryna 2018, 573).

Analytically, Petryna's conceptual framework allows us to examine how novel predictive tools might introduce new spatiotemporal reference points into clinical practice: Personal prognoses calculated by predictive algorithms can be understood as horizons—points from which clinicians imagine and organise responses to patients' crisis and unfolding clinical situations (Petryna 2022, 3; see also Esposito et al. 2024). In this sense, horizoning affords spelling out the spatiotemporalities of predictive algorithms; not only how they project patients' medical futures in probabilistic terms but also how these projections become guiding devices through which clinicians navigate patient trajectories in time and across clinical spaces. Whereas data‐intensive predictive tools promise to render patients' futures knowable and actionable, our ethnography highlights clinicians' delicate work of emplacing and anchoring prognoses in time and place, and the importance of the temporal logics and spatial organisation of clinical practice for how patients' futures can be meaningfully established and acted upon. Our analysis shows that machine learning algorithms give rise to new forms of interpretive work, in which clinicians retool—including sometimes ignoring—algorithmic predictions to provide meaningful prognoses for patients. By conceptualising prognostication with predictive algorithms as horizoning, we argue that clinicians must navigate new terrains of epistemic uncertainty to ensure that AI‐driven personal prognoses open actionable horizons rather than foreclose them.

Before diving into our ethnography, we present the study background, including the CARDIAIHD algorithm, and our ethnographic methods in more detail.

Study Background

Denmark is often promoted as one of the most digitised and datafied countries in the world. The long tradition of Danish authorities and public institutions collecting data from the entire population, combined with citizens' high level of trust in those very authorities and institutions, means that Denmark is considered an ideal country for developing and introducing new data‐intensive technologies and for driving AI innovation (Danish Life Science Cluster 2026). To leverage novel computational methods for more personalised risk predictions and thus precision medicine in clinical cardiology, in 2019, clinical researchers and data scientists from Denmark, Iceland and Norway embarked on a research project we term Predict‐IHD. This project used the rich Danish health data resources to develop a predictive algorithm, CARDIAIHD, and bring it into clinical use. The algorithm is a neural network‐based time‐to‐event model for predicting all‐cause mortality in ischaemic heart disease. Developed as a risk assessment tool for cardiologists, it predicts the 1‐year all‐cause mortality risk—the survival prognosis—for patients hospitalised with acute ischaemic heart disease.

Predict‐IHD researchers constructed a cohort of nearly 40,000 Danish patients who underwent coronary angiography between 2006 and 2016. Clinical and genetic data for this cohort were drawn from national registries and electronic health records, including diagnostic and procedure codes, laboratory test results, coronary angiographies (x‐rays of the coronaries visualising blood flow to the heart), prescribed medicines and clinical measurements. The final model incorporated up to 584 different features. Upon development, the model was validated in an Icelandic cohort of 8287 patients. Scientific evaluation showed that the algorithm improved accuracy in predicting survival outcomes for patients with acute ischaemic heart disease compared to the GRACE 2.0 risk score, the most recognised tool for estimating all‐cause mortality risk in this patient group (D'Ascenzo et al. 2012).

The hypothesis is that cardiologists who know an individual patient's AI‐calculated survival prognosis will be better informed and thus make better decisions about treatment and follow‐up care. In autumn 2023, the algorithm was integrated into EHR systems at hospitals in Eastern Denmark as part of a clinical randomised controlled trial (RCT). Approximately 10 min after a patient has undergone coronary angiography, the EHR automatically captures patient‐specific data, from which the algorithm calculates the survival prognosis. The EHR also automatically randomises patients to either an intervention group (the algorithm's prognosis is shown to clinicians) or a control group (the algorithm calculates a prognosis, but it remains hidden). The outcomes of this study are whether the clinical use of the algorithm improves 1‐year survival and/or reduces the risk of re‐admission for intervention group patients compared to control group patients. Importantly, the algorithm does not continue to ‘learn’ from new data in real time. Instead, it operates as a static model, meaning that its predictions remain stable throughout the trial. This non‐adaptive design makes it possible to assess the algorithm's impact on patient outcomes within the RCT framework. Whether knowledge of the CARDIAIHD algorithm's predictions improves patient care and outcomes can therefore only be determined once the RCT results are available.

Within the EHR, the algorithm places the patient into one of five risk categories, each visualised by colour: ‘best 1‐year survival compared to the average’ (green); ‘better 1‐year survival than the average’ (light green); ‘average 1‐year survival’ (yellow); ‘slightly lower 1‐year survival than the average’ (orange) and ‘lowest 1‐year survival compared to the average’ (red). In addition to risk categorisation, the algorithm lists up to 10 so‐called ‘explainable factors’ (‘SHAP values’ in machine learning terminology) to help cardiologists understand why the algorithm arrived at a given prediction. Specifically, it presents 5 factors driving the prediction towards death and 5 factors driving it towards survival. These explainable factors are mathematically correlated with, but not causally linked to, survival prognosis. The 10 factors most strongly correlated with mortality vary for each patient, so the listed factors differ between patients.

Neither the prediction nor the explainable factors instruct clinicians on what decisions to make. It is therefore left to the discretion of the cardiologist how, and to what extent, the algorithm's prediction is incorporated into clinical decision‐making. Regardless of the algorithmic output, cardiologists are required to adhere to recommendations for procedures and treatments with well‐documented clinical efficacy, that is, Class I recommendations in national and international guidelines (Eftekhari et al. 2025; Knuuti et al. 2020). However, guidelines also include recommendations for interventions whose effectiveness is less firmly established. Cardiologists may thus consider adjusting such recommendations in light of the algorithm's prognostic assessment. Examples include the extent of follow‐up investigations, the number, type and dosage of medications, length of hospital stay, recommendations for rehabilitation and the frequency and intensity of subsequent follow‐up care. Although the algorithm does not provide explicit treatment directives, its purpose is to generate prognostic estimates that extend beyond the immediate acute risks and prompt cardiologists to reflect on how aggressively and intensively a given patient should be treated, considering their long‐term prognosis. However, because of this non‐directiveness, and because cardiologists choose for themselves whether to use the algorithm, whether its clinical use leads to overtreatment or less intensive treatment of patients with pessimistic prognoses remains an open question.

We now account for the methodologies applied for our ethnographic study. This study was not designed as an integrative component of the clinical trial but as an independent qualitative exploration of how cardiologists experienced and used the algorithm in their everyday clinical work.

Methodologies

Empirically, this article draws upon ethnographic fieldwork aimed at ‘following the algorithm’ (Amelang and Bauer 2019) from its development to its implementation in the clinical setting. Fieldwork was conducted between 2019 and 2024 by both authors, who, as social scientists in the Predict‐IHD project, were responsible for investigating the social, ethical and organisational implications of developing the CARDIAIHD algorithm and moving it into clinical use. Together, we participated in 16 Predict‐IHD research meetings, each lasting between 1 and 1½ h. The first meetings were held in person, but when the COVID‐19 pandemic hit in Spring 2020, meetings switched to an online format. Occupying a dual role as both participants and observers, these meetings provided us with insights into how the development of the algorithm unfolded. In addition to meeting participation, we conducted nine qualitative interviews with researchers involved in the project, each lasting between 1 and 1½ h, and had ongoing exchanges with the Danish research team.

In autumn 2023, the algorithm was integrated into the electronic health record (EHR) system at hospitals in Denmark's Capital Region and Region Zealand, covering almost half of the Danish population of 6 million people. In the weeks preceding the go‐live, the PI of Predict‐IHD arranged a series of presentations at hospitals in Eastern Denmark that treat patients with acute ischaemic heart disease to inform cardiologists about the algorithm and explain how to use it in practice. Each presentation lasted 30–45 min and ended with a 10–15‐min Q&A session and joint discussion in which cardiologists could ask questions. Iben participated in six out of a total of 10 such presentations. Apart from these presentations, to the best of our knowledge, clinicians did not receive any practical training sessions. Each cardiology department received a written description of the project, a pocked‐sized guide for practical use, and a one‐page patient information sheet for distribution on the wards. A hotline and project‐specific email address were also created to address any questions.

As the algorithm entered clinical practice, the Iben conducted ethnographic fieldwork in autumn 2023 and autumn 2024 at a highly specialised hospital in Copenhagen, with additional visits to another Copenhagen hospital and a provincial hospital. Altogether, she conducted approximately 55 h of observation over 8 days, following cardiologists in their daily work of attending to and treating patients with acute ischaemic heart disease. In total, Iben shadowed five different cardiologists, but primarily the cardiologist referred to as ‘Hans’, who worked at the highly specialised hospital. This shadowing took place mainly in a room located centrally in the ward, where cardiologists and nurses reviewed, exchanged and documented information in patients' EHRs. She also followed cardiologists as they conducted rounds, took part in shorter clinical conferences during the day in which complex patients and their treatments were discussed. When sitting next to cardiologists working at the computer, Iben engaged them in informal conversations, asked questions about patients' conditions and the clinical decisions made in response and discussed patient trajectories with them. During ward rounds in patients' room, however, shadowing was purely observational.

The article draws primarily on this clinical fieldwork. Field notes were taken consistently during all fieldwork activities. Analytically, we draw inspiration from Tavory and Timmermans' (2014) abductive approach, characterised by an ongoing interplay between theoretically informed analysis and fieldwork in order to refine our understanding of how algorithmic predictions are integrated and mobilised in clinical practice. Following this approach, over the course of the fieldwork Iben developed a broader analytical sensitivity to how cardiologists enacted different temporalities in their encounters with patients—whether or not a prediction was available—in order to examine how the algorithm's prognosis interacted with already existing ways of practicing time in the treatment of patients with ischaemic heart disease. Mette, who also participated in the Predict‐IHD project and has extensive experience in the social study of medical technologies, contributed to the subsequent analysis and article writing. All names of informants, the Predict‐IHD project and the CARDIAIHD algorithm are pseudonyms to ensure anonymity. The Danish Data Protection Agency approved the ethnographic study of the algorithm. Danish law does not require formal ethical approval for ethnographic studies. OpenAI's ChatGPT (free version) was used solely for final proof‐reading of the manuscript.

Findings

By integrating and processing volumes of historical patient data beyond human cognitive capacities, the CARDIAIHD algorithm is intended to generate probabilistic knowledge about patients' chances of survival, thereby informing cardiologists' decisions in the present. Thus, the algorithm aims to reduce uncertainty about what the future holds and thereby enhance clinicians' ability to act upon the future, ultimately enabling personalised treatment responses and efficient healthcare services. In the following, we examine how clinicians understand and use algorithmic predictions in practice to establish meaningful horizons for their patients. Through empirical examples, we demonstrate that, for different reasons, clinicians struggle to mobilise the algorithm's personal prognoses as meaningful points of reference for responding to patients' current crises. Algorithmic outputs may even increase uncertainty and fail to align with the existing temporal logics that organise and structure clinical work.

Past, Present and Future: Making Sense of Algorithmic Predictions

Algorithms such as CARDIAIHD promise to enable comparisons between an individual patient's disease course and ever‐increasing volumes of data on other patients' disease courses, thereby predicting with unprecedented accuracy what the individual's future may hold. In practice, however, it proves challenging to mobilise the past and connect individual and population in a precise and meaningful prediction. One day in November 2023, Iben was shadowing Hans, a cardiologist working at a highly specialised hospital in Copenhagen. That day, one of Hans' patients was Peter, 1 a man in his mid‐fifties from a provincial town about an hour's drive from Copenhagen. The day before, Peter suffered an acute myocardial infarction. Just before arriving by ambulance at the local hospital, Peter went into cardiac arrest, and the ambulance was therefore redirected towards Copenhagen and a more specialised department. In the ambulance, Peter suffered several additional cardiac arrests and underwent both chest compressions and defibrillation. The resuscitation efforts were successful, and when Peter arrived in Copenhagen, he had a percutaneous coronary intervention (balloon angioplasty) performed, restoring blood flow to the heart. Before seeing Peter, Hans scanned through the many notes in the EHR to familiarise himself with Peter's acute medical course and disease history. An information box appeared saying that a CARDIAIHD prediction was available for Peter. Knowing about Peter's cardiac arrests, Iben was surprised to see that the algorithm classified Peter as having the best 1‐year survival compared to the average. Hans was not surprized, explaining: ‘Well, the algorithm does not know that he [Peter] suffered these cardiac arrests, so therefore, it's talking nonsense [den vrøvler]. I would estimate his survival prognosis as lower than the average.’ In Peter's case, the algorithm lacked critical input data from his prehospital events, meaning that it calculated an overly optimistic survival prognosis.

A cardiologist at another hospital on the outskirts of Copenhagen told Iben a similar story about a patient from Romania who was admitted in cardiogenic shock and thus life‐threateningly ill. Because this patient had no prior medical history in the Danish healthcare system, the algorithm only had very few data points to process and calculate its prediction. As in Peter's case, the algorithm classified the critically ill patient as having the best survival prognosis compared to the average. The cardiologists explained: ‘It takes only a few such errors before the algorithm loses credibility among us cardiologists, and we stop using it. We lose faith in it.’ One of the purported strengths of the CARDIAIHD algorithm is that it can operate despite lacking data points, which it then imputes to carry out its calculation. However, for the algorithm to predict precisely, clinicians and the healthcare system more broadly must ensure that all relevant parameters of a given patient are registered and available for the algorithm. In practice, this is a challenge. When the algorithm lacks critical input data, clinicians experience a stark contrast between the algorithm's predictions and the clinical reality of their patients.

According to Petryna, horizons expose ‘differences between meaningful and baseless projections’, stating that meaningful horizons are characterised by their ‘ability to retool complexity, making it navigable’ (Petryna 2022, 48). When clinicians experience discrepancies between the algorithm's prediction and their own assessment of the patient's condition and prognosis, they cannot use the algorithmic output to create a meaningful horizon that guides medical decisions in the present. In the cases of Peter and the patient from Romania, rather than retooling complexity, algorithmic outputs generated misalignments and ambiguities between the clinical state of patients and the algorithmic prediction, thus introducing new forms of interpretive work for Hans and his colleagues: When the algorithm's prognosis conflicted with their own, clinicians had to recognise what the algorithm did not ‘know’ or take into account in its calculation in order to judge the accuracy and thus the clinical value of its prediction. Just as the algorithm imputes missing data points to make its statistical calculations, so too must clinicians be able to identify and ‘impute’ the missing data points that the algorithm lacks for a given prediction and evaluate the significance of their absence. In other words, to understand the algorithmic output, clinicians must engage in interpretive work to align the patient's past, present and future in establishing a horizon that informs their decision‐making. However, due to the busy and demanding nature of clinical work, the cardiologists often ended up ignoring algorithmic outputs that did not spontaneously make sense or fit with their clinical judgements. With his comment about the CARDIAIHD algorithm ‘talking nonsense’, Hans treated the algorithmic horizon as a baseless forecast of the future.

Confused Chronologies: Misinterpretations of Correlation and Causality

All the cardiologists Iben met and spoke with during fieldwork expressed positive expectations for using AI in their clinical work, and they were enthusiastic about the implementation of the CARDIAIHD algorithm. They welcomed data‐driven and thus more ‘objective’ assessments of their patients' futures. Predictive algorithms such as CARDIAIHD identify statistical correlations in data, meaning that the explainable factors listed as driving the prediction towards either death or survival represent variables that are statistically correlated with the calculated survival prognosis—not causal relationships between the listed factors and survival outcomes (see e.g. Amoore and Piotukh 2016; Varga et al. 2020). In practice, translating such a statistical model into clinical use proved challenging.

On one of the last days of November 2023, Iben followed Hans as he conducted rounds and attended to an 84‐year‐old woman, Jenny. In the EHR, Hans learnt that Jenny had been admitted with a large myocardial infarction. She had undergone balloon angioplasty with stent placement, that is, insertion of a tiny metal tube in the affected artery to ensure blood flow. Although the treatment was lifesaving, the damage to Jenny's heart muscle meant that she should initiate lifelong heart failure treatment. While browsing previous journal notes and laboratory results, Hans noted that a CARDIAIHD prediction was available. The algorithm categorised Jenny as having a ‘slightly lower 1‐year survival than average’. Given her significant cardiac event, Hans agreed with the algorithm's prognosis. Interrogating the explainable factors, he noted that Jenny's age and ischaemic heart disease were listed as driving the prediction towards death, whereas her sex and normal levels of P‐creatinine, indicating normal kidney function, were listed as factors driving it towards survival. However, Hans was puzzled that the algorithm also listed ‘P‐Glucose is normal’ as a factor driving the prediction towards death, saying: ‘I think it will hinder the clinical uptake of the algorithm when it claims that a normal value is a disadvantage.’

Two weeks later, a similar situation arose as Hans attended to a 72‐year‐old man, Hassan, whose journey towards acute ischaemic heart disease had begun several days earlier. At home, Hassan had experienced chest pain for 3–4 days, during which time his family repeatedly contacted both the on‐call physician and his general practitioner. The doctors initially attributed Hassan's pain to musculoskeletal causes. The day before admission, however, the symptoms worsened, radiating towards the left shoulder. He was admitted to his local hospital and subsequently transferred to the specialised Copenhagen department after his electrocardiogram indicated myocardial infarction. Hassan was diagnosed with an acute myocardial infarction in the right coronary artery. An emergency angioplasty was performed, but despite removal of the thrombus, cardiac output remained poor post‐procedure. Hans agreed with the CARDIAIHD algorithm, which classified Hassan as having an ‘average 1‐year survival’. Yet, when interrogating the explainable factors, Hans noted that the algorithm listed variables related to Hassan's previous prostate cancer diagnosis as driving the prediction towards both death and survival. Even though Hans recognised that these correlations made sense statistically (he could make qualified guesses, though not explain the relationship due to the model's complexity), they came across as counterintuitive from a clinical perspective—implying, paradoxically, that prostate cancer was protective. Hans worried that ‘explanations’ like these could lead his colleagues to disregard the algorithm altogether, since clinicians search for causal relationships and are inclined to reject non‐causal correlations they cannot understand or that conflict with their worldview.

In the EHR, it is clearly stated under the list of explainable factors: ‘The explanatory factors cannot be considered as causal, but as correlations, which have been identified in a large patient group with IHD. Therefore, there may be risk factors that seem illogical’. Even so, Hans was not the only one to highlight the difficulty of interpreting these factors. During fieldwork at the outer Copenhagen hospital in autumn 2024, the cardiologist who had recounted his experience with the Romanian patient also explained: ‘The “explainable factors” are too difficult to interpret and apply. They might make sense statistically, but clinically, they don't’. Because the CARDIAIHD algorithm is a risk assessment tool, its purpose is not to guide intervention on specific risk factors but to provide a more precise estimate of the patient's overall mortality risk. However, clinicians are focused on identifying interventions that can improve a patient's condition, meaning that algorithmic information they cannot readily understand or act upon is experienced as irrelevant or even as ‘noise’. Although clinicians are explicitly notified that the explainable factors represent statistical correlations rather than causal relationships, those without detailed knowledge of the algorithm easily interpret them as causal determinants of survival.

Drawing on the concept of the ‘Freudian slip’, we propose that the practical use of complex predictive models such as CARDIAIHD might imply what we term a ‘causality slip’. Where the Freudian slip traditionally denotes unconscious thoughts or desires unintentionally revealed in speech, we use ‘causality slip’ to foreground the unintended tendency to read causality into correlational patterns. The term captures the slippage between levels of inference—from statistical association to causal explanation—and the structural ambiguity of many predictive models, where the boundary between correlation and causality is not clearly delineated. It surfaces the human often unconscious epistemological drift from detecting associations to inferring causal dynamics, highlighting how easily correlation is reinterpreted as causation, especially in clinical contexts marked by the pressure for actionable results (Gjødsbøl 2026; see also Varga et al. 2020).

The severity of this problem in everyday practice came as a surprise to Hans and the clinical team behind the algorithm. In a meeting held before implementation to which Iben was also invited to participate, a handful of cardiologists gathered to discuss how to present the algorithmic output in the EHR. Some suggested that only the quantitative risk estimate be presented, whereas others suggested displaying only the explainable factors that were modifiable, thus tying the risk estimate more closely to clinical action. However, because that model would filter out age, which is one of the strongest predictive factors for risk of death, it was decided not to select features for display. This decision was made out of the concern that if cardiologists were unable to understand the reasoning behind the prediction, they would end up not trusting it. However, our ethnography shows that the ideal of transparency and explainability might come at a cost, because clinicians tend to reject statistical relationships that may not be causal, and which they therefore do not understand or consider logical, thus paradoxically hindering clinicians' trust in the algorithm.

Our notion of ‘causality slip’ directs attention to temporal orderings, and exposes the epistemological tensions at play in predictive modelling when the imperative to act based on previous events converges with uncertain or non‐linear phenomena: Although the prowess of predictive algorithms lies in their ability to connect a patient's past, present and future with precision based on large‐scale historical data, their mathematical reasoning simultaneously disrupts chronological logics, reshuffling the way humans experience, categorise and practice time (Mattingly 1994; Ricoeur 1984). Paradoxically, even if the algorithm reinforces a linear chronology of past–present–future, it simultaneously disrupts the logic of chronological time. Although chronology and causality are distinct concepts, causality implies linear ordering with one event (the cause) preceding another (the effect). This means that, regardless of the potential accuracy of the algorithm's prediction (it has been shown to outperform state‐of‐the‐art models when having the necessary data available), when faced with non‐causal correlations in data, clinicians may struggle to construct meaningful and actionable horizons from its prognoses. When the algorithm's correlational and thus temporally non‐linear reasoning creates confusion about cause and effect and past and future, the clinicians in our study did not trust the algorithm but questioned the very baseline on which it projects the future.

Temporalities of the Organisation: Moving Patients in Time and Space

Conducting fieldwork among cardiologists taught Iben that the CARDIAIHD algorithm's prognosis constitutes only one of multiple futures enacted in everyday care for patients with acute ischaemic heart disease. One autumn day in 2023, she shadowed Hans, who, as mentioned previously, works at a highly specialised hospital in Copenhagen. On this day, Hans was seeing a 65‐year‐old man, James, who had been admitted the day before with increasing chest pain. The clinical examination indicated that James had suffered a myocardial infarction some days earlier, yet by the time of admission, the acute window had closed. Now, James' chest pain had decreased, and he was waiting for either a balloon angioplasty or a bypass surgery scheduled for the following day. During rounds, Hans first asked James to describe his experience of the cardiac event. He then told him: ‘The important thing is that your condition is stable (…). You're scheduled for surgery tomorrow. But if you experience chest pain beforehand, we need to know immediately, because in that case, the procedure will be performed as an emergency (…) For now, we'll take it one day at a time. Our goal is to get you through our part of the treatment alive. Things look promising, but it is a serious condition. If the procedure goes well, we'll transfer you to your home hospital shortly afterwards.’

In this short excerpt of their conversation, several futures with associated risks are at play. The first horizon Hans establishes for James is limited to the next 24 h. Hans must ensure that James' condition does not worsen or that he does not die from his heart disease before the scheduled procedure. By saying ‘We'll take it one day at a time,’ Hans emphasises the importance of managing the immediate risks in the critical hours after a heart attack and complex procedures such as bypass surgery. The next horizon Hans enacts involves the days after the operation, when James is at risk for new blood clots or disruptions in the heart's electrical system, potentially leading to cardiac arrest. By saying ‘We need to get you through our part of the treatment alive’, Hans highlights that prognosis for heart patients is not a fixed or static concept. In practice, James' immediate future is enacted as highly unpredictable and changeable, potentially swinging between life and death within a short timeframe.

In contrast, the CARDIAIHD algorithm offers a third horizon: A prediction of the patients' chances of survival in a 1‐year timeframe. The algorithm's prognosis aims to refine and nuance the clinician's risk assessment in complex situations by locating the patient within the historical dataset on which the model was trained and comparing the individual's chances of survival to those of similar patients. In other words, the algorithm provides clinicians with a longer‐term assessment of overall mortality risk—one that they are expected to integrate with more immediate horizons and risks. James' case illustrates how clinicians never operate within a single temporal frame but instead work with multiple overlapping timeframes that structure how prognosis is understood, communicated and acted upon. In our case, clinicians tended to focus on near‐term risks, as these are more likely to change dramatically in the acute phase. As a result, the CARDIAIHD algorithm's long‐term risk assessment was often deprioritised in favour of managing the immediate risks.

In her seminal book And a Time to Die: How American Hospitals Shape the End of Life, anthropologist Sharon Kaufman (2006) demonstrates how care for terminally ill patients is organised into ‘pathways,’ and thereby how health professionals' experiences and management of time shape the end of life. Kaufman (2006) argues that hospitals' institutional demands for clinical and economic efficiency make timing decisions and procedures a central concern for healthcare staff: ‘Time is the marker for things health professionals think should happen and for things that must get done, and it weighs heavily on everyone who works in a hospital’ (Kaufman 2006, 7). Like Kaufman (2006), Iben noted clinicians' intense preoccupation with moving patients along in both time and space. A significant task for cardiologists is to conduct rounds as efficiently as possible to discharge the most stable patients to their local hospitals. Hans emphasised the importance of patient flow, saying: ‘Some patients are waiting to be admitted here, but this cannot happen until others are discharged’. Through Kaufman's ethnography, we see that the prognosis for critically ill heart patients cannot be separated from the temporal logics and practices of time that define the organisation of clinical work and the hospital as an institution. For Hans, working at a highly specialised hospital, the priority is to ensure patients such as James survive the first few days after surgery, so they can be transferred to their local hospital, thus making room for new critically ill patients. The clinical handling of James' 1‐year survival prognosis is, in essence, embedded in other, more local institutions. Paradoxically, meant as a point of reference to improve clinicians' decision‐making in the face of an uncertain future, the algorithm's long‐term and static horizon is easily overruled by the short‐term and continuously shifting horizons engendered by the organisation of clinical work that requires clinicians to move patients in time and space. Institutional pressures for efficiency and patient flow thus not only limit clinicians' engagement with the algorithm but also highlight that long‐term predictive knowledge remains secondary to the immediate actionable realities of clinical care.

At the local hospitals, we also found that the temporal situatedness of the algorithmic prediction often did not align well with clinicians' needs. A cardiologist working at the Outer Copenhagen hospital was very enthusiastic about the CARDIAIHD algorithm and its possibilities for real‐time data use and integration. He nevertheless found it difficult to apply its predictions, because they are calculated before critical interventions such as balloon angioplasty are performed. Given that procedures such as bypass surgery and angioplasty carry considerable risks, he explained that patients' recovery from these interventions is decisive for their prognosis. From his standpoint in post‐surgical care, the algorithm's prognosis became outdated within hours or days as new events unfolded. Similarly, a cardiologist at a provincial hospital welcomed the idea of the algorithm, saying: ‘The idea is excellent—I wish we had more of such AI‐driven tools. However, I believe the prediction ought to be made before the coronary angiography is performed, as this could help determine whether the patient would benefit from the procedure and what the expected value would be for the individual patient.’ Several cardiologists expressed a wish for more dynamic predictive models that continuously incorporate new events and update the prognosis accordingly. Legally, however, such models are difficult to implement, as every new adaptation of the model is considered a new medical device requiring renewed approval (Gilbert et al. 2021; Onitiu et al. 2024). Yet clinicians' wishes for more adaptive tools reflect their different and shifting needs for when survival prognoses are calculated and whether they contribute to actionable horizoning in decision‐making. For an algorithmic prognosis to be meaningful, it must be situated spatially, temporally and institutionally in relation to the physician who is expected to act upon it.

Discussion

In this article, we have demonstrated that while AI‐driven tools promise more accurate predictions of patients' risk of future events, such as death, clinicians struggle to integrate a complex algorithm's survival prognosis as clinically meaningful knowledge when establishing actionable horizons for their patients. This discussion unpacks three temporal aspects of the CARDIAIHD algorithm that, taken together, challenge cardiologists' horizoning with predictive AI: (1) the incomplete information the algorithm sometimes has about patients' pasts; (2) the tension between the algorithm's statistical correlations and the chronological timeframe of past, present and future and (3) clinicians' difficulty in aligning algorithmic prognoses with organisational demands to move patients through time and space.

In sociology, the future is not conceived as a blank slate distinct from the present. Rather, sociologists have scrutinised how futures are socially, politically and materially constituted in the present (e.g., Adam and Groves 2007; Adams et al. 2009; Jasanoff 2016; Koselleck 2004; Tutton 2022). The sociology of expectations has highlighted the role of future‐oriented expectations in driving technological development and innovation (e.g., Borup et al. 2006; Brown and Michael 2003). In contemporary societies, computational techniques and tools labelled ‘AI’ constitute future‐engaging instruments surrounded by immense expectations. Whereas previous sociological work has examined how futures are constituted in the present and how imaginaries and expectations about the future shape the present, we know less about how the futures predicted—and thus actualised—by AI‐driven tools are experienced and acted upon in the present. This study begins to fill that gap. With Petryna's concept of horizoning, our study contributes to sociological scholarship on the future by emphasising how predictive technologies, employed in particular circumstances, place people in a socio‐temporal–material horizon while human actors simultaneously push or shape that horizon. Petryna (2018, 2022) has developed ‘horizon work’ to theorise human responses in situations in which projections about climate behaviour and climate change collapse. In employing this concept, we might risk foregrounding the novelty of algorithmic predictions and their implications for clinical practice while backgrounding the stability and continuity of this practice, which is to a large extent defined by ‘business as usual’ and standard‐of‐care. Nevertheless, horizon work provides a conceptual lens for examining the practices through which patients' AI‐generated futures are made—or not made—present, meaningful and actionable in clinical situations marked by critical illness and uncertainty.

First, our analysis shows that although the mobilisation of extensive Danish health data theoretically enables personalised predictions, data‐intensive algorithms such as CARDIAIHD are fragile infrastructures that may lack access to all necessary data. To use algorithmic output to establish a meaningful horizon for patients, clinicians must engage in ‘interrogation practices’ (Lebovitz et al. 2022) to determine what aspects of the past the algorithm incorporates and what may be missing. This challenge stems from the black‐box nature of algorithms, where users rarely see or understand how input becomes output (Gusterson 2019; Mittelstadt et al. 2016; Pasquale 2015). The cardiologists in our study were able to identify inaccurate prognoses in the cases of Peter and the Romanian patient; however, reliability may be harder to assess in less extreme situations. Such interrogative work required of clinicians stands in contrast to promises that predictive AI would relieve or substitute clinicians in complex interpretive tasks. Moreover, the empirical example with the patient from Romania, specifically, illustrates the challenge of algorithmic bias in healthcare (see, e.g., Ebeling 2022; Gusterson 2019; Prainsack and Van Hoywegen 2020). Algorithms are never better than the data on which they are trained and the data available to them (Haug and Drazen 2023). The CARDIAIHD algorithm was developed using health data from Danish heart patients, and therefore it performs best for patients whose characteristics match this population. As social science researchers have shown, algorithms are neither ‘objective’ nor capable of eliminating bias; rather, their increasing use may reinforce the existing biases that permeate the data on which they are developed and trained (Green and Svendsen 2021; Panch et al. 2019).

Second, our ethnography suggests that although AI‐driven predictive modelling enables the integration of data beyond human capacity, these tools also expose a temporal tension between mathematical reasoning and human reasoning. ‘Explainable AI’ has emerged as a proposed solution to the black‐box problem addressed above, aiming to enhance transparency and provide users with explanations for the relation between input and output (Arrieta et al. 2020; Mittelstadt et al. 2019). The explainable factors offered by CARDIAIHD are intended to support cardiologists' understanding of its predictions, yet as our analysis shows, transparency comes at a cost. Clinicians may read causality into explainable factors that are only mathematically correlated, not causally linked, to the survival prognosis. Such illogical explainable factors may unsettle clinicians' default horizoning, amplifying rather than reducing uncertainty about what decisions to enact. Consequently, many clinicians disregard the algorithm. Our ethnographic study thus demonstrates that the clinical use of data‐driven predictive tools might be challenged by not only missing—and thus too little—data (as in the case of Peter and the patient from Romania) but also too much data and information which might create confusion (as in the case of Jenny and Hassan). Both a lack and an overload of information about patients' medical past may thus lead to epistemic and actionable uncertainty (Tyskbo and Nygren 2024), pointing to the importance of careful curation of features with clinical relevance in the development of predictive tools (Amann et al. 2020).

Finally, our ethnography underscores the importance of the temporal and spatial organisation of clinical work for how patients' futures are established and acted upon. ‘Prognosis’ is not reducible to a statistical calculation; it constitutes a continuous and evolving practice of future‐making, established in the social interaction between doctor and patient within a specific institutional framework (Christakis 2001; Jain 2007). Prognosis, in other words, is always situated in both time, place and social relations, and the prognosis of the individual patient is deeply intertwined with how time is practiced and experienced within a given clinical context. In Death Foretold: Prophecy and Prognosis in Medical Care, physician and sociologist Nicholas Christakis (2001) examines how doctors understand prognosis and experience the act of prognosticating. He shows that physicians are often reluctant to foretell their patients' futures—especially death—and that prognostication entails profound ethical dilemmas, as many physicians fear that articulating a pessimistic prognosis to patients might hasten death itself. Christakis (2001) argues that, despite the inevitable uncertainty surrounding the foretelling of death, physicians have a moral obligation to communicate their best judgement about patients' future because they and their families rely on this knowledge to make the most of remaining time or plan for the end of life. He therefore welcomes data‐driven tools to aid clinicians' horizoning. Our findings highlight, however, that although predictive technologies promise improved decision‐making, they are introduced into clinical practices and organisational logics that prioritise efficiency through patient flow. This movement of patients in time and space heavily structures what decisions are made, where and by whom. Thus, however precise predictive tools become, their value for clinicians in establishing temporal horizons—and enacting decisions accordingly—depends on close alignment with the organisational context in which clinicians operate, as well as the temporal frameworks that define their clinical work.

Taken together, we suggest that cardiologists' responses to the algorithm's three temporal challenges—gaps in historical and real‐time data, misinterpretation of correlation and causality, and misalignment with the spatiotemporal organisation of clinical work—point to a new form of horizon work. Petryna (2018, 47–48) writes about her interlocutors: ‘…when data is no longer useful, and prediction capability derived from past or present information becomes misleading (or yields high computational cost or instability), a new predictive horizon must be put into place’. Similarly, when algorithmic projections fail or do not make sense, clinicians are tasked with finding ‘other meaningful points of reference from which to imagine how to organise a response’ (Petryna 2018, 3). In a future healthcare system operating through predictive modelling, clinicians must themselves become way‐finding tools, establishing actionable horizons for their patients amidst epistemic uncertainty and organisational demands. Such horizoning entails knowing what data are lacking, distinguishing correlation from causality and managing—and sometimes ignoring—different and layered prognoses. Importantly, our ethnography highlights that horizoning with algorithms turns ignorance of inaccurate and unreliable prognoses into an important clinical task. Although such ignorance may be clinically justifiable, it raises questions of accountability in complex human–algorithm relations, particularly when algorithmic predictions fail (Mittelstadt et al. 2016). In medical settings dominated by claims of AI objectivity, clinicians must navigate belief in data‐driven accuracy while critically challenging algorithmic outputs and deciding when to rely on their own judgement. Horizoning in algorithm‐informed healthcare thus denotes the complex navigational labour through which clinicians exercise situated judgement, orienting simultaneously towards imagined patient trajectories, algorithmic output and the organisational frameworks that structure clinical practice.

Conclusion

Artificial intelligence (AI) is widely promoted as a remedy for under‐resourced healthcare systems, with predictive modelling as one key application. Predictive algorithms promise more accurate forecasts of future events, expected to individualise care, enhance decision‐making and increase efficiency. Set against these promises, our ethnography demonstrates that AI‐driven personal prognoses do not necessarily generate certainty about an individual patient's future or simplify clinical decision‐making. Through the case of the CARDIAIHD algorithm, we have identified three key points: (1) Complex algorithms might introduce new forms of interpretive work and uncertainty for clinicians; (2) they can be difficult for clinicians to understand and use as intended when mathematical non‐linear reasoning conflicts with chronological time and clinical reasoning grounded in causal rather than correlational patterns; and (3) an actionable horizon does not simply arrive with a survival prognosis. Although complex, data‐intensive algorithms may predict outcomes with greater precision, their infrastructural frailty and the risk of information overload on clinicians should be considered when applied in practice.

Theoretically, our study advances debates in the sociology of the future and the sociology of expectations. Whereas prior work has shown how claims about the future are preformative at societal and technological levels—mobilising investment, spurring innovation and shaping expectations—we demonstrate that in everyday clinical practice, AI‐driven prognoses generate epistemic uncertainty and temporal tension, and must be carefully aligned with existing organisational structures of time to become clinically meaningful. For a prediction—a claim to the future—to function as a useful input into clinical decision‐making, clinicians must interpret the estimate, understand its rationale and anchor it in the specific temporal, spatial and social context of patient care. Conceptualising prognostication with AI as horizoning, our study highlights the epistemic labour required of clinicians to align AI‐generated predictions with patient realities, temporal logics and organisational imperatives, showing that the future is not a pre‐given horizon but a negotiated, relational and socially enacted terrain. In doing so, our study demonstrates that predictive analytics does not merely make futures knowable; instead, it reshapes the temporal, social and organisational conditions under which futures are interpreted and acted upon. Although AI‐driven tools may calculate probabilities of future outcomes, translating these forecasts into meaningful and actionable horizons that can guide clinical decisions remains a complex, social and irreducibly human task. Echoing Petryna (2022), we therefore end by advocating for the centrality of ‘human responsiveness’. In algorithmically informed healthcare, such responsiveness lies in rendering predictive horizons meaningful by anchoring them within the social, temporal and organisational situations in which clinicians act and decide for their patients.
