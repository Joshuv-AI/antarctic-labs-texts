Superior Capsule Reconstruction Augmentation for Massive Rotator Cuff Repair Using the Long Head of the Biceps Tendon
Authors: Roman Steika, Joseph Choi, Abdullah Foad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractManaging massive rotator cuff tears in patients with minimal arthropathy who maintain an active lifestyle remains a significant challenge for orthopaedic surgeons. There is still uncertainty regarding which joint-preserving surgical techniques offer the best clinical and functional outcomes while minimizing the risk of retears. For some time, superior capsular repair has been used to treat massive rotator cuff tears through the ability to restore glenohumeral stability and function, decrease superior capsular distance, and provide biologic augmentation. The use of the long head of the biceps tendon as augmentation for irreparable rotator cuff tears provides support while reducing concerns associated with allograft viability, rejection, infection, and costs. In this Technical Note, we present our preferred surgical technique for superior capsular repair with long head of the biceps tendon, with successful recovery of range of motion, strength, and function of the shoulder at a minimum of 2 years postoperatively.

Often, massive rotator cuffs will be deemed irreparable either preoperatively or intraoperatively based on the characteristics of the tear and the quality of the rotator cuff tendons. Rotator cuff tendons that are significantly retracted, contracted, atrophied, or all of the above may restrict mobilization and eliminate the possibility of reattachment to the footprint. Preoperative magnetic resonance imaging is often used to characterize cuff injury and can usually identify either massive or irreparable tears. Magnetic resonance imaging findings that have been reported to be characteristic of irreparable rotator cuff tears include Goutallier grade 3 to 4 fatty atrophy of the affected muscle, superior migration of the humeral head with an acromial humeral interval of <6 mm, evidence of pseudoparalysis, and large U-shaped tears that do not appear mobile.1, 2, 3, 4, 5

Surgical planning for known massive rotator cuff tears deemed irreparable includes interposition grafts and graft augmentation, partial repair, balloon spacers, tendon transfers, reverse total shoulder arthroplasty, and superior capsule reconstruction.6 The surgical technique described in this Technical Note offers a straightforward option for these large tears using local biology and decreasing costs or morbidities associated with other superior capsular repair (SCR) options.

After a thorough evaluation of the shoulder is performed and consent obtained, the patient is taken to the operating room to undergo induction of general anesthesia by the anesthesia service.

The patient is placed in the beach-chair position with all bony prominences well padded. Standard universal timeout is performed.

Standard portal incisions are made, and a systematic inspection of the glenohumeral joint is undertaken. The subscapularis tendon is addressed first and repaired arthroscopically in an anterograde fashion using one 5.5-mm double-loaded Arthrex Biotenodesis anchor and one 4.75-mm Arthrex SwiveLock anchor (Fig 1, Fig 2).

Arthroscopic view of the right glenohumeral joint from the posterior portal identifying a tear in the subscapularis tendon.

Arthroscopic view of the right glenohumeral joint through the posterior portal showing a completed repair of the subscapularis tendon.

Attention is then taken to the subacromial space for extensive bursectomy, lysis of adhesions, and evaluation of the rotator cuff tear (Fig 3).

Arthroscopic view of the right subacromial space from the posterior portal identifying a tear in the supraspinatus and infraspinatus tendons without adequate mobilization.

Next, a healthy, bleeding bone bed on the greater tuberosity is created (Fig 4). The edges of the supraspinatus and infraspinatus tendons are debrided to healthier tissue edges.

Arthroscopic view of the right subacromial space from the posterior portal showing debridement of the rotator cuff footprint on the humeral head.

An anterior interval slide in continuity (as described by Lo and Burkhart7) is performed to release attachments restricting the mobilization of the rotator cuff (Fig 5).

Arthroscopic view of the right subacromial space from the posterior portal showing anterior slide procedure with release of attachments using electrocautery.

The LHBT is used as an autologous graft to create a superior capsular reconstruction. Three lasso loops, using FiberLink sutures (Arthrex), are passed with a suture passer into the LHBT just proximal to where the tendon enters the bicipital groove (Fig 6). The remaining distal portion of the bicep tendon is tenotomized. This allows the bicep tendon to swing over and become anchored onto the greater tuberosity footprint with the shoulder in approximately 30° of forward flexion and 30° of abduction (Fig 7). The FiberLink sutures are strategically pulled to the center of the greater tuberosity footprint to help decide where the tendon will sit best. A 5.5-mm biocomposite Arthrex SwiveLock anchor is used to attach the biceps tendon to the humerus (Fig 8).

Arthroscopic view of the right subacromial space from the posterior portal showing a tenotomized biceps tendon with 3 Arthrex suture FiberLinks placed.

Illustration of the sling effect from the biceps tendon. Left side of the image shows fixation at 0° of abduction. Right side of the image shows fixation at 30° of abduction.

Arthroscopic view of the right subacromial space from the posterior portal showing fixation of the biceps tendon to the humerus using a 5.5-mm biocomposite SwiveLock anchor (Arthrex).

If there is a SLAP lesion, this can be stabilized on the glenoid side by placing two 2.9-mm biocomposite PushLock anchors (Arthrex) on the superior glenoid tubercle. One anchor and suture construct is placed anterior to the biceps-labral root, and one is placed posterior to the biceps-labral root, to obtain a stout glenoid fixation.

Next, repair of the infraspinatus and supraspinatus tendons onto the greater tuberosity footprint is performed with anchors (Fig 9). Side-to-side repair to the anterior and/or posterior border of the LHBT to the rotator cuff repair (RCR) may be done. By performing this single-row RCR with LHBT SCR augmentation, full coverage of the humeral head can be achieved without placing the cuff repair under supraphysiologic tension (Fig 9, Fig 10).

Arthroscopic view of the right subacromial space from the lateral portal showing final repair of the rotator cuff with two 5.5-mm SwiveLock anchors (Arthrex) and the biceps superior capsular repair.

Arthroscopic view of the right glenohumeral joint from the posterior portal showing final repair of the rotator cuff with biceps superior capsular repair.

Discussion

SCR was first described by Mihata et al.8 in 2013 for irreparable rotator cuff tears. The original strategy involved a tensor fascia lata (TFL) autograft that was anchored medially at the superior glenoid as well as laterally at the cuff footprint on the greater tuberosity. Supraspinatus, infraspinatus, or subscapularis tendons were then attached to the autograft via the suture technique. Mihata et al. reported excellent results, with improvement in active elevation postoperatively, as well as functional scores comparable to those of successful direct rotator cuff repairs. By 3 years, the retear rate was reported at only 16.7%.6 Further studies have identified alternative substrates for reconstruction, such as dermal autografts, with equally successful results.9 Even with the improvements in range of motion and function status following SCR, Greiner et al.10 were unable to find significant differences between the irreparable rotator cuff cohorts treated with SCR or partial repair alone.

More recently, the LHBT has been suggested as a local autograft available for SCR, while avoiding the morbidity associated with harvesting the TFL graft.11,12 A biomechanical study by Berthold et al.12 evaluated the kinematics of SCR with LHBT and its ability to reduce glenohumeral superior translation, with the conclusion that LHBT would be a cost-effective, time-saving, and technically feasible method for SCR, with favorable outcomes and the ability to restore glenohumeral joint mechanics.

A study by Kocaoglu et al.11 comparing the functional results and efficacy of irreparable cuff tears treated with LHBT SCR or TFL SCR found significantly improved functional scores in both groups with comparable outcomes. Additionally, Kawashima et al.13 have reported comparable clinical outcomes and improvement in the postoperative acromial humeral interval when compared to arthroscopic partial repair for irreparable cuff tears.

There are notable advantages and disadvantages to performing an SCR augmentation using the LHBT (Table 1, Table 2). The technique leverages the local availability and native fixation of the LHBT, eliminating the need for costly allografts or separate autograft harvest sites, which reduces surgical time and donor site morbidity. Additionally, using the biceps tendon in continuity or following tenodesis provides a biologic scaffold that helps restore superior glenohumeral stability, depress the humeral head, and enhance healing when combined with a partial or complete cuff repair. However, careful patient selection and surgical technique are critical. The quality of the bicep tendon may be compromised in chronic or elderly patients, and in some cases, it may be absent due to prior rupture or tenotomy. Furthermore, the limited length of the tendon can be a constraint in bridging larger defects, and improper tension may fail to restore superior restraint or lead to complications, such as anterior shoulder pain or cosmetic deformity. SLAP lesions that are not identified or stabilized may cause persistent pain if incorporated into an complete or partial RCR.
