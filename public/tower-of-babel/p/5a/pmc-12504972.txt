Sonication-assisted emulsification: Analyzing different polymers in aqueous systems for microparticle preparation by the double emulsion technique
Authors: Muhaimin Muhaimin, Anis Yohana Chaerunisaa, Roland Bodmeier
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSonication-assisted emulsification has emerged as a powerful technique for the preparation of microparticles in various fields, including pharmaceuticals, cosmetics, and food science. This study aims to investigate the impact of different polymers in an aqueous system on the preparation of microparticles by the double emulsion technique. By understanding the factors that affect emulsification and stability, we can optimize the production of microparticles with desired characteristics. This study discusses the mechanism behind sonication-assisted emulsification, the various polymers used, and the analysis of particle size, morphology, and stability. The microparticle were prepared with a water-in-oil-in-water (W/O/W) solvent evaporation method, for various polymers (including EC 4 cp, Eudragit® RS 100, Eudragit® RL 100, PLGA (RG503H) and PCL) that solvent used dichloromethane. The particle size/distribution of the emulsion droplets/hardened microparticles was monitored using FBRM. The morphology of polymeric microparticles was characterized using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). The transformation of the emulsion droplets into solid microparticles occured within the first 11.5, 20, 26, 30.5 and 56 min when EC 4 cp, Eudragit® RS 100, Eudragit® RL 100, PLGA (RG503H) and PCL were used respectively. The square weighted mean chord length of PCL microparticles was smallest, but the chord count was not the highest. The chord length distribution (CLD) measured by FBRM showed that a larger mean particle size gave longer CLD and a lower peak of particle number. SEM data revealed that the morphology of microparticles was influenced by the type of polymer. Sonicator helped in emulsification of polymeric system in aquous. FBRM can be employed for online monitoring of the shift in the microparticle CLD and detect transformation of emulsion droplets into solid microparticles during the solvent evaporation process. The microparticle CLD and transformation process were strongly influenced by polymer type.Keywords: Sonication-assisted emulsification, Microparticles, Solvents, Polymers, Double emulsion technique

Introduction

Emulsion systems play an important role in the agriculture, food, cosmetic, pharmaceutical, and biomedical industries. Ultrasound has gained significant attention for preparing emulsions, particularly micro- and nanoemulsions, due to its eco-friendliness, cost-effectiveness, and energy efficiency [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5]]. This study provides an overview of ultrasonic emulsification technology, introducing key concepts such as emulsion characteristics, acoustic cavitation, the emulsification mechanism, and applications in preparing polymeric microparticles using various solvents and polymers [1,[6], [7], [8], [9], [10], [11]].

Microparticles are solid or semisolid particles ranging in size from 1 to 1,000 µm and serve a wide range of uses in drug delivery, diagnostics, and various industrial applications [[12], [13], [14], [15], [16]]. They can encapsulate active compounds, which improves stability, bioavailability, and controlled release [12,15,[17], [18], [19], [20], [21], [22]]. Traditional microparticle preparation methods include spray drying, solvent evaporation, and emulsification [[23], [24], [25], [26], [27], [28], [29]]. Among these, emulsification followed by solidification stands out for its simplicity and efficiency [12,25,[30], [31], [32], [33], [34], [35], [36]]. Sonication-assisted emulsification uses high-frequency sound waves to create and stabilize emulsions [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5]]. Applying ultrasonic energy generates mechanical forces that disperse the dispersed phase within the continuous phase, resulting in smaller droplet sizes and improved stability [14,23,34,[37], [38], [39], [40], [41], [42]]. This study examines various solvents and polymers in aqueous systems to investigate their impact on microparticle preparation via sonication-assisted emulsification. The main principle behind this technique is acoustic cavitation. High-frequency sound waves applied to a liquid create pressure variations that form and collapse microbubbles. This repeated cycle generates localized high temperatures and intense shear forces, which break larger droplets into smaller, more stable ones.

During emulsification, the high shear generated by cavitation disperses the dispersed phase (typically an oil or polymer solution) throughout the continuous phase (usually an aqueous solution). This process forms an emulsion with droplet sizes determined by the energy input, the properties of the solvent and polymer, and the formulation conditions [[43], [44], [45], [46], [47]].

The stability of the emulsion plays a crucial role in the successful formation of microparticles. To produce a stable emulsion, it is essential to reduce surface tension, prevent droplet coalescence, and choose an effective emulsifying agent. Polymers and surfactants help maintain stability by providing the necessary interfacial protection [12,[24], [25], [26], [27],36,44].

Industries such as pharmaceuticals, cosmetics, and food commonly use solvent evaporation to prepare microparticles. This method can encapsulate a wide range of substances, from simple active compounds and drugs to proteins and DNA [[12], [13], [14], [15], [16], [17], [18], [19],[45], [46], [47], [48], [49], [50], [51]]. Researchers have developed several variations of the solvent evaporation technique to improve encapsulation efficiency for hydrophilic and hydrophobic drugs. For insoluble or poorly water-soluble drugs, the oil-in-water (O/W) method works well, while the water-in-oil-in-water (W/O/W) method suits water-soluble drugs [[12], [13], [14], [15], [16], [17],23,[26], [27], [28], [29],32,43,51].

When preparing polymeric microparticles for sustained drug release using the solvent evaporation technique, the solidification rate plays a crucial role in controlling drug release behavior. If the emulsion droplets harden too slowly, the drug can diffuse out of the droplets, reducing encapsulation efficiency. The solidification rate of polymeric microparticles during the solvent evaporation process depends on the solubility of the polymers in organic solvents and the solubility of the organic solvent in water. These factors directly affect microparticle properties such as particle size, drug incorporation, matrix porosity, residual solvent levels, and the initial burst release [[12], [13], [14], [15], [16], [17], [18]]. The solidification rate is a critical parameter that influences particle size, encapsulation efficiency, and the initial burst in microparticulate systems [[24], [25], [26], [27],[30], [31], [32], [33], [34],[46], [47], [48], [49], [50]]. A slow hardening rate can cause the drug to diffuse out of the droplets and precipitate in the external phase. The precipitation kinetics of the polymer solution droplets depend not only on the solvent’s affinity with the external phase but also on their phase ratio. The polymer’s solubility in the organic solvent determines how quickly the polymer solidifies during microparticle formation. This process affects key properties, including drug loading, porosity, and solvent residue levels [[12], [13], [14], [15],[23], [24], [25], [26],[34], [35], [36], [37], [38], [39],[47], [48], [49], [50], [51]].

Droplet step emulsification has been proven to possess the unique advantage of decoupling the flow parameters, which obviously contributes to the realization of the mass production of monodisperse droplets. The spontaneous formation mechanisms of droplets in step emulsification, thus providing theoretical guidance for precisely regulating the emulsification process [52]. Double emulsions with unique core–shell structures are widely used in numerous applications such as micro-reactors. Usually, to ensure the satisfactory application performances of double emulsions, the precise control over their size and monodispersity is of great importance [53,54]. Compared to traditional methods regarding stirring shearing, the microfluidic technology can accurately manipulate the multiphase fluid flow behaviors in microscale, thus offering a promising platform to prepare double emulsions with regulatable size and desired monodispersity. As the flow velocity of the middle fluid increases, the inner diameter of the resultant double emulsions remains constant within a specific range because the increase of the flow velocity always results in the decrease of the middle fluid viscosity, thus determining the nearly invariant viscous shearing force acting on the inner fluid [53,54].

Sonication-assisted emulsification and microfluidic technology are both employed to produce microparticles with controlled sizes, yet they differ significantly in several key aspects. Sonication systems are generally simpler to set up and operate, requiring less specialized equipment, making them more cost-effective, whereas microfluidic devices tend to be complex and expensive to fabricate and maintain [[52], [53], [54]]. In terms of throughput, sonication enables rapid processing of large volumes, which is advantageous for industrial-scale production, whereas microfluidic systems usually have lower throughput suited primarily for laboratory-scale experiments [52,54]. Scaling up sonication processes is relatively straightforward by increasing batch size or utilizing larger equipment, while microfluidic systems pose challenges due to their intricate designs and precise flow control requirements [52,54]. Additionally, sonication offers broad material compatibility, accommodating various materials and formulations without extensive customization, unlike microfluidic devices that often require specific channel designs or surface modifications for different materials. However, microfluidic technology excels in producing highly uniform microparticles with narrow size distributions owing to precise control over flow and droplet formation, a level of size control that sonication may not always achieve, often resulting in broader size distributions. Furthermore, microfluidic methods provide higher reproducibility due to controlled flow conditions [[52], [53], [54]], whereas sonication results can vary depending on parameters such as energy input and sample volume. In summary, while microfluidic technology excels in producing highly uniform microparticles with precise control, sonication-assisted emulsification offers advantages in simplicity, scalability, cost, and processing speed, making it suitable for large-scale production where ultra-fine size control is less critical.

Researchers have used various types of polymers with different physical properties—such as biodegradability, non-biodegradability, and permeability—to prepare microparticles. Commonly used polymers include poly(ε-caprolactone) (PCL), poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA), Eudragit® RS 100, Eudragit® RL 100, and ethyl cellulose 4 cP [[14], [15], [16], [17], [18],[20], [21], [22], [23], [24], [25], [26], [27]]. PCL and PLGA are biocompatible and biodegradable polyesters [[14], [15], [16], [17], [18],[20], [21], [22], [23], [24], [25], [26], [27]]. Eudragit® RS 100 and Eudragit® RL 100 are copolymers of ethyl acrylate and methyl methacrylate containing small amounts of a methacrylic acid ester with quaternary ammonium groups [[12], [13], [14], [15], [16], [17]]. Eudragit® RL 100 is more permeable than Eudragit® RS 100 because it contains a higher amount of co-trimethylammonioethyl methacrylate chloride. Meanwhile, ethyl cellulose 4 cP is a non-biodegradable, hydrophobic polymer [[14], [15], [16], [17], [18],[20], [21], [22], [23], [24], [25], [26], [27],36,37]. However, information about the hardening rates of microparticles prepared with these polymers remains limited. Understanding how polymer properties affect the solidification time of microparticles is essential for optimizing preparation processes.

To date, researchers have done very little work on real-time monitoring of microparticles during their formation. One effective technique for monitoring microparticle preparation by the solvent evaporation method is focused beam reflectance measurement (FBRM). FBRM relies on reflection measurement and depends strongly on the optical properties of the particles. It does not require sampling, which could alter the actual particle size distribution through breakdown or aggregation. FBRM continuously measures and monitors the chord length distribution (CLD), mean particle size, and chord count of microparticles in real time. These parameters depend on the geometry, size, and number of particles under analysis [[32], [33], [34], [35], [36], [37], [38], [39], [40],42,[44], [45], [46], [47], [48]]. Unlike other established particle sizing methods, FBRM uses a unique measurement principle. The instrument features a probe that is inserted into the reaction vessel or process stream. Within the probe, a laser focuses on the surface of a sapphire window using a set of rotating optics. When the laser beam strikes a particle, the light reflects to a detector. The reflected signal provides information about the number of particles present and one-dimensional size data—known as chord lengths—over time [[32], [33], [34], [35], [36], [37], [38], [39], [40],42,[44], [45], [46], [47], [48]]. This capability enables researchers to monitor the transformation of emulsion droplets into solid microparticles in real-time using FBRM.

Interestingly, most studies to date have not explored sonication-assisted emulsification combined with online monitoring of polymeric microparticle formation using FBRM with different solvents and polymers. To address this gap, this study applies FBRM to monitor the solidification rate as well as the particle size and distribution of emulsion droplets and hardened polymeric microparticles produced by the W/O/W method with various solvents and polymers.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and reagents used

Ethyl cellulose 4 cP (Ethocel® Standard 4 Premium, Colorcon Ltd., Kent, UK), ethyl acrylate–methyl methacrylate copolymers (Eudragit® RS 100 and Eudragit® RL 100, Evonik Röhm GmbH, Darmstadt, Germany), poly(D, L-lactide-co-glycolide) (Resomer® RG503H, Boehringer-Ingelheim Pharma GmbH & Co. KG, Ingelheim, Germany), and poly(ε-caprolactone) (PCL; Mn ∼ 10,000, Sigma-Aldrich Chemie GmbH, Steinheim, Germany) were used in this study. Furthermore, dichloromethane and ethanol (Carl Roth GmbH & Co. KG, Karlsruhe, Germany) served as solvents. Polyvinyl alcohol (PVA, Mowiol® 40–88, Kuraray Europe GmbH, Frankfurt, Germany), sodium chloride, sodium hydroxide, and potassium dihydrogen phosphate (Carl Roth GmbH & Co. KG, Karlsruhe, Germany) were also used.

Solubility determination

The solubility of five different polymers was determined in dichloromethane by adding each polymer to 1 mL of dichloromethane in a glass vial. The vials were sealed and shaken at 37 °C and 75 rpm using a horizontal shaker (GFL 3033, Gesellschaft für Labortechnik GmbH, Burgwedel, Germany) for 24 h (n = 3) to ensure saturation. Next, 1 mL of the saturated solution was filtered, and the solvent was allowed to evaporate at room temperature. After filtration, the mass of the residual ethyl cellulose was measured using an analytical balance (Sartorius Research A200S, Sartorius GmbH, Göttingen, Germany).

Preparation of emulsions by sonication

For the W/O/W multiple emulsion method, an aqueous solution was emulsified into a 7.5 % w/v polymer solution in dichloromethane using probe sonication (Sonoplus® HD 250, Bandelin Electronic GmbH & Co. KG, Berlin, Germany) for 30 s under ice cooling (see Fig. 1a).

Microparticle preparation

The solvent evaporation method, based on the formation of a water-in-oil-in-water (W/O/W) multiple emulsion, was used to prepare the microparticles. In this method, an aqueous solution was emulsified into a 7.5 % w/v polymer solution in dichloromethane using probe sonication (Sonoplus® HD 250, Bandelin Electronic GmbH & Co. KG, Berlin, Germany) for 30 s under ice cooling. The resulting primary W/O emulsion was then dispersed into an external aqueous phase consisting of 800 mL of a 0.25 % PVA solution. The emulsion was stirred at 500 rpm for 4 h using a propeller stirrer (Heidolph Elektro GmbH & Co. KG, Kelheim, Germany). After stirring, the microparticles were separated from the external phase by wet sieving, washed with 200 mL of deionized water, dried in a desiccator for 24 h, and stored in the desiccator until use.

Analysis of droplet size and distribution, and monitoring of emulsion stability through online chord length distribution

The FBRM probe (Lasentec® FBRM D600T, Mettler Toledo AutoChem, Inc., Redmond, WA, USA) was immersed and positioned in the emulsification vessel containing the W/O/W emulsions described above. The probe was positioned to ensure a good flow across the probe window, allowing for the measurement of a representative sample of the particle system (see Fig. 1b). The measurement range of the FBRM D600T probe is 0.25–4000 μm. A propeller stirrer (Heidolph Elektro GmbH & Co. KG, Kelheim, Germany) was placed beside the probe and operated at 500 rpm for 4 h. FBRM measurements were taken every 10 s over 4 h. All batches were measured in triplicate. Size information was collected and analyzed using iC FBRM® 4.0 software (Mettler Toledo AutoChem, Inc., Redmond, WA, USA).

Microparticle characterization

Optical microscopy

Microparticles were spread on microscope slides and observed under an optical light microscope (Axiotrop 50, Carl Zeiss AG, Jena, Germany) equipped with an image analysis system (INTEQ Informationstechnik GmbH, Berlin, Germany) that included a digital camera (type MC1) and EasyMeasure® software (version 1.4.1).

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

The external morphology of the microparticles was examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM). For surface imaging, microparticles were mounted on a sample holder using double-sided tape. All samples were coated with a fine layer of gold to a thickness of 8 nm under an argon atmosphere (SCD 040, Bal-Tec GmbH, Witten, Germany) in a high-vacuum evaporator. Samples were then examined with a scanning electron microscope (S-4000, Hitachi High-Technologies Europe GmbH, Krefeld, Germany).

Online monitoring of polymeric microparticle formation

Chord length measurement method

The FBRM technique measures chord lengths within a particle population. For a spherical particle, the instrument generates a range of chord length values from its detection limit up to the particle’s diameter. If the particle is not spherical, the “diameter” refers to the diameter of the sphere that would circumscribe the irregular particle [[14], [15], [16], [17], [18], [19],[22], [23], [24], [25],[38], [39], [40], [41], [42]].

A chord length is defined as a straight-line segment whose endpoints both lie on the surface of a particle. FBRM analyzers use a laser beam that randomly crosses particles, measuring a chord length each time the beam intersects a particle. The frequency with which each chord length occurs forms a probability density function. For spherical microparticles—such as those made from ethyl cellulose, polycaprolactone, and PLGA (RG503H)—the diameter corresponds to the largest possible chord, and the measured chord lengths are independent of the microparticle’s orientation relative to the laser beam (see Fig. 1). For irregular or non-spherical microparticles—such as those made from Eudragit® RL 100 and Eudragit® RS 100—the shape and orientation affect the measured chord lengths. Consequently, the chord length distribution (CLD) depends on the particle size distribution (PSD) and the particle shape. FBRM instruments rely on a laser beam for these measurements. The system calculates the chord length from the laser light that reflects off the microparticle and returns through the probe.

Microparticle formation and hardening rate

FBRM was used to qualitatively monitor shifts in the chord length distribution (CLD) at various stages of microparticle formation under controlled agitation rates. The primary focus of this study was to monitor microparticle formation, changes in particle size, the hardening rate, particle properties, and the chord length distribution. All polymers produced microparticles with small sizes (less than 300 μm). Fig. 4 shows the square-weighted mean chord length of the polymeric microparticles as a function of time.

The effect of polymer type on square-weighted mean chord length during microparticle formation by the W/O/W method [(↓): indicating the starting time of microparticle hardening].

The use of different polymers in microparticle formation led to distinct square-weighted mean chord length profiles during the solvent evaporation process. Initially, when the organic polymer solution was emulsified, FBRM detected larger mean droplet sizes for all polymers. As processing time increased, the particle size decreased until reaching a plateau, where the square-weighted mean chord length remained constant for each polymer. Ethyl cellulose microparticles exhibited a larger square-weighted mean chord length than the others, which is attributed to the higher viscosity of their polymer solution. A higher viscosity reduces the dispersibility of the organic phase into the aqueous medium, resulting in larger particles. The viscosity data for each polymer solution are shown in Table 1.

The solidification rate of polymeric microparticles was influenced by the solubility of the polymer in the solvent. In this study, the solubilities of the polymers in dichloromethane were compared (see Table 1). Ethyl cellulose had the lowest solubility in dichloromethane, while polycaprolactone (PCL) had the highest solubility among the polymers tested. As a result, the hardening rate of PCL-based microparticles was slower than that of the other polymers, whereas ethyl cellulose produced the fastest hardening rate. Eudragit® RL 100, Eudragit® RS 100, and PLGA (RG503H) were more soluble in dichloromethane than ethyl cellulose [[12], [13], [14], [15], [16], [17], [18],[22], [23], [24], [25], [26], [27],[35], [36], [37], [38], [39]], which resulted in a slower solidification rate for microparticles made from these polymers. Polymers with relatively high solubility in dichloromethane solidified more slowly [[12], [13], [14], [15], [16], [17], [18],[22], [23], [24], [25], [26], [27],[35], [36], [37], [38], [39]]. Because these polymers remained in a semi-solid state for a more extended period, the dispersed phase became more concentrated before complete solidification, resulting in denser microparticles [[12], [13], [14], [15], [16], [17], [18],[22], [23], [24], [25], [26], [27],[35], [36], [37], [38], [39]].

In general, droplet shrinkage occurred in two phases for all polymers. An initial phase of rapid shrinkage caused the droplet size to decrease markedly within about 9 min for ethyl cellulose 4 cP, 15 min for Eudragit® RS 100, 20 min for Eudragit® RL 100, and 25 min for PLGA (RG503H). This was followed by a second phase of minimal or slow shrinkage, during which no further significant size reduction was observed (see Fig. 5). This pattern indicates that the solvent was extracted rapidly during the first phase, while in the second phase, the embryonic droplets gradually solidified into stable microparticles. For polycaprolactone, droplet size continued to decrease up to 56 min, indicating that the embryonic microparticle droplets solidified between 50 and 56 min (see Fig. 4). Based on the FBRM data, the start of the plateau phase for each polymer showed that microparticle hardening occurred at 11.5 min (ethyl cellulose 4 cP), 26 min (Eudragit® RL 100), 20 min (Eudragit® RS 100), 56 min (polycaprolactone), and 30.5 min (PLGA (RG503H)) (see Fig. 4 and Table 2).

In the case of rapid solvent extraction for ethyl cellulose 4 cP microparticles (see Fig. 4), the polymer solidified quickly at the droplet surface. This caused the surface to scatter light diffusely to some degree. The square-weighted mean chord length of the polymeric microparticles remained constant throughout the extraction period, indicating that the optical properties did not change during the process. Therefore, the increase in the FBRM signal reflects changes in opacification and marks the point at which particle solidification occurs. This period has the most significant impact on the final particle morphology and should be the primary focus for controlling particle properties.

The trend of chord counts during the solvent evaporation process

Fig. 5 shows the chord count curves for polymeric microparticles as a function of time. The W/O/W method illustrates the chord count profiles for all polymers during the solvent evaporation process. At the start, when the W/O emulsion was dispersed into the external aqueous phase, FBRM detected high initial chord counts. As processing time increased, the chord counts continued to rise before reaching a plateau phase, where they remained stable. For Eudragit® RL 100, PLGA (RG503H), Eudragit® RS 100, and polycaprolactone, the chord count profiles showed a similar pattern. Initially, FBRM detected high chord counts for all these polymers. Over time, the counts increased, then gradually decreased, and ultimately reached a plateau where they stabilized.

The chord count of ethyl cellulose microparticles was lower than that of the other polymers. This is due to the production of microparticles with the largest size. The square-weighted mean chord lengths of Eudragit® RL 100, Eudragit® RS 100, and PLGA (RG503H) were smaller than those of ethyl cellulose, resulting in higher chord counts. Polycaprolactone produced the smallest square-weighted mean chord length, but its chord count was not the highest. This outcome can be attributed to the unique properties of the particles. Polycaprolactone forms microparticles that are slightly translucent compared to those produced by the other polymers (see Fig. 3d). When the laser beam hits a translucent microparticle, multiple reflections occur within the particle, causing the entire sphere to light up [[30], [31], [32], [33], [34],[37], [38], [39], [40]]. In contrast, when the laser beam strikes an opaque microparticle, the signal scatters directly to the detector. Opaque microparticles measured by FBRM generate higher scattering signals than translucent or transparent ones. Due to the high absorbance of opaque microparticles, the backscattered signal has a very high intensity, resulting in a high chord length value but lower chord counts. Droplets or microparticles that are opaque and highly reflective, with microstructured surfaces, provide reproducible and accurate FBRM measurements [[30], [31], [32], [33], [34],[37], [38], [39], [40]].

Based on Fig. 5, understanding the context involves analyzing measures such as chord count (square-weighted), which quantifies the morphology and size distribution of microparticles; a higher chord count indicates a greater number of smaller particles or more complex surface features, while a lower count suggests larger, smoother particles. The square-weighted mean chord length provides insight into the typical particle size within a sample, weighted by surface area or other factors. When examining the effects of different polymers, ethyl cellulose (EC) tends to produce larger microparticles with a lower chord count, likely due to its physical properties such as viscosity and solubility during solvent evaporation. In contrast, polymers like Eudragit® RL 100, Eudragit® RS 100, and PLGA generate smaller particles with higher chord counts, resulting in more numerous or surface-rich microparticles owing to their better emulsification behavior and lower viscosity. Polycaprolactone (PCL), on the other hand, produces the smallest mean chord length but does not have the highest chord count, possibly because its hydrophobicity, crystallinity, or melt behavior lead to smaller particles with less surface complexity or different morphology. These differences in particle size and surface features among polymers are influenced by factors such as polymer solubility, viscosity, molecular weight, crystallinity, surface properties, and interactions with solvents and stabilizers, which collectively determine droplet formation, stability, and solidification during solvent evaporation [16,23,27,34,44,47]. In summary, larger particles from polymers like EC tend to be fewer and bigger, resulting in lower chord counts, while smaller particles from Eudragit® and PLGA are more numerous with higher chord counts; PCL’s unique properties lead to very small particles that do not necessarily increase particle number, due to distinct formation mechanisms.

The chord length distributions and the cumulative volume chord length distributions

The chord length distributions measured by FBRM for various polymers at the same concentration differed (see Fig. 6). As expected, larger microparticles produced longer chord lengths and lower particle number peaks due to the reduced number of microparticles. The data showed that as the viscosity of the polymer solution increased, the square-weighted mean chord length also increased, and the chord length distributions broadened. The viscosity of the polycaprolactone solution (organic phase) was lower than that of the other polymers. As a result, it produced smaller microparticles with a smaller square-weighted mean chord length and narrower chord length distributions compared to microparticles made from the other polymers.

The square-weighted mean chord length offers a weighted average emphasizing larger particles, representing the typical particle size within the distribution, while particle number peaks indicate the abundance of particles within specific size ranges, with higher peaks reflecting more particles of that size [16,21,27,44,46]. Key observations show that ethyl cellulose (EC) produces the largest microparticles, evidenced by the longest chord lengths and the lowest particle number peaks, implying fewer, larger particles with a broad size distribution. Conversely, polycaprolactone (PCL) results in the smallest microparticles, with the shortest chord lengths and lowest peaks, indicating more uniform, smaller particles. Eudragit® RL 100, Eudragit® RS 100, and PLGA (RG503H) generate smaller chord lengths than EC, corresponding to smaller particle sizes, and higher particle number peaks, reflecting a higher abundance of fine particles due to more efficient emulsification or faster solidification. The viscosity of the polymer solution plays a crucial role; higher viscosity hinders droplet breakup, leading to larger particles and broader size distributions, whereas lower viscosity promotes finer droplet formation, resulting in smaller, more uniform particles. Mechanistically, viscous solutions resist deformation and breakup, forming larger droplets that solidify into larger microparticles, while low-viscosity solutions facilitate droplet breakup into smaller particles. The efficiency of emulsification and droplet stability, influenced by polymer viscosity, directly impact the final particle size distribution and chord length profiles [16,23,27,34,44,47]. In conclusion, polymer viscosity is a key parameter that affects particle size, distribution, and chord length profiles during microparticle fabrication, with larger particles producing longer chords and broader distributions, and smaller particles resulting in shorter chords and narrower distributions; thus, selecting polymers with suitable viscosities and properties allows for tailored microparticle size and uniformity suited to specific applications.

Conclusions

Sonication-assisted emulsification is a versatile technique for preparing microparticles, leveraging the interaction between polymer properties and characteristics. Understanding how different polymers influence emulsion stability and microparticle attributes enables researchers and practitioners to customize formulations for specific applications. This insight supports further exploration into optimizing sonication parameters and broadening the selection of polymers for microparticle fabrication. Based on focused beam reflectance measurement (FBRM) data, the transformation from emulsion droplets to solid particles during the solvent evaporation process occurs within the first 11.5–26 min when using ethyl cellulose 4 cp, Eudragit® RS 100, and Eudragit® RL 100. In contrast, PLGA (RG503H) completes this transformation within 30.5 min, while polycaprolactone takes up to 56 min. The choice of polymer has a significant impact on the properties of the resulting microparticles. Microparticles with good scattering characteristics yield comparable values for chord length distribution (CLD) and chord count, offering reliable particle size estimations. The polymer type plays a critical role in determining CLD, transformation dynamics, and the extent of agglomeration during microparticle formation.
