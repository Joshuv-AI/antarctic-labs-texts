The impact of lipid metabolism remodeling in the tumor microenvironment on angiogenesis
Authors: Chen Ye, Zhongqun Wang, Lihua Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTumour cells enhance their survival and proliferation through autocrine and paracrine signalling, thereby facilitating tumour progression. Sustained growth requires the formation of new blood vessels, making angiogenesis a major therapeutic target. Concurrently, tumour development involves metabolic reprogramming to support uncontrolled proliferation. While the role of tumour metabolism in immune regulation has been widely studied, its contribution to angiogenesis remains less understood.ObjectiveTo summarize current knowledge on how tumour-driven lipid metabolic reprogramming influences angiogenesis within the tumour microenvironment (TME) and to identify potential therapeutic opportunities.MethodsWe reviewed recent studies focusing on the intersection between tumour lipid metabolism and angiogenesis, integrating findings from experimental and translational research.ResultsEmerging evidence indicates that lipid metabolic alterations in tumour cells modulate endothelial function, vascular remodelling, and pro-angiogenic signalling pathways. These changes reshape the angiogenic landscape of the TME, contributing to tumour progression and therapy resistance.ConclusionUnderstanding the interplay between lipid metabolism and angiogenesis may uncover novel therapeutic vulnerabilities. Targeting metabolic reprogramming in the TME could provide new avenues for anti-angiogenic strategies and improve patient outcomes.

Main text

The tumor microenvironment (Tumor microenvironment, TME) is composed of the extracellular matrix (ECM), blood vessels, and non-transformed cells such as fibroblasts, adipocytes, glandular cells, and immune cells [26]. Fibroblasts are the principal source of ECM components, which not only provide structural support but also regulate tissue homeostasis, cell motility and viability [37]. Core processes such as angiogenesis, epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), and immune modulation are orchestrated within the TME and are integral to tumour progression across nearly all cancer types [5]. Aberrant angiogenesis, a hallmark of tumour development and metastasis, is both shaped by and contributes to the evolving TME [22]. The newly formed tumour vasculature facilitates metastatic dissemination, enabling tumour cells to intravasate, travel through the circulation, and colonize distant sites where secondary tumours can emerge [70].

Metabolic reprogramming is a hallmark of cancer that supports the bioenergetic and biosynthetic needs of uncontrolled cell proliferation. Although growing attention has been paid to the interaction between tumour metabolism and immune function, relatively little is known about how metabolic pathways, particularly lipid metabolism, influence angiogenic signalling. Lipid metabolic reprogramming not only affects tumour cells directly, but also modulates the phenotype and function of stromal and endothelial cells within the TME. Understanding these interactions is critical for uncovering novel metabolic vulnerabilities and advancing therapeutic strategies targeting tumour vascularization. Increasing evidence indicates that lipid metabolic reprogramming within the tumor microenvironment (TME) directly shapes endothelial cell metabolism and angiogenic sprouting. Landmark studies established the essential role of endothelial fatty acid oxidation in dNTP synthesis and vessel formation [15, 65]. More recent work has expanded these findings, highlighting how lipid metabolic pathways in endothelial cells integrate with tumor-driven signals to regulate angiogenesis [43]. These studies provide updated mechanistic insights and reinforce the therapeutic potential of targeting endothelial lipid metabolism in anti-angiogenic strategies. Nevertheless, the extent to which lipid metabolism uniformly promotes angiogenesis remains unclear, as emerging data suggest tumor type–, microenvironment–, and cell-specific variability.

This review focuses on the impact of lipid metabolism remodeling in the tumor microenvironment on angiogenesis, with particular emphasis on endothelial cell–intrinsic lipid metabolic programs that govern vascular sprouting, maturation, and dysfunction. Tumor metabolism remodeling

Reprogramming of energy metabolism is a hallmark of cancer and serves as a critical mechanism by which both tumour cells and immune cells adapt to the dynamic and often hostile conditions of the TME [36, 71]. Cancer cells demonstrate remarkable metabolic plasticity, enabling them to switch between energy sources in response to nutrient availability and the heterogeneous composition of the tumour milieu [24].

A classic example of metabolic reprogramming is the Warburg effect, whereby tumour cells preferentially engage in aerobic glycolysis and increase glucose uptake even in the presence of oxygen [44]. Beyond supporting bioenergetic demands, this shift in glucose metabolism also contributes to lipid biosynthesis by providing acetyl-CoA and other intermediates, modulating lipid-related signalling pathways, and protecting cells from oxidative damage through reduced lipid peroxidation. Importantly, most insights into lipid metabolic reprogramming have been extrapolated from tumor cell–centric models, whereas its functional relevance in endothelial cells during angiogenesis is less well characterized.

Furthermore, enzymes traditionally associated with gluconeogenesis have been implicated in the regulation of lipid metabolism, suggesting cross-talk between distinct metabolic pathways. Despite growing interest in metabolic reprogramming, lipid metabolism remains relatively understudied compared to glucose metabolism and warrants further investigation, particularly in the context of tumour angiogenesis (Fig. 2).

Lipids

Lipids, broadly categorized into simple and complex lipids, play essential roles in numerous cellular functions. Simple lipids include triglycerides (TAGs), which serve primarily as energy storage molecules, while complex lipids encompass sterols and their esters, phospholipids (PLs), and glycolipids—key components of biological membranes and signaling platforms. Although ketone bodies are not classified as lipids, they act as critical intermediates in fatty acid oxidation (FAO). Beyond serving as a dense energy source, lipids provide structural elements for membranes, function as precursors for bioactive molecules, participate in both intracellular and extracellular signal transduction, and contribute to post-translational modification of proteins [13, 31, 52, 75, 79].

Cellular lipid homeostasis is maintained through a tightly regulated balance of exogenous uptake, endogenous synthesis. Excess lipids are stored in lipid droplets (LDs) and can be mobilized through tightly regulated catabolic processes such as lipolysis and FAO. In tumors, however, this delicate balance is frequently disrupted due to genetic mutations, mitochondrial dysfunction, and environmental cues within the tumor microenvironment (TME) [40]. Lipid metabolism is extensively reprogrammed in cancer cells and surrounding stromal and immune cells to meet increased energy demands, sustain proliferation, modulate immunity, and promote angiogenesis. However, the functional consequences of these lipid alterations differ substantially across cell types within the tumor microenvironment, cautioning against generalized conclusions.

One of the most prominent features of lipid metabolic reprogramming in cancer is the upregulation of de novo fatty acid synthesis, even in the presence of ample extracellular lipids. Key enzymes such as ATP-citrate lyase (ACLY), acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC), and fatty acid synthase (FASN) are often upregulated in tumors and are under the control of sterol regulatory element-binding proteins (SREBPs) [60, 83]. These enzymes enable a continuous lipid supply for rapidly proliferating cells, while stearoyl-CoA desaturase 1 (SCD1) generates monounsaturated fatty acids that support membrane fluidity and oncogenic signaling.

Concurrently, fatty acid oxidation (FAO) is upregulated in certain cancer types and TME components, providing metabolic flexibility, sustaining NADPH production, and mitigating oxidative stress. Carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1 A (CPT1A), the rate-limiting enzyme of FAO, has emerged as a central metabolic node in both cancer cells and endothelial cells, linking lipid utilization to survival and angiogenic adaptation under metabolic stress. Notably, FAO may exert divergent effects depending on cellular context, supporting tumor cell survival in some settings while constraining endothelial proliferation under others. Cholesterol metabolism also undergoes significant remodeling in the TME. Tumor cells and associated stromal components regulate intracellular cholesterol through a dynamic balance of uptake, de novo synthesis, efflux, and esterification by acyl-CoA: cholesterol acyltransferase 1/2 (ACAT1/2). These processes influence membrane raft integrity, lipid raft–mediated signaling, and immune cell polarization. For example, tumor-associated macrophages and endothelial cells exhibit altered cholesterol handling, contributing to immunosuppressive and proangiogenic phenotypes.

The reprogramming of lipid metabolism extends beyond cancer cells themselves [45]. Interactions between tumor and TME cells, including immune and stromal populations, give rise to a highly heterogeneous and evolving metabolic landscape [14]. For instance, protumor immune cells such as regulatory T cells and M2 macrophages preferentially utilize lipid oxidation, whereas antitumor effectors like cytotoxic T cells depend on glycolysis [82]. Furthermore, metabolic stress in the TME can induce autophagy and non-apoptotic forms of cell death—such as ferroptosis [19], cuproptosis [35], and calcium overload-induced necrosis [32]—all of which are closely tied to lipid peroxidation and play roles in shaping the immune response.

Together, these distinct yet interlinked lipid metabolic pathways highlight the complexity and plasticity of the TME, revealing multiple opportunities for therapeutic intervention. Understanding how lipid metabolism supports tumor growth, immune evasion, and particularly angiogenesis is essential for identifying novel metabolic targets in cancer treatment.

Modes and mechanisms of tumor angiogenesis

Tumor-associated neovascularization plays a crucial role in supporting cancer progression by delivering nutrients and oxygen to tumor cells while facilitating the removal of metabolic waste. This process prevents the buildup of acidic metabolites, thereby creating a more favorable microenvironment for tumor growth and survival [70].

To date, several distinct mechanisms have been identified in the formation of tumor vasculature. These include sprouting angiogenesis, the classical pathway where new capillary branches emerge from pre-existing blood vessels through endothelial cell proliferation and migration; intussusceptive angiogenesis (non-sprouting angiogenesis), which involves the splitting of existing vessels via the formation of intraluminal tissue pillars; vascular co-option, in which tumor cells hijack and grow along the host’s pre-existing capillaries without inducing new vessel formation.

In addition to sprouting angiogenesis, tumors may exploit alternative vascularization mechanisms, including postnatal vasculogenesis and vasculogenic mimicry (VM). Postnatal vasculogenesis involves the recruitment of bone marrow–derived endothelial progenitor cells (EPCs) to the tumor site, where they can contribute to neovessel formation either through direct incorporation or paracrine pro-angiogenic signaling [61, 81]. EPCs represent a heterogeneous population commonly characterized by markers such as CD34, CD133, and VEGFR-2, although their precise identity and quantitative contribution to tumor vascularization remain context dependent and vary across tumor types and experimental models [46].

Vasculogenic mimicry (VM) refers to a distinct, endothelial-independent process in which aggressive tumor cells acquire endothelial-like properties and form perfusable, extracellular matrix–rich vascular-like networks [59]. VM structures are typically PAS-positive and CD31-negative and have been associated with tumor aggressiveness, metastatic potential, and resistance to anti-angiogenic therapies. Mechanistically, VM is driven by tumor cell plasticity and involves pathways such as VE-cadherin, EPHA2, PI3K/FAK signaling, and extracellular matrix remodeling [74]. Nevertheless, the functional relevance and clinical utility of EPC-mediated vasculogenesis and VM remain incompletely defined, owing to challenges in marker specificity, model dependence, and in vivo validation.

Among these, sprouting angiogenesis remains the most widely studied and is generally regarded as the predominant pathway in solid tumors. Tumor blood vessels lack the hierarchical organization of normal vasculature, presenting as irregular, tortuous, and heterogeneously sized vessels with inconsistent diameters, excessive and chaotic branching, and blind ends. These structural abnormalities arise from rapid and dysregulated angiogenic signaling and impaired vessel maturation, resulting in immature endothelial linings, discontinuous basal membranes, poor pericyte coverage, and high vascular permeability, which together contribute to vascular leakiness, heterogeneous blood flow, and elevated interstitial fluid pressure. Such aberrant morphology also compromises oxygen and nutrient delivery, creating hypoxic microregions that further drive angiogenesis and therapeutic resistance [47, 66]. Detailed morphologic analyses in breast cancer have documented dilated and atypical capillaries with chaotic endothelial cell arrangements compared with normal microvessels, underscoring the marked heterogeneity of tumor vessel architecture [23]. These abnormalities contribute to uneven perfusion, leading to hypoxic regions within the tumor mass.

Importantly, hypoxia acts as a key driver of angiogenesis by upregulating hypoxia-inducible factors (HIFs) and downstream pro-angiogenic molecules such as VEGF. At the same time, hypoxia and metabolic stress disrupt immune surveillance and promote an immunosuppressive microenvironment. Together, hypoxia, immune dysregulation, and aberrant angiogenesis form a self-reinforcing positive feedback loop that accelerates tumor progression and metastasis. Understanding the diverse modes of tumor angiogenesis is essential for the development of more precise and effective anti-angiogenic therapies. Nevertheless, the relative contribution of different angiogenic modes varies across tumor types and disease stages, complicating the interpretation of angiogenesis-targeted interventions.

The impact of lipid metabolism in the tumor microenvironment on angiogenesis

Given the extensive metabolic reprogramming of lipid pathways in tumor cells, increasing attention has been directed toward how these alterations influence various aspects of tumor progression beyond cellular proliferation—particularly tumor angiogenesis. Angiogenesis is not only essential for sustaining rapid tumor growth by supplying oxygen and nutrients but also facilitates immune evasion and metastatic dissemination. Recent studies have revealed that lipid metabolism plays a pivotal regulatory role in angiogenic processes, both by modulating the behavior of endothelial cells (ECs) and by shaping the tumor microenvironment (TME) to favor neovascularization.

Dysregulated lipid metabolism can affect angiogenesis at multiple levels: (1) through the production of lipid-derived signaling molecules that directly activate angiogenic pathways, (2) by altering the function and phenotype of stromal and immune cells that support angiogenesis, and (3) via metabolic adaptation of endothelial cells themselves under hypoxic or nutrient-deprived conditions. However, accumulating evidence suggests that the angiogenic consequences of lipid metabolic reprogramming are highly context dependent and may vary according to tumor type, metabolic state, and cellular compartment.

Overall, Lipid metabolic remodeling influences angiogenesis through modulation of canonical signaling pathways, including VEGF, Notch, and mTOR signaling. However, the magnitude and direction of these effects depend on lipid species, metabolic state, and the specific endothelial context. Alterations in membrane lipid composition affect receptor localization and signaling amplitude, while lipid-derived metabolites act as secondary messengers that fine-tune endothelial responses to angiogenic cues.

Tumor lipid metabolism

Simple lipids, such as triacylglycerols (TAGs), commonly known as fats, serve primarily as energy reserves. Complex lipids include sterols and their esters, phospholipids (PLs), and glycolipids, all of which have critical structural and signaling roles. Although ketone bodies are not lipids per se, they are important metabolic intermediates generated during fatty acid oxidation (FAO).

Lipids are indispensable for a wide range of cellular activities. Beyond serving as a high-energy fuel source [75], they also form the structural basis of biological membranes [79], act as precursors for key signaling molecules [52], participate in both intracellular and extracellular signal transduction [13], and modify a variety of proteins and other biomolecules via lipidation processes [31]. Cellular lipid homeostasis storage in lipid droplets (LDs), and catabolism via FAO. However, in cancer cells, lipid metabolism is profoundly reprogrammed due to oncogenic mutations, mitochondrial dysfunction, and the selective pressures of the tumor microenvironment (TME) [40]. This metabolic remodeling typically progresses through three stages to support malignant transformation and immune evasion. First, lipid uptake and de novo lipid synthesis are significantly upregulated to meet increased anabolic demands [60, 83]. Second, the synthesized lipids are preferentially channeled into metabolic and signaling pathways that support tumor growth, survival, and angiogenesis. Finally, these altered lipid metabolic programs exert long-term effects not only on tumor cells themselves but also on stromal and immune components within the TME, contributing to immune suppression and therapeutic resistance [55].

Key enzymes involved in lipid metabolism, such as acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC), fatty acid synthase (FASN), and carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1 (CPT1), may exhibit dysregulated activity or even acquire noncanonical (non-metabolic) functions that further promote tumor progression [45].

The most important function of lipids is to provide energy for cellular activities. Triacylglycerol (TAG) serves as a major energy storage and supply source for cells. Upon hydrolysis, TAG is broken down into fatty acids (FAs), which undergo fatty acid oxidation (FAO) and mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) to generate large amounts of ATP. Under normal conditions with sufficient nutrient supply, tumor cells and immune cells preferentially rely on glucose metabolism. However, when cells are activated or subjected to nutrient deprivation, particularly under the metabolic stress of the tumor microenvironment (TME), they shift to lipid utilization as a key source of energy and biosynthetic precursors [78].

The impact of lipid metabolism reprogramming on the tumor microenvironment (TME)

Lipid metabolic remodeling in tumor cells not only supports their intrinsic proliferative and survival needs but also profoundly reshapes the surrounding tumor microenvironment (TME), particularly the immune landscape. The TME is a complex and dynamic ecosystem composed of immune cells, stromal cells, vasculature, extracellular matrix, and soluble factors. Among these, immune cells play a central role in dictating tumor fate, yet their presence and function are highly heterogeneous across different tumor types and even within the same tumor.

Some tumors are described as “cold tumors” due to their lack of immune cell infiltration, exhibiting what is termed an “immune desert” phenotype. Others are considered “hot tumors,” enriched with active immune components and generally more responsive to immunotherapy. A major determinant of this dichotomy is the ability of immune cells to recognize and migrate toward tumor cells—a process that is tightly regulated by chemokine signaling networks. Chemokines direct the recruitment of distinct subsets of lymphocytes and antigen-presenting cells (APCs) into the TME through specific chemokine-receptor interactions [63].

Importantly, emerging evidence indicates that lipid metabolic pathways influence chemokine expression and signaling. For instance, high-density lipoproteins (HDL) [6] and oxidized lipids [3] have been shown to modulate the expression of chemokines and their receptors, thereby altering immune cell recruitment. In turn, certain chemokines may also impact cellular lipid metabolism, although the mechanisms underlying this bidirectional regulation remain largely elusive [16]. In this context, lipid metabolism reprogramming can shape immune accessibility and responsiveness within the TME, ultimately influencing the efficacy of immunotherapies and the trajectory of tumor progression.

Lipid rafts

Lipid rafts are specialized microdomains within the plasma membrane that are enriched in cholesterol and sphingomyelin, and contain long-chain saturated fatty acids (SFAs). These components contribute to the formation of relatively stable and ordered membrane regions that facilitate the anchoring of lipoproteins and the compartmentalization of signaling molecules. Tumor cell membranes are often characterized by elevated levels of lipid rafts, which serve as platforms for the localization and activation of various oncogenic proteins, including CD44, epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), and insulin-like growth factor receptor (IGF-R) [49].

Transmembrane transport of exogenous lipids

The transmembrane transport of exogenous lipids occurs through several distinct pathways. Low-density lipoproteins (LDL) are internalized via LDL receptor (LDLR)-mediated endocytosis and subsequently degraded by lysosomal acid lipase (LAL), releasing free fatty acids (FFAs) and cholesterol. LDLR is frequently overexpressed in tumors, where it promotes the activation of key oncogenic signaling pathways, including EGFR, PI3K/Akt, and sterol regulatory element-binding protein (SREBP) pathways. Persistent upregulation of LDLR has also been associated with enhanced metastatic potential in cancer cells [41].

Long-chain fatty acids (LCFAs) are primarily transported into cells through fatty acid translocase (CD36), a member of the scavenger receptor family, or fatty acid transport proteins (FATPs), which belong to the solute carrier family SLC27A [1]. CD36 also facilitates the uptake of oxidized LDL (oxLDL) and cholesterol. High expression levels or structural abnormalities of CD36 are closely linked to tumor progression, invasion, and metastasis. CD36 promotes fatty acid oxidation (FAO), angiogenesis, and resistance to radiotherapy and chemotherapy by activating the Src/PI3K/Akt signaling axis. Moreover, CD36 can induce epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and activate the Wnt and TGF-β signaling pathways to facilitate metastatic dissemination [72].

In addition, lipid droplet (LD) accumulation has been implicated in resistance to EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitors in lung cancer. Inhibitors such as A922500, which targets diacylglycerol acyltransferase 1, and neutralizing antibodies against CD36 have been shown to suppress lipid droplet formation and inhibit cancer cell invasion [12, 29]. Despite extensive characterization of CD36 in tumor cells and immune compartments, its endothelial-specific role in angiogenesis remains insufficiently resolved.

Abnormal cholesterol metabolism and angiogenesis - related signaling pathways

In the tumor microenvironment, both endogenous and exogenous signals can reprogram cholesterol metabolism, thereby promoting tumorigenesis [80]. Activation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway significantly upregulates intracellular cholesterol levels [17], which are essential for cell cycle regulation and are closely linked to cell dormancy, proliferation, carcinogenesis, and lifespan. Mevalonate, a key intermediate in cholesterol biosynthesis, can further stimulate PI3K, mTOR, and NF-κB pathways while inhibiting tumor suppressor genes such as p21 and p27, ultimately affecting apoptosis, cell cycle progression, autophagy, and cancer cell migration [29, 33]. Conversely, ATP citrate lyase (ACL), a key enzyme in lipid biosynthesis, can suppress tumor progression by downregulating the PI3K/AKT pathway [38].

A distinct cholesterol metabolic pathway has been identified in colorectal cancer (CRC) liver metastases, wherein hepatocyte growth factor (HGF) from the hepatic microenvironment activates the c-Met/PI3K/AKT/mTOR axis in CRC cells, inducing a SREBP2-dependent cholesterol biosynthesis program [7]. Moreover, lysosomal cholesterol can negatively regulate mTORC1 activity through the SLC38A9-Niemann Pick C1 (NPC1) complex [29]. Loss of NPC1 leads to cholesterol accumulation in lysosomes, causing hyperactivation of mTORC1, mitochondrial dysfunction, and even neurodegeneration [67]. In cancer cells, sodium mevalonate has also been shown to activate the Hippo signaling pathway via the TP53/SREBP axis [80]. As a central tumor suppressor, p53 maintains metabolic homeostasis and coordinates cellular stress responses. It synergizes with the Hippo pathway to regulate SREBP activity at multiple levels, thus modulating cholesterol and lipid levels [54].

Cholesterol metabolism influences numerous oncogenic signaling cascades, including PI3K/AKT/mTOR, NF-κB, WNT/β-catenin, Hedgehog, TP53/SREBP, NPC1/mTOR, and RTK/Ras pathways, all of which contribute to tumor cell proliferation, growth, and angiogenesis. Additionally, tumor-related transcription factors are also modulated by cholesterol homeostasis. Overall, deregulated cholesterol metabolism—particularly via its regulators such as SREBP2—plays a pivotal role in cancer progression and represents a potential therapeutic target.

Statins, including pitavastatin [27], fluvastatin [80], and simvastatin [28], have demonstrated anti-tumor efficacy by inhibiting tumor cell proliferation and angiogenesis while promoting apoptosis. Their mechanisms include suppression of the Akt and BRAF/MEK/ERK1/2 pathways, inhibition of tumor endothelial cell growth via prenylation blockade of the Ras/Raf/MEK and PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathways, and upregulation of miR-140-5p to inhibit proliferation and induce apoptosis [2, 67, 80]. Beyond their lipid-lowering effects, statins inhibit HMG-CoA reductase (HMGCR), disrupt lipid raft organization, and remodel the cytoskeleton by reducing membrane cholesterol, thereby impairing tumor proliferation and metastasis [4].

However, cholesterol regulators such as SREBP2 may also contribute to therapeutic resistance. Caspase-3-mediated activation of SREBP2 enhances cholesterol biosynthesis and has been implicated in resistance to anti-angiogenic agents like sorafenib or lenvatinib via activation of the Hedgehog pathway [48].

AMPK and mTOR are central antagonistic regulators of energy metabolism. Under energy stress, elevated intracellular AMP/ATP or ADP/ATP ratios, increased calcium levels, or oxidative stress activate AMPK, promoting catabolic processes, suppressing anabolic pathways, and conferring resistance to metabolic stress [25]. Specifically, AMPK enhances fatty acid uptake by upregulating CD36 and other lipid transporters and suppresses SREBP signaling. It also promotes autophagy by directly or indirectly inhibiting mTORC1 [64]. Furthermore, AMPK activation inhibits cell proliferation via upregulation of the p53 pathway and suppression of cyclin-dependent kinase (CDK) signaling [72], highlighting its tumor-suppressive potential. In contrast, in nutrient-rich conditions, mTOR becomes activated, promoting anabolic processes. Of its two complexes, mTORC1 (sensitive to rapamycin) drives protein synthesis through activation of ribosomal protein S6 kinase (S6K) and inhibition of eIF4E-binding protein (4EBP), while mTORC2 (insensitive to rapamycin) promotes cell survival via activation of AKT, SGK, and PKC [58]. Both S6K and AKT also stimulate HIF-1α and SREBP1, thereby enhancing angiogenesis and fatty acid synthesis [57].

Cholesterol metabolism contributes to tumor progression primarily by modulating membrane organization and signaling platforms. In the context of angiogenesis, altered cholesterol availability influences endothelial membrane lipid raft integrity, thereby affecting VEGFR localization and downstream signaling. However, compared with fatty acid metabolism, the direct contribution of cholesterol metabolism to endothelial angiogenic sprouting remains less well defined and appears to be highly context dependent. Compared with fatty acid metabolism, the direct contribution of cholesterol metabolic remodeling to endothelial angiogenic sprouting remains incompletely defined and appears to be highly context dependent.

Hypoxic-driven lipid metabolic reprogramming and angiogenesis

Hypoxia is a defining feature of the tumor microenvironment and a potent driver of pathological angiogenesis. In endothelial cells, hypoxia-inducible factors (HIF-1α and EPAS1/HIF-2α) coordinate metabolic and transcriptional programs that directly promote neovascularization. Beyond inducing VEGF expression, hypoxia profoundly reshapes endothelial lipid metabolism to support angiogenic adaptation.

Under hypoxic conditions, oxygen-dependent lipid desaturation is impaired due to reduced activity of stearoyl-CoA desaturase 1 (SCD1), leading to the accumulation of saturated fatty acids [34]. To maintain membrane fluidity and signaling competence, endothelial cells increase the uptake of exogenous unsaturated fatty acids and mobilize lipid droplets. Concurrently, HIF-1α enhances the expression of lipid transporters such as LDLR and FABPs, facilitating lipid acquisition and storage.

Hypoxia-driven lipid remodeling also modulates VEGF receptor signaling [76]. Cholesterol-enriched membrane microdomains influence VEGFR2 localization and phosphorylation, thereby fine-tuning angiogenic signal transduction. Moreover, lipid droplet accumulation protects endothelial cells from oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation, preserving endothelial viability during vessel sprouting. Together, hypoxia-induced lipid metabolic reprogramming acts synergistically with VEGF signaling to sustain angiogenesis in nutrient- and oxygen-deprived tumor regions. Most evidence supporting hypoxia-induced lipid remodeling in angiogenesis is derived from experimental models, and its relevance in human tumors requires further validation.

Intervention in fatty acid uptake

Inhibitors targeting CD36, a key fatty acid transporter, have demonstrated promising effects in impeding tumor progression, particularly by suppressing metastasis and angiogenesis within the tumor microenvironment (TME) [53]. When combined with fatty acid synthase (FASN) inhibitors and anti-programmed death receptor 1 (anti-PD-1) immunotherapy, CD36 inhibitors exhibit synergistic antitumor effects [73]. Mechanistically, CD36 inhibition enhances the antigen-presenting capacity of dendritic cells (DCs) and reduces the population of immunosuppressive regulatory T cells (Tregs) by downregulating the peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor (PPAR) signaling pathway. This modulation also leads to upregulation of PD-1 on immune effector cells, thereby promoting the survival and cytotoxic function of CD8⁺ T cells. Additionally, CD36 inhibitors are associated with the suppression of lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis in immune cells, further contributing to the preservation of immune cell viability and function within the TME [38].

CD36-mediated lipid uptake represents an important interface between the lipid-rich tumor microenvironment and endothelial cells. While CD36 has been extensively studied in tumor cells and immune compartments, emerging evidence suggests that endothelial lipid uptake via CD36 may modulate angiogenic responses indirectly by altering intracellular lipid availability and membrane composition. Nevertheless, the endothelial-specific contribution of CD36 to angiogenesis remains incompletely characterized and warrants further investigation.

Endothelial cell lipid metabolism in angiogenic sprouting

Endothelial cells (ECs) are central executors of angiogenesis, and accumulating evidence indicates that their metabolic programs are not merely supportive but actively instruct angiogenic behavior. While glycolysis is the predominant energy source for ECs during sprouting angiogenesis, lipid metabolism—particularly fatty acid oxidation (FAO)—plays a non-redundant and regulatory role in endothelial proliferation and vessel formation [15, 39, 65].

Seminal studies from Carmeliet and colleagues demonstrated that FAO in ECs is dispensable for ATP generation but essential for de novo deoxyribonucleotide (dNTP) synthesis, thereby sustaining endothelial cell proliferation during angiogenic expansion. Mechanistically, carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1 A (CPT1A), the rate-limiting enzyme of FAO, fuels the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle to provide carbon units for nucleotide biosynthesis. Genetic or pharmacological inhibition of CPT1A selectively impairs pathological angiogenesis without causing overt endothelial cell death, highlighting FAO as a metabolic checkpoint in vessel sprouting [15, 65]. It should be noted that these findings are largely based on murine models of pathological angiogenesis, and endothelial metabolic dependencies may differ under physiological or tumor-specific conditions.

Beyond FAO, endothelial lipid uptake and storage are dynamically regulated during angiogenesis. Lipid droplets accumulate transiently in activated ECs, serving as reservoirs that buffer oxidative stress and provide fatty acids during metabolic fluctuations. Moreover, endothelial cholesterol homeostasis influences membrane fluidity, lipid raft organization, and VEGFR2 clustering, thereby modulating VEGF signal transduction efficiency.

Importantly, endothelial metabolic heterogeneity mirrors vascular phenotypes. Tip cells exhibit heightened glycolytic flux and lipid remodeling, whereas stalk cells rely more heavily on FAO-supported proliferation. Disruption of lipid metabolic balance in ECs therefore alters tip–stalk cell specification and compromises vascular patterning.

In contrast to tumor cells, endothelial cells exhibit distinct lipid metabolic requirements during angiogenesis. Lipid metabolism directly regulates VEGF signaling at multiple levels, including receptor membrane localization, downstream signal amplification, and endothelial proliferative capacity. Alterations in fatty acid oxidation influence endothelial cell cycle progression, while lipid-derived metabolites modulate VEGFR2 signaling dynamics. These findings highlight endothelial lipid metabolism as a central determinant of angiogenic efficiency and vascular patterning.

Abnormal metabolism of tumor - associated immune cells

Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) predominantly exhibit an immunosuppressive M2-like phenotype, which plays a critical role in promoting tumor progression and shaping the tumor microenvironment (TME). M2 macrophages secrete various tumor-promoting factors, including arginase 1 (Arg1), and metabolically rely mainly on fatty acid oxidation (FAO) for energy production and functional maintenance. These cells increase lipid uptake from the TME and enhance de novo fatty acid synthesis to support their protein functions and sustain their immunosuppressive phenotype. For instance, oleic acid has been shown to induce M2 polarization through activation of the mTOR signaling pathway [56]. Nevertheless, lipid-driven immune polarization is not uniform across tumors and may vary with metabolic availability and inflammatory cues.

Cholesterol metabolism also plays an important role in M2 macrophage function. The cholesterol transporter ABCG1, which regulates cholesterol efflux and homeostasis, is highly expressed in M2 macrophages, promoting cholesterol removal and facilitating the secretion of anti-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-4 (IL-4). Additionally, 27-hydroxycholesterol (27-HC) acts as a signaling molecule that induces M2 polarization, likely through activation of liver X receptors (LXRs). The hypoxic environment within tumors further promotes M2 macrophage infiltration while suppressing the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype by releasing angiogenic factors, mitogens, and adenosine. Moreover, tumor-derived lactate enhances M2 polarization by stabilizing HIF-2α via downregulation of the M2-specific vacuolar ATPase subunit ATP6V0d2. Lactate also upregulates long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) that stabilize HIF-1α in M2 macrophages, reinforcing their immunosuppressive function [8, 51].

Dietary interventions have been explored for their effects on tumor immunity and metabolism. The ketogenic diet (KD), rich in “good lipids” such as polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), has been reported to regulate p53 and mTOR signaling pathways, reduce inflammation, and inhibit tumor angiogenesis [30]. In contrast, the typical high-fat diet (HFD) often contains “bad lipids,” including saturated fatty acids (SFAs) and cholesterol, which may exacerbate tumor progression [10]. However, adherence to KD is challenging for many patients. Recent studies suggest that β-hydroxybutyric acid (BHB), a key ketone body produced during ketosis, mediates much of KD’s anti-tumor effects. Supplementation with BHB may enhance the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), potentially overcoming compliance issues and improving immunotherapy outcomes. Nevertheless, the role of BHB in cancer remains controversial, with some evidence indicating it might promote the growth of certain tumors under specific conditions [20].

Collectively, these findings highlight the complex interplay between lipid metabolism and immune cell function within the TME. Targeting aberrant lipid metabolic pathways in tumor-associated immune cells, particularly TAMs, may offer novel therapeutic avenues to enhance anti-tumor immunity and improve patient outcomes.

Copper metabolism, cuproptosis, and angiogenic regulation

Copper is an essential trace element that exerts potent pro-angiogenic effects within the tumor microenvironment [9]. Elevated copper levels in tumors activate multiple angiogenic mediators, including VEGF, FGF2, and TNF-α, thereby promoting endothelial cell migration and neovascularization [11]. Copper can directly enhance VEGF signaling and stimulate endothelial proliferation.

While excessive intracellular copper triggers cuproptosis through mitochondrial proteotoxic stress, endothelial cells appear relatively resistant to copper-induced cell death under physiological conditions. Instead, copper primarily functions as an angiogenic cofactor in tumors. This dual role highlights the context-dependent effects of copper metabolism and suggests that therapeutic modulation of copper availability may simultaneously impair tumor angiogenesis and sensitize cancer cells to metabolic stress. The pro-angiogenic versus cytotoxic effects of copper appear to be highly dose- and context dependent, underscoring the need for careful therapeutic modulation.

Cancer - associated adipocytes promote tumor progression

Adipocytes are major stromal cells in various tissues and play an active role in the tumor microenvironment (TME), especially in the form of cancer-associated adipocytes (CAAs). These CAAs not only reside in close proximity to tumor cells but also communicate extensively with them by releasing a variety of factors that mediate both local and systemic effects. This bidirectional interaction alters the phenotypes and functions of both adipocytes and cancer cells, thereby promoting tumor progression [77]. CAAs have been closely linked to the progression of multiple cancers, including breast cancer [62], pancreatic cancer [69], and colorectal cancer [68]. Compared to normal adipocytes, CAAs exhibit distinct characteristics such as a fibroblast-like phenotype, reduced cell volume, smaller and more dispersed lipid droplets, overexpression of collagen VI, and decreased secretion of adipokines like adiponectin [18].

Breast cancer is currently the most extensively studied tumor type regarding the role of CAAs. Here, the mechanisms by which CAAs influence breast cancer progression can serve as a model to understand their impact on tumor angiogenesis and whether similar pathways operate in other cancers. CAAs promote tumor progression through several mechanisms: regulation of adipokines, metabolic reprogramming, remodeling of the extracellular matrix, and modulation of immune cells. They secrete a range of cytokines and chemokines, including chemokine ligand 2 (CCL2), chemokine ligand 5 (CCL5), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), and leptin. These factors collectively enhance breast cancer cell invasion and metastasis. For example, CCL2 promotes chemotaxis and angiogenesis by binding to chemokine receptors CCR2 and CCR4. Blocking the CCL5-CCR5 axis impairs the activation of the AKT/mTOR signaling pathway and disrupts tumor growth and blood vessel formation [21]. IL-1β enhances tumor angiogenesis and invasiveness via activation of the p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (p38/MAPK) and phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)/Akt pathways [50]. IL-6 facilitates invasion, metastasis, and angiogenesis primarily through the Janus kinase/signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (JAK/STAT3) pathway [42]. TNF-α stimulates breast epithelial cells to secrete matrix metalloproteinases, promoting epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and tumor cell invasion. Both TNF-α and IL-1β also upregulate VEGF expression. VEGF and its receptor VEGFR are highly expressed in the breast cancer microenvironment. They promote proliferation, survival, migration, and angiogenesis by activating oncogenic signaling cascades such as the MAPK and PI3K/AKT pathways [84].

In summary, interactions between tumor cells and CAAs induce metabolic reprogramming that supports cancer cell proliferation and promotes angiogenesis, invasion, and metastasis, highlighting the critical role of CAAs in tumor progression. However, most mechanistic insights into CAAs are derived from breast cancer models, and whether similar angiogenic mechanisms operate across other tumor types remains uncertain.

Challenges, limitations, and future perspectives

The emerging understanding of lipid metabolism remodeling in the tumor microenvironment (TME) offers exciting opportunities to modulate angiogenesis, yet several challenges remain. Technical challenges include limitations of current preclinical models, incomplete spatial resolution of metabolic fluxes, and difficulties in capturing dynamic lipid signaling in heterogeneous tumor and endothelial cell populations. While preclinical studies support targeting key metabolic nodes such as FAO and CPT1A, clinical trials remain limited, and potential resistance mechanisms and safety profiles are largely unknown. Biomarkers and metabolomics: Integration of metabolomic, transcriptomic, and spatial profiling may enable patient stratification and identification of actionable targets. Advances in single-cell spatial metabolomics and imaging mass spectrometry will be critical for dissecting endothelial and tumor cell metabolic interactions in vivo.

Future directions include: developing robust preclinical models that recapitulate human tumor vasculature, integrating multi-omics to identify predictive biomarkers, testing pharmacologic inhibitors in early-phase clinical trials, and exploring combinatorial strategies with existing anti-angiogenic or immunotherapies.

Collectively, these efforts will be essential to translate mechanistic insights on lipid metabolism and angiogenesis into clinically actionable strategies.

Conclusion

Lipid metabolism reprogramming emerges as a pivotal driver in the tumor microenvironment (TME), profoundly impacting tumor growth, immune modulation, and especially angiogenesis. Throughout this review, we have detailed the multifaceted roles of lipid species, the dysregulation of lipid metabolic pathways, and the intricate crosstalk between tumor cells, stromal cells, and immune components mediated by lipid metabolism. From altered lipid uptake and synthesis to aberrant cholesterol handling and hypoxia-driven metabolic shifts, the dynamic remodeling of lipid metabolism shapes the vascular landscape necessary for tumor progression and metastasis.

Furthermore, key molecular players such as fatty acid transporters, lipid raft-associated proteins, and cholesterol metabolism regulators intertwine with oncogenic signaling cascades (e.g., PI3K/AKT/mTOR, Wnt/β-catenin, Hippo pathways), collectively fostering a pro-angiogenic and immunosuppressive milieu. The reciprocal influence between lipid metabolites and immune cell phenotypes—highlighting the roles of tumor-associated macrophages, regulatory T cells, and cancer-associated adipocytes—underscores the complexity and heterogeneity of the TME.
