Preparation of Red Cabbage-Based Intelligent/Active Composite Films Loaded with Clove Essential Oil and Their Application in Fish Freshness Monitoring
Authors: Hongqin Guo, Chun Jiao, Lin Wang, Mengyue Qi, Huibin Wu, Shasha Li, Xiangyang Yuan, Wei Wang, Hejun Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In this study, red cabbage-based intelligent/active composite films loaded with different concentrations of clove essential oil were prepared using red cabbage slurry as the matrix, polyvinyl alcohol as the binder, glycerol as the plasticizer, and Tween 80 as the emulsifier via the casting method. The physicochemical properties, color response behavior, and antioxidant and antibacterial activities of the films were systematically evaluated and their application in fish freshness monitoring was further investigated. The results showed that the incorporation of clove essential oil significantly enhanced the antioxidant and antibacterial properties of the films and optimized their mechanical properties within a certain concentration range. Although high concentrations slightly reduced the pH response sensitivity of the films, all composite films exhibited significant color-changing ability, achieving a visible transition from red to yellow-green within the pH range of 2–12. In fish preservation experiments, the composite films not only reflected the freshness status of fish in real time through color changes but also effectively inhibited the increase in total volatile basic nitrogen, total bacterial count, and pH value, thereby delaying spoilage. In this study, a green packaging material with an intelligent indicating function was successfully developed, providing a novel solution for the quality monitoring of high-value aquatic products.

1. Introduction

The nutritional value of fish and foods made from them is remarkable, and they are an important source of high-quality protein and unsaturated fatty acids, so they are popular in the market. But at the same time, their widespread high water content, near-neutral pH, and highly unsaturated lipid composition make them highly susceptible to microbial proliferation and lipid oxidation during storage and distribution, leading to spoilage. This not only results in significant economic losses but also poses serious food safety risks [1,2]. Therefore, developing packaging technologies that can monitor fish freshness in real time, and intuitively, is of great importance for ensuring consumer safety and reducing food waste [3]. As an emerging technology, intelligent indicator packaging can respond to changes in the internal microenvironment of food through color changes in the packaging material, providing an effective approach to achieving this goal [4]. Furthermore, during fish spoilage, the metabolic activities of specific microorganisms lead to the accumulation of volatile nitrogen-containing substances such as trimethylamine, causing an increase in the product’s pH value, which offers an ideal target for developing pH-responsive intelligent indicator films [5]. However, most current indicator films only play a “passive” monitoring role and lack the ability to delay food spoilage, limiting their effectiveness in extending shelf life [6].

Among various intelligent indicator materials, pH-responsive films based on natural pigments have become a research hotspot due to their biodegradability, environmental friendliness, and excellent visual perception capabilities [7,8]. Among them, red cabbage anthocyanin, as a widely sourced and low-cost water-soluble pigment, undergoes reversible protonation and deprotonation reactions with respect to the phenolic hydroxyl group of this compound under different pH environments, displaying a rich color change from red to purple, blue, and even yellow-green. This highly aligns with the trend of increasing pH during fish spoilage; therefore, anthocyanin-rich red cabbage pulp is an ideal candidate for fabricating intelligent indicator films. [9,10]. Wu et al. [10] recently reported a pH-sensing film composite system with red cabbage pulp and polyvinyl alcohol as the substrate. This study directly used red cabbage homogenate as the film-forming matrix and anthocyanin source, avoiding traditional extraction processes. This not only simplified the process but also achieved full-value utilization of red cabbage. The film exhibited significant color changes from purple to yellow during fish storage, which were highly consistent with changes in total volatile basic nitrogen and total bacterial count during fish spoilage, verifying its feasibility in practical applications. Although existing studies have explored the application of red cabbage anthocyanin in food packaging, these systems typically face several key challenges: first, anthocyanins themselves are sensitive to light, heat, and oxygen, and are prone to degradation during processing and storage, leading to insufficient stability and durability of the indicator signal [11]; second, most studies focus solely on their indicator function, failing to integrate active antibacterial or antioxidant activities, which limits their preservation efficacy [12,13,14]; finally, the physicochemical interactions occurring between anthocyanins and the matrix employed for film formation may affect the mechanical properties and structural uniformity of the film [15]. Therefore, developing a multifunctional film system that combines high stability, precise indication, and active preservation remains an urgent challenge to be addressed. Additionally, Wu et al.’s study also pointed out that pure red cabbage films have deficiencies in mechanical properties and water resistance, while the introduction of polyvinyl alcohol significantly improved the film’s mechanical properties and structural uniformity, providing a good matrix foundation for subsequent functional modifications [10].

To overcome the above limitations, introducing natural antibacterial agents into the indicator film system to construct an integrated “intelligent/active” packaging is considered a forward-looking strategy for achieving technological breakthroughs [16]. Clove essential oil, as a generally recognized safe natural plant extract, has been proven to possess broad-spectrum and potent antibacterial and antioxidant activities due to its main active component, eugenol. It can effectively inhibit common spoilage and pathogenic bacteria in fish and delay lipid oxidation [17,18]. Based on the red cabbage/PVA composite film system established by Wu et al. [10], this study further introduces clove essential oil, aiming to construct an intelligent/active packaging material with both real-time monitoring and active preservation functions. Integrating clove essential oil with red cabbage anthocyanin is expected to achieve the following synergistic effects: on one hand, the introduction of clove essential oil can endow the film system with strong active preservation capabilities, compensating for the functional deficiencies of pure indicator films [19,20]; on the other hand, theoretically, it is speculated that the microenvironment formed by essential oil in the film matrix can block oxygen and light to a certain extent, thereby helping maintain the stability of anthocyanins and slow down their degradation. [21]. Through precise formulation design, clove essential oil can be released at the initial stage of fish spoilage (when pH begins to change), thereby providing effective antibacterial intervention during the critical window of rapid microbial proliferation [22]. This integrated design concept of “monitoring-warning-intervention” is expected to significantly enhance the comprehensive preservation performance of the packaging system [23,24].

Based on the above analysis, this study aims to further develop a red cabbage-based intelligent/active composite film loaded with clove essential oil, building upon existing research on red cabbage/PVA intelligent films, and systematically evaluate its potential application in fish freshness monitoring and preservation. The research will use red cabbage slurry as the composite film-forming matrix, polyvinyl alcohol as a binder, glycerol as a plasticizer, and Tween 80 as an emulsifier to prepare the red cabbage-based intelligent/active composite film loaded with clove essential oil via the casting method. This study will systematically investigate the effects of the addition of clove essential oil on the properties of the film, including its light transmittance, physical properties, mechanical properties, antioxidant activity, and antibacterial activity. Finally, by applying the optimized composite film in a freshness monitoring experiment, the correlation between its color parameters and key freshness indicators of the fish will be quantitatively analyzed, and its inhibitory effects on total volatile basic nitrogen generation, microbial growth, and pH changes will be comprehensively evaluated. This study aims to provide a novel green packaging solution that integrates real-time visual monitoring and efficient active preservation for high-value aquatic products, which not only has significant theoretical importance but also demonstrates broad application prospects.

2. Materials and Methods

2.1. Materials and Reagents

Red cabbage was purchased from a local supermarket (Ya’an, China); polyvinyl alcohol (PVA1799), glycerol, clove essential oil, Tween 80, citric acid, disodium hydrogen phosphate, hydrochloric acid, sodium carbonate, sodium bicarbonate, and sodium hydroxide were all purchased from Chengdu Kelong Chemical Co., Ltd. (Chengdu, China); Escherichia coli and Staphylococcus aureus were both purchased from the Shanghai Preservation Center of Microorganisms (Shanghai, China). LB liquid medium and nutrient agar medium and plate count agar medium were purchased from Hangzhou Microbial Reagent Co., Ltd. (Hangzhou, China). Unless otherwise specified, all chemicals and reagents were of analytical grade.

2.2. Preparation of Red Cabbage Pulp and Its pH-Responsive Behavior

Extraction of anthocyanins from red cabbage was conducted as detailed in the work of Ravanfar et al. [25] with appropriate modifications. Red cabbage without rot, visible damage, or pests was selected, cut into small pieces, and approximately 175 g of the chopped fresh cabbage was placed in a beaker with 325 g of room-temperature deionized water for soaking (175:325, w/w). The mixture was then placed in an ultrasonic cleaner (SB-5200DTN, Scientz, Ningbo, China) and sonicated for 30 min (frequency of 40 kHz, power of 250 W, and temperature of 25 °C). The soaked mixture was transferred to a fruit and vegetable wall-breaking machine (JYL-G12E, Joyoung, Jinan, China) and processed until it became a slurry. To obtain a fine and uniform slurry, a high-pressure homogenizer (GJJ-0.03/100, Shunyi, Shanghai, China) was used for further treatment. Homogenization was performed at 40 MPa for 10 cycles, with the sample cooled to room temperature after each cycle. The homogenized red cabbage slurry was finally collected and stored at 4 °C for later use.

Following the method of Sohany et al. [26], the pH-responsive color characteristics of the extracted anthocyanins were studied. A series of buffer solutions with pH values ranging from 2 to 12 were prepared to cover acidic, neutral, and alkaline conditions relevant to food spoilage monitoring. For pH 2.0 to 8.0, 0.2 M citrate–phosphate buffers were prepared by mixing 0.1 M citric acid and 0.2 M disodium hydrogen phosphate in appropriate proportions. For pH 9.0 and 10.0, 0.2 M sodium carbonate–bicarbonate buffers were used. For pH 11.0 and 12.0, the pH was adjusted using 0.2 M sodium hydroxide solution with the addition of 0.2 M disodium hydrogen phosphate to maintain ionic strength. The pH values of all prepared buffers were verified using a calibrated pH meter before use. One milliliter of red cabbage slurry was added to 20 mL of each of the above buffer solutions and allowed to stand at room temperature for 30 min to reach reaction equilibrium; after thorough mixing, the mixture was centrifuged at 4000 r/min for 5 min at room temperature. The supernatant was collected, and its UV-Vis absorption spectrum was immediately measured in the range of 400–800 nm using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer (UV-2800, Lengguang, Shanghai, China) with a 1 cm quartz cuvette. Simultaneously, photographs of the corresponding supernatant samples were taken to record the color changes of the samples at different pH values, with all images captured under a lightbox providing consistent illumination, against a white background, and with fixed camera settings to ensure the reliability and comparability of color representation.

2.3. Preparation of Red Cabbage-Based Intelligent/Active Composite Films

The preparation of red cabbage-based intelligent/active composite films was carried out with reference to the method of Pang et al. [27] with slight modifications. A total of 1.75 g of polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) was dissolved in 20 mL of deionized water at 80 °C for 30 min. It was then uniformly mixed with the aforementioned red cabbage slurry, followed by heating and stirring for 30 min. Subsequently, clove essential oil at gradient concentrations of 0%, 1%, 2%, 3%, 4%, and 5% (w/w, based on the weight of fresh red cabbage) (corresponding masses: 0.875 g, 1.750 g, 2.625 g, 3.50 g, and 4.375 g, respectively), 0.3% Tween 80 (emulsifier, mass = 0.2625 g), and 0.3% glycerol (mass = 0.2625 g) (both w/w, based on the weight of fresh red cabbage) were added into the film-forming slurry. The mixture was transferred into a 500 mL beaker and stirred at 500 rpm for 30 min at room temperature using a magnetic stirrer. No high-shear emulsification was used in this study, to ensure sufficient blending of the essential oil, Tween 80, and other components in the slurry. The mixed film-forming slurry was placed in a suction filter flask and degassed by vacuum filtration at a vacuum degree of 0.08 MPa for 10 min until no obvious bubbles were observed. Approximately 250 mL of the degassed slurry was poured onto a 15 cm × 15 cm glass film-forming plate with a controlled thickness of about 4 mm to form a film by casting. The glass plate was then placed in a constant temperature and humidity chamber (45 °C, 50% relative humidity, with controlled airflow and without covering the plate) and dried for 8 h. Finally, red cabbage-based intelligent/active composite films with uniform thickness were obtained.

2.4. Characterization of Composite Films

2.4.1. Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy

Using a Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (Bruker Tensor 27, Bruker, Ettlingen, Germany) equipped with an attenuated total reflection (ATR) accessory, the interactions between film components were analyzed. The measurements were performed in the wavelength range of 500–4000 cm−1 with a spectral resolution of 4 cm−1, averaging 32 scans [28].

2.4.2. Scanning Electron Microscopy

Microstructural features of the composite films, including both surface and cross-section, were examined using a scanning electron microscope (HITACHI SU8010, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan). Prior to observation, the films were processed by freezing in liquid nitrogen followed by fracturing to expose the cross-section. Subsequently, gold sputter coating was applied to the surface and cross-section of the samples via an ion sputtering apparatus, and the observation was implemented at an accelerating voltage of 10 kV [29].

2.4.3. Color and Optical Properties

Colorimetric properties of the film samples were determined by a high-accuracy colorimeter (Model NR 110, 3NH, Shenzhen, China). Color variations of the films were assessed based on L* (lightness), a* (red-green axis), and b* (yellow-blue axis) values, with a standard white plate adopted as the reference for all colorimetric characterizations. The color parameters of each film sample were measured at 5 random points using a colorimeter, and three replicate films were tested in parallel for each type of film. [30].

The films were cut into rectangular specimen strips (50 × 50 mm), and the transparency of the films was tested using a WGT-S light Haze/transmittance meter (WGT-S, INESA, Shanghai, China). For each type of film, five specimens were tested and the results were averaged. Three parallel films were measured for each film type [31].

2.4.4. Mechanical Properties

The mechanical properties of the films were tested following the method described by Wang et al. [32]. The thickness of the samples was measured using a digital micrometer, with five random points tested repeatedly and averaged as the final thickness value for the sample. According to the standard method of ASTM D882 [33], the films were preconditioned at constant temperature and humidity conditions (25 °C, 50% RH) for 48 h before testing. The tensile strength (TS) and elongation at break (EAB) of the films were determined using a universal material testing machine. The films were cut into 15 mm × 80 mm rectangular strips and placed in the middle of the fixture, with an initial grip distance of 40 mm and a tensile speed of 1 mm/s. Five replicate tests were carried out for each group of identical samples to obtain the mean value, and a total of three independent groups were examined. The unit of TS is MPa, and the unit of EAB is %. The calculation formula for TS is shown in Equation (1): (1)TS = FA where F is the maximum tension (N) at which the sample breaks and A is the cross-sectional area of the specimen (mm2).

The calculation formula for EAB is shown in Equation (2): (2)E = L−L0L0 × 100% where L0 is the length of the specimen before testing (mm) and L is the length of the specimen at fracture (mm).

2.4.5. Thickness, Moisture Content, and Water Solubility

The thickness of the film was measured using an electronic digital micrometer. Five random points were selected on each film, and the average value was taken for the calculation of other physical properties, with the unit in mm.

Moisture content (MC): A film sample with a mass of Mi was weighed and placed in a dry weighing dish, then dried in a 110 °C forced-air drying oven until a constant weight was achieved, and the mass was recorded as Mf. The moisture content was calculated based on the difference between the two dry masses, as shown in Equation 3. Three parallel determinations were conducted for each group of identical films, with three replicate groups in total.

Water solubility (WS): A dried film sample with a constant mass of Wi (g) was immersed in 50 mL of deionized water at room temperature and shaken at 25 °C and 100 rpm for 24 h. The solution was vacuum-filtered through filter paper, and the residue was dried at 105 °C to a constant weight, with the mass recorded as Wf (g); the filter paper was pre-weighed before use. After filtration and drying, the total mass of the filter paper with residue was weighed, and Wf (g) was obtained by subtracting the pre-weighed mass of the filter paper. The water solubility WS was calculated based on the difference between the two dry masses. Three parallel determinations were conducted for each group of identical films, with three replicate groups in total. The content of water-insoluble substances was calculated according to Equation (4):(3)Moisture content MC = (Mi − Mf)/Mi × 100% where Mi—mass of the membrane before drying; Mf—mass of the membrane after drying.(4)Water solubility WS = (Wi − Wf)/Wi × 100% where Wi—mass of the film sample; Wf—mass of the filter residue after drying.

2.4.6. Color Sensitivity of the Film

The color sensitivity of the film was evaluated through color changes under different pH conditions [34]. First, the film was cut into 40 × 30 mm rectangles and immersed in 10 mL of buffer solution at different pH values (pH 2, pH 12) for 10 min, after which images of the samples were taken simultaneously. The color of each film corresponding to specific pH values was measured at five random points using an automatic colorimeter. Color parameters, including L* (lightness), a* (red-green value), and b* (yellow-blue value), were obtained, and the total color difference (ΔE) was calculated using Equation (5). (5)∆E=(L*−L)2+(a*−a)2+(b*−b)2

In the equation, L, a, and b are the original color parameter values of the prepared film, while L*, a*, and b* are the color parameters of the film after color change under different pH values.

2.4.7. Antioxidant Activity

The antioxidant activity of the red cabbage-based intelligent/active composite film was evaluated using the DPPH free radical scavenging method, following the experimental procedure referenced in Maral et al. [35]. A film sample of 0.15 g was immersed in 10 mL of 60% (v/v) methanol aqueous solution and shaken in the dark at 25 °C for 24 h at 100 rpm to obtain the film soaking extract. A measured 3.0 mg of DPPH was added to 100 mL of methanol and mixed thoroughly for 30 min, to prepare a 0.0761 mM DPPH methanol solution, then stored in a dark environment for later use. One milliliter of the DPPH methanol solution was mixed with 9 mL of the soaking solution from the red cabbage-based intelligent/active composite film. The resulting mixture was kept in the dark at room temperature for 30 min, and its absorbance was measured at 517 nm, recorded as As. A control sample was prepared via replacing the film soaking solution with 9 mL of 60% (v/v) methanol aqueous solution deionized water, the absorbance of which was measured and recorded as ADPPH. Each purple cabbage-based intelligent/active composite film with different clove essential oil additions was tested at least three times, and the average value was taken. The calculation was performed according to Formula (6):(6)DPPH free radical scavenging rate (%) = (ADPPH − AS)/ADPPH × 100%

In the formula: ADPPH—the absorbance of the control mixture of water and DPPH methanol solution at 517 nm; As—the absorbance of the mixture of the composite film soaking solution and DPPH methanol solution at 517 nm.

2.4.8. Antibacterial Activity

The antibacterial activity of the composite film was quantitatively evaluated using the inhibition zone method [36]. The red cabbage-based active intelligent/active composite film was cut into 12 mm diameter discs using a hole puncher and stored in a desiccator for later use. Staphylococcus aureus and Escherichia coli were cultured in LB liquid medium until OD = 0.5 (bacterial concentration of approximately 1 × 108 CFU/mL). Using a sterile pipette, 200 μL of each bacterial suspension was added to LB solid medium and spread evenly. The prepared red cabbage-based intelligent/active composite films with different clove essential oil contents were placed on the surface of the culture medium plates. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. After incubation, the diameter of the inhibition zone around each well was measured using a vernier caliper. Each sample was tested in triplicate, and the average value was calculated.

2.4.9. Color Response of Composite Film

The color response of the red cabbage-based intelligent/active composite film was evaluated by observing color changes under different pH conditions [37]. First, the film was cut into 40 × 30 mm rectangles and immersed in 10 mL of buffer solution with varying pH values (pH = 2–12) for 10 min. Images of the samples were then captured simultaneously. An automatic colorimeter (CM2300D; Konica Minolta, Tokyo, Japan) was used to measure the color of each film at five random points corresponding to specific pH values. Color parameters, including L* (lightness), a* (red-green value), and B* (yellow-blue value), were obtained. The total color difference (ΔE) was calculated using Equation (5).

2.5. Study of Application of Red Cabbage-Based Intelligent/Active Composite Film for Monitoring Fish Freshness

2.5.1. Treatment of Fish Samples

Taking the ΔEpH2, ΔEpH12, tensile strength (TS), DPPH radical scavenging activity, and diameter of inhibition zone (against Escherichia coli) of the red cabbage-based intelligent/active composite film as the analysis indicators, the comprehensive score was calculated. The red cabbage-based intelligent/active composite film with a 3% clove essential oil addition exhibited the optimal comprehensive score. Fresh cultured crucian carp purchased from a local supermarket in Ya’an, China, were processed immediately after purchase. The skinned fish meat was cut into 30 g pieces and deboned for use. The film was cut into 3 × 3 cm square pieces and attached to the lid of a glass container containing the fish meat. The 250 mL container was sealed airtight, with the film attached to the inner side of the lid, positioned approximately 3 cm above the fish surface. The container was then placed at room temperature (25 °C). During the 0–48 h period, the fish meat was removed every 12 h (separate containers were used for each sampling) to measure the pH value, total volatile basic nitrogen, and total bacterial count [38]. Each time point and each treatment group were measured in triplicate (n = 3).

2.5.2. Determination of Total Volatile Basic Nitrogen (TVB-N) in Fish Meat

The determination of total volatile basic nitrogen (TVB-N) in fish meat was conducted following the method of Huang et al. [39]. First, 10 g of fish meat was weighed, minced, and mixed with 75 mL of water. After standing for 15 min, the mixture was filtered through filter paper, and the filtrate was sealed with plastic wrap for later use. A boric acid absorption solution (20 mL of 20 g/L boric acid containing a mixed indicator of methyl red and bromocresol green) was prepared. The condenser tube was inserted into the boric acid absorption solution, then 10 mL of the fish meat filtrate was mixed with 1 g of magnesium oxide and added to the reaction chamber. Distillation was carried out for 5 min, and the process was repeated at least three times. Finally, distilled water was used in place of the fish meat filtrate to repeat the above steps as a blank control. After the reaction was terminated, titration was performed using 0.01 mol/L hydrochloric acid solution. Titration ended when the solution turned bluish-purple and remained unchanged. The TVB-N content (mg/100 g) was calculated using the following Equation (6): (7)TVB-N = (V1 - V2) × C × 14 × 100m × 1075 where V1 is the titration volume of hydrochloric acid for the sample (mL), V2 is the titration volume of hydrochloric acid for the blank (mL), C is the concentration of hydrochloric acid (mol/L), 14 is the molar mass of nitrogen (g/mol), m is the sample mass (g), and the factor 10/75 accounts for the dilution ratio of the sample.

2.5.3. Determination of Total Viable Count (TVC) in Fish Meat

The total viable count in fish meat samples was determined according to the method of Moradi et al. [40]. A 25 g fish meat sample was weighed and added to a homogenization bag containing 225 mL of sterile physiological saline. The mixture was homogenized for 1–2 min, and 1 mL of the fish meat solution was transferred using a sterile pipette into 9 mL of physiological saline diluent. Two to three appropriate dilutions of the sample homogenate were selected. The sample solutions were inoculated onto plate count agar (PCA) using the spread plate method, and at least three plates were prepared for each dilution gradient. Two blank controls were prepared using 1 mL of sterile physiological saline diluent. The plates were incubated in a biochemical incubator at 36 °C ± 1 for 48 h, followed by colony counting.

2.5.4. Determination of Fish pH

A total of 10 g of fish sample was minced and placed in a beaker, mixed with 100 mL of distilled water, homogenized, and allowed to stand for 30 min. After filtration through filter paper, the pH of the fish extract was measured using a digital pH meter [41]. The pH measurement was performed in triplicate, and the pH meter was equipped with automatic temperature compensation.

2.6. Data Processing

A single-factor experimental design was adopted in this study, with the factor being the addition amount of clove essential oil, including the 0% clove essential oil film group and the clove essential oil film group. Data obtained from the experiments were processed using IBM SPSS Statistics (v. 29.0; IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). The average value was calculated for each measurement index, and the results were presented as (mean ± SD). Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (n = 3). Statistical significance of the data was analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Duncan’s multiple range test for post hoc analysis. Differences were considered statistically significant at a threshold of p < 0.05. Data were plotted using Origin 9.0 software to facilitate the visual analysis of trend changes.

3. Results and Discussion

3.1. Color Changes and UV Spectra of Pure Red Cabbage Pulp Suspension Under Different pH Conditions

The potential application of pure red cabbage pulp suspension (RCP) as a pH indicator was verified by evaluating its color changes. As shown in Figure 1, as the pH increased from 2 to 12, the color of the suspension gradually changed from red to yellow. At pH = 2–3, the suspension appeared bright red; at pH below 5, the color transitioned progressively from pink to purple; while at pH = 8, 9–10, and 11–12, it turned blue, green, and yellow, respectively. The color-changing behavior of the red cabbage pulp suspension corresponds to the structural transformations of anthocyanins under different pH conditions. At pH < 5, anthocyanins exist as red flavylium cations; in the pH range of 6–9, they transform into purple or blue quinoidal base forms; and at pH > 10, they further convert into colorless carbinol pseudobases and yellow chalcone structures [42].

Corresponding to the above color changes, the UV-Vis spectra of the red cabbage pulp suspension under different pH conditions also showed significant shifts in the maximum absorption peaks, as illustrated in Figure 2. This phenomenon is commonly referred to as “red shift” [43], primarily attributed to the structural transformations of anthocyanins present in the solution. At pH = 2, the suspension exhibited a characteristic absorption peak at 525 nm, corresponding to the flavylium cation. As the pH increased from 2 to 6, the intensity of this absorption peak weakened and gradually red-shifted to around 530–540 nm, indicating a significant conversion of the flavylium cation to the quinoidal base structure. When the pH exceeded 7, with the formation of deprotonated quinonoid base anionic species, the maximum absorption peak further shifted to approximately 602 nm, and its absorbance increased with rising pH in the range of pH = 7–9. In the pH range of 10–12, the absorbance at 602 nm gradually decreased as the pH increased. The observed spectral trends are consistent with the color and UV-Vis spectral test results reported in the literature for anthocyanin extracts from red cabbage [44] and purple sweet potato [45], confirming that the homogenized RCP suspension retains the complete pH-responsive characteristics of anthocyanins.

To investigate the effect of functional additives on the indicator system, the response behavior of a red cabbage pulp-based film-forming solution containing clove essential oil (RCP-CEO) within the same pH range was further analyzed. As shown in Figure 1, the color change trend of the RCP-CEO film-forming solution is completely consistent with that of the pure RCP suspension, both following the visual sequence from red to yellow, indicating that the introduction of clove essential oil does not alter the inherent pH response pathway of anthocyanins. The colors presented by the RCP-CEO film-forming solution at various pH points are slightly darker than those of pure RCP, and the transparency of the solution is slightly reduced. This may be attributed to the scattering effect of essential oil microdroplets and the interaction between hydrophobic components and the hydrophilic matrix. The UV-Vis spectral analysis in Figure 2 further supports this conclusion. The positions of the maximum absorption peaks of the RCP-CEO film-forming solution remain consistent with those of pure RCP at various pH levels, with no significant shifts observed. The main difference lies in the general enhancement of absorbance, which may be attributed to the superposition effect of the inherent UV absorption of aromatic compounds in the essential oil and the absorption of anthocyanins.

In summary, the red cabbage pulp suspension treated by high-pressure homogenization, due to its rich anthocyanin content, exhibits a color response sensitive to pH changes, indicating its potential as a pH indicator, with an effect comparable to that of anthocyanin extract. The addition of clove essential oil does not interfere with the structural transformation mechanism of red cabbage anthocyanins or their corresponding color response, ensuring the reliability of the core indicator function of the composite film. This lays a critical foundation for the subsequent construction of an intelligent active packaging system that integrates both “indicator” and “antibacterial” functions.

3.2. Characterization of Red Cabbage-Based Intelligent/Active Composite Films

3.2.1. FTIR Spectral Analysis

As shown in Figure 3, the main peak positions in the FTIR spectra of the pure red cabbage-based film without clove essential oil and the composite films with 3% and 5% clove essential oil are essentially the same, but there are significant differences in the intensity of the main peaks. As the amount of clove essential oil increases, the absorption peak at 3260 cm−1 gradually shifts toward higher frequencies at 3401 cm−1 and 3411 cm−1, indicating intermolecular interactions between the clove essential oil and the film matrix. Additionally, the introduction of clove essential oil causes changes in the C–H stretching vibration region between 2800–2900 cm−1, specifically manifested as the migration of the absorption band from 2924 cm−1 to 2927 cm−1, which is mainly attributed to the response of aliphatic C–H group (–CH) stretching vibrations. In the red cabbage-based intelligent/active composite films with clove essential oil, new absorption peaks appear at 1728 cm−1 and 1736 cm−1, which may correspond to the carbonyl (C=O) stretching vibration of ester compounds in clove essential oil. The appearance of this peak further confirms the existence of intermolecular interactions between clove essential oil and the film matrix [46]. The FTIR analysis results show that clove essential oil primarily interacts with polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) and red cabbage pigment (RCP) through physical interactions (such as hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions). These interactions alter the microstructure of the composite film to some extent but do not affect its response behavior to pH changes [47]. This result is consistent with a study by Sedlarikova et al. [48], who also found that the introduction of clove essential oil into zein-based edible films did not cause significant changes in the protein film structure, further supporting the physical nature of the interactions in this study.

3.2.2. Scanning Electron Microscopy Analysis

Figure 4 presents scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of the RCP/PVA film and red cabbage-based intelligent/active composite films with different clove essential oil contents, showing both surface and cross-sectional morphologies. As shown in Figure 4a,d, the RCP/PVA film exhibits a dense, uniform structure with no obvious pores, indicating good compatibility between the red cabbage film-forming slurry and PVA. With the addition of clove essential oil, the microstructure of the composite films undergoes significant changes, with both porosity and surface roughness gradually increasing as the essential oil content rises. As shown in Figure 4b,e, when the clove essential oil content is 3%, a small number of micropores begin to appear on the film surface, accompanied by localized unevenness. As shown in Figure 4c,f, when the content increases to 5%, the film surface further develops a rough, sponge-like porous structure, with a noticeable increase in the number and size of pores. These structural changes may be related to the volatilization behavior of clove essential oil during the film-forming process and its interaction with the film-forming matrix [49]. During the film solution preparation and drying stages, clove essential oil, as a volatile component, partially escapes, leading to the formation of a microporous structure within the film. Additionally, the interfacial compatibility between the essential oil and the film-forming matrix, as well as the dispersion state of the oil in the matrix, can influence the final film’s density and uniformity [50]. Although the introduction of essential oil leads to increased surface roughness and porosity, the overall film maintains structural integrity without large-scale cracking or delamination, indicating that PVA and the red cabbage matrix have a certain capacity to encapsulate and stabilize the clove essential oil. These observations are consistent with previous reports on structural changes in plant essential oil–polysaccharide/protein composite films, further confirming that the incorporation of functional essential oils can regulate the micro-topological features of the film without disrupting its continuity [51].

3.2.3. Color and Optical Property Analysis

Table 1 shows the effects of different clove essential oil contents on the color parameters and light transmittance of red cabbage-based composite films. The control film without clove essential oil exhibited high brightness (L* value of 51.86). As the clove essential oil content increased from 0% to 5%, the L* value significantly decreased to 36.58, the a* value increased from 17.79 to 20.51, and the b* value also increased from −11.36 to −6.85. This indicates that the overall color of the purple cabbage-based intelligent/active composite films with added essential oil gradually darkened, with the red–green index shifting toward red and the yellow–blue index also tending to increase. This color change is mainly attributed to the light absorption characteristics of natural phenolic compounds in clove essential oil in the low-wavelength region [52], while the inherent color of the essential oil itself also contributes to the film’s coloration.

In terms of optical properties, as the concentration of clove essential oil increased, the light transmittance of the films showed a significant decreasing trend. The control film had a light transmittance of 50.20%, which decreased to 18.40% after adding 5% clove essential oil, with significant differences observed among the groups (p < 0.05). However, as seen in the photograph of the film in Figure 5, the film still maintained good light transmittance, allowing clear visibility of text beneath it. This indicates that although the addition of essential oil reduced the light transmittance of the composite film, the film still retained a certain degree of visual transparency, making it suitable for packaging applications requiring some level of transparency. The decrease in light transmittance can be explained by two mechanisms: on one hand, the essential oil microdroplets dispersed in the film-forming matrix scatter incident light, reducing light transmission; on the other hand, the introduction of clove essential oil may disrupt the original ordered network structure of the film matrix to some extent, thereby affecting its optical uniformity. Additionally, the color components present in the essential oil also absorb light in the visible region, collectively contributing to the reduction in light transmittance [53]. These results are consistent with the trends observed by Yang et al. [54] in their study on the effect of clove essential oil on the optical properties of protein films, further validating the potential of plant essential oils as light-regulating components in edible/active packaging films.

3.2.4. Mechanical Properties

The effect of clove essential oil addition on the tensile strength and elongation at break of red cabbage-based composite films is shown in Figure 6. As the amount of clove essential oil increased, both the tensile strength and elongation at break of the films exhibited an initial increase followed by a decrease. Tensile strength is a direct indicator of a film’s ability to resist external forces, while elongation at break reflects the material’s flexibility and ductility. An ideal composite film should possess both good strength and appropriate toughness [55,56]. The results indicate that when the clove essential oil addition was 2%, the composite film achieved the best overall mechanical performance, with elongation at break reaching its maximum value. Further increases in essential oil content led to a decline in both indicators. This may be attributed to the uniform dispersion of clove essential oil within the film matrix at lower addition levels, where its active components form effective hydrogen bonds and van der Waals interactions with PVA/red cabbage polysaccharide chains, thereby enhancing intermolecular crosslinking density and improving the film’s strength and ductility. However, when the essential oil content increases further, its dispersion within the matrix deteriorates, leading to localized aggregation, reduced interfacial compatibility, and the formation of stress concentration points, ultimately resulting in a decline in mechanical performance [57]. This trend aligns with the SEM observations, which showed increased porosity and reduced uniformity in the film structure at higher addition levels, further validating the intrinsic relationship between structure and performance. In summary, the appropriate addition of clove essential oil helps improve the mechanical properties of the film, but excessive addition leads to performance degradation due to phase separation and structural defects. Therefore, precise control of the essential oil addition ratio is necessary in practical applications to achieve an optimal balance between film strength and toughness.

3.2.5. Thickness, Moisture Content and Water Solubility Analysis

The effect of clove essential oil addition on the thickness, moisture content, and water solubility of red cabbage-based intelligent/active composite films is shown in Table 2. Compared to the control group without essential oil, the thickness of the composite film significantly increased after the addition of clove essential oil, rising from 0.113 mm to 0.161 mm. This thickening phenomenon can be attributed to the interactions between the essential oil components and the biomacromolecules (such as PVA and red cabbage polysaccharides) in the film-forming matrix, which disrupt the orderly stacking of molecular chains and hinder the formation of a dense network structure. Additionally, the increase in solid content in the film solution and the presence of the essential oil as a filler phase further contribute to a looser film structure, resulting in increased thickness [58].

In terms of moisture content, when the clove essential oil addition was 2%, the moisture content of the composite film slightly increased compared to the control group, but the difference was not significant. This may be attributed to the partial network dissociation caused by a small amount of essential oil, providing more sites for water molecules to bind via hydrogen bonds.

The water solubility test results indicated that when the clove essential oil addition was low, the water solubility of the composite film showed a decreasing trend. When the essential oil addition reached 4%, the water solubility recovered to some extent, which may be attributed to the loosened film structure and increased porosity caused by the high content of essential oil, promoting water penetration and matrix swelling to a certain extent. Overall, the hydroxyl shielding effect and hydrophobic interactions induced by the essential oil enhanced the water resistance of the film, giving it better water stability [59]. These results further demonstrate that an appropriate amount of clove essential oil can effectively regulate the water-related properties of the film while maintaining its structural integrity, providing a theoretical basis for designing active packaging materials suitable for different humidity environments.

3.2.6. Color Sensitivity of Composite Films

As shown in Figure 7, the color difference results measured in different pH buffer solutions (pH 2 and pH 12) indicate that the color response behavior of the red cabbage-based intelligent/active composite films significantly changes with the addition of clove essential oil. The control group without clove essential oil exhibited the highest color difference value (ΔE) in both acidic and alkaline environments, indicating the most pronounced color change and reflecting the inherent high pH sensitivity of red cabbage anthocyanins. With the addition of clove essential oil, the pH response behavior of the composite films showed a regular pattern. At essential oil addition levels of 2% and 3%, the composite films still maintained relatively obvious color changes, suggesting their potential as pH indicators. However, as the essential oil content further increased, the ΔE values gradually decreased under both pH 2 and pH 12 conditions, indicating a reduction in the pH response sensitivity of the films with higher essential oil content. This phenomenon may be attributed to the interaction between clove essential oil and the red cabbage anthocyanins in the film matrix. Active components in the essential oil, such as phenolics, may partially embed or shield the chromophores of the anthocyanin molecules, thereby weakening their structural transformation ability and color intensity under different pH conditions [60]. Additionally, the loosening of the film structure and increased porosity caused by the introduction of essential oil may also affect the diffusion process of H+/OH− to the active components within the film, further slowing down the speed and extent of the color response [61]. Although the pH sensitivity of the films decreased at higher addition levels, all composite films containing essential oil still retained a certain degree of pH responsiveness, indicating that the indicator function of red cabbage anthocyanins was not completely suppressed. This characteristic endows these composite films with potential application value for real-time monitoring of pH changes caused by spoilage during food storage, providing experimental evidence for the development of active packaging materials with both antimicrobial and intelligent indicator functions.

3.2.7. Antioxidant Activity Analysis

As shown in Figure 8, the DPPH free radical scavenging ability of the red cabbage-based intelligent/active composite film exhibited a significant dose-dependent enhancement with increasing clove essential oil content. The control film without essential oil had a DPPH free radical scavenging rate of 38.21%, while as the clove essential oil content increased from 1% to 5%, the scavenging rates rose to 48.78%, 52.43%, 56.51%, 63.31%, and 71.39%, respectively. This indicates that the active components of the essential oil maintained good antioxidant activity within the composite film. Clove essential oil is rich in phenolic compounds, such as eugenol and β-caryophyllene, which can effectively neutralize free radicals by providing hydrogen atoms or electrons, thereby exerting antioxidant effects [62]. The significant improvement in the antioxidant capacity of the composite film suggests that during the film-forming process, some of the active components of clove essential oil were not completely embedded in the matrix or formed strong interactions but existed in a free or releasable form, retaining their ability to capture free radicals [63]. Although there is some interaction between the film matrix and the essential oil components, this interaction did not completely mask the antioxidant properties of the essential oil. On the contrary, the film structure may have played a role in protecting and slowly releasing the active components, endowing the composite film with sustained antioxidant potential. This characteristic is of great significance for developing active packaging materials that combine intelligent indication and antioxidant functions, effectively delaying lipid oxidation and food spoilage, and providing dual protection for maintaining food quality.

3.2.8. Antibacterial Activity Analysis

As shown in Table 3 and Figure 9, neither the blank control group nor the red cabbage-based intelligent/active composite film with 1% clove essential oil exhibited significant inhibitory effects against Staphylococcus aureus and Escherichia coli. When the clove essential oil content reached 2%, the composite film began to show inhibitory effects against both tested bacterial strains, producing observable inhibition zones. As the essential oil content further increased, the diameter of the inhibition zones for both bacterial strains gradually enlarged, indicating a dose-dependent enhancement of its antibacterial activity. The strong antibacterial effect of clove essential oil is primarily attributed to its active components, such as eugenol and β-caryophyllene. In the composite film system, the incorporation of essential oil may enhance the film’s moisture absorption to some extent, promoting moderate swelling of the film in aqueous media, thereby accelerating the migration and release of active antibacterial components from the film [64]. During the experiment, eugenol released onto the surface of the composite film and the surrounding medium, along with inherent phenolic compounds such as anthocyanins in red cabbage, can act on the protein and lipid structures of bacterial cell membranes, causing membrane disruption and functional loss, thereby exerting antibacterial effects [65].

Furthermore, the results indicated that the composite film’s inhibitory effect on Gram-positive bacteria was generally superior to that on Gram-negative bacteria. This difference is closely related to the distinct cell wall structures of the two types of bacterial strains. Phenolic compounds in plant essential oils can act on microbial cell membrane phospholipids, leading to increased membrane permeability and cytoplasmic leakage, and can also interact with enzymes in the cell membrane [66]. The relatively simple cell wall structure of Gram-positive bacteria makes them more susceptible to penetration and disruption by hydrophobic phenolic compounds. In contrast, the lipopolysaccharide-rich outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria effectively hinders the penetration of hydrophobic active components in essential oils, resulting in higher tolerance [67]. These findings are consistent with the inhibitory patterns of most plant essential oil-based antibacterial agents against different types of microorganisms, further validating the suitability and potential of clove essential oil as a natural antibacterial agent in active packaging.

3.2.9. Color Reaction of Red Cabbage-Based Intelligent/Active Composite Film

Table 4 shows the visible color changes of the red cabbage-based intelligent/active composite film when in contact with different pH buffer solutions. As the pH increases from 2 to 12, the color gradually transitions from red-pink to yellow-green. The color response behavior of the film to pH changes is basically consistent with that of the corresponding film-forming solution, which is attributed to the structural transformation of anthocyanins with pH variation [68]. In addition, after immersion in buffer solutions, the intelligent/active composite film can still be completely removed and maintains structural stability. Based on the recorded color changes, the color parameters (L, a, b, and ΔE) of the composite film were further measured. Due to the presence of anthocyanins in red cabbage, the prepared composite film initially appears purple, which is used as the baseline for calculating the total color difference (ΔE). At pH ≤ 11, the a* value is positive and gradually decreases with increasing pH, reflecting the higher intensity of the red component at lower pH values. When the pH rises to 11–12, the a* value significantly decreases to below zero with increasing alkalinity, reaching a minimum of −6.58 at pH = 12, indicating that the green color becomes more prominent as alkalinity increases. The b* value is always positive and increases significantly in the high pH range (11–12), indicating a stronger yellow tendency of the film under these conditions [69]. The total color difference ΔE is generally greater than 5, indicating that the color changes of the film can be clearly perceived by the human eye over a wide pH range [70]. This result is highly consistent with the film photographs shown in Table 4. Similar pH-responsive behaviors have also been observed in other studies on intelligent indicator films based on red cabbage anthocyanins and biopolymers [71]. The color response of the film formed by blending red cabbage slurry with polyvinyl alcohol, glycerol, and clove essential oil in this study is comparable to the results reported in the literature for red cabbage-based indicator films, further demonstrating the good applicability of this composite film for intelligent indication [72]. In summary, the red cabbage-based intelligent/active composite film prepared in this study demonstrates potential as a novel multifunctional material and has been successfully applied for real-time monitoring of fish freshness.

3.3. Application of Red Cabbage-Based Intelligent/Active Composite Films for Monitoring Fish Freshness

Fish are prone to spoilage during storage due to enzymatic reactions and microbial contamination, which not only leads to a decline in nutritional value but may also cause foodborne illnesses, posing a threat to consumer health. Therefore, freshness is widely regarded as one of the key indicators determining fish quality and consumer acceptance [73]. Based on the pH-sensing properties of purple cabbage intelligent/active composite films, this study applies them to the real-time monitoring of fish spoilage processes and comprehensively evaluates the degree of fish spoilage using indicators such as total viable count (TVC), total volatile basic nitrogen (TVB-N), and pH value. Figure 10 shows the application procedure of the red cabbage-based intelligent/active composite film in fish freshness monitoring, as well as the color changes of the film during fish storage.

Color change of red cabbage-based intelligent/active composite film during fish storage.

3.3.1. Changes in Total Volatile Basic Nitrogen (TVB-N) in Fish

TVB-N content is an important parameter for assessing the freshness of fish, primarily composed of ammonia and amine compounds. These substances accumulate post-mortem and serve as characteristic indicators of fish quality. During storage, the enzymatic decomposition of trimethylamine oxide by specific spoilage organisms, along with increased microbial activity and other biochemical reactions, leads to a gradual rise in volatile nitrogenous compounds, thereby accelerating fish spoilage [74]. As shown in Figure 11 and Figure 12 under 25 °C storage conditions, the TVB-N value of fish increased continuously over time. According to previous studies, the TVB-N threshold for fresh fish products is typically set at 20 mg/100 g. In this experiment, the initial TVB-N value of the fish was 6.30 mg/100 g, indicating high initial freshness. As storage time extended, the TVB-N value reached 12.17 mg/100 g at 24 h and further increased to 21.25 mg/100 g at 36 h, indicating that the samples had entered an unacceptably fresh state. The experimental group packaged with red cabbage intelligent/active composite film exhibited significantly lower TVB-N values compared to the control group. This result demonstrates that the composite film effectively inhibits the formation of volatile nitrogenous compounds, thereby delaying the increase in TVB-N values and maintaining fish freshness to some extent.

3.3.2. Changes in Total Viable Count (TVC) of Fish

As shown in Figure 11 and Figure 12 under 25 °C storage conditions, the total viable count (TVC) of fish increased continuously with prolonged storage time. According to relevant literature [75], the TVC limit for fresh fish products is 7 lg CFU/g. In this experiment, the initial TVC value of the fish was 4.95 lg CFU/g, indicating that the samples were relatively fresh. After 24 h of storage, the TVC value increased to 6.75 lg CFU/g. After 36 h of storage, the TVC value increased to 8.21 lg CFU/g, exceeding the aforementioned limit and indicating that the fish was no longer fresh. The time point at which TVC exceeded the limit differed from the time point at which TVB-N exceeded its threshold. This lag phenomenon may be attributed to the delayed accumulation of volatile nitrogenous compounds relative to initial microbial growth, as their production is directly related to microbial activity and population expansion.

3.3.3. Changes in pH of Fish

As shown in Figure 11 and Figure 12 during storage, the pH values of fish in both the control group and the red cabbage-based intelligent/active composite film experimental group gradually increased with prolonged storage time. However, the pH values in the composite film experimental group were consistently lower than those in the control group. The increase in pH of fish meat during storage may be caused by ammonia production via protein degradation and the continuous accumulation of metabolites from spoilage microorganisms [76]. These results indicate that the red cabbage-based intelligent/active composite film exerted a significant antibacterial effect during storage, thereby delaying the spoilage process of the fish. This finding aligns with previous studies on the storage properties of fish samples at 25 °C. Based on the Wu et al. [10] results with respect to the fish preservation application results of a red cabbage-based membrane without the previous addition of clove essential oil, combined with the threshold and dynamic changes of TVC and TVB-N, the key corruption period at room temperature is about 18–30 h, which is consistent with the findings of Zhang et al. [77]. In this experiment, the red cabbage intelligent/active composite membrane with clove essential oil added can effectively inhibit the increase in TVC, TVB-N and pH during the storage process. It can be inferred that the key corruption period at room temperature is about 26–35 h, which effectively prolongs the shelf life of fish. Numerous studies have confirmed the significant antibacterial activity of clove essential oil, highlighting its potential as a natural preservative, particularly in the preservation of meat products [77]. This study further demonstrates that incorporating clove essential oil into the red cabbage-based intelligent indicator film system significantly enhances its antibacterial properties against fish, thereby improving overall preservation effectiveness.

3.3.4. Color Difference and Color Change Results of Red Cabbage-Based Intelligent/Active Composite Film

The changes in TVC, TVB-N, and pH values of fish during storage, as well as the visual color and corresponding color parameters of the red cabbage-based intelligent/active composite film, are shown in Figure 10 and Figure 11. As illustrated in Figure 10, the color of the indicator film changed significantly over storage time, as it transitions from the initial purple to purplish-pink at 24 h, turns blue-green at 36 h, and finally becomes green at 48 h. This color evolution closely corresponded with the spoilage process of the fish. The TVC value of the fish sample exceeded the acceptable threshold at around 36 h, while the TVB-N value surpassed the limit at around 36 h. In terms of color parameters, the a* value of the composite film gradually decreased with storage time, while the b* value and total color difference (ΔE) continuously increased over 48 h. According to previous research [70], a color change becomes perceptible to the human eye when ΔE > 5, and a ΔE > 12 indicates a highly noticeable color difference, easily recognizable even by non-professionals. In this study, the ΔE value of the film reached 11.69 at 12 h of storage and increased further to 41.73 by 24 h, indicating that the color change was very easily detectable by consumers. The significant color transition point (ΔE > 12) observed around 24 h aligned with the fish spoilage stage determined based on physicochemical indicators in Figure 11. Compared with the previous research results of Wu et al. [10], the introduction of clove essential oil into the red cabbage intelligent/active composite membrane can not only delay the corruption of fish, but also have the effect of discoloration. Therefore, these results demonstrate that the intelligent/active composite film, using anthocyanin-rich red cabbage slurry as the matrix and loaded with clove essential oil, can achieve real-time monitoring of fish freshness through visible color changes, showing potential for application as a colorimetric pH indicator film in intelligent packaging.

4. Conclusions

This study successfully developed a smart/active composite film based on red cabbage loaded with clove essential oil and evaluated its application in monitoring and maintaining fish freshness. The film exhibited excellent pH-sensitive colorimetric properties, transitioning from purple to green as pH increased, which closely corresponded to the spoilage process of fish. The incorporation of clove essential oil not only improved the mechanical properties of the film but also endowed it with significant antioxidant and antibacterial activities. Fish preservation experiments confirmed that the film effectively inhibited the growth of total bacterial colonies in fish samples, reduced the accumulation of volatile basic nitrogen, and delayed the increase in pH. The color changes of the film were clearly visible and strongly correlated with key freshness indicators, demonstrating its potential as a visual freshness sensor. This study provides a feasible strategy for developing multifunctional packaging materials that combine real-time monitoring and active preservation, offering a sustainable and effective solution for extending the shelf life and ensuring the safety of perishable foods.
