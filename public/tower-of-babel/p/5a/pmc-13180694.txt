Ecopipam, a Dopamine D1 Receptor Antagonist: Pharmacokinetics, Mass Balance, and Metabolism in Healthy Volunteers
Authors: Virginia D Schmith, Stephen English, Angela Dymond, Catherine Holdsworth, Ruth Stow, Emma Holbrook, Joy Schleyer, Stephen P Wanaski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEcopipam is a dopamine‐1 (D1) receptor antagonist in development for Tourette syndrome. In vitro data showed that ecopipam is metabolized to ecopipam glucuronide through uridine diphosphate‐glucuronosyltransferase 1A9 and to EBS‐101‐40853 (previously called N‐desmethylecopipam or SCH 40853) through cytochrome P450 3A4. This open‐label, non‐randomized, mass balance study investigated metabolism and elimination pathways of ecopipam. A single oral dose of ecopipam 179.2 mg containing 88.5 μCi (3.27 MBq) of [14C]‐labeled ecopipam was administered to 8 healthy males. Total radioactivity, ecopipam, and ecopipam metabolite concentrations were measured in blood, plasma, urine, and feces periodically until discharge (between Days 8 and 15 postdose). Pooled plasma was used to identify and quantify unknown metabolites. Overall, 83.3% of radioactivity was recovered in urine as metabolites (< 1% as ecopipam), and 8.27% was excreted in feces (6.43% as ecopipam). Ecopipam glucuronide accounted for 80.0% of radioactivity in plasma and 66.9% of radioactivity in urine. EBS‐101‐40853 accounted for 10.5% of the plasma AUC∞ for ecopipam. Geometric mean half‐lives of ecopipam, EBS‐101‐40853, and total radioactivity in plasma were 17.3, 25.6, and 94.1 h, respectively. Uncharacterized metabolites P3 and P5, ‘corrected’ for extraction efficiency, had plasma half‐lives (97.4 and 122 h, respectively) similar to total radioactivity. Unknown P3, P4, and P5 metabolites accounted for 4.62%, 0.243%, and 6.03%, respectively, of plasma radioactivity. Ecopipam is primarily metabolized to ecopipam glucuronide, with only 10.5% metabolized to EBS‐101‐40853. The long half‐life of plasma radioactivity was attributed to previously uncharacterized metabolites that each accounted for < 10% of radioactivity and were considered not clinically relevant.Keywords: dopamine antagonists, drug metabolism, ecopipam, glucuronidation, unidentified metabolites

Introduction

Tourette syndrome (TS) is a chronic neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by motor and vocal tics that can substantially diminish quality of life [1, 2]. Ecopipam (previously referred to as EBS‐101 or SCH 39166) is a first‐in‐class, potent, selective D1 receptor (D1R) antagonist currently in late‐stage clinical development for the treatment of TS. A phase 2b trial of 153 patients aged ≥ 6 to < 18 years with TS showed significant improvements in tic severity scores following 12 weeks of treatment with oral ecopipam tablets (1.8 mg/kg/day, using a clinical weight‐based dosing paradigm) compared with placebo [3]. Tic severity and quality of life improvements were also observed during 12 months of ecopipam treatment in an open‐label extension (OLE) study [4]. Ecopipam had an acceptable safety profile and was well tolerated in both trials, with most adverse events (AEs) being mild or moderate in intensity [3, 4]. The most commonly reported AEs among patients receiving ecopipam in the phase 2b trial were headache, insomnia, fatigue, and somnolence [3] and in the open‐label extension were nasopharyngitis, anxiety, depression, diarrhea, headache, and insomnia [4]. Neither study demonstrated clinically significant weight gain nor changes in electrocardiogram (ECG) measurements, with very low rates of drug‐induced movement disorders (OLE only: Tremor (1.7%), akathisia (0.8%), tongue biting (0.8%), and joint lock (0.8%)), suggesting that ecopipam may have safety advantages over currently approved pharmacotherapies for TS [3, 4, 5, 6].

Radiolabeled mass balance studies are needed to characterize the absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion of an investigational compound in humans and animals, as well as to identify and quantify primary metabolites and their pharmacokinetics (PK) [7, 8]. Unpublished in vitro data suggested that ecopipam is primarily (95%) metabolized in humans by uridine diphosphate‐glucuronosyltransferase 1A9 (UGT1A9) to ecopipam glucuronide, with a minor metabolism pathway (5%) forming an active metabolite, EBS‐101‐40853 (also referred to as N‐desmethylecopipam or SCH 40853), through cytochrome P450 3A4 (CYP3A4). The objectives of this study were to determine the PK, mass balance, elimination pathways, and metabolite profile of ecopipam by measuring radioactivity and unchanged drug in whole blood, plasma, urine, and feces after a single oral dose of [14C]‐labeled ecopipam in healthy volunteers.

Methods

The study protocol and informed consent form were approved by the North East York Research Ethics Committee (London, UK) and LabCorp Drug Development Clinical Research Unit Radiation Safety Committee (Burlington, NC, USA). The study was conducted in compliance with the Guideline for Good Clinical Practice of the International Council for Harmonization, the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and local regulations. All participants provided written informed consent. The study was conducted at Covance Clinical Research Unit (Leeds, UK).

Participants

Eligible participants were males aged 35 to 55 years who had a body mass index between 18 and 30 kg/m2, ≥ 1 bowel movement daily, and were considered healthy based on medical history, physical examination, laboratory evaluations, vital signs, and ECGs. Key exclusion criteria included a history of alcohol or drug abuse, positive alcohol or drug screen, positive hepatitis panel or HIV test, participation in an investigational drug study during the previous 3 months, lifetime history of suicide attempt, history of active suicidal thoughts within the previous 5 years, and exposure (diagnostic, therapeutic, or occupational) to significant radiation.

Study Design

This was a phase 1, open‐label, non‐randomized study (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT04881955). On Day 1 (after ≤ 28 days of screening), participants were admitted to the clinical research unit and received a single oral dose of ecopipam 179.2 mg that contained 88.5 μCi (3.27 MBq) of [14C]‐ecopipam. Participants were discharged between Day 8 and Day 15, when plasma radioactivity levels were below the limit of quantitation for 2 consecutive collections, ≥ 90% of the administered radioactivity had been recovered in combined excreta (urine and feces), and the 24 h total radioactivity in combined excreta was ≤ 1% of the total administered dose for 2 consecutive collections.

Dose Rationale

To keep within the “As Low As Reasonably Achievable” principle, doses of radioactivity are typically targeted at an effective dose of 0.1 to 1.0 mSv (International Commission on Radiological Protection Risk Category IIa). Based on unpublished rodent quantitative whole‐body autoradiography data, administration of a single 88.5‐μCi (3.27‐MBq) oral dose of [14C]‐ecopipam to healthy male volunteers was expected to deliver an effective dose of 1.0 mSv, thus not posing a substantial radiation exposure risk to study participants.

Sample Collection and Assessments

Serial blood samples were collected for quantification of radioactivity in plasma and blood and ecopipam and EBS‐101‐40853 concentrations in plasma at predose; 0.5, 1.5, 2, 2.5, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 16, 24, 36, 48, 72, 96, 120, 144, and 168 h postdose; and 24‐h intervals thereafter until discharge. Blood samples were collected for metabolite profiling and identification in plasma at 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 24, 36, 48, 72, and 96 h postdose and 24‐h intervals thereafter until discharge. Urine was collected for quantification of radioactivity, ecopipam concentration, and EBS‐101‐40853 concentration at predose; 3 intervals (0 to 6, 6 to 12, and 12 to 24 h) postdose; and 24‐h intervals thereafter until discharge. Feces were collected for quantification of total radioactivity, ecopipam concentrations, and metabolite identification at predose (−24 to 0 h) and 24‐h intervals postdose until discharge. Safety assessment included treatment‐emergent AE monitoring, vital sign measurements, clinical laboratory evaluations (screening/baseline, 24 h postdose, discharge/early termination), 12‐lead ECG (baseline, 2 h postdose, discharge/early termination), and physical examinations.

Analytic Methods

Ecopipam and EBS‐101‐40853 were quantified in plasma and urine using liquid–liquid extraction and liquid chromatography (LC) followed by tandem mass spectrometry (MS–MS), with deuterium‐labeled ecopipam and EBS‐101‐40853 as internal standards. The quantification ranges were 0.5 to 500 ng/mL for ecopipam and 10 to 10,000 pg/mL for EBS‐101‐40853 (Table S1).

Total radioactivity in plasma and urine was determined by liquid scintillation counting. Total radioactivity in blood and feces was determined after an aqueous homogenate was analyzed using combustion analysis followed by liquid scintillation counting. Radioactivity in each sample was measured for 5 min using a Packard Tri‐Carb liquid scintillation counter Model 2100TR, 2900TR, or 3100TR (Canberra Packard, Pangbourne, Berks, UK) capable of computing quench‐corrected disintegrations per minute. The limit of quantitation for each batch of samples analyzed by direct counting was defined as twice the mean background disintegration rate obtained from vials containing liquid scintillant.

Due to the low levels of radioactivity in the plasma sample, plasma profiling was performed separately using accelerator mass spectrometry (AMS). Individual subject samples were thawed, vortex‐mixed at ~1,500 rpm, and pooled across all subjects using an area under the curve (AUC) approach (AUC0‐336), prior to AMS. Samples for plasma profiling (7 μL per timpoint) were extracted using acetonitrile, vortexed for 15 min, sonicated for 5 min, and centrifuged at 3200 × g (4°C) for 10 min; the supernatant was transferred to a microcentrifuge tube and 1,500 μL was added to the plasma pellet; this process was repeated, and then the supernatants were combined during evaporation to complete dryness under a stream of N2 gas. The dried extracts were reconstituted with acetonitrile: Water (10:90, v/v, 750 μL). The LC method used a 10‐mM ammonium formate +0.1% formic acid/acetonitrile +0.1% formic acid gradient as the mobile phase and an ACE Excel C18‐PFP 3‐μm high‐performance liquid chromatography column, with a flow rate of 1 mL/min at 45°C. Plasma extracts (10 μL) were injected into the LC, and the LC eluate was collected as a series of fractions across the entire LC run. These were then analyzed using AMS using the methods outlined in Table S2.

The initial pool (AUC0‐336) assumes that AUC from time 0 to time infinity (AUCinf) after a single dose is equivalent to AUC during a dosing interval at steady‐state (AUCtau), which is true for ecopipam and its known metabolites. However, a long half‐life of radioactivity of 94 h was observed. Therefore, additional post hoc analyses were conducted to characterize the time course of unknown metabolites. First, plasma samples were pooled across participants at specific timepoints (2, 4, 8, 12, 24, 48, 96, 144, and 192 h postdose) using the following method. For this timepoint pooling, a constant proportion of each subject's respective 2, 4, 8, 12, 24, 48, 96, 144, and 192 h samples was combined to yield nine cross‐subject time point pools. Each pooled sample was vortex mixed for 5 min at ~1500 rpm, and extracted as described earlier. After injection into the LC, the eluate from 12 to 14 min (for ecopipam glucuronide as a control) and 15 to 20 min (for P3, P4, and P5) were collected for each timepoint and analyzed by AMS, as a semi‐quantitative method for the determination of unknown metabolite concentrations.

Results

Participants

Eight healthy adult male volunteers were enrolled. All participants were White and did not identify as Hispanic or Latino. The mean age, body weight, and body mass index were 47 years, 77.5 kg, and 24.8 kg/m2, respectively.

Excretion of Radioactivity (Mass Balance)

The mean total recovery of radioactivity in urine and feces was 91.6% of the administered dose, with 83.3% recovered in the urine (0.115% as unchanged ecopipam) and 8.27% recovered in the feces (6.43% as unchanged ecopipam). Ecopipam glucuronide in urine accounted for 66.9% of the radioactivity, while EBS‐101‐40853 in urine accounted for 0.0645% of the radioactivity.

Pharmacokinetics

The concentration‐time profiles for plasma ecopipam, plasma EBS‐101‐40853, plasma total radioactivity, and whole blood total radioactivity are shown in Figure 1. Mean plasma ecopipam concentration was substantially lower than mean total radioactivity in plasma, and the terminal phase was steeper for plasma ecopipam than for total radioactivity in plasma or blood. Mean plasma EBS‐101‐40853 concentrations were substantially lower than plasma ecopipam concentrations over time, and EBS‐101‐40853 had a slower terminal phase than ecopipam.

Concentration–time profiles of total radioactivity in whole blood, total radioactivity in plasma, plasma ecopipam concentrations, and plasma EBS‐101‐40853 concentrations over time after a single oral dose of [14C]‐ecopipam.

PK parameters of plasma ecopipam, plasma EBS‐101‐40853, and total radioactivity in whole blood and plasma are summarized in Table 1. The geometric mean ratios of the AUC∞ for ecopipam or EBS‐101‐40853 to that of total radioactivity in plasma were very low (0.0169 and 0.00178, respectively). The geometric mean ratio of the AUC∞ of EBS‐101‐40853 to that of ecopipam was 0.105. The geometric mean AUC∞ ratio of whole blood to plasma total radioactivity was 0.666, indicating that there was no preferential association of radioactivity with red blood cells. In addition, the geometric mean t 1/2s for total radioactivity in plasma and blood (94.1 h and 122 h, respectively) were notably longer than those of ecopipam (17.3 h) and EBS‐101‐40853 (25.6 h) observed in the present study and ecopipam glucuronide (13.2 to 13.5 h) observed previously in healthy adults (NCT04902105) [9]. Hence, the long plasma total radioactivity indicated the presence of other persistent circulating metabolites.

Metabolite Identification and Profiling

Plasma samples were pooled across participants and timepoints in this study using a Hamilton AUC pooling strategy [10]. The resultant extract generated a radiochromatogram, with the percentage of radioactivity accounted for by each analyte illustrated in Figure 2. Most of the radioactivity in plasma (80.0%) was accounted for by ecopipam glucuronide, with 2.66% of the radioactivity identified as ecopipam, 0.260% identified as EBS‐101‐40853, and 3.09% identified as EBS‐101‐40853 glucuronide. There were 3 peaks representing unidentified metabolites in plasma (P3, P4, and P5), which accounted for 2.27%, 2.82%, and 8.62% of radioactivity, respectively. Given the long t 1/2 of plasma total radioactivity, it was hypothesized that one of these unknown metabolites may account for the long terminal phase of radioactivity.

Radiochromatogram of pooled plasma after a single oral dose of [14C]‐ecopipam. ROI, region of interest.

Post hoc analyses were performed to investigate whether one or more of these unknown metabolites accounted for > 10% of the total radioactivity in plasma. First, curve stripping [11] was conducted and showed that the AUC of persisting radioactivity accounted for 39.4% of the overall plasma total radioactivity AUC∞ (data not shown). Secondly, plasma samples were pooled across participants at specific time points, effluent was collected at specific intervals during LC MS–MS, and radioactivity was measured to allow a semi‐quantitative time course for these three unknown metabolites that could verify that the single‐dose AUC∞ (from pooled plasma) was equal to AUC from time zero to the end of the dosing interval at steady state (where metabolites may accumulate).

Figure 3 shows the ‘corrected’ and ‘uncorrected’ plasma concentration–time data for P3, P4, and P5, and Table 2 summarizes the PK parameters estimated from ‘corrected’ and ‘uncorrected’ concentration–time profiles for P3, P4, and P5. The decline in concentrations over time was reasonably similar between P3 and P5 (Figure 3) based on ‘corrected’ t 1/2 (97.4 h and 122 h, respectively) and ‘uncorrected’ t 1/2 (35.8 h and 38.1 h, respectively; Table 2), whereas P4 concentrations were lower and declined more rapidly over time (‘corrected’ t 1/2, 26.0 h; ‘uncorrected’ t 1/2, 17.7 h). As the ‘corrected’ t 1/2 for P3 and P5 most closely resembled that observed for plasma total radioactivity (94.1 h), ‘corrected’ parameters were considered more accurate than ‘uncorrected’ parameters. Additionally, these data indicate that P3 and P5 in plasma may explain the long terminal phase of radioactivity. The ‘corrected’ ARpred values for P3, P4, and P5 were 6.92, 1.49, and 5.74, respectively, which are substantially larger than the accumulation ratio for ecopipam [1]. The estimated ‘corrected’ ratios of AUC∞ (equivalent to predicted area under the concentration vs. time curve for the dosing interval [AUC(0‐τ)] at steady state) for P3, P4, and P5 relative to plasma total radioactivity were 4.62%, 0.243%, and 6.03%, respectively. Thus, none of these metabolites accounted for > 10% of the total radioactivity in plasma.

Safety

Overall, 3 participants (37.5%) experienced a total of 7 AEs (agitation, depressed mood, diarrhea, chills, fatigue, dizziness, and headache; each reported in 1 participant [12.5%]) that were mild in intensity and considered by the investigator to be possibly or probably related to ecopipam (Table S4). There were no serious AEs during the study, and no participants discontinued due to an AE. No clinically significant abnormalities or trends in clinical laboratory evaluations, vital signs measurements, ECGs, or physical examinations were observed.

Discussion

This mass balance study of 8 healthy volunteers who received a single oral dose of [14C] ecopipam showed that ecopipam was extensively metabolized, with metabolites primarily excreted in the urine (83.3%), with only 8.27% of radioactivity recovered in feces. The majority (66.9%) of radioactivity in urine was accounted for by ecopipam glucuronide. Neither renal nor biliary elimination of unchanged ecopipam was considered an important elimination pathway, with < 1% and 6% of radioactivity in urine and feces, respectively, accounted for by ecopipam. Neither ecopipam nor EBS‐101‐40853 was the primary drug‐related material in systemic circulation, accounting for only 1.69% and 0.178% of the radioactivity in plasma. These data are consistent with the low oral bioavailability of ecopipam and extensive first‐pass metabolism after oral ecopipam administration.

The proposed metabolic pathway for ecopipam is illustrated in Figure 4, which combines the results from this clinical study with unpublished in vitro data. Ecopipam is primarily metabolized by UGT1A9 to form ecopipam glucuronide (80.0% of the total plasma radioactivity and 66.9% of total urine radioactivity). Only 10.5% of ecopipam is metabolized through CYP3A4 to form EBS‐101‐40853 based on relative AUC∞ values; this estimate is more accurate than the in vitro estimate of 5%. EBS‐101‐40853 is then metabolized by UGT1A9 into EBS‐101‐40853 glucuronide. Three unknown metabolites (P3, P4, and P5 [P5 was subsequently identified as EBS‐101‐40853 sulfate]) were also identified in post hoc analyses; each of these accounted for < 10% of the total radioactivity in plasma. Using the pooled plasma AUC0‐336, the EBS‐101‐40853 metabolic pathway included approximately 12% of the radioactivity (0.26% as EBS‐101‐40853, 3.09% as EBS‐101‐40853 glucuronide, and 8.62% as EBS‐101‐40853 sulfate) and does not meet the category for a major metabolite.

Single‐dose administration of ecopipam was well tolerated, with all treatment‐emergent AEs (agitation, depressed mood, diarrhea, chills, fatigue, dizziness, and headache; each reported in 1 participant [12.5%]) being mild in intensity and none leading to study discontinuation. The tolerability profile of ecopipam reported in this study was consistent with data reported in another phase 1 single‐dose ecopipam study in healthy volunteers [9], as well as two phase 2b randomized controlled trials of ecopipam and a 12‐month open‐label extension in patients with TS [3, 4, 12].

The previous sponsor conducted in vitro and in vivo studies with conflicting data regarding the relative potencies of ecopipam and EBS‐101‐40853 [13, 14]. Therefore, the authors conducted in vitro assays to evaluate the potencies of ecopipam and its metabolites at the human D1R and dopamine‐5 (D5) receptor (D5R). Relative to ecopipam, the potency of antagonist activity at D1R and D5R, respectively, was 15.6% and 13.3% for EBS‐101‐40853 and 0.540% and 0.437% for ecopipam glucuronide (unpublished). In addition, EBS‐101‐40853 glucuronide was not active at the D1R or D5R. These results indicate that ecopipam, EBS‐101‐40853, and ecopipam glucuronide have the potential to contribute to the efficacy and safety of ecopipam administration.

Conclusions

In conclusion, ecopipam is extensively metabolized, with glucuronidation being the primary metabolic pathway and elimination of the metabolites through renal excretion. Only 10.5% of ecopipam is metabolized to EBS‐101‐40853. The long t 1/2 of total plasma radioactivity was consistent with the t 1/2s of the unknown metabolites P3 and P5 (subsequently identified as EBS‐101‐40853 sulfate). Importantly, P3, P4 (unknown), and P5 each accounted for < 10% of radioactivity in plasma and were not clinically relevant.
