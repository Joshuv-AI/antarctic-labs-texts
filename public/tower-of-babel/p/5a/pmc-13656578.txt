A pan-genomic perspective: comprehensive dissection of the FIG superfamily unravels its evolution, expansion, and environmental adaptation in wheat
Authors: Hui Wang, Jingjing Liang, Xiao Li, Guoteng Ou, Jiaheng Xing, Yan Lang, Yonghai Fan, Xiaowei Shi, Ruina Liu, Lili Wang, Yuchen Rao, Haoru Guan, Mengqi Qu, Jiaming Song, Kun Lu, Mingqi Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAs a major global food crop, wheat faces dual pressures from population growth and deteriorating agricultural environments in efforts to increase its yield. The members of the FIG superfamily are associated with photosynthetic carbon metabolism and stress-responsive pathways in plants.ResultsIn this study, we conducted the first systematic pan-genomic analysis to investigate the evolution and function of the FIG superfamily in wheat. The results revealed that this family underwent significant expansion during plant evolution from aquatic to terrestrial habitats and from lower to higher forms, with functional divergence apparently predating the green algal stage as inferred from phylogenetic patterns. The members of this family were primarily derived from three ancestral species, and their expansion was largely driven by whole-genome duplication. Most members were found to be under purifying selection, whereas the TaVTC4-4B was subjected to positive selection. This gene is constitutively highly expressed in green tissues, and its promoter is enriched with cis-regulatory elements associated with light responsiveness, JA/ABA signaling, and stress responses. Expression analysis indicated its strong responsiveness to salt stresses.ConclusionsThis study elucidates the evolutionary trajectory and functional landscape of the wheat FIG superfamily, laying a theoretical foundation for the potential genetic improvement of photosynthetic efficiency and stress resilience in wheat.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09530-6.Keywords: Wheat, FIG superfamily, Evolutionary analysis, Pan-genome, Pan-transcriptome, Abiotic stress response

Introduction

Wheat (Triticum aestivum) is a major global staple crop, providing a significant share of the world’s dietary energy and protein [1]. Accordingly, increasing yield has long been a key objective in wheat breeding [2, 3]. Carbon and nitrogen metabolism are central biochemical pathways that govern wheat growth and development [4, 5]. These processes interact through both synergy and competition, collectively regulating plant development, and are influenced by genetic and environmental factors [6]. Therefore, concurrent improvement of photosynthetic performance and stress adaptation represents a critical strategy for yield enhancement. However, the allohexaploid nature of wheat, along with its large genome and high repetitive DNA content, has presented considerable challenges for genetic research and breeding applications [7]. Recent advances in sequencing technologies have enabled the construction of high-quality reference genomes and pan-genomes [8–11]. Large-scale transcriptomic studies have further elucidated the expression patterns of homologous genes across various developmental stages and stress conditions, providing a solid foundation for in-depth functional studies [12]. To advance wheat research, the Wheat Multiomics platform has been established as an integrated resource, strengthening the bioinformatics foundation for genetics, genomics, and breeding efforts [13].

The FIG domain (FBPase/IMPase/glpX-like) is a key feature of a metal-dependent phosphatase superfamily that acts on various substrates and is often inhibited by Li⁺ [14]. A prominent enzyme in this superfamily is fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase (FBPase), found in both classical and glpX-encoded forms. This enzyme catalyzes the hydrolysis of FBPase to fructose-6-phosphate, an essential step in gluconeogenesis [14]. In plants, FBPase has two main isoforms: the plastidic type, mainly located in chloroplasts, which supports the Calvin cycle and photosynthetic carbon fixation; and the cytosolic type, involved in gluconeogenesis and crucial for sucrose biosynthesis [15]. The cytosolic FBPase also plays a major regulatory role in photosynthetic carbon partitioning, aiding sucrose translocation from source leaves to sink tissues like roots, fruits, and seeds [16–18]. Another key enzyme in this superfamily is inositol monophosphatase (IMPase), which hydrolyzes inositol monophosphate esters to produce free myo-inositol (MI) and inorganic phosphate (Pi) [19–22]. Free inositol serves as a versatile metabolic precursor with several essential roles [23]. It aids in the synthesis of galacturonic acid and galactose, key components of cell wall polysaccharides like pectin and hemicellulose, supporting structural integrity and cell adhesion [24–26]. Additionally, it acts as a precursor for signaling phosphoinositides (such as IP₃ and IP₆) that mediate Ca2⁺ and hormone-dependent pathways [27–29]. Inositol can also be further phosphorylated to form phytic acid (phytate), the main storage compound for phosphorus and minerals in seeds [30, 31]. During germination, phytic acid is degraded to provide phosphate for developing seedlings [32]. Furthermore, inositol serves as a biosynthetic precursor for trehalose and raffinose family oligosaccharides (RFOs), which enhance tolerance to abiotic stresses, including drought, high salinity, and cold, by acting as osmoprotectants and antioxidants [33, 34].

The FIG superfamily shows considerable promise for improving agronomic traits and enhancing stress resistance in crops. High biomass serves as a prerequisite for high yield, and increasing photosynthetic efficiency promotes dry matter accumulation in plants [35, 36]. In Arabidopsis, overexpression of FBPase genes enhances plant height, leaf number, chlorophyll content, water use efficiency, and stomatal conductance, thereby boosting photosynthetic performance [37]. By contrast, FBPase mutants show reduced biomass and compromised photosynthesis [38]. Similarly, in rice, mutations in MOC2 lead to inadequate sucrose supply and suppression of axillary bud growth [39, 40]. Expression of a salt-tolerant chloroplast FBPase in rice has been demonstrated to improve photosynthetic efficiency, increase yield, and confer broad stress tolerance [41]. Additionally, overexpression of the OsIMP gene elevates inositol levels and enhances cold tolerance in tobacco by regulating antioxidant enzyme activities [42]. In higher plants, MI fulfills diverse functions in growth and development, including the maintenance of membrane integrity, serving as an osmoprotectant, and facilitating responses to environmental stresses [43, 44]. For example, MI is involved in salicylic acid-mediated programmed cell death and pathogen defense in Arabidopsis [45, 46]. Moreover, elevated MI accumulation under cold and salt stress highlights its protective role against abiotic challenges [47, 48]. Furthermore, various MI-derived metabolites, such as ononitol, pinitol, phytate, phosphatidylinositol, galactinol, and RFOs, contribute to phosphate storage, auxin signaling, cell communication, and stress adaptation in plants [19, 49–54].

Previous studies have established that the FIG superfamily plays important roles in plant carbon metabolism, signal transduction, and stress responses. FBPase, in particular, has been shown to promote stress resilience and growth maintenance under adverse conditions [55–57], yet its functional characterization in crops remains sparse. To address this gap, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of the wheat FIG superfamily. Through these multi-dimensional approaches, we aimed to elucidate the roles of this gene superfamily in key physiological processes such as abiotic stress in wheat. Our study not only helps to address the current lack of knowledge regarding the FIG superfamily in major crops but also enhances the understanding of abiotic stress in plants. Furthermore, this work offers a theoretical and genetic basis for developing strategies to improve wheat yield and stress resilience via gene editing and molecular breeding.

Materials and methods

Identification and characterization of the FIG superfamily

Reference genome sequences, corresponding proteomes, and functional annotation files were obtained from the JGI Genome Portal (https://phytozome-next.jgi.doe.gov/) and Ensembl Plants database (https://plants.ensembl.org/index.html) [58, 59]. The Triticum aestivum reference genome used in this study was the IWGSC RefSeq v1.0 (and its subsequent annotation v1.0) from Ensembl Plants. Member of the Arabidopsis FIG superfamily was identified through hidden Markov model (HMM)-based searches using the canonical FBPase_N, FBPase_C, FBPase_glpX, and Inositol_P profiles (PF00316, PF18913, PF03320, and PF00459) from the Pfam database. Computational screening was performed using the hmmsearch tool in TBtools-II software with stringent parameters (E-value < 1 × 10–5) to ensure robust identification of FIG homologs [60]. A multi-step validation pipeline was implemented as followed: (i) Primary screening: domain architecture verification using the SMART database (v9.0) to exclude false-positive matches [61]; (ii) Transcript processing: retention of the longest coding sequence per locus, with all alternative splice variants excluded, which is consistent with the definition of canonical transcripts adopted by Ensembl; (iii) Final yield: four phylogenetically conserved FIG genes were identified in the Arabidopsis genome, representing the complete complement of this gene family.

The identical analytical pipeline was subsequently applied to Porphyra umbilicalis, Dunaliella salina, Chlamydomonas reinhardtii, Ostreococcus lucimarinus, Botryococcus braunii, Marchantia polymorpha, Physcomitrella patens, Selaginella moellendorffii, Ceratopteris richardii, Diphasiastrum complanatum, Thuja plicata, Amborella trichopoda, Oryza sativa, Hordeum vulgare, Setaria italica, Setaria viridis, Aegilops tauschii, Triticum dicoccoides, Triticum urartu, Zea mays, Nicotiana attenuate, Glycine max, Solanum lycopersicum, and Triticum aestivum [58]. Cross-species protein sequence alignments (E-value threshold < 1 × 10–10) enabled systematic identification of FIG gene superfamily members in these species [62]. Similarly, a comprehensive identification of the FIG superfamily members was conducted across 17 wheat pangenomes [10].

Naming of FIG genes

A unified nomenclature system was established for the FIG genes encompassing investigated species, including angiosperms, monocots, and eudicots. This nomenclature integrates both subfamily classification and protein sequence alignment results. Each gene designation combines the abbreviated species name with the name of its Arabidopsis homologous gene (e.g., GmCYFBP). Genes belonging to the same subfamily were consecutively numbered (e.g., GmCYFBP1-1, GmCYFBP1-2, and GmCYFBP1-3). Given that wheat is an allohexaploid species, subgenome information was incorporated into its gene nomenclature while adhering to the above conventions (e.g., TaCYFBP-1D.1 and TaCYFBP-1D.2). Due to the limited homology between the FIG genes of gymnosperms, ferns, bryophytes, algae, protists and those of Arabidopsis, these taxa were excluded from the unified nomenclature system.

Multiple sequence alignment and phylogenetic tree construction

A total of 343 FIG protein sequences were aligned using MUSCLE with default parameters [63, 64]. Phylogenetic reconstruction was performed using the neighbor-joining method in MEGA12, with 1,000 bootstrap replicates to assess nodal support [65]. Note that while neighbor‑joining is widely used for its speed, it has known limitations compared to maximum likelihood, including sensitivity to rate variation and long‑branch artifacts. The resulting phylogenetic tree was annotated and visualized using the Interactive Tree of Life platform (iTOL v6.7.4) [61].

Collinearity analysis and selection pressure analysis

We employed TBtools [60, 66] to investigate the interspecific synteny between modern wheat varieties and their ancestral species. Based on the identified collinear gene pairs, a positive selection analysis was performed by calculating the nonsynonymous-to-synonymous substitution ratio (Ka/Ks). Gene pairs with a Ka/Ks ratio exceeding 1 were considered to be under positive selection. To infer the potential biological functions of these positively selected genes, their encoded protein sequences were used as queries to conduct a BLASTP search against the Arabidopsis reference proteome (TAIR, Araport11). The search parameters were set with thresholds of ≥ 80% sequence identity and E-value ≤ 1e-5.

Cis-regulatory element profiling in putative promoter regions

The cis-regulatory architecture of promoter regions plays a critical role in determining spatiotemporal gene expression patterns and functional diversification (Veitia, 2025). To elucidate the transcriptional regulatory network underlying TaFIG gene superfamily expression, 2,000 bp promoter sequences upstream of the translation initiation site were extracted from the IWGSC RefSeq v2.1 genome assembly [67]. Putative cis-regulatory motifs were identified in silico using PlantCARE with stringent parameters (E-value < 1 × 10–5), followed by manual curation of known plant cis-element signatures [68].

Analyzing high selection pressure genes using pan-transcriptomics

Expression data across various tissues, developmental stages, and treatments were obtained from the WheatOmics and Wheat Expression Database (https://www.wheat-expression.com/), with heatmap visualization performed using TBtools [60].

Expression profiling of FIG superfamily via quantitative real-time PCR

Seeds of six wheat cultivars (Ningmai13, Jimai23, Yannong1212, Zhengmai366, Jinmai47, and Xinong928) were surface-sterilized, rinsed, and germinated on wet filter paper. After two weeks of growth, uniform seedlings were subjected to either salt stress (200 mM NaCl) or drought stress (withholding water) for 10 consecutive days; control plants received an equivalent volume of water (without NaCl) at 2‑day intervals to maintain normal growth. Leaf tissues were collected, frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C. Total RNA was extracted from the samples, and first‑strand cDNA was synthesized. Quantitative real‑time PCR (qRT‑PCR) was performed using gene‑specific primers for FIG superfamily members and TaActin, an internal reference gene (Table S1). Relative expression levels were calculated using the comparative Ct method. All experiments were performed with three biological replicates.

Protein domain analysis using PROSITE

To validate the functional annotation of TaVTC4-4B at the protein level, the deduced amino acid sequences of TaVTC4-4B and its homoeologs were submitted to the PROSITE database (https://prosite.expasy.org/) using the ScanProsite tool [69]. The analysis was performed with default parameters to identify conserved protein domains, families, and functional sites. Only matches with an E‑value < 0.01 and a PROSITE profile score above the threshold were considered significant. The identified domains were cross‑referenced with the PROSITE documentation to confirm their functional associations.

Results

Phylogenetic analysis of FIG superfamily

To elucidate the evolutionary history of the FIG supergene family, we selected 25 plant genomes representing a broad spectrum of phylogenetic diversity, spanning from early-diverging lineages to highly derived species to capture a gradient of evolutionary complexity. The selected species comprised one protist (Porphyra umbilicalis), four algae (Dunaliella salina, Chlamydomonas reinhardtii, Ostreococcus lucimarinus, and Botryococcus braunii), two bryophyta (Marchantia polymorpha and Physcomitrella patens), three ferns (Selaginella moellendorffii, Ceratopteris richardii, and Diphasiastrum complanatum), one gymnosperm (Thuja plicata), one angiosperm (Amborella trichopoda), nine monocots (Oryza sativa, Triticum aestivum, Hordeum vulgare, Setaria italica, Setaria viridis, Aegilops tauschii, Triticum dicoccoides, Triticum urartu, and Zea mays), and four eudicots (Arabidopsis thaliana, Solanum lycopersicum, Nicotiana attenuate, and Glycine max). A phylogenetic tree was constructed using the FIG protein sequences from the aforementioned species.

The FIG superfamily comprises three distinct gene families: FBPase, glpX like, and IMPase. A total of 343 FIG members were identified across the 25 species and classified into three major clades: clade I (glpX like), clade II (FBPase), and clade III (IMPase), which is consistent with the established composition of the FIG gene superfamily (Fig. 1; Table 1, S2). Among the three clades, Clade II and Clade III contain three and seven distinct groups, respectively, while Clade I contain a single group comprising only two genes, both originating from the algal species Chlamydomonas reinhardtii and Botryococcus braunii (Fig. 1; Table 1). These findings may suggest that the glpX‑like gene family was not retained during the evolutionary transition from algae to bryophytes, which could account for its absence in the higher plant genomes analyzed here.

Evolutionary history and divergence of FBPase and IMPase genes

Elucidating the evolutionary dynamics of the FIG superfamily in plants would provide important insights into its role in environmental adaptation as well as the molecular mechanisms underlying plant evolution and fitness. An interesting observation was that while clade II could be divided into three groups, higher plants were classified into four groups (Fig. 1, S2). This divergence was attributed to the presence of three specific genes, ABO97968, Rna5958, and Rna10752, within Ostreococcus lucimarinus and Porphyra umbilicalis. Phylogenetic analysis placed these genes (ABO97968, Rna5958, and Rna10752) at the base of the clades containing the modern CFBP1 and CYFBP genes, and they showed the highest sequence similarity to the inferred ancestral sequences of these two subfamilies. Phylogenetic placement and sequence similarity suggest that these algal genes might represent evolutionary relicts that have largely remained undifferentiated, potentially resembling the common ancestral gene before its duplication and subsequent diversification into the CFBP1 and CYFBP lineages in higher plants.

In particular, we conducted a comprehensive phylogenetic analysis encompassing a broad spectrum of plants, ranging from early-diverging lineages to highly derived taxa. This investigation revealed previously unrecognized evolutionary patterns of FBPase genes. Consistent with previous reports indicating the emergence of the first WOX genes [70, 71], we identified the onset of novel FBPase variants (e.g., ABO96248) beginning with Ostreococcus lucimarinus, an early-diverging green alga, ultimately leading to the establishment of the FBP1 clade in plants (Figure S2). These results underscored the pivotal role of Ostreococcus lucimarinus in the evolutionary history of plants. Functional divergence between CFBP1 and CYFBP initially occurred in Botryococcus braunii, whereas the four major extant lineages of FBPase first emerged in Physcomitrella patens (Figure S2). Collectively, FBPase experienced repeated neofunctionalization events during early plant evolution, highlighting its contribution as a molecular driver of evolutionary innovation. By contrast, the SBPASE clade displayed a more conserved evolutionary trajectory throughout plant diversification (Figure S2).

Based on the phylogenetic patterns observed across diverse plant lineages, we tentatively propose an evolutionary model for the FBPase gene family (Figure S2). In this model, the ancestral FBPase gene first duplicated and diverged, giving rise to two distinct lineages: one encoding SBPASE and the other representing ancestral gene B, which may have served as the common precursor of CFBP1, CYFBP, and FBP1. Subsequent divergence of ancestral gene B in algae might have led to the formation of CFBP1, CYFBP, and FBP1, and through prolonged evolution, these lineages could have further diversified, ultimately establishing the four distinct FBPase subfamilies observed in modern plants, as exemplified in the moss Physcomitrella patens. It is important to note, however, that this model is primarily inferred from phylogenetic relationships and sequence-based observations; its validity as an evolutionary scenario awaits further functional and experimental testing.

Similarly, a stepwise phylogenetic reconstruction was performed for clade III across divergent plant lineages, from early-diverging species to angiosperms. A relatively continuous evolutionary trajectory was observed for groups V, VI, VII, X, and XI (Fig. 1, S3). On the other hand, three members of group VIII (Rna639, Dusal.0277s00016.1, and PNW80057) were exclusively identified in protists and algae, which is compatible with a possible loss of this lineage during the evolution of land plants, although alternative explanations—such as incomplete genome representation or sequence divergence—cannot be ruled out. A novel branch, group IX, emerged in gymnosperms, A novel branch, group IX, was observed to emerge in gymnosperms, which might represent a relatively recent lineage diversification that arose during the divergence of ferns and gymnosperms, possibly in response to environmental challenges.

The pan-genome analysis of the FIG superfamily of wheat

Cereal crops, such as wheat, typically grow in monospecific stands characterized by intense competition for resources. This high-density stress is likely a key driver in the evolution of regulatory mechanisms that optimize tiller (branch) development and ultimately enhance reproductive allocation and seed yield. We performed a comprehensive comparative analysis of the FIG superfamily across 21 plant accessions, encompassing three ancestral species (Triticum urartu, Triticum dicoccoides, and Aegilops tauschii) and 18 modern hexaploid wheat cultivars (Alchemy, Arinalrfor, Cadenza, Claire, Jagger, Julius, Kariega, Lancer, Landmark, Mace, Norin61, Paragon, Chinese Spring, Renan, Robigus, Stanley, Sy Mattis, and Weebill). Phylogenetic reconstruction revealed that all cultivated wheat cultivars harbor between 32 to 43 FIG superfamily members, which were distributed across ten groups (Fig. 2; Table S3). The observed disparity in gene copy number could be attributed to both variations in genome assembly quality and the natural genetic variation present among different wheat cultivars, although the relative contribution of each factor remains to be determined.

Duplication and expansion dynamics of FIG genes

Gene annotation in the wheat pan-genome ranges from 91,300 to 142,252 genes, which shows certain divergence from the reference genome (107,550 genes). Genome-wide analysis of duplication events across the pan-genome revealed that whole-genome duplication (WGD) predominates in most varieties (Fig. 4A). Notably, however, four varieties (Cadenza, Claire, Robigus, and Weebil) exhibited dispersed duplication as the major duplication type, while in Kariega, both WGD and dispersed duplication appear to be predominant (Fig. 4A). These differences in duplication patterns may be attributed to variations in genetic background.

Further analysis of duplication events within the FIG superfamily showed a consistent trend with the genome-wide pattern (Fig. 4B). Specifically, four duplication types were identified in the FIG superfamily: WGD, dispersed duplication, tandem duplication, and proximal duplication. With the exception of the four varieties (Cadenza, Claire, Robigus, and Weebil) mentioned above, which are dominated by dispersed duplication, WGD remains the predominant event in all other varieties. Among the 689 FIG members, WGD accounts for 65.6%, dispersed duplication for 30.5%, proximal duplication for 2.8%, tandem duplication for 0.58%, and 0.58% are of unknown type (Table S4).

In summary, the variation in duplication patterns among wheat varieties is consistent at both the whole-genome and FIG superfamily levels, likely driven by variety-specific genetic backgrounds and annotation heterogeneity. The distribution of duplication types in the FIG superfamily further supports that WGD serves as the primary mechanism for genome expansion in wheat, whereas the prevalence of dispersed duplication in certain varieties suggests distinct evolutionary trajectories.

Patterns of selection pressure in the FIG gene superfamily

Hexaploid wheat is widely believed to have originated from natural hybridization between Triticum dicoccoides and Aegilops tauschii. Based on this evolutionary origin, we identified homologous gene pairs through synteny analysis and calculated the Ka/Ks ratios for members of the FIG superfamily. The results showed that the majority of genes exhibited Ka/Ks values less than 1 (Fig. 4C), with only one gene, TraesCS4B02G161300 (TaVTC4‑4B), displaying a Ka/Ks ratio greater than 2. Similarly, analysis across seven wheat pan‑genomes also revealed a Ka/Ks ratio > 2 for this gene. While Ka/Ks ratios have been widely used as indicators of selection pressure, this metric reflects long‑term average selection and may not capture episodic or lineage‑specific selective events. Nevertheless, the consistently elevated Ka/Ks value observed for TaVTC4‑4B suggests that this gene may have experienced positive selection, warranting further investigation.

Further alignment of protein sequences among TaVTC4‑4B pan‑gene family members revealed two conserved sequence differences between wild emmer wheat and common wheat: a 16‑amino‑acid deletion near the translation initiation site and a 6‑amino‑acid insertion at position 36 (Fig. 4D, Figure S3). Beyond these variations, three major haplotypes were identified in wheat: haplotype I includes 16 varieties, whereas haplotypes II and III each contain one variety. Notably, all genes with Ka/Ks > 2 belong to haplotype I. Within this haplotype, both Ka/Ks > 2 and Ka/Ks < 1 genes coexist, a variation that may be attributed to differences in genetic background or gene annotation among varieties.

Analysis of promoter regions in TaVTC4-4B pan-gene family

To elucidate the regulatory mechanisms controlling FIG gene expression, we performed a comprehensive analysis of cis-regulatory elements within the promoter regions of FIG gene members in wheat (Figure S4). The cis-elements were categorized into three groups: hormone response, growth and development, and environmental signaling. The results revealed that the promoter elements of the FIG superfamily were predominantly enriched in light-responsive elements, as well as those involved in ABA and JA signaling pathways. FBPase acts as a gatekeeper that directs carbon flux from primary metabolism (photosynthesis) toward energy storage and biosynthetic pathways (gluconeogenesis) [72]. Meanwhile, IMPase functions as a recycler and amplifier within the light signaling cascade. It regenerates MI, thereby sustaining the cyclic operation of the phosphoinositide signaling pathway and ensuring efficient transduction of light signals via the IP₃-Ca2+ pathway [73–75]. This was consistent with their functional roles with respect to photosynthesis and light signaling cascade. Given that ABA mediates drought and salt tolerance while JA participates in defense signaling, these cis‑elements may enable FIG genes to modulate carbon metabolism and phosphoinositide signaling under stress. The coexistence of light‑ and hormone‑responsive elements further implies cross‑talk between photosynthesis and stress adaptation, facilitating flexible energy allocation and recovery.

To further elucidate the regulatory mechanism of TaVTC4-4B, we extracted and analyzed the 2 kb promoter sequences upstream of the start codon from its pan-gene family members. The results show that the promoter regions are primarily enriched with light-responsive elements, drought stress-responsive elements, hormone-responsive elements (e.g., ABA, JA, GA), along with a smaller number of binding sites related to cell cycle and meristem regulation (Fig. 5A). This cis‑regulatory profile indicates that the transcriptional control of TaVTC4-4B is largely driven by environmental signals, with particular emphasis on light and drought stress. The abundance of light-responsive elements suggests a potential role in photosynthesis and/or photomorphogenesis, whereas the prevalence of drought-responsive elements implies a key function in drought adaptation (Fig. 5 A; Table S5). The presence of multiple hormone-responsive elements suggests that its expression may be finely tuned by ABA, JA, and GA signaling, potentially integrating environmental and developmental cues. In contrast, the relatively few cell cycle- and meristem-associated elements are consistent with the possibility that TaVTC4-4B may be more preferentially involved in environmental adaptation than in fundamental growth and development, although this interpretation is based primarily on promoter architecture and requires additional functional evidence.

Collectively, we hypothesize that TaVTC4-4B may serve as an environmental signal integrator, potentially coordinating responses to light and drought to help plants balance photosynthetic efficiency and stress tolerance under fluctuating conditions. We emphasize, however, that this interpretation is primarily derived from the observed promoter architecture and expression patterns, and its validity as a functional role awaits direct experimental validation. This regulatory pattern aligns with the strong positive selection signal (Ka/Ks > 2) detected in its evolutionary history, together suggesting an important role in the environmental adaptation of wheat.

To further substantiate the functional inference drawn from cis‑regulatory elements, we analyzed the deduced protein sequence of TaVTC4-4B using the PROSITE database (https://prosite.expasy.org/) [69]. This analysis confirmed the presence of the conserved inositol monophosphatase (IMP) domain (PROSITE profile PS00629) in the TaVTC4-4B protein, which is the hallmark of the L-galactose‑1-phosphate phosphatase family responsible for ascorbate biosynthesis. Importantly, the stress- and hormone‑responsive cis‑elements identified in the promoter (light, drought, ABA, JA) are functionally congruent with this IMP domain. Ascorbate is a central regulator of oxidative stress responses, and its biosynthesis is transcriptionally regulated by ABA, light, and JA signaling pathways. Thus, the co‑occurrence of stress‑related cis‑elements and a conserved ascorbate‑biosynthetic domain provide convergent evidence, from both the transcriptional and protein levels, for the involvement of TaVTC4-4B in stress adaptation.

Pan-transcriptomic analysis of candidate genes in wheat

The expression of TaVTC4-4B exhibited tissue‑, cultivar‑, and stress‑dependent differences (Fig. 5B; Table S6). The transcript level was highest in leaves and displayed a diurnal rhythm, suggesting photoperiodic regulation. Salt stress induced a biphasic response in leaves, with an initial up‑regulation followed by a sharp decline at 12 h and 24 h; low‑temperature stress also reduced leaf expression (Fig. 5C). Under drought stress, leaf expression was significantly down‑regulated (Fig. 5D). Collectively, these results indicate that TaVTC4-4B expression is finely regulated by light, drought, and salt signals, and its promoter contains corresponding light‑ and drought‑responsive cis‑elements. The stress‑responsive, tissue‑specific, diurnal, and genotype‑dependent expression profile shows that TaVTC4-4B is responsive to environmental signals, which is consistent with a possible role in stress adaptation. However, expression responsiveness does not directly establish biological function; functional validation is required to confirm this hypothesis. The positive selection signature (Ka/Ks > 2) associated with this gene further highlights its potential interest.

Expression profiles of FIG superfamily members in response to salt and drought stresses

Six wheat cultivars were subjected to drought (water withdrawal) or salt stress (200 mM NaCl) for 10 days after two weeks of growth (Fig. 5E). The relative expression levels of 15 genes (including TaCFBP1-4, TaCYBP4-1, TaCYFBP-3, TaFBP1-7, TaHL-6, TaLMPL1-6, TalMPL2-3, TalMPL3, TaSAL1, TaSBPASE-3, and TaVTC4-4) were examined in leaves (Table S7). The results revealed distinct response patterns to the two stresses.

Under salt stress, most genes showed significant up‑ or down‑regulation, with consistent trends across cultivars (Fig. 5G). Specifically, TaSAL1-4A, TaSAL1-5A, TaSAL1-5B, TalMPL3-6A, TalMPL3-6B, TalMPL3-6D, TaVTC4-4A, and TaVTC4-4D were significantly up‑regulated (p < 0.05 to p < 0.001), whereas TaHL-6A, TaLMPL1-6A/B, and TaVTC4-4B were significantly down‑regulated (Table S7). Notably, TaVTC4-4B was strongly suppressed by salt stress in all cultivars, with expression dropping to 0.04–0.16‑fold of the control (p < 0.01). In comparison, drought stress did not significantly alter its expression (Fig. 5F). This gene is under positive selection (Ka/Ks > 2), and its specific down‑regulation under salt stress may reflect an adaptive response, although alternative interpretations are equally plausible. For example, the suppression could be a consequence of general stress‑induced metabolic reprogramming, or it could be mediated by upstream regulators that coordinate broader transcriptional changes. Functional assays are needed to determine whether this down‑regulation directly contributes to salt tolerance or represents a correlated response to other stress‑related pathways.

Under drought stress, most genes decreased in expression (Fig. 5F). For example, TaCFBP1-4A/B/D, TaCYFBP-3A/B/D, TaFBP1-7A/B/D, TaLMPL1-6A/B, and TaSBPASE-3A/D were significantly reduced (p < 0.05 to p < 0.01), while TaCYBP41A/B and TalMPL2-3B/D were up‑regulated. Overall, drought mainly suppressed photosynthesis‑ or carbon metabolism‑related genes, whereas salt stress both activated certain stress‑responsive genes (e.g., TaSAL1 family) and repressed others (e.g., TaVTC4-4B).

Collectively, these results indicate that the regulation of wheat FIG superfamily members is stress‑specific. The positively selected gene TaVTC4-4B exhibits rapid transcriptional repression under salt stress, which we speculate could be consistent with a role in salt tolerance, although this functional relationship was not directly tested in the present study.

Discussion

Evolutionary conservation and functional divergence of the FIG superfamily: implications for trait enhancement

Gene loss and expansion have played critical roles in the evolution of the FIG superfamily from algae to higher plants. For instance, glpX and IMPase (group VIII) were retained only in certain algal lineages but were absent in later-diverging species such as Ostreococcus lucimarinus, indicating lineage-specific loss (Fig. 1). In this organism, functional differentiation of FBPase genes gave rise to the ancestral variant ABO96248 (Figure S1), and the emergence of the first WOX gene might have contributed to the genetic basis for multicellularity in bryophytes [71]. During the terrestrialization of plants, FBPase was diversified into four distinct branches, while IMPase remained largely conserved except for losses in group VIII, suggesting that the molecular evolution of FIG genes was largely complete prior to the emergence of modern crops (Figs. 1, S1, S2). Collinearity analyses confirmed that wheat FIG genes are of ancient origin, underscoring their essential and conserved biological roles (Fig. 3B). Strong sequence and structural conservation imply central functions in core processes such as photosynthetic carbon fixation. Although such conservation warrants caution in genetic modification, it allows functional inferences from orthologs in related crops like maize and rice.

Despite deep evolutionary conservation, functional divergence of FIG superfamily is evident. Tissue-specific expression patterns varied among homoeologous groups—for example, CYFBP homoeologs on chromosome group 3 were predominantly expressed in leaves and sheaths, whereas the group 1 copy was root-specific, implicating subfunctionalization (Figure S4B).

Consequently, evolutionary conservation underpins the synergistic enhancement of both photosynthesis and abiotic stress responses, with functional diversification facilitating precise modulation of traits. This modularity supports both broad improvements through editing of conserved genes and fine-tuning via selection of natural haplotypes, positioning the FIG superfamily as a promising target for genetic improvement aimed at enhancing photosynthetic efficiency and stress resilience in wheat.

The dual role of the FIG superfamily: a molecular bridge connecting photosynthetic carbon assimilation and abiotic stress adaptation

The FIG superfamily in higher plants such as wheat comprises mainly FBPase and IMPase families. FBPase functions as a key rate-limiting enzyme in photosynthetic and gluconeogenic pathways [16, 76, 77], catalyzing the dephosphorylation of fructose-1,6-bisphosphate to direct carbon toward sucrose and starch biosynthesis. This activity critically influences photosynthetic efficiency and regulates carbon metabolic homeostasis [37, 78–80]. In contrast to that, IMPase acts as a core enzyme in inositol metabolism, producing inositol as a precursor for phosphoinositide signaling molecules [43, 81, 82], thereby bridging primary metabolism with stress response and signal transduction pathways [30, 43, 82–84]. These functional distinctions suggest an early divergence of the FIG superfamily that has been maintained throughout plant evolution.

In line with this dual functionality, our quantitative expression analysis under salt and drought stresses revealed that FIG superfamily members exhibit highly divergent responses: most genes were significantly down‑regulated by drought, while salt stress induced both strong up‑regulation (e.g., TaSAL1 and TalMPL3 subfamilies) and down‑regulation (e.g., TaVTC4‑4B and TaHL‑6A) depending on the specific gene (Table S7; Figs. 5F–5G).

Collectively, our analysis of the promoters of the FBPase and IMPase subfamilies in wheat revealed a shared enrichment of cis-acting elements responsive to light, drought, abscisic acid (ABA), and jasmonic acid (JA) (Figure S4A). This strongly suggests that both subfamilies are co-regulated by similar environmental and hormonal pathways. Even if their biochemical functions have diverged, this conserved regulatory architecture implies that they may act synergistically—via shared transcriptional modules—to coordinate carbon metabolism and stress adaptation in response to light and abiotic stress, thereby constituting a common environmental adaptation strategy.

This regulatory conservation can be viewed as a form of redundancy at the transcriptional level, which ensures reliable and robust activation of relevant metabolic pathways under stress conditions, ultimately enhancing the plants adaptive resilience [85, 86]. Building on these observations, we propose an evolutionary model in which the FBPase and IMPase subfamilies of the FIG superfamily underwent functional specialization at the biochemical level, possibly to accommodate distinct substrates or cellular compartments, while retaining highly conserved cis-regulatory elements at the transcriptional level. This functional differentiation coupled with conserved regulation enables plants to utilize a limited set of gene family members to simultaneously fine-tune two core physiological processes, photosynthetic carbon assimilation and stress response, through shared signaling pathways (e.g., ABA/JA), thereby optimizing metabolic homeostasis and adaptive fitness.

Deciphering the evolutionary and functional landscape of TaVTC4‑4B, a positively selected gene associated with environmental adaptation in wheat.

Against the backdrop of intensifying global climate change and the increasing frequency of extreme weather events, mining genetic resources for stress resilience and elucidating their molecular regulatory mechanisms are of paramount importance for ensuring food security. Wheat, as a staple food crop, exhibits remarkable environmental adaptability, which implies a rich genetic foundation for stress tolerance. Although our Ka/Ks‑based selection pressure analysis suggests that TaVTC4‑4B has undergone strong positive selection during wheat evolution, it is important to acknowledge that the Ka/Ks ratio alone is a coarse measure reflecting long‑term average selection, and it may not capture transient, lineage‑specific, or episodic selective events. Therefore, our inference of positive selection should be considered as an initial indicator rather than a definitive functional proof. Nevertheless, the positive selection signal on TaVTC4‑4B points to potential adaptive significance, which is reinforced by the conserved functions of VTC4 orthologs. In Arabidopsis, VTC4 acts as a key enzyme in ascorbate biosynthesis and participates in both biotic and abiotic stress responses [87–90]; its rice homolog (Os03g0162725) similarly regulates ascorbate levels under light stress [91], affecting ROS homeostasis and photosynthesis [92]. Given these roles in stress tolerance and redox regulation, we hypothesize that positively selected TaVTC4‑4B may have contributed to enhanced stress adaptation and ecological niche expansion after hexaploid wheat formation. However, direct functional validation, e.g., via transgenic complementation, gene editing, or phenotypic assays, remains necessary to confirm its physiological roles and the adaptive value of its variants.

To support the functional assignment of TaVTC4‑4B, we analyzed its protein sequence using PROSITE [69] and identified the canonical inositol monophosphatase (IMP) domain (PS00629), a signature of the L‑galactose‑1‑phosphate phosphatase family involved in ascorbate biosynthesis [87, 88]. This conserved domain provides protein‑level evidence for its annotation, consistent with its Arabidopsis and rice orthologs. However, given documented differences between dicots and monocots [93], inferences from sequence similarity and cis‑elements should be interpreted with caution. While PROSITE analysis partially alleviates this concern, direct experimental validation in wheat, via transgenic or gene‑editing approaches, remains essential for definitive conclusions.

Systematic studies on the function of VTC4 genes remain relatively limited. Previous heterologous expression experiments in Arabidopsis demonstrated that the chickpea VTC4 gene enhanced stress tolerance during seed germination and seedling growth, whereas loss-of-function mutants exhibited heightened stress sensitivity [94], preliminarily revealing the important role of VTC4 in stress response. Our research further reveals that the promoter region of TaVTC4‑4B is enriched with various cis-acting elements associated with abiotic stress responses—such as light and drought—as well as hormone-responsive elements including those for ABA and JA. This indicates that TaVTC4‑4B possesses the capacity to integrate diverse environmental and endogenous signals, likely acting downstream of ABA and JA signaling pathways to participate in the crosstalk between stress response and developmental regulation. The combination of these elements also suggests that TaVTC4‑4B may serve as a bridge coordinating carbon metabolism (e.g., photosynthesis) and stress adaptation.

Expression pattern analyses support these inferences: TaVTC4‑4B shows high expression in green tissues and is light-inducible, implying its potential involvement in photosynthesis-related metabolism or photomorphogenesis. Concurrently, its expression is significantly modulated under various stress conditions including drought, temperature extremes, and salt stress, further confirming its role as a multifunctional gene broadly involved in abiotic stress responses.

Conclusion

The FIG superfamily shows both conservation and functional divergence from algae to higher plants, underpinning core roles in photosynthesis. Its dual function bridges carbon assimilation and stress adaptation in wheat. This study identifies TaVTC4‑4B as a promising candidate gene for future functional studies. Its promoter architecture and expression profiles collectively support its potential role in environmental adaptation, providing novel genetic resources and a theoretical framework for stress-resilient wheat breeding. Future work should employ genetic transformation and gene-editing approaches to functionally validate its specific roles in photosynthetic efficiency and stress tolerance, and to dissect its regulatory network, with the ultimate goal of facilitating molecular breeding for high-yielding, stress-resistant wheat cultivars.
