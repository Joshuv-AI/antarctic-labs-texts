Functional analysis of natural variation in the RNA-binding protein CsrA across the bacterial domain predicts regulatory activity
Authors: Jared T Winkelman, Ethan Yarberry, Georgia Fanouraki, Jonathan D Winkelman, Sampriti Mukherjee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBacteria employ sophisticated post-transcriptional regulatory mechanisms to adapt to environmental changes. Carbon storage regulator A (CsrA), a highly conserved RNA-binding protein, serves as a critical post-transcriptional regulator by typically recognizing GGA-containing hairpin loops in target mRNAs and repressing translation. However, how this conserved regulator evolved diverse species-specific regulatory networks remains unclear. We developed Swarm-seq, a high-throughput platform assessing CsrA homologs across the bacterial domain for regulating flagella-dependent swarming in Bacillus subtilis. Testing over five-hundred codon-optimized csrA homologs revealed functional divergence, partitioning CsrAs into two broad classes. Class I (CsrAHp, CsrASm, RsmNPa) strongly inhibited swarming, while Class II (CsrAEc, RsmAPa) failed despite sequence conservation. This differential activity occurred despite canonical GGA motifs in flagellin (hag) transcript, suggesting evolutionary plasticity in RNA-binding specificity beyond motif recognition. Leveraging this dataset, we trained machine learning algorithms to predict CsrA functionality, experimentally validating Bdellovibrio bacteriovorus CsrA (CsrABb) and Pseudomonas putida RsmA (RsmAPp) as Class I. Our findings establish Swarm-seq as a powerful platform for characterizing CsrA homologs from genetically intractable or unculturable bacteria and demonstrate the potential for machine learning-guided discovery of functional regulatory proteins, providing insights into post-transcriptional regulatory network evolution.

Introduction

The exponential growth in bacterial genome sequencing—now exceeding 1.9 million complete genomes representing all major bacterial phyla—has created an unprecedented catalog of microbial genetic diversity at the sequence level [1]. Yet, even when gene annotation is technically feasible, defining the precise function of a gene in context remains a major challenge. This limitation is especially acute for post-transcriptional regulators such as RNA-binding proteins (RBPs), whose function depends not only on conserved domains but also on highly dynamic and context-specific interactions with their RNA targets. Unlike metabolic enzymes, whose functions can often be inferred from conserved active sites, RBPs exhibit regulatory specificity that can rapidly shift across species due to minor changes in binding motifs, expression patterns, or co-regulatory partners. As a result, although RBPs are known to control fundamental processes including carbon metabolism [2–5], biofilm formation [6–8], pathogenesis [9], and antibiotic resistance [10–12], their regulons are highly flexible and not predictable from sequence alone. Most experimentally defined regulatory networks remain restricted to fewer than 50 well-studied model organisms, primarily γ-proteobacteria like Escherichia coli and Pseudomonas aeruginosa, leaving the vast majority of bacterial regulatory space functionally uncharted.

Among bacterial RBPs, the CsrA/RsmA (carbon storage regulator A/repressor of secondary metabolites A) family represents an ideal test case for cross-species functional prediction. These small homodimeric proteins (61–100 amino acids per monomer, ∼7 kDa subunits) exhibit remarkable phylogenetic conservation, with homologs identified in sequenced bacterial genomes spanning all major phyla from α-proteobacteria to firmicutes [13]. Structural studies of CsrA homologs from multiple species, including E. coli CsrA, Bacillus subtilis CsrA, P. aeruginosa RsmA, Pseudomonas fluorescens RsmE, and Yersinia enterocolitica RsmA, have revealed a conserved architecture where two interdigitating monomers create RNA-binding surfaces formed by β-strand interactions between homodimeric subunits (Fig. 1A) [14–16]. CsrA proteins function as global post-transcriptional regulators by binding to mRNA 5′-leader sequences that typically contain a GGA motif in the loop of a hairpin (hereafter, we refer to this as CsrA binding site), sterically blocking 30S ribosomal subunit access to ribosome binding sites and inhibiting translation initiation [17–22]. Although CsrA predominantly represses translation, it can also activate translation by remodeling RNA structures that sequester ribosome binding sites [23, 24], and in at least one case, CsrA binding to the pgaABCD leader promotes Rho-dependent transcription termination by exposing a Rho utilization site [25]. In well-characterized systems like E. coli, CsrA functions as a global regulator with pleiotropic effects, targeting hundreds of transcripts involved in central carbon metabolism, motility, quorum sensing, extracellular product synthesis, and biofilm formation [20, 26–29]. CsrA also plays critical roles in virulence factor regulation across diverse bacterial pathogens affecting both animals and plants [30–33].

Despite extensive biochemical characterization revealing that canonical CsrA binding sites contain conserved GGA motifs within ∼10–12 nucleotide recognition elements, and detailed structural analysis of RNA-binding domains, functional studies have been largely restricted to a narrow phylogenetic range. Cross-complementation experiments, while demonstrating some functional conservation between genera such as Sinorhizobium meliloti csrA complementing P. fluorescens rsmA mutants [34], have yielded inconsistent results even between closely related species, with recent reports documenting failed complementation between CsrA homologs from closely related bacterial strains [35]. This limitation has profound implications for synthetic biology applications requiring predictable regulatory behavior and our fundamental understanding of how post-transcriptional regulatory networks evolve across bacterial diversity.

To address this challenge, we developed Swarm-seq, a quantitative high-throughput platform that systematically assesses CsrA function across bacterial phylogeny. Our methodology leverages B. subtilis as a heterologous chassis to evaluate hundreds of representative CsrA homologs selected from phylogenomic analysis of thousands of bacterial proteomes for their ability to inhibit flagella-dependent swarming motility—a phenotype directly controlled by CsrA-mediated translational repression of the flagellin gene hag [20, 22]. The platform enables simultaneous screening of hundreds of natural variants of CsrA in pooled competition assays, combining comprehensive phylogenomic analysis spanning bacterial diversity with high-resolution phenotypic screening to functionally characterize regulatory proteins from genetically intractable organisms while maintaining the quantitative rigor necessary for systematic cross-species comparisons.

Our systematic analysis reveals that functional divergence among CsrA homologs is far more extensive than anticipated from previous complementation studies. Despite high amino acid conservation in RNA-binding domains and conserved structural features, CsrA homologs exhibited striking phenotypic differences: Helicobacter pylori CsrA (hereafter CsrAHp) and P. aeruginosa RsmN (hereafter RsmNPa) completely abolished swarming motility with potent regulatory activity (Class I CsrAs), while the extensively characterized E. coli CsrA (hereafter CsrAEc) and P. aeruginosa RsmA (hereafter RsmAPa) showed no detectable activity in our assay system (Class II CsrAs). Critically, this functional divergence occurred despite all proteins targeting identical GGA motifs within the hag 5′-leader, indicating that regulatory differences occur through sophisticated mechanisms likely involving RNA-binding specificity, protein–RNA complex stability, cofactor requirements, or regulatory network integration that are not captured by current sequence-based predictive models. Furthermore, we trained a random forest machine learning model with the Swarm-seq dataset to predict functionality of ∼7500 CsrA homologs. We experimentally validated four predictions and demonstrated that Bdellovibrio bacteriovorus CsrA (CsrABb) and Pseudomonas putida RsmA (RsmAPp) function as a Class I regulator, i.e. these inhibit Hag production. Overall, these findings establish a scalable experimental framework for functional annotation of conserved regulatory proteins and demonstrate that phylogenetic conservation is insufficient to predict regulatory function in heterologous systems. Our results reveal evolutionary plasticity in post-transcriptional control mechanisms with direct implications for synthetic regulatory circuit design and provide a systematic approach for bridging the gap between genetically encoded capacity and its realized regulatory function across bacterial diversity.

Materials and methods

Strains and growth conditions

Bacillus subtilis and E. coli strains were grown in lysogeny broth (LB) (10 g tryptone, 5 g yeast extract, 5 g NaCl per liter) or on LB plates fortified with 1.5% Bacto agar at 37°C for most experiments. For reporter assays, B. subtilis strains were grown in 1% tryptone. The following antibiotic concentrations were used when necessary: ampicillin 100 μg/ml (amp), carbenicillin 100 μg/ml (carb), kanamycin 50 μg/ml (kan), chloramphenicol 5 μg/ml (cm), spectinomycin 100 μg/ml (spec), and tetracycline 10 μg/ml (tet). The strains, plasmids, and primers used in this study are listed in Supplementary Tables S1–S3.

Local genome database and representative tree

We constructed a local genome database by downloading representative genomes from GTDB release 226 [36]. To ensure high-quality annotations, we retained only genomes with available NCBI annotations, yielding a final dataset of 84 384 annotated genomes. For phylogenetic context (Fig. 1B), each genome in our local database was matched to its corresponding leaf in the GTDB tree, and GTDB taxonomic assignments were used for classification at the phylum, class, order, family, genus, and species levels. To generate a reduced genus-level tree, we randomly selected one genome per genus and pruned all other leaves using the Python library ETE3 v3.1.2 [37].

Identification of CsrA homologs

To identify putative CsrA homologs within our local genome database, we used hmmsearch v3.3.2 [38] to scan all proteomes against the PF02599 CsrA hidden Markov model (HMM) profile. Protein hits with an E-value below 1e-5 were retained for downstream analyses. These identified sequences were then realigned to the HMM profile using hmmalign, and unaligned regions were trimmed from the alignment ends.

Phylogenetic analyses

Species trees were inferred following a pipeline similar to Parks et al. (2022) [37]. Python code for constructing the full bac120 tree from proteomes and HMM profiles is available at https://github.com/JonWinkelman/bac120_tree_inference. For the CsrA protein tree, we aligned all curated CsrA sequences to the PF02599 HMM, trimmed unaligned terminal residues, and removed alignment columns where >90% of sequences contained gaps. Phylogenetic trees were inferred using FastTree v2.1.11 [39] with the LG amino acid substitution model [40]. Final trees were visualized and annotated using iTOL v6 [41].

Selection of csrAs to be synthesized as oligo pool

To construct a diverse and non-redundant library of CsrA-like sequences, we identified UniRef50 clusters [42] where the representative sequence was annotated as a “Translational regulator CsrA,” yielding over 1500 clusters. From these, we selected the representative sequence from 550 distinct clusters to include in our curated CsrA library. Sequences were aligned to the PF02599 CsrA HMM profile from the Pfam database [43] using hmmalign v3.3.2. Selected csrA genes were codon optimized for expression in B. subtilis. Constant sequences were added to the 5ʹ (5ʹ-CTAAAGTCACGGAGGTGTCAGTCAC-3ʹ) and 3ʹ (5ʹ-AAGTCACAAAGTCAGAAATCGTGGC-3ʹ) ends of the coding sequences to amplify and clone the csrAs. The pool of 555 sequences were purchased from IDT.

Pairwise sequence distance distributions

To compare sequence diversity within the CsrA library against a broader CsrA database, pairwise sequence distances were calculated from aligned amino acid sequences using a BLOSUM62-based distance metric. A random subset of 1000 sequences were sampled from the full filtered CsrA database alignment. Pairwise distance matrices were generated independently for the database subsample and the library alignment using Bio.DistanceCalculator with the BLOSUM62 substitution model in python. Distance distributions were visualized as histograms with overlaid GMM probability density functions. Gaussian mixture modeling was performed with scikit-learn using a fixed random seed (random_state = 1) for reproducibility.

Swarm expansion assay

Cells were grown to mid-log phase at 37°C in LB broth, harvested by centrifugation, and resuspended to 10 OD600 in PBS buffer (137 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl, 10 mM Na2HPO4, and 2 mM KH2PO4) containing 0.5% India ink. LB containing 0.7% Bacto agar was prepared the day before the experiment and 25 ml was added to each petri dish. For all experiments, swarm plates were made with and without 1 mM IPTG (Isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside). On the day of the experiment, the plates were dried for 5 min in a laminar flow hood before being centrally inoculated with 10 µl of the cell suspension, and dried for another 5 min. For all experiments, a single cell suspension was added to a plate with IPTG and a plate without IPTG. Plates were incubated at 37°C in an incubator with humidity maintained over 40% for 7 h before measuring the radius of the swarm edge. The India ink demarcated the origin of the colony and the swarm radius was measured relative to the origin. Swarm-seq procedure

Cells containing the Physpank-csrA library were grown to mid-log phase at 37°C in LB broth, harvested by centrifugation, and resuspended to 10 OD600 in PBS buffer (137 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl, 10 mM Na2HPO4, and 2 mM KH2PO4). Plates were prepared fresh as described above. Cells were incubated for 4 h, resulting in a swarm leading-edge that reached within 10 mm of the edge of the plate. Cells from the leading-edge (i.e. outer fraction; see Fig. 2C) were harvested with a sterile cotton swab and inoculated into LB broth. Similarly, cells from the center of the swarm (i.e. inner fraction; see Fig. 2C) were also harvested. Cultures were grown O/N at 37°C with shaking. Genomic DNA was harvested from 1 ml of the O/N culture and 40 ng of genomic DNA was used as template for a 15-cycle PCR with primers JW194 and JW207. The resulting PCR product was amplified in a 15-cycle PCR using primers JW197 and JW196, JW210, JW211, JW212, or JW213. These primers contain 3-nt barcodes that allowed us to pool the resulting PCRs and amplify the pool with primers JW198 and JW199 and then assign the resulting sequences to a specific sample. This final PCR was sequenced on an Illumina Hiseq with 2 × 150 by Seqcoast genomics.

Swarm-seq analysis

For each fastq file, raw fastq reads were searched for the constant upstream sequence 5ʹ-CTAAAGTCACGGAGGTGTCAGTCAC-3ʹ. Reads having a perfect match were filtered and the 25-nt sequence downstream of the constant sequence was then used to assign the csrA in each read. Each csrA was then counted and normalized as a fraction of total reads in the library. For each csrA, the normalized count in the file from the inner fraction was then divided by the normalized count from the file for the outer fraction. Activity binning via gaussian mixture modeling

To categorize continuous “mean inhibition” scores into two classes, we log₂‐transformed the inhibition data and fit a two‐component GMM using python’s sklearn library. Each sequence’s posterior probabilities were computed, and the label was set by applying the GMM’s hard cluster assignments. We removed records from the dataset that had mean inhibition scores whose probability of belonging to either distribution was below 0.85.

Purification of his-tagged CsrAs

BL21(DE3) E. coli cells containing pET28b-csrABs (for expression of CsrBs) or pET28b-csrASm (for overexpression of CsrASm) were grown at 37°C in LB broth and 50 µg/ml of kanamycin to an OD600 of 0.6, at which time 1 mM IPTG was added to the culture and incubation was continued for 3 h. Elutions were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue to verify purification of the His–CsrA fusion. Fractions containing pure His–CsrA were combined and dialysed against 20 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, 200 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, and 1 mM EDTA and stored at −80°C.

Structural prediction

Multimer structure prediction of CsrA dimers was performed using Alphafold3 [44] and the top ranked model was visualized using the PyMOL Molecular Graphics System, Version 2.5.0 (Schrödinger, LLC).

Random forest model

Feature encoding

Filtered, aligned amino‐acid sequences were one‐hot encoded (via a custom make_onehot_of_alignment), yielding a binary feature for each residue–position pair. Two additional Boolean features recorded whether (i) the alignment covered ≥40 residues in the HMM (match_HMM = 40) and (ii) the HMM bit‐score exceeded 60. This expanded matrix served as the input to the classifier.

Random‐forest training and hyperparameter tuning

We tuned a Random Forest for binary classification of binned_activity. A grid search (GridSearchCV) explored max_depth ∈ {2, 3, 4, 5, 10, 15, None} and min_samples_leaf ∈ {3, 4, 5, 10} using five-fold stratified CV and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) area under the curve (AUC) as the scoring metric. The optimal parameters (e.g. max_depth=5, min_samples_leaf=3) were then used to train the final model with 100 trees (n_estimators=100, oob_score=True, random_state=1). The out-of-bag score was reported as an internal estimate of generalization accuracy.

Model validation and performance evaluation

We assessed model performance via multiple approaches:

Results

Development of a phylogenetically diverse library of CsrAs

To build a library that broadly samples CsrA diversity, we first sought to understand CsrA primary sequence diversity. CsrA is present in many bacterial genomes, but the extent of its distribution and sequence variation remained unclear. To assess its natural taxonomic and sequence diversity, we first examined CsrA distribution across bacterial taxa. We leveraged the GTDB database, which contains >80 000 curated, nonredundant bacterial proteomes that have matching annotated genomes in NCBI [36, 45]. Using stringent HMM matching criteria, we identified >20 000 CsrA homologs from this dataset (Supplementary Fig. S1A). These representatives were found across a wide range of bacterial phyla, including some of the most distantly related branches of the bacterial tree (Fig. 1B and Supplementary Fig. S1B). Approximately 30% of bacterial genomes contained at least 1 csrA gene, with most encoding a single ortholog, although some genomes carried up to 15 (Fig. 1C). While CsrA is broadly distributed and present in most phyla, its prevalence was not uniform across the bacterial tree (Fig. 1D and Supplementary Fig. S1B). It was widely found in certain phyla, such as Bacillota, Planctomycetota, and Pseudomonadota, whereas others, such as Bacteroidota, contained relatively few representatives. These findings highlight both the evolutionary conservation of CsrA and its uneven phylogenetic distribution, suggesting that its regulatory role may be more important in some lineages than in others.

To systematically investigate the natural diversity of CsrA function, we assembled a library of 555 CsrA homologs from diverse bacterial lineages using UniRef50 clustering, which groups sequences at ≥50% identity to reduce redundancy while maintaining phylogenetic diversity (Supplementary Data S1). The 555 CsrA representatives in our library span a broad phylogenetic range, demonstrating a diverse representation of CsrA across taxa (Fig. 1B and Supplementary Fig. S1B). Notably, our library did not include CsrA from the Bacteroidota and only a few examples from non-gammaproteobacterial Pseudomonadota, reflecting its lower prevalence in these taxa. To quantitatively assess whether the phylogenetic distribution of CsrAs in our library reflects that of the broader CsrA population, we counted the number of CsrAs in each Phyla in our database and in the library and compared the distribution of CsrA across phyla in both (Fig. 1D). The distribution of CsrA variants in our library closely matched the overall distribution across phyla, with only minor differences. We conclude that our library effectively captures the natural taxonomic diversity of CsrA.

In addition to taxonomic variation, we aimed for our library to be representative of CsrA amino acid sequence diversity. One self-imposed limitation was that to facilitate the commercial synthesis of csrA genes we restricted our library to CsrA representatives with 100 amino acids or fewer. However, the size distribution of CsrA proteins in our dataset essentially mirrored that of all bacterial CsrAs, despite the size criterion (Fig. 1E). We note that some variants with 30 amino acids or fewer, while annotated as CsrA in UniProt, likely represent incomplete or misannotated sequences rather than authentic CsrA homologs. Further supporting its representativeness, sequence logos comparing all known CsrAs to those in our library revealed similar patterns of conserved residues and relative information, indicating that our dataset captures key functional elements (Fig. 1F). To more quantitatively compare our library to the natural variation in CsrA amino acid sequence, we calculated entropy values across CsrA positions. Figure 1G shows that the positional entropy [46] (a parameter that quantifies the randomness or conservation of amino acids at specific sites in a protein) in our library was greater than a random equivalently sized subsample from the full database. The increased entropy in our library is likely a consequence of using UniRef50 clustering, which ensures variability. Consistent with this, when we randomly sampled 550 CsrAs from our database, the positional entropy values were virtually the same to that of all CsrAs (Fig. 1G). As a final test of library diversity, we performed pairwise sequence similarity comparison among all CsrAs in our library and found that CsrAs differed from one another by an average of 55%, compared to a mean difference of 49% among database CsrAs, suggesting that the library captured substantial sequence diversity (Fig. 1H). Nearly no CsrAs were >80% similar to one another (Fig. 1H). The broad unimodal distribution suggests that the library samples a wide range of sequence diversity rather than being dominated by highly similar variants. Together, these analyses confirm that our CsrA library is a robust and representative dataset for functional studies. By encompassing a wide phylogenetic range while preserving key sequence features and diversity, our library provides a powerful framework for systematically probing the functional evolution of CsrA across bacteria.

Swarm-seq: identification of functional classes of CsrAs

To functionally assess our synthetic library of CsrAs, we developed Swarm-seq, a high-throughput assay that uses B. subtilis swarming motility as a readout for each CsrA’s ability to inhibit translation of hag transcript. Bacillus subtilis normally exhibits robust swarming, but overexpression of its native CsrA (CsrABs) inhibits motility by preventing translation of flagellin (called Hag in B. subtilis) [22]. CsrABs binds to two GGA-containing stem loops in the 5′-leader of the hag transcript to prevent ribosome binding and translation initiation [22] (Fig. 2A), and unlike its Gram-negative counterparts is antagonized by the protein FliW [20, 47–50]. The role of CsrABs is to coordinate hag expression with the assembly state of the flagellum, and homeostatically restrict hag translation by a negative feedback loop involving FliW and CsrA. Thus, to determine whether csrA homologs from evolutionarily distant bacteria can complement for each other in B. subtilis, we engineered a strain where we eliminated the FliW feedback loop and assessed swarming motility upon overproduction of heterologous CsrAs in a ∆csrA∆fliW double mutant. This genetic background ensured that swarming motility was directly linked to heterologous CsrA’s RNA-binding function. As an initial control, we ensured that this strain was motile when no CsrA was expressed but cells producing CsrABs from an ectopic chromosomal locus upon IPTG induction did not exhibit swarming motility (Fig. 2B). We conclude that overexpression of CsrAs in this background enables the assessment of CsrA inhibitory function.

We next leveraged CsrA-dependent swarming inhibition to systematically assess all 555 CsrA variants in our library (Fig. 2C). First, we generated an IPTG-inducible overexpression library of the csrAs. As controls our library included a construct without csrA (negative control) and a construct that overexpressed CsrABs (positive control). We grew the library overnight, plated it on the center of a swarm agar plate, allowed the cells to swarm for 7 h (the amount of time required for the leading edge of the swarm to reach the outer edge of the plate in the absence of IPTG), and then isolated cells from the outer edge of the plate. We purified genomic DNA from the motile outer sub-population and the inner subpopulation of the plate that should contain genetically motile and non-motile cells, amplified the csrA locus from both populations, and sequenced the csrA locus. We identified reads for over 400 of the CsrAs in the library (Supplementary Fig. S2A). We defined motility inhibition as the number of normalized counts in the inner fraction divided by the number of normalized counts in the motile outer fraction. CsrAs that inhibited motility were expected to be depleted in the motile fraction, while non-functional CsrAs would be evenly distributed or depleted in the non-motile fraction. Figure 2D and Supplementary Fig. S2B show the distribution of motility inhibition scores and that the distribution contains two populations that represent functional (Class I) and non-functional (Class II) classes of CsrA where function is strictly defined as repression of swarming motility. As expected, B. subtilis expressing its native CsrABs exhibited a high inhibition score, whereas the no CsrA control had a low inhibition score, suggesting Swarm-seq was accurately separating functional and nonfunctional CsrA variants. Surprisingly, the Swarm-seq data indicate that while approximately half of the CsrAs in our library showed some level of swarming inhibition, the other half of the CsrA population was unable to inhibit swarming motility. To validate the Swarm-seq results, we individually isolated strains expressing CsrAs from pathogenic bacteria, including E. coli, P. aeruginosa, and Helicobacter pylori, and assessed their effects on swarming in isogenic conditions. Consistent with the Swarm-seq data, we found that CsrABs, RsmNPa, and CsrAHp inhibited swarming (Fig. 2E). In contrast, and consistent with the Swarm-seq data, CsrAEc and RsmAPa did not inhibit swarming (Fig. 2E).

To further test the robustness of Swarm-seq and ensure that the observed functional differences were not artifacts of pooled competition effects, we assessed the swarming behavior of individual isogenic library members. Specifically, we grew our library, plated it on selective medium to isolate single colonies, and randomly selected 69 colonies for individual analysis. Of these isolates, 46 exhibited motility and 23 were non-motile when assessed individually. This result confirmed the surprising Swarm-seq finding that at least half of the tested CsrAs were unable to inhibit motility, indicating genuine functional divergence rather than pooled assay artifacts. To directly compare these individually assessed results with Swarm-seq classifications, we pooled cells from the motile isolates and separately pooled cells from the non-motile isolates. After growing these pooled samples, amplifying CsrA, and sequencing, we compared the distribution of motility inhibition scores between the two groups. This comparison mirrored our Swarm-seq data, with the non-motile isolate pool exhibiting a significantly higher mean inhibition score (Fig. 2F). Together, these results confirm that Swarm-seq is a sensitive and scalable method for classifying CsrAs based on their ability to inhibit motility, providing a robust functional readout of presumed RNA-binding activity across diverse bacterial CsrAs.

To determine whether the ability of CsrA homologs to inhibit motility correlates with phylogeny, we mapped Class I and Class II CsrAs onto a maximum-likelihood phylogenetic tree and found that these variants were interspersed throughout the tree, without clustering into distinct clades (Fig. 2G and Supplementary Fig. S3). This pattern suggests that the sequence space compatible with binding and repression of hag is broad, but functional activity of CsrA is also sensitive to amino acid differences. Additionally, the high phylogenetic divergence among homologs and the coarse sampling of sequence space likely limit our ability to identify individual amino acid changes that govern functional differences. However, our data clearly show that CsrA homologs that deviated substantially from the CsrA HMM consensus were enriched among sequences that failed to inhibit motility (Fig. 2H). Together, these results indicate that while functional CsrAs are scattered across the bacterial phylogeny, maintenance of core sequence features is associated with their regulatory activity.

Class I CsrA variants inhibit expression of hag

We hypothesized that Class I CsrA variants inhibited motility via repression of hag translation. To directly test this, we fused the hag promoter and 5′-leader to mNeonGreen as a CsrA-responsive reporter (Fig. 3A). Microscopy confirmed that the hag reporter functioned as expected, with green fluorescence observed in the absence of CsrABs or when IPTG was not added, while fluorescence signal was diminished upon IPTG induction of CsrABs (Fig. 3B). We then tested reporter fluorescence during log-phase growth (Supplementary Fig. S4A) in different strains, including a strain without CsrA, a strain expressing CsrABs, and strains expressing CsrAEc, RsmAPa, RsmNPa, CsrASm (Stenotrophomonas maltophilia CsrA), and CsrAHp. By simultaneously monitoring growth, the reporter experiments showed that none of the tested CsrAs affected logarithmic growth rates or max ODs, suggesting that most of the effects of CsrA expression on swarming or reporter activity are not due to effects on growth (Supplementary Fig. S4B). Consistent with the Swarm-seq data, we found that CsrAEc, RsmAPa, and the no-CsrA control were unable to inhibit wildtype hag-mNeonGreen, while CsrABs, RsmNPa, CsrASm, and CsrAHp successfully inhibited hag reporter expression (Fig. 3C, top panel). We conclude that the motility inhibition observed in Swarm-seq data is due to the inhibition of hag expression, presumably due to inhibition of hag translation. As a specificity control, we fused a mutant 5′-leader carrying a G-38A (with respect to the start codon) substitution to mKate2 fluorescent reporter (Fig. 3A). This substitution changed the GGA motif in the upstream stem–loop to GAA and was previously shown to abolish inhibition by CsrABs [20, 22]. As expected CsrABs and RsmNPa failed to inhibit the G-38A mutant 5′-leader (Fig. 3C, bottom panel). Surprisingly, CsrASm and CsrAHp retained the ability to inhibit the mutant leader. These results suggest that Class I CsrA variants differ in their sensitivity to hag RNA sequence or they have distinct sequence preferences for target recognition.

To further investigate the unexpected specificity observed in our reporter assays, we purified CsrABs and CsrASm and assessed their RNA-binding capabilities using an electrophoretic mobility shift assay. When incubated with wild-type (WT) hag 5′-leader RNA, CsrABs exhibited strong binding but showed little to no interaction with the mutant hag 5′-leader, indicating that it can effectively discriminate between the two target RNA sequences (Fig. 3D). In contrast, CsrASm bound both the WT and mutant hag 5′-leaders with comparable affinity, with less than a two-fold difference (Fig. 3D). These results suggest that while CsrABs exhibits sequence-specific recognition of the hag 5′-leader, CsrASm has a broader binding profile that may allow it to regulate a more diverse set of targets.

Class I CsrA variants have overlapping but distinct determinants for target recognition

We next sought to identify determinants of CsrASm and CsrAHp RNA recognition. To determine whether these CsrAs were recognizing the GGA motif, we completely eliminated the GGA motif by changing positions −39, −38, and −37 to CCT (Fig. 3E). The results showed that CsrAHp and CsrASm, like CsrABs, failed to inhibit hag expression. We conclude that CsrAHp and CsrASm are still sensitive to hag leader sequence and the GGA motif, but that a GGA to GAA substitution is not sufficient to abolish RNA recognition.

CsrA inhibits hag translation by binding the leader near the Shine–Dalgarno sequence, thereby blocking ribosome access. A C-17T mutation in the hag leader was previously shown to prevent CsrABs motility inhibition [20], presumably by disrupting the interaction between CsrA and this region of the leader. To test whether this mutation affects CsrAHp and CsrASm inhibition, we introduced the C-17T substitution into our reporter construct and measured inhibition upon CsrA induction. The results showed CsrABs failed to inhibit expression at all, while CsrAHp and CsrASm inhibited <50% of expression (Fig. 3E). We conclude that CsrAHp and CsrASm are sensitive to the RNA sequence or structure near the ribosome binding site.

The number of nucleotides in the unpaired portion of the stem–loop is known to affect the binding of some CsrAs [19]. To determine whether decreasing the number of nucleotides in the unpaired region of the stem–loop affects inhibition, we first deleted −36C (the last unpaired nucleotide in the upstream stem; Fig. 3E) and measured inhibition of fluorescence upon CsrA production. CsrABs failed to inhibit expression with this leader, while CsrAHp and CsrASm retained their activity against this sequence. Deletion of −36C reduces the loop to 5 and simultaneously repositions the GGA motif adjacent to the stem. We also made a separate single nucleotide deletion that removed −40A from the loop. We found that CsrABs retained some activity against this leader and was able to inhibit >50% of expression. CsrAHp and CsrASm were also active against this leader (Fig. 3E). In this case, the changes in sequence at position −40 are not the cause, since all of the CsrAs were active against single substitutions (Supplementary Fig. S4C). We conclude that reductions in loop size or GGA position within a loop are not determinants for CsrAHp and CsrASm inhibition. Notably, the two single deletions yielded loops of identical size (five nucleotides) with an intact GGA, yet only the −36C deletion abolished CsrABs activity; loop size alone therefore does not predict CsrABs inhibition. The two deletions differed in the side from which the loop was shortened: −40A trimmed the 5′ side of the GGA and preserved activity, whereas −36C trimmed the 3′ side and abolished it. We conclude that CsrABs is differentially sensitive to deletions in the loop, specifically to shortening on the 3′ side of the GGA. One possible interpretation is that CsrABs requires the sequence 3′ of the GGA to remain accessible, although our deletions do not separate this from loop length or from the identity of the deleted nucleotide. Taken together, these results revealed that CsrABs, CsrAHp, and CsrASm have overlapping but distinct RNA sequence and structural requirements for target recognition.

To investigate whether structural features might explain differences in CsrA function, we predicted the structures of several CsrA homologs such as CsrABs, CsrAEc, RsmAPa, RsmNPa, CsrAHp, and CsrASm using AlphaFold3 [44] (Supplementary Fig. S4D). The most apparent structural difference among these homologs is the length of the C-terminal helices, which serve as binding sites for regulatory proteins (such as FliW) in some species, including B. subtilis. Another prominent feature is unique to RsmNPa: an enlarged loop containing an additional α-helix near the RNA-binding site (visible in the top-middle portion of the structure in Supplementary Fig. S4D). However, this feature does not correlate with functional differences, as RsmNPa behaves similarly to CsrABs in our assays. In addition, aligning the primary sequence of the CsrAs shown in Supplementary Fig. S4E did not exhibit a clear pattern that enabled prediction of function. For example, RsmNPa, which was only 24.53% identical, complemented the lack of CsrABs, while RsmAPa, which was 41% identical, did not (Supplementary Fig. S4E). The overall inability to predict function from sequence or structural analysis highlights the importance of high-throughput functional assays like Swarm-seq.

A machine learning model predicts functional CsrAs from across the bacterial domain

To leverage the Swarm-seq functional data for broader inference, we trained a machine learning model to classify the functionality of natural CsrA homologs based on their primary amino acid sequence (Fig. 4A). First, we used a GMM to assign each CsrA to a functional or non-functional class, retaining only those homologs with a classification probability greater than 0.85 (Supplementary Fig. S5A). We then trained a Random Forest classifier using this curated set of ~400 confidently classified CsrAs. To evaluate model performance, we performed a stratified split in which two-thirds of the data were used for training and the remaining one-third for testing. The model’s precision and recall were both substantially higher than expected by chance, indicating effective identification of functional CsrAs (Fig. 4B, top and Supplementary Fig. S5B). In addition, the ROC curve demonstrated a true positive rate well above the false positive rate across thresholds, with an AUC substantially greater than 0.5 (Fig. 4B, bottom and Supplementary Fig. S5B), further confirming predictive power beyond random classification. This indicates that the CsrA primary sequence encodes features predictive of functional activity. No single residue alone strongly predicted functional activity, suggesting multiple sequence solutions for building a CsrA capable of inhibiting hag. However, several key features of the CsrA sequence had predictive value, including the presence of a serine at position 11, an isoleucine at position 25, a leucine at position 47, and a glutamate at position 45 in the alignment (Fig. 4C). Consistent with the Random Forest model, the top three features all significantly affected the mean motility inhibition score, and combinations of these features generally led to even larger increases in mean inhibition (Fig. 4D).

We tested several additional approaches to identify relevant sequence features. First, we used the Gaussian mixture-curated set to generate sequence logos from confidently classified motility-inhibiting (Class I) and non-motility-inhibiting (Class II) CsrAs. Although there were subtle differences between the two groups, it was difficult to visually identify specific positions of interest (Supplementary Fig. S5C). However, when we created a logo from a differential matrix, a few positions emerged as enriched in Class I, including K7, S11, I25, E45, and L47-residues, that were also identified by the Random Forest model (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig. S5D). We also explored additional models, including ElasticNet (Supplementary Fig. S5E and F) and regularized logistic regression (Supplementary Fig. S5G and H). These models all identified overlapping sets of positions as having predictive power, providing additional confidence that these sequence features are robustly associated with CsrA functionality. Notably, the positions highlighted by ElasticNet included I25, E45, and L47, and those highlighted by regularized logistic regression included I25, E45, L47, and S11 echoing the findings from the Random Forest.

To assess whether the model could identify functional CsrAs from bacteria not represented in the Swarm-seq training set, we applied it to a larger dataset of ~7500 CsrA homologs spanning the bacterial domain. Application to this expanded dataset revealed substantial variation in the predicted proportion of functional CsrAs across bacterial phyla (Fig. 4E and Supplementary Fig. S6). For example, groups such as the Campylobacterota and Bdellovibrionota were enriched for predicted functional CsrAs, whereas phyla like the Krumholzibacteriota and Eisenbacteria showed low frequencies of predicted functional homologs. Bdellovibrio bacteriovorus is a predatory bacterium from the Bdellovibrionota that invades and kills other Gram-negative bacteria by entering their periplasm and consuming them from within, a unique lifestyle that makes it a promising candidate for novel antimicrobial therapies [51]. Very little is known about CsrA-dependent post-transcriptional regulation in this predator, but a previous transposon mutagenesis screen has implicated the putative CsrA homolog in B. bacteriovorus HD100 (CsrABb) to be crucial for predation [52]. Since CsrABb has 6 of the top 10, including 3 of the top 4, amino acid features predicting function (Fig. 4C and F), we reasoned that it was a good candidate to test our Random Forest model. To do this, we cloned csrABb downstream of the Physpank promoter and overexpressed it in the hag reporter strain used for the experiments in Fig. 3. Consistent with the model’s prediction, CsrABb inhibited expression from the wild-type Phag reporter and, similar to CsrAHp and CsrASm, also inhibited expression from the mutant reporter (Fig. 4G).

Many bacterial species encode more than one CsrA, which likely differ in their RNA target repertoires. Pseudomonas putida, encodes for three CsrA paralogs; RsmAPp, RsmEPp, and RsmIPp (Supplementary Fig. S7A); providing a useful real-world test of the Random Forest model. RsmAPp and RsmEPp both have the majority of the residues predictive of Class I behavior, while RsmIPp only had a few of the residues predictive of Class I behavior (Fig. 4C and Supplementary Fig. S7B). Consequently, both RsmAPp and RsmEPp were predicted by the random forest model to be Class I CsrAs, while RsmIPp was predicted to be a Class II CsrA. To test these predictions experimentally, we cloned these csrA paralogs downstream of the Physpank promoter and overexpressed them in the hag reporter strain. Consistent with the model predictions, RsmAPp inhibited hag expression, while RsmIPp failed to inhibit expression (Fig. 4G). However, RsmEPp also failed to inhibit expression of the hag reporter despite being the paralog with the strongest Class I feature signature. Although it is yet unclear why RsmEPp did not behave as expected, the fact that the model accurately classified three out of the four CsrA variants tested is consistent with the Random Forest model’s overall accuracy of 0.7 (Supplementary Fig. S5B). We conclude that the Random Forest model can predict the functionality of untested CsrA homologs, supporting its utility for broad functional inference across the bacterial domain. Improvements in the quantity of training data are likely to improve the predictive power and this modeling framework will be useful when expanding to other CsrA targets and for studying target recognition with other RBP-RNA.

B. subtilis as a chassis for CsrAs from challenging-to-study bacteria

Our library includes CsrAs from industrially, environmentally, and microbiome-associated bacteria spanning the bacterial tree (Supplementary Fig. S8). For example, Congregibacter litoralis CsrA, involved in marine carbon cycling via aerobic anoxygenic phototrophy [53], and Blochmannia floridanus, an obligate endosymbiont of carpenter ants lacking many genes required for independent survival [54], are both challenging to study in their native contexts. Similarly, Pseudomonas chlororaphis, an agriculturally important biocontrol bacterium producing antifungal compounds [55], and Nitrospira defluvii, a key comammox organism in wastewater treatment with slow growth [56], are difficult to manipulate genetically. Using Swarm-seq, we demonstrated that CsrAs from these bacteria cluster within the functional region of our motility score histogram, indicating their ability to inhibit motility in B. subtilis (Supplementary Fig. S8). Notably, structural predictions revealed an unexpected extra loop in the RNA-binding interface of N. defluvii CsrA despite its functionality, highlighting the limitations of structure-based function prediction (Supplementary Fig. S8). Likewise, CsrAs from microbiome-associated species such as Roseburia inulinivorans, Agathobacter rectalis, Oribacterium sp., and an uncharacterized Lachnospiraceae species that are key butyrate producers and fiber fermenters contributing to gut and oral health [57–60] also inhibited motility (Supplementary Fig. S8). By characterizing these diverse CsrAs in a genetically tractable system, we provide insights into their regulatory potential that would be difficult to obtain in their native hosts.

Discussion

Our comprehensive analysis of CsrA distribution across bacterial genomes provides the first systematic assessment of this critical post-transcriptional regulator’s phylogenetic breadth. The identification of >20 000 CsrA homologs across diverse bacterial phyla, with ~30% of bacterial genomes containing at least one csrA gene, while some carrying up to 15 paralogs, underscores the evolutionary importance of this regulatory system. The uneven distribution across phyla, with high prevalence in Bacillota, Planctomycetota, and Pseudomonadota but relative scarcity in Bacteroidota, suggests that CsrA-mediated regulation may be more important in some lineages than others, reflecting diverse ecological niches and regulatory demands. Against this backdrop, Swarm-seq enabled us to test >500 codon-optimized homologs in a standardized B. subtilis chassis, an approach that revealed a striking and unexpected result: roughly half of the CsrAs assayed were unable to complement the absence of CsrABs, partitioning into a clear bimodal distribution of Class I (motility-inhibiting) and Class II (motility non-inhibiting) variants. Validation through individual testing of 69 randomly selected isolates (46 motile, 23 non-motile) confirmed the robustness of the screen, and the resulting dataset of Class I CsrA variants represents, to our knowledge, the largest experimentally validated functional census of any bacterial post-transcriptional regulator to date.

The Class I/Class II partition is a key biological finding of this study and challenges the long-standing assumption that sequence similarity within a conserved regulator predicts conserved function. Despite all CsrAs in our library targeting GGA-containing binding sites and sharing a near-invariant architecture predicted by AlphaFold3, functional and non-functional variants are interspersed throughout the bacterial tree without clear phylogenetic clustering. Targeted dissection of the hag leader using two distantly related Class I homologs, CsrAHp and CsrASm, shows that this divergence is not explained by simple differences in motif preference: both require the GGA motif in the CsrA binding site and remain sensitive to sequences near the Shine–Dalgarno region, but unlike CsrABs, neither depends on the size or precise GGA positioning of the upstream loop. This reduced sensitivity to target architecture may reflect a more distributed binding mode, in which affinity is spread across multiple partial contacts rather than concentrated at any single structural feature. Even functionally interchangeable homologs therefore may read out target architecture in subtly different ways. Structural comparison of CsrABs, CsrAEc, RsmAPa, RsmNPa, CsrAHp, and CsrASm reinforces this point: visible features such as C-terminal helix length, or the additional α-helix near the RNA-binding site of RsmNPa, do not track with function, and pairwise identity is similarly uninformative, with a 24.5%-identical homolog (RsmNPa) complementing CsrABs, while a 41%-identical one (RsmAPa) does not. This pattern indicates that CsrA regulatory specificity is far more evolutionarily labile than previously appreciated, with individual components gaining and losing function repeatedly across lineages. Such plasticity is consistent with prior observations that CsrA regulons differ dramatically even between closely related species [61–63], and it complements deep mutational scanning approaches that probe the local sequence-function landscape of a single protein [64]. By instead sampling natural variation across the entire bacterial domain, Swarm-seq captures functional diversity shaped by evolution, including substitutions across the protein that may be deleterious, neutral, or compensated by co-evolving residues. The observation that high structural conservation does not predict functional capability also exposes a clear limitation of current computational approaches for inferring RNA-target interactions from structure alone. Together, these findings show that current sequence- and structure-based predictors fall short of capturing the determinants that actually matter, underscoring the value of high-throughput functional assays like Swarm-seq.

While Swarm-seq addresses a critical bottleneck in microbiology, namely the functional characterization of regulatory proteins from the vast majority of bacterial species that remain genetically intractable or unculturable, the platform has important limitations that constrain interpretation of our results. By design, the screen relies on CsrA-mediated repression of a single target, the hag 5′-leader, in a single host. The assay therefore reports on one specific mode of CsrA function: translational repression of a Gram-positive flagellin transcript through CsrA binding sites in its leader. CsrA homologs scored as non-functional (Class II) should not be interpreted as inactive RBPs, since they may still recognize and regulate other transcripts in their native hosts, depend on species-specific cofactors absent from our B. subtilis chassis strain (such as cognate FliW partners or antagonistic sRNAs like CsrB/C), or operate through mechanisms not captured by the hag reporter, including mRNA stabilization, translational activation, or repression of structurally distinct targets. The readout also cannot, on its own, distinguish loss of hag-specific repression from indirect effects such as growth impairment or perturbation of the flagellar cascade upstream of hag. Our pipeline mitigates this by computing phenotypes from input/output abundance comparisons of the variant pool before and after swarming, but we did not systematically measure single-variant growth, and secondary validation in defined backgrounds will be useful to fully separate direct hag repression from pleiotropic effects. Nonetheless, as depicted in Supplementary Fig. S4B, the key Class I and Class II variants that were analyzed downstream of the Swarm-seq experiment did not show any defects in growth compared to the no-CsrA control strain. Relatedly, the pooled Swarm-seq score also reflects competitive behavior within the variant pool, so a CsrA that represses hag effectively but carries a slight expression-level or fitness disadvantage can appear weak in the pooled score yet perform robustly as a monoclonal strain, as we observe for CsrAHp. Furthermore, generalizing the functional divergence we observe to the full scope of CsrA biology will require complementary approaches such as RNA-seq, CLIP-seq, or in vitro binding assays in heterologous or native backgrounds. Despite these caveats, the successful characterization of CsrAs from industrially relevant organisms like P. chlororaphis and environmentally critical bacteria such as N. defluvii illustrates how a tractable heterologous chassis can unlock the regulatory potential of Earth’s microbial diversity, and establishes a precedent for the systematic characterization of other regulatory protein families.

To extend our experimental classifications across the full set of ∼7500 CsrA homologs, we trained a random forest classifier on the ∼400 confidently classified library variants. The model identified key sequence determinants, including serine at position 11, leucine at position 47, glutamate at position 45, lysine at position 7, and isoleucine at position 25—all mapping to the RNA-binding surface. The strong enrichment of predictive positions at the RNA-binding interface, particularly at the junction between the C-terminal helix and main protein body, suggests that subtle changes in CsrA’s RNA binding interface can alter specificity. The Random Forest model predicts function across ∼7500 CsrA homologs, and indicates dramatic phylogenetic variation with groups like Campylobacterota and Bdellovibrionata enriched for predicted functional CsrAs, while others like Krumholzibacteriota and Eisenbacteria show low frequencies. Experimental validation of CsrABb and RsmAPp, which successfully inhibited hag reporter expression as predicted, demonstrates practical utility.

The model represents significant progress given CsrA’s complex evolutionary history. Phylogenetic reconstruction suggests an ancestral CsrA-FliW system in flagellated bacteria [20], yet multiple independent losses occurred during evolution. Despite high sequence conservation (85% identity between E. coli and P. aeruginosa), regulatory targets show rapid evolutionary turnover with dramatically different target gene sets between species [61–63]. This indicates frequent regulatory rewiring and evolutionary flexibility in target recognition while maintaining core protein structure. These evolutionary complexities create particular challenges for ML-based prediction: patchy phylogenetic distribution, mechanistic diversity across lineages, rapid target turnover, and limited training data. Future improvements might incorporate phylogenetic context, integrate multiple data types beyond sequence, and leverage the growing structural database to enhance predictions for this important but evolutionarily dynamic regulatory system. Remarkably, with ∼400 experimentally validated CsrAs, our dataset exceeds previous computational approaches [65], and crucially, every CsrA has functional validation through Swarm-seq providing ground truth validation rather than relying completely on computational inference.

Taken together, this study provides the first comprehensive functional characterization of CsrA diversity across the bacterial tree of life, revealing unexpected functional heterogeneity within this conserved protein family and demonstrating the power of combining high-throughput experimental screening with computational prediction methods. The bimodal distribution of activity we observe across hundreds of homologs implies that regulon-level inferences drawn from genomic data alone are likely to be unreliable for post-transcriptional regulators, and that comparable heterologous-chassis screens can help refine functional assignments and improve their reliability. Swarm-seq and the successful application of B. subtilis as a heterologous chassis provide valuable tools for the broader microbiology community while enhancing our understanding of post-transcriptional regulation and evolution. Future applications could extend to other post-transcriptional regulators, enabling systematic characterization of regulatory protein families across the bacterial domain. The combination of high-throughput screening methods with advanced genetic engineering tools, including CRISPR-based genome editing systems, offers exciting possibilities for engineering novel regulatory circuits and understanding regulatory network evolution.
