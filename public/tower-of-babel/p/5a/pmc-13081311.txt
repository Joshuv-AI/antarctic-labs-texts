Comparative biomechanical analysis of hinge fracture risk between medial open-wedge high tibial osteotomy and distal tuberosity osteotomy: a porcine tibia study
Authors: Hibiki Kakiage, Yoshiya Nibe, Tsuneari Takahashi, Katsushi Takeshita, Hirotaka Chikuda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMedial open-wedge high tibial osteotomy (OWHTO) is widely performed to correct varus malalignment associated with medial compartment osteoarthritis of the knee. Despite its popularity, this technique is frequently accompanied by undesirable complications, such as lateral hinge fractures, an increase in posterior tibial slope (PTS), and a reduction in patellar height. As an alternative, the open-wedge distal tuberosity osteotomy (OWDTO) has been introduced to preserve the patellar position. However, limited biomechanical information exists regarding its influence on hinge fracture stability and PTS changes.PurposeThe present study aimed to compare the biomechanical consequences of OWHTO and OWDTO under cyclic loading conditions, with a particular emphasis on the incidence of hinge fractures, displacement across the osteotomy site, and alterations in PTS.MethodsForty-eight fresh porcine tibiae were allocated into three groups: OWHTO (n = 16), OWDTO with anterior–posterior (AP) cortical screw fixation (n = 16), and OWDTO with AP locking screw fixation (n = 16). All specimens were stabilized using a medial locking plate and subjected to cyclic loading up to 2000 cycles at 800 N. Measurements included anterior and posterior gap variations, displacement along the mechanical axis, and PTS changes. Occurrence of lateral hinge fracture was also recorded. Statistical comparisons were performed using ANOVA with Tukey post hoc testing for continuous outcomes and Fisher’s exact test for categorical data.ResultsThere were no significant group differences in axial displacement or in anterior/posterior gap variation or PTS change. However, OWDTO fixed with AP locking screws demonstrated a significantly higher incidence of lateral hinge fractures compared with OWHTO and OWDTO fixed with cortical screws (p < 0.05).ConclusionThis study demonstrates that hinge fracture susceptibility during OWDTO is dependent on the fixation strategy. While overall construct displacement under axial cyclic loading did not differ among groups, OWDTO with locking screw fixation showed a higher incidence of hinge fracture. These findings highlight the importance of fixation choice when performing OWDTO.

Introduction

Medial open-wedge high tibial osteotomy (OWHTO) has been established as a surgical option for treating patients with medial knee osteoarthritis in the setting of varus deformity [1–3]. Despite its widespread application, OWHTO is known to produce several drawbacks, including patella baja, which can accelerate degeneration of the patellofemoral joint [4, 5]. In an effort to preserve patellar height, Gaasbeek and colleagues described the open-wedge distal tuberosity osteotomy (OWDTO), in which the tibial tubercle is maintained on the distal bone fragment rather than the proximal one [6]. Subsequent clinical series have demonstrated that this modification can effectively mitigate postoperative patellar descent [7, 8].

While OWDTO may resolve one limitation of conventional OWHTO, other important complications remain a concern. These include hinge fractures and increased posterior tibial slope (PTS) [9, 10]. Moreover, the technical demands of OWDTO are high, and a greater overall rate of intraoperative or postoperative complications has been reported compared with OWHTO [8, 11, 12]. Particularly, fractures of the tibial tuberosity and lateral hinge are issues that warrant close biomechanical scrutiny.

Various strategies have been suggested for fixation of the tibial tubercle during OWDTO. Some groups employ cortical screws, which provide compression, whereas others adopt locking screws, which primarily act as positional devices. Finite element analyses and fracture models in different anatomical regions have suggested that cortical screws confer greater stability by producing interfragmentary compression [13–15]. However, whether such biomechanical advantages apply specifically to OWDTO remains poorly defined. Indeed, few comparative experiments have tested the stability of OWHTO and OWDTO using different screw types under physiologic loading conditions [13, 16, 17].

The purpose of this investigation was therefore to perform a controlled biomechanical analysis comparing OWHTO and OWDTO using porcine tibiae. We evaluated the effects of fixation method (cortical vs. locking screw) on hinge fracture incidence, mechanical displacement, and PTS variation following cyclic axial loading. We hypothesized that OWDTO fixed with AP locking screws would demonstrate inferior biomechanical stability and a higher rate of hinge fracture compared with OWHTO or OWDTO fixed with cortical screws.

Methods

Specimens: Forty-eight fresh porcine tibiae (age approximately 6 months, weight range 180–200 kg) were prepared and randomly assigned to three groups: Group H (OWHTO, n = 16), Group C (OWDTO with AP cortical screws, n = 16), and Group L (OWDTO with AP locking screws, n = 16). All procedures were performed in a biomechanics laboratory under standardized conditions. Ethical approval was waived due to the ex vivo nature of the study.

Surgical technique: OWHTO was conducted according to established protocols, with the osteotomy ending 5 mm from the lateral cortex to preserve a hinge. The hinge position was standardized in all specimens using a guide pin directed toward the proximal tibiofibular joint. A medial ASPIC MOW plate (AUSPICIOUS, Tokyo, Japan) was applied for stabilization (Figs. 1, 2 and 3). In OWDTO, a separate osteotomy of the distal tibial tubercle was carried out, leaving the proximal tubercle attached to the main tibial fragment. The osteotomy gap was gradually opened to 6 mm and stabilized with the same medial plate. Group C specimens received AP cortical screws for tubercle fixation (Fig. 4), while Group L received AP locking screws (Fig. 5). Cortical screws (diameter 3.5 mm, full threaded, 50 mm length, with washers) and locking screws (diameter 5.5 mm, full-thread, 50 mm length) were used. In Group C, AP cortical screws were applied with the intent to achieve interfragmentary compression. A 2.5-mm bit was used for drilling, and washers were applied to allow compression of the anterior fragment by screw tightening, similar to the technique used for cancellous lag screw fixation. All screws were inserted manually using the two-finger technique to approximate consistent tightening torque. In all groups, no bone grafts were inserted into the osteotomy gap. After osteotomy opening and fixation, all specimens were evaluated by direct visual inspection to exclude obvious complete lateral hinge fractures before biomechanical testing.

Biomechanical testing: Following osteotomy and fixation, specimens were mounted in a custom jig and loaded axially using a tensile testing machine (Fig. 6). Cyclic loading was performed at a maximum of 800 N for 2000 cycles at 0.5 Hz, simulating repeated postoperative weight-bearing. Displacement in the axial direction was measured at cycles 10, 100, 500, 1000, 1500, and 2000 (Fig. 7). Anterior and posterior osteotomy gap widths were recorded with calipers, and PTS was calculated from the difference between anterior and posterior gap changes relative to the anteroposterior dimension of the tibial plateau (Fig. 8). Computed tomography was not performed. The presence of hinge fractures was assessed after biomechanical testing based on direct visual inspection and schematic image evaluation.

Statistical analysis: Continuous variables were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s post hoc test. Categorical data, including hinge fracture incidence, were evaluated with Fisher’s exact test. Significance was set at p < 0.05. Power analysis confirmed a sample size of 16 per group as adequate.

Results

Displacement: Across all cycles of loading, there were no statistically significant differences among the three groups (Fig. 9). Specimens in Groups H, C, and L maintained comparable stability during the early (10th, 100th cycle) and later stages (500th–2000th cycle) of cyclic loading (Tables 1 and 2).

Anterior/posterior gap and PTS: Changes in both anterior and posterior osteotomy gap dimensions were similar among groups. Although OWDTO groups demonstrated a slight trend toward greater PTS increases, these differences did not reach statistical significance (Table 3).

Post hoc testing revealed that Group L exhibited a markedly higher rate of hinge fractures than Group H and Group C (p = 0.027). The incidence in Group C did not differ significantly from Group H. The relative risk and odds ratio for hinge fracture in Group L compared with Group H were 5.0 (95% CI 1.30–19.30) and 11.67 (95% CI 1.94–69.98), respectively, and compared with Group C were 2.0 (95% CI 0.88–4.54) and 3.67 (95% CI 0.85–15.8),respectively. Most hinge fractures were classified as Takeuchi type Ⅱ.

Discussion

This biomechanical analysis revealed that OWDTO stabilized with AP locking screws showed a significantly higher hinge fracture incidence compared with both OWHTO and OWDTO using cortical screw fixation. These findings indicate that the method of tibial tubercle fixation plays a decisive role in the stability of OWDTO.

From a biomechanical standpoint, cortical screws provide interfragmentary compression that counteracts tensile forces from the patellar tendon on the anterior tibial fragment and promotes more uniform stress distribution at the tuberosity–osteotomy interface, which may reduce localized stress near the lateral hinge. In contrast, locking screws function primarily as positioning devices without compression and may increase hinge fracture susceptibility. Similar principles have been reported in other fracture fixation models; however, the present study extends these findings to OWDTO under cyclic loading conditions [17–19].

From a clinical perspective, hinge fractures are associated with delayed union, loss of correction, persistent knee pain, and worse short-term functional scores [18, 20–23]. Previous clinical studies have demonstrated that unstable or displaced hinge fractures can delay bone union and lead to poorer clinical outcomes after opening-wedge osteotomy [18, 23]. Although the present biomechanical study did not include clinical evaluation, our findings suggest that hinge preservation is important for maintaining construct stability under cyclic loading. Thus, the higher rate of hinge fractures observed in OWDTO with locking screw fixation is clinically relevant. Surgeons should recognize that improper fixation choices in OWDTO may translate into inferior outcomes for patients.

Although an association between hinge fracture and increased displacement was observed, this relationship should be interpreted cautiously. Given the in-vitro design and the absence of progressive loading to failure, the present findings do not establish a causal relationship. Rather, the results indicate an associative trend that warrants further investigation under more physiologic and demanding loading conditions.

Although the Group L exhibited a higher incidence of hinge fractures, no significant differences were observed in total axial displacement among the groups. This finding highlights a dissociation between hinge fracture susceptibility and overall construct displacement, suggesting that hinge fracture occurrence does not necessarily translate into an immediate reduction in global construct stiffness within the loading range tested. This observation may be explained by the limited loading conditions of the present study. The cyclic test involved only 2000 cycles of axial compression, which may not have generated sufficient mechanical stress to induce measurable interfragmentary micromotion differences. Furthermore, the experimental setup did not reproduce rotational or anterior-posterior shear components that are typically encountered during physiological loading. These factors likely reduced the apparent effect of hinge fractures on axial displacement in this in-vitro setting.

Interestingly, although PTS changes did not differ significantly, both OWDTO groups showed a numerical trend toward greater slope increases. This observation is consistent with previous work suggesting that OWDTO can predispose to PTS elevation [13]. However, it is possible that hinge fractures themselves confounded slope measurements, as structural collapse at the lateral hinge could mask true angular changes. Therefore, the lack of statistical significance should be interpreted with caution.

Overall, these results highlight the critical importance of fixation strategy in OWDTO. Cortical screws, by providing compression, enhance mechanical stability and reduce hinge fracture risk, whereas locking screws without compression create mechanical disadvantages. Although the occurrence of hinge fractures is multifactorial, including factors beyond osteotomy type and fixation method [24], careful selection of fixation strategy remains a key modifiable factor [25]. Clinical translation of these findings underscores the need for meticulous surgical technique and careful implant choice.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, porcine tibiae differ from human bone in geometry and mechanical properties, limiting direct clinical extrapolation. The differences between human tibias and porcine tibias in terms of cancellous bone properties might have influenced fixation strengths. However, porcine models are widely accepted for biomechanical knee studies [26, 27]. Second, the evaluation was confined to the immediate postoperative period, without accounting for biological healing processes. Third, loading conditions were restricted to axial compression and did not include rotational or shear forces, which occur in daily activities. Fourth, minor malpositioning of osteotomy or plate placement cannot be excluded despite careful technique. Fifth, the cyclic loading protocol was limited to 2000 cycles, which may not adequately represent the long-term mechanical environment after osteotomy. Finally, no strain gauge or digital image correlation system was employed to quantify hinge microstrain, which could have provided further insight into early crack initiation. Accordingly, the present findings should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory and should not be interpreted as clinically definitive.

Conclusion

OWDTO with locking screws showed a higher incidence of hinge fractures compared with OWHTO and OWDTO using cortical screws, while no significant differences were observed in axial displacement or PTS under cyclic loading. These findings indicate that hinge fracture risk in OWDTO is fixation-dependent and should be interpreted independently of overall construct stiffness. Careful selection of fixation strategy may help reduce hinge-related complications when performing OWDTO.
