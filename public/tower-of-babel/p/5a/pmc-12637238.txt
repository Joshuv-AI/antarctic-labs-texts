Phantom design for tracked ultrasound-based surgical navigation systems
Authors: V.J. van Boheemen, J.M. van der Zee, M. Kuipers, M. Fitski, M.H.W.A. Wijnen, F.J. Siepel, A.F.W. van der Steeg
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
For pediatric oncology patients, radical tumor removal is crucial. Surgical navigation using tracked ultrasound may improve radical removal rates in pediatric surgery, based on positive clinical outcomes in adult tumor surgery. Prior to implementation, such surgical navigation systems must be validated under controlled conditions. This requires surgical phantoms with a speed of sound comparable to human tissue to enable accurate tracked ultrasound imaging and which include anatomical structures with ultrasound and Computed Tomography (CT) contrast. However, scarcity exists of such phantoms. This study presents a method for developing anatomical phantoms tailored for evaluating tracked ultrasound-based surgical navigation systems.•A gelatin-vinegar matrix was selected for its speed of sound, durability and stability.•Tumors were developed using polyvinyl alcohol mixed with glass microspheres and barium sulfate to achieve ultrasound and CT contrast.The speed of sound of the gelatin-vinegar matrix was investigated by depth measurements on both ultrasound and CT imaging. The resulting phantoms are durable, low-cost (<50$ p/phantom) and have a speed of sound approximating human tissue to enable tracked ultrasound. Moreover, the phantom materials are widely available, and the phantoms are easy to make. With this protocol, we offer a method for preclinical validation of ultrasound-based surgical navigation systems.

Background

Accurate intraoperative tumor localization is crucial for complete tumor removal, that is essential for clinical outcomes in pediatric oncology patients [1,2],. However, reliable tumor localization during surgery can be challenging. Especially if tumors are small, non-palpable and non-visible, due to biological changes of the tumor caused by neoadjuvant chemotherapy. Surgical navigation systems that integrate preoperative imaging data (CT or MRI) with the real-time intraoperative environment offer a promising solution to this problem [3,4]. One approach to integrate these modalities is to register anatomical structures from preoperative data to the intraoperative situation using tracked ultrasound images. In this way, surgical navigation systems can provide real-time guidance to surgeons.

We have developed a tracked ultrasound-based surgical navigation system designed for intraoperative use in pediatric tumor removal, using electromagnetic tracking. Before this system can be clinically validated, system validation in a controlled and reproducible environment is crucial to assess technical performance. In literature a scarcity exists for phantoms presenting compatibility for tracked ultrasound to test tracked ultrasound-based surgical navigation systems in a standardized and reproducible manner. Conventional medical phantoms are usually oil-based (i.e. paraffin gel) or water-based (i.e. gelatin, agar or polyvinyl alcohol (PVA)) [5]. Gelatin-based phantoms have acoustic properties close to human tissue which is important for the validation of surgical navigation systems [6,7]. However, phantoms of gelatin alone are not durable, due to microbial growth [8].

To create a standardized test environment for the validation of surgical navigation technology, phantoms should mimic relevant surgical scenarios. The speed of sound in the phantom material should approximate the speed of sound in human tissue (1540 m/s) to be compatible with tracked ultrasound. Other factors for a suitable phantom were durability, fixed anatomical structures to enable repeatable registration and incorporated pseudotumors containing ultrasound and CT contrast. This manuscript presents the design of such phantoms to eventually use them to evaluate the technical performance of tracked ultrasound-based surgical navigation techniques. By addressing this, we aimed to facilitate low-cost systematic validation for tracked ultrasound surgical navigation systems prior to clinical validation.

Method details

Anatomical gelatin-vinegar phantoms were developed with PVA-based tumors located near bone structures. Furthermore, to simulate soft tissue organs, PVA-based phantoms of an organ containing a tumor were developed using casting molds. The phantoms were intended for the evaluation of a tracked ultrasound-based surgical navigation system that uses electromagnetic or optical tracking during surgical procedures. The fabrication pipeline enables the creation of phantoms for a wide range of tracked ultrasound-based surgical navigation technologies, and for various anatomical structures, such as all kinds of bones and soft tissues.

Speed of sound requirement

An essential requirement for the phantoms was that their acoustic properties, specifically the speed of sound, closely match the human tissue. This is crucial for tracked ultrasound-based navigation systems, in which accurate spatial information from tracked ultrasound images is essential for guiding the surgeon precisely towards the tumor. For anatomical structures to appear in their correct spatial location within the ultrasound image, the speed of sound within the phantom must correspond to that assumed by the ultrasound system. Clinical ultrasound systems are calibrated based on a speed of sound assumption in human soft tissue of 1540 m/s [9]. Therefore, to ensure spatial accuracy in navigation processes, the phantom material must consist of a material possessing a speed of sound that approximates 1540 m/s. Simulated tumors

To be able to test the surgical navigation system, simulated tumors in phantoms were required with distinct contrast to surrounding material in both ultrasound and CT scans. Such pseudotumors were fabricated using PVA. Glass spheres were added to enhance ultrasound contrast, and barium sulfate was included to provide radiodensity for CT imaging. A detailed overview of the compositions is shown in Table 1. Pseudotumors were shaped by designing 3D casting molds in Autodesk Fusion 360 and were subsequently printed in polylactic acid (PLA) on a Bambu X1 carbon 3D printer. To fabricate the pseudotumors, distilled water with 7 weight/weight % (w/w %) PVA powder (Mw 89,000–98,000) was heated to 80 °C under continuous electronically stirring until the mixture became transparent (+- 1 h for 200 ml on 100 °C). After cooling to room temperature (+- 5 h on 20 °C), 10 w/w % barium sulfate together with 0.5 w/w % glass microspheres were added and manually stirred through the PVA for 1 min on room temperature (20–22 °C). Prior to the addition, the glass microspheres were pre-dissolved in a few drops of water (+- 5 ml), just enough for the glass microspheres to dissolve. The resulting mixture was poured into the 3D printed tumor casting mold, which was sealed with caulk to prevent leakage. The filled casting mold underwent three freeze and thaw cycles to achieve the desired rigidity. Each cycle consisted of freezing at −20 °C for 16 h followed by thawing at 7 °C for 8 h.

Phantom material

Phantoms representing tumors located near bone structures were positioned in a 2 mm-thick polycarbonate box (20 × 15 × 10 cm) to ensure rigidity of internal components. The phantom material consisted of gelatin mixed with cleaning vinegar. Gelatin was used because of its low cost (<$15 p/phantom), speed of sound and ability to be poured into the phantom and solidify when cooled [7]. Vinegar was added to increase the phantom's durability by lowering the pH, which inhibits microbial and fungal growth, thereby preventing mold formation [14]. Precise composition of the phantom material is detailed in Table 2. The phantom material was assumed to have a speed of sound matching that of human tissue, based on values in literature [6,7]. In our setup, bones from an animal skeleton were embedded. Bones and tumors were positioned within the gelatin-vinegar matrix using a combination of suture fiber fixation and sequential layering of gelatin during the pouring process. Phantoms were created representing tumors near a femur, pelvic nodes and rib tumors, as shown in Fig. 2. The cost of each phantom is approximately $20–50, including materials such as gelatin, vinegar, PVA, 3D printing filament, contrast agents and plastic box. To prepare the gelatin–vinegar matrix, a solution consisting of 55 w/w % distilled water and 33 w/w % vinegar was heated to 60 °C. This amount of vinegar was used due to the ability to prevent microbial growth [14]. Separately, 12 w/w % gelatin sheets were softened by soaking in cold distilled water for five minutes, then added to the heated water and vinegar mixture. This concentration of gelatin is chosen for the stiffness and speed of sound [15,16]. The solution was manually stirred for five minutes and optionally reheated to 60 °C until the gelatin was fully dissolved. Then, the resulting liquid mixture was slowly poured into the phantom, and air bubbles were removed by using a piece of paper to puncture the air bubbles. Finally, the phantoms were cooled in the refrigerator to allow the mixture to solidify, which happened by temperatures below 33 °C [17].

Phantom of organs with a tumor

Specific phantoms were required for the validation of surgical navigation systems on organs. These phantoms simulate the soft tissue and should be resistant to deformation during validation (e.g., organ movements), resulting in the need for a specific phantom material as the gelatin-vinegar is mechanically fragile and therefore ruptures when deformed. Therefore, PVA was chosen due to the high fracture toughness even though its higher costs and lower resistance to fungus development, compared to the gelatin-vinegar matrix. Therefore, in the organ phantom both the organ and tumor material were PVA, following the protocol of Fitski et al. [12]. The PVA material for the organ was equally made as the pseudotumors, however without addition of glass microspheres and barium sulfate to obtain contrast. Again, tumors were made in casting molds first. Subsequently, a tumor was positioned within a 3D printed casting mold of the organ, and the PVA solution for the organ was poured over it to form the composite phantom, as shown in Fig. 4. In this study, kidney casting molds were designed according to CT scans of a pediatric patient with a nephroblastoma (i.e., Wilms tumor). PVA at a concentration of 7 w/w % with three freeze (−20 °C) and thaw (5 °C) cycles is used to effectively mimic kidney tissue in terms of elastography properties, speed of sound and compressibility [10,11,18],. Durability

The resistance to fungus development of gelatin-based phantoms was achieved by the addition of 33 w/w % vinegar, which lowered the pH and inhibited microbial growth. When stored in a refrigerator and covered with cling film to prevent dehydration, the gelatin-vinegar phantoms remained stable and usable for at least six months. During this period, the phantoms remained intact after repeated user handling representative of typical use, including mechanical stress (ultrasound scanning) and thermal stress (repeated transitions between room temperature (20 °C) and refrigerator (5 °C)). In contrast, the PVA kidney phantoms were durable for only six-eight weeks, after which microbial growth became visible. They were stored in distilled water in the refrigerator to prevent dehydration. During their usable period, the PVA kidney phantoms maintained their resistance to both mechanical and thermal stress associated with intended use.

Broad applicability

We provide a protocol to develop a gelatin-vinegar phantom incorporating rigid anatomical structures with embedded tumors that are simultaneously visible on both ultrasound and CT. The phantom is durable, low-cost (<$50 p/phantom) and has a speed of sound close to the average value used in clinical ultrasound (1540 m/s), making it suitable for tracked ultrasound applications. This fabrication approach can be used to create phantoms representing various anatomical regions. Furthermore, the phantoms provide a robust setting for testing multiple medical technologies, including augmented reality systems and automated ultrasound segmentation algorithms.

Method validation

To visually confirm the speed of sound of the gelatin-vinegar phantom material, the depth of a selected point within the phantom was measured using both CT and ultrasound imaging. At the same location in the phantom, measured depth was 53.76 mm on the CT scan and 53.73 mm on ultrasound. This near-identical correspondence indicates that speed of sound through the phantom material (1541 m/s), approximating the average speed of sound in human soft tissue (1540 m/s), which is the calibration standard for most clinical ultrasound systems. This correspondence is important for validating tracked ultrasound-based surgical navigation systems, where accurate 3D spatial information from ultrasound images is critical for the safe guidance during surgery. The speed of sound of the organ phantom and pseudotumor based on PVA had a relatively smaller impact on localization accuracies due to smaller size. This speed of sound is comparable to that of human soft tissue [10,18,19]. Furthermore, the phantom demonstrates durability. The long lifespan of the gelatin-based phantom is primarily due to the addition of 33 w/w % vinegar. The gelatin-vinegar phantom maintained its structural and acoustic properties, including stiffness and speed of sound, for at least six months, and no microbial growth was observed. Lastly, the phantoms were composed of materials that are safe to use, with no toxic substances released from either the gelatin-vinegar matrix or PVA during development or after finalization.

Limitations

The most important limitation of this study regards the measurement used to determine speed of sound in the gelatin-vinegar phantom material. This measurement method is prone to errors, primarily due to manual measurement and relatively small depth of 5.4 cm at which it was performed. The speed of sound was estimated by comparing depth measurements of the phantom obtained from ultrasound imaging and CT. While this method provides a practical approximation, it does not offer the precision of dedicated acoustic measurements. More accurate determination of the speed of sound typically requires time-of-flight measurements using ultrasonic transmission setups with precisely known transducer distances and high temporal resolution (e.g., via digital oscilloscopes) [7,20]. However, due to equipment limitations such high-precision measurements were not feasible. Therefore, a comparative imaging-based approach was employed. This method yields sufficient approximation for our intended purpose, namely, validating a surgical navigation system.
