Leveraging ChatGPT to support terminology learning in oral anatomy: a mixed-methods study among linguistically diverse dental students
Authors: Avita Rath
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEnglish-medium instruction (EMI) in health professional education presents linguistic barriers for non-native English-speaking students, particularly in terminology-dense subjects like dental anatomy. While artificial intelligence (AI) has shown promise in educational contexts, its application to support terminology learning among multilingual learners remains underexplored. This study evaluated the impact of ChatGPT-assisted learning on terminology comprehension and student engagement among international dental students in Malaysia.MethodsA convergent mixed-methods pilot study was conducted with 35 Chinese international Year 1 and Year 2 dental students enrolled in an oral anatomy course at SEGi University. Students completed pre- and post-intervention comprehension tests and engagement surveys. Over an eight-week period, students used ChatGPT to clarify terminology and explore anatomical content. ChatGPT usage logs were analyzed, and post-intervention focus group discussions were conducted. Quantitative data were analyzed using paired t-tests and Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Tests; qualitative data underwent thematic analysis.ResultsComprehension scores improved significantly post-intervention (mean increase = 5.7 points, p< 0.001; Cohen’s d = 1.91). Engagement scores also increased significantly (p < 0.001), reflecting enhanced autonomy and motivation. ChatGPT was used primarily for term clarification (45%), function explanation (32%), and structural comparisons (23%). Thematic analysis revealed four key themes: (1) Clarity and Confidence, (2) Self-Directed Learning, (3) Concerns About Accuracy and Overreliance, and (4) Ethical and Privacy Awareness.ConclusionsAI-assisted terminology learning significantly improved comprehension and engagement among linguistically diverse dental students, aligning with principles from Cognitive Load Theory (CLT) and Self-Determination Theory (SDT). However, ethical concerns—particularly around data privacy and unchecked reliance—highlight the importance of faculty guidance and institutional oversight. These findings underscore the value of hybrid AI-human approaches that are pedagogically sound, ethically responsible, and culturally adaptive.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-07968-0.Keywords: Artificial intelligence, Dental education, Terminology comprehension, ChatGPT, Multilingual learners, English-medium instruction, Self-directed learning, Student engagement, Ethics in AI

Introduction

Higher education continues to globalize, with increasing numbers of students enrolling in institutions outside their home countries. This transnational mobility is often facilitated through English-medium instruction (EMI), in which academic content is delivered in English to students whose first language is not English [1]. This is particularly true in non-Anglophone countries, where English is viewed as a gateway to international academic and professional competitiveness [2, 3]. While EMI enables internationalisation, it also introduces significant challenges—especially for students who must simultaneously master specialised disciplinary content and navigate academic English [4, 5]. This is particularly acute in health professions education, where dense technical terminology—often embedded within disciplinary discourse—can become a cognitive burden for linguistically diverse learners [6, 7]. In these contexts, supporting comprehension of terminology is critical to enabling equitable participation and academic success.

Malaysia has emerged as a regional hub for international education, with policy initiatives such as the Malaysia Education Blueprint 2015–2025 aimed at boosting foreign student enrollment [8]. At SEGi University, for instance, a significant proportion of students in the Bachelor of Dental Surgery program are from China and other countries where English is not the first language. These students often encounter linguistic barriers in terminology-intensive subjects, such as oral anatomy, where a deep comprehension of structural vocabulary underpins all future clinical training. While traditional supports (e.g., glossaries or supplemental language instruction) offer some assistance, they are not always responsive to students’ real-time learning needs, prompting interest in more adaptive and interactive solutions.

Artificial intelligence (AI) tools—particularly those leveraging natural language processing—offer promising scaffolds for multilingual students in EMI contexts. In dental education, where conceptual precision is vital, AI-powered platforms like ChatGPT can assist learners by simplifying complex terminology, contextualising unfamiliar words and responding to iterative queries in a judgment-free space [9, 10]. These features may reduce extraneous cognitive load [11], enabling learners to focus more effectively on core conceptual content. Importantly, such tools can also promote learner autonomy—an essential component of self-directed learning and motivation—when embedded thoughtfully into pedagogical practices [12, 13]. Luckin et al. further emphasise that AI’s value lies not only in content delivery but in empowering learners to ask questions, receive feedback, and engage in self-regulated learning [12]. Learner personality may also play a significant role in shaping the educational impact of AI-supported tools. For example, recent research by Özbey and Yasa found that students with high levels of Openness and Agreeableness showed greater receptivity toward AI in clinical education. At the same time, those scoring lower in Extraversion and Neuroticism reported less trust and confidence in AI-generated information [14]. Similarly, Goolsby et al. observed that personality traits, such as Conscientiousness and Emotional Stability, were predictive of academic success in dental students, influencing how learners engage with feedback and adopt self-regulated study practices [15]. These insights suggest that student engagement with ChatGPT may not be uniform but is shaped by individual cognitive-emotional profiles. Moreover, a 50-year meta-analysis by Li et al. (2024) demonstrated dominant personality profiles among dental students—particularly ESTJ and ISTJ types—who may favour structured, guided learning environments, potentially shaping their responsiveness to AI-mediated learning [16].

From a motivational lens, the use of AI-driven scaffolding supports the principles of Self-Determination Theory (SDT), which emphasises the importance of autonomy, competence, and relatedness in fostering intrinsic motivation and engagement [13]. Students can independently access support without feeling stigmatised, fostering confidence and encouraging self-directed learning. While AI tools offer potential benefits, they also raise ethical concerns—including overreliance on automated feedback, superficial engagement, algorithmic bias, and risks to data privacy—especially for linguistically diverse student populations [17, 18]. Without appropriate oversight, students may uncritically accept AI-generated explanations, which could compromise deeper learning. These risks underscore the importance of structured, faculty-guided implementation to ensure pedagogically sound and ethically responsible use of AI in educational contexts. Accordingly, this study not only evaluates the educational impact of AI-assisted learning, but also incorporates ethical safeguards through student guidelines, faculty oversight, and monitoring of responsible AI engagement.

In dental education, AI-assisted learning has been explored primarily in diagnostic support and simulation, with limited focus on terminology acquisition—particularly for linguistically diverse learners [9, 10]. Existing studies highlight AI’s potential to improve conceptual clarity and support adaptive learning, but they emphasize the need for structured, pedagogically aligned implementation [19]. Moreover, little is known about how students interact with AI tools in real time, and how such interactions influence learning outcomes, autonomy, and ethical engagement.

Faculty involvement is also a key determinant of successful AI integration. Studies suggest that without faculty oversight, AI adoption can become inconsistent and fragmented, especially if driven solely by students [20]. Yet, research on faculty engagement with AI in dental education remains sparse.

This study aims to address these gaps by evaluating the effectiveness of AI-assisted terminology support for international dental students in a Malaysian university context. Specifically, the study investigates:

Whether AI-assisted tools improve students’ comprehension of dental terminology.

By adopting a mixed-methods design, this study contributes to the growing body of evidence on AI integration in health professional education. It offers practical insights for institutions seeking to implement AI in an ethical and effective manner—particularly in linguistically diverse, resource-constrained contexts.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a convergent mixed-methods design, wherein quantitative and qualitative data were collected and analysed separately but interpreted together to generate integrated insights [21]. A pilot implementation approach was adopted to evaluate the effectiveness of AI-assisted terminology learning among Chinese international dental students at SEGi University, Malaysia. This design enabled concurrent assessment of both outcome trends and student experiences, enhancing triangulation and methodological robustness [22]. The study focused specifically on oral anatomy, a subject characterised by high terminology density and foundational relevance in early dental education. This scope provided a controlled context for assessing the AI tool’s impact on terminology comprehension and student engagement.

Participants and sampling

Participants were Year 1 and Year 2 Chinese international students enrolled in the Bachelor of Dental Surgery (BDS) program. A total of 35 students participated (20 Year 1 and 15 Year 2), representing a purposive sample selected based on their enrollment in oral anatomy and their status as non-native English speakers in an English-medium instruction (EMI) setting [1, 6]. This sampling strategy ensured the relevance of the intervention to students most likely to benefit from AI-based terminology support. The sample size aligns with recommendations for pilot studies in education research and is sufficient to detect preliminary effects in mixed-methods investigations [23]. Additionally, purposive sampling enabled balanced inclusion of Year 1 and Year 2 students, capturing variation in early program experience, as recommended in mixed-methods education research [24].

Ethical approval was obtained from SEGi University’s Ethics Committee (SEGiEC/StR/FOD/201/2024–2025). Informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki [25] and the Belmont Report [26]. Confidentiality and anonymity were maintained through the use of coded identifiers, and all data were stored in password-protected systems accessible only to the research team.

Intervention: AI terminology support implementation

The AI tool used in this study was ChatGPT (OpenAI, GPT-4), selected for its natural language processing capabilities, accessibility, and suitability for academic tasks. Students received a brief orientation on effective prompt engineering and safe usage. Over the eight-week intervention, students were encouraged to use ChatGPT at least twice weekly to support terminology comprehension in oral anatomy. They engaged with ChatGPT for clarification of concepts, definitions, and application-based queries related to course content.

Faculty were informed about the intervention and provided with an orientation to ensure ethical transparency and institutional alignment. However, their involvement was intentionally limited to a non-participatory role. This approach was chosen to preserve the exploratory and student-centered nature of the study and to avoid influencing student behavior or creating perceptions of academic surveillance. Limiting faculty presence allowed for a more authentic assessment of how students independently engaged with ChatGPT during self-directed study.

The intervention followed three structured phases:

Pre-Intervention Phase: Students completed a baseline test on terminology comprehension and a validated engagement questionnaire [27]. They were introduced to ChatGPT in a supervised session that included ethical guidance on usage, including limitations, data privacy, and academic integrity. Students were provided with a briefing sheet illustrating safe query practices (e.g., avoiding personally identifiable or patient-related details) and examples of effective prompts. These guidelines were reiterated at weekly check-ins to reinforce ethical AI usage. This approach aligns with emerging best practices in AI ethics education within health professions contexts [28, 29].

Data collection

A combination of qualitative and quantitative data collection methods was employed in this study.

For the quantitative component, a researcher-developed 20-item multiple-choice test was used to assess students’ comprehension of oral anatomy terminology before and after the intervention. The test items were developed in direct alignment with the Year 1 and 2 oral anatomy course objectives and focused on foundational terminology necessary for understanding anatomical relationships, functions, and structures. The intended difficulty level was moderate, emphasizing conceptual understanding rather than rote recall, in line with recommended practices for assessment in medical education [30]. The test was reviewed by two subject matter experts—one specializing in dental anatomy and the other in medical education—to ensure content accuracy, clarity, and curricular alignment. A pilot test was subsequently conducted with five third-year dental students (not included in the study sample) to evaluate item clarity, cognitive load, and completion time. While formal calculations of a content validity index or inter-rater reliability were not conducted, iterative revisions were made based on expert feedback and pilot responses to improve the tool’s relevance and usability.

In addition to the terminology test, the adapted Utrecht Work Engagement Scale for Students (UWES-S) was administered pre- and post-intervention to measure changes in student engagement levels across the domains of vigour, dedication, and absorption [31]. To supplement these measures, ChatGPT usage logs were analysed to capture the frequency and type of queries submitted by students, as well as average interaction time per week. These quantitative data provided triangulated insights into the impact of AI-assisted learning on comprehension and learner engagement.

For the qualitative component, student perspectives on AI-assisted learning were explored through open-ended survey questions and post-intervention focus group discussions. Focus groups were selected as the primary qualitative method to enable open, dialogic exploration of students’ learning experiences with AI. This approach allowed participants to reflect on their individual and shared use of ChatGPT, surface emergent concerns, and elaborate on aspects of comprehension and engagement not easily captured through surveys alone [7]. A total of four focus groups were conducted, each comprising 6 to 8 students, stratified across Year 1 and Year 2 cohorts to capture a range of learning experiences. While the primary stratification was by year of study to capture different levels of program immersion, informal efforts were made to ensure demographic balance (e.g., gender, prior exposure to AI tools) within focus groups, enhancing diversity of perspectives. Stratification by year of study enabled inclusion of perspectives reflecting different levels of program immersion and exposure to terminology challenges, as recommended for enhancing variation in qualitative samples [24].

The focus group discussions were guided by a semi-structured interview protocol (see Supplementary File 1) designed to elicit views on ChatGPT’s perceived usefulness, its influence on autonomy and comprehension, and ethical concerns such as overreliance and data privacy. Discussions were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and anonymised to ensure confidentiality. Transcripts were analysed thematically using NVivo 12, following an inductive coding approach that allowed for the emergence of context-specific themes. Investigator triangulation and peer debriefing were used to enhance the trustworthiness of findings [32]. Although English language proficiency and digital literacy were not formally measured in this pilot, students’ self-reported confidence with technology was informally noted during onboarding to inform facilitation.

Data analysis

Quantitative data were analysed using SPSS (v28.0). All statistical analyses were conducted within SPSS, with a significance level set at p < 0.05. Prior to inferential testing, data distributions were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Terminology test scores met assumptions of normality, permitting the use of a paired t-test to evaluate pre- and post-intervention differences. Normality was confirmed using the Shapiro-Wilk test and inspection of Q-Q plots, meeting the assumptions required for parametric testing [33]. The potential influence of unmeasured variables (e.g., prior English proficiency, digital literacy) was taken into consideration during interpretation. These factors will be systematically included as covariates in future larger-scale studies [34].

Student engagement scores from the UWES-S, which deviated from normality, were analysed using the Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Test. Descriptive statistics were used to summarise ChatGPT usage patterns, including frequency, type of queries, and duration of engagement. There were no missing values, as all participants completed the required assessments. Outliers were checked via boxplots, and none exceeded ± 3 standard deviations or warranted exclusion. A priori power analysis was not conducted due to the fixed size of the Year 1 and Year 2 cohorts. However, effect sizes (Cohen’s d for the t-test and r for non-parametric analysis) were calculated and reported to aid in interpretation and future study design. Although prior English proficiency and digital literacy were not systematically assessed, their potential influence was considered during interpretation, and future studies will incorporate these as measured variables to reduce confounding. Thematic analysis was conducted following Braun and Clarke’s six-phase approach [31]. Two researchers independently coded the data, followed by peer debriefing to ensure coding reliability. Triangulation of findings was achieved by comparing themes across focus group transcripts, survey responses, and AI usage patterns [35].

Rationale for mixed-methods pilot design

This pilot study was designed to generate preliminary evidence and inform future longitudinal or controlled trials. While the absence of a control group is a limitation, the use of triangulated data sources, structured implementation, and integration of student voice enhances the credibility and contextual relevance of the findings [35].

Results

Quantitative findings

Terminology comprehension scores

A total of 35 students (20 Year 1, 15 Year 2) completed both the pre- and post-intervention assessments. Pre-intervention scores on the 20-item oral anatomy terminology test averaged 10.4 (SD = 2.8), while post-intervention scores improved significantly to a mean of 16.1 (SD = 2.4). A paired-sample t-test revealed a statistically significant increase in scores, t(34) = 11.28, p < 0.001, indicating a large effect size (Cohen’s d = 1.91), suggesting substantial improvement in terminology comprehension following the AI-assisted intervention (Fig. 1; Table 1).

AI usage patterns

ChatGPT usage logs showed that students used the AI tool an average of 2.6 times per week, with an average interaction duration of 8.4 min. Most frequent query types included: (1) clarification of anatomical terms (45%), (2) explanations of functions or clinical relevance (32%), and (3) comparisons between similar structures (23%) (Fig. 2). Usage was higher in Year 1 than in Year 2 students. Representative queries included: “What is the difference between enamel and cementum?”, “Can you explain the role of the trigeminal nerve in tooth sensation?”, and “How does the periodontal ligament support the tooth during mastication?” These examples illustrate how students used ChatGPT to clarify both structural definitions and applied anatomical functions relevant to their course content. Clarity and confidence

Students reported increased clarity in understanding anatomical terms and described feeling more confident participating in class discussions. A Year 1 student noted, “ChatGPT helped me learn words that I didn’t understand in class, and I felt less scared to ask questions later.”

Self-directed and personalized learning

Participants appreciated the autonomy in using ChatGPT to guide their own learning. Many described the tool as a “private tutor” that responded without judgment. A Year 2 participant shared, “I liked that I could ask the same question multiple times and get better explanations each time.”

Concerns about over-reliance and accuracy

While most responses were positive, some students expressed caution about depending too much on AI. A few reported that not all responses were accurate or relevant, highlighting the need for faculty guidance. One student commented, “Sometimes the answer was too general or not related exactly to dental anatomy.”

Ethical and privacy awareness

tudents demonstrated awareness of ethical considerations, such as the need to cross-check information and concerns about data privacy. Most students appreciated the ethical briefing provided at the start of the intervention, stating that it helped them navigate AI use with greater caution and responsibility. One year 2 student noted, “Even though we were told it was safe, I still avoided typing anything too personal into ChatGPT—just to be careful”. However, a few participants remained wary of entering sensitive or personally identifiable information into the AI system despite reassurances, reflecting an ongoing ambivalence about AI’s role in educational contexts.

Triangulation of findings

Quantitative improvements in terminology comprehension and engagement were corroborated by qualitative insights reflecting increased confidence, motivation, and active learning behaviours. Triangulation also revealed a shared student awareness of AI use’s limitations and ethical considerations, reinforcing the need for structured, guided implementation. This coherence between quantitative gains and qualitative themes enhances the credibility of our findings and supports their interpretability despite the study’s pilot nature.

Discussion

The integration of AI in medical and dental education has garnered considerable attention, with growing evidence supporting its role in enhancing student engagement, comprehension, and self-directed learning [9, 10, 36]. The present study reinforces these perspectives, demonstrating that AI-assisted terminology support significantly improved comprehension among Linguistically diverse dental students in oral anatomy. The statistically significant improvement from 10.4 to 16.1 in comprehension scores, coupled with enhanced engagement levels, supports the premise that AI can serve as an effective supplement to traditional pedagogy, particularly for students facing linguistic barriers.

From a theoretical standpoint, the findings are well-aligned with both Self-Determination Theory (SDT) and Cognitive Load Theory (CLT) [11, 13]. CLT posits that instructional design should reduce unnecessary mental effort (extraneous load) so that learners can devote cognitive resources to meaningful processing [11]. In this study, AI reduced linguistic barriers, enabling students to concentrate on core anatomical concepts rather than struggling with unfamiliar terminology. The positive impact on student engagement also aligns with SDT’s dimensions of autonomy and competence, as students experienced greater control over their learning and confidence in navigating challenging content [13]. For example, the predominance of term clarification queries (45% of total) directly illustrates learners’ attempts to reduce extraneous cognitive load as posited by CLT [11]. The significant gains in absorption and dedication scores map onto SDT’s constructs of competence and autonomy [13], reflecting genuine motivational benefits.

Crucially, these results address a notable research gap. While prior studies have focused on AI’s use in diagnostic simulation and image recognition in dental education, few have explored its capacity to support terminology learning for non-native English-speaking students [9, 36]. The current findings extend this literature by demonstrating that AI can scaffold terminology acquisition in context-sensitive and adaptive ways—particularly valuable in EMI-based and multilingual education settings. This also aligns with Airey’s (2011) concept of disciplinary literacy, which emphasises the need for students not only to acquire technical vocabulary but also to develop fluency in the discourse practices specific to their academic discipline. By simplifying and contextualising complex anatomical terms, ChatGPT may support learners in navigating the linguistic demands of disciplinary knowledge in EMI environments [7].

This study also confirms that student engagement is not merely a by-product of novelty but reflects authentic motivation, confidence, and clarity in learning. The increase in UWES-S engagement scores—corroborated by qualitative insights—suggests that AI-supported learning, when appropriately structured, can promote deeper learner investment and classroom participation.

Thematic findings from the focus group interviews closely aligned with quantitative results, demonstrating effective triangulation [22]. For example, students who reported increased comprehension and confidence in survey responses also shared during focus groups that ChatGPT helped them “check understanding immediately” and “learn at [their] own pace.” These insights contextualized the upward shifts in UWES-S engagement scores and terminology test performance. From a theoretical perspective, the reduction in linguistic and cognitive strain supports Cognitive Load Theory (CLT) by suggesting that AI scaffolding reduced extraneous load, allowing students to focus on core anatomical content [11, 17]. This is consistent with recent work suggesting that AI can support deeper conceptual learning by alleviating language and cognitive barriers [18]. Furthermore, the emphasis on self-paced exploration and improved confidence aligns with Self-Determination Theory (SDT), particularly the satisfaction of autonomy and competence needs [13], echoing prior research on AI-mediated environments that foster motivation and learner agency [19]. These findings may also be informed by learner personality traits. As Özbey and Yasa (2025) argue, individual variation in personality significantly affects how students perceive and engage with AI in educational settings [14]. Students with high openness may be more willing to experiment with ChatGPT, while more anxious or less extroverted students may exhibit caution or resistance. This aligns with the broader literature on personality in dental education, where traits such as Conscientiousness and Agreeableness are associated with stronger academic performance and learner autonomy [15]. Furthermore, Li et al. (2024) found that dominant MBTI profiles among dental students—particularly ISTJ and ESTJ types—often prefer structured, reliable information sources, which may explain varied patterns of interaction with ChatGPT’s open-ended, iterative interface [16].

Nonetheless, ethical considerations merit emphasis. As students themselves noted, AI-generated content may vary in accuracy, and unchecked reliance could foster passive learning or misinformation [19, 20]. Faculty involvement thus remains essential to guide appropriate AI use, verify information accuracy, and model critical appraisal [20, 23]. Without such oversight, students may adopt AI as a shortcut rather than a scaffold.

Furthermore, the study surfaces important implications regarding institutional preparedness. The need for faculty training in AI literacy is crucial to prevent digital divides and ensure equitable learning. This aligns with emerging calls for health professional education institutions to co-design AI integration strategies that balance innovation with pedagogical responsibility [20, 28].

This pilot study demonstrates that AI tools like ChatGPT can provide accessible, personalized support for linguistically diverse learners, particularly in subjects with dense terminology such as oral anatomy. However, it also underscores that AI should not be viewed as a standalone solution. A hybrid AI-human approach, incorporating faculty feedback, ethical policies, and curriculum alignment, is essential to ensure responsible and sustainable AI adoption [29].

Finally, while the results are promising, they are tempered by limitations, including a small sample size, the absence of a control group, and reliance on self-reported data. These were acknowledged and mitigated through methodological triangulation [22], but they nevertheless restrict generalizability. Additionally, we acknowledge that factors such as students’ prior English language proficiency, digital literacy levels, and pre-existing familiarity with AI platforms may have influenced both engagement and comprehension outcomes. These potential confounding variables were not systematically assessed in this pilot study and should be addressed in future research designs to enhance validity and generalizability. Future research should investigate the longitudinal effects of AI-supported learning across diverse educational settings and examine the perspectives of educators, whose roles are pivotal in shaping the AI-learning ecosystem [37]. They should also explore how learner personality traits shape interaction patterns, trust, and cognitive engagement with AI tools like ChatGPT, building on the findings of recent scholarly works [14–16]. Studies should systematically measure and adjust for potential confounders such as prior English proficiency and digital literacy to strengthen validity and interpretability of findings. While purposive sampling enabled the inclusion of students most relevant to the study aims, this strategy limits representativeness. Future research will aim for more diverse sampling strategies, including random or stratified approaches, to enhance generalizability [24, 35].

Conclusion and implications for practice

This study provides empirical evidence that AI-assisted terminology learning, particularly through ChatGPT, can enhance comprehension and engagement among linguistically diverse dental students. AI-enabled tools align well with both Cognitive Load Theory and Self-Determination Theory by reducing linguistic cognitive load and supporting learner autonomy. These technologies offer promising scaffolds for self-directed learning and can be thoughtfully integrated into multilingual, EMI-based programs where terminology mastery is essential.

Importantly, this study highlights that AI integration must be pedagogically sound and ethically guided. While AI can support learner independence and access to knowledge, unchecked use may lead to superficial understanding or overreliance on potentially inaccurate outputs [18, 19]. Faculty involvement and structured institutional policies remain critical to model responsible AI use, foster critical thinking, and mitigate risks.

For educators and institutions, this research underscores the value of AI as a supplemental—not substitutive—tool within guided learning ecosystems. As AI platforms become increasingly accessible, structured training initiatives must be implemented to build AI literacy among both students and faculty. Attention to digital equity, algorithmic transparency, and ethical safeguards will be essential to ensure inclusive, context-sensitive adoption.

Future research should explore the longitudinal impacts of AI-supported learning across diverse cultural and linguistic contexts, the role of faculty in co-mediating AI engagement, and the development of adaptive AI models aligned with disciplinary depth and learner diversity [36, 37]. It should also explore faculty perspectives and institutional readiness for AI integration, as co-designed approaches are critical to developing sustainable, context-sensitive educational strategies. As this field matures, dental and health professions education must lead by example in ensuring that AI technologies are deployed not only for innovation’s sake, but for the advancement of equity, understanding, and human learning.
