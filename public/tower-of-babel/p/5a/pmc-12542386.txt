The impact of using “teaching, exercising, and practicing” three-step training mode on the performance and satisfaction of internship nursing students in the training of fall prevention education ability: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Jie Yang, Tianyang Mao, Li Zhou, Ningying Li, Hongyan Wu, Qiaoling Wang, Yangyang Zeng, Ke Liu, Juan Zhou, Jun Wang, Bing Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundClinical internships are a crucial component of nursing education, offering students the opportunity to apply theoretical knowledge in a clinical setting. Patient falls represent a common issue in hospitals and are a central focus of patient safety management. Enhancing nursing students’ proficiency in fall prevention education during their internships is essential for advancing patient safety and improving future care quality.MethodsThis study involved 159 nursing students who were randomly assigned to either an experimental group or a control group. The experimental group, consisting of 80 students, participated in a three-step training mode labeled ‘teach, exercise, and practice’ Meanwhile, the remaining 79 students in the control group received training through traditional teaching methods. Both groups underwent training to enhance their skills in fall prevention education. Following the training, assessments included a theoretical examination, an Objective Structured Clinical Examination (OSCE), and a teaching satisfaction survey. Data collected were analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test and χ² test.ResultsIn the theoretical examination, both the experimental group and the control group demonstrated equivalent effectiveness, with significant improvements post-intervention; however, no significant differences were observed between the two groups (P = 0.884). In the Objective Structured Clinical Examination (OSCE), the experimental group performed notably better in several areas: evaluation of patients and their families (P = 0.044), environmental assessment and education (P = 0.003), education on patient mobility out of bed (P < 0.001), and communication skills (P < 0.001). Regarding satisfaction, the experimental group, which followed the “teaching, exercising, and practicing” three-step training mode, reported higher levels of satisfaction (P < 0.001).ConclusionCompared to traditional teaching methods, the “teaching, exercising, and practicing” three-step training mode reinforces the knowledge among internship nursing students regarding risk assessment for falls and strengthens their ability to provide health education on fall prevention. It enhances the satisfaction of nursing students with their training. Furthermore, it facilitates the integration of fall prevention theory with clinical practice for internship nursing students, thereby improving their proficiency in delivering fall prevention education.Clinical trail numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-025-03895-z.Keywords: Students nursing, Education, Satisfaction, Three-step training mode, Fall prevention education

Background

Nurses play a pivotal role in the delivery of medical services. Health education, primarily conducted by nurses, significantly contributes to patient rehabilitation [1, 2]. Enhancing the effectiveness of nursing health education is vital for advancing patient health capabilities and developing the nursing profession [3]. Clinical internships form an essential segment of nursing education, providing students the opportunity to apply their theoretical knowledge in clinical settings [4].

Falls are a common issue among hospitalized patients and a central concern in patient safety management [5]. Incidents of falling during hospitalization can lead to severe complications, prolong hospital stays, increase costs, and potentially diminish the future quality of life for patients [6, 7]. Consequently, fall prevention is critical for patient prognosis. Research by Oliver et al. has demonstrated that fall rates can be significantly reduced through specific interventions [8]. Similarly, Dykes et al. have shown that enhancing nurses’ knowledge of fall prevention contributes to lower fall rates [9]. It is also crucial to reduce hospital fall incidents through structured nursing interventions [10–12], alongside cultivating a robust knowledge base among nurses. For nursing students, there is limited literature on research aimed at enhancing their health education abilities in fall prevention. However, some studies suggest that the structure and processes of nursing curricula may inadvertently foster student errors and compromise patient safety [13]. Vaismoradi et al. argue that nursing educators need to develop strategies to promote the explicit application of safety knowledge and skills in nursing practice. Additionally, nursing managers and clinical nurses should actively mentor nursing students, as this approach is more conducive to ensuring patient safety [14]. Therefore Improving the capability of nursing students in fall prevention education during their internships is imperative for enhancing patient safety and future care quality.

Currently, theoretical training alone is insufficient for equipping nursing students with effective fall prevention skills. To address these deficiencies, Our research center has improved upon the traditional theory of scenario simulation teaching and established a “teaching, exercising, and practicing” three-step training mode. On the basis of the routine scenario simulation teaching conducted for nursing students, the element of clinical practice has been incorporated. Under the accompaniment and guidance of teaching instructors, intern nursing students are enabled to apply the personal abilities enhanced during theoretical learning and simulation training to the process of clinical practice. Through practice, they can truly experience the problems that theoretical knowledge and skills may encounter in this process, identify the deficiencies in their learning, bridge the gap between teaching and practice, and thereby facilitate the mastery of fall prevention knowledge by intern nursing students. This study primarily aims to explore the impact of the “teaching, exercising, and practicing” three-step training mode on the knowledge level of fall prevention and the proficiency of skill operation in the health education ability of fall prevention among nursing interns. The secondary aim is to assess nursing students’ satisfaction with this training approach.

Methods

Study design and participants

This experimental study was conducted at The People’s Hospital of Leshan from September to December 2021. The sample comprised 159 nursing students who had completed courses in basic nursing, surgical nursing, internal medicine nursing, and health assessment. Using a random lottery method, the 2021 cohort of interns was divided into two groups: the experimental group, which employed the “teaching, exercising, and practicing” three-step training mode with 80 participants, and the control group, which followed traditional teaching methods, including 79 participants(Fig. 1). Post-training, theoretical examinations were administered, and objective structured clinical examinations were conducted by two independent researchers who were unaware of the study’s purpose and the participants’ group assignments. In cases of discrepancies in results or opinions, a consensus was reached through discussion. Subsequently, the interns participated in a teaching satisfaction survey.

Study flowchart

The calculation of the sample size was based on the study by Dykes et al. The data indicated that the knowledge test scores before and after fall education were 5.1 ± 1.8 and 6.6 ± 1.7, respectively. With α = 0.05 (two-tailed test), power = 1-β = 0.9, and equal sample sizes for the experimental and control groups, PASS 15.0 software was used to determine that 30 participants were required per group. Considering a 20% dropout rate and other factors, 38 participants were included in each group, totaling at least 76 subjects, ensuring the scientific validity of the study design.

“Teaching, exercising and practicing” three-step training mode

“Teaching”: In the first week, nursing students receive courseware and a list of 35 key knowledge points, which include topics such as Fall Risk Assessment and Health Education Process, Use of Fall Risk Assessment Measures, Significance of Special Project Screening and Marking, Prevention of Falling: Passport Nursing Risk Assessment Screening, and General Measures and Requirements for Fall Prevention. Students are expected to independently study these materials based on the provided list. Students will establish corresponding study schedules based on their individual learning abilities. On the final day of the first week, the lead teacher will dedicate 3–5 h to provide supplementary training on knowledge points that students find challenging to master, ensuring comprehensive understanding for all participants.

“Exercising”: In the second week, scenario drills for internship nursing students are conducted. The study includes a total of three practice scenarios, each based on actual clinical cases: “Postoperative patients preparing to exercise prior to receiving health education,” “Health education prior to activities for patients with lower limb weakness,” and “Health education for high-risk fall patients moving to the toilet during the infusion process.” In each scenario, two researchers assume the roles of patients and escorts. Nursing students provide health education to these “patients and escorts” following the actual ward nursing workflow. The entire process is evaluated by a teacher using a specific assessment form, each scenario-based training session lasts 1–2 h. Following the training, teachers provide feedback and guidance based on the scores. Nursing students who score below 60 points must repeat the exercise until they meet the qualification standards.

Practicing

In the third week, nursing students will lead risk assessment and health education activities. Initially, the responsible nurse will determine the necessity of conducting a fall risk assessment for each patient based on the Morse Fall Scale at critical moments such as admission, transfer, post-operation, or when a patient’s condition changes. Once patients requiring evaluation are identified, internship caregivers will assess the patient using various scales [15]. These assessments include fall risk, lower limb weakness risk, the need for assistive devices such as wheelchairs or walkers, the risk of getting up unassisted at night, and the need for escorting. After recording the assessment results, targeted health education is provided to patients and their escorts based on identified risks. The entire process lasts about one hour. The responsible nurse then evaluates the effectiveness of the health education, grading the patient’s and accompanying nurse’s understanding of preventive measures as “known,” “partially known,” or “unknown.” The following day, nursing students will revisit and reinforce topics that were “partially known” or “unknown” to ensure that patients and their families are fully informed and able to implement the necessary precautions.

Traditional education model

The teacher will instruct nursing students in fall prevention using the designated learning courseware. Supplementary training will be provided on knowledge points that students do not fully comprehend until mastery is achieved. The training consists of 6 sessions in the first week, with each session lasting 1 to 2 h. Following the theoretical training, the responsible nurse will employ the Morse Fall Risk Assessment Scale to screen all patients for fall risks, place risk signs on high-risk patients, and conduct a health lecture. Give lectures to different patients, each lasting from half an hour to one hour. Additionally, the nurse will accompany nursing students to observe and study, randomly selecting students to participate in guiding patients on fall prevention behaviors. The lead teacher will oversee and provide feedback on these activities. This event lasts for 2 to 3 h.

Training assessment

Theoretical examination

One day prior to the commencement of the training and at its conclusion, two groups of nursing students will undergo centralized theoretical examinations through questionnaires. The assessment tool is titled “Fall Prevention Related Knowledge Assessment Questionnaire“(Supplementary document 1). Both the three-step training mode group and the traditional education mode group will answer the same set of test questions, consisting of 50 choice questions, each worth one point for a total possible score of 50 points.

Objective structured clinical examination

The Objective Structured Clinical Examination (OSCE) is an objective method used to evaluate a variety of clinical skills [16]. Two days after the conclusion of fall prevention instruction, both groups of nursing students were assessed using an OSCE format that included simulated clinical fall scenarios with Standardized Patients across three stations [17]. The first station assessed the nursing students’ ability to evaluate patients and their families, with the assessment content focusing on the awareness of fall prevention among patients and their families, worth 12 points. The second station focused on environmental assessment and education, worth 20 points, and the third station tested the students’ skills in instructing patients on how to safely get out of bed, with a maximum of 60 points. Additionally, an overall on-site expression was evaluated, worth 8 points. Full score is 100 points, the higher the score, the stronger the ability in this aspect.

The data recorder was a nurse with over 10 years of nursing experience, who was unaware of the student nurse grouping. All objective test scores were recorded in Microsoft Excel.

Teaching satisfaction survey

Following the completion of the study, we assessed the two teaching models using the satisfaction questionnaire developed by Falahan et al. [18]. The questionnaire comprises 11 items designed to gauge the participants’ responses to the teaching method. The questions are as follows: (1) I find this teaching method very engaging. (2) This method adequately meets my informational needs. (3) The knowledge and skills acquired through this method are relevant to my professional development. (4) I believe that my performance has improved with this method. (5) This method allows for more effective learning. (6) I perceive this method as a more effective way to learn. (7) This method increases my motivation to learn. (8) I find the topics more appealing with this method. (9) This method aids in enhancing my clinical judgment. (10) Overall, I am highly satisfied with this teaching method. 11. I recommend the adoption of this method in other educational settings. Each question is scored on a scale of 1 point, making the total possible score 11 points.

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software (version 26.0). The general characteristics of nursing students conform to a normal distribution and are expressed as mean ± standard deviation, with intergroup comparisons conducted using t-tests. The theoretical knowledge, OSCE scores, and satisfaction survey results of nursing students follow a skewed distribution and are presented as median (interquartile range), with intergroup comparisons performed using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical data were reported as frequency (percentage) [n(%)], with the chi-squared test employed for intergroup comparisons. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data visualization was performed using GraphPad Prism (version 9.5.1) and Origin Pro 2024.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by of the Clinical Ethics Committee of Leshan People’s Hospital (No: KY2021-07-27), all methods are implemented in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations (such as the Helsinki Declaration). Participation is voluntary and informed written consent is obtained from all participants.

Result

Characteristics of participants

From September 2021 to December 2021, a total of 159 nursing students participated in the study. The experimental group comprised 80 students, including 11 males and 69 females, aged between 19 and 22 years, with a mean age of 20.84 ± 0.754 years. The control group included 79 students, with 12 males and 67 females, aged 19 to 22 years, and a mean age of 20.73 ± 0.796 years. Statistical analysis revealed no significant differences in the general characteristics between the two groups (P > 0.05) (Table 1).

Prior to the intervention, the median theoretical score for the experimental group was 31 (interquartile range: 28, 33), compared to 30 (30, 32) for the control group (Table 2). No significant difference was observed between the two groups (P = 0.884) as shown in Table 1. Notably, both groups demonstrated a significant improvement in theoretical performance post-intervention compared to their baseline scores (P < 0.001).

Discussion

This study aims to assess the efficacy of a “teaching, exercising, and practicing” three-step training mode in enhancing nursing students’ proficiency in fall prevention education and their overall satisfaction. Prior to the intervention, both the experimental and control groups exhibited low scores on the fall-related knowledge assessment with no significant differences observed. This outcome suggests that nursing students at the internship stage possess inadequate fall knowledge, potentially due to passive knowledge acquisition in academic settings where teaching content and clinical application are misaligned. This misalignment likely contributes to a discrepancy between clinical practice and the preliminary health education knowledge acquired. Moreover, the absence of relevant clinical practice activities for nursing students may result in an incomplete understanding of the knowledge. To address these issues, it is recommended to enhance education through organizing diverse activities and thereby improve the comprehensive capabilities of nursing students.

The transition of nursing students from academic settings to novice nurse roles necessitates the conversion of theoretical knowledge acquired in school into practical clinical skills, among which health education competency is essential. A meta-analysis by Tian indicates that nurse-led education programs significantly influence patient re-admission rates [19]. Furthermore, a randomized controlled trial by Diriba demonstrated that nurse-led health education could enhance diabetic patients’ self-management practices and quality of life [20]. These findings underscore the critical impact of health education skills on future nursing practices and the nurse-patient relationship. Additionally, research by Khaledi suggests that employing gamification and role-playing as educational methods can boost nursing students’ self-efficacy in performing cardiopulmonary resuscitation [21]. Numerous studies have confirmed that diverse learning models markedly enhance educational outcomes [22, 23]. Consequently, we have adopted the “teach, exercise, and practice” three-step training mode to augment the health education capabilities of nursing students in fall prevention, thereby addressing the educational deficiencies observed in school settings.

Our research findings indicate that both the experimental and control groups exhibited significant improvement after the intervention compared to before; however, there were no significant differences between the two groups. This suggests that nursing students are capable of learning from a designated list of relevant knowledge points, achieving outcomes comparable to those guided by direct teaching. So “teaching, exercising, and practicing” three-step training mode facilitates the enhancement of independent learning skills among nursing students. In the Objective Structured Clinical Examination (OSCE), a marked difference was observed, with the experimental group scoring significantly higher than the control group. Nurses report that learning through clinical simulation boosts their confidence and practical abilities. Research has demonstrated that simulation training enhances the skills of new clinical nurses in delivering health education [24]. Furthermore, the study by Son et al. supports that simulating a variety of real clinical scenarios during educational processes improves nursing students’ coping skills [25], corroborating our study’s findings. The “teaching, exercising, and practicing” three-step training mode integrates theoretical training with clinical practice, employing real-life clinical scenarios for instruction. This approach not only makes theoretical knowledge more engaging but also boosts nursing students’ interest in learning and their competency in fall prevention education within clinical settings.

In the teaching satisfaction survey, the “teaching, exercising, and practicing” three-step training mode achieved higher scores, suggesting that nurses recognize the effectiveness of this training mode. The internship stage serves as a transitional period for nurses to move from theoretical learning to practical application. The core objective of this training mode is to enhance the future self-confidence of nursing students in clinical settings by simulating clinical scenarios. Alsadi’s research during the Covid-19 pandemic revealed that nursing students expressed higher satisfaction with simulation-based education, which also fostered greater self-confidence [26]. Ergezen’s cross-sectional study highlighted that nursing students anticipate receiving teacher support and supervision in clinical settings [27]. Moreover, certain studies have indicated that an effective clinical training environment, coupled with robust training methods and programs, can assist nursing students in addressing various challenges encountered during clinical internships [28].

This study categorizes health education during the internship into three stages of training: teaching, exercising, and practicing. By leading fall assessment and health education activities, nursing students integrate theoretical knowledge with patient interactions, refine their thoughts, and enhance their verbal communication and adaptability, grounded in a thorough understanding of the theory required for health education. Immediate evaluation and supervision by teaching staff allow nursing students to experience the complete health education process—including assessment, education, re-assessment, and further education—and to appreciate the feedback on the effectiveness of the implemented health education. This approach not only enables internship hospitals to effectively enhance the health education capabilities of nursing students but also indirectly meets patient needs, leading to increased satisfaction with nursing services. Ultimately, this training helps patients acquire essential medical-related knowledge, establish correct health concepts, develop healthy living habits, and yield significant social benefits.

Limitation

Our study has several limitations. Firstly, it was conducted with a limited sample size at a single center, which constrains the generalizability of our findings. Secondly, the “teaching, exercising, and practicing” three-step training mode required nursing students in the experimental group to engage in more practical learning tasks. The evaluation criteria were predominantly focused on practical assessments, potentially biasing the results towards the description of the intervention. Lastly, in the satisfaction survey, there is a risk that participants may not respond honestly. However, they were assured prior to the survey that their responses would be confidential and that no personal identifiers needed to be provided, in an effort to minimize bias related to this aspect.

Conclusion

Compared to traditional teaching methods, the “teaching, exercising, and practicing” three-step training mode reinforces the knowledge among internship nursing students regarding risk assessment for falls and strengthens their ability to provide health education on fall prevention. It enhances the satisfaction of nursing students with their training. Additionally, this mode facilitates a more effective integration of fall prevention theory with clinical practice among internship nursing students. Ultimately, the model contributes to improving the competency of these students in delivering health education aimed at preventing falls.
