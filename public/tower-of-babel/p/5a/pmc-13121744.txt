Exploring the gravito-optic effect for gravity sensing applications
Authors: Enbang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accurate measurements of Earth gravity and its variations with time are crucial not only for fundamental research on gravitation in physics, but also for other scientific areas including Earth sciences, geophysics and various applications such as geodesy, resource exploration, climate monitoring and navigation as well as natural hazard assessment. Current test mass-based gravimeters are inherently sensitive to vibrations and inertia accelerations, which limits their sensitivity and accuracy in moving platform applications. Conventional mass-based gravimeters are also subject to drift. Here we report an optical fibre sensing system for measuring gravity using a recently demonstrated gravito-optic effect. Initial test results of this current study show that photonics-based solid-state gravimeters without using test-mass in the systems could be developed for moving platform gravimetry. These have particular applications for airborne gravity mapping and underwater navigation applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-44668-1.

Introduction

The gravitational acceleration experienced by an object on the Earth surface is not uniform but varies with changes in topography, subsurface geology, and the Earth’s shape and rotation. Accurate measurements of Earth gravity and its variations with time at a particular location are critically important for fundamental research and applications such as geodesy, resource exploration, climate monitoring and navigation as well as natural hazard assessment1. To measure and model the Earth’s gravitational field, gravimetry which uses various kinds of methodologies and techniques for different applications has been developed2–12.

Gravimeters, the instruments used to measure gravitational acceleration, can be classified into two categories, the absolute gravimeters and relative gravimeters. Absolute gravimeters measure the value of gravitational acceleration directly. Their operational principle is quite simple and involves dropping a test mass in a vacuum chamber and calculating the free-fall acceleration. Normally laser interferometers are used to precisely track the motion of test masses in the absolute gravimeters13. Recent advancements in the free-fall absolute gravimeters use cold atom interferometry where cold atoms, such as Rb, are utilised as the test mass14–20. After the cold atoms are released in free-fall, they undergo three stimulated Raman transitions, which separate, redirect, and recombine the atomic wave function, resulting in an atomic interferometer. The total phase shift between the two paths of the atomic interferometer depends on the local gravitational acceleration and scales with the square of the time interval between two consecutive Raman pulses. For the free-fall absolute gravimeters, an accuracy of a few µGals (1 µGal = 10⁻⁸ m s⁻²) can be achieved under well controlled laboratory conditions. Absolute gravimeters are normally more accurate but typically more bulky, complex and expensive than relative gravimeters as described below. Therefore, they can be utilised for establishing gravity reference points, calibrating relative gravimeters, and geophysical monitoring where high precision is critical21.

The second class of gravimeters are relative gravimeters, which measure the difference in gravitational acceleration between two or more points on the Earth’s surface. In such gravimeters the gravitational force is proportional to change in length of a spring supporting a test mass22. Another type of relative gravimeters is the superconducting gravimeters, where superconducting spheres are levitated in magnetic fields23,24. Superconducting gravimeters are extremely sensitive and can be used for long-term monitoring. However, superconducting gravimeters are not portable and normally are installed in measurement stations25. The rapid development of the micro-electromechanical systems (MEMS) has provided unique opportunities for developing compact and low-cost relative gravimeters26–28. Relative gravimeters are crucial tools in geophysics and engineering. Their ability to detect µGal-level gravity changes makes them invaluable for monitoring geological activity, mapping underground features, and supporting resource exploration. Although relative gravimeters must be calibrated by using absolute gravimeters, their portability and precision make them the essential tool of field-based gravimetry29.

Gravitational fields can also be measured and characterised by using gravity gradiometers, which measure spatial variations (gradients) in the gravitational field30. Unlike standard gravimeters, which detect the magnitude of gravity at a point, gravity gradiometers measure the rate of change of gravity across space. Their primary strength lies in revealing fine-scale geological details and mapping the distribution of mass underground and beneath the ocean floor.

The operational principle of the existing gravimeters is measuring the gravitational acceleration generated by the Earth gravity. Therefore, all of them are inherently sensitive to the vibrations from the operation environments besides the gravity. Although various kind of techniques have been implemented to mitigate the influence and impact of the vibrations, it is still a key problem in the measurement of gravity from moving platforms. On top of that, the inertial accelerations of the moving platforms also must be separated from the gravitational accelerations. Beyond the need to isolate the gravitational signal from the specific forces of the moving platform, several other factors limit accuracy in shipborne and airborne gravimetry31,32.

In this paper, we report a novel gravity sensing method based on the recently demonstrated gravito-optic effect, where the local speed of light varies with the gravitational potential33. Based on the gravito-optic effect, experiments aiming for gravity sensing have been designed and carried out. The experimental results show that photons instead of test masses could be utilised for gravity sensing applications. Unlike conventional technologies, photonic gravimeters are fully solid-state systems with no moving parts. This design inherently eliminates the effects of platform vibration and inertial acceleration. A unique feature of photonic gravimeters is that sampling rates of kHz or MHz are possible.

Principle and description of the sensing system

In a previous study, we experimentally demonstrated that light could interact with the Earth gravitational field and consequently the speed of light would be affected by the Earth gravity33. The previous experimental results show that the speed of light increases with the increase of gravitational field. This phenomenon could be explained by the gravitational time dilation which is predicted by the general theory of relativity and has been verified by experiments and observations34. The interactions between the gravity and light or photons, which can be defined as gravito-optic effects, should be further investigated as fundamental research in physics and explored for developing novel gravity sensing technologies.

In the previous experiments, we utilised ultrafast laser pulses to measure the travel time in an optical fibre spool with a total return length of 20 km at different heights above the ground. Although various measures were implemented to isolate Earth’s gravity from the environmental variables (e.g., temperature, air pressure, air humidity), further experimental validation is essential and necessary. This will confirm the earlier results with greater confidence and ensure the exclusion of possible non-gravity factors. To that end, we designed and built a fibre-optic system in which two identical optical fibre spools were arranged in a differential mode to overcome any possible common-mode influences.

A schematic diagram of the optical fibre sensing system. The setup is configured as a gravity gradiometer based on the dependence of the speed of light in two optical fibre spools under different gravitational fields.

A diagram of the sensing system is shown in Fig. 1. Two optical fibre spools (labelled as Fibre spool 1 and 2) with the same length of 10 km are arranged in the vertical direction with a spacing of 1 m between them. Laser pulses from a femtosecond fibre laser (FemtoFiber pro, from TOPTICA Photonics) first pass an optical isolator which stops any back-reflected light, and each pulse is split into two by a 50/50 directional fibre coupler. The two output ports of the coupler are connected to the fibre spools. The laser pulses are reflected back by two in-fibre Faraday mirrors connected at the ends of the fibre spools. By this way, the actual travel length of each laser pulse is 20 km, which means that 20 km is the forward and backward travel distance for each laser pulse in each fibre pool. The reflected laser pulses coming out of the directional coupler are connected to a chromatic dispersion compensator (DCMS-SM-C100%-020-LCP-05, from Yangtze Optical System Corp. Ltd, Wuhan, China) to compress the broadened laser pulses in time domain. The lengths of the fibre spools need to be matched precisely to ensure that the two adjacent pulses are reflections from a single input pulse. The fibre length matching is achieved by using an optical time-domain reflectometer (OTDR) and is checked by using a picosecond gain-switched laser (GSL155A from Thorlabs).

In this setup, the time delay differences generated by the two fibre spools could be accurately measured. With this arrangement, the setup shown in Fig. 1 is in fact configured as a gravity gradiometer, and any difference in gravitational field experienced by the fibre spools would affect the time delay between the two adjacent pulses. Therefore, by measuring these time delays, we can determine the relative changes in gravity experienced by the fibre spools.

The compressed optical pulses are detected by an ultrafast photodetector (DXM30BF, from ThorLabs) with a bandwidth larger than 30 GHz in the 750–1650 nm wavelength range. After converting the laser pulses to electrical signals, a real-time digital oscilloscope (DPO 73308 SX, from Tektronix) with a signal bandwidth of 33 GHz and a sampling rate of 100 GSa/s is used to record the pulses and to measure the time delays between the two adjacent pulses.

To achieve precise temperature control for the optical system, three copper enclosures, two for the fibre spools (called ENC1 and 2 for fibre spool 1 and 2 respectively) and one for other optical components (called ENC3), were constructed. Silicone rubber heating wires are unfirmly wound around the enclosures as the heating elements and 2 cm thick insulating foams are glued to the outsides of the enclosures for thermal insulation and sealing purpose. The copper enclosures are also electrically grounded to shield any possible EM interference. PT100 temperature sensors are attached to the copper enclosures and connected to three independent digital temperature controllers. The fibre patch cords linking ENC3 to ENC1 and ENC2 are inserted in temperature-controlled pipes which are parts of the temperature control system of ENC3. All the three enclosures are mounted on a rigid aluminium frame which ensures that there are no relative movements between the two fibre spools. Plastic panels are installed for all surfaces of the metal frame to further isolate the passive optical fibre components from the environment (as shown in Fig. 2).

Experiments and results

For testing the gravity sensing system, we need to generate gravity gradients in a controllable way. Here we proposed to utilise a moveable metal block as the source mass to generate variable gravitational potentials on the fibre spools. As both the source mass and the fibre spools are not point objects, the set-up we should consider is in fact a near-field gravitational system. We designed a motorised moving mass set-up, as shown in Fig. 2. The test mass sits on a motorised cart which moves on two aluminium tracks. The design of the set-up guarantees that there are no vibrations when the cart moves on the tracks. With this set-up, we could put a source mass directly underneath the fibre spool 1, and move it away from the fibre spool by a distance of 1.5 m. When the source mass is moved in, it generates a differential gravitational force between fibre spool 1 and fibre spool 2, thus a gravity gradient could be generated to the sensing system. The source mass can be moved in and out of the sensing system periodically and the period can be accurately set by from a controller. A steel source mass of 72 kg was used for all tests reported here.

The tests were carried out in an optics laboratory which is fully airconditioned and locates in vibration-free building. The room temperature in the laboratory was set at 23 °C and the temperatures of all three enclosures were set at 30.0 °C. The humidity inside the laboratory was monitored by using a TRH22 digital relative humidity meter. The humidity reading was 45% and no changes were observed during the test period. The air pressure in the laboratory was measured as 101 kPa with a Testo 512 digital manometer. During the experiments, all these environmental parameters were recorded, and no changes were observed, hence could be treated as constants within the accuracies of the measurement devices.

We operated the FemtoFiber pro at 80 MHz repetition rate for the 1560 nm fundamental wavelength. The average power of the seed laser pulses from the FC/APC fibre output port was 1 mW and the pulse width (after a 2 m long fibre patch cord) was measured as 550 fs by using an auto-correlator.

Shown in Fig. 3 are the recorded real-time pulse waveforms by using the photodetector and the digital oscilloscope with a sampling rate of 100 Gs/s. The leading pulse in Fig. 3(a) was from fibre spool 1 and the second pulse was from fibre spool 2. The time delay between the two pulses was what we needed to measure in the experiments, and it was done by utilising the build-in functions of the digital oscilloscope. Figure 3(b) is a zoom-in waveform of the leading pulse from which a pulse width of around 90 ps was measured. The difference between the initial input pulse width (550 fs) and that directly measured by using the photodetector and the digital oscilloscope should be determined by the uncompensated dispersion, the bandwidths and time responses of the measurement instruments.

In the experiments, we set the period of the oscillating mass at 16.5 s, which is equivalent to a changing rate of 0.061 Hz in the gravitational field. Displayed in Fig. 4(a) are the measured time delays between two reflected laser pulses as a function of test time when the source mass was moving in and out of the sensing system with the preset period of 16.5 s. It can be seen that the time delays fluctuate in a few picoseconds range and it is unclear if the periodical motion of the source mass producing any influence on the recorded data. As a further analysis, we performed FFT of the recorded time delay data and the power spectra are presented in Fig. 4(b). From the FFT spectra, we can see that a peak (circled in the figure) appears at a frequency of 0.064 Hz. To further confirm this result, we repeated the tests for multiple times and obtained an average frequency of 0.062 Hz with a standard deviation of 0.004 Hz. The experimental results match the moving frequency of the source mass, which clearly demonstrates that the designed sensing system responses to the periodic gravitational field changes introduced by the 72 kg moving mass.

It should be noted that the instantaneous signals as shown in Fig. 4(a) contain not only the contributions from the periodic gravitational field changes we intend to detect, but also those from other unknown sources which should be further investigated.

Discussion and conclusion

We have designed a gravity sensing system by exploiting a recently demonstrated gravito-optic effect and tested it by using an oscillating source mass. The experimental results not only confirm the results reported previously33 but also show the potential for developing photonic systems for gravity measurements. Even though we implemented various methods to isolate the changes of local gravity generated by the moving mass from other possible environmental disturbances, including temperature, air pressure, air humidity and vibrations, there are still more work which should be done to further identify the sources for generating the fluctuations in the measured time delay signals.

Further research aiming for quantitative measurements is currently under way. Previous work shows the gravitational modulation of the speed of light is extremely weak, generating time delays on the order of picoseconds for a total fibre length of 20 km33. This dictates a measurement accuracy in the sub-picosecond regime—a specification fulfilled by our present apparatus. To obtain higher accuracy, better dispersion management and optimized input pulse shapes are needed to compress the reflected signal’s pulse width. In addition, photodetectors with broader bandwidths or faster time responses should be utilised.

All the existing gravimeters (both absolute and relative) are prone to vibrations or inertial accelerations, affecting the operations of the gravimeters. Photonics-based gravimeters or gravity gradiometers overcome this drawback since photons, instead of test masses, are utilised for sensing purposes. Furthermore, photonics-based gravimeters can support kHz or MHz sampling rates, given sufficiently fast recording and processing electronics, by measuring every laser pulse. Solid-state gravimeters with fast data sampling rates but without any moving parts is desirable particularly for airborne gravity mapping and underwater navigation applications.
