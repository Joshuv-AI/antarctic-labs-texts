The Burden of Cardiac Arrhythmias in Loeys-Dietz Syndrome
Authors: Hussein Abdul Nabi, George Bcharah, Hend Bcharah, Luke Dreher, Mohammed Alaa Raslan, Amal Youssef, Christopher Kanaan, Linnea M. Baudhuin, Chadi Ayoub, Fadi E. Shamoun, Hicham El Masry
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLoeys-Dietz syndrome (LDS) is a rare connective tissue disorder with vascular, skeletal, and cutaneous abnormalities, with elevated aneurysm risk. Although vascular complications are well studied, cardiac arrhythmias remain underexplored.ObjectivesThe objectives of the study were to evaluate the prevalence and clinical implications of cardiac arrhythmias, as well as complications of electrophysiological interventions, in LDS patients.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted on LDS patients treated at Mayo Clinic sites. Chart reviews identified arrhythmia types, timing relative to cardiac surgery, and interventions. Descriptive statistics assessed prevalence and associations.ResultsThe cohort included 94 patients (mean age 44.7 ± 18.8 years; 50% female). Atrial arrhythmias were present in 31.9%. Atrial fibrillation occurred in 28.7% and atrial flutter in 12.7%, with some experiencing both. Supraventricular tachycardia occurred in 19.1%. Ventricular arrhythmias were identified in 20.2%, with most cases unrelated to surgery. Conduction abnormalities were found in 37.2%, and atrioventricular block in 31.9%, with all third-degree atrioventricular block occurring after cardiac surgery. Mitral valve prolapse (MVP) and mitral annular disjunction (MAD) were common (35.1% and 11.7%, respectively) and positively associated with a higher likelihood of ventricular arrhythmias (MVP: OR: 8.253; 95% CI: 2.623-25.961; P < 0.001; MAD: OR: 10.354; 95% CI: 2.625-40.844; P < 0.001). Interventions included ablation (10.6%), pacemakers (7.5%), and implantable cardioverter-defibrillators (2.2%), with a high complication rate.ConclusionsCardiac arrhythmias occur frequently in LDS. Structural abnormalities like MVP and MAD are linked to ventricular arrhythmias. Frequent electrophysiological interventions and complications underscores the importance of tailored arrhythmia surveillance and management in this population.

Loeys-Dietz syndrome (LDS) is a rare autosomal dominant connective tissue disorder (CTD) caused by pathogenic variants in genes involved in the transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) signaling pathway, including TGFBR1, TGFBR2, SMAD3, TGFB2, and TGFB3, with TGFBR1 and TGFBR2 being the most frequently involved.1, 2, 3 LDS is clinically characterized by arterial aneurysms, arterial tortuosity, hypertelorism, bifid uvula or cleft palate, and skeletal abnormalities.4,5 The cardiovascular manifestations of LDS are particularly severe, with a high risk of aortic dissection occurring at smaller aortic diameters and younger age, compared to other CTDs, as well as the presence of aneurysms throughout the arterial tree.4,5 These features contribute to a challenging prognosis, requiring early surgical intervention and lifelong, comprehensive vascular surveillance. However, arrhythmic manifestations in LDS remain poorly understood and are not currently included in its diagnostic criteria or standard clinical descriptions.

In Marfan syndrome (MFS), ventricular ectopy and nonsustained ventricular tachycardia (VT) are relatively common, with 1 study reported nonsustained VT in 20 of 86 patients (23%); these were divided into 2 groups: 31 patients with valvular disease or prior cardiovascular surgery, of whom 12 (38.7%) had nonsustained VT, and 55 patients without such history or with only mild valvular disease, of whom 8 (14.5%) had nonsustained VT.6 In another study of 213 patients with MFS and FBN1 variants, atrial arrhythmias occurred in 35 (16%) and ventricular arrhythmias in 10 (5%); among those with atrial arrhythmias, 33 (94%) had atrial fibrillation, 1 (3%) atrial flutter, and 1 (3%) supra-VT (SVT); and of those with ventricular arrhythmias, 2 (20%) had frequent ectopy and 8 (80%) had VT.7 In the multicenter data on 385 patients with pathogenic variants in the TGF-β signaling pathway that define LDS, 13 were found to have nonaortic cardiac disease. Of those, ventricular arrhythmias or sudden cardiac death occurred in 46%, at a median age of 22 years. Atrial arrhythmias were more rare (7%).8 In another electrocardiographic survey of 119 CTD patients from 3 referral centers (including individuals with TGF-β-pathway mutations), abnormal ventricular repolarization was found in 29% of the cohort, QTc prolongation in 4%, VT in 7%, and 1 sudden death in a 12-year-old SMAD3 carrier.9 These studies emphasize the need for proactive electrical surveillance in this vulnerable patient population.

Although emerging data highlight arrhythmias as a significant component of LDS phenotype, their burden, management and risk-benefit profile of available treatments remain poorly defined. This study aims to address that gap by providing the first systematic analysis of arrhythmia prevalence in patients diagnosed with LDS, along with the indications for and complications associated with electrophysiological studies. By broadening the recognized cardiac phenotype of LDS, this work supports a more comprehensive clinical approach to a condition traditionally defined by its vascular complications.

Methods

Study population

This retrospective cohort study analyzed patients diagnosed with LDS between 2018 and 2024 at Mayo Clinic sites in Rochester, Arizona, and Florida. Patients were identified using the International Classification of Diseases-10 code, and diagnoses were confirmed through manual chart review. Individuals under 18 years of age were excluded. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board, and only patients with informed consent were included. Genetic data were systematically collected and reviewed by a clinical geneticist to document all associated pathogenic, likely pathogenic, and variants of uncertain significance (VUS) relevant to LDS.

Comprehensive chart reviews were conducted to collect demographic data and detailed information on arrhythmias, cardiac surgeries, and procedures. Cardiac surgeries and their corresponding dates included aortic root replacement, aortic valve repair or replacement, tricuspid valve repair or replacement, mitral valve repair or replacement, atrial septal defect or ventricular septal defect closure, and patent foramen ovale closure.

Arrhythmia data included atrial fibrillation, atrial flutter, and SVT, as well as ventricular arrhythmias and conduction abnormalities such as left bundle branch block (LBBB), right bundle branch block (RBBB), and atrioventricular (AV) block (1st, 2nd, and 3rd degree). Ventricular arrhythmias included ventricular fibrillation (VF) and VT, further categorized into sustained and nonsustained types, with nonsustained arrhythmia defined as at least 3 consecutive beats lasting <30 seconds that terminate spontaneously.10 For all arrhythmias except for LBBB and RBBB, the date of diagnosis was recorded. In addition, for VF cases, the presumed cause was also collected. Arrhythmias were further categorized based on their timing in relation to cardiac surgery. Events that occurred within 2 months following a cardiac surgical intervention were classified as occurring within the postsurgical period, whereas those without a temporal association to surgery were classified as not related to recent cardiac surgery. This distinction was used to assess whether arrhythmias may have been influenced by recent surgical intervention. This 2-month window was selected to account for potential postoperative complications. Data on interventional procedures, including catheter ablations, implantable cardioverter-defibrillators (ICDs), and pacemakers, were also collected. For each, the indication for intervention and any associated complications were documented.

For all patients, echocardiographic data were reviewed, including the first and last available echocardiograms. From these studies, the left atrial volume index (LAVI), left ventricular ejection fraction (EF) (LVEF), as well as left ventricular end-diastolic diameter (LVEDD) and end-systolic diameter (LVESD) were collected, given their association with atrial and ventricular arrhythmias, respectively. For EF measurement, three-dimensional assessments were used when available; where not available, two-dimensional biplane measurements were used.11 An in-depth chart review was also performed, focusing on echocardiograms obtained before any valvular or aortic interventions. Valvular abnormalities, including mitral, tricuspid, and aortic regurgitation, as well as aortic stenosis and bicuspid aortic valve, were systematically documented, with detailed classification of regurgitation and stenosis severity. Due to the correlation between mitral valve prolapse (MVP) and both CTD and arrhythmia, patients were also assessed for the presence of MVP. Among those with MVP, further imaging review was conducted to evaluate for the presence of mitral annular disjunction (MAD). Moreover, all patients were reviewed for the availability of cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) at any point during their care, with specific attention to the presence or absence of late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) as a marker of myocardial fibrosis.

Study outcomes

The primary aim of this study was to evaluate the prevalence of arrhythmias in patients with LDS to understand the importance of ongoing electrophysiological surveillance in this population. The study also aimed to investigate whether arrhythmias arise from the underlying disease or occur as complications of the frequent cardiac surgeries LDS patients undergo. In addition, we assessed the prevalence and indications for invasive electrophysiological interventions and examined the rate of major complications associated with these procedures.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics. Frequencies and percentages were used for categorical variables; percentages represent relative percentages and are rounded to 1 decimal place. Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test and presented as mean ± SD or median with IQR, depending on normality of distribution. For analysis, some continuous variables were dichotomized into categorical variables, with abnormal values coded as 1 and normal values as 0. Associations between categorical variables, including these dichotomized variables, and the presence of atrial and ventricular arrhythmias were assessed using chi-square tests and binary logistic regression. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 94 patients diagnosed with LDS were included in the analysis, with an equal gender distribution (47 females and 47 males). The mean age at the time of data collection was 44.7 ± 18 years. The median body mass index was 24.25 [IQR: 19.5–29.77], and the majority of patients were White (n = 87, 92.5%). Echocardiographic data were available for all patients. Genetic testing

Of the 94 patients in this study, 80 (85.1%) were found to have a pathogenic or likely pathogenic variant in a canonical LDS associated gene (TGFBR1, TGFBR2, SMAD3, TGFB2, or TGFB3). Specifically, variants were identified in TGFBR1 in 22 patients (23.4%), TGFBR2 in 28 (29.8%), SMAD3 in 21 (22.3%), TGFB2 in 6 (6.4%), and TGFB3 in 3 (3.2%). An additional 9 patients (9.6%) carried VUS in these genes but were included due to a severe phenotype consistent with LDS and expert clinical interpretation suggesting these variants are likely to be reclassified as pathogenic or likely pathogenic. The VUS were distributed as follows: 1 in TGFBR1, 2 in TGFBR2, 2 in SMAD3, 3 in TGFB2, and 1 in TGFB3. Five patients (5.3%) declined genetic testing but were included based on a clinical diagnosis of LDS, supported by typical phenotypic features and multisystem involvement. No variants, including pathogenic, likely pathogenic, or VUS, were identified in FBN1 in any patient.

Prevalence and distribution of arrhythmias

Arrhythmias were prevalent in this LDS cohort. Atrial arrhythmias were observed in 30 patients (31.9%). Atrial fibrillation occurred in 27 patients (28.7%), including 18 cases (19.1%) that were not temporally associated with surgery and 9 cases (9.6%) that occurred within 2 months following cardiac surgery. Atrial flutter was identified in 12 patients (12.7%), with 9 cases (9.6%) not postoperative. Notably, there was overlap in 9 patients who experienced both atrial fibrillation and atrial flutter, accounting for the total of 30 patients with atrial arrhythmias. SVT was present in 18 patients (19.1%), of which 14 (14.9%) were nonpostoperative and 4 (4.2%) occurred within 2 months of cardiac surgery. Ventricular arrhythmias and conduction abnormalities

Conduction abnormalities were common in patients with LDS: 35 (37.2%) had LBBB or RBBB. AV block was present in 30 patients (31.9%), including first-degree (n = 18, 19.1%), second-degree (n = 6, 6.4%), and third-degree (n = 6, 6.4%) block. Among these, 18 (19.1%) were nonpostoperative and 12 (12.8%) occurred within 2 months following cardiac, with all third-degree AV block occurring within 2 months of cardiac surgery.

Ventricular arrhythmias were present in 19 patients (20.2%). VT occurred in 15 patients (16%), including sustained VT in 2 patients (2.2%) and nonsustained VT in 13 (13.8%). Most VT cases (n = 14, 14.9%) occurred independently of any recent cardiac surgical intervention, whereas 1 (1.1%) occurred within 2 months following such a procedure. VF occurred in 6 patients (6.4%), 5 of whom had no recent cardiac surgery and 1 who developed VF within 2 months of surgery. Notably, there was overlap in 2 patients who experienced both nonsustained VT and VF, accounting for the total of 19 patients with ventricular arrhythmias. The cases of sustained VT did not occur in the postsurgical period setting and were not associated with reduced LVEF (EF<50%), long QT syndrome, or myocardial infarction.

In the 5 patients who experienced VF without recent cardiac surgery, identified clinical contexts included the following: 1) anesthesia-related cardiac arrest on 2 separate occasions in the same patient without QT prolongation; 2) bacteremia and sepsis; 3) spontaneous coronary artery dissection; 4) VF triggered by paired premature ventricular complexes; and 5) VF in the setting of a large diaphragmatic hernia and bowel incarceration. Notably, all 6 patients who experienced VF had preserved EF (EF ≥ 50%) at the time of VF; mean EF at the time of VF diagnosis was 58.4 ± 4.76%.

Of the 94 patients, only 6 had cardiac MRI performed at some point during their care. Of these, just 1 patient demonstrated LGE, and this individual was the only one among the six who experienced both nonsustained VT and VF. The remaining 5 patients had normal cardiac MRI findings and did not experience any ventricular arrhythmias.

Valvular findings and ventricular arrhythmias

Before any valvular or aortic interventions, ascending aortic aneurysm was present in 76 patients (81%). Mitral regurgitation (MR) was observed in 36 patients (38.3%), with severity classified as severe in 9 patients (9.6%), moderate in 6 patients (6.4%), and mild in 21 patients (22.3%). Tricuspid valve regurgitation was present in 37 patients (39.4%), including 2 (2.1%) with severe regurgitation, 6 (6.4%) with moderate regurgitation, and 29 (30.9%) with mild regurgitation. Aortic regurgitation was documented in 57 patients (60.6%), with 26 patients (27.7%) having severe regurgitation, 14 (14.9%) moderate, and 17 (18.1%) mild. In addition, a bicuspid aortic valve was identified in 11 patients (11.7%), and 8 patients (8.5%) had moderate to severe aortic sclerosis before any surgical intervention.

MVP was identified in 33 patients (35.1%). Among those with MVP, further imaging confirmed the presence of MAD in 11 patients (11.7% of the total cohort; 33.3% of those with MVP). A significant association was found between MVP and ventricular arrhythmias (including nonsustained VT, sustained VT, and VF) (OR: 8.253; 95% CI: 2.623-25.961; P < 0.001). Specifically, 12 patients with nonsustained VT, 1 patient with sustained VT, and 3 patients with VF had MVP. Similarly, a significant association was also observed between MAD and ventricular arrhythmias (OR: 10.354; 95% CI: 2.625-40.844; P < 0.001). Among the 11 patients with MAD, 5 had non-sustained ventricular tachycardia, 1 had sustained VT, and 1 had VF.

Ventricular arrhythmias were also significantly associated with moderate or severe aortic regurgitation, present in 12 of 19 patients (63.2%) with arrhythmias (P = 0.042; OR: 1.057; 95% CI: 1.014-8.166). In addition, elevated LVEDD was observed in 8 of 19 patients (42.1%) (P = 0.019; OR: 1.274; 95% CI: 1.188-10.765), and elevated LVESD was found in 10 patients (52.6%) (P = 0.015; OR: 1.601; 95% CI: 1.679-14.640), both showing significant associations with ventricular arrhythmias.

No significant associations were observed between ventricular arrhythmias and age >40 years (P = 0.442), gender (P = 0.199), hypertension (P = 0.088), ascending aortic aneurysm (P = 0.085), or type A aortic dissection (P = 0.782).

Associations with atrial arrhythmias

A statistically significant relationship was found between left atrial enlargement (increased LAVI) and the presence of atrial fibrillation and atrial flutter (OR: 4.468; 95% CI: 1.699-11.752; P = 0.002). Among patients with atrial fibrillation or flutter, 56.7% had an elevated LAVI, compared to only 22.6% of those without atrial arrhythmias.

In addition, elevated LAVI was significantly associated with moderate or severe MR, with 12 of 29 patients (41.4%) having moderate to severe MR (P < 0.001; OR: 2.465; 95% CI: 2.961-46.747). Atrial arrhythmias were also significantly associated with moderate or greater MR, seen in 12 of 30 patients (40%) with atrial arrhythmia (P < 0.001; OR: 2.607; 95% CI: 3.444-53.348). A significant association was also observed between atrial arrhythmias and hypertension, present in 19 of 30 patients (63.3%) (P = 0.019; OR: 1.057; 95% CI: 1.172-7.071). Finally, atrial fibrillation was significantly associated with age, with 26 of 30 patients (86.7%) being older than 40 years (P < 0.001; OR: 2.251; 95% CI: 2.964-30.453).

No significant associations were found between atrial arrhythmias and gender (P = 1.0) or type A dissection (P = 0.235). Although the presence of an ascending aortic aneurysm was common among patients with atrial fibrillation (93.3%), this did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.06).

Invasive electrophysiological interventions and complicationS

Ablation procedures were performed in 10 patients (10.6%), including 6 for atrial arrhythmias (60%) and 4 for SVT (40%). One patient (10%) experienced cardiac perforation following ablation. Pacemakers were implanted in 7 patients (7.5%) for indications including complete heart block (n = 4, 57.1%), second-degree AV block (n = 2, 28.6%), and tachy-brady syndrome (n = 1, 14.3%).

Complications occurred in 3 patients (42.9%): 1 with recurrent infective endocarditis and 1 with lead fracture, both of which occurred after 30 days following implant. The third patient experienced hemodynamically significant bleeding requiring mechanical circulatory support, which occurred early postimplant. ICDs were placed in 2 patients (2.2%). One ICD was placed for primary prevention, and the other following an episode of VF. Both experienced complications—one with pocket-site bleeding requiring a blood transfusion and an admission to the intensive care unit, which occurred early within the first 30 days, and the other with lead-associated thrombosis, which occurred after 30 days. Discussion

In this study, cardiac arrhythmias represented a significant clinical burden in patients with LDS. Key findings included high prevalence of all arrhythmia types in this patient population, ranging from atrial fibrillation (AF) to life threatening ventricular tachyarrhythmias. These findings broaden the current understanding of the cardiovascular phenotype of LDS beyond the propensity for aortic aneurysms and dissections. Our cohort’s arrhythmias are more prevalent compared to those of the general population, and trend similarly toward other CTDs, such as MFS,3,6,12,13 suggesting that arrhythmogenic risk is an important consideration in LDS management.

The high prevalence of arrhythmias observed in our LDS cohort may be explained by the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms inherent to the syndrome. LDS arises from pathogenic variants in the genes regulating the TGF-β signaling pathway, leading to dysregulation and often excess expression of TGF-β.3 TGF-β plays a central role in fibrosis and tissue remodeling, where excessive signaling can promote myocardial fibrosis and valvular degeneration.14 Such fibrosis can disrupt normal electrical conduction, which predisposes to reentrant and focal arrhythmias, as seen in other fibrotic cardiomyopathies.15, 16, 17 The high rates of both atrial and ventricular arrhythmias may reflect this diffuse myocardial remodeling. This is further supported by studies on SMAD3-associated LDS (type 3), where AF and left ventricular hypertrophy have been observed in the absence of significant valvular or hemodynamic abnormalities, which might suggest a primary cardiomyopathic process driven by TGF-β–mediated signaling pathways.3 Similarly, a cardiac-MRI study of 32 FBN1-positive MFS patients reported diffuse myocardiac fibrosis in 21% of cases, without focal LGE and with no direct link to ventricular dysfunction or arrhythmias.18 MAD in that cohort correlated with left-ventricular volumes, suggesting that connective-tissue–driven valvular stress, chamber remodeling, and low-grade fibrosis may together create an arrhythmogenic substrate.18 This same mechanism likely extends to LDS. Pediatric data support this view. Cardiac-MRI mapping in 21 losartan-naïve children with LDS showed significantly elevated native T1 and extracellular-volume fraction (mean extracellular volume ≈ 27%) vs healthy controls, accompanied by mild biventricular dysfunction.19 This reinforces the hypothesis that LDS is not solely a disease of the vasculature but also a myocardial disease with increased vulnerability to arrhythmias.

Structural cardiac features of LDS may further predispose patients to arrhythmias. MVP was identified in 35.1% of patients in our cohort, and MAD in 11.7% (one-third of those with MVP). Both MVP and MAD were significantly associated with the occurrence of ventricular arrhythmias (including nonsustained VT, sustained VT, and VF; P < 0.001 for each). This high prevalence of MVP in LDS, compared to 2% to 3% in the general population, suggests an intrinsic connective tissue basis for valvular changes.20 Recent studies also indicate MAD itself as an arrhythmogenic substrate linked to ventricular arrhythmias, independent of MVP presence.21 MAD- and MVP-related mechanical stress and systolic traction induce fibrosis in the papillary muscles and inferobasal left ventricular wall.22 Our findings parallel those seen in arrhythmic MVP syndrome, suggesting similar mechanisms involving valvular abnormalities and myocardial fibrosis may significantly contribute to the risk of arrhythmias in LDS patients.

The arrhythmic burden in LDS patients is consistent with observations in other CTDs. Notably, MFS also shows a significant arrhythmic burden. In a prospective series of young patients with MFS, 21% experienced ventricular arrhythmias and 4% suffered arrhythmic sudden cardiac death despite aggressive aortic management.23 These arrhythmias were strongly associated with MVP, left ventricular enlargement, and repolarization changes.23 In contrast, studies on arrhythmias in LDS have been limited. However, available studies indicate arrhythmia prevalence in LDS may be similar to MFS in certain contexts. In one study, AF was observed in roughly 24% of patients with SMAD3-mutant LDS (LDS type 3), a subset known as aneurysm osteoarthritis syndrome.3 Our study aligns with these findings and reinforces that arrhythmias are not uncommon in LDS, potentially occurring across multiple genotypes. However, their incidence in non-SMAD3 LDS remains to be investigated.

Interestingly, the type of arrhythmias and their clinical impact appear to differ between CTDs. In our LDS cohort, we observed a spectrum of arrhythmias ranging from sustained atrial arrhythmias and nonsustained VT. This mirrors MFS, where both atrial and ventricular arrhythmias have been described.6,23 In contrast, Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (EDS) has not been associated with clearly elevated incidence of arrhythmias in most studies.24,25 Although prior studies have reported occasional SVTs and sinus node arrhythmias, no ventricular arrhythmias were observed, and no strong epidemiologic link between EDS and pathological arrhythmias has been established.24,25 The arrhythmic burden in LDS appears more similar to MFS compared to EDS.

Invasive electrophysiological interventions in patients with LDS were not without considerable risk and were associated with higher-than-expected complication rates. Catheter ablation was performed in 10 patients, with 1 case complicated by cardiac perforation. Pacemaker resulted in major complications in 3 of 7 patients who underwent the procedure. These complications included infections, lead-related issues, and bleeding. Although ICDs were implanted in only 2 patients, both experienced device-related complications. These rates exceed those reported in the general population, where catheter ablation carries a complication rate of about 2% to 5%.26 Although pacemaker and ICD implantation complication rates range from 3% to 12%.27,28 Given this, the decision to pursue invasive electrophysiological therapy in patients with LDS should be made through careful risk-benefit analysis and shared decision-making based on the severity of arrhythmia symptoms or risk against the patient’s underlying vascular abnormalities. In certain cases, conservative management with close surveillance and medical therapy may be considered to ensure patient safety.

Given the high prevalence of atrial and ventricular arrhythmias and conduction abnormalities observed even before any surgical intervention, we recommend routine electrocardiogram and Holter monitoring every 6 to 12 months for patients with LDS. Monitoring is guided by individual risk factors, such as elevated LAVI, which correlated with atrial arrhythmias, and the presence of MVP or MAD, which were associated with ventricular arrhythmias. Although all patients benefit from regular surveillance, those with higher-risk features may require more frequent monitoring. This approach facilitates early detection and timely management of arrhythmic events, ultimately improving outcomes. Arrhythmias are a significant and under-recognized component of the clinical spectrum of LDS, comparable in many aspects to MFS. Although vascular complications have been the management focus in LDS, our findings underscore the importance of incorporating arrhythmia surveillance and cardiomyopathy screening into routine care. Similar to other aortopathies, arrhythmias in LDS likely result from a combination of genetic variants, structural remodeling, and myocardial fibrosis.3,5,6 The high rates of both atrial and ventricular arrhythmias, along with the procedural complications associated with EP interventions in LDS, emphasize the need for a tailored, multidisciplinary approach to care. Larger cohort studies will be essential for risk stratification, understanding genotype-phenotype correlations, and establishing evidence-based arrhythmia management strategies.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations, most importantly its retrospective nature, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. The relatively small sample size limits statistical power and may not capture rare arrhythmic events. Moreover, the sample was predominantly White, reducing generalizability to more diverse populations. Arrhythmia screening was variable among providers and the potential underdiagnosis especially asymptomatic episodes cannot be discounted.

Conclusions

LDS is associated with a significant burden of cardiac arrhythmias. Atrial arrhythmias, ventricular arrhythmias, and conduction abnormalities were common in this cohort, with many cases occurring independent of surgical interventions. Invasive electrophysiological interventions, such as ablation, pacemaker implantation, and ICD placement, were necessary for a subset of patients, with some experiencing major complications. These findings highlight the need for increased awareness and monitoring of arrhythmic risk in LDS patients, along with a more comprehensive approach to their clinical management, especially considering the potential for arrhythmias to arise from both the underlying genetic disorder and its respective surgical treatment.
