Impaired MAPT/tau-secretory lysosomes are linked to cognitive vulnerability in Alzheimer patients
Authors: Preeti Sharma, Anuma Pallavi, Ananya Chatterjee, Vidya Mangala Prasad, Nikhil R. Gandasi, Sivaprakasam R. Saroja
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMAPT/tau proteins propagate between brain regions in a prion-like manner, driving the onset and progression of dementia in Alzheimer disease (AD). However, the basis for variability in dementia progression among AD patients remains poorly understood. Here, we demonstrate that cognitively resilient AD patients, characterized by reduced MAPT/tau pathology, maintain lysosomal integrity, whereas cognitively vulnerable patients, exhibiting greater MAPT/tau burden, display lysosomal dysfunction. Lysosomes in cognitively vulnerable AD brains contain partially digested, seed-competent MAPT/tau species composed mainly of the amyloidogenic core with degraded peripheral regions. These pathogenic MAPT/tau forms are secreted via lysosomal exocytosis, facilitating MAPT/tau propagation and contributing to cognitive decline. Cognitively vulnerable female AD patients show increased lysosome-mediated MAPT/tau secretion relative to their male counterparts. Our findings suggest that lysosomal dysfunction, marked by altered protein expression, pH dysregulation, and MAPT/tau accumulation, underlies the heterogeneity in dementia severity. Targeting lysosomal exocytosis and the amyloidogenic core of MAPT/tau fibrils offer a promising therapeutic avenue to mitigate MAPT/tau pathology and promote cognitive resilience in AD and related dementias.Abbreviation: AD: Alzheimer disease, LAMP1; lysosomal associated membrane protein 1, NFT: neurofibrillary tangles; MAPT: microtubule associated protein tau; PHF: paired helical filaments; TIRF: total internal reflection fluorescence; TARDBP/TDP-43:TAR DNA binding protein.

Introduction

Alzheimer disease (AD) is the most prevalent neurodegenerative disorder and the leading cause of dementia, characterized by the accumulation of two hallmark proteins: MAPT/tau and β-amyloid [1,2]. MAPT/tau pathology, in the form of neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs), plays a crucial role in the onset and progression of dementia in AD patients [3–5]. MAPT/tau is a highly soluble, natively unfolded protein primarily expressed in neurons, where it stabilizes microtubules and contributes to axonal transport [6,7]. Under pathological conditions, MAPT/tau becomes hyperphosphorylated, dissociates from microtubules, and aggregates into insoluble paired helical filaments (PHFs), which form the NFTs in AD patients [8–10]. Other post-translational modifications such as acetylation and ubiquitination are also detected in MAPT/tau fibrils that potentially facilitates the aggregation of MAPT/tau proteins [11–14].

The progression of MAPT/tau pathology in AD follows a predictable pattern known as the Braak or NFT staging system, which correlates with the clinical severity of dementia [1,15]. The evidence suggests that MAPT/tau behaves in a prion-like manner, propagating from one brain region to another and spreading pathology throughout the brain [16–18]. This spreading of MAPT/tau aggregates contributes to the worsening cognitive decline observed in AD patients, making MAPT/tau a critical target for understanding disease progression and identifying potential therapeutic strategies [19]. MAPT/tau accumulation in the frontal cortical regions is particularly associated with advanced disease stages and cognitive impairment [1,15,20]. Interestingly, two recent case studies have reported that limited MAPT/tau propagation to the frontal lobe may be associated with delayed onset of dementia in familial AD [21,22]. MAPT/tau pathology in the frontal lobe of patients with frontotemporal lobar dementia/FTLD is sufficient to induce brain atrophy and drive behavioral abnormalities [23,24]. Despite the recognized role of frontal lobe MAPT/tau pathology in dementia progression, the molecular mechanisms underlying MAPT/tau propagation and the observed heterogeneity in dementia progression remain poorly understood.

Disturbances in proteostasis are a hallmark of neurodegenerative diseases, including AD [25–27]. Two main cellular pathways – proteasomal degradation and macroautophagy/autophagy – are responsible for degrading misfolded proteins [28,29]. Lysosomes, membrane-bound organelles that contain a variety of proteolytic enzymes, are integral to this process, maintaining an acidic pH necessary for optimal enzyme function [30]. The lysosomal V-ATPase complex plays a crucial role in maintaining this acidic environment, and LAMP1 (lysosomal associated membrane protein 1) is involved in lysosomal trafficking and fusion with other cellular compartments [30]. It has been shown that lysosomal dysfunction contributes to the accumulation of misfolded proteins in neurodegenerative diseases [31–33].

Studies have demonstrated that MAPT/tau aggregates can be secreted extracellularly via lysosomal exocytosis, which may facilitate the spread of MAPT/tau pathology across different brain regions [34,35]. However, it is not yet clear how lysosomal dysfunction might contribute to MAPT/tau aggregation and propagation, particularly in the context of cognitive resilience in AD. In this study, we propose that alterations in lysosomal activity contribute to MAPT/tau protein aggregation within the lysosomes of cognitively vulnerable AD patients. We demonstrate that in these patients, the lysosomes contain partially degraded MAPT/tau aggregates that are resistant to further degradation and are secreted via lysosomal exocytosis, potentially facilitating the spread of MAPT/tau pathology. Conversely, cognitively resilient AD patients exhibit preserved lysosomal activity, which may protect against the accumulation of MAPT/tau aggregates and slow the progression of dementia. This study provides new insights into the role of lysosomal dysfunction in the heterogeneity of dementia in AD and offers potential avenues for therapeutic intervention to modulate MAPT/tau pathology and improve cognitive outcomes in AD patients.

Results

Lysosomal integrity is preserved in cognitively resilient AD individuals, and its disturbance leads to MAPT/tau protein accumulation

The brains of Alzheimer patients are classified using NFTs or Braak staging (I-VI) based on the distribution and amounts of NFTs, with stage I representing the early stage and stages V/VI representing the advanced stage [1]. Clinical dementia rating scale (CDR) is used to measure the degree of dementia [36]. Further, the extended CDR scale (1–5) is used to predict the course of disease in AD patients [37–39]. Within Braak staging VI AD patients, there was a heterogeneity in dementia rate: some AD patients are severely demented (cognitively vulnerable or CDR-5), while others exhibit mild to moderate dementia (cognitively resilient or CDR-2) (Figure 1(A)). Cognitively vulnerable AD individuals showed elevated MAPT/tau protein aggregation in the prefrontal cortical regions (Figure 1(B-D)). Autophagy functions are reported to be preserved in early-AD individuals [40]. To explore whether the proteins associated with autophagy are altered between CDR-2 and CDR-5 groups, we analyzed key autophagy markers (Figure 1(E) and Figure S1A). There were no significant association between postmortem interval and autophagy-related proteins (Fig. S1C-1 H). While autophagy proteins such as MAP1LC3/LC3 and ATG5 were reduced from normal to AD groups, no significant differences were observed between CDR-2 and CDR-5 groups (Figure 1(F-I)). Further, no significant differences in autophagy-related proteins were found between males and females or between APOE genotypes within the AD groups. (Figure S2A and S2B).

We next investigated the expression of lysosomal proteins, including LAMP1 and V-ATPase subunit ATP6V1A (Figure 1(J) and Figure S1B). LAMP1 expression increased not only from normal to AD individuals but also from CDR-2 to CDR-5 groups (Figure 1(K)). Conversely, ATP6V1A expression relative to LAMP1 decreased from CDR-2 to CDR-5 groups (Figure 1(L)), suggesting lysosomal pH disturbance in the CDR-5 group. Similar to LAMP1 protein expression, the number and size of the LAMP1 puncta increased from normal to CDR-5 group but remained significantly unchanged between normal and CDR-2 group (Figure 1(M, N) and Figure S2C-E).

Although ATP6V1A expression relative to LAMP1 decreased from CDR-2 to CDR-5 group (Figure 1(L)), its expression relative to total proteins remained unaltered (Figure 1(J) and Fig. S2F). This suggests that lysosomal pH disruption in CDR-5 group is influenced more by lysosomal size than by ATP6V1A abundance. To explore this possibility, we segregated LAMP1 puncta into low and high groups from human brain tissues and measured ATP6V1A levels (Figure 1(O)). The data indicated that ATP6V1A expression did not align with increased LAMP1 puncta (Figure 1(P)).

To test whether increased LAMP1 is associated with altered pH, we treated cells with MAPT/tau seeds, which led to the expected increase in LAMP1 puncta (Figure S2G). However, the intensity of LysoSensor dye, which detects lysosomal acidity, significantly decreased in MAPT/tau-treated cells, indicating that increased LAMP1 size may elevate lysosomal pH (Figure 1(Q, R)). Treating cells with ammonium chloride, which raises lysosomal pH [41], resulted in increased MAPT/tau aggregation and secretion in MAPT/tau FRET biosensor cells (Figure S3A-D). Together, these data suggest that preserved lysosomal integrity is associated with reduced MAPT/tau pathology and cognitive resilience in AD.

The presence of undigested lysosomal Mapt/tau proteins in Alzheimer brains

Previous studies have shown that isolated 20S proteasomes degrade MAPT/tau proteins in vitro [28]. To demonstrate lysosome-mediated MAPT/tau degradation, we isolated the lysosomes as described previously and incubated them with MAPT/tau proteins [42]. The lysosomes efficiently degraded recombinant MAPT/tau proteins over time (Figure 2(A)). Next, we isolated the lysosomes from HEK 293 cells and incubated them with tissue lysates of normal and AD brain cases. Compared to MAPT/tau proteins from normal brain samples, MAPT/tau derived from AD brains showed greater resistance to lysosomal degradation (Figure 2(B)), suggesting that aggregated MAPT/tau proteins are resistant to lysosomal degradation. Further, acidic pH was important for efficient lysosomal degradation of MAPT/tau proteins (Fig. S3E, S3F). Given that our data showed that AD MAPT/tau is resistant to lysosome-mediated degradation, we hypothesized that lysosomes in AD brains would contain undigested MAPT/tau proteins. To test this, we examined the postmortem brains of AD individuals for the presence of MAPT/tau in lysosomes. Lysosomes were isolated from normal and AD brains, and we observed that lysosomes from AD brains contained an abundance of MAPT/tau proteins (Figure 2(C)). Co-immunoprecipitation using the LAMP1 antibody confirmed the presence of MAPT/tau in lysosomes (Figure 2(D)). Furthermore, immunohistochemical staining with pTau (AT8) showed their colocalization with LAMP1 (Figure 2(E, F)). To directly visualize MAPT/tau fibrils inside lysosomes, we utilized transmission electron microscopy (TEM). TEM images clearly revealed the presence of PHF MAPT/tau fibrils within isolated lysosomes (Figure 2(G)). Since MAPT/tau fibrils derived from AD brains are known to induce MAPT/tau aggregates, we investigated the pathogenic potential of undigested lysosomal MAPT/tau. Sarkosyl-insoluble MAPT/tau fibrils were isolated from lysosomes and added to MAPT/tau biosensor cells. These fibrils induced MAPT/tau aggregates (Figure 2(H)). As expected, lysosomes from CDR-5 group contained more MAPT/tau aggregates compared to those from CDR-2 group (Figure 2(I, J)). While lysosomal MAPT/tau levels were not significantly changed between male and female within CDR-2 group, female had significantly higher levels of lysosomal 3 R proteins compared to male in CDR-5 group (Figure 2(K)). Altogether, these findings demonstrate the presence of undigested pathogenic MAPT/tau proteins in the lysosomes of cognitively vulnerable AD brains. Lysosomes contain pathogenic core Mapt/tau fibrils

We treated primary neuronal cultures with lysosomal MAPT/tau fibrils and observed a significant reduction in the number of synaptic puncta (Figure 3(A-C)). Additionally, we found that MAPT/tau fibrils pre-incubated with isolated lysosomes (from HEK293 cells) induced more MAPT/tau seeding in MAPT/tau FRET biosensor cells (Figures 3(D, E)). This suggests that lysosomal MAPT/tau fibrils may have distinct properties compared to total MAPT/tau fibrils. To investigate this further, we performed western blotting using Tau-5 and 3 R antibodies, which recognize the proline-rich N-terminal and PHF core regions of MAPT/tau proteins, respectively (Figure 3(F) and Figure S3G). The intensity of 3 R levels was similar in both total and lysosomal fractions; however, Tau-5 levels were reduced in the lysosomal fractions compared to the total fractions (Figure 3(G)). Pronase enzyme digestion quickly degraded the peripheral MAPT/tau region, while the core 3 R region of AD fibrils remained resistant (Figure 3(H)). This suggests that the N-terminal regions of MAPT/tau are more susceptible to lysosomal enzyme digestion, while the core regions are more resistant. Thioflavin T, which binds to amyloidogenic core regions, showed greater intensity with lysosomal fibrils than with total MAPT/tau fibrils (Figure 3(I)). To confirm these findings, we collected TEM images of MAPT/tau fibrils from both total and lysosomal fractions (Figure 3(J)). The lysosomal MAPT/tau fibrils were significantly narrower than the MAPT/tau fibrils from the total fractions (Figure 3(K)), further supporting the idea that lysosomal enzymes digest the peripheral parts of MAPT/tau fibrils, leaving the PHF core resistant to degradation. Increased lysosomal Mapt/tau secretion in cognitively vulnerable AD individuals

Pathogenic proteins, such as MAPT/tau and SNCA/α-synuclein, can be secreted via lysosomes [34,35]. To directly observe the secretion of MAPT/tau proteins, we induced MAPT/tau aggregates in MAPT/tau FRET biosensor cells using AD seeds and found that MAPT/tau was secreted as particles (Fig. S4A and Video. S1). To visualize the lysosome-mediated MAPT/tau secretion specifically, MAPT/tau FRET biosensor cells were transfected with LAMP1-RFP plasmids, and MAPT/tau aggregation was induced. These cells were live imaged using total internal reflection fluorescence (TIRF) microscopy (Figure 4(A)). The analysis revealed that MAPT/tau proteins were secreted from LAMP1+ lysosomes and the LAMP1+ lysosomes can go into the recycling process (Figure 4(A-C)). Next, we assessed lysosomal exocytosis-mediated MAPT/tau secretion by treating the MAPT/tau FRET biosensor cells with AD fibrils or AD fibrils combined with the lysosomal exocytosis inhibitor vacuolin-1 [43]. Vacuolin-1 treatment reduced both the intensity of MAPT/tau secretion and the number of exocytosis events (Figures 4(D, E)). Additionally, we measured secreted MAPT/tau levels in the media and found that while AD fibrils increased MAPT/tau secretion, this effect was reversed in the presence of vacuolin-1 (Figures 4(F,g)). LAMP1 is present on the plasma membrane when lysosomes fuse with it, facilitating lysosomal exocytosis [44] (Fig. S4B and S4C). The addition of AD MAPT/tau fibrils resulted in an increase in the surface expression of LAMP1 proteins (Figure 4(H, I)). Similarly, elevated levels of synaptosomal membrane LAMP1 were observed in CDR-5 group compared to CDR-2 group (Figures 4(J, K)). Female, from CDR-5 group, had more synaptosomal membrane LAMP1 expression compared to Male (Figure 4(L)). The level of synaptosomal membrane LAMP1 were positively correlated with the levels of surface MAPT/tau assemblies in these AD groups (Figure 4(M-O)). Like synaptosomal membrane LAMP1 expression, the surface 3 R MAPT/tau proteins were also higher in female compared to male in CDR-5 group (Figure 4(P)). These data suggest that high levels of lysosomal MAPT/tau secretion may be linked to enhanced MAPT/tau propagation and severe cognitive decline. Discussion

There is a substantial association between MAPT/tau pathology and cognitive deterioration according to Braak staging [21,45]. Studies have shown that cellular molecules differ between early and late AD [46–48]. However, among the neuropathologically advanced Braak stage VI AD patients, the degree of dementia is heterogeneous [15,20]. We and others have shown that the levels of MAPT/tau pathology in the frontal lobe, specifically in BA10/BA9, represent the dementia severity [15,20,21]. The molecular mechanisms underlying heterogeneity in dementia or heterogeneity in disease progression are not clear [49]. In this study, we demonstrate that lysosomal integrity plays a critical role in modulating MAPT/tau pathology and cognitive resilience in the BA10 brain regions of individuals with AD. We further suggest that cognitively resilient AD individuals (CDR-2) maintain intact lysosomal function compared to cognitively vulnerable individuals (CDR-5). The increased expression of LAMP1 and altered ATP6V1A:LAMP1 ratios in CDR-5 group suggest that lysosomal pH regulation is impaired, contributing to their vulnerability. Impaired proteostasis is a hallmark of neurodegenerative disease and amelioration of lysosomal function reduce AD pathology [25,26,50–52]. A previous study has shown that manipulating the V-ATPase complex in a mouse model, potentially altering lysosomal pH, led to the aggregation of MAPT/tau proteins [53]. Our data using human samples indicate altered ATP6V1A:LAMP1 ratios rather than reduced expression of ATP6V1A. This suggests that a combined approach would be needed, rather than targeting a single protein or gene, to rescue the impaired lysosomes, reduce MAPT/tau pathology and restore cognitive function.

The lysosomes are shown to degrade MAPT/tau proteins in vitro [54,55]. Impaired lysosomal function results in worsening MAPT/tau pathology [34,53]. Our data showed that lysosomes efficiently degrade MAPT/tau proteins from normal brains, but MAPT/tau from Alzheimer’s brains resists lysosomal degradation, resulting in an accumulation of MAPT/tau fibrils within lysosomes. Our TEM images revealed the presence of intact MAPT/tau fibrils within lysosomes. Functional assays revealed that lysosomal MAPT/tau fibrils retain their seeding potential, causing MAPT/tau aggregates and synaptic loss in neuronal cultures, linking them to disease progression. Our data further demonstrated that lysosomes contain core MAPT/tau fibrils, suggesting that lysosomal enzymes tend to degrade the peripheral regions of MAPT/tau fibrils while leaving the amyloidogenic core intact. Although a recent study investigated SNCA/α-synuclein within lysosomes in mouse models, the morphological and structural details of SNCA/α-synuclein, in Parkinson patients remain to be investigated [56]. Likewise, an invitro based study showed that proteolytic cleavage of TARDBP/TDP-43 filaments and subsequent exposure of the amyloidogenic core were essential for the propagation of TARDBP/TDP-43 pathology and for enhancing the seeding ability of brain-derived TARDBP/TDP-43 aggregates [57]; however, human-based data is still missing. It is plausible that lysosomes digest the peripheral regions of not only MAPT/tau fibrils but also other fibrils such as SNCA/α-synuclein and TARDBP/TDP-43, while their amyloidogenic cores are resistant to lysosomal enzymes. These core fibrils may be more prone to seeding and could facilitate propagation across different brain regions. Therefore, further studies are needed to determine whether other fibrils exhibit MAPT/tau-like behavior within lysosomes. In this context, it would be essential to develop conformation-specific antibodies or compounds that target the amyloidogenic core rather than the peripheral regions of the fibrils.

Extracellular MAPT/tau secretion is possibly associated with its propagation from one brain region to another [58]. It can be secreted in a vesicular form or in free form [59]. Recent studies by we and others have characterized the pathophysiological nature of extracellular vesicular MAPT/tau proteins in AD brains [15,60]. Further, lysosomes are shown to release pathogenic proteins such as MAPT/tau and SNCA/α-synuclein [34,35,56]. Our study identifies lysosome-mediated MAPT/tau secretion as a critical mechanism for MAPT/tau propagation in cognitively vulnerable AD individuals. MAPT/tau aggregates are secreted via LAMP1-positive lysosomes, as demonstrated by live-cell imaging and biochemical approaches. This lysosomal exocytosis pathway is upregulated in CDR-5 group, as evidenced by increased surface LAMP1 and MAPT/tau assemblies. Lysosomal MAPT/tau secretion likely facilitates its uptake by synaptically connected neurons, promoting further MAPT/tau propagation in CDR-5 group. Women represent nearly 70% of the AD population [61] and exhibit greater memory impairment than men [62]. Our data show higher levels of synaptosomal membrane-associated LAMP1 and MAPT/tau proteins in women compared to men, suggesting that increased MAPT/tau secretion may be linked to the greater memory decline observed in women. However, no significant differences in membrane-bound MAPT/tau and LAMP1 proteins were observed between CDR-2 and CDR-5 in the male group, suggesting a potential sex-specific mechanism of MAPT/tau secretion that warrants further investigation. Furthermore, as the majority of our analyzed samples were from individuals of Caucasian descent, future studies examining MAPT/tau secretion mechanisms in other populations will be essential to broaden the generalizability of our findings. Although the APOE/APOE4 (apolipoprotein E) allele is the strongest genetic risk factor for AD [63], our limited sample size restricted analysis of its effect on synaptosomal membrane-associated LAMP1 and MAPT/tau proteins. Future investigations into the role of APOE variants in MAPT/tau secretion and propagation may help explain why individuals with the APOE4/4 genotype experience more rapid dementia progression and earlier mortality than carriers of other APOE variant.

In conclusion, our study underscores the pivotal role of lysosomal integrity in modulating MAPT/tau pathology and cognitive outcomes in AD. Dysfunctional lysosomes, marked by altered protein expression, pH dysregulation, and MAPT/tau accumulation, can drive MAPT/tau propagation and severe cognitive decline. Further, our studies reveal that lysosomes contain seed-competent MAPT/tau fibrils retaining amyloid core, while their peripheral regions are degraded. Therapeutically targeting lysosomal exocytosis pathways and the amyloidogenic core of these fibrils offer a promising avenue for mitigating MAPT/tau pathology and promoting cognitive resilience in AD.

Materials and methods

Frozen human post-mortem brain tissues

Human autopsy brain tissue (frozen) from the prefrontal cortex (Brodmann area 10) was obtained from Mount Sinai NBTR Neurobiobank. All the samples are from Braak stage VI with different CDR scores. Only pathologically confirmed cases were used for further experiments. In addition, cognitively normal individuals without AD pathology were used as controls in the experiments. All the cases were unbiased on the levels of gender, race, and education (Table S1). Post-mortem human brain tissues were obtained from a total of 34 individuals, including cognitively normal controls (n = 6) and Alzheimer disease (AD) cases with CDR-2 (n = 15) and CDR-5 (n = 13), all at Braak stage VI. The average age across the samples was 82.4 years, ranging from 64 to 103 years. The average postmortem interval (PMI) was 400 min. Both males and females were represented. A subset of Braak stage V brains was used for protocol optimization and was not included in the final quantitative analysis.

Isolation of lysosomes

The lysosomal fraction was isolated as described [64]. Briefly, approximately 200 mg of human brain tissue from the prefrontal cortex (Brodmann area 10) was homogenized in 0.25 M sucrose (Sigma-Aldrich 573,113) containing a protease inhibitor (cOmplete™, EDTA-free Protease Inhibitor Cocktail; Sigma-Aldrich 04,693,132,001). The homogenate was centrifuged at 2,000×g for 5 min at 4°C. This step was repeated three times to maximize the supernatant, which was pooled. The collected supernatant was then centrifuged at 17,000×g for 12 min. The resulting pellet was used for further ultracentrifugation steps. A gradient was prepared by mixing the sample with a 60% (w:v) Nycodenz (SERVA 3,100,001) solution in water. Successive layers of 26, 24, 20, and 15% Nycodenz gradients were added on top, followed by a layer of 0.25 M sucrose. The sample was subjected to centrifugation in an MLA-55 rotor using an Optima-MAX-XP ultracentrifuge from Beckman Coulter at 104,000×g for 3 h at 4°C. The lysosomal layer, located between the 26% and 24% gradients, was collected. This layer was resuspended in 0.25 M sucrose and 1X PBS (Sisco Research Laboratories Pvt. Ltd 78,529) with protease inhibitor, then centrifuged at 30,000×g for 1 h at 4°C. The resultant lysosomal pellet was resuspended in 1X PBS for further analysis.

Gel electrophoresis and western blot

For tissue homogenization, approximately 200 mg of frozen tissue stored at −80°C was thawed and homogenized in 400 µL of 1X PBS containing a protease inhibitor using a Dounce homogenizer (DWK Life Sciences 357,422). The homogenate was transferred to a microcentrifuge tube and centrifuged at 15,000×g for 20 min at 4°C. The homogenized tissue samples were used for western blot analysis. Protein samples were prepared by mixing them with 2X Laemmli buffer (Bio-Rad 1,610,737) and 50 mM DTT (Sigma-Aldrich 58,615,523). The mixture was boiled at 95°C for 5 min. Proteins were separated by molecular weight using SDS-PAGE and transferred onto a PVDF membrane (Bio-Rad 1,620,177). The membrane was blocked with 5% skimmed milk prepared in PBST (pH 7.5; HIMEDIA, GRM1254) for 1 h at room temperature. Primary antibodies were incubated with the membrane overnight at 4°C. Following incubation, the membrane was washed six times for 10 min each with 1X PBST solution. A corresponding secondary antibody was added, and the membrane was incubated for 1 h. Subsequently, the membrane was washed again 6 times for 10 min each with PBST solution. The membrane was developed using ECL solution (Bio-Rad, 170–5050) and imaged with a Bio-Rad imager. Signal intensities for each blot were quantified using ImageJ software. The following antibodies were used: Anti-MAPT/Tau (3-repeat isoform RD3) antibody (Sigma Aldrich 05,803, RRID- AB_310013) at 1:3000; MAPT/Tau monoclonal antibody Tau-5 (Thermo Scientific, AHB0042, RRID- AB_2536235) at 1:3000; LC3B (Cell Signaling Technology, 2775S, RRID- AB_915950) at 1:2000, BECN1/Beclin-1 (Cell Signaling Technology, 3738S, RRID- AB_490837) at 1:2000, ATG5 (Cell Signaling Technology 12,994, RRID- AB_2630393) at 1:2000, ATG7 (Cell Signaling Technology, 8558, RRID- AB_10831194) at 1:2000, ATP6V1A (Cell Signaling Technology 39,517, RRID- AB_3083783) at 1:2000, LAMP1 (Cell Signaling Technology, 9091S, RRID- AB_2687579) at 1:3000, GFP (Cell Signaling Technology, 2956, RRID- AB_1196615) at 1:2000, ATP1A1 (Cell Signaling Technology, 3010, RRID- AB_2060983) at 1:3000, PINK1 (Cell Signaling Technology, 6946, RRID- AB_11179069) at 1:3000, Anti-mouse IgG, HRP-linked Antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, 7076) at 1:6000, Anti-rabbit IgG, HRP-linked Antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, 7074) at 1:6000. Amido black (Sigma-Aldrich, A8181-1EA) was used to stain the total proteins on the PVDF membrane.

Immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence

Frozen human tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and kept at 4°C for 48 h. After fixation, 30% sucrose was added as a cryoprotectant, and the samples were left at 4°C for another 48 h. For sectioning, a cryostat (Leica, CM1520) was used to prepare blocks of postmortem tissue embedded in OCT media. 30-μm slices were prepared from each tissue and collected onto positively charged, PDL-coated multi-frosted (+) glass slides. The sections were allowed to dry at room temperature for 30–60 min. Cultured cells on coverslips were also fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min. Both the tissue sections and cultured cells were then blocked with 5% donkey serum (prepared in 0.3% Triton X-100 [Sigma-Aldrich, X100] in 1X PBS) for 30 min, followed by incubation with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. After primary antibody incubation, the slides and wells were washed six times for 5 min each with 1X PBS. The tissue sections and cell cultures were then incubated with corresponding secondary antibodies for 1 h and washed again six times for 5 min each with 1X PBS. The samples were counterstained with DAPI (1:1000 dilution). Finally, the tissue sections and coverslips were mounted using Antifade mounting medium (Vectashield, H-1000) and imaged using Olympus FV3000 confocal microscopy. The following primary antibodies with 1:200 dilutions were used: ATP6V1A (Cell Signaling Technology 39,517, RRID- AB_3083783), BSN/bassoon (Enzo Life Sciences, ADI-VAM-PS003-F, RRID-AB_11181058), HOMER1 (Synaptic Systems 160,002, RRID-AB_2120990), LAMP1 (Cell Signaling Technology, 9091S, RRID- AB_2687579) and AT8 (Thermo Scientific, MN-1020, RRID-AB_223647).

Dyes

After fixing the cells with 4% PFA, wheat germ agglutinin (Thermo Scientific, W32466,) was added and incubated for 10 min. The cells were washed briefly with PBS and then processed with regular immunostaining. The LysoSensor Blue DND-167 (Thermo Scientific, L7533) was added to live cells and incubated for 2 h. After incubation, the cells were washed, fixed, and processed with regular immunostaining.

In vitro MAPT/Tau seeding assay

MAPT/tau FRET biosensor cells (ATCC, CRL‐3275, RRID- CVCL_DA04) [65], which stably express the repeat domain of MAPT/tau conjugated with CFP and YFP, were cultured on 24-well plates with coverslips at 37°C and 5% CO₂ in a complete medium. The composition of the complete medium (50 mL) was as follows: 45 mL DMEM (Thermo Scientific 11,995,073), 5 mL FBS (Thermo Scientific 30,021,290), 500 µL PenStrep 100x (Thermo Scientific 38,089,999), 5 mL glutamine 100x (Thermo Scientific 29,241,900), and 5 mL sodium pyruvate 100x (Thermo Scientific 29,183,090). For transfection, 3% Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Scientific 11,668,019) was mixed with MAPT/tau protein (100 ng/well) and incubated for 30 min. The mixture was then added to the cells and incubated for 2 days. After incubation, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and counterstained with DAPI. Images were acquired using Olympus FV3000 confocal microscopy. 488-positive puncta, resulting from CFP and YFP aggregates, were quantified using ImageJ software.

Immunoprecipitation

Frozen human brain tissues were homogenized in 1X PBS containing a protease inhibitor using a homogenizer (IKA, T10 basic ULTRA-TURRAX®). The homogenates were then centrifuged at 15,000×g for 20 min. The resulting supernatant was used for immunoprecipitation. First, the samples were incubated with the primary antibody overnight at 4°C. The next day, Dynabeads conjugated with protein G (Sigma Aldrich, P3296) were washed 4–5 times with 1X PBS and added to the antibody-lysate mixture. The mixture was placed on a rocker for at least 4 h. The beads were subsequently washed three times and resuspended in SDS lysis buffer for further processing via immunoblotting.

Preparation of sarkosyl-insoluble Mapt/tau from human postmortem AD brain tissues

The isolation of sarkosyl-insoluble MAPT/tau was performed as previously described [18]. Briefly, 50–100 mg of tissue from the frontal cortical area (Brodmann area 10) was homogenized in nine volumes (v:w) of high-salt buffer. The buffer composition included 10 mM Tris-HCl (Sisco Research Laboratories Pvt. Ltd 99,438), pH 7.4, 0.8 M NaCl (Sisco Research Laboratories Pvt. Ltd 33,205), 1 mM EDTA (Sisco Research Laboratories Pvt. Ltd 43,272), 2 mM DTT, 10% sucrose, 0.1% sarkosyl (Sigma-Aldrich, L9150), PMSF (Sigma-Aldrich, P7626), cOmplete™ EDTA-free Protease Inhibitor Cocktail and PhosSTOP™ (Sigma-Aldrich 4,906,845,001). Homogenization was carried out using a Dounce homogenizer, and the homogenate was centrifuged at 10,000×g for 10 min at 4°C. This centrifugation step was repeated at least twice with the same buffer, and all supernatants were pooled. To the pooled supernatant, additional sarkosyl was added to achieve a final concentration of 1%. The mixture was incubated on a shaker for 1 h at room temperature. Subsequently, the sample was centrifuged at 300,000×g for 1 h using a TLA 100.3 rotor in an Optima-MAX-XP ultracentrifuge (Beckman Coulter). The resulting high-speed supernatant (sarkosyl-soluble fraction) was stored at −80°C for future use. The sarkosyl-insoluble pellet was washed once with 1X PBS, resuspended in six volumes of 1X PBS, and ultracentrifuged at 100,000×g for 30 min. The resultant pellet was resuspended in 1X PBS, sonicated with 20 short pulses (0.5 s per pulse), and centrifuged at 10,000×g for 30 min at 4°C to remove large debris.

Quantification of secreted Mapt/tau in media

MAPT/tau-biosensor cells (ATCC, CRL‐3275) were cultured in 24-well plates at 37°C in 5% carbon dioxide. Once the cells reached 50–60% confluency, vacuolin-1 (500 μM/mL; Selleckchem, S6912) was added, and the cells were incubated for 24 h. AD lysate containing 100 ng of MAPT/tau proteins, pre-incubated with Lipofectamine 2000 for 30 min, was added to each well and incubated for 48 h. Subsequently, the media from each condition was collected and centrifuged at 300,000×g for 1 h at 4°C. The resulting pellet was mixed with 2X Laemmli buffer (Bio-Rad 1,610,737) and processed for western blotting. The blots were probed with GFP antibodies, which recognize YFP proteins.

Live cell imaging

MAPT/tau FRET biosensor cells (HEK-293) cells that express MAPT/tau repeat domain having P301S mutation fused with CFP and YFP (ATCC, CRL‐3275) were cultured in 24 well plates at 5% CO2, 37°C. To prepare 50 ml of complete media, (45 ml) DMEM, 5 mL fetal bovine serum, 500 μl (100x) glutamate, 500 μl (100x) sodium pyruvate, and 500 μl (100x) Penstrep were mixed. AD lysate (100 ng/well concentration) was incubated in 3% Lipofectamine 2000 for 30 min and added to the cells. After 24 h, live imaging was performed using inverted confocal microscopy (Olympus FV3000). Image sequences were then processed using IMARIS software and exported as.avi files and mp4 files).

Total internal reflection (TIRF) microscopy and image analysis

The MAPT/tau aggregates in MAPT/tau FRET biosensor cells were induced as described above (live imaging). LAMP1-RFP plasmids (Addgene, 1817; deposited by Walther Mothes) were transfected two days before the MAPT/tau induction. Cells were imaged using a Nikon Ti2 Eclipse total internal reflection (TIRF) microscope with a 100x/1.45 objective (Nikon). Time-lapse images were taken at 10 s intervals. Excitation was done by DPSS laser at 488 and 561 nm at 100 and 200 ms, respectively. The emission light was captured on an sCMOS camera (Hamamatsu ORCA-Flash 4.0). Scaling was maintained at 0.07 μm/pixel [66].

To analyze the lysosomal-dependent MAPT/tau exocytosis, we tracked the movement of MAPT/tau and LAMP1 puncta at the plasma membrane using the MTrackJ plugin. We marked the MAPT/tau and LAMP1 puncta at the plasma membrane in each frame based on the blob diameter [67,68]. To evaluate the extent of exocytosis events of MAPT/tau in control and vacuolin-treated conditions, we analyzed the changes in the fluorescence intensity of the plasma membrane using Metamorph software. We considered the exocytosis events of MAPT/tau puncta after the appearance of puncta at the membrane, which stays at the same position for at least two frames and disappears suddenly. Fluorescence changes associated with MAPT/tau exocytosis events captured for a period. The analysis involved calculating the fluorescence of MAPT/tau granules in two regions: (1) a central circular region (c) with a diameter of 3 pixels and (2) an annular region (a) surrounding the circle with a diameter of 5 pixels. The granule fluorescence change (∆F) was obtained by subtracting the circle fluorescence (c) from the annulus fluorescence value (a) (∆F = c−a). The ∆F values were expressed as a per-pixel average for the 3-pixel diameter circle, encompassing the entire puncta. Fluorescence intensity changes (∆F) of the MAPT/tau puncta were plotted against time [69,70].

Sample preparation and data acquisition of room temperature negative staining TEM

Isolated lysosomes were added on 300-mesh Cu TEM grids (Ted Pella Inc 08,143) which were glow-discharged (negative polarity) for 30 s in a Pelco easiGlow glow-discharge system. For whole lysosomal imaging, we isolated the lysosomes from AD brains as described above. To obtain MAPT/tau fibrils, the sarkosyl-insoluble fractions were prepared from total lysates and lysosomal lysates. The sample was incubated at room temperature for 1 min. Additional solution was carefully blotted off using Whatman Filter paper. This was followed by touching the grid to a drop of 1% freshly prepared uranyl-acetate solution and blotting the excess liquid. The TEM data were acquired on a 120 kV Talos L120C RT electron microscope equipped with a bottom-mounted Ceta camera (4k × 4k) at different magnifications ranging from 57,000× and 92,000× magnifications between calibrated pixel sizes of 2.45 to 1.52 Å/pixel at specimen level.

Pronase assay

A reaction mixture containing 30 µL of sarkosyl-insoluble MAPT/tau, isolated from AD postmortem brain tissues, was incubated with the Pronase enzyme (Sigma Aldrich 10,165,921,001) at a concentration of 0.2 mg/mL. The mixture was incubated at 37°C for 0, 10, 30, and 60 min. To inhibit proteolysis, a blocking buffer was added to the reaction. The blocking buffer consisted of 2X Laemmli buffer (Bio-Rad 1,610,737), DTT (50 mM; Sigma Aldrich 58,615,523), and PMSF (25 mM; Sigma Aldrich 10,837,091,001). Samples collected at each time point were analyzed via western blot. The blots were probed with the following antibodies: Anti-MAPT/Tau (3-repeat isoform RD3) antibody (Sigma Aldrich 05,803), MAPT/Tau monoclonal antibody (TAU-5; Thermo Scientific, AHB0042), anti-mouse IgG, HRP-linked Antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, 7076).

Lysosomal degradation

Lysosomal fraction was isolated from the HEK 293 cells as described previously [64]. The lysosomal pellet was subjected to freeze and thaw cycles to release the lysosomal enzymes. The isolated lysosomal fraction was further used for the assays mentioned below.

To stop the reaction, 2X Laemmli buffer was added and proceeded with western blotting and probed with Tau-5 antibody (Thermo Scientific, AHB0042).

The reaction mixture was further proceeded with western blotting and probed with MAPT/tau monoclonal antibody (TAU-5) with dilution of 1:3000.

Lysosome-digested and -undigested MAPT/tau fibrils for FRET culture: Isolated lysosomes were subjected to freeze- thaw cycles to release the enzyme. The cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and counterstained with DAPI. Images were acquired using Olympus FV3000 confocal microscopy. GFP positive puncta were quantified using imageJ software.

Isolation of plasma membrane from culture cells

To isolate plasma membrane fraction from the HEK 293 cells, from the control and MAPT/tau treated cells, media was removed from the cells and 300 µl cell lysis buffer was added which constituted 25 mM HEPES buffer (pH-7.4), 150 mM Nacl and 5 mM EDTA buffer. The cells were collected in tube and further proceeded with homogenization using Dounce homogenizer and centrifuged at 1,000×g for 5 min at 4°C. The resulting supernatant was again centrifuged at 16,000×g for 5 min. The collected supernatant was transferred to an ultracentrifuge tube and centrifuged at 100,000×g for 30 min at 4°C using a TLA 100.3 rotor in Optima-MAX-XP ultracentrifuge from Beckman Coulter. The pellet was suspended in 1X PBS. For western blot, the pellet was mixed with laemmli buffer (2X) (Bio-Rad 1,610,737) and DTT (50 mM) and boiled at 95°C for 5 min. The blot was probed with LAMP1 antibody having the dilution (1:3000) (Cell Signaling Technology, 9091).

Isolation of synaptosomal membranes from frozen human brain tissues

The synaptosomal membranes were isolated as previously described, with a few modifications [71,72]. Briefly, human brain tissue was homogenized in ice-cold homogenization buffer (10 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 300 mM sucrose, and one cOmplete protease inhibitor tablet from Roche Molecular Biochemicals per 50 mL) with an Ultra-Turrax homogenizer (IKA, Germany). The homogenate was centrifuged at 1,000xg for 10 min, and the resulting pellet was discarded. The supernatant was then centrifuged at 50000xg for 30 min using an ultracentrifuge (Thermo scientific). The resulting pellet was resuspended in wash buffer (homogenization buffer without sucrose), incubated on ice for 30 min, and centrifuged again at 50000xg for 30 min to obtain synaptosomal pellet. Sucrose gradient mixture was prepared starting from 69, 54, 45, 41 and 37% sucrose solution prepared in wash buffer. The synaptosomal pellet was loaded on the top of sucrose gradient mixture and ultracentrifuged at 70000xg for 3 h. The synaptosomal membrane fraction that formed at 41% was collected, mixed with wash buffer and ultra centrifuged at 100,000xg for 30 min. The resulting synaptosomal membrane was used to conduct subsequent experiments.

Thioflavin assay

Thioflavin T dye (6 µM; Sigma-Aldrich, T3516) was mixed with sarkosyl-insoluble lysosomal and total MAPT/tau fibrils were incubated for 2 h at room temperature in the dark. The sample was loaded onto coverslip and imaged using Olympus FV3000 confocal microscopy.

Statistical analysis

For all the experiments, values of n, p- values, and the type of statistical test being used can be found in respective figure legends. The sample size was determined based on the previously published articles [15,46]. For the tests, the P-value, p ≤0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using Python (version 3.11.13) in Google Colab and GraphPad Prism software. Unpaired two-tailed t-tests were used for comparisons between two groups, and one-way ANOVA was used for comparisons involving more than two groups. Wherever appropriate, analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was used to adjust for age as a covariate and Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) post-hoc test was applied for multiple group comparisons. For western blot quantification, each band was selected in a rectangle box, and with the help of wand option, area intensity was measured in ImageJ software. Quantification of MAPT/tau-free lysosomes was performed using ImageJ software with the Coloc 2 plugin. We calculated Mander’s coefficient (M2), which determines the fraction of AT8 signal overlapping with LAMP1. To estimate the proportion of LAMP1+ lysosomes that do not contain AT8 signal (i.e., MAPT/tau-free lysosomes), M2 values were subtracted from 1 (1 – M2). These values are shown in Figure 2F to compare normal and AD samples.
