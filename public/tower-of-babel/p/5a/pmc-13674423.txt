Gene expression signatures from single‐cell transcriptomics predict Sjögren's syndrome
Authors: Shumin Cheng, Ling Yan, Yilin Wu, Yan Li, Ziheng Ye, Zhen Zhang, Yi Zhang, Jingyun Ma, Yuhong Liang, Zhaofan Luo, Huacheng Wang, Meili Gao, Chao Qin, Ke Zhu, Yun Leng, Kamran Ullah, Jun Liang, Haiyan Yan, Guan Yang, Yingqian Mo, Bihui Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSjögren's syndrome (SjS) is a systemic autoimmune disorder characterized by lymphocytic infiltration of exocrine glands, leading to dry eyes and mouth. While previous genome‐wide association studies (GWAS) and transcriptomic analyses have identified genes associated with SjS, predictive models based on single‐cell resolution are limited. In this study, single‐cell RNA sequencing (scRNA‐seq) data from peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) of SjS patients were analyzed to map immune cell alterations linked to the disease. Compared with healthy controls, SjS patients displayed decreased proportions of naïve CD8 + T cells and Helios FOXP3 lo CD4 + Tregs, alongside increased frequencies of CTLA−4+ CD8 + inhibitory T cells and TRDC γδ T cells. Using machine learning, a predictive model for SjS diagnosis was developed based on a 12‐gene signature (SjS. Sig: GIMAP7, PSMB8, CD27, CCR7, TAGAP, UQCR10, HCLS1, LCK, TNFAIP3, ISG15, GIMAP4, and HLA‐DRB1), which effectively differentiated patients from healthy individuals. Key genes such as CD27, PSMB8, HCLS1, LCK, UQCR10, and GIMAP4 were validated in clinical samples through flow cytometry and real‐time quantitative PCR. These findings provide insights into the immune landscape of SjS at a single‐cell resolution and propose a reliable molecular signature for diagnosis and immune monitoring.Keywords: immune landscape, predictive signature, single‐cell RNA sequencing, Sjögren's syndrome

INTRODUCTION

Sjögren's syndrome (SjS) is a common autoimmune disorder predominantly affecting middle‐aged to elderly women, with a global prevalence of approximately 0.06%. The disease demonstrates a male‐to‐female ratio of 1:9, typically presenting in individuals over 50 years of age [1]. SjS is characterized by lymphocytic infiltration of the exocrine glands, particularly the salivary and lacrimal glands. However, the inflammatory process can extend beyond these glands, potentially affecting the skin, lungs, kidneys, gastrointestinal system, nervous system, and musculoskeletal system, leading to a range of symptoms including dry skin, reduced lung function, kidney damage, and peripheral neuropathy, alongside the hallmark dry mouth and eyes [2].

The pathogenesis of SjS is multifactorial, involving genetic, environmental, and immune system factors. Although the precise cause remains unclear, several genes have been implicated, including interferon regulatory factor 5 (IRF5), signal transducer and activator of transcription 4 (STAT4), interleukin‐12 subunit alpha (IL‐12A), and B lymphocyte kinase (BLK) [3, 4]. A meta‐analysis of 23 studies has identified a strong association between SjS and specific human leukocyte antigen (HLA) class II alleles (DRB1, DQA1, and DQB1), suggesting that antigen presentation to CD4 + T cells and subsequent immune activation play critical roles in disease pathogenesis [5]. These associations have been further supported by genome‐wide association studies (GWAS) conducted on populations of European and Han Chinese descent [6, 7].

Currently, SjS diagnosis relies on a combination of clinical assessments (notably chronic dryness of the eyes and mouth), ocular tests (such as the Schirmer test), laboratory tests (including serological testing and minor salivary gland biopsy), and imaging studies to evaluate salivary gland function [8, 9]. However, achieving a precise differential diagnosis remains challenging due to the disease's heterogeneity, complex clinical phenotypes, and absence of specific molecular biomarkers, especially in the early stages. With the rapid advancements in single‐cell RNA sequencing (scRNA‐seq) technology and the availability of relevant datasets, identifying characteristic SjS signatures at the single‐cell level is now possible.

This study conducted an in‐depth analysis of the publicly available SjS scRNA‐seq dataset (GSE157278), focusing on immune cell molecular signatures to develop a novel predictive model for SjS. Four key immune cell subpopulations were identified as being significantly associated with SjS onset: Naïve CD8 + T cells, TRDC γδ T cells, Helios FOXP3 lo CD4 + regulatory T cells (Tregs), and CTLA‐4 + CD8 + inhibitory T cells. These subpopulations form the biological foundation for the SjS.Sig diagnostic model, integrating their characteristic gene expression signatures (e.g., PSMB8, CD27, UQCR10) to capture immune dysregulation specific to SjS. Based on these findings, this study constructed the SjS.Sig model and validated its predictive performance using six independent bulk RNA‐seq datasets. A core set of seven genes with high diagnostic potential was identified, including two SjS‐specific differentially expressed genes (PSMB8, CD27). The model's accuracy was further validated using newly collected samples from SjS patients through flow cytometry, real‐time quantitative PCR (qPCR), and immunoblotting. This study enhances understanding of SjS pathogenesis and provides a clinically applicable diagnostic tool based on key immune cell signatures for early and accurate disease detection.

RESULTS

Reanalysis of scRNA‐seq data reveals cell subpopulations associated with SjS

Using the 10x Genomics platform, this study reanalyzed scRNA‐seq data from 10 participants (five SjS patients and five healthy controls [HCs]) from a previous study [10]. After performing quality control and data integration, a total of 57,626 single cells were obtained. Clustering was performed using the “clustree” R package with a resolution of 0.8, resulting in 22 initial clusters (Figure 1A and Figure S1A,B) [11]. Cell annotation was carried out by integrating automatic annotations from “SingleR” (Figure S1C), marker genes from “CellMarker” [12], and literature‐reported markers [13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21], leading to the identification of 10 major cell types (Figure 1B).

Monocytes/macrophages were defined by high expression of CD68, CD163, MS4A7, FCN1, and S100A12 [15, 21]. Dendritic cells (DCs) exhibited high expression of CD1C, FCER1A, and CLEC10A [22]. Natural killer (NK) cells were characterized by elevated levels of NKG7, CD247, KLRC1, and KLRF1 [23]. Platelets displayed high expression of ITGA2B (CD41), PF4, GP1BA (CD42b), and TUBB1 [24]. B cells were identified by high expression of MS4A1 (CD20), CD79A, and CD79B [25]. T cells expressed CD3D, CD3E, CD4, CD8A, CD8B, TRDC, TRGC1, and TRGC2 [26, 27, 28], and were further categorized into three main subgroups: CD4+, CD8+, and γδ T cells (Figure 1C). Finally, the 22 cell subpopulations were precisely annotated following the method described by Sun et al. [29] (Figure 1D).

This study analyzed the percentage of each cluster relative to the total in each participant and compared the differences between HCs and SjS patients (Figure S2A−C and Table S1). Among the 22 clusters, cluster 3 (Naïve CD8 + T) and cluster 17 (Helios FOXP3 lo CD4 + Tregs) were significantly reduced in SjS patients compared with HCs (p < 0.05). In contrast, cluster 15 (TRDC γδ T) and cluster 19 (CTLA‐4 + CD8+ inhibitory T cells) were significantly increased in SjS patients (p < 0.05). These four cell clusters were selected for further analysis.

Naïve CD8+ T (cluster 3) was decreased in SjS

Cluster 3 accounted for 39% of the total CD8 + T cells, which included clusters 2, 3, 11, and 19 (Figures 1D and 2A). This cluster was characterized by high expression of LINC02446, PASK, LEF1, and CCR7 (Figure 2B,C and Table S2). Differential expression analysis (“FindMarkers”) identified specific overexpression of genes such as SNHG25, GIMAP5, GIMAP4, PPP1CA, CD27, GIMAP7, YPEL3, LINC01619, SELENOH, and KRT10 in this cluster (Figure 2D and Table S3). Pathway enrichment analysis based on cluster‐specific genes identified by Seurat revealed that naïve T cells were enriched in pathways such as structural constituent of ribosome, rRNA binding, translation regulation, mRNA 5’‐UTR binding, and ubiquitin‐protein transferase regulator activity (Figure 2E and Table S4). These findings align with the relatively high expression of ribosomal proteins (RPS3A, RPS5, RPS18) and eukaryotic translation elongation factors (EEF1B2, EEF1G) in Naïve CD8 + T cells.

CTLA‐4 + CD8+ inhibitory (cluster 19) was increased in SjS

Cluster 19 accounted for 3% of the total CD8+ T cells (Figure 2A). This cluster exhibited overexpression of genes such as TYMS, PCLAF, CD38, MCM5, CENPM, and RRM2 (Figure 2B,C and Table S5). Compared with other CD8 + T‐cell clusters, this cluster demonstrated significantly higher expression of immune checkpoint inhibitors, including CTLA‐4, TIGIT, and PDCD1 (Figure 1D), and was thus designated CTLA‐4 + CD8 + inhibitory T cells [30]. Differential expression analysis using the “FindMarkers” function revealed high expression of HLA‐DRB5, ABHD17A, UBE2J2, LYZ, TAF10, HEBP2, EMC6, TRG‐AS1, HIST1H1D, and MGST3 (Figure 2F and Table S4). Pathway enrichment analysis indicated that CTLA‐4 + CD8+ inhibitory T cells (cluster 19) were significantly enriched in pathways related to energy metabolism, including oxidoreduction‐driven active transmembrane transporter activity, electron transfer activity, NADH dehydrogenase activity, primary active transmembrane transporter activity, and single‐stranded DNA binding (Figure 2E).

TRDC γδ T cell (cluster 15) was increased in SjS

Among the γδ T cell populations (clusters 9, 10, and 15), only cluster 15 exhibited a significant percentage difference between groups (Figure 1D and Figure S2C; p < 0.05). This cluster represented 18% of γδ T cells (Figure S3A). In contrast to other γδ T cell clusters, TRDC γδ T cells displayed high expression of XCL1, KLRF1, KLRC2, and TRDC (Figure S3B,C, and Table S6). Differential expression analysis revealed that the TRDC γδ T cells from SjS patients exhibited characteristic overexpression of HLA‐DRB5, PPP1CA, MYOM2, S100A11, GIMAP7, YPEL3, and GIMAP4 (Figure S3D and Table S7). Pathway enrichment analysis demonstrated that TRDC γδ T cells were enriched for gene ontology (GO) terms such as phosphotyrosine residue binding, nonmembrane spanning protein tyrosine kinase activity, and retinoic acid receptor binding (Figure S3E and Table S8).

Helios FOXP3lo CD4+ Treg (cluster 17) was decreased in SjS

Among the CD4 + T cell populations (clusters 0, 1, 12, and 17; Figure 1D), only cluster 17 exhibited a significant percentage difference between groups (Figure S2C; p < 0.05). This cluster accounted for 4% of CD4 + T cells (Figure S4A) and was characterized by high expression of MED13L, ZSWIM6, IL2RA, and IKZF2, and was thus designated Helios FOXP3 lo CD4 + Treg (Figure S4B,C and Table S9). Differential expression analysis identified characteristic overexpression of LINC01619, PPBP, IL2RG, and SNHG25 in this subgroup (Figure S4D and Table S10). Pathway enrichment analysis indicated that the Helios FOXP3 lo CD4 + Treg subpopulation was enriched for terms related to protein serine kinase activity, GTPase regulator activity, nucleoside‐triphosphatase regulator activity, transcription coregulator activity, and ubiquitin‐like protein transferase activity (Table S11).

Validation of four featured clusters

The overexpressed genes and pathway‐enriched genes for the four previously identified clusters were compiled, and gene set scoring (GSS) and enrichment methods were applied for single‐cell gene scoring, confirming the specificity of each cluster (Figure 3A,B and Figure S5A,B). Genes highly expressed in SjS patients relative to HCs were then selected. These candidate genes underwent training using Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression across six publicly available gene expression datasets (Table 1 and Figures S6−S9). The results indicated that gene set variation analysis (GSVA) scores for each SjS patient cluster were significantly higher than those for the HCs (Figure 3C,D and Figure S5C,D). Detailed analyses of three SjS‐specific subsets, including heatmaps of highly expressed genes in CD8 + T cells, γδ T cells, and CD4 + T cells, as well as network correlation diagrams of GO term enrichment for each subset, are provided in Figure S10. Ultimately, cellular signatures for cluster 3 (20 genes), cluster 19 (21 genes), cluster 15 (23 genes), and cluster 17 (19 genes) were established (Table S12). Fluorescence‐activated cell sorting (FACS) analysis of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from 20 participants (9 HCs and 11 SjS patients; Table S13) confirmed these findings (Figure 4A–D). Compared with controls, cluster 3 (Naïve CD8 + T cells) and cluster 17 (Helios FOXP3 lo CD4 + Treg) were significantly reduced, while cluster 15 (TRDC γδ T cells) and cluster 19 (CTLA‐4 + CD8 + Inhibitory) were significantly elevated in SjS patients (Figure 4E–H; p < 0.05). Subsequent transcriptional validation of these signature genes is presented in Figure 4I–N, following the construction of the prediction model.

Construction and validation of a gene prediction model

To refine the prediction model, characteristic genes from the four highlighted clusters were consolidated, with duplicates removed (Table S12). A protein–protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed using search tool for the retrieval of interacting genes/proteins (STRING), comprising 14,542 nodes and 61,820 edges. Key subnetworks were identified using the Molecular Complex Detection (MCODE) algorithm, resulting in two modules: one with 106 nodes and 554 edges, and another with 154 nodes and 499 edges (score > 5; Figure 5A,B). Hub genes (degree > 20) were identified through CytoHubba, and their intersection with the characteristic genes from the four clusters led to the development of a 12‐gene signature (GIMAP7, PSMB8, CD27, CCR7, TAGAP, UQCR10, HCLS1, LCK, TNFAIP3, ISG15, GIMAP4, and HLA‐DRB1), hereafter referred to as SjS.Sig, reflecting their potential regulatory roles in SjS (Figure 5B).

(B) Molecular Complex Detection (MCODE) subnetwork analysis and hub gene identification. Two significant gene clusters (score > 5) were extracted using MCODE: subnetwork1 (106 nodes, 554 edges, up) and subnetwork2 (154 nodes, 499 edges, down). The central expanded nodes represent the top 10 hub genes identified by the CytoHubba Degree algorithm, highlighting the core regulatory components within each functional cluster. (C) The predictive performance of SjS.Sig when validating GSE143153 and GSE84844.

To verify the predictive performance of SjS.Sig, it was evaluated in two independent gene expression datasets (GSE84844 and GSE143153), neither of which was used in the model development. For the GSE84844 dataset (n = 60, HCs vs. SjS: 30 vs. 30), SjS.Sig successfully distinguished HCs from SjS patients with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.88 (95% confidence interval [CI], 84%–93%; Figure 5C), sensitivity of 89% (95% CI, 80%–94%), and specificity of 87% (95% CI, 72%–95%). Similarly, for the GSE143153 dataset (n = 32, HCs vs. SjS: 15 vs. 17), SjS.Sig achieved an AUC of 0.84 (95% CI, 77%–90%; Figure 5C), sensitivity of 85% (95% CI, 77%–92%), and specificity of 82% (95% CI, 65%–92%). These results demonstrate that SjS.Sig is an effective biomarker for accurately predicting SjS patients.

Calibration plots and Brier scores confirmed both strong discrimination and reliable calibration (Figure S11A,B). These additional analyses reinforce that SjS.Sig not only offers excellent discriminatory power but also shows reliable calibration, enhancing its potential as a robust biomarker for SjS prediction. Furthermore, the performance of SjS.Sig in rheumatoid arthritis (RA) was assessed through receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis, calibration plots, and Brier scores (Figure S11C–F). The results indicated poor performance in RA, further validating the specificity of SjS.Sig for SjS.

Six genes (PSMB8, CD27, UQCR10, HCLS1, LCK, and GIMAP4) were significantly upregulated in SjS patients in scRNA‐seq data (Figure S12). To validate their expression and predictive value, new blood samples were collected from 20 participants (10 non‐SjS and 10 SjS patients; Table S14) to compare the transcriptional levels of these genes between the two groups using real‐time qPCR. The results showed that all six genes were significantly upregulated in the SjS group compared with controls (p < 0.05) (Figure 4I–N). Additionally, PBMCs from another eight participants (four non‐SjS and four SjS patients) were collected, and the protein expression levels of PSMB8, HCLS1, and GIMAP4 were compared using Western blot analysis (Figure S13), confirming the validity of the predictive model.

To further validate the robustness of the identified gene clusters, Random Forest (RF) analysis was performed on four cell subpopulations across six Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) datasets (Figures S14 and S15). For each dataset, genes were ranked based on their importance scores, derived from the mean decrease in accuracy (MDA) metric. Genes exceeding the predefined threshold were selected as RF‐identified features. An intersection analysis was then conducted to identify genes consistently selected across various datasets and cell populations. This integrative analysis resulted in a robust candidate gene set consisting of 21 genes.

To assess the generalizability of the RF‐derived gene clusters, external validation was performed using the independent GSE143153 and GSE84844 datasets. In the GSE143153 dataset, the identified gene cluster effectively distinguished HCs from SjS patients, achieving an AUC of 0.81 (95% CI, 0.75%–0.89%; Figure S16A). Similarly, in the GSE84844 dataset, the gene cluster demonstrated strong predictive performance with an AUC of 0.88 (95% CI, 0.85%–0.92%; Figure S16B).

Although the results from the RF analysis were slightly less optimal than those from the “LASSO + PPI” approach, the gene set identified by RF was significantly larger. This suggests that while RF may provide a broader range of potential biomarkers, the larger gene set may limit its practical applicability in clinical settings due to challenges in clinical validation and implementation.

Cellular crosstalk and immune dysregulation in SjS

Compared with HCs, the overall cell–cell communication strength was significantly increased in SjS (Figure 6A,B). Notably, there were distinct communication changes between specific immune cell subsets. Interactions between Naïve CD8 + T cells and other cell populations were significantly reduced, both in terms of incoming and outgoing interaction strength (Figure 6C,D), suggesting that the immune activity of this subset may be suppressed. Intriguingly, while the overall communication of Naïve CD8 + T cells was diminished, the activity of specific pathways diverged. We observed a decrease in major histocompatibility complex (MHC)‐I signaling, which is consistent with the dampened outgoing/incoming interaction strength of these cells. Notably, the MHC‐II pathway did not show a parallel reduction, indicating a selective alteration in communication pathways rather than a blanket suppression (Figure 6A,E,F). This dissociation might suggest a shift in the functional state or interacting partners of Naïve CD8 + T cells in SjS, possibly related to aberrant immune regulation.

In contrast, interactions of TRDC γδ T cells and CTLA‐4 + CD8 + inhibitory T cells with other cell populations were significantly increased (Figure 6B), suggesting these subsets may be in a more activated immune state in SjS, potentially exhibiting enhanced immune‐suppressive functions. This increased cell–cell communication may be closely related to the breakdown of immune tolerance, chronic inflammation, and tissue damage in SjS.

These findings highlight the altered immune function of Naïve CD8 + T cells in SjS, as well as the critical roles of TRDC γδ T cells and CTLA‐4 + CD8 + inhibitory T cells in immune regulation. Overall, the immune environment in SjS patients is characterized by significant reshaping of cell–cell communication patterns, which may provide valuable insights into the immune mechanisms underlying the disease.

DISCUSSION

Dysregulation of immune cells, leading to autoantibody production and subsequent tissue damage, is a key mechanism in the pathogenesis of SjS [31]. However, genetic analyses of SjS have predominantly relied on meta‐analyses, GWAS, and bulk transcriptomics, often overlooking the contributions of specific immune cell types. Moreover, existing predictive models typically utilize mixed samples, failing to capture the distinct molecular signatures of individual immune cell populations. To address this gap, this study developed a predictive model for SjS diagnosis at single‐cell resolution.

By reanalyzing publicly available scRNA‐seq data from SjS patients, this study identified four key immune cell subsets associated with disease risk: a reduction in naïve CD8 + T cells and Helios FOXP3 lo CD4 + Tregs, along with an increase in TRDC γδ T cells and CTLA‐4 + CD8 + inhibitory T cells. These dynamic changes reflect a profound imbalance in immune homeostasis in SjS. Specifically, the reduction in Helios FOXP3 lo CD4 + Treg cells undermines immune tolerance, while the expansion of TRDC γδ T cells acts as an “inflammation amplifier,” exacerbating local tissue damage by secreting proinflammatory cytokines. In response to this imbalance, the increase in CTLA‐4 + CD8 + suppressor T cells may represent a compensatory mechanism; however, chronic inflammation often leads to the exhaustion or ineffectiveness of this suppressive function. Ultimately, the exhaustion of naïve CD8 + T cells reflects a gradual loss of functional resilience and diversity within the immune system, driven by persistent and ineffective self‐attack. Thus, the progression of SjS is attributed not only to the overactivation of effector immunity but also to the dysfunction of regulatory functions and the depletion of immune resources.

Leveraging these findings, this study constructed SjS.Sig, a novel machine‐learning‐based diagnostic model incorporating characteristic genes from the four identified cell clusters. This biologically informed model includes 12 genes: GIMAP7, PSMB8, CD27, CCR7, TAGAP, UQCR10, HCLS1, LCK, TNFAIP3, ISG15, GIMAP4, and HLA‐DRB1. Each has biological plausibility in the context of SjS pathogenesis. The GIMAP gene family (GIMAP4 and GIMAP7) encodes GTPases essential for lymphocyte survival and homeostasis, and their dysregulation has been implicated in autoimmune susceptibility [32]. PSMB8, a component of the immunoproteasome, is critically involved in MHC class I antigen processing and has been associated with type I interferon‐driven autoimmunity [33]. CD27, a tumor necrosis factor receptor superfamily member, regulates B‐ and T‐cell activation and differentiation; its altered expression in SjS patients may reflect dysregulated lymphocyte co‐stimulation [34]. LCK, a Src‐family kinase indispensable for T‐cell receptor (TCR) signal transduction, and HCLS1, a hematopoietic cell‐specific adaptor protein involved in antigen receptor signaling, collectively implicate dysregulated proximal TCR signaling in SjS pathophysiology. UQCR10, a subunit of mitochondrial respiratory chain complex III, reflects the metabolic reprogramming evident in CTLA‐4⁺ CD8⁺ inhibitory T cells, consistent with the energy metabolism pathway enrichment observed in this subpopulation. TNFAIP3 (A20) is a ubiquitin‐editing enzyme with critical roles in terminating NF‐κB signaling and maintaining immune homeostasis; loss‐of‐function variants in TNFAIP3 have been associated with multiple autoimmune disorders, including SjS [35]. ISG15, an interferon‐stimulated gene, reflects the prominent type I interferon signature that is a hallmark of SjS pathogenesis [36]. CCR7 and HLA‐DRB1 further corroborate the involvement of T‐cell trafficking and antigen presentation in disease mechanisms, consistent with established GWAS findings [5, 6, 7]. In addition to the characteristic genes of the four cell subpopulations, the model integrates six differentially expressed genes in SjS: CD27, PSMB8, HCLS1, LCK, UQCR10, and GIMAP4.

Notably, our study differs from the original scRNA‐seq publication due to variations in dimensionality reduction parameters and clustering nomenclature. The markers used for clustering CD4 + T cell or CD8 + T cell subpopulations in this study were compared with those in the original article (Figure S17 and Table S15). Notably, CD4 + CTLs (characterized by high expression of GZMH, GZMA, and GZMB) and CD4+ TRAV13‐2 + T cells (characterized by high expression of TRAV13‐2) in the original study were dispersed into CD25 + FOXP3 hi CD4 + Tregs (cluster 1) and FOXP3 hi CD4 + Tregs (cluster 12) in this study. Similarly, Th17 cells (characterized by high expression of RORA, IL6ST, and IL17RA) were dispersed into Naïve CD4+ T cells (cluster 0) and Helios FOXP3 lo CD4 + Tregs (cluster 17), with a significant decrease in Helios FOXP3 lo CD4 + Tregs observed in SjS. Additionally, due to more detailed clustering of CD8 + T cells, naïve CD8 + T cells and CTLA‐4 + CD8 + inhibitory T cells exhibited characteristic immune features of SjS, despite neither CD8 + T‐cell subpopulation reaching statistical significance in the original study. The new findings in this study were validated by a substantial amount of bulk RNA‐seq data and clinical fresh samples.

While this study offers novel insights and a promising diagnostic tool for SjS, several limitations warrant consideration. First, the discovery‐phase scRNA‐seq cohort (GSE157278) consisted of a relatively small sample size (five SjS patients and five HCs), which is a common limitation in initial exploratory single‐cell studies. To address this, a comprehensive validation strategy was employed. The identified cellular alterations were validated through flow cytometry in an independent clinical cohort, and the molecular signature (SjS.Sig) was rigorously tested across multiple larger bulk transcriptomic datasets and by qPCR in fresh samples. Second, while genes in the SjS.Sig are implicated in immune regulation and metabolism; the precise functional roles of certain genes (e.g., HCLS1, UQCR10) in SjS pathogenesis remain unclear and require further mechanistic investigation. Third, the model is primarily based on peripheral blood signatures; future studies should examine its correlation with tissue‐specific immune pathology in salivary glands and assess its utility in monitoring disease activity or treatment response.

In summary, this study presents the first scRNA‐seq‐based predictive model for SjS diagnosis, integrating single‐cell molecular signatures with machine‐learning approaches. Given the accessibility of peripheral blood testing, SjS.Sig offers a practical and robust tool for clinical applications, providing both diagnostic accuracy and valuable insights into disease pathogenesis.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, through the re‐analysis of scRNA‐seq data from PBMCs of SjS patients, Naïve CD8 + T cells, Helios FOXP3 lo CD4 + Treg cells, TRDC γδ T cells, and CTLA‐4 + CD8 + inhibitory T cells were identified as key differentiators between SjS patients and HCs. Additionally, this study introduces a novel prediction model (SjS.Sig) utilizing machine learning methods at the single‐cell level and validates it through a clinical cohort, providing a new tool for clinical diagnosis and prognosis of SjS.

METHODS

Study design

Single‐cell RNA‐sequencing (scRNA‐seq) data of PBMCs from SjS patients and HCs from the original study (accession number GEO: GSE157278) were downloaded and reanalyzed [10]. For training, six transcriptome‐level gene expression datasets (accession numbers: GSE208260, GSE159574, GSE127952, GSE97614, GSE40611, and GSE7451) were also downloaded [25, 26, 27, 28, 29]. Additionally, two transcriptome‐level datasets (accession numbers: GSE84844 and GSE143153) were utilized for validation in this study [31, 37]. To assess model specificity, two datasets related to RA were included (accession numbers: GSE153015 and GSE224842). Details of the platform, number of HCs and patients, and data categories for each dataset are provided in Table 1 and Table S16.

Single‐cell RNA sequencing data analysis

Data quantification, quality control, and integration

The scRNA‐seq data from previous SjS studies were reanalyzed. Specifically, Cell Ranger (version 7.0) from 10x Genomics (https://support.10xgenomics.com/single-cell-geneexpression/software/overview/welcome) was used to map and quantify the FASTQ data against the GRCh38 Human reference genome [10]. The “Seurat” (v4.3.0) R package was then applied for quality control, filtering out cells with detected genes fewer than 300 or greater than 7000, a total gene expression exceeding 100,000, mitochondrial content greater than 10%, and red blood cell content greater than 3% [38].

For normalization, Seurat's “NormalizeData()” function was used to normalize gene expression for each cell by scaling the data based on the total expression per cell. This step ensures gene expression levels are comparable across cells, reducing the influence of sequencing depth. Data were then scaled using Seurat's “ScaleData()” function, which centers each gene expression value by subtracting the mean and scaling by the standard deviation, preventing genes with larger variances from disproportionately affecting the analysis.

Next, Seurat's “Harmony” algorithm was used for data integration, correcting for batch effects between samples. Harmony aligns shared biological signals across datasets while removing technical variation, operating in the principal component analysis (PCA) space and adjusting principal components (PCs) to minimize batch‐related differences while preserving biological variance. After batch correction, dimensionality reduction was performed using PCA to project the data into a lower dimensional space. Subsequently, t‐distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t‐SNE) and Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) were applied for 2D visualization.

Selection of a suitable resolution

The “clustree” R package was used to visualize cluster interactions across different resolutions (Figure S1A,B) and determine the optimal resolution for downstream analysis [11]. As shown in the figure, larger clusters (e.g., cluster 0) did not subdivide when the resolution exceeded 0.8, while smaller clusters (e.g., clusters 8 and 12) became increasingly intertwined, suggesting potential over‐clustering. Therefore, a resolution of 0.8 was selected for the preliminary grouping in subsequent analyses.

Cell annotation from different techniques

For each cell subset identified through clustering, cell annotations were first performed using the “SingleR” package with the preloaded Human Primary Cell Atlas (HPCA) dataset (Figure S1C) [39]. The HPCA dataset, derived from the GEO repository, consists of 713 microarray samples classified into 38 major cell types [40]. The “SingleR” algorithm calculated the correlation between each cell in our dataset and the 713 reference samples, assigning the cell type label with the highest average correlation to each individual cell.

In addition, precise cell annotations were further refined using marker genes from the “CellMarker” database [12] and verified markers from the literature [22]. The “CellMarker” database includes 13,605 cell markers for 467 cell types across 158 human tissues and sub‐tissues. Sun et al. [22] classified CD4+ and CD8+ T cells into more specific subgroups based on their functional characteristics.

Major cell type annotation: Density map

The “Nebulosa” R package [41], which utilizes kernel density estimation, was employed for advanced cell feature analysis. By integrating similarities between cells, Nebulosa enables the recovery of signals from gene deletions and facilitates the identification of double‐positive cell populations for specific genes. This function, in contrast to “Seurat,” provides enhanced visualization and analysis of cell populations based on gene expression patterns.

Identification of differentially expressed genes

To identify specific highly expressed genes within each cluster, the “FindAllMarkers” function from Seurat was used. Genes showing a minimum log2‐fold change (logfc. threshold) > 0.25 in mean expression relative to all other clusters, and with adjusted p‐values < 0.05, were considered differentially expressed. p‐values were adjusted using Bonferroni correction. The top 10 most significantly expressed genes per cluster were selected for heatmap visualization (Figure S1D). Similarly, differential gene expression between SjS patients and HCs was analyzed using the “FindMarkers” function. Genes with a logfc. threshold > 0.25, pct.1 ‐ pct.2 > 0.1, and pct.2 < 0.5 were classified as highly expressed in each cluster. Differential gene expression was then displayed using a cross‐volcano map created with the “ggplot2” R package, omitting nonsignificant p‐values to emphasize the most biologically relevant findings.

There were two primary objectives for differential expression analysis in this study:

1)Defining Cell Subpopulations: The “FindAllMarkers” function was first used to identify genes that differentiate each cluster from others. These genes served as annotation markers to define cellular identities and subtypes (e.g., distinguishing subsets within γδ T cells). These markers are critical for consistent cell identification, but they are not necessarily disease‐specific, as their expression can vary across different physiological and pathological contexts.

Single‐cell GSS

Single‐cell GSS is commonly employed in single‐cell RNA‐seq data analysis to capture cell‐type‐specific variability and heterogeneity by leveraging functional gene sets. In this study, single‐cell gene scoring was performed using the R package “AUCell” [42].

Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis

Functional (GO terms) analysis of the identified subgroups was conducted using the “compareCluster” function from the “clusterProfiler” R package [43], to determine significantly enriched molecular functions (MF), cellular components (CC), and biological processes (BP). Initially, gene IDs were converted from SYMBOL to ENTREZID using the “bitr“ function in the "org.Hs.eg.db”R package [44]. The subsequent functional analysis was applied to multiple gene sets simultaneously with the “compareCluster” function. GO terms with p‐values < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched for the respective gene sets (Figure 3F and Figures S3E and S4E). Network plots linking genes to GO terms are shown in Figure S6B,D,E.

Risk assessment model construction and evaluation

To minimize the risk of overfitting and information leakage, all feature selection procedures were strictly confined to the derivation cohort. Candidate genes were selected based on three criteria: (1) differentially expressed genes with higher expression in SjS patients within the four significantly altered clusters, (2) cluster‐specific signature genes, and (3) genes involved in pathways enriched in SjS. These procedures were exclusively applied to the training datasets.

Variables derived from the training cohort were then entered into a Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression model [45], using the “glmnet” R package (https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=glmnet). Model tuning was conducted via 10‐fold cross‐validation (cv.glmnet function) within the training data, and the optimal regularization parameter was determined by selecting the lambda.min value corresponding to the minimum mean squared error.

Six publicly available gene expression datasets (GSE208260, GSE159574, GSE127952, GSE97614, GSE40611, and GSE7451) were used for independent validation. These datasets, generated on different platforms (RNA‐seq and microarrays), were analyzed separately to avoid cross‐platform bias, ensuring that confounding factors from batch correction methods were not introduced. No additional feature selection was performed on the subsequent validation datasets.

For model evaluation, risk scores were calculated for each dataset using GSVA [46]. GSVA scores were computed sample‐wise based on the gene sets defined in the training cohort, maintaining the independence between training and validation datasets.

To assess the robustness and generalizability of the identified gene clusters, RF, an ensemble learning algorithm based on multiple decision trees, was employed as an additional validation approach. RF classifiers were trained for each dataset using the candidate gene set derived from the LASSO model to differentiate between SjS patients and HCs. Models were constructed with 500 trees (ntree = 500), and the number of variables sampled at each split was set to the square root of the total input gene count. A fixed random seed (set.seed(123)) ensured reproducibility. Variable importance was evaluated using the MDA metric, which measures the reduction in classification accuracy when a specific variable is permuted. Genes with an MDA value exceeding a predefined threshold were considered key contributors to disease classification. RF analyses were conducted independently across six datasets, and genes consistently identified as significant across datasets or cell populations were integrated to define a robust candidate gene set. Model performance was assessed using standard classification metrics and validated in independent external datasets.

Establishment of PPI network and identification of hub‐genes

The PPI network was constructed using the STRING database (http://string-db.org; version 11.5), from which all PPI data were downloaded [47]. The complete protein network data and protein annotation information were retrieved, and the SjS‐related protein interactions were extracted and visualized using Cytoscape 3.9.1. The MCODE clustering algorithm [48] was applied to identify densely connected regions in the PPI network, allowing for the extraction of highly interacting hub genes.

Verification of SjS.Sig

To evaluate the classification performance of SjS. Sig as a diagnostic tool for SjS, the R package “cancerclass” [49] was employed for validation and testing. The cancerclass protocol begins with feature selection, followed by nearest centroid classification and predictor construction. A ROC curve with an area under the curve (AUC) between 0.85 and 1.0 was considered excellent, indicating robust predictive performance [50]. The Wilson method, implemented in the cancerclass R package, calculated binomial confidence intervals for sensitivity and specificity. Fisher's exact test was applied to categorical variables, and all confidence intervals were reported as two‐sided binomial 95% confidence intervals.

RNA isolation and real‐time quantitative PCR (qPCR)

Peripheral blood was collected, diluted with an equal volume of PBS, and PBMCs were isolated using Ficoll‐Hypaque solution after differential centrifugation. RNA was extracted using a Total RNA Extraction Kit (AG21022, Accurate Biotechnology). cDNA was synthesized from 500 ng of total RNA using a Reverse Transcription Premix Kit (AG11706, Accurate Biotechnology). Real‐time quantitative PCR was conducted using a SYBR Green Premix qPCR Kit (AG11701, Accurate Biotechnology) on a Bio‐Rad CFX96 (USA). Primers for real‐time quantitative PCR are listed in Table S17.

Western blot analysis

Protein expression was assessed by Western blot. PBMCs from an additional eight participants were lysed, and protein concentrations were determined using a bicinchoninic acid assay assay (G2026, Servicebio). Equal protein amounts were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PG112, Yeasen) and transferred to PVDF membranes (G605‐0.45, Servicebio). Membranes were incubated with primary antibodies against PSMB8 (14859‐1‐AP, Proteintech), HCLS1 (25003‐1‐AP, Proteintech), and GIMAP4 (11852‐1‐AP, Proteintech), followed by horseradish peroxidase‐conjugated secondary antibodies (SA00001‐2, Proteintech). Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence substrate (P0018AS, Beyotime), and band intensities were quantified and normalized to β‐Actin (GB15003, Servicebio).

FACS analysis

Isolated PBMCs were resuspended in Cell Staining Buffer (Biolegend) and incubated on ice for 15 min to block nonspecific binding. The cells were then stained with fluorescent‐labeled antibodies at room temperature for 20 min. Following staining, the cells were analyzed using a BECKMAN COULTER CytoFLEX flow cytometer, with FlowJo software used for data analysis. The antibodies employed included allophycocyanin anti‐human CD8 (1 μL per 1 × 106 cells, Biolegend, 344722), PE‐Cy7 Rat Anti‐Human CCR7 (CD197) (1 μL per 1 × 106 cells, BD Pharmingen, 557648), fluorescein isothiocyanate anti‐human CD7 (1 μL per 1 × 106 cells, Biolegend, 343104), Anti‐T cell receptor gamma delta (TCR‐γ/δ) phycoerythrin (PE) (1 μL per 1 × 106 cells, BD, 333141), CD152 (CTLA‐4) monoclonal antibody (14D3), PE (1 μL per 1 × 106 cells, Invitrogen, 12‐1529‐41), Alexa Fluor 647 anti‐mouse/rat/human FOXP3 (1 μL per 1 × 106 cells, Biolegend, 320013), and Pe‐Cyanine7 Anti‐Human CD25 (Bc96) (1 μL per 1 × 106 cells, Tonbo, 60‐0259‐T100).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using R (version 4.1.3). For comparisons of flow cytometry and qPCR data, a two‐tailed Student's t‐test was applied. A p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. For multiple comparison analyses, the Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) procedure was applied to control the false discovery rate (FDR), adjusting p‐values to reduce the likelihood of false positives. Adjusted p‐values are reported where applicable.
