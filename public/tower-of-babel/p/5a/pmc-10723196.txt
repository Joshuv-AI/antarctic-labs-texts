Malnutrition in Allergic Bronchopulmonary Aspergillosis complicating asthma
Authors: Venkateshan Madhavan, Valliappan Muthu, Inderpaul Singh Sehgal, Sahajal Dhooria, Kuruswamy Thurai Prasad, Ashutosh Nath Aggarwal, Ritesh Agarwal
Published: 2023

Dear Editor,

Allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis (ABPA) is caused by an immunological response to the fungus Aspergillus fumigatus.[1] Chronic inflammation against A. fumigatus results in structural lung damage in ABPA and may lead to a catabolic state. We have previously observed the body weight of asthmatic subjects with ABPA lower than those without ABPA.[2] However, body weight may not accurately reflect a loss in muscle mass[3] and can be preserved till late, especially among subjects on systemic glucocorticoids (e.g., ABPA) or obese individuals.[4] Therefore, other nutritional indices should be assessed. We hypothesized that ABPA is associated with a higher prevalence of malnutrition and a loss in skeletal muscle mass (SMM) than asthmatics without ABPA. Herein, we aimed to compare the body mass index (BMI), anthropometric measurements and body composition analysis (BCA) in asthmatic subjects with and without ABPA.

We conducted a prospective cross-sectional study at our centre between 25 April 2020 and 30 April 2021. The Institute Ethics Committee approved the study protocol. After written informed consent, we enrolled consecutive participants with asthma (with and without ABPA). We diagnosed asthma as per the current national guidelines.[5] ABPA was diagnosed in subjects meeting the modified International Society for Human And Animal Mycology (ISHAM)-ABPA working group criteria: (1) serum A. fumigatus–specific IgE >0.35 kUA/L and serum total IgE ≥500 IU/mL and (2) two of the following (i) A. fumigatus–specific IgG >27 mgA/L), (ii) peripheral blood eosinophil count >500 cells/µL and (iii) computed tomography (CT) findings consistent with ABPA.[6] We recorded the clinical features, demography and the presence of unintentional weight loss (in the preceding 3–6 months). Physical examination included clinical assessment and anthropometric indices (height, weight, BMI, mid-upper arm circumference [MUAC, measured midpoint between the olecranon and acromion process], waist–hip ratio [WHR] and triceps skin fold thickness). We measured patient height using a calibrated stadiometer with the subject standing (heels together, with the heels, calves, buttocks and back touching the stadiometer) and head positioned in the Frankfurt plane. Spirometry was performed as per the national guidelines, and we recorded the forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1), forced vital capacity (FVC), and the ratio of FEV1/FVC.[6]

We performed BCA in all the study participants using a body composition analyser (Bodivis, Tongfang Health Technology, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer's recommendation. The instrument uses a direct segmental multifrequency bioelectrical impedance analysis method [DSM-BIA]) based on the principle of differential rates of conduction of electricity depending upon the body composition. Initially, we entered the subject's age, sex and height into the analyser. We ensured the participants were free of metallic objects and asked them to remain with arms stretched and open at nearly 30°. The study subjects stood barefoot on the instrument by holding the hand grip firmly throughout the measurement. We noted the following parameters: (1) protein mass (kg), (2) total body water (kg), (3) SMM (kg), (4) bone mass (kg), (5) body fat mass (BFM, kg), (6) the percentage of body fat, (7) fat-free mass (FFM, kg) and (8) basal metabolic rate. The FFM index (FFMI, expressed as kg/m2) and BFM index (BFMI, kg/m2) were calculated as a ratio of FFM and BFM to height in m2, respectively. We categorized patients as underweight (<18.5 kg/m2), normal weight (18.5–22.9 kg/m2), overweight (23–24.9 kg/m2) and obese (>25 kg/m2) based on their BMI.[7]

The study's primary outcome was to compare the prevalence of undernutrition among asthmatics with and without ABPA. We used BMI <18.5 kg/m2 to diagnose undernutrition, as defined by the European Society of Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism.[4] The secondary objectives were (1) to compare other nutritional indices (FFM, FFMI, BFMI, muscle mass, triceps skin fold thickness and MUAC) between the two study groups and (2) to assess the correlation of various nutritional indices with the imaging and immunological parameters (serum total IgE, A. fumigatus–specific IgE and IgG, total eosinophil count) in ABPA subjects. We also note the proportion of subjects with low FFMI and low BFMI in both study groups. The cut-offs for FFMI and BFMI were obtained from a population-based study conducted in Sweden.[8] Descriptive data are presented as mean (standard deviation) for continuous variables and numbers (percentage) for categorical variables. We tested the differences between the two study groups using the Student t-test (continuous variables) or the Chi-square test (categorical variables). We evaluated the association between immunological parameters and nutritional indices using the Pearson correlation (correlation co-efficient r = 0–0.19 indicated weak to no correlation; 0.20–0.40, weak; 0.41–0.60, moderate; 0.61–0.80, strong; ≥0.81, very strong). A correlation heat map (correlogram) between the different variables was generated. We also performed a multivariable linear regression to identify the influence of various factors on BMI.

We included 29 treatment-naïve ABPA and 37 subjects with bronchial asthma. The age, sex distribution and duration of asthma were not different between the two groups [Table 1]. A history of unintentional weight loss was present in over one-fourth of the subjects with ABPA and was significantly more frequent than in asthmatic subjects (8.6%). Bronchiectasis and high-attenuation mucus (HAM) on CT were noted in 82.8% and 20.7% of subjects with ABPA. The body weight, BMI, MUAC, triceps skin fold thickness, body fat, percentage of body fat and WHR were significantly lower in the ABPA group than in the asthma group [Table 2]. The prevalence of undernutrition in ABPA (primary outcome) was 20.7%, significantly higher than in asthmatics (2.7%). Nearly 75% of asthmatics were overweight or obese compared to 45% of the ABPA subjects. The SMM, FFM (>4 kg lesser) and FFMI were lower in ABPA than in asthmatics, although not statistically significant [Table 2]. Low FFMI was significantly more frequent among ABPA subjects than asthmatics. We observed a statistically significant moderate correlation of a few nutritional indices (BMI, MUAC, body fat percentage and BFM) with the total eosinophil count [Figure 1a]. None of the other immunological markers had a statistically significant correlation with the nutritional indices. Triceps skin fold thickness, WHR, FFM and SMM were weakly correlated (non-significant negative correlation) with the total eosinophil count. The other immunological parameters (serum total IgE, A. fumigatus–specific IgE and A. fumigatus–specific IgG) negatively correlated with a few nutritional indices, although not statistically significant. There was no statistically significant correlation of bronchiectasis and HAM with any of the nutritional indices, including BMI [Figure 1b]. We found age, eosinophil count and ABPA independently associated with BMI after adjusting for sex and total IgE [Table 3]. In an alternate model, we replaced ABPA with the extent of bronchiectasis. Here, we found age and eosinophil count independently associated with BMI adjusted for sex, the extent of bronchiectasis and total IgE.

Chronic respiratory diseases, particularly chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and bronchiectasis, are known to be associated with malnutrition.[91011] Patients of cystic fibrosis bronchiectasis have been previously shown to have a high prevalence of undernutrition attributable to gastrointestinal involvement, severe lung disease and chronic systemic inflammation.[1213] The mechanism of malnutrition in ABPA may differ from other causes of bronchiectasis. In the former, immunological activity is profound and could explain the catabolic state, while in the latter recurrent infections may be a dominant cause. We also failed to observe an association between the extent of bronchiectasis and BMI, while age was significantly associated with the same [Table 3]. Malnutrition in ABPA-complicating asthma has not been systematically studied before. In the current cross-sectional study, we found a higher prevalence of undernutrition (21%) in ABPA than in asthmatic subjects. We also found reduced triceps skin-fold thickness, MUAC and BFM in ABPA subjects. The subjects enrolled in our study showed decreased fat and SMM.

Body weight has two major components, the BFM and the FFM. However, all three parameters (weight, BFM and FFM) are influenced by height. Hence, height-normalized measures such as BMI, FFMI and BFMI are more reliable in assessing nutritional status.[14] We found a lower BFM, body fat percentage and BFMI in subjects with ABPA than in asthma, indicating decreased fat in these individuals. On the contrary, SMM, FFM and FFMI were not significantly lower in ABPA subjects than asthmatics. Whether the fat loss is disproportionate to the muscle (or FFM) loss is not clear. Interestingly, the proportion of subjects with a low FFMI was significantly higher in ABPA than in asthma (31% vs. 8%). Due to the small sample size, we could not demonstrate a statistically significant difference in the FFM, FFMI and SMM. The current study was affected by the COVID-19 pandemic in India, and we could recruit only fewer subjects than initially planned. Also, we could not compare the prevalence of undernutrition among the various subsets of ABPA (ABPA with and without bronchiectasis and ABPA-HAM) or identify factors predicting malnutrition in ABPA. Of the various immunological parameters, we found total eosinophil count to have the maximum negative correlation with various indices of malnutrition. ABPA-HAM is associated with higher eosinophilic inflammation and may have a higher prevalence of malnutrition.

Apart from being a small single-centre study, the study has a few other limitations. The normal range of FFMI and BFMI for the Indian population was not available. We could have underestimated FFMI and BFMI as we used Western data.[8] We have not accounted for the socioeconomic status of the participants. We have not evaluated the pathophysiologic mechanisms behind malnutrition (e.g. interleukin-6 or other cytokine levels).[12] We also did not assess the therapeutic and prognostic impact of malnutrition in ABPA. Other measures to estimate sarcopenia (CT, magnetic resonance imaging and dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry [DXA]), functional assessment of muscle strength (handgrip strength) or exercise performance tests were not performed.[15] BIA or skin fold thickness may overestimate the FFM than the reference standard (DXA).[9] It may have been prudent to include a matched control group of normal subjects, given the lack of nutritional standards available for the country, and this is a limitation of the study.

In conclusion, we found a high prevalence of undernutrition in ABPA-complicating asthma. Further research is required to elucidate the pathophysiology of malnutrition in ABPA and its impact on patient outcomes.
