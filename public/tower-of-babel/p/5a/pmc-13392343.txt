Multi-Target Cardioprotective Potential of Ajuga bracteosa Phytochemicals: An Integrated Network Pharmacology and Molecular Docking Approach
Authors: Abdul Rehman, Imran Ahmad Khan, Qasim Ali Ghauri, Maliha Khalid Khan, Muhammad Usman, Muhammad Umair Saleem, Muhammad Imran Ali Khan, Ahsan Anjum, Hafiza Hamed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMyocardial infarction (MI), especially its adrenaline-induced myocardial injury, remains a leading cause of global mortality, often resulting in irreversible cardiac damage and heart failure due to stress cardiomyopathy.ObjectivesAjuga bracteosa Wall. ex Benth. (Lamiaceae). It is a medicinal plant used in the Himalayas for a long time in folk medicine for heart diseases, hypertension, and inflammatory diseases. Studies have reported the antioxidant and anti-inflammatory ability of its crude extracts. However, the active phytochemicals and their multi-target molecular mechanisms against adrenaline-induced MI remain unclear.DesignThe current study investigated the predicted cardioprotective potential of A. bracteosa, uses integrated network pharmacology, ADMET profiling, and molecular docking to investigate A. bracteosa phytochemicals.MethodThirty bioactive compounds from the whole plant were identified. Of these, eight lead phytochemicals, including neo-clerodane diterpenoids and phytoecdysteroids, were subjected to ADMET screening. They passed the screening showing desirable drug-like properties. From network pharmacology analysis, 278 overlapping targets were identified between MI-associated genes and those in the ingredient database. The top three hub proteins were AKT1 (node degree 151), GAPDH (150), and TNF (149), respectively.ResultsGO and KEGG enrichment analyses showed that these genes were primarily involved in the regulation of the apoptotic process, oxidant stress response, and the PI3K-Akt, TNF, and MAPK signaling pathways. Molecular docking (MOE 2019.01) results show that Ivain II showed the strongest binding affinity score with value of −9.42 kcal/mol for GAPDH and TNF-α through multiple hydrogen bonds, lipophilic contacts, and π-alkyl interactions.ConclusionThese findings provide the first computational mechanistic insight into the predicted multi-target cardioprotective potential of A. bracteosa and identify Ivain II, 20-Hydroxyecdysone, and Ajugalactone as promising lead compounds warranting further experimental validation.

Introduction

Myocardial infarction (MI), commonly referred to as a heart attack, occurs when an obstruction blocks the blood supply to the heart. MI could result in life-threatening consequences, like heart failure, arrhythmias, or fatality, depending on the degree of obstruction. MI is becoming an increasingly important contributor to the global burden of cardiovascular disease. According to the WHO, cardiovascular disease is the leading cause of death worldwide. 17.9 million people die every year of CVDs, accounting for 31% of all global deaths. 1 Additionally, it is expected that by 2025, almost three-fourths of the people with hypertension, a major risk factor for MI, will be living in low and middle-income countries. If hypertension levels go beyond the normal levels, it can pose major health threats, including stroke, heart attack, eye damage, and kidney disease. 2 Modern therapy for cardiovascular diseases (CVDs), like stents, blood thinners, or ACE inhibitors, can be useful for people with hypertension but carries the risk of side effects like bleeding, resistance to treatment, and failure to fully protect against future scarring and damage. 3 Consequently, researchers are undertaking a natural approach for hypertension and diseases associated with it by targeting multiple biological pathways, particularly inflammation and oxidative stress. 4

Medicinal plants have shown potential to reduce oxidative stress and inflammation and to improve vascular function. 5 Ajuga bracteosa Wall. Ex Benth. of family Lamiaceae, is indigenous to the subtropical Himalayan region of Pakistan, India, Nepal, Bhutan, and Kashmir. Kori booti or neelkanthi, a herb widely used in folk medicine for the treatment of toothache, malaria, joint pain, gout, high blood pressure, heart disease, sore throat, acne, and headache.6,7 Besides that, Ajuga bracteosa has been traditionally known for its blood-purifying, diuretic, cooling, and blood-flow-promoting properties.8,9 It may also be anti-inflammatory and antioxidant.

The studies have revealed the antioxidant effects of plant extracts. These extracts enhance the activity of protective enzymes, reduce the levels of substances measured in the brain and other tissues, and deactivate free radicals that damage tissue. Additionally, they also showed anti-inflammatory effects by inhibiting cyclooxygenase enzymes and decreasing pro-inflammatory cytokines, i.e., interleukin-6 and tumor necrosis factor-α. Research conducted on animals indicates that extracts of Ajuga bracteosa enhance vasodilation and ameliorate oxidative stress via the nitric oxide and cyclic guanosine monophosphate (cGMP) pathways. This therapy could target important pathological components of MI, such as blockage of blood flow, cell death through hypoxia, and scarring of the heart that becomes chronic. 10

Chemical analysis oftable the Ajuga bracteosa plant confirmed the presence of ajugarin II, clerodin, 14,15-dihydroajugapitin, ajugalactone, and ivain II. These neoclerodane diterpenoids 11 have powerful anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects. Among other substrates, the plant’s phytoecdysteroids, 20-hydroxyecdysone and cyasterone, may also help with proliferation. Moreover, phenolic compounds such as gallic acid, caffeic acid, vanillic acid, benzoic acid, syringic acid, and p-coumaric acid are found. Other constituents include coumarin (an anticoagulant), sterols such as β-sitosterol (which lowers cholesterol), and analgesic alkaloids. The combinations have a synergistic effect. Ajuga bracteosa has shown efficiency against diabetes, pathogens, and cancer in vitro. Epinephrine is the primary hormone that mediates the fight-or-flight response. In excessive amounts, adrenaline can overload the heart, cause spasm of blood vessels, increase calcium, and pro-oxidant and inflammatory activity. It can also harm heart muscle cells during stress, anxiety, and depression. Stress cardiomyopathy, also referred to as broken-heart syndrome, Takotsubo cardiomyopathy, or MI without coronary artery obstruction, can be triggered by these effects. Stress is induced in an animal model by injecting adrenaline to evaluate a therapeutic intervention for cardiac injury caused by stress.

Network pharmacology and molecular docking provide insights into the protective mechanism of Ajuga bracteosa against myocardial injury and help identify responsible phytoconstituents. Network pharmacology maps interactions between plant-derived compounds with disease pathways using available data on their biological targets. In other words, molecular docking helps to check the binding affinities of these compounds with the protein. AKT1, GAPDH, and TNF-α. These methods have been effectively used to clarify the mechanisms of action of other natural remedies that prevent MI by ameliorating oxidative stress and modulating cell death pathways, such as the PI3K/Akt pathway.12-14 A network pharmacology-based approach will be evaluated, targeting the commonality of Ajuga bracteosa compounds and adrenaline-induced myocardial infarction pathways. Through PPI networks, plant constituents will be linked to their common targets’ analysis. Gene Ontology (GO) will help infer essential biological processes, while Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) will help identify pathways. Molecular docking will evaluate the binding affinities of the top-ranked compounds, including diterpenoids and ecdysteroids, to target proteins involved in cell survival (AKT1), energy metabolism (GAPDH), and inflammation (TNF-α). The study will determine whether these compounds enhance antioxidant defenses, reduce inflammation, or promote nitric oxide production. This study aims to computationally identify the most promising candidate compounds that could serve as a foundation for future experimental testing in adrenaline-induced myocardial infarction models, thereby generating a scientific rationale for the cardioprotective potential of A. bracteosa.15,16 This ancient plant may be used to develop novel cardioprotective agents.

Materials and Methods

Identification of Bioactive Compounds and Literature Mining

We conducted a systematic literature review to identify bioactive compounds from A. bracteosa using Google Scholar (https://scholar.google.com, accessed on 20 February 2026) and Elicit (https://elicit.com/, accessed on 20 February 2026). Relevant scientific articles and specialized databases, including the Traditional Chinese Medicine System Pharmacology (TCMSP) database, were reviewed to compile a comprehensive library of phytochemicals reported in the aerial parts of the herb. The Canonical SMILES (Simplified Molecular Input Line Entry System) notations and PubChem Compound IDs (CIDs) for the selected compounds were retrieved from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/, accessed on 22 February 2026) to facilitate subsequent computational analyses.17-19

ADME Screening of A. bracteosa Compounds

The absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity (ADMET) characteristics of the identified constituents of Ajuga bracteosa were evaluated using the admetSAR 3.0 web server (https://lmmd.ecust.edu.cn/admetsar3/index.php; accessed on 23rd February 2026).20,21 The physicochemical characteristics of compounds, as well as drug-likeness metrics such as Human Intestinal Absorption (HIA), Caco-2 permeability, and Blood-Brain Barrier (BBB) penetration, were rigorously analyzed to identify compounds with the best oral bioavailability profiles. To streamline the prioritization of lead compounds from a pool of 30 phytoconstituents, a multi-parameter filtering pipeline was used. This pipeline applied predefined ADMET threshold values: molecular weight (MW) less than 560 Da, lipophilicity (logP) less than 3.5, Ames mutagenicity score less than 0.50, CYP3A4 inhibition probability less than 0.20, CYP2D6 inhibition probability less than 0.20, topological polar surface area (TPSA) less than 160 Å2, hydrogen bond acceptors (HBA) no more than 10, hydrogen bond donors (HBD) no more than 5, human intestinal absorption (HIA) greater than 0.30, and blood-brain barrier penetration (BBB) greater than 0.70. Any compound that failed to meet even one threshold was excluded. Ultimately, eight lead compounds that met all the criteria were selected for network pharmacology and molecular docking studies.

Target Prediction and Data Filtration

We used SwissTarget Prediction to predict the molecular targets of the screened A. bracteosa (https://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/, retrieved on 24 February 2026). 22 Predictions were filtered to retain only those with a binding probability score of ≥ 0.10 (10% confidence threshold) in the SwissTargetPrediction output for Homo sapiens. Targets falling below this threshold were considered low-confidence and excluded from further analysis. Only macromolecular targets with documented human protein associations were retained, and targets were ranked according to descending binding probability. Microsoft Excel was used for manual data curation and purification to eliminate duplicate entries and keep only targets with a high prediction probability. Protein names were then mapped to official gene symbols using the STRING database mapping (https://string-db.org, retrieved on 24 February 2026). STRING analysis was performed for Homo sapiens with a default confidence score (0.4).

Identification of MI-Associated Targets and Intersection Analysis

We extracted the Genes linked to Myocardial Infarction from the GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org/, accessed on 25 February 2026). 23 Using the Venny 2.1.0 web tool (https://bioinfogp.cnb.csic.es/tools/venny/, accessed on 25 February 2026), we identified intersecting genes between compound-predicted targets and disease-associated genes to evaluate potential therapeutic targets of A. bracteosa against MI. The overlapping targets were considered potential proteins that mediate their cardioprotective, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant actions.

Protein–Protein Interaction Network Construction and Pathway Analysis

To clarify functional relationships between proteins, a protein–protein interaction (PPI) study of the intersected targets was carried out using the STRING database (https://string-db.org/; accessed on 26th February 2026). 24 In order to find biological pathways that might be involved in the modulation of MI, KEGG pathway information relating to these targets was simultaneously retrieved from STRING.

Network Visualization and Compound–Target–Pathway Integration

To create an integrated compound–target–pathway network (C-T-P). The most recent Cytoscape 3.10.4 version (https://cytoscape.org/) was used. 25 This network provided a systems-level view of the multi-component and multi-target therapeutic mechanisms of Ajuga bracteosa against MI by enabling the visualization of interactions among A. bracteosa bioactive chemicals, intersecting target proteins, and enriched KEGG pathways.

GO and KEGG Enrichment

We used ShinyGO v0.85 for Gene Ontology (GO) functional annotation and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis to examine the biological roles and signaling pathways associated with intersected target genes identified through network pharmacology analysis.16,26 After uploading the list of overlapping targets to ShinyGO, the Homo sapiens background database was used for enrichment analysis. GO enrichment was categorized into biological processes, molecular function, and cellular components. Significantly related signaling pathways were found using KEGG pathway analysis. The integrated hypergeometric test with Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied to assess statistical significance. Enriched terms with FDR-adjusted p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Results were ranked by FDR value, and gene ratio (number of query genes in the pathway divided by total query genes) and fold enrichment (observed/expected gene ratio) were calculated for each enriched term to quantify pathway representation. ShinyGO graphical outputs were used to visualize enriched GO terms and KEGG pathways, which made it easier to interpret functional clustering and pathway involvement associated with adrenaline-induced myocardial infarction.

Molecular Docking

The three-dimensional crystal structures of the prioritized hub protein targets AKT1 (PDB: 6HHF), GAPDH (PDB: 6IQ6), and TNF-α (PDB: 1TNF) were retrieved from the RCSB Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org/) in PDB format. 27 Structural refinement and preprocessing were conducted using Molecular Operating Environment (MOE) 2019.01(https://www.chemcomp.com/en/Products.htm), which included the removal of co-crystallized water molecules, non-standard side chains, and inhibitory ligands. In order to maintain biological applicability only significant chains were kept and hydrogen atoms were provided using HBu2 to complete physiological pH requirements. The AMBER10: EHT force field was used to perform energy minimization of the protein structures to a gradient of 0.05 kcal mol-1 × Å-1. The ligands of interest, 8-O-Acetylharpagide, 14,15-Dihydroajugapitin, 20-Hydroxyecdysone, Ajugalactone, Ajugarin I, Clerodin, Cyasterone, and Ivain II were retrieved from the PubChem database. Ligands were optimized and minimized using the MMFF94x force field and their ionization states assigned at a pH of 7.4 utilizing the Protonate3D module of MOE. A rigid-receptor/flexible-ligand molecular docking simulation was conducted utilizing the standard protocol as defined in MOE 2019.01. 1. Initially, the Triangle Matcher algorithm was used to create initial poses followed by refinement and score pose using GBVI/WSA dG free-energy calculation to calculate the binding affinity to ligand-protein complex (Kcal/mol); 2. 10 poses were generated for each ligand-protein complex, and the pose with the lowest S-Score was used to find the best pose; 3. The second analysis included creating 2D and 3D interaction diagram(s) to determine the specific molecular interactions such as hydrogen bond(s), hydrophobic contact(s), and pi-system interactions. 28

To validate the geometric reliability and predictive accuracy of the Molecular Operating Environment (MOE) docking workflow, a method-validation strategy was conducted using native ligand redocking experiments.29-31 The co-crystallized native ligands were extracted from the active binding sites of the target protein structures and computationally redocked back into their designated biological coordinate binding cavities using identical optimization parameters, force field choices, and grid configurations. The Root-Mean-Square Deviation (RMSD) between the native co-crystallized conformation and the calculated regenerated docking pose was mathematically determined to evaluate structural matching, with a stringent validity threshold of less than 2.0 Å. It is worth mentioning that only AKT1 (PDB ID: 6HHF) and GAPDH (PDB ID: 6IQ6) undergo protocol validation through redocking. The redocking validation protocol wouldn’t be performed for TNF-α (PDB ID: 1TNF) because there’s no defined native co-crystallized ligand in its reference repository file.

Comparative Docking With Standard Reference Drug

To evaluate the therapeutic and binding potential of prioritized lead phytochemicals from Ajuga bracteosa, a comparative molecular docking simulation was performed using Losartan (PubChem CID: 3961), a reference drug with established cardioprotective and anti-inflammatory effects in the clinic. The PubChem database was used for the three-dimensional structure of Losartan, which was further minimized and docked against the functional binding pockets of three key hub proteins: AKT1 (PDB ID: 6HHF), GAPDH (PDB ID: 6IQ6), and TNF-α (PDB ID: 1TNF) under the same grid, force fields, and scoring configuration used for the plant-derived compounds.

We use the admetSAR 3.0 platform for the determination of pharmacokinetic characteristics of the lead compounds from A. bracteosa. The majority of the lead compounds had good physicochemical properties like lipophilicity (SlogP values from 1.8 to 3.4) and high predicted HIA. In particular, the neo-clerodane diterpenoids Ajugarin I, Clerodin, and Ivain II had excellent HIA (90%) and high Caco-2 permeability, making them particularly well suited for oral administration.

Each of the compounds also meets the required measures of drug-likeness, including having a molecular weight in the 434 to 522 Da range and a polar surface area that is acceptable, and therefore all the A. bracteosa compounds are likely to have good pharmacological characteristics for use as multi-targeted cardioprotective therapies. Target Prediction and Compound Filtering

The ADMET-based pharmacokinetic screening removed phytochemicals with low-target-prediction probabilities. The study aimed to examine eight lead phytochemicals of Ajuga bracteosa: 8-O-acetylharpagide, 14,15-dihydroajugapitin, 20-hydroxyecdysone, ajugalactone, ajugarin I, clerodin, cyasterone, and ivain II; all of which were forecasted for macromolecular targets using SwissTargetPrediction. Only targets meeting a minimum binding probability threshold of ≥ 0.10 (10% confidence) for Homo sapiens macromolecular interactions were retained after SwissTargetPrediction analysis. Targets falling below this confidence threshold were systematically excluded to ensure only biologically plausible compound-target associations were carried forward into the network pharmacology pipeline. Target name redundancies were manually corrected using Microsoft Excel. Final target names were standardized against official Gene Symbols by mapping through the STRING database.

A detailed examination of the eight prioritized leads provided a broad spectrum of target proteins relevant to cardiovascular function. The identified targets can be classified into three functional classes: kinases, proteases, and G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs), with each target exhibiting multiple binding profiles. For example, neo-clerodane diterpenoids (such as Ivain II and Ajugarin I), as well as phytoecdysteroids (such as 20-Hydroxyecdysone), were predicted to interact with important core regulators of survival signaling and metabolic activity (e.g., AKT1, GAPDH, and TNF). The standardized target library provided crucial biological data needed for the next step of comparing this list to a set of disease-related genes.

Molecular Docking

We conducted a molecular docking analysis using MOE 2019 to validate the binding interaction of the major bioactive compounds of Ajuga bracteosa with the hub proteins identified from our network pharmacology analyses AKT1, GAPDH, and TNF-α. These three hub proteins were selected as they all display high node degree and are centrally regulatory of multiple signaling pathways to promote cellular survival through the PI3K/Akt signaling axis, maintenance of energy through the oxidative stress response signaling pathway, regulation of systemic inflammation following acute myocardial infarction.

Prior to evaluating the binding interactions of the prioritized lead phytocompounds from Ajuga bracteosa, a verification phase was completed using native ligand redocking experiments to establish baseline computational accuracy. The native ligands were successfully extracted and structurally redocked back into the catalytic binding pockets of their respective hub proteins. Alignment of the conformations of the original experimental crystals with the computationally generated docking poses yielded excellent RMSD values of 0.3859 Å for AKT1 (PDB ID: 6HHF) and 0.3416 Å for GAPDH (PDB ID: 6IQ6), as calculated by computational overlay analysis. Because both calculated measurements fall significantly below the universally established structural threshold of 2.0 Å, the computational environment, scoring functions, and force field parameters are validated as highly accurate and reliable for virtual screening.

It is critical to note that native ligand redocking was not performed for the third hub protein, TNF-α (PDB ID: 1TNF). Because the reference crystal structure for 1TNF does not contain a co-crystallized native ligand pocket occupant, a self-redocking validation experiment could not be mathematically conducted for this specific target. However, the high geometric reliability confirmed by the low RMSD values of the AKT1 and GAPDH complexes systematically establishes the overall performance and alignment reliability of the identical docking settings utilized across the entire study database.

Comparative Binding Affinity Analysis With Standard Drug

To verify the structural potency of the prioritized Ajuga bracteosa bioactives, their binding scores were systematically compared against the reference clinical agent, Losartan. The standard drug exhibited moderate docking affinity across the hub targets, yielding energy scores of −7.3 kcal/mol for AKT1, −7.0 kcal/mol for GAPDH, and −7.1 kcal/mol for TNF-α. Remarkably, the candidate phytochemicals from Ajuga bracteosa demonstrated significantly superior binding profiles compared to the standard reference drug. Specifically, Ivain II (−9.42 kcal/mol), 20-Hydroxyecdysone (−9.16 kcal/mol), and Ajugalactone (−9.10 kcal/mol) formed much more stable low-energy complexes within the active domain of AKT1 than Losartan (−7.3 kcal/mol). Similarly, Ajugarin I demonstrated superior structural affinity towards GAPDH (−9.0 kcal/mol) and TNF-α (−8.9 kcal/mol) compared to the standard drug values of −7.0 kcal/mol and −7.1 kcal/mol, respectively. These insights highlight that the core secondary metabolites of Ajuga bracteosa adapt exceptionally well within the catalytic pockets of these key cardiac targets, surpassing the binding threshold of an established standard medication.

Discussion

The goal of this research was to evaluate how A. bracteosa substances might help prevent heart attacks that occur when there is an inadequate blood supply to the heart due to an excessive amount of adrenaline. 32 Four methods were used here: network pharmacology 33 ; predicting pharmacokinetics (using modeling software) to estimate how the herb would likely be absorbed, distributed, metabolized, and excreted; assessing potential toxicity 34 ; and finally performing molecular docking studies on the herb’s active components. 35 Cardiovascular diseases represent one of the most severe forms of heart disease resulting from a heart attack. Unfortunately, because of the many adverse side effects associated with current treatment options, like the risk of bleeding, drugs not functioning as they are intended to, as well as the inability to completely protect the heart, many people are beginning to explore natural methods that will provide benefit on multiple levels, like A. bracteosa.

A. bracteosa is an important traditional medicinal plant from the Himalayas that consists of a range of phytochemicals. 36 We hypothesized that these phytochemicals would collectively exert beneficial effects on the major mechanisms underlying adrenaline-induced heart damage. They include oxidative stress, systemic inflammation, and the ability of heart cells to survive. We used AdmetSAR 3.0 to generate pharmacokinetic profiles of the compounds being screened for reasonable drug-like properties, 21 with the aim of identifying those suitable for further testing at our institutions. The literature review identified thirty compounds with eight leading constituents like 8-O-Acetylharpagide, 14,15-Dihydroajugapitin, 20-Hydroxyecdysone, Ajugalactone, Ajugarin I, Clerodin, Cyasterone, and Ivain II, being classified due to their favorable ADMET profile. 37

To eliminate selection bias and systematically screen the initial database of thirty identified Ajuga bracteosa phytochemicals, strict drug-likeness filters and inclusion/exclusion criteria were established based on definitive ADMET cut-offs. 38 Compounds were prioritized for downstream multi-target network simulations only if they safely fulfilled the following pharmacokinetic thresholds: Molecular Weight (MW) < 560 Da, Lipophilicity (logP) score < 3.5, Hydrogen Bond Donors (HBD) ≤5, Hydrogen Bond Acceptors (HBA) ≤10, and a Topological Polar Surface Area (TPSA) ≤160 A ˚ 2. Furthermore, an exclusion filter was applied to eliminate compounds exhibiting high probability scores for mutagenicity (Ames toxicity < 0.50) or severe drug-induced liver injury (DILI) indicators. Out of the initial thirty constituents, twenty-two compounds were systematically excluded due to poor intestinal permeability metrics, non-compliance with drug-likeness rules, or high predicted hepatotoxicity risks. Consequently, eight core lead candidates consisting of highly compliant neo-clerodane diterpenoids and phytoecdysteroids successfully satisfied all rigorous ADMET baseline cut-offs and were uniquely carried forward into the network pharmacology pathway alignment.

It should be noted that compounds such as Quercetin, Kaempferol, and other phenolic acids have well-established antioxidant and cardioprotective activities in the literature, but were excluded from the lead pool due to severe violations in their pharmacokinetic or mutagenicity profiles that would render them suicidal or non-therapeutic as drug candidates. Thus, justifying bioactivity cut-offs and ADMET-based filtering systematically.

Clerodin (94.7% HIA), Ajugarin I (92.9% HIA) and Ivain II (91.1% HIA) possess very high oral bioavailability and also possess some of the most favourable permeability characteristics as determined by Caco-2 data (permeability). Ivain II has the highest SlogP value (3.41) but also has a much higher pharmacokinetic balance of 90% HIA, making Ivain II the compound with the best pharmacokinetic properties of these three candidates. With respect to safety, Ajugalactone has the lowest DILI risk score of the compounds tested with a score of 0.091 indicating that it has potential for a good liver safety profile.

Nevertheless, 8-O-Acetylharpagide has borderline intestinal absorption (30% HIA), This may prevent 8-O-Acetylharpagide from being utilized as a standalone oral agent. However, it does not eliminate the potential of utilizing 8-O-Acetylharpagide in combination with the herb’s topical application or other compounds that synergistically contribute to polypharmacological activity. Collectively, the available ADMET parameters support that selected A. bracteosa compounds have physiochemical properties that satisfy the established thresholds necessary for selecting potential drug candidates for cardioprotection across multiple doses including M.W. of 434 – 522 Da, sufficient lipophilicity, and acceptable toxicity.

The analysis of network pharmacology provides an understanding of how plant-derived compounds alter the molecular network involved with myocardial infarction. 39 By combining predicted targets of compounds with MI-related genes from GeneCards, 278 common targets were identified that include key hub proteins (AKT1, GAPDH, TNF, IL6, IL1B, STAT3). 40 The presence of many bioactive components converging on these common targets suggests that there is a complex, synergistic, multi-target mechanism, rather than a simple one compound to one target approach. 41 This finding has particular implications in the case of adrenaline-induced myocardial infarction, as MI involves activation of multiple adrenergic receptors, calcium overload, oxidative stress, release of inflammatory cytokines and apoptosis of cardiomyocytes, a complex cascade that cannot be effectively treated with single-target therapies. 42

Using node degree analysis within the compound-target-pathway network to identify nodes which have a high degree helped identify the best candidates for molecular docking; while all 4 are highly connected nodes within the C-T-P network with AKT1, GAPDH, TNF and IL6 being their individual node degrees of 151, 150, 149 and 149, respectively. AKT1, GAPDH, TNF and IL6 are also of interest based on their centrality ranking to major pathways associated with MI. AKT1 has been shown to be an important regulator of cardiomyocyte cell survival through its association with the PI3K/Akt pathway. GAPDH is involved in the overall energy metabolic processes of the heart and is a contributor to the cellular damage associated with ischemia; furthermore, GAPDH has been previously shown to be a contributor to the damage associated with oxidative stress. TNF is the major contributor of pro-inflammatory cytokines and acts to contribute to the systemic inflammatory response following an injury to the heart.43-46

There must be some specific mechanistic justification for suggesting GAPDH as a hub target. GAPDH is predominantly a glycolytic housekeeping enzyme; however, increasing evidence implicates it in an important non-canonical role in cardiovascular disease.47-49 During oxidative stress, as occurs with adrenaline-induced MI, GAPDH (glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase) is S-nitrosylated and translocated to the nucleus, where it activates apoptotic pathways in cardiomyocytes.50,51 This occurs independently of its metabolic function. Moreover, GAPDH-dependent glycolytic flux induced by ischemia-reperfusion injury leads to ATP depletion and mitochondrial dysfunction in the heart. The calcium overload induced by adrenaline activates calpains, which degrade GAPDH and promote cardiomyocyte death.52,53 Ivain II Docking Affinity for GAPDH (−9.42 kcal/mol) Reveals Stable Function and Attenuation of Oxidative Stress-Mediated Apoptosis in Ischemic Myocardium Facilitated by Preservation of Glycolytic Energy Production Event on A Cell. Likewise, AKT1 is a recognized druggable cardiovascular target. The PI3K/AKT1 signaling pathway regulates cardiomyocyte survival and inhibits mitochondrial permeability transition pore opening and caspase-mediated apoptosis in response to ischemia. TNF-α, another hub target, induces systemic inflammatory amplification following cardiac stress induced by adrenaline. This occurs through NF-κB activation, upregulation of adhesion molecules, and promotion of apoptosis in cardiomyocytes, all of which are potentially modifiable by identified phytochemicals.

The Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of the 278 intersected targets supported these results. The biological processes were mainly significantly enriched in apoptotic process regulation, oxidative stress response, and positive regulation of inflammatory response. A strong enrichment was observed in protein kinase activity, cytokine binding, and oxidoreductase activity. The KEGG pathway analysis results also predicted considerable enrichment of PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, TNF signaling pathway, MAPK signaling pathway, apoptosis, NF-κB signaling, and oxidative phosphorylation. The high binding affinities of the hub proteins (AKT1, GAPDH, and TNF-alpha) correspond to these enriched terms and pathways. This clearly establishes that the Ajuga bracteosa phytochemicals are well-positioned to enhance cardiomyocyte survival and inhibit oxidative damage and inflammatory pathways. The evaluation results, taken together, provide strong and reliable biological evidence for the plant’s multi-target cardioprotective potential.

These enriched pathways are well-associated with experimental data on A. bracteosa. Earlier in vivo research has shown that A. The bracteosa extracts exert cardioprotective effects by improving nitric oxide/cGMP signaling, attenuating oxidative stress, and reducing pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6 and TNF-α). In animal models of hypertension and vascular dysfunction, bracteosa extracts show cardioprotective effects. Our finding that TNF and AKT1 are top hubs and strongly predicted binding of Ivain II and 20-Hydroxyecdysone to these targets provides a mechanistic explanation for these anti-inflammatory and pro-survival effects. In addition, the strong enrichment in oxidative stress response and apoptotic regulation is consistent with the plant’s antioxidant activities.

The molecular docking analysis reveals strong binding affinity of the selected compounds with the chosen receptors. According to the results of this work, the lead ligand is Ivain II with an S-score value of -9.42 kcal/mol. The binding affinities of both 20-Hydroxyecdysone and Ajugalactone were obtained in the AKT1 complex, with maximum binding affinity of 20-Hydroxyecdysone at –9.16 kcal/mol and that of Ajugalactone at –9.10 kcal/mol. Both ligands likely play a crucial role in determining the plant’s cardioprotective and anti-apoptotic effects. Ajugarin I also exhibited good docking and binding affinities for GAPDH (-9.0 kcal/mol) and TNF-α (-8.9 kcal/mol).

The evaluation of the active sites of the target proteins showed that many ligands interact with the targets through various bond types. The interactions were found to primarily involve hydrogen bonds, however, there were many hydrophobic, van der Waals, and π-alkyl/π-cation interactions generated between the ligands and the active pockets of their target proteins. Hydrogen bonds were formed between AKT1 kinase active pocket residues Thr82, Asp292, and Asn279 and GAPDH active pocket residues Asn287, Ser283, Thr182, and Asp205, confirming structural stability and affinity of each complex. H-bonds with other matching residues (Ser60, Tyr119, Gln149, Leu120 at the TNF-α interface) further stabilize the interaction with Ivain II and TNF-α.

The results suggest that the identified phytochemicals may have a structural basis for modulating stress-induced protein dysfunction and inflammatory signaling in cardiac tissues. In addition, the docking outcomes offer further corroboration of the network pharmacology studies. The computational analysis indicates that A. bracteosa phytochemicals interact with multiple targets associated with adrenaline-induced myocardial infarction. Based on the current computational evidence, A. bracteosa phytochemicals may represent promising candidates for facilitating cardioprotection and attenuation of inflammation, pending rigorous in vitro and in vivo experimental validation.

Limitations

Though the computational results of this study appear promising, it still has limitations. The study is entirely in silico, and all results (like target predictions, network interactions, enrichment analyses, and molecular docking scores) are computational predictions that are not experimentally verified. The probabilistic target associations generated by SwissTargetPrediction may not reflect the in vivo binding behavior. This sentence says that analysis using network pharmacology can identify mechanistic associations but cannot establish causality. Molecular docking scores reflect thermodynamic binding affinity but do not account for protein dynamics. The results of this research should be seen as generating hypotheses, not definitive conclusions. Future studies should focus on cell-based validation using cardiomyocyte injury models, enzyme inhibition assays of AKT1, GAPDH, and TNF-α, molecular dynamics, and binding free energy calculations for docking stability confirmation and in vivo validation using well-established adrenaline-induced myocardial infarction animal models.

Conclusion

This research provides the first insight into the potential cardioprotective effects of Ajuga bracteosa against adrenaline-induced myocardial infarction via network pharmacology, ADMET profiling, and molecular docking. Eight lead phytochemicals were prioritized based on their favorable ADMET properties. Three key hubs were identified through network analysis, yielding 278 overlapping targets. The analysis showed that the overlapping targets included AKT1, GAPDH, and TNF. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses revealed involvement in apoptotic regulation, oxidative stress response, and PI3K-Akt, TNF, and MAPK pathways. The lead compounds showed strong binding affinities to these hubs as revealed by molecular docking with Ivain II showing the highest score of −9.42 kcal/mol. These findings offer a mechanistic basis for the traditional use of A. bracteosa in cardiovascular ailments and identify Ivain II, 20-Hydroxyecdysone, and Ajugalactone as promising candidates for further investigation. As this study is entirely computational. Future experimental studies, including in vitro cardiomyocyte models and in vivo validation of adrenaline-induced MI, are essential to confirm these predictions.
