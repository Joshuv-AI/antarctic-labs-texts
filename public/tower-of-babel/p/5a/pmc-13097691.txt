MRI‐based qualitative, quantitative, and radiomics/deep learning methods for assessing treatment response after neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy in patients with locally advanced rectal cancer
Authors: Shuang Chen, Lin Liu, Guangwei Tian, Ruimei Chai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (nCRT) followed by total mesorectal excision is standard treatment for locally advanced rectal cancer (LARC). This approach allows a subset of patients to achieve a pathological complete response (pCR), thereby improving surgical outcomes, anal preservation rates, and disease‐free survival. An accurate preoperative assessment of pCR is crucial for guiding treatment decisions. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), owing to its superior soft‐tissue contrast and spatial resolution, has become the preferred noninvasive modality for assessing nCRT efficacy. Advances in functional MRI (fMRI) techniques include diffusion‐weighted imaging, derived sequences, perfusion‐weighted imaging, and neuro‐fMRI. fMRI sequences provide not only a qualitative assessment but also quantitative parameters derived from various imaging principles, thereby significantly enhancing the clinical utility of MRI. Beyond conventional and functional MRI, this field is rapidly evolving with the integration of radiomics and deep learning approaches. Radiomics involves the high‐throughput extraction of minimal quantitative features from medical images, which can reveal tumor heterogeneity and phenotypic characteristics that are invisible to the human eye. This review summarizes current research and future perspectives on MRI‐based qualitative, quantitative, radiomic, and deep‐learning approaches for assessing nCRT efficacy in patients with LARC.Keywords: deep learning, diffusion‐weighted imaging, locally advanced rectal cancer, magnetic resonance imaging, neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy, perfusion‐weighted imaging, Radiomics

INTRODUCTION

The incidence and mortality of colorectal cancer rank third in the world. 1 Furthermore, the incidence of rectal cancer is increasing, accounting for 38% of the total incidence rate of colorectal cancer. 2

Neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (nCRT) followed by total mesorectal excision (TME) is the standard treatment for locally advanced rectal cancer (LARC). 3 TME improves sphincter preservation, increases R0 resection rates, and reduces local recurrence. Clinical studies indicate that 15%–27% of patients undergoing nCRT achieve a pathological complete response (pCR), characterized by no viable tumor cells and only residual fibrosis, whereas limited survival benefit from radical surgery is gained and many face complications. 4 , 5 , 6 TME following nCRT for rectal cancer is associated with substantial morbidity, including urinary dysfunction (15%–30%), bladder emptying disorders (20%–40%), sexual dysfunction (30%–55% male erectile dysfunction, 25%–40% female orgasmic dysfunction), 5 , 6 and low anterior resection syndrome (60%–80% overall, 30%–45% moderate to severe). 4 , 7 These complications often represent irreversible long‐term impairments that significantly compromise quality of life. 8 , 9 Alternatively, a watch‐and‐wait strategy may substantially reduce such morbidity. Regarding efficacy evaluation, postoperative pathology, including tumor regression grade (TRG) and ypT stage, remains the gold standard for assessing nCRT response. The assessment of tumor regression following nCRT employs three primary TRG systems: the Mandard, American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC), and Dworak. These systems demonstrate critical distinctions in defining pCR. The Mandard system (5‐tier) designates TRG1 as a pCR, defined by the complete absence of viable tumor cells with only fibrotic tissue or inflammatory changes remaining, with high sensitivity to minimal residual disease. The AJCC system (4‐tier) defines TRG0 as pCR, requiring both the absence of primary tumor (ypT0) and negative lymph node status (ypN0), establishing the most stringent criteria through the mandatory incorporation of nodal evaluation. The Dworak system (5‐tier) identifies TRG4 as a pCR, manifested by complete tumor eradication with fibrotic replacement and potential mucosal regeneration. Given its comprehensive nature, the AJCC system has been adopted in previous studies to interpret therapeutic responses and compare research data. Although the TRG systems based on postoperative specimens represent the gold standard for defining pCR, this method has an inherent lag that precludes its utility in real‐time preoperative clinical decision‐making. Consequently, the development of a robust assessment framework capable of accurately identifying patients who have achieved a clinical complete response (cCR) after treatment completion and who may thus be candidates for non‐operative management has become a central focus of clinical research. In this context, multimodal clinical assessment strategies have emerged to define a cCR. Cerdan‐Santacruz et al. 10 suggested cCR be determined through multidisciplinary assessment integrating clinical (digital rectal examination showing smooth rectal wall with only mild induration and no mass/ulcer/stenosis for distal tumors within 7 cm from anal verge), endoscopic (presence of white scar without ulceration/nodularity/stenosis and no abnormal vessels on narrow‐band imaging, where positive biopsy excludes cCR), and imaging modalities (magnetic resonance imaging [MRI] with T2‐weighted imaging [T2WI] demonstrating mrTRG 1–2 and no high signal on diffusion‐weighted imaging [DWI] in the tumor bed, whereby dynamic contrast‐enhanced MRI [DCE‐MRI] and PET‐CT serve supplementary roles), all compared with baseline findings, which combine mrTRG 1–2 and negative DWI to achieve 85%–90% concordance with pCR.

MRI, a noninvasive imaging modality, enables early and serial assessment of the nCRT response in patients with LARC owing to its superior soft tissue contrast and spatial resolution. However, conventional MRI has limited diagnostic accuracy because: (1) T2WI does not reliably distinguish residual tumors from treatment‐induced fibrosis or edema, 11 and (2) DWI relies on Gaussian diffusion assumptions, which inadequately represent restricted water motion in biological tissues. 12 Recent advances in functional MRI (fMRI) techniques, including advanced DWI sequences (e.g., intravoxel incoherent motion [IVIM], diffusion kurtosis imaging [DKI], stretched exponential modeling [SEM]), and perfusion‐weighted imaging (PWI) (e.g., DCE‐MRI), are increasingly applied to evaluate nCRT responses in LARC. These techniques provide morphological information and quantitative functional parameters that reflect the diffusion patterns of water molecules and microcirculatory changes. This multimodal approach offers profound insights into the tumor microenvironment. A systematic literature search was conducted in PubMed and Web of Science databases for studies on the diagnostic accuracy of MRI in predicting pCR in patients with LARC after nCRT, covering research up to February 2025.

This review aimed to summarize the current evidence on MRI‐based assessment of nCRT response in LARC and discuss future research directions.

QUALITATIVE MRI ASSESSMENT OF TUMOR RESPONSE

In current clinical practice, conventional MRI sequences, particularly high‐resolution T2WI 13 and DWI 14 , 15 , 16 , remain indispensable for evaluating tumor response following nCRT in patients with LARC. T2WI provides exceptional soft‐tissue contrast, enabling detailed visualization of the rectal wall anatomy and clear demarcation of the tumor‐normal tissue boundaries. In contrast, DWI detects restricted diffusion resulting from altered cellular density within tumor tissues by exploiting the motion of molecules. These complementary sequences characterize residual tumors by delineating key features including tumor size, morphology, location, and signal intensity. Consequently, they represent essential tools for assessing response to nCRT and guiding clinical decision‐making.

Volume changes

Tumor volume reduction is critical when evaluating the response to nCRT. Tumor regression after nCRT may facilitate organ‐preserving surgical approaches and enhance sphincter preservation. Although tumor volume delineation is straightforward, the current standard is manual slice‐by‐slice segmentation of pretreatment oblique axial T2‐weighted MR images (Figure 1). Zhong et al. 17 identified tumor volume as a significant predictor of pCR, reporting a threshold of 21.1 cm3 with 84% sensitivity and 47% specificity for predicting pCR. Yang et al. 18 demonstrated a strong correlation between the baseline tumor volume and pCR rates in patients with LARC undergoing nCRT. Their analysis established 37.3 cm3 as a critical threshold. Notably, >78% of patients with tumor volumes >37.3 cm3 failed to achieve pCR, indicating that larger baseline tumor volumes adversely affected the nCRT response and long‐term outcomes. Despite interstudy‐variations in optimal volume thresholds, the current evidence consistently supports tumor volume as a reliable predictor of nCRT response, potentially guiding the selection of candidates for watch‐and‐wait strategies. Although quantitative tumor volume thresholds demonstrate the potential for predicting the treatment response to nCRT in rectal cancer, the accuracy and reproducibility of these thresholds are significantly influenced by several methodological factors. These include variations in contouring planes, inconsistent criteria for including or excluding suspicious peritumoral edema, and a lack of consensus regarding the optimal timing for volumetric response assessment (mid‐versus post‐treatment). Automated segmentation techniques and longitudinal studies to define the optimal assessment time may address these limitations. However, further validation studies are required before routine clinical adoption.

Signal changes

On T2WI, post‐nCRT residual tumors in rectal cancer patients typically demonstrate an isointense signal, whereas fibrotic or scar tissues exhibit a hypointense signal (Figure 2). 19 However, the assessment of nCRT response based solely on T2WI signal alterations has notable limitations. The primary challenges include distinguishing radiation‐induced fibrosis from residual tumors and differentiating the treatment effects from peritumoral edema. DWI can be used to characterize tissue microenvironmental changes by quantifying water molecule diffusion restriction. This technique enables the early detection of tumor microstructural and metabolic alterations before morphological changes occur. 20 Following nCRT, tumors undergo complex pathological changes, including cellular debris, necrosis, stromal fibrosis, and mucinous degeneration. The combination of DWI and T2WI facilitates the differentiation of these tissue types (Table 1). Recent advances in DWI have significantly enhanced the qualitative assessment of the responses to nCRT response in rectal cancer. Chandramohan et al. 21 demonstrated that DWI achieved an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.887 for detecting pCR after nCRT and could improve the diagnostic ability of MRI for evaluating the efficacy of nCRT in LARC. Jang et al. 22 found that the combination of T2WI and DWI showed a higher specificity for diagnosing pCR after nCRT for rectal cancer.

QUANTITATIVE ASSESSMENT OF TUMOR RESPONSE AFTER NCRT BY DWI AND ITS DERIVED SEQUENCES

Conventional MRI can only evaluate the efficacy of nCRT in patients with LARC based on morphological and signal changes, failing to microscopically reflect the tumor response to treatment from a cytological perspective. With the continuous development of imaging techniques, functional MRI (fMRI), rather than neuro‐fMRI such as DWI and its derivative sequences (IVIM, DKI, SEM) can reflect the physiological and biological characteristics of tumors at the cytological level, thus providing more comprehensive information for evaluating the efficacy of nCRT. 23

Diffusion‐weighted imaging

DWI is a functional MRI technique that quantifies the tissue microstructure by measuring alterations in the Brownian motion of water molecules within the extracellular space. Tumor hypercellularity, which is characterized by abnormal cell proliferation and reduced extracellular space, restricts water diffusion. 24 DWI quantifies water mobility using the apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC), with restricted diffusion appearing as DWI hyperintensity and the corresponding ADC hypointensity. The ADC value is inversely correlated with tumor cellularity and directly correlated with extracellular space volume, serving as a crucial biomarker for tumor characterization and treatment response evaluation. 25 , 26 , 27 , 28

Current clinical practice has evolved from qualitative visual assessment to quantitative DWI evaluation using ADC values to determine pCR following nCRT in LARC. In nCRT‐responsive patients, early treatment primarily involves alterations in the tumor microenvironment, including (1) tumor cell membrane disruption, (2) extracellular space expansion, and (3) increased free water content. These changes increase ADC values. ADC values serve as quantitative biomarkers for assessing radiation sensitivity in rectal cancer during nCRT by reflecting changes in water diffusion dynamics. These changes exhibit a distinct temporal evolution: an initial ADC increase occurs early in treatment owing to acute cellular toxicity and damage, followed by a progressive increase to a stable plateau post‐treatment because of extensive tumor necrosis and fibrosis. This elevation was most pronounced in patients achieving a pCR. However, caution is warranted against confounding factors causing pseudonormalization of ADC values, including intratumoral hemorrhage, mucinous components, therapy‐related edema, and liquefaction of necrotic foci, which may lead to misinterpretation of therapeutic efficacy. Similar to the ADC, other quantitative parameters from advanced diffusion (e.g., IVIM‐D and DKI‐MD) and perfusion models frequently show concordant changes post‐nCRT, as they are fundamentally linked to the underlying processes of cellularity loss and perfusion modulation. Currently, most studies commonly use parameters such as ADC values before and after treatment (ΔADC, and ΔADC%) to comprehensively evaluate tumor response. A meta‐analysis by Chen et al. 29 confirmed the predictive value of ΔADC% for identifying pCR patients, reflecting more extensive tumor necrosis in responders. Similarly, Boraschi et al. 30 reported post‐treatment ADC and ΔADC% as reliable predictors of tumor regression grade (TRG) in 41 patients with LARC, although baseline ADC showed no significant correlation. Currently, the DWI model has been widely adopted in clinical practice due to its simplicity, convenience, and ease of interpretation. However, research on the use of ADC values to predict TRG has not yet reached a consensus. Furthermore, because DWI relies on the unrestricted free‐diffusion hypothesis, it cannot accurately reflect water movement in biological tissues that exhibit non‐Gaussian diffusion. In addition, evaluation of mucinous tumors using T2WI and DWI presents specific diagnostic challenges. Due to their abundant extracellular mucin content, mucinous tumors typically demonstrate marked hyperintensity on T2WI, which can obscure signal alterations in solid tumor components and compromise accurate response assessments. For instance, hyperintense mucinous pools may be misinterpreted as residual viable tumor, whereas fibrotic areas following regression of solid components might be underestimated. Furthermore, the intrinsic heterogeneity of mucinous tumors, comprising solid cellular regions, mucinous lakes, and necrotic zones, generates an uneven distribution of ADC values. Dense cellular regions typically exhibit low ADC values, whereas mucinous lakes exhibit elevated ADC values owing to unrestricted water diffusion. Consequently, conventional T2WI and ADC measurements fail to adequately capture the spatial heterogeneity of mucinous tumors, limiting their reliability in evaluating treatment responses.

Consequently, advanced non‐Gaussian diffusion models have been developed to evaluate the efficacy of nCRT in patients with LARC.

Diffusion kurtosis imaging

Diffusion kurtosis imaging (DKI) is an advanced non‐Gaussian diffusion model that quantifies tissue complexity using two principal parameters: mean diffusivity (MD) and mean kurtosis (MK). MD represents the corrected ADC that accounts for non‐Gaussian effects, reflecting the average rate of water molecule diffusion. MK quantifies the degree of water diffusion deviation from Gaussian behavior. Yang et al. 31 analyzed 42 patients with LARC prospectively and compared the utility of the DWI, IVIM, and DKI models in evaluating the response to nCRT. The pCR group had significantly higher post‐treatment ADC, pseudo‐diffusion coefficient (D*), perfusion fraction (f), and MD values than the non‐pCR group. Among these, MD demonstrated the highest diagnostic accuracy (AUC = 0.788), indicating that the DKI model can more precisely reflect the diffusion dynamics of water molecules within a tumor. Similarly, Li et al. 32 reported that MD provided better diagnostic performance than the ADC and MK values, with the percentage change in MD showing the highest diagnostic efficacy (AUC = 0.939). However, several challenges limit the clinical use of DKI, including the requirement for multiple high b‐values (typically >2000 s/mm2), which leads to signal‐to‐noise ratio (SNR) reduction and MK value overestimation, and a limited sample size reduction, which reduces statistical reliability and argumentative validity. Therefore, multicenter studies using standardized protocols and larger cohorts are required to validate the DKI of DKIs in in managing rectal cancer.

Intravoxel incoherent motion diffusion‐weighted imaging

Molecular water dynamics in biological tissues encompass complex processes involving both intracellular and extracellular diffusion, and microcirculatory perfusion effects. To differentiate between these components, Le Bihan et al. 33 pioneered IVIM imaging, which employs multi‐b‐value acquisition and biexponential modeling to quantify three physiologically distinct parameters. The parameters include true diffusion coefficient (D), D*, and f values. These parameters effectively reflect the degree of restricted diffusion of water molecules in tissue cells and the status of microcirculatory perfusion. Hu et al. 34 found that IVIM quantitative parameters allowed high diagnostic performances in pCR and good response after nCRT treatment; after nCRT treatment, the pCR group had higher D*, Δ%ADC, and Δ%D values than the non‐pCR group; the tumor good response group had higher D, Δ%ADC, and Δ%D values than the poor response group. Hu et al. 34 also found that perfusion‐related parameters D* and f were higher in the pCR group than in the non‐pCR group before treatment, suggesting that patients with higher levels of perfusion and greater vascularization of the primary foci were more likely to achieve pCR. These studies have opened the way for the precision diagnosis and evaluation of treatment effects in rectal cancer. An in‐depth study of IVIM parameters will further improve the accuracy and effectiveness of rectal cancer diagnosis and treatment in the future. However, in the IVIM model, the estimation of the perfusion‐related parameters D* and f is inherently unstable, primarily due to strong parameter coupling between D* and f, combined with the weak perfusion signal relative to noise in low b‐value images. 35 To address this challenge, a segmented (two‐step) fitting approach has been widely adopted. This method first utilizes high b‐value data to robustly estimate the true diffusion coefficient D, and then fixes D to subsequently derive f and D*. 36 By reducing the degrees of freedom, this strategy significantly improves the robustness and reproducibility of parameter estimation and has become the standard methodology for current IVIM analysis. Of note, this “robustness” is highly dependent on the specific acquisition protocol. Variations in the number, values, and distribution of b‐values across studies systematically influence parameter estimates, hindering the direct comparability of the results. 37 Therefore, establishing a standardized b‐value acquisition protocol is essential for enabling cross‐study and multicenter applications of IVIM, as well as facilitating its clinical translation.

Stretched exponential model

The stretched exponential model (SEM) characterizes the water diffusion heterogeneity in biological tissues and typically requiring high b values (>1200 s/mm2) for accurate parameter estimation. The average diffusion rate in a voxel is expressed as the diffusion coefficient (DDC). In contrast, the deviation from the attenuation of the signal in the form of a single exponential is expressed as an index of heterogeneity (alpha, α). The DDC value is considered the weighted sum of several ADC values with multiexponential decay characteristics, reflecting the multiexponential decay feature and representing the average diffusion rate within the voxel. The α value (range: 0–1) quantifies deviation from monoexponential decay, where lower values indicate greater tissue complexity. Zhang et al. 38 found higher pretreatment DDC values in patients without pCR than in those with pCR. Zhu et al. 39 demonstrated that patients achieving pCR exhibited higher α values post‐nCRT, reflecting simpler tissue composition due to tumor necrosis and fibrosis. The increase in α values was more pronounced in the pCR group. These findings indicated that SEM parameters effectively reflected water diffusion and tissue heterogeneity and exhibited reproducibility. Additional research is required to validate the role of SEM in evaluating the efficacy of nCRT in patients with LARC.

Although the parameters derived from these models have demonstrated potential to predict the efficacy of nCRT for rectal cancer, their clinical translation remains limited. These parameters are primarily obtained through complex model fitting rather than direct measurements, leading to poor reproducibility. Specifically, IVIM perfusion parameters are highly susceptible to noise and the results are significantly influenced by the region of interest (ROI) selection strategy. Different delineation approaches introduce substantial inter‐observer and intraobserver variability; for instance, a small ROI may fail to represent overall tumor heterogeneity, whereas a whole‐tumor ROI may include necrotic components and dilute functional signals. Furthermore, fluctuations in the SNR directly affect parameter accuracy, including the overestimation of DKI kurtosis values at low SNR and unstable fitting of IVIM parameters. Finally, the absence of standardized protocols for image acquisition and postprocessing significantly impedes the translation of these advanced models to clinical practice. Although most studies report encouraging intra‐ and inter‐observer reproducibility, as indicated by high intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs), this consistency is confined to single‐institution studies that use specific post‐processing workflows. The robustness and generalizability of these models remain insufficiently validated, as their performance is susceptible to multiple variables, including but not limited to different MRI scanner manufacturers and magnetic field strengths, diverse image reconstruction algorithms, heterogeneous methods for segmentation and feature extraction, and scanning protocols across institutions. Therefore, future research must shift its focus from pursuing high performance on single datasets to establishing large, standardized, multicenter imaging databases that span different platforms. Building on this foundation, rigorous prospective multicenter validation studies are essential to systematically evaluate model performance across these diverse technical variables.

QUANTITATIVE ASSESSMENT OF TUMOR RESPONSE AFTER NCRT BY DYNAMIC CONTRAST‐ENHANCED MRI

DCE‐MRI quantitatively assesses changes in the tumor microcirculation. This method evaluates tumor angiogenesis and biological aggressiveness through three primary pharmacokinetic metrics: the volume transfer constant (Ktrans), rate constant (Kep), and extravascular extracellular volume fraction (Ve). Ktrans reflects the transfer rate of contrast agents from the plasma to the extravascular extracellular space (EES), indicating microvascular blood flow and permeability. Kep quantifies the return rate of contrast agent from the EES to the plasma, whereas Ve represents the proportion of EES per unit volume. Tong et al. 40 conducted an in‐depth quantitative analysis of the transfer constant (Ktrans), volume fraction of the extracellular extravascular space (Ve), and the rate constant (Kep). At the time of pCR diagnosis, the AUC of Ktrans was 0.837, whereas the AUCs of Ve and Kep reached 0.655 and 0.654, respectively. In addition, Dijkhoff et al. 41 quantitatively concluded that the higher the Ktrans value, the better the degree of tumor remission. At the semi‐quantitative level, the late tilt rate of the pretreatment contrast uptake curve was negatively correlated with the tumor remission effect, and the lower the late tilt rate, the stronger the tumor remission effect. Zou et al. 42 found that the Ktrans value showed a more significant decreasing trend in the pCR group, thus confirming the clinical value of DCE‐MRI parameters in predicting pCR after nCRT in patients with LARC. However, the reproducibility of quantitative DCE‐MRI parameters is challenged by multiple sources of variability spanning the technical, physiological, and modeling domains. Inadequate temporal resolution (>15–20 s) fails to accurately capture the rapid kinetics of the contrast agent first‐pass through arteries and extravasation into tissue, leading to a significant underestimation of Ktrans and Kep. Although a very high temporal resolution (<5 s) improves kinetic characterization, it often compromises spatial resolution, anatomical coverage, or the SNR. Selection of the arterial input function (AIF) substantially influences absolute kinetic parameter quantification. 43 , 44 Pharmacokinetic modeling introduces additional complexity, with options ranging from standard and extended Tofts models to more sophisticated two‐compartment exchange models. Applying different models to the same dataset systematically yields divergent estimates of Ktrans, Kep, and Ve. Adopting a comprehensive standardization framework, such as the Quantitative Imaging Biomarkers Alliance (QIBA) profile, along with digital reference objects as objective validation tools can systematically identify, quantify, and mitigate these variabilities, transforming Ktrans, Kep, and Ve into reliable cross‐platform quantitative imaging biomarkers.

Advanced MRI techniques offer valuable complementary insights for the clinical monitoring of tumor responses beyond conventional imaging. IVIM provides a non‐contrast alternative for evaluating both diffusion and perfusion useful for detecting very early treatment effects within 1–2 weeks, although its perfusion parameters can be inconsistent. DKI is particularly superior to standard DWI in the post‐treatment setting, where its parameter MK helps differentiate ambiguous cases by distinguishing residual tumors (high MK) from treatment‐related fibrosis (low MK), which is best assessed at 8–12 weeks. DCE‐MRI remains a key tool for evaluating vascular changes, as its qualitative “wash‐in/wash‐out” kinetics can suggest residual disease, whereas quantitative analysis supports mid‐treatment strategy adjustments and post‐treatment “watch‐and‐wait” decisions, despite the inherent risks of contrast agents. Finally, SEM can serve as a robust alternative when other techniques are unreliable, offering an improved characterization of tumor cellularity and fibrosis. However, the accuracy of the quantitative parameters derived from these fMRI models is highly dependent on the precise sequence parameter configuration. For diffusion models (DWI, IVIM, and DKI), the fitted quantitative parameters are sensitive to factors such as the b‐value distribution and echo time, which directly impact the model fitting precision. The reliability of DCE‐MRI is significantly influenced by the temporal resolution, spatial resolution, and the method used for AIF determination. Therefore, achieving standardized acquisition and reproducible analysis of these biomarkers requires strict standardization of technical parameters. EVALUATION OF TUMOR RESPONSE AFTER NCRT BY RADIOMICS

Conventional radiomics

In recent years, radiomics has emerged as a promising tool for the noninvasive prediction of nCRT response in LARC, demonstrating increasingly significant clinical value owing to its unique technical advantages. The traditional radiomics process includes several key aspects: the acquisition of high‐quality medical images; the accurate segmentation of the ROI; and feature extraction and selection of the most valuable feature. Finally, a predictive model is constructed and subjected to rigorous performance validation to ensure its diagnostic or prognostic accuracy. This standardized and comprehensive extraction of high‐dimensional features from medical images facilitates the development of predictive models with significant clinical utility. Cui et al. 45 innovatively combined the application of multiparameter magnetic, including T2WI, ADC, and DCE‐MRI, to construct a radiomic model and fully considered a variety of clinical factors, which were incorporated into the drawing of the column line diagram. After validation, the AUC values of the model in the training and validation cohorts reached 0.948 and 0.966, respectively, demonstrating extremely high prediction accuracy. Shin et al. 46 demonstrated comparable performance between T2WI‐based (AUC = 0.82) and combined T2WI‐ADC models (AUC = 0.82), surpassing the ADC‐only model (AUC = 0.79). Wang et al. 47 systematically compared the performance of machine learning classifiers (decision trees, random forests, support vector machines [SVM], logistic regression, and adaptive boosting), and the model built by SVM had the best diagnostic efficacy for patients with LARC after nCRT.

Deep learning‐based radiomics

DL has revolutionized medical image analysis through hierarchical feature learning, inspired by biological neural networks. Unlike conventional radiomics, DL allows automated feature extraction without manual ROI delineation, eliminates inter‐observer variability, and improves reproducibility. Classical DL architectures include convolutional neural networks, recurrent neural networks, and multilayer perceptrons (MLP). In recent years, DL has significantly advanced the prediction of nCRT response in patients with LARC.

Bibault et al. 48 developed a DNN radiomics model and compared it with traditional linear regression and SVM models. The DNN model achieved a prediction accuracy of 80.00%, outperforming the traditional models (69.5% and 71.58%, respectively). Similarly, Zhou et al. 49 constructed DL models based on multisequence fusion with and without attention mechanisms as well as radiomics models and conducted comparative analyses. The DL model incorporating the attention mechanism performed best in predicting the response of patients with LARC after nCRT, with AUC values of 0.898 and 0.873 for the internal and external validation sets, respectively. The comparison clearly indicated that the model using the attention mechanism significantly outperformed the model without this mechanism in terms of performance.

Recent studies have expanded beyond conventional MRI sequences by integrating functional sequences to improve diagnostic and assessment capabilities. Zhang et al. 50 employed DL techniques based on T2WI and DKI, and demonstrated the strong performance of their DL model in predicting pCR. These results provide valuable support for radiologists evaluating treatment responses of patients with LARC following nCRT.

Most previous DL‐based radiomics models used 2D images for prediction. However, a 2D model can omit essential features owing to the spatial heterogeneity. of the tumor Although 3D models theoretically capture tumor characteristics more comprehensively, limited clinical sample sizes often lead to excessively high dimensionality, hindering effective modeling. To address this challenge, a 2.5D DL architecture was proposed to balance information integrity and data complexity. When compared to full 3D models, the 2.5D strategy decomposes the 3D volume into a series of multi‐slice 2D inputs. This approach effectively exploits 3D contextual information while maintaining the same parameter scale with only a minimal increase due to additional input channels. Crucially, it avoids the cubic growth in both the parameter count and GPU memory consumption inherent to 3D convolutions, thereby achieving substantial efficiency gains. This approach integrates partial 3D spatial information into 2D slices and significantly reducing the complexity by segmenting the original 3D data into subsets. POON et al. 51 developed both 2.5D and 3D models and through comparative analysis determined that the 2.5D model achieved a higher diagnostic performance. Prior to the introduction of ResNet, increasing the network depth often led to performance degradation. ResNet addressed this issue by incorporating residual modules and skip connections, which alleviated gradient vanishing and information loss, thereby enabling stable training of deeper networks. Wan et al. 52 compared 2D and 3D ResNet models of varying depths (i.e., ResNet18/50/101/152) and demonstrated that 3D ResNet‐based models outperformed radiologists in predicting lymph node metastasis in T1–T2 stage rectal cancer.

In radiomics studies predicting the treatment response to nCRT in rectal cancer, the transparency and robustness of feature selection methods was critical for model reproducibility. Among the most widely used feature reduction techniques in current research are LASSO regression, minimum Redundancy Maximum Relevance (mRMR), and Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE). These methods excel in handling parameter collinearity, selecting complementary features with low redundancy, and capturing complex nonlinear relationships between features and treatment responses. Crucially, in high‐dimensional, small‐sample‐size studies where the number of features substantially exceeds the number of cases, preventing model overfitting and performance evaluation bias is essential. The adoption of nested cross‐validation is strongly recommended as a necessary methodology for model training and evaluation, because it provides unbiased estimates of generalization errors, thereby mitigating optimistic bias in predictive accuracy and ensuring scientifically reliable research outcomes.

With the rapid digitization of medical data, research on radiomics for predicting nCRT responses in patients with LARC has grown exponentially. Notable results in treatment response assessment and pCR prediction have been achieved by analyzing rich texture, morphology, and functional features of MRI images using machine learning and statistical models. These developments provide significant technical support for personalized and precision treatment. Radiomics models have demonstrated promising potential for predicting the nCRT response in patients with LARC; however, their clinical translation remains hindered by three major challenges, necessitating further optimization of conventional workflows to enhance research quality. First, the limited model generalizability stems primarily from multicenter imaging heterogeneity; variations in scanners and acquisition protocols introduce significant feature distribution discrepancies. For instance, external validation across 11 studies by Shahzadi et al. 53 revealed markedly lower AUC values than those reported in the individual studies. To improve robustness, a three‐tier validation framework is recommended (internal cross‐validation, multicenter external validation, and temporal validation) coupled with standardized imaging protocols as per the Image Biomarker Standardization Initiative (e.g., T2WI slice thickness of 3 mm and DWI b‐values of 0–1000 s/mm2). Furthermore, integrating transfer learning and ComBat‐based feature harmonization can mitigate interscanner bias. Second, inconsistent annotations adversely affect feature reliability. The inherent peristalsis and significant anatomical variability of the rectum complicate the consistent and accurate delineation of the ROI on MRI. Peristaltic motion can lead to image blurring and misregistration, whereas differences in morphology and distension hinder the standardization of ROI placement across patients and time points. Zukauskaite et al. 54 reported a median Dice similarity coefficient of only 0.73 (95% CI: 0.53–0.86) between manually delineated tumor volumes by different physicians and the STAPLE consensus reference, with significant differences (P<0.05). Standardized contouring guidelines for whole‐tumor 3D segmentation should be adopted, and U‐Net‐based semi‐automatic tools with manual refinement can increase the inter‐observer ICC to >0.85. Third, low clinical acceptance arises from the “black‐box” nature of the models, which raises trust concerns in high‐stakes decision‐making (e.g., surgical planning). Explainability techniques, such as gradient‐weighted class activation mapping (Grad‐CAM), can generate decision heatmaps for intuitive interpretation, whereas clinical case‐based retrieval systems that compare historical predictions with actual outcomes may enhance transparency and facilitate AI integration into practice. Furthermore, to ensure reproducibility, generalizability, and clinical applicability in traditional radiomics workflows, several refinements are advised. Beyond conventional performance metrics (e.g., AUC, sensitivity, and specificity), calibration curves and decision curve analysis (DCA) should be provided to quantitatively assess model calibration and net clinical benefit. If automated segmentation is employed, detailed algorithm descriptions (e.g., U‐Net, Transformer), evaluation of consistency (e.g., Kappa, ICC) and robustness is essential. Prior to clinical deployment, models must be rigorously validated using external test sets from diverse institutions, scanner models, and patient populations. Domain adaptation techniques can also be incorporated to alleviate domain shifts and ensure broad applicability.

ADJUSTMENT OF MRI AND RADIOMICS FOR IMMUNOTHERAPY

Immunotherapy has emerged as a significant modality for the neoadjuvant treatment of patients with LARC. This approach often induces lymphocyte infiltration and localized inflammatory reactions by activating T‐cell–mediated antitumor immune responses These processes can manifest radiologically as transient tumor enlargement or altered signal intensity, mimicking disease progression, a phenomenon known as pseudoprogression, observed in 7%–10% of patients receiving immunotherapy. 55 This distinctive response pattern, characterized by delayed effects and pseudoprogression, poses substantial challenges to conventional imaging evaluation, particularly in patients with mismatch repair deficiency (dMMR).

Consequently, a systematic adaptation of imaging assessment strategies is required. Initial treatment response evaluation should be postponed to 12–16 weeks after treatment initiation to accommodate delayed responses. 56 When T2WI demonstrates tumor volume increase, integration with DWI is crucial. A reduced DWI signal accompanied by a significant elevation in ADC values (typically >30%) may indicate pseudoprogression, 57 necessitating confirmatory imaging within 4–8 weeks. Conventional radiomics models require revalidation in immunotherapy cohorts, as texture features reflecting tumor heterogeneity (such as entropy from gray‐level co‐occurrence matrices) and may demonstrate divergent trends between pseudoprogression and true progression. 58 Furthermore, adoption of the Immune‐Related Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (iRECIST) is recommended, 59 wherein suspected progression is categorized as “unconfirmed progressive disease” to prevent premature discontinuation of effective treatment.

Future research should prioritize prospective studies to establish immunotherapy‐specific imaging biomarkers, investigate correlations between radiomic features and immune microenvironment characteristics (e.g., CD8+ T‐cell density), and develop standardized imaging guidelines for patients with dMMR rectal cancer undergoing neoadjuvant immunotherapy, ultimately enabling a more accurate differentiation between immune cell infiltration and tumor progression to optimize therapeutic decision‐making.

MULTIMODAL MRI

Predictive models using multimodal fusion strategies have emerged as pivotal tools for evaluating the efficacy of nCRT in patients with LARC. Studies integrating MRI radiomics features with other modal data show improved predictive performance. Li et al. 60 developed a multimodal model by combining computed tomography (CT) and T2‐weighted MRI (T2WI‐MRI) features, achieving an AUC of 0.93, which was significantly superior to those of models based on single modalities (CT, 0.77; MRI, 0.86). The superior performance of CT+MRI multimodal models in predicting nCRT efficacy for rectal cancer stems from the powerful and interpretable complementarity between the two imaging modalities in revealing tumor biology. MRI, particularly functional sequences, provides real‐time information on the functional status of a tumor, including cellular density, microcirculatory perfusion, and tissue heterogeneity, serving as a direct biological indicator of early treatment response. Conversely, CT contributes essential anatomical context and chronic pathological information that MRI cannot clearly delineate, such as the sensitive detection of mesorectal fascia invasion, calcified lymph nodes, and assessment of peritumoral vascular calcification. Thus, multimodal fusion essentially integrates MRI‐derived “tumor functional status” with CT‐provided “anatomical substrate,” enabling the model to learn complex patterns, including “high heterogeneity (MRI) combined with extensive vascular calcification (CT)”, which synergistically indicate poor prognosis, thereby achieving more comprehensive and robust predictive performance. Furthermore, the combination of MRI with emerging technologies, such as circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) and digital pathology imaging, has enhanced the accuracy of nCRT efficacy prediction by integrating imaging phenotypes, molecular characteristics, and histopathological information, and represents a current research focus. A meta‐analysis 61 demonstrated that postoperative ctDNA positivity was significantly correlated with poor prognosis (hazard ratio [HR] = 14.94), and the AUC for predicting pCR increased by 0.12–0.18 after fusion with MRI radiomics features. In the context of pathological integration, Shao et al. 62 constructed an artificial intelligence model by extracting macroscopic MRI imaging features (∼1000) and microscopic whole‐slide digital pathology features (∼700), confirming that the multimodal model outperformed any single‐modality approach. These advances demonstrate that multimodal omics models, by integrating multidimensional information, including imaging, molecular, and pathological data, can more comprehensively characterize tumor heterogeneity, thereby providing robust support for individualized efficacy prediction and informing “watch‐and‐wait” strategy decisions.

IMPACT OF THE RE‐STAGING INTERVAL ON THE SENSITIVITY OF DETECTING CLINICAL COMPLETE RESPONSE OR ONCOLOGICAL OUTCOMES

For patients with LARC, the interval between nCRT and surgery, commonly termed the “waiting period”, represents a critical clinical decision point, influencing both the extent of tumor regression and the accuracy of cCR assessment. 63 , 64 Prolonged intervals correlate with increased rates of pCR, as demonstrated in the landmark Lyon R90‐01 trial, 65 which found superior tumor response in patients undergoing surgery after 6–8 weeks compared with earlier intervention. Current practice often extends this interval up to 12 weeks to maximize tumor response. 66 , 67 Although patients achieving a good response benefit from this delay through improved long‐term prognosis, those with a poor response may experience diminished oncologic outcomes, including increased risks of tumor progression and distant metastasis. Tumor regression rates decelerate over time, being the most rapid initially and slowing preoperatively. 68 Consequently, prolonged intervals in poor responders have been associated with reduced overall and disease‐free survival, whereas earlier surgical intervention in this subgroup may improve survival and mitigate metastatic risk. 68 , 69 , 70 , 71 Therefore, precise imaging evaluation plays a pivotal role in clinical decision‐making. The optimal timing for imaging studies should capture the most predictive kinetic information regarding tumor regression. Early phase imaging (e.g., 2–4 weeks after nCRT) may help identify non‐responders, whereas end‐of‐waiting‐period assessments (e.g., 10–12 weeks) are critical for confirming a cCR. Current MRI‐based serial follow‐up strategies, particularly when integrated with functional sequences, demonstrate considerable potential in differentiating these distinct response patterns. Future research should focus on longitudinal multi‐timepoint imaging acquisition to characterize individual tumor regression trajectories, thereby enabling earlier and more accurate predictions of tumor behavior. Such advances will facilitate the development of surgical timing strategies tailored to patients with varying therapeutic responses.

CONCLUSIONS

Accurate assessment of tumor response following nCRT, particularly the prediction of pCR, is critical for therapeutic decision‐making for LARC. MRI serves as a key tool in this field, with its evaluation framework evolving substantially from morphological and functional quantitative analyses to artificial intelligence‐driven approaches. Conventionally, morphological assessments based on T2WI and DWI are widely used; however, they remain limited in distinguishing residual tumors from post‐treatment changes. With advances in functional MRI techniques, such as IVIM, DKI, SEM, and DCE‐MRI, quantitative parameters related to water diffusion, tissue heterogeneity, and microvascular perfusion have provided more sensitive biological indicators of treatment response at the microstructural level.

Radiomics combined with DL has further unlocked the potential for medical imaging. By extracting subvisual features from large‐scale imaging data and building predictive models, these approaches offer new pathways for individualized pCR prediction. Notably, the 2.5D DL architecture has emerged as an effective technical compromise, balancing 3D spatial contextual information with computational feasibility.

However, several challenges exist in this field. The stability of fMRI parameters and the reproducibility of radiomic features are influenced by multicenter data heterogeneity, variations in imaging protocols, and inconsistencies in ROI delineation. Moreover, predictive models often achieve limited generalizability and “black‐box” decision‐making, which hinder their clinical translation and application. To overcome these bottlenecks, concerted efforts are needed in three key areas: technical standardization, robust model validation, and enhanced clinical interpretability. Specific strategies include adhering to the IBSI guidelines and previous studies, establishing a standardized protocol and harmonization guide for key quantitative MRI sequences in rectal cancer, implementing ComBat‐based feature harmonization, standardizing image acquisition and ROI segmentation protocols, applying domain adaptation, and integrating quality control measures. Furthermore, multicenter external validation is essential, alongside the adoption of explainable AI tools such as Grad‐CAM, to improve model transparency. Current research on predicting nCRT response in rectal cancer remains overly reliant on discriminative metrics, such as AUC, and lack critical clinical applicability. Key limitations include: (1) poor interpretability, without tools such as Grad‐CAM or SHAP to reveal decision rationale or feature contributions; (2) absence of clinically validated thresholds to convert continuous scores into actionable decisions, neglecting methods such as DCA; and (3) disconnection from ultimate clinical endpoints, seldom evaluating long‐term survival via Cox models rather than surrogate markers such as pCR. Integrating interpretability tools, clinically informed cutoff selection, and survival analysis is essential for bridging the gap between model performance and practical utility.

The following directions deserve greater emphasis: promoting multimodal data integration by combining MRI, CT, ctDNA, and digital pathology to establish a more comprehensive evaluation system; optimizing imaging criteria and timing for novel therapies such as immunotherapy; and clarifying the optimal interval between nCRT and surgery to maximize patient benefits. Through large‐scale prospective studies and by closely linking imaging biomarkers with long‐term survival outcomes, MRI has been poised to solidify its central role in the precision management of LARC.
