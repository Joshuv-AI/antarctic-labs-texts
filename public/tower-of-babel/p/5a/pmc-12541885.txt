Mapping Human Proprioceptive Projections of Upper Limb Muscles Through Spinal Cord fMRI
Authors: Raphaëlle Schlienger, Caroline Landelle, Sergio Daniel Hernandez‐Charpak, Daniela Maria Pinzon‐Corredor, Jeanne Caron‐Guyon, Julien Sein, Bruno Nazarian, Jocelyne Bloch, Olivier Felician, Jean‐Luc Anton, Grégoire Courtine, Anne Kavounoudias
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe functional organization of the human spinal cord has primarily been derived from clinical observations and invasive electrophysiological studies. Recent methodological advances opened the possibility of studying the neuronal activity of the spinal cord in humans using noninvasive functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI). Here, we took advantage of fMRI to map the patterns of activity elicited by muscle‐specific proprioceptive information along the whole cervical cord. We quantified the fMRI signals of the cervical spinal cord in 24 healthy participants who received mechanical muscle tendon vibration to stimulate proprioceptive afferents. The wrist flexor, biceps, and anterior deltoid muscles were independently stimulated while the upper limbs were stationary to avoid movement artifacts. To account for anatomical variability among participants, we optimized activity pattern localization by identifying individual rootlets and determining corresponding spinal levels using a trained deep‐learning model. Distinct activation patterns emerged based on the stimulated muscle and body side, which coincided with well‐established myotome maps. Concretely, the vibration‐induced proprioceptive stimuli activity circumscribed to the ipsilateral ventral horn with a rostrocaudal distribution that reflected the proximo‐distal location of the stimulated muscles. This spatial organization supported the proprioceptive origin of the response. This study demonstrates that muscle tendon vibration combined with spinal cord fMRI enables the noninvasive identification of upper‐limb myotomes within the cervical spinal cord, offering new possibilities for studying the functional organization of the spinal cord and for clinical applications.Keywords: mechanical vibration, movement illusions, muscle proprioception, spinal cord

Introduction

Muscle proprioception, an essential sensory modality in body movement perception and control, is central to the sensorimotor network. However, noninvasively investigating proprioceptive pathways in humans has been challenging due to the difficulty of independently stimulating deep muscle spindles. Single afferent recordings in humans have shown that low‐amplitude mechanical vibration applied to muscle tendons can preferentially activate muscle spindle endings, evoking an illusion of movement in motionless participants (for review, see Kavounoudias et al. 2023). The development of MR‐compatible vibrators for proprioceptive stimulation has emerged as a prominent noninvasive tool for exploring proprioceptive networks at the brain level, revealing robust brain activity within a large sensorimotor network (Duclos et al. 2007; Kavounoudias et al. 2008; Cignetti et al. 2014; Amemiya and Naito 2016; Landelle et al. 2020). However, the proprioceptive circuitry spanning multiple spinal levels remains largely unexplored.

The current understanding of the functional organization of the spinal cord along the rostro‐caudal axis is based on segmental innervation patterns, as well as dermatome and myotome maps, derived from clinical observations and invasive electrophysiological studies (Lee et al. 2008; Schirmer et al. 2011; Cramer and Darby 2017; McIntosh et al. 2023; Sharrard 1964; Phillips and Park 1991; London et al. 2022). Recent years have seen increased exploration of spinal sensory and motor processing with functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), but specific investigations of proprioceptive pathways at the spinal level remain scarce. This is partly due to technical challenges restricting the deployment of spinal fMRI, such as the spinal cord's small size and sensitivity to motion and physiological perturbations. Recent methodological advances, including refined acquisition sequences and improved data processing, have opened new avenues of research (De Leener et al. 2017; Barry et al. 2018; Landelle et al. 2021; Kinany et al. 2022). To date, most spinal fMRI studies have focused on measuring spinal cord activity at rest or during tasks involving body movements or sensory stimuli, including thermal, painful, or tactile stimuli, but very few used proprioceptive stimulation (see reviews by Stroman et al. 2014; Powers et al. 2018; Landelle et al. 2021; Tinnermann et al. 2021; Kinany et al. 2022). However, muscle spindle afferents project into the spinal cord, forming the myotatic loop, which holds the potential to selectively engage proprio‐motor circuits. A few fMRI studies used unilateral passive movement paradigms and reported widespread bilateral and rostro‐caudal activation patterns likely due to nonselective muscle mobilization (Kornelsen and Stroman 2004; Valsasina et al. 2008). To personalize preoperative protocols for spinal cord stimulation, Rowald et al. (2022) successfully mapped proprioceptive‐motor circuits in three patients with complete spinal cord injuries using passive lower limb mobilization and muscle tendon vibration. In contrast, spinal fMRI has effectively captured motor activity within the spinal cord during simple active movement tasks involving distal body segments. However, a high rostro‐caudal variability in reported activity locations, along with some inconsistent activations during unilateral motor tasks, may reflect difficulties in selectively recruiting muscle groups (Weber et al. 2016; Kinany et al. 2019; Hemmerling et al. 2023; Hernandez‐Charpak et al. 2025). Active or passive movements recruit both the moving limb's muscles and synergistic muscles, making the exploration of selective proprio‐motor networks challenging. Additionally, fMRI's motion limitations restrict active mobilization of certain body movements or proximal muscles.

The present study aimed to characterize the spatial distribution of spinal cord activation in response to proprioceptive stimulation, to examine how these activation patterns vary with both the stimulated muscle and body side, and to explore the extent of the interparticipant variability in anatomical spinal levels and functional responses. Mechanical vibration was applied at three levels along both motionless arms to stimulate muscle spindles and induce an illusion of movement, with the expectation that this would reveal proprio‐motor activity patterns, while minimizing artifacts associated with actual arm movement. Given the anatomical variability among participants (i.e., spinal levels versus vertebrae), the localization of activity patterns was refined by identifying individual spinal levels. Both individual and group‐level analyses were performed to assess the extent to which the fMRI approach could contribute to a comprehensive understanding of the functional sensorimotor organization of the cervical spinal cord and its variability across participants, with potential implications for both fundamental and clinical applications.

Material and Methods

Participants

Twenty‐five healthy young adults were included in the present study conducted at Aix‐Marseille University (Marseille, France). Participants were right‐handed and reported no neurological disorders or contraindications for MRI. All participants gave their written informed consent in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration, and the experiment was approved by the local Ethics Committee (Comité de Protection des Personnes Sud‐Est VI, n°2021‐A01733‐38). Among the 25 participants, one was excluded due to a shifted field of view in MRI acquisitions. The analyses were run on the remaining 24 young adults (mean age ± one standard deviation [SD] 27.1 ± 2.6 years, 13 females).

Experimental Protocol

Proprioceptive Stimulation

During the fMRI session, participants underwent proprioceptive stimulation at three different locations on both arms, delivered through specifically designed pneumatic vibrators (Figure 1B). The vibrators had two different cylindrical sizes: small (53 mm long, 26 mm diameter) and large (102 mm long, 40 mm diameter), tailored for an optimal fit on wrist and elbow/shoulder joints, respectively. Vibration was induced by airflow entering the cylinder and spinning an asymmetric and amagnetic ceramic mass around an axis. A custom‐made bench simultaneously piloted and monitored the six vibrators using six independent air pressure regulators driven by an analog 0–10 V command. Vibration frequency was adjusted by regulating airflow pressure. Notably, this proprioceptive device has already been employed in previous brain and spinal cord fMRI studies during which the absence of artifact generation in functional images or impact on the signal‐to‐noise ratio (SNR) was confirmed (Duclos et al. 2007; Kavounoudias et al. 2008; Landelle et al. 2020; Rowald et al. 2022; Hernandez‐Charpak et al. 2025). The stimulation onsets were synchronized with MR acquisition using a custom real‐time software developed in LabVIEW 2020. This software‐controlled hardware synchronization via a PCIe‐6353 device and issued analog voltage commands through a USB‐6221 multifunction DAQ (both from National Instruments, Austin, USA).

In addition, FSR polymer isometric sensors with a diameter of 25 mm (OHMITE FSR03BE, 1000 Hz sampling rate) were positioned between the vibratory device and the participant's skin. These sensors allowed frequency adjustment before the start of the session to adjust the stimulation to each participant. Vibration signals delivered by each of the six vibrators were also recorded in real time during all the functional runs. We assessed the frequency of the delivered vibrations on the participants' skin across all trials and stimulation conditions, maintaining it within the range of 55–75 Hz, which aligns with the preferred frequency range for muscle spindle afferents (Roll and Vedel 1982). Except for four participants for whom the vibration signals were not processable due to occasional measurement failures, we verified that the vibrations were delivered during the scanning procedure, with an average frequency of 65 Hz for all stimuli and participants. We also compared the vibration frequency between the left and right conditions for each of the three body sites tested using a nonparametric Wilcoxon Signed‐rank test (two‐sided). We found no significant difference between the mean vibration frequencies delivered to the anterior deltoids on the left (mean ± SD; 67 ± 3.8 Hz) and on the right (68 ± 3.4 Hz) (W = 78, p = 0.52). Biceps muscles also received similar stimulation at 62 ± 6.0 Hz on the left and 60 ± 7.9 Hz on the right (W = 42, p < 0.05). However, vibration frequency on wrist flexors slightly differed by 6 Hz between the left (63 ± 3.8 Hz) and right (69 ± 5.5 Hz) sides (W = 2, p < 0.001) (See Figure S1 for more details regarding the analysis of the FSR sensor signal).

Experimental Procedure

Before the MR acquisition, participants were familiarized with the proprioceptive equipment for 15 min. They were asked to relax and close their eyes to feel movement illusions induced by applying the mechanical vibration on different muscle tendons of the upper limbs. After becoming acquainted with the vibration‐induced illusion, three vibrators were fastened on the ventral side of each arm at both the left and right wrist flexor, biceps, and anterior deltoid tendons with Velcro or elastic straps (Figure 1A,B). Vibrator location and frequency were carefully adjusted to evoke movement illusions, expected to mimic muscle elongation. MR‐compatible headphones (OptoActive from OptoAcoustics) were used to deliver instructions to participants.

The MRI protocol consisted of the acquisition of one high‐resolution anatomical image followed by four functional runs covering the cervical and upper thoracic spine (Figure 1C). During each run, each of the six muscles was stimulated for 8 s in eight repeated trials, presented in a pseudo‐randomized order. A no‐vibration (NoVib) condition was also randomly repeated eight times per run. Consecutive trials were separated by an interstimulus interval (ISI) of 0.5–1.5 s to ensure random subsampling of the spinal cord relative to each condition (Figure 1D). To prevent the stimulation posteffects classically reported after 30 s of sustained muscle tendon vibration (Duclos et al. 2007), a pseudo‐randomization protocol was employed to ensure that no stimulation condition was repeated more than twice consecutively. Participants were alerted to the beginning of both proprioceptive stimulation and NoVib conditions by an auditory beep, ensuring they were aware of each new trial. They were instructed to close their eyes throughout the run and focus on the salience and direction of movement illusions. The NoVib condition was therefore designed to control for potential changes in spinal cord activity related to the participant's attentional state—particularly the possibility that hearing the auditory cue (beep) might modulate postural tone, as well as the influence of fusimotor drive, which is known to affect muscle spindle activity when attention is directed toward one's own body (Hospod et al. 2007). Each run lasted 9 min and 20 s (280 fMRI volumes), comprising a total of 56 trials (48 stimulation trials and eight NoVib trials). At the end of each run, and for each of the six muscle groups stimulated, participants were asked to report orally the direction and salience of their movement illusions on a scale from 0 to 4 (0: no illusion; 4: very clear movement illusion). Salience ratings between left and right arm stimulation and between arm levels were compared using Wilcoxon signed‐rank tests.

Image Acquisition

MRI data was acquired with a 3.0‐Tesla MRI Scanner (MAGNETOM Prisma, Siemens AG, Erlangen, Germany) at Aix‐Marseille University, equipped with a 64‐channel head and neck coil and a 32‐channel spinal coil embedded in the patient table. Participants were placed in a supine position and were instructed to relax and minimize motion.

A high‐resolution T2‐weighted anatomical image was acquired using a SPACE (Sampling Perfection with Application‐optimized Contrasts using different flip angle Evolution) sequence, covering the entire cervical spine and upper thoracic spine (single slab 3D turbo spin echo sequence with a slab selective, variable excitation pulse, repetition time (TR) = 1500 ms, echo time (TE) = 135 ms, echo train length = 74, resolution = 0.8 × 0.8 × 0.8 mm3, matrix size = 320 × 320 × 60).

Functional MRI data were acquired using a gradient‐echo echo‐planar (EPI) ZOOMit sequence (selective excitation), set with the following parameters: TR = 2 s, TE = 30 ms, FOV = 128 × 48 mm2, flip angle = 80°, in‐plane resolution = 1 × 1 mm2, slice thickness = 4 mm, 30 ascending slices, GRAPPA acceleration factor = 2, Receiver Bandwidth = 1300 Hz/pix. The functional sequence covered the spinal cord from C2/C3 vertebral disks to T1/T2 vertebrae, depending on the participant's anatomy. Slices were positioned perpendicular to the cross‐section of the spinal cord. Physiological data were recorded during fMRI runs using a respiration belt and a pulse transducer placed on the index or middle finger (Siemens Physiology Monitoring Unit).

Individual Spinal Level Identification

Normalization of individual spinal cords onto the PAM50 template is essential for group‐level analysis. However, this procedure traditionally relies on vertebral landmarks (vertebral levels or intervertebral disc positions, SCT toolbox, De Leener et al. 2017), despite reported variability in the lengths and alignments of vertebrae and spinal levels (Cadotte et al. 2015; Cramer and Darby 2017; Frostell et al. 2016; Nunès et al. 2023). Spinal levels are defined based on spinal nerve rootlets merging with the spinal cord. To enhance accuracy in identifying the location of BOLD signal activity relative to spinal levels, we employed a novel approach that enables precise automatic delineation of nerve rootlets at the individual level and creates a specific probabilistic map in the PAM50 template space of spinal levels based on the current study population.

To this end, we first estimated the warping field corresponding to the coregistration of each participant's individual T2 SPACE image to the T2 PAM50 template based on traditional vertebral landmarks (i.e., disk positions) and spinal cord segmentation (V warp on Figure 2A). Concurrently, we automatically segmented the nerve rootlets of each participant using a previously trained self‐configuring multiclass nnUNet v2 3D model (Valošek et al. 2024). This algorithm automatically identified the dorsal spinal rootlets in each participant's T2 SPACE image (Figure 2B). After a visual verification, the resulting binary segmented image was then coregistered to the PAM50 template rootlets, producing a second warping field (R warp). The concatenation of the two warping fields (V + R warps) enables registration from native to template space based on rootlet landmarks. Based on the position of their own spinal rootlets relative to their vertebrae, we then obtained individual spinal levels in template space (Figure 2C) and established a probabilistic spinal level map for the 24‐participant population. The probabilistic map was thresholded to ensure continuity of spinal levels with no overlap, with a threshold corresponding to the common area for at least half of the participants (Figure 2C). This approach was used to enhance the precise localization of activity at the participant level by preserving the specificity of each individual spinal level, and more importantly, at the group level, to ensure that the results are representative of the entire group of participants in our study.

(B) Spinal rootlets (in blue) identified with the deep‐learning‐based model in native space are normalized to template space using the V warp, and subsequently manually realigned (red) to fit the PAM50 template rootlets (green), generating the R warp in the process. R warp = Rootlet warp. The combination of V + R warps thus allows registration from native to template space based on the spinal rootlets, i.e., rootlet landmarks. To further examine interindividual variation in spinal levels among the participants, we extracted the length and center of gravity (COG) along the Z‐axis (Z‐COG) of each spinal segment. For computing the Z‐COG, the starting point of the scale was defined as the bottom of the T1 PAM50 spinal level, with measurements expressed in millimeters (mm). These length and Z‐axis position measures were compared to those of the PAM50 spinal levels using Wilcoxon signed‐rank tests after verifying the normality of data distribution with Shapiro–Wilk tests.

Image Preprocessing

All preprocessing steps were performed using the Spinal Cord Toolbox (SCT, versions 5.8–6.1) (De Leener et al. 2017), except for a few steps run with Oxford's FMRIB Software Library (FSL version 6.0) (Jenkinson et al. 2012) and AFNI (Cox 2012), with data converted to BIDS format using in‐house conversion software from DICOM to BIDS, based on dcm2niix software (v1.0.20220720).

Motion Correction

We first applied intrarun motion correction (SCT's sct_fmri_moco) using slice‐wise realignment, in a 20 mm diameter manually adjusted mask centered on the cross‐section of the spinal cord, to cover the cord (gray matter and white matter) and the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). Slice‐wise translations were regularized along the z‐axis using a second‐order polynomial function to avoid major jumps in signal between adjacent slices. The FNIRT algorithm (FSL) was then employed to correct for motion across the four runs by performing nonlinear registration of the mean corrected image of each run to the mean corrected image of the first run. As performing multiple interpolations can lead to an increasingly smoothed image with potentially lower tSNR, warping fields from each of these steps were saved and concatenated with later transformations to perform a single spline interpolation step (sct_apply_transfo).

Volume outliers were identified using FSL's fsl_motion_outliers on motion‐corrected images masked to the cord and CSF, with the DVARS metric (i.e., the root mean square intensity difference of volume N to volume N + 1, Power et al. 2014) set to the 75th percentile + 1.5× the interquartile range. On average, 8 ± 5 outliers per run (280 volumes) were detected, totaling 2.8% of the volumes.

Segmentation and Normalization

Registration of functional images to anatomical space was performed based on the mean motion‐corrected image from the first run. Through automatic segmentation and manual corrections, when necessary, a mask combining spinal cord and CSF was extracted from this functional image for each participant. Similarly, the cord and CSF segmentation was obtained from the anatomical image, enabling the generation of a co‐registration warp from functional to anatomical space, the two first co‐registration steps being based on segmentation masks and the last one on the images themselves (sct_register_multimodal function).

Normalization from anatomical space to PAM50 template space was performed using spinal cord segmentation masks and vertebral disc labels, resulting in an additional warping field.

The final interpolation step to register functional images to PAM50 template space (0.5 × 0.5 × 0.5 mm3) was achieved by concatenating slice‐wise motion correction warping fields with the obtained inter‐run registration, functional‐to‐anatomical, and anatomical‐to‐template warping fields, and applying these combined transformations to each slice of each volume of all four runs using spline interpolation.

Spatial Smoothing

An anisotropic three‐dimensional Gaussian kernel was applied with minimal smoothing in the x and y directions (1.5 × 1.5 mm2) and stronger smoothing in the z‐direction (6 mm). Anisotropic smoothing seemed more appropriate due to the anisotropic spatial resolution of the native images (1 × 1 × 4 mm3). The anisotropic spatial smoothing was confined to the cord area of the preprocessed and normalized images using a mask of the PAM50 template cord. This was achieved using AFNI's BlurInMask function with a Gaussian anisotropic kernel and a full width half maximum (FWHM) of 1.5 × 1.5 × 6 mm3. This procedure increases the SNR while minimizing contributions from the CSF signal (Eippert et al. 2017).

Quality Check of the Functional MR Data

We analyzed the quality of the data and considered two criteria for possible data exclusion at the individual level: the displacement amplitude between two consecutive volumes and the signal quality evaluated with the temporal Signal‐to‐Noise Ratio (tSNR) metric, which serves as a proxy for the noise level in the images.

In addition to excluding DVARS‐based motion outlier volumes, Framewise Displacement (FD), that is, volume‐to‐volume positional changes along the X and Y axes, was computed as an additional quality control metric to monitor possible excessive motion. A threshold of 0.2 mm was used, as this value corresponded to less than three SDs above the group mean in our data set (mean x‐FD: 0.094 ± 0.04 mm; mean y‐FD: 0.133 ± 0.04 mm) and has also been applied in previous spinal fMRI studies (Kinany et al. 2020). No participant exceeded the 0.2 mm threshold in both directions, and thus no participant was excluded based on this criterion.

Additionally, tSNR variation was computed in motion‐corrected but nonsmoothed images in template space, as smoothing artificially inflates tSNR without accurately reflecting signal quality (Triantafyllou et al. 2006). The mean tSNR was extracted within the spinal cord's gray matter, between spinal levels C4 and C8 (the functional regions of interest), for each run and participant. tSNR is calculated for each voxel as the mean signal over time divided by the SD over time. Then, the tSNR values for all voxels within the C4–C8 region of interest were averaged to provide a representative tSNR. Data were denoised using Nilearn's clean_img algorithm, applying the same nuisance regressors as in the GLM. This was done prior to computing the SD and tSNR, to provide a more accurate reflection of the final data quality.

Note that the population mean tSNR computed within only the gray matter (8.59 ± 0.09) or across the entire cord area (including gray and white matter) (8.31 ± 0.15) was very similar. These tSNR values aligned with those found for functional acquisition of the spinal cord in a motor‐task related fMRI study (Kinany et al. 2019: 7.92 ± 0.15). Since tSNR was computed from task‐based fMRI images where signal variance reflects both neural activity and noise, it was expected to be lower than the tSNR values typically reported in resting‐state fMRI studies (tSNR above 15 in Vahdat et al. 2020, around 26 in Kowalczyk et al. 2024). In addition, individual tSNR values were consistent across the population tested, with no participant exceeding the mean ±2 SDs. As no participant exhibited out‐of‐range motion or tSNR, all 24 participants who completed the session were selected for further analysis.

Data quality was finally assessed by averaging normalized images across all participants. The resulting image depicts a contrasted spinal cord with identifiable gray matter area, although we note an expected signal loss in intervertebral areas (see Figure S2).

Statistical Analysis

Physiological Noise Correction

Spinal cord imaging is sensitive to physiological noise, particularly from vascularization and surrounding organs (i.e., the heart and lungs). Breathing and blood pulsations can introduce nonneural variations in the BOLD signal, which must be carefully accounted for when scanning the cervical spinal cord. We modeled nuisance regressors using the CompCor approach (Behzadi et al. 2007) and RETROspective Image CORection (RETROICOR, Glover et al. 2000) procedures with TAPAS' PhysIO toolbox (Kasper et al. 2017) based on preprocessed functional time series.

For each participant, we extracted the first 15 principal components from a CSF mask, eroded to capture only nonneural fluctuations. Additionally, we modeled 18 nuisance regressors based on peripheral physiological recordings: six cardiac, eight respiratory, and four multiplicative regressors (Harvey et al. 2008; Tinnermann et al. 2021). In total, 33 physiological noise regressors were therefore extracted for each participant and run (except for some runs of five participants: their respiratory data were excluded due to poor quality).

Univariate Analysis

Univariate analyses were carried out using FSL's fMRI Expert Analysis Tool (FEAT). For each participant, the preprocessed images (i.e., normalized to the PAM50 template, smoothed, and high‐passed filtered at 128 s) were entered into a first statistical level of analysis, incorporating the runs as repeated measures. In this statistical model (FMRIB's Improved Linear Model with prewhitening), the onsets and durations of proprioceptive stimulation and NoVib conditions were modeled using a boxcar function convolved with a gaussian hemodynamic response function. Seven regressors of interest were thus modeled corresponding to the NoVib condition and the six stimulation conditions (Left and Right for each of the three arm levels tested: wrist, elbow, shoulder). Furthermore, the 33 physiological noise regressors, two movement parameters (X and Y), and all the volume outliers were implemented as regressors of non interest. As the NoVib condition was a fully specified experimental condition, identically structured to the six other conditions in our design, and served as a control condition, not a baseline or rest, the NoVib condition included a clearly defined onset and offset, an auditory cue (beep), the same number of repetitions, and matched duration. All seven conditions (six stimulation conditions plus NoVib) were separated by jittered ISIs, which minimized collinearity among regressors. This was confirmed by computing the rank of the final GLM design matrix (using numpy.linalg.matrix_rank), which verified that the matrix was full rank.

To refine the identification of proprioceptive projections in the ipsilateral cord, we created a first‐level contrast between right and left stimulation (i.e., Right > Left and Left > Right). We additionally contrasted each vibration condition to the NoVib condition (i.e., Left or Right Delt > NoVib, Biceps > NoVib, and Wrist > NoVib) to further explore the full activation pattern, including potential contralateral activations. For each participant, the four run‐level contrast maps were combined using a fixed‐effects model to obtain participant‐level contrasts of parameter estimates (e.g., stimulation > NoVib, Left > Right, and Right > Left for each stimulation site). For each contrast, these individual combined maps were concatenated across participants into a single 4D dataset, which served as input for the group‐level analysis using randomise. Nonparametric one‐sample t tests were conducted with Threshold‐Free Cluster Enhancement (TFCE; Smith and Nichols 2009; Winkler et al. 2016) and 10,000 permutations to produce group‐level statistical maps.

We obtained group‐level statistical maps corrected for multiple comparisons (Family‐Wise Error correction, pFWE‐corr ≤ 0.05) on a mask of our population's probabilistic spinal levels C4–C8. We also displayed uncorrected (puncorr ≤ 0.001) group‐level statistical maps, primarily for the general estimation of lateralization of the activity along the spine in all stimulation conditions > NoVib contrasts, or for visualization purposes in L > R and R > L contrasts.

We further conducted an in‐depth analysis of individuals' activation maps across their respective spinal levels and generated functional density maps to further examine the variability in participant BOLD responses. This analysis was designed to replicate, using fMRI, the approach traditionally used in electrophysiology to establish myotomal maps, where the presence of a muscle contraction evoked by nerve root stimulation is recorded for each participant at each tested spinal segment (Schirmer et al. 2011; London et al. 2022). For each participant and muscle vibration condition, we reported spinal levels containing cluster‐corrected activations (more than 5% of its voxels in a spinal level for the level to be counted). For each left versus right arm contrast, we calculated the number of participants showing activation within their respective C4–C8 spinal levels and projected this onto a probabilistic spinal cord map. This resulted in a density metric reflecting the proportion of participants having activated each spinal level, providing a comprehensive view of rostro‐caudal variability across the cohort. The analysis was based on individual t stat maps (one‐sample t tests; T > 2.6, puncorr < 0.005).

Laterality Index Computation

At the group level, we computed two standard laterality indices on the group‐level activation maps (TFCE, puncorr ≤ 0.001). We extracted the number of activated voxels within the different hemicords. To this end, the COG of the gray matter mask was extracted in each slice. Based on this midpoint voxel, the mask was divided into left and right, or ventral and dorsal hemicords.

At the participant level, given the variability of activation intensities in individual statistical maps, we used an alternative approach to computing laterality indices (Wilke and Schmithorst 2006). We ran the combined bootstrap/histogram analysis developed by Wilke and colleagues (Wilke and Lidzba 2007), which is less dependent on fixed thresholds and much less sensitive to participant outliers (Seghier 2008). This method selected a range of 20 different thresholds based on the individual map's minimum and maximum statistical values. At each given threshold, 25% of activated voxels were randomly chosen from each hemicord independently, and the standard laterality index was computed based on those voxel statistical values (LRindex previously defined). The random pick of 25% voxels was done 100 times on either side, leading up to 10,000 laterality indices computed per threshold, that is, 200,000 values given the 20 thresholds. From these values, an overall mean of these combinations was computed. This was done separately on activation maps for each participant's spinal levels C4 to C8. Group statistical analyses performed on individual laterality indices used nonparametric Wilcoxon signed‐rank tests, as the Shapiro–Wilk test showed nonnormal data distribution.

Results

Proprioceptive Illusion Salience Ratings

To evaluate the perceived direction and salience of movement illusions, we collected the participants' verbal rating on a scale ranging from 0 to 4 (with “0” = No illusion, “4” = illusion perfectly clear) at the end of each functional run (Figure 3). The analysis of individuals' reports consistently revealed that all participants experienced sensations of movement corresponding to the elongation of each stimulated muscle. Specifically, stimulation of the wrist flexor led to an illusion of hand extension, biceps stimulation resulted in elbow extension illusion, and anterior deltoid stimulation induced sensations of shoulder retropulsion and depression, characterized by a backward and downward illusory movement of the shoulder (Figure 3A). In detail, wrist flexors and biceps movement illusions occurred 100% of the time on both sides for all participants, while illusions for the left and right anterior deltoids were less frequent, averaging 84% (Figure 3A—top pictogram). Comparison of salience ratings between left and right arm stimulation revealed no significant differences at the wrist, elbow, or shoulder levels (Table S1). By considering only 84% of trials giving rise to an illusion during the deltoid stimulation, the salience reported by the participants was 2.44 ± 0.3 for the left anterior deltoid and 2.45 ± 0.4 for the right anterior deltoid. For the two upper limbs confounded, the salience of illusions evoked by vibration of the anterior deltoids was significantly lower than those elicited by the biceps (W(23) = 12, p < 0.001) and the wrist flexors (W(23) = 12, p < 0.001), while the salience of illusions did not significantly differ between the biceps and the wrist flexors (W(23) = 144, p = 0.88).

Interindividual Spinal Level Disparities

The analysis of the individual spinal level maps computed by a deep learning‐based model applied on T2w Space images (Valošek et al. 2024) revealed a slight interindividual variation of spinal level positions despite a good alignment of vertebrae (Figure 4A). Disparities in individual spinal levels were assessed in terms of both length and Z‐COG with respect to the PAM50 template space. As illustrated in Figure 4C, the length of the C7 spinal level significantly deviated from the reference PAM50 level lengths (Figure 4C). Specifically, the C7 spinal level within the present population was significantly smaller compared to its PAM50 counterpart (W(23) = 35, p = 0.007). However, for other spinal segments, the mean difference in length from the PAM50 levels did not reach statistical significance (Wilcoxon tests, p > 0.05).

Group Level Activity for the Left and Right Arm Stimulation: Lateral and Dorso‐Ventral Organization

Left–right and dorso‐ventral patterns of proprioceptive‐related activity in the spinal cord across stimulation sites were first assessed using group‐level activation maps from the six contrasts comparing each muscle vibration condition to the NoVib condition. To examine the overall spatial distribution of activations for each arm, the three contrasts of interest were combined for each side: ([Left deltoid > NoVib] U [Left biceps > NoVib] U [Left wrist flexor > NoVib]), and similarly for the right arm. The resulting activations are presented in Figure 5A (puncorr ≤ 0.001) and in Table S2 where T‐statistics are also reported. This group‐level result revealed a clear lateralization of activity toward the hemicord ipsilateral to the vibration task. To further characterize this lateralization at each stimulation site, we computed a laterality index on each statistical map, based on the ratio of the number of activated voxels in the right versus left hemicords over the total number of voxels (Figure 5B). The Left > NoVib contrasts showed preferential activation in the left hemicord, with negative laterality indices for the anterior deltoid (−1.0) and biceps (−0.37), except for the wrist flexor, which exhibited a neutral laterality index (−0.02). Conversely, all the Right > NoVib contrasts exhibited predominant right lateralization, with positive laterality indices for the anterior deltoid (0.22), biceps (1.0), and wrist flexor (0.72).

Discussion

In this study, we mapped spinal proprioceptive neural circuits capitalising on recent advances in the spinal fMRI field combined with upper limb proprioceptive stimulation. We used muscle tendon vibration, a motionless task, targeting the wrist flexors, biceps, and anterior deltoid muscles of both arms, to precisely stimulate muscle afferents with minimal recruitment of neighboring muscle fibers in 24 healthy individuals. The present findings showed distinct proprioceptive‐specific activation patterns depending on the muscle and arm stimulated, consistent with established myotome maps. In addition to the primary ipsilateral and rostro‐caudal distribution of spinal activations, substantial interparticipant variability was observed, stressing the need to refine functional activity localization at the individual level for clinical application. These results demonstrate that spinal cord fMRI can effectively capture proprioceptive activation patterns, with potential fundamental and clinical implications.

Muscle Tendon Vibration to Uncover the Spinal Proprioceptive Network in vivo

To stimulate the proprioceptive system in vivo in humans, we used muscle tendon vibration, a well‐established method to preferentially activate primary muscle spindle afferents (Kavounoudias et al. 2023). So far, the investigation of spinal proprioceptive circuitry using fMRI has been overlooked and limited to passive movement paradigms (Kornelsen and Stroman 2004; Valsasina et al. 2008), except for two studies using muscle tendon vibration on a few participants (Rowald et al. 2022; Hernandez‐Charpak et al. 2025). These studies were faced with the difficulty of maintaining a consistent direction and speed of movement while limiting motion of the spine induced by the experimenter, who probably mobilizes several muscles around the joint and activates cutaneous afferents, often resulting in bilateral activity with a wide rostro‐caudal distribution: from C5 to C8 during passive wrist flexion (Valsasina et al. 2008) and from L1 to L5 during passive feet pedaling (Kornelsen and Stroman 2004). In contrast, the MR‐compatible muscle tendon vibration device offers controlled proprioceptive stimulation while the participant remains stationary, preferentially targeting Ia afferents from a specific muscle and inducing movement illusions in the direction of the muscle stretch (Kavounoudias et al. 2023). In the present study, this approach eliminated the need to exclude participants due to excessive motion.

The effectiveness of the tendon vibration applied in this study was confirmed by real‐time frequency monitoring (optimal range: 55–75 Hz) and by the participants' reports of illusory movement sensations, felt more than 80% of the time across all muscle sites. As expected, all movement illusions reported were directed toward the elongation of the stimulated muscle. Illusions were consistently strong (mean rating of 3.5 out of 4) for the two wrist flexors and the two biceps, while the anterior deltoid stimulation had a lower occurrence (84% of trials) and salience (around 2.5), likely due to the conflicting supine position affecting proprioceptive messages of shoulder retropulsion. Nonetheless, all participants experienced movement illusions in at least one trial of each condition, supporting effective recruitment of proprioceptive afferents at the peripheral level, even if it did not always result in movement perception due to other conflicting information.

A Predominantly Lateralized and Ventral Organization of Proprioceptive Activity Patterns

The spatial organization of vibration‐induced activity patterns found in the present study supports the proprioceptive origin of the responses. Five out of six stimulated muscles consistently displayed a stronger activity in the spinal cord horn ipsilateral to the stimulated body side, with main activation clusters in the ventral horn, particularly evident in the left/right contrast analyses. This lateralization to the ipsilateral hemicord is consistent with recent fMRI studies involving unilateral upper‐limb tactile stimulation (Weber et al. 2020) or motor tasks (Weber et al. 2016; Hemmerling et al. 2023), although earlier studies mostly obtained bilateral activations. The present findings are in line with the anatomical organization of the somatosensory system, in which sensory afferents enter the spinal cord on the same side as their peripheral origin. By minimizing overlapping activity due to intrinsic spinal connectivity, the left/right contrasts likely enhanced the detection of ipsilateral‐specific projections of proprioceptive afferents.

Another compelling finding is the predominant ventral distribution of activations. Given that BOLD signal changes in the spinal cord are known to be coupled with synaptic activity (Piché et al. 2017; Wu et al. 2019), it is reasonable to speculate that ventral horn activations may reflect recruitment of monosynaptic proprio‐motoneuronal synapses. Indeed, proprioceptive afferents enter the ipsilateral dorsal horn and partly project toward motoneurons of the homonymous muscle, forming the myotatic loop (Pierrot‐Desseilligny and Burke 2005).

Although electromyographic activity was not recorded during vibratory stimulation, previous studies have shown that muscle tendon vibration, which induces illusory movement in relaxed participants, is not accompanied by concomitant contraction of the stimulated muscle, suggesting that Ia afferent activation results in excitatory postsynaptic activity without triggering a myotatic reflex response. Conversely, small involuntary isometric motor contractions in the antagonist muscle can occur during kinesthetic illusions (Roll et al. 1980). Thus, the ventral activations observed may reflect the complex interplay between proprioceptive and motor signals involved in kinesthetic perception. However, a case study involving a patient with a complete spinal lesion revealed that passive movement of leg segments and tendon vibration applied to the same muscles elicited similar activation patterns (Rowald et al. 2022). This finding underscores that both vibration and passive movement may engage comparable spinal circuits, independent of influence from higher central nervous structures. Future studies comparing isometric muscle contraction and proprioceptive stimulation tasks coupled with electromyographic recordings may help to disentangle these sensorimotor activities.

On the other hand, the exploratory analysis of uncorrected data revealed dorsal activations that may be partly due to the activation of cutaneous mechanoreceptors in contact with the vibrator. The dorsal activation clusters at C6–C7 during wrist flexor stimulation (Figures 6 and S5) could be associated with the dermatome covering the external edge of the ventral side of the wrist (Lee et al. 2008). Both slowly and rapidly adapting cutaneous mechanoreceptors are sensitive to mechanical vibration applied perpendicularly to their receptive fields (Lundstrom 1986; Ribot‐Ciscar et al. 1989). Given the projections of tactile afferents within the dorsal horn and intermediate zone of the spinal cord, it is likely that related spinal activity predominantly occurs in these regions. High‐field spinal fMRI at 9.4 T has shown that low‐frequency tactile vibration (8 Hz) on digits of nonhuman primates primarily activates laminae IV to VI in the ipsilateral dorsal horn of the corresponding spinal segment (Yang et al. 2015). However, as these secondary activations were detected only at an uncorrected threshold (puncorr ≤ 0.001), this interpretation remains to be confirmed by future study, ideally through direct comparison of pure tactile versus proprioceptive stimulation performed on the same participants.

Proprioceptive Stimulation Revealed Spinal Level Organization

The primary objective of the present study was to identify proprioceptive afferent projections to the spinal cord and compare the resulting projectome maps with established myotomal distributions. To this end, we directly contrasted left and right arm stimulation to minimize potential confounding contralateral activity arising from bilateral interneuronal connections within the spinal cord. This approach allowed us to focus specifically on ipsilateral activations, thereby testing whether fMRI can detect proprioceptive inputs at their point of entry into the spinal cord. Notably, the observed activations were not only ipsilateral but also precisely localized within the ventral horns of consistent spinal levels corresponding to known myotomal organization. By stimulating three distinct muscles in both arms, we observed a clear rostro–caudal organization of spinal activations where stimulation of proximal muscles (anterior deltoids) elicited activation in rostral cervical levels (C5), biceps stimulation recruited mid‐cervical levels (C7), and distal wrist stimulation resulted in activations at more caudal levels (C8). These robust activation patterns corrected for multiple comparisons are consistent with classical myotome maps, which indicate that rostral cervical levels innervate the upper arms, while caudal cervical levels contribute to hand and finger innervation (Lee et al. 2008; Schirmer et al. 2011; Cramer and Darby 2017).

Notably, the present task‐related study showed very focal segmental activations at the group level, in contrast to earlier spinal fMRI studies that reported activation spanning multiple spinal levels during sensory stimulation (Weber et al. 2020) or motor tasks (Maieron et al. 2007; Weber et al. 2016; Kinany et al. 2019; Hemmerling et al. 2023). One possible explanation for the broader activation across adjacent levels observed at the group level could be the substantial interindividual variability in spinal cord anatomy (Cadotte et al. 2015; Frostell et al. 2016; Mendez et al. 2021; Nunès et al. 2023). In the present study, we may have mitigated that risk by defining a cohort‐specific probabilistic spinal level map, which improves spatial alignment across individuals.

Furthermore, the interindividual variability observed in the present fMRI‐based density maps is broadly consistent with that reported in intraoperative myotomal mapping studies using direct nerve root stimulation (London et al. 2022; Schirmer et al. 2011). Despite differences in methodology and a smaller sample size (N = 24 participants against N = 57 participants and 495 cervical stimulations in Schirmer et al. 2011), the proportions of participants showing activation in specific spinal levels in response to muscle vibration were comparable to the response frequencies reported with electrical stimulation. For instance, Schirmer et al. (2011) found that nerve root stimulation at the C4 or C5 level elicited anterior deltoid activation in 34%–36% of cases across stimulation and participants, while in our study, anterior deltoid vibration led to activation in 22% and 21% of participants at C4 and C5, respectively. Similarly, wrist flexors were activated in 27% of cases by electrical stimulation at C7 in Schirmer et al.'s study, whereas in our study, vibration‐related activation was most prevalent at C8 in 27% of participants (See Table S5).

More generally, clinical observations have extensively documented the variability of muscle innervation across patients, as well as discrepancies between individual anatomical organization and classical myotome maps. These studies have provided evidence that a given muscle can receive innervation from several root levels (Phillips and Park 1991), that the muscles affected by a specific cervical lesion do not systematically correspond to those predicted by the standard myotome map (Levin et al. 1996), and that segmental innervation can be asymmetrical between limbs (Phillips and Park 1991; London et al. 2022; Georgoulis and Sindou 2020), whereas standard myotomal maps typically aggregate data from both sides of the body.

Taken together, the convergence of the present findings with clinical observations and intraoperative electrophysiological data supports the potential of spinal fMRI for mapping the functional proprio‐motor organization of the spinal cord at both the group and individual levels.

Conclusion

Muscle tendon vibration combined with spinal cord fMRI allows for the identification of proprio‐motor circuits without introducing artifacts from actual movement (passive or active). By accounting for individual variability in spinal levels, we provided a comprehensive view of the functional organization of the cervical spinal cord at both the group and individual levels. Based on the present approach, spatially distributed patterns of activation associated with complex movements could be further explored by combining simultaneous muscle vibrations, eliciting writing or reaching movement illusions. Additionally, it should drive the development of noninvasive tools for diagnosing the integrity of sensorimotor circuits following medullary or peripheral lesions.
