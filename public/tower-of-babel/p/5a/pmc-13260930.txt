Machine and deep learning-based forecast of Tehran’s accelerating nighttime surface heat island crisis
Authors: Motahhareh Zargari, Sarmad Ghader, Isidro A Pérez, M Ángeles García
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe nighttime Surface Urban Heat Island (SUHI) phenomenon in the metropolis of Tehran poses severe risks to urban climate and sustainability, representing a potential crisis for the entire Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region. A critical research gap persists in long-term predictive modeling for rapidly urbanizing arid regions like Tehran, particularly using advanced hybrid artificial intelligence (AI) architectures that integrate spatial and temporal learning. This study addresses this gap by developing and benchmarking a novel ensemble of six hybrid models, including three machine learning (ML) and three deep learning (DL) models to forecast nocturnal SUHI in Tehran through 2030. The methodology leverages a 22-year MODIS Aqua LST time series, processed with a rigorous spatiotemporal gap-filling process, to train models including innovative combinations such as U-Net with Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks. The primary objective is to provide a robust, actionable forecasting framework aligned with Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs). Key findings indicate that parametric normal distribution models suggest temporal land surface temperature (LST) will rise by more than 2°C across four study areas (Tehran, Tehran Downtown, the suburbs, and the combined Tehran–suburbs), a trend further supported by non-parametric approaches, which also show that Tehran Downtown has the highest spatial LST. R-squared (R2), root mean square error (RMSE), and mean absolute error (MAE) were used to assess and compare the three ML and three DL models. The optimal model (U-Net + LSTM) projects a sustained annual SUHI intensity (SUHII) increase of 0.08 °C, potentially reaching a mean of 5.96 °C by 2030—a rise of 0.11–0.39 °C above historical levels. Spatial analyses of SUHI, supported by longitudinal and latitudinal profiles, reveal that Tehran is transitioning toward a “super-nighttime SUHI” state. Moreover, statistical and practical analyses confirm that warming is accelerating and will be disproportionately concentrated in the extreme upper percentiles, significantly amplifying the frequency and severity of hot nights. The broader significance of this work lies in its proof-of-concept for an AI-driven urban climate forecasting framework, which provides urban planners and policymakers with evidence-based insights for targeted mitigation and climate adaptation strategies, thereby supporting the development of resilient urban systems in Tehran and similar metropolitan areas worldwide.Keywords: Nighttime Surface Tehran Heat Island, Machine Learning, Deep LearningSubject terms: Climate sciences, Environmental sciences, Environmental social sciences

Introduction

In the 21st century, cities have become the center of numerous topical environmental challenges1, and one of the most critical local climate concerns is the Surface Urban Heat Island (SUHI) phenomenon2,3, whose effects are extremely detrimental to the sustainability of metropolitan areas. SUHI is characterized by an ‘dome’ of temperature4,5 or ‘island’6 reflecting the hotter urban core and the cooler surrounding rural areas, particularly at night7. Numerous studies have been conducted on this phenomenon and its associated effects8–11. Simply put, the dramatic rise in the global population has become one of the defining challenges for construction and urban development in the modern era12–15, with the emergence and intensification of SUHI representing one of its most critical consequences. The rate of urbanization—and the expansion of urban populations— continues to accelerate. Today, more than 55% of the world’s population resides in urban areas. This figure stands in stark contrast to 1950, when just 30%—around 750 million people—lived in cities, while the remaining two-thirds inhabited rural regions16,17. Accounting for both natural growth and rural-to-urban migration18–20, an estimated 47% of the world’s population lived in urban centers by 2000, and this share was projected to account for 55% in 201816. Further, projections suggest that by 2030, approximately 60% of the world’s population will be urban dwellers, accompanied by a growing number of cities exceeding 5 million inhabitants21. Some countries are expected to experience more than a fivefold increase in population by mid-century22. By that time, it is projected that more than one-third of the world’s population will live in cities with over half a million residents23. According to the United Nations Development Programme (UNDP), Africa and Asia will remain the most rapidly urbanizing and fastest-growing regions24, and Iran reflects this broader global trend. In Iran, its urban population was 64.20% in 2000 and rose to 75.94% by 2019. Projections indicate that this share will continue to grow steadily, with urbanization rates rising further by 203016.

Since the 1920s, urbanization in Iran has been highly centralized around primate cities such as Tehran. Despite decentralization measures introduced in the 2000s to promote a more balanced distribution of population, Tehran has continued to experience rapid growth and increasing population concentration. Currently, the city is 93.85% urbanized25. Tehran, with a population exceeding 15 million26, stands as Iran’s largest political and economic capital and experiences the nighttime SUHI phenomenon to a significant degree27,28. Several factors exacerbate this effect, including rising surface temperature29, extensive impervious infrastructure30, and limited vegetation cover31. Together, these issues paint a stark picture of Tehran’s urban trajectory. The city’s residents face a gradual decline in quality of life, creating profound challenges for urban resilience and casting doubt on the sustainability of its future32.

Consequently, the intensification of Tehran’s SUHI has led to a wide range of socio-economic, environmental, and public health challenges. These include, but are not limited to, accelerated climate change33,34, excessive water and energy consumption35–39, worsening air pollution40–42, heightened health risks43–45, ecological and environmental degradation46,47, declining climate quality48, intensified thermal discomfort49,50, and increasingly erratic precipitation patterns51,52.

To understand this challenge, over the past two decades, the SUHI phenomenon and remote sensing studies of metropolitan Tehran have drawn considerable scholarly attention27,28 and form the backbone of the present research. In the first of these studies, the authors investigated both daytime and nighttime SUHI in Tehran and its surrounding areas using data from MODIS Aqua, Sentinel-3, and Landsat 8 satellites. They concluded that MODIS land surface temperature (LST) products are the most reliable for analyzing Tehran’s nighttime SUHI compared to other available datasets27. In the subsequent study, examining the climatology of Tehran’s SUHI, the authors analyzed 19 years of MODIS satellite data (2003–2021) and demonstrated that nighttime data provide a more robust and consistent method for detecting and characterizing SUHI in Tehran than MODIS Aqua daytime LST. Importantly, this study highlighted that Tehran does not display a classic SUHI pattern with sharply defined temperature contrasts, largely due to its mountainous geography and widespread suburban sprawl. Instead, it exhibits a pronounced and statistically significant warming trend, with a LST increase of 0.74 °C per decade—amounting to an overall rise of 1.48 °C during the study period28. This rapid warming suggests that Tehran’s heating rate is unprecedented and cannot be attributed solely to seasonal or summer-related temperature fluctuations53.

To address these challenges, researchers have increasingly turned to advanced analytical approaches—such as machine learning (ML), deep learning (DL), and remote sensing—to investigate and model SUHI dynamics54–58. This local progress reflects a broader, transformative trend in environmental informatics, where hybrid methodologies integrating signal-processing techniques with core artificial intelligence (AI) models are becoming the standard for high-accuracy forecasting.

For instance, decomposition methods (e.g., VMD/EMD) combined with ML models have drastically improved lake temperature predictions59, while wavelet-transform-enhanced ML/DL models have set new benchmarks in drought forecasting60,61. These successes underscore a key principle: tackling complex geoscientific processes like SUHI requires moving beyond standalone algorithms toward sophisticated, multi-stage frameworks designed to extract and model multi-scale spatiotemporal patterns.

This principle is evident in Tehran’s SUHI research. Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks have been used to enhance LST resolution62, while hybrid models like Wavelet-BiLSTM excel in forecasting air pollutants63,64. Other approaches include evolutionary OA-LSTM for predicting PM2.565, GA-integrated GTWR models for summer LST66, CA-Markov models for projecting future LST via land cover change29, Random Forest (RF) for identifying key UHI drivers58 and ML model for investigating SUHI67.

Therefore, the application of hybrid ML and DL models introduces advanced techniques for analyzing dynamically changing geo-environmental systems and holds significant promise for forecasting SUHI36, and satellite observations enable the measurement of SUHI at multiple scales68,69, and allow for precise spatial delineation of temperature variations across different urban domains.

These analytical efforts are crucial because the United Nations (UN) Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs)—particularly Goal 11 (Sustainable Cities and Communities)70,71 and Goal 13 (Climate Action)72,73—provide a strategic framework for building fair, resilient, and sustainable urban environments by 203074. These goals align closely with the urgent need to address rising urban temperatures and advance innovative approaches to urban planning and governance75. Accordingly, the present moment offers a realistic strategic opportunity to raise awareness among urban managers and to propose actionable solutions that support the UN’s 2030 vision for sustainable development. Research highlights a suite of practical measures that can be implemented in cities such as Tehran during and after 2030. However, given the accelerating nighttime SUHI, projecting a fully sustainable urban center for Tehran by 2040 may be overly optimistic76. Instead, focusing planning efforts on the nearer-term period of 2027–2030 is more rational and achievable. The 2030 vision is transformative because it encourages cities like Tehran to develop targeted, data-driven interventions to mitigate the SUHI effect and enhance urban resilience75.

Despite these advancements, a critical gap remains. Previous research has primarily focused on LST downscaling62, short-term prediction66, or indirect projection via land cover29. In other words, no study has conducted long-term, direct forecasting of nocturnal SUHI intensity (SUHII) using a benchmarked ensemble of purpose-built hybrid DL architectures. Thus, a critical research gap exists in the lack of a robust spatiotemporal framework for quantitatively predicting future nighttime SUHI at the urban scale. Addressing this gap is essential for formulating effective strategies aligned with SDGs 11 and 13. Such a predictive capability directly supports SDG 11 by providing the data needed for resilient urban planning—enabling the identification of heat hotspots, optimization of green spaces, and prioritization of cooling interventions. Simultaneously, it advances SDG 13 by offering a tool for local climate action, allowing for the evaluation of emission reduction scenarios and the assessment of urban heat adaptation policies.

To bridge this gap, the present study contributes to this goal by providing a decision-support framework. The study’s core innovation is a novel hybrid ML-DL framework for predicting Tehran’s SUHI. Unlike prior studies that used ML or DL separately, this hybrid approach uniquely combines their strengths—deep learning’s pattern recognition with classical models’ efficiency—to overcome individual limitations like overfitting or an inability to model complex spatio-temporal dependencies. This not only improves forecast accuracy but also advances methodological knowledge for modeling complex urban environments.

Beyond a mere technical application, its novelty lies in designing a targeted solution for Tehran’s specific data challenges. It also delivers practical innovation by directly linking model outputs to SDGs (SDGs 11 and 13), transforming findings into an actionable decision-support tool for planners. This enables hotspot identification, scenario simulation, and intervention assessment, providing a concrete step toward climate-resilient, sustainable urban planning. Thus, this work provides a tailored comparative framework, advancing urban climate methodology from description to actionable long-term prediction.

Methods

Study area

Iran, located in Western Asia, is the second most populous country in the Middle East and faces several critical environmental challenges, including air pollution, water scarcity and quality impairment, mismanagement of municipal and industrial waste, and the intensifying impacts of climate change77. Tehran, the nation’s capital and primary economic hub, is a megacity with a population exceeding 15 million and a daily traffic flow of more than 20 million trips26,78. Covering an area of approximately 730 km²79, it is not only Iran’s largest and most important metropolis but also one of the largest urban centers in the Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region. Despite its moderate population density relative to its size, Tehran is subject to severe climatic stresses. The city’s climatic variability is shaped by distinct geographical features: its southern districts are predominantly hot and dry, while the northern districts are comparatively cold and dry. Tehran lies within a network of valleys and is encircled by mountain ranges rising between 1,000 m and 3,800 m to the north, northwest, east, and southeast. These mountains block the inflow of humid winds and contribute to the accumulation of air pollutants over the city. To the south, Tehran gradually transitions into desert terrain26,80. Tehran is situated at an elevation ranging from 1,050 m to 1,800 m above sea level, with surrounding mountain peaks forming a distinctive topographic landscape. Its topographical variability— characterized by steep elevations in the north and east and broad plains in the south and west—has historically directed the city’s spatial expansion, particularly toward the southern plains and areas closer to the desert margin. The surrounding landscapes consist of sparse shrublands and barren fields or rangelands. Climatically, Tehran is classified as a continental cold semi-arid zone. The city experiences significant thermal extremes, with recorded maximum temperatures reaching 43 °C and minimums dropping to − 15 °C, placing Tehran in a transitional range between semi-arid and cold continental climates (Fig. 1)81.

The designated study area encompasses the Greater Tehran metropolitan region, including administrative boundaries of Tehran and surrounding peripheral lands. Data preprocessing and gap-filling methodology

After the daily temperature data for the 22-year period (2003 to 2024) were obtained, a series of preprocessing steps were conducted using Climate Data Operators (CDO)85. These steps included conversion from daily to annual averages and from Kelvin to Celsius. Subsequently, the Python (version 3.11.0) code for LST and SUHI forecasting was developed in the Jupyter IDLE, incorporating the following considerations.

Quality assurance filtering (Bitmasking)

The MODIS QC_Night layer provides per-pixel quality information in an 8-bit unsigned integer format. Following the official MODIS LST User’s Guide, we applied a conservative bitmasking strategy to retain only the highest quality LST values. Pixels were kept if and only if the last two least significant bits (Bits 0–1) equaled 00, which signifies “LST produced, good quality, not necessary to examine more detailed QA”. All other pixels were discarded and treated as missing data. This step resulted in a quality-controlled but gapped dataset, with NaN values representing cloud-contaminated or low-quality observations.

Spatiotemporal gap-filling algorithm

To reconstruct a continuous spatiotemporal LST field, we developed a sequential two-step interpolation framework that respects the distinct nature of spatial and temporal autocorrelation in environmental data.

Spatial interpolation via ordinary kriging

Two spatiotemporal interpolation techniques were applied to address missing data. Spatial gaps were filled using ordinary kriging (OK), while temporal gaps for each pixel were filled using linear interpolation. This step was essential for compensating for data losses caused by cloud cover and the reduced frequency of satellite observations during the COVID-19 period. OK is a best linear unbiased estimator (BLUE) for spatial interpolation86 and is a geostatistical method that estimates unknown values based on the spatial correlation structure of observed data. Unlike inverse distance weighting (IDW) methods, which rely solely on distance-based weights, OK derives its weights from the spatial covariance function. This approach produces more statistically robust and unbiased estimates by explicitly accounting for the spatial dependence among sample points when predicting values at unsampled locations87.

For each daily timestamp (t) containing missing pixels, spatial interpolation was performed using OK, a BLUE in geostatistics. The spatial covariance structure was modeled through a variogram via OK, providing both interpolated values and estimation variance. A linear variogram model was selected, which is suitable for the relatively small spatial domain (≈ 50 km extent). The interpolation was implemented using the pykrige.rk.Krige module in Python. This step generated a spatially complete daily LST snapshot but left temporal gaps at pixels that were persistently cloudy across multiple days.

Temporal Interpolation via Linear Spline

For each individual pixel location (i, j), the remaining temporal gaps in its 22-year time series were filled using piecewise linear interpolation for 1D. This method was selected based on three specific justifications:

Physical Plausibility: The diurnal and seasonal cycle of LST, especially at night, exhibits gradual changes. Over short temporal gaps (1–5 days, which constitute > 95% of our gaps), the assumption of linear change between two known high-quality observations is physically reasonable and computationally parsimonious.

Dataset Characteristics: The MYD11A1 product has a high temporal revisit frequency (daily observations). The average gap length in our filtered dataset was 2.3 days, making higher-order interpolation methods (e.g., cubic splines) unnecessary and prone to overfitting.

Computational Efficiency and Reproducibility: Linear interpolation is algorithmically simple, deterministic, and fast—a critical consideration when processing over 10,000 pixel time series. The selection of an appropriate interpolation method depends on factors such as dataset characteristics, desired accuracy, computational efficiency, and geographic complexity88. Linear interpolation demonstrates strong compatibility with dynamic temporal variations over time. Conversely, it also provides high classification accuracy and enables better discrimination of land use classes89.

It is assumed that LST changes linearly, resulting in similar variations for adjacent features with similar characteristics. Consequently, missing LST values can be recovered using data obtained at another time by establishing a linear relationship with the nearest matching pixels90. MODIS satellite data are acquired on a regular grid with a 1 km spatial resolution. Studies have shown that temporal linear interpolation using Aqua satellite data yields the best performance in reconstructing MODIS LST compared to other methods90. Therefore, temporal linear interpolation was employed in the present study.

Final dataset

The output of this process was a spatiotemporally continuous 3D array of dimensions [Time = 8035 days, Latitude = 55 pixels, Longitude = 85 pixels], representing nightly LST over Tehran from 2003 to 2024, ready for feature extraction and model training.

Train-validation-test split with temporal blocking

To ensure a realistic evaluation of forecasting capability, a chronological split was employed, strictly preserving the temporal order of observations—a critical guard against data leakage. The training set (80% of data) was used for model parameter training, while the test set (20%) was reserved exclusively for the final evaluation, simulating a true out-of-sample forecast. No random shuffling was applied. This split strategy tests the model’s ability to extrapolate trends into the near future, which represents the core objective of this study.

Input data structure for different model types

For traditional ML models, Input shape was (n_samples, 1), representing the scaled year. For DL hybrid models, Input was reshaped to (n_samples, timesteps = 1, features = 1) to fit the sequential input structure expected by LSTM layers, where timesteps = 1 signifies we are using a single time step (the current year) per prediction.

For a comprehensive representation of the environment, remote sensing data were integrated into the model. For instance, the NVDI layer (1 km) (https://appeears.earthdatacloud.nasa.gov)91 was used to capture vegetation cover, and additional topographic (NOAA 2 min) (https://www.ncei.noaa.gov)92 and land use/land cover (LULC) data (Sentinel-2 10 m) (https://www.arcgis.com)93 were incorporated. The final, standardized, and preprocessing multi-source dataset was then used as input for the AI models. The standardisation process improved predictive accuracy, ensuring that the intelligent models could better rely on environmental predictive data for decision-making under real-world constraints.

SUHI evolution is governed by complex, non-linear interactions between long-term climate trends (temporal component) and heterogeneous urban morphology (spatial/feature component). Single-model approaches often fail to capture this duality. Hybrid models were therefore developed to explicitly combine strengths: LSTMs to model temporal sequences and dependencies, and CNNs (U-Net) or Dense Networks (ANN/DNN) to learn complex, non-linear transformations of the input feature space. This architecture is inspired by recent successes in spatiotemporal forecasting in environmental sciences.

Training protocol for DL models

All neural network models were implemented using TensorFlow 2.8 / Keras API. They were trained for a fixed 200 epochs with a batch size of 16 using the Adam optimizer (learning rate = 0.001, default β parameters). Early stopping was not applied to ensure all models were trained for an identical number of epochs, guaranteeing a fair comparison. Training was performed on an NVIDIA V100 GPU, and the final model weights from the last epoch were used for forecasting.

Hybrid model architecture

Baseline and hybrid ML models: MLR, MLR + RF, MLR + XGB

Regression models are widely used due to their interpretability, low computational cost, and strong generalizability, making them powerful tools across various domains94,95 (Fig. 3). Multiple linear regression (MLR), a fundamental statistical approach, determines the optimal linear relationship between a dependent variable and multiple independent variables96. Unlike simple linear regression, MLR accounts for the combined effects of two or more predictors, estimating their influence on the dependent variable through a linear function.

MLR has been extensively applied in climatology research because of its simplicity and minimal computational requirements94,95. In this study, MLR was used as a baseline model against which more complex models were evaluated. In addition, hybrid approaches combining MLR with Random Forest (RF) and XGBoost (XGB) have been investigated in the literature, offering potential improvements in predictive performance by capturing nonlinear interactions while retaining the interpretability of MLR97,98.

The development of RF has remained a crucial non-parametric advancement, extending the capabilities of Classification and Regression Trees (CART) to improve prediction and estimation accuracy99,100. RF combines the classical CART algorithm with random feature selection and bootstrap aggregating (bagging), allowing each regression tree to grow to its full depth before aggregating their outputs into a low-bias ensemble101,102. Bagging and random feature selection mitigate tree-to-tree correlation within the ensemble, thereby enhancing generalization performance103. The combination of MLR with RF (MLR + RF) has been shown to be particularly effective in addressing the issues related to error variance104,105. This ensemble model combines predictions from MLR and a RF regressor by taking their unweighted average. The RF component uses n_estimators = 100 and max_features=’sqrt’. The final output is the ensemble mean of both models’ predictions.

Gradient Boosting Decision Trees (GBDT) enhance both prediction accuracy and stability by sequentially constructing an ensemble of models, where each subsequent model corrects the residual errors of its predecessor. XGBoost, a highly optimized implementation of GBDT, offers significant improvements in computational efficiency and predictive power through algorithmic enhancements, regularization mechanisms, and parallel processing capabilities106,107. The integration of MLR with XGBoost (MLR + XGBoost) further improves predictive accuracy, benefiting from both the interpretability of MLR and the strong nonlinear modeling capacity of XGB108,109. This ensemble model averages predictions from MLR and an XGBoost regressor. The XGBoost component is configured with n_estimators = 100 and learning_rate = 0.1. Like the MLR + RF ensemble, the output is the mean of both models’ predictions.

Hybrid DL models

To capture the spatiotemporal dynamics of SUHI, this study employs three hybrid DL architectures. All three models merge spatial feature extractors with a temporal sequence model (LSTM). This combination allows them to learn from both the spatial structure and temporal progression of the data, which is essential for accurate forecasting of geospatial phenomena such as LST and the SUHI effect (Fig. 4). The models were optimized using the Adam optimizer (with a learning rate of 0.001) and trained to minimize a combined loss of root mean squared error (RMSE) and mean absolute error (MAE).

ANN + LSTM

The Artificial Neural Network (ANN) + Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) model is composed of three layers: an input layer, a hidden layer, and an output layer. The input layer incorporates a LSTM network, which is widely used in sequence data analysis due to its ability to capture long-term dependencies110. The hidden and output layers are structured as a simple ANN. The combination of ANN and LSTM is particularly advantageous for modeling complex nonlinear relationships111,112. The implemented hybrid model consists of two parallel branches. The first branch is a dense layer (64 units, ReLU activation) and the second is an LSTM layer (32 units). Their outputs are concatenated and passed to a final dense output layer (1 unit).

U-Net + LSTM

In this research, the U-Net model employs Conv1D layers for spatial feature extraction, while LSTM layers handle the temporal sequence modeling. The concatenation of CNN- and LSTM- derived features allows for the simultaneous representation of both spatial and temporal patterns. U-Net113 is a sophisticated architecture designed for image segmentation and has proven to be highly effective for spatial feature extraction114. LSTM networks, on the other hand, are well known for their ability to learn sequential patterns and predict subsequent events115. They excel at capturing complex temporal dependencies, accommodating nonlinear and dynamics variations, and adapting to morphological changes in the data. Consequently, the integration of U-Net and LSTM has been widely adopted in research for predictive modeling, demonstrating strong performance across diverse applications116,117. The model combines a Conv1D-based feature extractor (with 64 and 128 filters) for spatial analysis with an LSTM path (64 units) for temporal modeling. Extracted features are pooled, concatenated, and processed by a dense layer (128 units, ReLU activation) before the final Dense output layer (1 unit).

DNN + LSTM

A Deep Neural Network (DNN) is a type of ANN that incorporates multiple hidden layers and is trained using the supervised learning technique of backpropagation. A standard feedforward neural network consists of an input layer, one or more hidden layers, and an output layer118. Each neuron not only receives input signals but also applies a nonlinear activation function. When a network contains more than one hidden layer, it is considered “deep,” giving rise to the term DNN119. LSTM networks, a specialized type of Recurrent Neural Network (RNN), were developed to address the long-term dependency problem by selectively retaining relevant past information120. Each LSTM neuron contains three gate structures—an input gate, an output gate, and a forget gate—controlled by sigmoid activation functions, which collectively regulate the cell state121,122. Our implementation is a dual-branch model. One branch is a dense layer (128 units, ReLU activation) and the other is an LSTM layer (64 units). The outputs from both branches are concatenated and fed into a final dense output layer (1 unit).

Model evaluation, validation, and forecasting protocol

This phase focused on evaluating the performance of the models in forecasting SUHI in Tehran. The evaluation metrics included the coefficient of determination (R²), RMSE and MAE. For the DL models, the Adam optimizer was applied with a learning rate of 0.001 over 200 training epochs. All models were implemented in Python and were executed on a NVIDIA V100 processor using TensorFlow 2.8 and Scikit-learn 1.0.2. Models accuracy was assessed using R², while model errors were quantified using RMSE and MAE. The ML and DL models were compared using these metrics, and the model achieving the highest R² and the lowest RMSE and MAE was considered the optimal model123. The formula for R² is shown in Eq. 3. R² is a metric which assesses the strength and character of the correlation between an independent variable and a dependent variable. It is bounded between 0 and 1. R² values near 1 indicate a stronger correlation and more explanatory power whereas values near 0 suggest a weaker or nonexistent correlation124.

RMSE measures the average magnitude of the prediction errors by calculating the square root of the mean of the squared differences between observed and predicted values (Eq. 4.). A lower RMSE indicates a better-performing model123.

MAE is the total absolute actual and prediction values (Eq. 5.), and also equals the disregard of the lowest lower value the better the model123.

Validation strategy to ensure robustness

Beyond the simple train-test split, we implemented a nested validation approach:

Hyperparameter Sensitivity Check: A grid search on the training set was performed for key hyperparameters (e.g., number of LSTM units, learning rate) using a 5-fold time-series cross-validation scheme, where folds were created chronologically to prevent look-ahead bias.

Final Model Training: The best configuration from Step 1 was used to retrain the model on the entire training set.

Final Evaluation: This final model was evaluated once on the untouched test set to report the definitive performance metrics.

Forecasting procedure

To generate forecasts for 2027, 2028, 2029, and 2030, the scaled year values for these future time points were fed into the trained models. For ensemble models (e.g., MLR + RF), forecasts from each base model were averaged. The best-performing model (U-Net+LSTM) was used to produce the authoritative projections discussed in the results. Uncertainty intervals were derived by propagating the standard error of the model’s predictions on the test set.

Uncertainty analysis

Model uncertainty was evaluated using multiple approaches: temporal bootstrapping to estimate prediction confidence intervals, Monte Carlo simulations to capture spatial variability, and sensitivity analyses based on SDGs scenarios. In the model design, SDG-related indicators were incorporated, with Goals 11 and 13, LULC and NDVI-vegetation cover included as as independent variables.

Selection of the 2027–2030 forecasting period and its connection to sustainable development goals for spatiotemporal prediction

In assessing LST and SUHI phenomena, forecasts at different temporal and spatial scales were used to generate charts and maps illustrating the spatial distribution of the phenomena. The selected forecasting period (2027–2030) was based on an SDG timeframe86, on the scientific rigor of the prediction and on the criteria for urban planning described below.

Strategic alignment with the SDGs timeframe

The dataset spanning 2003 to 2024 (22 continuous years), was prepared to project trends through 2030. This four-year period coincides with the United Nations’s 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development. Under SDGs 11 (Sustainable Cities and Communities)125, an SUHI monitoring system is required for Tehran to track and evaluate the effectiveness of LST mitigation policies and associated land-use changes. In SDG 13 (Climate Action)126, analyzing LST trends and monitoring changes is a key component of local climate change assessment and provides a foundation for developing climate adaptation strategies.

Scientific considerations and methodological justification for selecting the 2027–2030 period

Derived from the latest available observational data, these inputs enhance the initial validation process and improve the overall prediction accuracy. Short-term predictive errors, which primarily arise from weather variability, transient climatic fluctuations, and recent abrupt LULC changes, are minimized during this period. This period offers a clear advantage: 2027 serves as an optimal starting point to assess the effectiveness of existing policies, while 2030 marks the SDG target year and a strategic “golden horizon” for global macro-policy formulation127.

Starting in 2027 allows for the evaluation of SUHI mitigation and urban green space policies well before the 2030 deadline. This period facilitates the analysis of long-term trends rather than short-term variations. In conjunction with the urban planning framework for the city of Tehran, it provides a practical timeframe for revising and optimizing policies ahead of 2030.

Requirements for urban planning

This period is particularly well-suited for assessing strategic action plans aimed at mitigating SUHI effects, in alignment with the 2030 SDGs Target 11.3128. Furthermore, the results of this research can directly contribute to the revision of the Tehran master urban plan, the Tehran climate action plan, and the country’s SDG implementation report. By integrating 22 years of MODIS satellite data, hybrid modeling techniques, and an optimized spatio-temporal framework, this research delivers both scientific rigor and practical relevance, providing actionable insights for decision-makers tasked with implementing SDG-related policies. The focus on the 2027–2030 period underscores the timeliness of this study in offering policy-relevant solutions to the pressing urban challenges facing the city of Tehran.

Such conditions are typically linked to areas with higher vegetation cover, proximity to surface water bodies, and days with thick cloud cover or moderate-to-strong winds that enhance surface cooling. The interquartile range (IQR), representing the middle 50% of the predicted observations (25th and 75th percentiles), reflects the central tendency of the data. This range indicates moderate variability in SUHI values over short time periods. In contrast, the upper percentiles (above the 90th percentile), which exceed (µ + σ)—approximately 6.06 °C—represent extreme SUHI intensity. These extreme values are typically found in highly urbanized zones with dense construction, little to no vegetation, high levels of anthropogenic activity, industrial zones, and areas with severe automobile congestion.

On clear and calm summer days, these phenomena are particularly pronounced in the city’s core and in densely built commercial and administrative zones. The increased demand for air conditioning during such periods significantly impacts energy consumption. Public health is also affected, as the number of heat-related illnesses rises, accompanied by a decline in air quality across the city. The primary purpose of the percentile chart is to visualize the dispersion of critical values defined by the percentile endpoints, particularly those that capture key values for the years 2027–2030. This visualization is especially valuable for urban planning—for instance, this visual analysis helps identify years at heightened risk of extreme SUHII, offering valuable preliminary insights independent of a formal normality assessment (b). Combining the output of both graphs is essential for a comprehensive understanding. The percentile chart identifies which years are most critical, while the Q-Q plot verifies the data quality by confirming the absence of extreme outliers. The more sophisticated statistical analysis is carried out in the first, former, whereas the robust conclusions are strengthened by integrating the chart results with the Q-Q plot findings.

As demonstrated, this conclusion is statistically validated through regression analysis, with both charts confirming and reinforcing the earlier findings from the Q-Q plot. The statistical conclusions are therefore closely aligned with the practical insights revealed by the percentile chart. Both charts illustrate the impacts of SUHII on Tehran and how it is projected to change by 2030. The average SUHII hotspot value of 5.96 °C indicates that the region is experiencing the SUHI effect. This can be attributed to the widespread use of construction materials with high heat capacity, limited vegetation and evapotranspiration, and intensive human activity. The projected temperature increase of 0.08 °C per year between 2027 and 2030 further signals a strengthening of the SUHI effect. Implementing cooling strategies—such as creating new green spaces, applying reflective materials in construction, and installing cool roofs on existing buildings—can effectively lower temperatures, particularly at the upper percentiles of the distribution. The relationship between SUHII and urban activity in this area highlights the insufficient control currently exercised over the SUHI effect.

Analysis of historical data

Based on historical records from 2022 to 2024, LST values ranged from 10.01 °C to 10.27 °C, whereas the projected data indicate a wider range of 10.88 °C to 13.03 °C, with peak values between 11.35 °C and 11.84 °C in Tehran. In Tehran downtown, historical records show temperatures ranging from 13.73 °C to 13.96 °C, while projections extend from 10.61 °C to 15.90 °C, with peak mean temperatures between 13.21 °C and 13.71 °C. For the suburbs, historical temperatures ranged from 8.04 °C to 8.26 °C, while projected values range from 8.16 °C to 10.62 °C, with peak mean values between 8.83 °C and 9.31 °C. When considering the entirety Tehran and its adjacent areas collectively, historical LST values ranged from 8.61 °C to 8.73 °C, while predicted data show a range from 8.36 °C to 11.16 °C, with mean peak values between 9.17 °C and 9.53 °C. These findings clearly demonstrate an intensification of the SUHI effect in Tehran and its surrounding areas, underscoring the urgent need for targeted mitigation strategies.

Discussion

Application of ML and DL for forecasting nighttime SUHI in Tehran

By employing hybrid ML and DL models to predict LST across different zones—Tehran, Tehran Downtown, the suburbs, and the combined Tehran–suburbs —this study shows that the city exhibits the highest LST values, while the surrounding zones display comparatively lower temperatures. All four zones demonstrate an overall increasing trend in LST. The sharpest increases are expected in densely populated areas with high-rise and mid-rise buildings, as well as in heavy industrial zones132. Tehran’s LST is strongly influenced by interactions among elevation, NDVI, road networks, and land use/land cover (LULC) patterns133.

This accelerating warming trend, projected by our data-driven models, is grounded in well-established physical processes associated with urbanization. Similar to findings from process-based models like the Weather Research and Forecasting (WRF) system applied in other rapidly urbanizing regions134,135, the primary physical drivers include: (1) an altered surface energy balance, where impervious surfaces increase heat storage and reduce evaporative cooling, elevating sensible heat flux; (2) anthropogenic heat release from buildings and traffic; and (3) urban geometry that traps longwave radiation and inhibits nocturnal cooling. While ML/DL models extract these patterns from data, their alignment with physical mechanisms reinforces the plausibility of forecasts.

The study of SUHI using hybrid ML and DL methods indicates that their highest values occur in the center of Tehran, compared to other areas of the city. This elevated temperature in the city center is also clearly reflected in the longitudinal and latitudinal spatial profiles37.

The concentration of SUHI in central districts (6 to 8 and 10 to 14) is not coincidental but stems from a confluence of anthropogenic and geophysical factors. The phenomenon of SUHI has been extensively studied in cities worldwide136. Compared to global patterns, Tehran’s SUHI core aligns with cities where historical unplanned growth has led to extreme land cover imperviousness. For instance, while the average nighttime SUHII for over 1,000 global cities is ~ 1.8°C137, Tehran’s intensity (> 5 °C)28 rivals or exceeds that of major cities in dry climates (e.g., Milan: 5.1–6.3°C138). The underlying cause in Tehran is the synergistic effect of its dense built-up fabric (low sky view factor), minimal green space, and high anthropogenic heat flux from traffic and energy use. This creates a positive feedback loop: less vegetation → higher LST → increased cooling energy demand → more waste heat release → further LST increase. This loop explains why warming is most acute in these established urban cores, contrasting with suburban areas where land cover is more heterogeneous.

In other words, the city’s evolution is characterized by the presence of a dome of stagnant warm air4, which is depicted in a dual-graph view of the city. Notably, the latitudinal profiles of the six SUHI models are significantly warmer than their longitudinal counterparts.

The stronger warming in our latitudinal profiles aligns with physical models showing urbanization impacts minimum temperatures most severely. This occurs due to the slow nocturnal release of heat stored in urban materials—a process amplified by Tehran’s morphology and basin topography, which trap heat and reduce nighttime cooling rates. This physical mechanism underpins the “stagnant warm air dome” and validates our focused forecast of accelerating nighttime SUHI134.

Tehran is situated near the center of a north-south latitudinal temperature gradient, where temperatures are influenced by elevation differences relative to the southern plains. There is a sedimentary basin in the southern belt which gradually transitions into a high North basin which affects the north-south temperature gradient. Urban planners and decision-makers should consider these pronounced latitudinal and longitudinal profiles when designing and implementing SUHI mitigation policies133.

To gain insights from prediction models, historical data are essential, spanning 22 years of MODIS observations. Remote sensing techniques, specifically MODIS-based Satellite data, are crucial for studying SUHI trends due to the clarity and extensive coverage of the dataset27,28,139. Integrating remote sensing with ML and DL techniques in UHI Studies enables accurate and rigorous results. Recently, DL approaches in SUHI research have gained significant traction35, offering numerous advantages such as adaptability, parallel processing, weight sharing, and enhanced data and computational capabilities. These benefits improve the ability to solve complex, high-dimensional problems. Consequently, the optimization of DL parameters has advanced, prompting many researchers to explore the value of DL in addressing SUHI phenomena36.

In this study, we propose a novel bidirectional U-Net architecture combined with LSTM networks. By combining the powerful image-processing capabilities of U-Net with the temporal sequence modeling strength of LSTM, this hybrid model achieves high accuracy in forecasting the spatiotemporal distribution of LST and SUHI in Tehran113. The LSTM component preserves the sequential order of data elements while providing U-Net with enriched feature representations through deep LSTM layers. Together, these layers effectively capture the spatiotemporal dynamics of the input data, enhancing both the accuracy and robustness of the model’s predictions114.

The superior performance of hybrid U-Net+LSTM model, particularly over standalone MLR or ensemble models like MLR + RF, warrants further analysis. The key differentiator is its ability to simultaneously model non-linear spatial hierarchies and long-term temporal dependencies. Traditional ML models (e.g., MLR, RF, XGB) often struggle with the high-dimensional, autocorrelated nature of spatiotemporal LST data. In contrast, architectures like U-Net+LSTM explicitly encode these structures. This aligns with findings from other environmental forecasting domains in Tehran, where hybrid or advanced DL models63,64 consistently outperform conventional methods. Our results contribute to this growing consensus, demonstrating that for forecasting a complex, spatially explicit phenomenon like SUHI, sophisticated hybrid DL architectures that mirror the underlying data structure are not merely beneficial but essential for achieving high-fidelity, long-term projections.

The UN has endorsed the SDGs, also known as the Global Goals, since 2015. These goals represent a universal call to action to eliminate extreme poverty, protect the planet, and ensure peace and prosperity for all by 203075. Of the 17 goals, this research concentrates on SDG 11 (Sustainable Cities and Communities)122 and SDG 13 (Climate Action)123. Specifically, these goals focus on the LST and SUHI dynamics in the Tehran’s metropolitan region between now and 2030 to assess the effectiveness of heat mitigation strategies and policies.

Accurately predicting temperature increases and SUHII allows city planners and policymakers to design and implement mitigation strategies well in advance of 2030, contributing directly to the achievement of the United Nations’ SDGs74. The year 2030 serves not only as a target for planning and urgent action but also as a symbolic milestone, as many SDGs are set to be reached by then. By delivering scientifically robust projections on urban and climate indicators, this research supports global efforts to monitor and advance progress toward sustainability on a worldwide scale.

Forecasting nighttime SUHII in Tehran: from prediction to early warning and immediate mitigation measures

This research forecasts an annual increase of 0.08 °C in Tehran’s nighttime SUHII, leading to a projected mean of 5.96 °C by 2030. Placing this trend in a broader context is revealing. Our projected rise is consistent with the 0.74 °C trend previously observed in Tehran28, indicating an acceleration or persistence of the driving forces. When compared to other arid urban areas, the magnitude is significant. For example, a study in Punjab, Pakistan, projected a 0.1 °C annual increase54—a comparable rate suggesting that rapidly urbanizing arid regions share a common vulnerability to intensifying nighttime SUHI. However, Tehran’s baseline SUHII is already exceptionally high (> 5 °C). Therefore, even a “modest” annual increase compounds into a more severe absolute thermal stress by 2030. The primary causal explanation for this persistent rise lies in Tehran’s continued urban expansion trajectory, which outpaces the implementation of large-scale cooling interventions, locking in future warming based on current urbanization patterns.

A greater amount of construction activities combined with a reduction of vegetation leads to a rise in temperature due to an increase in absorption of solar radiation. In addition, the increase in the level of background temperature due to emissions of greenhouse gases also strengthens the SUHI effect. An increase in the level of population, vehicle, and energy use in the city and also corresponds to the growth of urbanization, further enhances the effect. Extreme urbanization and negligence of landscape design in densely populated regions leads to heat stress and increase in energy supply and demand. Soil and vegetation loss in these woven city structures due to the heat, leads to drying and erosion. Attention to the mitigation strategies that address the SUHI effect ensure improved biodiversity through reflective materials, park construction, smart city design, effective design.

Synthesizing data-driven forecasts with physical mechanisms

Our forecast of a 0.08 °C annual SUHII rise and a “super-nighttime SUHI” state is not just a statistical outcome but aligns with the physics of urbanization and climate change. Process-based models (e.g., WRF) show that urbanization elevates minimum temperatures by over 3 °C by altering the surface energy balance, and when combined with climate change, the warming intensifies significantly (increases of 4–7 °C)134. Although magnitudes vary by region, the underlying physical drivers are analogous. In Tehran, rapid urban expansion (more impervious surfaces, less vegetation) amplifies regional climate warming. Thus, our AI-based projections quantify the expected thermal outcome of these physical processes, bridging data-driven prediction with mechanistic understanding.

Urban planning and climate action

The transition of Tehran toward a “super-nighttime SUHI” state, as forecasted, necessitates an urgent dissection of its root causes to inform actionable climate adaptation. Compared to coastal cities140, Tehran’s arid subtropical climate exacerbates the SUHI effect due to limited moisture availability for evaporative cooling. Furthermore, unlike cities with mandated green ratios or cool material standards, Tehran’s historical urban sprawl has prioritized impervious surfaces, creating a legacy of high thermal inertia. The projected intensification is therefore a direct function of continued urban densification without proportional integration of mitigation infrastructure. This places Tehran in a cohort of dryland megacities—such as Riyadh, Saudi Arabia141—where water scarcity limits traditional greening solutions, necessitating innovative approaches like high-albedo materials and targeted urban ventilation corridors.

This phenomenon known as SUHI occurs widely across warmer climates and dry countries2. The situation for a city with Tehran’s dry climate is thermally stressful enough as it is due to the water shortage crisis, future expected droughts, inadequate per capita greenery, and dire health concerns. Thus, for the reasons outlined above, the choice of the present time is vital when it comes to raising awareness and informing responsible decision-makers about the necessary steps which need to be taken to enable proper planning for the UN 2030 agenda. This research can contribute to informing policymakers about the necessary actions which need to be taken for the improvements which most urban centers are currently lacking, which is the SUHI effect, and the SUHI, impact. It is expected that the time frame of 2027–2030 will allow for the concentration on balancing the large scale trends that can be seamless integrated, were able to do. In this time span, the expected and proven climate related features will be able to undergo the most testing, and we can allow for extreme implementations at scale.

Reviewing and developing new programs

The comprehension of the 2027–2030 range as a ‘golden period’ suggests that the outputs of this time slice are of top priority for the order of business in urban planning, including the formulation of urban budgeting protocols and the setting of overarching urban planning frameworks. The models of the next few years can be used for validation of the forecasts through 2030. This provides an enriched perspective for researchers to evaluate the degree of congruence of the forecasts with the actual scenario. Though it is true that forecasts extending to 2040 or 2050 are of much greater value, they are also burdened with great uncertainties.

Based on our forecasts, Tehran’s decision-makers can implement targeted interventions. High-priority zones, identified by our spatial models as experiencing the most severe projected warming (e.g., districts 6–8 and 10–14), should be mandated for the use of high-albedo (reflective) building materials and prioritized for precision urban greening. The model’s LST-NDVI relationship can be used to calculate the exact vegetation cover needed to achieve specific cooling targets in these hotspots27,28. This data-driven approach transforms our predictive maps into a direct spatial guide for deploying cooling infrastructure, directly supporting SDGs for sustainable and resilient urban planning.

Research limitations

Use of remote sensing data with high spatial resolution

One of the main limitations of this study lies in the use of remote sensing data with higher spatial resolution. Since the SUHI effect in Tehran is the most pronounced at night, the selection among Landsat 8, Sentinel-3, MODIS Terra, and MODIS Aqua depended on the availability of nighttime data. Although the use of higher-resolution data could improve the accuracy of spatial models, under current conditions, MODIS Aqua represents the most suitable option for Tehran.

Landsat 8 provides higher spatial resolution than Sentinel-3 and MODIS (Aqua and Terra) and also offers a longer observational record. However, its limited temporal resolution, absence of nighttime data, and unsuitable imaging times make it impractical for UHI climatology studies. The 16-day revisit cycle further reduces its temporal usefulness, and its imaging window does not adequately cover Tehran and its surroundings. Although Sentinel-3 captures both daytime and nighttime data, its relatively short observational record and suboptimal imaging times similarly limit its applicability for long-term climatic studies in Tehran. For this reason, and given the need for reliable climatological and spatial analysis across the region, MODIS Aqua emerges as the most suitable choice. MODIS Aqua has been identified in previous research as the most appropriate satellite for nighttime UHI studies. Its advantages include a favorable overpass time (1:30 a.m., south–north), adequate temporal resolution (twice daily), reasonable spatial resolution (1 km), a relatively long data record (since 2002), and the use of an effective Split-Window (SW) algorithm well suited to arid regions such as Tehran. Additionally, MODIS Aqua provides enhanced data resolution capabilities and easy access to corrected, research-ready datasets via various platforms. These factors have contributed to its widespread adoption in UHI research in recent years. Importantly, the availability of nighttime LST data is particularly valuable for countries like Iran, where climatic data for urban areas remain severely limited28.

Assessing the impact of atmospheric pollutants

This study did not account for the role of atmospheric pollutants (such as particulate matter, nitrogen dioxide, ozone, and aerosols), which can influence LST through complex and sometimes opposing mechanisms. On one hand, aerosols such as black carbon can intensify warming by absorbing radiation, while on the other, aerosols such as sulfates may enhance surface albedo and produce a cooling effect. Excluding these variables may therefore result in misestimations of both the magnitude and even the direction of future temperature changes under different pollution reduction or increase scenarios. Consequently, future studies are encouraged to integrate atmospheric pollutant data in order to achieve a more comprehensive understanding of the driving mechanisms behind the SUHI phenomenon in Tehran.

Modeling limitations

Although advanced ML and DL models were employed in this study, these models are inherently trained on patterns derived from historical datasets. Consequently, they are unable to accurately predict the impacts of unexpected or abrupt future events. For instance, prolonged and severe droughts, sudden shifts in atmospheric circulation patterns, rapid implementation of large-scale green urban policies, or other external shocks absent from the training data may significantly affect the accuracy of the predictions. Therefore, the forecasts presented in this study should be interpreted as valid under stable conditions, without accounting for such unforeseen events.

The inherent complexity of hybrid architectures—particularly the DL models applied in this research (e.g., U-Net+LSTM)—while contributing to high predictive accuracy, may reduce the interpretability of the models. In other words, fully understanding how and why a specific prediction is generated, or disentangling the precise contribution of each input variable to the final output, remains a significant challenge. Furthermore, this study is specifically focused on the Tehran metropolitan area, taking into account its unique climatic conditions, topography, and patterns of urban development. As such, the findings and the developed model may not be directly transferable to other cities with different characteristics (e.g., coastal cities, low-density urban areas, or cities located in markedly different climatic zones). Validation—and in many cases, retraining of the model using localized data—will be essential to ensure predictive accuracy and reliability in new geographical contexts.

Conclusions

This research integrates nighttime MODIS satellite data with hybrid modeling using six ML and DL models to analyze the nighttime SUHI in Tehran. It provides important insights for the 2027–2030 period, directly aligning with SDGs 11 and 13.

The findings from the two forecasting techniques—parametric normal distribution and non-parametric kernel smoothing—indicate a steady and considerable increase in average nighttime LST across Tehran and its surrounding areas. LST will increase by over 2 °C across all four study zones—Tehran, Tehran Downtown, the suburbs, and the combined Tehran–suburbs area—while also revealing that Tehran Downtown exhibits the highest spatial LST. Moreover, the forecasted standard deviations show a substantial rise, suggesting that the average number of warm nights will increase. Longitudinal and latitudinal graphs, combined with SUHI analyses, highlight that SUHI is not only persistent but has also escalated into a pressing urban crisis.

The MLR + XGB, MLR + RF, and MLR models produced reasonable projections of temperature increases. However, the DL models—ANN+LSTM, U-Net+LSTM, and DNN+LSTM—offered greater accuracy. Among these, the U-Net+LSTM model outperformed all others, yielding the highest R² values and the lowest prediction errors. This makes the analysis a valuable starting point for urban climatology, providing guidance for selecting the most effective predictive models. Consequently, SUHII calculations in this study were based on the U-Net+LSTM model. The increasing temperatures projected between 2027 and 2030 (0.08 °C annually) and the mean SUHII (5.96 °C) suggest that the SUHII will continue to intensify. Combined Q-Q and percentile analyses reveal that the projected temperature rise by 2030 is significant and concentrated in the upper ranges of the forecasts, reinforcing the urgent need for mitigation strategies to control the SUHI effect. The findings emphasize that rising temperatures in Tehran must be addressed through SUHI mitigation. Overall, this study demonstrates that hybrid DL models can effectively predict LST in urban areas, offering critical insights for urban planners to prioritize temperature reduction strategies and support sustainable urbanization.
