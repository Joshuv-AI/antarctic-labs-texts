Integrated analysis of disulfidoptosis-related genes identifies NRP1 as a novel biomarker promoting proliferation of gastric cancer via glutamine mediated energy metabolism
Authors: Qiuhua Li, Guofeng Shi, Yuebo Li, Ren Lu, Zhaozhe Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe incidence and mortality of gastric cancer rank fifth and fourth worldwide among all malignancies, respectively. Additionally, disulfidoptosis, a recently identified form of cellular demise, is closely linked to the initiation and advancement of malignancies. This study aims to create a novel signature of disulfidptosis-related genes (DRGs) and to further explore its association with the tumor immune microenvironment. Based on our comprehensive study, a prognostic signature consisting of 31 DRGs in stomach adenocarcinoma (STAD) was identified and characterized. Through the integrative analyses involving gene expression profiling, machine learning algorithms, and Cox regression models, the prognostic significance of these DRGs was demonstrated. Our findings highlight their strong predictive power in assessing overall survival across diverse patient datasets, and their better performance than traditional clinicopathological factors. Moreover, the DRGs signature showed association with the characteristics of the tumor microenvironment, which has implications for the immune modulation and therapeutic strategies in STAD. Specifically, NRP1 emerged as a key DRG with elevated expression in STAD, showing correlation with the advanced stages of diseases and poorer outcomes. Functional studies further revealed the role of NRP1 in promoting STAD cell proliferation through the modulation of glutamine metabolism. Overall, our study underscores the clinical relevance of DRGs as biomarker and potential therapeutic targets in STAD management, providing insights into disease biology and personalized treatments.Keywords: Stomach adenocarcinoma, NRP1, Disulfidptosis-related genes, Glutamine mediated energy metabolism, Prognosis

Introduction

With more than 1,000,000 diagnoses and 769,000 deaths worldwide each year, gastric cancer (GC) is among the most common cancers and ranks as the fourth most deadly [1]. Stomach adenocarcinoma (STAD) is the most common type of GC [2]. STAD is widely distributed globally, with notably higher incidence rates in East Asia (such as China, Japan, and Korea), as well as in parts of Eastern Europe and South America [3]. In contrast, the incidence rate of STAD has significantly declined in developed countries, possibly due to the changes in dietary habits, improved sanitation conditions, and the widespread adoption of early screening and treatment [4]. The major risk factors for STAD include Helicobacter pylori infection, high-salt diet, smoking, alcohol consumption, the long-term exposure to certain chemicals or asbestos, and various genetic factors such as family history [5, 6]. STAD is detected mostly in advanced stages due to few early warning signs and symptoms. At present, the adoption of endoscopic procedures has significantly enhanced the survival rate of STAD patients, reducing mortality by 30% through screening [7, 8]. Nevertheless, STAD remains one of the deadliest malignancies, with a 5-year survival rate hovering around 20%. Hence, there is an urgent need to discover more dependable prognostic biomarkers and investigate the additional therapeutic targets in STAD.

Disulfidptosis is a novel mechanism of cell death distinct from traditional programmed cell death modes such as cuproptosis, necroptosis, ferroptosis and apoptosis [9, 10]. It primarily results from the excessive accumulation of intracellular cystine, leading to disulfide stress and rapid cell death. Disulfide bonds are the specific chemical bonds formed between two sulfur atoms, typically linking two cysteine residues within the same protein chain or different protein chains [11]. The formation of disulfide bonds involves an oxidation-reduction reaction that occur under oxidative conditions and appropriate structural contexts. In eukaryotic cells, disulfide bonds are commonly formed in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and cytoplasm, which is facilitated by various proteins and small molecules [12, 13]. Research has shown that under glucose-deprived conditions, the cells overexpressing SLC7A11 experience severe depletion of NADPH, resulting in the abnormal accumulation of cystine and subsequent disulfide stress. This leads to rapid cell death. The formation of disulfide bonds is a complex catalytic process involving numerous proteins and small molecules, with mitochondria and the ER playing a critical role in ensuring proper protein folding and functionality [14, 15]. In cancer-related proteins, the widespread formation of disulfide bonds has been observed, which not only underscores their pivotal role in cellular biology and pathophysiology, but also suggests that they can be taken as potential targets for novel therapeutic strategies. Disulfide bonds regulate the structure and function of proteins, influencing the key biological processes in cancer cells such as proliferation, metastasis, and drug resistance. Therefore, the interventions with the process of targeting disulfide bonds hold promise for advancing cancer treatment [16]. However, the specific role of disulfidptosis in STAD remains unclear and further investigation is required.

Glutamine-mediated energy metabolism plays a crucial role in cellular bioenergetics and various physiological processes [17]. As a form of non-essential amino acid, glutamine functions prominently in multiple metabolic pathways, particularly in providing energy and supporting biosynthetic processes. This is crucial to the rapid proliferation of cells such as cancer cells [18, 19]. The primary metabolic pathways of glutamine include glutaminolysis, where glutamine is converted into glutamate and subsequently into α-ketoglutarate for ATP production via the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle [20, 21]. Additionally, glutamine plays a crucial role in anaplerosis by replenishing intermediates of the TCA cycle and participates in reductive carboxylation to generate NADPH, which is essential for lipid synthesis and antioxidant defense. In the context of cancer metabolism, the increased uptake and metabolism of glutamine address the elevated energy and biosynthetic demands of tumor cells, making it a critical target for research and therapeutic interventions [22, 23]. Overall, glutamine-mediated energy metabolism not only underpins cellular functions but also holds potential as a pivotal therapeutic target, particularly in such diseases as cancer. The association between disulfidptosis and glutamine-mediated energy metabolism remains largely unclear.

In this study, a disulfidptosis-related gene signature in stomach adenocarcinoma (STAD) was identified through gene expression analysis and survival assessment. It further integrated machine learning algorithms to establish effective prognostic models. The research delved into the correlation between these gene signatures and the tumor microenvironment, in addition to exploring the specific genes like NRP1 and their potential as independent prognostic factors in STAD. These findings provide crucial theoretical insights and clinical implications for the development of personalized treatment and precision medicine strategies in STAD.

Materials and methods

Datasets

In our study, the clinical and mRNA expression data on 375 STAD tissues and 32 non-tumor specimens were retrieved from TCGA Data Portal. Additionally, RNA-Seq and clinical data for an external validation cohort, identified as GEO ID: GSE84437, were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. Both datasets underwent a log2(x + 1) transformation to stabilize variance and make the data more normally distributed, which is a common preprocessing step in RNA-Seq data analysis. Following this, normalization was performed using the “limma” package in R. The “limma” package adopts robust statistical methods to ensure that the differences in mRNA expression levels across samples arise from biological variation rather than technical artifacts. To account for the potential batch effects that could arise from the differences in sample processing or sequencing runs, the “Combat” function derived from the “sva” package was applied. This function implements an empirical Bayesian framework to adjust batch effects, thereby harmonizing the data collected from different sources and ensuring that downstream analyses are not confounded by technical variability. In order to examine the frequency and type of gene alterations in two NRGPI subgroups, the oncoplot was drawn using the R package “maftools”. Based on an extensive review of the existing literature, a list of 2774 potential disulfidptosis-related genes (DRGs) was obtained. Disulfidptosis is a form of programmed cell death characterized by the disruption of disulfide bonds within proteins, leading to cellular dysfunction and death. The identification of these DRGs was critical for our subsequent analyses, as it allowed the researcher to focus on those genes potentially involved in this specific pathway of cell death.

Signature construction based on integrative machine learning algorithms

To identify differentially expressed disulfidptosis-related genes (DE-DRGs), the Mann–Whitney test was performed using the “Wilcox.test” function and the “limma” R package. This analysis made it possible to pinpoint the genes with significantly altered expression levels between STAD tissues and adjacent normal tissues. The rigorous statistical framework provided by these tools ensures that the identified DE-DRGs are robust and reliable. Then, the focus was shifted to the overall survival (OS) data within the TCGA datasets for evaluating the prognostic value of the identified DRGs. Univariate Cox regression analysis was conducted to assess the ability of each gene to predict the outcome for patients. Those genes that overlapped between the prognostic DRGs and the disulfidptosis-related DEGs were further categorized as prognostic disulfidptosis-related DEGs. Ten STAD-based integrative machine learning algorithms were applied to produce the prognostic DRGs signature. Among the various models constructed, the top five models with the highest average C-index across all datasets were identified as the best-performing models. This selection process ensures that the final prognostic signature is not biased by a single dataset and it performs well in diverse clinical scenarios. Previous studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of these machine learning algorithms in similar contexts, providing a strong rationale for their use in the present study. After the optimal DRGs prognostic signature was determined, the specific genes included in the signature and their corresponding coefficients were extracted. These coefficients represent the weight of each gene in the final model, indicating their relative importance to the prediction of outcome for patients. Each STAD patient’s risk score might be determined using gene expressions and a coefficient. The risk score is equal to the sum of the two factors.

Evaluation of the performance of DRGs prognostic signature

The overall survival (OS) curves were generated using the “survival” package in R. This package provides comprehensive tools for survival analysis, including the Kaplan–Meier estimator used in this study to plot survival curves. To further evaluate the prognostic performance of the DRGs signature, time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was conducted using the “timeROC” package. The area under the curve (AUC) was calculated for each time point, so as to measure the sensitivity and specificity of the prognostic signature in predicting survival at different time points. A higher AUC indicates a better performance of the signature in distinguishing between the patients who will experience an event (e.g., death) and those who will not. The concordance index (C-index) was calculated using the “rms” package to quantify the discriminatory power of the DRGs prognostic signature. The C-index measures the consistence between predicted risks and actual outcomes, with its values ranging from 0.5 (no better than chance) to 1.0 (perfect prediction). The C-index of the DRGs signature was compared with those of traditional clinical factors such as stage and grade, enabling a comprehensive evaluation of its relative prognostic value. To identify the independent risk factors for the clinical outcome of STAD, univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were performed. These analyses considered both the DRGs prognostic signature and clinical characteristics (e.g., age, sex, stage, and grade). NomogramEx is an R package primarily used for the construction and visualization of nomograms. A nomogram is a statistical tool typically used for visualizing and interpreting predictive models, especially in the medical field for individualized predictions, such as cancer prognosis. The nomogramEx package provides a range of functionalities to simplify the process of building and evaluating nomograms, which enables users to convert complex regression models into intuitive charts easily. Following this, the DRGs prognostic signature and STAD clinical characteristics were used to build a predictive nomogram using the “nomogramEx” R program.

Immune infiltration analysis

ESTIMATE method is a computational algorithm used to infer the fraction of stromal and immune cells in tumor tissues through gene expression data. Developed to provide insights into the tumor microenvironment, ESTIMATE calculates the scores that reflect the presence of stromal and immune cells, which can be critical to understanding tumor biology, prognosis, and the response to therapy. The immunity score and ESTIMATE score of each STAD case were calculated using the ESTIMATE method. Subsequent to thorough benchmarking, seven approaches were adopted to assess the quantity of immune cells in STAD: quanTIseq, EPIC, CIBERSORT-ABS, CIBERSORT, MCP-counter, xCell and TIMER. To investigate the relationship between the DRGs prognostic signature and immune checkpoint expression, the relative expression of those key immune checkpoints across different risk score groups was visualized. This analysis was conducted using the “ggpubr” and “ggplot2” packages in R. These packages provide versatile tools for creating high-quality visualizations, which allows the expression levels of immune checkpoints to be effectively compared between high-risk and low-risk groups. The single-sample Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (ssGSEA) approach, which is implemented using the “GSVA” package in R, was taken to assess the infiltration of immune cells and the activity of immune functions in STAD. This allowed a further investigation into the immunological landscape of the disease. With the use of ssGSEA, the gene set enrichment in individual samples can be measured, which sheds light on the quantity of different immune cell types and the activation state of those pathways associated to the immune system.

Therapy benefits analysis

To assess the efficacy of the new model in predicting the immunotherapy efficacy of STAD patients, the following metrics were used: tumor immune dysfunction and exclusion score from TIDE (http://tide.dfci.harvard.edu/), immunophenoscore from The Cancer Immunome Atlas (https://tcia.at/home), immune escape score from an earlier study, and tumor mutation burden (TMB) score from TCGA. The TIDE score, obtained from the TIDE website (http://tide.dfci.harvard.edu/), was used to evaluate the likelihood of immune escape in tumors by assessing the mechanisms of T cell dysfunction and exclusion. A higher TIDE score indicates a higher probability of immune evasion by the tumor, suggesting that the patient may have a reduced response to immunotherapy. Tumor Mutation Burden (TMB), calculated from TCGA data, represents the number of mutations per megabase of DNA. A lower TMB indicates fewer mutations and potentially a weaker response to immunotherapy, as the tumors with higher mutation burdens are more likely to be immunogenic. An immune escape score, derived from previous studies, was used to assess the tumor’s ability to evade immune detection and destruction. This score provides another dimension to evaluate the immune evasion capabilities of the tumor. The immunophenoscore, sourced from The Cancer Immunome Atlas (https://tcia.at/home), quantifies the immunogenicity of tumors based on the expression of immune-related genes. A lower IPS suggests a less favorable immunogenic profile and a potential decrease in the effectiveness of immunotherapy. Immune escape is more likely to occur and immunotherapy treatment is less successful when TIDE, tumor escape, immunophenoscore, and TMB scores are all high.

Cell culture and cell transfection

The samples of normal gastric epithelial cell line GES-1, as well as GC cell lines AGS, MKN28, BGC823, HCG27, and MGC803, were acquired from the Type Culture Collection of the Chinese Academy of Sciences. The purpose of selecting these cell lines was to mimic various stomach cancer subtypes and stages. DMEM (Invitrogen) was used for the cultivation of MKN28, HCG27, MGC803, and MKN45. The cells used to culture AGS were grown in F12 (Invitrogen), while those for BGC823 were grown in RPMI-1640 (Invitrogen). A 10% supplement of fetal bovine serum (GIBCO) was added to every culture media to supply growth hormones and vital minerals. At 37 °C, with 5% CO2 and 95% air, the cells were kept in a humidified incubator, which ensured the best possible physiological conditions for cell growth and stability. A MycoGuardTM Mycoplasma PCR detection kit (GeneCopoeia) was used to screen the cell lines for possible mycoplasma contamination. The results showed that the cell lines were mycoplasma negative. The siRNAs targeting NRP1 (si-NRP1) were manufactured and supplied by Beijing Tsingke Biotech firm based in Daxing, Beijing, China. Liposome 3000 regent kits, obtained from Invitrogen, were used to transfect the plasmids and siRNAs in accordance with the instructions provided. The kits were located in Pudong, Shanghai, China. In brief, upon reaching approximately 70% confluence, the GC cells were transfected with 50 nmol of siRNAs or 10 µg of plasmids complexed with Lipofectamine 3000. After a 6-h incubation period, the transfection medium was replaced with fresh complete growth medium to maintain cell viability and support recovery. Finally, the cells were incubated for an additional 48–72 h to ensure the effective knockdown (in the case of siRNAs) or expression (in the case of plasmids) of the target genes.

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from the aforementioned STAD cell lines (AGS, MKN28, BGC823, HCG27, MGC803, MKN45) and tumor tissues using TRIzol reagent kits (Invitrogen). TRIzol effectively isolated RNA by disrupting cells and denaturing proteins, followed by phase separation with chloroform and precipitation with isopropanol. After that, PrimeScript RT kits from TaKaRa (Dalian, China) were used for reverse transcription. Then, RT-qPCR was performed using SYBR Premix Ex Taq II from TaKaRa and an Applied Biosystems 7500 Real-Time PCR system (Foster City, CA, USA). The levels of NRP1 expression were adjusted relative to GAPDH. The 2−ΔΔCT method was applied for relative quantification, with the cycle threshold values of NRP1 compared against GAPDH. Each RT-qPCR experiment was conducted with three biological replicates to ensure the consistency and reliability of results. Biological replicates involve the independent samples obtained from the same experimental condition, thus minimizing the impact of random variation and enhancing statistical validity. Primer sequences are as follows: NRP1, 5′-GGCGCTTTTCGCAACGATAAA-3′ (forward) and 5′-TCGCATTTTTCACTTGGGTGAT-3′ (reverse); GAPDH, 5′-GGAGCGAGATCCCTCCAAAAT-3′ (forward) and 5′-GGCTGTTGTCATACTTCTCATGG-3′ (reverse).

In vitro viability assay (CCK-8)

Following transient transfection, 96-well culture dishes were seeded with BGC823 and MKN45 cells in triplicates at a density of 2 × 10¹ cells/well. The cells were tested using the CCK-8 assay 48 h after plating in accordance with the instructions provided by Beyotime, Shanghai, China. Following the addition of 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent per well, the mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. Then, the absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a plate reader (BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA).

Colony formation assay

Logarithmic phase cells in all research groups were treated with 0.25% trypsin for digestion. Afterwards, the cells were separated using a plastic pipette and resuspended in 10% FBS RPMI 1640 medium. After 1 week of incubation, the cells were spread out in a six-well plate with a density of 1 × 10¹ cells per well. Before being stained with crystalline violet (0.1%) for half an hour, the cells were fixed with paraformaldehyde (4%). A microscope was employed to evaluate the ability to produce colonies.

Glutamine uptake assays

BGC823 and MKN45 cells were seeded at a density of 3 × 105 cells per well in 6-well plates and allowed to adhere overnight in complete growth medium. Following adherence, cells were transfected with specific siRNAs or plasmids targeting relevant genes, with transfection efficiency confirmed using fluorescence microscopy or a transfection marker. After transfection, cells were washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove residual transfection reagents, and the media was replaced with pre-warmed glutamine-free medium. Cells were then incubated at 37 °C for 30 min to adapt to the glutamine-free environment. For the glutamine uptake assay, cells were incubated with 400 nM 3H-glutamine (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA) for 20 min, with the concentration optimized to ensure it was within the linear range and non-toxic. Following incubation, cells were harvested using a cell scraper and transferred to a 96-well plate harvester (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA). The harvested cells were then placed on filter papers, dried completely, and exposed to scintillation fluid (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA) for optimal radioactivity detection. The filter papers with cells were analyzed using a liquid scintillation counter (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA) to measure 3H-glutamine uptake. Counts were corrected for background radioactivity and normalized to cell protein content or total cell number. Statistical analyses were performed to assess differences between treated and control groups.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R 4.2.2. For comparing two separate samples of nonparametric data, the Wilcoxon test was performed. To examine the parametric data, t-test and one-way ANOVA were performed. Statistical significance was assumed when the p-value fell below 0.05, unless otherwise stated.

Results

Identification of potential prognostic biomarkers

By using the “limma” package in R, a comprehensive analysis of gene expression variations among the stomach adenocarcinoma (STAD) patients was conducted. Our investigation revealed significant alterations in gene expression profiles, highlighting 586 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) with up-regulated expression and 78 DEGs with down-regulated expression, all of which exhibited |log2FC| > 0.5. These findings were visualized in the form of a heat map generated using the “pheatmap” R package (Fig. 1A), so as to provide a clear overview of the expression patterns across the studied samples. Furthermore, to elucidate the clinical relevance of these DEGs, univariate Cox regression analysis was performed on a total of 664 DEGs. This analysis identified 93 genes that exhibited significant associations with overall survival (OS) (P < 0.05) (Fig. 1B). These OS-associated genes could potentially serve as prognostic biomarkers in STAD, providing valuable insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying disease progression and patient outcomes. In summary, our study not only characterized the differential gene expression landscape in STAD but also pinpointed a subset of genes crucially linked to patient survival. The results of this study enrich the knowledge of STAD biology and could lead to the future progress in targeted treatments and individualized approaches to treatment.

The identification of the prognostic DRGs in STAD patients. A Heat map displaying the expression patterns of DE-DRGs in STAD patients. B Univariate Cox regression analysis identified 93 DRGs associated with overall survival

Machine learning algorithms based prognostic DRGs

Subsequently, the 93 identified potential prognostic DRGs underwent an integrative analysis involving 10 distinct machine learning methods developed to predict prognosis for patients. Figure 2A shows the Concordance Index (C-index) of 101 prediction models across all datasets evaluated. Among these models, the top five performers included Lasso, Elastic Net with alpha values of 0.8, 0.7, and 0.5, as well as a combined Lasso and Stepwise Cox regression model (referred to as Lasso + StepCox[both]). These models exhibited a notably high average C-index of 0.60 (Fig. 2A), indicating an excellent predictive performance. To stratify patients into high and low-risk groups based on their prognostic profiles, the median value of the risk score obtained by using each model was taken as the cutoff. Figure 2B–F illustrate the prognostic efficacy of these top models on both TCGA and GSE84437 datasets: Lasso (Fig. 2B), Elastic Net with alpha values 0.8 (Fig. 2C), 0.7 (Fig. 2D), and 0.5 (Fig. 2E), and the combined Lasso and Stepwise Cox model (Fig. 2F). Notably, the Elastic Net model with alpha value of 0.8 emerged as the most effective, demonstrating a strong association between high-risk scores and the lower overall survival rates in STAD patients across both TCGA and GSE84437 datasets. This finding underscores the potential of integrating machine learning approaches to enhance prognostic stratification and develop personalized treatment strategies in STAD.

Prognostic models based on machine learning algorithms. A C-index of 101 prediction models evaluated across all datasets. B–F Prognostic value of the top five models in the TCGA and GSE84437 datasets

Evaluation of the performance of DRGs signature

To further explore the prognostic significance of the DRGs signature for STAD patients, both univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were conducted to reveal that the DRGs signature is an independent risk factor affecting the prognosis of STAD cases across the datasets from TCGA and GEO (Fig. 3A, B). This comprehensive analysis underscores the robustness and reliability of the DRGs signature in predicting outcome for patients. Moreover, the predictive performance of the DRGs signature-derived risk score (AUC = 0.770) was compared against traditional clinicopathological features, demonstrating that the risk score outperforms conventional factors in forecasting STAD prognosis (Fig. 3C). The assessment of model accuracy through ROC curves further validated its sensitivity and specificity at different time intervals, showing promising AUC values for 1-year (AUC = 0.770), 3-year (AUC = 0.752), and 5-year (AUC = 0.744) survival predictions (Fig. 3D). Furthermore, the DRGs signature exhibited the highest Concordance Index (C-index), reaffirming its robust performance in prognostic evaluation for STAD patients (Fig. 3E). This highlights its applicability as a superior tool for risk stratification and clinical decision-making. To enhance the practical effectiveness of the risk model, a nomogram integrating gender, age, stage, grade, and the DRGs signature-based risk score was developed. This nomogram enables clinicians to estimate the probability of survival at 1, 3, and 5 years for STAD patients (Fig. 3F). Notably, the risk score emerged as the most influential factor in predicting overall survival (OS), underscoring the enhanced predictive accuracy achieved with a DRGs-based risk model. Moreover, calibration curves were drawn to illustrate the favorable agreement between predicted and observed survival probabilities at 1, 3, and 5 years (Fig. 3G). This substantiates the reliability of the nomogram and the DRGs signature-based risk model in clinical practice. In conclusion, our study highlights the pivotal role of the DRGs signature in prognostic assessment for STAD patients. The integration of advanced analytical approaches not only enhances prognostic accuracy but also facilitates the development of personalized treatment strategies, ultimately improving clinical outcomes in STAD management.

DRGs signature showed significant correlation with tumor microenvironment in STAD

The study identified six distinct tumor immune landscapes, categorizing TCGA STAD cases predominantly into C2 type, with a notable prevalence of C1 type observed among the high-risk score group compared to the low-risk score group (Fig. 4A). This observation suggests a potential association between tumor immune phenotype and prognostic risk stratification based on the DRGs signature. A further analysis was conducted to reveal a negative correlation between the risk score derived from the DRGs signature and the abundance of various immune cell types (Fig. 4B). Specifically, higher risk scores were associated with reduced immune cell infiltration, highlighting a potential mechanism through which DRGs affect immune modulation in STAD. In the process of exploring the tumor microenvironment characteristics, the high-risk score group exhibited significantly elevated ESTIMATE score and immunescore compared to the low-risk score group (Fig. 4C, all p < 0.05). These findings suggest a more pronounced presence of stromal and immune components within the tumors associated with higher risk scores, potentially indicating a more immunosuppressive microenvironment in these cases. Moreover, higher Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) scores were observed among the high-risk score group in this study (Fig. 4D), indicating a greater likelihood of immune evasion mechanisms operating within the tumors characterized by elevated risk scores. To evaluate the implications for immunotherapy, the expression levels of immune checkpoint markers were examined in this study. As shown in Fig. 4E, many immune checkpoints were more highly expressed in the low-risk score group, suggesting a potentially more immunoresponsive phenotype in these patients. Furthermore, it was revealed by the functional assessment of DRGs in relation to immune-related functions in STAD that the cases with high-risk scores exhibited lower scores for immune-related functions compared to those with low-risk scores (Fig. 4F). These results indicate a potential impairment in immune response pathways associated with higher risk scores, further emphasizing the complex interrelationship between DRGs, tumor immunity, and clinical outcomes in STAD. Finally, the results of GSEA analysis were obtain to demonstrate that CALCIUM_SIGNALING_PATHWAY, COMPLEMENT_AND_COAGULATION_CASCADES, ECM_RECEPTOR_INTERACTION, FOCAL_ADHESION and NEUROACTIVE_LIGAND_RECEPTOR_INTERACTION were active in the high-risk score group (Fig. 4G). However, CELL_CYCLE, DNA_REPLICATION, PYRIMIDINE_METABOLISM, RNA_DEGRADATION and SPLICEOSOME were active in the low-risk score group (Fig. 4H).

The expression of NRP1 in STAD and its prognostic value

Among the 31 prognostic DRGs, NRP1 was the focus. It was found out that NRP1 expression was distinctly increased in STAD specimens compared with normal specimens (Fig. 5A, B). In addition, the association between NRP1 expression and several clinical factors was analyzed to find out that high NRP1 expression was positively associated with advanced grade and clinical stage, but not associated with age and gender (Fig. 5C–I). Moreover, the results of Kaplan–Meier analysis revealed that those patients with high NRP1 expression showed a shorter overall survival then those with low NRP1 expression (Fig. 6A). According to the results of time-ROC analysis, NRP1 expression is highly sensitive and specific depending on the AUC values for NRP1 expression, 1, 3, and 5-years (AUC = 0.612, 0.645, 0.705) (Fig. 6B). In addition, univariate assays underscored the considerable impact of certain factors on the overall survival rate. Specifically, age, grade, stage and NRP1 expression emerged as significant contributors (Fig. 6C). Furthermore, a comprehensive multivariate Cox regression analysis confirmed that NRP1expression is an independent prognostic predictor for the overall survival of STAD patients (Fig. 6D).

Knockdown of NRP1 suppressed the proliferation of STAD cells via glutamine mediated energy metabolism

Furthermore, the role of NRP1 in STAD cell lines was explored. To narrow down the pool of potential STAD cell lines for use in our research, their NRP1 expression levels were examined at first. Then, qRT-PCR tests were conducted to quantify NRP1 in multiple conventional STAD tumor cell lines. Six STAD cell lines showed significantly higher NRP1 expression compared to normal cells, as indicated by the data (Fig. 7A). Moreover, among these STAD tumor cell lines, BGC823 and MKN45 cells exhibited the highest amounts of NRP1 (Fig. 7A). Hence, these two STAD cell lines were used in the subsequent experiments to explore the roles of NRP1 in STAD. Subsequently, an investigation was conducted into the effects of NRP1 on STAD cell activities, such as proliferation. Firstly, the siRNAs targeting NRP1 were generated. Then, BGC823 and MKN45 cells were transfected with these siRNAs. According to Fig. 7B, the qRT-PCR experiments showed that the siRNAs successfully silenced NRP1 expression in both cells. Moreover, the cellular proliferation of NRP1 knockdown was measured using CCK-8 assays. Depleting NRP1 expression significantly reduced STAD cell line proliferation, as suggested by the results (Fig. 7C). It was also revealed by colony formation experiments that NRP1 deletion decreased the amount of STAD cells colonized (Fig. 7D). Afterwards, the molecular pathways by which NRP1 could influence the course of STAD were determine. Therefore, the status of glutamine uptake in STAD cell lines was identified in the absence of NRP1 expression. Our results show that knocking down NRP1 considerably reduced glutamine absorption in BGC823 and MKN45 cells (Fig. 7E).

Discussion

The prognostic biomarkers for gastric cancer encompass a variety of molecular and clinical-pathological features critical to the assessment of patient prognosis and the development of treatment strategies [24, 25]. The common markers include HER2, microsatellite instability (MSI), PD-L1 expression, and histological type and grade. HER2-positive gastric cancer benefits from targeted therapy, while MSI-H patients may respond favorably to immune checkpoint inhibitors [26, 27]. Although PD-L1 positivity suggests the potential benefits created by immune therapy, there remain challenges in ensuring the consistency and predictive value of its detection [28]. The significant limitations include the rarity of HER2-positive cases limiting the targeted therapy benefits, and the relatively low proportion of MSI-H patients restricting the widespread use of immune checkpoint inhibitors [29, 30]. Furthermore, the variability in PD-L1 testing methods and standards raises concerns about its accuracy in predicting treatment responses. Additionally, while traditional histological classifications and pathological grading provide valuable information, they are affected by tumor heterogeneity and diagnostic subjectivity, which can impact their predictive performance. Therefore, despite the clinical significance of these prognostic markers, their individual limitations highlight the need to explore integrated biomarker approaches to improve the accuracy of prognostic assessments and the outcomes of personalized treatments.

Programmed cell death mechanisms, including necroptosis, autophagy, and apoptosis, are among the many ways cells can die. When it comes to controlling tumor immune microenvironment and tumor metastasis, these pathways are vital [31]. Apoptosis is a gene-regulated process of cellular suicide, which is typically initiated when a cell is damaged or irreparable, involving the activation of a series of intracellular enzymes. This ultimately leads to cell death [32, 33]. Autophagy is the process by which the cells degrade their own damaged or unnecessary organelles to maintain cellular homeostasis. The excessive activation of autophagy can also result in cell death [34]. Necroptosis is a programmed form of necrosis accompanied by the activation of inflammatory responses. The use of drugs to induce specific cell death pathways has been demonstrated as an effective strategy of tumor treatment [35]. For example, the drugs targeting the apoptotic pathway can induce programmed death in tumor cells, thereby reducing their survival. Similarly, inducing autophagy can help eliminate the harmful substances within tumor cells, thus leading to cell death. Additionally, the activation of necroptosis can suppress tumor growth by triggering inflammatory responses within the tumor microenvironment [36]. Lipid peroxidation, which is dependent on iron, triggers ferroptosis, a controlled cell death mechanism. In terms of cell physiology and pathology, ferroptosis is distinct from more conventional cell death processes like necrosis and apoptosis. Erastin, RSL3, and Sorafenib are only a few of the available ferroptosis inducers. Each of these drugs works in its own unique way to trigger ferroptosis [37, 38]. The effectiveness of ferroptosis in treating gastric cancer can be improved by combining it with other tumor therapies like immunotherapy or radiochemotherapy. Separate from ferroptosis and cuproptosis, disulfidptosis represents a new way cells die. Currently, the research on disulfidptosis is quite limited. In recent literature, prognostic models have been constructed through disulfidptosis-related genes across several different types of tumors [39–41]. In this study, disulfidptosis-related genes were used to construct an innovative prognostic model by integrating ten different machine learning algorithms. This comprehensive approach allowed the capture of various predictive features and the improvement in robustness of this model, which was validated through extensive bioinformatics analysis to demonstrate its high accuracy in predicting patient outcomes. Specifically, the model was applicable to stratify patients into different risk categories effectively, providing valuable prognostic information that could guide personalized treatment strategies. By identifying those patients with a higher risk of poor outcomes, this model can support clinicians in tailoring more aggressive or targeted therapies to those who need them most, which has a potential in improving overall survival rates and treatment efficacy.

In addition, this study demonstrated a significant correlation between the risk score based on DRGs signature and the tumor microenvironment and immune phenotype in STAD. The high-risk score group showed lower immune cell infiltration and higher immune evasion mechanisms, suggesting a more pronounced immunosuppressive microenvironment. Additionally, the high-risk score group exhibited significantly elevated ESTIMATE and Immunescore compared to the low-risk score group, indicating an increase in stromal and immune components. Conversely, the low-risk score group showed higher expression levels of many immune checkpoint markers, suggesting a potentially stronger immune responsiveness. GSEA revealed higher activity in various pathways such as calcium signaling, complement and coagulation cascades among the high-risk score group, whereas the pathways like cell cycle and DNA replication were found more active in the low-risk score group. These findings highlight that the DRGs signature effectively stratifies the risk in STAD patients and correlates with tumor immune phenotypes, providing crucial insights for personalized immune therapies.

Among the prognostic genes, NRP1 was the focus. Neuropilin-1 (NRP1) is a transmembrane glycoprotein receptor involved in various biological processes, particularly in angiogenesis and neuronal development [42]. NRP1 consists of extracellular domains, a transmembrane domain, a brief cytoplasmic tail, and a big extracellular portion. It is a receptor for vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and specific isoforms of the class 3 semaphorin family. The connections between these proteins allow NRP1 to regulate angiogenesis-the formation of new blood vessels—and axon guidance throughout neuronal development [43, 44]. Tumor growth, angiogenesis, and metastasis are all associated with the dysregulation of NRP1 expression, which is frequently observed in cancer cells. Dysregulated NRP1 may enhance tumor cell motility and promote VEGF signaling, both of which contribute to tumor angiogenesis. Recent studies have increasingly highlighted that NRP1 may play a role in various types of malignancies [45, 46]. For instance, Chen et al. reported that NRP1 enhances radiation-induced epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) in A549 cells by promoting the TGF-β1/Smad2/3 signaling pathway, possibly involving the increased expression of EMT-related transcription factors. Additionally, NRP1 significantly enhances the invasion, migration capabilities, and CXCL-12 expression in A549 cells under radiation induction [47]. Xu et al. explored the role of NRP-1 in primary liver cancer cells. Silencing NRP-1 has been shown to reduce the proliferation, migration, and invasion of primary liver cancer cells. They underscored the importance of hepatic stellate cells in the tumor microenvironment to promoting the progression of primary liver cancer cells. Immunofluorescence confirmed NRP-1 expression in HSCs and its co-expression with platelet-derived growth factor receptor-β. The knockdown of NRP-1 in LX2 HSCs using short hairpin RNA lentiviral vectors decreased their activation and inhibited the proliferation, migration, and invasion of co-cultured HepG2 cells. In vivo xenograft models supported these findings, which demonstrates that NRP-1 knockdown suppressed tumor growth and α-smooth muscle actin expression. This suggests NRP-1 as a potential therapeutic target for PLC [48]. In GC, Yu et al. reported that the Wnt/β-catenin signaling system was affected when NRP1 was knocked down, which in turn reduced the proliferation and autophagy of gastric cancer cells. NRP1 expression was found high in GC tissues [49]. Previous research has shown that NRP1 is significantly expressed in GC tissues and cell lines, and our results demonstrate that this is an effective indicator of a poor prognosis for GC patients. It was also found out that GC cell proliferation was significantly reduced when NRP1 was knocked down.

Glutamine-mediated energy metabolism plays a crucial role in GC. Glutamine serves as a critical nutrient needed to support various metabolic processes essential for cancer cell survival and proliferation [17]. In GC cells, glutamine is taken as a major source of carbon and nitrogen to drive energy production and biosynthesis. GC cells exhibit increased dependency on glutamine due to several reasons. Firstly, glutamine can be converted into glutamate, which enters the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, thus contributing to ATP production through oxidative phosphorylation [50, 51]. Additionally, glutamine provides nitrogen for the synthesis of nucleotide and amino acid, which are crucial for supporting rapid cell growth in cancerous tissues. Furthermore, the glutamine metabolism in GC is closely linked with oncogenic signaling pathways. Various oncogenes such as c-Myc can upregulate the expression of glutamine transporters and enzymes involved in glutamine metabolism, enhancing the reliance of cancer cells on this nutrient [52, 53]. This increased dependence on glutamine metabolism enables GC cells to adapt to the nutrient-deficient tumor microenvironment and sustain their malignant characteristics. In conclusion, glutamine-mediated energy metabolism is closely associated with GC by providing essential energy and building blocks for tumor growth and progression. Targeting glutamine metabolism represents a potential therapeutic strategy to disrupt cancer cell metabolism and inhibit tumor proliferation in GC. In this study, it was found out that the uptake of glutamine significantly decreased in BGC823 and MKN45 cells after NRP1 was knocked down. Additionally, it was observed that NRP1 knockdown inhibited the proliferation of GC cells. To sum up, these findings suggest that NRP1 may play a role in regulating GC cell proliferation through its influence on glutamine metabolism. It is implied that NRP1 might promote GC cell growth and metabolism by enhancing glutamine uptake, and that its reduction can suppress cell proliferation.

Conclusions

In the present study, the DRG profiles in STAD were comprehensively investigated and a disulfidptosis-related prognostic model was established to demonstrate the remarkably high accuracy in predicting clinical outcomes and immunotherapeutic responses. In addition, NRP1 emerged as a critical biomarker in STAD, which is associated with advanced tumor stage and reduced survival rates. It was validated as an independent predictor of poor prognosis. Functional studies showed that NRP1 knockdown suppressed glutamine-mediated energy metabolism, thus inhibiting STAD cell proliferation. In summary, our findings provide insights into STAD biology, highlight prognostic biomarkers, and suggest NRP1 as a potential therapeutic target to improve patient outcomes.
