Effects of Qili Qiangxin granules in combination with Entresto on quantitative parameters of myocardial motion in patients with cardiomyopathy in end-stage renal disease
Authors: Yanxia Li, Lin Liu, Fengrong Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCardiomyopathy is a devastating complication of end-stage renal disease (ESRD), characterized by complex remodeling and high cardiovascular mortality. While sacubitril/valsartan (Sac/Val) and Qili Qiangxin (QLQX) granules have shown individual benefits in heart failure, their combined efficacy in ESRD patients remains poorly defined.ObjectiveTo evaluate the impact of adjunctive QLQX therapy with Sac/Val on quantitative myocardial motion, cardiac function, and hemodynamic parameters in patients with ESRD.MethodsIn this retrospective cohort study, 109 ESRD patients with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) were enrolled. Patients were divided into a control group (n = 53, Sac/Val alone) and an observation group (n = 56, Sac/Val plus QLQX). Comprehensive 2D and 4D echocardiography, including speckle-tracking for global longitudinal strain (GLS), was performed after 3 months of treatment.ResultsCompared to the control group, the observation group demonstrated significantly higher left ventricular ejection fraction ( LVEF, 45.2 ± 5.8% vs. 40.5 ± 5.2%, t = 4.446, P<0.0001) and improved GLS (-17.1 ± 2.3% vs. -15.2 ± 2.1%, P = 0.018). Significant enhancements were also observed in peak systolic myocardial velocity, cardiac output, and left atrial emptying fraction (all P < 0.05). Furthermore, the observation group showed favorable shifts in diastolic indices (E/E’ ratio and isovolumic relaxation time) and right ventricular performance (TAPSE and Tei index) (P < 0.05).ConclusionThe combination of QLQX and Sac/Val is associated with modest but statistically significant improvements in biventricular systolic and diastolic function in ESRD patients. These findings suggest a potential synergistic effect on myocardial mechanics, providing a hypothesis-generating basis for larger prospective trials.

Introduction

Cardiomyopathy in end-stage renal disease (ESRD) is a significant complication of end-stage renal disease, contributing to substantial morbidity and mortality in affected patients [1]. This condition is characterized by multifactorial pathophysiological mechanisms, including volume overload, electrolyte abnormalities, vascular calcification, myocardial fibrosis, and inflammation [2, 3]. As a consequence, patients with ESRD often experience impaired cardiac function, compromised hemodynamics, and elevated cardiovascular risk. (Figure 1).

In recent years, the management of ESRD has increasingly focused on optimizing pharmacological interventions to address the complex pathophysiology and improve patient outcomes [4]. Among the pharmacological agents used in this context, sacubitril/valsartan (Sac/Val) has emerged as a key therapeutic option for heart failure and associated cardiovascular conditions [5]. The distinctive amalgamation of neprilysin inhibition and angiotensin receptor blockade present in Sac/Val has been shown to have favorable impacts on cardiac remodeling, neurohormonal regulation, and clinical outcomes in individuals with heart failure [6]. Due to its beneficial impact on cardiac function and remodeling, Sac/Val has become a fundamental component of treatment for a range of heart failure types, including ESRD [7].

In parallel, traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) has long been recognized for its potential in managing cardiovascular conditions, offering complementary and alternative approaches to conventional pharmacotherapy [8–10]. Qili Qiangxin granules is a standardized Chinese patent medicine compound, approved by the National Medical Products Administration (NMPA) of China for the treatment of chronic heart failure [11]. Its pharmacological actions are multi-faceted, encompassing improvement of myocardial energy metabolism, inhibition of neurohormonal overactivation (e.g., renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system and sympathetic nervous system), anti-inflammatory, and anti-fibrotic effects [12]. This multi-target mechanism distinguishes it fundamentally from digoxin (Lanoxin), which is a purified cardiac glycoside that works primarily by inhibiting the Na+/K+ ATPase pump to increase intracellular calcium and contractility [13].

Qili Qiangxin granules, which are a traditional Chinese medicine formulation containing a blend of herbal components, have exhibited potential in enhancing cardiac function, alleviating symptoms, and improving the quality of life for individuals with heart failure [12, 14, 15]. The multi-faceted pharmacological actions of Qili Qiangxin, including its effects on myocardial contractility, neurohormonal modulation, and anti-inflammatory properties, have positioned it as a potential adjunctive therapy in the management of cardiac disorders [16–18].

While both Sac/Val and Qili Qiangxin granules have individually demonstrated beneficial effects on cardiac function, their combined use in the context of ESRD remains relatively unexplored. Hence, the objective of this study was to explore the impact of combining Qili Qiangxin granules with Sac/Val on the quantitative parameters of myocardial motion, cardiac function, and hemodynamic parameters in patients with ESRD.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board and Ethics Committee of The Third Affiliated Hospital of Jinzhou Medical University (Approval No. JYDSY-KXYJ-IEC-2022-042). The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study and the use of de-identified data, ensuring no impact on patient care. The research adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. This was a single-center, retrospective, observational cohort study.

Consecutive patients diagnosed with ESRD who met the inclusion and exclusion criteria between January 2022 and December 2023 were screened and enrolled. Treatment allocation was based on actual clinical decisions: patients receiving Sac/Val alone were assigned to the control group (n = 53), while those receiving Sac/Val plus Qili Qiangxin granules were assigned to the observation group (n = 56). The choice of therapy was at the discretion of the treating physician in consultation with the patient, reflecting routine clinical practice. To mitigate potential selection bias, we applied uniform inclusion/exclusion criteria and thoroughly compared baseline characteristics between the groups. Due to the non-randomized, real-world nature of the study, treatment allocation was based on actual clinical decisions: patients receiving Sac/Val alone were assigned to the control group, while those receiving Sac/Val plus Qili Qiangxin granules were assigned to the observation group. To mitigate potential selection bias and confounding, we employed several strategies: (1) Consecutive patient enrollment based on pre-defined eligibility criteria was strictly adhered to. (2) A comprehensive comparison of baseline demographic, clinical, and laboratory characteristics was performed, which confirmed the two groups were well-balanced across a wide range of measured covariates (Table 1). (3) Furthermore, to statistically adjust for any residual confounding, multivariable linear regression analyses were planned for the primary echocardiographic outcomes.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion Criteria: (1) Patients diagnosed with clinical uremia [19]; (2) Blood dialysis duration > 3 months; (3) Normal mental and cognitive function; (4) Age > 18 years; (5) High patient compliance; (6) Complete medical records; (7) Patients with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF), defined as LVEF ≤ 40% as assessed by echocardiography prior to enrollment.

Exclusion Criteria: (1) Contraindications to relevant medication use; (2) Concurrent malignancy; (3) Acute phase of infectious diseases; (4) Complex patients with multiple systemic diseases; (5) Patients presenting with severe impairments in liver, coagulation, immune, circulatory, and respiratory function.

Treatment methods

Both groups of patients received symptomatic treatment including blood pressure control, diuresis, and cardiac support. The control group was treated with Sac/Val (Manufacturer: Novartis Farma S.p.A.; National Drug Approval Number: HJ20170362; Specification: 50 mg), orally, starting at a dose of 25 mg twice daily, with the dose increasing every 2 to 4 weeks based on patient tolerance, up to 75 mg per dose [20, 21]. The observation group administered Qili qiangxin capsules (National Drug Approval Number: Z20040141; Specification: 0.3 g) as an adjunct to the standard treatment received by the control group, orally at a dosage of 4 capsules three times a day. Both patient groups underwent continuous treatment for a period of 3 months [22].

Echocardiographic acquisition and atrial strain analysis

Echocardiographic examinations were performed using a phased-array transducer with a frequency of 2–4.5 MHz and frame rates of 50–70 frames/s. Two-dimensional left atrial diameter and volume were measured in the apical four-chamber view, and values have now been added to the Results section. For atrial strain analysis, commercially available 4D speckle-tracking software (GE Healthcare, version BT13–BT20) was used. Left ventricular global longitudinal strain (GLS) was also analyzed using the same speckle-tracking software. From the apical four-chamber, two-chamber, and long-axis views, the endocardial border was automatically traced and manually adjusted if necessary. GLS was calculated as the average peak systolic longitudinal strain from all three apical views. Strain parameters were obtained at standardized time phases: reservoir strain at end-systole, conduit strain at pre-atrial contraction, and booster strain at end-diastole. Heart rate and brachial blood pressure were measured noninvasively in the supine position immediately before echocardiographic acquisition.

Observation parameters

Patient general data, including gender, age, dialysis duration, primary disease, residual urine volume, was retrieved from the medical records. Upon admission, a 3 mL fasting antecubital venous blood sample was obtained for serum isolation. Subsequently, a fully automatic biochemical analyzer (BS-280, Mindray, China) was used to test for hemoglobin, serum creatinine, blood potassium, phosphate, and calcium levels. Blood urea nitrogen levels were assessed employing the glutamate dehydrogenase method [23], whereas blood albumin levels were evaluated using the bromcresol green method [24].

Ultrasound Diagnosis: The main outcomes of the study are quantitative parameters of myocardial motion. Ultrasound diagnostic equipment (Vivid E95, GE, USA) equipped with M5Sc 2D probe (2 ~ 4.5 MHz) and 4Vc 4D probe (2.5 ~ 4 MHz) was used for routine 2D echocardiography examinations. The subjects were positioned in the left lateral decubitus position, and synchronized electrocardiography was recorded. While undergoing image acquisition, patients were directed to maintain calm breathing or to hold their breath as needed to facilitate clear and steady image capture. Echocardiography was utilized to monitor cardiac output, cardiac index, pulmonary artery pressure, left ventricular end-diastolic volume, ventricular relaxation time, isovolumic relaxation time, left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), left ventricular mass index (LVMI), left ventricular stroke volume (LVSV), right ventricular stroke volume (RVSV), fractional area change (FAC), peak systolic velocity, early diastolic peak velocity, and late diastolic peak velocity. Pulsed-wave Doppler sampling at the mitral valve was conducted in the apical 4-chamber view, acquiring Doppler spectral analysis of early diastolic mitral inflow velocity (E), late diastolic mitral inflow velocity (A), and determining the E/A ratio. Continuous-wave Doppler was employed to measure tricuspid regurgitation peak velocity (VTR), tricuspid annular systolic velocity, and tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE). Following this, the probe was adjusted to 4D echocardiography (4DE) mode, optimizing the sector size, depth, and gain of the apical four-chamber view to comprehensively display the atria. Patients were guided to maintain calm breathing and to hold their breath at the end of expiration, and a dynamic 4D full-volume image of the atria over 3 consecutive cardiac cycles was acquired. Cardiac output was calculated using the left ventricular outflow tract velocity-time integral (LVOT-VTI) method, where CO = (π × (LVOT diameter/2)² × LVOT-VTI × Heart Rate).

All images were stored and analyzed offline using the EchoPAC software (EchoPAC, GE Medical Systems, USA) measuring left and right atrial volumes, left and right atrial emptying fractions. Additionally, the software automatically delineated the endocardial border of the left atrium and generated left atrial 4D strain parameters, including global longitudinal strain and global circumferential strain. The parameters were subsequently transformed into tissue Doppler imaging (TDI) spectral Doppler format, showcasing distinct tissue signals. A 2 mm sample volume was positioned at the tricuspid annulus of the right ventricular wall, with the Tei index computed as (a-b)/b, where a denotes the interval from the end of diastolic tricuspid annular velocity to the commencement of the subsequent cycle, and b signifies the tricuspid annular systolic velocity interval.

Statistical analysis

The primary efficacy endpoint of this study was left ventricular global longitudinal strain (GLS) after 3 months of treatment, as it is a sensitive and quantitative marker of myocardial deformation. Secondary endpoints included left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), peak myocardial velocities, cardiac output, left and right atrial volumes and emptying fractions, E/E’ ratio, isovolumic relaxation time, tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE), Tei index, and other echocardiographic parameters listed in Tables 2, 3 and 4.

Sample size calculation was performed using G*Power 3.1.9.7 based on the primary endpoint. Assuming a two-tailed t-test for two independent means, an effect size (Cohen’s d) of 0.6, an alpha error of 0.05, and a power (1-β) of 0.80, the minimum required sample size was 45 patients per group. To account for potential dropouts and ensure robustness, we enrolled 53 patients in the control group and 56 in the observation group. It is acknowledged that this sample size provides adequate power for the primary endpoint but may be limited for detecting smaller differences in secondary endpoints; thus, analyses of secondary endpoints should be considered exploratory.

The data were analyzed using SPSS 29.0 statistical software. Categorical data were shown as [n(%)] and were analyzed with the chi-square test using different formulas based on sample size and theoretical frequency. For sample sizes ≥ 40 and theoretical frequency T ≥ 5, the standard chi-square test formula was used. If the sample size was ≥ 40 but the theoretical frequency was between 1 and < 5, an adjusted formula was applied. Fisher’s exact probability method was used for sample sizes < 40 or theoretical frequency T < 1. The normal distribution of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk method. Normally distributed continuous variables were displayed as (X ± s) and analyzed using the t-test with adjusted variance, while non-normally distributed data were presented as median (25th percentile, 75th percentile) and analyzed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. A two-tailed P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Then, to provide a more rigorous assessment and address the potential for type I error, we performed a post-hoc sensitivity analysis using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method to control for the expected proportion of false positives among the significant findings. Furthermore, all statistically significant findings (P < 0.05) are interpreted with caution in the discussion, with emphasis placed on the consistency and physiological plausibility of the overall pattern of results rather than on any single isolated significant P-value.

Results

General information and demographic characteristics

A total of 109 patients with ESRD were enrolled, with 53 in the control group and 56 in the observation group. As shown in Table 1, there were no statistically significant differences between groups in demographic characteristics including gender (P = 0.917), age (P = 0.603), dialysis duration (P = 0.490), or primary disease distribution (P = 0.990). Baseline hemodynamic parameters such as heart rate and blood pressure, as well as laboratory indices including serum creatinine, blood urea nitrogen, hemoglobin, serum albumin, and electrolytes (potassium, calcium, phosphorus), were comparable (all P > 0.05). Echocardiographic parameters available at baseline, including urine volume and LVEF (34.2 ± 5.6% vs. 35.8 ± 5.9%, P = 0.73), also showed no significant inter-group differences, confirming group comparability at baseline.

However, due to the retrospective nature of the study and limitations in data acquisition, comprehensive baseline echocardiographic parameters were not systematically collected, precluding within-group longitudinal analyses and definitive assessment of true treatment effects over time. To mitigate this limitation, extensive baseline comparisons and multivariable adjustments were performed to enhance the validity of between-group comparisons at the 3-month follow-up. Nonetheless, prospective studies with systematic baseline and follow-up echocardiographic assessments are warranted to unequivocally establish treatment efficacy dynamics.

Indices of systolic function

Table 2 presents the echocardiographic parameters of systolic function assessed at the 3-month follow-up. When comparing the quantitative myocardial motion parameters between the control and observation groups, notable distinctions were identified in multiple parameters. The LVEF (45.2 ± 5.8% vs. 40.5 ± 5.2%, t = 4.446, P<0.0001), left ventricular global longitudinal strain (-17.1 ± 2.3% vs. -15.2 ± 2.1%, t = 2.415, P = 0.018), peak systolic myocardial velocity (8.98 ± 0.68 cm/s vs. 8.69 ± 0.72 cm/s, t = 2.133, P = 0.035), peak early diastolic myocardial velocity (6.02 ± 0.59 cm/s vs. 5.78 ± 0.63 cm/s, t = 2.093, P = 0.039), cardiac output (4.78 ± 0.36 L/min vs. 4.62 ± 0.38 L/min, t = 2.362, P = 0.020), left ventricular stroke volume (88.23 ± 4.56 mL vs. 86.13 ± 4.89 mL, t = 2.312, P = 0.023) and pre-ejection period(103.35 ± 6.28 msec vs. 105.94 ± 6.75 msec, t = 2.073, P = 0.041) was notably higher in the observation group in comparison to the control group (Table 2). Nevertheless, there were no statistically significant variances detected in the LVMI (P = 0.057), peak late diastolic myocardial velocity (P = 0.426), cardiac index (P = 0.465) and right ventricular stroke volume (P = 0.549) between the two groups. These results indicate that incorporating Qili qiangxin into Sac/Val led to enhancements in LVEF, myocardial velocities, cardiac output, left ventricular stroke volume, and pre-ejection period, signifying potential positive impacts on myocardial function in patients with ESRD.

Deformation parameters of myocardial mechanics

The observation group exhibited a significantly higher left atrial emptying fraction (36.78 ± 4.11% vs. 35.08 ± 4.25%, t = 2.120, P = 0.036), the right atrial volume (70.24 ± 3.45 mL vs. 68.61 ± 3.78 mL, t = 2.345, P = 0.021), the atrial contraction velocity (4.89 ± 0.56 cm/s vs. 4.65 ± 0.58 cm/s, t = 2.183, P = 0.031), global circumferential strain (-21.05 ± 1.98 vs. -20.25 ± 1.86, t = 2.168, P = 0.032), peak tricuspid regurgitation velocity (2.32 ± 0.17 m/s vs. 2.25 ± 0.16 m/s, t = 2.359, P = 0.020) in comparison to the control group (Table 3). Nevertheless, there were no statistically significant variances noted in the left atrial volume (P = 0.123), right atrial emptying fraction (P = 0.079), global longitudinal strain (P = 0.051), mitral E velocity (P = 0.308), mitral A velocity (P = 0.095), E/A ratio (1.24 ± 0.07 vs. 1.22 ± 0.08, t = 1.814, P = 0.073) between the two groups. These findings indicate that incorporating Qili qiangxin alongside Sac/Val may yield favorable impacts on specific facets of atrial function. It should be noted that although statistically significant, the absolute differences in several parameters (e.g., LVEF, myocardial velocities) were modest in magnitude. Therefore, these observations should be interpreted cautiously and regarded as preliminary indications rather than conclusive evidence of substantial clinical benefit.

Indices of diastolic function and filling pressure

In the comparison of left ventricular diastolic function between the control and observation groups, several significant observations were noted (Table 4). Despite the early and late mitral inflow velocity(P = 0.088 and P = 0.242, respectively), deceleration time(P = 0.137), the fractional area change (P = 0.108) and pulmonary artery systolic pressure(P = 0.080) not displaying statistically significant differences between the two groups, the E/E’ ratio (8.37 ± 0.61 vs. 8.11 ± 0.64, t = 2.237, P = 0.027), isovolumic relaxation time(88.23 ± 4.56 msec vs. 86.17 ± 4.89 msec, t = 2.274, P = 0.025), the tricuspid annular systolic velocity(9.67 ± 0.76 cm/s vs. 9.35 ± 0.78 cm/s, t = 2.116, P = 0.037), the TAPSE and Tei index(1.78 ± 0.12 cm vs. 1.73 ± 0.11 cm, t = 2.124, P = 0.036, 0.48 ± 0.12 vs. 0.43 ± 0.11, t = 2.093, P = 0.039) exhibited a notable increase in the observation group compared to the control group. These results indicate that incorporating Qili qiangxin with Sac/Val may affect the E/E’ ratio and isovolumic relaxation time, TAPSE, Tei index, and pulmonary artery systolic pressure, indicating favorable impacts on left and right ventricular systolic function with the addition of Qili qiangxin to Sac/Val.

Discussion

ESRD is a serious complication of end-stage renal disease, contributing to significant morbidity and mortality in affected patients [25]. Our study provides evidence suggesting that the combination of Qili Qiangxin granules with Sac/Val may have beneficial effects on various aspects of myocardial motion, cardiac function, and hemodynamic parameters in patients with ESRD.

The combined therapy significantly improved key indices of systolic function, including LVEF, GLS, peak systolic myocardial velocity, and cardiac output. LVEF remains an essential indicator of global pump function, whereas GLS serves as a more sensitive, early marker of subclinical myocardial dysfunction, especially at the subendocardial level [9, 26, 27]. The concurrent improvement in these parameters suggests that Qili Qiangxin granules may enhance intrinsic myocardial contractility and cardiomyocyte mechanics beyond the effects of Sac/Val alone, consistent with its documented multi-target inotropic and anti-fibrotic properties [12, 22, 28, 29].

Beyond left ventricular systolic function, the combination therapy showed potential benefits in diastolic function, atrial mechanics, and RV performance. Increased left atrial emptying fraction, right atrial volume, TAPSE, and tricuspid annular systolic velocity collectively indicate improved biventricular-atrial coupling and RV systolic function [30, 31]. Although some myocardial deformation parameters such as global circumferential strain did not achieve statistical significance, the overall multi-parametric echocardiographic pattern suggests a comprehensive myocardial and hemodynamic response reflecting enhanced neurohormonal modulation and optimized volume status [16–18, 31].

The pathophysiological milieu of ESRD involves persistent low-grade inflammation and oxidative stress, which drive myocardial fibrosis, apoptosis, and adverse remodeling [32]. While inflammatory biomarkers were not measured in this study, the observed functional improvements are consistent with the anti-inflammatory and anti-fibrotic effects ascribed to Qili Qiangxin granules [12, 29]. Moreover, metabolic factors such as elevated uric acid, a hallmark of uremia, have been implicated in arrhythmogenic and fibrotic remodeling, especially in the atria [33, 34]. The combined therapy’s potential to modulate these intertwined inflammatory and metabolic pathways may uniquely benefit the ESRD myocardium by attenuating the chronic injurious cascade.

Besides, our observation of higher peak tricuspid regurgitation velocity and Tei index in the combination therapy group warrants careful interpretation. These parameters, indicating elevated pulmonary artery systolic pressure and impaired global ventricular function, may reflect baseline disease severity or hemodynamic differences not fully captured despite balanced clinical features. Although the combination therapy improved left ventricular function, it may not have addressed chronic pulmonary vascular remodeling or right ventricular adaptation short-term. Additionally, statistical significance amid multiple comparisons may overstate biological relevance. These findings underscore the complexity of ESRD cardiac management, where improvements in one domain do not necessarily imply parallel benefits across all cardiac parameters.

It is worth noting that from a clinical safety perspective, Sac/Val use in maintenance hemodialysis patients remains off-label but was well-tolerated in our cohort with cautious dose titration and monitoring, consistent with emerging observational data [34]. Qili Qiangxin granules are generally considered safe, with mild gastrointestinal side effects reported in rare cases [11, 22]. However, further prospective studies with rigorous safety assessments are warranted to establish the full benefit-risk profile.

An important consideration for clinical translation is the safety of the combined regimen. Based on the drug label and prior clinical studies, Qili Qiangxin is generally well-tolerated [28]. Commonly reported adverse drug reactions include mild gastrointestinal discomfort, which are often transient [28]. Contraindications include allergy to any component and pregnancy, due to the lack of safety data in these populations [28]. Specific risks of acute overdose are not well-documented in the literature, underscoring the importance of adherence to the prescribed dosage [35]. In the future, prospective trials with proactive safety monitoring are essential to fully establish the benefit-risk profile of this combination in patients with uremic cardiomyopathy.

This study has important limitations that warrant cautious interpretation. The retrospective design and lack of baseline echocardiographic data preclude definitive conclusions on within-subject changes, and residual confounding by unmeasured factors such as dialysis parameters and concomitant therapies cannot be excluded [23, 36]. Nonetheless, the two groups were well-matched on demographic, clinical, and laboratory characteristics, which supports the likelihood that observed differences were related to the intervention. The modest magnitude of echocardiographic changes should be understood in the context of the severely compromised cardiac reserve typical of ESRD patients, where even minor functional gains may be clinically meaningful. Larger, prospective, randomized controlled trials with comprehensive baseline and longitudinal assessments are essential to validate these initial findings and clarify the clinical impact of combined Qili Qiangxin and Sac/Val therapy in ESRD cardiomyopathy.

Conclusion

This study indicates that combining Qili Qiangxin granules with Sac/Val is associated with statistically significant yet modest improvements in quantitative myocardial motion and cardiac function parameters in ESRD-related cardiomyopathy. Given the severely compromised myocardial reserve and chronic inflammatory milieu of this high-risk population, even subtle functional stabilization or attenuation of deterioration may hold physiological and clinical relevance. Notably, enhancements in sensitive markers of intrinsic contractility—such as myocardial velocities and deformation indices—precede changes in ejection fraction and, together with concurrent improvements in LVEF, stroke volume, and cardiac output, suggest a coherent augmentation of ventricular systolic performance. Despite the retrospective design and limited effect size, these preliminary findings provide a plausible mechanistic basis and hypothesis-generating evidence for additive benefits of the combination therapy. Confirmation of whether these echocardiographic changes translate into meaningful long-term clinical outcomes mandates well-designed prospective randomized trials.
