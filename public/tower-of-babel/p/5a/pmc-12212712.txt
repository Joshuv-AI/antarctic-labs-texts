Advances in diagnostic ultrasound techniques for assessing liver steatosis in nonalcoholic fatty liver disease
Authors: Zhaoxin Wang, Yarong Ma, Xiaolei Wu, Yingxin Lu, Qiuyun She
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe incidence of nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), a common chronic liver disease, is increasing yearly. With increasing degrees of liver steatosis, NAFLD can progress to varying degrees of hepatic fibrosis, cirrhosis, and even hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), with a concomitant increase in the risk of metabolic syndrome and cardiovascular disease. Therefore, early diagnosis and accurate assessment of NAFLD are particularly significant. Although liver biopsy is regarded as the standard for evaluating the degree of hepatic steatosis in NAFLD, it is not frequently utilized due to its invasiveness. Ultrasound technology as a noninvasive diagnostic method has the advantages of operating simplicity and economy. It can effectively diagnose and assess the disease of NAFLD. This article mainly summarizes the current status and progress of research on the assessment of NAFLD and liver steatosis by two main types of ultrasound techniques, semi-quantitative and quantitative ultrasound, as well as other emerging techniques, and briefly describes the strengths and limitations of B-mode ultrasound, controlled attenuation parameters (CAP), and attenuation imaging (ATI) in this field.Keywords: Nonalcoholic fatty liver disease, Hepatic steatosis, Ultrasound technology, Controlled attenuation parameters, Attenuation imaging

Introduction

Nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) is a chronic liver disease characterized by excessive fat deposition in the hepatocytes due to nonalcohol and other well-defined hepatopathogenic factors. Its disease spectrum includes two disease states: non-alcoholic fatty liver (NAFL) and nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), and progresses to varying degrees of hepatic fibrosis, cirrhosis, and potentially hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) at various stages of the disease [1]. Liver failure diseases such as hepatic fibrosis and cirrhosis caused by hepatic steatosis, as well as comorbid cardiovascular diseases, are the most common risk factors for patients with NAFLD [2]. Epidemiologic surveys have shown that 3%–6% of the U.S. population is affected by NAFLD, with approximately 20% of patients progressing to cirrhosis, which is more prevalent in those with metabolic diseases and obesity [3]. Over the past 20 years, the incidence of NAFLD has been on the rise, and it is now one of the most common liver diseases in China [4]. Genetic studies have demonstrated that genetic factors play a critical role in various diseases, such as NAFLD, with a higher prevalence of NAFLD patients among their first-degree relatives than other groups. Moreover, the pharmacological treatment system for NAFLD is yet to be perfected, with most current treatments involving lifestyle changes such as sports and exercise, and weight reduction to enhance the disease condition [5]. Therefore, it is particularly important to accurately assess liver fat content for early recognition of NASH and to monitor disease changes to help patients improve their disease outcomes.

Although liver biopsy plays an indispensable role in identifying the severity of various liver diseases such as liver fibrosis and cirrhosis, and is considered the standard for the diagnostic evaluation of liver diseases, patients are not easy to accept because of its invasiveness [6]. With the development of various noninvasive examination techniques, the magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) technique represented by IDEAL-IQ can accurately quantify hepatic steatosis, but it has some shortcomings, such as long examination time, respiratory coordination, and inapplicability for patients with metal implants [7]. Ultrasonic technology is popular because of its simple operation and economy. Semi-quantitative and quantitative ultrasound techniques, as part of the noninvasive imaging assessment tools, have significant advantages when used alone or in combination with other techniques to establish a diagnostic model [8]. In addition, with the rapid development of medical imaging technology and computer technology, the combination of artificial intelligence and medical imaging technology is gradually gaining attention. In the diagnostic evaluation of NAFLD, many studies have confirmed that artificial intelligence has high potential for quantitatively detecting the degree of hepatic steatosis in patients with NAFLD, as well as for NASH and liver fibrosis [9]. Faced with the development of various diagnostic techniques, we will summarize and analyze the current status and progress of research on the assessment of NAFLD and liver steatosis using two main types of ultrasound techniques: semi-quantitative and quantitative ultrasound, as well as other emerging techniques.

Semi-quantitative ultrasound diagnosis

B-mode ultrasound

The diagnosis of a fatty liver through B-mode ultrasound offers numerous benefits, including a quick and dependable diagnosis that is rarely influenced by liver fibrosis and liver water content. Fatty liver also displays specific features on B-mode ultrasound images. Additionally, B-mode imaging enables the assessment of the degree of fatty infiltration in liver tissue to determine the grade of fatty liver [10]. A meta-analysis by Hernaez R et al. [11] examined the characteristics of parenchymal brightness, liver–kidney contrast, bright vessel wall, and diaphragm under B-mode ultrasound compared with the histological imaging of liver biopsy. The study used 20%–30% fat infiltration as a criterion for defining a fatty liver, which showed that ultrasound could be accurately detected and screened for moderately to severely fatty livers, with a sensitivity of 84.8% and a specificity of 93.6%. The combination of echogenic enhancement, or “bright liver” with portal vein blurring also had high sensitivity and specificity, although at relatively low levels of histologic fatty infiltration. Furthermore, to improve the diagnostic yield of NAFLD, Hamaguchi M et al. [12] assessed the degree of hepatic steatosis by means of a score based on another grading criterion developed under B-mode ultrasound, achieving 91.7% sensitivity and 100% specificity. Although the B-mode ultrasound technique has high sensitivity and specificity for screening asymptomatic patients, this method depends on the operator and clinical setting, and the accuracy is reduced in patients with more abdominal gas, morbid obesity, and small amounts of fat infiltration [13].

Hepatorenal steatosis index

The hepatorenal steatosis index (HRI) identifies NAFLD presence by taking the average brightness of liver parenchyma by the average brightness of kidney parenchyma, and the HRI provides a semi-quantitative scoring system, enabling more precise quantification of mild steatosis [14]. In an observational study [15], 82 participants examined all segments of the liver and kidneys using B-mode ultrasound, selected the region of interest, and calculated the HRI for histogram analysis. This study's data shows a good correlation between the ultrasound liver–kidney ratio and the grades of hepatic steatosis (r = 0.80, p < 0.01). The optimal threshold for NAFLD diagnosis using the ultrasound liver–kidney ratio is 1.24, with a sensitivity of 92%. Seven percent with a specificity of 92.5%. HRI compensates for the lack of B-mode ultrasound for diagnosis of NAFLD in some aspects, but HRI still has weaknesses: biased results under the premise of hepatic fibrosis; severe renal disease leading to shifted results; lack of a large amount of clinical data validation, etc., so the use rate in the clinic is gradually declining [16].

Quantitative ultrasound diagnosis

Controlled attenuation parameters

The controlled attenuation parameter (CAP) is a new method for the quantitative determination of the degree of intrahepatic steatosis based on transient elastography of the liver, which provides data on liver fat content. CAP and liver stiffness measurement (LSM) data can be obtained simultaneously using a machine equipped with vibration-controlled transient elastography (VCTE), an elastography technique widely used in the assessment of fibrosis, that provides an estimate of the degree of degeneration of the fat content of the liver through CAP measurements [17]. Currently, CAP technology with two devices, the FibroScan system (Echosense, Paris, France) and the FibroTouch system (China), are prevalent in both domestic and international clinical settings. A prospective study analyzed the effectiveness of CAP and LSM under the FibroScan system in diagnosing potential NAFLD patients. This study used the histologic findings of liver biopsy as reference standards to determine critical values for clinical use of CAP and LSM. It was found that CAP is positively linked to hepatic steatosis, with the greatest accuracy when steatosis (S) ≥ 1, and the optimal CAP threshold is 302 dB/m. Furthermore, detecting moderate steatosis is more crucial in clinical settings than identifying S2 and S3, and hence a CAP value of 331 dB/m is a key indicator for recognizing the severity of steatosis when S ≥ S2 [18]. This study confirms the advantage of VCTE in ascertaining NAFLD diagnosis by measuring CAP, with high applicability and low failure rates. In recent years, research on CAP technology has gradually increased in China, but the FibroTouch system device is mainly used in China. Fu et al. [19] in the study of the diagnostic value of CAP measured by FibroTouch for NAFLD, patients with NAFLD were clarified through liver biopsy, and while completing the measurement of the CAP, the serological noninvasive model was calculated using the hepatic steatosis index (HSI) and fasting triacylglycerol glucose product (TyG) to analyze the correlation between the three and hepatic steatosis. The study revealed a gradual increase in the CAP, HSI, and TyG values along with the hepatic steatosis grading elevation (p < 0.05). By analysis, hepatic steatosis grading emerged as the independent influencing factor of CAP, proving its diagnostic efficacy superior to the other two serological tests. This study affirmed that the CAP can accurately determine the degree of hepatic steatosis and achieve dynamic monitoring of hepatic steatosis in clinical practice. In regards to the choice of M and XL probes when measuring CAP using the FibroScan system and any potential difference in diagnostic accuracy between the two probes, research has demonstrated [20] that the decision of which probe to use is dependent on whether the distance from the skin to the hepatic peritoneum is greater than 25 mm in diverse populations. In the same population, the LSM provided by the M probe is higher than that of the XL probe, and the CAP is lower than that of the XL probe, but there is no statistical difference in the diagnostic accuracy between the two probes.

Attenuation imaging

Attenuation imaging (ATI) is another novel ultrasonic quantitative technology. Its principle is that the attenuation of ultrasonic waves in the medium is positively correlated with the signal intensity, and the attenuation degree of different media is different under the same signal intensity, so the slope attenuation coefficient (AC) of the signal intensity curve obtained by computer processing can be used to quantitatively evaluate the degree of fatty liver tissue by measuring the AC value [21]. Unlike CAP technology,the ATI is measured by selecting the liver tissue of the region of interest under the guidance of two-dimensional images, avoiding areas that affect the measurement results such as large blood vessels and strong artifacts, and so on [22]. In recent years, numerous domestic and international scholars have concentrated on the diagnostic value of ATI for NAFLD, providing a basis for the mature development of this technology. Tada T et al. [23] performed liver biopsy and ATI examination on 148 subjects, and the results showed that, in the whole subjects and the NAFLD patients within, the ATI was positively correlated with the steatosis grading (p < 0.001), and in the obese patients, the values increased significantly with the grade of steatosis (p = 0.002). This study confirms that the ATI technique has a good ability to recognize different grades of hepatic steatosis. In a study on the diagnostic performance of ATI for low-grade hepatic steatosis, Jang JK et al. [24] analyzed and compared the accuracy of ATI and CAP in the diagnosis of low-grade hepatic steatosis, taking liver biopsy as the reference standard and grading the liver fat fraction by histology. In low-grade steatosis without fibrosis, the ATI technique showed a degree of sensitivity and specificity in diagnosing and quantifying hepatic steatosis, with a sensitivity of 61.5% and a specificity of 90.3%. Histopathology established that in low-grade steatosis, only the hepatic fat fraction had a significant effect on AC values (p < 0.001). There is also a lack of studies comparing ATI with other imaging tools. Kwon EY et al. [25] evaluated the performance of ATI in the diagnosis and grading of hepatic steatosis by comparing it with the magnetic resonance imaging-proton density fat fraction (MRI-PDFF) of the mDIXON-Quant sequence. The study involved the categorization of 100 patients into 3 groups according to their MRI-PDFF Q (%), and measuring the AC values for each level of steatosis. Statistical analysis revealed a significant difference in the mean AC values for steatosis in each group (p < 0.001), and there was also a strong correlation between AC and MRI-PDFF (r = 0.751, p < 0.001). Studies have confirmed that ATI has high diagnostic performance for NAFLD and hepatic steatosis grading. Jeon SK et al. [26] conducted a study to assess the reproducibility of ATI across different platforms in patients with NAFLD. The researchers recruited 46 patients with clinically confirmed NAFLD and used three different platforms (ATI, Canon Medical System; tissue attenuation imaging (TAI), Samsung Medison; ultrasound-guided attenuation parameters (UCAP), GE Healthcare). The mean AC was significantly different between the three platforms (p < 0.001), and inter-platform variability was significant. It has been concluded that, for longitudinal follow-up studies of patients with NAFLD, the consistency of platforms should be ensured despite the reproducibility of acoustic attenuation between them. This is due to the variability of AC values measured by different platforms in clinical practice.

Remaining elastography

In addition to the previously mentioned VCTE, there is a growing number of studies on newer “ultrasound elastography” techniques such as shear wave elastography (SWE) and acoustic radiation force impulses (ARFI). SWE is based on B-mode ultrasound two-dimensional images of a homogeneous, well-filled area free of large vascular structures, and the tissue in the area of interest is quantified to reflect its stiffness. The ARFI technique, similar to the SWE technique, quantifies the degree of hepatic steatosis by applying focused acoustic pulses to the tissue in the region of interest to obtain shear wave velocity values [17]. Podrug K et al. [27] included 232 participants (164 in the NAFLD group and 68 in the healthy control group) who were measured by the CAP parameter of the VCTE technique as a reference and the CAP parameter of the VCTE technique as a reference. The CAP parameters of the technique were used as a reference, and two-dimensional shear wave elastography (2D-SWE) combined with propagation maps were used as a research method, and the following results were obtained: there was a significant correlation between 2D-SWE and VCTE (r = 0.71, p < 0.000 1), and the specificity thresholds of 2D-SWE for the measurement of liver stiffness in NAFLD were as follows: F (degree of liver fibrosis in patients with NAFLD), 7.9 kPa at F ≥2; 10 kPa at F ≥3; and 11.4 kPa at F = 4. The optimal 2D-SWE measurement of liver stiffness for the diagnosis of cirrhosis was 15.5 kPa according to the Baveno VI criteria. This study demonstrated that 2D-SWE, together with the propagation map, is reliable for quantitative analysis of liver fibrosis and fat in NAFLD patients. There are fewer clinical studies on the value of ARFI technology for NAFLD diagnosis, but some experimental studies based on NAFLD animal models have some significance for the development of this technology. Guo et al. [28], in a study used ARFI elastography to identify NAFLD and different stages of hepatic fibrosis in a rat model. It was found that this technique can distinguish normal rats from rats with simple steatosis (SS) as well as SS from NASH, with AUROC of 0.963 and 0.882, respectively. The research also assessed the degree of hepatic fibrosis (F ≥ 2) in NAFLD rats with an AUROC of 0.963. The measurement of shear wave velocit (SWV). values by the ultrasound ARFI technique can be used to evaluate the different degrees of NAFLD and different stages of hepatic fibrosis and is of good evaluative value. Noninvasive model

Recently, studies of various noninvasive models for identifying NASH and liver fibrosis have been reported. LSM and CAP measured by a device equipped with VCTE (FibroScan) were combined with aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and were designated FibroScan-AST (FAST). This new model can accurately identify high-risk NASH patients [29]. FAST can accurately assess for high-risk NASH in NAFLD patients with or without metabolic risk factors, and selecting the appropriate threshold may prevent some patients from undergoing liver biopsy [30]. In a comparative study of three noninvasive stiffness-based models, including magnetic resonance elastography [MRE] plus FIB-4 (MEFIB), magnetic resonance imaging [MRI]-AST (MAST), and FibroScan-AST (FAST), all three models provide a practical basis for risk stratification in NAFLD. MEFIB is superior and faster at detecting significant fibrosis and high-risk NASH [31].

Conclusion

Among the various ultrasound techniques discussed in this article, semi-quantitative ultrasound technology remains inadequate for evaluating the level of hepatic steatosis, whereas quantitative ultrasound technology provides more alternatives to measuring hepatic steatosis. In clinical practice, the degree of hepatic steatosis can be regularly monitored, thus allowing for noninvasive grading of NAFLD and achieving follow-up and timely intervention in disease progression. Although the application of CAP technology has been developed over a long period of time, several studies have demonstrated that the introduction of new technology and the combined application of multiple quantitative ultrasound techniques have more unique and extensive diagnostic value in the assessment and tracking of hepatic steatosis in NAFLD. Of course, individual emerging ultrasound technologies are not yet mature in terms of development and application, and the lack of a large number of studies with high-quality evidence still requires further research and confirmation. In conclusion, this article summarizes the assessment and diagnostic value of various ultrasound technologies for NAFLD and liver fibrosis and hopes to provide ideas for the development of ultrasound technology in liver diseases.
