A new late Cretaceous squamate from Patagonia sheds light on Gondwanan diversity
Authors: Federico L. Agnolín, Mauro Aranciaga-Rolando, Gerardo Álvarez-Herrera, Martín D. Ezcurra, Ana Moreno Rodríguez, Pablo Chafrat, Nahuel Vega, Agustín Scanferla, Krister T. Smith, Fernando E. Novas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The fossil record of Mesozoic terrestrial squamates is very poor in the Southern Hemisphere. Currently recognised species represent less than 6% of the number described for Laurasian landmasses. Here, we describe the most complete Late Cretaceous terrestrial squamate known from South America. The specimen was collected in the Maastrichtian Allen Formation of northern Patagonia (Río Negro Province, Argentina) and represents a single individual that preserves a partial skull, vertebrae, and appendicular bones. The skeleton shows a unique combination of character states that allow erecting the new genus and species Paleoteius lakui. Morphological phylogenetic analyses recovered Paleoteius lakui as a Scincomorpha more closely related to Xantusiidae than to other species. When a molecular backbone is applied to those analyses, Paleoteius lakui is found outside Scincomorpha, but within a clade composed of Jurassic to Palaeogene species found as non-crown scincomorphans in previous studies. The affinities of Paleoteius lakui either as a stem-xantusiid or as a more early-diverging clade indicate the presence of a yet unrecognised lineage of squamates in South America. Paleoteius lakui is related to Laurasian forms with a probable cosmopolitan ancestry during the Jurassic–earliest Cretaceous. The new finding helps fill the still poorly known record of Mesozoic Gondwanan squamates.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-40914-8.

Introduction

Living squamates include approximately 128,000 species distributed worldwide, except Antarctica1. Despite their great diversification and wide geographical distribution, the Mesozoic squamate record is incomplete and biased, with most findings restricted to the Northern Hemisphere2–7. As expressed by several authors, probably the main obstacle for understanding the early evolution of squamates is the poor fossil record in the Southern Hemisphere6,8,9. Only a dozen squamate species have been described from the Cretaceous of the Southern Hemisphere (excluding mosasaurs), which strongly contrasts with the more than 150 species (an order of magnitude more) described from the northern landmasses (e.g.,8,9). The fossil record of Mesozoic terrestrial squamates in South America is sparse, with most informative specimens coming from the Early Cretaceous of Brazil9–14, but preserved as two-dimensional specimens that makes it difficult to observe detailed anatomical features. Late Cretaceous findings in Brazil are restricted to incomplete specimens, including the Turonian-Campanian acrodontan iguanian Gueragama sulamericana9, the Campanian, possible chamopsiid, Brasiliguana prudentis15,16, the Maastrichtian iguanomorph Pristiguana brasiliensis17, and other unnamed records18. In Argentina, the record is even poorer, with a few scattered remains belonging to innominate iguanians and scincomorphs collected in Upper Cretaceous units10,19–21. The only nominal species is the polyglyphanodont Paleochelco occultato, represented by a partial skull from the Upper Cretaceous (Santonian) of Neuquén Province, northwestern Patagonia16. These discoveries demonstrate that the diversity of terrestrial squamates in the Cretaceous of South America is higher than previously thought, but still very poorly sampled and understood. New efforts to systematically collect small fossils in the Upper Cretaceous units, with various techniques (e.g., picking, dry- and screen-washing, quarrying), started to show that the apparent near-absence of squamates is in all probability a collection bias.

Here, we describe the most complete squamate specimen known so far from the Late Cretaceous of South America. This single individual preserves a partial skull, some vertebrae, and partial appendicular elements that allow erecting a new genus and species. The completeness of the specimen allows testing its phylogenetic position quantitatively and sheds light on the diversity of squamates before the end of the Mesozoic Era.

Diagnosis

Small-sized squamate (complete mandible length is 21.6 mm) distinguished by the following combination of character states: skull roof bones sculptured by large, sub-oval to sub-triangular tubercles generally well separated from each other; elongate and dorsoventrally low dentary with a high tooth count (37 tooth positions); anterior region of the Meckelian canal ventrally facing; dentary with posteriormost mental foramen larger than the more anterior ones; splenial with a large mylohyoid foramen housed within a wide longitudinal groove (autapomorphy); presence of more than 35 tooth on the dentary; mandibular adductor fossa without distinct medial wall; retroarticular process posteroventrally directed; posterior region of the lower jaw with a prearticular crest; subdental shelf prominent and thick, delimiting dorsally a deep and broad dental sulcus (= “sulcus dentalis” or “subdental gutter”) that is not filled with cementum (autapomorphy); and marginal teeth with pleurodont implantation and relatively small and straight crowns without accessory cusps nor enamel ornamentation.

Etymology

‘Paleo’ in reference to ‘old’, from the ancient Greek παλαιός (palaiós) and ‘teius’, from ‘teyú’, in Tupi-Guaraní language, which is the most common name for squamates in the Argentinean territory. The specific epithet is derived from the term ‘laku’, meaning grandfather in the Mapuche language Mapudungun.

Locality and horizon

The specimen comes from Maastrichtian rocks of the mid-section of the Allen Formation24,25 at the Salitral Ojo de Agua area, Arriagada Farm, 70 km south of General Roca city, Río Negro Province, northern Patagonia, Argentina (Fig. 1).

Results

Description

The specimen was found associated, but disarticulated, in a small block of rock (Figs. 1,2). There is no evidence of body osteoderms.

The maxilla lacks the tip of the anterior and posterior processes and most of the ascending process (Fig. 1). The bone is subtriangular in lateral view, with a short anterior process and a longer posterior one. The dorsal half of the external surface of the maxilla is sculptured by rugosities with a random pattern. Ventrally, the surface of the bone is smooth and bears seven supralabial foramina. The alveolar margin of the maxilla is slightly convex in lateral view. In medial view, the vomerine and palatal shelves are well-developed longitudinally and medially. The facet for the jugal is developed as a shallow longitudinal and dorsally facing furrow. The maxilla is nearly straight in ventral view.

The prefrontal preserves its ventral process and the orbitonasal flange, lacking the anterior and posterior rami (Figs. 1, S3, S4). The ventral process is a transversely broad lamina that completely encloses the lacrimal foramen. The lateral edge of the ventral process possesses an anteriorly directed, thin, and dorsally curved process that represents the facet for reception of the lacrimal. Dorsomedially to this process, there is a small nutrient foramen on the posterior surface of the prefrontal. The lachrymal duct is observed as an oval foramen completely enclosed by the bone.

The jugal only preserves a small portion of the orbital margin. As in other skull bones of Paleoteius, it is strongly sculptured by irregular tubercles.

The skull roof is represented by a partial left frontal and parietal (Figs. 1, S3, S4). The external surface of both bones is strongly sculptured by sub-oval to sub-triangular, well-developed tubercles. The inner surface of the frontal has a low, mound-like subolfactory process that bows medially. The posteromedial margin of the bone has a distinct groove that received a tapering anterior projection of the parietal. The inner surface of the parietal has a prominent epipterygoid process that is connected with the lateral wall of the parietal fossa by a medially bowing ridge. The posterolateral process of the parietal is short, and its inner surface has a distinct facet for the supratemporal bone adjacent to the lateral margin of the bone.

The quadrate head is preserved. The quadrate head is rounded, notably convex, and slightly posteriorly directed. The preserved portion of the central column is wide and shows a circular cross-section. The quadrate head lacks an accessory process, as in Xantusiidae26. The distal condyles are preserved in articulation with the mandible. This exhibit two round distal condyles.

The lower jaw is represented by the dentary, splenial, coronoid, angular, and fused surangular-prearticular-articular (Figs. 2, S1). The complete mandible length is 21.6 mm and the tooth row length is 8.8 mm. The dentary is a slender and elongated bone with a slight dorsal curvature. The lateral surface of the bone is smooth and bears at least 10 mental foramina, of which the posteriormost is the largest. In the posterior end of the dentary, the coronoid and surangular (posterodorsal) processes are subequal in size and extension, being separated from the angular process by a wide concave notch. The angular process is not entirely preserved, but it was the longest based on its preserved articular facet on the post-dentary bones. The dentary does not form part of a coronoid eminence. In ventral view, the dentary distinctly curves medially throughout its length. The Meckelian groove is centred on the medial surface of the posterior two-thirds of the dentary, but it gradually moves ventrally to fully face ventrally on the anterior end of the bone. The dentary possesses a flat and short symphyseal surface ventrally delimited by the Meckelian groove. The subdental shelf is prominent and thick, becoming progressively thinner posteriorly. This shelf delimits dorsally a deep and broad dental sulcus that is not filled with cementum. The subdental shelf abuts the splenial ventrally.

The splenial is elongate with a large mylohyoid foramen close to its anterior margin, which is housed within a relatively wide longitudinal groove. The dorsal margin of the bone inserts into a complex ridge and groove articulation with the dentary. A long, slender sliver of the angular extends between the splenial and dentary up to the level of mid-length of the splenial. The coronoid extends dorsally to the subdental ridge in medial view, and on the dorsolateral surface of the dentary there is a small facet where the coronoid would have extended, but it is positioned far posteriorly from the level of the last tooth.

The prearticular, articular and surangular are fused to form a single, elongate element. It extends anteriorly as a short, tall, and subtriangular process that reaches the level of the last dentary tooth. The adductor fossa is elongate, transversely narrow, and relatively shallow, being medially delimited by a thin and low crest. The glenoid fossa is cup-shaped and still retains the quadrate condyles in articulation. This fossa is anteriorly delimited by a deep, funnel-shaped groove that ends in an anterior foramen.

The retroarticular process is hatch-shaped, with a distal subtriangular posterior extension and a short and acute anterior one separated from the rest of the bone by a deeply concave surface.

The dentition is pleurodont, and the tooth bases are supported by the subdental shelf on the dentary and a supradental shelf on the maxilla (Figure S2). The lateral wall of the dentary is clearly distinguishable from each tooth crown. The dentition is homodont on both lower and upper dental arcades. The teeth extend less than halfway down the medial surface of the mandible. The dentary has 37 and the maxilla 27 tooth positions. The teeth are subequal in size and shape; the symphyseal teeth of the dentary are lower and narrower. The teeth are closely packed, tall and slender, slightly expanding towards their bases. The crown apices are subconical and relatively straight in lingual view, being slightly labially oriented. The teeth lack additional cusps and are devoid of ornamentation, yet they show a distinct cuspis lingualis, as is common in Scincoidea and certain other squamate groups. Subtle resorption pits are observed on the lingual margin of some maxillary teeth; no replacement teeth are preserved (see SI).

The preserved presacral vertebrae are procoelous and anteroposteriorly elongated (Figs. 1, S5, S6). The anterior articular surface of the centrum is slightly anteroventrally oriented. The ventral surface shows a longitudinal excavation delimited by two subparallel ridges. They have subcentral foramina, and the zygosphene shelf is strongly anteriorly notched.

The femoral head is dorsally convex and strongly dorsomedially upturned in posterior view. In proximal view, the articular surface of the femoral head runs perpendicular to the major axis of the proximal end of the bone. The intertrochanteric fossa is deep, showing a step-like margin close to the femoral head. The internal trochanter is only preserved in the left femur. It is small and dorsally projected.

Phylogenetic results

In the analysis without a molecular backbone (see Appendix for more details about the results), the global strict consensus tree (GSCT) is generally well-resolved, and Iguania, Polyglyphanodontia, Gekkota, and Anguimorpha (including varanoids as sister taxon to a clade composed of mosasaurs and snakes) are the successively closer sister taxa to Scincomorpha (Fig. 3). Paleoteius lakui is recovered within Scleroglossa and Scincomorpha. In particular, the new species is found as a member of Scincomorpha to the exclusion of the lineage leading to Scincidae because of the presence of a reduced angular medial exposure (character 383: 0 → 1), within the clade composed of Cordylidae (including gerrhosaurids) and Xantusiidae because of a distinct parietal epipterygoid process (108: 0 → 1), and as a stem-Xantusiidae because of a lacrimal duct enclosed entirely in the prefrontal (141: 0 → 2) and a splenial extending anteriorly around one-third (or less) relative to the dentary tooth row length (375: 1/2 → 0). Paleoteius lakui is excluded from the group formed by Tepexisaurus tepexii and Xantusiidae because it lacks a dentary Meckelian canal with upper and lower borders forming a sutural contact anterior to the splenial (372: 0 → 226;). In the case of the analyses under a molecular backbone, the GSCT has a massive polytomy composed of the main clades of Squamata (Supplementary Information). The pruning of some species from the GSCT (i.e., Carusia intermedia, Myrmecodaptria microphagosa, Paleochelco occultato, Calanguban alamoi, Eoscincus ornatus, and Eichstaettisaurus) produced a considerably better resolved topology, in which species often regarded as pan-scincoids (sensu23) are recovered as a polyphyletic group (Figure S9). In these analyses, Scincoidea (and Scincomorpha) is recovered as the sister taxon to Toxicofera, and it comprises the extant clades Scincidae, Cordylidae, and Xantusiidae. By contrast, several scincomorphan species found as stem taxa in the analysis without molecular backbone and previous studies (e.g.,23) are recovered here in a clade sister taxon to Unidentata (i.e., Toxicofera + Scincoidea). Paleoteius lakui is found as a member of this clade of purported ‘stem-scincoids’, which is also composed of Late Jurassic and Palaeogene species from the Northern Hemisphere. The new species from Patagonia is supported as a member of this clade of purported ‘stem-scincoids’ by the presence of a parietal supratemporal process reduced to less than 25% of parietal width (character 101: 0 → 1), character 375 (1/2 → 0, see state description above), and a prearticular crest (403: 0 → 1). The resampling frequencies of the higher-level branches and around the base of the purported ‘stem-scincoids’ are low (< 50%).

Discussion

Paleoteius lakui represents the most complete terrestrial squamate from the Upper Cretaceous of South America, preserving both cranial and postcranial bones. The completeness of the new species allows identifying an entirely novel combination of character states among the known Cretaceous squamates, including a very long, low, and unsculptured dentary firmly sutured to the splenial, very high tooth count, homodont teeth lacking cusps and enamel ornamentation, and skull roof bones strongly sculptured by sub-oval to sub-triangular tubercles well differentiated from each other, and well anteroposteriorly elongated trunk vertebrae.

Paleoteius lakui possesses some features widespread among members of Iguanomorpha, which is a clade that has been regarded as having a Southern Hemisphere origin27. Among these features we can list: a skull roof strongly sculptured by tubercles, a concave orbital margin that results in an hourglass-shaped interorbital bar and dentary with well-developed subdental shelf and gutter3,28. However, despite such similarities, Paleoteius lakui differs from iguanomorphs in the presence of features such as a medially opened Meckelian canal, absence of a prearticular angular process, and coronoid bone lacking well-developed anteromedial and anterolateral processes5,29.

Paleoteius lakui is clearly distinguishable from previously described squamates from the Upper Cretaceous of South America. The presence of pleurodont, tall, and slender teeth with subconical crown apices differs from the broad subacrodont teeth of the acrodontan iguanian Gueragama sulamericana, the barrel-shaped teeth of the iguanian Brasiliguana prudentis9,15, the labiolingually expanded teeth of the polyglyphanodontian Paleochelco occultato16, and the tricuspid dentary teeth of the iguanian Pristiguana brasiliensis17. In addition, the strongly sculptured skull roof bones of Paleoteius lakui differ from the smooth condition observed in Paleochelco and Pristiguana16,17.

Our phylogenetic analyses showed that Paleoteius lakui is a member of Scincomorpha, closely related to the extant family Xantusiidae26, or alternatively that it is in a clade of Jurassic-Palaeogene species usually identified as stem-scincoids (but nested within a clade outside of Scincomorpha in ouranalyses under a molecular backbone). In particular, these two alternative topologies, invite further exploration of the phylogenetic relationships of species frequently considered as stem-scincoids. In agreement with the scincomorphan affinities of Paleoteius lakui, this species shows features traditionally regarded as diagnostic of the groupScincomorpha, such as a relatively large splenial bone that leaves a medially open Meckelian canal, a non-laterally exposed coronoid bone, a mylohyoid foramen anterior to the coronoid apex, and a shallow surangular external fossa3,22,28.

Scincomorpha is a distinct clade of squamates that, excepts for Xantusiidae, frequently present dorsal scales or osteoderms forming a plate armour see30–33. Scincomorpha includes several extant and extinct lineages with more than 1,800 modern species distributed worldwide (except Antarctica), occurring in most temperate and tropical terrestrial habitats33. Fossil scincomorphs are relatively rare. Paramacellodidae is one of their earliest diverging clades[34,35; but here recovered outside of Scincomorpha in the analyses under a molecular backbone], being known from the Middle Jurassic to the Late Cretaceous of the Northern Hemisphere and they were probably widespread across Pangaea during their early evolutionary history12,36–45. By the Early Cretaceous, scincomorphans are recorded in most continents, including South America12, and the Late Cretaceous occurrences in the Southern Hemisphere include a cordylid from Madagascar40 and two fragmentary lower jaws from northern Patagonia that are reminiscent of scincomorphs10,21.

The taxonomic and structural diversity patterns among the Cretaceous squamate clades remain virtually unknown in the Southern Hemisphere41. This is why fossils like that of Paleoteius lakui are of major relevance for understanding the evolutionary history of the southern squamates. In this sense, despite the poor record of Cretaceous squamates in South America, their meagre fossil record so far indicates the presence of Polyglyphanodontia, Iguanomorpha, Paramacellodidae, and now Scincomorpha or a clade composed of purported ‘stem-scincoid’ species12,13. This bolsters the idea of an early radiation and broad geographical distribution for these squamate clades, weakening the view that they were not speciose in the Southern Hemisphere9,12,21,40,42. Recent discoveries suggest a complex biogeographical scenario, following the report of the occurrence of typically Laurasian taxa in Lower and Upper Cretaceous beds of South America13,16. Our phylogenetic results imply a long ghost lineage for the branch of Paleoteius lakui that extends back to at least the Early Cretaceous. Thus, the occurrence of Paleoteius lakui indicates that scincomorphans or the members of a clade composed of purported ‘stem-scincoids’ have deep roots in South America, as is the case for Paramacellodidae12.

Bittencourt et al.12 stated that the Cretaceous South American squamates had a relatively high degree of cosmopolitanism and were part of a broader distribution of their respective clades, in contrast to the high endemicity of modern faunas. They also proposed that some of the oldest known South American squamate clades include taxa that are more closely related to the extant fauna of the Old World continents rather than those of current-day South America. However, it is well-known that well-sampled Late Cretaceous assemblages were strongly geographically restricted (e.g.,43,44), and Benson et al.4 indicated that lepidosaurs showed increasing geographic diversification during the Cretaceous. The highly apomorphic body plan of Paleoteius lakui suggests that it was part of a unique radiation of scincomorphans (or a lineage composed of purported ‘stem-scincoids’) with a long evolutionary history of at least 40 million years. Polyglyphanodonts are the numerically dominant species in the squamate assemblages of the Maastrichtian of North America6,45. Thus, the occurrence of Paleoteius lakui, together with the so far absence of polyglyphanodonts in the Maastrichtian beds of Patagonia, supports the hypothesis of Nydam45 that the squamate faunas of the Northern and the Southern hemispheres were very different from each other at least in taxonomic composition.

The fossil record of South American Mesozoic squamates includes species belonging to extinct lineages that are not closely related to extant ones. This was regarded as evidence of a post-Cretaceous explosive evolutionary radiation of squamates after the Cretaceous-Palaeocene mass extinction event6,29. The apomorphic body plan of Paleoteius lakui without closely related forms in the Palaeogene of South America could reinforce the proposal of a major turn over after the K-P extinction.

In sum, the new species Paleoteius lakui constitutes the most complete Late Cretaceous terrestrial squamate specimen from South America. The finding of Paleoteius helps to fill one of the most important geographical biases in the squamate record6 and constitutes an important addition to the inferred high squamate diversity for the Mesozoic of South America. Because of its distinctiveness, Paleoteius lakui also points to the existence of long and still unrecognised evolutionary histories of South American squamates. It reinforces the idea that squamates were also well-diversified in the southern continents and that a major turnover occurred in their assemblages around the Cretaceous-Palaeocene mass extinction event.

Methods

Specimen

The specimen here described comes from the Bonapartenykus site (GPS 67°17′42.10"W, 39°26′2.20"S24;), located within the Arriagada Farm, 70 km south from the General Roca city, Río Negro Province, Argentina. This area is known as Salitral Ojo de Agua24 and exposes rocks of the Allen Formation dated as Maastrichtian46. This stratigraphic unit is composed of 70-m-thick sandstones, mudstones, and pelites that represent shallow water courses and preserve freshwater and brackish vertebrates and molluscs47–49. See specific information about the preparation of the specimen in the Supplementary Information.

CTScan settings

The specimen was scanned using a Nikon XT H 225 ST 2 × system controlled by the Inspect-X XT 6.13 software. The acquisition was performed with an accelerating voltage of 150 kV and a tube current of 75 µA. A 0.5 mm-thick aluminium filter was used to reduce beam hardening effects. The scan comprised 2,200 projections, each with a single frame and an exposure time of 354 ms. The detector pixel size was 150 µm, and the effective voxel size was approximately 25 µm, calculated by dividing the detector pixel size by the geometric magnification factor (6.03 ×).

Imaging

Virtual three-dimensional images were obtained using standard procedures and visualized using the software Amira 5.3.3. 3D data were exported and processed in Blender to obtain detailed views and renders. Scans and 3D data were upload in Morphosource: https://www.morphosource.org/concern/biological_specimens/000806266?locale=en.

Phylogenetic analyses

The phylogenetic relationships of the new Cretaceous squamate species described here were assessed using a modified version of the data matrix of Gauthier et al.22, as recently modified by Brownstein et al.50 (see Supplementary Information for more details about the methods of these analyses). The dataset comprises 130 active terminals and 622 characters (122 of them ordered), and was analysed under maximum parsimony using TNT 1.651. The analyses were conducted under implied weighting with concavity constants (k) between 8 and 14 (i.e., a total of seven analyses). The decision of weighting against homoplasy follows results that showed that implied weighting outperformed equal weighting in topological accuracy and stability52,53. The range of k-values used here follows the recommendation of Ezcurra53 based on the number of terminals of the data matrix. The tree search strategy initially used a combination of the tree-search algorithms sectorial searches, drifting, ratchet, and tree fusing, until 100 hits of the same minimum tree length were achieved. The shortest trees obtained were then subjected to a final round of tree-bisection-reconnection branch swapping. Zero-length branches in any of the recovered most parsimonious trees were collapsed. Group supports were quantified using no-zero weight symmetric resampling analyses after 10,000 pseudoreplicates, in which prior weights were doubled or halved with the same probability of 25%. Both absolute frequency and frequency difference (i.e., GC = groups recovered versus groups contradicted) were reported on the strict consensus tree of each analysis. A global strict consensus tree was generated from all the trees retained in the different analyses under implied weighting with the seven different k-values. Similarly, absolute and GC resampling frequencies were calculated from all the resampling trees recovered in the different analyses. This analytical protocol was used for a first analysis of the matrix without topological constraints, and a second analysis using a topological constraint based on a molecular backbone that mostly followed the results recovered by Reeder et al.54 (see Supplementary Information).

Anatomical nomenclature

It follows Hernández-Morales et al.55, with jaw nomenclature and tooth attachment following Longrich et al.29.
