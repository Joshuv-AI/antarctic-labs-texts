A basement membrane-related signature for prognosis and immunotherapy benefit in bladder cancer based on machine learning
Authors: Yunuo Zhang, Jingna Wu, Xinhong Liang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBladder cancer has a poor clinical outcome because of its high aggressiveness. Basement membrane plays vital functions in tumor invasion and migration. Invasion and distant metastasis of cancer are facilitated by degradation of the basement membrane and extracellular matrix.MethodsTen machine learning methods were utilized to develop the basement membrane-related signature (MRS) using datasets from TCGA, GSE13507, GSE31684, GSE32984 and GSE48276. Three anti-PD1 or anti-CTLA4 datasets and several predicting scores were used to investigate the performance of MRS in predicting the immunotherapy benefits.ResultsA predicting model based on the Enet algorithm (alpha = 0.1) was chosen as the optimal MRS since it had the highest average C-index being 0.72. According to TCGA data, the MRS showed good performance in predicting bladder cancer patients' clinical outcomes, with area under curves of 0.744, 0.766 and 0.817 for 1, 3, and 5-year receiver operating characteristic curve, respectively. PD1 and CTLA4 immunophenoscopes were associated with a low MRS score, as well as a lower tumor immune dysfunction and exclusion score. As MRS score increased, immune-activated cells levels decreased, tumor immune dysfunction and exclusion score decreased, immune escape score decreased, intratumor heterogeneity score decreased, PD1&CTLA4 immunophenoscore increased, and tumor mutational burden score increased in bladder cancer, suggesting better immunotherapy benefits. Bladder cancer cases with high MRS score was correlated with higher cancer related hallmark scores, including NOTCH and glycolysis signaling.ConclusionA new MRS has been developed for bladder cancer, which could be used to predict prognosis and the success of immunotherapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-024-01381-7.Keywords: Bladder cancer, Basement membrane, Prognostic signature, Machine learning, Immunotherapy

Introduction

Most common malignancy of the urinary tract, bladder cancer results in over 570,000 new diagnosed cases and 210,000 deaths globally annually [1]. The molecular subtypes and biological functions of bladder cancer can be separated into non-muscle invasive and muscle invasive types [2]. Some risk factors have been identified for the oncogenesis of bladder cancer, including cigarette smoking [3]. Due to its high aggression and invasion, bladder cancer patients ‘s prognosis are discouraging [4]. At present, multidisciplinary management approaches (surgery, chemoradiotherapy, targeted therapy and immunotherapy) have been applied for the treatment of bladder cancer patients, however, many of them still experience tumor progress and relapse, resulting in treatment failure [5, 6]. The limited clinical use of prognostic and therapeutic sensitivity markers in patients with bladder cancer is also the case.

The basement membrane, an extracellular matrix, is formed by laminin and type IV collagen [7]. Increasing evidences highlight the vital role of variants of basement membrane related genes (MRGs) in health problems [8]. Typically, MRGs are expressed in fibroblasts and myoepithelial cells, which are responsible for maintaining and producing the basement membrane [9]. Cell migration, tumor growth, and metastasis are all affected by basement membrane, which acts as a structural barrier to invasion and metastasis of tumors [10]. Degradation of the basement membrane and extracellular matrix is a prerequisite for cancer invasion and distant metastasis [11]. Invasion and distant metastasis of cancer are facilitated by degradation of the basement membrane and extracellular matrix [12]. Moreover, basement membrane-related signature (MRS) was suggested as prognostic marker in certain types of cancer, including lung adenocarcinomas [13], hepatocellular carcinoma [14], gastric cancer [15], and breast cancer [16]. Thus, deep elucidation of MRGs is vital for bladder cancer patient’s prognosis and treatment.

Therefore, our study developed a MRS using the data from the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO). The role of MRS in predicting the clinical outcomes and therapy benefits in bladder cancer were also detected. Our results may provide insights into the prognosis and immune landscape in bladder cancer.

Materials and methods

Acquiring data and identifying genes with differential expression

Supplementary Table 1 showed the MRGs (n = 224) being collected from previous studies [17]. Bulk mRNA data of bladder cancer patients was collected from TCGA dataset (n = 396), GSE13507 (n = 165), GSE31684 (n = 90), GSE32984 (n = 223) and GSE48276 (n = 73) dataset following strict inclusion criteria (pathological diagnosis of bladder cancer and completed prognostic information) and exclusion criteria (followed up for less than one month or died within one month after surgery and with other malignancies). Three immunotherapy datasets, including GSE78220 (cutaneous melanoma, n = 28), IMvigor210 (urothelium carcinoma, n = 298), and GSE91061 (cutaneous melanoma, n = 98), were used to investigate the functions of MRS in assessing the therapy benefits. The identification of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was determined by “limma” packages (|Log2fold change|> 1.5 and p-value < 0.05).

The optimal MRS was constructed by integrating machine learning algorithms

We then constructed a stable prognostic MRS with integrative analysis procedure using 10 machine learning methods. Following the process of previous study (R scripts in https://github.com/Zaoqu-Liu/IRLS) [18, 19], we constructed the MRS in four steps: (1) Univariate cox analysis was conducted to screen potential prognostic biomarkers among DEGs with p < 0.05 as the threshold value. (2) In TCGA dataset, prediction model was fitted with 101 algorithms combinations using potential biomarkers; (3) In GEO cohorts, these algorithms combinations were verified; (4) C-index was calculated across all cohorts. The MRS score (risk score) was calculated with the expression of MRS genes and their coefficient. The high and low MRS score group was separated by the best cutoff, determining by "survminer" R package.

The role of MRS in predicting the clinical outcome of bladder cancer patients

Survival curves was drawn with “survival” R package. The “survivalROC” R package was used for the generation of receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve and C-index curve. A total of 52 gene signatures (Supplementary Table 2) were collected and their C-indexes were calculated, with which we could compare the performance of MRS and other signatures in predicting the clinical outcomes of bladder cancer patients. The risk factors in bladder cancer were determined by univariate and multivariate Cox analyses. The predictive nomogram was constructed for predicting the clinical outcomes of bladder cancer patients using “nomogramEx” package. The difference between actual and predicted survival was visualized using the calibration curve.

Immune infiltration analysis

ESTIMATE algorithm was used to determine the immune score and ESTIMATE score of bladder cancer cases [20]. Seven methods were used to investigate the association between MRS and immune cells [21]. The gene set scores correlated with immune cells and immune-related activities or functions in bladder cancer were determined by “GSVA” package.

Drug sensitivity analysis

From TIDE website (https://tide.dfci.harvard.edu/), we downloaded the tumor immune dysfunction and exclusion (TIDE) score of bladder cancer patients [22]. Immunophenoscore was collected from the Cancer Immunome Atlas website (https://tcia.at/home) [23]. TISCH2 method was used to explore the intra-tumoral heterogeneity (ITH) score of bladder cancer patients [24]. These scores were used to evaluate the performance of MRS in predicting the immunotherapy response of bladder cancer cases. Based on the data of Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (https://www.cancerrxgene.org/), the half maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of drugs in each bladder cancer case was determined with the “oncoPredict R” package.

The human protein atlas

The Human Protein Atlas (https://www.proteinatlas.org/) is open and accessible database aim to map all the human proteins in cells, tissues, and organs using an integration of various omics technologies [25]. The representative image of immunohistochemistry of MRS genes in bladder cancer and normal bladder tissues were obtained from the “Pathology” section and “Tissue” section.

Results

Machine learning developed a prognostic MRS

As shown in Fig. 1A, a total of 7270 DEGs in bladder cancer were obtained (p < 0.05). Among these DEGs, 117 genes were MRGs (Fig. 1B). Univariate cox analysis suggested a poor prognosis in bladder cancer patients with high expression of ROBO1, GPC6, ITGA6, ADAMTS9, ANG, ITGAX, ITGB1, COL2A1, TGFB2, EGFLAM, COL4A3, SERPINE2, NFIA and low PTPRF expression (Fig. 1C). Machine learning-based integrative procedure for MRS construction was applied to these 14 genes. As shown in Fig. 1D, A predicting model based on the Enet algorithm (alpha = 0.1) was chosen as the optimal MRS since it had the highest average C-index being 0.72. This algorithm is based on 10 genes and the MRS score for each bladder cancer patient = 0.1188 × ROBO1exp + (−0.1596) × PTPRFexp + 0.1519 × ITGA6exp + 0.1558 × ANGexp + 0.0667 × ITGAXexp + 0.0878 × ITGB1exp + 0.0241 × TGFB2exp + 0.1379 × EGFLAMexp + 0.1555 × COL4A3exp + 0.0904 × NFIAexp. After separating bladder cancer cases into high and low MRS groups using the best cut-off, prognostic analysis indicated a favorable overall survival rate in TCGA, GSE13507, GSE31684, GSE32894 and GSE48276 datasets, with the AUCs of 1-, 3- and 5-year ROC curve being 0.744, 0.766 and 0.817 in TCGA cohort; 0.703, 0.771 and 0.795 in GSE13507 cohort; 0.646, 0.685 and 0.729 in GSE31684 cohort; 0.710, 0.752 and 0.757 in GSE32894 cohort, NA, 0.655 and 0.720 in GSE48276 cohort, respectively (Fg. 1E–I).

Evaluation of the performance of MRS

After calculating the C-index of MRS and clinical characters, we were able to compare their performance in evaluating the clinical outcome of bladder cancer cases. As shown in Fig. 2A, MRS performed better than clinical characters in all TCGA and GEO datasets. According to additional univariate and multivariate cox regression analyses of TCGA and GEO datasets, MRS is an independent risk factor for the clinical outcomes of bladder cancer cases (Fig. 2B–C, all p < 0.05). As many signatures have been constructed for bladder cancer, we then randomly collected 52 published prognostic signatures and calculated their C-indexes (Supplementary Table 2). Compared with other prognostic signatures, the C-index of MRS was higher in TCGA cohort (Fig. 2D). We also constructed a nomogram for predicting the clinical outcomes of bladder cancer patients (Fig. 2E). Further analysis showed that the nomogram was in good agreement with the observed 1-, 3- and 5-year overall survival rates in the TCGA cohort (Fig. 2F). Thus, MRS have a powerful and stable performance in predicting the clinical outcomes of bladder cancer cases.

Dissection of the correlation between MRS and tumor immune microenvironment

The overall correlations between MRS score and the abundance of immune cells was shown in Fig. 3A. MRS score showed negative correlations with the abundance of CD8+ T cells, NK cell and macrophages M1 (Fig. 3B–D). As shown in Fig. 3E, high MRS score indicated a lower score of B cells, CD8+ T cells, NK cells, and neutrophils (all p < 0.05). High MRS score also suggested lower gene set scores involved in APC and T cell_co_stimulation, cytolytic activity and IFN response (Fig. 3F, all p < 0.05). As shown in Fg. 3G–I, bladder cancer with lower MRS score had a higher stromal score, immune score and ESTIMAE score (all p < 0.001).

MRS as an indicator for predicting therapy benefits in bladder cancer

High TMB score and Immunophenoscore indicated higher immunotherapy benefits [26]. As shown in Fig. 4A–B, bladder cancer cases with low TMB score had a higher PD1&CTLA4 immunophenoscore and TMB score (all p < 0.05). TIDE score and ITH score were biomarkers for predicting the response to immunotherapy [27–29]. Low MRS score indicated a lower score of TIDE, immune escape and T cell exclusion and dysfunction in bladder cancer patients (Fig. 4C–E, all p < 0.05). High expression of HLA-related genes and immune checkpoints suggested a higher likelihood of immunotherapy benefits [30]. As shown in Fig. 4F–G, low MRS score suggested higher expressions of immune checkpoints and HLA-related genes (all p < 0.05). Thus, bladder cancer with low MRS score may have a better immunotherapy benefit. Three immunotherapy cohorts were used to verify our results. In IMvigor210 cohort, in which bladder cancer patients receiving anti-PD1 therapy, a lower MRS score was obtained in responders (Fig. 4H, p < 0.05). High MRS score indicated a poor clinical outcome (Fig. 4H, p = 0.0061). Moreover, high MRS score correlated with a lower immunotherapy response rate (p < 0.01). As shown in Fg. 4I–J, in GSE91061 and GSE78220 cohort, we obtained similar results. The IC50 value of some drugs for the therapy of bladder cancer was also explored. The result suggested a higher IC50 value of some drugs in chemotherapy and target therapy, including Docetaxel, Epirubicin, and Crizotinib (Fig. 5A, B, all p < 0.05).

Differential functional enrichment between different MRS scores

We then explored functions in different MRS score groups to clarify how MRS genes contributing to the progression of bladder cancer. As shown in Fig. 6A, high MRS score was significantly linked to JAK-STAT signaling, ECM_receptor interaction and pathways in cancer. Chemokine signaling, Ribosome and ASTHMA correlated with the genes in low MRS score group (Fig. 6B). As shown in Fig. 6C, bladder cancer cases with low MRS score had a lower gene sets score involved in E2F target, coagulation, angiogenesis, glycolysis, IL2-STAT5 signaling, hypoxia, NOTCH signaling, and P53 pathway.

Discussion

In our study, a total of 10 integrative machine learning methods were used to construct a powerful MRS for bladder cancer. A predicting model based on the Enet algorithm (alpha = 0.1) was chosen as the optimal MRS since it had the highest average C-index being 0.72. The MRS showed a good performance in predicting the clinical outcome and immunotherapy benefits of bladder cancer patients.

The MRS was constructed by using 10 genes, including ROBO1, PTPRF, ITGA6, ANG, ITGAX, ITGB1, TGFB2, EGFLAM, COL4A3, NFIA. These genes have been suggested to play a vital role in bladder cancer and other types of cancer. PTPRF inhibited the biological functions of gastric adenocarcinoma via the deactivation of ERK1/2 signaling [31]. N6-methyladenosine modification of ITGA6 accelerated the migration and invasion of bladder cancer [32]. ANG was suggested as a biomarker for bladder cancer [33]. SERPINB8 affected the proliferation and invasion of melanoma by regulating ITGAX expression [34]. ITGB1 enhanced the radio-resistance of via DNA damage response and EMT in lung cancer [35]. COL4A3 expression was associated with pathologic behaviors and prognosis of gastric cancer [36]. NFIA functioned as tumor suppressors in high-grade glioma in mice [37].

The data suggested MRS as an independent risk factor, which could predict the clinical outcome of bladder cancer patients. MRS could also predict the clinical outcomes of lung adenocarcinoma cases [13]. In breast cancer, MRS acted as a prognosis marker and correlated with the tumor immune microenvironment [16]. MRS predicted prognoses and responses to diverse therapies in hepatocellular carcinoma [38]. Moreover, MRS could also predict the clinical outcomes in patients with renal cell carcinoma [39].

Our finding also found a lower TIDE score, lower immune escape score, higher PD1&CTLA4 immunophenoscore, higher TMB score, and higher ITH score in high MRS score in bladder cancer. TMB score was suggested as an immunotherapy indicator and low TMB score indicated a lower response to immunotherapy [40, 41]. Higher TIDE score suggested a higher likelihood of immune escape [27]. Higher ITH score suggested a lower immunotherapy response rate in bladder cancer [42]. These evidences show that MRS could serve as an immunotherapy indicator and low MRS score suggested a favorable immunotherapy benefit.

The results also suggested a higher cancer related hallmark scores (angiogenesis, glycolysis, hypoxia, NOTCH signaling et al.) in bladder cancer with high MRS score. Glycolysis exerted vital functions in the carcinogenesis of bladder cancer [43]. Angiogenesis correlated with tumor growth and metastasis and prognosis in bladder cancer [44]. NOTCH signaling also favored bladder cancer progression [45]. Hypoxia was involved in immune evasion in cancer [46].

Our study also has some limitations. First, our research is not validated by an internal clinical cohort; Second, all of our studies are performed on mRNA levels but they do not represent protein levels; Third, the underlying mechanism mediated by basement membrane-related genes remains to be elucidated. Moreover, this study is performed using muscle-invasive bladder cancer cases and the results may not suitable for non- muscle-invasive bladder cancer patients.

Conclusion

A new MRS has been developed for bladder cancer, which could be used to predict prognosis and the success of immunotherapy.
