Stopping versus continuing renin-angiotensin system inhibitors before surgery: An updated systematic review and meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials
Authors: Marwah Algodi, Omar Saab, Alhareth Al-Sagban, Hashim T. Hashim, Ahmed D. Al-Obaidi, Mohanad Albayyaa, Bashar Al-Hemyari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background and Aims:The optimal perioperative management of patients chronically using renin-angiotensin system inhibitors (RASIs) is still uncertain. This study aims to compare the outcomes of withholding versus continuing RASIs before surgery, focusing on efficacy and safety.Methods:A systematic review and meta-analysis synthesising evidence from randomised controlled trials (RCTs) obtained from PubMed, CENTRAL, Scopus, and Web of Science until September 2024. Using Stata MP v. 17, we used the fixed-effects model to report dichotomous outcomes by using the risk ratio (RR) with a 95% confidence interval (CI).Results:Ten RCTs with 3,740 patients were included. There was no statistical difference between both groups regarding the incidence of major adverse cardiac events (MACE) [Risk ratio (RR): 0.99; 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.84, 1.16; P = 0.88], all-cause mortality (RR: 0.88; 95% CI: 0.44, 1.78; P = 0.72), myocardial infarction (MI) (RR: 1.67; 95% CI: 0.61, 4.58; P = 0.32), heart failure/acute pulmonary oedema (RR: 1.87; 95% CI: 0.51, 6.84; P = 0.34), stroke (RR: 1.22; 95% CI: 0.35, 4.24; P = 0.75), postoperative hypotension (RR: 0.85; 95% CI: 0.66, 1.10; P = 0.22), perioperative hypertension (RR: 1.21; 95% CI: 1.00, 1.46; P = 0.05), and acute kidney injury (AKI) (RR: 1.01; 95% CI: 0.80, 1.26; P = 0.97). However, withholding RASIs was significantly associated with a decreased incidence of intraoperative hypotension (RR: 0.82; 95% CI: 0.75, 0.89; P < 0.001).Conclusion:Stopping RASIs in patients undergoing surgery was not associated with a higher risk of postoperative complications, such as MACE, all-cause mortality, myocardial infarction, heart failure/acute pulmonary oedema, stroke, or AKI. Conversely, discontinuing RASIs notably reduced the incidence of intraoperative hypotension.

INTRODUCTION

Physicians frequently encounter challenges in deciding how to manage a patient’s long-term medications.[1] During the perioperative period, which is the most critical stage, patients undergoing major abdominal, vascular, and orthopaedic surgeries have a 25% chance of experiencing cardiac or vascular complications.[2] A considerable number of patients who undergo surgical procedures have a medical background of comorbidities, such as hypertension, diabetes, heart failure, or a combination thereof.[3] In many cases, these conditions involve the regular use of renin-angiotensin system inhibitors (RASIs), either angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEIs) or angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs).[4] Consistent findings from prospective cohort studies reveal that around 30%–40% of elective surgical patients are on chronic RASIs treatment.[56]

The continuation of RASIs perioperatively has been proven to cause intraoperative hypotension, which can sometimes be challenging to control.[17] Accordingly, leading cardiovascular societies have released guidelines advising RASI withdrawal before surgical procedures.[1] Previously, the 2014 guidelines from the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) and European Society of Anaesthesiology recommended considering continuing RASIs during non-cardiac surgery for stable patients with heart failure and left ventricular systolic dysfunction; however, it is recommended to consider the temporary discontinuation of RASIs in hypertensive patients before non-cardiac surgery (class IIa, level of evidence C).[8] In addition, the 2021 guidelines from the American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology (AHA/ACC) reported that temporarily stopping RASIs before surgery might lower the risk of myocardial injury. However, the evidence supporting this is limited to observational studies.[8] Finally, the recent guidelines from the ESC in 2022 stated that there is a lack of conclusive data on the perioperative use of RASIs.[9] The most updated guidelines from the AHA/ACC support the continuation of RASI in patients with heart failure reduced ejection fraction (class IIa, level of evidence C); however, it is reasonable to withhold RASI used for hypertension 24 hours before elevated risk NCS to limit intraoperative hypotension (class IIb, level of evidence B).

Nevertheless, the current guidelines depend on limited evidence from observational studies and small randomised controlled trials (RCTs).[17] Several large RCTs have recently been published with data not supporting the discontinuation of RASIs before surgery.[41011] Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis aims to evaluate the effect of stopping versus continuing RASIs before surgery on efficacy and safety outcomes based on evidence from RCTs, informing future guidelines with the most comprehensive, contemporary evidence.

METHODS

Protocol registration

Protocol was registered on the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) (ID: CRD42024598576). This systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement.[12] and the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses.[13]

Data sources and search strategy

A systematic electronic search was conducted by M.Alg. and O.S. on 12th September 2024 using the following databases: PubMed (MEDLINE), Web of Science (WOS), Scopus, and CENTRAL. The search strategy included the following search string “(“Angiotensin-Converting Enzyme Inhibitor*” OR “Angiotensin-Converting Enzyme Antagonist*” OR “ACE Inhibitor*” OR ACE-I* OR “Angiotensin II Receptor Blocker*” OR “Angiotensin Receptor Blocker*” OR “Angiotensin II Receptor Antagonist*” OR “Angiotensin Receptor Antagonist*” OR “Angiotensin II Type 1 Receptor Blocker*” OR ARBs) AND (operative OR surgical OR surger* OR “surgical procedure*”) AND (discontinue* OR omit* OR cease* OR stop* OR withhold* OR stop* OR withdraw*).” We did not apply any filters or constraints during the search, except for Scopus, where we restricted it to titles, abstracts, and keywords. Further details on each database’s search terms and results are shown in Supplementary Table 1. Finally, a thorough manual search of the trial list references was performed to ensure no eligible records were overlooked.

Search strategy

Eligibility criteria

We included RCTs that followed the following PICO criteria: population (P), adult patients who are taking RASIs, either ACEIs or ARBs regardless of the dosage and any drug combinations, and undergoing surgery, irrespective of the type or extent of surgery; intervention (I), stopping RASIs preoperatively either on the day of surgery or the day before surgery; control (C), the continuation of RASIs during the perioperative period; and outcomes (O): the efficacy outcomes included major adverse cardiac events (MACE), all-cause mortality, myocardial infarction (MI), heart failure/acute pulmonary oedema, stroke, length of hospital stay, and length of intensive care unit (ICU) stay. In contrast, the safety outcomes included intraoperative hypotension, postoperative hypotension, perioperative hypertension, and the incidence of acute kidney injury (AKI). Furthermore, we excluded RCTs investigating stopping RASIs during percutaneous cardiac interventions, observational studies, conference abstracts, posters, reviews, and animal studies.

Study selection

Two independent reviewers (M.Alg and O.S.) conducted the screening process by using the Covidence online tool (Veritas Health Innovation Ltd., Melbourne, Victoria, Australia). Once duplicates were removed and after utilising the software’s automation tool to help exclude studies that were not RCTs, they independently reviewed each obtained record in two steps: screening the title and abstract, and screening the full text. Through discussion, any discrepancies were resolved to achieve a consensus.

Data extraction

A pilot extraction was performed after obtaining the full texts of the relevant publications to design an Excel extraction form. The form was divided into three sections: summary characteristics of the included trials (first author name, year of publication, country, study design, number of centres, sample size, treatment regimen, hypotension definition, primary outcome, and follow-up duration); baseline characteristics of the included participants (number of patients in each group, age, gender, class of drug taken, and comorbidities); and the outcome data as previously described.

Two reviewers (M.Alg and A.Als.) independently extracted the data, and any differences were settled by discussion and agreement with a senior author. Event and total formats were used for extracting dichotomous outcome variables, while mean and standard deviation were used for continuous outcome variables. We utilised the formulas given by Wan et al.[14] to convert the data from median and interquartile range to mean and standard deviation.

Outcomes: The efficacy outcomes included MACE, all-cause mortality, MI, heart failure/acute pulmonary oedema, stroke, length of hospital stay, and length of ICU stay. In contrast, the safety outcomes included intraoperative hypotension, postoperative hypotension, perioperative hypertension, and the incidence of AKI.

Risk of bias and certainty of evidence

We used the revised Cochrane Collaboration tool for RCTs (ROB 2) to assess the risk of bias in included studies.[15] Two reviewers (M.Alg and A.Als) evaluated each study independently for selection, performance, reporting, attrition, and overall biases, with disagreements resolved through consensus. In addition, the certainty of evidence was assessed using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) framework, which considered factors such as inconsistency, imprecision, indirectness, publication bias, and risk of bias.[1617] Each factor was individually evaluated, and the decisions were duly justified and documented. Any inconsistencies were resolved through discussion.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was performed using Stata MP v. 17 by Stata Corp. We utilised the risk ratio (RR) to combine dichotomous outcomes and the mean difference (MD) to combine continuous outcomes, along with a 95% confidence interval (CI). We utilised the fixed-effects model unless there was significant heterogeneity, in which case we employed the random-effects model. Statistical heterogeneity among the studies included was assessed using the Chi-square and I-squared tests (I2). A P value of the Chi-square test <0.1 was considered significant for possible heterogeneity, while I2 values ≥50% indicated significant heterogeneity.

A sensitivity analysis was conducted using the leave-one-out model to account for significant heterogeneity. By excluding each study individually, the potential impact on the overall effect estimate was observed, ensuring that no single study had a disproportionate influence. The Galbraith plot was also utilised to identify any variation among the studies. In addition, publication bias was not investigated as we included less than 10 RCTs.[18]

Finally, trial sequential analysis (TSA) was conducted to evaluate the robustness and conclusiveness of the meta-analytic results, as TSA considers the information’s size and the cumulative z-curve to determine the sufficiency and robustness of available evidence. Boundary controls were established to manage the risks associated with type I and type II errors. TSA was conducted using the Trial Sequential Analysis software.[19]

RESULTS

Search results and study selection

We identified 966 studies based on the search results and screened these studies based on their titles and abstracts. Twenty-five full-text articles were identified for further review after removing 615 irrelevant records and 326 studies that did not meet the inclusion criteria after title and abstract screening. Fifteen were found to be unrelated and excluded, leaving 10 records to be included in qualitative and quantitative assessments [Figure 1].

Risk of bias and certainty of evidence

Three RCTs showed a low risk of bias,[101126] four raised some concerns of bias,[4222324] and three showed a high risk of bias[202125] [Figure 2]. The high risk of bias was mainly due to deviation from intended interventions due to the open-label design and the lack of appropriate statistical measures. Four studies had some concerns about selection bias due to the lack of details on the randomisation process.[20212324] A GRADE evidence profile highlights the certainty of evidence assessment [Table 3].

Efficacy outcomes

There was no difference between both groups regarding the incidence of MACE (RR: 0.99; 95% CI: 0.84, 1.16; P = 0.88) (high certainty of evidence) [Figure 3a], all-cause mortality (RR: 0.88; 95% CI: 0.44, 1.78; P = 0.72) (low certainty of evidence) [Figure 3b], MI (RR: 1.67; 95% CI: 0.61, 4.58; P = 0.32) (very low certainty of evidence) [Figure 3c], heart failure/acute pulmonary oedema (RR: 1.87; 95% CI: 0.51, 6.84; P = 0.34) (low certainty of evidence) [Figure 3d], stroke (RR: 1.22; 95% CI: 0.35, 4.24; P = 0.75) (low certainty of evidence) [Figure 3e], length of hospital stay (MD: 0.03; 95% CI: −0.29, 0.35; P = 0.86) (high certainty of evidence) [Figure 4a], and length of ICU stay (MD: −0.25; 95% CI: −0.57, 0.07; P = 0.13) (high certainty of evidence) [Figure 4b].

DISCUSSION

After synthesising 10 RCTs with 3,740 patients,[4101120212223242526] stopping RASIs before surgery had no effect on MACE, all-cause mortality, MI, heart failure/acute pulmonary oedema, stroke, length of hospital stay, and length of ICU stay, compared with continuing RASIs. In addition, stopping both groups had similar rates of safety outcomes, including postoperative hypotension, perioperative hypertension, and AKI. However, stopping RASIs significantly decreased the risk of intraoperative hypotension with high-certainty evidence.

The controversy of either stopping or continuing RASIs before surgery continued for a while due to the lack of high-quality evidence and the fact that either strategy can impact the patient’s outcomes. Stopping RASIs can lead to several complications, such as postoperative hypertension, heart failure, and arrhythmia.[10] However, continuing RASIs also has the potential to cause intraoperative hypotension, a condition that has been associated with postoperative complications, notably MACE and AKI.[27] Our analysis supported the idea that stopping RASIs can decrease the risk of intraoperative hypotension without affecting MACE and AKI.

A significant association has been noted between hypotension and postoperative complications, such as MI, AKI, and mortality.[282930] Additionally, a notable association has been observed between the duration of hypotension and the development of postoperative stroke in patients who underwent non-cardiac and non-neurosurgical procedures.[31] Accordingly, the quick management of intraoperative hypotension and the relatively short duration of low blood pressure contribute to the lack of subsequent effect of low risk of intraoperative hypotension with stopping RASIs on MACE.[10] In addition, there has been a shift towards more frequent administration of intraoperative vasopressors due to improved knowledge of the risks associated with prolonged hypotension.[32]

Nevertheless, several theories may question the assumption that intraoperative hypotension is the cause of postoperative complications.[1] First, findings from six trials suggest that intraoperative hypotension is likely a marker of postoperative complications rather than the root cause.[33] Second, there is evidence to support the benefits of specific perioperative interventions that result in hypotension, as in high cardiac-risk patients, controlled hypotension has been established as a safe and effective method for limiting intraoperative blood loss.[34] In addition, epidural anaesthesia/analgesia frequently decreases blood pressure but has been proven to improve postoperative outcomes.[35] Finally, the perioperative investigations conducted on beta-blockers[36] and clonidine[37] have demonstrated that they increase the risk of perioperative hypotension; however, only beta-blockers, which exhibit the ability to reduce myocardial infarction, have been linked to increased mortality rates after non-cardiac surgery.[1]

Two prior systematic reviews have presented data on the effects of perioperative RASI therapy on mortality and morbidity.[3839] The primary findings indicated no statistically significant difference in MI or mortality,[38] but a notable 50% rise was observed in the occurrence of postinduction hypotension with continuing RASIs.[39] However, due to limited statistical power, these meta-analyses cannot adequately investigate potential correlations between perioperative RASIs and morbidity.[3839] Another recent meta-analysis by Hollmann et al.[7] had findings similar to ours, with stopping RASIs only decreasing the risk of intraoperative hypotension with no effect on postoperative adverse outcomes. Nevertheless, Hollmann et al.[7] included nine studies with only five RCTs, and observational studies investigating the relationship between hypotension severity and/or duration with major postoperative adverse outcomes possess several bias domains.[1]

First, it should be noted that the specific group of surgical patients observed with an arterial blood pressure catheter is inherently at an increased risk of haemodynamic instability (confounding by indication).[1] Furthermore, it is expected that there will be instances of intraoperative hypotension, and in such cases, the anaesthesiologist promptly responds to treat it. Therefore, episodes of severe and prolonged hypotension are commonly treated but pose a challenge to resolve (outcome bias).[1] Finally, observational studies have significantly varied definitions of hypotension,[1] and the relationship between hypotension and postoperative adverse events is influenced by the specific definition of hypotension (heterogeneity).[40]

Our systematic review and meta-analysis, which includes 10 RCTs, confirms and expands upon the findings of two other recent meta-analyses in this field.[4142] Both Saad et al.,[42] who included nine RCTs, and Shalabi et al.,[41] who included seven RCTs, found that continuing RASIs was associated with a significantly higher incidence of intraoperative hypotension but had no significant effect on all-cause mortality, MACE, or AKI. Our findings align with these conclusions. Nevertheless, our analysis delivers an updated synthesis through the incorporation of additional trials not encompassed in these reviews, thus presenting the most current and thorough evidentiary foundation to date. The agreement among these three independent meta-analyses strengthens the conclusions, implying that although intraoperative hypotension remains a legitimate concern, the method of perioperative RASI management does not seem to impact major postoperative morbidity or mortality.

Strengths and limitations

To the extent of our knowledge, this is the first and most comprehensive meta-analysis of RCTs, implementing PRISMA reporting guidelines and the GRADE framework, that investigates the effects of stopping versus continuing RASIs before surgery. Still, our review is limited by the following:

First, we included a wide array of surgical procedure types, cardiac and non-cardiac, and severity; some studies included patients with minor and others with major surgery, aiming to provide a pragmatic approach for perioperative RASIs management. Second, the types and dosages of RASIs were inconsistent among the included studies, and the effect of co-administered medications may have impacted our results. Despite that, we did not observe significant heterogeneity in our outcomes. Third, none of the included studies stopped RASIs according to the pharmacokinetic properties of each agent, except for the SPACE trial.[10] Fourth, the definitions of key efficacy and safety outcomes, as well as the duration of follow-up, were not standardised across the included trials. For instance, the follow-up period for mortality ranged from the duration of the hospital stay to a fixed 30-day period. Similarly, the components and assessment period for MACE varied. In addition, the definition of AKI differed, with some studies using KDIGO criteria and others not formally defining it. The definitions of hypotension were also not consistent among the included trials. This heterogeneity in outcome definitions could impact the pooled effect estimates. While our analysis did not show significant statistical heterogeneity for most outcomes, this clinical variability should be considered when interpreting the results. Fifth, some of the included studies showed concerns about risk of bias, mainly due to deviations from the intended interventions, which could have been raised from clinical concerns that could have been prospectively anticipated in the RCT protocols. Finally, the GRADE framework yielded low to very low certainty in some of the assessed outcomes; hence, the results should be interpreted cautiously.

Implications for future research

Some points remain underinvestigated. First, most studies do not consider the pharmacokinetic properties of each RASI agent. The inconsistent findings observed in studies may be due to the variability in the pharmacokinetic profiles of RASIs.[4] It is common practice for clinicians to stop ACEIs the day before surgery; however, studies on pharmacokinetics have shown that a pause of less than 24 hours does not impact the drugs’ biological activity.[43] Second, the effect of the time till resuming RASIs postoperatively on MACE remains unknown. Two extensive database studies conducted in non-cardiac and cardiac surgery have found that not resuming RASI treatment is strongly linked to higher rates of morbidity and mortality,[4445] indicating that having a standardised protocol for restarting therapy is crucial in accurately interpreting outcomes beyond the immediate postoperative period.[4] Third, there remains a need to standardise a definition of intraoperative hypotension. Finally, the effect of some confounding variables, such as co-administered drugs, surgery type, and surgery severity, should be considered while planning for future trials.

CONCLUSION

Stopping RASIs in patients undergoing surgery was not associated with an increased risk of postoperative complications, including MACE, all-cause mortality, MI, heart failure/acute pulmonary oedema, stroke, and AKI. Conversely, discontinuing RASIs significantly reduced the incidence of intraoperative hypotension. These findings highlight a trade-off between haemodynamic stability during surgery and the lack of harm from stopping therapy, suggesting that the decision for preoperative RASI management should be individualised based on patient and surgical risk factors.
