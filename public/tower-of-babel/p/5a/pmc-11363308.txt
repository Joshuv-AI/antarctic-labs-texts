MICU1 and MICU2 control mitochondrial calcium signaling in the mammalian heart
Authors: Prottoy Hasan, Elena Berezhnaya, Macarena Rodríguez-Prados, David Weaver, Carmen Bekeova, Benjamin Cartes-Saavedra, Erin Birch, Andreas M. Beyer, Janine H. Santos, Erin L. Seifert, John W. Elrod, György Hajnóczky
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SignificanceCalcium enters mitochondria via the calcium uniporter to regulate energy production and survival. The uniporter activity is controlled by calcium via binding the EF-hands of the regulatory MICU subunits. There is controversy in the presence and relevance of this mechanism in the heart. We developed both chronic and acute cardiomyocyte-specific genetic models for MICU1/MICU2 and provide biochemical, genetic, and pharmacological evidence that MICU1/2 are present and important in the mammalian heart. MICU1/2 loss increases mitochondrial calcium influx and sensitizes mitochondria to calcium overload injury. This happens despite a compensatory downregulation of other uniporter components that allow survival. Thus, our findings highlight both the primary consequences of MICU1/2 loss and the ensuing robust compensation in the beating heart.

Mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake has long been recognized in the positive regulation of oxidative metabolism in a spectrum of cells and tissues (1, 2). In the heart, it has been shown to support homeostatic cardiac metabolism and function under basal conditions (3, 4) and be particularly important under stress conditions helping the heart to meet the increased energetic demand during exercise and sympathetic fight-or-flight responses (5–8). In parallel, excessive mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake was found to cause mitochondrial membrane permeabilization and cell death (9–11). In the ischemic heart, it has been reported to contribute to mitochondrial Ca2+ overload and cell death, while during heart failure both excessive and decreased mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake were found to contribute to the pathology (12–14). Evidence not only supports the importance for health of mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake per se, but also for keeping mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake under control. (15–17).

Mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake is mediated by the mitochondrial Ca2+ uniporter (mtCU). The mtCU is a Ca2+ channel whose pore is formed by four MCU subunits scaffolded by EMRE (18–21). The MCU-EMRE pore alone could mediate mitochondrial Ca2+ influx even at the lowest levels of cytoplasmic free calcium concentration ([Ca2+]c) because of the robust driving force provided by the inner mitochondrial membrane potential (22, 23). However, both MCU and EMRE bind to a homo- or heterodimer consisting of the MICU1 and MICU2 or MICU3 proteins that inhibits mitochondrial Ca2+ entry at resting [Ca2+]c and mediates cooperative activation of the Ca2+ uptake as the [Ca2+]c increases (24–26). The suppression of mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake under resting [Ca2+]c by MICUs protects cells from mitochondrial Ca2+ overload and ensuing cell death (16, 27). Cooperative activation of mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake by MICUs is required for effective mitochondrial Ca2+ influx responses during [Ca2+]c signals that mostly occur as short-lasting brisk increases (24). The switch in the MICU1/2/3 function is mediated by Ca2+ binding to the EF-hand domains on each MICU (24, 26). The proximal component in the MICU1/2/3 dimers is MICU1, the only isoform that can bind MCU/EMRE, and which binds to MICU2, MICU3, or MICU1 itself (28–30). Because of the different Ca2+ binding affinities and the tissue-specific expression of the MICU isoforms (26, 31, 32), the MICU1/2/3 dimers can provide great flexibility in both Ca2+-dependent gatekeeping and cooperative activation of the mtCU Ca2+ uptake.

Positive regulation by frequency-encoded [Ca2+]c oscillations has been exemplified by the liver, where the effective response of the mtCU allows oxidative metabolism to be tuned to the changing needs of the hepatocytes (33, 34). In the liver and in many other cell types the frequency of [Ca2+]c oscillations is below 0.2 Hz and the individual [Ca2+]c spikes are separated by baseline periods of seconds or minutes. However, in the heart, beating cardiomyocytes (CM) display a frequency range of 0.5 to 10 Hz in most mammals, and the [Ca2+]c spikes very closely follow each other. Many murine models used in research are on the high end of this frequency range, beating at 4 to 10 Hz. A fundamental question is if and how the MICU1/2/3 control of Ca2+ uptake can be effective in this frequency range. Previous studies have asked the question whether mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake is beat-to-beat regulated or shows summation of the individual transients, dependent on the beating frequency, and gave different responses (35–38). We have shown that the MICU1:MCU stoichiometry is low in mouse heart compared to liver, resulting in weaker Ca2+-dependent control of the mitochondrial Ca2+ flux (39). Most recently, Lederer et al. suggested that the cardiac mtCU lacks Ca2+-dependent control and Tsai et al. reported the effective absence of MICU1-dependent mtCU gating in the heart (3, 40, 41). This suggests that the heart mtCU lacks MICU1-dependent regulation. However, these studies lacked any genetic models and the cardiac mitochondrial Ca2+ transport studies did not test the range of [Ca2+]c where the gatekeeping by MICU1/2/3 is most effective. Thus, an open question is if cardiac mitochondria show a persistent Ca2+ uptake without suffering Ca2+ overload injury. Part of the answer might be the low mtCU flux density (42) and high abundance of exchangers in cardiac mitochondria relative to many other tissues. However, the subject of Ca2+ flux control by MICU1/2/3 in CM remains a major void.

We here, introduce both lifelong and short-term mouse genetic models for MICU1 and MICU2 in the heart and expand the Ca2+ transport studies with assays that allow mtCU Ca2+ transport to be tested at the lowest range of [Ca2+]c and during physiological conditions in beating CM. Furthermore, we also deepen the investigation of the human nonfailing heart.

Results

MICU1 and MICU2 Abundance and Complex Formation by MCU in the Human Heart.

To test for the presence of the mtCU components in human heart mitochondria, we first extracted the MICU1, MICU2, and MCU protein abundance from a published quantitative mass spectrometry dataset of 32 normal adult human tissue types (43). MICU1, MICU2, and MCU are all detectable in each sample. We normalized the abundance of the mtCU components to the mitochondrial protein content (Methods) and plotted both MICU1 (Fig. 1A) and MICU2 (Fig. 1B) as a function of MCU. Strikingly, both MICU1 vs. MCU and MICU2 vs. MCU are well described by a linear regression in most tissues (Pearson R: 0.936 and 0.918, respectively). This holds for skeletal muscle, in which MCU, MICU1, and MICU2 are all abundant (purple symbols). The brain cortex and cerebellum are also rich in MCU and MICU1 but their MICU2 is >80% less than the linear regression would dictate (blue symbols). Liver mitochondria are scarce in MCU and contain both MICU1 and MICU2 in proportion to MCU (green symbols). Heart mitochondria are also on the lower end for MCU, MICU1, and MICU2 abundance among all tissues. MICU1/MCU and MICU2/MCU are below the predicted value by <40%.

Next, we isolated mitochondria from 12 frozen nonfailing human hearts (left ventricle anterior wall) for biochemical analysis of the mtCU. Based on immunoblotting, all major mtCU components, including MICU1 and MICU2 are present in each sample (Fig. 1C). The relative abundance of each component was also quantified by mass spectroscopy (SI Appendix, Table S1). This confirms with high confidence the presence of MICU1 and MICU2, and shows a correlation between MICU1 and MCU in the donor hearts (SI Appendix, Table S1; Pearson R: 0.646, P = 0.023).

Measuring the mtCU Ca2+ flux is difficult with frozen heart-derived mitochondria. Therefore, to test whether MICU1 is part of the mtCU in the heart, we used blue native-protein electrophoresis (BN-PAGE) in the first dimension (1D), then in the second dimension (2D) under denaturing electrophoresis [sodium dodecyl sulphate (SDS)-PAGE], and immunodetection to test whether MICU1 and MCU were in the same complex in human heart mitochondria (Fig. 1 D and E). The 1D shows both MICU1 (1D left) and MCU (1D right) immunoreactivity just under 1 MDa, indicating that they are part of a complex consistent in size with the previously described mtCU complex (44). The 2D SDS-PAGE reveals MICU1 and MCU in the same spot (Fig. 1E), supporting that MICU1 is associated with the pore of the mtCU in the human heart.

Pharmacological Evidence for the Gatekeeping by MICU1 in Mouse CM.

To directly evaluate the role of MICU1/2 in cardiac mitochondrial Ca2+ transport, we turned to mouse models. We first set up a fluorometric Ca2+ clearance assay, where we measured Ca2+ uptake by mitochondria in permeabilized CM as the decrease in the [Ca2+] in the cytosolic buffer ([Ca2+]c) (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). Upon addition of CGP37157 (20 µM), an inhibitor of mitochondrial Ca2+ efflux, the [Ca2+]c decreases to ~0.5 µM. Elevation of [Ca2+]c by addition of boluses of CaCl2 (3 and 5 µM) is followed by clearance of Ca2+ and return of [Ca2+]c to <1 µM (black trace). The [Ca2+]c clearance is eliminated by addition of Ru265 (10 µM), an inhibitor of the mtCU (gray trace). We next used MCU-i11, a less potent but MICU1-dependent mtCU inhibitor (45, 46). We modeled the calcium clearance kinetics following addition of CGP37157 and 3 µM CaCl2 by exponential decay curves and used the parameters of the fits to estimate instantaneous rates of clearance and steady-state [Ca2+]c levels. In the presence of MCU-i11 (10 µM), the [Ca2+]c clearance is slower and the predicted steady-state levels are higher in CM isolated from both male and female mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S1B). The inhibition of Ca2+ clearance by MCU-i11 is not related to an effect on the driving force, because mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) is unaffected by MCU-i11 (SI Appendix, Fig. S1C). Thus, the effect of MCU-i11 indicates MICU1 function in the Ca2+ uptake.

Genetic Evidence for Gatekeeping by MICU1/2 in Mouse CM.

The above results provide biochemical and pharmacological support to the relevance of MICU1/2 in the cardiac mtCU but genetic approaches are required to establish the point. First, we set out to examine how chronic loss of MICU1/2 affects the mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake in the heart. Because the whole-body Micu1KO/KO mice are not viable after birth (16, 17), we used CM isolated from a mouse with striated muscle–specific MICU1 loss mouse (47) (M1smKO, red) and a whole-body Micu2KO/KO mouse (M2KO, green). Strikingly, we find that mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake is almost abolished in the M1smKO (95% inhibition) and is decreased by ≅30% in the M2KO with the fluorometric Ca2+ uptake assay (Fig. 2A). Ca2+ uptake inhibition is observed in the absence of a change in ΔΨm (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 A and B).

A limitation of the fluorometric assay was that the steady-state [Ca2+]c was above the physiological range of [Ca2+]c in CM, because mitochondrial Ca2+ clearance didn’t lower it to <500 nM. Therefore, we switched to a 45Ca2+ uptake assay in which [Ca2+]c was set by a Ca2+ chelator, ethylene glycol-bis(β-aminoethyl ether)-N,N,N′,N′-tetraacetic acid (EGTA), to concentrations as low as <100 nM (Fig. 2B and SI Appendix, Fig. S1 D and E). When [Ca2+]c is 20 µM (Fig. 2 B, Upper), the initial, Ru265-sensitive 45Ca2+ uptake is decreased by 95% in the M1smKO (red bar) and by 30% in the MICU2KO (green) permeabilized CM, consistent with what we measured with the fluorometric Ca2+ clearance assay. This suggests that the maximal Ca2+ uptake capacity is greatly decreased in the M1smKO.

To test the [Ca2+]c dependence of the uptake, we measured 45Ca2+ uptake at various [Ca2+] down to 80 nM in the absence and presence of Ru265 (Fig. 2 B, Lower). The Ru265-sensitive 45Ca2+ uptake is shown in a double logarithmic plot where the slope of the linear fit reflects the cooperativity of the mtCU, reflecting the presence of Ca2+ dependence of mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake. Consistent with our previous studies in isolated cardiac mitochondria (39), in permeabilized CM, the slope is 1.51 and 1.47 in the controls of M1smKO (Micu1fl/fl) and M2KO (Micu2fl/fl) respectively (Control [CTRL], black). However, the M1smKO shows a slope of 1.03, whereas the M2KO is 1.28 indicating the cooperativity observed in both CTRLs is absent in the M1smKO and decreased in the M2KO. These changes in the cooperativity demonstrate a Ca2+-dependent gatekeeping function of MICU1/2 in CM.

mtCU Abundance and Composition upon Stable MICU1 and MICU2 Loss in the Heart.

The change in gatekeeping can be explained by MICU1/2-deletion but the decrease in Ca2+ uptake capacity cannot. To test potential underlying alterations in mtCU abundance and composition, we first assessed the transcript level of each component in CM (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 C–F). The decrease of mRNA encoding MICU1 in M1smKO and MICU2 in M2KO is confirmed and no other changes are significant.

We then assessed the protein abundance of each mtCU constituent in CM by immunoblotting (Fig. 2C). In the M1smKO, not only is MICU1 less but EMRE and MCU are also greatly decreased (red bars). In the M2KO, the decrease of the pore-forming components seems to be less than in the M1KO (green bars). In summary, stable loss of MICU1/2 in CM results in profound mtCU-wide changes, which likely reflect adaptation to the loss of gatekeeping that would make mitochondria prone to Ca2+ overload (16, 27). The changes are likely due to enhanced protein breakdown because the mRNA levels were not significantly altered.

Outcomes of Acute Knock-Out of MICU1 and MICU2 in CM.

To minimize the compensation triggered by chronic loss of MICU1/2 in the heart, we crossed Micu1fl/fl and Micu2fl/fl mice with CM-specific α-MHC-MerCreMer mice. The MerCreMer was induced by Tamoxifen intraperitoneal (IP) injections and the M1iKO (Micu1fl/fl x α-MHC-MerCreMer) and the M2iKO (Micu2fl/fl x α-MHC-MerCreMer) CM were harvested and phenotyped 16 d after completion of the Tamoxifen treatment (Fig. 3A). By immunoblotting we confirm a decrease of MICU1 in both male and female M1iKO when compared with their respective CTRLs, Micu1fl/fl(CTRL) and MHC-Cre (CTRLc) (Fig. 3B and SI Appendix, Fig. S3 A, B, D, and E). A decrease of EMRE in both sexes and a decrease in MICU2 in males is also detected. In the M2iKO, we find a significant decrease only in MICU2 (Fig. 3B and SI Appendix, Fig. S3 C and F). Thus, changes beyond the targeted gene are found in the MICU1iKO but less than in the MICU1smKO CM, and are absent in the MICU2iKO.

45Ca2+ uptake at 20 µM [Ca2+]c, a measure of mtCU maximum capacity, is reduced by 40 to 50% in male M1iKO, 30 to 40% in female M1iKO and 20 to 30% in male M2iKO compared to their respective CTRLs (Fig. 3B). By contrast, at 100 nM [Ca2+]c, M1iKO and the M2iKO show higher Ca2+ uptake than the CTRLs (Fig. 3B lowest row). By calculating in each genotype the ratio of 45Ca2+ uptake for 100 nM to that for 20 µM from the values shown in Fig. 3B, we could compare “gatekeeping” as the relative fraction of channels that mediated Ca2+ uptake during low Ca2+ exposure in these conditions. Taking 45Ca2+ uptake at 20 µM as a measure of total channel availability, we find that 2.5 to 3 times more channels are used at the 100 nM Ca2+ concentration in both male and female M1iKO CM. For, M2iKO it is two times more than in the CTRLs (Fig. 3C). The double logarithmic plots calculated from the dose–response show slopes around 1.4 for all the CTRLs and 1.25 and 1.06 for M1iKO males and females respectively, and 1.23 for the M2iKO male (Fig. 3D). Together, these data show the presence of moderate cooperativity in mtCU activation in murine CM, and the dependence of cooperativity on MICU1/2.

We also note that the gatekeeping patterns are similar in male and female mice but the maximal 45Ca2+ uptake capacity is significantly lower in females than in males (Fig. 3C). Proteomics of mouse heart mitochondria (SI Appendix, Fig. S3G) show lower abundance of mtCU subunits in the females.

MICU1 and MICU2 Control Mitochondrial Calcium Signaling in Intact Murine CM.

To study the role of MICU1 and MICU2 in calcium signaling in intact CM, we measured mitochondrial matrix free [Ca2+] ([Ca2+]m) during cytoplasmic [Ca2+] ([Ca2+]c) elevations induced by different stimuli in MICU1- and MICU2-deficient CM. For this, we isolated CM from M1KO (Micu1KO/KO) and M2KO ~e15 embryonic hearts and corresponding control embryos, Micu1fl/fl/Micu1fl/KO and Micu2fl/fl(marked as CTRL). Using embryonic CM allowed us to culture cells for several days for genetic manipulations that would be difficult in adult CM, and to use Micu1KO/KO to study almost complete protein loss that is difficult to achieve in the other KO models that also have more pronounced off-target changes in the mtCU composition (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 A–D).

Isolated ~e15 CM were infected with adenoviral mtRCamP (KD ~ 1.47 µM, SI Appendix, Fig. S4E) and 48 h later loaded with fura-2 AM to measure [Ca2+]m and [Ca2+]c, respectively. To estimate how MICUs control [Ca2+]m during [Ca2+]c oscillations accompanying heart beating, we first electrically paced the CM at 0.25 Hz, 1 Hz, and 4 Hz with 20 V/cm (Fig. 4 H–O) with 30 s rest periods. Then, to evaluate how MICUs affect [Ca2+]m during transient [Ca2+]c elevations, single electrical pulses were applied with increasing power, 20 V/cm, 40 V/cm, and 80 V/cm with 30 s rest periods (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 G, Bottom). Finally, to assess the role of MICUs in the control of [Ca2+]m during prolonged [Ca2+]c elevations we increased extracellular potassium chloride (KCl) concentration to 50 mM causing sustained depolarization (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 A–G).

[Ca2+]c signals induced by different stimuli are similar for all the genotypes studied (Fig. 4 A–C and SI Appendix, Fig. S4G). Prolonged [Ca2+]c elevations induced by 50 mM KCl are followed by [Ca2+]m rise in all genotypes. However, the [Ca2+]m is progressive and much greater when MICU2 or MICU1 is absent (Fig. 4 A–G). These latter observations are consistent with literature data on the gatekeeping function of MICU1 and MICU2 of the mtCU within this low [Ca2+]c range (26). Because of the more serious phenotype for MICU1 than MICU2, in the next experiments, we focused on MICU1.

Transient [Ca2+]c elevation from single electric field pulses does not affect [Ca2+]m measured by mtRCamP in control and M1KO (SI Appendix, Fig. S4, Bottom). Consistent with this, electrical pacing at 0.25 Hz also does not measurably increase [Ca2+]m (Fig. 4 H, K, N, and O). However, increasing pacing frequency to 1 Hz and 4 Hz results in a progressive increase in [Ca2+]m in M1KO, but not in the CTRL (Fig. 4 I, J, and L–O). Thus, MICU1 controls mitochondrial calcium uptake during [Ca2+]c oscillations and reduces [Ca2+]m at higher frequencies when mitochondrial calcium buffering and slow extrusion might be less effective at countering the uptake.

We also assessed the role of MICU1 in the control of [Ca2+]m during [Ca2+]c oscillations in ~p15 CM that still can be cultured for several days which allows time for genetic manipulations. For this, we isolated CM from ~p15 CTRL (Micu1fl/fl) and M1smKO hearts, infected them with adenovirus mtRCamP, and 48 h later loaded them with fura-2 AM to measure [Ca2+]m and [Ca2+]c, respectively. With electrical pacing at 1 Hz, the [Ca2+]c oscillations are similar in both genotypes and are followed by a [Ca2+]m rise, which is greater in M1smKO (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 A–D). Thus, MICU1 reduces [Ca2+]m uptake during [Ca2+]c oscillations both in ~e15 and ~p15. We should note that a higher [Ca2+]m rise during 1 Hz electrical pacing in ~p15 when compared to ~e15 might be due to the higher amplitude of [Ca2+]c oscillations at this age. A smaller difference in [Ca2+]m rise might be due to incomplete downregulation of MICU1 with more pronounced secondary changes in other mtCU subunits at this timepoint in M1smKO (SI Appendix, Figs. S4 A–D and S5E).

The absence of a [Ca2+]m rise detected during transient [Ca2+]c elevation in ~e15 CTRL CM is inconsistent with some previous reports (48–51) and might be explained by the KD ~1.5 µM of RCaMP for Ca2+ (SI Appendix, Fig. S4E), slow sampling rate (~100 ms), and low [Ca2+]c signals in ~e15 CM (Fig. 4). In support of the latter, in ~p15 CM, 1 Hz pacing induced stronger [Ca2+]c signals that resulted in [Ca2+]m signals detected by mtRCamP (SI Appendix, Fig. S5A). The absence of [Ca2+]m transients detected by mtRCamP is unrelated to the probe expression in our experiments as we did not observe a higher fold response in cells with higher intensity (SI Appendix, Fig. S4F).

[Ca2+]m dynamics in beating CMs remains controversial in the literature and two modes were suggested for [Ca2+]m during beating: integration of [Ca2+]c signals and beat-to-beat transmission (52). While Roberts et al. (48) reported beat-to-beat synchronization of [Ca2+]m in intact neonatal rat ventricular CM using aequorin (Kd ~ 1 μM) and pericam (Kd ~ 1.7 μM), Chen et al. (49) were not able to detect any increase in [Ca2+]m with GCamP2 (Kd ~ 0.195 μM) in intact adult rat CM in the presence of blebbistatin (myosin/contraction inhibitor) and treated as motion artifacts the [Ca2+]m spikes they initially observed. Sedova et al. (53) observed integration of the [Ca2+]c signal by mitochondria rather than beat-to-beat synchronization in permeabilized cat CM with mitochondria loaded with fluo-3 AM (Kd ~ 0.39 μM) and rapid perfusion mimicking [Ca2+]c signal during beating. Similar data pointing toward integration and not beat-to-beat synchronization were reported by Wescott et al. (41) in intact adult mouse CM using mitycam (Kd ~ 0.2 μM). Recently, Luongo et al. (51) using the same model and probe demonstrated both beat-to-beat synchronization and integration similar to what was observed using mitycam in rabbit CM (50). Our results do not exclude a beat-to-beat mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake but show the presence of some integration of [Ca2+]c signals.

Altogether, these results indicate the importance of MICUs during the physiological functioning of murine CM in the control of [Ca2+]m during [Ca2+]c oscillations that resemble those occurring during heart beating.

MICU1 Loss Affects Oxidative Metabolism and Sensitizes Mitochondria to Ca2+ Overload.

We evaluated whether any of the Ca2+-sensitive matrix dehydrogenases (CSMDH) that mediate the effect of [Ca2+]m on oxidative metabolism were affected by MICU1 loss. Ca2+-induced activation of mitochondrial pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH) is mediated by dephosphorylation by a Ca2+-sensitive phosphatase (54). To assess the activated fraction of PDH, we immunoblotted M1KO (Micu1KO/KO) and CTRL ~e15 heart tissue lysate for both phosphorylated (p-PDH) and total-PDH and also calculated the ratios (Fig. 5 A and B). The total-PDH does not seem to be altered but the phosphorylated fraction is decreased indicating increased PDH activity upon MICU1 loss in the embryonic heart.

(E) Mean [Ca2+]c signals in MI1KO (red trace) and control (CTRL, Micu1fl/fl/Micu1fl/KO, black trace) ~e15 CM measured by fura-2 AM simultaneously with (A). (F) Cell survival upon MICU1 loss without and with compensation for the EMRE decrease, by transfection with pcDNA and EMRE, respectively.

To see whether MICU1 loss might be deleterious for CM during pathological conditions, we tested whether prolonged [Ca2+]c elevation with increased ROS might affect mitochondrial membrane permeabilization that can initiate cell injury or death. For this, we extended our experiments on KCl-induced depolarization in M1KO (Micu1KO/KO) and corresponding CTRL (Micu1fl/fl/Micu1fl/KO) ~e15 CM. To increase cell stress, we supplemented the medium with 200 μM H2O2 and 8 mM CaCl2 and measured [Ca2+]c with fura-2 and ΔΨm with TMRE for 20 min following addition of 50 mM KCl. We observe a similar initial [Ca2+]c rise immediately after the addition of KCl in both genotypes (Fig. 5 C–E) and minimal changes in ΔΨm. However, the course of subsequent depolarization over 20 min is faster in MICU1-deficient mitochondria (Fig. 5 C and D). Thus, along the line of findings in other cell types (16, 24, 27), the loss of MICU1 may promote mitochondrial membrane permeabilization and CM damage under pathological conditions.

In summary, the increased [Ca2+]m rise in MICU1-deficient CM might support energy metabolism through the CSMDH but the ungated mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake is a permanent risk for Ca2+ overload. Because the tendency for Ca2+ overload was detected in Micu1KO/KO CM that have an adaptive decrease in other mtCU components, it was important to test whether the compensatory changes were required to avoid cell death. Therefore, we overexpressed EMRE + mtYFP or pcDNA + mtYFP in MICU1-deficient mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs) and assessed the survival of transfected cells by quantifying the YFP-positive cells (Fig. 5F). The cells in which EMRE was restored failed to stay alive or divide like the unrescued MICU1-deficient cells. Overexpression of EMRE in Micu1KO/KO MEFs also caused an increase in the resting [Ca2+]m and the MCU levels (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 F–H). Notably, overexpression of EMRE in cells with unperturbed mtCU (MICU1) does not alter the resting [Ca2+]m and survival/growth (Fig. 5F and SI Appendix, Fig. S5F). Based on this, the adaptation to MICU1 loss of EMRE’s abundance is central to maintaining normal cell function and viability.

Discussion

Mitochondrial Ca2+ influx supports excitation-metabolism coupling in the heart but can overload mitochondria to induce cell death. MICU1/2 serve as the Ca2+ sensing gatekeepers to control mitochondrial Ca2+ influx, but MICU1/2’s relevance in the heart mtCU has been questioned. Our results provide biochemical, genetic, and pharmacological evidence that MICU1/2 are present and important for the mammalian heart mtCU. The loss of MICU1/2 causes more than a 2-fold increase in mitochondrial Ca2+ influx at resting [Ca2+]c and enhances [Ca2+]m rise during electrical pacing. This leads to activation of Ca2+-sensitive steps of energy metabolism but also sensitizes mitochondria to Ca2+ overload and membrane injury. This happens in the face of a dramatic compensatory downregulation of the mtCU-mediated mitochondrial influx in MICU1-deficient heart. This is mediated by increased turnover of EMRE early on and, later, of MCU as well in the absence of MICU1. Reversing the compensation interferes with cell survival and/or proliferation.

Some tissue-specific differences in mtCU activity were shown long ago (55, 56). Still, it was surprising that mitoplast electrophysiology showed a dramatically lower mtCU current density in heart mitochondria than in mitochondria of other tissues (42). Furthermore, the mtCU in many cell types was found to detect each [Ca2+]c spike individually, but in the CMs, where high frequency [Ca2+]c oscillations drive muscle contraction, skepticism grew about “beat-to-beat” activation of the mtCU (35). Upon molecular identification of the mtCU components, the pore-forming component, MCU, and even more so the gatekeeper, MICU1, were found to be low in the heart compared to other tissues (39). Most recently, Lederer et al. and Tsai et al. claimed the lack of Ca2+-dependent control and the lack of MICU1 regulation of mtCU, respectively, in the heart (3, 41). Based on this, the heart would function with mtCUs that are permanently open mediating a Ca2+ influx linearly dependent on the extramitochondrial [Ca2+], and Ca2+ influx would be compensated by vigorous extrusion mediated by Ca2+ exchangers including the Na+/Ca2+ exchanger, Na/Li/Ca exchanger, the acute deletion of which is indeed detrimental in the heart (51). If this model were correct, genetic ablation of MICU1/2 in the heart would not cause any alterations in Ca2+ uptake via mtCU. The results we obtained using 45Ca uptake, fluorometric, and fluorescence imaging in permeabilized and intact CM under genetic and pharmacological targeting of MICU1/2 all support their role in regulating the cardiac mtCU. The likely explanation for missing this in earlier studies is that no genetic models for MICU1/2 were tested and Ca2+ flux measurements in the heart did not include the ≤100 nM [Ca2+]c range where the impact of gatekeeping by MICU1 is most noticeable (3, 40, 41). Furthermore, while all the calcium measurements and genetic perturbations were performed in rodents, our detection of MICU1/2 proteins and MICU1–MCU complex formation in nonfailing human hearts supports a broad mammalian presence of mtCU gatekeeping by MICU1/2.

If MICU1/2 were absent or irrelevant in the heart mtCUs, their ablation would not be followed by major compensatory changes in the mtCU abundance and Ca2+ flux. On the contrary, we found major decreases in the abundance of the pore stabilizing/forming components upon both acute and chronic MICU1/2-deletion in CM that was more severe for MICU1 indicating its importance for mtCU stability. The recent linking of MICU1 to the mitochondrial crista organizing complex (57–59) could indicate that a compensation might be needed for this function of MICU1, however, the compensation we observed highlights a dramatic decrease in the mtCU flux capacity in CM. Lowering of EMRE upon MICU1 targeting has been shown before in both physiological and model systems and might be due to its vulnerability to mAAA proteases when it is not bound to MICU1/MCU (17, 40, 44, 60). We extend the evidence to the heart and also show that restoring EMRE following MICU1 deletion interferes with survival and growth. In addition to the decrease in EMRE, we also show a decrease in MCU abundance in long-term MICU1 depletion. Strikingly, the combined lowering of EMRE and MCU results in 95% reduction in the mtCU Ca2+ flux capacity. Based on this finding and previous studies employing mtCU genetic models in the heart and showing heart tolerance to the MCU and EMRE loss at least in unstressed conditions (5, 6, 61), we conclude that MICU1/2 is primarily needed to suppress mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake likely in the periods when the average [Ca2+]c is low.

We presented a clue to increased activity of the CSMDH upon MICU1 deletion in CM that indicates a potential relevance in oxidative metabolism though this has not been explicitly tested. What is clear is that MICU1-deficient CM were prone to mitochondrial Ca2+ overload and membrane permeabilization. Along this line, restoring EMRE in the MICU1-deficient MEFs was fatal to many cells. Thus, the primary danger upon MICU1 loss in the heart is mitochondrial Ca2+ overload and the ensuing cell death. To avoid this, a robust adaptive mechanism at the level of mtCU pore stability is present. Consistent with this, MICU1 increase was shown to exert protection upon ischemia–reperfusion injury (62). However, MICU1 is also elevated in the failing heart (51, 63), which can reflect either an adaptive mechanism or a pathogenic role for MICU1.

Among the different MICU isoforms, only MICU1 can be the anchoring component in the MICU dimer that serves mtCU gatekeeping (28, 60) and MICU2 seems to act only through MICU1. MICU2’s loss has been linked to diastolic dysfunction (64). We extended those findings by showing that MICU2 is also required to maintain effective gatekeeping in CM. Compensatory changes follow MICU2 loss but the decrease in mtCU Ca2+ flux capacity is milder than that upon MICU1 deletion. This is likely because MICU2 can be replaced by MICU1 in the MICU dimers (26, 65). However, MICU1–MICU1 has higher affinity for Ca2+ than MICU1–MICU2 (66), and therefore allows more Ca2+ uptake that requires compensations.

An accidental observation of our study was that the abundance of the mtCU components and the mtCU Ca2+ flux capacity are lower in CM derived from female mice than that from males. The quantitative relationship of the different mtCU components to each other seems to be similar in females and males. We observed the female vs. male difference to be present when the mice were of reproductive age, so this might be related to hormonal differences. To establish the exact reason for the difference and to extend the study to humans, a great deal of further effort is likely required. This is certainly justified because of potential relevance in differential disease pathogenesis and therapy sensitivity in the different sexes.

Methods

Human Subjects.

Subjects included in this study were Caucasian males of 50 to 70 y with no known cardiac morbidity and mortality was caused by mostly motor vehicle accidents. Rejected donor hearts were collected via Versiti Blood Center of Wisconsin donor network/organ procurement system who obtained consent from next of kin to donate the organs to scientific research without specific project. As tissue donors were deceased, the organs were collected as discard tissue, and deemed exempt by IRB but are encompassed under IRB PRO00010828. All samples were shared with investigative the team in deidentified manner.

Human LV (Left Ventricle) Cardiac Mitochondria Isolation.

At the time of cardiac explant, heart tissue was immediately cooled in ice-cold oxygenated Tyrode’s solution in the operating room. Ventricular tissue was rapidly dissected into pieces of ~100 mg and was flash frozen and stored at −80 °C until further use.

During isolation of mitochondria, one piece of frozen LV was thawed, fat was carefully trimmed off, and the remaining muscle was washed in ice-cold isolation buffer containing 225 mM Mannitol, 75 mM sucrose, 20 mM N-2-hydroxyethylpiperazine-N'-2-ethanesulfonic acid (HEPES), and 0.1 mM EGTA, at pH 7.4. Heart tissue was finely minced and incubated for 1 min in the isolation buffer with 3 U/g of tissue of protease inhibitors (PI, leupeptin, antipain, pepstatin, 1 µg/mL each). Then, the tissue was homogenized with a glass Teflon potter homogenizer with 450 rpm and 20 strokes while keeping on ice and the homogenate was centrifuged at 500 g/10 min/4 °C to remove nuclei and cellular debris. The supernatant was next centrifuged at 2,500 g/10 min/4 °C and the pellet (mitochondria) was resuspended in 225 mM mannitol, 75 mM sucrose, and 10 mM HEPES, pH 7.4 (storage buffer), and kept on ice. Protein content was determined by the Lowry method (BioRad). The protein yield was 2 to 7 mg from 100 to 110 mg cardiac tissues. The preparations were frozen and later on used for immunoblotting (25 µg protein) and proteomics (50 µg protein diluted in 15 µL of storage buffer).

Mouse Models.

All the animal protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Thomas Jefferson University and adhered to NIH standards.

M1KO.

To obtain whole-body germline stable Micu1 knockout mice, the Cre-loxP system was used to target exon 3 of the Micu1 gene for removal as described before (16).

M2KO.

To obtain whole-body germline Micu2 knockout mice, the Cre-loxP system was used to target exon 3 of the Micu2 gene for removal. Micu2WT/flFLP+/0 mice (on C57BL/6J background) were generated by Ingenious Targeting Laboratory, and then delivered to Thomas Jefferson University for subsequent breeding. Micu2WT/fl FLP+/0 mice were bred with wild-type C57BL/6J mice to remove the FLP transgene. A successive generation of breeding generated Micu2fl/fl mice, which were then bred with germline-expressing Cre-eIIa mice (Jackson Laboratories; C57BL/6J background). These offspring were bred with homozygous floxed mice to remove the Cre transgene, and establish whole-body heterozygous knockout mice Micu2KO/fl. Heterozygote male and female mice were bred to generate whole-body germline knockouts Micu2KO/KO (indicated here as M2KO) as well as control homozygous floxed. Only male mice were used for experiments.

M1smKO.

To obtain mice with MICU1 loss in striated muscle (M1smKO), Micu1fl/fl mice were bred with B6- Tg(Ckmm-Cre) (C56BL/6J background, Jackson Laboratory) mice in which Cre recombinase is driven by the muscle creatine kinase promoter Tg(Ckmm-Cre). These offspring were bred with homozygous Micu1fl/fl mice to establish homozygous Micu1fl/fl carrying the CKM-cre (M1smKO) transgene (47). For ~p15 age all pups were used whereas for studies on adult mice only males were used.

M1iKO and M2iKO.

Micu1fl/fl and Micu2fl/fl mice (C57BL/6J background, Jackson Laboratory) were crossed with cardiac-specific α-MHC-MerCreMer mice (MCM+). Both males and females for Micu1 and only males for Micu2, were used for experiments. Gene targeting (starting at 12 wk of age) was induced by Tamoxifen via IP injections. Tamoxifen (100 mg) was mixed with 500 µL of ethanol and then diluted with 9.5 mL corn oil and vigorously mixed. Tamoxifen (40 mg/kg body weight) was injected daily for 7 d followed by a resting period of 16 to 20 d.

CM Isolation from ~p80 Mice.

Mice were injected intraperitoneally with 100 units of heparin. After 5 min, mice were sacrificed by cervical dislocation, the chest opened, and the heart excised with well-exposed ascending aorta, which was then cannulated for retrograde perfusion. CM were isolated as described (67).

CM Isolation from ~e15 Mice.

Embryonic mice were removed on gestational days 14 to 16, and the hearts were harvested. For each embryo heart, atria were removed and only ventricles were dissected in ice-cold Phosphate Buffered Saline and then chopped in 50 µL medium with a mix 4:1 of Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) and Medium 199, Earle’s Salts (Gibco Cat no. 12350-039) containing 1 mg/mL collagenase type II (Worthington Biochemical) and incubated for 30 min. The digestion reaction was then stopped by adding 1 mL of the same medium without collagenase supplemented with 20% FBS (Fetal Bovine Serum), and the pellet was resuspended by pipetting 10 times and spun for 5 min at 1,000 rpm. The pellet was further resuspended with 100 µL of the mix 4:1 DMEM:M199 with 10% FBS, Penicillin/Streptomycin, and glutamine and plated as a drop on 18-mm or 25-mm coverslips (Carolina Biological Supply Co.) coated with laminin 2 mg/mL (Corning Cat no. 354232) and maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C in 5% CO2 for 2 to 3 h to allow the cells to attach. Then 500 µL of the same medium was added and maintained in an incubator with the same conditions (68). Cells were then infected with adenoviral mtRCaMP (4,000 pfu/coverslip, ~10 pfu/cell, stock Vector Biolab). Twenty-four hours later the medium has been replaced. The cells were examined in live cell imaging 48 h after the infection.

Mitochondria Isolation from ~p80 Mice.

Mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation. The heart was rapidly excised and washed in cold isolation buffer containing 225 mM mannitol, 75 mM sucrose, 20 mM HEPES, and 0.1 mM EGTA, at pH 7.4. Heart tissue was finely minced and incubated for 1 min in the isolation buffer with 0.5% BSA (Bovine Serum Albumin) and 3 U/g of tissue of proteases (Sigma). Then, the cardiac tissue was homogenized with a glass Teflon potter homogenizer and the homogenate centrifuged at 500 g/5 min/4 °C to remove nuclei and cellular debris. The supernatant was next centrifuged at 10,000 g/10 min/4 °C and the mitochondrial pellet was resuspended in 225 mM, Mannitol, 75 mM Sucrose, 10 mM HEPES at pH 7.4 and kept on ice. Protein content was determined by the Lowry method (BioRad).

Fluorimetry of Mitochondrial Ca2+ Uptake and ΔΨm in Permeabilized CM.

Ca2+ uptake measurements were performed in an intracellular-like medium (ICM: 120 mM KCl, 10 mM NaCl, 1 mM KH2PO4, 20 mM Tris-HEPES at pH 7.2) similar to as described in ref. 69. Cell suspensions (containing ~800,000 CM/run) were permeabilized by digitonin (Sigma, 40 μg/mL) for 5 min at 37 °C with stirring in the presence of PI, 10 μM Thapsigargin (Tg, Enzo BioScience) and 10 µM EGTA. After permeabilization, CM were pelleted and resuspended in 1.5 mL ICM and maintained in a stirred thermostated cuvette at 37 °C in the presence of PI, 10 μM Tg, 1 mM Malate (Sigma), 1 mM Pyruvate (Sigma), and 100 µM MgCl2.

The extramitochondrial [Ca2+] was assessed using a ratiometric Ca2+ probe fura-FF-FA (1 μM, TEFLabs) and ΔΨm was measured with 1.5 μM TMRM (TetraMethylRhodamine Methyl ester) (Invitrogen) in a multiwavelength excitation dual-wavelength emission fluorimeter (DeltaRAM, PTI). Fura and TMRM fluorescence were recorded simultaneously using 340 and 380 nm excitations and 500 nm emission and 545 nm excitation and 580 nm emission, respectively. Complete depolarization (maximum dequench of TMRM fluorescence) was elicited using the protonophore FCCP (Sigma, 2 μM) and Oligomycin (Sigma, 5 µg/mL). ΔΨm and fura-FF traces were analyzed as described before (69). At the end of each experiment, the CM fraction was recovered by centrifugation and used to estimate mtCU components by immunoblot (69).

Measurements of 45Ca2+ Uptake in Suspensions of Permeabilized Cells.

Isolated CM (~3 × 106 CM/mL in 170 µL) were washed in 1.2 mL of ICM containing 10 µM Tg, 10 µM EGTA, 20 µM CGP37157, 1 µg/mL PI each and 0.0005 N KOH (Fisher) and pelleted down with 1,000 g for 5 min at RT (Room Temperature). Then the pellet was resuspended in 1.2 mL of prewarmed (5 min for 37 °C) ICM with the same constituents, plus saponin (40 µg/mL), 1.8 mM MgATP, and 1 mM of Malate + Pyruvate each. The permeabilized CM suspensions were split to two aliquots 600 µL, each. Ru265 (8 µM) was added to one. The aliquots were incubated for 4 to 5 min at 37 °C. For each time point and [Ca2+] of the 45Ca uptake assay, 100 µL CM suspension was mixed with ~1.5 µCi 45Ca2+ (5.34 µM CaCl2 final concentration) and different concentrations of EGTA or CaCl2 to attain various [Ca2+] from 80 nM to 20 µM. The [Ca2+] was measured by fluorometric assay. For the time course studies, at 30, 60, and 90 s after the addition of 45Ca2+, the 100 µL aliquots were diluted in 5 mL ice-cold washing buffer (KCl 140 mM, HEPES/TRIS 10 mM, EGTA/TRIS 0.5 mM, pH7.2) and filtered through 0.45 µm nitrocellulose membranes (Millipore). From here, 30 s time point was selected as initial uptake of 45Ca2+ by the mitochondria, and subsequent 45Ca2+ uptake assays with different [Ca2+] were done with only 30 s time point. 45Ca2+ in the cells remained on the filters after washing was quantified by liquid scintillation counting (PerkinElmer TRI-CARB 5110TR 110 V).

BN PAGE.

100 μg of isolated mitochondria were solubilized using 4% digitonin in extraction buffer (30 mM HEPES, 12% glycerol, 150 mM potassium acetate, 2 mM aminocaproic acid, 2 mM EDTA disodium salt, PI tablet, pH 7.4), at 4 °C for 30 min, with constant shaking at 1,500 rpm. Samples were then centrifuged at 25,000 g, 20 min, 4 °C, and the supernatant was added to 1:400 diluted 5% Coomassie Blue G-250 (1 µL for 10 µL of sample, ThermoFisher). The samples were loaded into a NativePAGE™ NovexR 3 to 12% Bis-Tris, 10-well gel. The gel was run (on ice, 30 V) in 1× NativePAGE™ Running buffer (anode; Invitrogen) and 1× dark blue buffer (cathode buffer) for 1 h 30 min then switched to 1× light blue cathode buffer for the rest of the run (overnight). To analyze native-protein complexes, gels were transferred onto 0.22 µm low-fluorescence polyvinylidene difluoride membranes, destained using 8% acetic acid for 15 min, and then air-dried. Membranes were rewetted with methanol, rinsed with milliQ, and blocked with 3% BSA in TBS-T (Tris Buffered Saline, Tween-20) for 1 h, and then incubated with anti-MCU (Cell signaling, 14997, 1:500) or anti-MICU1 (Sigma, HPA034780, 1:500) antibodies diluted in TBS-T at 4 °C overnight. For colorimetric detection, the membrane was incubated with anti-rabbit HRP secondary antibody (Cell signaling 7074S, 1:5,000).

For 2D BN electrophoresis, each sample was run in duplicate: one lane was Coomassie blue-stained and the second lane was unstained and used for the 2D denaturing (SDS) gel. The lane for the 2D gel was excised, equilibrated in an Equilibration (Eq) buffer (50 mM Tris, 6 M Urea, 30% glycerol, 2% SDS, pH 8.5) for 5 min, then switched to Eq. buffer + 200 mM DTT for 30 min, and then to Eq. buffer + 135 mM iodoacetamide for 15 min and 2× Eq. buffer for 15-min incubations at RT. The lane was then inserted into a cassette containing a NuPAGE™ 4 to 12% Bis-Tris Gel and run in 1× 3-(N-morpholino)propanesulfonic acid (Invitrogen) running buffer. Proteins were transferred onto 0.2 µm nitrocellulose membrane (Invitrogen), stained with Ponceau dye, destained by 1× TBS-T, 8% acetic acid + MetOH for 15 min, then air-dried. The membrane was then rewetted with methanol, rinsed with milliQ, and blocked with 3% BSA in TBS-T for 1 h, and then incubated with antibodies overnight at 4 °C: anti-MCU (Sigma Aldrich AM Ab91189, 1:1,000) or anti-MICU1 (Sigma, HPA034780, 1:500) diluted in TBS-T. For colorimetric detection, the membrane was incubated with anti-rabbit alkaline phosphatase-conjugated secondary antibodies (Promega, S373B, 1:5,000 in TBS-T).

Live Cell Imaging of [Ca2+]m, [Ca2+]c, and ΔΨm.

Isolated ~e15 were preincubated for 5 min at 37 °C in a serum-free extracellular medium (120 mM NaCl, 5 mM NaHCO3, 10 mM Na-HEPES, 4.7 mM KCl, 1.2 mM KH2PO4, 1.2 mM MgSO4, 2 mM CaCl2, 10 mM glucose, pH7.4) containing 2% BSA, then rinsed once and loaded with 2 μM fura-2 AM (Invitrogen) and 0.003% Pluronic F-127 (Invitrogen) to measure [Ca2+]c at 37 °C. P15 CM were treated similarly, but incubation media contained 10 mM BDM. In the experiments where ΔΨm was measured, 25 nM TMRE (Invitrogen) was included at the same time. After loading, cells were transferred to an imaging chamber containing Hanks’ balanced salt solution (HBSS; Gibco, 14025076) that for ΔΨm experiments also contained 2 nM TMRE. For [Ca2+]c and [Ca2+]m or ΔΨm imaging, we used a Hamamatsu Photonics ORCA-Fusion C14440-20UP fitted to an Olympus IX83 inverted microscope with a 20× water-immersion objective (UAPON20XW340 Olympus with Zeiss Immersol Immersion oil W 2010) and Lambda DG4 wavelength-switch xenon short-arc light source (Sutter Instruments) operated by MetaMorph® software (Molecular Devices). The temperature inside the microscope incubation system was maintained at 37 °C by TempController 2000-2 cellVivo (Pecon). Fura-2 fluorescence was recorded at 340 and 380 nm excitation, and the mitochondrial matrix-targeted RCaMP R386V modification from RCaMP1h with KD ~ 1.47 µM (mtRCamP; see SI Appendix, Fig. S4E and Supplemental Method section) was excited at 577/20 nm using dual-band dichroic and emission filters (Chroma set 59022 customized). The images were acquired at 100 ms ([Ca2+]c and [Ca2+]m studies) and 1 to 10 s ([Ca2+]c and ΔΨmito studies) intervals. Fura-2 was calibrated in vitro by adding 1 mM CaCl2, followed by 10 mM EGTA/Tris, pH 8.5. [Ca2+]c was determined by the following formula: [Ca2+] = Kd × (Sf2/Sb2) × (R − Rmin)/(Rmax − R), where Kd is the Ca2+-dissociation constant (0.224 μM), R is the ratio of the fluorescence intensities at 340/380 nm excitation, Rmin and Rmax are the fluorescence ratios in Ca2+-free and Ca2+-saturated conditions, respectively, Sf2/Sb2 is the ratio of fluorescence intensities measured at 380 nm excitation in Ca2+-free (f)/Ca2+-saturated (Ca2+-bound, b) conditions. For the ΔΨmito studies, FCCP (Sigma, 10 μM) and oligomycin (Sigma, 5 μg/mL) were added at the end of the experiment to achieve complete mitochondrial depolarization that was taken as 0. Obtained image sets were analyzed in Fiji 2.14.0 using custom macros (https://sites.imagej.net/Theweave) for background subtraction. Individual cells were masked and mean gray values were exported and further processed in Excel, Visual Studio Code 1.76.2 (using Python 3.9.12, Numpy 1.21.5, Matplotlib 3.5.1, Statsmodels 0.13.2, Pandas 1.4.2), SigmaPlot 12.5, and GraphPad Prism 9.3.0. Traces for fluorescence intensities were obtained by taking average from all cells in a field and were considered as one measurement corresponding to one embryonic heart. This measurement was used to calculate areaunder the curve and for statistical analysis. For ΔΨm experiments, due to higher variability, daily run means were used for further analysis.

Pulse Field Electric Stimulation and KCl Induced Depolarization.

For electrical stimulation, isolated ~e15 or ~p15 CM were placed on an O-ring–based perfusion chamber with a stimulator (RC-49MFSH) and filled with 500 μL of imaging buffer (HBSS). In case of ~p15 CM, HBSS was supplemented with 10 mM BDM. During field stimulation, CM were continuously perfused with HBSS heated by SH-27B solution heater (Warner) set to 37 °C by TC-344C dual automatic temperature controller using a gravity-based custom-made perfusion system equipped with a Fisherbrand™ Variable-Flow Peristaltic Pump or Vacuum Waste Kit (ALA Scientific Instruments). The cells were subjected to electrical field stimulation at 20, 40, and 80 V/cm achieved by passing 2-ms current pulses at different frequencies using a platinum field stimulation electrode (RC-49MFSH) and High-Power Bi-Phase Stimulator 701C (Aurora Scientific) under the control of Optogenetic TTL pulse generator with corresponding software (Doric lenses). First, the cells were subjected to single 2-ms pulses at 20, 40, and 80 V/cm with 30-s rest period. Then, CM were electrically paced at 0.25, 1, and 4 Hz at 20 V/cm by applying sequences of two 2-ms pulses at 400 Hz in biphasic mode with 30-s rest periods.

After the electrical stimulation, CM were depolarized by substituting 50% of the imaging solution with 50 mM KCl-containing solution (NaCl 20 mM, KCl 105 mM, NaHCO3 5 mM, NaH2PO4×2H2O 1.2 mM, MgCl2×6H2O 1 mM, glucose 10 mM, HEPES 10 mM, CaCl2 dihydrate 2 mM, pH 7.4 with NaOH). For the ΔΨm experiments, electrical stimulation was omitted and 50% of the imaging solution was substituted with 50 mM KCl-containing solution supplemented with 200 µM H2O2 and 8 mM CaCl2 in the usual imaging chamber.

The [Ca2+]c and [Ca2+]m or ΔΨm signals evoked by electrical field stimulation and KCl-induced depolarization were imaged as described above in imaging panel.

Data Analysis and Statistical Processing.

Statistical analysis was performed using SigmaPlot 12.5 (Systat Software) or SPSS (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences) (28.0.0.0, International Business Machines). For comparisons between groups to choose appropriate test, first, it was determined whether the data were normally distributed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and passed the equal variance test. In some cases, the data were rank transformed to meet these requirements for further processing. The tests used and significance levels are indicated in the figure legends. To determine differences between slopes in 45Ca uptake experiments we used univariate analysis with a generalized linear model in SPSS. Specifically, we fit the log(Ca2+ uptake) values by genotype with the log([Ca2+]c) as a covariate and the interaction between log([Ca2+]c) and genotype.
