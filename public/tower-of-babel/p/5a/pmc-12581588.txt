COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy continuum and its psycho-behavioral predictive models among general population: a cross-sectional study in Ethiopia
Authors: Dawit Getachew, Nahom Solomon, Melsew Setegn Alie, Mengistu Ayenew
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy continuum reflects an individual decision-making process regarding vaccination, ranging from fully willing to accept the vaccine to outright refusal. This study assessed COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy and compared the explanatory power of selected psycho-behavioral models for its variability among adults living in Southwest Ethiopia.MethodsA community-based cross-sectional study was conducted in Southwest Ethiopia from January 1 to 15, 2022. The calculated sample size was 879. A multistage sampling technique was used to select study participants. Data were collected using an interviewer-administered questionnaire comprising four sections: socio-demographic characteristics, COVID-19-related covariates, psycho-behavioral constructs, and a vaccine hesitancy scale. The data were entered into Epi-Data, and analysis was performed using Python in Jupyter Notebook. Descriptive statistics and hierarchical multiple linear regression were performed to compare the predictive ability of four psycho-behavioral models: the health belief model (perceived susceptibility, severity, benefit, barrier, and cues to actions); the theory of planned behavior (attitudes, subjective norms, behavioral control, and anticipated regret); five psychological antecedents (confidence, complacency, constraints, calculation, and collective responsibility); and vaccine conspiracy belief (conspiracy related to infection and the vaccine). Model comparison was done using adjusted R-squared and model selection criteria.ResultsThe mean age of the participants was 31.63 ± 8.28, and 365 (44.3%) of them were female. The mean score of the COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy continuum was 5.11 ± 2.41. Among the respondents, 91 (11%) were fully willing to accept the COVID-19 vaccine, while 97 (11.8%) were completely resistant. The remaining 624 (77.18%) participants were hesitant to make a decision about vaccination.The vaccine conspiracy belief demonstrated the most predictive ability (adjusted R2 = 0.43), followed by the 5C psychological antecedent (adjusted R2 = 0.36), the theory of planned behavior (adjusted R2 = 0.26), and the health belief model (adjusted R2 = 0.20).ConclusionCOVID-19 vaccine hesitancy in Ethiopia is most predicted by vaccine conspiracy belief. Therefore, public health intervention should focus on addressing misinformation and conspiracy narratives.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24470-w.

Introduction

COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy is a delay in acceptance or refusal of COVID-19 vaccination despite the availability of safe and effective vaccines [1–3]. This hesitancy poses a major challenge to global immunization efforts aimed at achieving herd immunity [4–7]. While the majority of high-income countries reached herd immunity by vaccinating 60–70% of their population, fewer than 10 African nations had vaccinated 40% of their population by mid-2021[8–10].

Vaccine hesitancy is complex and context-specific, varying across time, place, and vaccine type [3]. According to the World Health Organization Strategic Advisory Group of Experts (SAGE) on Immunization, vaccine hesitancy exists on a continuum from full acceptance to complete refusal. Hesitant individuals may delay vaccination, accept some vaccines but not others, or receive a vaccine while remaining uncertain. Therefore, measuring hesitancy requires tools that capture nuanced decision-making processes. In addition to the pandemic urgency, the nature of mass vaccination campaigns can provoke hesitancy due to psychological, social, and cultural factors [2, 3].

Globally, the COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy rate ranged from 16 to 36% in 2020, with the rate between 20 and 30% in Europe and the United States and exceeding 50% in many African countries [11–15].

Beyond socio-demographic and contextual factors, psycho-behavioral factors play a critical role in the continuum of vaccine hesitancy. The psycho-behavioral model, such as the health belief model (HBM), the theory of planned behavior (TPB), the 5 C psychological antecedents of vaccination, and vaccine conspiracy belief (VCB), might have explained the COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy (Fig. 1) [16–20].Fig. 1A conceptual frame work showing COVID-19 Vaccine Hesitancy and its predictor

According to the HBM, individuals’ perception of disease severity, susceptibility, benefit of vaccination, and barrier to access influence their decision to vaccinate [11, 20–23]. TPB emphasizes intention and social influence, suggesting vaccine hesitancy is influenced by the individual attitude towards vaccination, subjective norms, perceived behavioral control, and anticipated regret [20, 23, 24]. The 5 C model identifies five psychological antecedents: confidence (trust in vaccine and health authorities), complacency (belief COVID-19 is not a serious threat), constraints (practical barriers), calculation (deliberative cost–benefit analysis), and collective responsibility (willingness to protect others), which influence COVID-19 hesitancy [11, 23, 24]. The VCB, on the other hand, highlights the impact of misinformation and distrust, including the belief that the virus was deliberately created, the vaccine contains microchips, pharmaceutical companies are profiting from the pandemic, and the vaccine is a tool for population control [25–27].

Identifying which of these models best predicts COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy is essential for designing targeted, evidence-based interventions, especially in the post-pandemic era. However, limited data are available in Ethiopia. Therefore, this study assessed the level of COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy and compared the predictive power of four psycho-behavioral models among adults in southwest Ethiopia.

Objective

This study aimed to assess COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy and to identify the most predictive psycho-behavioral model among the four theoretical frameworks: the Health Belief Model, Theory of Planned Behavior, Psychological Antecedents, and Vaccine Conspiracy Beliefs among adults living in Southwest Ethiopia.

Methods and material

Study area and period

The study was done in Bench-Sheko zone, located in the Southwest Ethiopian Peoples Regional State. The zone is administratively divided in to eight unit; two town administration and six district administrations. The total population was 681,549. The zone is served by one teaching and referral hospital, one district hospital, 26 health centers and 128 health posts (Fig. 2). This study was done from January 1 to 15, 2022.Fig. 2Area map

Area map

Study design

A community-based analytical cross-sectional study was done.

Source population and study population

The source populations were all adults who had been living in the Bench-Sheko zone for at least six months prior to the study. The study population included adult people living in zone during the study period.

Eligibility criteria

Adult aged 18 years and above who had resided in the Bench-Sheko zone for the last six month prior to the study were eligible to participate.

Inclusion criteria and exclusion criteria

All adult individuals living in selected households aged ≥ 18 years were included in the study. Individuals who were critically ill were excluded from the study.

Sample size determination

The sample size was calculated using Open-Epi software, based on an estimated population mean of COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy standard deviation (SD) as 2.99 derived from a pilot study from pilot study. Sampling technique

A multistage sampling technique was employed. All eight administrative unit in the Bench-Sheko zones were included. Within each district and town administration, 30% of the kebeles (the lowest legal administrative unit in Ethiopia) were randomly selected. Using the kebele family folder, a complete list of households was compiled, Households were then randomly selected based on proportional allocation from each kebele. From each selected household, one adult was randomly chosen and interviewed.

Data collection methods and materials

The data were collected using a structured interviewer-administered questionnaire; which was adapted from validated instrument used in previous studies and modified to fit the local context [28–30]. The questionnaire was composed of four parts; Part 1 socio-demographic information. Part 2: COVID-19 related covariate. Part: 3 psycho-behavioral models. Part 4 COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy scale.

Data quality control

The data collection tool was first prepared in English, then translated to Amharic and retranslated to the original language to ensure consistency. The tool was pretested on 5% of the sample size in neighboring district not included in the main study. Data collector were health professional familiar with the local context; they were trained on the objective of the study, ethical consideration, and interview techniques. Also, supervisors monitored the data collection daily to ensure completeness.

Measurements and variables

Dependent variable: COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy

Vaccine hesitancy was measured using two items rated on a 6-point Likert-type scale. First, respondents were asked whether they had already received the COVID-19 vaccine. Based on their vaccination status, the first hesitancy scale was tailored. For the vaccinated individual, the item assessed their initial reaction prior to receiving the vaccine. For the unvaccinated individual, the item measured their current intention to receive the vaccine. Both versions used the same response scale (0 = Surely, I will take it; 1 = Most likely, I will take it; 2 = I will delay taking it; 3 = I am not sure what I will do; 4 = Most likely, I will not take it; 5 = Surely, I will not take it). The second item assessed the individual decision process regarding the vaccination of their families or friends (0 = Strongly encouraged; 1 = Encouraged; 2 = Ask them to delay receiving the vaccine; 3 = I will not say anything about it; 4 = Discourage them to receive the vaccine; and 5 = Forbid them to receive the vaccine) [28].

A composite vaccine hesitancy score was calculated by summing the responses of participant for the two-item hesitancy scale. The possible total score ranges from 0 to 10, which referred to as the COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy spectrum, with a score 0 indicating complete acceptance of the vaccine, and a score 10 indicating total refusal. Scores between 1 and 9 referred to as the vaccine hesitancy continuum indicates varying level of hesitancy with higher score indicating greater hesitancy. The vaccine hesitancy continuum was further sub divided to stratify the hesitancy level; score 1–3 is low hesitancy, score 4–6 is mild or ambivalent, and score 7–9 is high hesitancy level.

Predictor variables: the psycho behavioral predictors

The HBM Constructs: measured on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly Disagree to 5 = Strongly Agree) for the following components: perceived susceptibility (which included two items), perceived severity (which included two items), perceived benefits (which included three items), perceived barriers (which included five items), and cues to action (which included self-reported COVID-19-positive status for self and family members).

The TPB Constructs: it consisted of four domains: attitudes toward vaccines (including six items), subjective norms, perceived behavioral control, and anticipated regret. Each item of the four domains is assessed on a 5-point scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree).

The 5 C Psychological Antecedents of Vaccination: It consisted of 5 antecedents: confidence (which included three items), complacency (which included three items), constraints (which included a single item), calculation (which included three items), and collective responsibility (which included two items). Each item of the five antecedent domains will be assessed on a 5-point scale (1 = strongly disagree; 5 = strongly agree). Except for the constraint domain, a higher score indicates stronger agreement in the corresponding domain.

The vaccine conspiracy belief: measured using four Likert scale constructs; The virus is deliberate, the vaccine contains microchips, the vaccine producing company were profiting, the vaccine is used secretly to control the world’s population.

COVID-19 related covariables

Socio-demographic characteristics: age, sex, marital status, religion, educational status, occupational status, average monthly income, and place of residence.

Knowledge about COVID-19 infection: a 27 binary response (yes = 1, no = 0) questions response summed, score ranged from 1 to 27, a higher score indicating better knowledge [28].

Knowledge about COVID-19 vaccines: Ten binary response (yes = 1, no = 0) questions response summed, total score ranged 0—10, with a higher score indicating greater knowledge [28].

Knowledge about the vaccination process: eight binary response (yes = 1, no = 0) questions response summed, total score ranges 0–8, a higher score indicating better knowledge [28].

Data processing and analysis

The data were entered into Epi-Data version 4.2.0 and analyzed in SPSS. Descriptive statistics such as means ± standard deviations (SD) and percentages were calculated. Normality, linearity, homoscedasticity, and multicollinearity were diagnosed. In the hierarchal multiple regression analysis; in step 1, socio-demographic and COVID-19-related covariates were entered, the HBM construct in step 2, the TPB construct in step 3, and the 5 C construct entered in step 4 and final the CT constructed. The model with the largest R square was considered the best model.

Discussion

According to the score from the COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy scale, 91 (11%) of the participants were fully willing to accept the COVID-19 vaccine, while 97 (11.8%) were completely resistant. The remaining 624 (77.18%) participants were hesitant to make a decision about vaccination. Among those hesitant, 179 (28.14%) individuals demonstrated low hesitancy, 249 (39.15%) showed moderate or ambivalent hesitancy, and 208 (32.7%) exhibited a high level of hesitancy.

Despite the socio-demography, geography, and methodological differences in how hesitancy levels were measured, the current finding shows relatively higher level of vaccine hesitancy compared to previous reports [11, 28, 31, 32]. In addition, the current study was done while the pandemic was on the verge of containment and COVID-19 immunization camping were under way in Ethiopia.

Based on the hierarchical linear regression analysis, after controlling for socio-demographic and COVID-19-related covariates, the VCB model better explained the variance in COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy compared to the HBM, TPB, or 5 C models. This finding suggests that misinformation and disinformation were stronger predictors of vaccine hesitancy withinthe study population. This result contrasts with finding from Bangladesh, where the TPB model accounted for COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy more effectively, followed by the 5 C and HBM models [28]. One possible explanation for this discrepancy could be the previous study did not assess vaccine conspiracy belief, and also the outcome variable, the COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy, in the current study was the continuum between full acceptance and complete refusal, while the previous study included the acceptance and refusal as hesitant.

The final model of the hierarchical regression analysis showed significant predictors of COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy in each block of variables. Within the HBM, perceived susceptibility of COVID-19 infection reduced COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy, whereas an increase in perceived barriers to the vaccination increased hesitancy. These findings align with similar research in Bangladesh, although cues to action were not emerged as significant predictors in the current study [28].

From the TPB constructs, higher perceived behavioral control regarding vaccination and subjective norm significantly increases COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy. This result is in line with similar study done in Bangladesh [28].

Similarly, from the 5 C psychological antecedents, increased confidence and greater calculation of its risk and benefits significantly reduced hesitancy. While increased constraint on the vaccination increased hesitancy level, the complacency and collective responsibility did not predict COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy. This finding is also in line with similar studies performed in Bangladesh [28].

In the VCB models, the conspiracy belief in the COVID-19 infection and the COVID-19 vaccine significantly increased hesitancy. This result underscore the importance of actively debunking misinformation and false claims to counteract the spread of vaccine- and disease-related conspiracy theories [25].

Limitation of the study

Although this study is the first of its kind to measure vaccine hesitancy as a continuum between full acceptance and outright refusal and to compare the predictive power of the four psycho-behavioral models in explaining COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy, some potential limitations may affect the generalizability of its findings. However, mitigation efforts such as accounting for non-response rate and using standardized tools reduced non-response bias and social desirability bias. Moreover, since the current study was done in one zone, generalizability needs caution. In addition, stratified analysis of vaccine hesitancy may help to identify predictors that are more important at each hesitancy zone.

Conclusion

To reduce the COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy in the study area and similar settings, behavioral interventions should focus on counteracting conspiracy theories and misinformation related to both the infection and the vaccine and psychological antecedents of vaccination, such as building confidence of the community regarding the effectiveness and safety of the COVID-19 vaccine, removing practical constraints of vaccination, and supporting weighing the risk and benefit of getting vaccinated. In addition, from the TPB, perceived behavioral control and vaccination are social norms, and from the HBM construct, communicating susceptibility and barriers to vaccination should be addressed through tailored and effective health communication.
