Impact of smoking cessation on cardiovascular outcomes in patients with myocardial infarction with nonobstructive coronary arteries
Authors: Side Gao, Sizhuang Huang, Xinming Liu, Mengyue Yu, Lin Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Nearly 3 in 5 patients with MINOCA were current smokers at their index MI.•More than half of patients continued to smoke within 1 year after MINOCA.•Current smokers with MINOCA had worse outcomes compared to non-smokers.•Smoking cessation was associated with a reduced risk of MACE after MINOCA.

Introduction

Smoking is a strong but preventable risk factor for cardiovascular disease (CVD) 1. It accounts for 10 % CVD-related deaths worldwide and still causes a heavy burden of cardiovascular (CV) risk despite the efforts on tobacco control [2], [3]. To date, smoking cessation as a health priority has been highly recommended to improve cardiovascular health both in primary and secondary prevention settings [4], [5]. However, some earlier studies reported the confusing data regarding the “smokers’ paradox”, that is, smokers with acute myocardial infarction (AMI) had a lower mortality rate than non-smokers in the short term [6], [7]. This so-called paradox is likely due to the difference in age and misclassification of smoking habits. Indeed, the non-smokers were almost 10 years older than smokers in many cohorts. The possibility that ex-smokers were mislabeled as never smoked may also lead to the seemingly higher rates of event for non-smokers, indicating that such paradox is not really evident in AMI [8], [9]. Smoking is undeniably harmful for CV health in the long term. Emerging data have reported a dose-response relationship between smoking intensity and the CVD risk 10. Recent studies further confirmed that smoking significantly increased the risk of CV events in patients with AMI, and smoking cessation was associated with improved outcomes after AMI [11], [12], [13], [14].

Despite the solid evidence for harmful effects of smoking in coronary artery disease (CAD), the implication of smoking in a distinct population with myocardial infarction with nonobstructive coronary arteries (MINOCA) remains unclear. MINOCA occurs in 5 % to 10 % of patients with AMI and has been increasingly recognized due to the widespread use of coronary angiography 15. Although these patients are younger and have no significant obstruction in coronary arteries, their outcomes are not necessarily benign given the considerable risk of adverse events observed after MINOCA 16–17. As many patients continued to smoke after cardiac attack, the importance of smoking cessation in MINOCA should be highlighted for outcome improvement. To date, it is unknown if such a paradox may exist in MINOCA and smoking quitters may have a reduced CV risk. To address this issue, we aimed to assess the prevalence of cigarette use and its prognostic impact in MINOCA. We hypothesize that current smokers may have poorer outcomes than that of non-smokers following MINOCA, while smoking cessation reduces the risk of future CV events as compared to continued smoking.

Methods

Study population

As previously reported, we conducted a prospective and observational cohort study of MINOCA derived from a single center 18. Patients were diagnosed with MINOCA if they met the 4th universal definition of AMI 19 with absence of obstructive CAD confirmed by angiography results showing a coronary artery stenosis of <50 % 15. Individuals who presented symptoms mimicking AMI with non-coronary related reasons for myocardial injury (e.g., acute heart failure) were excluded. Patients with missing data of cigarette consumption and those who lost to follow-up were excluded. After thorough screening, a total of 1179 eligible MINOCA patients were enrolled at Fuwai hospital over the period from January 2015 to December 2019 (Fig.1). Patients were stratified as non-, former- and current smokers based on their smoking status at baseline. Current smokers at index MI were further classified as persistent smokers or smoking quitters. Patients with or without ST-segment elevation (STEMI or NSTEMI) were routinely prescribed guideline-directed medications, including dual anti-platelet therapy, statins, renin-angiotensin-aldosteron system inhibitors and β-blockers unless there were contraindications. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Fuwai hospital and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Data collection and definitions

Patients’ baseline profiles were retrieved via a comprehensive review of electronic medical records. Fasting biochemical tests including blood glucose, lipid profiles and high-sensitive C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) were performed. Peak cardiac troponin I (TnI) and N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) at admission were recorded. Left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was measured using the biplane Simpson method by echocardiography. Hypertension was defined by repeated blood pressure of ≥140/90 mmHg, a past history, or were on antihypertensive medications. Diabetes mellitus (DM) was identified if patients had a fasting blood glucose of ≥7.0 mmol/L, a 2-hour plasma glucose of ≥11.1 mmol/L, or known DM. Dyslipidemia was diagnosed based on medical history or the current use of lipid-lowering medications.

Smoking and smoking cessation were exposure variables of this study. Information on self-reported smoking status was obtained at the index hospitalization and at 1 year after MI. Current smoking was defined as a confirmed history of smoking any type or amount of cigarettes within 1 month before admission. For current smokers, smoking duration and daily cigarette consumption were asked. A person’s cumulative smoking exposure was quantified in pack-years (PYs), calculated by multiplying the number of cigarettes smoked per day by the number of years of smoking maintenance. Current smokers at their index hospitalization were further categorized into persistent smoking or smoking cessation group. Persistent smokers reported continued tobacco use within 1 year after their index MI, while quitters reported sustained abstinence from tobacco for at least 3 months in the 1-year follow-up without any documented relapse. Beyond this primary criterion of quitters, a stricter definition of smoking cessation (abstinence ≥6 months or throughout the first year after index MI) was analyzed as a supplement. Continued smokers who reduced daily cigarette consumption by >20 % were considered partial smoking reducers, and a smoking reduction of >75 % was considered substantial reduction. Otherwise, active smokers who reduced the number of cigarettes by <20 % and those who sustained or even increased the number of cigarettes were defined as sustainers. Persistent smokers were further classified as E-cigarette switchers and continued combustible cigarette users. Data on second-hand smoke, cessation counselling, and interventions (e.g., nicotine replacement therapy or non-nicotine medications) were not adequately captured and thus were not included in the analysis. Although smokers would receive education and personalized advice on smoking cessation as a routine in clinical practice before discharge, the variability in each individual’s counseling intensity and consistency can naturally occur due to the varied resources and patient preferences. Thus, a standardized and uniform counseling approach was not guaranteed for all patients. The smoking behavior questionnaire and STROBE checklist of this study were shown in the Supplementary Materials.

Endpoints and follow-up

The primary endpoint, assessed in a time-to-first-event analysis, was a composite of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), including all-cause death, nonfatal MI, nonfatal stroke, ischemia-driven unplanned revascularization, and hospitalization for unstable angina (UA) or heart failure (HF). The secondary endpoints included each component of MACE and the hard composite endpoints. Reinfarction was defined by the fourth universal definition of AMI 19. Stroke was diagnosed by the presence of neurological dysfunction and cerebrovascular brain injury 20. Revascularization was performed if patients had ischemia and obstructive coronary arteries at the discretion of the operators. Hospitalization for UA with objective evidence of ischemia indicated a high risk of plaque instability and a threat to the patients’ CV health. Hospitalization for HF due to compromised cardiac function, to some extent, reflected deterioration of cardiac status and life quality following the index MI. Patients were actively followed up by means of in-person visits or telephone at 6 months, 1 year, and yearly thereafter until the end of observation period (Dec. 2021). Apart from the hospital records from outpatient revisits and recurrent admissions, we established a regular and well-defined follow-up schedule using a pre-designed structured questionnaire to inquire that if any new events or hospitalizations had occurred since the last contact. All the information were cross-checked and verified by a team of trained researchers. Event adjudication was performed by an independent committee of cardiologists and statisticians blinded to the study design to ensure data accuracy and reliability. Endpoints were evaluated against pre-specified criteria, with discrepancies resolved through consensus.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were compared using Student’s t-test, Mann-Whitney U test, analysis of variance or Kruskal-Wallis H test for continuous variables as appropriate and Pearson’s χ2 or Fisher’s test for categorical variables. Kaplan-Meier curves were depicted to illustrate the cumulative hazard of MACE, and a log-rank test was used to assess the differences. Survival analyses based on univariable and multivariable Cox regression models were conducted to explore the impact of baseline smoking status (current, ex- or non-smokers), cumulative smoking exposure (PYs ≥10 or <10), and smoking cessation (continued or quit smoking) on the risk of MACE. The hazard ratio (HR) and its 95 % confidence interval (CI) were computed. The clinically relevant and unevenly distributed covariates were parsimoniously selected in the multivariable Cox model to ensure statistical adequacy, including age, sex, MI classification (NSTEMI or STEMI), LVEF, hsCRP, hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia. These covariates may generally reflect the overall risk profiles. The other covariates such as peak TnI, NT-proBNP values, and medication were comparable among the groups and were not included. Yet, some potential confounders such as exercise, dietary habit and lifestyles were not measured. Additionally, an inverse probability of weighting (IPW) analysis between persistent smoker and quitters was conducted to address baseline imbalances and examine the robustness of results from Cox regression. A subgroup analysis was performed to evaluate the effect of smoking cessation in various subsets of patients. The impact of smoking cessation duration, partial smoking reduction and switching to E-cigarettes were also analyzed. Restricted cubic splines (RCS) analyses were used to show how the risk of MACE altered with the accumulated smoking amount in current smokers. The reference value, with a HR of 1, was defined at the median level of PYs. The four knots were set at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of PYs. All the analyses were conducted using SPSS (version 22.0) and R Software (version 4.0.2), and statistical significance was declared if two-tailed P < 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics

There were 283 (24.0 %) non-smokers, 186 (15.7 %) ex-smokers, and 710 (60.2 %) current smokers at baseline. For current smokers, more than half of them continued to smoke (54.5 %) while the others quit smoking within 1 year after MINOCA (Fig.1). The mean age of the study cohort was 55.7 ± 11.8 years and 867 patients (73.5 %) were men. As shown in Table 1, current cigarette users were younger, more often male, and more likely to present with STEMI when compared to non- and ex-smokers. Current smokers had lower LVEF and higher hs-CRP, while non-smokers had higher levels of FBG and HDL-C. The prevalence of comorbidities and in-hospital medication did not differ significantly among the groups. Smoking status and clinical outcomes

A total of 168 patients developed MACE (18 died, 41 suffered reinfarction, 12 had stroke, 46 had revascularization, 71 were hospitalized for UA and 48 for HF) during the median follow-up of 41.7 months (Table 2). Current smokers had a higher rate of MACE compared to that of non- and ex-smokers (8.8 %, 11.2 %, 17.1 % in non-, ex-, and current smokers, respectively; p = 0.001). The rate of the composite hard endpoints also increased in current smokers. The Kaplan-Meier curves further revealed a higher cumulative incidence of MACE in current smokers (log rank p = 0.004) (Fig.2A). At Cox analysis, current smoking was significantly associated with an increased risk of MACE as compared to non-smokers after multivariable adjustment (HR 1.53; 95 % CI: 1.22–1.89, p < 0.001), while former smokers had a comparable risk of MACE with that of non-smokers (Table 3). Current smokers with cumulative exposure of ≥10 PYs had a higher risk of MACE than smokers with exposure of <10 PYs (HR 1.71; 95 % CI: 1.21–2.42, p = 0.001). The RCS model showed the dose-response relationship between PYs and risk of MACE in current smokers (Fig.3). Although a slight curvilinear trend was observed, the test indicated a linear association (p < 0.001 for association; p = 0.472 for non-linearity). Interestingly, the risk of MACE was relatively flat until around 11 PYs and then started to increase rapidly afterwards, suggesting a potential threshold effect between accumulated smoking amount and The effect of smoking cessation on clinical outcomes

Individuals who continued smoking within 1 year after index MI had a significantly higher MACE rate than that of quitters (20.6 % vs. 13.0 %, p = 0.007; log rank p = 0.006) (Supplementary Table S2, Fig.2B). At multivariable Cox model, persistent smoking was associated with an increased risk of MACE compared to smoking cessation (HR 1.70; 95 % CI: 1.17–2.47, p = 0.005) (Table 3). This association remained significant across various subgroups stratified by the demographics and comorbidities except for those aged <60 years (Fig.4). Due to the limited event counts, no significant differences were observed in the risk of each hard endpoint (e.g., death, MI, or stroke) between continued smokers and quitters, although there was a tendency. However, the analyses on the composite hard endpoints were consistent with the primary analysis of MACE (Supplementary Table S3). Smoking cessation continued to show significant benefits in the IPW analysis (Supplementary Table S4). Sensitivity analyses showed that the number of quitters was lower with a stricter definition for cessation, yet the benefits of smoking cessation became more prominent as the duration of abstinence increased (Supplementary Table S5). Further, substantial smoking reduction, but not mild or moderate reduction, was associated with a reduced risk of MACE. Discussion

In the present study, we showed that current smokers had a poorer prognosis than that of non-smokers in patients with MINOCA. Smoking cessation was significantly associated with a reduced risk of MACE when compared to continued smoking. The effect of smoking cessation remained consistent in subgroup and sensitivity analyses, and a longer cessation duration conferred a lower risk. If it is not possible, substantial smoking reduction and switching to E-cigarettes might serve as an alternative strategy for risk reduction in MINOCA patients. Yet, the small sample size and event numbers may limit the statistical power and the results should be verified by future studies. In sum, our findings expand insights into the detrimental effects of smoking in MINOCA and reinforce the necessity of smoking cessation for improved cardiovascular health.

MINOCA represents a heterogeneous clinical entity and its underlying mechanisms may include plaque rupture, plaque erosion, vasospasm, embolization, dissection and microvascular dysfunction [15], [16], [17]. Thus, MINOCA is not a single disease but a working diagnosis with a collection of diverse causes, and it should serve as a starting point for discovery but not an endpoint. The diagnostic workflow (e.g., multi-modality imaging and functional testing beyond angiography) and tailored, etiology-driven therapies are essential for outcome improvement in the contemporary management of MINOCA 15. Despite their younger age, less comorbidities and seemingly normal coronary arteries, the prognosis of MINOCA has raised growing concerns as several studies reported a considerably high risk of adverse events after MINOCA [21], [22], [23]. In our cohort, 14.2 % of patients suffered a MACE despite the evidence-based optimal therapies. Some studies even showed a similar prognosis between patients with MINOCA and obstructive MI [22], [23], suggesting that MINOCA should not be trivialized and deserve enhanced focus. Alarmingly, we noted the critical issue of smoking in MINOCA patients. Almost 3 in 5 were current smokers with a median cumulative exposure of 11 PYs, and only 45 % of them successfully quit smoking within 1 year after index MI. It is reported that the rate of current smoking ranged from 15 % to 50 % in different populations with CAD, and this rate was higher in younger and male patients [6], [7], [8], [9], [10], [11], [12], [13], [14]. Approximately 50 % to 70 % of patients continued to smoke after AMI since smoking cessation at once is not easy in real practice [11], [12], [13], [14]. These data indicate an urgent need to address smoking as a major risk factor for this specific population.

The long-term CV risk associated with smoking has been confirmed both in general population and in patients with established CAD, including stable CAD, AMI and all-comers undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) [24], [25], [26], [27], [28], [29]. Smoking can induce endothelial dysfunction, enhance inflammation and atherogenesis, promote a prothrombotic status and lead to plaque vulnerability [30], [31]. These harmful effects as mediated by tobacco substances like nicotine largely offset the benefits of optimal medical treatment and PCI. However, earlier studies reported the “smoker’s paradox”, that is, smoking was a predictor of more favorable short-term outcomes after AMI [6], [7], [8]. In these cohorts, non-smokers were nearly 10 years older and had more comorbidities than current smokers. The likelihood that former smoking was misclassified as never smoking also explained such a paradox. It was often observed in the thrombolytic era, whereas no studies of a contemporary population with AMI have found evidence for this phenomenon 8. Hence, it is more likely a misleading appearance caused by bias rather than an evident fact in AMI. Recent data have confirmed that smoking was a strong predictor of adverse outcomes after PCI 9, [27], [28], [29], and current smokers had lower life expectancy and large numbers of life-years lost after AMI [11], [12], [13], [14]. In line with prior studies, we found that current smokers with MINOCA had a significantly higher risk of MACE than that of non-smokers, while ex-smokers had a similar risk of MACE to non-smokers. Given a younger population and comparable baseline profiles between non- and current smokers, this paradox was not observed in our study. Meanwhile, the outcomes were similar between non- and ex-smokers, indicating that the risk of prior smoking may reduce to near baseline levels for those who quit smoking before their index MI. In addition, the risk of MACE increased in parallel with PYs of smoking, and a near-linear association between smoking quantity and MACE risk was observed. Of note, the rise in risk was steeper after surpassing 11 PYs. This potential threshold effect aligns with previous studies showing that light smokers may have a smaller or delayed risk increase, but the risk accelerates sharply with higher cumulative exposure. It is possibly due to that chronic smoking may overwhelm the CV repair mechanisms above a threshold, leading to irreversible vascular damage and plaque instability [30], [31]. These findings confirmed the noxious effects of smoking in MINOCA population.

The benefits of smoking cessation as an important lifestyle modification have been undoubtedly acknowledged. It is highly recommended to quit smoking after AMI as early as possible in order to reduce the risk of future events [4], [5]. A systematic review including 20 studies revealed a 36 % reduction in the crude mortality for patients with CAD who quit smoking compared to continued smokers 24. Recent evidence also supports smoking cessation for improved CV outcomes either in community dwellers or in population with CAD [25], [26], [27], [28], [29]. However, a majority of patients with AMI still chose to smoke or relapsed even after receiving personalized smoking cessation counseling 32. A Korean nationwide study reported that smoking cessation, but not reduction, reduced the risk of incident CVD 33. The excess risk existed regardless of the degree of smoking reduction, and relapsed smokers had an increased risk of CVD compared to those who remained smoke-free 33. In our cohort, we identified a 36.8 % reduction in the rate of MACE for smoking quitters as compared with persistent smokers. The benefits of smoking cessation remained significant across subgroup analysis, and such benefits seemed to be more prominent with a longer cessation duration. Of note, only substantial smoking reduction or near-cessation, but not mild or moderate reduction, was associated with a lower risk of MACE. These results support prior findings that CV risk attenuates substantially after complete and sustained smoking cessation rather than reduced or relapsed smoking. Hence, smoking cessation and maintenance should be emphasized in MINOCA patients. While complete cessation remains the optimal goal for maximum benefit, substantial reduction also confers meaningful advantages and may serves as a critical bridge toward full abstinence. As many patients perceive complete cessation as an insurmountable hurdle, clinicians can motivate them through an intermediate yet beneficial goal, gradually shifting their mindsets from reluctance to engagement with the ultimate goal of complete cessation. This approach highlights a patient-centered paradigm that prioritizes realistic and stepwise progress over rigid, all-or-nothing targets. Of note, the study did not analyze the effect of smoking cessation on non-cardiovascular health. The impact of smoking cessation on each hard endpoints was not statistically significant, likely due to statistically underpowered analyses from low event counts. Thus, interpretations of hard endpoint results should remain cautious, and replication of our findings in more diverse populations is warranted by future multi-ethnic studies with larger samples.

Effective approaches to smoking cessation include behavioral support, counselling, and pharmacological intervention such as nicotine replacement therapy. More recently, E-cigarettes as alternatives of tobacco product have become a popular choice despite the ongoing debates about their safety and efficacy as smoking cessation aids [34], [35]. A recent study revealed that changing from combustible to E-cigarettes was associated with a reduced risk of MACE after PCI, similar to smoking cessation 36. Consistently, we found that smokers who switched to E-cigarettes had a lower risk of MACE when compared to continued combustible cigarette users. Meanwhile, a study reported that dual users of combustible and E-cigarettes had a higher risk of CVD than those who used combustible cigarettes alone if they did not decrease cigarettes consumption 37. Another study showed that E-cigarette use (either nicotine-containing or nicotine-free) did not offer superior CV benefits compared to routine behavior support 38. Therefore, it should be noted that no level of smoking is truly safe, and cessation remains the most effective strategy to mitigate harm. In daily practice, the best lifestyle change for smokers with CAD is to stop smoking entirely without any tobacco supply. However, if this is not possible, it may be worth considering switching to E-cigarettes to reduce the use of combustible cigarettes. Future randomized controlled trials are needed to determine the long-term effects of E-cigarettes on CV outcomes in MINOCA patients.

Limitation

The present study had several limitations. First, the status and amount of smoking were self-reported without objective measures of nicotine intake. The recall bias and misclassification may exist and potentially affect the accuracy of our results. Also, we did not obtain information on the exposure to second-hand smoke, smoking cessation counseling and medications. Likewise, we cannot ensure identical counseling for each patient in both continued smoking and cessation groups as this was an observational study and the counseling may have varied. The variations by different counselors and unequal access to specialized services (e.g., pharmacotherapy) may lead to substantial discrepancies in smoking cessation outcomes. Second, smoking status was checked at two timepoints while the real-time changes of smoking were not captured. By limiting cessation to within 1-year period, we cannot rule out the possibility that some patients may quit smoking at a later point and those who stopped smoking may have relapsed at a later time. Each of these scenarios would bias the results. Besides, we used PYs to estimate cumulative exposure, but this method cannot differentiate long-term smoking at low doses from short-term smoking at high doses. Third, the potential unmeasured confounders such as lifestyle and behavioral factors may influence the results due to the observational study design. The effect of smoking cessation can be overestimated despite multivariable adjustment and sensitivity analyses. Fourth, we mainly analyzed the composite endpoint, while the analysis on each hard endpoint may be insufficient for statistical significance given the limited event number. Future research with large samples is warranted to offset this limitation. Fifth, the exact mechanism of MINOCA was not adequately captured for all participants given the low usage of intravascular imaging and cardiac magnetic resonance imaging. The role of smoking in different phenotypes of MINOCA needs to be further clarified. Finally, our findings may not be representative as the cohort was derived from a single center and primarily enrolled a homogeneous population. Future multicenter studies in ethnically diverse populations are essential to validate our results and show how racial, demographic, and biological factors may modify the impact of smoking on outcomes in MINOCA patients.

Conclusions

By contrast to the “smoker’s paradox” observed in AMI, current smokers with MINOCA had a significantly higher risk of adverse events compared to non-smokers despite optimal medical therapies. Smoking cessation was associated with a reduction in the risk of MACE compared with those who continued smoking after the index MI, reinforcing the necessity of smoking cessation as a fundamental measure of improved clinical outcomes for patients with MINOCA.
