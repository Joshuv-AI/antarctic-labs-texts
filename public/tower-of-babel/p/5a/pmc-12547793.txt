Effect of Maleic Anhydride and Glyceryl Methacrylate Cografted Reclaimed Rubber on Improving the Performance of Styrene–Butadiene Rubber/Silica
Authors: Hu Chen, Jiuming Liang, Zhu Luo, Juqiao Su, Hao Zhang, Ruhui He, Jikai Xie, Shenglong Yang, Yehan Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Thermoplastic desulfurized reclaimed rubber is a low-cost and environmentally friendly material. However, its complex composition and severely reduced molecular weight can impair the performance of rubber products when blended with other rubbers. Silica is currently the primary filler for producing green tires, owing to its environmentally friendly comprehensive performance. However, achieving uniform dispersion of high silica content in nonpolar rubbers, such as styrene–butadiene rubber (SBR), remains challenging owing to the strong interactions among polar silica particles. Herein, desulfurized natural reclaimed rubber (NRR) was modified by simultaneous grafting with glyceryl methacrylate and maleic anhydride, which can form significantly stronger interactions with polar silica via hydrogen bonding, improving the processing performance and mechanical properties. Compared to ungrafted SBR/silica/NRR composites, the grafted SBR/silica/NRR composites improved the tensile strength (15.8%), elongation at break (33.8%), tear strength (2.4%), and modulus at 100% elongation (3.3%) while reducing fatigue heat generation. Through this cografting modification, the obviously reduced elongation at break caused by the addition of unmodified reclaimed rubber was highly improved. This modification strategy enables high-value reuse of reclaimed rubber.

Introducion

Desulfurized reclaimed rubber refers to thermoplastic recycled rubber produced from waste vulcanized rubber products through high-temperature and -shear desulfurization treatment. − This reclaimed rubber can be reused to manufacture rubber products. The use of reclaimed desulfurized rubber exhibits significant economic benefits and mitigates environmental harm (incineration or landfilling) caused by waste vulcanized rubber, making it highly significant for environmental protection. − Silica-filled rubber tires exhibit characteristics such as low rolling resistance, antiskidding, and less pollution. The development of green tires with silica as a filler is a high scientific and technological advancement in the tire industry; therefore, rubber composites filled with silica have become a research hotspot by leveraging the synergistic of both materials, combining reclaimed rubber with silica, and it is possible to produce economical and eco-friendly green composites. However, because the source of reclaimed rubber is mainly waste rubber products, such as waste tape, hose, and tires and the scraps of other vulcanized rubber products, the composition of reclaimed rubber is complex. Moreover, the nonselective destruction of the molecular chain of reclaimed rubber during desulfurization significantly reduces the molecular weight of rubber. Based on these factors, the performance of rubber composites blended with reclaimed rubber declines, posing challenges for researchers attempting to utilize large quantities of desulfurized reclaimed rubber. Meanwhile, the use of highly filled silica may present challenges such as poor dispersion of the filler within the matrix and even agglomeration issues. Studies indicate that silica should be evenly distributed within the rubber matrix to obtain optimal overall properties. ,

Modifying rubber is a common method to enhance its performance. Li et al. successfully prepared NR-g-GMA/GO composites by grafting glycidyl methacrylate (GMA) onto natural rubber. Compared with NR/GO composites, this material exhibited superior tensile strength and elongation at break. However, the modification led to varying degrees of reduction in tear strength and could not effectively improve the dispersion of silica. Improving the compatibility between components is the main method for enhancing the dispersion of polymer blends. Maleic anhydride (MA) grafts are widely used to improve the compatibility between polar and nonpolar components. Sahakaro and Beraheng grafted NR onto MA to enhance the filler–rubber compatibility through the hydrogen-bonding interactions between anhydride groups and silica surfaces. The incorporation of MA improved the dispersion of silica within the rubber matrix; however, it concurrently reduced the material’s elongation at break. Luo et al. added fumed silica to epoxidized NR (ENR) and discovered that the dispersion of silica in ENR was better than that in conventional NR. Wang et al. reported the presence of hydrogen bonds and chemical interactions between ENR and silica.

This study innovatively utilized the low viscosity and easy-grafting characteristics of the NRR to simultaneously graft both GMA and MA onto NRR molecular chains. The cografted NRR was then blended with the SBR matrix, producing high-silica-content SBR composites with excellent comprehensive properties. Compared with other singly grafted NRR composites, the cografted NRR composites exhibited improvements in all mechanical properties and overcame the drawback of simultaneous reductions in tensile elongation and tear strength. Additionally, they significantly enhanced rubber–silica interfacial bonding, improved silica dispersion in the SBR matrix, and increased the silica incorporation ratio. This method not only solved the mechanical performance degradation problem caused by adding reclaimed rubber (which previously limited its practical applications) but also improved composite processing performance through enhancing silica dispersion. The cografted NRR-modified SBR/silica composites provide a new strategy for improving the blending quality and properties, thereby facilitating the high-quality application of desulfurized reclaimed rubber.

Experiments

Materials

SBR (type: 1500e) was obtained from PetroChina Lanzhou Petrochemical Company. Silica (industrial grade, particle size 48 μm) was supplied by Zhuzhou Xinglong New Material Co., Ltd. NRR supplied by Guizhou Tyre Co., Ltd. (China) exhibited low viscosity, and its Mooney viscosity could not be measured. Natural latex (NL, 60% solid content) was purchased from China Jinan Xinhe Chemical Co., Ltd. Si69 was supplied by Hangzhou Jexika Chemical Co., Ltd. MA (grade: analytical reagent) was supplied by Tianjin Shengxinyuan Weiye Co., Ltd. Dibenzoyl peroxide (BPO, grade: analytical reagent) was supplied by Maoming Runjing Chemical Co., Ltd. GMA (grade: analytical reagent) was supplied by Guangdong Wengjiang Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. All reagents were used as received without further purification.

Sample Preparation

Silica Modification

Drying of Silica and Hydrolysis of Si69

Silica (300 g) was placed on an iron dish and dried in an oven at 80 °C for 12 h. Then, 6 g of Si69 was hydrolyzed with 20 g of deionized water and 180 g of anhydrous ethanol in a beaker for 12 h (three groups were prepared). The hydrolyzed Si69 was stored in a dark place at room temperature.

Coupling and Covering Treatment of Silica

Dried silica (300 g) was evenly divided into three parts of 100 g each, which were then placed in three beakers. An equal amount of hydrolyzed Si69 was added to each beaker. The mixture was subsequently combined with a specific amount of deionized water and anhydrous ethanol under continuous stirring until an emulsion was formed and then was placed in an ultrasonic machine (model 230HTD, Henan Huayi Technology Co., Ltd.) at 24 °C for 15 min, and the three groups of the experimental materials were transferred into a high-speed mixer machine (model SHR-10A, Zhangjiagang City YuSheng Mechanical and Electrical Equipment Manufacturing Factory) under the conditions of 80 °C and 200 r min–1 for 3 h. A small amount of silica treated with a coupling agent was washed with deionized water using an ultrasonic machine and then dried for spectral analysis. Wang et al. found that coating silica with NL could enhance its dispersion within the rubber matrix, significantly improving the interfacial compatibility between silica and rubber and strengthening the interfacial bonding and mechanical strength of the silica–rubber blend system. NL (250 g, solid content 60% diluted in a certain amount of deionized water before addition) was mixed with treated silica for 30 min. The mixture was poured into an iron tray and dried in an oven at 80 °C for 12 h. This mixture was then evenly divided into three parts to obtain three groups of modified silica, denoted as SN-Silica.

Grafted Reclaimed Rubber

NRR (50 g) was placed in a torque rheometer (model XSS-500, Shanghai Kechuang Rubber & Plastics Machinery Equipment Co., Ltd.) at 130 °C and 80 r min–1. Then, 2 g of BPO, 3.5 g of GMA, and 3.5 g of MA were added to 10 g of toluene. The toluene solution was then drizzled into the torque rheometer using a straw, allowing it to mix with NRR for 3 min to obtain grafted NRR. The possible reaction for the simultaneous grafting of MA and GMA onto the NRR is presented in Figure . The peroxide initiator decomposes to generate free radicals that react with the rubber molecular chains of MA and GMA. This process produces macromolecular free radicals and opens the CC bonds in MA and GMA, leading to the simultaneous grafting of MA and GMA onto the rubber molecular chain.

Possible reaction of MA and GMA cografted NRR.

Purification of Grafted Reclaimed Rubber

An appropriate amount of cografted NRR was weighed and placed in a 200 mL beaker. A specific amount of xylene was added to the mixture in the beaker, which was heated using an oil bath under stirring at 60 °C for 3 h. The solution was filtered and cooled to 24 °C. Next, a specific amount of methanol was added to the filtrate. The obtained brown solid precipitate was washed with deionized water to obtain the purified grafted NRR, which was used for infrared characterization experiments.

Preparation of Composites

Preparation of Masterbatch

SBR (100 g) was crushed into small pieces and placed into a torque rheometer at 130 °C and 80 r min–1 and was plasticized for 2 min, followed by the addition of stearic acid and zinc oxide (supplied by Guizhou Tyre Co., Ltd.) for 2 min. Then, SN-Silica was added, with either NRR or cografted NRR. The mixture was mixed for 7 min to obtain two types of masterbatch: SBR/SN-Silica/NRR and SBR/SN-Silica/G3.5M3.5-NRR.

SBR/SN-Silica was prepared by processing 150 g of SBR in a torque rheometer under the same conditions, plasticizing for 2 min, followed by the addition of stearic acid and zinc oxide for 2 min. Subsequently, SN-Silica was added this time and mixed for 7 min, yielding one group of masterbatch: SBR/SN-Silica.

Preparation of Rubber Composites

The masterbatch from Section was placed in a two-roll mill (model XK-160-A, Fujian Yongchun Light Industry Machinery Factory) and mixed for 1–2 min, followed by the successive addition of the mentioned additives: accelerator 2-mercaptobenzothiazole (M), 1,3-diphenylguanidine (D), 2,2′-dibenzothiazole disulfide (DM), p-phenylenediamine antioxidant (4010), tetramethylthiuram disulfide (TMTD), and sulfur (S) as vulcanizing agents. All of the above rubber additives were supplied by Guizhou Tyre Co., Ltd. For each rubber additive, the mixture was milled one to two times, with S finally added. The rubber was then milled approximately 7–8 times. The rubber sheet was removed, with each piece having a thickness of ∼2 mm. The unvulcanized rubber was laid flat for 24 h to relieve the internal stress. This process was repeated three times, resulting in three groups of different unvulcanized rubber samples: SBR/SN-Silica, SBR/SN-Silica/NRR, and SBR/SN-Silica/G3.5M3.5-NRR.

Preparation of Vulcanized Rubber Samples

The optimal vulcanization time (tc90) of the unvulcanized rubber was tested using a rheometer (model M2000FAN, High-Speed Iron Testing Instrument Dongguan Co., Ltd.) under the following conditions: temperature (150 °C), frequency (1.67 Hz), test duration (40 min), rotation angle (0.5°), and stable range (0.5 °C). After the test, tc90 was recorded.

Next, the unvulcanized rubber was placed on a heating plate of a pressure vulcanizer (model XLB, 25t, Jiangdu Mingzhu Experimental Machinery Factory) to perform the vulcanization process. The curing conditions were set to a temperature of 150 °C, a pressure of 12 MPa, and a curing time equal to that of tc90. On completion of vulcanization, three groups of vulcanized rubber were obtained: SBR/SN-Silica, SBR/SN-Silica/NRR, and SBR/SN-Silica/G3.5M3.5-NRR. These vulcanized samples were then placed flat for 24 h to ensure that the composites were fully cured and cross-linked.

Finally, the composites were cut into standard tensile samples and right-angle tear samples by using a pneumatic plate punching machine.

For comparative studies with cografted NRR composites, single-grafted NRR control samples were prepared following identical processing procedures, with the only variation being the exclusive addition of either MA or GMA during the grafting process. Accordingly, the GMA single-grafted composites were designated as SBR/SN-Silica/G3.5-NRR, while the MA single-grafted composites were named SBR/SN-Silica/M3.5-NRR.

Experimental Instruments and Tests

Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) Spectroscopy Analysis

To verify the successful modification of silica by Si69 and NRR grafting, attenuated total reflectance FTIR (ATR-FTIR) was performed using a NEXUS670 instrument (alpha Co., Ltd.). The test conditions were set to the ATR mode in the scanning range of 4000–400 cm–1. Then, the samples were scanned 32 times. Silica was dried after washing multiple times, and the grafted NRR was purified before the analysis.

Determination of Static Mechanical Properties

The mechanical properties of the rubber composites were determined by using a universal material testing machine (model WdW-10C, Shanghai Hualong Testing Instrument Co., Ltd.). The tensile samples were in a type 1 dumbbell test piece (ISO 6943:2007), and the tear samples were in a trouser test piece (ISO 34-1:2004). The speed of test used was 500 mm/min, and the average value of five measurements from each group was considered as the final result.

Determination of Dynamic Mechanical Properties

The thermal mechanical properties of the rubber composites were analyzed at different temperatures by using a dynamic thermomechanical analyzer (model Q800, TA Instruments). The test conditions were as mentioned: double cantilever beam mode, a temperature range of −70 to 70 °C, an amplitude of 8 μm, a heating rate of 3 °C min–1, and a frequency of 1 Hz.

Fatigue Cyclic Tensile Test of the Composite

A dynamic fatigue testing machine (model DF-8000-5, Dongguan High-Speed Railway Testing Instrument) was used to test the hysteresis loop area of the rubber composites. Fatigue tests were conducted under displacement control (for hysteresis ring and hysteresis loop area testing). The test conditions for non-notched samples were as mentioned: the maximum displacement was set to 20 mm with a sinusoidal load, an amplitude of 10 mm, a frequency of 5 Hz, and 20000 fatigue cycle stretches.

Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM)

Field-emission SEM (Quanta FEG250, FEI Company, USA) was used to observe the brittle surface morphology of the rubber composites. The samples were immersed in liquid nitrogen for 2 h and then immediately fractured on removal. All samples were gold-coated before scanning, and the experimental voltage was set to 10 kV.

Results and Discussion

Functional Group Analysis of Modification Silica and Grafting NRR

The successful modification of silica by Si69 was verified via an ATR-FTIR analysis. Figure A shows the characteristic peaks representing the Si–O–Si groups at 1061 and 1055 cm–1 in the silica and S-silica curves, respectively. S-silica denotes Si69-modified silica. Additionally, the peak at 1390 and 2971 cm–1 in S-silica corresponds to the Si–C bond and −CH2 stretching vibrations of Si69, indicating that Si69 is successfully grafted onto silica, confirming the successful modification of silica. ,

ATR-FTIR results: (A) FTIR spectra of silica before and after modification and (B) FTIR spectra of NRR before and after grafting. (C) FTIR spectra of the single-grafted NRR.

In Figure B, the GM-NRR curve represents the infrared spectrum of the purified cografted NRR. Figure B shows peaks at 1602 and 835 cm–1 in NRR, corresponding to the CC bond and the vibration absorption peak of C–H of the NR composition in NRR, respectively. The GM-NRR curve also exhibits a characteristic peak at 835 cm–1, while the peak at 1180 cm–1 is attributed to the epoxy group of GMA, indicating that GMA is successfully grafted onto the molecular chain of NRR. Additionally, the peak at 1720 cm–1 in the GM-NRR curve represents the characteristic peak of the carbonyl group of MA, indicating that MA is successfully grafted onto the molecular chain of NRR. This is attributed to the breaking of the CC bonds in GMA and MA molecules, allowing them to be grafted onto the NR molecular chain.

Sahakaro and Beraheng used the ratio of the peak area of the carbonyl groups of MA at 1720 cm–1 to that of NR at 835 cm–1 for an approximate characterization of the grafting rate of MA. Thus, the peak areas in our experiment at the same position presented in Figure B were integrated, yielding a ratio of ∼0.189 for the peak area at 1720 cm–1 to that at 835 cm–1. Sahakaro and Beraheng reported a peak area ratio of 0.19 and a grafting rate of MA at 3.4%. As our obtained ratio (∼0.189) is nearly equal to the reported value, the grafting rate of MA is also estimated to be ∼3.4% in the cografted NRR. The same methodology reported by Wu et al. was used to calculate the ratio of the peak area of GMA at 1180 cm–1 to that of NR at 835 cm–1, which was ∼0.766, and the corresponding grafting rate of GMA was 8.1 ± 0.5%. Through calculation, the ratio of the peak area at 1180 cm–1 to that at 835 cm–1 is ∼0.414 in this study. Based on these data, the grafting rate of GMA in the cografted NRR is estimated to be ∼4.4%.

In Figure C, the G-NRR curve represents the infrared spectrum of purified single-grafting NRR with GMA, while the M-NRR curve corresponds to the infrared spectrum of purified single-grafted NRR with MA. Similar to the cografted NRR, both curves exhibit an absorption peak at 835 cm– 1 attributed to the C–H vibration of NR. The G-NRR curve shows an epoxy absorption peak at 1180 cm– 1, and the M-NRR curve displays an absorption peak at 1720 cm– 1 corresponding to the carbonyl group. These results indicate that both types of single-grafted NRR control groups were also successfully grafted. It can be observed that the peak intensities of the single-grafted NRR are obviously weaker than those of the cografted NRR, indicating that the grafting degree of the cografted NRR is higher than that of the single-grafted NRR. This difference may be attributed to the stabilizing effect resulting from the interaction between MA and GMA.

Analysis of Silica Dispersion in Internal Mixing

The three masterbatches with different formulations (SBR/SN-Silica, SBR/SN-Silica/NRR, and SBR/SN-Silica/G3.5M3.5-NRR) obtained after internal mixing for 7 min in the torque rheometer are presented in Figure .

Masterbatch of different composites: (A) SBR/SN-Silica, (B) SBR/SN-Silica/NRR, and (C) SBR/SN-Silica/G3.5M3.5-NRR.

Figure A shows the overall appearance of the SBR/SN-Silica masterbatch. The white sample resembles silica with noticeable network roughness and holes. Although silica is modified with coupling agents and latex, the result indicates that the mixing quality of SBR/SN-Silica is unsatisfactory as silica is not uniformly dispersed during mixing. Compared to SBR/SN-Silica, Figure B shows that SBR/SN-Silica/NRR significantly reduced roughness, meaning that the dispersion of silica in SBR/SN-Silica/NRR is improved, and the mixing effect is also better than SBR/SN-Silica. However, silica is still not sufficiently mixed. After grafting modification of NRR, the whitening phenomenon of silica-filled SBR/SN-Silica/G3.5M3.5-NRR is absent in Figure C, and the color of the compound is uniform. The surface roughness and hole cracks are more visibly reduced compared to the other two masterbatch samples. These results indicate that the dispersion in SBR/SN-Silica/G3.5M3.5-NRR is significantly enhanced, and the processing performance of this masterbatch is significantly improved.

Vulcanization Characteristics of Rubber Composites

Figure illustrates the vulcanization curves of SBR/SN-Silica, SBR/SN-Silica/NRR, and SBR/SN-Silica/G3.5M3.5-NRR. The vulcanization characteristics of different composites are shown in Table . The results demonstrate that both SBR/SN-Silica/NRR and SBR/SN-Silica/G3.5M3.5-NRR exhibit higher vulcanization rates and maximum torque (MH) values than SBR/SN-silica. This enhancement originates from NRR’s dual composition of natural rubber (NR) with carbon black and residual vulcanization accelerators. The linear molecular chains and abundant double bonds in NR, synergized with residual accelerators, accelerate vulcanization rates in uncured composites. Concurrently, carbon black reinforcement elevates the modulus of NRR-incorporated composites, thereby increasing MH values. Theoretically, an increase in the difference between MH and ML (ΔM) is related to the enhanced stiffness of the composites, which is induced by cross-linking. Compared with SBR/SN-Silica, the ΔM value of SBR/SN-Silica/NRR increased; this is attributed to the addition of NRR with a branched structure, which increases the cross-linking density. Furthermore, the ΔM value of SBR/SN-Silica/G3.5M3.5-NRR showed no significant difference from that of SBR/SN-Silica/NRR. This indicates that grafting NRR does not effectively enhance the cross-linking degree of the composites. Notably, the grafted SBR/SN-Silica/G3.5M3.5-NRR composites display smoother vulcanization curves and marginally higher MH compared to their ungrafted counterpart (SBR/SN-silica/NRR) while also increasing tc90. This phenomenon confirms that cografted NRR enhances rubber–silica dispersion. The improved dispersion allows silica to form a better filler network within the matrix. Moreover, the modified NRR enables the rubber matrix and silica to form a cross-linked network with each other via hydrogen bonding. Under the combined influence of these factors, the modified polymer ultimately achieves an enhanced modulus after vulcanization.

Mechanical Property Analysis of the Rubber Composites

Figure shows the mechanical properties of SBR/SN-Silica, SBR/SN-Silica/NRR, SBR/SN-Silica/G3.5M3.5-NRR, SBR/SN-Silica/G3.5-NRR, and SBR/SN-Silica/M3.5-NRR. Figure A,C–E shows that the tensile strength, tear strength, modulus at 100% elongation, and modulus at 300% elongation of SBR/SN-Silica/NRR increase by 0.87, 9.6, 44.7, and 56.7%, respectively, compared with SBR/SN-Silica. This improvement might be attributed to the complex and diverse composition of NRR, which likely includes components such as carbon black filler and NR. However, as shown in Figure B, the elongation at break of SBR/SN-Silica/NRR significantly decreases from 519.85 to 371.45%, indicating that the large content of ungrafted NRR is not suitable for high comprehensive performance applications, which can be attributed to the low molecular weight of NRR.

Mechanical properties of the different composites: (A) Tensile strength; (B) elongation at break; (C) tear strength; (D) modulus at 100% elongation; and (E) modulus at 300% elongation. 1# SBR/SN-Silica; 2# SBR/SN-Silica/NRR; 3# SBR/SN-Silica/G3.5-NRR; 4# SBR/SN-Silica/M3.5-NRR; 5# SBR/SN-Silica/G3.5M3.5-NRR.

Figure A–D demonstrates that SBR/SN-Silica/G3.5M3.5-NRR exhibits higher tensile strength (15.8%), elongation at break (33.8%), tear strength (2.4%), and modulus at 100% elongation (3.3%) than SBR/SN-Silica/NRR. This enhancement is likely owing to the presence of cografted GMA and MA, which introduce polar anhydride and epoxy groups to the molecular chain of NRR. These groups enhance the interaction between polar silica and nonpolar SBR, adhesion of NRR and SBR/Silica, and uniform dispersion of silica in the SBR matrix.

As clearly illustrated in Figure A–E, compared with SBR/SN-Silica, adding GMA-grafted NRR leads to an obvious increase in elongation at break but reductions in tensile strength, tear strength, and modulus, while adding MA-grafted NRR improves tensile strength, tear strength, and modulus although increase in elongation at break is not obvious. Compared with single-grafted NRR, the cografted NRR significantly enhances the comprehensive properties of the rubber composites and overcomes the performance defects of the rubber composites caused by adding the single-grafted NRR. Most importantly, through the synergistic effect of both GMA and MA in the cografted system, the elongation at break is restored to the original level of the rubber composites with NRR.

Dynamic Mechanical Analysis of the Rubber Composites

Figure A–C illustrates the changes in storage and loss moduli, and loss factor of the different rubber composites within the temperature range of −70 to 70 °C. The glass transition temperatures of the three composites SBR/SN-Silica, SBR/SN-Silica/NRR, and SBR/SN-Silica/G3.5M3.5-NRR are −27.1°, −29°, and −31.3°, respectively.

DMA values of the different composites. Relationship between (A) energy storage modulus, (B) loss modulus, and (C) loss factor and temperature.

Results demonstrate that compared to SBR/SN-Silica, the SBR/SN-Silica/NRR composites exhibit increased storage modulus and loss modulus, along with a decreased glass transition temperature. The increase in storage and loss moduli is attributed to the carbon black filler present in NRR, which enhances modulus upon blending into rubber composites and increases filler content, heightened internal friction of SBR/filler. Additionally, low-viscosity NRR accumulates at the interface, reducing the constraint of silica on the SBR molecular chain. This interaction lowers loss factor and glass transition temperature. ,

After NRR grafting modification, the storage modulus and loss modulus of SBR/SN-Silica/G3.5M3.5-NRR are lower than those of SBR/SN-Silica/NRR, approaching those of SBR/SN-Silica. This reduction likely occurs because grafted low-molecular-weight NRR accumulates at the rubber–silica interface, where it adopts an oriented arrangement, thereby inducing stress relaxation. Moreover, SBR/SN-Silica/G3.5M3.5-NRR exhibits the lowest loss modulus above the glass transition temperature. Here, cografted NRR enriched at filler–rubber interfaces acts as a lubricant, reducing internal friction and consequently lowering the loss modulus, which is conducive to reducing heat generation of the rubber composite during cyclic stress–strain. Furthermore, SN-Silica/G3.5M3.5-NRR shows the lowest glass transition temperature among all samples due to improved silica compatibility from MA and GMA grafted onto the NRR molecular chains.

Hysteresis Loop and Hysteresis Loop Area Analysis of the Rubber Composites

Figure shows the hysteresis loops and hysteresis loop areas of the different rubber composites during the fatigue test. The fatigue test uses displacement control for the non-notched samples with a maximum displacement of 20 mm, sinusoidal loading amplitude of 10 mm, frequency of 5 Hz, and 20,000 cyclic stretching repetitions. The lagging effect occurs during the cyclic stress process. In this process, molecular chains undergo stretching deformation from a coiled state when loading. When the stress is relieved, as the molecular chains need to overcome internal friction to return to the coiled state as a reversible change, the stress and strain are not synchronized during the recovery process, thus forming a hysteresis loop.

Fatigue properties of different composites: (A) hysteresis loops and (B) hysteresis ring areas.

The hysteresis effect of rubber composites reflects their ability to absorb and release energy under cyclic stress and strain, while the area of the hysteresis loop indicates damping characteristics and internal friction loss. The results demonstrate that both the hysteresis loop stress and the hysteresis loop area of SBR/SN-Silica/NRR are higher than those of the other composites. This is attributed to the complex composition of NRR, which contains a carbon black filler. Thus, it accommodates higher stress. Additionally, SBR/SN-Silica/NRR absorbs more energy and generates higher heat during alternating strain compared to the other two samples. This is because carbon black generates high heat in NRR, and the molecular chain is broken during NRR desulfurization to form a large number of free end chains, thereby increasing internal friction.

The hysteresis loop height of SBR/SN-Silica/G3.5M3.5-NRR is similar to that of SBR/SN-Silica, likely owing to the stress relaxation effect produced by cografted NRR, which enriches the interface. Additionally, SBR/SN-Silica/G3.5M3.5-NRR exhibits the lowest hysteresis loop area, indicating reduced internal friction under cyclic strain. This reduction is associated with the stress relaxation occurring at the interface between rubber and the filler owing to the presence of grafted low-molecular-weight NRR. The results show that the internal friction of rubber composites containing cografted NRR is lower than that of the composite without NRR, which can decrease heat generation of the tire rubber.

SBR/SN-Silica presents a large number of small white silica spots, stripping lines, and holes on its fracture surface, indicating that silica agglomerates in the SBR matrix to a certain degree and interface adhesion is weak, which negatively impacts the overall performance of the composite. By comparison, SBR/SN-Silica/NRR exhibits fewer stripping patterns and holes, which improves interfacial bonding between silica and rubber, although many exposed small white silica spots remain.

After the NRR grafting modification, the interfacial adhesion in SBR/SN-Silica/G3.5M3.5-NRR was significantly improved. The exposure of silica particles was nearly eliminated, and most of the lines generated by interface stripping are absent. This indicates that cografted NRR improves the dispersion of silica within the SBR matrix and enhances the binding strength of silica and SBR. Therefore, the cografted NRR composites not only achieve further improvements in tensile strength, tear resistance, and modulus but also mitigate the significant reduction in elongation at break caused by the incorporation of NRR.

Conclusions

Low-molecular-weight desulfurized NRR comprising a carbon black filler provided a better reinforcement effect than silica in SBR. Thus, NRR blended with SBR/Silica improved the strength and modulus of rubber composites. However, desulfurized NRR severely reduced the elongation at break of rubber composites and increased dynamic heat generation during cyclic stress–strain.

During cografting modification, GMA and MA were successfully grafted onto the NRR molecular chain with grafting rates of about 3.4 and 4.4%, respectively. Compared to the rubber composites with unmodified NRR, the modified cografted NRR introduced polar anhydride and epoxy groups, which promoted interfacial bonding between silica and SBR, silica dispersion within the SBR matrix, improved the mixing quality of SBR–silica, and decreased the glass transition temperature. The mechanical properties of the rubber composites were significantly improved by adding 25 phr of cografted NRR to 50 phr of filled SBR/Silica. Due to the synergistic effect of the cografting modification of NRR, compared to SBR/SN-Silica, the tensile strength, tear strength, and stress at 300% of SBR/SN-Silica/G3.5M3.5-NRR increased by 16.8, 12.3, 49.6, and 38.1%, respectively, while the elongation at break only decreased slightly, indicating that the severely reduced elongation at break by the addition of NRR was mitigated, and the performance in several aspects was enhanced. During cyclic stress–strain, the hysteresis ring area of SBR/SN-Silica/G3.5M3.5-NRR was lower than that of SBR/SN-Silica, indicating that dynamic heat generation was reduced.
