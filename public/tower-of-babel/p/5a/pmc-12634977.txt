Sodium Zirconium Cyclosilicate Duration and Hyperkalemia-Related Healthcare Encounters in Patients with Heart Failure and Cardio/Renal Dysfunction: The GALVANIZE Outcome Study
Authors: Abiy Agiro, Fan Mu, Erin E Cook, Ali Greatsinger, Jingyi Chen, Manasvi Sundar, Angela Zhao, Ellen Colman, Arun Malhotra
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivePatients with heart failure or cardio/renal dysfunction (chronic kidney disease, end-stage kidney disease, or heart failure) are at increased risk of hyperkalemia. Sodium zirconium cyclosilicate (SZC) treats hyperkalemia, but the impact of SZC treatment duration on healthcare resource utilization in these populations is unclear. This study, GALVANIZE Outcome, assessed healthcare resource utilization associated with short-term versus long-term outpatient SZC use in matched cohorts of SZC users with heart failure and, separately, cardio/renal dysfunction.MethodsData from a large US claims database (7/2018–12/2022) were used to identify adults with heart failure or cardio/renal dysfunction initiating outpatient SZC (index date). Long-term (> 90 days) and short-term SZC users (≤ 30 days) were matched on key baseline characteristics using a two-step approach: exact matching followed by propensity score matching. Rates of hyperkalemia-related hospitalizations or emergency department visits and hyperkalemia-related hospitalizations were compared between long-term and short-term SZC users during the follow-up from index to the earliest of 6 months post-index, end of data availability, new potassium binder use, or reinitiation of SZC post-discontinuation.ResultsAmong 942 matched heart failure pairs and 2892 matched cardio/renal pairs, long-term SZC users had fewer hyperkalemia-related hospitalizations or emergency department visits compared with short-term users, with reductions of 36% in the heart failure sample and 40% in the cardio/renal dysfunction sample; the respective reductions were 39% and 37% for hyperkalemia-related hospitalizations alone (all p < 0.001).ConclusionsLong-term SZC use is associated with significant reductions in hyperkalemia-related hospitalizations or emergency department visits compared with short-term use among patients with heart failure or cardio/renal dysfunction.Graphical AbstractThe GALVANIZE Outcome RWE study was a comparison of HK-related HRU between matched long-term (> 90 days) and short-term (≤ 30 days) SZC users with heart failure or cardio/renal dysfunction (CKD, ESKD, or heart failure) using real-world data from HealthVerity spanning 7/2018–12/2022. Adult patients with heart failure or cardio/renal dysfunction and long-term outpatient SZC use had significantly lower rates of HK-related hospitalizations or ED visits, HK-related hospitalizations and HK-related ED visits than exactly and propensity-score matched patients with short-term outpatient SZC use. Abbreviations: CI, confidence interval; CKD, chronic kidney disease; ED, emergency department; ESKD, end-stage kidney disease; HK, hyperkalemia; HRU, healthcare resource utilization; RWE, real world evidence; SZC, sodium zirconium cyclosilicate

Key Points

The GALVANIZE Outcome study assessed the relationship between the duration of sodium zirconium cyclosilicate therapy and healthcare resource utilization outcomes in matched cohorts of patients with heart failure, as well as in separate matched cohorts with cardio/renal dysfunction.We found that long-term use of sodium zirconium cyclosilicate (> 90 days) was associated with significantly fewer hyperkalemia-related and all-cause hospitalizations or emergency department visits compared with short-term use (≤ 30 days) in the respective matched cohorts.The findings suggest that extended sodium zirconium cyclosilicate treatment is linked to reduced rates of hyperkalemia-related hospital or emergency department encounters among patients with heart failure or cardio/renal dysfunction, relative to those receiving short-term therapy.

Introduction

Hyperkalemia, defined as a serum potassium level above 5.0 mmol/L, is a common and potentially life-threatening complication of impaired renal function that can manifest in patients with heart failure, chronic kidney disease (CKD), or end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) [1, 2]. The prevalence of hyperkalemia among patients with these cardio/renal conditions in the USA has been estimated at 6.4%, approximately four times higher than that in the general US population [3].

Treating hyperkalemia in patients with cardio/renal conditions often requires careful clinical management [4], as hyperkalemia in these patients has been associated with poor clinical outcomes, including an increased risk of cardiac arrhythmia, CKD progression, and mortality compared with patients without hyperkalemia [5–7]. Furthermore, patients with heart failure and CKD are commonly managed with renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system inhibitors (RAASi) [1]; however, the use of RAASi may increase the frequency of hyperkalemia [2, 8], which often mandates RAASi dose reduction or treatment discontinuation, thereby compromising the therapeutic benefits of RAASi [9].

Patients with cardio/renal conditions who have hyperkalemia relative to those who have normokalemia tend to experience a higher healthcare resource utilization (HRU) and cost burden [6, 10–12]. The increased burden may arise directly from hyperkalemia, as the development of hyperkalemia can result in greater hospitalizations and emergency department (ED) visits [12, 13], or indirectly from the increased risk for adverse clinical outcomes of the related cardio/renal conditions (e.g., hospitalized heart failure) [6]. Prior research has shown that treatment of hyperkalemia with long-term sodium zirconium cyclosilicate (SZC) therapy is generally associated with reduced rates of hyperkalemia-related HRU [14]. For instance, the RECOGNIZE I real-world evidence (RWE) descriptive cohort study found that patients with hyperkalemia receiving outpatient SZC therapy for at least 90 days had a significantly lower rate of hyperkalemia-related hospitalizations than patients receiving the therapy for a shorter period (10.1% vs 15.1%) [14]; however, the RECOGNIZE I study did not adjust for differences in patient characteristics, which is necessary to establish the benefit of long-term hyperkalemia treatment.

There has been a paucity of RWE research characterizing the relationship between the duration of SZC therapy and HRU outcomes adjusting for patient population differences, especially among patients with cardio/renal dysfunction who are at a higher risk of adverse outcomes. We therefore conducted the GALVANIZE Outcome RWE study to assess HRU outcomes associated with short-term versus long-term outpatient SZC use in matched cohorts of SZC users. This article separately presents the results among the sub-populations of patients with heart failure and with cardio/renal dysfunction. Results from the analysis of the overall population and a sub-study of RAASi users have been previously published [15].

Methods

Study Design

The GALVANIZE Outcome RWE study was a non-interventional, retrospective, observational cohort study comparing HRU outcomes in matched patients treated with short-term (≤ 30 days) or long-term (> 90 days) outpatient SZC therapy. This analysis was conducted among matched cohorts of SZC users with heart failure and matched cohorts of SZC users with cardio/renal dysfunction (defined as CKD, ESKD, or heart failure).

The index date was the date of the first record of SZC in the outpatient setting occurring between 1 January, 2019, and 30 June, 2022. The baseline period was the 6 months prior to the index date; a 6-month baseline period was used to increase the likelihood of capturing incident users who had an initial outpatient SZC fill. The follow-up period was a maximum of 6 months after the index date, with patients censored if they reinitiated SZC after discontinuation (defined as at least a 30-day gap without a new SZC fill after the end of the prior fill), at the initiation of another potassium binder, at the end of data availability, death, or 6 months after the index date, whichever occurred first. Specifically, patients were censored at SZC reinitiation to evaluate the impact of the duration of the initial outpatient SZC therapy use on HRU outcomes.

Data Sources

Data were sourced from two linked databases maintained by and licensed from HealthVerity, comprising closed medical and pharmacy claims for patients in the USA from July 2018 to December 2022. The study was considered exempt research under 45 CFR § 46.104(d)(4) as it involved only the secondary use of data that were de-identified in compliance with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act, specifically, 45 CFR § 164.514. Patient data were used in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration as revised in 2013. The study team received permission from HealthVerity to access and use the study data.

Study Population

Participant Eligibility Criteria and Cohorts

The following inclusion criteria were applied to all patients in the GALVANIZE Outcome RWE study: (1) had a record for SZC in the outpatient setting; (2) were aged 18 years or older on the index date; and (3) had continuous medical and pharmacy enrollment for at least the 6 months prior to the index date and 30 days after the index date. Patients in the heart failure sample were additionally required to have a diagnosis of heart failure during the baseline period. Patients in the cardio/renal dysfunction sample were additionally required to have a diagnosis of CKD, ESKD, or heart failure during the baseline period. As such, the heart failure sample was a subset of the cardio/renal dysfunction sample. Patients were classified as the short-term SZC use cohort (≤ 30 days) or the long-term SZC use cohort (> 90 days) based on the sum of days of supply of SZC prior to discontinuation.

Matching

Short-term SZC users were matched to long-term SZC users in each sample (heart failure and cardio/renal) separately using a two-step process: (1) exact matching followed by (2) propensity score matching. In the first step, patients were exact matched on the following key baseline characteristics: diagnosis of ESKD or receipt of hemodialysis, a diagnosis of CKD (any stage), a diagnosis of hyperkalemia, a diagnosis of acute kidney injury, any RAASi use on the index date, Medicare Advantage coverage, Medicaid coverage, and any hospitalization during baseline. The second step was the propensity score matching. We first generated the propensity score using a logistic regression model to predict short-term versus long-term SZC use based on baseline characteristics that were and were not exactly matched on. The variables included in the final propensity score model included demographics (age, sex, region, index year); baseline medication use (any RAASi, patiromer, sodium polystyrene sulfonate); CKD stage (as individual variables); Charlson Comorbidity Index (continuous); all exact-matching variables; baseline conditions (renal disease, type 1 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, coronavirus disease 2019, stroke, chronic pulmonary disease); inpatient stays with a diagnosis code for a key condition (renal disease, any CKD or ESKD, heart failure, type 2 diabetes, chronic diabetes, hypertension, hyperkalemia, chronic pulmonary disease, acute kidney injury), and any all-cause outpatient visits during baseline. Patients were matched 1:1 within each coarsened exact match category using the propensity score and caliper matching using a caliper of 0.05.

Variables and Outcomes

Patient characteristics (i.e., demographics, clinical, and treatment characteristics), hyperkalemia-related HRU (defined as HRU with a diagnosis code for hyperkalemia), and all-cause HRU were measured during the baseline period for the matched short-term and long-term SZC use cohorts. Healthcare resource utilization outcomes in the matched cohorts were assessed during the follow-up period. The composite primary outcome was hyperkalemia-related hospitalizations or ED visits, and the secondary outcome was hyperkalemia-related hospitalizations. Exploratory outcomes included composite all-cause hospitalizations or ED visits, all-cause hospitalizations, hyperkalemia-related and all-cause ED visits, and hyperkalemia-related and all-cause outpatient visits. An HRU outcome was considered hyperkalemia related if the patient had a diagnosis of hyperkalemia at any diagnosis position.

Statistical Analyses

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline patient characteristics and HRU using means and standard deviations (SDs) for continuous variables and counts and percentages for categorical variables. The balance of baseline patient characteristics between the matched short-term and long-term SZC use cohorts was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMDs); cohorts were considered to be well matched if the SMD was < 0.20 [16].

Matched HRU outcomes were compared between the short-term and long-term SZC use cohorts using generalized estimating equations to account for matching. Outcomes were reported as rates per person-year (PPY) and incidence rate ratios (IRRs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and p values (< 0.05 was considered statistically significant). No comparisons between the heart failure and cardio/renal dysfunction samples were conducted. All analyses were conducted using R 3.6.3.

Results

Before matching, 18,454 patients met the inclusion criteria (Fig. 1), including 9629 short-term SZC users and 4452 long-term SZC users. After matching, the heart failure sample comprised 1884 patients, with 942 matched pairs in each of the short-term and long-term SZC use cohorts, and the cardio/renal dysfunction sample included 5784 patients, with 2892 matched pairs in each of the short-term and long-term SZC use cohorts.

In the heart failure sample, the mean age was 65.4 years among short-term SZC users versus 65.8 years among long-term SZC users, and just over half of the patients were male (56.9% vs 55.9%) [both SMDs < 0.025]. Among both short-term and long-term SZC users, the mean Charlson Comorbidity Index was 5.2 (SMD = 0.009), and 96.0% of patients had any CKD or ESKD, 43.4% had ESKD, and 76.3% had a hyperkalemia diagnosis code recorded during baseline. Other common comorbidities at baseline were hypertension (both 97.6%), diabetes (short-term vs long-term: 77.8% vs 81.4%), and acute kidney injury (both 47.5%) [all SMDs < 0.1].

Half of the patients in the heart failure sample (50.5% in both short-term and long-term SZC use cohorts) had RAASi use on the index date. Other treatment and medication use during baseline was similar between the two cohorts, with the most common treatments overall being beta blockers (short-term vs long-term: 74.2% vs 77.6%), diuretics (63.6% vs 65.0%), calcium channel blockers (53.5% vs 52.1%), and vasodilators (34.0% vs 31.0%) [all SMDs < 0.1]. Overall, the baseline hyperkalemia-related and all-cause HRU was well balanced between the two cohorts (SMDs ≤ 0.1). Slightly over half of patients (short-term vs long-term: 56.7% vs 52.6%) had any hyperkalemia-related hospitalization or ED visit during baseline, and over three quarters (77.3% vs 76.2%) had an all-cause hospitalization or ED visit during baseline (both SMDs < 0.1).

Baseline characteristics were similar in the cardio/renal dysfunction sample, although patients in the cardio/renal dysfunction sample had a lower average Charlson Comorbidity Index compared with patients in the heart failure sample (3.3 vs 5.2) and lower rates of hyperkalemia-related and all-cause inpatient and/or ED visits during baseline (Table 1).

HRU Outcomes

Patients with Heart Failure

In the heart failure sample, short-term SZC users had a shorter mean (SD) follow-up of 135.2 (57.3) days compared with 170.2 (25.4) days in long-term SZC users (p < 0.001). The mean (SD) initial SZC treatment duration was 20.0 (11.6) days for short-term SZC users and 168.3 (59.3) days for long-term SZC users (p < 0.001).

During the follow-up period, patients with heart failure with long-term SZC use had a 36% lower rate of hyperkalemia-related hospitalizations or ED visits than those with short-term SZC use (short-term vs long-term: 1.95 PPY vs 1.24 PPY; IRR 0.64 [95% CI 0.52, 0.78]) and a 39% lower rate of hyperkalemia-related hospitalizations (1.58 vs 0.96; IRR 0.61 [95% CI 0.50, 0.75]) [both p < 0.001; Fig. 2a]. The rates of hyperkalemia-related ED visits (0.37 vs 0.28; IRR 0.76 [95% CI 0.52, 1.12]; p = 0.162) and hyperkalemia-related outpatient visits were similar between short-term and long-term SZC users (2.75 vs 2.52; IRR 0.92 [95% CI 0.67, 1.26]; p = 0.597).

Comparison of healthcare resource utilization (HRU) rates during the follow-up by duration of sodium zirconium cyclosilicate (SZC) use among matched patients with heart failure. Among patients with heart failure, patients with long-term (> 90 days) SZC use had lower rates of hyperkalemia (HK)-related hospitalizations or emergency department (ED) visits, HK-related hospitalizations, and HK-related ED visits as well as all-cause hospitalizations or ED visits, all-cause hospitalizations, and all-cause ED visits, than patients with short-term (≤30 days) SZC use. Matched pairs were compared using generalized estimating equations; the difference between cohorts was statistically significant (p < 0.05) for all outcomes except HK-related ED visits (p = 0.162). CI confidence interval, IRR incidence rate ratio

The rates of all-cause hospitalizations and/or ED visits during the follow-up were also significantly lower among long-term vs short-term SZC users with heart failure (Fig. 2b). Compared with short-term users, long-term SZC users had a 32% lower rate of all-cause hospitalizations or ED visits (short-term vs long-term: 5.38 PPY vs 3.67 PPY; IRR 0.68 [95% CI 0.60, 0.78]), a 35% lower rate of all-cause hospitalizations (3.21 vs 2.09; IRR: 0.65 [95% CI 0.56, 0.75]), and a 27% lower rate of all-cause ED visits (2.17 vs 1.59; IRR 0.73 [95% CI 0.61, 0.89]) [all p < 0.01]. The rate of all-cause outpatient visits was similar between short-term and long-term SZC users (28.51 vs 30.01; IRR 1.05 [95% CI 0.96, 1.16]; p = 0.295).

Patients with Cardio/Renal Dysfunction

In the cardio/renal dysfunction sample, short-term SZC users had a shorter mean (SD) follow-up of 134.5 (57.3) days compared with 171.8 (23.9) days among long-term SZC users (p < 0.001). The mean (SD) initial SZC treatment duration was 21.0 (11.5) days for short-term SZC users and 167.8 (53.4) days for long-term SZC users (p < 0.001).

Patients with cardio/renal dysfunction with long-term SZC use had significantly lower rates of hyperkalemia-related HRU during the follow-up compared with those with short-term SZC use (Fig. 3a). Relative to short-term SZC users, long-term SZC users had a 40% lower rate of hyperkalemia-related hospitalizations or ED visits than short-term SZC users (short term vs long term: 1.26 PPY vs 0.75 PPY; IRR 0.60 [95% CI 0.51, 0.69]), a 37% lower rate of hyperkalemia-related hospitalizations (0.92 vs 0.57; IRR 0.63 [95% CI 0.54, 0.73]), and a 49% lower rate of hyperkalemia-related ED visits (0.35 vs 0.18; IRR 0.51 [95% CI 0.37, 0.71]) [all p < 0.001]. The rates of hyperkalemia-related outpatient visits were similar between short-term and long-term SZC users (3.12 vs 2.71; IRR 0.87 [95% CI 0.73, 1.03]; p = 0.110).

These trends in the cardio/renal dysfunction sample persisted in the all-cause HRU rates (Fig. 3b). Long-term SZC users had a 32% lower rate of all-cause hospitalizations or ED visits than short-term SZC users (short term vs long term: 3.54 PPY vs 2.41 PPY, IRR 0.68 [95% CI 0.62, 0.75]), a 34% lower rate of all-cause hospitalizations (1.94 vs 1.28; IRR 0.66 [95% CI 0.59, 0.73]), and a 29% lower rate of all-cause ED visits (1.59 vs 1.14; IRR 0.71 [95% CI 0.63, 0.81]) (all p < 0.001). The rates of all-cause outpatient visits were similar between long-term and short-term SZC users (27.32 vs 26.96; IRR 0.99 [95% CI 0.93, 1.05]; p = 0.657).

Discussion

In this retrospective matched cohort analysis of patients with heart failure and cardio/renal dysfunction receiving outpatient SZC therapy, patients with long-term SZC use had lower rates of hyperkalemia-related hospitalizations and/or ED visits than those with short-term SZC use. By contrast, similar rates of outpatient visits were observed between patients receiving short-term versus long-term SZC therapy in both the heart failure and the cardio/renal dysfunction samples. These results suggest that long-term SZC therapy may be associated with a reduced risk of acute hyperkalemia-related health events necessitating hospitalizations or ED visits compared with short-term therapy in patients with heart failure or cardio/renal dysfunction.

The findings of this study build upon the results from the prior RECOGNIZE I descriptive cohort study, which reported a 33% reduction in hyperkalemia-related hospitalizations and a 22% reduction in all-cause hospitalizations in long-term versus short-term SZC users in an unmatched sample of patients with hyperkalemia [14]. The current study substantiates these results by replicating the analysis in a sample of patients with comorbid heart failure and/or renal dysfunction who were matched on key characteristics to minimize potential confounding, which increases confidence in the findings.

Further, the reduction of HRU observed in the current study is consistent with other studies of potassium binders in patients with cardio/renal dysfunction, although there is limited research on these populations, particularly regarding SZC use [17–19]. In a 2023 study that included 36 patients with systolic heart failure and hyperkalemia in Japan, patients who continued SZC therapy during the 2-year follow-up period had lower medical costs (a proxy for HRU) than patients who discontinued the therapy [17]. Meanwhile, a 2022 US study of Medicare Advantage beneficiaries with hyperkalemia and heart failure (96% with comorbid CKD) found that patients treated with patiromer had a 68% reduced odds of hospitalizations and ED visits over 6 months compared with matched patients who did not receive any potassium binder [18]. Together, these results underscore the potential benefit of long-term potassium binder use in mitigating HRU outcomes among patients with cardio/renal conditions.

Results of the current study suggest that a longer duration of SZC therapy relative to a shorter duration may help lower the rates of HRU via reductions in hyperkalemia-related events among patients with heart failure or cardio/renal dysfunction. Notably, the rates of hyperkalemia-related HRU among long-term users were lower in the inpatient and emergency settings but similar in the outpatient setting compared with short-term users, suggesting that long-term SZC use may reduce acute hyperkalemia-related events without necessitating an increase in monitoring and routine care for hyperkalemia management. This may be particularly important for patients with cardio/renal conditions who may already be experiencing a high degree of clinical monitoring and management [20]. It is also worth mentioning that the burden of hyperkalemia-related HRU in this study may be underreported because of the under-coding of hyperkalemia in claims data [21].

Proper hyperkalemia management can be particularly beneficial for patients with heart failure or cardio/renal dysfunction, as the risk of developing hyperkalemia increases with cardio/renal comorbidities, and the severity of hyperkalemia has also been shown to increase with worsening CKD stage [22]. In addition, patients with heart failure and comorbid CKD are often treated with high doses of RAASi [23, 24], which can further increase the risk of hyperkalemia [25]. Patients who develop hyperkalemia during the course of RAASi therapy are typically managed by a RAASi dose reduction or treatment discontinuation; however, such changes in management strategies may increase the risk of adverse clinical outcomes, including cardiovascular disease or CKD progression [9, 26–28]. Meanwhile, adherence to RAASi therapy has been shown to improve cardiovascular outcomes and reduce the risk of mortality among patients with CKD [29] and patients with heart failure [30], highlighting the importance of RAASi continuation in patients with cardio/renal conditions. In this regard, treatment of hyperkalemia with potassium binders, including SZC, has been shown to enable the long-term continuation of RAASi therapy and optimal RAASi dosing in patients with hyperkalemia [31–34]. Furthermore, prior studies suggest that potassium binder use in patients with comorbid CKD and/or ESKD was associated with RAASi continuation and fewer hospitalizations and ED visits [31]. Therefore, based on the literature evidence, the reduction in acute HRU in patients receiving long-term SZC therapy for hyperkalemia in this study may be in part due to the continuous and effective use of RAASi among patients with renal dysfunction and/or heart disease. Future research may explore HRU outcomes following concurrent use of long-term SZC therapy with an optimized dose of RAASi.

Strengths and Limitations

The findings of this study may have broad generalizability as the data were drawn from a large closed database that captured all healthcare encounters covered by health plan providers from a broad range of real-world patients treated with outpatient SZC therapy. The size of the database also allowed for the identification of large samples of patient subgroups with heart failure and cardio/renal dysfunction. Furthermore, the patient cohorts were exact and propensity score matched on key characteristics, and this tight double matching helped minimize potential confounding that could have impacted the outcomes.

The interpretation of the findings should consider certain limitations. First, the duration of SZC therapy used for cohort assignment was assessed during the follow-up period; however, it is possible that the follow-up HRU could be biased by the exposure of the duration of SZC use, as acute health events that resulted in healthcare encounters could also influence clinical management and SZC use (e.g., initiation of another potassium binder or reinitiation of SZC). To mitigate this concern such that an acute health event that resulted in a healthcare encounter would not contribute to cohort assignment, patients were censored upon reinitiating SZC after discontinuation or upon initiating a different potassium binder. After matching, short-term SZC users had slightly higher but non-significant rates of hyperkalemia-related hospitalizations and ED visits during baseline than long-term users, suggesting possible residual confounding. However, SMDs across hyperkalemia-related HRU outcomes were <0.2, indicating any bias is likely small. Second, claims data do not contain all variables of interest, which may include treatment compliance, reasons for dosing or treatment changes, and reasons for hospitalizations or ED visits. Notably, claims data do not capture reasons for discontinuation (e.g., toxicity, cost, adverse events, or belief that only short-term treatment is needed for hyperkalemia correction), so it is possible that the short-term SZC cohort could include patients who discontinued SZC because of experiencing an adverse event. However, the reported rates of adverse events due to SZC use are small and the onset of these events typically occurs beyond the maximum 30-day treatment duration among short-term users [35]. Claims data also do not capture laboratory test results and electrocardiograph findings; hence, the confounding effect of hyperkalemia severity on study outcomes could not be accounted for. Other limitations of claims data include a narrow set of code choice options that may result in misclassification of disease status, study outcomes, and covariates in claims data. Finally, as there was a relatively low prevalence of hyperkalemia-related ED visits in the heart failure sample, the analysis may have not had enough statistical power to detect small but statistically significant differences between the cohorts.

Conclusions

In this RWE study of US patients with heart failure and patients with cardio/renal dysfunction, patients receiving long-term outpatient SZC therapy had significantly lower rates of hyperkalemia-related hospitalizations or ED visits and hyperkalemia-related hospitalizations than matched patients receiving short-term SZC therapy. The findings suggest that continued use of SZC for at least 3 months is associated with decreased acute hyperkalemia-related healthcare encounters compared with temporary use of SZC in patients with heart failure and cardio/renal dysfunction.
