Rocker-Switch Mechanism of Choline Transmembrane Transport in FLVCR2: Dynamic Latch Residues and Cooperative Regulation of Transmembrane Helices
Authors: Xiaoyu Zhao, Feng Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
As a key member of the major facilitator superfamily, FLVCR2 plays a crucial role in choline transport. However, the kinetic mechanism and substrate recognition mode of its transmembrane transport remain elusive. Here, we integrate long-time-scale molecular dynamics simulations, potential of mean force, and functional validation of a key substrate-anchoring residue to elucidate the complete conformational cycle and energy regulation network of FLVCR2 at the atomic level, revealing its characteristic “rocker-switch” mechanism. We find that choline transmembrane transport is synergistically regulated by the electrostatic environment and hydrophobic surface of the extracellular tunnel, ensuring stable substrate migration within the channel lumen and efficient entry into the binding site. A network of negatively charged residues at the extracellular tunnel entrance drives the stepwise entry of choline through strong interactions, while W125 facilitates initial anchoring and avoids abnormal release. Subsequently, the rigid support of the transmembrane helix bundles and flexible hinge-like movements of the cytoplasmic loop drive the conformational transition of FLVCR2 from the outward-facing to the occluded state, accompanied by the closure of the extracellular tunnel and simultaneous opening of the intracellular tunnel. The latch residue pairs dynamically alternate closure, precisely recognizing the directionality of the conformational transitions. Prolonged simulation completely captures the coordinated sliding of transmembrane helices and dynamic latch regulation, revealing a continuous conformational transition from outward-facing to inward-facing. Periodic domain rocking correlates strongly with choline transport, while dynamic remodeling of the channel pore provides a physical pathway for substrate permeation. Intracellular choline release is coordinated through hydrophobic anchor disengagement, electrostatic gating regulation, and positively charged residue guidance. Electrostatic gate residues ensure directional substrate migration through a spatiotemporal balance of repulsion and attraction. Following choline release, FLVCR2 resets to its occluded state through reverse sliding of transmembrane helices and cytoplasmic latch tightening, maintaining structural stability to initiate new transport cycles.

Introduction

Choline is an essential water-soluble nutrient that supports cellular and neurological homeostasis. Through the Kennedy pathway, it is converted into phosphatidylcholine, a key structural component of cell membranes that maintains membrane fluidity and integrity. − Beyond its structural contribution, mitochondrial oxidation converts choline into betaine, a methyl donor for epigenetic regulation (Figure ). , Simultaneously, choline serves as a precursor for acetylcholine, a neurotransmitter critically required for mediating synaptic transmission.

Enrichment of FLVCR2 in the cerebral vasculature and its involvement in choline uptake. Choline enters the cell via the transporters FLVCR2, subsequently participating in the Kennedy pathway for the biosynthesis of phospholipid phosphatidylcholine (PC) and serving as a precursor for mitochondrial betaine. The lipid tail of PC undergoes deacylation to generate lysophosphatidylcholine, which then dynamically regulates membrane lipid composition via the Lands cycle.

In membrane transport systems, the Major Facilitator Superfamily (MFS) represents a critical group of membrane transport proteins extensively involved in the translocation of diverse small-molecule substrates, including monosaccharides, metabolites, and drugs. Their transport mechanism involves conformational changes that drive the transitions between functional states. MFS transporters are typically associated with the “rocker-switch” mechanism, a classical form of the alternating access model. This mechanism facilitates substrate translocation by cyclically alternating the accessibility of the binding site between extracellular and intracellular tunnels. − Their broad substrate specificity enables them to participate in various cellular physiological processes such as ion homeostasis, signaling, and nutrient absorption.

Feline leukemia virus subgroup C receptor 2 (FLVCR2), also known as SLC49A2, is a member of the MFS widely expressed in vital mammalian organs. − Early studies suggested its possible role in mediating heme iron transport in macrophages, though its physiological significance remained controversial. − Recent evidence, however, has demonstrated its critical role in choline transport. −

Traditional perspectives suggest that choline uptake across the blood–brain barrier (BBB) is highly dependent on active transport systems. The high-affinity choline transporter CHT1 has long been regarded as the key protein responsible for choline supply to the central nervous system. However, the function of CHT1 is largely restricted to the neuronal microenvironment, failing to account for the efficient choline uptake mechanism in BBB endothelial cells. In recent years, FLVCR2 has attracted increasing attention for its specific expression in BBB endothelial cells. Several recent studies have progressively revealed that FLVCR2, as the principal choline transporter at the BBB, can effectively mediate the transport of both choline and ethanolamine and significantly affect choline homeostasis in the brain (Figure ). − These discoveries resolve a long-standing scientific puzzle that has persisted for over half a century.

In a groundbreaking study, Mancia’s team confirmed that FLVCR2 is the primary choline transporter in the BBB. They demonstrated that FLVCR2 expression significantly enhances choline uptake, indicating that choline transport in the brain largely depends on the FLVCR2 functionality. Additionally, they determined the high-resolution three-dimensional structure of FLVCR2 in its choline-bound state. Notably, a concurrent study by Safarian’s group provided independent confirmation of the essential role of FLVCR2 in choline transport.

However, traditional structural biology techniques are limited by temporal resolution, providing only discrete conformational snapshots rather than capturing the millisecond-scale dynamics of hotspot residues. FLVCR2 undergoes a series of complex structural rearrangements during choline transport, which forms the basis of its transport efficiency. Despite recent advances in static structural characterization of FLVCR2, the dynamic coupling processes between conformational states during its transport cycle remain unvalidated by integrative experimental-computational analyses, primarily due to limitations in continuous conformational capture techniques and the scarcity of functional dynamic data sets.

To address these gaps, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations integrated with enhanced sampling techniques have emerged as powerful tools, successfully applied to diverse membrane transporter systems over the past decade. These approaches enable the characterization of large-scale conformational rearrangements, such as elevator-like domain movements governed by the alternating access model, , revealing dynamic regulation of free energy landscapes during transport cycles. − Building on these advances, we aim to refine the understanding of FLVCR2’s dynamic behavior during choline transport by revealing how distinct conformational states facilitate substrate translocation and elucidating the molecular basis of its potential “rocker-switch” mechanism.

Here, we employed all-atom MD simulations with a flexible design to investigate the conformational transitions of FLVCR2 during choline transport (Table S1). By capturing dynamic transitions between different states, we provided a detailed mechanistic understanding of how FLVCR2 facilitates transmembrane choline transport. Our results reveal that FLVCR2 drives sequential transitions between outward-facing, occluded, and inward-facing states through progressive tilting and rotation of helical bundles, a dynamic process consistent with the classical alternating-access model. By employing the enhanced sampling method, we simulated the complete transport process of choline from substrate binding and translocation to intracellular release, capturing the molecular interactions within the system and quantitatively characterizing the critical roles of hotspot residues during transport. These findings provide novel insights into the structural and dynamic basis of the putative “rocker-switch” mechanism of FLVCR2 and reveal the spatiotemporal coordination of functionally critical residues through atomic-level interaction profiles, which may offer a new perspective for developing brain-targeted therapeutics based on specific conformations.

Results and Discussion

Extracellular Latch Gating and Energy Coupling in the Choline Entry Pathway of FLVCR2

In this section, we investigated the gating mechanism of the extracellular latch and its associated energy coupling effects within the extracellular tunnel during choline translocation from the extracellular side to the binding site in FLVCR2. Specifically, we analyzed the key protein–ligand interactions and energy variations, focusing on charge distribution and energy landscapes.

The outward-facing FLVCR2 exhibits the typical MFS transporter fold, with two pseudosymmetrical six-helix bundles (TM1–6 and TM7–12) forming a central tunnel (Figure ). Based on the results of the US simulation, we constructed the potential of mean force (PMF) and critical noncovalent interactions for choline translocation from its binding site through the extracellular tunnel toward the extracellular side (Figure a).

The results revealed a deep energy well (z ≈ 0 Å) at the central binding pocket, corresponding to the most stable choline-bound state. As choline migrated outward, the potential energy gradually increased, reaching an energy barrier of ∼4.8 kcal/mol after 5 Å. Within this range, the indole ring of the conserved residue W125 stabilized choline’s trimethylammonium group through π–cation interaction, ensuring choline remained properly positioned and oriented. Simultaneously, W125 served as a gatekeeper to prevent the abnormal release of choline from the binding site (Figure b,c). Beyond this point, the energy barrier dropped as the anchoring effect of W125 diminished.

We consider choline’s approach to the binding site as the reverse process of its dissociation pathway. This implied that W125 played a pivotal role in energy barrier formation by stabilizing choline within the extracellular tunnel. At approximately 9 Å from the binding site, where the choline–W125 distance measures ∼4 Å, we identified the initial π–cation interaction between W125 and choline, establishing a preliminary anchoring effect (Figure a,c). As choline approached the central pocket, persistent π–cation interactions with W125 fine-tuned the spatial orientation of choline’s trimethylammonium group, ultimately facilitating its docking within the N domain binding cavity.

Subsequent analysis focused on the interaction patterns between choline and the key residues during its translocation through the FLVCR2 extracellular tunnel, elucidating how these forces modulate energy barrier variations during choline migration (Figure d and Table S2). Our results indicated that enhanced vdW interactions during choline’s approach to the central binding pocket correlated with stabilized intermediate states. Specifically, the electrostatic environment and hydrophobic surface properties of the N and C domain tunnel walls facilitated the choline entry. The predominantly neutral charge distribution and hydrophobic residues lining the tunnel lumen effectively mitigated unfavorable charge repulsion and structural instability, particularly during intermediate translocation stages (Figure b).

We further characterized the spatial organization of the “latch” and “anchor” residues that regulated choline’s movement within the extracellular tunnel (Figure c). The tunnel entrance featured clustered negatively charged patches (N domain: D147; C domain: E365, E366, D485), which generated a favorable electrostatic environment that guided the initial approach and entry of choline’s trimethylammonium group (Figure b and S1b). This process was regulated by latch residue pair D147–R356. R356 exerted electrostatic repulsion to promote inward choline translocation, whereas D147 maintained a persistent electrostatic attraction, particularly within the 10–15 Å range from the central pocket. Polar residues such as N132 also showed repulsion within this interval. Furthermore, residues 132–135 contributed favorable vdW interaction energies at the entrance (Figure S1a). Previous studies demonstrated that mutation of site 132 to lysine renders FLVCR2-mediated choline transport negligible, as does deletion of sites 133–135 or stop of site 157. −

Moreover, the hydrophobic regions may restrict the lateral diffusion of choline through hydrophobic effects, ensuring its directed migration along a specific pathway toward the binding site (Figure e). During choline’s progressive movement through the extracellular tunnel, residues in the C domain, especially the polar residues near S351, further facilitated translocation via electrostatic attraction (Figure S1b). As choline approached the binding site, it came into closer proximity to W125 and Y348, resulting in not only stronger vdW interactions with both residues but also favorable Coulombic interactions with W125 (Figure S1). Sustaining π–cation interactions between the aromatic rings and choline’s cationic headgroup trapped choline behind W125 after it surmounted the high-energy barrier, preventing its release and emphasizing the robust anchoring role of W125 in the later binding stages (Figure S2). Additionally, residues A349, G435, and N466 played favorable roles in this process. These three sites have been identified as mutated in Fowler syndrome, among which the mutation of site 435 to arginine can abolish choline transport activity. −

Overall, the extracellular tunnel coordinated choline translocation to the central binding pocket through the dual regulatory effects of electrostatic guidance and hydrophobic restriction. This mechanism ensured stable migration through the extracellular tunnel, while preventing abnormal choline dissociation. In particular, W125 directed the initial choline orientation through electrostatic attraction and π–cation interactions. These coordinated molecular features collectively optimized the efficiency of transport of choline through the extracellular tunnel.

Molecular Basis of Choline Binding and Stabilization in the FLVCR2 Central Pocket

Upon successful entry into the central binding pocket, a stable interaction network formed between choline and multiple critical residues of FLVCR2, which maintained its position within the binding site for a certain period (Figure ).

Three parallel conventional molecular dynamics (cMD) simulation trajectories were performed to examine the interactions between choline and FLVCR2 near the binding pocket. System stability was first assessed through root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) (Figure S3a). FLVCR2 exhibited initial RMSD fluctuations, which reflected the system relaxation from its starting structure toward equilibrium. As the simulation progressed, the RMSD values in all three trajectories gradually stabilized, indicating the attainment of dynamic equilibrium. The root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF) of residues in FLVCR2 further characterized its structural dynamics. Choline was anchored to the N domain binding site via a residue interaction network, effectively suppressing large-scale conformational changes in the pocket (Figure a,b). Six transmembrane α-helices surrounding the central pore exhibited low flexibility, which suggested their critical roles in maintaining channel structural stability and binding pocket geometry (Figure b). Notably, the midsegments of these six helices displayed extremely low RMSF values, which may be attributed to their dual functional role: direct participation in choline binding and serving as a pivotal axis in the “rocker-switch” mechanism. These rigid midsegments acted as static pivots during conformational transitions, facilitating coordinated motions of the N and C domains about their axes by restricting midregion motions. In contrast, residues in the cytoplasmic loop connecting the N and C domains exhibited higher flexibility, suggesting that these regions may function as dynamic hinges to mediate relative domain displacement during the conformational transition.

The alanine scanning (AS) mutagenesis was employed to calculate the binding free energy of choline with hotspot residues within an 8 Å radius of the central pocket. Energy variations revealed that coordinated electrostatic and hydrophobic interactions stabilized choline within the central pocket. Specifically, residues from the N exhibited distinct energetic contributions to choline binding (Figure a and S3b,c).

Residues such as W125, I126, I217, L218, V242, and I249 demonstrated a strong affinity for choline, each exceeding 3 kcal/mol, primarily contributing to the hydrophobic binding interface. Among them, the indole ring of W125 was closely stacked with choline’s trimethylammonium group, leading to a significant binding energy loss (7.21 kcal/mol) in the W125A mutant compared to other hotspots (Figure S4 and Table S3). In addition, I249 and M154 flanked choline’s β-hydroxyethyl group, forming a hydrophobic splint that restricted its lateral displacement. Polar residues Q214 and Q246 further stabilized choline from opposite sides, contributing 4.52 and 3.22 kcal/mol, respectively, which enhanced its placement in the N domain. The isobutyl side chain of L218, in conjunction with the indole ring of W125, further compressed the hydrophobic cavity volume of the binding pocket, deeply embedding choline. Residues N121 and N245 stabilized the orientation of choline through hydrogen bonds and C–H···O interactions (Figure c). Energy decomposition revealed that vdW stabilization was primarily provided by Y153, M154, Q214, and L218, whereas electrostatic stabilization was dominated by W125, I126, I217, V242, and I249 (Figure S3b,c). In contrast, residues from the C domain made limited direct contributions to ligand binding. Y348 contributed the most favorable vdW stabilization, while V377, T439, and F446 served as the primary affinity sources in this region.

Collectively, choline was stabilized within the central pocket of outward-facing FLVCR2 by N-domain hotspot residues, primarily via a W125-mediated π–cation interaction and extensive hydrophobic contacts.

Domain Cooperation-Driven Conformational Transition Path of FLVCR2 from Outward-Facing to Occluded States

We subsequently elucidated the molecular mechanism underlying the domain cooperation-driven transition from the outward-facing to the occluded state. All MD simulations were initiated from outward-facing FLVCR2. Three independent 500 ns cMD replicas initially revealed consistent closure trends across segmented regions relative to the initial conformation. To fully capture this transition, we extended these simulations to 1 μs and performed trajectory clustering based on the RMSD of transmembrane helices (Table S1). Transition segments corresponding to the fully occluded conformations were extracted from each simulation replicate for the conformational analysis herein. The complete conformational cycle is elaborated in detail in later sections.

To quantify this conformational rearrangement while minimizing the influence of loop flexibility, we segmented the 12-transmembrane helix bundles of FLVCR2’s N and C domains into extracellular and cytoplasmic regions, resulting in four regions and their corresponding centers of mass (COM) (Table S4). Intriguingly, by monitoring distance and contact dynamics between the 12-transmembrane helix bundles, interdomain rotation angles defined by COMs, latch residue pairs distances (extracellular latch D147–R356; cytoplasmic latch S226–E458), and dynamic cross-correlation matrix (DCCM) of the transmembrane helix bundles, we revealed a characteristic “rocker-switch” motion (Figure and S5, S6).

In the outward-facing crystal structure, the COM distances between COM1–COM2 and COM3–COM4 are 26.00 and 21.00 Å, respectively. Throughout the 500 ns simulation time of run 1, both sets of COM distances predominantly exhibited a trend toward values progressively lower than 26.00 Å and progressively higher than 21.00 Å (Figure a). The observed convergence or divergence of these centroid distances suggested that FLVCR2 underwent conformational transitions via domain rocking motions, accompanied by changes in the interdomain rotation angles. Specifically, the extracellular tunnel gradually closed (extracellular aperture angle progressively smaller than the reference value of 67.65° from the outward-facing crystal structure), while the intracellular tunnel gradually opened (intracellular aperture angle progressively larger than the reference value of 56.64°) (Figure b). The closure of the extracellular tunnel led to a progressive increase in the number of contacts between region 1 and region 2 relative to the reference structure, concomitant with the approach of D147 and R356. Conversely, the opening of the intracellular tunnel correlated with the separation of S226 and E458, accompanied by a progressive decrease in contacts between regions 3 and 4 (Figure c,d). These two processes occurred in a coordinated manner, establishing a dynamic balance between closure of the extracellular tunnel and opening of the intracellular tunnel.

Protein conformations sampled at 100 ns intervals revealed this distinct conformational change (Figure e). By 400–500 ns, a pronounced occluded conformation emerged, as evidenced by significant deviation when aligned with the outward-facing crystal structure. These phenomena were consistently reproduced across two additional independent simulations (Figures S5a,b and S6a,b). Notably, in run 2, the distance changes for the cytoplasmic latch residue pair (S226–E458) were relatively subtle compared to the other trajectories. This may stem from their proximity to the flexible cytoplasmic loop; nevertheless, an unlocking trend was still observed, as the distance was predominantly greater than the 6.5 Å reference value during the outward-facing to occluded transition.

Intriguingly, choline exhibited a progressive impact toward the cytoplasmic side in late simulation stages, with dynamic distance fluctuations in its distance along the z-axis relative to the binding pocket. This mechanical stress likely promoted the gradual opening of the intracellular tunnel and the disengagement of the cytoplasmic latch, thereby driving transitions toward occluded and inward-facing states to facilitate substrate release (Figure f).

The molecular basis of these conformational changes can be attributed to coordinated movements of the transmembrane helix bundles. Specifically, across all three simulation trajectories, a consistent phenomenon was observed: helices in region 1 and region 2 exhibited predominantly anticorrelated motions (red dashed circles), as did helices in region 3 and region 4 (Figure S6c). In the other two quadrants, helices in region 1 and region 3 exhibited predominantly anticorrelated motions, as did those in region 2 and region 4 (pink). Correspondingly, positively correlated motions occurred between helices in regions 1 and 4, and between helices in regions 2 and 3 (cyan). These analyses indicated that relative sliding between TM1–6 and TM7–12 helix bundles established a rigid structural framework for domain motions. The high flexibility of the cytoplasmic loop enables it to function as a regulatory switch in these transitions. Polar interactions between the extracellular latch residue pair D147 and R356 induced adaptive conformational adjustments in the extracellular vestibule, generating additional atomic contacts among extracellular residues. This structural rearrangement propagated to the cytoplasmic side via helix sliding, triggering dissociation of the latched S226–E458 pair and disruption of hydrogen bonds involved in substrate exit (Figure S5c). These conformational dynamics align with the “rocker-switch” mechanism.

In summary, the rigid transmembrane helix framework and flexible cytoplasmic loop hinge collectively drove the allosteric transition from the outward-facing state to the occluded state. Specifically, the gradual closure of the extracellular tunnel and coordinated opening of the intracellular tunnel were accompanied by dynamic distance changes between the latch residue pairs. The spatiotemporal coordination of latch residue pair distances elucidated the channel gating process, while mechanical stress from cytoplasmic-directed choline displacement may trigger the gradual opening of the intracellular tunnel, promoting transitions into the occluded state.

Mechanistic Dissection of FLVCR2W125A-Induced Choline Escape: Energy Landscape, Pathway Transition, and Anchoring Residue Disruption

Prior analyses indicated that W125 plays a critical role in stabilizing choline via π–cation interactions. However, the substitution of W125 with alanine (W125A) completely abolished this anchoring effect, resulting in a markedly altered choline binding mode.

In the W125A system, the potential energy profile for choline migration through the extracellular tunnel toward the extracellular side was reconstructed. Previous results demonstrated that the wild-type system featured a deep energy well (∼4.8 kcal/mol) at the central binding pocket. This deep well trapped choline through steric occlusion by W125 and π–cation interactions, generating a local free energy barrier near W125 that effectively restricted choline mobility. Upon removal of this steric hindrance, the central energy markedly decreased, while a minor energy barrier emerged at approximately 7 Å. Consequently, the free energy barrier for choline escape from the binding site decreased to ∼1.9 kcal/mol, significantly weakening its binding stability (Figure a). The elimination of steric hindrance induced a redistribution of energy barriers that governed the kinetic behavior of choline within the binding pocket. This alteration suggests a reduced choline residence time in the mutant system, thereby facilitating rapid dissociation from the binding pocket and subsequent outward movement along the extracellular tunnel.

The shaded regions show the standard deviations based on 3 replicas. Analysis of the noncovalent interaction network revealed that the W125A mutation reshaped choline’s binding pathway. Specifically, the loss of Tryptophan-mediated π–cation interactions redirected choline transport toward Y348 in the C domain. Although Y348 partially impeded choline release via π–cation interactions, its off-axis position relative to the binding pocket ultimately permitted choline to escape into the transition zone of the extracellular tunnel within a shorter time scale (Figure b and S7).

Further analysis of interaction patterns along the binding pathway revealed that the W125A mutation significantly weakened multiple retention points for choline, facilitating its escape from the binding pocket (Figure S8). Choline binding along the 30 Å pathway can be divided into three key stages.

In the initial binding phase, choline was electrostatically guided into the extracellular tunnel by negatively charged residues, such as E365, E366, and D147. However, the absence of the anchoring residue (W125) induced the disruption of electrostatic balance, leading to prolonged metastable trapping of choline by the carboxyl group of E366 (Figure S8). In the intermediate transition state, a transient anchoring was produced by a π–cation interaction between the trimethylammonium group of choline and the benzene ring of Y348, redirecting the transport orientation. The final binding phase was characterized by complete destabilization: the W125A mutation eliminated both the energy barrier and the hydrophobic splint previously formed by L218’s isobutyl side chain and W125’s indole ring. This triggered the lateral displacement of choline and ultimately disrupted the stability of the original binding interface. Moreover, compared to the wild-type system, the progressive strengthening of choline–protein vdW interactions was significantly attenuated in the mutant system, suggesting that the W125A mutation may loosen the hydrophobic interface, thereby compromising choline stabilization within the tunnel (Figure c and Table S5).

Analysis of cMD trajectories for the W125A mutant system revealed rapid choline dissociation from the binding pocket toward the extracellular side within 43, 10, and 50 ns (Figure e and S9). Nonetheless, FLVCR2 preserved its coordinated global conformational dynamics (Figure d, S10 and S11). The distance fluctuations of COM1–COM2 and COM3–COM4 were comparable to those in the wild-type system. Concomitant variations in aperture angles indicated preserved domain pivoting, a motion supported by the rigid transmembrane helix bundles (Figure S11f). Closure of the extracellular tunnel and opening of the intracellular tunnel drove a progressive increase in inter-residue contacts between region 1 and region 2, while contacts between region 3 and region 4 progressively decreased. Distance variations of the latch pairs suggested that the mutation did not disrupt the dynamic regulatory role of the latch residues. Notably, in run 3, conformational analysis based on COM pair distances and aperture angles indicated that FLVCR2 adopted a partially occluded conformation shortly after equilibration (i.e., early in the simulation). Enhanced flexibility driven by dynamic fluctuations in TM3 and TM4 then induced a drift in the system’s center of mass (COM) position. By integrating heavy atom contact data with three-dimensional structural features, the system was determined to reach the occluded state by 500 ns (Figure S11e).

These observations were further validated in three independent apo-state simulations in which FLVCR2 retained conformational transitions driven by intrinsic interaction networks (Figures S12 and S13). The six transmembrane helices forming the pore of FLVCR2 maintained low structural flexibility, particularly in the central core, a characteristic unaffected by the absence of the ligand (Figure S13a). These rigid midsegments functioned as static pivots during conformational changes, facilitating coordinated counter-rotation of domains around their axes. DCCM analysis further revealed strong anticorrelated motions between the extracellular helix bundles and the intracellular helix bundles (Figure S13f). These results delineated a ligand-independent “rocker-switch” mechanism for FLVCR2.

Collectively, the W125A mutation disrupted the critical anchoring network in FLVCR2, significantly reducing choline binding stability and transport efficiency while simultaneously promoting extracellular tunnel-mediated escape. It destabilized the hydrophobic clamp and reshaped the noncovalent interaction pathways, forcing choline to rely on transient anchoring by Y348. Nevertheless, FLVCR2 retained global structural transitions, with transmembrane helix coordination and latch residue dynamics remaining unaffected by mutation or ligand absence.

Rocker-Switch Mechanism of FLVCR2 Unveiled: Conformational Cycling from Outward-Facing to Inward-Facing States via Collective Motions and Pore Remodeling

Previously, we characterized the conformational transition of FLVCR2 from the outward-facing to the occluded state by isolating fully occluded segments. To further investigate the complete transport cycle of FLVCR2, we extended three independent cMD simulations to 1 μs, capturing the full transition pathway from outward-facing through occluded to inward-facing states by clustering analysis (Figure ).

Dashed lines and numerical labels indicate initial values from the outward-facing crystal structure. Choline dissociation was defined as a sustained centroid distance exceeding 20 Å from the binding pocket. For run 1, the centroid distance revealed periodic fluctuations between the substrate entry (COM1–COM2) and exit (COM3–COM4) regions. Following a brief occluded state, the progressive closure of the extracellular tunnel and opening of the intracellular tunnel culminated in a stable inward-facing state at ∼520 ns. Throughout this transition, the extracellular aperture angle (β) contracted steadily, while the intracellular aperture angle (α) expanded markedly. This resulted in increased contacts between regions 1 and 2, accompanied by a progressive decrease in contacts between regions 3 and 4. This transition was accompanied by contraction of the extracellular latch (D147–R356) and expansion of the cytoplasmic latch (S226–E458). Around 595 ns, choline was rapidly released into the cytoplasm as FLVCR2 adopted an inward-facing state (Figure S14). Subsequent reopening of the extracellular tunnel and gradual closure of the intracellular tunnel restored the occluded state.

In run 2, two distinct inward-facing transitions were observed. The first inward-facing conformation was initiated at ∼740 ns. Following rapid choline release at ∼770 ns, FLVCR2 proceeded to the occluded state. Subsequently, after a brief outward-facing state at ∼850 ns, closure of the extracellular tunnel coupled with reopening of the intracellular tunnel facilitated a second inward-facing transition. These conformational changes were reflected in cyclic variations of centroid distances at substrate entry and exit regions, heavy atom contact numbers, and extra- and intracellular aperture angles. Run 3 recorded a complete conformational cycle. The inward-facing state emerged after ∼650 ns, with choline released into the cytoplasm at ∼713 ns. Subsequently, progressive opening of the extracellular tunnel and concurrent closure of the intracellular tunnel culminated in the reappearance of the outward-facing conformation within a 1 μs simulation time.

Notably, during the simulations, although the gating residues exhibited a clear trend toward closure, their periodic variation appeared less pronounced. This behavior likely stems from the fact that they are either adjacent to the flexible cytoplasmic loops or part of the loops themselves and also due to the spatially offset positioning within the three-dimensional structure of the two residues forming the gates. However, systematic analysis revealed that these residues still displayed locking/unlocking trajectories synchronized with global conformational reorganization (Figure S15). Specifically, the extracellular/cytoplasmic latch residues established a spatiotemporally coordinated “alternating gating” pattern.

Analysis of the two-dimensional RMSD (2D-RMSD) matrices revealed significant increases after ∼400, ∼600, and ∼500 ns for each run, respectively, coinciding with occluded state formation (Figure S16a). Upon transitioning into the inward-facing state, all three trajectories maintained significant structural changes (RMSD > 2.0 Å). Subsequently, RMSD values of run 1 gradually decreased, returning to the occluded state. After the first inward-facing event, the RMSD of run 2 rapidly decreased to lower levels, then jumped to >2.0 Å, initiating a second conformational cycle and re-entering the inward-facing conformation. Run 3 achieved complete conformational reset to the initial outward-facing state. Despite differing transition rates, all trajectories spontaneously sampled occluded, inward-facing, and outward-facing states.

DCCM analysis further revealed characteristic coordinated motions of FLVCR2’s transmembrane helices during transitions (Figure b). These motions displayed significant regional associations, including anticorrelated motions between transmembrane helix bundles (region 1 and 2, region 3 and 4), anticorrelated motions within helix bundles (region 1 and 3, region 2 and 4), and positively correlated motions between diagonal regions (region 1 and 4, region 2 and 3). This pattern indicated that the TM1–TM6 and TM7–TM12 bundles formed a rigid sliding framework underlying domain movement, with flexible cytoplasmic loops modulating gating transitions through conformational plasticity, and the domain rocking process was accompanied by the unlocking and locking of the latch residue pairs.

To further characterize conformational dynamics, a principal component (PC) analysis was performed. The first three PCs accounted for the majority of variance, indicating that FLVCR2’s conformational transitions are dominated by a few key motions. Among these, PC1 represented a global allosteric effect involving cooperative rocking of transmembrane helices, resulting in opposing displacement vectors between the extracellular and intracellular regions (Figure a). This pattern was consistent with extracellular closure and intracellular opening. PC2 and PC3 partially reflected the domain sliding, with PC2 primarily characterized by vertical displacement of the N domain (arrows perpendicular to the membrane plane) (Figure S16b).

Pore radius evolution mirrored these motions (Figure b). In the outward-facing state, FLVCR2 exhibited a wide extracellular entry and narrow cytoplasmic exit, aligning with the structural requirements for substrate binding. Transition to the occluded state gradually constricted the extracellular pore, forming a region that accommodated choline but restricted full translocation. The inward-facing state maximized the pore radius of the cytoplasmic side to facilitate choline release, followed by pore resetting to the occluded state for subsequent transport cycles. Pore remodeling was synchronized precisely with latch residue distance changes: extracellular latch closure coupled with entry constriction, while cytoplasmic latch opening coincided with exit expansion (Figure S15). This spatiotemporal coordination of pore dynamics provides a structural basis for directional choline transport.

These dynamics demonstrated pronounced conformational plasticity of FLVCR2 over extended time scales, where coordinated sliding of transmembrane helix bundles (TM1–6 and TM7–12) drove domain rocking, enabling efficient transitions between outward-facing and inward-facing states. To validate the reliability of the simulated conformations, we aligned the inward-facing states obtained from cMD simulations with the corresponding crystal structure (Figure c). This analysis revealed low RMSD values for the helix bundles between the two structures, with discrepancies primarily localized to loop regions, indicating that the simulations successfully captured motion patterns highly consistent with the experimental structure. Crucially, helices surrounding the pocket exhibited high conformational consistency.

To summarize, the simulations successfully captured the continuous conformational transition of FLVCR2. Periodic domain rocking and dynamic regulation of latch residues collectively drove alternating channel gating, while pore remodeling provided a physical pathway for substrate permeation. Their spatiotemporal coordination underlies the “rocker-switch” mechanism of FLVCR2.

Stepwise Intracellular Release of Choline in FLVCR2: Binding Path Reconstruction and Recognition of Electrostatic Regulatory Residues

By resolving the complete conformational cycle of FLVCR2 during choline transport, we found that while the inward-facing state remained stable for a period, choline release exhibited distinct kinetic features: rapid cytoplasmic release occurred once choline reached the intracellular side. To elucidate how choline gradually exits the binding site, we reconstructed its dissociation pathway and examined the regulatory role of electrostatic gate residues in the exit region (Figure and S17).

The PMF revealed a low free energy barrier (∼2.4 kcal/mol) for dissociation of choline from the binding site, consistent with highly spontaneous release. This barrier corresponded to choline exiting the central hydrophobic pocket formed by W125, M154, L218 and I249. During the transition of FLVCR2 from outward-facing to inward-facing, choline’s β-hydroxyethyl group reoriented, while its trimethylammonium group remained stable. Disruption of π–cation interactions between W125 and choline facilitated release, underscoring the critical anchoring role of W125 (Figure a and S18).

We further quantified the vdW and electrostatic interaction energies of choline along its dissociation pathway (Figure b). Near the binding site, choline was primarily stabilized by strong vdW interactions. Further into the intracellular tunnel, vdW contributions decreased and electrostatic interactions emerged as the dominant factor governing choline release.

During initial choline release, binding pocket residues W125, S150, and Y348 maintained strong affinity for the substrate, while residue N466 exhibited significant Coulombic attraction (Figure e and S17), and its alanine mutation is linked to Fowler syndrome. , The subsequent energy variations correlated with choline overcoming the electrostatic gate at the exit. Charge analysis revealed a highly asymmetric distribution near the exit, with the C domain enriched in negatively charged residues (D392, E447, E451, and E458), whose dynamic repulsion with N domain residues (D232 and E233) facilitated exit expansion (Figure c,d). Free energy decomposition showed pronounced electrostatic contributions from E447 and E458, confirming their roles as core electrostatic gatekeepers and suggesting transient trapping states during choline release (Figure e and S17). Meanwhile, positively charged residues R223, K395, and R516 guided choline directionally via electrostatic repulsion, preventing backflow and accelerating dissociation near the cytoplasm. This coordinated action of charged residues ensured unidirectional release and dynamically optimized the pore, with the energy changes along the path supporting a stepwise release mechanism (Table S6).

To sum up, these analyses unveiled the stepwise intracellular release mechanism of choline in FLVCR2, integrating hydrophobic anchor disengagement, electrostatic gating regulation, and positively charged residue guidance. The collapse of the hydrophobic interface triggered the initial release, while spatiotemporally tuned electrostatic attraction and repulsion ensured directional substrate translocation.

Three independent cMD simulations initiated from the inward-facing FLVCR2 revealed rapid dissociation of choline into the cytoplasm within 84–112 ns (Figure f and S19), consistent with energy calculations indicating weak binding affinity. Following choline release, FLVCR2 underwent conformational adjustments characteristic of the inward-to-occluded transition. Similarly, the 12-transmembrane helix bundles were partitioned into four distinct regions with their corresponding centroids (COM1–4). The centroid distances, heavy atom contacts, aperture angles, and latch residue separation were monitored (Figure a–d). The results revealed that the centroid distances on the cytoplasmic side gradually contracted toward the occluded state geometry. The cytoplasmic centroid distance (COM3–COM4) progressively contracted below the inward-facing reference (24.70 Å), while the extracellular distance (COM1–COM2) increased. These changes were accompanied by enhanced contacts in regions 3 and 4, reduction of the intracellular aperture angle (α < 64.11°), expansion of the extracellular angle (β > 56.79°), and separation of regions 1 and 2, collectively defining the occluded geometry. Notably, the six transmembrane helices comprising the FLVCR2 pore exhibited minimal structural flexibility due to their role as static pivots (Figure S20a). Concurrently, closure of the cytoplasmic latch (S226–E458) and formation of a stabilizing hydrogen bond network at the substrate exit reinforced the intracellular tunnel, preventing choline backflow (Figure S20b).

These findings indicated that after choline was fully released, FLVCR2 transitioned from the inward-facing state to the occluded state, characterized by cytoplasmic latch closure and stabilization of the intracellular tunnel, thereby preventing nonspecific channel opening in the choline-free state. Within 400–500 ns, the conformations clearly deviated from the inward-facing geometry and exhibited occluded features (Figure e). This process was consistently reproduced in two additional simulations (Figure a,b and S20c–e).

2D-RMSD matrices confirmed the conformational shift, with deviations from the initial structure occurring at ∼330, ∼600, and ∼550 ns (Figure S21a). PCA further demonstrated a global contraction (Figure c and S21b). Still, the extracellular and intracellular regions displayed opposite displacement vectors. The reverse sliding of transmembrane helices and cytoplasmic loop tightening sealed the intracellular tunnel, demonstrating a path-conserved conformational resetting and the cytoplasmic latch’s checkpoint role in the translocation cycle. DCCM analysis revealed the coordinated motion of these transmembrane helix bundles (Figure d). Consistent with conclusions drawn for the complete conformational cycle, this motion displayed significant regional associations: anticorrelated motions between transmembrane helix bundles, anticorrelated motions within helix bundles, and positively correlated motions between diagonal regions, supporting a rigid sliding framework.

Collectively, these results establish that FLVCR2 resets to the occluded state through transmembrane helix reverse sliding and cytoplasmic latch tightening. As a conformational checkpoint, the cytoplasmic latch coupled with the gradual consolidation of a hydrogen bond network at the substrate exit maintains channel closure, structural stability, and readiness for subsequent substrate binding. These findings support and validate prior conclusions regarding the complete conformational cycle of FLVCR2.

Finally, based on all cMD trajectories covering the choline transport process, we constructed the free energy landscape of the FLVCR2 conformational cycle (Figure ). Projected onto principal components describing key conformational motions, the landscape revealed the stability of distinct functional states and the energy barriers along the transition pathways within the transport cycle. The extracted minimum-energy conformations comprehensively mapped the three functional states: outward-facing, occluded, and inward-facing. These structures closely matched the corresponding experimental structures. The lowest-energy pathway followed the sequence outward-facing → occluded → inward-facing → occluded → outward-facing, underscoring the occluded state as a stable and obligatory intermediate for conformational resetting. In conclusion, this landscape provided comprehensive validation for the “rocker-switch” mechanism, establishing its molecular underpinnings for efficient transport.

Conclusions

This study elucidates the conformational dynamics and interaction mechanisms underlying FLVCR2-mediated choline transport by integrating long-time scale MD simulations, PMF, and functional validation of a key substrate-anchoring residue. We reveal the conformational transitions of the “rocker-switch” mechanism, the energy regulatory network governing substrate binding/release, and functional partitioning of key residues.

Our findings indicate that choline translocation relies on precise regulation of the extracellular tunnel, where synergistic electrostatic and hydrophobic effects stabilize choline within the channel cavity while preventing abnormal dissociation. The tunnel entrance, governed by a negatively charged residue network and an extracellular latch pair, drives the stepwise entry of choline. W125 directs the initial choline orientation through electrostatic attraction and π–cation interactions. Subsequently, the rigid support of the transmembrane helix bundles and flexible hinge-like movements of the cytoplasmic loop drive the conformational transition of FLVCR2 from the outward-facing to the occluded state. The W125A mutation can disrupt the critical anchoring network of choline–FLVCR2, significantly reducing choline binding stability. The disruption of the hydrophobic clamp forces choline to rely on transient anchoring by residue Y348. Extended cMD simulations successfully captured the continuous conformational transition of FLVCR2 from outward-facing through occluded to inward-facing states. Periodic domain rocking and dynamic regulation of latch residues collectively drive alternating channel gating. Sliding of the transmembrane helices controls conformational switching, while pore remodeling provides a physical pathway for substrate permeation. The intracellular choline release is coordinated through hydrophobic anchor disengagement, electrostatic gating regulation, and positively charged residue guidance. FLVCR2 efficiently resets to the occluded state after choline release via transmembrane helices’ reverse sliding and cytoplasmic latch tightening.

In summary, this study elucidates how domain-coordinated motions and pore remodeling enable efficient choline transport across FLVCR2’s full conformational cycle. These findings provide critical molecular insights into the functional regulation of MFS transporter proteins.

Materials and Methods

System Preparation

The initial structures of FLVCR2 in the outward-facing and inward-facing states were obtained from the cryo-EM structures resolved by Cater et al. (PDBID: 8VZO and 8VZN). The protonation states of individual residues were predicted using the PDB 2PQR program. Four initial systems were constructed: (1) outward-facing wild-type FLVCR2 with choline bound in the cavity; (2) outward-facing W125A mutant FLVCR2 with choline bound in the cavity; (3) outward-facing apo wild-type FLVCR2; and (4) inward-facing wild-type FLVCR2 with choline bound in the cavity (Table S1). Each system was then embedded in a lipid bilayer composed of POPE and POPG at a 3:1 ratio using the Membrane Builder Tool in CHARMM-GUI to restore the physiological membrane environment. , The positioning of proteins within the flat lipid bilayer was determined using PPM 2.0 from the Orientations of Proteins in Membranes (OPM) server. − Then, all systems were solvated in TIP3P water containing 0.15 mol/L NaCl to accurately reproduce hydration effects, ensuring charge neutrality and a stable ionic environment. The minimum water thickness at the top and bottom of the membrane was set to 30 Å. The CHARMM36m force field was used to describe interactions involving FLVCR2, lipids, and ions, while ligand parameters were generated using the CHARMM General Force Field (CGenFF). ,

System Initialization for MD Simulations

All complexes were initially configured using the Gromacs-2024 software package. Energy minimization was performed using the steepest descent algorithm to eliminate high-energy atomic contacts, ensuring reasonable system conformations. Position restraints were applied to the protein backbone, side chains, and lipid molecules along with dihedral angle restraints. A multistage equilibration strategy was then implemented, consisting of six progressive steps to gradually release system degrees of freedom by decreasing the restraint force constants. The first two stages were conducted in the NVT ensemble for 10 ns, with a V-rescale thermostat maintaining a constant temperature of 310 K and an integration time step of 1 fs. Subsequent equilibration under the NPT ensemble was performed in two phases: an initial 15 ns equilibration using a C-rescale barostat under semi-isotropic conditions to maintain a pressure of 1 bar, with the integration time step increased to 2 fs, followed by a 25 ns unrestrained relaxation. Each trajectory accumulated an equilibration time of 50 ns. To efficiently account for intermolecular interactions, the Verlet cutoff scheme was applied. The van der Waals (vdW) interactions were computed using a cutoff radius of 1.2 nm, with the Force-switch modifier and a switch radius of 1.0 nm. The particle mesh Ewald (PME) method was used to handle long-range electrostatic interactions, with an electrostatic cutoff radius of 1.2 nm.

cMD Simulation

Four systems, including the outward-/inward-facing wild-type FLVCR2–choline complex and apo-FLVCR2, as well as the outward-facing W125A mutant FLVCR2–choline complex, were subjected to three independent cMD simulations in the NPT ensemble until discernible conformational changes were detected (Table S1). All simulations utilized a 2 fs integration time step, with vdW and electrostatic cutoffs maintained at 1.2 nm. Long-range electrostatic interactions were treated using the PME method, while the V-rescale thermostat and C-rescale barostat were used under semi-isotropic conditions to maintain the physiological conditions of 310 K and 1 bar, respectively. To capture the continuous conformational transition of FLVCR2 from outward-facing to inward-facing, the simulations of the outward-facing FLVCR2–choline complex were extended to 1 μs. Trajectory frames were clustered using a bottom-up hierarchical agglomerative approach with average linkage based on the RMSD of the 12 transmembrane helices. Clustering was stopped when the minimum inter-cluster distance exceeded 3 Å, yielding three clusters representing the inward-facing, occluded, and outward-facing states.

Umbrella Sampling (US) Calculation

After equilibration, steered molecular dynamics (SMD) simulations were performed on the outward-facing wild-type FLVCR2–choline complex to generate initial configurations for the US. The choline binding site in FLVCR2 was defined as all residues within 8 Å of the binding pocket. The reaction coordinate (RC) was defined as the instantaneous distance between the choline’s COM and the COM of its binding site along the z-axis, with the z-axis perpendicular to the membrane (positive direction toward the extracellular side). A constant force was applied to pull choline along the extracellular tunnel at a rate of 0.1 nm/ns over 30 ns, resulting in a trajectory covering a displacement range of 30 Å.

As an enhanced sampling method, US accelerates sampling along predefined RC by applying a biasing potential, significantly improving sampling efficiency in high-energy barrier regions. − A total of 61 sampling windows spaced at 0.5 Å intervals along the RC (30 Å total range) were defined, with a harmonic potential 1 ui=ki(r−ri)2 where r is the current position and r i is the reference position for the ith window. Each window underwent a 20 ns simulation under the NPT ensemble, where the V-rescale thermostat and C-rescale barostat controlled the temperature and pressure, respectively. A harmonic restraint (k i ) of 3.58 (kcal/mol·Å2) was applied, with the first 10 ns dedicated to equilibration and the remaining 10 ns for production sampling. The entire process was repeated three times. Finally, the weighted histogram analysis method (WHAM) was employed to integrate data from multiple windows and reconstruct the free energy landscape of choline entry into the binding site. The quality of the US was assessed by plotting the histogram overlap of the sampling windows along the RC (Figure S22).

For the W125A mutant, the SMD and US parameters were identical. In particular, for choline release from the binding site into the cytoplasm, we extracted structures from the cMD trajectory (run1 of the inward-facing FLVCR2–choline system) at 0.5 Å intervals cover a 30 Å release path defined by the distance between the COM of choline and the binding pocket. These configurations served as the initial structures for US simulations. Ultimately, three US simulations were performed: (1) choline transport through the outward-facing wild-type FLVCR2 tunnel; (2) choline transport through the outward-facing W125A mutant FLVCR2 tunnel; and (3) choline transport through the inward-facing wild-type FLVCR2 tunnel into the cytoplasm (Table S1). Additionally, extended simulations with 10 ns equilibration followed by 30 ns production produced PMF profiles that showed overall agreement with those obtained from the original protocol (Figure S23), confirming the convergence of the simulations and the robustness of the energy results.

MM/PBSA Method and Energy Decomposition

The molecular mechanics/Poisson–Boltzmann surface area (MM/PBSA) method was applied by the gmx_MMPBSA tool to estimate the binding free energy (ΔG bind) between FLVCR2 and choline , 2 ΔGbind=EComplex−EFLVCR2−ECholine where ΔG bind consists of gas-phase free energy and solvation free energy. The gas-phase free energy includes contributions from vdW interaction (ΔE vdW) and Coulombic energies (ΔE ele), while the solvation free energy comprises polar (computed via the Poisson–Boltzmann equation) and nonpolar (estimated based on the solvent accessible surface area) components 3 ΔGnp=γ·SASA+β

The γ, β, and SASA represent the surface tension, correction term, and the solvent accessible surface area calculated by the MSMS program, with γ and β set to 0.0378 kcal/(mol·Å2) and −0.5692 kcal/mol, respectively. ,

Notably, as the charge-view method is not supported by the gmx_MMPBSA tool for membrane protein systems, the particle–particle particle-mesh (P3M) algorithm was employed. It integrates reaction field energy (ΔG pb) and Coulombic energy (ΔE ele) into a single electrostatic term. Consequently, the electrostatic term (ΔE ele + ΔG pb) is presented as a single entity, and we collectively refer to this term as the electrostatic interaction energy. Nevertheless, since ΔG bind comprehensively accounts for all energy contributions, its reliability remains unaffected by the decomposition approach. To identify key interaction residues in the binding site, the energy contributions from residues within 8 Å of the binding pocket were calculated for each US window, while the entropy term was not considered due to its high computational cost and limited predictive accuracy. ,

Analysis of Interactions and Pore Dimensions

Noncovalent interactions were analyzed using the Protein–Ligand Interaction Profiler (PLIP). It should be noted that additive fixed-charge force fields exhibit limitations in accurately calculating the energetics of π–cation interactions, which involve significant polarization effects. , In this study, π–cation interactions were identified by PLIP based on static geometric criteria, employing a cutoff distance of 6 Å between the cation center and the centroid of the aromatic ring, with a horizontal offset cutoff in the plane of the ring set at 2.3 Å. , HOLE2 was employed for the analysis of pore dimensions. ,

Given the minimal energetic impact of alanine’s methyl side chain, AS mutagenesis was used to evaluate the contributions of individual residues to choline binding. , With particular focus on the central choline-binding pocket of FLVCR2, all residues within 8 Å of this pocket in the outward-facing FLVCR2–choline complex were individually mutated to alanine.
