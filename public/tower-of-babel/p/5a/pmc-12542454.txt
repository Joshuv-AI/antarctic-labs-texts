Enhanced creative thinking performance: the role of alpha frequency transcranial alternating current stimulation in the parieto-occipital region
Authors: Runze Zhou, Jinqian Wang, Xiaotong Man, Xinying Huang, An’ning Zhan, Chunlei Liu, Jiaqin Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrevious research has demonstrated a link between creative thinking and alpha oscillations, with individuals exhibiting stronger fronto-parietal alpha synchronization during creative tasks. Effective creative thinking is thought to rely on the coordinated activity of the default mode network (DMN) and the executive control network (ECN), particularly in the frontal and parieto-occipital regions, as reflected by variations in alpha oscillatory activity recorded via EEG. Prior studies have shown that transcranial alternating current stimulation (tACS) at the alpha frequency (α-tACS) can enhance DMN activity in the parieto-occipital cortex, and that 10 Hz tACS may increase both alpha power and creative performance. However, the precise effects of α-tACS on creative thinking remain to be fully elucidated.MethodsTo investigate the effects of α-tACS applied to the parieto-occipital region on creative thinking and its underlying cognitive neural mechanisms, we employed a within-participant design using EEG and the Alternative Uses Task (AUT). 28 participants completed two experimental sessions—one with sham stimulation and one with α-tACS—separated by a 24 ~ 48 h interval. In each session, participants performed the AUT immediately following stimulation.ResultsBehavioral results showed that participants receiving α-tACS exhibited significantly greater levels of novelty, flexibility, fluency, originality, and elaboration compared to the sham condition. Time–frequency analysis of EEG data further revealed that α-tACS significantly increased alpha power in the parieto-occipital regions relative to sham stimulation.ConclusionsThese findings suggest that α-tACS applied to the parieto-occipital cortex, with an inter-session interval of 24 to 48 h, enhances creative thinking performance. This supports the potential of α-tACS as a neuromodulatory technique for facilitating creative cognitive processes.Keywords: Alpha oscillation, Parieto-occipital region, Transcranial alternating current stimulation, Creative thinking

Introduction

Creative thinking

Creativity is typically defined as the ability to produce ideas or outcomes that are both novel and appropriate within a specific sociocultural context [24, 55, 56]. It is a multidimensional construct encompassing cognitive processes, personality traits, behaviors, and accomplishments [27]. Among these dimensions, creative thinking represents the cognitive foundation of creativity, with divergent thinking widely regarded as its central component [60]. Creative thinking not only reflects the observable manifestation of creative potential [14, 46] but is also essential for the generation of creative output [18, 24]. Guilford, a seminal figure in creativity research, identified three core features of creative thinking: fluency, flexibility, and originality. Advances in cognitive neuroscience have facilitated the exploration of creativity at the neural level through techniques such as electroencephalography (EEG) and functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI). These methods enable researchers to directly examine the neural dynamics associated with creative cognition, offering deeper insight into the brain mechanisms underlying creative thought.

Creative thinking and alpha oscillations

Alpha oscillations have emerged as a key neural marker in the investigation of creative cognition. Typically defined as rhythmic neuronal activity in the 8 ~ 13 Hz frequency range, alpha oscillations are implicated in a variety of cognitive and internal processes [42]. For example, alpha activity has been shown to reduce long-range temporal dependencies in spontaneous brain activity, thereby facilitating adaptive regulation of large-scale neural dynamics over slower time scales—ultimately influencing internal cognitive states [5]. Among the various neural correlates of creativity, alpha oscillations have been most consistently linked to creative thinking [71]. Recent research has explored this relationship from multiple perspectives. One notable finding is that individuals performing the Alternative Uses Task (AUT), a classic measure of divergent thinking [25], exhibit significantly greater alpha event-related synchronization (ERS) and increased dimensional complexity in alpha-band EEG activity compared to individuals engaged in convergent tasks such as mental arithmetic [20]. Similarly, Fink et al. [19] reported enhanced alpha ERS during imagery of improvisational dance, relative to imagery of structured waltz movements [19]. These findings suggest that alpha oscillations are reliably elevated during creative task engagement and tend to increase proportionally with task demands [18]. Contrary to earlier theoretical accounts that associated elevated alpha activity with reduced arousal or passive associative thinking, the current body of evidence suggests that increased alpha synchronization during creative cognition reflects enhanced, not diminished, cognitive processing. This growing consensus highlights the potential facilitatory role of alpha oscillations in supporting creative thought.

Transcranial alternating current stimulation (tACS) is a non-invasive brain stimulation (NIBS) technique that delivers low-intensity oscillatory currents to the scalp via electrodes, aiming to entrain endogenous cortical oscillations at targeted frequencies and thereby modulate neural activity [30, 48, 63]. tACS has provided causal evidence implicating neural oscillations in key cognitive functions, including memory consolidation, motor coordination, sensory perception, and fluid intelligence [21, 30, 47]. Alpha oscillations, in particular, are known to originate from thalamocortical and intracortical networks [9, 31], making them especially susceptible to modulation via transcranial electrical stimulation. EEG-tACS studies have demonstrated that weak microcurrents (1 ~ 2 μA) applied at alpha frequencies (8 ~ 13 Hz) can selectively enhance alpha-band activity both during and shortly after stimulation [28, 29, 73]. For instance, Castellano et al. [10] employed an interleaved EEG-tACS protocol delivering 10 Hz stimulation in 5-s intervals over a 27-min period to the parieto-occipital region [10]. Their findings showed a significant increase in individual alpha amplitude following the stimulation, further supporting the efficacy of tACS in modulating alpha oscillatory activity. Given the established association between alpha oscillations and creative thinking, it has been hypothesized that alpha-frequency transcranial alternating current stimulation (α-tACS) may enhance creativity by facilitating the generation of alpha activity. Supporting this, Lustenberger et al. [39] reported that participants receiving α-tACS demonstrated superior creative performance compared to a sham-stimulated control group [39]. Similarly, Stevens and Zabelina [58] proposed that tACS may modulate cortical excitability in ways that influence creative cognition, particularly when applied to frontal or right temporal regions [58]. Their findings suggest that neuromodulation through tACS holds promise as a tool for enhancing creative thinking. Despite these encouraging results, the specific effects of α-tACS on creative cognition remain inconclusive. Therefore, the present study aims to further investigate the potential relationship between α-tACS and creative thinking.

Brain mechanism and cognitive process of creative thinking

Creative thinking has been consistently associated with specific neural substrates, particularly within the parieto-occipital regions, which play a pivotal role in supporting cognitive processes relevant to creativity. Empirical evidence indicates that the strength of alpha oscillations in these areas increases as individuals engage in the generation of original and novel ideas [20]. Notably, during tasks that impose high creative demands—such as the Alternative Uses Task (AUT)—task-related alpha power in the parietal cortex shows a marked increase [32]. Individuals who perform well in divergent thinking tasks tend to maintain elevated levels of alpha activity in the parietal region for extended periods. Further studies reveal lateralization effects: greater alpha synchronization has been observed in the right parieto-occipital cortex compared to the left during AUT performance. Participants who generated a larger number of creative ideas exhibited significantly stronger right-lateralized alpha synchronization [6]. Complementing these findings, Eymann et al. [17] analyzed alpha dynamics across the early, middle, and late phases of divergent and convergent thinking, showing that divergent thinking is associated with greater frontal and parietal alpha synchronization following the ideation phase [17]. These results reinforce the critical role of the parietal cortex in creative cognition. Moreover, a recent study by Handel et al. [26] found that restricting motor activity can modulate occipital alpha power, which was positively linked to enhanced divergent thinking performance [26]. Collectively, these findings suggest a robust positive relationship between alpha oscillatory activity in parietal regions and creative thinking. This association is further supported by neuroimaging studies. fMRI evidence from Fink et al. [19] and Gonen-Yaacovi et al. [22] demonstrated increased activation in the parietal lobe during divergent thinking tasks, mirroring the alpha power enhancements detected via EEG [19, 22]. Taken together, these studies underscore the value of examining alpha activity within parietal and occipital cortices to better understand the neural mechanisms underpinning creative cognition.

Recent advancements in the neuroscience of creativity have shifted the research focus from isolated brain regions to the dynamic interactions among large-scale brain networks [27]. Distinct neural networks are thought to support different stages of creative cognition and to differentially influence specific facets of creative performance. Among the most extensively studied networks are the Default Mode Network (DMN) and the Executive Control Network (ECN), both of which have been strongly implicated in creative thinking [4]. The DMN comprises a set of brain regions that are more active during internally oriented cognition and resting states. These regions are associated with spontaneous thought processes, including mental simulation, autobiographical memory, and future thinking [1, 3]. Growing evidence suggests that the DMN plays a central role in idea generation and other key components of creativity [37]. Functional MRI studies employing both resting-state and task-based paradigms have identified elevated DMN activity—particularly in midline structures and the posterior inferior parietal cortex—when external cognitive demands are minimal [45]. In contrast, the ECN becomes more active during externally focused, goal-directed tasks and is essential for top-down regulation of higher-order cognitive functions, such as attentional control and conflict resolution [68]. The ECN encompasses regions in the prefrontal, parietal, and parieto-occipital cortices, which coordinate during task execution to manage complex information processing [15, 49]. These areas are particularly critical for monitoring behavior, managing cognitive resources, and resolving competing demands—functions that are vital in creative tasks requiring cognitive flexibility and adaptability [2]. Taken together, current evidence suggests that the parieto-occipital activation frequently observed during creative thinking likely reflects the dynamic interaction between components of the DMN and ECN. The integration of these networks supports both spontaneous ideation and goal-directed evaluation, thereby facilitating the emergence of creative thought.

Several theoretical models have been developed to explain the cognitive mechanisms underlying creative thinking, among which the dual-process model of creativity is particularly influential. This model distinguishes between two key stages: idea generation and idea evaluation [16]. During the idea generation phase, individuals produce a wide range of exploratory ideas based on prior knowledge and experience, largely through spontaneous associative processes. This phase is primarily associated with the fluency and flexibility of creative cognition. In contrast, the idea evaluation phase involves the critical assessment, selection, and refinement of generated ideas. It engages more deliberate, top-down cognitive processes and is linked to the originality and appropriateness of creative output [12, 27, 44, 54]. Given its role in internally directed thought, the DMN is considered central to the idea generation phase [3, 33]. Activity within the DMN facilitates divergent thinking by enhancing cognitive fluency and flexibility. Conversely, the idea evaluation phase is closely tied to the ECN, which governs cognitive control and regulatory processes. The ECN is therefore crucial for monitoring and selecting ideas, contributing to the novelty and appropriateness of creative responses [51]. Empirical evidence supports this dual-network framework. For instance, Ellamil et al. [16] conducted an experimental study distinguishing the neural correlates of the two phases of creative thinking [16]. Their findings revealed heightened activation in the occipital-parietal subregions of the DMN during idea generation, and co-activation of both frontal-parietal regions of the ECN and occipital-parietal regions of the DMN during idea evaluation. Similarly, Shofty et al. [52] demonstrated that disrupting DMN-related recognition areas through direct cortical stimulation during performance on the Alternate Uses Task (AUT) led to a significant reduction in idea fluency, while originality remained unaffected. These results provide further support for the distinct roles of the DMN and ECN in different phases of creative thinking [52]. Moreover, neurostimulation studies offer additional insights into the relationship between neural oscillations and creative cognition. Clancy et al. [11] reported that applying alpha-frequency transcranial alternating current stimulation (α-tACS) to the parieto-occipital region enhanced DMN activity [11]. This finding suggests a direct link between alpha oscillations and large-scale brain network dynamics, indicating that modulating alpha activity may facilitate idea generation by influencing neural mechanisms associated with the DMN.

Purpose and significance of the study

In summary, drawing on the established relationships among creative thinking, alpha oscillations, and α-tACS, as well as the neural and cognitive mechanisms underlying creative cognition, the present study aims to investigate whether α-tACS applied to the parieto-occipital region modulates creative thinking. To achieve this, neurophysiological techniques such as EEG and α-tACS will be employed to examine whether stimulation of the parieto-occipital region at the alpha frequency influences creative performance. This approach aims to elucidate the cognitive and neural mechanisms associated with alpha oscillatory activity in the parieto-occipital region during creative cognition, thereby enriching and refining current theoretical models of the neural basis of creativity. Accordingly, it is hypothesized that, compared to sham stimulation, α-tACS applied to the parieto-occipital region will enhance creative performance, as reflected by increased fluency, flexibility, novelty, originality and elaboration, alongside elevated alpha power.

Method

Participants

The sample size was determined using G*Power 3.1.9.7, applying a repeated measures ANOVA model for the calculation. The following parameters were specified: effect size f = 0.3, significance level α = 0.05, and statistical power 1—β = 0.8, resulting in a required sample size of 23. In total, 28 undergraduate students from Qufu Normal University participated in the experiment. The participants had an average age of 20.25 years (± 1.27), with 9 males and 19 females. All participants had normal or corrected-to-normal vision, were right-handed, had no congenital or acquired physical or mental health conditions, and had not previously participated in similar studies. Participants were randomly assigned to either the experimental or control group and received compensation upon completion of the study. Prior to participation, all individuals voluntarily signed an informed consent form. All procedures of the study received approval from the Research Ethics Board of Qufu Normal University.

Materials

The experimental task was designed to be presented using E-Prime 2.0.10 software, which served this purpose effectively. The paradigm employed was the Alternative Uses Task (AUT), a widely recognized method in the study of creative thinking. In a typical AUT, participants are asked to brainstorm novel uses for an item displayed at the center of the screen and to provide a verbal report or complete their answers once they have finished the thinking process. The experimental materials were sourced from Jauk's [32] research on creative thinking [32].

Experimental design

The present study employed a within-participant design with a single factor: type of electrical stimulation (Sham Stimulus vs. α-tACS). The independent variable was the stimulation condition, while the dependent variables included various dimensions of creative thinking: fluency, flexibility, novelty, originality, elaboration, and alpha power. Specifically, fluency was measured by the total number of creative ideas generated during the task; flexibility was assessed via the semantic distance between participants’ ideas and the target object words; novelty was evaluated based on novelty ratings of the ideas; originality was measured through originality ratings; and elaboration was assessed by the level of detail provided in each idea.

Process

The experimental design for this study was informed by the methodologies of Lustenberger et al. [39], Stevens and Zabelina [57], and Gu et al. [23]. The experiment comprised two treatments: Sham Stimulus and α-tACS. To mitigate potential carry-over effects, task variation and a wash-out interval were introduced; however, these procedures may not have completely eliminated the influence of the first treatment on the second. Participants engaged in the first treatment and subsequently completed the second treatment 24 to 48 h later. The two sessions were identical in structure, differing only during the stimulus phase. To address potential order effects, half of the participants began with the α-tACS treatment, followed by the Sham Stimulus treatment 24 to 48 h later, while the other half followed the reverse sequence. In particular, Three participants had a treatment interval of 24 h, four participants had an interval of 36 h, and twenty-one participants had an interval of 48 h. The experimental procedure is depicted in Fig. 1. The study utilized a double-blind design, ensuring that both the participants and the experimenters were unaware of the specific treatments administered. Specifically, the assignment of the two stimulation conditions was predetermined by a research assistant who was not involved in data collection or analysis, and the conditions were administered automatically via a computer program. During the experiment, the primary experimenter was responsible only for instructing participants and monitoring task progress, without knowledge of the specific condition assigned to each participant. Participants were also kept blind to the type of stimulation they received during the experiment.

Experimental Process. Participants received two types of electrical stimulation, completed an AUT task after each treatment, and provided feedback on their physical and psychological status at the end of the experiment

Stimulation stage

The electrode placement for electrical stimulation and the attention task conducted during stimulation are informed by a prior study by Clancy et al. [11]. The electrical stimulation was carried out using the NeuStim transcranial electrical stimulation system, developed by Neuracle. This wireless tES system offers three configurations: single-channel, four-channel, and eight-channel. It consists of several components, including a stimulation host, a splitter, an intelligent synchronization center, electrodes, a cap, a headband, stimulation software, and additional elements. The device is capable of delivering electrical stimulation in both tACS and sham stimulation modes, with the type of electrical stimulation selectable through the accompanying software, TXCS.

The return electrode was positioned at the POz throughout the experiment, while the input electrodes were placed at the four positions of P3, P4, O1, and O2 in a 4 × 1 configuration (with four input electrodes and one center return electrode forming a closed circuit; see Fig. 2 for a schematic representation of the electrode placement). Before starting the stimulation phase, the electrical stimulation equipment was switched on, the electrode settings and impedance were checked, and the stimulation process could begin when the electrode placement was correct and the impedance of each electrode was reduced to 10 kΩ. During stimulation, the frequency of α-tACS was set at 10 Hz, with impedance maintained below 10 kΩ. Most tACS studies use current intensities ranging from 1 to 2 mA [43, 59, 67]. However, due to limitations in the experimental equipment, which only allows for the setting of a constant current intensity, the stimulation current in the present study was set to a fixed value of 1.6 mA. Specifically, each of the four input electrodes delivered 400 μA, resulting in a total current of 1.6 mA. Due to impedance fluctuations and equipment limitations, the actual current delivered during the stimulation may have been slightly lower than the intended value. To minimize participant awareness of experimental conditions, the Sham Stimulus treatment group received α-tACS for 10 s at the beginning and end of the phase [11]. The duration of stimulation for both treatments was 20 min. Additionally, participants were required to complete a Go-Nogo task concurrently for approximately 10 min during the stimulation period [53]. This was done to ensure that participants remained attentive and alert during the stimulation process, thereby enhancing the effect of α-tACS [34]. The locations of the electrical stimulation electrodes and the flow of the Go-Nogo task are illustrated in Fig. 2. The electrical stimulation equipment was withdrawn after the stimulation procedure to circumvent any potential impact on the subsequent acquisition of EEG.

Placement of Electrical Stimulation Electrodes and Go-Nogo Task Procedure. A Placement of Electrical Stimulation Electrodes. Task stage

Before the commencement of the task phase, the primary participants withdrew the electrical stimulation device and donned the EEG acquisition device. The AUT was conducted with E-prime 2.0.10, and the task commenced with the presentation of the fixation " + " for a duration of 2 s. Thereafter, a stimulus word was presented in the center of the screen for a duration of 0.5 s, serving as a starter word. Following the presentation of the starter word, the screen displayed the stimulus word and the phrase "thinking…", at which point the participant initiated the thinking process. Once the participant has generated an idea, they should press the number "1" key to indicate their reaction. Following this, a green mark appeared in the center of the screen for 1 s, serving as a reminder for the participant to continue their thinking process. This green mark then disappeared, and the same item word was presented once more, allowing the participant to resume their thinking and generate further ideas. Once an idea had been generated, the participant should press the number "1" key to indicate their reaction. The thinking stage lasted for 2 min, during which the participant was required to generate as many novel ideas or uses as possible. This was indicated by the screen displaying the instruction "Please think of as many creative answers as you can in 2 min." At the end of the 2 min, the screen displayed "Please write the most creative two answer" [36]. During this time, the participant prepared an answer sheet on which they recorded two of their most creative answers. They then filled out the answers and press the "space" button. Subsequently, the participant pressed the space bar for a period of 5 s to maintain attention, after which they proceeded to the next trial. The task comprised a single block, comprising eight trials. The total duration of the task was approximately 30 min. To ensure full comprehension of the task content, participants were required to perform two practice trials before the commencement of the formal task. The item words presented in the task phase of the two treatments were different to minimize the influence of knowledge, experience and heuristic effects. The number of ideas generated during the EEG and thinking phases, as well as the responses associated with each idea, were recorded during the experiment. The experimental procedure is illustrated in Fig. 3.

AUT process for the task stage. EEG markers were recorded when stimuli were presented and when participants outputted thoughts for keystroke responses

Feedback

During the feedback stage, the participant was interviewed and recorded to investigate whether they could discriminate the current stimulus. Secondly, it was investigated whether any adverse reactions occurred, such as nausea or headaches.

Data analysis

Behavioral data analysis

Novelty

The participants' creative ideas, which were elicited during the Sham Stimulus and α-tACS processes, were organized in Excel and subsequently rated for novelty by four participants who had not participated in the formal experiments. This rating was conducted using the empathy assessment technique. The ideas were evaluated on a five-point Likert scale, with 1 indicating the least novel and 5 indicating the most novel. The final novelty score for each participant was calculated as the mean of the total ratings provided by the four raters. The reliability of the scoring was evaluated using a reliability analysis in SPSS 26.0, which yielded a Cronbach's α of 0.747. This value indicates that the scores assigned by the four raters exhibited a satisfactory degree of consistency. The completed dataset was imported into SPSS 26.0 for a one-way repeated-measures analysis of variance.

Flexibility

The statistical index of flexibility, Semantic Distance, was calculated using the TransDis platform developed by Yang et al. [70], which is a scoring program based on Transformer's language model that effectively evaluates the flexibility of ideas in the Chinese AUT task. The item words in the AUT task are imported into TransDis to correspond to the ideas that individuals think about. The higher the score, the greater the semantic distance between the item words and the ideas, indicating a higher level of flexibility in creative thinking [70]. In the study, the organized creative ideas were imported into the TransDis platform, and the semantic distance between creative ideas and item words was calculated via an online run. The calculated semantic distances were imported into SPSS 26.0 for a one-way repeated-measures analysis of variance.

Fluency

The number of ideas generated and the reaction time (RT) for each idea during the thinking phase of both the Sham Stimulus and α-tACS conditions, as recorded by E-Prime, were imported into Excel for preliminary processing. Ideas with an RT of 0 ms were excluded. After filtering, the valid number of ideas generated by each participant across the eight formal task trials was obtained. The cleaned dataset was then imported into SPSS 26.0 for a one-way repeated-measures analysis of variance.

Originality

The scoring procedure was identical to that used for the novelty ratings. A 5-point Likert scale was employed, ranging from 1 ("least original") to 5 ("most original"). Each participant’s final originality score was calculated as the average of the total scores assigned by four independent raters. Inter-rater reliability was assessed in SPSS 26.0 using reliability analysis, yielding a Cronbach’s α of 0.787, indicating an acceptable level of consistency among the raters. The finalized scores were then imported into SPSS 26.0 for a one-way repeated-measures analysis of variance.

Elaboration

The scoring procedure was identical to that used for the novelty ratings. A 5-point Likert scale was applied, ranging from 1 ("least elaborate") to 5 ("most elaborate"). Each participant’s final elaboration score was calculated as the average of the total scores assigned by four independent raters. Inter-rater reliability was assessed in SPSS 26.0 using reliability analysis, yielding a Cronbach’s α of 0.680, indicating an acceptable level of consistency among the raters. The finalized scores were then imported into SPSS 26.0 for a one-way repeated-measures analysis of variance.

EEG data analysis

EEG data were recorded using the Newsen W device, manufactured by Neuracle, which contains 16 electrodes that can be set according to the specific requirements of the study. Before the start of the experiment, the electrodes for EEG acquisition were installed and configured. Following the stimulation phase, the electrical stimulation equipment was removed, and the EEG acquisition equipment was applied to the participants. An impedance check was then performed. EEG acquisition could only commence when the equipment was correctly positioned and the impedance for each electrode was reduced to 10 kΩ. In this study, 16 electrodes were positioned for EEG acquisition, and the corresponding acquisition settings are illustrated in Fig. 4.

EEG Electrodes settings. 16 adjustable electrodes primarily in the frontal (AF3, AFz, AF4, F3, Fz, F4, FCz) and parieto-occipital (PO3, Poz, PO4, P3, Pz, P4, Oz) regions, of which A1 and A2 were placed in the postauricular mastoid as reference electrodes

In the experiment, EEG data were acquired at a sampling rate of 500 Hz, and preprocessing was conducted using the EEGLAB toolbox in MATLAB. The preprocessing steps included: (1) Determine the lead distribution. (2) Re-referencing: The bilateral mastoid electrodes (A1, A2) were employed as reference points for re-referencing. (3) Filtering: Trap filtering with a lower limit of 48 Hz and an upper limit of 52 Hz was initially employed to eliminate the 50 Hz IF interference. This was followed by band-pass filtering with a lower limit of 1 Hz and an upper limit of 40 Hz. (4) Independent component analysis (ICA). (5) Segmentation: Initially, the period of less than 6 s of idea thinking was removed. Subsequently, the study by Bieth et al. [8] was referenced to select two time periods of interest (ROIs). The first was selected from a time window of −1 s before stimulus presentation to 3 s after presentation, which was defined as the pre-thinking stage. The second was selected from a time window of 3 s before idea-generating keystrokes, which was defined as the post-thinking stage [8]. (6) Artifact removal: The process of eliminating artifacts, including ocular, cardiac, and EMG. A total of 28 data sets underwent preprocessing and were thus available for analysis of the EEG results.

The EEG analysis is based on the methodology described by Luft et al. [38]. The Fieldtrip extension package in MATLAB was used to process and analyze the preprocessed EEG data. The following steps constitute the analysis: (1) Data format conversion: the preprocessed EEGLAB format data was converted to Fieldtrip format data. (2) Time–frequency analysis: a time–frequency transformation was conducted using the wavelet transform method, wherein the frequency was set to 1 ~ 30 Hz, the step size was set to 0.5 Hz, and the time window was set to 6. A time–frequency analysis was conducted on the two ROIs in the preprocessed data set [38].

Results

Behavioral results

A one-way repeated-measures analysis of variance was conducted in SPSS 26.0 for novelty, flexibility, fluency, originality, and elaboration with stimulation type (Sham Stimulus vs. α-tACS) as the independent variable. (1) Novelty: A significant difference between stimulation types was found (F(1,27) = 110.472, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.804). Participants who received α-tACS had significantly higher novelty scores compared to those who received Sham Stimulus (N = 28; Msham = 2.473, SDsham = 0.067; Mα-tACS = 3.199, SDα-tACS = 0.075), indicating that α-tACS significantly enhanced novelty. (2) Flexibility: A significant difference was observed for stimulation type (F(1,27) = 22.430, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.454). Participants receiving α-tACS showed significantly greater semantic distance compared to those receiving Sham Stimulus (N = 28; Msham = 0.389, SDsham = 0.009; Mα-tACS = 0.432, SDα-tACS = 0.006), suggesting that α-tACS significantly improved flexibility. (3) Fluency: A significant difference was found between stimulation types (F(1,27) = 8.158, p = 0.008, ηp2 = 0.232). Participants in the α-tACS condition generated significantly more ideas than those in the Sham Stimulus condition (N = 28; Msham = 87.821, SDsham = 56.232; Mα-tACS = 97.964, SDα-tACS = 58.768), indicating that fluency significantly increased following α-tACS. (4) Originality: A significant difference was found between stimulation types (F(1,27) = 93.713, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.776). Participants in the α-tACS condition had significantly higher originality scores than those in the Sham Stimulus condition (N = 28; Msham = 2.100, SDsham = 0.350; Mα-tACS = 2.810, SDα-tACS = 0.384), demonstrating that α-tACS significantly enhanced originality. (5) Elaboration: A significant difference was found for stimulation type (F(1,27) = 17.210, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.389). Participants receiving α-tACS showed significantly higher elaboration scores compared to those receiving Sham Stimulus (N = 28; Msham = 1.920, SDsham = 0.216; Mα-tACS = 2.092, SDα-tACS = 0.239), indicating that α-tACS significantly improved elaboration. EEG results

Global alpha power

The Alpha band (8 ~ 12 Hz) was selected, and the two ROIs under both Sham Stimulus and α-tACS treatments were analyzed for alpha power. A 2 (Types of electrical stimulation: Sham Stimulus/α-tACS) × 2 (ROIs: Pre-Thinking Stage/Post-Thinking Stage) repeated-measures ANOVA was conducted in SPSS 26.0, with both the types of electrical stimulation and ROIs as within-participant variables, and the dependent variable was alpha power. The results showed that the main effect of electrical stimulus types was not significant (F(1,27) = 1.685, p = 0.183, ηp2 = 0.065), and there was no significant increase in individual alpha power after participants received α-tACS as compared to receiving Sham Stimulus (N = 28; Msham = 2.407 × 105, SDsham = 3.245 × 104; Mα-tACS = 2.655 × 105, SDα-tACS = 4.096 × 104); the main effect of ROIs was not significant (F(1,27) = 0.331, p = 0.570, ηp2 = 0.012), and there was no significant reduction in participants' alpha power in the later stages of thinking compared to the pre-thinking stage (N = 28; Msham = 2.505 × 105, SDsham = 3.540 × 104; Mα-tACS = 2.557 × 105, SDα-tACS = 3.680 × 104), and the interaction between types of electrical stimulation and ROIs was not significant (F(2,27) = 0.762, p = 0.391, ηp2 = 0.027). Discussion

Parieto-occipital region α-tACS can improve creative thinking to a certain extent

This study utilized EEG and tACS to examine the influential relationship between parieto-occipital region alpha transcranial alternating current stimulation (α-tACS) and creative thinking. The behavioral results indicate that α-tACS in this region has a significant impact on creative thinking, across the three macroscopic features of creativity. Furthermore, the EEG findings shed light on the cognitive neural mechanisms associated with creative thinking by observing changes in alpha neural oscillations before and after α-tACS. Overall, the EEG results revealed a significant increase in alpha power in the parieto-occipital region following α-tACS treatment compared to the baseline condition. This outcome aligns with the findings reported by Clancy et al. [11].

Based on the results of novelty, originality, and elaboration, individuals who received occipital α-tACS treatment showed significantly higher scores in novelty, originality, and elaboration compared to those who did not receive the occipital α-tACS treatment. This suggests that, to some extent, the novelty, originality, and elaboration scores of individuals improved significantly after receiving occipital α-tACS. An in-depth analysis of the reasons for this improvement in novelty, originality, and elaboration from the perspective of EEG results might suggest that the α-tACS applied to the occipital region promoted the alpha power in areas related to the executive control network of the occipital lobe. The executive control network, during the creative thinking process, inhibits spontaneous thought wandering through controlled processing, allowing for the evaluation, revision, and refinement of already generated ideas, thus leading to more creative output [27]. This aligns with existing views on the role of the executive control network in creative thinking. In divergent thinking, the brain needs to suppress external distractions and focus on internal cognitive processing [7, 40]. The combination of this function of the executive control network and the stimulation of the occipital region by α-tACS may have further optimized this process, leading to ideas with greater novelty, originality, and elaboration.

In terms of cognitive flexibility, individuals who received occipital α-tACS exhibited significantly greater semantic distances between the ideas they generated compared to those without stimulation. This finding suggests that occipital α-tACS may enhance not only novelty but also flexibility in creative thinking. From an electrophysiological perspective, two potential neural mechanisms may underlie this enhancement. First, α-tACS over the occipital cortex may increase alpha power within key regions of the occipital default mode network (DMN), promoting alpha synchronization across its subregions. As these DMN areas are implicated in spontaneous thought and the generation of remote associations, enhanced alpha oscillations in this network may facilitate the production of more distantly related concepts, thereby increasing idea flexibility. Second, paralleling the mechanism proposed for novelty, α-tACS may also upregulate alpha power in regions of the occipital executive control network (ECN). The ECN plays a critical role in evaluating and refining creative ideas by monitoring and integrating remote associations. Enhanced alpha activity in these regions may thus improve the recombination of distant concepts, resulting in ideas that are more semantically remote from the original stimulus—an established marker of cognitive flexibility. These two mechanisms are likely complementary and align with prior research on the interactive contributions of the DMN and ECN in creative cognition. The DMN has been consistently linked to associative and internally directed thought processes, while the ECN is involved in the top-down regulation, selection, and integration of these spontaneous outputs [3, 15, 49]. The current findings suggest that α-tACS modulates both networks, thereby supporting the flexible generation of creative ideas.

Regarding fluency, individuals who received occipital α-tACS generated a significantly greater number of ideas compared to those who did not receive the stimulation. This finding indicates that occipital α-tACS effectively enhances idea fluency in creative thinking tasks. From the perspective of neural mechanisms, this improvement in fluency may be attributed to increased alpha power in regions of the occipital default mode network (DMN) induced by α-tACS. The DMN is primarily associated with spontaneous, internally directed thought processes, which allow for the free generation of ideas without strong cognitive inhibition. By enhancing alpha oscillatory activity in occipital DMN regions, α-tACS may facilitate access to spontaneous associative processes and reduce constraints imposed by habitual thinking patterns or cognitive set. This neural modulation may enable individuals to break through cognitive inhibition, thereby generating a greater number of ideas and ultimately improving creative fluency.

Taken together, the behavioral and EEG findings provide converging evidence that occipital α-tACS enhances creative thinking. Specifically, it promotes improvements in fluency, flexibility, novelty, originality, and elaboration. Concurrently, individuals who received occipital α-tACS exhibited increased alpha power in the occipital region, consistent with previous findings reported by Castellano et al. [10]. Prior research has suggested that maintaining relatively high levels of alpha activity may be essential for the generation of creative ideas. The occipital region includes both low-level visual cortices (striate and extrastriate) and high-level sensory association areas, which are capable of exhibiting enhanced alpha synchronization during the generation of uncommon or original ideas. This synchronization reflects attenuated processing of external stimuli and a shift toward internal cognition, which supports the integration of existing information and the generation of novel concepts [41]. Therefore, compared to the sham stimulation condition, α-tACS-induced enhancement of alpha synchronization may more effectively activate cortical regions in the occipital lobe, thereby facilitating the production of more creative ideas. Analysis of alpha power further revealed a significant main effect of electrode location within the parietal-occipital region. Post hoc comparisons showed that alpha power was significantly greater in the right than in the left occipital-parietal region—an observation in line with Benedek et al. [6], who found that during creative thinking tasks, the right hemisphere parietal-occipital regions exhibit stronger alpha synchronization compared to the left hemisphere. These findings collectively suggest a meaningful relationship between occipital α-tACS and creative cognition, with α-tACS over the occipital cortex supporting the neural dynamics conducive to creative ideation. However, it is worth noting that the ANOVA of occipital alpha power also revealed some significant interaction effects between variables. As these interactions were not directly relevant to the primary research question concerning the impact of α-tACS on creative thinking, no further simple effects analyses or detailed discussions were conducted regarding these results.

In summary, the experimental findings demonstrate that α-tACS applied to the occipital region significantly enhances multiple dimensions of creative thinking—including fluency, flexibility, novelty, originality, and elaboration—compared to the sham condition. Moreover, in terms of neural oscillatory activity, individuals who received α-tACS exhibited significantly greater alpha power in the occipital region relative to those who did not, suggesting that α-tACS can modulate creative cognition by regulating Alpha oscillations within the occipital components of both the default mode network (DMN) and the executive control network (ECN). However, the analysis of global alpha power revealed no significant differences between the α-tACS and sham conditions, indicating that the effect of α-tACS is localized and does not influence Alpha activity in unstimulated brain regions. This suggests a region-specific influence of α-tACS on Alpha oscillatory dynamics. Given that both the DMN and ECN are composed of distributed and interconnected regions, and that the occipital-parietal cortex represents only a subset of these networks—with relevant nodes also present in frontal and other parietal areas—the exclusive use of occipital α-tACS in the present study may limit the specificity of conclusions regarding the unique contribution of this region to creative performance. Therefore, future research should build upon these findings by employing α-tACS stimulation across a broader set of cortical sites, such as the frontal cortex, to more comprehensively investigate the functional contributions of Alpha-frequency transcranial alternating current stimulation to creative thinking across distributed brain networks.

Limitations and perspectives of the research

Although the present study provides preliminary evidence that Alpha-frequency transcranial alternating current stimulation (α-tACS) over the occipital region can positively influence creative thinking, the within-participant experimental design inevitably introduces certain methodological limitations. These limitations in both the design and implementation of the experiment may have, to some extent, compromised the overall validity of the findings.

Firstly, the relatively short interval of 24 to 48 h between the two processes can lead to two key issues: (1) there is the challenge of mitigating the carry-over effect. Research has demonstrated that this effect can occur when an individual completes a behavioral task [50]. Specifically, when a person engages in an Alternative Uses Task (AUT) during the second treatment, the results can be influenced by the AUT completed in the first treatment. This phenomenon is characterized by an unconscious phase in which individuals may explore alternative activities or thoughts, allowing the brain to tackle problems without conscious effort [64]. Most previous studies have utilized pre- and post-tests of creative tasks [39] to measure this effect. However, employing such tests can lead to a more pronounced carry-over effect. In the current study, a within-participant experimental design was used, with a 24- to 48-h interval between the two experimental treatments. Importantly, the stimulus words in the task phases of both treatments were varied to minimize carry-over effects. The findings suggest that, while the carry-over effect was somewhat alleviated by regulating the time interval and changing the stimulus words, this mitigation has its limitations. It was observed that a shorter time interval might not be adequate to effectively reduce this effect. Therefore, future research might consider extending the time interval between treatments (for example, to one week or more) or implementing a between-participant experimental design to more effectively avoid carry-over effects.

(2) Limited mitigation of residual stimulation effects. Previous studies on non-invasive brain stimulation (NIBS) have raised concerns regarding the duration of aftereffects associated with transcranial alternating current stimulation (tACS). Two potential mechanisms have been proposed to explain how tACS facilitates neural modulation: (a) entrainment, and (b) spike-timing-dependent plasticity (STDP). Notably, STDP has been linked to long-term potentiation (LTP), which may induce more enduring neurophysiological changes [13, 62, 63]. In light of these concerns, many within-participant tACS studies have adopted relatively long washout periods—often one week or more—between stimulation sessions to minimize potential carry-over effects resulting from STDP [35]. In contrast, the present study implemented a shorter interval of 24 to 48 h between the two stimulation conditions, which may have been insufficient to fully eliminate residual effects. While some studies have adopted similarly short intervals—often dictated by specific research objectives—this approach is generally more appropriate for investigations into short-term cognitive effects of tACS. For instance, Yang et al. [69] employed a minimum 24-h interval to assess the impact of tACS on working memory, while Wischnewski and Compen [66] used a minimum of 48 h in studies examining decision-making processes. Following this precedent, the current study adopted a 24–48 h interval to explore the short-term cognitive effects of tACS on creative thinking. Nevertheless, potential residual effects driven by STDP cannot be entirely ruled out, and the lack of a longer inter-session interval (e.g., one week or more) remains a limitation of this study. Despite efforts to reduce carry-over effects—such as employing different sets of object-related words across sessions—the within-participant design inherently poses challenges for isolating stimulation-specific effects. Therefore, the findings of this study should be interpreted with caution, as they only suggest that α-tACS may enhance creative thinking performance to some extent under conditions of a short interventional interval.

Secondly, the present study employed a fixed-frequency 10 Hz tACS protocol, which may have influenced the effectiveness of the stimulation to some extent. In line with conventional alpha-band tACS studies, we used a commonly adopted stimulation frequency and current intensity (10 Hz, 1 ~ 2 mA) as reported in previous literature [43, 59, 67]. However, the effectiveness of tACS is known to be modulated by several individual-specific factors, such as psychological traits, current mental state, and experimental context. These factors may result in variability in individuals’ optimal alpha oscillation frequencies [61]. To improve stimulation efficacy, some recent studies have adopted personalized tACS protocols that tailor the frequency and intensity within the alpha range based on the individual's intrinsic oscillatory characteristics. This approach ensures that the stimulation targets functionally relevant frequencies for each participant [28, 29, 65]. Future research could thus benefit from applying individualized α-tACS to further explore its relationship with creative thinking and the underlying neural mechanisms. Moreover, given that tACS is a non-invasive form of brain stimulation, its capacity to precisely target neural regions and to effectively measure stimulation effects is limited compared to invasive methods (e.g., intracranial tACS). Future studies might consider refining the stimulation techniques to improve focality and efficacy. To mitigate placebo effects, we conducted brief post-session interviews during the debriefing phase, asking participants whether they could detect the stimulation and whether they experienced any adverse effects. This helped to some extent in reducing expectation biases. However, we did not assess participants’ psychological states (e.g., stress, comfort levels) at the onset of stimulation, which could have influenced their responsiveness to the stimulation. Future studies should incorporate baseline assessments of psychological factors such as stress and mood prior to stimulation, to further control for placebo effects and enhance the interpretability of stimulation outcomes.

Finally, the attention-control task used in the stimulation phase of this study was the Go-Nogo task, which may have influenced the subsequent task phase to some extent. From an experimental design perspective, the effects of the Go-Nogo task in the two stimulation phases were balanced to some degree, yet the potential impact of the task itself on creative thinking cannot be ignored. Previous research has shown that the Go-Nogo task can enhance cognitive flexibility, which may, in turn, positively influence creative performance [53]. While our original intention was to utilize an effective attention-control task to enhance the effects of tACS, we may have overlooked the potential influence of the secondary task on the primary task. Therefore, for future research involving electrical stimulation, we recommend employing more commonly used methods (e.g., eyes-closed relaxation) to ensure the effectiveness of the stimulation, minimizing any unintended effects from secondary tasks.

Prospective exploration of dual processing succession

The serial position effect of divergent thinking suggests that there exists a cyclical interplay between idea generation and evaluation during the creative process of producing and articulating ideas. This process encompasses two sequential steps for each idea: generating the idea followed by its evaluation. This transition exemplifies a systematic approach [12]. Building on this theoretical framework, the present study posits that creative idea generation and evaluation occur in succession. Research by Bieth et al. [8] and Yu et al. [72] has experimentally confirmed that an idea is articulated only after it has been both generated and evaluated, indicating a cognitive shift from generation to evaluation. This shift is encapsulated in what is referred to as the Alpha State [8, 72]. According to Yu's microstate analysis utilizing the Hidden Markov Model (HMM), the Alpha State is significantly elevated during the idea generation phase—specifically within the time frame of −0.5 to 2.5 s following stimulus generation. In this phase, the Alpha State starts at a certain level, increases, and then declines. Additionally, individuals display relatively high alpha power during this stage. Conversely, during the idea evaluation phase, which is based on individual responses from −3 to 0 s, the overall Alpha State is lower and shows a continuous decline, with alpha power remaining at a comparatively reduced level [72].

The current study offers a prospective examination of the obtained results. Although the EEG data indicated that alpha power was higher during the initial stages of creative thinking compared to the later stages in both parieto-occipital and frontal regions, the significance analysis demonstrated that this difference did not reach statistical significance. This finding stands in contrast to Yu's earlier research, which suggested that the complexities involved in transitioning from the idea generation phase to the idea evaluation phase could not be fully captured by the results of this study. In our view, there are two primary reasons for the challenge in providing a preliminary description. Firstly, the EEG acquisition in our experiment was somewhat limited, hindering our ability to capture activity throughout the entire brain. Secondly, the statistical methods we employed varied from those used by Yu and Bieth. Yu applied Hidden Markov Models (HMM) for microstate analysis, whereas Bieth utilized the Multitaper Time–frequency Transform. In our study, however, we relied exclusively on Wavelet Transform (WT) for our preliminary time–frequency analysis. This approach does not represent cognitive state transitions as distinctly as microstate analysis and does not effectively capture subtle changes in alpha power across different time courses, unlike the Multitaper method. As a result, our findings may not accurately capture significant changes in alpha power at the EEG level, given that subtle variations are more effectively analyzed through a Taper perspective. Moreover, since resting-state Alpha oscillations were not included in this study, we were unable to examine the transitions between event-related synchronization and desynchronization of Alpha oscillations. This limitation hinders our ability to address the complexities involved in creative idea processing, which encompasses both idea generation and evaluation. Future research should prioritize the analysis of the sub-states within the creative thinking process by utilizing more precise EEG signal acquisition and advanced analytical techniques. Additionally, it should aim to improve experimental designs by incorporating diverse paradigms and manipulations to explore the dynamic neural mechanisms underlying the creative thinking process, thereby enriching and refining the existing theories of creativity.

Conclusion

Under an experimental interval of 24 to 48 h between the sham stimulation and α-tACS sessions, our findings indicate a modulatory relationship between parieto-occipital alpha-frequency transcranial alternating current stimulation (α-tACS) and creative thinking. Specifically, α-tACS appears to enhance creative performance.
