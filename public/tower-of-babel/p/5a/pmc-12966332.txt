Fragmentomic liquid biopsy enables early breast cancer detection, molecular subtyping and lymph node assessment
Authors: Yuxuan Zhu, Siwei Zheng, Yinkuan Shao, Jun Zhou, Xidong Gu, Lesang Shen, Xue Li, Wenjia Liu, Wei Xue, Haiqi Lu, Jun Zhou, Jinghua Ding, Haiming Deng, Jiayin Chen, Zhuohang Yu, Yao Yao, Wenjie Xia, Wuzhen Chen, Shanshan Sun, Zheng Wang, Tianyi Qian, Xiuyan Yu, Jian Liu, Yiding Chen, Ziao Lin, Jian Huang, Chao Ni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBreast cancer remains a leading global health concern in women, while screening is still limited by imaging accessibility and reduced sensitivity in dense breasts. Here we conduct a multicenter case-control study including 503 breast cancer patients and 289 benign controls to develop TuFEst, a machine learning model based on genome-wide cell-free DNA fragmentomic features. TuFEst achieves high sensitivity (95%) and specificity (78.3%) for early cancer detection and reliably identifies malignancies missed by conventional imaging. Extension of this framework enables non-invasive molecular subtyping (TuFEst-MS) and lymph node status prediction (TuFEst-LN), with strong performance in independent validation cohorts and imaging-pathology discordant cases. Transcriptomic profiling of paired bulk tumor samples (n = 79) demonstrates that elevated TuFEst-derived cancer scores reflect tumor aggressiveness and immune-related biological programs. Together, these findings support cfDNA fragmentomics as an integrated liquid biopsy strategy for breast cancer management, enabling concurrent detection, molecular subtyping, and lymph node evaluation with potential clinical utility.Subject terms: Breast cancer, Next-generation sequencing, Machine learning

Introduction

Breast cancer (BC) is the most common malignancy and a leading cause of cancer-related mortality in women worldwide, accounting for approximately 30% of female cancers and 15% of cancer deaths1. In China, its incidence has surged, with new cases rising from 3 million in 2015 to 4.2 million in 2020, now ranking as the fourth most prevalent cancer overall2.

Early detection is critical to reduce mortality. The U.S. Preventive Services Task Force strongly endorses population-level BC screening because of its proven survival benefits3. However, global screening practices vary widely. In the U.S., annual or biennial mammography is standard for women aged 40-74, with intervals tailored to individual risks3. In contrast, China’s “Two Cancers” program (BC and cervical cancer) recommends triennial to quinquennial screenings, contingent on local resources. During 2018–2019, only 22.3% of eligible Chinese women underwent BC screening4. This limited coverage stems from both structural and clinical factors. On the infrastructure side, many primary-level hospitals lack mammography equipment, which creates significant access barriers. On the clinical side, while national guidelines favor ultrasound over mammography for better detection of typically dense breast tissue in Asian women4, this preference comes with operational challenges. Diagnostic inconsistencies lead not only to missed cancers (higher interval rates) but also to overtreatment, straining healthcare insurance funds. These disparities directly impact diagnosis timing: 24.1% of Chinese BC patients are diagnosed at Stage III+ versus 11.8% in the U.S., which significantly impairs the prognosis and increases treatment costs2.

These challenges underscore the urgent need for standardized operator-independent screening solutions. Liquid biopsy approaches, particularly cell-free DNA (cfDNA) analyses, offer a promising opportunity to overcome the current limitations. Unlike imaging modalities, cfDNA-based detection requires minimal technical expertise for sample collection and processing, making it particularly suitable for implementation in diverse healthcare settings. Current liquid biopsy approaches for BC screening – including mutation analyses and methylation profiling – face practical clinical limitations for early-stage disease. Mutation-based tests often require large blood volumes and ultra-deep sequencing because variant allele fractions are extremely low in early cancers, which limits sensitivity and inflates cost and complexity5. Meanwhile, methylation-based assays achieve high specificity but only modest stage-I sensitivity in large studies6, and typically rely on computationally intensive pipelines which restrict their widespread implementation7. In contrast, fragmentomic analyses, which profile patterns in cfDNA, including size distributions, end motifs, and fragmentation patterns, provide an alternative method that circumvents these limitations. This approach requires minimal blood volume (≤5 mL), enables high-throughput processing, and preserves tissue-specific signatures, making it ideal for population screening. While fragmentomic methods like DELFI (DNA evaluation of fragments for early interception) show multi-cancer detection potential8, early BC studies remain limited by small sample sizes, inconsistent methodologies, and underrepresentation of early-stage cases. Large-scale multicenter validation is needed to establish standardized protocols and to demonstrate their clinical utility. Recent studies have shown that cfDNA fragmentation patterns are shaped not only by nucleosome positioning but also by transcription factor (TF) binding and repeat element–associated chromatin architecture9. These signals provide complementary layers of biological information, enhancing early cancer detection beyond what can be achieved with size-based fragmentation alone. Motivated by these findings, our study integrates multiple fragmentomic modalities—including TF motif–associated fragmentation, repeat-element instability, and genome-wide nucleosome footprints—into a unified machine learning framework for early BC detection.

Here, we conducted a multicenter case-control study (NCT06016790) across diverse clinical settings, developing TuFEst (Tumor Fraction Estimator), a machine-learning framework that leverages ultra-low-coverage whole-genome cfDNA sequencing to quantify tumor burden through fragmentomic analyses. To the best of our knowledge, this represents the largest and most comprehensive investigation of fragmentomics for early-stage BC, systematically evaluating its clinical utility for screening while extending applications to molecular subtyping and lymph node metastasis prediction. Our integrated approach using a single blood test represents a potential shift toward more comprehensive and personalized BC management via cfDNA fragmentomics.

Results

Study design and clinical characteristics

Our multicenter case-control study enrolled 503 patients with histologically confirmed malignant BC (77.1% early-stage [0-IIa], n = 388) from six tertiary Grade-A hospitals and one community healthcare center, along with 289 benign controls (Supplementary Table 1). Following standardized collection protocols, we obtained peripheral blood samples from all participants, isolated approximately 1 mL of plasma per case, and performed low-pass whole-genome sequencing (WGS) at 2× average coverage. All blood samples were collected, processed, and transported in accordance with uniform standard operating procedures across participating centers to ensure preanalytical consistency. To investigate the clinical utility of cfDNA fragmentomics in early BC characterization, we developed a machine learning framework capable of simultaneously discriminating malignant from benign breast lesions, classifying molecular subtypes, and predicting lymph node metastasis status (Fig. 1).

cfDNA Fragmentation Reflects Transcription Factor Activity in Early BC

Through a comprehensive evaluation of genome-wide cfDNA fragmentation patterns across all chromosomes, we identified distinct chromatin accessibility signatures correlated with disease progression. While individuals with benign breast lesions demonstrate remarkably consistent fragmentation profiles, invasive BC cases exhibited progressively more disorganized fragmentation landscapes with increasing tumor stage. This phenomenon was detectable in Stage I tumors and became more pronounced in advanced stages (Stage II-III) and metastatic disease (Stage Ⅳ), where widespread fragmentation abnormalities were observed across multiple chromosomal regions (Fig. 2A). This heterogeneity reflects the accumulation of genomic alterations during the development and progression of BC.

Because cfDNA fragmentation reflects nucleosome positioning and TF binding, we reasoned that altered TF activity may imprint detectable footprints on plasma DNA. Building on prior fragmentomic studies showing that TF-site coverage enhances detection and biological interpretability9,10, we hypothesized that TF binding alterations contribute to early-stage fragmentation patterns. By combining chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) data from the ReMap 2020 database with our cfDNA fragmentation profiles, we performed a comprehensive comparison of TF binding site coverage between patients with BC and benign controls. Using stringent filtering criteria (read count >25, read coverage≥0.9, fold change ≤0.5, or ≥2), we identified 146 differentially fragmented genomic regions, corresponding to 844 potential TF regulators (Fig. 2B). Subsequent validation in BC cell lines (MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231; ReMap 2020) refined this set to 148 high-confidence TFs with conserved binding patterns in malignant cells (Fig. 2C).

KEGG pathway enrichment analyses revealed that these selected TFs were significantly involved in key BC-related processes, including tumor pathogenesis, endocrine therapy resistance, estrogen signaling, and transcriptional dysregulation – all hallmarks of malignant progression (Fig. 2D). Among the most significantly altered TFs are several with well-characterized roles in BC biology: FOXA1 (Forkhead Box A1), a pioneer factor critical for ERα-positive BC proliferation and endocrine resistance11; AR (Androgen Receptor), which promotes tumor progression and therapeutic resistance particularly in triple negative breast cancer (TNBC)12; HDAC2 (Histone Deacetylase 2), associated with advanced disease and poor outcomes13; RELA (RELA Proto-Oncogene NF-κB Subunit), the central NF-κB subunit driving inflammatory responses, tumor proliferation, and metastasis14; YBX1 (Y-box Binding Protein 1), a known promoter of Epithelial-Mesenchymal Transition (EMT), metastasis, and drug resistance15; ESRRA (Estrogen-Related Receptor Alpha), involved in metabolic reprogramming16; and SMARCE1 (SWI/SNF Chromatin Remodeling Factor), a chromatin remodeler frequently dysregulated in metastatic BC17. Conversely, TFs showing reduced fragmentation signals included tumor suppressors and differentiation factors, such as REST (RE1 Silencing Transcription Factor)18, CREB1 (cAMP Responsive Element Binding Protein 1)19, and CEBPB (CCAAT Enhancer Binding Protein Beta)20, which maintain normal breast tissue homeostasis and oppose malignant transformation (Table 1).

These findings revealed that cfDNA fragmentation patterns capture dual clinical insights: transcriptionally active programs driving early breast carcinogenesis and machine-learning-ready biomarkers, with the identified TF-associated signatures linking epigenetic reprogramming to actionable targets for therapy and liquid biopsy monitoring.

TuFEst model for BC detection

We developed a machine-learning framework to evaluate whether cfDNA fragmentomic features could reliably distinguish malignant from benign breast lesions. We systematically compared six algorithms: Generalized Linear Model (GLM), Support Vector Machine (SVM), eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), Multilayer Perceptron (MLP), Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM), and Stacked Ensemble Model (SEM). The TuFEst model employs a comprehensive multi-modal approach to cfDNA fragmentomic analyses, integrating (1) repeat element fragmentation (Alu, Alternating locus units; MER, Medium Efficiency Repeats; LTR, Long Terminal Repeats; families and DNA_TcMar-Tigger) derived from WGS21; (2) quantitative fragmentation indices including the N-index and E-index for DNA end characterization22; and (3) TF binding site coverage analyses capturing chromatin accessibility signatures23,24. Multi-dimensional features – encompassing end motifs (4 bp), breakpoint (6 bp), fragment-size distributions, and regulatory element coverage – were evaluated across six machine learning architectures. SEM emerged as the top-performing classifier, demonstrating a robust discriminative ability across all datasets. In the training cohort (Fig. 3B), both SEM and SVM showed comparable classification accuracies (SEM AUC = 0.942, 95% CI: 0.918-0.967; SVM AUC = 0.944, 95% CI: 0.918-0.969). This robust performance persisted in internal validation (Fig. 3C), with SEM (AUC = 0.937, 95% CI: 0.903-0.970) outperforming other models, including GBM (AUC = 0.927) and XGBoost (AUC = 0.928). Similarly, SEM maintained superior generalizability in external validation (Fig. 3D), achieving an AUC of 0.968 (95% CI: 0.941-0.995). Based on this demonstrated stability and predictive superiority across diverse datasets, we selected SEM as our final model for the subsequent analyses (Supplementary Table 2). We also evaluated the individual performance of each modality, including DELFI profiles, end-motif signatures, and repeat-element features. The results show that each modality demonstrated meaningful discriminatory power when assessed independently, whereas the integrated TuFEst model achieved the highest overall performance (Supplementary Fig. 1). Confusion matrix analyses (Fig. 3E–G) further validated the diagnostic reliability of SEM. The model demonstrated particularly strong performance in external validation, correctly classifying 91 true-positive BC cases while maintaining low misclassification rates (4 false negatives and 8 false positives). These results translated to clinically robust predictive values [positive predictive value (PPV) = 0.919, negative predictive value (NPV) = 0.930], meeting the key thresholds for practical cancer screening applications. Moreover, to evaluate the robustness of TuFEst under ultra-low-coverage conditions, we down-sampled the original 2× LP-WGS (Low-pass Whole-genome Sequencing) data to 1×, 0.5×, and 0.1× (Supplementary Table 3). Across all three datasets—training, internal validation, and external validation—the model maintained highly stable performance over the 1× and 0.5× settings. For example, AUCs changed minimally (ΔAUC ≤ 0.02 in all datasets), and sensitivities remained within 1–3% of the 2× baseline. Performance deterioration became more apparent only at 0.1×, where AUCs decreased modestly by 0.03–0.11 and specificity dropped more substantially, particularly in the external validation cohort. These findings indicate that sequencing depths around 1×–2× provide an optimal balance between cost efficiency and diagnostic accuracy, while confirming that TuFEst retains strong discriminative power even under ultra-low-coverage conditions.

Further investigation of the SEM classifier revealed the key fragmentomic signatures driving its diagnostic performance. Notably, we identified significant enrichment of particular 4 bp (e.g., AAGC, AGGC) end motifs and 6 bp (e.g., CTTGTT, TGGGAC) breakpoint motifs in BC samples, which showed marked differences between malignant and benign lesions (all P < 0.001). In addition, repetitive genomic elements with Alu sequences (e.g., AluSx and AluY), long terminal repeats (e.g., LTR12C, LTR37B), and satellite repeats (e.g., Tigger4b) all showed significant frequency alterations in BC patients compared to benign controls (all P < 0.001). Hierarchical clustering analyses demonstrated that these combined fragmentomic features achieved robust separation of malignant and non-malignant samples (Supplementary Fig. 2), strongly supporting their value as biomarkers for cfDNA-based diagnostic applications.

Comparative analyses of TuFEst-derived cancer scores revealed significantly elevated values for malignant cases across all cohorts versus benign controls (training set 9.98-fold; internal validation 4.49-fold; external validation 10.4-fold). Notably, the median cancer scores demonstrated remarkable consistency between cohorts (training set 0.8676; internal validation 0.8671; external validation 0.8659), indicating the robust quantitative performance of the TuFEst algorithm (Fig. 3H–J).

To evaluate TuFEst’s potential in addressing imaging false-negatives, we analyzed blood samples from 26 early-stage BC patients from four hospitals (SAHZU, HZFH, ZJTCM, and LHLI) who had been classified as BI-RADS 3 on both ultrasound and mammography during routine screening. Despite their initially benign imaging assessment, all patients subsequently underwent biopsy because of lesion enlargement within six months, with pathological confirmation of invasive carcinoma. In a single-blind validation using blood samples collected at the time of initial imaging, TuFEst correctly identified 25 of the 26 cases (96.2% sensitivity). Notably, all detected cases represented early-stage cancers (DCIS n = 13; pT1a n = 1; pT1b n = 2; pT1c n = 10) without lymph node involvement, demonstrating both the model’s high sensitivity for early-stage BC detection and its potential to minimize false-negative imaging (Fig. 3K, L).

Collectively, these results demonstrate the clinical validity of our cfDNA fragmentomic approach, achieving high diagnostic accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity in distinguishing malignant from benign breast lesions, thereby addressing a critical unmet need in current screening paradigms.

cfDNA fragmentomics predicts BC molecular subtypes

BC treatment decisions are fundamentally guided by molecular subtyping based on hormone receptor and HER2 status, as distinct subtypes demonstrate characteristic biological behaviors and tumor microenvironment profiles25. Although liquid biopsies have shown promise in detecting tumor-associated genetic alterations, the ability of cfDNA fragmentomic patterns to discriminate molecular subtypes remains unexplored. To address this gap, we developed a fragmentomics-based machine learning classifier, TuFEst-MS (TuFEst-Molecular Subtype), designed to non-invasively predict BC molecular subtypes using cfDNA fragmentation profiles. Patients with BC were classified into three molecular subtypes: ER+/PR+HER2-, HER2+ (triple-positive and HER2-enriched), and TNBC (ER−PR−HER2−). Given the limited size of TNBC and HER2-enriched subgroups, we combined all available samples and used a unified training-validation split rather than partitioning into separate internal and external validation sets. The TuFEst-MS model demonstrated robust predictive performance across all subtypes, with training set AUCs of 0.906 (95% CI: 0.872-0.940) for ER+/PR+HER2-, 0.925 (95% CI: 0.890-0.961) for HER2+, and 0.891 (95% CI: 0.820-0.963) for TNBC (Fig. 4B). Remarkably, the performance of the validation set showed excellent consistency, achieving AUCs of 0.939 (95% CI: 0.907–0.970), 0.925 (95% CI: 0.887–0.964), and 0.893 (95% CI: 0.813–0.974) for the respective subtypes (Fig. 4C). This minimal performance variance between the training and validation sets confirms the robustness and generalizability of the TuFEst-MS classifier.

Our fragmentomic analyses revealed that subtype-specific patterns in end motifs, breakpoint motifs, CNV-related features, and repetitive element distributions were the key discriminative features in the TuFEst-MS model (Supplementary Fig. 3). These findings demonstrate that cfDNA fragmentation profiles can mirror the transcriptional and genomic heterogeneity underlying different BC subtypes.

Notably, molecular profiles (e.g., hormone receptor/HER2 status) often differ between primary and metastatic lesions owing to tumor evolution and heterogeneity26. While the current ABC5 (5th ESO-ESMO international consensus guidelines for advanced breast cancer) guidelines recommend treatment strategies based on metastatic lesion profiling, biopsy inaccessibility frequently limits clinical implementation27. To address this, we evaluated TuFEst-MS in 21 oligometastatic patients from two centers (SAHZU and SRRZU) (Supplementary Table 4). The model achieved an accuracy of 85.7% (18/21) in predicting metastatic lesion subtypes (Fig. 4D), including an 87.5% accuracy (7/8) in primary-metastatic discordant cases. Three classification discrepancies were observed, all involving HER2 status interpretation: one biopsy-confirmed ER+/PR+/HER2+ (IHC 3 + ) case was misclassified as HER2-negative by TuFEst-MS; two metastatic lesions confirmed as HER2-negative (IHC 2 + /FISH-) were incorrectly predicted as HER2-positive. These discordances mirror the well-documented challenges in HER2 assessment28, where spatial intratumoral heterogeneity and temporal expression dynamics during disease progression frequently yield discordant pathological results based on biopsy samples.

cfDNA fragmentomics for lymph node metastasis detection

The accurate detection of metastatic lymph node (mLN) remains a significant clinical challenge in early BC management. While mLN represents the most common initial metastatic event, conventional imaging modalities (ultrasound, mammography, and MRI) demonstrate suboptimal accuracy29, potentially leading to dual clinical consequences: missing the opportunity for neoadjuvant therapy for node-positive patients while subjecting node-negative cases to unnecessary axillary surgeries. Considering that mLN reflects more aggressive tumor biology, likely to release greater amounts of circulating tumor DNA, we sought to explore cfDNA fragmentomic signatures for LN status assessment – an approach that has merely been investigated to date. In our cohort of 115 mLN BC patients, we developed the TuFEst-LN (TuFEst-Lymph Node) model through comprehensive training (n = 250) and validation (n = 154) analyses (cohort allocation summarized in Fig. 5A). The model demonstrated strong discriminatory performance, achieving AUCs of 0.900 (95% CI: 0.856-0.945) in the training set and 0.874 (95% CI: 0.816-0.931) in the validation (Fig. 5B, C). Detailed evaluation revealed robust NPV (95.2% in training and 90.3% in validation), although PPV was more moderate (51.5% and 54.9%, respectively), likely due to the relatively low axillary tumor burden in our cohort, as 62.8% of all LN-positive patients were classified as pN1 (Fig. 5D, E).

To further assess clinical utility, we applied TuFEst-LN to two challenging patient groups with discordant imaging and pathological findings that were not included in the training or validation sets. Among cases with imaging-negative but pathology-confirmed LN involvement (Group A), the model correctly identified 60% (24/40) as LN-positive. Conversely, in patients with imaging-positive but pathology-negative results (Group B), 97.6% (83/85) were accurately classified as LN-negative (Fig. 5F). These independent validations confirmed the model’s excellent performance in LN assessment, with particularly high specificity in identifying patients without mLN (NPV = 97.6%), which could potentially support clinical decisions to omit axillary surgery in cN0 patients with negative TuFEst-LN predictions.

TuFEst cancer scores reflect BC molecular subtype aggressiveness

Although stringent standardization of sample collection and processing protocols was implemented across all participating centers, we observed notable inter-sample variability in cancer scores. Given the well-established differences in clinical aggressiveness among BC molecular subtypes, we performed a stratified analysis comparing cancer score distributions across the ER+/PR+HER2-, HER2-positive, and TNBC groups. Using multiple threshold definitions for high (top 10–30%) and low (bottom 10-30%) cancer score groups, we consistently found that HER2-positive and TNBC subtypes were significantly enriched in high cancer score populations across all cut-off values (Supplementary Fig. 4A). In contrast, low cancer score distributions remained relatively balanced across molecular subtypes (Supplementary Fig. 4B). These findings corroborate the known biological behavior of TNBC and HER2-positive tumors, which demonstrate greater metastatic potential even at early disease stages compared to the ER+/PR+HER2- subtype, and validate the TuFEst model’s ability to identify high-risk molecular subtypes at early detection phases, potentially facilitating timely interventions to improve patient prognosis.

Transcriptomic profiling links TuFEst-derived cancer scores to BC biology

Because cfDNA fragmentation is closely linked to chromatin accessibility and transcriptional activity, transcriptomic profiling provides a direct means to interpret the biological programs underlying TuFEst-derived Cancer Scores and further enables inference of the tumor immune microenvironment through an established deconvolution framework. Thus, to molecularly characterize early-stage BC detected by liquid biopsy, we performed bulk RNA sequencing on 79 matched primary tumors from patients whose blood samples were used to develop the TuFEst model. Focusing on the top and bottom 20% of cases based on cancer scores (n = 15 per group), we found no significant differences in clinicopathological features between the high- and low-score groups (Supplementary Table 5), ensuring comparability for further analyses. Transcriptomic profiling revealed distinct biological patterns between the groups. Both the xCell and ESTIMATE algorithms—widely used and well-validated tools for inferring tumor microenvironment composition—consistently showed significantly higher immune infiltration (xCell: P = 0.0453; ESTIMATE: P = 0.0408) but comparable stromal scores in high-score tumors (Fig. 6A). CIBERSORT analyses identified plasma cells (P = 0.0404) and resting mast cells (P = 0.0179) as being enriched in low-score tumors – a cellular profile characteristic of immunologically “cold” microenvironments30. In contrast, high-score tumors exhibited increased infiltration of cytotoxic immune populations (T cells, NK cells) and M0/M1 macrophages, suggesting enhanced immune activation even at early disease stages and potentially indicating favorable responses to immunotherapy regimens (Fig. 6B).

Tumors from the high-score group exhibited significant enrichment across multiple malignancy-associated hallmark pathways31, revealing a coordinated activation of proliferative programs (E2F Targets, G2M Checkpoint, MYC Targets V1/V2), microenvironment remodeling processes (Angiogenesis, EMT), metabolic reprogramming (glycolysis), and diverse immune-inflammatory networks (Inflammatory Response, TNF-α Signaling, IFN-γ Response) (Fig. 6C). The top differentially expressed genes have been functionally implicated in fostering a pro-tumorigenic microenvironment by inducing chronic inflammation (S100A8, S100A9, CXCL8), immune suppression (BCL2A1, OSM, TNFAIP3), and tumor cell survival (BCL2A1, S100A8, S100A9)32–37 (Fig. 6D). These findings were strongly corroborated by gene set enrichment analyses, where EMT (NES = 2.647, FDR < 0.001), Inflammatory Response (NES = 2.800, FDR < 0.001), and TNF-α/NF-κB signaling (NES = 3.026, FDR < 0.001) emerged as the most significantly activated pathways in high-scoring tumors (Fig. 6E). Collectively, our bioinformatic analyses support the notion that our cfDNA fragmentomic pattern-based model effectively captures the critical biological characteristics of BC, especially those linked to great metastatic potential and immune microenvironment remodeling.

Discussion

Our multicenter case-control study suggests that cfDNA fragmentomics is a powerful approach for early BC detection, with TuFEst, a machine-learning–based model, demonstrating superior sensitivity and specificity. By leveraging genome-wide fragmentation patterns rather than relying on specific mutations, TuFEst aims to address the fundamental limitations of conventional liquid biopsy methods while providing clinically actionable biological insights beyond simple malignancy classification. Our validation of fragmentomics for molecular subtyping and LN assessment addresses potential gaps in the current diagnostic paradigms. This is a large multicenter case-control study validating cfDNA fragmentomics for early BC diagnosis, with robust performance maintained across diverse clinical settings and patient populations.

The clinical implications of our findings are potentially significant in China’s healthcare landscape, where early BC detection is often challenged by limited access to mammography and shortages of experienced ultrasound technicians in primary care settings. While Artificial Intelligence(AI)-enhanced radiomics has improved diagnostic accuracy and cost-effectiveness in recent studies, existing implementations still rely heavily on mammography—an imaging modality that remains unavailable in many primary healthcare institutions and offers limited biological insight beyond basic benign/malignant classification38,39. Moreover, in real-world early detection, the optimal balance between sensitivity and specificity must be tailored to the clinical context. For population-level screening, particularly among Asian populations with dense breast tissue and suboptimal mammographic performance, higher sensitivity is essential to minimize the risk of missed early-stage cancers. Since our cfDNA fragmentomics approach is intended to complement routine ultrasound at the initial diagnostic stage, adopting a higher-sensitivity threshold supports timely referral to MRI or biopsy and reduces the likelihood of false-negative outcomes. At the same time, maintaining reasonable specificity is crucial to avoid unnecessary imaging or invasive procedures. In this regard, the TuFEst model achieves a clinically practical balance (specificity: 0.783 in training database; 0.869 in external validation database), enabling flexible integration across diverse healthcare settings and diagnostic workflows.

Current liquid biopsy methodologies primarily rely on Next-Generation Sequencing (NGS) mutation panels, methylation-based assays (such as bisulfite sequencing), and fragmentomic approaches. While targeted NGS mutation panels of cfDNA have proven useful in advanced cancer management, they struggle for early detection. The extremely low tumor DNA fraction in early-stage malignancies (typically ~0.01% mutant allele frequency) presents a fundamental limitation, as even high-volume blood sampling with deep sequencing may fail to identify most early cancers. Additional challenges arise from background noise caused by clonal hematopoiesis (non-tumor blood mutations) and technical artifacts from PCR/sequencing processes, necessitating sophisticated error-correction algorithms40. Methylation-based assays, while circumventing the need for specific mutations and demonstrating high specificity in multi-cancer detection, are hampered by the destructive nature of bisulfite conversion, which degrades approximately 90% of input DNA41. Consequently, methylation assays achieve only moderate sensitivity for early-stage cancers, with reported Stage I detection rates of 50-76% for colorectal and lung malignancies41. Recent enzymatic and nanopore-based approaches, such as Enzymatic Methyl-seq (EM-seq), TET-assisted pyridine borane sequencing (TAPS), and Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT) direct 5mC profiling, have been proposed to overcome the DNA-damage and conversion inefficiencies of bisulfite-based workflows. However, as summarized in Supplementary Data 1, these emerging methods still remain constrained by high sequencing-depth requirements, reagent and hardware costs, and limited large-scale clinical validation—particularly for early breast-cancer detection. In contrast, fragmentomic approaches offer an orthogonal, sequencing-depth-efficient strategy by analyzing genome-wide cfDNA fragmentation features that mirror the chromatin architecture of the originating tissues. Low-pass sequencing is inherently well-suited for fragmentomics because its biological signal arises from genome-wide coverage variability, nucleosome-positioning patterns, and sequence-context features, rather than from high-depth variant detection such as single-nucleotide variants or variant allele frequencies8,9. Importantly, increasing sequencing depth yields diminishing returns, as fragmentomic features tend to plateau once sufficient uniformity of coverage has been achieved. Using advanced AI algorithms, these multidimensional features can be integrated to amplify subtle cancer signatures. Notably, fragmentome-based assays have demonstrated exceptional sensitivity (95%) for early detection of liver, colorectal, and lung cancers42,43, though their performance in BC detection requires further elucidation. While the DELFI model has shown promising results in pan-cancer screening, particularly for gastrointestinal malignancies, its performance in BC detection was suboptimal (70% sensitivity and 57% specificity), potentially due to the limited cohort size of 54 BC cases8.

Our study advances this field by systematically analyzing cfDNA fragmentomes in patients with BC and benign breast lesions using comprehensive genome-wide profiling and multifactorial AI modeling. We observed significant variability in cfDNA fragmentation patterns among cancer patients, with progressive intensification as disease progressed. Paired tissue RNA-seq analyses revealed elevated cancer scores in high-risk subgroups, including TNBC, HER2-positive tumors, and those with EMT features, validating the TuFEst model’s alignment with aggressive tumor biology. Beyond confirming the clinical validity of cfDNA fragmentomics for early detection, our findings extend its potential applications to molecular subtyping and LN status evaluation – capabilities that surpass current liquid biopsy platforms. Looking forward, the integration of fragmentomics into routine screening holds particular promise for advancing early detection paradigms, especially for aggressive subtypes where timely intervention is critical.

In addition, our work provides an alternative approach to address the evolving challenges in axillary management for patients with early-stage BC. The demonstrated 97.6% NPV of our TuFEst-LN model could directly address the current clinical dilemmas in nodal staging, where conventional approaches struggle to balance accuracy and invasiveness. Current clinical practice for axillary management reflects an evolving paradigm, as evidenced by trials such as SOUND (Sentinel Node vs. Observation after Ultrasound in Node-negative BC)44, INSEMA (Intergroup Sentinel Mamma)45 and SOAPET (Sentinel Node Biopsy vs. Observation After Axillary PET Examination)46, which have proven the safety of omitting sentinel LN biopsy in selected cN0 patients. However, the NATALEE (New Adjuvant Trial with Ribociclib [LEE011]) trial47 revealed concerning recurrence rates in patients with 1-3 positive nodes, highlighting the need for accurate LN staging to guide the intensification of adjuvant treatment in high-risk cases. This clinical dilemma persists because conventional radiology evaluation of LN status remains limited (ultrasound sensitivity: 50–60%; specificity: 90%)48, while advanced imaging modalities like lymph node-specific PET-CT face prohibitive cost barriers to widespread adoption49. Consequently, surgical axillary staging remains the standard practice for cN0 patients, particularly for HR+/HER2- patients eligible for ribociclib treatment. Our cfDNA fragmentomics approach offers a potential solution for this clinical impasse. Despite training predominantly on pN1 cases (62.8%), the TuFEst-LN model achieved 90.3% NPV in the validation cohort and, more importantly, demonstrated 97.6% NPV in an independent cohort of imaging-discordant cases. This exceptional performance in ruling out LN metastasis could enable more confident surgical de-escalation for cN0 patients while simultaneously identifying those who would benefit from LN assessment and subsequent systemic therapy intensification.

In addition to addressing LN staging challenges, our study demonstrates that cfDNA fragmentomic signatures can accurately distinguish BC molecular subtypes using the TuFEst-MS model. This breakthrough demonstrates the unique clinical versatility of our approach, which simultaneously predicts malignancy status, molecular subtype, and LN metastasis, thereby addressing multiple unmet needs in contemporary BC management. Our approach’s rapid turnaround (72 h versus 7 days for IHC) could significantly accelerate treatment decisions, particularly for patients being considered for axillary de-escalation strategies. For operable cT1-2N0 patients, the concurrent availability of molecular subtype and LN status information facilitates a more precise screening for clinical trial eligibility and neoadjuvant therapy planning. Importantly, this non-invasive approach may prove invaluable for patients with limited mobility or contraindications to invasive procedures, such as identifying candidates for endocrine monotherapy.

Although these findings represent significant progress in early BC detection, several limitations merit consideration. The relatively small proportion of DCIS cases (7.95%) in our cohort precluded a robust evaluation of the method’s performance for this precursor lesion. Additionally, we did not explore the potential correlations between cfDNA fragmentation patterns and specific tumor mutations (e.g., BRCA1/2, ATM, and PALB2). Additionally, triple-positive BC (ER⁺/PR⁺/HER2⁺) was grouped within the HER2⁺ subtype due to their small representation in our cohort, limiting our ability to assess them as an independent subgroup. Most crucially, our method was specifically designed for patients with radiologically detectable lesions; for those without imaging findings, this approach may not be applicable. Additionally, the ability of TuFEst to detect radiologically occult BC requires validation in prospective population-based screening studies. Despite these limitations, our multicenter investigation provides compelling evidence that machine-learning-driven cfDNA fragmentomics is a powerful, noninvasive tool for comprehensive BC assessment. The ability of the technology to accurately detect early-stage malignancies, stratify molecular subtypes, and identify negative LN metastasis with high confidence represents a potential advance toward precision screening and personalized therapeutic decision-making.

Methods

Ethical statements

All procedures involving human participants complied with all relevant ethical regulations and were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All datasets were collected under institutional review board approval (Approval No.2023-1048 by the Second Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine, Approval No.IIT-20230612-0118-01 by Hangzhou First People’s Hospital, Approval No.2024-KLS-013-02 by Zhejiang Provincial Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine, Approval No.2024-033 by Ningbo Medical Center Lihuili Hospital, Approval No.IRB-20230357-R by Women’s Hospital School of Medicine, Zhejiang University, Approval No.2023-0548 by Sir Run Run Shaw Hospital, Approval No.2024-002 by XiangFu Community Health Service Center). All data were de-identified before model development. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to study participation.

Study population

This study was designed as a prospective multicenter case-control study (NCT06016790) that continuously enrolled 839 participants, including 550 BC patients and 289 benign controls from seven Chinese medical institutions: the Second Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine (SAHZU), Hangzhou First People’s Hospital (HZFH), Zhejiang Provincial Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine (ZJTCM), Ningbo Medical Center Lihuili Hospital (LHLI), Women’s Hospital School of Medicine Zhejiang University (WHZJU), Sir Run Run Shaw Hospital (SRRSH), and Xiangfu Community Health Service Center (XFCHC). Eligible participants were females aged 18-70 years with pathologically confirmed diagnoses of either: (1) primary invasive breast carcinoma or carcinoma in situ, or (2) benign breast lesions (fibroadenoma/adenosis/fibrocystic hyperplasia). All participants provided their plasma samples. Exclusion criteria included pregnancy/lactation, prior malignancies (5-year window), suspected but unconfirmed malignancies within the preceding year, recent blood transfusions (30-day window), active mastitis, and other investigator-determined contraindications.

Sample collection and processing

Peripheral blood (8–10 mL) was collected in specialized cfDNA tubes (Ardent BioMed Cat. # BY10240301) during routine blood tests or before surgery. Samples were subjected to dual centrifugation (1,600 × g → 16,000 × g, 10 min each at 4 °C) for plasma isolation, followed by immediate storage at −80 °C. Frozen plasma was batch-shipped on dry ice to the central laboratory (OmixScience Research Institute, Hangzhou, China) for processing.

cfDNA assays and whole-genome sequencing

cfDNA was extracted from a median plasma volume of 1 mL using the VAMNE MagUltra circulating cell-free DNA isolation kit (Vazyme Biotech, Nanjing, China, Cat. # N913) according to the manufacturer’s protocol, followed by precise quantification using a Qubit 4.0 fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Lenexa, KS, USA) and storage at −80 °C. For library preparation, 5–20 ng of the extracted cfDNA was processed with the VAHTS Universal DNA library prep kit for Illumina V3 (Vazyme Biotech, Nanjing, China, Cat. # N610) and barcoded using VAHTS multiplex oligos set 5 for Illumina (Vazyme Biotech, Nanjing, China, Cat. # N322). The quality and integrity of the libraries were evaluated using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) to confirm that the libraries met the necessary standards for optimal fragment size distribution. The final libraries were sequenced on the NovaSeq X Plus platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) using paired-end 150 bp reads, with an average coverage depth of 2× for the cfDNA samples.

Raw WGS data underwent rigorous quality control beginning with FastQC (version 0.12.1, https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc) evaluation, followed by quality filtering and adapter trimming using Cutadapt (version 4.5, https://github.com/marcelm/cutadapt) and Ktrim (version 1.4.1, https://github.com/hellosunking/Ktrim). The processed reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh37) using BWA-MEM (version 0.7.17, https://github.com/lh3/bwa) with default settings, ensuring that only high-quality reads with a Phred score above the threshold were retained. Further analyses were restricted to uniquely mapped reads that were free of PCR duplicates. These mapped reads were subsequently sorted and indexed using Samtools (version 1.9, https://github.com/samtools/samtools), and duplicates were identified and eliminated using Picard (version 2.18.29, https://github.com/broadinstitute/picard). The distribution of fragment sizes and relevant genomic features was inferred based on the coordinates of mapped read pairs, following established protocols using Picard. To identify large-scale epigenetic variations in cfDNA fragmentation across the genome, which can be detected with low-coverage WGS, the hg19 reference genome was segmented into non-overlapping 5 Mb bins.

Fragmentomic feature identification

We systematically quantified the frequencies of 4-bp end motifs and 6-bp breakpoint motifs, normalizing total motif occurrences to unity within each category to enable comparative analyses. For the 6 bp motifs, we characterized the 3 bp sequences flanking cfDNA 5’ breakpoints (both upstream and downstream) and established genome-wide distribution profiles. To maintain consistency, the overall frequency of all 6 bp breakpoint motifs was normalized to one. We further organized the motif features into an I × J matrix, where each row corresponds to a participant identified by their sample ID. This matrix format allows for efficient interpretation and comparison of motif patterns within the cohort, leading to deeper insights into cfDNA motif distributions.

Regional fragment size analyses employed 100 kb genomic bins using Deeptools (version 3.1.2, https://github.com/deeptools/deepTools), optimized to balance resolution with sufficient read depth (~25,000 reads/bin). Each bin contained short (S, 100–150 bp) and long (L, 151-220 bp) fragments. To ensure data accuracy, regions overlapping the ENCODE blacklist or the hg19 gap track from the UCSC Genome Browser were excluded, as these regions typically show poor mappability. This approach yielded robust S/L ratios for the fragmentation pattern analyses. By focusing on fewer, higher-quality bins, we improved the reliability of our fragment-size distribution analyses.

Alu repeats analyses of cfDNA

We categorized all elements from the gene and pseudogene families as transfer RNA (tRNA), signal recognition particle RNA (srpRNA), small nuclear RNA (snRNA), small cytoplasmic RNA (scRNA), and ribosomal RNA (rRNA). Likewise, we grouped elements from the DNA, retroposon, and Rolling Circle (RC) families into the transposable element category. This led to eight primary groups for analysis: LINE, SINE, Medium Repeat (MER), DNA_TcMar-Tigger (DNA Transposable elements), Long Terminal Repeat (LTR), satellites, transposable elements, and RNA elements. In our cfDNA feature analyses and validation, we focused on critical elements, including Alu sequences, long terminal repeats (LTRs), RNA elements, and transposable elements. Through this systematic classification, we adopted a thorough approach to understand cfDNA features, which are essential for investigating their roles in BC. The TuFEst algorithm integrates these repeat elements with the N-index and E-index and incorporates TF features to predict non-cancerous and cancerous status.

Machine learning and deep learning algorithm model

In this study, TuFEst was implemented as a binary classification model50. It outputs a “cancer score” between 0 and 1, representing the probability that a plasma cfDNA sample originates from an individual with cancer. A cutoff of 0.5 was applied to this score to classify samples as “cancer” or “non-cancer.” Although the score is a surrogate measure of the relative burden of tumor-derived DNA and the name suggests its long-term potential for quantification, the current application and validation focus on classification performance.

A GLM with a binomial distribution and a logit link function, equivalent to logistic regression, was developed for binary classification. The model utilized fragmentomics features as predictors, with the outcome variable coded as 1 for malignant and 0 for benign status. To address potential sample dependencies, group information was incorporated using SampleID. The analytical pipeline included feature standardization (StandardScaler) followed by logistic regression with a maximum of 1000 iterations. Model performance was assessed through 10-fold stratified cross-validation with shuffling (random state = 42) to ensure reproducibility while maintaining class balance across folds.

A SVM with a linear kernel was implemented for classification. The model employed standardized fragmentomics features, with malignant status encoded as 1 and benign as 0. Sample identifiers were retained for group-level analysis. The pipeline consisted of feature scaling followed by SVM classification with probability estimation enabled. Performance was evaluated using 10-fold stratified cross-validation, with metrics including accuracy, AUC, sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV. Probability thresholds at specificity levels of 0.9 and 0.95 were derived from ROC analysis to support clinical decision-making. All predictions and probabilities were systematically recorded for aggregated analysis.

An XGBoost classifier was implemented using a reproducible pipeline (random state = 42). Fragmentomics features were standardized prior to model training, with binary outcomes indicating malignant (1) or benign (0) status. The model was evaluated using 10-fold stratified cross-validation, with comprehensive metrics recorded for each fold. Clinical decision thresholds were established by identifying probability values corresponding to 0.9 and 0.95 specificity levels from ROC curves. All predictions and sample metadata were stored for subsequent analysis, and the final pipeline was saved for potential future use.

A MLP neural network was developed with fixed random state (42) for reproducibility. Features were standardized before being input to the MLP classifier. The model was evaluated using 10-fold stratified cross-validation, with performance metrics including accuracy, AUC, sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV. Probability thresholds for specific specificity levels (0.9 and 0.95) were determined from ROC analysis to define clinical decision boundaries. The trained pipeline, including preprocessing and classification steps, was preserved for potential application, and overall discriminative ability was validated through aggregated ROC analysis.

A GBM was implemented using scikit-learn’s GradientBoostingClassifier. The model was trained on standardized fragmentomics features and evaluated via 10-fold stratified cross-validation (random state = 42). Each fold yielded probability estimates and performance metrics, including accuracy, AUC, sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV. Clinical thresholds were identified by setting specificity levels at 0.9 and 0.95 in ROC analysis. Predictions, true labels, and group identifiers were stored for combined analysis, and the final model was saved for potential deployment.

A SEM integrated predictions from five base learners-SVM, GLM, GBM, XGBoost, and MLP-using a logistic regression meta-learner. The pipeline included feature standardization and was evaluated through 10-fold stratified cross-validation. Performance metrics were calculated for each fold, and probability thresholds were established at specificity levels of 0.9 and 0.95. The ensemble demonstrated strong discriminative ability (AUC = 0.943), with all predictions and sample information systematically recorded. The complete pipeline was saved to support potential clinical implementation.

For tumor detection and classification, we screened six distinct machine learning algorithms to build the models: GLM, SVM, XGBoost, MLP, GBM, and SEM. These models were rigorously trained and evaluated using bootstrapping combined with 10-fold cross validation. During the 10-fold cross-validation process, the training set was randomly divided into 10 subsets, with nine subsets used for training and the remaining one used for testing, cycling 10 times to ensure that each subset was used for testing once. We only used training cohort samples from benign individuals and cancer patients, training classifiers in the machine learning algorithms with features such as motif feature frequencies, and generating models to predict cancer scores for each sample. Notably, all validation datasets remained untouched during model training. The cancer score ranged from 0 to 1, with a higher score indicating a greater probability of cancer. After the evaluation, we selected the best-performing model for the downstream analyses.

These models were subsequently applied to the validation datasets to generate cancer prediction scores for each validation sample and assess the model performance. We compared the AUC values of the different models in the validation cohort and the sensitivity/specificity at a fixed specificity threshold in the internal validation cohort.

Three specialized models have been developed for different clinical applications. The TuFEst model was designed to distinguish between benign individuals and cancer patients using a stacked ensemble that incorporates feature information, including TF binding site coverage, to enhance predictive accuracy. The TuFEst-MS model employs a GLM method to classify three BC subtypes: ER+/PR+, HER2+, and TNBC. This classification capability is particularly important for predicting the subtype of metastatic lesions in patients with BC. The TuFEst-LN model utilized GLM to predict whether a patient had mLN, specifically for analyzing patients with radiological evidence of mLN but no surgical confirmation, or those with discordant imaging-pathological results. All models underwent rigorous training using 10-fold cross validation, with prediction scores calculated for each sample. The models were assessed using the AUC-ROC metric to ensure their accuracy and reliability in practical applications.

Selection and analyses of differential features

We utilized the wilcox.test() function to perform the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, assessing the statistical significance of differences in various features across different classifications. The parameter exact = FALSE indicates the use of an approximate computational method. Features with a p-value less than 0.05 were selected to ensure statistical significance and eliminate the influence of random factors. Additionally, we applied a fold change threshold greater than 1, retaining only features that exhibited significant differences between the two groups. Subsequently, the top and bottom 30 features were identified, and the median ratio between the benign and malignant groups was calculated for each feature. Finally, a standardized feature heatmap was generated using the pheatmap() function from the pheatmap package, while the scale() function was applied for Z-score normalization, enhancing visualization and facilitating further analyses.

Genome-wide TF analyses

ChIP-seq peaks from 5620 experiments were downloaded from the ReMap 2020 database (https://remap2020.univ-amu.fr/download_page), selecting the CRMs (Cis-Regulatory Modules) data type, which includes a total of 1,732,560 peaks.

For each autosomal peak, the peak center was designated as position 0, and the mean coverage was computed for each sample within a ± 1,000 bp window relative to the peak center. The processed data were then incorporated as a feature into the model training pipeline to distinguish between benign individuals and patients with cancer.

A series of filters were applied to ensure sufficient sequencing depth across benign and cancer samples, retaining only peaks with read counts >25, read coverage ≥0.9, and fold change ≤0.5 or ≥2.

To investigate the biological processes associated with the identified TFs, we performed KEGG pathway enrichment analyses using the clusterProfiler package in R. TFs were first mapped to Entrez IDs, and the KEGG enrichment analysis was applied to identify significantly enriched pathways. Pathways were ranked based on their adjusted p-values, and the enriched pathways were visualized in a bubble chart using ggplot2, with bubble size representing gene count and color indicating p-value significance.

Estimation of the tumor immune microenvironment

To comprehensively profile the tumor immune microenvironment, we employed two computational deconvolution methods: CIBERSORT51 and xCell52, which were used to quantify the relative proportions of distinct tumor-infiltrating immune cell populations from the bulk transcriptomic data. In addition, the ESTIMATE algorithm53 was applied to calculate immune and stromal scores, reflecting the overall infiltration levels of immune and stromal components within the tumor tissue.

Gene enrichment and pathway analyses

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was conducted using the “clusterProfiler” R package54 to identify significantly enriched hallmark gene sets. GSVA was performed using the “GSVA” R package55 to compute sample-wise enrichment scores for specific gene sets. Differentially expressed genes between the Cancer Score-High and Cancer Score-Low groups were identified using the “limma” R package, with thresholds set at |log₂(fold change)| >1 and P < 0.05.

Statistics & reproducibility

For statistical analysis, a ROC curve was generated using the pROC package (v. 1.18.5). Based on the true positives (TP), true negatives (TN), false positives (FP), and false negatives (FN) for cancer prediction, we calculated the sensitivity [TP/(TP + FN)], specificity [TN/(Tn + FP)], positive predictive value (PPV) [TP/(TP + FP)], negative predictive value (NPV) [TN/(Tn + FN)], and accuracy [(TP + TN)/(TP + FP + Tn + FN)]. These metrics were computed using the confusion matrix function of the caret package (v. 6.0.94) in R (v. 4.4.1). The preProcess function (v.1.2.2) from the caret package was used for feature standardization. Additionally, the confusion matrix algorithm was employed to compare ROC curves, providing a comprehensive evaluation of the diagnostic test performance and ensuring accurate differentiation between benign and malignant cases. No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. No data were excluded from the analyses. Sample processing for cfDNA library preparation and sequencing was randomized by case/control status to minimize batch effects. The Investigators were not blinded to allocation during the experiments or during outcome assessment.
