Strain–Engineered Method for Atomically Sharp Interfaces in 2D TMDC Lateral Heterostructures
Authors: Mariam Hakami, Jui‐Cheng Kao, Vincent Tung, Takahito Takeda, Chun‐Wei Pao, Areej Aljarb
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWhile strain is widely used to tune the properties of two–dimensional transition–metal dichalcogenides (TMDCs), its role in controlling chalcogen exchange and alloy formation at interfaces of lateral heterostructures (LHSs) needs further exploration. Here, we demonstrate that the tensile strain in monolayer WSe2 controls the atomic sharpness of interfaces formed during subsequent MoS2 growth. Using a two‐step chemical vapor deposition (CVD) process on Al2O3 (0001), highly tensile‐strained WSe2 suppresses chalcogen intermixing, whereas strain‐relaxed WSe2 promotes alloy‐mediated interfacial evolution. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations reveal how tensile strain modifies vacancy formation, substitutional energetics, and alloy stability, while high‐resolution scanning transmission electron microscopy (HR–STEM) confirms the sharp atomic interfaces in high‐strain WSe2. Together, these results establish tensile strain as a control parameter for lateral interface formation in TMDC heterostructures and provide a generalizable strain–engineering strategy for scalable fabrication of precise 2D LHSs for electronic and optoelectronic applications.Keywords: alloy, atomic–sharp interfaces, intermediate species, lateral heterostructure, strain

Introduction

Two‐dimensional (2D) transition‐metal dichalcogenide (TMDC) monolayers and their heterostructures have attracted significant interest due to their tunable optical and electronic properties [1, 2], which can be engineered through composition, stacking order, doping, and other structural modulation [3, 4]. Among these architectures, lateral heterostructures (LHSs) with atomically sharp interfaces are particularly attractive, as they enable in‐plane junctions with controllable band alignment for high‐performance devices such as field‐effect transistors, p–n junctions, diodes, and photodetectors [5, 6, 7]. Compared to vertical heterostructures (VHSs), where high‐quality interfaces are typically formed via mechanical stacking or epitaxial growth [8, 9, 10], the synthesis of LHSs for scalable device integration critically relies on precise control of interfacial chemistry during lateral epitaxy. Even small degrees of interdiffusion or alloying at the growth front can renormalize band offsets, increase recombination or contact resistance, and introduce device–to–device variability, thereby degrading device performance [11, 12, 13, 14].

Among available approaches, sequential two‐step chemical vapor deposition (CVD) has emerged as the most promising route for synthesizing LHSs, enabling atomic‐scale control over interface composition and, in principle, suppressing alloying even in the presence of lattice mismatch [15, 16]. However, maintaining sharp lateral interfaces during sequential growth is challenging because TMDC growth is very sensitive to factors such as precursor diffusion, interfacial reactions, defect formation, and strain in the initially grown monolayer. In the second growth step, the pre‐existing TMDC lattice encounters a high‐temperature reactive environment, which can lead to chalcogen and metal diffusion and alloying. These processes often cause compositionally graded interfaces and make it harder to control junction properties [17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23].

Strain engineering and lattice–mismatch–induced strain in 2D TMDC LHSs provide a powerful platform for modulating electronic and optoelectronic properties. Strain has been extensively explored for modulating band gaps, photoluminescence (PL) response, and excitonic behavior, as well as for influencing defect formation and diffusion energetics [24, 25, 26]. Moreover, high and low tensile strain values in an initially grown TMDC flake may influence the nucleation and vertical growth of subsequent TMDC layers [27], while strain relaxation has been linked to changes in chemical reactivity and vacancy formation energies [28, 29]. Moreover, studies have demonstrated that strain fields at lateral TMDC interfaces strongly modulate local chemical reactivity, adsorption, and diffusion pathways [30]. For instance, Park et al. exploited periodic interfacial strain in MoS2–MoSe2 lateral superlattices to drive area–selective atomic layer deposition, highlighting that strain is an active parameter in controlling interfacial chemistry rather than a passive byproduct of growth [31].

Nevertheless, how strain governs the chemical stability and interface sharpness of LHSs during sequential growth, particularly in systems involving different metal and chalcogen species such as MoS2–WSe2, remains insufficiently understood. In most reported TMDC LHS studies, emphasis has primarily been placed on precursor delivery and growth temperature control, while strain is typically characterized only after heterostructure formation. Consequently, strain is rarely leveraged as a proactive control parameter to stabilize the initial WSe2 lattice against atomic substitution and unintentional alloying during the subsequent MoS2 growth step.

This study demonstrates that the tensile strain in the first‐grown TMDC monolayer affects interface stability and sharpness during sequential growth. Using MoS2–WSe2 as a model, highly–tensile strained WSe2 (≥ 0.7%, HS–WSe2) suppresses alloy formation, while low–strain WSe2 (≤ 0.5%, LS–WSe2) promotes WS2xSe2(1–x) alloy intermediates. The different strain values in WSe2 were tuned by adjusting the WO3–to–Se precursor ratio during the initial growth step. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations show that tensile strain modifies the energetics of selenium vacancy formation, sulfur substitution, and alloy stabilization, thereby suppressing chalcogen exchange in HS–WSe2. High‐resolution scanning transmission electron microscopy (HR–STEM) directly confirms the atomic abruptness of interfaces. Since strain can be engineered through growth conditions, this approach offers a practical, scalable method for controlling interface formation in TMDC LHSs beyond MoS2–WSe2 systems and provides a general framework for controlling heterojunctions in 2D LHSs.

Results and Discussion

Strained WSe2 monolayer flakes were synthesized on Al2O3 (0001) in a CVD furnace with dual heating zones, as shown in Figure S1. The chalcogen source was placed in the lower‐temperature heating zone 1, while both the metal oxide (WO3) source and the sapphire (Al2O3 (0001)) substrates were placed in the higher‐temperature heating zone 2. The WO3 boat was positioned at the center of heating zone 2, and Al2O3 (0001) substrates were placed adjacent to the WO3 boat. Throughout the 15–min growth period, Zone 1 was maintained at 270°C and Zone 2 at 900°C; the complete temperature profile is detailed in Figure S2. During the synthesis, the WO3 concentration decreases along the direction of gas flow, while the chalcogen partial pressure remains relatively uniform compared to the WO3 flux, creating a spatial gradient in the metal–to–chalcogen ratio across the substrate [32, 33, 34]. Consistent with previous reports, this varying chemical environment affects local growth behavior, including flake size, density, and morphology (Figure S3). Furthermore, it influences flake–substrate interactions during nucleation, resulting in position–dependent strain within the WSe2 flakes [27, 35]. To map the impact of WO3/Se gradient on WSe2 growth, we employed PL characterization across the furnace gradient, revealing a systematic blueshift in peak energy as the sampling moved away from the metal source toward downstream.

Accordingly, this spectral shift signifies a gradual relaxation of tensile strain, progressing from HS–WSe2 flakes in the upstream region (∼1–2 cm) to LS–WSe2 in the downstream region (3–4 cm). Using this position‐dependent strain as a controllable parameter, we investigate its influence on LHSs formation as illustrated in Figure 1a. A two‐step growth process was employed, in which monolayer WSe2 was first synthesized on sapphire, followed by edge epitaxial growth of MoS2. This approach enables a direct comparison of heterostructure growth on WSe2 templates with varying tensile strain.

The optical image (OM) in Figure 1b shows the WSe2 flake synthesized in the upstream region, and the corresponding PL mapping in Figure 1c reveals a systematic tensile strain gradient across the flake. A significant redshift of ∼89 meV is observed in the central region (∼1.561 eV), which corresponds to a tensile strain of ε ∼1.5%. Moreover, the periphery exhibits a smaller redshift of ∼65 meV (∼1.585 eV), indicating a lower strain of ∼1.08% relative to the center. Although a notable blueshift occurs as the measurement moves from the center toward the edge, the PL emission across the entire flake remains significantly redshifted relative to the unstrained monolayer WSe2 (∼1.65 eV) [36, 37]. The observed strain gradient within the flake is consistent with strong substrate coupling during the early nucleation under high WO3/Se growth conditions in the upstream region. Figure S4a reveals a distinct seed at the center of the same flake shown in Figure 1b, suggesting a multilayer or WO3–rich nucleus that was not fully selenized.

Although WSe2 and Al2O3(0001) have a large direct lattice mismatch of (∼31%), they can form a coincidence superlattice during nucleation with a reduced effective mismatch of ∼4% [38, 39]. However, because fully selenized WSe2 (downstream growth) typically interacts with sapphire via a weak van der Waals interface, lattice mismatch alone is insufficient to maintain the high tensile strain observed in the upstream samples, as partial strain relaxation occurs via interfacial sliding. Under High WO3/Se, the partially selenized W–O–Se nucleus remains strongly pinned to the sapphire substrate surface through the interfacial bonding, suppressing the interfacial sliding or strain relaxation at the center. During cooling, the mismatch in thermal expansion coefficients between the pinned WSe2 and sapphire induces the high tensile strain. As lateral growth proceeds away from the strongly coupled center, the substrate interaction weakens, allowing partial strain relaxation and resulting in the observed center–to–edge PL gradient.

To verify that the strain gradient is not dominated by defect‐related effects, we examined the spatial distribution of the PL Full Width at Half Maximum (FWHM). As shown in Figure S4b, the PL linewidth is uniform across the flake, indicating that the energy variation in the peak–position map (Figure S4c) arises from strain and not variation in defect density, which produces non‐uniform PL linewidth broadening. The presence of local strain is further supported by Raman spectroscopy. As shown in Figure 1d, the in‐plane E′ mode shows a clear peak splitting compared to the unstrained WSe2 [40].

In reference to the WSe2 in Figure S4d, the E′ mode appears as a single peak near 250 cm−1, whereas HS–WSe2 shows two components near ∼249 and ∼253 cm−1. The splitting is most pronounced at the flake center and gradually diminishes toward the edges as the strain is reduced by lattice relaxation. Together, the uniform PL linewidth, Raman analysis, and characteristic redshift confirm that the energy gradient arises from variations in high tensile strain rather than from defect–related non–uniformity.

To investigate the effect of strain on LHS formation, MoS2 was subsequently laterally grown from the edges of the HS–WSe2 flakes in a separate CVD furnace to avoid cross‐contamination (schematic in Figure 1a) with the growth temperature set at 790°C under 30 Torr with Ar as the carrier gas (detailed temperature profile in Figure S5). The structural integrity of the as‐grown lateral heterojunction was examined by OM images and PL mapping (Figure 1e,f) over the energy range 1.550–1.950 eV, covering the characteristic emission energies of monolayer WSe2 and MoS2. The PL map shows a clear spatial separation between WSe2 and MoS2 emission, with no intermediate features at the interface, indicating a laterally sharp heterojunction without alloy‐related optical signatures, such as Mo1–xWxS2 and WS2xSe2(1–x).

To further probe possible alloy formation, PL mappings in Figure S6 were conducted over selective energy ranges (1.80–2.00 eV for WS2 and 1.50–1.70 eV for MoSe2). No emission features corresponding to these species were observed, indicating the absence of laterally extended alloyed regions within the optical probe area. Single‐point PL spectra collected at the junction (Figure S7, yellowish‐brown point) show two well‐defined peaks at 1.60 and 1.87 eV, corresponding to WSe2 and MoS2, respectively. As the measurement position moves away from the junction toward either the WSe2 or MoS2 region, one peak diminishes while the other becomes dominant. Importantly, no PL peaks associated with chalcogenide or metal–intermixed alloys beyond the initial WSe2 and MoS2 were detected throughout the transition region, confirming a sharp optical contrast and minimal interdiffusion across the junction.

In contrast, the LS–WSe2 flake synthesized in the downstream region of the horizontal CVD furnace exhibited a markedly different response to the second growth step of MoS2. The OM in Figure 2a and the corresponding PL mapping (Figure 2b) show that the initial LS–WSe2 flakes prior to the MoS2 growth were characterized by a uniform peak at ∼1.63 eV across the entire flake. This value is significantly closer to the unstrained WSe2 monolayer, indicating a substantially reduced built‐in tensile strain, a conclusion further corroborated by the absence of E′ Raman mode splitting (Figure S8a). To ensure a direct comparison, HS–WSe2 and LS–WSe2 samples were positioned side by side simultaneously within the same heating zone under identical MoS2 growth conditions (Figure 1a and Figure S5d). However, rather than forming a stable heterojunction, the LS–WSe2 flake presents chemical instability under these growth conditions. The OM image in Figure 2c and the PL mapping in Figure 2d collectively show that the majority of the LS–WSe2 flake undergoes progressive chemical transformation during the MoS2 growth step, with WSe2 remaining only in the innermost core of the flake.

To elucidate the mechanism of this transformation, single‐point PL spectra were extracted from the map in Figure 2d and plotted in Figure 2e at different positions across the flake. The outer region exhibits a dominant PL peak at ∼1.87 eV (red spectrum in Figure 2e), confirming the conversion of WSe2 to MoS2 (Figure S8b) at the outer edge. Moving inward, the blue spectrum in Figure 2e exhibits a peak at ∼1.98 eV corresponding to WS2 emission, indicating a chalcogen–exchange process in which sulfur progressively substitutes for selenium within the lattice (Figure S8c). Finally, the innermost region retains WSe2, as indicated by the purple spectrum PL peak at ∼1.63 eV (Figure S8d).

The transition zones, highlighted by the black, green, and orange arrows in Figure 2e, represent the junctions between MoS2/WS2, WS2/WSe2, and the MoS2/WS2/WSe2 interfaces, respectively. These results reveal a multi‐stage transformation pathway: the LS–WSe2 flake first undergoes chalcogenide exchange to form WS2xSe2(1–x). Under a continued supply of MoO3/S, intermediate phases do not persist. The absence of WO3/Se precursors drives the system away from the W–based lattice via Mo incorporation and lattice reconstruction, ultimately yielding MoS2. This behavior contrasts sharply with the HS–WSe2 case and indicates that the observed structural degradation is a strain‐dependent response rather than a limitation of the growth process, since both samples were grown under identical conditions. To rule out differences in crystal quality, we compared the PL FWHM of HS– and LS–WSe2 prior to MoS2 growth. Both show similar linewidths (Figure S9), indicating comparable defect densities. Nevertheless, LS–WSe2 undergoes pronounced chemical transformation, highlighting the role of tensile strain in stabilizing the lattice during the second growth step.

To examine the atomic structure and interdiffusion across the LHSs, we conducted HR–STEM. Figure 3a shows the interface between MoS2 and the lateral edge of HS–WSe2, confirming an atomically sharp junction. For a quantitative evaluation of the interface, atomic‐resolution intensity profiles were extracted from three regions in Figure 3a. The WSe2 core (red rectangle), the MoS2 region (grey rectangle), and the heterojunction region (orange rectangle). As shown in Figure 3b, the intensity profile across the interface confirms an abrupt change, and the absence of intermediate phase intensity levels indicates negligible alloying and interdiffusion, confirming that the high‐strain template supports the formation of a well‐defined lateral interface (see Figure S10 for additional HR–STEM images). However, the MoS2/LS–WSe2 interface in Figure 3c exhibits a markedly different atomic structure. Instead of an abrupt junction, pronounced contrast variations are observed near the interface, indicating a compositionally graded transition region. A magnified view (blue square in Figure 3c) and the corresponding intensity profile across the purple rectangle in Figure 3d reveal non‐uniform atomic contrast, with bright columns appearing within the MoS2 lattice. These features are consistent with residual W atoms incorporated near the interface, suggesting intermixing and the formation of a W‐rich sulfide intermediate phase. This observation is consistent with the PL analysis (Figure 2).

Furthermore, to quantitatively distinguish between low– and high‐strain samples, the PL peak shift was converted into tensile strain using a calibration coefficient of ∼60 meV per 1% strain, consistent with literature values [39]. In LS–WSe2 regions, the PL peak energies observed in our experiments range from 1.62 to 1.64 eV, corresponding to energy differences of ΔE ∼ 0.01 to 0.03 eV relative to the unstrained WSe2, yielding tensile strain values of ∼0.2%–0.5%. In contrast, the HS–WSe2 regions show a pronounced redshift, with PL peak energies between 1.56 and 1.61 eV, corresponding to ΔE ∼0.04–0.09 eV. Using the same calibration, the tensile strain in these regions is estimated to be ∼0.7%–1.5%. Using this estimate, a consistent correlation between strain and LHS growth behavior is observed, as shown in Figure S11. This correlation between strain magnitude and growth outcome is further corroborated in Figure S12, where HS–WSe2 flake (∼1.45%, 1.563 eV, ΔE ∼ 87 meV) shows successful LHS growth without alloy formation. However, the LS–WSe2 flake (∼0.32%, 1.631 eV, ΔE ∼ 19 meV) undergoes chalcogen exchange. Furthermore, HS–WSe2 samples with strain values ranging from 1.1% to 1.3%, across different flake sizes and morphologies, consistently exhibit stable LHS growth without conversion, demonstrating the robustness of the proposed strain‐engineered approach (Figure S13).

To determine whether the stability of WSe2 during MoS2 growth is governed primarily by tensile strain rather than sulfur chemical potential, HS– and LS–WSe2 flakes were examined under deliberately varied sulfur conditions while keeping all other growth parameters constant (Figure 4). In this design, the sulfur temperature was adjusted to test whether strain–dependent behavior persists in both sulfur‐rich and sulfur‐poor environments. Specifically, HS–WSe2 flakes were exposed to sulfur‐rich conditions (155°C), whereas LS–WSe2 flakes were subjected to sulfur‐poor conditions (145°C), as it is well‐established that a higher chalcogenide chemical potential is the main thermodynamic driving force for the substitution of selenium or sulfur atoms in TMDC lattices [41]. Pre–growth OM and PL mapping (Figure 4a,b) confirm the high tensile strain of ∼1.13% (PL ∼1.582 eV, ΔE ∼68 meV) in the HS–WSe2 flakes. Following MoS2 growth under sulfur‐rich conditions, OM imaging (Figure 4c) and PL mapping (Figure 4d) reveal extensive lateral and vertical MoS2 growth on both sapphire and WSe2. Despite the high sulfur chemical potential, no WS2–related emission (∼1.97 eV [42]) is detected (Figure S14), indicating that chalcogen–exchanged intermediate phases are suppressed. Single–point PL spectra (Figure S14e,f) show: (i) MoS2 growth on sapphire (∼1.89 eV, red spectra), (ii) overlapping MoS2 and WSe2 signals at the lateral interface (blue spectra), and (iii) broadened MoS2 emission from multilayer regions (green, yellow, and pink spectra) with a persistence WSe2 PL signal beneath the MoS2, confirming vertical stacking rather than chemical transformation, demonstrating that HS–WSe2 remains chemically stable even under sulfur–rich conditions. This behavior indicates that applying a high built‐in tensile strain prevents vacancy‐mediated chalcogen exchange and maintains WSe2 lattice stability, even at an increased sulfur chemical potential.

HS–WSe2 remains chemically stable under both high and low sulfur chemical potential (µ S ), preserving sharp MoS2/WSe2 interfaces. In contrast, LS–WSe2 undergoes different transformation pathways depending on sulfur chemical potential. Under sulfur‐rich conditions (high µ S ), LS–WSe2 follows a vacancy–mediated chalcogen exchange pathway through WS2xSe2(1−x) intermediates, whereas under sulfur–poor conditions (low µ S ), vacancy accumulation leads to etching–mediated degradation before MoS2 re–nucleation.

On the contrary, MoS2 growth on LS–WSe2 flakes (Figure 4e–h) with a tensile strain of ∼ 0.18% (PL ∼ 1.639 eV, ΔE ∼ 11 meV) shows a different pattern under sulfur–poor conditions. A gradual transformation from the edges toward the center of the flake is observed, with MoS2 replacing the original material. Unlike the vacancy‐mediated process seen in Figure 2, there is no evidence of a WS2xSe2(1–x) intermediate phase, as indicated by the lack of WS2 emission in PL maps (Figure 4h and Figure S15). The OM images reveal discontinuous edges between the remaining WSe2 core and the newly formed MoS2 (Figure S15), indicating material loss of Se and W. Under these sulfur–poor conditions, a reduced chalcogen chemical potential promotes Se–vacancy formation and edge–etching–mediated processes before a stable WS2‐based intermediate can form. Because HS– and LS–WSe2 have similar initial optical quality (Figure S16), the different behavior is not due to defect density but is instead controlled by the initial tensile strain different value. The strain–engineering growth mechanism is summarized in Figure 4i. Under sulfur‐rich conditions, LS–WSe2 follows a substitution pathway, while under sulfur‐poor conditions, it undergoes etching‐driven degradation. In both cases, HS–WSe2 remains stable, demonstrating that strain is the dominant factor controlling interface integrity.

To further evaluate the role of chalcogen compatibility, we examined a chalcogen‐matched system by exposing WS2 flakes to the same MoO3/S growth conditions. This allows us to test whether strain within the WS2 lattice could promote lattice destabilization and metal exchange (Mo1−xWxS2), as previously reported in the literature [23]. As shown in Figure S17, PL mapping of WS2 reveals spatial variation in emission energy, indicating a low tensile strain (∼14 meV shift) across the flake. However, after MoS2 growth under identical conditions, no structural degradation or evidence of Mo–W intermixing is observed. Instead, MoS2 forms laterally along the WS2 edges, indicating that even if chalcogen vacancies form locally, the continuous supply of sulfur can effectively compensate for these sites, maintaining the integrity of the W–S lattice. In contrast to the WS2 system, sulfur cannot directly preserve the W–Se framework during MoS2 growth because the supplied chalcogen species differs from the original lattice composition. Under these conditions, the stability of WSe2 becomes strongly dependent on the ability of tensile strain to suppress vacancy‐mediated chalcogen exchange and subsequent lattice reconstruction.

To validate the experimentally observed strain‐dependent stability of WSe2 during MoS2 growth, DFT calculations were performed to assess how tensile strain affects the thermodynamic driving forces for vacancy formation, chalcogen substitution, and alloy formation in monolayer WSe2.

To understand this behavior, we investigated the effects of tensile strain on selenium vacancy formation (VacSe), sulfur substitution at selenium sites (SSe), and the stability of mixed WS2xSe2(1−x) alloys under tensile strain ranging from 0% to 5%. Because chalcogen exchange in TMDCs proceeds via a vacancy‐mediated pathway, the probability of chalcogen exchange is strongly influenced by the selenium vacancy formation energy in WSe2. The formation energy of a selenium vacancy (VacSe) is defined asEf (VacSe ) = Etotal (VacSe ) − Etotal (perfect) + µ Se , where Etotal (VacSe ) and Etotal (perfect) are the total energies of the defective and pristine monolayer WSe2, respectively, and µ Se is the selenium chemical potential. As shown in Figure 5a, the formation energy of a selenium vacancy increases monotonically with increasing tensile strain. This indicates that vacancy formation or Se removal becomes thermodynamically less favorable as the lattice expands, implying a lower equilibrium concentration of selenium vacancies. Since vacancy formation is a prerequisite for chalcogen exchange, this trend suggests suppression of vacancy‐mediated reaction pathways in HS–WSe2. The subsequent substitution step was evaluated by calculating the formation energy of sulfur substitution at a selenium site (SSe), which is given by Ef (SSe ) = Etotal (SSe ) − Etotal (perfect) + µ Se − µ S , where µ S is the sulfur chemical potential. At zero strain, Ef (SSe ) = −0.34 eV, indicating that sulfur substitution is thermodynamically favorable. With increasing tensile strain, this value increases to −0.16 eV at 5% tensile strain (Figure 5b), suggesting a reduced driving force for sulfur incorporation. As the W–Se lattice expands from 2.54 to 2.58 Å, incoming sulfur atoms are incorporated into an expanded WSe2 lattice in which the local W–S bond environment deviates from the equilibrium geometry of pristine WS2 (Figure S18). The greater mismatch between the strained lattice and the preferred sulfur–bonding configuration increases elastic strain energy, thereby reducing the thermodynamic favorability of SSe substitution. To further evaluate alloy evolution beyond isolated substitutions, the energetics of multiple sulfur substitutions were calculated as in Figure S19. Both the average and incremental substitution energies increase with sulfur concentration and tensile strain, indicating that progressive sulfur incorporation becomes increasingly thermodynamically unfavorable under higher strain. At 300 K (Figure 5c), the mixing free energy (Gmixing) remains positive over most of the composition range, indicating that alloy formation is thermodynamically unfavorable at low temperature. Even in the case of 0% strain, (Gmixing ) becomes positive as (x) increases, demonstrating that the enthalpy of mixing (Hmixing ) outweighs configurational entropy (ΔSconfig). Increasing tensile strain further increases (Gmixing) across the entire composition range due to bond–length mismatch and elastic distortion resulting from multiple sulfur incorporation. As a result, WS2xSe2(1–x) intermediates are thermodynamically unfavorable, and tensile strain further reduces alloying stability.

We further examined the stability of the alloy intermediate under growth‐relevant conditions. At elevated temperature (1000 K, Figure 5d), configurational entropy lowers the mixing free energy. At 0%–1% strain, (Gmixing) becomes negative over a wide range of sulfur concentrations, indicating that the formation of WS2xSe2(1–x) is energetically favorable. However, with increasing tensile strain (2%), (Gmixing) remains near zero or slightly negative at low sulfur concentrations, but increases with increasing (x), reflecting the competition between the configuration entropy and mixing free energy, reduced alloy stability. At higher tensile strain (3%–4%), (Gmixing) becomes positive even at low sulfur concentrations, indicating a loss of thermodynamic stability for the WS2xSe2(1–x) alloy intermediates despite the elevated temperature.

This behavior aligns with the mechanism described by Li et al. for MoSe2, where incorporating smaller sulfur atoms into a larger lattice induces local lattice contraction and compressive strain fields [29]. While Li et al. describe a self‐limiting substitution process (in which strain is generated after significant substitution), our results demonstrate that pre‐existing high tensile strain acts as a lattice constraint, suppressing substitution before alloying progresses. Because the W–Se bonds are already stretched by the initial tensile strain, the lattice is even less accommodating to the contraction required by the shorter W–S bonds. Consequently, at higher tensile strain (3%–4%), Gmixing becomes positive even at low sulfur concentrations, indicating that the WS2xSe2(1–x) intermediate is thermodynamically unstable despite the high temperature. Within our experimental range (∼0.7%–1.5%), our DFT analysis shows that tensile strain reduces the driving force for both vacancy formation and alloy stabilization. While this addresses thermodynamic trends rather than explicit kinetics, the reduced driving force directly correlates with a lower probability of atom exchange. This provides a consistent explanation for the experimentally observed suppression of alloy formation and preservation of sharp MoS2/WSe2 interfaces in highly strained WSe2, in contrast to the extensive chalcogen exchange observed in low‐strain regions.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this work identifies tensile strain as a key control parameter for interface quality and stability in 2D LHSs. By systematically engineering the tensile strain of the first‐grown WSe2 flake, we show that HS–WSe2 suppresses chalcogen exchange, preserving chemically distinct domains with atomically sharp MoS2/WSe2 heterojunctions. In contrast, relaxed or LS–WSe2 undergoes progressive sulfur substitution, intermixing, and eventual structural transformation, resulting in diffuse alloyed interfaces and degraded junction quality. The proposed mechanism is supported by correlated HR–STEM analysis, PL/Raman characterization, and DFT calculations. Together, these findings demonstrate that tensile strain increases the energetic cost of selenium vacancy formation, sulfur substitution, and alloy stabilization. Instead of serving as a post‐growth bandgap tuning tool, strain actively modulates interfacial reaction pathways during lateral epitaxy. These findings establish strain engineering as a viable strategy to control interface abruptness and stability in lateral 2D heterostructures, enabling reproducible fabrication of precise junctions for future electronic and optoelectronic devices.

Materials And Methods

Chemical Vapor Deposition (CVD) of TMDCs

Monolayer WSe2 was synthesized in a two‐zone CVD furnace at a chamber pressure of 10 Torr. The Se powder (Sigma–Aldrich, 99.99%) was placed in the upstream heating zone 1 and heated to 270°C under an argon/hydrogen flow rate of 65/5 sccm. The Se source was introduced 5 min prior to WO3 sublimation to ensure effective selenization. WO3 powder (Sigma–Aldrich, 99.9%) was loaded in a partially covered quartz boat (∼2 mm opening) to regulate vapor release and placed at the center of heating zone 2. Al2O3 (0001) substrates positioned closer to the WO3 source (∼1–2 cm) are referred to as upstream (HS–WSe2), while those farther away (∼3–4 cm) are defined as downstream (LS–WSe2), establishing a controlled WO3/Se ratio gradient along the substrate. Prior to growth, the system was purged with Ar to remove residual oxygen and moisture. Zone 2 was ramped to 900°C at 23.8°C min−1 under continuous Ar/H2 flow, and growth was maintained for 15 min. After growth, rapid cooling was achieved by opening the furnace lid under Ar flow (100 sccm), assisted by external airflow directed toward the furnace tube.

For the heterostructure synthesis of MoS2–WSe2, sulfur powder was placed upstream of the furnace (heating zone 1), and the temperature was maintained at 150°C during the reaction. In heating zone 2, at the center of the reaction chamber, the MoO3 powder (0.8 g) was placed in a ceramic boat, with a previously synthesized WSe2/Al2O3 substrate positioned downstream of the MoO3 source to suppress continuous film formation and favor edge–selective growth on WSe2. The carrier gas was Ar (70 sccm), and the chamber pressure was maintained at 30 Torr. The furnace center was heated from room temperature to 790°C in 22 min (35°C min−1) and held at this temperature for 10 min. Sulfur reached the target temperature 7 min prior to MoO3 sublimation to ensure sufficient sulfurization in the absence of H2. The HS–WSe2 and LS–WSe2 samples were placed at identical positions to ensure identical growth conditions. The sulfur temperature was typically maintained at 150°C and varied between 145°C and 155°C to tune the sulfur chemical potential (Figure 4). For the heterostructure synthesis of MoS2–WS2, the WS2 sample was pre‐synthesized on Al2O3(0001), and the MoS2 sample was grown using the standard growth recipe with a sulfur temperature of 150°C.

Characterization

The Raman and photoluminescence (PL) spectra on WSe2 and MoS2–WSe2 were collected using a Witec alpha 300 confocal Raman microscope equipped with a RayShield coupler. A 532 nm solid‐state laser was the excitation source. The excitation light at 2.5 mW was focused onto the sample using a 100× objective lens (numerical aperture (NA) = 0.9). The signal was collected by the same objective lens, analyzed by a 0.75 m monochromator, and detected by a liquid–nitrogen–cooled charge–coupled device (CCD) camera. High‐resolution Transmission Electron Microscopy (STEM) samples were prepared by wet‐transfer methods. Poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA) was spin‐coated onto the as‐grown MoS2/WSe2/sapphire samples as a supporting substrate. The PMMA/ MoS2/WSe2/sapphire was fully soaked in the HF solution. After rinsing with large volumes of deionized water (DI–H2O), the suspended MoS2/WSe2 were transferred onto transmission electron microscopy (TEM) grids. Imaging was performed by a ThermoFisher Titan Themis Z TEM (40−300 kV) equipped with an XFEG electron gun, a double Cs spherical aberration corrector, and an electron beam monochromator. The microscope operated at 80 kV to minimize electron–beam–induced sample damage. The HAADF STEM images were acquired in diffraction mode with a camera length of 115 mm using a Fischione ADF detector.

Density Function Theory Simulation

The first‐principles calculations based on the density functional theory (DFT) were carried out by the Vienna ab initio simulation package (VASP) [43, 44] combined with the projector augmented wave (PAW) [45] method. The Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) [46] functional within the generalized gradient approximations (GGA) [47] was used to perform the structure optimization. DFT–D3 van der Waals (vdW) correction developed by Grimme [48] et al. was considered. The 450 eV and 12 × 12 × 1 Γ–centered k–point meshes were set to the cutoff energy of the plane–wave basis and were used to describe the Brillouin zone of the monolayer WSe2 primitive cell, respectively. The vacuum region along the surface normal direction exceeded 15 Å to avoid artificial interactions between periodic images. We used a 1.0 × 10−5 eV energy convergence criterion for electronic relaxation, and the optimized lattice constant was 3.325 Å. The 6 × 6 supercell was modeled for the S–substituted WSe2 system. The mixing free energy (Gmixing) is defined as: Gmixing = Hmixing − TdSconfig , where dSconfig=−kB[xWS2lnxWS2+(1−xWS2)ln(1−xWS2)]. T, kB , dSconfig and xWS2 represent the temperature, Boltzmann's constant, configuration entropy, and molar fraction of the WS2 mixing with the WSe2 monolayer, respectively. Herein, we treat the mixing energy (E mixing) as the mixing enthalpy (H mixing).
