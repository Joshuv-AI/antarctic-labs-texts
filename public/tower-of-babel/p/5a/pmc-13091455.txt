An overview of recent flexible- and soft-biomaterial applications in myocardial infarction and other cardiovascular diseases
Authors: Andrea Roberto Calore, Veronica Torresan, Matteo Marchionni, Alessandro Gandin, Erica Basso, Diego Biasion, Stefano Mingoni, Alessia Molena, Gabriela Sartor, Giovanna Brusatin, Vladimiro Vida
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cardiovascular diseases are currently the leading cause of mortality globally, with myocardial infarction representing one of the most prevalent and severe condition greater than even cancer. Current medical approaches are generally based on preventing and treating various post-infarction complications, failing to restore functional myocardial tissue. In this context, recent advances in the field of tissue engineering have been explored in an attempt to provide successful strategies for myocardial infarction repair, in addition to a multitude of other pathologies affecting the cardiovascular system. Biomaterials represent an essential component in developing innovative treatments such as tissue substitutes, drug/cell delivery vehicles, and prosthetic solutions, potentially avoiding invasive surgical procedures or, ultimately, transplantation. This review presents the various biomaterials that have found application in the last 10 years in the development of tissue engineering-based solutions for myocardial infarction repair. The discussion covers thermoplastic and elastomeric polymers, hydrogels, hybrid and smart material systems, and highlights their distinctive properties and the specific biological requirements they are able to meet. Additionally, recent applications in clinical trials are reviewed, describing their potential for clinical translation. This comprehensive overview aims to support researchers in selecting the most adequate biomaterial for myocardial infarction application.

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases and traditional treatments

“Cardiovascular diseases” (CVDs) are a broad term encompassing all disorders affecting the heart and blood vessels. Despite the remarkable advances achieved over the past half-century in preventing and treating various CVD-related complications, these conditions are expected to cause up to 20 million deaths worldwide by 2030 [1,2]. According to the World Health Statistics 2020 report, the mortality rate of CVDs in 2016 ranked first among all noncommunicable diseases, far exceeding that of cancer (43.66% vs. 21.95%) [3]. Even today, CVDs are still the leading cause of death globally [4].

Among the various pathological disorders of the cardiovascular system, atrial fibrillation, congenital heart defects, heart valve disease, and myocardial infarction (MI) have the broadest impact. Atrial fibrillation represents the most common complication after open-heart surgery, with an incidence of up to 65% [5]. It is associated with an increase in the frequency of strokes, MI, and mortality, causing longer hospital stays [6]. Typically, it occurs within the first postoperative week, though later-onset cases have also been reported [7]. Currently, amiodarone remains the most effective pharmacological treatment for the therapy and prevention of atrial fibrillation, but its clinical use is limited by the risk of extra-cardiac side effects [5]. Therefore, it appears clear how a localized cardiac delivery may represent a more promising approach by minimizing systemic toxicity.

Congenital heart defects represent the most common congenital disorder, affecting roughly 1% of all live births, 25% of which require surgery already within the first year of life [[8], [9], [10]]. Traditional repair strategies rely on bioinert polymeric patches or fixed pericardium to close septal defects and reconstruct stenotic or overriding outflow tracts. However, these materials lack growth potential, and their encapsulation by scar tissue may impair electrical and mechanical coupling with the native myocardium. This can ultimately result in arrhythmias, heart failure, and cardiac death [11,12]. For this reason, an engineered patch able to initiate a regenerative response in the surrounding tissue is highly desirable. The subsequent remodeling of the patch into functional heart tissue would result in a seamless mechanical and electrical integration, thereby enabling longer-term function compared with current standard approaches [11].

Heart valve disease represents another major cause of morbidity and mortality among CVDs, with mitral and aortic regurgitation or stenosis being frequent pathologies. The incidence of different etiologies varies significantly based on age and geography, but commonly involves degenerative, rheumatic, and inflammatory processes, as well as ischemic cardiomyopathy and congenital malformations. The demand for valve replacement and repair procedures has led to a global market, estimated at $2.8 B, with an expected compound annual growth rate of 9.1% [13].

MI accounts for approximately 46% of the deaths related to CVDs and remains the main cause of death in Europe and in the United States [14,15]. MI results from the occlusion of a coronary artery, which leads to a sudden reduction in blood flow (ischemia) and consequent hypoxia. This event causes loss of cardiomyocytes (CMs) in the left ventricle (LV) and ultimately leads to myocardial necrosis [16,17]. Advances in surgical interventions, pharmacological therapies, and mechanical support devices have significantly improved post-MI survival [18]. In particular, thrombolysis, percutaneous coronary intervention, and coronary artery bypass grafting can restore coronary artery perfusion [19]. However, in some cases, coronary reperfusion may cause additional injury due to pressure overload and the excessive generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in the infarcted area, leading to ischemic reperfusion injury, further CM damage, and heart failure [20].

Nevertheless, CMs possess limited regeneration potential (∼0.5% per year), which is further hindered by insufficient angiogenesis in post-MI [21,22]. Therefore, massive cell death following ischemia triggers an inflammatory response, during which macrophages remove necrotic debris and release cytokines and ROS, stimulating fibroblast activation and the formation of collagen-rich fibrotic scar tissue. However, ROS also disrupts the homeostasis of the surviving cells, further damaging the CMs [23]. The newly formed scar tissue lacks the contractility of the previous healthy myocardium, leading to mechanical weakening of the ventricular wall [17,23]. This structural and functional impairment, along with the limited availability of CMs after MI, contributes to the progressive development of arrhythmias and heart disease in the long term, ultimately leading to heart failure [24,25]. During the first week post-MI, a continuous imbalanced extracellular matrix (ECM) turnover takes place, characterized by reduced matrix synthesis and increased degradation. This phenomenon, coupled with the heart's inability to regenerate, results in transmural thinning and expansion, and subsequent LV geometrical distortion [26,27]. These alterations compromise the LV pump function, potentially resulting in progressive chronic dysfunction and, ultimately, heart failure [26,28]. In addition, the fibrotic tissue exhibits poor electrical conductivity, causing electrical decoupling at the scar border zone and consequent asynchronous ventricular contractions. Cardiac arrhythmia may arise, often causing sudden cardiac death, which is responsible for between 50 and 60% of mortalities in MI [17,29]. Such events may happen to asymptomatic MI patients as well, even after years. Pharmacological therapies can reduce arrhythmias but do not act against sudden cardiac death and may have off-target effects.

Current treatments for heart failure post-MI include pharmacological interventions, percutaneous coronary intervention, coronary artery bypass grafting, cardiac transplantation, and cellular therapies. The impact of pharmacological treatment is usually very limited, while lack of donors, lifelong use of immunosuppressive drugs, and potential acute or chronic rejection make transplantation a last line of therapy for the most severe cases [30,31]. In addition, percutaneous coronary intervention and coronary artery bypass grafting are mainly focused on symptom relief and on preserving the remaining cardiac functionality by preventing the progression of maladaptive remodeling and thus disease progression. In fact, the remodeling is generally irreversible since the damaged myocardium cannot self-repair [32]. Therefore, current standard approaches do not restore lost myocardial tissue and fail to halt disease progression [33,34]. Even when temporary remission is achieved, re-hospitalization and mortality rates remain high [20]. As such, future therapeutic strategies for MI should aim to limit adverse LV remodeling, reduce fibrotic scar formation, and promote myocardial regeneration, thereby preventing end-stage heart failure and restoring functional tissue integrity [23,25].

Alternatively, cell-injection therapies have emerged as more targeted and less invasive alternatives compared to conventional treatments, particularly to treat the initial stage (within the first week) [35]. However, several limitations have so far hindered the widespread clinical adoption of these strategies. Among the available delivery routes, systemic infusion is the most straightforward but also the least efficient, as it often leads to off-target cell accumulation and potential systemic side effects [36]. Intracoronary injection offers a more targeted delivery but is associated with the risk of plug formation within the capillaries towards the infarcted zone. Intramyocardial infusion provides higher precision and rapid administration, although open-chest surgery is required for epicardial access [37]. Currently, endocardial injection represents the most promising translational approach, allowing catheter-based, minimally invasive delivery directly into the LV [18]. Despite these advances, poor cell retention and minimal engraftment remain major challenges. Up to 80% of the injected cells are expelled within the first hours after administration, primarily due to the lack of anchorage sites in the degraded ECM [27,32,38]. In addition, the hypoxic and nutrient-deprived environment of the ischemic myocardium severely limits cell survival, leading to rapid death of injected cells [39]. Recent studies have shown that only a small fraction of mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) remain viable after injection into the infarcted myocardium, approximately 1% at 24h and less than 0.44% at 4 days [30]. This low survival rate, together with safety and efficacy concerns, complicates clinical translation. Immature or improperly coupled stem-cell-derived CMs can exhibit pro-arrhythmic behavior [17], while certain stem cell populations are associated with a significant risk of immune rejection and oncogenesis [40]. Moreover, implantation in ischemic areas characterized by limited nutrient availability could further reduce cell survival and integration [41]. A combination of cells with biomaterials has the potential to efficiently increase cell retention and survival [42]. Consequently, scaffold-based cell delivery and cell-free therapeutic approaches have gained growing attention, thanks to their inherent ability to circumvent these adverse effects and leverage the paracrine effects of cell-secreted factors such as growth factors, cytokines, and genetic material [43]. Although preclinical studies have demonstrated promising results in mitigating ventricular remodeling and promoting MI healing, directly targeting the remodeling of the infarcted tissue remains a major challenge and an active field of investigation [26,[44], [45], [46], [47]].

Novel treatments

Recently, the scientific community has increasingly focused on regenerating cardiac tissue through the delivery of bioactive molecules or cells using biocompatible scaffolds as carriers, aiming to enable personalized precision therapy [20]. In addition, the use of natural or bioactive materials allows the surrounding biological environment to recognize and metabolically process the implanted constructs through established pathways [48]. Nonetheless, researchers are still striving to develop strategies that ensure complete integration with host tissues and restoration of functional performance [49]. In fact, to fully exploit the therapeutic potential of cytokines or cells, it is essential to design effective approaches for their targeted and controlled delivery. For instance, systemic infusion is often limited by a short half-life of the molecules and poor myocardial uptake [21]. Consequently, significant efforts have been directed toward developing sustained and localized release systems, which remain a central focus of ongoing research.

Two main biomaterial-based approaches have been used for functional reconstruction of the heart following MI: functional cardiac implants mechanically support the infarcted heart, and biomaterials to deliver remodeling factors and/or cells to the damaged tissue [50]. Implants are meant to support the dilated LV like a bandage, preventing further expansion and counteracting pathological remodeling [48,51,52]. This is achieved by confining the MI area and improving strain distribution, thus diminishing ventricular wall stress. Since the device is implanted in the high-tension region of the LV, its mechanical properties must mimic the host tissue. In particular, the materials must be highly compliant to adapt to the long-term elasticity of the myocardium under the dynamic conditions given by the systolic and diastolic functions [48]. Additionally, the material should exhibit an adequately long-term degradation rate to provide continuous structural support during the regeneration of the ischemic area [53]. Remodeling of the infarcted area can also be promoted through cell- or cytokine-loaded materials, which enable the controlled release of regenerative agents at the site of injury [[54], [55], [56]]. The incorporation of cells within the scaffold enhances their retention at the damaged site, providing sustenance and granting their viability and functions in an extremely harsh microenvironment [57]. This strategy can therefore overcome the major limitations associated with direct cell injection therapies. Furthermore, the system should favor cell integration and coupling with the host tissue, thereby promoting effective regeneration [58]. In drug delivery systems, the matrix is designed to retain bioactive compounds in the area of interest, preventing dispersion and providing sustained supply over an adequate period of time [20,24]. Importantly, in situ delivery results in therapeutically relevant local concentrations that would be otherwise unattainable through systemic administration without inducing toxicity [26]. Proper remodeling of the damaged myocardium also helps protect the heart from mechanical overload by preventing further deterioration and improving the mechanical performance of the affected region. Through mechanotrasduction pathways, remodeling-promoting implants can reduce myocardial wall stress, thereby shielding the heart from excessive mechanical loads [51,59]. In both strategies, a key requirement is to mitigate the harsh microenvironment surrounding the implant, ensuring seamless integration with the native tissue [60].

In the literature, the term “patch” was originally used to describe a scaffold applied to the surface of the damaged myocardium to provide mechanical support to failing hearts, thereby referring to constructs that fulfill the first therapeutic strategy. Specifically, these structures served to “patch” the necrotic region of the myocardium following MI [61]. As patches began to be employed for the delivery of bioactive molecules and cells to improve the therapeutic effect, the term has acquired a broader meaning [62].

Cardiac patches have usually been implanted via open-chest surgery, a highly invasive procedure particularly required for constructs fabricated from stiff thermoplastic polymers or preformed hydrogels that cannot be molded in situ. However, the clinical translation of therapies for cardiac regeneration will further depend on the development of minimally invasive and effective delivery methods for introducing engineered tissues or cells into the heart [58]. In recent years, injectable cardiac patches made of in-situ forming materials have emerged, enabling delivery through minimally invasive procedures [20]. These polymers are typically in situ gel-forming hydrogels existing as a liquid (sol state) that can be injected via syringe and subsequently reticulate in vivo through biocompatible chemical crosslinking or in response to physical stimuli, such as temperature, light, pH, or their combinations [63]. Compared with traditional hydrogels, these systems offer simpler and minimally invasive delivery and can easily adapt to various irregular damaged tissue geometries [64]. Furthermore, non-hydrogel polymers have been explored, such as highly resilient elastomers that can be squeezed into a syringe and recover their original shape upon injection.

In general, the material choice depends primarily on the specific therapeutic goal. Natural biomaterials, including polysaccharide-based (e.g., alginate, chitosan, hyaluronic acid) and protein-based (collagen, fibrin), offer clear advantages due to their biocompatibility and ECM-like properties. Nevertheless, they present important drawbacks, such as their limited availability and shelf life, poor mechanical properties, limited tunability, as well as batch-to-batch variation and high costs [39].

Synthetic polymers, on the other hand, provide tunable physical, chemical, and mechanical properties, as well as controlled degradation rates and virtually unlimited availability [65]. However, they are generally bioinert, requiring surface modification or blending to promote cell adhesion and tissue integration [66]. Natural materials typically occur in the form of hydrogels, polymer networks capable of absorbing a large amount of water, a property that significantly influences their physicochemical properties. On the other hand, synthetic materials can be synthesized as either hydrogels or thermoplastic polymers/elastomers. While thermoplastics and elastomers are usually mechanically robust, they may hinder CM contractility. In contrast, hydrogels exhibit elasticity similar to soft tissue but may lack the mechanical strength necessary to withstand systolic loading [67].

As a result, hybrid systems combining hydrogels and elastomers are frequently employed to balance flexibility and mechanical stability. Alternatively, strategies incorporating nanoparticles or other compounds to enhance bioactivity, cell adhesion, tissue regeneration, and electrical conductivity are used [39]. For instance, biomaterials are often incorporated with conducting compounds, as their low conduction velocity does not allow them to keep pace with the contraction rhythm of the native myocardium. Under such conditions, arrhythmia may occur after the implantation of the biomaterial [68].

Overall, the treatment of MI and CVDs in general requires careful consideration of multiple, interrelated factors. In the last decade, considerable efforts have been made to address these challenges by developing biomaterial systems able to fulfill the described requirements to improve patient outcomes. This review will discuss biomaterial-based strategies for the treatment of CVDs, with a particular emphasis on MI, highlighting the advances reported since 2014.

The following sections discuss thermoplastic polymers and elastomers, traditional hydrogels, stimuli-responsive biomaterials, and hybrid systems, also highlighting translational efforts toward clinical applications.

Thermoplastic polymers and elastomers

Thermoplastic polymers and elastomers are of particular interest due to their high tunability, straightforward processing, and moderate production costs [25]. These materials offer molecular-level design flexibility, allowing adjustments in molecular weight and polymer composition to modulate their mechanical properties and degradation profiles within specific ranges. Compatibility with several processing techniques further enables the production of constructs tailored to the mechanical and structural requirements of target tissue [61]. However, most thermoplastic polymers are hydrophobic and lack intrinsic bioactive functional groups, which can lead to poor cell adhesion and limited integration with host tissues upon implantation. To address this limitation, surface modification or bulk functionalization is typically required to impart bioactivity to the material. Fortunately, synthetic thermoplastic polymers can be easily functionalized, resulting in customized functionalization to meet the specific needs of each construct [69]. Considering the broad diversity of available polymeric chemical structures, the specific fabrication strategy must be carefully selected based on a combination of the material's intrinsic properties and the physiological demands of the application. In particular, constructs for cardiac tissue engineering (TE) must exhibit an optimal balance of elasticity and mechanical strength to endure the cyclic deformations and high stresses characteristic of the beating heart. To achieve such a combination of properties that a single material would not ensure, hybrid cardiac scaffolds have been developed by blending different polymers [61].

Alternatively, advanced processing techniques can offer a valid alternative to obtain scaffolds exhibiting the desired mechanical flexibility. Among these, electrospinning (ES) has been extensively employed to manufacture highly flexible scaffolds from otherwise stiff thermoplastic polymers, producing micro-to sub-microfibers. Although the degree of fiber alignment can be controlled up to a certain extent, ES does not allow for precise fiber stacking or architectural regularity.

The more recent melt electrowriting (MEW) technique overcomes this limitation, enabling the precise deposition and stacking of fibers in three-dimensional architectures, thus producing highly deformable, structurally organized scaffolds. The fibrous morphology of constructs produced with ES and MEW induces the alignment of adhered cells along the fiber direction, and the consequent formation of highly aligned ECM proteins, which is essential for the regeneration of anisotropic tissues such as the cardiac one [70]. Overall, the combination of enhanced flexibility and fibrous architecture achieved through ES and MEW has been employed in the manufacturing of constructs for cardiac TE made of relatively stiff thermoplastic polymers.

The most commonly used thermoplastic and elastomeric polymers are polycaprolactone (PCL), poly(lactic acid) (PLA), poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA), poly(L-lactide-co-ε-caprolactone) (PLCL), biodegradable polyurethane (PU), and poly(glycerol sebacate) (PGS). A summary of the main features is provided in Table 1. PCL, PLA, and PLGA are aliphatic polyesters that undergo hydrolytic degradation upon exposure to water. All three polymers are semicrystalline and relatively stiff, although PCL is more ductile thanks to its low glass transition temperature (≈– 40 °C). In addition, PCL has a relatively low melting temperature (≈60 °C), which facilitates its processing through melt-based techniques by reducing both energy consumption and equipment requirements. Thermoplastic/elastomeric polymers employed in cardiovascular applications.

PCL has been employed in cardiovascular TE for the fabrication of epicardial heart patches [[72], [73], [74], [75], [76]] (Fig. 1A), small-diameter vascular grafts [77,78], large vascular structures [79], microfibrous scaffolds for heart valves [70,80], and aortic root supports with controlled micrometric and patient-specific anatomic features [81]. To better recapitulate the electromechanical properties of the native cardiac tissue, PCL has also been used in combination with electroconductive compounds, such as gold nanoparticles [82] and polypyrrole (PPy) [83]. PLA, while stiffer and more thermally sensitive during processing, is a biobased and cost-effective polymer derived from starch, which has led to its extensive use in TE. PLA has been applied to produce cardiac patches [[91], [92], [93]], heart valves [157], cardiovascular stents [94,146], vascular [95] and small diameter arterial grafts [158], nanoparticles releasing miRNA [96] and other bioactive compounds [97], and in combination with conductive polymers such as polyaniline (PANI) [98,99]. Due to its hydrophobicity, PLA is often copolymerized with polyglycolic acid (PGA), which is more hydrophilic but extremely stiff and brittle. The resulting PLGA exhibits tunable hydrophilicity, and consequently degradation rate and mechanical properties, depending on the PLA/PGA ratio [159]. The material can be resorbed over periods ranging from several years (high PLA content) to a few weeks (high PGA content). PLGA has been employed to fabricate cardiac patches [[110], [111], [112]], microparticles for the release of bioactive compounds [[113], [114], [115], [116], [117]], small-diameter vascular grafts [118], and stent coatings [119]. All the thermoplastic polymers discussed so far are characterized by relatively stiff mechanical behavior. When greater flexibility and toughness are required, more compliant polymer grades have been developed. One such example is PLCL, a copolymer between PLA and PCL developed to overcome the inherent brittleness of PLA. PLCL combines enhanced deformability and a strength typical of an elastomer [160], making it suitable for soft-tissue applications. It has been used to fabricate cardiac patches [[100], [101], [102]], vascular grafts [[103], [104], [105], [106]] (Fig. 1B), vascular stents [150] (Fig. 1C), sheaths for coronary artery bypass grafts [107], and as a coating [108]. PLCL has also been employed with conductive compounds, such as reduced GO, to enhance electrical conductivity and cellular response [109].

Another relevant material is PGS, a cost-effective thermoset elastomer whose mechanical and degradation properties can be finely adjusted by controlling several factors along its synthesis process [161]. PGS exhibits mechanical properties close to native myocardium, which has led to its widespread use in cardiac patches [[84], [85], [86]] and vascular grafts [87,88,144,145] (Fig. 1D). Furthermore, PGS has been combined with conductive polymers to manufacture electroactive cardiac patches, such as PANI [89] and conductive aerogels [90].

In addition to the polymers described above, PUs have attracted attention in cardiovascular TE because of their notable mechanical versatility and biological compatibility. Their properties can be tailored from rigid and glassy to highly elastic, depending on the composition and synthesis parameters [162,163]. The more compliant formulations have been extensively used in cardiac TE to accommodate the large cyclic deformations experienced by the myocardium. Additionally, PUs were shown to promote a CM-friendly environment via their microbial- and thrombotic-resistance, further favoring their use, especially as heart patches [56,164]. The great versatility of the synthesis process also allows for the integration of ROS-cleavable chain extenders in the polymer backbone [23,165] (Fig. 2A). In infarcted regions, where ROS are abundant, this design enables the controlled degradation of the PU matrix, leading to the release of embedded therapeutics while simultaneously neutralizing excess ROS. To further enhance their functionality, PUs have also been combined with several conductive compounds, including carbon nanotubes (CNTs) [124], reduced GO-silver nanocomposites [125], graphene nanoplatelets [126], and gold nanocomposites [127]. Among the various formulations, urea-based PUs have shown particularly promising results for the manufacturing of biodegradable heart valves. For example, poly(ester urethane) urea has been used to fabricate heart valve leaflets [13,128] while poly(carbonate urethane) urea has facilitated the production of stentless tricuspid valves via double-component deposition ES [129]. Implants for cardiovascular applications must fulfill a wide range of mechanical, biological, and functional requirements to provide controlled and effective therapeutic outcomes. To meet these complex demands, polymers commonly used in TE are often blended, allowing each component to contribute its own advantages and synergistically enhance the overall implant functionality. For instance, PLA/PCL and PU/PCL blends have been used to recapitulate the three-layer structure of blood vessels [134], while blends of PLA and PCL found application in the production of bioresorbable stents via 3D printing [147]. A PCL/PEU blend was employed to fabricate small-diameter vascular grafts exhibiting the characteristic J-shaped stress–strain curve of native vessels [132]. PCL was further blended with another PU, previously made electroactive by the introduction of an aniline pentamer, to create cardiac tissue substitutes [138]. Other reported blends are PCL-blends, such as PCL/PGA [133], PGS/PCL [[139], [140], [141], [142], [143]], PCL/PLCL [135,166] and PCL/PLGA [136,137]; PLA-blends, including PLA/PGS [148,149] (Fig. 2B) and PLA/PLCL [151,152]; and hybrid blends, such as PLCL/PGA [153,154].

In addition to these widely used materials, several innovative thermoplastic/elastomeric polymers have been developed to overcome the limitations of solutions manufactured with more “standard” materials. For instance, to address the hydrophobicity and bioinertness of PCL, a PCL-based hydroxyl-functionalized polyester, poly(hydroxymethylglycolide-co-ε-caprolactone) (pHMGCL), was used to manufacture cardiac patches [34]. pHMGCL exhibited superior hydrophilicity, a tunable degradation rate, and hydroxyl groups for further biofunctionalization. Another material, polyglactin, an early resorbable polyester copolymer between glycolide and lactide, has been employed as a fibrillar resorbable mesh to reinforce the pulmonary autograft following the Ross procedure [123], providing mechanical strength comparable to PLA while exhibiting a faster degradation profile. Likewise, PC has found application in the ES of microfibrous heart valves [71] (Fig. 2C) while a novel block copolymer, poly(γ-methyl-ε-caprolactone)-b-poly(ε-caprolactone)-polyethylene glycol-poly (ε-caprolactone) -b-poly(γ-methyl-ε-caprolactone) (PMECL) was developed by Huang et al., combining thermoplastic processability with elastomeric behavior [122] (Fig. 2D). This material was 3D-printed into cardiac patches exhibiting an elongation at break of up to 700%. Finally, the elastomer poly(octamethylene maleate (anhydride) citrate) (POMAC) was used to fabricate flexible scaffolds that could be delivered via epicardial injection and recover their original shape post-deployment [51]. Notably, the viability and functionality of the loaded CMs were successfully preserved, demonstrating the potential of POMAC-based constructs for minimally invasive cardiac cell delivery.

Hydrogels

In recent years, the development of tissue-engineered cardiac patches has achieved remarkable progress in replicating both the functional and structural properties of native myocardium. However, the successful placement of a cardiac patch fabricated with a thermoplastic or elastomeric polymer usually requires invasive open-heart surgeries, as their stiff mechanical behavior limits their use to patients already undergoing major interventions [51]. Conversely, natural hydrogels have gained wide adoption in the treatment of MI due to their injectability, which enables minimally invasive delivery. These materials provide structural support to the infarcted tissue while creating a favorable microenvironment that supports tissue integration and neo-vascularization [167,168]. Often derived from natural sources, hydrogels inherently possess soft mechanics and biochemical cues suitable for cell survival and functions. Common examples include protein-based materials (e.g., collagen, gelatin, fibrin, decellularized ECM), polysaccharide-based materials (e.g., alginate, chitosan, HA), and polyethylene glycol (PEG)-based materials. Despite these advantages, some hydrogels lack intrinsic cell-adhesion motifs, which can hinder cell attachment and proliferation [169]. This, along with their low mechanical strength and limited processability, poses challenges for clinical translation [170]. Therefore, injectable hydrogels are often functionalized, loaded with therapeutic agents, or used in combination with other materials to enable localized, controlled release of biologically active compounds in a minimally invasive manner [43,171]. Hydrogel materials can be applied to the damaged area as pre-gelled patches, offering enhanced mechanical support to the necrotic tissue and sustained delivery of bioactive compounds, though requiring complex surgical procedures [24]. Alternatively, in situ gelling pre-gels can be designed, which can be injected and subsequently reticulate within the body, enabling non-invasive administration [172]. Because of these features, injectable hydrogels have gained increasing attention as promising candidates for next-generation cardiac therapies. Protein-based hydrogels

Among protein-based hydrogels, those derived from natural ECM components such as collagen, gelatin, fibrin, and decellularized extracellular matrix (dECM) are particularly relevant for cardiac applications due to their intrinsic biocompatibility, bioactivity, and similarity to native tissue composition.

Collagen, the most abundant protein in the human body, constitutes roughly 75-85% of the cardiac ECM [238]. It provides biomechanical stability, and it is deeply involved in cell adhesion and migration. There are various types of collagen molecules, but the most naturally occurring and used is type I, which is biocompatible, easily degradable, and has been shown to provide a good extracellular environment [239,240]. Due to its thermally driven gelation, collagen has long been used to fabricate cardiac patches that promote angiogenesis, increase infarct wall thickness, inhibit ventricular remodeling, and improve overall cardiac function [180,225,241]. When loaded with stem cells, progenitor cells, or CM cells, collagen gels have shown to enhance vascularization and promote recovery of cardiac function after implantation into infarcted tissues [181,182]. Injectable collagen type I hydrogels have also shown promise in mouse MI models, as they can be delivered directly to the infarcted region [183] (Fig. 3A). However, pure collagen patches exhibit poor mechanical properties, making them unable to withstand the stress exerted by the ventricle [48]. To address this issue, many studies in the literature have proposed systems in which a second component can improve collagen properties. For example, spherical nanogold [182] or gold nanoparticles [184] (Fig. 3B) have been added to collagen matrices. In both cases, the resulting patches exhibited increased stiffness, along with enhanced contractility, electrical conductivity, and sustained drug release. Similarly, CNTs increased the gel tensile stress, Young's modulus, toughness, and electrical conductivity [67,185,186]. Closely related to collagen, gelatin, the denatured derivative of collagen, is another well-established material in cardiac regeneration. Gelatin supports CMs adhesion and proliferation but, like collagen, suffers from poor mechanical resilience. Mechanical reinforcement can be achieved by crosslinking gelatin or combining it with synthetic polymers, which improve its mechanical properties and expose the RGD sequence to support cell adhesion [169]. In an innovative approach, M. Lee et al. developed a paintable cardiac patch composed of gelatin, titanium carbide MXene, and dextran aldehyde [204], achieving improved mechanical support, electrical conductivity, and tissue adhesion. Crosslinking with genipin, a naturally derived biocompatible agent, has also been shown to produce micropatterned gelatin hydrogels that guide CM alignment and enable synchronized beating for up to 3 months [205] (Fig. 3C). Chemical modification has further expanded gelatin's versatility. The synthesis of gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA), a photocrosslinkable derivative, allows precise tuning of mechanical stiffness and degradation rate, while maintaining excellent biocompatibility. Hong et al. demonstrated the potential of GelMA by injecting human vascular progenitor cells encapsulated in a GelMA matrix directly into infarcted mouse hearts, leading to improved vascularization and contractility [18] (Fig. 3D). Other studies have used GelMA to deliver derived extracellular vesicles into infarcted mice, enhancing contractility, reducing fibrosis, and promoting angiogenesis, with controlled EV release for up to 3 weeks [20,43]. Furthermore, GelMA-based composites have been engineered to enhance performance under hypoxic conditions, for example, by incorporating PCL microspheres or calcium peroxide particles for sustained oxygen release over several weeks [207,208]. Blending GelMA with polyethylene glycol diacrylate (PEGDA) improved myocardial engraftment 7 days post-MI [209], while the inclusion of gold/silica nanoparticles enhanced mechanical strength and supported CM adhesion, viability, and proliferation [210].

In addition to collagen and gelatin, fibrin, another natural ECM-derived protein, has also been extensively studied for cardiac tissue regeneration due to its biological properties. Fibrin, formed by the thrombin-mediated polymerization of fibrinogen monomers, is highly biocompatible and promotes angiogenesis and wound healing. Its gelation rate and mechanical properties can be easily controlled by the fibrinogen-to-thrombin ratio [238]. Its main limitations, however, are its rapid degradation, which can restrict long-term stability [11]. To address this, Jabbour et al. created a fibrin-based cardiac patch seeded with human induced pluripotent stem cell-derived-CMs, which exhibited cell alignment, synchronous contractions, and mature calcium signaling after 3–4 weeks [32]. Other strategies include covalent functionalization of fibrinogen with a bi-functional amine-reactive PEG to form crosslinked vascular constructs with slower degradation, and able to support CM regeneration [11,[228], [229], [230]]. Further innovations, such as aligned fibrin micro-threads embedded in a fibrin hydrogel [202] or aligned electrospun fibrin fibers [203], have promoted cell alignment, enhanced contractility and CM conduction, and supported endothelial network formation without compromising cardiac cell function.

A particular type of protein-based hydrogels can be derived through the removal of cells from native tissues to obtain decellularized ECM. The use of this material has been encouraged by its inherent biocompatibility and the presence of a rich milieu of proteins, proteoglycans, glycosaminoglycans, cytokines, and provascular factors [188,242]. In particular, these dECM components allow to mimic tissue-specific composition and play an important role in cell adhesion, proliferation, differentiation, and mechanotransduction [25,243]. For this reason, plenty of examples can be found in the literature of the use of dECM, in particular cardiovascular engineering purposes [[189], [190], [191], [192], [193], [194], [195], [196]]. Acellular constructs were shown to induce the recruitment of endogenous progenitor cells and favor cellular infiltration [195]. On the other hand, dECM hydrogels have shown an enhanced capacity to promote cell-matrix interactions and tissue-specific differentiation, resulting in a higher regenerative potential than conventional hydrogels [244]. For this reason, dECM has also been used to deliver cells to the infarction site, allowing the topical and persistent delivery of paracrine moieties to improve cardiac function and reduce adverse remodeling in rats [197].

It is paramount to note that tissue origin and age have been identified as important factors in determining the overall ECM properties, considering their influence on tissue composition, microstructure, and mechanical properties [243]. Decellularized matrix for cardiac applications was initially derived from human skin, porcine urinary bladder, porcine small intestinal submucosa, and other more readily-available biological sources [[198], [199], [200],245]. However, functional differences could be noticed with native cardiac ECM, such as in protein composition, macrostructure, and induced cell lineage commitment [[246], [247], [248], [249]]. At a later stage, progress in decellularization protocols allowed to directly employ the more specific heart-derived dECM. Nevertheless, dECM from secondary sources is less complicated to obtain and has lately exhibited more limited batch-to-batch variability [250]. With aging, the overall microstructure of the ECM does not undergo significant modifications. Nevertheless, the composition and macrostructure of its components, particularly proteins, change substantially, further modulating the final dECM mechanics and the subsequent cellular response [243]. For instance, in the case of cardiac ECM, these alterations are due to the different phenotypes expressed by cardiac cells over tissue development, going from proliferative, immature progenitor cells to mature and contractile CMs [250]. In particular, the cardiac ECM from more adult tissues exhibits a stiffer collagenous matrix and a more mature basement membrane [201].

Besides the criticalities related to the specific source, the use of hydrogels from dECM is often hindered by other factors. Firstly, the production of dECM-based hydrogels generally requires large amounts of native tissue. To circumvent this issue, researchers explored the possibility of generating ECM to be decellularized by in vitro culturing the needed cells [188]. Second, the precise isolation and quantification of the ECM components are associated with relatively high costs that may limit scale-up and clinical translation [194]. Further affecting application in the clinic, the properties and composition of decellularized ECM can vary between batches, resulting in inhomogeneous structure and properties over different sources [251]. Finally, effective but harsh decellularization protocols may cause structural damage and impair the mechanical response of the final hydrogel [25].

Polysaccharide-based hydrogels

Polysaccharide-based hydrogels include alginate, chitosan, and HA, are biocompatible and have tunable physicochemical properties, making them suitable for cardiac TE.

Alginate, a natural polysaccharide derived from brown algae, has excellent mechanical and gel properties. It has been widely used in regenerative medicine for its biocompatibility, controllable gelation, and high availability [252,253]. Alginate was the first injectable acellular biomaterial tested in a clinical trial for the treatment of MI [254]. However, due to the lack of cell-adhesion domains, it is often modified by combining it with other materials, such as gelatin [187]. Crosslinking with gelatin has been shown to improve hydrolysis, cell adhesion, and cell viability up to 24 h after injection [173]. Alginate has also been used for drug delivery, for example, to encapsulate crocin, a compound known to reduce oxidative stress in infarcted myocardium [30]. In a more complex design, Rodness et al. combined microspheres and patches based on alginate to deliver vascular endothelial growth factor to the heart of rats after myocardial injury [21] (Fig. 4A). The tunable degradation profile of microspheres supported the prolonged release of the soluble factor, while the patches provided mechanical support to the LV. To further enhance functionality, alginate has also been combined with conductive compounds such as reduced GO to promote patch electroactivity, either dispersed within the gel or applied as a coating [174,175]. Another important natural polysaccharide is chitosan, derived from the N-deacetylation of chitin, which is extracted from arthropods' cuticles and cephalopods' endoskeletons [24]. The degree of acetylation determines both the degradation rate and the biological behavior [57]. Chitosan is an abundant, inexpensive, biodegradable, and biocompatible polysaccharide with intrinsic antibacterial properties, making it a valuable material for cell or drug delivery, particularly for MI treatment [176,177,255]. In 2021, Domengé et al. reported the fabrication of acellular chitosan hydrogels for epicardial implantation in two murine models of cardiomyopathy to evaluate their potential to integrate into host tissue and restore heart function [57]. In a follow-up study, the same group biofunctionalized chitosan patches with MSC-derived secretome, achieving preserved suturability, controlled protein release, and improved cardiac function after 30 days in a murine model of chronic ischemia [24]. However, chitosan hydrogels typically exhibit poor mechanical properties and stability. To address this, chemical or physical crosslinking (e.g., with aqueous NaOH or gaseous NH3) has been introduced, improving mechanical performance through increased chain entanglements and intermolecular junctions. This allows the gels to provide temporary structural support to damaged myocardium and promote mononuclear cell infiltration, suggesting good epicardial integration [57,178,256]. Furthermore, Mawad et al. immobilized a conductive polymer on chitosan films, achieving electroactivity for two weeks and enhancing cardiac electrophysiology without proarrhythmic effects [179].

Hyaluronic acid (HA), another biodegradable and biocompatible polysaccharide, is widely used due to its natural presence in connective tissues and ability to interact with cells through specific receptors. Derived from animal or microbial sources, HA possesses an acidic chemical structure that supports CM proliferation and differentiation [257]. The biological activity of HA is strongly influenced by its molecular weight: high molecular weight HA (HMW-HA) tends to exhibit anti-inflammatory and homeostatic functions in tissue microenvironments, whereas low molecular weight HA (LMW-HA) has pro-inflammatory effects, which can activate different cell signaling pathways and influence inflammation and cell behavior [211]. Therefore, high-molecular-weight HA (HMW HA) is usually preferred for cardiac tissue engineering, because it helps reduce inflammation and supports cell survival and tissue repair after myocardial infarction. Advances have focused on shear-thinning HA hydrogels, which can be injected through a syringe and self-assemble in situ via non-covalent guest–host interactions. To enable these dynamic crosslinking mechanisms, HA is chemically modified to introduce specific functional groups that serve as a guest or host. A recent example is the shear-thinning HA hydrogel (STGel), in which HA is modified with β-cyclodextrin and adamantane to form a reversible host-guest complex enabling injectability and in situ self-assembly. In detail, in this study, HA was used to encapsulate a matrix metalloproteinase inhibitor for sustained release in a porcine ischemia–reperfusion model [26]. Other modifications, such as norbornene-functionalized HA (Nor-HA), can also be used in guest-host strategies with complementary moieties, while methacrylated HA (HA-MA) can be employed to obtain photocrosslinkable hydrogels, enhancing scaffold stability. These chemical and physical properties make HA-based hydrogels highly suitable for in vivo cardiac applications. Indeed, when applied in preclinical models, HA-based systems not only support local drug delivery and cell retention but also lead to functional improvements: treated hearts exhibited reduced infarct expansion, limited LV dilation, improved contractility, and decreased inflammation, while supporting long-term cell survival, engraftment, and progressive cardiac and vascular differentiation [231]. Together, these results highlight the potential of HA hydrogels as versatile platforms for minimally invasive, localized cardiac therapy that integrates both biochemical and biomechanical cues.

PEG-based materials

As an alternative to protein- and polysaccharide-based hydrogels, synthetic hydrogels have gained interest for their precisely tunable properties, high reproducibility, and chemical versatility. By enabling fine control over polymer composition, crosslinking density, and functionalization, these materials allow researchers to tailor mechanical, chemical, and biological characteristics to specific therapeutic needs. Among synthetic systems, the most widely studied and used is PEG, which is a nontoxic, water-soluble polymer. Thanks to its biocompatibility and hydrophilic nature, PEG has been employed in diverse cardiovascular applications, from injectable hydrogels for MI therapy [212], to engineering vascular networks [[213], [214], [215]] (Fig. 4B). Moreover, PEG has been used to modify nanoparticles, improving their biocompatibility and circulatory stability [216,258], as well as to create anti-adhesive coatings that prevent postsurgical cardiac adhesions [217]. Despite its many advantages, PEG is intrinsically bioinert and lacks natural cell-adhesion motifs, which limit direct cell interaction in tissue engineering applications. For this reason, it is often combined with bioactive hydrogels or other polymers to enhance cell adhesion and tissue integration [241]. The chemical simplicity of PEG also makes it possible to directly bind bioactive moieties to the polymer backbone. A relevant example is the work of Yuan et al. [218], who developed an injectable PEG-based hydrogel incorporating citrate-derived oligomers covalently bound to the polymer backbone, designed for controlled release, and physically encapsulating growth factors. When injected into the LV of post-MI rats, it improved myocardial structure and function, reduced scar size, and promoted neovascularization. Like the other hydrogels, PEG has been integrated with conductive nanomaterials, such as graphene [219], gold nanoparticles [220,221], titanium carbide MXene [222], and PEDOT:PSS [223] (Fig. 4C), to better replicate the electromechanical behavior of native myocardium.

Composite materials

To address the intrinsic limitations of single-component systems, composite hydrogels have emerged as a powerful strategy for cardiac TE, enabling the integration of multiple biomaterials to achieve enhanced mechanical strength, improved bioactivity, and superior functional performance. A number of studies have demonstrated the potential of such composite approaches. Ding et al. developed a chitosan-gelatin hydrogel enriched with angiogenic factors, which successfully promoted vascularization and cardiac recovery after injection in MI models [64]. Similarly, an interpenetrating network of collagen and PEGDA [232] was shown to exhibit enhanced stiffness, thromboresistance, and structural stability, while maintaining high cell viability and allowing precise control over cell morphology. Xu et al. produced injectable collagen-PEG gels for cell delivery onto the infarcted area of mice [233] while a PEG-gelatin composite was developed as a cardiac hemostatic bioglue [234]. In parallel, combining natural polymers with conductive compounds has proven effective in recapitulating the electromechanical activity of the cardiac tissue. For instance, Pournemati et al. incorporated gold nanoparticles into an alginate-gelatin hydrogel, increasing matrix conductivity and improving cardiac cell adhesion, differentiation, and function in a rat model of MI [169]. PEGylated chitosan hydrogels were reinforced with TiO2 nanoparticles by Liu et al., enhancing mechanical properties and promoting CM retention and network formation in vitro [235]. Advanced fabrication techniques like 3D printing have further expanded the potential of composite hydrogels. Shih et al. printed patches using photo-crosslinked gelatin and PU hydrogels: the former promoted angiogenesis, while the latter increased microvessel density and supported tissue remodeling [206]. Similarly, a bioink combining GelMA, collagen methacrylate (ColMA), and adhesion proteins was developed to 3D-print cardiac tissues with functional chambers, enabling stem cell maturation and contraction [227]. Simińska-Stanny et al. combined tyramine-modified PEG, gelatin, and alginate to coaxially 3D-bioprint vascular structures [236]. Conductive composites based on synthetic polymers have also gained attention. PEDOT:PSS was combined with polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) to form a stretchable, conductive hydrogel patch that was functionalized with adhesion peptides and remained stable under cyclic strain [224]. Likewise, PPy nanoparticles were incorporated into a GelMA-PEGDA matrix, improving CM synchronization and cardiac performance post-MI [226]. An illustrative example is provided by Bao et al., who demonstrated the integration of multiple engineering strategies within a single platform [237] (Fig. 4D). Their melamine-based multi-armed PEG was crosslinked with thiol-modified HA, incorporated with GO, and employed to deliver cells to the infarcted sites of rats.

Altogether, these studies highlight how composite hydrogels can be rationally designed to synergistically combine mechanical robustness, biological signaling, electrical conductivity, and controlled release, thereby addressing the multifaceted challenges of myocardial repair and regeneration through a single, integrated material platform.

Hybrids

Both thermoplastic/elastomeric polymers and hydrogels play a central role in the design of biomaterials for cardiac TE, yet each class presents distinct advantages and limitations. Thermoplastic and elastomeric polymers offer tunable mechanical properties and excellent mechanical strength, making them suitable for load-bearing applications. However, their synthetic nature limits biointegration. Conversely, hydrogels can better mimic the ECM, promoting cell adhesion and tissue formation, although their poor mechanical performance may compromise stability in dynamic environments [25,259,260]. To reconcile these opposing characteristics, hybrid systems have been developed by combining thermoplastics/elastomers with hydrogels, thus enhancing mechanical integrity while preserving biomimetic features essential for tissue regeneration [50,61].

In a related approach, gelatin combined with PLA yielded tubular vascular scaffolds with tailored mechanics [265], and jet-spun with poly-4-hydroxybutyrate into fibrous semilunar heart valve structures that required no post-processing [262] (Fig. 5B). Electrospun nanofiber systems have been particularly successful in producing hybrid scaffolds with tissue-like architecture. PCL nanofibers with collagen and elastin reduced infarct area and improved LV remodeling in mice, promoting bone-derived MSCs differentiation into cardiac lineage [268]. PCL blended with gelatin and PANI yielded conductive nanofibrous patches [269], whereas MEW-reinforced GelMA bioinks optimized with collagen, fibrinogen, and Matrigel supported the fabrication of myocardial constructs with pre-vascular pathways while maintaining precise control over cell distribution [263] (Fig. 5C). In another example, Akentjew et al. reproduced the J-shaped mechanical profile of coronary arteries by reinforcing GelMA–alginate gels with PCL fibers, fabricating biomimetic multilayered constructs [264] (Fig. 5D).

Other hybrid systems have focused on optimizing mechanical resilience and bioactivity simultaneously. PU/ethyl cellulose nanofibrous scaffolds showed elastomeric properties and supported cell adhesion, with thicker fibers enhancing proliferation [164]. Crosslinked HA and PCL porous membranes were fabricated using UV-polymerized ethylene glycol dimethacrylate [27,270,271], and their pores were filled with a RADA-based peptide hydrogel loaded with cells, combining structural support with a bioactive matrix. This partially resorbable system improved cell retention, vascularization, and cardiac function after implantation in large animal models of MI. A notable example of material synergy is the tri-composite patch developed by Kim et al., in which electrospun PCL meshes were sequentially coated with fibrin and alginate [49] (Fig. 6A). In this design, each material served distinct functions: PCL provided structure and alignment, fibrin promoted healing and angiogenesis, while alginate modulated fibrin's immunogenicity, achieving mechanical robustness and a physiological microenvironment. Similarly, collagen–alginate hydrogels with electroconductive PEDOT:PSS formed fibrous matrices that enhanced CM alignment, elongation, sarcomeric organization, and synchronous contraction without the need for external stimulation [17] (Fig. 6B). Further examples demonstrate how structural reinforcement and biological functionality can be achieved together. A PEGylated-fibrin patch reinforced with electrospun poly(ether ester urethane) mesh improved cardiac function and angiogenesis in infarcted rat hearts, reducing arrhythmias and fibrosis, while increasing muscle tissue and vascular density [12]. In a follow-up, the same patch was pre-vascularized in vitro, forming rudimentary capillary networks, and showed enhanced vascularization and muscularization two months post-implantation [273]. Smart materials

While traditional cardiac patches and injectable traditional hydrogels have shown significant promise in supporting cardiac repair, recent efforts have focused on further enhancing their functionality and adaptability. In this context, smart materials have emerged as an innovative approach, offering dynamic responsiveness principally to physical (e.g., temperature, mechanical forces, light, magnetic fields), chemical (e.g., pH, specific compounds), and biological (e.g., enzymes, cellular signals) stimuli [287]. Smart materials are designed to undergo changes in their structure or function in response to external stimuli, enabling a wide range of applications in cardiac TE. For instance, many systems have been developed to remain in a liquid state before implantation and to rapidly reticulate upon exposure to physiological conditions. This in situ gelation not only allows for minimally invasive delivery but also ensures improved retention of encapsulated cells or therapeutic agents and provides controlled release over time. Such capabilities exemplify the broader potential of stimuli-responsive materials to combine the structural advantages of conventional systems with enhanced integration and functional performance. By combining the advantages of traditional materials with dynamic responsiveness, smart materials offer a versatile and promising platform for cardiac TE. Although there appears to be a dominance of hydrogel-based formulations, several examples of smart thermoplastic/elastomeric polymers can also be found in the literature. The following sections will examine these materials according to their dominant type of stimulus: physical, chemical, biological, and combined stimuli, highlighting their potential to advance cardiac repair strategies. Physical stimuli-responsive materials

Among the different categories of smart materials, those responsive to physical stimuli, including temperature, mechanical forces, light, and magnetic fields, have demonstrated good potential in cardiac TE due to their ability to dynamically adapt to the physiological environment. Within this class, thermosensitive hydrogels have been extensively explored for their ability to undergo sol–gel transitions in response to body temperature, forming three-dimensional scaffolds directly at the defect site and allowing for minimally invasive delivery [310]. A number of thermoresponsive systems are based on synthetic block copolymers capable of temperature-triggered phase transitions. For instance, thermoresponsive gels based on PLLA–PEG/poly(D-lactic acid) (PDLA)–PEG stereocomplexes were developed to undergo sol–gel transition near body temperature and, when injected in a rat MI model, improve fractional shortening and reduce infarct size and LV cavity area [286]. Chen et al. developed Col@gel, a PLGA–PEG–PLGA-based hydrogel that undergoes sol-gel transition at 35 °C and enables local release of colchicine [293] (Fig. 7A). Similarly, Yoshizaki et al. developed an ABA-type triblock copolymer composed of poly(ε-caprolactone-co-glycolic acid) and PEG that undergoes a temperature-induced sol-gel transition and, with partial chemical crosslinking, enhanced stability and cell retention in ischemic myocardium [63]. Another fully-synthetic important family of thermoresponsive materials is represented by poly(N-isopropyl acrylamide) (NIPAAm)-based materials. Yoshizumi et al. reported the development of a poly(NIPAAm-co-(2-hydroxyethyl methacrylate)-co-methacrylatepolylactide) hydrogel exhibiting mechanical robustness and thermal sensitivity, which improved LV wall thickness and vascularization in MI models [288]. Matsumura et al. extended this work in a porcine model, confirming its ability to attenuate maladaptive remodeling [289]. In a similar approach, Hayashi et al. employed a PNIPAAm-co-N-vinylpyrrolidone-co-methacrylatepolylactide system optimized for ECM delivery and functional cardiac improvement [290]. Complementarily, Rocker et al. also utilized PNIPAAm, grafted onto a glycol chitosan backbone, to develop a thermoresponsive injectable platform for sequential protein release, demonstrating therapeutic efficacy [295]. Beyond synthetic designs, natural polymer-based thermogels have gained increasing attention. Tohidi et al. proposed a collagen–HA matrix incorporating bacterial cellulose and gold nanoparticles, enabling gelation at 32 °C, with self-healing and conductive properties suitable for CM stimulation [310]. A similar system using dopamine-grafted HA with Ca2+-loaded liposomes and alginate achieved gelation in only 3 min at 37 °C [292] (Fig. 7B). Similarly, chitosan-based systems have been tailored for thermal responsiveness and bioactivity. Baei et al. combined chitosan and β-glycerophosphate to enable thermally induced gelation and conductivity via gold nanoparticles, enhancing MSC proliferation and cardiogenic differentiation [303]. Another chitosan-poloxamer hydrogel developed by Tohidi et al. featured thermoresponsive sol-gel transition and tunable mechanical/electrical properties, fostering cell infiltration and adhesion [304]. Lastly, more complex systems have sought to integrate multiple physical stimuli, particularly temperature and mechanical shear, to enhance injectability and responsiveness. Wang et al. designed a shear-thinning, self-healing hydrogel based on elastin-like protein and HA, where physical gelation was triggered at 37 °C and complemented by dynamic covalent crosslinking. This system provided mechanical protection to encapsulated cells and enabled localized delivery of miRNA-loaded nanoparticles, enhancing cardiac repair [171,306]. Following a similar concept, Steel et al. designed a shear-thinning, self-healing hydrogel based on a heteroarm star copolymer conjugated with engineered polypeptides [294]. This system flows under shear during injection and rapidly reforms a gel in situ without the need for non-physiological crosslinkers, preserving cytokine activity. Chemical stimuli-responsive materials

Chemical-responsive materials represent another promising class of smart materials, capable of responding to specific chemical cues such as pH changes or specific molecules. These systems enable precise, localized, and on-demand delivery of therapeutic agents tailored to the pathological environment. A clear example of this concept is provided by Bastings et al., who designed a pH-sensitive PEG-based hydrogel capable of controlled release of various growth factors, effectively reducing scar formation in a chronic MI pig model [307] (Fig. 7C). Building on similar principles, a 2022 study introduced an injectable hydrogel releasing oncostatin M, a cytokine involved in CM proliferation and inflammation regulation, in response to physiological pH (7.4), enabling continuous localized therapy [296]. A different but related approach was developed by Li et al., who synthesized a pH-triggered injectable hydrogel that remained liquid at neutral pH but rapidly reticulated in the mildly acidic conditions typical of ischemic and infarcted tissues [298] (Fig. 7D). Formed via free radical polymerization of NIPAAm, propylacrylic acid, and other monomers, this system allowed minimally invasive administration and promoted upregulation of cardiac-related genes in vitro.

These examples show how chemically stimuli-responsive hydrogels can adapt dynamically to the microenvironment of diseased myocardium, enabling controlled therapeutic release in response to specific triggers while limiting systemic exposure. By using stimuli typical of pathological environments, such systems can improve the selectivity, timing, and efficacy of treatments for MI and cardiac remodeling.

Biological stimuli-responsive materials

Biological stimuli-responsive materials can be considered an advanced class of smart materials designed to sense and react to biochemical signals naturally present within the physiological or pathological microenvironment. In cardiac TE, these systems enable precise, on-demand release of bioactive molecules, such as cytokines, growth factors, or drugs, in response to specific biological triggers, including redox potential, enzymatic activity, or inflammatory mediators. This adaptive behavior ensures that therapeutic action occurs only where and when it is needed, thereby increasing efficacy while minimizing off-target effects. Among these biological cues, ROS, which is abundantly produced in ischemic myocardium following MI, have been most frequently exploited as endogenous triggers. Based on their interaction with oxidative stress, ROS-related biomaterials can be broadly classified into two main categories: (i) ROS-responsive materials, which undergo physicochemical changes in the presence of elevated ROS levels, enabling controlled drug release or dynamic modulation of material properties [23,[299], [300], [301],308], and (ii) ROS-scavenging materials, which are designed to neutralize excessive ROS and thereby attenuate oxidative damage [165,297,299,300,302]. In the following, we focus on representative examples of ROS-responsive materials, which exploit the oxidative post-MI microenvironment to achieve stimuli-triggered therapeutic functions. For instance, Wang et al. developed a ROS-sensitive hydrogel based on hyperbranched poly-β-amino esters, enabling controlled release of anti-inflammatory drugs and improved cardiac function [301] (Fig. 8A). Similarly, Li et al. synthesized a ROS-cleavable PVA hydrogel crosslinked with N1-(4-boronobenzyl)-N3-(4-boronophenyl)-N1,N1,N3,N3-te-tramethylpropane-1,3-diaminium (TSPBA), enabling minimally invasive pericardial delivery of bFGF. Upon ROS exposure, the cytokine was released and penetrated the epicardium, reducing fibrosis and enhancing angiomyogenesis in both rat and pig MI models [308] (Fig. 8B). Whereas, ROS-scavenging materials, which primarily aim to neutralize excessive oxidative stress, will be discussed later within the section on multi-stimuli-responsive materials. Beyond ROS, other endogenous biological signals have been employed. Fu et al. reported a glutathione-sensitive disulfide-crosslinked chitosan hydrogel for bFGF delivery. Degradation was triggered by thiol–disulfide exchange in the reductive environment, improving LV function and reducing fibrosis even in the absence of the cytokine [256]. Matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), overexpressed during post-MI remodeling, represent another relevant biological trigger. Purcell et al. developed an HA-based hydrogel crosslinked with MMP-cleavable peptides and incorporating dextran sulfate to regulate protein release [305]. In a pig MI model, the system mitigated LV remodeling, reduced profibrotic gene expression, and improved diastolic function. In a series of works, Gao's group developed a ROS-degradable PU scaffold incorporating thioketal linkages [23,165,299,300]. Electrospun into patches and loaded with methylprednisolone or endothelial-attracting peptides, the material reduced oxidative damage, fibrosis, and inflammation while promoting angiogenesis and CM survival. Multi-stimuli-responsive materials

Multi-stimuli-responsive materials are an advanced class of smart biomaterials that integrate multiple responsiveness mechanisms, such as pH, temperature, redox potential, ROS, or other biochemical cues, to more closely mimic the dynamic and multifactorial environment of the infarcted myocardium. By combining different triggering modalities, these systems achieve fine spatiotemporal control over drug delivery, degradation, and tissue interaction, thus providing an adaptive therapeutic response that evolves with the changing physiology of the damaged heart. This multimodal responsivness enables precise, localized release of therapeutic agents in response to pathological changes, offering the potential to improve myocardial repair outcomes.

A representative example is the injectable, self-healing PVA hydrogel engineered by Hu et al., which combines ROS- and pH-responsive phenylboronic acid-modified carboxymethyl cellulose with PLGA-curcumin nanoparticles and collagen type III. This system promoted angiogenesis and inhibited apoptosis in vitro, while in vivo administration reduced inflammation and fibrosis, ultimately restoring cardiac function [309]. The pathological environment of MI is characterized by metabolic acidosis and bursts of ROS production, both of which can be exploited for controlled hydrogel degradation and therapeutic release. Garbern et al. advanced this concept with a thermo- and pH-responsive hydrogel capable of sustained local delivery of basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF), which enhanced angiogenesis and improved cardiac function [291]. Similarly, Jiang et al. developed a chitosan–citric acid-NIPAM composite hydrogel with pH- and temperature-sensitive behavior. This injectable hydrogel delivered oncostatin in response to acidic ischemic conditions, promoting CM proliferation, angiogenesis, and reducing fibrosis and apoptosis in a rat MI model [296]. In a large animal model, Spaulding et al. injected a comb-like NIPAAm-PEG methacrylate hydrogel combining ROS-scavenging and thermoresponsive properties [297]. The PEG components acted as an antioxidant, neutralizing ROS, while the thermoresponsive backbone enabled minimally invasive delivery through injection. In sheep models of MI, this treatment increased ventricular wall thickness, lowered ROS levels, and improved contractile protein function, highlighting the therapeutic potential of multi-responsive hydrogels in cardiac repair. A notable example of this class is the Janus hydrogel developed by He et al., a bifacial cardiac patch designed with distinct adhesive and functional properties on each side [302] (Fig. 8C). The bottom layer, an ionic adhesive hydrogel, provides strong mechanical adhesion and electrochemical stability, while the top layer exhibits anti-adhesive and non-fouling characteristics, creating an optimal environment for CM proliferation and maturation. This system leverages dynamic crosslinking through N,N′-bis(acryloyl)cystamine, enabling adaptive mechanical responses with strain-stiffening behavior that helps prevent post-surgical scar tissue formation. Furthermore, by promoting the release of superoxide dismutase, the Janus hydrogel effectively modulated oxidative stress, attenuated inflammation, and enhanced angiogenesis in preclinical models. Although not injectable, this bifunctional construct offers a promising multifunctional platform for myocardial regeneration by synergistically integrating biological and mechanical stimuli responses. Further research could explore its potential for minimally invasive delivery and scar tissue remodeling post-MI.

One of the earliest clinical examples was reported by Frey et al., in 2014, who evaluated a 1% sodium alginate and 0.3% calcium gluconate solution in patients recovering from MI [314]. In particular, the formulation was delivered intracoronary in patients with moderate-to-large ST-segment elevation MIs, after successful revascularization. The procedure can be performed using a relatively straightforward approach, via percutaneous radial artery access under local anesthesia, thereby avoiding the need for open surgery. Upon injection, the alginate solution permeated the infarcted myocardium and gelled in situ through calcium-dependent reversible crosslinking, thanks to the elevated extracellular calcium concentration in the infarcted area. A temporary bioabsorbable cardiac scaffold was formed that mimicked the ECM and provided mechanical reinforcement. No adverse events or compromise of blood flow were observed, confirming the safety and feasibility of catheter-based hydrogel delivery in MI patients.

In 2015, the use of a fibrin scaffold was reported to deliver human embryonic stem cell-derived progenitor cells onto the infarcted area in a 68-year-old patient who had suffered from severe heart failure [315]. After comparing several candidate materials, fibrin was selected for its biocompatibility, cell-supportive properties, and tunable mechanical behavior (∼6 kPa), suitable for intraoperative handling. The fibrin patch was easily applied onto the infarcted region during open-heart surgery, and the postoperative course was uneventful. Over the following months, the patient's symptoms and regional contractility improved, marking a significant milestone in cell-seeded scaffold therapy.

Another report from 2015 described the direct injection of an alginate-hydrogel scaffold into the dilated LV of patients with advanced chronic heart failure (HF), to modify the LV shape and size [316]. LV dilation during HF progression causes wall thinning and increases myofiber load, perpetuating cardiac damage. The minimally invasive implantation of the alginate-based material improved exercise tolerance and clinical symptoms after six months, likely due to stress redistribution and reduced LV expansion. The research group ascribed these results to the effective reduction of wall stress and prevention of further LV enlargement. In addition, no increase in ventricular arrhythmias or ectopy was observed, despite these side effects being a potential risk related to intramyocardial injections/implants. At the 1-year follow-up, results were consistent with the previous endpoint, showing a continuous improvement and the durability of the treatment [317].

Another alginate-based hydrogel was employed by Lee et al. to augment the LV wall in patients with dilated cardiomyopathy undergoing open-heart surgery [318]. The material consisted of two aqueous components mixed immediately before use and delivered intramyocardially through injection during a planned surgical procedure. After a few minutes, the calcium-alginate hydrogel could reach its final strength of 3-5 kPa. The follow-up period up to 24 months showed no adverse events attributed to the implants and a general improvement in LV function. In particular, improvements were evident as early as day 8 after surgery and continued through 12 months post-discharge. The authors stated that the early improvements represented a significant advancement compared with the previous strategies based on passive restraint devices. In fact, the beneficial effects of these were usually seen only months after the application. The application of the hydrogel to the LV myocardial free wall increased wall thickness and altered LV geometry to a more ellipsoidal shape. Specifically, the LV augmentation led to a decrease in the amount of ventricular ectopy and non-sustained ventricular tachycardia. In addition, numerical simulations estimated the decrease in myofiber stress at the end-of-diastole and at end-of-systole of 35%, with a more uniform stress distribution.

Besides the treatment of MI, hydrogels have been employed in clinical trials to evaluate their efficacy in other applications as well. As an example, Hasaniya et al. tested a hydrogel based on PEG to prevent adhesions between the epicardium, the sternum, and the surrounding structures during open-heart operations in infants [320]. The bicomponent pre-gel was sprayed onto the mediastinal surfaces before chest closure after the first surgery, taking care to cover the visible surfaces of the heart, the great vessels, the diaphragm, and the lateral sides of the pericardial space. After the two components were mixed, the hydrogel formed and swelled up to four times its original volume, resulting in the separation of the different surfaces. At reoperation, a reduction in adhesion formation was observed, along with no interference with the wound healing process.

Similarly, Wang et al. used a two-component PEG hydrogel as a local drug carrier for amiodarone, aiming to prevent postoperative atrial fibrillation (AF) in patients undergoing coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) [319]. For all the patients, the hydrogel was administered at the end of conventional CABG, before the closure of the sternum. Briefly, the two pre-gel components were thoroughly and easily sprayed in a thin layer onto the exposed epicardial surfaces, where they immediately adhered and gelled. The inclusion of amiodarone in the PEG matrix allowed for local administration of the drug and achieved sustained therapeutic myocardial concentrations. This resulted in the effective suppression of atrial tachyarrhythmias while keeping low systemic drug levels. In particular, the drug concentration was found to be persistently higher in the atria compared with the extracardiac tissues, preventing potentially toxic systemic side effects.

Finally, Shvatz et al. confirmed the effectiveness of localized amiodarone delivery using a sodium–alginate hydrogel sprayed on both atria of 60 CABG patients [5]. The clinical trial showed the effectiveness of the treatment in preventing atrial fibrillation, reducing the probability of occurrence by 18.9-fold.

Cardiac patches based on dECM also found applications in clinical trials. Traverse et al. published in 2019 the findings from a first-in-man clinical trial in post-MI patients, using an ECM hydrogel derived from decellularized porcine myocardium [321]. The authors reported improvements in left ventricular remodeling and in 6-min walk test distances. In 2020, Svystonyuk et al. described the modification of a commercially-available matrix from porcine small intestinal submucosal ECM and its implantation in patients with coronary artery disease [323]. The researchers found improved perfusion of the infarcted region, reduced scar size, and reverse remodeling. A work from 2024 describes the translational process to the clinic of another dECM patch, derived from human pericardial matrix and loaded with umbilical cord Wharton's jelly mesenchymal stromal cells [322]. Implanted in patients with non-acute myocardial infarction, the patch was found to have anti-inflammatory properties by promoting the differentiation of monocytes involved in myocardial repair into inflammation-resolving macrophages. In addition, lower levels of monocyte chemoattractants were found, further highlighting the overall modulation effect on systemic inflammation.

Together, these clinical studies underscore the translational potential of hydrogel-based therapies in diverse cardiac conditions, from MI and heart failure to postoperative adhesion prevention and arrhythmia control. The collective evidence suggests that injectable and sprayable hydrogels can be safely applied in humans, offering mechanical support, controlled local drug release, and enhanced tissue healing, laying the groundwork for next-generation bioengineered cardiac therapies.

Conclusions and future perspectives

The complex structural and functional organization of the heart, valves, and vasculature continues to make the design and fabrication of suitable cardiovascular implants a considerable challenge. The choice of material remains the first and most critical step in manufacturing effective constructs. Although a broad spectrum of processing techniques allows for partial tuning of macroscopic mechanical properties, thereby expanding the range of useable materials, several essential features remain intrinsically material-dependent, including surface mechanics, degradability, and surface chemistry or bioactivity.

Despite the plethora of materials evaluated in the literature of the past 10 years, no single “ideal” material has yet been identified for any specific cardiovascular application. This reflects the fact that each material class offers distinct advantages but also inherent drawbacks, compelling researchers to prioritize particular biological or mechanical requirements at the expense of others. The situation is well illustrated by the comparison between thermoplastic/elastomeric polymers and hydrogels. Thermoplastics and elastomers offer highly tunable chemistries, enabling the tuning of their mechanical response and degradation profiles. Their robust mechanical strength makes them particularly suitable for load-bearing cardiovascular applications, and their versatile processability allows for further adjusting their mechanical response and fabrication into a variety of complex geometries. However, these materials are generally hydrophobic and lack intrinsic bioactive cues, necessitating additional surface or bulk functionalization to improve cell adhesion and integration.

On the other hand, hydrogels mimic the biochemical and mechanical properties of the ECM that are needed to support cell attachment, proliferation, infiltration, and integration of the implant in the host environment. Nevertheless, traditional hydrogels are limited by soft mechanics, low mechanical resilience, and limited stability in dynamic environments, which can compromise their performance in load-bearing contexts.

As can be understood, a univocal material choice suitable for all the desired therapeutic requirements does not currently exist. Therefore, in every specific application, researchers must weigh the several challenges to tackle and prioritize what they consider the most critical. This need for compromise appears particularly clear in the field of MI treatment. Most constructs based on thermoplastic polymers are hardly injectable, thus requiring invasive procedures for patch application. Nevertheless, the bulk stiffness of the employed material and the toughness of the overall implant provide a solid and resilient matrix for the containment of the LV following MI, as can be seen in the work of Olvera et al. [76]. A meticulous design of the implant structure resulted in highly stretchable patches that could provide adequate support to the LV while also accommodating the physiological deformation of the heart. However, these constructs are generally implanted with no pre-seeded cells. In certain cases, they have been pre-populated in vitro to favor patch acceptance and integration by the host tissue. This is obtained through long or elaborate culture procedures to enable cells to attach and secrete their own ECM to tolerate handling and implantation. For instance, Castilho et al. manufactured PCL-based cardiac patches, which could be cell-seeded only through the use of custom-made Teflon molds [74]. On the other hand, hydrogels allow the direct incorporation of cells into their matrix thanks to their softer nature. In addition, they are usually derived from natural components and thus offer cells an in vivo-like environment that grants viability and physiological activity. Therefore, hydrogels provide a more straightforward solution to provide cell-laden materials for MI treatment. Even more interestingly, properties such as in situ gelation, shear thinning, and self-healing enable the direct injection onto the damaged area in a minimally invasive manner. The recent work of Pournemati et al. describes the relatively facile injection of an alginate/gelatin gel with a 28-gauge needle into the infarction sites of rats [169]. The gel was previously loaded with embryonic cardiac cells to promote tissue regeneration and gold nanoparticles to stimulate electrical activity, thus fulfilling simultaneously two important biological requirements. Nevertheless, their relatively soft mechanics often require additional crosslinking procedures to obtain materials that can better endure the cyclic stress generated by cardiac contractions. Strategies include the addition of reticulation agents such as genipin [205] or the inclusion of photocrosslinkable groups, as in the case of methacrylated gelatin [18]. In both examples, the researchers succeeded in improving the mechanical responses of the gels, obtaining more suitable implants for myocardial regeneration. However, the application of these solutions adds a further step to the device manufacturing process, discouraging scaling-up. Furthermore, the properties of the modified materials must be carefully re-evaluated to avoid any potential damage to cells or changes in their biological activity.

Given these complementary advantages and limitations, recent and ongoing research has increasingly focused on hybrid systems that combine thermoplastic/elastomeric polymers with hydrogels. These multicomponent constructs synergistically integrate mechanical robustness with biological activity, representing one of the most promising directions in cardiovascular TE. However, scalability and manufacturing complexity remain major bottlenecks. Current fabrication approaches often require multi-step coupling of materials with differing processability, slowing down translation to clinical practice.

To date, the majority of research has relied on long-established polymer families that have dominated TE for over two decades. This continued reliance reflects a lack of new polymer formulations with optimized biofunctionality and mechanical performance. The absence of widely adopted next-generation polymers highlights the ongoing need for materials that simultaneously address mechanical strength, biointegration, and manufacturability. The recent emergence of smart hydrogels represents an important step towards environment-responsive biomaterials with tailored, site-specific properties. Research in this field resulted in gels with tunable properties able to compete with hybrid scaffolds. By designing innovative chemical compositions and tailoring their structure, smart hydrogels have the potential to provide localized functionality and better precision in clinical treatment. The introduction of specific and adjustable cross-linking strategies has permitted improving their mechanics with respect to traditional gels, along with precise drug release. Nevertheless, this is still a young and rapidly evolving field, with ongoing engineering challenges such as the need for non-toxic synthesis routes, simplified fabrication workflows, reproducibility at scale, and true cell- or tissue-specific responsiveness [324]. Furthermore, robust in vivo data and long-term follow-up studies are still limited, and clinical translation of smart materials will require more comprehensive evaluation of performance, safety, and manufacturability.

Despite current limitations, beneficial effects in small and large animal models have been achieved so far, and a few human clinical trials have been conducted with relative success. However, the limited number of resorbable cardiovascular implants currently available on the market underscores how far translation still has to go. The commercial success of future biomaterials will depend on meeting multiple, interdependent criteria, including biocompatibility, mechanical adequacy, predictable degradation kinetics, and ease of processing. Designing polymers that simultaneously fulfill all these requirements remains a complex challenge, explaining the current reliance on multicomponent or hybrid systems.

Future research should focus on the rational design of new polymer compositions capable of unifying these properties within single-material systems or, at the very least, simplifying the production of existing hybrids. Streamlining fabrication while maintaining functional performance will be essential for industrial scalability and clinical adoption. Once such materials are available, treatment outcomes for patients with cardiovascular pathologies are expected to improve dramatically, paving the way toward safer, more effective, and longer-lasting regenerative therapies, and ultimately, toward a better quality of life for affected individuals.
