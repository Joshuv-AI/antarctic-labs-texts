A review of multidisciplinary care in metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatohepatitis and cardiometabolic disease, with a focus on Canada
Authors: James Kim, Paolo Raggi, Anne‐Marie Carreau, Sean Wharton, Alice Y Y Cheng, Mark G Swain
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCardiometabolic disease (CMD) is associated with an increased risk of metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatohepatitis (MASH). Most patients develop MASH in association with type 2 diabetes and obesity. Optimal disease management requires effective multidisciplinary collaboration between primary care physicians and specialists from different medical fields; however, awareness of the risks, association with CMD, diagnosis, complications, and management strategies of MASH is low among non‐liver specialists. In Canada, variable access to diagnostic testing and, until recently, the lack of national MASH guidelines, are also barriers to effective disease management. Ongoing cross‐disciplinary education and wide systemic changes are required to ensure timely patient identification and the establishment of holistic patient care pathways that can begin to address MASH and associated CMD.Keywords: cardiovascular disease, fatty liver disease, liver, obesity care, primary care

INTRODUCTION

Metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatohepatitis (MASH), previously known as non‐alcoholic steatohepatitis, is a severe subtype of metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD; Figure 1). 5 MASH is associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD), metabolic dysfunction, and related morbidity and mortality. 6 , 7 Growing rates of MASLD diagnoses are propelling a rapidly increasing health and economic burden globally, 8 including in Canada, 9 exacerbated by the presence of inter‐related comorbidities, e.g. obesity and type 2 diabetes (T2D), 8 , 10 , 11 and elevated risk of hepatic complications, e.g. cirrhosis, hepatic decompensation, and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). 12 In addition, adverse extrahepatic outcomes of MASLD, such as cardiometabolic morbidity and non‐hepatic cancers (e.g., uterine, gallbladder, kidney, thyroid, gastrointestinal), 8 , 13 extend beyond economic burden and are associated with significant societal impact. 8 Notably, patients with MASLD often have low quality of life, 8 reporting low emotional well‐being, fatigue, sleep disturbances, anger, and worry, not only as symptoms of MASLD itself, but also as a result of stigma and discrimination. 14 , 15 , 16

Diagnosis and identification of patients with progessive or advanced MASLD usually involves noninvasive tests (NITs) such as Fibrosis‐4 (FIB‐4). FIB‐4 has shown prognostic utility in clinical practice by stratifying patients into three risk categories based on scores relating to risk of advanced liver fibrosis: high (>2.67), indeterminate (1.30–2.67), and low (<1.30). 17 Scoring requires age and the measurement of aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels and is calculated as age (years) × (AST [U/L])/(platelets [109/L] × ALT [U/L]½). 17 However, many patients with progressive or advanced MASLD have AST and ALT levels within normal ranges and therefore remain undetected. 18 , 19 Second‐line NITs (e.g., transient elastography [TE] or the enhanced liver fibrosis [ELF] test), after initial FIB‐4 risk stratification, can identify patients who are more likely to benefit from speciality care. 20 However, access to these tests is usually limited to speciality clinics. 21 Patients with significant (≥stage 2) liver fibrosis are considered to have a higher risk of developing progressive MASH. 1 Those with advanced liver fibrosis (stage ≥3–4) are considered as having advanced liver disease or cirrhosis. 1

Due to the multiple facets of MASLD and the broad range of manifestations, it can be challenging to formulate a patient‐centric care plan. There is a clear link between MASLD and cardiometabolic disease (CMD), hence patients with additional cardiometabolic risk factors are candidates for additional evaluation. 22 Despite the intertwined nature of MASLD and CMD (Figure 2), many patients experience siloed care, which often fails to address all components of related comorbidities. 25 While there is a shift towards prioritising patient outcomes and keeping the patient at the forefront of clinical decision‐making, disconnected care is common, possibly due to lack of funding, traditional reimbursement models, and/or clinic structures. 26

Until there are widely available, effective treatments for MASH, primary management should be focused on treatment of metabolic comorbidities which can prevent progression to advanced fibrosis or even lead to fibrosis regression in patients with MASH. 27 , 28 However, many healthcare professionals (HCPs) who manage patients with advanced liver disease do not necessarily have the expertise to treat MASH‐associated CMD. Therefore, multidisciplinary care is essential to prevent the progression of MASH‐related hepatic and CVD to more advanced and irreversible stages. Collaborative, multidisciplinary MASH management is recognised as a desirable standard of care by medical societies, 29 , 30 with an emphasis on involving primary as well as specialist care. Henceforth in this article, collaborative groups including primary care physicians (PCPs), internal medicine specialists, hepatologists, gastroenterologists, endocrinologists, obesity specialists, and cardiologists, as well as other allied healthcare workers who are involved in MASH management will be referred to as ‘multiple specialty teams’. However, it is notable that the creation of effective care approaches involving these teams and multidisciplinary co‐management programmes for MASLD and CMD have rarely been established, including in Canada.

AIMS

Using published literature and expert opinion, we evaluated how Canadian primary care and specialist physicians could work collaboratively within a multidisciplinary setting to better manage patients with MASLD/MASH and CMD. Additionally, we examined the educational needs of primary care and specialist physicians and highlighted any gaps and limitations in multidisciplinary care model implementation that need to be addressed to enhance patient care.

HCPs INVOLVED IN THE CARE OF PATIENTS WITH MASLD/MASH AND CMD

Screening and diagnosis of MASLD and MASH

The initial diagnosis of MASLD is often established by PCPs 31 ; however, most individuals with MASH and clinically significant fibrosis remain unidentified in primary care. 32 Low awareness among PCPs may account for low diagnosis rates. Almost half (47%) of PCPs in the United States (US) surveyed in 2021 reported no knowledge of MASH clinical practice guidelines. 33 In 2020, 58% of Canadian PCPs reported being somewhat familiar or unfamiliar with MASLD, and 53% underestimated the prevalence of MASLD in Canada by over 10%. 34 In addition, only 48% of Canadian PCPs knew that patients with T2D are at higher risk for MASLD‐related liver fibrosis. 34

Despite evidence suggesting that screening for fibrosis in high‐risk patient populations is cost‐effective in the long‐term, 35 the importance of screening is not universally accepted, and there is a lack of awareness of screening technologies. In a 2019 survey of internal medicine, paediatric medicine, family medicine, geriatric medicine, endocrinology, and cardiology specialists, 19.3% reported that they strongly agreed that they should screen for MASLD, and 38.8% reported that they somewhat agreed. 36 In the AwareNASH survey, hepatologists, internists, PCPs, and cardiologists all reported ‘lack of awareness’ as the primary reason why physicians do not regularly screen patients for MASLD/MASH. 37 Other reasons included suboptimal diagnostic methods, lack of treatments and lack of awareness of intervention‐related MASH reversal. 37

The most common comorbidity to prompt screening for MASLD among internal medicine specialists is obesity (91.5%), followed by metabolic syndrome (86.4%) and diabetes (78.6%). 36 Similar patterns can be seen for subspecialist groups comprising endocrinologists and cardiologists. Non‐liver specialists are therefore aware of the risk factors associated with MASLD; however, this does not necessarily translate into effective screening and risk stratification. Overall, 85.7% of internal medicine specialists use liver enzymes to screen for MASLD, and 22.2% and 21.6% use serum biomarkers and TE for risk stratification, respectively. 36 However, only 17.9% of internal medicine specialists find biomarkers useful and 22.6% find TE useful, 36 which highlights the need for greater awareness of the full potential of risk stratification among these specialists.

The cost of FIB‐4 screening is a potential barrier to the performance of screening patients at risk of MASLD/MASH in Canada. In Ontario, AST testing (required for FIB‐4 calculations) is usually paid out‐of‐pocket by patients, and can therefore act as a barrier to determining the FIB‐4 scores. Furthermore, the time required for PCPs to calculate FIB‐4 scores is a drawback, even when AST is available. Notably, in Quebec, if ALT levels are normal then AST testing is not commonly pursued by the testing laboratory, despite the American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLD) guidelines recommending that ALT results should not be used in isolation to exclude MASH. 2 Furthermore, in 2007, AST testing was replaced by ALT on the Ontario laboratory requisition form, resulting in a substantial decline in availability. This, plus further restrictions placed on AST in 2013 due to evidence of inappropriate testing, has limited its use to patients under the care of liver specialists. There is further documented evidence that AST testing continues to decline in Ontario without the need for restrictions on testing. 38

Second‐line NITs such as TE can be utilised to confirm FIB‐4 stratification. 20 However, a recent publication by Burnside et al. identified a shortage of TE in Canada and raised concern regarding its sole availability in specialised settings, 21 which is not appropriate for general screening in patients at risk of MASLD. As of 2022, only 94 TE devices were available across Canada, 82% of which were located in large urban centres. By province, the largest number of TE devices per capita is found in British Columbia (4 per million inhabitants), followed by New Brunswick (~3.8 per million), Quebec (~2.7 per million), and Alberta (~2.6 per million). Ontario, Newfoundland, and Manitoba have the least TE devices per capita, with Ontario and Newfoundland each having ~2.0 devices and Manitoba having 1.5 devices per million. When available, the cost of TE poses a significant drawback for its regular use. Currently, there is no fee code for FibroScan® in Canada, and private companies typically charge patients ~$150 for a FibroScan®. Notably, only Quebec reimburses the cost of TE. 21

In 2015, a call to action was issued due to the lack of uniformity of TE availability in Canada, seeking to develop a national policy to improve access to this technology. 39 In Alberta, lack of access to FibroScan® within primary care was addressed by the implementation of shear wave elastography through community‐based radiology providers, 40 and this has been shown to be cost effective for diagnosing significant (i.e., stage ≥2 and ≥3) liver fibrosis, as an adjunct to FIB‐4 scoring. 41 However, the National Institute of Health and Care Excellence (NICE) has cited concerns regarding greater availability of FibroScan® within primary care, potentially leading to its use in wider populations and affecting its performance. 42

Subsequent management of patients with MASLD/MASH

PCPs

Published guidelines recommend referral to hepatology or gastroenterology if a patient has progressive or advanced MASH. 1 , 31 , 32 Canadian guidelines also recommend that patients with MASLD and less advanced liver disease can safely remain in the care of PCPs, with longitudinal repeat testing for progression. 3 , 43 However, many PCPs feel they have not received sufficient training on when to refer patients with MASLD/MASH to be assessed by a liver specialist, 16 , 44 resulting in either unnecessary or delayed referral. In an international survey of hepatologists, gastroenterologists, and internal medicine specialists conducted in 2020, almost half (49%) of respondents reported that <5% of patients with MASLD referred to them from PCPs had a previous FIB‐4 test. 45 Additionally, 82% of respondents reported that more than half of the patients referred to them did not have a MASLD diagnosis made by the referring physician. 45

In Canada, 20% of PCPs use FIB‐4 to stage liver fibrosis risk. This contrasts with 61% of specialists (including gastroenterologists, hepatologists, and internal medicine specialists) and 56% of hepatology nurses. 34 This discrepancy may stem from inconsistent information between guidelines, as well as a lack of updated guidelines overall and lack of access to NITs. 34 The Alberta and Manitoba MASLD guidelines recommend referring patients with FIB‐4 scores of ≥1.30 to specialists trained in the management of liver disease. 43 , 46 In contrast, in European and US guidelines, referral is not advised until FIB‐4 scores exceed 2.67 (unless secondary assessment identifies an elevated risk of adverse liver‐related outcomes), 2 , 30 possibly due to better access to second‐line NITs in these regions than in Canada, 47 especially within primary care. Other potential barriers to referral include: a lack of awareness of current guidelines, including the guidance for use of NITs such as FIB‐4; lack of established care pathways; and the burden of an additional screening technique. 16

Most patients that are screened for MASLD within primary care have FIB‐4 scores of <1.30. 48 Cardiometabolic risk management is recommended for all patients, regardless of FIB‐4 score. 30 Therefore, PCPs must be equipped with the skills and resources to screen for MASLD and manage cardiometabolic risk factors whilst initiating appropriate treatments. In the first instance, lifestyle modifications should be encouraged, and guidance for the management of more complex conditions should be sought from specialists once non‐invasive treatment strategies have been exhausted.

Hepatologists and gastroenterologists

Hepatologists and gastroenterologists play an essential role in monitoring disease progression in patients with MASH. 1 , 31 Despite these prominent roles, the number of gastroenterologists within Canada is disconcertingly low. In 2022, half of Canadian provinces had fewer than 2.0 gastroenterologists per 100,000 people. 21 Quebec had the largest concentration of gastroenterologists at just 2.9 per 100,000 people, followed by Alberta (2.8), Nova Scotia (2.3), and Ontario (2.0). No gastroenterologists were present in the Northwest Territories of Canada. Hence, access to specialised liver care can act as a bottleneck in the patient care journey.

Hepatologists are also involved in the surveillance and management of patients with cirrhosis, who are at higher risk of HCC and liver decompensation and may require liver transplantation. 49 Due to the limited and variable published data pertaining to post‐transplant outcomes in patients with MASLD/MASH, 50 including the presence of de novo metabolic syndrome and major cardiovascular events, 51 many hepatologists are unsure about the appropriate timing to recommend liver transplantation in these patients. 52 Indeed, only 29% of discussions between hepatologists/gastroenterologists and patients with MASH involve the possibility of liver transplantation. 52 Furthermore, conditions such as obesity and diabetes may worsen after liver transplantation and increase the risk of post‐transplant morbidity and mortality, 53 and a very high body mass index (BMI) is considered a relative contraindication for liver transplant due to the association of severe obesity with other conditions that preclude surgery, such as CVD, renal dysfunction and hypertension. 54 Although the presence of such conditions should not be considered on their own as a reason not to explore liver transplantation, conversations with non‐liver specialists and rigorous pre‐operative assessments are paramount to prospectively examine potential complications. 54

Endocrinologists and internal medicine specialists

Since MASLD is closely linked to conditions that involve the endocrine system, 55 there is a clear role for endocrinologists in MASLD management. Endocrinologists can either directly diagnose MASLD among their patients with cardiometabolic disorders or advise on appropriate treatments for those under the care of other HCPs. 56 In addition, some endocrinologists are trained in obesity medicine, which highlights an important potential role for multidisciplinary management of MASLD as both obesity and T2D are major risk factors for MASLD, MASH and more advanced liver disease. 55 , 57 In the US, the estimated prevalence of MASLD is >70% in people with T2D, 12%–20% of whom are at risk of MASH. 1 In addition, more advanced MASH is commonly encountered in patients with T2D, 1 and these patients have a higher risk of faster disease progression, cirrhosis and HCC. 58 All these factors combined contribute to a roughly threefold elevated risk of liver‐related mortality among patients with T2D compared with individuals without T2D. 58

Internal medicine specialists care for patients with a wide range of conditions, including those with a specific diagnosis of MASLD, or conditions that place patients at high risk of developing MASLD. In a 2019 nationwide survey of specialists based in the US, 86.6% of internal medicine specialists believed that some of their patients had MASLD, and 13.6% thought that most of their patients had MASLD. 36

There is an overall lack of knowledge about MASLD and MASH among endocrinologists and internal medicine specialists, 32 as well as uncertainties pertaining to the optimal treatment approach and differences relating to screening techniques and referral. 36 Given the strong relationship between MASLD and the chronic diseases that endocrinologists and internal medicine specialists manage regularly, it is imperative that these specialities become familiar with the identification, stratification, monitoring, and management of patients with MASLD and MASH. They, too, should become familiar with the appropriate referral of patients with CMD and liver disease for speciality care.

Obesity specialists

Referral of patients with overweight or obesity who are at high risk of developing MASLD or MASH to obesity specialists is becoming increasingly common. Approximately 75% of people with overweight have MASLD, rising to 90% in people with severe obesity. 59 Pharmacological interventions are the most scalable and accessible treatments for obesity, and there are medications that are recommended in obesity guidelines that are showing therapeutic benefits in clinical trials for MASLD/MASH. 60 , 61 , 62 Lifestyle modifications and interventions that promote weight loss in patients with MASLD have been shown to be associated with improvements in biomarkers for liver disease. 63 Unfortunately, lifestyle interventions alone are often not effective in sustaining these improvements due to multiple factors, including weight regain and inconsistencies with diet and exercise. 64 Indeed, metabolic surgery may be considered in some individuals with obesity or obesity‐related complications and MASLD. 65 The Canadian Association of Bariatric Physicians and Surgeons and Obesity Canada recommend that bariatric surgery be performed in individuals with BMI ≥40 kg/m2, regardless of the presence of comorbidities, and in individuals with metabolic disease and BMI 35–39.9 kg/m2. 66 , 67 There are other guidelines with more liberal cutoffs. 68 Bariatric surgery may be an effective therapeutic option for patients with obesity and MASH, and can lead to regression of steatosis, reduction of fibrosis, and in some cases complete resolution of MASH. 69 However, the majority of patients that qualify for bariatric surgery do not choose surgical options for weight management, 70 and this may be due to fear of surgery complications. 71 Unfortunately, patients with MASLD and very high BMI have a higher risk of developing adverse extrahepatic outcomes, as well as liver decompensation, which itself is an absolute contraindication for bariatric surgery. 72 , 73 Early intervention with bariatric surgery for those who qualify and understand the risk continues to be an option for treating MASLD and MASH.

Cardiologists

Cardiologists are dealing with an increasing proportion of patients with metabolic syndrome and T2D, 74 and both patient groups are at risk of MASLD. As CVD is the leading cause of mortality in individuals with MASLD, 75 cardiovascular risk reduction in this population is vital.

Patients with MASLD that are deemed to be at low risk of hepatic fibrosis based on FIB‐4 score (i.e., <1.30) can be managed by cardiologists when necessary, without referral to a liver specialist. 76 Management strategies should primarily focus on lifestyle modifications, cardiovascular risk reduction, and regular assessment of liver fibrosis using NITs. Further metabolic assessment may warrant referral of patients to speciality care. 76 However, some PCPs and endocrinologists are also highly skilled in cardiometabolic and cardiovascular risk reduction, thus not all patients with CVD will be treated by cardiologists. Still, the role of the cardiologist in managing patients with T2D and obesity, many of whom have concomitant MASLD, and especially those with high cardiometabolic risk, is prominent. Patients with T2D, with and without additional CVD risk, are often referred to cardiology from primary care. In 2017, the number of US cardiology outpatient encounters for patients with T2D exceeded endocrinology encounters by a ratio of 2:1 overall and 4:1 for patients with comorbid CVD. 77 Several medications that are regularly prescribed by cardiologists (e.g., sodium‐glucose transport protein 2 inhibitors and glucagon‐like peptide 1 receptor agonists) have potential therapeutic effects in MASLD including MASH. 25 In addition, patients with prior cardiovascular events should be referred to rehabilitation programmes, as an opportunity for education on diet and exercise and for possible drug implementation, to reduce the impact of MASLD or MASH on further cardiovascular events. 78 , 79 , 80

Despite the large number of consultations, cardiologists are the least familiar with symptoms and diagnostic criteria for MASLD, and feel the least confident in diagnosing and managing this condition compared with other specialists. 37 Worsening of liver fibrosis, denoted as progression from low‐risk (<1.30) to high‐risk (>2.67) FIB‐4 scores, is associated with an increased risk of adverse cardiovascular outcomes in patients with MASH. 81 In addition, high FIB‐4 and high non‐alcoholic fatty liver disease score (NFS) have been shown to correlate directly with coronary artery disease severity and coronary artery calcium (CAC) scores. 82 Thus, in patients with MASLD and MASH, FIB‐4, NFS, and CAC scores could serve as prognostic tools for CVD. An increased understanding of the usefulness of these tools could help to determine the appropriate timing of cardiology referral and specialist support, which may only be warranted once FIB‐4 scores exceed 2.67. However, limited knowledge of these tools, coupled with multiple aspects of a patient's cardiometabolic profile and lack of awareness among cardiologists of the influence of MASLD on CVD risk, 74 , 83 highlights a need for education and application of guideline‐based MASLD care pathways to optimise overall patient care.

CURRENT GUIDELINES FOR THE MANAGEMENT OF MASH

Several international clinical practice guidelines have been developed for the diagnosis and management of MASLD and MASH (Table 1). In the United States, AASLD, American Association of Clinical Endocrinology (AACE), and the American Diabetes Association (ADA) have published guidance pertaining to the management of patients with MASLD and MASH by an interprofessional team, including the use of pharmacotherapy in individuals with obesity and/or T2D. 1 , 2 , 29 In Europe, the European Associations for the Study of the Liver (EASL), Diabetes (EASD) and Obesity (EASO) have published guidance for specialists caring for patients with conditions associated with MASLD/MASH and CMD. 30 Although these MASLD guidelines are broadly consistent with each other, there is discordance amongst some key recommendations, including FIB‐4 thresholds for high‐risk MASLD, the sequence of tests to determine hepatic steatosis, as well as the presence of concise guidance regarding pharmacotherapy, bariatric surgery, and lifestyle interventions. 85 These differences may reflect local availability of treatment options, clinic infrastructures, and reimbursement models, but they may also be partially responsible for the low rate of guideline implementation. 85 A cross‐sectional study of MASLD experts conducted between 2018 and 2019 revealed that out of 29 participating European countries, none had written action plan strategies specifically addressing this condition. 86 MASLD was specified in only two obesity strategies (in Portugal and the United Kingdom) and in one alcohol strategy (in Portugal). Furthermore, only 10 countries had national guidelines specifically addressing MASLD, and three had national registries. Notably, 11 countries recommended screening for MASLD in individuals with diabetes, obesity, and/or metabolic syndrome, and five countries had referral algorithms for follow‐up and specialist referral from primary care. 86

Until recently, in Canada, information pertaining to MASLD management mainly existed in the form of provincial clinical guidelines (Table 2). Diabetes Canada has now published a series of recommendations for people living with diabetes who have, or who are at risk of having, MASLD and MASH. 3 These are the first Canadian national guidelines that are dedicated to addressing MASLD management in the context of diabetes, unifying provincial recommendations and, as a result, encouraging a more consistent approach to patient care. In addition to providing guidance on how to recognise, stage, and address MASLD in individuals with diabetes, Diabetes Canada highlights the importance of multidisciplinary care models and provides further support for their integration into practice. 3 Recommendations are largely reflective of those published by AASLD, including the screening of individuals with diabetes for MASLD‐related fibrosis, the use of NITs in addition to ALT and AST to determine the presence of clinically significant fibrosis, and the referral of patients with MASH or high risk of advanced fibrosis to specialist care. 2 , 3

WHAT CAN MULTIDISCIPLINARY CARE IN MASH ACCOMPLISH?

Establishment of a holistic, patient‐centred model of MASLD and MASH care requires transparent communication and exchange of information between specialists, PCPs, and allied HCPs at different stages of the patient journey (Figure 3). 31 In a modelled study for renal failure, multidisciplinary care allowed for coordinated monitoring of disease, leading to reduced medical expenditure, therapeutic optimisation, and enhanced continuity of care. 87

Timely and accurate diagnosis of patients with MASH is needed for implementing the most appropriate treatment plan, which may or may not involve specialist involvement. As PCPs are often involved in the initial diagnosis of MASLD, 16 , 31 and it is from these physicians that most patients with MASLD are referred to speciality care, 16 it is important that an accurate diagnosis is made at the primary care level to ensure prompt treatment intervention. Furthermore, awareness of MASH among non‐liver specialists is vital to synergise processes of care and result in an optimal treatment strategy. This goes hand in hand with consideration of the comorbid conditions of patients with MASH, as well as their diverse health needs, in order to deliver comprehensive care and disease management. 25

Use of, and access to, accurate and appropriate diagnostic testing to identify patients with MASH who are at risk for progressive liver disease and may require additional testing is an important step towards improving patient outcomes and reducing medical expenditure. This may involve advocating for enhanced public health attention towards MASLD to facilitate the relocation of resources for liver disease care, especially in Canada. 21

There are few studies investigating multidisciplinary care in MASLD, although there have been attempts to capture its effectiveness during pilot trials. 88 In a retrospective survey of clinical practice from a MASLD multidisciplinary clinic comprising hepatology, diabetology, dietetic, and exercise therapy input, patients attending the clinic for 5 years exhibited improvements in liver biochemistry and cardiovascular risk factors. 89 Specifically, there were significant decreases in ALT, weight, and total cholesterol from baseline to the latest clinic visit. Normalisation of ALT (≤40 U/L) occurred in 36.7% of patients, and 26.7% and 16.8% of patients achieved weight reductions of ≥5% and ≥7%, respectively. 89 In another observational cohort study of patients with MASLD, including some with T2D, a multidisciplinary approach to disease management resulted in significantly improved hepatic and cardiometabolic health parameters, including reduced ALT levels, weight, glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c), and liver stiffness. 90 Specifically, ALT reduced by 11 IU/L in the total cohort and by 10 IU/L in patients with T2D. 90 Relative cardiovascular risk assessed by the QRISK3 score 91 was reduced in all patients (p = 0.0001 for the total cohort). Health economic modelling also revealed that multidisciplinary intervention for patients with poorly controlled T2D was cost‐effective. 90 A more recent study demonstrated that multidisciplinary collaboration in the context of MASH can lead to clinically meaningful improvements in liver and metabolic health. 92 Almost half (42%) of patients in the programme had comorbid T2D, 69% of whom achieved HbA1c levels of <7.0%, and all received individualised treatment plans, including education, lifestyle intervention, and drug therapy, which were centred around pre‐specified and agreed‐upon therapeutic targets. 92

PRACTICAL STEPS TO ACHIEVE IDEAL MULTIDISCIPLINARY CARE

Establishing clear objectives and expected outcomes is essential to identify the necessary members required to build an effective multiple specialty team. 93 Regular outcome assessments and evaluation of goals are required to ensure success and provide opportunities for team members to learn and adapt processes. 93 These assessments should include careful consideration of individual patient motivators as they are the key to driving meaningful improvements in healthcare. 94

The establishment of a successful multiple specialty team requires re‐organisation of tasks among HCPs, aiding the integration of care and implementation of various perspectives. 95 Specialists selected to take part in these teams should have a diverse skillset relevant to the condition—the inclusion of PCPs, hepatologists and gastroenterologists is necessary, but the involvement of other specialists (e.g., obesity specialists, endocrinologists, cardiologists) may also be appropriate. Furthermore, allied healthcare workers who are not necessarily involved in the care of patients with MASLD but have a particular interest in metabolic conditions can provide valuable input into the management of patients. Establishing specific roles and responsibilities for each member, as well as effective systems for coordinating care, is important to ensure streamlined processes. 96 Within Canada, formally established multiple specialty teams for the management of patients with MASLD/MASH are not common within primary care, and collaborative teams are brought together using community allied HCPs and other resources that are available in each region. This highlights that tailored guidance for the establishment of multiple speciality teams that is relevant and adaptable to different clinic infrastructures is vital for effective, all‐encompassing patient care, although it is important to consider different funding models that may hinder the integration of these teams into practice.

Establishing a means for multidisciplinary education can address current health system challenges and create space for collaboration. 26 Multi‐stakeholder projects such as educational meetings, 25 handbooks, and awareness campaigns for at‐risk communities can be of benefit. 34 Educational curricula are effective among internal medicine specialists, leading to significantly increased comfort with hepatology and liver‐related topics, as well as the ability to answer more hepatology‐related questions correctly. 97 This is important as the shortage of liver specialists within Canada means that non‐liver specialists will need to develop greater knowledge of liver disease. Moreover, in Canada, there is a particular need for improved education about non‐invasive screening and confirmatory tests among patients, treating physicians, and health system stakeholders. Integration of FIB‐4 and intelligent liver function tests (iLFTs) into laboratory forms could help improve the early detection of MASLD within primary care. In particular, iLFTs are being increasingly recognised as a cost‐effective tool that can be used alongside traditional LFTs to increase liver disease diagnosis rates. 98 Furthermore, improved patient–provider partnerships and discussions are likely to lead to better treatment outcomes as greater understanding may lead to improved adherence to management strategies. 94 However, there continues to be insufficient information about MASH, 99 and patients often report negative experiences with HCPs, citing lack of empathy by the HCP, lack of condition‐related awareness by the HCP, and dissatisfaction with the healthcare visit in general. 100 Despite these experiences, few patients look for online peer opinions following healthcare visits. 100 In Canada, the Fatty Liver Alliance provides invaluable resources for patients to educate and advocate for themselves. 101 Encouraging patients to explore such resources and share their perceptions, concerns, and treatment journeys can help identify areas for improved education as well as inform future directions for disease management. 99

Ensuring access to screening and diagnostic tests, a lack of which is notable in Canada, 21 is paramount for reducing screening inequalities and expanding the pool of identifiable at‐risk populations. Unfortunately, people from lower socioeconomic groups, who are most in need of healthcare interventions given the association of low socioeconomic status with increased risk of diabetes, 102 obesity, 103 and MASLD, 104 are less likely to have access to screening services. 105 These patients experience a notably higher burden of MASLD that is often driven by a lack of early disease intervention and monitoring, as well as other socioeconomic factors such as low income, low education, and geographic disparities that impact MASLD directly and indirectly through its related diseases. 106 Conversations with screening providers to expand access to diagnostic tests may involve highlighting the need for more vigorous screening of at‐risk individuals, focusing on the potential economic implications of underdiagnosis. Other avenues could involve evaluation of how screening and coordinated management strategies fit into different payment programmes, and these are likely to differ across different regions, provinces, and insurance groups.

OTHER STEPS TO OPTIMISE PATIENT CARE FOR MASLD

Creating time for conversations with patients about the importance of long‐term lifestyle changes, even in the context of asymptomatic disease, is a key step towards optimal disease management. This would involve changing reimbursement models, as time spent in a busy clinic under traditional fee‐for‐service models is not remunerated in the same way as seeing another patient at a separate clinic visit.

Additionally, non‐invasive interventions such as lifestyle modifications and weight management, that benefit both cardiovascular and hepatic outcomes, should be offered at the time of MASLD diagnosis. 29 However, despite the fact that weight loss is the preferred treatment option for patients with MASLD, 72% of physicians globally, including PCPs, internal medicine specialists, cardiologists, and liver specialists, experience difficulty in providing lifestyle recommendations for patients with MASLD. 37 In an international survey of patterns of practice in treating MASLD conducted in 2020, 56% of surveyed hepatologists and gastroenterologists reported that less than half of patients had attempted lifestyle modification prior to being referred to them for specialist care. 45 This may reflect socioeconomic realities that prevent certain groups of patients with MASLD, MASH, and related comorbidities from undertaking and adhering to lifestyle recommendations. 102 , 104 In a recent study that mapped social media patient‐centric data on experiences with MASLD across eight countries, 64% of patients reported difficulty in adhering to lifestyle changes and 18% struggled to manage their condition using lifestyle modifications. 100 Indeed, the lack of proper education on nutrition was identified as a key unmet need. 100

MASH pharmacotherapy within a multiple speciality team

In the past, pharmacotherapy for treating MASH was limited, but today many drug classes are being studied, and some may soon become standard of care (Table 3). The expansion of pharmacotherapy for MASH will require appropriate non‐invasive monitoring, which will further underscore the need to engage a multiple‐speciality team.

Importantly, holistic disease management requires a full understanding of the pharmacology of drugs, as they can impact both cardiovascular and liver disease, including their possible mixed benefits and synergistic effects in patients. Patients with MASLD can exhibit altered drug metabolism which may lead to altered pharmacologic efficacy or adverse drug reactions 117 ; therefore, prescription of drugs to treat comorbidities requires careful consideration of potential hepatotoxicity.

Education about the appropriateness of pharmacological management strategies for certain patients with MASLD is required. 32 Therapies that target comorbidities such as obesity and T2D could impact MASLD morbidity and mortality, 118 and also improve cardiovascular health. 25 In addition, as CVD is the leading cause of mortality in MASLD patients, there is a need to prioritise therapies that provide additional cardiovascular benefits. 25 A recent study found that the use of statins in patients with MASLD significantly reduces the incidence and risk of liver‐related events, as well as HCC, decompensated liver cirrhosis, and mortality (all‐cause, CVD‐related, and liver‐related). 119 PCPs and hepatologists need to recognise the interplay between CVD and MASLD, and look for therapeutic strategies that can reduce the burden of both diseases. 120

CONCLUSIONS

There is a need for improved educationand collaboration between specialists and PCPs involved in the management ofMASLD. Lack of understanding of how multiple aspects of care are linkedtogether can lead to suboptimal disease management and worsened health outcomes.Improved collaboration within multiple speciality teams can overcome thelimitations of traditional siloed care pathways. Furthermore, multidisciplinarypartnerships can lead to more optimal resource use and reallocation of tasks toreduce the burden for treating PCPs. In the Canadian healthcare system, multidisciplinary care championing will be required to address the barriers forthe effective management of MASLD and MASH, including lack of awareness andknowledge and scarcity of screening and diagnostic resources.
