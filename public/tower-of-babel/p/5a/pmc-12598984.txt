Risk of hepatitis B virus reactivation associated with interleukin inhibitor therapies: protocol for a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Marouf Mouhammad Alhalabi, Hussam Aldeen Alshiekh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe widespread application of interleukin (IL) inhibitors for various conditions, including gastrointestinal, rheumatologic, dermatologic and pulmonary diseases, has raised concerns regarding the potential for hepatitis B virus reactivation (HBVr). However, the precise risk of HBVr remains unclear due to inconsistencies in existing research. This systematic review aims to quantify the risk of HBVr in patients receiving IL inhibitor therapies.Methods and analysisThis systematic review will follow Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines. A comprehensive search will be conducted in MEDLINE, PubMed, Google Scholar, CENTRAL, Scopus, Embase, Web of Science and ClinicalTrials.gov up to October 2025. Two reviewers will independently screen studies and extract data, resolving discrepancies by consensus. Eligible studies will include HBV-infected patients receiving IL inhibitors. HBVr rates will be estimated using a generalised linear mixed model with a binomial distribution, applying random-effects models to account for interstudy variability. Heterogeneity will be assessed using I², Cochran’s Q and τ². Leave-one-out sensitivity analyses will evaluate robustness. Subgroup analyses will consider HBV serostatus, IL inhibitor type and study characteristics. Studies including patients on antiviral prophylaxis will be excluded from the primary analysis. A secondary analysis will assess prophylaxis efficacy in preventing HBVr according to hepatitis B surface antigen status. Publication bias will be evaluated using Doi plots and the Luis Furuya-Kanamori index.Ethics and disseminationThis study does not require ethics approval as it involves no patient-specific data. Findings will be disseminated at gastroenterology conferences and through peer-reviewed publications.PROSPERO registration numberThe protocol is registered in PROSPERO (CRD42024614179).

Introduction

Interleukin (IL) inhibitors have revolutionised the management of chronic inflammatory diseases, offering substantial benefits across diverse conditions, including inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), rheumatoid arthritis, psoriasis, psoriatic arthritis and ankylosing spondylitis.17 Despite their efficacy, concerns about hepatitis B virus reactivation (HBVr) have emerged, particularly in patients with underlying HBV infection.812 Evidence on HBVr risk specific to IL inhibitors is both limited and fragmented, with much of the data extrapolated from studies on broader categories of cytokine inhibitors.9 13 This lack of specificity highlights the need for focused research into IL-based therapies. Previous meta-analyses evaluating HBVr risk often categorised IL inhibitors under the general umbrella of cytokine inhibitors, leading to the exclusion of relevant studies due to overly broad search terms.9 Emerging evidence from meta-analyses of ustekinumab, an IL-12/IL-23 inhibitor used in IBD and psoriasis, suggests a relatively low HBVr risk—estimated at 10% in patients with chronic HBV infection and 3% in those with occult HBV infection.14 However, these findings vary across studies, with other meta-analyses reporting different risks for ustekinumab and other IL inhibitors.91115 These discrepancies underscore the need for further validation, particularly as current evidence is based on limited sample sizes and retrospective designs. The role of ILs in immune regulation suggests that IL inhibition may contribute to HBVr risk, influenced by factors such as the phase of HBV infection, immune status and the specific IL inhibitor used. While antiviral prophylaxis has been shown to reduce reactivation risk, knowledge gaps remain regarding drug-specific risks and the implications for patients with varying HBV serostatuses.8 15 Chronic hepatitis B infection affects approximately 316 million individuals worldwide, predominantly in the Western Pacific and African regions.1618 Given the severe complications of HBVr in immunocompromised patients, a thorough understanding of these risks is critical. This meta-analysis aims to provide precise estimates of HBVr risk in patients receiving IL inhibitors, stratified by HBV serostatus, while assessing the impact of key factors such as antiviral prophylaxis and concomitant immunosuppressants. By reconciling discrepancies in reported HBVr rates and elucidating associated risk factors, this study seeks to optimise therapeutic strategies and minimise reactivation risks in this vulnerable population. A critical clinical gap this study addresses is the absence of drug-specific risk estimates for HBVr associated with IL inhibitors. Current evidence often generalises IL inhibitors under broader categories of immunosuppressive or cytokine-targeted therapies, making it difficult to discern the specific risks of individual agents. This limitation leaves clinicians relying on incomplete or extrapolated data when assessing HBVr risk in patients with chronic or occult HBV infection. By providing a comprehensive analysis of HBVr risk stratified by specific IL inhibitors and patient HBV serostatus, this study aims to generate precise and actionable insights. These findings could refine existing HBV management guidelines by emphasising individualised risk assessments and potentially introducing IL inhibitor-specific recommendations for HBV screening, risk stratification and antiviral prophylaxis. Such tailored guidance could improve therapeutic decision-making and optimise patient outcomes.

Methods and analysis

Overview and objectives

This systematic review will follow the requirements established by the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses.1921 Our protocol has already been registered with PROSPERO (CRD42024614179).

Criteria for eligibility

Participants

Patients diagnosed with IBD, rheumatologic disorders, dermatologic conditions, or other disorders, according to established diagnostic criteria, who have received anti-IL therapy for a minimum of 12 weeks will be included. Studies will be eligible regardless of participants’ age, gender, ethnicity or nationality. HBV status will be assessed by including studies that evaluate participants with either a positive hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) test or a positive hepatitis B core antibody (anti-HBc) test. Studies involving paediatric populations, case reports, patients receiving more than one concurrent biological agent or review articles will be excluded. Additionally, studies with a sample size of fewer than three patients, or an anti-IL treatment duration of less than 12 weeks will not be accepted.9 10 22 The purpose of these exclusion criteria is to improve the robustness and clinical relevance of the findings. Studies with extremely small sample sizes lack statistical power and are prone to bias, reducing the reliability and generalisability of their findings. Furthermore, HBVr typically requires sustained immunosuppression, and shorter durations of IL therapy may not provide sufficient exposure to adequately assess the risk.23 By focusing on studies with a minimum sample size and therapy duration, the analysis ensures that the included data reflect real-world clinical scenarios, offering a more meaningful and reliable estimation of HBVr risks associated with IL inhibitors.

Outcome measures

Primary outcomes

This systematic review and meta-analysis aims to evaluate the risk of HBVr in patients undergoing anti-IL therapy. The findings will contribute to clinical practice guidelines and enhance the management of HBV patients requiring anti-IL treatments. treatments.

Secondary outcomes

This research will assess the efficacy of hepatitis B antiviral prophylaxis and compare HBVr rates across different IL inhibitors. Secondary outcomes include evaluating the incidence of adverse events related to HBVr episodes and identifying potential side effects associated with the treatment regimens.

Search strategy

We will conduct a systematic search across multiple electronic databases, including the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, MEDLINE, PubMed, Google Scholar, Scopus, Web of Science, Embase via the Ovid, and ClinicalTrials.gov.24 25 The PubMed search strategy will employ a combination of Medical Subject Headings terms and relevant keywords, applied with Boolean operators to ensure comprehensive coverage. For HBVr, search terms will include hepatitis B, hepatitis B virus, hepatitis B surface antigen, HBsAg, anti-HBc, anti-HBs, virus activation, reactivation, recurrence, recurrent infection, prophylaxis and prophylactic. Additional terms addressing HBV-associated complications, such as ascites, hepatic encephalopathy, liver failure and hypoalbuminemia, will also be incorporated. For IL therapies, search terms will encompass anti-IL therapy, IL inhibitors, cytokine inhibitors and immune modulators, alongside specific IL targets, including IL-1, IL-2, IL-6, IL-12, IL-17, IL-23 and IL-31. The search will also include drug-specific terms such as anakinra, tocilizumab, sarilumab, ustekinumab, secukinumab, ixekizumab, brodalumab, guselkumab, tildrakizumab and risankizumab. To ensure thoroughness, backward and forward citation tracking will be performed. Backward citation tracking is looking through the reference lists of included research and pertinent review papers to find additional sources. Forward citation monitoring, using Google Scholar, will find current publications that cite the included research and include the most recent available evidence. If full-text access to critical publications is unavailable, we will contact the authors directly to seek more information. All search strategies, including keywords and database filters, will be meticulously documented. To promote transparency and reproducibility, the full search methodology is included in an online supplemental file 1 titled “Search Strategy.”

HBVr definitions

HBVr reflects a loss of immune control with renewed viral replication and is variably defined across international guidelines, particularly regarding HBV DNA thresholds and the role of HBsAg seroreversion. For the purpose of this review, we distinguish between patients with chronic HBV infection (HBsAg positive, anti-HBc positive) and those with resolved HBV infection (HBsAg negative, anti-HBc positive, with or without anti-HBs). Study selection

We included observational studies (in any language) investigating patients with gastroenterological, dermatological, rheumatological or other conditions treated with anti-IL therapy for at least 12 weeks.22 23 Studies involving paediatric populations, case reports, patients receiving more than one concurrent biological agent or review articles were excluded. Additionally, studies with a sample size of fewer than three patients,911 or an anti-IL treatment duration shorter than 12 weeks were not considered.

Our primary analysis will estimate the inherent risk of HBVr attributable to IL inhibitors by including only patients who did not receive antiviral prophylaxis; studies restricted to prophylaxis-treated populations will be excluded, and for mixed cohorts we will request disaggregated data on non-prophylaxis subgroups, excluding the study entirely if such data are unavailable. A secondary analysis will evaluate the efficacy of antiviral prophylaxis by pooling studies that directly compare reactivation rates between prophylaxis and non-prophylaxis groups, with outcomes expressed as risk ratios (RRs) or risk differences and 95% CIs. For both analyses, we will extract detailed information on prophylaxis regimens, including the antiviral agent used, timing of initiation relative to IL therapy, duration of prophylaxis and follow-up, and criteria for initiation. Where possible, subgroup analyses will explore whether efficacy varies according to drug type, initiation strategy or consistency of use. For studies with partially missing data (eg, number of events), we will first attempt to obtain complete datasets from the authors; if unavailable, imputation methods will be considered. For dichotomous outcomes, when group sizes are known but event counts are missing, best-case and worst-case scenario imputations will be applied; for continuous outcomes, when means are not reported but medians with ranges or IQRs are available, means and SD will be estimated using the method of Wan et al.26 To assess the robustness of imputations, sensitivity analyses will compare pooled results including imputed data with analyses excluding such studies; consistent findings will suggest that imputations did not materially affect results, while notable discrepancies will be explicitly acknowledged as a limitation.

To enhance the robustness of the analysis and minimise the impact of missing or incomplete data, we employed the following strategies.

Exclusion of studies with substantial missing data

Studies with critical missing information necessary for calculating effect estimates (eg, the number of HBVr events or total sample size) were excluded if the missing data could not be retrieved from the authors or online supplemental file 1.

Retrieval efforts for partially missing data

For studies with partially missing data, we contacted the associated authors to clarify. If no answer was received within a set timeframe (4 weeks), we evaluated the feasibility of imputing missing data using observed trends. Sensitivity studies were performed to determine the possible impact of such imputations.

Subgroup analysis of incomplete study characteristics

In situations where crucial factors, such as IL therapy duration or dose, were missing from trials, subgroup analyses were conducted to establish their impact on the overall results.

Documentation and transparency

All instances of missing data were documented, and their potential implications were addressed in the discussion section to ensure transparency and rigour in reporting. These strategies aimed to ensure that the findings were both robust and reflective of real-world clinical scenarios while addressing potential biases introduced by missing data.

Data extraction

Two independent researchers will use a standard data extraction form to carefully obtain information from the identified research. This form will collect research data (author, year, design, sample size, data collection period, continent), patient demographics (age, sex, HBV status, pretreatment antiviral use), anti-IL therapy details (specific agent, dosage, duration) and outcomes of interest (rate and severity of HBVr events, liver-related morbidity and mortality). The type of antiviral prophylaxis used, its duration, and its relationship to the timing of initiation and discontinuation of anti-IL therapy. A predefined, hierarchical process will be used to resolve disagreements between the two independent reviewers during study selection and data extraction. First, the reviewers will discuss the discrepancy to reach a consensus. If consensus cannot be achieved, the disagreement will be escalated to a third senior reviewer (MH), whose decision will be final. Any differences discovered during extraction will be handled through conversation or the participation of a third reviewer.

Risk of bias

The selected observational studies’ risk of bias will be analysed using the ROBINS-I, the Risk Of Bias In Non-randomized Studies - of Interventions (ROBINS-I) toolmethod, which is particularly intended to evaluate non-randomised research.27 This tool addresses potential biases across key domains, including confounding, participant selection, group comparability and outcome measurement. To ensure consistency and reduce subjectivity, two independent reviewers will perform the assessments, with disagreements resolved through discussion and, if necessary, arbitration by a third reviewer. Inter-rater reliability will be quantified using Cohen’s kappa coefficient, interpreted according to established thresholds: values ≥0.80 will indicate excellent agreement, 0.60–0.79 substantial agreement (requiring review of assessment criteria) and <0.60 moderate or poor agreement, in which case all reviewers will meet to recalibrate their application of the ROBINS-I tool before repeating the assessments. If a sufficient number of studies are available (generally >10), we will perform univariate meta-regression to examine whether study-level covariates account for variability in effect estimates. Potential moderators to be explored include the mean or median age of participants, duration of anti-IL therapy, proportion of male patients and baseline liver function parameters where reported.

Detection of heterogeneity

Heterogeneity will be assessed using Cochran’s Q statistic (p<0.10 indicating significance) and quantified by the I² statistic, which categorises heterogeneity as low (0–25%), moderate (25–75%) or high (>75%).28 If significant heterogeneity is discovered (I²>75%), we will investigate likely origins using multiple methodologies. Predefined subgroup analyses will be performed based on trial design (prospective vs retrospective), geographic location, type of IL inhibitor, primary condition (IBD vs rheumatologic disorders) and HBV serostatus (HBsAg+ vs HBsAg−/Anti-HBc+). Where sufficient studies are available (generally >10), univariate meta-regression will be used to assess the impact of continuous variables such as mean age and treatment duration on effect estimates. Sensitivity analyses will be performed by sequentially excluding studies judged at high risk of bias to evaluate their influence on pooled results and heterogeneity. Finally, the clinical and methodological features of outlying studies will be examined qualitatively, and their findings interpreted with caution, prioritising narrative synthesis when quantitative pooling is not appropriate.

Assessment of publication bias

A comprehensive investigation of publications associated with significantly heterogeneous outcomes will be conducted, seeking potential differences in methodological approaches or preoperative factors that might explain the observed variations in effect sizes. Potential publication bias will be assessed using Doi plots and the Luis Furuya-Kanamori (LFK) index, which provide a more robust evaluation of asymmetry than traditional funnel plots, particularly in meta-analyses of proportions or studies with small sample sizes. An LFK index between −1 and +1 will be interpreted as no asymmetry, −2 to −1 or +1 to +2 as minor asymmetry, and values beyond ±2 as major asymmetry, indicating potential publication bias.29 30 Forest plots were used for data visualisation.

Statistical analyses

The major goal of this meta-analysis is to assess the risk of HBVr in patients on anti-IL treatment. To estimate this risk, we will use a generalised linear mixed model (GLMM) that takes into account both fixed and random factors to account for heterogeneity within and across studies. Fixed effects will include variables such as antiviral prophylaxis, type of IL inhibitor and HBV serostatus, while random effects will capture variability at the study level, reflecting differences in baseline risks. The GLMM will use a binomial distribution with a logit link function to model the probability of HBVr incidence. The results will be expressed as HBVr with 95% CIs, providing a detailed evaluation of reactivation risks under varying clinical conditions.

Secondary outcomes and analyses

Secondary analyses will focus on the efficacy of antiviral prophylaxis in reducing HBVr risk and comparing reactivation rates across different IL inhibitors. ORs will be calculated by comparing the proportions of patients with HBVr in prophylaxis and non-prophylaxis groups. Time-to-event data will be analysed using HRs to evaluate the effect of prophylaxis on delaying HBVr. Additionally, binary outcomes, such as complications associated with HBVr episodes, will be assessed using RRs. Subgroup analyses will explore the variability in reactivation risks among specific IL inhibitors. To account for heterogeneity, a random-effects model will be used, assuming that real effect sizes differ between studies. The I² statistic will be used to quantify heterogeneity, with values above 50% indicating significant heterogeneity. Doi plots and the LFK index will assess publication bias.

Sensitivity analyses

By running leave-one-out analysis and successively eliminating each research to examine its impact on the total estimations, sensitivity studies will evaluate the robustness of the findings. The effects of study-level features and other statistical models will be investigated in further analyses. A proportionate meta-analysis will be carried out, classifying studies according to pertinent traits and results. The GLMM will be used to compute pooled proportions under both fixed-effects and random-effects models due to the low incidence of HBVr.

Analytical approach

Statistical analyses will be conducted using R, leveraging packages such as meta, metafor and lme4 for meta-analysis and GLMM implementation. These advanced statistical techniques address challenges such as sparse data and unbalanced datasets, enhancing the reliability of results. By systematically analysing observational data, this study aims to derive precise HBVr risk estimates for specific IL inhibitors, filling critical gaps in the literature.

Mitigating limitations

The reliance on observational studies introduces potential limitations, including selection bias, reporting bias and unmeasured confounders. To address these, we will rigorously assess bias using the ROBINS-I tool and perform sensitivity analyses to test the robustness of results. This rigorous methodology ensures clinically actionable insights, supporting updates to HBV management guidelines for patients receiving IL inhibitors.

Patient and public involvement

In this study, there was no engagement of patients or the public in the design phase, nor will they participate in the interpretation of the findings.

Discussion

Treatment recommendations for biologic therapy in hepatitis B patients show some alignment across major liver disease societies, including the European Association for the Study of the Liver, the American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases and the Asian Pacific Association for the Study of the Liver. However, these guidelines primarily address immunosuppressants, while supporting evidence leans heavily on research with oncology drugs. Additionally, a standardised definition for hepatitis B reactivation following immunosuppressive therapy remains lacking.3133 Clinical practice guidelines for hepatitis B reactivation associated with immunosuppressive therapy exhibit convergence on a risk stratification system (low, intermediate and high) based on the overall reactivation risk. However, significant discord remains regarding the classification of anti-IL therapy. Specifically, the guidelines lack consensus on whether anti-IL therapy poses a high, intermediate or low risk for reactivation in patients who are HBsAg negative but anti-HBc positive.8 12 13 34 Papatheodoridis et al categorised the risk of HBVr associated with anti-IL therapy as unknown for HBsAg-negative patients and low for anti-HBc-positive patients. However, their valuable meta-analysis did not evaluate the risk for individual anti-IL agents.9 El Jamaly et al10 conducted a meta-analysis that estimated the risk of HBVr associated with different anti-IL agents. This work addressed the crucial question of heterogeneity in HBVr among anti-IL therapies. However, a potential limitation of their study is the inclusion of patients receiving antiviral prophylaxis. Since prophylaxis can lower the true incidence of HBVr, its presence might introduce confounding bias in the risk estimates for individual anti-IL agents.10 To address this knowledge gap, we propose a meta-analysis that estimates the risk of HBVr specifically associated with each anti-IL therapy. By rigorously excluding patients receiving antiviral prophylaxis, this analysis will provide a more precise assessment of the inherent HBVr risk posed by individual anti-IL agents. This information can directly translate into improved clinical decision-making, ultimately enhancing patient care.

Ethics and dissemination

This study does not necessitate research ethics approval due to the absence of patient-specific data collection. The findings will be shared with local, national and international gastroenterology and endoscopy societies and will also be submitted to both national and international gastroenterology conferences, as well as peer-reviewed journals.
