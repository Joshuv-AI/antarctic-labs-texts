Magnetic Resonance Imaging Guided Stereotactic Body Radiation Therapy for Definitive Treatment of Prostate Cancer After Prior Chemoradiation and Laparoscopic Low Anterior Resection
Authors: Nikitha Kalahasti, Daniel Pham, Yu Gao, Nicholas Ngo, Hilary P. Bagshaw
Published: 2026

Introduction

Prostate cancer is the second most common diagnosed cancer in men, with 313,780 new cases and 35,770 deaths estimated in 2025.1 Treatment options include surgery, radiation therapy, and hormone therapy, alone or in combination, depending on the risk group. However, treatment selection is challenging for patients with prior pelvic radiation and/or surgery.

Patients treated for rectal cancer often have prior radiation and surgery, with non-native rectal tissue serving as a stool reservoir. Colorectal anastomosis, including low anterior resection (LAR) or ileal pouch–anal anastomosis, is used for several colorectal conditions.2 Patients presenting with prostate cancer after these procedures are uncommon, and standardized treatment is not established.

We present a case of a patient treated with MRI-guided stereotactic body radiation therapy (SBRT) for prostate cancer after prior pelvic surgery and radiation for rectal cancer.

Case

A 68-year-old male presented with newly diagnosed cT1cN0M0, Gleason 4+4=8 (ISUP grade group 4) adenocarcinoma of the prostate. Clinically significant is a medical history of a cT3N1M0 rectal adenocarcinoma treated 7 years earlier with neoadjuvant chemoradiation with concurrent Xeloda and a laparoscopic LAR with a stapled side-to-end descending colon-to-rectal anastomosis and loop ileostomy (ypT3 ypN0). At prostate cancer diagnosis, he was without evidence of rectal cancer with regular bowel function consisting of one bowel movement daily following a warm tap water enema. He was diagnosed with prostate cancer in 2023 after a Prostate-Specific Antigen (PSA) rise to 14.43. Prostate magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed 2 PI-RADS 5 lesions, and a Prostate-Specific Membrane Antigen (PSMA) Positron Emission Tomography (PET/CT) scan indicated no distant disease. A transrectal MR ultrasound fusion biopsy revealed Gleason grade group 4 disease in 1 of 1 core (no systematic cores taken). Notably, the patient only tolerated a single MRI-guided core biopsy due to extreme pain during the procedure.

After staging workup, he was confirmed to have high-risk prostate cancer. The patient was presented with 2 treatments options: radical prostatectomy or radiation therapy (RT) with androgen deprivation therapy (ADT).

Radical prostatectomy was determined to have a high risk of morbidity due to prior pelvic surgery and possible violation of the pelvic tissue planes. The high likelihood of fibrous adhesions secondary to the LAR would increase the difficulty of a second pelvic surgery,3, 4, 5, 6 with risk of damage to the colorectal anastomosis during prostatectomy.

Three radiation modalities were considered: brachytherapy, external beam RT (EBRT) with conventional fractionation, or SBRT with extreme hypofractionation. Brachytherapy was not recommended because the effect of transrectal ultrasound on the integrity of the anastomosis was uncertain, and the patient had previously not tolerated this procedure during biopsy. The patient’s primary goal was to maintain baseline rectal and bowel function, which would be challenging if the anastomosis were damaged.

Conventional EBRT on a linear accelerator (LINAC) was considered. However, even with techniques such as volumetric arc therapy, conventional x-ray–based imaging guidance, such as cone-beam CT, provides limited soft-tissue contrast, limiting precise assessment of prostate and rectal positioning during daily treatment and potentially allowing significant rectal dose. Furthermore, intrafraction motion monitoring is limited without fiducial markers. Due to the patient’s rectal reconstruction, the dose previously received by the reconstructed segment is uncertain. It is possible that the descending colon was spared much of the high dose; however, there could be significant overlap with his prior pelvic radiation field. A second volumetric arc therapy course could therefore exceed colonic tolerance. Potential toxicities of pelvic re-irradiation were reviewed with the patient, including fistula, bowel obstruction, abscess, proctitis, pouchitis, cystitis, genitourinary bleeding, and urinary strictures.7, 8, 9, 10

MRI-guided SBRT with extreme hypofractionation, adaptive capability, and soft-tissue-based intrafraction motion management on the ViewRay MRIdian was considered.11 The 0.35T on-board MR scanner enables daily MRI-based setup with excellent soft-tissue contrast for prostate and rectum visualization. Daily online adaptation accounts for interfraction anatomic variation and minimizes dose to the rectal reconstruction. Moreover, soft-tissue based intrafraction motion management can monitor target movement in real time and ensure precise beam delivery. Collectively, this technique offers advantages over LINAC-based EBRT, for which adaptation or soft-tissue-based setup and motion management was not possible at our institution. In addition, since the LINACs in our department lack intrafraction motion monitoring without fiducials, uncertainty would remain regarding movement of the anastomosis or target during delivery. Therefore, definitive prostate SBRT with long-term ADT was recommended (neoadjuvant, concurrent, and adjuvant for a total of 2 years for high-risk disease). Nodal radiation was not recommended to limit overlap with prior pelvic fields.

The patient opted for MRI-guided SBRT to the prostate with ADT as recommended. He started on oral ADT with Orgovyx (relugolix) 2 months before RT. At simulation, a diagnostic MRI of the prostate and simulation high-resolution 0.35T MRI (1.5 mm isotropic) on the ViewRay machine were obtained for treatment planning. The prostate, “descending colon” (rectal reconstruction), rectum (native), bowel, bladder, and femoral heads were contoured (Fig. 2). The planning target volume (PTV) was an expansion of the prostate by 5 mm in all directions, except 3 mm posteriorly (Fig. 2). The prescription dose was 3625 cGy in 5 fractions (725 cGy per fraction). PTV coverage was reduced to 83% at 3625 cGy as we prioritized organs at risk (OAR) constraints given the patient’s history (Fig. 3). Our typical prostate SBRT dose constraints12,13 were adapted as this was a second course of pelvic radiation, and biologically effective dose calculations accounted for prior dose and tissue repair due to the time interval between courses. Using an α/β of 3 for normal tissue, the goal was to keep the additional rectal dose to approximately 40 Gy3, given that rectal re-irradiation literature14 suggests second-course doses of up to 63 Gy3 are safe. Before each treatment, the patient performed an enema per his routine practice, and bladder filling was monitored for consistency. Treatments were delivered twice weekly with at least 1 day between fractions. The patient was positioned supine, and a high-resolution 3D MRI was obtained for position verification. Throughout all 5 fractions, the prostate and OARs (bladder, descending colon, and rectum) were well-aligned, and adaptation was deemed unnecessary (Fig. 4). During delivery, 2D CINE MRI at 4 frames per second monitored the prostate position. The patient completed treatment without significant toxicity. He reported grade 1 urinary symptoms, including dysuria and incomplete emptying, managed with Flomax and ibuprofen, and grade 1 loose stool managed by Imodium and a low-fiber diet. Symptoms largely resolved by 3-month post follow-up, although he remained on daily Flomax.

After treatment, PSA was monitored every 3 months while he continued Relugolix for a planned duration of 2 years. At his last follow-up, 1 year and 6 months after the radiation treatment completion, urinary and rectal acute side effects had resolved. His PSA was appropriately low on hormonal therapy.

Discussion

Treatment selection for patients with prior colorectal anastomosis and radiation is challenging due to the limitations of another pelvic surgery and the potential toxicity of re-irradiation.

Radical prostatectomy is not preferred as prior surgery can cause fibrous tissue that increases technical complexity and risk of urinary and rectal complications.3, 4, 5, 6,15 However, in a single-institution analysis, pouch function was subjectively unchanged postoperatively, although pelvic adhesions were commonly identified intraoperatively in radiation-native patients with prior proctocolectomy and ileal pouch–anal anastomosis undergoing radical prostatectomy.5

Radiation after colorectal anastomosis carries risk, as the colon and small bowel replacing the rectum cannot tolerate native rectal-level doses.16 Small institutional reports describe variable radiation approaches in the setting of a non-native rectum and/or prior pelvic radiation and surgery. Pelvic re-irradiation toxicity is well characterized; in a retrospective study of 31 rectal cancer patients, acute toxicity rates were 32.3% grade 2 (nausea/vomiting, skin toxicity, diarrhea) and 3.2% grade 3 (diarrhea requiring hospitalization during chemoradiation).17 Brachytherapy in patients with a J-pouch anastomosis showed no long-term genitourinary toxicity, though 2 of 7 developed late grade 2 gastrointestinal toxicity, including one case of chronic proctitis.18

MRI-guided SBRT allowed dose limitation to the rectal reconstruction and previously irradiated colon via improved MRI imaging and intrafraction motion management. This approach avoided risks of additional pelvic surgery and preserved baseline bowel function. MRI-guided SBRT with adaptive capability and intrafraction motion management may be considered for prostate cancer treatment in patients with prior radiation and/or pelvic surgery with MRI-guided SBRT treatment capabilities.

A limitation of this case report is the lack of long-term follow-up, as toxicity and outcome data are only available up to 18 months after treatment completion, and late toxicity outcomes remain unknown.

Despite this limitation, treatment was delivered safely with minimal acute toxicity. Disease control will be assessed over the subsequent years as the patient is further out from completion of long-term hormonal therapy.
