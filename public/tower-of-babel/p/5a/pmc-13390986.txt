Effects of Complex and Contrast Training on Lower Limb Explosive Power and Eccentric Strength in Elite Male Track and Field Athletes: A 24-Week Randomized Trial
Authors: Zhiyi Xu, Yu Lin, Fucheng Zhang, Qiang Li, Liyuan Yu, Mingjie Lin, Feng Ning, Xiangdong Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Supplemental Digital Content is Available in the Text.

Introduction

Lower-limb explosive power and eccentric strength are cornerstone biomechanical attributes for track and field athletes, directly influencing critical technical movements such as sprint acceleration, vertical jump performance, and rapid deceleration with directional changes (18,22). In recent years, complex training, typically associated with postactivation potentiation (PAP), and contrast training, typically associated with postactivation performance enhancement (PAPE), have gained prominence for enhancing neuromuscular adaptations in the lower-limb kinetic chain due to their distinct but related mechanisms. In this study, PAP and PAPE are used as theory-informed conceptual frameworks rather than strict operational labels: we use PAP to frame complex training formats that emphasize short-term potentiation after heavy or explosive loading, and PAPE to frame contrast training formats that emphasize more sustained performance enhancement after longer recovery intervals. However, prior studies have largely concentrated on short-term interventions or the isolated effects of individual training modalities (10,33), leaving the long-term impacts and mechanistic differences between complex and contrast training underexplored.

Consistent with recent conceptual work (3,15,24), twitch-based PAP is generally described as a brief, involuntary potentiation of muscle twitch force following a high-intensity conditioning activity, largely attributed to myosin regulatory light chain phosphorylation and increased calcium sensitivity. In applied settings, this transient potentiation is assumed to be most prominent within a narrow time window (approximately 1–3 minutes) after the conditioning activity and has been used to justify complex training structures that pair high-load and plyometric exercises in rapid succession. For instance, Hao et al. demonstrated that a 10-week complex training regimen (combining hang cleans and half-squat jumps) significantly enhanced peak power (+15.4%) and jump height (+9.2%) in divers (12). However, the fleeting nature of PAP limits its capacity for sustained adaptations, necessitating repeated stimuli to maintain training effects (42).

By contrast, the term PAPE has been proposed to describe acute improvements in voluntary performance (e.g., jump height, sprint speed) observed several minutes after maximal or near-maximal conditioning activities, where the net outcome reflects the balance between potentiation-related factors (e.g., elevated muscle temperature, increased neural drive, and altered muscle-tendon behavior) and fatigue rather than twitch potentiation alone (3,29,40). Contrast training capitalizes on PAPE by alternating high- and low-intensity stimuli within sets. Feng et al. found that an 8-week contrast training intervention significantly outperformed traditional training in enhancing jump height (SMD = 0.68) and sprint performance (SMD = 0.84) (4). Recent evidence synthesis using a 3-level meta-analytic approach suggests that PAPE-related performance gains are, on average, small, but are meaningfully moderated by factors such as warm-up structure, recovery duration, and training status (40). This reinforces the rationale for treating PAP and PAPE as conceptually distinct time-course frameworks when designing sequencing strategies, and motivates examination of how PAP- and PAPE-oriented prescriptions translate into longer-term adaptations in athletic populations. Accordingly, the present trial did not attempt to directly measure physiological markers of PAP or PAPE; instead, these constructs guided the design and timing of the complex (PAP-oriented) and contrast (PAPE-oriented) training protocols rather than serving as mechanistic outcome variables. However, the literature lacks a systematic understanding of how eccentric utilization and arm-swing strategies interact to shape drop-jump performance, particularly within long-term complex versus contrast training interventions (1,19).

The impact of complex and contrast training on lower-limb eccentric strength—encompassing countermovement impulse and maximum drop height—remains elusive, despite the critical role of force attenuation and transfer during the eccentric phase in explosive performance (4,22,35). Eccentric strength optimization hinges on efficient utilization of the stretch-shortening cycle (SSC), with drop-jump tests serving as standardized tools to evaluate SSC efficiency. Drop height and movement patterns critically influence test reliability (21,41). Studies suggest that varying drop heights modulate eccentric impulse, which governs the efficiency of concentric force production. Higher drop heights can amplify concentric output by increasing eccentric loading, creating a “storage-release effect” that markedly enhances jump height (36). However, excessively high drop heights may induce excessive knee flexion and energy dissipation, undermining SSC efficiency (28). In addition, arm swing bolsters trunk stability through shoulder-hip momentum transfer, potentially augmenting vertical jump power output (30,38), though its role in modulating eccentric impulse storage and release efficiency remains unclear. Given that arm swing is a natural component of most athletic jump movements, its contribution requires further biomechanical clarification.

Importantly, prior studies have often employed drop jumps as standalone interventions, neglecting the dynamic interplay between eccentric and concentric phases under standardized testing conditions. Moreover, there is a paucity of systematic investigations into how neuromuscular differences between arm-swing and non–arm-swing conditions influence performance outcomes, alongside limited quantitative analyses of the dynamic relationships between eccentric parameters and concentric power (27). Another important consideration is intervention duration. Previous studies have typically relied on relatively short-term designs, often ranging from 6 to 10 weeks. By contrast, this study employed a 24-week training program, representing a comparatively long and relatively rare intervention period in this field. This extended duration provides a valuable opportunity to examine longitudinal neuromuscular adaptations to complex and contrast training, highlighting both the novelty and applied significance of this research.

Taken together, there is currently no long-term study that has systematically compared complex and contrast training while simultaneously examining eccentric utilization and the role of arm swing in explosive performance. To address these gaps, this study adopted a 24-week randomized controlled design to compare the longitudinal effects of complex (PAP-based) and contrast (PAPE-based) training on lower-limb explosive power and eccentric utilization, while integrating biomechanical analyses of arm-swing kinematics and eccentric-concentric coupling efficiency. We hypothesized that complex training would lead to greater improvements in eccentric strength and push-off power—defined here as the peak concentric power during the upward phase of the jump—due to PAP-driven adaptations, whereas contrast training would produce broader improvements in jump performance through PAPE-related mechanisms.

Methods

Experimental Approach to the Problem

This 24-week randomized controlled trial systematically evaluates the differential effects of complex training (PAP) and contrast training (PAPE) on lower-limb explosive power and eccentric strength in elite track and field athletes. By integrating biomechanical analyses of arm-swing kinematics and eccentric-concentric coupling efficiency, this study addresses 3 unresolved questions: (a) Do long-term complex and contrast training interventions differentially enhance lower-limb explosive power (jump height, push-off power) and eccentric strength (drop height, countermovement impulse)? (b) Does arm swing improve eccentric-to-concentric force transfer efficiency by optimizing upper-limb-trunk-lower-limb kinetic chain coordination? (c) Can eccentric strength indicators (e.g., countermovement impulse) independently predict jump performance, and is their relationship modulated by training modality?

Subjects

This study recruited 45 male track and field athletes holding National First-Class or Second-Class athlete certifications (as per China's Athletic Performance Ranking System) from Jimei University, Xiamen City, Fujian Province, China. All subjects had no history of lower-limb injuries and possessed at least 3 years of systematic training experience (mean training duration: 5.2 ± 1.3 years, primarily in sprinting or jumping events). Subjects voluntarily joined this study and signed informed consent forms after being briefed on this study's objectives, procedures, potential risks, and rights protection measures.

Only male athletes were included to reduce potential variability in lower-limb explosive power and eccentric strength outcomes. Prior studies suggest sex differences in muscle fiber composition, hormonal profiles (e.g., testosterone levels), and stretch-shortening cycle efficiency, which could influence responses to postactivation potentiation and performance enhancement protocols (3,37).

To minimize the influence of recent training effects, all subjects followed a standardized low-intensity training program (3 sessions/week, 60–70% of maximum heart rate) for 4 weeks before the intervention. Baseline tests confirmed no significant differences among groups in jump height, push-off power, or drop height (p > 0.05), indicating comparable ability levels at baseline (see Table 1, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/JSCR/A817).

The sample size of 45 athletes (15 per group) was determined through an a priori power analysis using G*Power (version 3.1.9.7). For the primary outcome (jump height), a conservative effect size of f = 0.31 was selected based on the meta-analytic findings of Wilson et al. (37), which reported a pooled effect size of Cohen's d = 0.81 for power augmentation in athletic populations (95% CI: 0.44–1.19). To align with repeated-measures ANOVA conventions, this d value was converted to an f effect size using the formula f=d2√2, yielding f=0.29. We conservatively rounded to f=0. 31 to account for potential ceiling effects in elite athletes.

With parameters set to α = 0.05, power = 0.80, 3 groups, 3 repeated measures, within-between interaction, correlation among measures = 0.5, and nonsphericity correction ε = 1, the analysis indicated a required total sample size of 42 (14 per group). To ensure robustness against attrition and interindividual variability in elite athletes, we recruited 45 subjects (15 per group). Post hoc power analysis confirmed a statistical power of 0.87 for detecting the group × time interaction effect, given the observed effect size (f = 0.35) exceeded our a priori estimate.

The study protocol was approved by the Jimei University Ethics Committee (Institutional Review Board) and adhered strictly to the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All data were anonymized (removing identifiers such as names and student IDs) and accessible only to the research team, with raw data stored on an encrypted server. Subjects were granted the unconditional right to withdraw, and data from withdrawn subjects were retained in the analysis to mitigate selection bias.

Procedures

Experimental Duration

The experiment spanned 24 weeks, with three 90-minute training sessions per week (Monday, Wednesday, Friday). Standardized assessments were conducted at baseline (week 0), week 12, and week 24.

Training Parameters

Performance testing occurred at 3 key time points: baseline (week 0), mid-intervention (week 12), and post-intervention (week 24). Training parameters, such as resistance load and plyometric intensity, were progressively adjusted over the 24-week period based on the outcomes at week 0 and week 12, ensuring individualized progression.

Group Allocation

Subjects were randomly assigned to 3 groups using a random number table: complex training group (group 1), contrast training group (group 2), and control group (group 3), with 15 subjects per group. Baseline characteristics (age, height, body mass) are presented in Table 1.

Complex Training Group

Based on postactivation potentiation (PAP) theory, this group employed a sequential model combining high-load resistance and plyometric training. Each session began with high-intensity resistance or explosive movements, followed by plyometric exercises within the PAP effect window (1–3 minutes rest between movements). This approach periodically activates type II muscle fiber phosphorylation to enhance force production and elastic energy storage efficiency (17). To mitigate central nervous system (CNS) fatigue and ATP-CP system depletion, 5–8 minutes of recovery were allocated between training units to support energy regeneration and neural drive capacity (11).

Contrast Training Group

Grounded in postactivation performance enhancement (PAPE) theory, this group used alternating high- and low-load stimuli within sets. High-intensity resistance or explosive movements were paired with plyometric exercises, with extended intraset rest intervals (4–10 minutes) to promote synchronized neural drive and delayed elastic energy release, prioritizing movement chain coordination. Interset rest intervals of 4–6 minutes balanced fatigue recovery and technical stability while maintaining muscle temperature and neural excitability to align with PAPE's delayed adaptation window (7).

Control Group

Subjects followed Jimei University's standard track and field physical training program, which included traditional squats and loaded sprints for basic strength and power development but excluded PAP- or PAPE-specific interventions. The program was designed to maintain general physical fitness and consisted of lower-body resistance training (e.g., back squat, deadlift, lunges; 65–75% 1RM, 3–4 sets of 8–10 reps), sprint drills (30–60 m with sled resistance ≤10% body mass), and core stability exercises. Training frequency (3 sessions per week) and duration (90 minutes per session) were consistent with those of the experimental groups to ensure comparability.

Equipment and Software

Ground reaction force data were collected using a Kistler 3D force plate (Model 9287CA, Switzerland) at a sampling frequency of 1,000 Hz. The MARS data acquisition and analysis software (Kistler Group, Version 6.0) was used for synchronized signal processing. The force plate's vertical accuracy was ±0.5%, enabling precise measurement of kinetic parameters during jumps.

Testing Procedures

Subjects performed drop jumps starting from a 35-cm height, with increments of 5 cm until they could no longer achieve the maximum jump height corresponding to the current drop height. A 3-minute rest was provided between jumps to prevent fatigue accumulation. The drop height that produced the greatest jump height was explicitly defined as the subject's optimal drop height. This individualized optimal drop height was subsequently used as the standardized testing height for each subject, and it was re-determined at each testing session (week 0, week 12, week 24) to account for training-induced changes.

Jumps were conducted under 2 conditions: (a) with arm swing, allowing natural arm movement consistent with habitual jumping patterns; and (b) without arm swing, with hands on hips to restrict arm motion. Each test was repeated 3 times, and the best performance (jump height error <2%) was recorded. During all jump tests, subjects were instructed to “jump as fast and high as possible,” and standardized verbal cues were provided by the same investigator at each testing session to ensure consistency.

Data Extraction and Processing

(a) Raw parameter extraction: The MARS software exported parameters, including eccentric impulse, concentric impulse, peak relative eccentric force, peak relative power, peak concentric power, and temporal metrics (eccentric and concentric phase durations). Drop heights were recorded, starting at 35 cm and increasing by 5 cm until the maximum jump height was unattainable; (b) Jump height calculation: Jump height was calculated using the flight-time method: H=18gT2, where T is the flight time between take-off and landing derived from the vertical ground reaction force signal recorded by the force plate, and g is gravitational acceleration (9.81m/s2). Recent methodological work has shown that impulse-momentum calculations from a force platform are generally preferable when the aim is to obtain the most accurate absolute estimate of jump height from take-off to the apex, whereas double-integration approaches are recommended when jump height is defined as the center-of-mass displacement from quiet standing to peak height (39). Although impulse-momentum and double-integration methods can also be implemented when force-platform data are available, in the present trial, we deliberately selected the flight-time approach. Our primary aim was to examine relative changes in jump performance over time and between groups across a large number of trials, and using a simple, fully automated flight-time-based processing pipeline reduced additional analysis-related variability while aligning with common applied and field-testing practice. Under the highly standardized jumping instructions used in this study, flight-time-based and impulse-momentum-based estimates of jump height are expected to differ mainly by a small, systematic offset rather than by differences in the pattern of training-induced changes. Accordingly, the jump heights reported in this study should be interpreted as center-of-mass displacement from take-off to the apex of the jump (JH2), and the absolute values should not be directly compared with studies that report JH1 derived from double-integration or motion-capture approaches; (c) Peak value matching: The MARS software identified the peak jump height per test and matched it to the corresponding maximum drop height; (d) Data cleaning: Outliers (impulse values ± 3 SD from the mean) due to landing errors or movement inaccuracies were excluded. Missing values were imputed using linear interpolation of adjacent time points to ensure data continuity.

Statistical Analyses

Data Preprocessing and Descriptive Statistics

The Shapiro-Wilk test confirmed that all variables followed a normal distribution (p > 0.05). Means and standard deviations were calculated for each variable, grouped by condition (complex training, contrast training, control) and time point (baseline, week 12, week 24) to provide a baseline reference for subsequent analyses.

Longitudinal Intervention Effect Analysis

Repeated-measures analysis of variance (RM-ANOVA) with Greenhouse-Geisser correction for sphericity violations was used to evaluate within-group (time effect: baseline, week 12, week 24) and between-group (intervention effect: group differences) interactions. Significant main effects were followed by Bonferroni-corrected post hoc tests, reporting adjusted p-values and effect sizes (Cohen's d).

Correlation Analysis

Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to examine relationships between eccentric strength indicators (eccentric impulse, drop height) and concentric performance metrics (jump height, concentric power). Correlation coefficients (r), p-values, and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported, with distinctions made between arm-swing and non–arm-swing conditions.

Regression Modeling and Mechanistic Exploration

Multiple linear regression models were constructed with jump height and concentric time as dependent variables and eccentric impulse, drop height, and group interaction terms as independent variables. Stepwise regression selected variables, calculating ΔR2. Standardized regression coefficients (β) quantified the independent predictive role of eccentric strength on jump performance, while group interaction terms assessed moderating effects.

Model Diagnostics and Statistical Control

(a) Multicollinearity check: Variables with variance inflation factors (VIF > 10) were excluded to ensure model robustness; (b) Residual analysis: Model assumptions (linearity, normality, homoscedasticity) were verified, with log transformation applied to nonnormal residuals; (c) Statistical software and significance thresholds: Analyses were performed using SPSS 29.0, with significance set at α = 0.05 and marginal significance at α = 0.1.

Correlation analysis revealed distinct patterns across the groups (Figure 3). Discussion

Although complex and contrast training have been studied previously, this research provides a unique contribution by demonstrating the long-term neuromechanical differentiation between the 2 modalities and clarifying the modulatory role of arm swing in shaping training outcomes. These findings offer a more integrated understanding of how eccentric and concentric capacities contribute to movement efficiency and sport-specific performance adaptations.

The results demonstrate that the complex training group exhibited significant advantages in multiple eccentric strength indicators, including drop height (increased by 30.4% under arm-swing conditions, d = 2.09) and countermovement impulse (increased by 24.3%, p < 0.001), as well as concentric power output (relative maximum power during push-off increased by 28.7%, d = 1.35). By contrast, the contrast training group showed greater potential for improvement in movement speed–related parameters, such as countermovement time (reduced by 5.8%, p = 0.004) and push-off time (reduced by 5.5%, p = 0.006). These effect differences reflect fundamental distinctions in the physiological adaptation profiles of the 2 training modalities.

Taken together, these patterns are in line with our a priori hypothesis that complex training would preferentially enhance eccentric strength and push-off power, whereas contrast training would exert relatively greater effects on movement speed–related aspects of jump performance. At the same time, the generally comparable gains in maximal jump height between groups suggest that our expectation of clearly superior “overall” jump performance in the contrast training condition was only partially supported, indicating that both interventions were effective but mode-specific in their primary adaptation profiles.

In line with cross-sectional work by Matic et al. (25), who showed that the optimum drop height for maximizing power output is higher in stronger than in weaker individuals (0.62 vs. 0.32 m), the present 24-week intervention extends this evidence by demonstrating that different training prescriptions (complex vs. contrast) bias athletes toward distinct neuromechanical profiles over time rather than a single uniform pattern of improvement.

Complex training prioritizes force generation through phased integration of high-intensity resistance and explosive training and plyometric exercises, utilizing 1–3-minute interset rest periods to align with the short-term phosphorylation effects of PAP (10). These adaptations may be associated with increases in tendon stiffness and improvements in elastic potential energy storage efficiency, potentially involving periodic activation of type II muscle fiber myosin light chain phosphorylation, though this mechanism was not directly assessed.

Such adaptations could contribute to a leftward shift in the force-velocity curve and a potential reduction in movement speed (countermovement time increased by 6.4%) (24), although this interpretation should be considered speculative. The present findings also suggest that long-term PAP-related effects may help overcome the “plateau” of traditional strength training and facilitate gains in elastic potential energy storage efficiency, though these conclusions are inferred rather than directly measured (28).

By contrast, contrast training employs a neural coordination-focused strategy, which may involve delayed PAPE mechanisms with 4–10-minute recovery periods, contributing to improved neural drive synchronization and elastic energy release efficiency, although this explanation is inferential (3,10,29).

The observed improvements may reflect a rightward shift in the force-velocity curve, but this interpretation is tentative and based on indirect indicators. Long-term effects in this training modality appear to rely more on adaptive changes in neuromotor-tendon coupling efficiency, as suggested by correlations between push-off impulse and temporal parameters, though these relationships should be interpreted cautiously.

Mechanistically, the potential structural adaptation effects of complex training, such as tendon stiffness increases, may relate to collagen fiber synthesis stimulated by high-load resistance training, while the neural advantages of contrast training could involve synaptic plasticity changes; however, these mechanisms were not directly examined in this study (31,43). These differentiated outcomes suggest that complex training is more suitable for sports requiring efficient stretch-shortening cycles (SSCs), such as high jump, whereas contrast training shows greater potential in sports demanding high integration of speed and strength, such as sprint starts (30).

The results demonstrate that all experimental groups exhibited significantly greater intervention effects in the first 12 weeks compared with the subsequent 12 weeks. For instance, the complex training group's jump height increment reached +20.0% in the first 12 weeks (d = 1.26) but only +9.1% in the subsequent 12 weeks (d = 0.89). This time-dependent pattern may indicate a balance or interplay between neural and structural adaptations rather than a definitively established “competition.” In the first 12 weeks, neural drive optimization dominates, with complex training might enhance type II muscle fiber calcium sensitivity through periodic PAP stimulation (countermovement maximum relative force increased by 18.6%, p < 0.001) (15), and contrast training may facilitate high-threshold motor unit synchronization through alternating loads (push-off impulse increased by 17.1%, p < 0.001) (34). Neural adaptations in the early phase (8–12 weeks) could contribute to improved power output efficiency by influencing neural drive frequency (e.g., motor unit recruitment rate increased by 22.3%) (14), but this remains speculative. By contrast, the subsequent 12 weeks shift toward greater contributions from structural adaptations, with tendon stiffness (drop height increased by 9.2%) and myofascial elasticity (countermovement impulse increased by 12.1%) potentially requiring longer accumulation periods (p = 0.013) (3,29). Matic et al.’s study (25) suggests that optimal drop height varies with individual muscle strength (MSmax) levels, with stronger individuals achieving maximum power output at higher drop heights and weaker individuals performing better at lower drop heights. This study found that the complex training group's drop height in the latter 12 weeks approached this adaptive range (from 0.46 m at baseline to 0.60 m), indicating that connective tissue remodeling may require sustained load stimulation, though this is inferred from indirect evidence. These results reflect a conflict between neural and structural adaptations. The complex training group's countermovement time further increased in the latter 12 weeks (+4.3%), possibly indicating an adaptive shift in the performance-rate relationship—high-load resistance-induced muscle fiber hypertrophy enhances force generation but may reduce movement speed by increasing deformation delays in serial elastic components (24,27). This phenomenon is beneficial in force-dominant sports but requires periodized programming to balance strength and speed gains in speed-dominant sports.

The results demonstrate that jump height gains in the complex training group were significantly higher under arm-swing conditions (+30.9%) compared with non–arm-swing conditions (+17.4%), with push-off impulse increasing by 26.8% (p < 0.001). These findings support Feltner et al.'s (9) and Shetty et al.'s (32) conclusions on “upper-limb-trunk synergistic enhancement,” which can be analyzed through 2 mechanisms. First, momentum transfer compensates for eccentric loading: under non–arm-swing conditions, eccentric impulse showed a stronger correlation with jump height (r = 0.708 vs. r = 0.162 under arm-swing), indicating that arm swing converts upper-limb kinetic energy into vertical jump power through shoulder-hip momentum transfer, partially offsetting the load dependency of the lower-limb SSC (Figure 2) (6). Hara et al. (13) found that arm swing significantly increases peak vertical ground reaction force, and this study further quantifies its modulation efficiency across training modalities: the complex training group's arm-swing gain (+13.5%) was significantly higher than that of the contrast training group (+7.2%), likely due to its greater tendon stiffness enabling a higher proportion of kinetic energy conversion (2). Second, neural preactivation enhancement occurs, with arm swing activating synergistic contractions of the trapezius and posterior deltoid, enhancing preactivation levels of lower-limb extensor muscles (quadriceps, gastrocnemius) through spinal-cortical pathways, thereby reducing eccentric-concentric transition time (arm-swing: −7.3% vs. non–arm-swing: −2.1%) (8,26). However, this interpretation should be viewed as a theoretical explanation supported by prior studies rather than a directly measured mechanism. Feltner et al. (9) suggest that arm swing optimizes kinetic chain stiffness by enhancing trunk stability, and this study found that the complex training group's drop height-to-jump height conversion efficiency under arm-swing conditions (β = 0.499, p = 0.049) was significantly higher than that of the contrast training group (β = 0.307), indicating a synergistic effect between elastic potential energy storage and arm-swing momentum transfer. These findings further suggest sport-specific considerations for training method selection. The contrast training group showed a stronger association between push-off power and jump height under non–arm-swing conditions (β = 0.307, p < 0.001), indicating greater reliance on lower-limb local coordination. Thus, complex training with arm-swing techniques may be optimal for sports emphasizing upper-lower-limb coordination (e.g., basketball dunking), while contrast training's non–arm-swing mode is more targeted for sports requiring independent lower-limb power output (e.g., sprint starts) (5,20).

The results demonstrate that multiple regression models identified drop height and countermovement impulse as jointly explaining 43.1% of jump height variance (R2 = 0.431, p < 0.001), with predictive efficacy significantly modulated by training mode (group interaction term β = 0.499, p = 0.049). In the complex training group, drop height exhibited the highest independent contribution to jump height (β = 0.396, p < 0.001), indicating that its intervention effects primarily enhance elastic potential energy storage. Matic et al.'s (25) study suggests that the optimal drop height for depth jumps should be adjusted based on individual maximum muscle strength (MSmax) to maximize power output, with stronger individuals achieving higher power at greater drop heights and weaker individuals performing better at lower drop heights. Thus, individual eccentric tolerance and muscle strength levels are critical factors in determining optimal drop height and should be considered in training and testing. In the contrast training group, push-off impulse (β = 0.320) and movement time parameters (β = −0.678) showed a more significant combined effect, reflecting reliance on neural drive temporal optimization (e.g., reduced eccentric-concentric transition latency). This aligns with Maio et al.'s (23) findings that contrast training enhances time-integrated efficiency of push-off impulse through improved motor unit synchronization. In addition, athletes with initial drop heights ≤45 cm exhibited greater gains in contrast training (ΔR2 = +0.134), while those with initial heights ≥60 cm benefited more from complex training (ΔR2 = +0.338). This suggests that personalized training protocols can be tailored based on baseline eccentric strength: individuals with low eccentric strength should prioritize contrast training to develop neural coordination, while those with high eccentric strength can leverage complex training to surpass elastic potential energy thresholds. However, related studies caution that higher drop heights may increase knee joint injury risk, necessitating dynamic load adjustments through biomechanical monitoring (e.g., knee valgus angle) (16). Taken together, these regression findings further reinforce that complex training primarily augments the eccentric strength “reserve” that underpins higher drop heights and elastic energy storage, whereas contrast training primarily refines movement efficiency and temporal coordination, which is broadly consistent with our a priori hypotheses while indicating that neither mode is universally superior for all aspects of jump performance.

Despite systematically revealing the long-term differentiation effects of complex and contrast training and the modulatory mechanisms of arm swing, this study has several limitations. First, regarding sample characteristics, subjects were exclusively male track and field athletes (level 1 or 2), limiting the generalizability of findings due to the absence of female, adolescent, or older athlete populations. Second, in terms of measurement methods, although 3-dimensional force plates accurately captured kinetic parameters, the lack of synchronized surface electromyography (sEMG) or joint kinematic data hindered the analysis of micro-level neural drive and muscle-tendon synergy mechanisms (e.g., motor unit recruitment patterns, tendon strain rates), and jump height was derived from flight time rather than from impulse-momentum or double-integration methods based on force-time integration. This choice prioritized a simple, fully automated, and reproducible processing pipeline for repeated testing in a large cohort, but it may have introduced small systematic differences in the absolute magnitude of jump height compared with alternative calculation methods, even though the pattern of training-induced changes is unlikely to be substantially affected. In line with recent methodological recommendations that favor impulse-momentum and double-integration calculations as criterion measures of jump height (39), future work should, where feasible, combine flight-time estimates with these force-platform-based approaches to improve cross-study comparability. Third, regarding intervention design, while the 24-week period enabled observation of adaptive differentiation, it did not track the retention period of effects post-intervention or include subgroup analyses for different sports (e.g., sprinting vs. jumping), making it challenging to clarify training mode specificity across disciplines. Fourth, in terms of theoretical modeling, although multiple regression models quantified the predictive role of eccentric strength on jump performance, they did not incorporate real-time biomechanical feedback (e.g., joint torque, center-of-mass trajectory) to develop dynamic load regulation models.

Future research could address the following directions to strengthen the current understanding: (a) Shorter interventions: Future studies could explore shorter intervention periods (e.g., 6–12 weeks) to investigate how more rapid adaptation occurs and whether these changes are as effective in the long term; (b) Replication in team sports populations: It would be valuable to replicate these findings in populations from team sports (e.g., football, basketball, rugby) where movement patterns and demands differ from those in track and field athletes; (c) Further exploration of eccentric utilization: More research should focus on how eccentric strength specifically influences performance across different sports, particularly in movements that heavily rely on eccentric loading, such as deceleration phases in sprints or landing dynamics; (d) Arm-swing mechanisms: Further studies should investigate the specific effects of arm swing on kinetic chain coordination, potentially including detailed biomechanical models and a broader range of exercise types to understand its role in enhancing movement efficiency; and (e) Injury risk assessment: There should be more attention paid to the biomechanical risks of high-intensity training (e.g., high drop heights) on joint health, especially regarding knee structures like the patellofemoral joint or anterior cruciate ligament, to better inform safety guidelines and injury prevention strategies.Practical ApplicationsThis 24-week randomized controlled trial provides practical insights for coaches and practitioners working with elite male track and field athletes, focusing on how complex and contrast training can enhance lower-limb explosive power and eccentric strength. Athletes with higher eccentric strength (drop height ≥60 cm) tend to benefit more from complex training, while those with lower eccentric strength experience greater gains from contrast training. Coaches should assess baseline eccentric strength using drop-jump tests and adjust drop heights accordingly to optimize power output. In addition, careful monitoring of knee joint stress through biomechanical tools (e.g., force plates) is essential to minimize injury risk.
