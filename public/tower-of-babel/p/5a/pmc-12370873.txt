Elucidating the Neuroprotective Mechanism of 5,7-Dimethoxyflavone and 5,7’,4’-Trimethoxyflavone Through In Silico Target Prediction and in Memory-Impaired Mice
Authors: Nur Intan Saidaah Mohamed Yusof, Kalavathy Ramasamy, Fazlin Mohd Fauzi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract5,7-dimethoxyflavone (DMF) and 5,7,4’-trimethoxyflavone (TMF) are natural methoxyflavones known for their potential neuroprotective properties. This study investigates their mechanisms of action through in silico target predictions and memory-impaired mice. Ligand-based and proteochemometric models were used to predict potential protein targets, followed by molecular docking using PyRx and Discovery Video Visualiser. To validate these findings, DMF and TMF (10/20/40 mg/kg) were administered to LPS-induced mice for 21 days. Morris Water Maze (MWM) and Open Field Test (OFT) were conducted to assess cognitive functions. Expression levels of predicted targets were determined by RT-PCR, and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay was conducted to measure BDNF, Aβ, and pro-inflammatory markers. GABRA1, GABRG2, 5-HT2A, IGF1R, and 5-HT2C were predicted for DMF. Meanwhile, GABRG2, 5-HT2A, 5-HT2B, and 5-HT2C were predicted for TMF. Molecular docking showed DMF formed strong binding interactions with GABRA1 and GABRG2 (9.40 kcal/mol), interacting with His102 and Tyr160. Meanwhile, TMF formed strong binding interactions with 5-HT2A (− 9.30 kcal/mol) interacting with Ser242 and Ser159. TMF enhanced spatial memory in MWM, while both compounds reduced anxiety-related measures in OFT. DMF significantly upregulated hippocampal mRNA of GABRA1, 5-HT2A, and 5-HT2C, while TMF increased GABRG2, 5-HT2B, and 5-HT2C expression. Additionally, both compounds significantly reduced Aβ, IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α levels, while DMF-treated groups significantly increased BDNF level. These findings suggest that DMF and TMF exert neuroprotective effects when administered prophylactically, acting through distinct molecular targets involved in neurotransmission and inflammation. Introduction

Alzheimer’s Disease (AD) is a complex disease associated with memory loss and cognitive impairment. The incidence of AD is predicted to rise significantly as the older population increases due to a longer life expectancy. According to Martin, Aday [1], over 57 million people worldwide were afflicted with AD and other dementias in 2021. The incidence of AD in people age 65 years and above is expected to double every 5 years, and is predicted to increase 4-fold by the year 2050 [2]. Despite the increased number of incidences, there are only seven drugs that has been approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for AD, which are donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine, memantine, a combination capsule of memantine and donepezil, aducanumab, and lacenamab [3]. These drugs are acetylcholinesterase inhibitors, antagonists of the N-methyl-D-aspartic Acid (NMDA) receptor, and amyloid beta-directed monoclonal antibodies. Most AD drugs target the symptoms rather than the underlying cause of the disease except for aducanumab and lacenamab, where both are anti-amyloid antibodies. Aducanumab binds to amino acids 3–7 of the Aβ peptide and removes Aβ, while lacenamab targets soluble Aβ aggregates and clears the formation of amyloid plaques in the brain [4–6]. However, as of 31st January 2024, it has been announced that aducanumab would be discontinued in May 2024 due to efficacy concerns, safety issues, and high cost [7–9]. Despite numerous clinical trials in AD, 99% have showed no drug-placebo difference [3]. This high attrition rate can be attributed to the inability to determine the right target, biomarkers, drugs, trials, and patients [10]. The complexity of AD, along with gaps in understanding its underlying mechanisms, makes drug development particularly difficult. One approach to overcoming these barriers and accelerating the discovery of effective treatments is the use of in silico methods. In silico methods provide a powerful approach for identifying potential drug compounds in AD. These computational techniques enable virtual screening of large chemical libraries, optimizing lead compounds, and assessing toxicity profiles before laboratory testing. Once promising candidates are identified, in vitro and in vivo experiments are conducted for further validation. Recently, in silico approaches have gained significant attention for their ability to accelerate compound discovery, streamline screening processes, and reduce the time and cost associated with traditional drug development [11]. Additionally, since AD is a multifactorial disease, natural compounds have the advantage of a multitarget approach, which can target different molecular sites in the brain compared to the single-target activity of most drugs used for AD treatment [12]. Moreover, the bioactive compounds found in natural products with complex chemical compositions and synergistic effects might prevent the adverse effects associated with synthetic drugs [13]. These bioactive compounds also exhibit a vast biological activity, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antibacterial effects [12].

Natural compounds such as polyphenols, flavonoids, and alkaloids found in various alimentary sources may be potential lead compounds for AD. Methoxyflavones, a flavonoid found in Kaempferia parviflora (KP) are known for their neuropharmacological and neuroprotective properties [14] (Fig. 1). KP extracts has been shown to reduce Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) [15], increase Brain-derived Neurotrophic Factor (BDNF) [15], and reduce apoptosis in microglia, neuronal stem, and hippocampal HT-22 neuronal cells [16]. However, studies on the neuroprotective effect and its precise molecular mechanism of methoxyflavones have not been established. In this study, target prediction models were employed to identify potential targets of methoxyflavone compounds, specifically within AD pathways. The structure-activity relationship of the methoxyflavones was determined by molecular docking and validated in LPS-induced neuroinflammation mice. This model was used to assess the potential of DMF and TMF as early intervention candidates for AD.

Materials and Methods

In Silico Target Prediction

In silico target prediction models were employed to predict the protein targets of AD for two methoxyflavones, 5,7-dimethoxyflavone (DMF) and 4’,5,7–trimethoxyflavone (TMF). In this study, a ligand-based target prediction model was built to predict the targets. Additionally, a proteochemometric (PCM) model that was previously developed was also employed for this exercise to extend the chemical space covered. The ligand-based predictive model only uses information of the ligand, while in the case of PCM, it integrates both ligand and protein information. For further information on the PCM model, readers are directed to Fauzi, John [17]. For the ligand-based predictive model, bioactivity data was first collected from several databases as training set. Then, the chemical structure of compounds was translated into a format readable by the computer, known as descriptors. Then, through a machine learning algorithm, the association between structural features of the compounds and their bioactivity are functionally mapped. Lastly, the predictive model was internally and externally validated, and the performance of the model was measured.

Training Set

The model was built using a training set obtained from public databases. In this study, the training set was obtained from KEGG Pathway [18], UniProt [19], and ChEMBL [20]. In the KEGG Pathway database, protein targets that are associated with the nervous system such as glutamatergic synapse (04724), GABAergic synapse (04727), cholinergic synapse (04725), dopaminergic synapse (04728), serotonergic synapse (04726), long-term potentiation (04720), long-term depression (04730), retrograde endocannabinoid signaling (04723), synaptic vesicle cycle (04721), and neurotrophin signaling (04722) were compiled. For each protein obtained from the KEGG pathway, the active and inactive compounds against them were retrieved from ChEMBL database. The active and inactive compounds were classified as follows:

Active compounds: Ki/Kd/IC50/EC50 value of 10 nM or below.

Inactive compounds: Ki/Kd/IC50/EC50 value of more than 100 nM.

There must be a minimum of 20 active and 20 inactive compounds for each protein to be retained in the training set. After screening, the training set consists of 26,487 active and 87,473 inactive compounds against 105 proteins. For full information of the training set, see Supplementary Table S1.

Chemical Descriptor

MACCS keys were employed as chemical descriptors in the Ligand-based model. MACCS keys are substructure key-based fingerprints where each bit represents a specific substructure. If a substructure is present, it will be denoted as ‘1’ and ‘0’ if otherwise. MACCS keys were generated from RDKit, which has 166 predefined substructures [21].

Machine Learning Algorithm

In the Ligand-based Model, random forest was employed as the machine learning algorithm. Random forest is a supervised learning algorithm that is used for classification and regression. One vs. one classification for each protein target was employed for prediction, where the two classes are active and inactive compounds against the protein target. There are two stages in the random forest algorithm, which are: (1) random forest creation and (2) random forest prediction. To create a decision tree, k features was selected randomly from m features, where k < < m. Based on the selected features, a numbers of decision trees was built with each tree initiated by a single node. Single node was split into daughter nodes and calculated by the best split point using the Gini index. All the steps were repeated until the number of nodes reached 100.

Internal and External Validation

In this study, five-fold cross-validation was used for internal validation and the average performance of the model was calculated. Meanwhile, external validation was employed to validate the performance of the models outside of the training dataset. Compounds for external dataset were collected from PubChem Database based on their activity against the 105 protein targets in the training set. Similar criteria and methods were applied for data collection and pre-processing. There were 127,605 compounds with 33,339 active and 94,266 inactive compounds that were used as external validation datasets against 98 protein targets (see Supplementary Table S2).

Performance Measure

Sensitivity and specificity were employed to measure the performance of the model. Sensitivity evaluates the true positive rate (TPR), which is the proportion of positive cases or active compounds. Meanwhile, for specificity, it evaluates the true negative rate (TNR), which is the proportion of negative cases or inactive compounds.

Molecular Docking

Molecular docking was used to establish the binding modes and predict the structure of the intermolecular complex formed between protein and ligands. Based on the result of the predictive model, protein targets that include GABAA receptor (GABRA1 and GABRG2 subunits), 5-HT2A, 5-HT2C and insulin-like growth factor 1 receptor (IGF1R) were subjected to molecular docking against DMF. Meanwhile, protein targets that include GABAA receptor (GABRG2 subunit), 5-HT2A, 5-HT2B, 5-HT2C were docked against TMF. The crystal structures of the proteins were obtained from the Research Collaboratory for Structural Bioinformatics (RCSB) Protein Data Bank (PDB). Table 1 shows the PDB ID of all proteins.

In Vivo Validation

Reagents and Drugs

DMF and TMF were obtained from Indofine (USA). Prior to injection, both compounds were dissolved in 10% DMSO (Sigma-Aldrich, Germany), 40% PEG300 (Sigma-Aldrich, Germany), 5% Tween80 (Systerm) and 45% distilled water [24–26]. Ibuprofen was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich, Germany, where it was dissolve in DMSO, (Sigma-Aldrich, Germany) (50 mg/ml) [27, 28]. Meanwhile, LPS (E.coli O55:B5) was purchased from Santa Cruz, USA and was dissolved in distilled water [29].

Experimental Animals

A total of 108 mice (ICR/CD1), male and 8–12 weeks of age, were collected from the Laboratory Animal Facility and Management (LAFAM), UiTM Puncak Alam. The mice were housed in an individually ventilated caging (IVC) system with an Allentown cage (72” L X 31” W X 80” H), where 3–4 animals were housed per cage. Corn cob (Biocorb) was used for bedding, and the mice were housed in standard environmental conditions with a temperature of 23 ± 2̊ C, humidity at 30–70%, and a 12-h light-dark cycle and ad libitum food and water. The animals were acclimatized before starting the experiment and were randomly divided into nine groups, with 12 animals per group. This is to minimize selection bias in this study. A sample size of 12 animals per group was determined based on LaMorte’s power calculation. The mean and standard deviation were estimated from reported levels of Aβ42 in the brains of mice treated with quercetin versus vehicle control [30, 31]. The groups consisted of control (saline), Lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced group (0.25 mg/kg) [32], LPS-positive control group (Ibuprofen-40 mg/kg) and LPS- methoxyflavones groups (DMF 10, 20, 40 mg/kg and TMF 10, 20, 40 mg/kg). DMF, TMF, and Ibuprofen were given orally for 21 days based on their groups, while all mice were subjected to LPS using intraperitoneal administration on D18 - D21. The mice received LPS injections once daily for four consecutive days where acute LPS administration triggers systemic and central neuroinflammation, leading to the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and TNF-α (32). These cytokines can upregulate the expression and activity of β-secretase (BACE1), an enzyme involved in the cleavage of amyloid precursor protein (APP), thereby increasing Aβ production (32). Ibuprofen was used as a positive control to counteract LPS-induced neuroinflammation in mice (28). All mice received humane care according to the criteria outlined in the Good Laboratory Practice/Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care (GLP/AAALAC) as prescribed by Laboratory Animal Facility and Management (LAFAM), Faculty of Pharmacy, UiTM. The ethic was approved by the Committee on Animal Research, Universiti Teknologi MARA, Malaysia (376/2022).

Behavioural Test

Morris Water Maze

Morris Water Maze (MWM) was conducted in this study to evaluate spatial memory and learning. This test was conducted over a six-day period to evaluate learning acquisition and short-term memory in the LPS-induced AD model. This duration is commonly used to assess how effectively mice learn the location of the hidden platform over repeated trials. However, because the testing did not include delayed probe trials, the paradigm primarily reflects short-term memory performance rather than long-term memory consolidation [33]. The MWM is made up of a black circular pool (height: 70 cm, diameter: 90 cm) with an escape platform (area: 13cm2, diameter: 4.06 cm, height: 50 cm) [33]. Habituation trials (once a day) were performed on D15-17. In the habituation trials, each mouse was subjected to three training sessions per day from three fixed points of each quadrant. If the mice did not find the platform within 60 s, mice were placed on the platform for 10 s at the end of the trial. During the test phase (D18-D20), mice were treated with LPS and after 4 h, the mice were placed into the pool at a fixed starting position where it was allowed to swim. Escape latency, escape distance and swimming speed were recorded, and cut-off time was 60 s. On the final day of the test phase (D20), a probe trial was conducted, and the platform was removed from the maze. The probe trial was conducted after the test session to evaluate recent memory acquisition [34, 35]. The time spent by each mouse in the target quadrant was recorded for 60 s by allowing them to swim freely in the pool.

Open Field Test

Open Field Maze Test was conducted on D21, 24 hours after the final day of MWM testing. This test was performed to measure locomotor and anxiety-like behaviour in mice by analysing the movements of animals throughout the respective quadrant of the maze. After 30 min of drug administration, mice were acclimatized in the experiment room of their home cages for about 30 min before the experiment began to minimize stress. Each mouse was placed in the middle of the box (50 cm x 50 cm x 38 cm) and allowed to explore freely for about 10 min. A 10 min for each session provides a balanced assessment and reliable measurement of both initial exploratory behaviour and sustained locomotor activity [36]. The floor of the box was cleaned with 70% ethanol after each test. Total distance travelled, total time in zone and total faecal boli (open field behaviour) count were recorded and analysed. Measurement of Gene Expression

On D22, the mice were euthanised by zoletil (0.1 mL/100 g) through intraperitoneal administration and were sacrificed. Hippocampus of the brain was collected and kept in RNAprotect Tissue Reagent (Qiagen, USA) and in -80 °C freezer. Hippocampus samples of the mice were processed for gene expression analysis. First, total RNA was extracted from the hippocampus using RNeasy Plus Universal Kit (Qiagen, USA). RNA quantification and purity were measured by using NanoDrop 2000 Spectrophotometer (ThermoFisher Scientific). Next, cDNA was synthesized by using the RT2 First Strand Kit (Qiagen, USA).

Finally, the gene expression was measured by RT-qPCR analysis using RT2 Sybr Green ROX qPCR Mastermix. The QIAGEN’s real-time PCR cycler, the Rotor-Gene Q was used with cycling conditions of 1 cycle at 95 °C for 10 min for HotStart DNA Taq polymerase to activate, 40 cycles at 95 °C for 15 s, and 60 °C for 30 s to perform fluorescence data collection. All the primers were selected based on the proteins predicted from Ligand-based model and PCM models (Table 3). These primers were purchased from Qiagen, USA. Each experiment was performed with three biological and technical replicates. The expression for each gene was measured according to the quantity of GAPDH expressed and the fold expression was calculated using 2-ΔΔct.

Measurement of BDNF and Aβ Level in Hippocampus

The hippocampus of the mice collected in D22 was used and homogenized to determine the BDNF and Aβ1-42 levels. BCA Protein Colorimetric Assay kit from Elabscience Bionovation Inc., USA was used to measure total protein content in the hippocampus. Once the total protein for each sample was calculated, BDNF and Aβ1-42 levels were measured using ELISA kit from Elabscience Bionovation Inc., USA.

Measurement of Inflammatory Markers

Inflammatory markers were determined using ELISA kit from Elabscience Bionovation Inc., USA. The same techniques were used to measure the level of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, except that serum samples were used to measure these markers and total protein content was not required to be measured.

Statistical Analysis

All the data were tested for normality by using D’Agostino, Shapiro-Wilk and Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests. All the results from behavioural test and gene expression were analysed using GraphPad Prism software (Version 10.0.2 (171), July 31, 2023, GraphPad Software, Inc., USA). Significant differences between the groups were identified by using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s test. However, escape latencies, travelled distance and average speed recorded for 3 days in MWM were evaluated by repeated measure two-way ANOVA and followed by Tukey’s test. Results with p < 0.0001 were considered significant and data were expressed as the mean ± SEM.

Results

In Silico Target Prediction Models of Methoxyflavones

Internal and External Validation of the Model

The average sensitivity and specificity of the internal validation were 0.668 and 0.931, respectively. Meanwhile, the average sensitivity and specificity of the external validation were 0.796 and 0.962, respectively, higher than the internal validation. For full information of the internal and external validation results, refer to Supplementary Tables S3 and S4.

Prediction of Protein Targets for Methoxyflavone

The targets predicted for DMF and TMF in both models can be seen in Tables 4 and 5. To be included in the list, a protein target must have a score of 0.5 or higher. This indicates possible activity against the protein target. From the ligand-based model, only two targets were predicted for DMF, which were GABRA1 and GABRG2 with a score of 0.54 and 0.5, respectively. Meanwhile, only one target was predicted for TMF, which was GABRA1 with a score of 0.505. These targets are involved in GABAergic Pathway. Furthermore, the top three targets for DMF from the PCM model were 5-HT2A, IGFR1, and 5-HT2C, with a score of 0.814, 0.767, and 0.711, respectively. For TMF, the targets predicted were 5-HT2A, 5-HT2B, and 5-HT2C, with a score of 0.667, 0.550, and 0.521, respectively (Table 5). Most of the targets predicted in the PCM model involved the serotonergic pathway.

Discussion

Two main components of Kaempferia parviflora, which are DMF and TMF, are known for their neuroprotective properties. However, their precise molecular mechanisms have not been established. Here, in silico target prediction models were employed to predict potential targets for these two methoxyflavones, followed by validation through molecular docking and LPS-induced rats. Based on the results, several key observations can be deduced.

Firstly, TMF may improve memory, locomotor and anxiety-like behaviours in LPS-induced mice, as evidenced by the improved performance in MWM and OFT tests, alongside reduced Aβ levels in hippocampus. These cognitive benefits may be attributed to the modulation of GABRG2 and 5-HT2B receptors by TMF, supported by the target prediction, molecular docking, and gene expression. Neuroinflammation and Aβ accumulation are known to downregulate serotonergic receptors such as 5-HT2B receptor [37]. This downregulation may impair GABAergic signalling by disrupting GABRG2 trafficking or reducing receptor stability, leading to decreased inhibitory neurotransmission [38]. As a result, neuronal excitability may increase, potentially triggering excitotoxicity through calcium influx - a mechanism associated with neuronal death and memory impairment [39, 40]. This proposed pathways is aligned with previous studies, where the flavonoid-rich extracts from Ziziphus jujuba seeds increases the mRNA levels GABAA and 5-HT receptors in neuroblastoma SH-SY5Y cells [41], and improved spatial learning performance in animal models [42]. Together, these findings suggest that TMF may exert neuroprotective effects by supporting serotonergic and GABAergic signalling and mitigating Aβ-related pathology.

Secondly, DMF showed a promising anxiolytic effect in LPS-induced mice, as evidenced by increased time and entries into the inner zone and a reduction in faecal boli in the OFT study. These behavioural improvements suggest reduced anxiety-like behaviour in the DMF-treated group. Based on target prediction models and molecular docking, DMF is proposed to act on GABAA (GABRA1 and GABRG2 subunits) and 5-HT2A/2C receptors. This is further supported by upregulation of these genes in the hippocampus. Notably, DMF-treated mice also showed increased hippocampal BDNF levels, which may indicate involvement of BDNF-mediated pathways in modulating GABAergic and serotonergic signalling. However, we acknowledge that this mechanistic link remains hypothetical and was not directly validated in this study. Anxiety is a common neuropsychiatric symptom in AD and is associated with behavioural disturbances such as restlessness and irritability, which can contribute to accelerated cognitive decline [43]. The ventral hippocampus has been implicated in anxiety regulation [44–46], and dysregulation of 5-HT2 receptors in this region may reduce BDNF expression and disrupt GABAergic signalling [47, 48]. While our findings align with this proposed pathway, further studies are needed to confirm the causal relationships between DMF, receptor activity, BDNF modulation, and anxiety-related behaviours. Importantly, the observed differences between DMF and TMF suggest that these compounds may act through distinct but potentially complementary mechanisms. DMF may exert its anxiolytic effect via a serotonergic–BDNF–GABAergic axis, whereas TMF likely modulates 5-HT2 and GABRG2 receptors independently of BDNF. These preliminary findings support the potential of DMF and TMF as multi-target agents for addressing both cognitive and neuropsychiatric symptoms of AD.

Apart from their direct effects on memory and anxiety, both DMF and TMF demonstrated significant anti-inflammatory activity. In LPS-induced mice, treatment with these compounds led to a marked reduction in pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α. As discussed earlier, chronic neuroinflammation contributes to the progression of AD by promoting neuronal damage and downregulating key neurotransmitter receptors such as 5-HT2A/B/C and GABAA subunits (GABRA1, GABRG2) [49–51]. The anti-inflammatory properties observed here may help preserve these receptor pathways and support both cognitive and emotional regulation. These findings are consistent with previous studies on flavonoid-containing natural products. For example, low-dose Ginkgo biloba, which contains flavonoids such as quercetin, kaempferol, and ginkgetin, has been reported to suppress pro-inflammatory cytokines [52]. Similarly, methoxyflavones like 5-methoxyflavone and 6-methoxyflavone have shown anti-inflammatory effects in vitro. Specifically, 5-methoxyflavone inhibited LPS-induced NOX4 and TLR4 expression and downstream NF-κB and MAPK signaling in BEAS-2B cells, while 6-methoxyflavone suppressed inflammatory mediators and oxidative stress markers in BV2 microglial cells through modulation of the NF-κB/IκB and TLR4/MyD88 pathways [53, 54]. Together, these findings suggest that the anti-inflammatory activity of DMF and TMF may contribute to their neuroprotective effects by both preventing neuroinflammatory damage and indirectly supporting neurotransmitter receptor expression.

The structure-activity relationship analysis suggests that the number and position of methoxy groups in DMF and TMF play a critical role in their binding affinity to protein targets. TMF carries an additional methoxy group at the C4′ position on ring B, whereas DMF has two methoxy groups at C5 and C7 on ring A. This additional substitution in TMF may introduce steric hindrance, limiting its ability to fit into certain binding pockets [55]. Thus, TMF formed fewer hydrogen bonds and other stabilizing interactions, leading to weaker binding energies compared to DMF. For example, in docking studies that were performed to study the effects of flavonoid as BACE1 inhibitors, where flavanone compound such as didymin has the most hydrogen bonds (6 interactions) with low binding energy (-9.80 kcal/mol) [56]. Additionally, the interaction with the receptors may be weakened from substitutions on Ring B. This can be seen in the 5-HT2C receptor, where when bound to TMF, it forms lesser interactions, specifically one hydrogen bond, one π-anion, and one π-sigma interaction, with binding energy of -8.30 kcal/mol. Meanwhile, when DMF bind to 5-HT2C, it formed three hydrogen bonds, three π-π T shaped, one π-sigma, and one π-alkyl interactions, with binding energy of -9.00 kcal/mol. Notably, 5-HT2C is part of the excitatory serotonergic system, and interaction with DMF and TMF may enhance receptor excitability or signalling activity. These findings are consistent with previous structure-activity relationship study on methoxyflavones from Kaempferia parviflora, where dimethoxylation at C5 and C7 enhanced AChE and BChE inhibition [57]. Similarly, DMF was the most potent PDE5 inhibitor (IC50: 10.64 ± 2.09µM), while addition of the methoxy group at C4’ on methoxyflavone was not beneficial for PDE5 and PDE6 inhibition (IC50: 37.38 ± 1.15µM and 27.33 ± 2.46µM, respectively) [16]. Further studies are needed to systematically investigate how different functional groups and their positions influence binding interactions and biological activity.

In summary, this study highlights the potential of DMF and TMF as multi-target agents for early intervention in AD. Both compounds modulate GABAergic and serotonergic pathways, reduce pro-inflammatory cytokines, and show behavioural improvements related to memory and anxiety in an LPS-induced model. TMF demonstrated statistically significant improvements in memory and anxiety-like behaviour, while DMF exhibited similar trends, including increased BDNF expression, although some results did not reach statistical significance. These differences suggest that DMF and TMF may act through distinct but complementary mechanisms: TMF likely exerts its effects through 5-HT2B and GABRG2 modulation, while DMF may act via a serotonergic–BDNF–GABAergic axis (see Fig. 13). However, several limitations must be considered. First, the neuroprotective effects observed in this study were demonstrated in a prophylactic model of LPS-induced neuroinflammation, which may reflect preventive potential rather than therapeutic efficacy in individuals with established AD. Future studies should investigate whether these compounds remain effective in models that mimic later or chronic stages of the disease. Second, while in silico predictions guided target selection, certain biologically plausible targets, such as 5-HT2B for DMF and IGF1R or GABRA1 for TMF, were not predicted by the model. This may be due to limitations in the training dataset, which is largely composed of synthetic compounds and may not fully capture the structural diversity of natural products. Although DMF and TMF fell within the model’s applicability domain (data not shown), this highlights the importance of complementing computational predictions with biological validation. Finally, while gene expression analysis provided supportive evidence, the lack of protein-level confirmation (e.g., Western blot) remains a key limitation. Future studies should incorporate protein validation and assess region-specific effects within the hippocampus to better understand the mechanisms of action.

Meanwhile, both DMF and TMF demonstrate a promising role in alleviating anxiety by distinct mechanism, where DMF enhances serotonergic signalling that leads to increase BDNF levels and subsequently enhance the GABAergic signalling, while TMF modulates 5-HT2B and GABRG2 receptor activity without involving the BDNF pathway. Moreover, DMF and TMF also exhibit anti-inflammatory effects that can directly enhance memory by preventing neuroinflammation and indirectly by upregulating the expression of serotonin and GABA receptors

Conclusion

In this study, the potential neuroprotective mechanisms of DMF and TMF were explored using in silico predictions and an LPS-induced memory impairment model in mice. Based on the results, several key points can be deduced where: (i) TMF appears to enhance memory and reduce anxiety-like behaviour in LPS-induced mice by enhancing serotonergic signalling and mitigating Aβ accumulation, (ii) DMF shows a promising anxiolytic effect, likely by enhancing serotonergic signalling, which may increase BDNF levels and subsequently support GABAergic neurotransmission, (iii) both compounds exhibit anti-inflammatory effects that can directly enhance memory by preventing neuroinflammation and indirectly by upregulating the expression of serotonin and GABA receptors, and (iv) structure–activity relationship analysis suggests that the number and position of methoxy groups in DMF and TMF influence their binding affinity to protein targets. In conclusion, both DMF and TMF demonstrate neuroprotective potential and may serve as early intervention candidates AD. Their ability to act on distinct molecular targets is particularly advantageous given the multifactorial nature of AD. These findings provide a basis for further investigation into the therapeutic potential of methoxyflavones in neurodegenerative disorders.
