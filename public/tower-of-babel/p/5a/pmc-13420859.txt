Applied comparison of stepwise Cox and Cox-LASSO regression in selecting determinants of joint replacement: insights from the Geelong Osteoporosis Study
Authors: Aminu Suleiman, Mojtaba Lotfaliany, Lana J Williams, Richard Page, Stephen Gill, Anusha P Budehal, Amanda L Stuart, Julie A Pasco
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundJoint replacement (JR) is influenced by multiple determinants. Identifying an appropriate variable selection method is essential for accurate and interpretable models in musculoskeletal epidemiology. Although penalized methods such as LASSO are increasingly recommended, stepwise regression remains common in applied research. We compared these approaches for predicting JR in a large musculoskeletal cohort.MethodsData from 2931 participants aged 20-96y from the Geelong Osteoporosis Study were analysed. Potential determinants included sociodemographic, lifestyle, body composition, and comorbidity variables. Stepwise Cox regression was implemented using the stepAIC() function in the ‘MASS’ package, and Cox-LASSO regression was performed using cv.glmnet() in the ‘glmnet’ package, using R version 4.3.1. Model performance and generalizability were assessed through independent test validation and 5-fold cross-validation. Bootstrap resampling was used to evaluate performance differences between models.ResultsOver a median follow-up of 16.7y (IQR:9.7–23.2), both models consistently identified age, spine bone mineral density, and prior fracture as key determinants of JR. Stepwise Cox regression using forward selection achieved slightly higher C-index scores across independent train–test splits and 5-fold cross-validation. All models demonstrated good discriminatory ability (C-index > 0.7). Bootstrap resampling confirmed that the performance difference was unlikely due to random variation (95%CI: 0.0004–0.0190).ConclusionsThis case study revealed the practical implications of variable selection in musculoskeletal epidemiology. Stepwise Cox regression may remain useful for moderate-sized, low-dimensional datasets, while penalized methods are better suited to larger, more complex data. Background

Joint replacement (JR) is a major outcome in musculoskeletal health, reflecting advanced joint disease and substantial functional decline. Identifying its relevant determinants is essential for understanding disease risk, planning interventions, improving healthcare outcomes, and informing policy development. In research, statistical modelling has long been used to identify determinants of JR. Although demographic and clinical factors such as age, sex, obesity, and osteoarthritis severity are well-established predictors, emerging evidence suggests that broader determinants, including body composition, metabolic health, and lifestyle behaviours, may also contribute to JR risk [1–7]. However, these factors are not consistently incorporated into existing JR prediction models, and their independent contributions remain unclear.

Cox proportional hazards regression has been extensively used for time‒to-event analysis [8], with advanced statistical techniques such as stepwise regression and least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression used as alternatives for variable selection and model optimisation [9]. Due to recent developments in clinical and epidemiological studies, longitudinal data characterised by repeated measures over time with intrinsic complexities such as censoring, time-varying covariates, and correlated observations are made available [10, 11], yet few have systematically compared different variable-selection strategies to determine which combinations of predictors offer the strongest prognostic value.

These types of longitudinal data are frequently used to investigate multifactorial determinants of survival outcomes and disease trajectories [12, 13]. In this study, we compare two variable-selection approaches, stepwise regression and LASSO regression, in their ability to identify the most relevant determinants of JR within a time-to-event framework. Although both methods are widely applied, there is no clear consensus regarding their relative performance or the circumstances under which one should be preferred, particularly in low- to moderate-dimensional datasets. To address this gap, we apply both approaches to a real-world cohort and evaluate their performance using robust validation techniques, including cross-validation and bootstrap resampling. The comparison vehicle involved JR at the hip or knee as the primary endpoint for evaluating risk and protective factors through time‒to-event modelling of longitudinal health data.

Methods

Cox proportional hazards regression

Cox proportional hazards regression is a semiparametric model developed by David Cox in 1972 [8]. This method is widely used in medical research to analyse time‒event data (survival data) with the power to simultaneously evaluate the effects of several factors on survival [13]. In Cox regression, the event and time of origin must be clearly defined. The event is the outcome of interest; in this study, the event is JR, but in other studies, it could be death, surgery, disease progression, recurrence, or recovery. Survival time is the time from the origin (starting time of the study) until the occurrence of the event, loss to follow-up, death, or study end. The proportional hazards model is a powerful model that censors data and provides interpretable hazard ratios. However, when high-dimensional data are used, Cox regression can become unstable and prone to overfitting, particularly in the presence of multicollinearity. As such, this instability can lead to unreliable hazard ratios, wide confidence intervals, and poor generalizability. Therefore, most relevant predictors might be missed, while specious associations may appear significant. In clinical settings, this could translate into misleading conclusions about risk factors and poor decision-making.

Stepwise regression

Stepwise regression is used to build a model with the most significant predictors of the outcome by automatically selecting variables based on criteria such as the Akaike information criterion (AIC), Bayesian information criterion (BIC), or P-value. This method performs variable selection by adding (forward selection) or removing (backwards elimination) covariates, aiming to create a model with the most relevant predictors that balances the model fit and complexity.

LASSO regression

LASSO regression, an extension of linear regression, imposes an L1 penalty on the absolute values of regression coefficients, which shrinks less important coefficient estimates towards zero while performing variable selection [9]. It is designed to reduce overfitting with regularisation. In addition to LASSO, several other regularisation methods, including ridge regression and elastic net methods, exist. LASSO regression is marked for its ability to address high-dimensional data, thus allowing it to effectively select a subset of important predictors by filtering out the less important ones. An advantage is that it can handle multicollinearity by shrinking the coefficients of the correlated variables.

Study design

The data were obtained from the Geelong Osteoporosis Study (GOS), a population-based cohort study conducted in southeastern Australia. At baseline, 1494 women and 1539 men aged between 20 and 96 years were recruited. Participants were followed from baseline, providing measures of demographic and lifestyle factors, medication use, and comorbid conditions using a questionnaire. Bone mineral density (BMD) and body composition were measured via dual energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA, Lunar; Madison, WI, USA) together with anthropometry and clinical factors during the clinical visit. Further details concerning recruitment and data collection have been published elsewhere [14]. JR was detected by a combination of data linked with the Barwon Joint Registry [15], medical records, and self-reports in combination with dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry imaging. Death dates were identified through data linkage with the National Deaths Index via the Australian Institute for Health and Welfare (AIHW). The study was approved by the Barwon Health Human Research Ethics Committee (ID 92/01 and 00/56). All participants provided written informed consent to participate in the GOS.

From the 1494 women and 1539 men recruited, 29 women and 50 men were excluded from this analysis because JR occurred prior to baseline, and an additional 16 women and one man were excluded because of loss to follow-up or incomplete data. Additionally, two women and four men who had undergone shoulder replacement were excluded, as this analysis focused on joint replacement surgeries at the weight-bearing joints. Thus, 2,931 participants were included in the analysis. The incident JRS was identified for 224 (7.6%) of the participants.

Statistical analysis

Baseline data from the GOS were used to test each model’s ability to identify the best determinants of the JR. To perform Cox regression, we followed participants from baseline until the first occurrence of JR, death, loss to follow-up, or the end of the study period (31/05/2023), whichever occurred first. Missing data were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) implemented in the ‘mice’ package in R. Five imputed datasets (m = 5) were generated using 20 iterations each. Continuous variables were imputed using predictive mean matching (PMM) and were not transformed before imputation, as PMM preserves the original scale and distribution of the variables. Binary variables were imputed using logistic regression (logreg), and ordinal variables using proportional odds models where applicable. After confirming convergence and consistency across imputations, one completed dataset was used for all subsequent model development and validation procedures. Rubin’s rules were not required because model fitting was not repeated across imputations. Missingness proportions for all variables included in the imputation model are reported in Supplementary Table 1. Imputation was performed after data cleaning and prior to model development, ensuring that the multivariate structure of the dataset was preserved before any resampling, variable selection, or model fitting took place.

For each Cox-based model (full multivariable, forward stepwise, backward stepwise, and the refitted Cox model based on LASSO-selected variables), the proportional hazards (PH) assumption was evaluated using the Schoenfeld residuals test using the ‘cox.zph’ function in R. Scaled Schoenfeld residual plots were examined to identify potential time-dependent patterns. Variables with borderline statistical evidence of non-proportionality were inspected graphically. The statistical analyses were conducted via the R version 4.3.1.

Cox regression and stepwise Cox regression

Initially, a multivariable Cox regression model included all variables to identify predictors of JR. For the second and third models, Cox proportional hazards models were developed via stepwise regression in the forward and backwards directions via the stepAIC() function from the MASS package in R version 4.3.1. Model selection was based on the AIC to identify the most relevant predictors of the JR. The predictive accuracy of the model was validated via Herrell’s concordance index (C-index).

Cox-LASSO regression

Cox-LASSO regression was used to identify relevant determinants of JR via the ‘survival’ and ‘glmnet’ packages in R version 4.3.1. The JR was modelled as a survival object via the ‘Surv()’ function. The predictors were transformed into a design matrix for use in the Cox-LASSO model. Here, Cox-LASSO simultaneously performs survival analysis and variable selection by extending the Cox proportional hazards model with LASSO regularisation, which adds an L1 penalty to the loss function. Cross-validation was performed to choose the optimal value of the penalty parameter λ; for the LASSO penalty, we set the alpha value at 1, as this reduced the less important predictors to zero by facilitating automatic variable selection.

Model comparison

To ensure fair comparison across modelling strategies, all performance evaluations were based on unpenalized Cox proportional hazards models. Forward and backward stepwise selection were performed using standard Cox regression. For the LASSO approach, the penalized model was used solely for variable selection; the final set of selected variables was then refitted in an unpenalized Cox model. These models were evaluated based on their predictive performance. The AIC, BIC, and C-index were used to assess the model fit, complexity, and predictive accuracy, respectively.

Additionally, to obtain a more robust estimate of the models’ predictive performance and generalizability, independent test validation and 5-fold cross-validation were performed. The independent validation involved randomly partitioning the data into training sets (90%) and testing sets (10%), using stratified sampling based on the event indicator. All model development steps, including variable selection, hyperparameter tuning, and cross-validation, were performed exclusively within the training set. The test set was held out until the final evaluation to prevent information leakage. For the stepwise Cox models, we fitted a null model and a full model that included all potential predictors. Forward and backward selection were performed using AIC. Specifically, variable selection was repeated within each cross-validation fold, ensuring that the selection process did not use information from the validation fold; the Cox-LASSO model was fitted using the glmnet package. The penalty parameter λ was tuned using 10-fold cross-validation performed only within the training set. The optimal λ (λ_min) was then used to refit the model. Similar to stepwise models, LASSO tuning was repeated within each cross-validation fold. Internal model stability was assessed using 5-fold cross-validation. Here, we repeated the entire training-set workflow, where each fold served once as a validation fold. For each fold, the model was re-trained from scratch, including stepwise selections or LASSO tuning. Performance metrics were averaged across folds, which were used as the internal performance estimate.

The C-index was the primary evaluation metric, and results were interpreted according to a commonly used threshold, whereby a C-index less than 0.6 was considered poor, 0.6 to 0.7 was considered moderate, above 0.7 was considered good, and greater than 0.8 was considered excellent model performance [16, 17]. Moreover, the differences between the C-index results were evaluated via bootstrap resampling analysis with 1,000 iterations estimated at a 95% confidence interval (CI) to gain strong statistical evidence of the difference in performance metrics (the observed differences were not due to random variation). Additionally, we also considered the ability of each model to precisely identify the relevant determinants of the JR and their simplicity in the interpretation of the results, as well as the clarity of the identified determinants.

Proportional hazards assumption

For the full multivariable model, the global test indicated no evidence of violation (p = .244). For the forward stepwise and backward stepwise models, the global tests showed borderline statistical evidence of non-proportionality (p = .046 and p = .031, respectively). For the LASSO-selected refitted Cox model, the global test again indicated no evidence of violation (p = .090). Across all models, age was the only covariate that consistently showed a statistically significant individual test (p-values between 0.021 and 0.031). All other covariates satisfied the PH assumption. Moreover, graphical inspection of the scaled Schoenfeld residuals for age did not reveal systematic time-dependent trends in any of the models. The smoothed curves were approximately horizontal and remained within wide confidence bands (Fig. 1). These findings indicate that the proportional hazards assumption was acceptable for all models, and no time-varying coefficients or stratification were required.

Schoenfeld residual plot for age across all models

All the models successfully converged without warning. Cox-LASSO converged faster with fewer iterations and shorter computational times and better efficiency than stepwise Cox regression because of its convex optimisation framework and regularisation, which is less sensitive to multicollinearity and overfitting. Moreover, stepwise Cox regression requires numerous iterations of model fitting and is prone to collinearity and variable scaling.

The stepwise Cox regression model using forward selection identified six predictors, the stepwise Cox regression model using backwards elimination identified five predictors, and the Cox-LASSO regression model identified four predictors. A subset of variables (age, spine BMD, and prior fracture) was selected by all the models. The stepwise Cox regression model with forward selection included all the variables selected via Cox-LASSO regression, and the stepwise Cox regression model with backwards elimination was used. However, Cox-LASSO excluded some variables that were retained in the stepwise Cox regression models, likely due to their weaker penalised contributions.

Moreover, the variables selected by stepwise Cox regression models using both forward and backwards directions were very similar except for body mass index (BMI), which was not selected by stepwise Cox regression using backwards elimination. Notably, appendicular lean mass adjusted by height in square meters (ALM/height2) and BMI were selected by stepwise Cox regression via forward selection. While ALM/height2 was statistically significant in the model, BMI was not. The inclusion of BMI in the model, despite its p-value > 0.05, might be due to its predictive contribution to the model. Although ALM/height² and BMI were positively correlated in our dataset, leaving both variables in the model could lead to collinearity. BMI became statistically significant when ALM/height² was removed from the model, likely due to reduced collinearity between these related body composition measures (Tables 2 and 3, and 4).

Model performance

When the full dataset was used, both models achieved similar performance. The stepwise Cox regression models had slightly better AIC and BIC values than did the Cox-LASSO regression model. However, the differences observed between the models were relatively small, and the C-index results showed good predictive accuracy across all the models (C-Index > 0.7). Additional results from the bootstrap resampling analysis confirm that the slight difference observed in the C-index between the stepwise Cox regression model using forward selection and the Cox-LASSO regression model was not due to random variation (95% CI: 0.0004–0.0190), suggesting that their performance is comparable. However, the observed differences in C indices between the stepwise Cox regression model using backwards elimination and the stepwise Cox regression model using forward selection, and between the stepwise Cox regression model using backwards elimination and the Cox-LASSO regression model were not statistically significant (95% CI: − 0.0009–0.0041 and − 0.0029–0.0185, respectively).

The results of the cross-validation using a test set revealed that the stepwise Cox regression model using forward selection had a slightly highest C-index value, followed by the Cox-LASSO regression model and then the stepwise Cox regression model using backwards elimination. Moreover, in 5-fold cross-validation, stepwise Cox regression using forward selection maintains the highest average C-index, followed by stepwise Cox regression using backwards elimination and Cox-LASSO with the lowest average C-index.

Across all the validation strategies, stepwise Cox regression using forward selection consistently yielded a slightly higher C-index, although the differences in the C-index were modest (≤ 0.01). Stepwise Cox regression using backwards elimination showed poorer performance on the test set, suggesting potential overfitting. This demonstrated that while stepwise Cox regression using backwards elimination performed well on the full dataset, it could not be generalized to new data. Discussion

Summary of findings

This applied comparison between stepwise Cox regression and Cox-LASSO models offers insights into their relative strengths in selecting predictors of health outcomes within moderate-sized, low-dimensional observational data.

Overall, stepwise Cox regression and Cox-LASSO regression models demonstrated good predictive performance, each reporting a C-index greater than 0.70. Stepwise Cox regression, which relies on the iterative inclusion and exclusion of variables on the basis of information criteria, yields slightly better model fit, reflected by lower AIC and BIC values and stronger test set discrimination than the Cox‒LASSO regression model does. In particular, the stepwise Cox regression model using forward selection showed better stability and generalizability. However, the Cox-LASSO regression model provides automatic regularisation, with good computational efficiency. It shrinks less relevant predictors to zero, producing a more parsimonious model. Although its performance on the full dataset was slightly lower than that of the stepwise Cox regression models, the Cox-LASSO model maintained good predictive ability in both cross-validation and independent training-test splits, with comparable performance to the stepwise Cox regression models.

Multicollinearity was effectively addressed in the Cox-LASSO regression model, which retained BMI as a relevant predictor. In contrast, the stepwise Cox regression model using forward selection included both BMI and ALM/height², but only ALM/height² was statistically significant. The inclusion of BMI as a predictor of JR, despite its nonsignificant p-value, may reflect its contribution to overall model performance. ALM/height² was positively correlated with both BMI and waist circumference in the dataset. When both ALM/height² and BMI were included in the model, ALM/height² was consistently selected as a significant predictor. However, when either ALM/height² or BMI was retained individually, BMI emerged as statistically significant. This pattern suggests collinearity between these variables, with only one appearing significant when modelled together; a similar trend was observed for waist circumference. These findings indicate the presence of multicollinearity, which may lead to unreliable estimates and inflated standard errors when such correlated variables are included simultaneously. Comparable results were observed in the multivariable Cox regression model.

The inclusion of prior fracture as a relevant predictor of JR in both the stepwise Cox regression and Cox-LASSO regression models, even though its p values exceeded the conventional significance threshold, likely reflects its contribution to the overall performance of the model in predicting JRS.

This study demonstrated that different variable selection methods can provide slightly different predictive performances. In examining how each model performed following variable selection using a complete dataset, the stepwise Cox regression model using backwards elimination had the lowest AIC and BIC compared with the other models. The stepwise Cox regression model using forward selection had the second lowest AIC, which strongly supports it as the second-best fitting model, despite its BIC being higher than that of the Cox-LASSO regression model, because the AIC is a suitable method in regard to predictive accuracy [17]. Considering model performance, the C-index results revealed that both models had reasonable predictive performance, with a C-index > 0.7. While the stepwise Cox regression model using forward selection has a statistically significant edge over the Cox-LASSO regression model, the difference in performance is relatively small. Although the bootstrap resampling test validated the observed differences in C indices, the small difference observed between the stepwise Cox regression model using forward selection and Cox-LASSO regression was not due to random variation.

Additionally, the C-index results from independent train‒test split cross-validation revealed that the stepwise Cox regression model using forward selection had better discriminative ability on the independent validation test data. The stepwise Cox regression model using forward selection also has a higher average C-index during 5-fold cross-validation, showing a more consistent performance on different training sets.

The consistency of the highest C-index results for the stepwise Cox regression model using forward selection across both independent train‒test splits and 5-fold cross-validation indicated superior model stability. While its AIC and BIC values were lower than those of the backwards elimination model on the full dataset, the forward selection approach demonstrated more robust predictive performance. The Cox-LASSO model, although yielding a slightly lower average cross-validation C-index, may still be appropriate when the objective is to develop a parsimonious model that effectively addresses multicollinearity and overfitting. In contrast, a stepwise Cox regression model using forward selection might be preferable when model consistency and stability are prioritised. Moreover, the strong performance of the stepwise Cox regression model using backwards elimination on the full dataset suggests potential overfitting, as its performance deteriorated on training-test splits and 5-fold cross-validation. While it demonstrated good predictive ability within the original dataset, it failed to generalize effectively to unseen data.

Previous studies that compared LASSO regression and stepwise regression revealed that LASSO regression performs better than stepwise regression, but acknowledged that the better performance of LASSO regression was not completely true in all situations [18, 19]. Additionally, our findings contradict those of recent studies comparing stepwise regression and LASSO regression via simulation and psychological data from the Stanford Early Life Stress Study, which reported that LASSO regression has better performance than stepwise regression [20]. Using both simulation and empirical data, the performance of LASSO regression was comparable to that of stepwise regression because the magnitude of improvement between the models was relatively small [20–22]. This suggests that while LASSO is another alternative for variable selection, it may not always improve prediction in low- to moderate-dimensional clinical datasets. The study also emphasised that, in datasets with a small number of predictors, LASSO may exhibit higher sensitivity but lower specificity [20]. This limitation is less relevant to our dataset, where the number of predictors was moderate and preselection was clinically guided.

This real-world comparison of Stepwise Cox regression and Cox-LASSO regression in predicting JR highlights both methodological and clinical considerations. These findings suggest that, in this moderate-sized, low-dimensional time-to-event dataset, LASSO regression does not offer a clear advantage in terms of model stability or generalizability over stepwise regression with forward selection. While penalized regression methods such as LASSO are generally favoured in the methodological literature for their robustness in high-dimensional and correlated predictor settings, our findings showed that Stepwise Cox regression using forward selection performed slightly better in this musculoskeletal dataset. This apparent contradiction with prior evidence reflects the specific characteristics of our cohort: a moderate sample size, relatively low predictor correlation, and a limited number of clinically relevant variables. Under these conditions, Stepwise Cox regression was able to identify a parsimonious set of predictors with strong predictive accuracy.

Strengths and limitations

This study highlights the strengths and weaknesses of stepwise Cox regression models and the Cox-LASSO regression model. Several points need to be considered between the models when performing variable selection. The multivariable Cox regression model remains valuable when the number of predictors is limited according to the rule of thumb (10 events per predictor) [23, 24] if the number of predictors becomes comparable with the number of events in the model. A multivariable Cox regression and stepwise Cox regression models can be prone to overfitting, particularly in a situation where many predictors are correlated or weakly associated with the outcome. Overfitting transpires when the model captures noise or peculiarities of the training data instead of fundamental patterns, which can result in misleading hazard ratios and lower predictive accuracy. The regularisation mechanism of Cox-LASSO regression provides a sturdy solution for multicollinearity. This was achieved by shrinking less important covariates to zero. In the presence of many covariates, Cox-LASSO regression provides a better interpretable model, although it may exclude some variables that have a weak but real association with the outcome.

It is important to emphasize that our results should not be interpreted as a general methodological claim that Stepwise Cox regression is superior to Cox-LASSO, but rather to illustrate how each method performs in a moderate-sized, low-dimensional real-world dataset. The analysis was conducted using a single cohort, and the results may be dataset-specific. Differences in population characteristics, measurement protocols, or event distributions in other settings may yield different model performance profiles. In addition, a low number of JR (7.6%), our outcome of interest, might also affect the statistical power and increase the risk of overfitting, and reduce the reliability of variable selection procedures, particularly for penalised and stepwise methods. Low event numbers also limit the precision of performance estimates and may contribute to the small differences observed between modelling strategies. Although imputation was used to address missing data, imputation itself introduces uncertainty that may influence model estimates.

Finally, in applied epidemiological research, particularly in musculoskeletal health, researchers often work with moderate-sized cohorts and a manageable number of predictors. In such contexts, traditional approaches like Stepwise Cox regression may remain useful and yield clinically interpretable models. Conversely, in larger, more complex datasets with higher dimensionality or stronger correlations, penalized regression methods are likely to offer greater stability and generalizability. Future studies are warranted to evaluate these methods in independent cohorts to assess generalizability.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that both stepwise Cox regression and Cox-LASSO regression modes are valuable tools for variable selection in identifying the determinants of JR, but their advantages depend on the nature of the data. All the models demonstrated broadly similar predictive performance, with only small differences in C-index and information criteria. The stepwise Cox regression model using forward selection selects six variables and performs consistently well across all the validation strategies. The Cox-LASSO model, while selecting fewer variables, achieved comparable performance, with only a minor reduction in the C-index. Although the stepwise Cox regression model using backwards elimination performed well on the full dataset, its reduced accuracy in external validation suggests potential overfitting. These differences, while statistically observable, may have limited clinical or practical significance. The findings should therefore be interpreted cautiously and may be specific to the characteristics of this cohort, including its relatively low event rate and the distribution of predictor variables. Further validation in external datasets with different population structures and event rates is warranted to confirm the robustness and generalisability of these modelling strategies. By situating our results within the musculoskeletal context, this work offers guidance for researchers and clinicians seeking to balance methodological rigour with practical utility in predicting musculoskeletal outcomes.
