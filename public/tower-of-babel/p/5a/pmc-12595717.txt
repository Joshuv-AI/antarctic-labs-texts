The impact of dance self-efficacy on dance program participation and health outcomes among older adults: a secondary analysis of the DaNCE cluster-RCT
Authors: Martha Waugh, Rhiannon Lee White, Celia B Harris, Dafna Merom
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWhile dance gains recognition as a health resource for older adults, empirical evidence remains inconclusive. Personal factors likely to influence participation in structured dance programs have been overlooked, potentially contributing to the conflicting results. Like exercise self-efficacy, dance self-efficacy may affect engagement with group dancing considering the physical, cognitive, and social challenges involved. This study examines the associations between dance self-efficacy, program participation and satisfaction, and health outcomes.MethodsParticipants of the DAnCE cluster-RCT in Sydney, Australia, (n = 217) received folk or ballroom dancing, twice weekly for 12 months. Quantitative measures of dance self-efficacy, program factors (attendance, satisfaction, and difficulty ratings), the Short Physiological Performance Battery (SPPB), Rey Auditory Visual Learning Tasks, and Trail Making Tests A&B were completed. ANCOVA investigated associations between dance self-efficacy and program factors. Linear regression models, adjusted for co-variates, examined dance self-efficacy as a moderator and mediator of the effect of attendance on physical (SPPB) and cognitive (composite) health outcomes.ResultsParticipants mean (SD) age was 79 years (7.19). High dance program attendance (≥ 56/85 sessions) was associated with improved dance self-efficacy, while low attenders (≤ 25/85 sessions) decreased in dance self-efficacy (F = 10.85, η2 = 0.11, p < .01). Both high attendance and increased dance self-efficacy were associated with greater program satisfaction (p < .01) and lower perceived difficulty (p < .01). Baseline dance self-efficacy moderated the effect of program attendance on physical function (SPPB; p = .03 for interaction) such that only participants with low dance self-efficacy at baseline realised physical functional gains. Change in dance self-efficacy fully mediated effect of attendance on physical function outcomes (b = 0.20, BCa 95%CI: 0.04, 0.41), but not cognitive outcomes.ConclusionsDance self-efficacy appears to influence both dance program participation and physical function outcomes, suggesting it may be a key mechanism driving health benefits. Incorporating a dance self-efficacy tool in older adult dance research and practice may improve initial assessments of dance program participant needs, guide program design and evaluation, and lead to more consistent health outcomes.Trial registrationThe Australian New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry ACTRN12612000889853 (21.08.2012).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-025-06544-x.Keywords: Dance, Dance for health, Older adults, Dance intervention, Dance self-efficacy, Self-efficacy, Program design, Personal factors, Mechanisms

Background

Dance is an accessible and enjoyable physical activity that aligns with recommendations for both active aging [1, 2] and creative aging [3, 4]. Though dancing is promoted as exercise for older adults [5], it is experienced as a meaningful, artistic activity that ‘doesn’t feel like exercise’ [3, 6, 7]. Dance integrates music, social interaction, cognitive stimulation, and embodied arts experiences [3, 6, 8–11]. However, dance activities require cognitive-motor skill learning [12], and are complex, multifaceted and dynamic interventions, with many interactive program components driving health outcomes through multiple mechanisms [11, 13–16]. Dance programs will therefore affect people differently [11].

Despite its potential, empirical evidence for the health and social benefits of dancing remains inconsistent, with substantial variability in outcomes across both primary studies and systematic reviews [7, 17–20]. The inconsistency may be partially attributed to personal factors such as self-efficacy, preference, motivation, and social support, which are overlooked in dance research [11, 21]. Individual differences may critically influence how older adults engage with and benefit from dance programs. In this study we examined the role of dance self-efficacy in a dance intervention for older adults through secondary data analysis.

Self-efficacy, a central construct in Bandura’s Social Cognitive Theory [22, 23], refers to an individual’s belief in their ability to accomplish specific tasks or behaviours. In exercise interventions for older people, stronger exercise self-efficacy has been consistently linked with improved exercise adoption and adherence [24, 25], with programs often designed to enhance self-efficacy to support participation [26–28]. Factors such as enjoyment, satisfaction, and perceived capability are closely related to self-efficacy [29], and play important roles in determining exercise participation [30].

General exercise self-efficacy measures focus on self-regulatory efficacy for maintaining routine exercise under common barriers, such as being busy, tired, or bored [27, 31–33]. Compared to other recommended aerobic exercise for older people, like walking or recumbent cycling [5], group dancing presents additional, unique challenges [11]. Dance is a complex cognitive-motor skill [12, 34] with varied task demands not typically addressed by exercise self-efficacy measures, such as learning and recalling dance routines, coordinating movement with music, and dancing with new people in a social setting. Bandura [31] stressed that self-efficacy scales must reflect the specific demands of the task to maximise predictive value, particularly when the task involves multiple complexities like dance.

Given the diversity in physical and cognitive ageing [35], exercise capacity [27, 36], and prior dance experiences, older adults are likely to have varied self-efficacy and capabilities for dance [11]. Qualitative research has shown that older adults in dance programs can struggle with following instructions, memorising and recalling dance sequences, and can experience nervousness or frustration if they cannot learn dance routines as quickly or easily as expected [37–40]. Pre-existing health conditions may further limit older people’s ability to perform certain dance movements and sequences, requiring adaptations [39, 41]. While research emphasises the importance of matching program difficulty to participant ability for complex motor activities [5, 34, 42], little attention has been given to establishing the suitability of dance program content for older participants and evaluating how self-efficacy influences dance participation and health outcomes [11].

The Dance, Aging, Cognition, and Economics (DAnCE) cluster-randomised controlled trial [43] investigated the effects of social dancing on incident falls and associated physical and cognitive risk factors in 530 older adult retirement village residents. The study did not show significant between-group reductions in falls or differences in fall-related risk factors. The trial included a novel dance-specific self-efficacy measure, subsequently validated using trial data [44]. The dance self-efficacy items assessed confidence for specific dance tasks such as moving to music, remembering dance steps, and managing physical effort. Findings indicated that baseline self-efficacy for dance was lower in participants with less dance experience, older age, poorer physical and cognitive function, poorer mental health, a smaller social network, and who exercised less frequently [44]. Importantly, DAnCE program participation improved dance self-efficacy compared to wait-listed controls. Hence, the dance self-efficacy scores appear to reflect objective capabilities for group dance participation and confirmed that the program led to improvements in participants’ confidence to perform the dance tasks.

According to Social Cognitive Theory, improvements in self-efficacy should support program participation and engagement, driving greater health benefits [23, 31]. Given the growing popularity of dance as a health resource for older populations [7, 11], understanding how personal factors, particularly self-efficacy, influence both participation and outcomes may be essential to optimise dance programs.

Study objectives

The aim of this study was to examine personal factors influencing dance program participation and health outcomes in older adults, with a particular focus on dance self-efficacy as a potential moderator and mediator of intervention effects. Given the limited effectiveness of the original DAnCE trial, we sought to understand both dance self-efficacy-specific and broader factors that may have impacted program effectiveness. We therefore had three objectives:

To assess the associations between dance self-efficacy at baseline and pre-post program change in dance self-efficacy with dance program attendance, program satisfaction, and perceived difficulty.

To assess the associations between dance self-efficacy at baseline and pre-post program change in dance self-efficacy with dance program attendance, program satisfaction, and perceived difficulty.

To investigate whether dance self-efficacy moderated and/or mediated the effects of program participation on physical function and cognitive health outcomes.

To investigate whether dance self-efficacy moderated and/or mediated the effects of program participation on physical function and cognitive health outcomes.

To explore implications of dance self-efficacy findings for understanding the trial outcomes and identify additional contributing program factors that may inform future dance program design and implementation.

Materials and methods

The DAnCE fall prevention cluster randomised controlled trial [43] was conducted in 23 self-care retirement villages in NSW, Australia (Western Sydney University Ethics ref: 9468). Full details on recruitment, intervention allocation, and data collection procedures can be found in the trial protocol [45]. The present study is a secondary analysis of data from DAnCE program participants allocated to receive the intervention and who completed both pre- and post-program measures.

DAnCE program participants

All DAnCE program participants resided in the self-care retirement villages recruited for the study. Eligible residents had to be able to walk at least 50 m; agree to undergo physical and cognitive assessments at baseline and 12mo; and obtain medical clearance to participate in the dance study. Participants scoring < 24 on the Mini Mental State Examination (MMSE [46]), a brief cognitive screening tool, indicating cognitive impairment were excluded.

DAnCE program development, design, and delivery

The DAnCE study delivered hour long dance sessions, twice a week, for a total of 80 h over 12 months (with short breaks for holidays) in 12 villages allocated to receive the dance intervention. The objective of the study was to assess the effectiveness of group dance programs commonly available in the community as an alternative to existing falls prevention exercise. Older adult participant residents were offered folk dancing (5 villages) or ballroom dancing (7 villages). The folk dancing sessions included traditional dances from several countries including Israel, Greece, and the United Kingdom. The ballroom dancing sessions included classic ballroom dances such as the Waltz, Foxtrot, and Rumba, as well as more modern styles such as Rock and Roll and Salsa. Over the course of the study, cognitive and physical demand and cardiovascular effort were increased by introducing more complex dance movements and sequences and having participants perform more dances in succession without rest. Program intensity was estimated during the pilot and at session numbers 20, 40, 60 and 79 using Borg rating of perceived exertion ratings [47].

The DAnCE program was developed and delivered by professional, qualified dance teaching artists with experience delivering dance sessions to seniors living in the community. The program was first trialled on 9 community-dwelling older adults for 12 weeks. These volunteers provided feedback on program elements including the dance sequences, the music, the pedagogical approach, and how comfortable the movements were for them to perform. The final program was standardised via two workshops, a guidebook, and a DVD. Four ballroom dance instructors and four folk dance instructors received training on the DAnCE program via the workshops. The instructors were selected through a process involving 20 older people who were experienced folk dancers who provided feedback on class demonstrations given live by potential instructors.

Dance self-efficacy measure and past dance experience

Dance self-efficacy was measured with six survey questions developed for the older adult population. The items were introduced with the statement ‘Whether or not you have danced before, please rate how confident you feel that you can do the following when dancing”: 1) Keep up with the tempo; 2) Remember the steps; 3) Remember directions; 4) Enjoy doing the classes; 5) Overcome the physical effort involved; and 6) Enjoy dancing with a partner you do not know. Responses on a 5-point Likert scale ranged from 1 = ‘Not at all confident’ to 5 = ‘Extremely confident’. Item scores were summed for a total score/30, with higher scores indicating stronger self-efficacy for dance. The dance self-efficacy items were developed specifically for older adult dance participants, as detailed in the previous validation study [44], and demonstrated strong internal consistency, good criterion and construct validity, and reasonable responsiveness to change within this study population.

Previous dance experience was assessed with a main survey item, ‘Have you ever participated or do you currently participate in social dancing (examples given) or structured dance classes (examples given) regularly, i.e., at least once a month, not including free dancing in parties?’. Negative responses were categorised as dance novice. Positive responders were directed to answer, ‘did/do you dance regularly for recreation, exercise or learning the skill at least once a week?’ Negative responses were classified as moderate, and positive responses, high past dance experience.

Program attendance, satisfaction, and difficulty ratings

Teachers reported attendance at each dance sessions in a standardised form and posted their diaries at the end of the program. Attendance was measured as a categorial variable with three levels: Low attendance ≤ 25 dance sessions; moderate attendance 26–55 dance sessions; and high attendance ≥ 56 dance sessions. For the initial analysis, we retained the three groups to maximise detail. However, the low and moderate attendance groups were combined into a single group for moderation and mediation analysis due to substantially uneven group sizes (n: low = 31, moderate = 55, high = 156). Furthermore, neither low nor moderate attenders achieved the recommended training dose of 120 min/week for 6 months to improve falls-related physical and cognitive risk factors, while the high attenders did achieve the recommended dose [48, 49].

Satisfaction with the DAnCE program and perceived difficulty of the dance program were assessed at the end of the study using two sets of survey items answered on a 5-point Likert scale. The first item set asked respondents to rate their satisfaction with: (1) their overall experience participating in the dance program; (2) the dance program content; (3) the music selection; and (4) the progression of the classes. Items were summed to create a program satisfaction total score out of 20, where higher scores indicated greater satisfaction. The second set of items asked participants to rate how difficult they found: (1) the dance program content; (2) the pace of the classes; (3) the length of the sessions; and (4) the frequency of the sessions. Items were summed for an overall difficulty score out of 20, where higher scores indicated greater perceived program difficulty.

Physical and cognitive measures

Physical function was evaluated with the Short Physiological Performance Battery (SPPB), which is a well-established composite measure of functional mobility [50]. The SPPB includes gait speed over 3 m, time to complete 5 chair rises, and tests of side-by-side, semi-tandem and tandem standing for over 10 s.

Overall cognitive performance was assessed with a composite score to allow for general comparisons. The scores were constructed by summing z-transformed scores from several cognitive measures, the standard approach for producing composite measures of cognitive ability [10, 51]. Cognitive and executive function was tested with Trail Making Tests A and B [TMT A&B; , 52] and Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Tasks [RAVLT; 53]. The TMTs are dot-to-dot puzzles connecting numbers only (A) capturing cognitive processing speed, and then alternating numbers and letters (e.g., 1-A-2-B). The executive domain of cognitive flexibility is the difference in time between tests A and B. The RAVLT involves word learning, recollection and delayed recall, and the tests measure immediate memory span [sensitive to attentional capacities, 54], verbal learning capacity (sum of 5 recollection trials), immediate recall (post interference), and delayed recall.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using JASP 0.17.1, and the PROCESS macro v3.5 by Hayes with SPSS 26.0. Parametric test assumptions were checked using the Shapiro-Wilk test, histograms, and normal probability plots, and Levene’s test of homogeneity of variance, to ensure data met the requirements for valid parametric statistical inference [55]. All analyses were two-tailed and statistical significance was set at p =.05.

We used partial eta squared to establish the effect sizes for ANCOVA, with η2 = 0.06 indicating moderate-sized effects and η2 = 0.14 indicating large-size effects [55]. Bonferroni adjustments were used to correct for inflated error rates when conducting multiple t-tests to compare outcomes from more than two groups. Covariates were selected based on their established relationships with both self-efficacy and program participation in older adult exercise research. Age was included as older adults show declining self-efficacy with age, past dance experience influences dance motor skills and confidence for dance tasks, and program satisfaction is closely linked to both self-efficacy and continued participation.

ANCOVA analysis was used to evaluate dance program satisfaction and perceived difficulty at different levels of dance program attendance. We also used ANCOVA models, adjusted for covariates age and dance program satisfaction, to determine the capacity of baseline dance self-efficacy to predict dance program attendance, as well as establishing pre- to post-program change in dance self-efficacy by program attendance levels. Planned contrasts between levels of attendance were tested using Bonferroni adjustments for multiple tests. Linear regression models adjusted for age and past dance experience assessed the relationship between baseline and post-program dance self-efficacy, and program satisfaction and difficulty ratings.

Separately, we evaluated dance self-efficacy at baseline as a moderator, and program-related change in dance self-efficacy as a mediator, of the effect of dancing on health outcomes. Low and moderate attendance groups were combined due to uneven group sizes and similar training dose exposure. For the moderation analysis, two regression models tested the effect of dance program attendance (dose of dance achieved) on physical function scores (SPPB) and cognitive composite scores respectively, first directly, and then with baseline dance self-efficacy entered as a moderator variable. Mediation models were then conducted to establish whether pre-post program change in dance self-efficacy mediated the effect of dance program attendance on physical and cognitive health outcomes.

First, we ran a series of linear regression models to determine whether the conditions necessary to establish mediation were achieved. The predictor variable (attendance) must significantly predict the outcome variable (physical/cognitive function; Model 1) and the mediator (post-program dance self-efficacy; Model 2), and the mediator must also predict the outcome [Model 3, 56]. If the pre-conditions were satisfied, mediation models were conducted assessing the effect of low vs. high dance program attendance on post-program physical function scores and cognitive composite scores respectively with post-program dance self-efficacy entered as a mediator variable. Age, baseline dance self-efficacy, and baseline SPPB or cognitive composite scores were included in the models as covariates. Confidence intervals (CI) for the indirect effect are bias-corrected and accelerated (BCa) bootstrapped CI based on 5000 samples, which provides improved accuracy over standard bootstrap methods [57].

Discussion

This study examined the role of personal factors in the relationship between group dance participation and health outcomes in older people. Specifically, we explored the impact of dance self-efficacy on key outcomes including attendance and program satisfaction, and both physical and cognitive health improvements. Our findings indicate that dance self-efficacy played a key role both as a moderator and mediator in enhancing physical function through participation in the DAnCE program. Additionally, we observed a complex interplay between dance self-efficacy, attendance levels, program satisfaction and perceived difficulty.

Previous research has established exercise self-efficacy as a key determinant of exercise adherence in older people [25, 27], and successful exercise interventions frequently enhance exercise self-efficacy [60, 61]. In line with this research, our study found that improved dance self-efficacy was associated with higher program attendance after accounting for age and program satisfaction—factors known to influence both program attendance and self-efficacy [29]. Notably, older people who attended more sessions had greater improvements in dance self-efficacy, were more satisfied with the program and rated it as less difficult compared to low attenders.

These results support a reciprocal positive relationship between dance participation and dance self-efficacy, consistent with the broader self-efficacy literature [23, 27]. We also observed similarly modest improvements when comparing the current results to research on exercise self-efficacy as a mediator of interventions on exercise behaviour [28, 62] and program health outcomes [27]. Overall, dance self-efficacy and exercise self-efficacy appear to have comparable effects on activity participation and health outcomes.

Our findings further indicate a mechanistic link between enhanced dance self-efficacy and dance-related improvements in physical function. Importantly, our mediation analysis demonstrated that improved dance self-efficacy was more important than attendance alone for achieving physical functional benefits, suggesting that self-efficacy enhancement is a core feature of successful dance interventions. We propose that changes in dance self-efficacy may influence not only the quantity of dance participation (the dose), but also how participants engage in group dance activities [44].

This concept is particularly relevant in dance because dance activities require cognitive-motor skill acquisition and embodied knowledge development [12]. While we did not directly measure dance quality or movement characteristics, improved self-efficacy for dance may theoretically lead to stronger engagement with dancing, potentially including increased physical effort, more expansive movements, and willingness to take risks and attempt challenging dance sequences. These hypothesised changes in behaviour may even have implications for physical activities and tasks outside the dance sessions, contributing to physical functional benefits. We propose referring to this concept as the ‘Bananarama Confidence-Performance Pathway’, referencing the famous song lyric “It ain’t [just] what you do (it’s the way that you do it)”, to emphasise that self-efficacy, or dance confidence, changes performance such that it may meaningfully impact health outcomes.

Potential factors contributing to limited success of the DAnCE trial

The original DAnCE trial tested the effectiveness of folk and ballroom dance programs to reduce falls and falls-related risk factors in older adults. The trial was designed as an accessible, scalable on-site dance program delivered by local teachers, similar to programs already available in the community. This pragmatic approach resulted in a participant group with a wide range of dance capabilities. The original study authors attributed the program’s limited success to low attendance and insufficient balance challenges [43]. They further suggested that the varied abilities among participants led instructors to adapt the pace and progression to suit those with mid-range proficiency, making the program less suitable for participants with the highest and lowest capabilities.

Our moderation analysis indicates that the impact of dance program participation on physical function outcomes varied depending on baseline dance self-efficacy. Participants with lower self-efficacy at baseline experienced greater improvements in physical function with higher attendance, consistent with the link between self-efficacy, physical activity, and better health outcomes [23, 27]. In contrast, older adults with stronger dance self-efficacy at program start did not benefit from the DAnCE program, and for some, participation may have represented an opportunity cost. These participants likely had higher objective capabilities for dancing, potentially creating a ceiling effect where the program offered insufficient challenge for further improvement. The time spent in a program not tailored to their abilities may have prevented engagement in more beneficial activities suited to their skill level. Overall, the program was most effective for participants with the lowest capabilities at baseline, provided they maintained consistent attendance.

Although dance self-efficacy at baseline did not predict DAnCE program attendance, declines in self-efficacy from pre- to post-program were associated with poorer attendance, decreased satisfaction, and greater perceived difficulty. While our analysis did not explore causal factors responsible for decreased dance self-efficacy, the program may not have adequately addressed the diverse needs and abilities within this older population, potentially making it too difficult or fast-paced for some participants.

Open-level group dance classes pose compounded challenges to self-efficacy, particularly among older people with lower objective capabilities for group dancing. The demand associated with a dance task is determined by physical fitness, neuromotor learning and control capacities, attentional and memory capacities, and skill level [44]. When task demands exceed capabilities, motivation may decrease, hindering participation and skill acquisition. Additionally, feelings of embarrassment and discomfort from perceived lack of ability can become barriers to participation in complex group motor activities [34]. Programs that better support dance self-efficacy may be essential to overcoming these challenges. Tailored programs that match participants’ abilities could boost both self-efficacy and health outcomes [11, 42].

Despite the positive effects of dance self-efficacy on physical function, dance self-efficacy did not impact cognitive improvement. The near-significant effect of attendance on cognitive outcomes suggests that while there may have been some cognitive benefits of participation in the DAnCE program at higher attendance levels, these effects were modest and not mediated by dance self-efficacy mechanisms.

Several factors may explain the limited cognitive effects observed in the DAnCE study. First, older people with poorer cognitive abilities may face barriers to enhancing their dance self-efficacy, including difficulties processing instructions, memorising steps, and coordinating movements, which could limit both engagement quality and cognitive benefits. Second, ceiling effects may have limited cognitive gains, as 65% of participants were already achieving recommended exercise levels before the trial and were socially active through retirement village living, potentially reducing room for improvement in cognitive domains.

Third, meta-analytic evidence for dance effects on cognitive function found benefits only for global cognition and task switching [17], the latter of which featured in the DAnCE test battery, but no benefits for verbal learning and memory or complex attention, which were also tested and may have diluted overall effects. While meta-analyses demonstrate that dance is at least equivalent to exercise for achieving improved cognitive function [63, 64], cognitive improvement through dance may be more difficult to achieve than physical functional improvement. A scoping review of 103 dance trials for older people found that 48.5% of physical function tests demonstrated positive change in response to dancing, compared to only 16.4% of tests of cognitive and executive function [11].

Physical and cognitive demands in dance are interdependent. More complex tasks demand greater attention and conscious control before neuro-muscular co-ordination is achieved, leading to less efficient performance and reduced physical intensity [65]. Additionally, increased cognitive load can impair balance control, particularly in older adults with existing balance issues and poorer working memory [66–69]. This is relevant for falls prevention. Even with challenging balance training, open-level dance programs may not address falls risk factors due to participant variability.

Evidently, not all dance programs benefit all older adults. Open-level programs may struggle to provide appropriate tasks and accommodate a wide range of skills and abilities, particularly for participants with impaired cognition. Program design and delivery, physical intensity, dose, strategies to support adherence and engagement, and more tailored motor-cognitive learning challenges may need optimisation to maximise cognitive benefits in older adult populations [11]. Furthermore, observational studies demonstrating a protective effect of dancing on cognitive abilities and dementia [70, 71] may be influenced by reverse causation.

Strengths and limitations

This study identified several factors that may contribute to the lack of positive health outcomes in dance for health programs, while establishing the utility, validity, and predictive capacity of a dance self-efficacy measure for older adults. Furthermore, as the arts in health research field increasingly focuses on causal processes that drive health benefits of arts activities [14], identifying personal factors like self-efficacy is crucial to understanding how group dance programs impact health and who they work for.

However, several limitations must be considered. First, the six-item dance self-efficacy tool does not capture certain aspects of group participation, such as scheduling efficacy (e.g., confidence for regular dance session attendance, including when busy), which can be essential for commitment to ongoing activities [72, 73]. Second, dance self-efficacy was only measured at two time points, potentially overlooking the trajectory of dance self-efficacy throughout the program, especially considering novices’ tendencies to overestimate efficacy for unfamiliar activities [61]. These limitations may explain why baseline dance self-efficacy did not predict future program participation, contrasting with findings in research on exercise self-efficacy [74].

Third, motivation for participating in group dance was not assessed, even though it is a key driver of engagement in exercise programs. Older adults often prefer programs that are not too taxing [75], but motivations for dance can differ [37] and older people may, therefore, have different preferences for difficulty. Some may participate for opportunities for fun and social interaction, while others are more interested in learning something new and developing their dance skills [37, 76]. These varying motivations can be difficult to balance and may influence preferences for program content, pedagogical approach, and class progression, presenting challenges in designing programs that suit a wide range of participants.

Lastly, while we controlled for age and self-rated program satisfaction, other confounding variables like social support, pre-existing health conditions, or previous exercise habits, which could also affect participation and outcomes, were not included in the analysis. Future research could consider these factors to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the complex dynamics at play.

Implications for practice and research

This study, along with the concept of the ‘Bananarama Confidence-Performance Pathway’, has important implications for the design, implementation, and evaluation of dance for health programs. Below are key takeaways for both practice and research:

Program evaluation: The dance self-efficacy scale developed in this study can enhance the evaluation of dance programs by providing an ongoing, potentially standardised measure of participants’ confidence levels. Successful dance programs should ideally improve or maintain dance self-efficacy over time. Future programs could integrate this scale into their evaluation processes to track how participants’ confidence develops across the intervention, which may help explain differences in participation and outcomes.

Individual screening and monitoring: The dance self-efficacy tool offers a simple approach to participant screening and ongoing progress monitoring. It allows facilitators to identify people with low dance self-efficacy who may need additional support early on, and implement strategies—such as individual coaching [32, 77], and peer-mentoring [78] —to strengthen efficacy beliefs, encourage sustained participation, and ultimately optimise program outcomes.

Program tailoring: The dance self-efficacy scale can assist in streaming participants into progressively challenging levels [30] or tailor dance programs to suit current participants’ needs and abilities [34] by, for example, implementing in-program learning support strategies [37].

Future research opportunities include:

Dance self-efficacy scale development: The dance self-efficacy tool for older people is currently being expanded to account for scheduling efficacy and a wider range of dance tasks using a representative sample. Further refinement of the scale could provide a more comprehensive assessment of participants’ confidence and its role in program success.

Testing the ‘Bananarama Confidence-Performance Pathway’: Future research could use experimental methods, such as motion-capture technology, to examine whether improved dance self-efficacy influences movement characteristics as theorised. This testable framework could reveal the behavioural mechanisms that link confidence with enhanced health outcomes in dance programs. The concept may also apply more broadly to other skilled arts-for-health activities such as learning musical instruments, crafting, or other creative pursuits that involve skill acquisition and embodied learning.

Tailored and responsive dance programs: Experimental studies could investigate the benefits of responsive dance programs that adjust to participants’ abilities in real time. Additionally, researchers could explore how various strategies aimed at improving dance self-efficacy, such as adaptive teaching methods or peer support systems, affect program adherence, skill acquisition, and health outcomes.

Investigating multifactorial influences: Finally, more research is needed to investigate the combined influence of personal factors (such as motivation, cognitive abilities, and physical health) and program elements on dance participation and health outcomes [11]. This line of inquiry could deepen our understanding of how, and under what conditions, group dance impacts health [14], helping to design more effective interventions for different populations.

Conclusion

This study emphasises the crucial role of dance self-efficacy in dance program participation and physical function outcomes and establishes dance self-efficacy as a potential active mechanism influencing health benefits. The application of the dance self-efficacy tool for older adults can improve program evaluation and design and identify program needs and program effects. By prioritising participant engagement and focusing on the alignment between people and programs, dance for health programs may optimise health benefits. This research contributes to the field of dance for health by deepening our understanding of the personal factors involved in group dancing and providing insights for program development and evaluation.
