Emerging cardiovascular risks of micro- and nanoplastics: toxic effects and mechanistic pathways
Authors: Gang Liu, Qiuya Cai, Tingchen Qin, Xiatian Yu, Binbin Pan, Lin Ye, Lisha Jia, Xianwei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Micro- and nanoplastics (MNPs), due to their widespread distribution and chemical stability, have emerged as novel environmental contaminants threatening cardiovascular health. Evidence links MNPs to conditions such as atherosclerosis, cardiac fibrosis, myocardial infarction, and other cardiovascular diseases (CVDs). Recently, MNPs have been found within atherosclerotic plaques and in the myocardial tissues of surgery patients, highlighting their close association with cardiovascular pathology. Investigations have demonstrated that MNPs can enter the human body through oral ingestion, inhalation, dermal contact, and medical procedures, and subsequently accumulate in the blood, blood vessels, and cardiac tissues. Once deposited, these particles induce oxidative and endoplasmic reticulum stress, disrupt mitochondrial function, and activate inflammatory signaling pathways, ultimately triggering cell death. These processes contribute to endothelial dysfunction, cardiac damage and fibrosis, vascular smooth muscle cell phenotypic switching, and macrophage foam cell formation, thereby likely contributing to the onset and progression of CVDs. Despite these findings, the potential cardiovascular risks of MNPs and the underlying mechanisms remain largely unexplored. This review summarizes the basic properties of MNPs, their biodistribution within the cardiovascular system, toxic effects, and underlying molecular mechanisms, providing a foundation for future risk assessment and the development of preventive strategies.

Introduction

The rapid surge in global plastic use over recent decades has led to a widespread environmental crisis called “white pollution”, referring to the persistent and long-term harm caused by plastic waste. Once released into natural ecosystems, plastic waste undergoes progressive fragmentation under the influence of wave action, ocean currents, ultraviolet radiation, environmental weathering, air oxidation, and microbial degradation [1–3]. These processes generate particles of varying shapes and sizes, which are subsequently dispersed across the planet by oceanic and riverine currents, wind, precipitation, and even biotic transport [4, 5]. On the basis of their size, they are categorized into microplastics (MPs, < 5 mm) and nanoplastics (NPs, < 1 μm), collectively referred to as micro- and nanoplastics (MNPs) [6].

MNPs are present in atmospheric dust, surface and groundwater, soils, and numerous consumer products, including food, drinking water, beverages, and personal care items [7, 8]. When introduced into the body via ingestion, inhalation, dermal absorption, or medical interventions, MNPs can traverse biological barriers and accumulate in organs such as the brain, kidneys, liver, and heart, with a tendency to accumulate in the cardiovascular system [9–13]. Mounting experimental and clinical research highlights the cardiovascular system as a principal target of MNPs’ toxicity [6, 14]. Once translocated into vascular and cardiac tissues, MNPs induce oxidative and endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, disrupt mitochondrial homeostasis, stimulate inflammatory signaling, and trigger various forms of cell death [15–19]. This results in myocardial injury and fibrotic remodeling, impaired endothelial function, macrophage foam cell formation, and phenotypic switching in vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) [20–24]. These pathological changes could promote the development and progression of cardiovascular diseases (CVDs), including atherosclerosis, cardiac fibrosis, and ischemic heart disease [25–28]. Clinical data reveal that individuals with MNPs deposits in carotid plaques have a markedly increased risk of myocardial infarction, stroke, and mortality [26, 29, 30], providing an association between MNPs and adverse cardiovascular events.

Given the increasing evidence linking MNPs to cardiovascular injury, systematically elucidating their properties, exposure routes, biodistribution, and underlying mechanisms is therefore essential. However, a complete understanding of MNPs’ cardiotoxicity remains limited by scarce epidemiological data, heterogeneous detection methods, and diverse particle types. This review compiles current knowledge on how MNPs access the cardiovascular system, where they accumulate and exert toxic effects, and which molecular pathways drive cardiovascular dysfunction, aiming to guide the design of effective prevention and treatment strategies.

Basic characteristics and exposure routes of MNPs

MNPs are commonly classified according to their polymer composition and surface chemistry, including, but not limited to, polystyrene (PS), polypropylene (PP), polyethylene terephthalate (PET), polyethylene (PE), polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA), polyvinyl chloride (PVC), polyurethane (PU) [31]. Nevertheless, many MNPs types likely remain undetected due to current methodological limitations. The above-listed polymers are among the most widely used plastics in everyday life and are therefore prevalent in animal species and humans. Indeed, most food, beverage, and cosmetic containers, as well as water distribution pipes, are manufactured from these materials.

The physical properties of MNPs, including size, shape, and surface charge, critically influence biological interactions and toxicological effects. Smaller particles generally present greater toxicity risks because they can more readily cross epithelial barriers, enter circulation, and exhibit higher surface reactivity with cells and tissues [32]. In the environment, MNPs have diverse morphologies, such as spheres, fibers, and irregular fragments, which are shaped by weathering, environmental residence time, and surface degradation processes [33]. Surface charge is another important determinant of bioaccumulation and toxicity. In experimental research, commercial standard particles are often modified to bear amino groups (NH₂, positively charged) or carboxyl groups (COOH, negatively charged) to mimic environmentally relevant conditions [24]. However, these laboratory-synthesized particles differ from those in natural environments, which limits how well laboratory findings can be generalized to real-world settings. Differences in weathering, exposure to environmental conditions, and complex chemical interactions present in natural settings may significantly alter the retention time and impact of MNPs in vivo. Therefore, future studies should focus on developing MNPs that more closely replicate the characteristics of environmental plastics. This would improve the relevance and accuracy of experimental findings while avoiding the limitations of current models.

Given their basic properties and widespread presence in human environments, MNPs enter the human body via four main routes: oral ingestion (from contaminated food and beverages), inhalation (from airborne particles indoors and outdoors), dermal contact (through skin exposure to products containing MNPs), and medical procedures (such as infusion bag and face masks) (Fig. 1).

Oral ingestion

The omnipresence of MNPs in daily food has raised growing concerns regarding their potential health impacts. MNPs have been identified in drinking water, various beverages, and numerous processed food products, including teabags, milk, and soft drinks [34, 35]. Since water constitutes the primary component of beverages, the contamination of water sources with MNPs is a key contributor to overall beverage contamination [36, 37].

Another main route of MNPs exposure is through the food chain [38, 39]. MPs have been found in 93% of marine species, including commonly consumed seafood such as fish, mollusks, and crustaceans [40, 41]. These organisms ingest MNPs, which then bioaccumulate and biomagnify through the food web, ultimately resulting in human dietary intake via seafood and meat consumption. Terrestrial crops also absorb MNPs from soil and the environment and carry them into the human food supply [42, 43]. Products such as rice and meat bought from markets can also be contaminated by MNPs during production, packaging, and transport [44, 45].

After ingestion, MNPs can cross the gastrointestinal barrier, with smaller particles more readily entering systemic circulation via the portal vein [46]. Toxicity increases as particle size decreases, because small particles are more readily taken up by gastric epithelial cells, leading to oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction [47]. As apex consumers, humans are highly susceptible to the biomagnification of MNPs, and the cumulative toxic effects driven by metabolic disturbance and gut microbiota imbalance may further increase the risk of CVDs.

Inhalation

MNPs from both indoor and outdoor air represent another major pathway of human exposure. These airborne particles originate from numerous human activities, including the emission of vehicle exhaust, construction-related dust, and secondary emissions produced through waste incineration [48]. Studies have shown that the concentration of MPs in outdoor air varies by region, with higher levels detected in urban environments than in rural areas, and in northern Chinese cities than in southern Chinese cities [49, 50].

Indoor air has also been shown to contain MPs, which account for approximately 4% of indoor particulate matter [50, 51]. Common indoor sources include plastic products, toys, furniture, textiles, clothing, and personal care items [52, 53]. Additionally, outdoor air carrying MPs can infiltrate indoor spaces through open windows, doors, or human movement. Given that individuals typically spend the majority of their time indoors, exposure to indoor MNPs may pose a significant health concern.

MNPs can deposit in the alveolar regions of the lungs, cross the air-blood barrier, enter the circulation, and then be distributed to various organs [54]. Autopsy analyses have revealed the presence of MPs with a size less than 5.5 μm in 65% of human lung tissue samples [55]. While the detection of MNPs in human lung tissue confirms exposure and biodistribution, it does not, by itself, constitute direct evidence of consequent toxicity at these levels in humans. Experimental animal studies have shown that the inhalation of MPs induces pulmonary inflammation, characterized by leukocyte infiltration and elevated serum IgG1, while also impairing respiratory function in mice [56]. One study demonstrated that airway exposure to MNPs can impair myocardial structure and function in a dose-dependent manner, potentially through mitochondrial injury within cardiomyocytes [57, 58]. Although these findings derive from murine models, they suggest potential toxicological pathways that may be relevant under similar exposure conditions or in susceptible human populations.

Dermal contact

Dermal exposure to MNPs occurs primarily through the use of personal care products and the deposition of environmental dust on the skin [59, 60]. The extent to which MNPs penetrate the skin barrier is influenced by their physicochemical characteristics. NPs smaller than 100 nm, particularly those with cationic surface charges, exhibit greater skin permeability, whereas larger particles may enter through hair follicles, sweat glands, or damaged skin [60, 61]. Additionally, plastic components in prosthetic devices can degrade over time due to mechanical wear, resulting in the release of MNPs and direct contact with the skin [62].

Dermal exposure models have demonstrated that MPs from cosmetics and household dust possess measurable bioaccessibility, suggesting the possibility of systemic absorption following skin contact [63]. Skin exposure to NPs has been shown to induce cytotoxic effects in keratinocytes, including oxidative stress, reduced cell proliferation, and premature cellular aging [64, 65]. While dermal absorption is generally considered a less significant exposure route compared to ingestion and inhalation, research confirms that MNPs can penetrate the dermis and subsequently enter the circulatory system [61].

Medical procedures

Medical procedures have recently emerged as an underappreciated pathway of human exposure to MNPs, primarily through two principal routes: direct contact release and long-term degradation of plastic materials. Plastic-based medical devices, such as catheters, guidewires, and infusion sets, generate MPs through mechanical friction, temperature fluctuations, or chemical interactions with medications [66, 67]. Clinical evidence supports this route of exposure: for instance, patients undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) showed a marked postoperative increase in circulating MPs (200–300 μm), implicating procedural abrasion as a source of particle release [66]. Likewise, intravenous infusion equipment has been shown to release MNPs, with infusion fluids containing particles as small as 20 nm [68].

Implanted medical devices, such as artificial joints and surgical sutures, also degrade over time due to mechanical stress, oxidative conditions, or enzymatic activity within the body, gradually releasing MNPs [69, 70]. These particles can cause localized inflammation and may migrate systemically through the lymphatic system. In addition, respiratory equipment, including nebulizers and ventilators, may emit inhalable MPs when plastic components degrade due to airflow erosion or repeated high-temperature sterilization [71]. The COVID-19 pandemic has further exacerbated this issue through the widespread use of face masks and other respiratory protective equipment [72]. The repeated use of disposable masks has emerged as a notable source of airborne MPs, which are capable of releasing significant quantities of particles directly into the breathing zone [73].

The health risks associated with MNPs from medical sources depend on their size, chemical makeup, and potential for bioaccumulation. These particles can provoke oxidative stress, trigger inflammatory responses, and act as carriers for hazardous substances, thereby posing significant health threats. These findings underscore the urgent need to evaluate and improve the biocompatibility and safety of plastic-based medical devices.

Distribution and accumulation of MNPs in the cardiovascular system

MNPs have been detected across nearly all major organs of the human body, indicating their ability to evade immune cells and cross biological barriers to enter the circulation, thereby having a pronounced propensity to accumulate in the cardiovascular system (Fig. 2). Evidence from multiple analytical techniques, including micro-Fourier transform interferometer (µFTIR), pyrolysis gas chromatography/mass spectrometry (Py‑GC/MS), and Raman spectroscopy has demonstrated the infiltration of MNPs into arterial walls and their accumulation within atherosclerotic plaques [12, 74]. Each of these techniques has its advantages and limitations (Table 1), and their combined application can largely compensate for individual methodological shortcomings, thereby enhancing detection sensitivity and spatial resolution. However, they remain limited in their ability to comprehensively characterize extremely small nanoparticles.

Blood

MNPs were first detected and quantified in whole human blood via Py-GC/MS [90]. This analysis identified PET, PE, PS, and PMMA as the most abundant polymers, with mean concentrations of 1.6 µg/mL and particle sizes ranging from 700 nm to 500 μm [90]. However, subsequent research has questioned the suitability of Py-GC/MS for detecting PE and PVC in blood, citing interference and nonspecific pyrolysis byproducts as limitations of this method [91]. In a separate study involving 20 healthy individuals, MPs were detected in 90% of the participants via µFTIR spectroscopy, exhibiting up to 24 different polymer types, predominantly PE, ethylene propylene diene (EPDM), and ethylene-vinyl-acetate/alcohol, at concentrations between 1.84 and 4.65 µg/mL [92]. Similarly, Raman spectroscopy of patient blood samples confirmed the presence of PE, PP, and polyethylene-co-polypropylene, while mass spectrometry identified associated plasticizers such as phthalates [93]. These findings indicate that diverse MNPs can enter the circulation and may perturb vascular and haematological functions. Furthermore, circulating NPs have been shown to adhere to erythrocyte surfaces, with amino-modified nanoparticles causing hemolysis [94]. Experimental evidence also indicates that MNPs exert cytotoxic and genotoxic effects on human peripheral blood lymphocytes, stimulate immune cell activation, and promote platelet aggregation [95–98]. Importantly, elevated levels of circulating MPs have been observed in patients with extracranial artery stenosis, suggesting an association between MPs concentrations and the severity of arterial narrowing [30].

Blood vessels

The µFTIR imaging has detected MPs in human saphenous vein samples, providing concrete evidence of vascular infiltration [74]. Another study demonstrated the presence of MPs in all 17 human vascular samples analyzed, including coronary and carotid arteries with atherosclerotic plaques, as well as plaque-free aortic tissue. The four most abundant polymers identified were PET, polyamide (PA), PVC, and PE [12]. Quantification by Py-GC/MS revealed significantly higher concentrations of MNPs in atherosclerotic plaques of femoral, coronary, and carotid arteries compared to non-plaque arterial tissue [12, 99], suggesting a link between the accumulation of MNPs and atherosclerosis. Similarly, MNPs were detected in carotid endarterectomy specimens from 58.4% of patients. A 3-year follow-up further revealed that patients with plaques containing MNPs had a 4.53-fold increased risk of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) [29]. Additionally, analysis of thrombi from cardiovascular surgery patients showed MPs in 60% of samples, with detection rates increasing to 80% in thrombi from individuals with ischemic stroke, myocardial infarction, and deep vein thrombosis, demonstrating a clear correlation with disease severity [100]. Although these clinical studies provide important epidemiological insights, several methodological limitations should be acknowledged. In particular, unavoidable laboratory contamination during tissue collection and processing may influence quantitative measurements, potentially resulting in an overestimation of MNPs concentrations. These limitations underscore the need for additional validation studies and the development of more rigorous and standardized analytical protocols in future human biomonitoring investigations.

Heart

One study provided direct evidence that MPs exist in human cardiac tissues using laser direct infrared (LDIR) chemical imaging and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) [13]. Tissue samples were collected from 15 patients undergoing cardiac surgery, including the pericardium, epicardial and pericardial adipose tissues, myocardium, and left atrial appendages. MPs were detected in all five tissues, with particle sizes ranging from 20 to 469 μm. PET and PU emerged as the most dominant polymers, together comprising approximately 90% of the total MPs content. PE, although accounting for only 1% of the total MPs, was consistently found across all tissue types. The detection of PMMA in the left atrial appendages, epicardial adipose tissues, and pericardial adipose tissues supports that MNPs are endogenous deposits rather than surgical contaminants [13]. Upon accumulation in cardiac tissue, MNPs impair cardiac structure and function, with studies demonstrating that they promote cardiomyocyte apoptosis and contribute to the development of cardiac fibrosis [22, 101, 102]. These findings highlight the urgent need to explore the pathways by which MNPs infiltrate cardiac tissue and to assess their potential long-term impact on heart structure and function.

Toxic effects of MNPs on the cardiovascular system

Environmental pollutants are established contributors to CVDs pathogenesis [103, 104]. Long-term exposure to MNPs has deleterious effects on cardiomyocytes, VSMCs, endothelial cells, and immune cells. In vitro and in vivo studies have shown that MNPs exposure can induce myocardial injury, fibrotic remodeling, endothelial dysfunction, phenotypic switching of VSMCs, and macrophage foam cell formation (Fig. 3) [17, 19, 20, 22, 23]. These interconnected pathological changes, driven by oxidative stress, inflammatory signaling, and cell death pathways, highlight the complex role of MNPs in CVDs onset and progression.

Myocardial injury

Myocardial injury refers to structural and functional damage to the myocardium, including cardiomyocyte death, inflammatory infiltration, and impaired cardiac function [105]. Clinically, myocardial injury is indicated by elevated serum biomarkers such as cardiac troponins (cTnI and cTnT) and creatine kinase-MB (CK-MB), and manifests as arrhythmias, reduced ejection fraction, and structural remodeling [106].

Recent studies have demonstrated that MNPs exert deleterious effects on cardiac structure and function. Mice exposed to PS-NPs (100 nm; 30, 60, and 100 mg/L) for 42 days exhibited multiple forms of myocardial damage, including reduced heart rate, ventricular dilation, thinning of the myocardial wall, decreased ejection fraction and fractional shortening, as well as reduced blood pressure [102]. Zebrafish exposed to PE-MPs (100 nm; 1 and 10 mg/L) showed dose-dependent bradycardia and aggravated myocardial damage [107]. Histology revealed disorganized myocardium, interstitial widening, vascular congestion, apoptosis and inflammatory cell infiltration [102, 108]. Transmission electron microscopy has demonstrated PS-NPs accumulating in sarcoplasmic reticulum and mitochondria with cristae disruption and autophagosome buildup [102, 108, 109]. These structural injuries are accompanied by elevated serum levels of CK-MB and cTnI [22].

Similarly, human pluripotent stem-cell cardiac organoids exposed to PS-MPs (956 nm; 0.025, 0.25, and 2.5 µg/mL) developed septal thickening and fibrosis with upregulation of MYH7B, ANP, BNP and COL1A1 [110]. In H9C2 cells, 100 nm PS-NPs activated TNF-α/NF-κB and P38/MAPK pathways, promoting apoptosis and hypertrophy [102]. The underlying mechanisms of MNPs-induced myocardial injury include pyroptosis, inflammation, disrupted mitochondrial dynamics, and energy metabolic dysfunction [15, 57, 111].

Myocardial fibrosis

Myocardial fibrosis is defined as the pathological and excessive deposition of extracellular matrix (ECM) components like collagen I/III and fibronectin, resulting in increased myocardial stiffness, electrical conduction abnormalities, and progressive ventricular dysfunction [112, 113]. Recent studies have confirmed that MNPs exposure promoted this process. In rats, exposure to 0.5 μm PS-MPs (5 and 50 mg/L) for 90 days induced myocardial fibrosis with increased fibronectin and collagen I/III expression [22]. Mice exposed to PE-NPs (100 nm; 30, 60, and 100 mg/kg) for 28 days showed exacerbated myocardial fibrosis through increased expression of collagen I, α-SMA, and TGF-β [114]. In addition, mice exposed to PET-NPs (50 µg/mL in drinking water) for 90 days had worsened fibrosis after myocardial infarction [16]. The fibrotic response involves profibrotic mediators from apoptotic cardiomyocytes, stimulating fibroblast conversion and activating the TGF-β1/Smad2/3 pathway, which upregulates fibrotic markers [109, 115, 116].

MNPs have also been detected in human placental tissue, and rodent studies have confirmed their ability to cross the placental barrier, eliciting detrimental effects in developing offspring [117]. One study demonstrated that exposure to PS-NPs during pregnancy induces cardiac fibrosis in adult mouse offspring, with a more pronounced effect in female offspring compared to male offspring. This sex-specific susceptibility may be linked to oxidative stress and altered expression of estrogen receptor-related genes in the offspring’s heart [118].

Endothelial dysfunction

The endothelium regulates vascular homeostasis, and its dysfunction is a key event in CVDs [119–121]. Reactive oxygen species (ROS), while essential as intracellular second messengers, become deleterious when their production exceeds the capacity of antioxidant defenses, representing a principal mechanism underlying endothelial dysfunction [122].

PS-MPs exacerbated oxidative stress in human vascular endothelial cells (EA.hy926) by downregulating the antioxidant enzyme heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1), disrupting mitochondrial potential, reducing tight junction protein ZO-1, and promoting apoptosis [123]. Similar observations have been reported in retinal microvascular endothelial cells [124, 125]. PS-MPs (3.9 ± 0.9 μm) also modulated Rho-associated protein kinase-1 (ROCK1) expression, suppressing NF-κB nuclear translocation and NLRP3 inflammasome activation [123]. In contrast, other studies have reported that PS-NPs (80 nm and 500 nm) stimulate inflammatory responses in human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) through activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome pathway [17, 126]. These discrepancies may be attributed to differences in cell type, particle size, or exposure concentrations. In vivo, both unmodified PS-NPs (80 nm; 5, 10, and 20 mg/kg) and their amino (-NH₂) or carboxyl (-COOH) functionalized analogs (80 nm; 0.05, 0.5, and 5 mg/kg) induced endothelial injury and vascular inflammation in murine models [24].

Moreover, PS-NPs (22.41 ± 0.63 nm; 10 µg/mL) have been shown to induce premature senescence in endothelial cells, accompanied by upregulation of sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) [127]. The SGLT2 inhibitor empagliflozin reversed senescence and restored endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) function via suppression of NADPH oxidase subunits (NOX2 and p22phox) [127]. Consistent with these findings, another study confirmed that PS-NPs (25 nm; 0.1, 1, and 10 µg/mL) promote endothelial senescence in porcine coronary arteries via increased β-galactosidase activity, ROS accumulation, and elevated expression of senescence-associated proteins p53, p21, and p16 [128].

Phenotypic switching of VSMCs

VSMCs normally maintain a contractile phenotype characterized by high expression of contractile proteins, such as α-smooth muscle actin (α‐SMA), smooth muscle myosin heavy chain (SM-MHC), and smooth muscle 22α (SM22α) [129]. In response to pathological factors, VSMCs undergo phenotypic switching toward a synthetic phenotype, characterized by downregulation of these contractile markers. The synthetic phenotype is associated with increased proliferation, migration, and inflammatory signaling and is central to atherogenesis, vascular calcification, and aneurysm formation [130].

Accumulating evidence implicates MNPs in driving VSMCs phenotypic modulation and consequent vascular injury [131–133]. A recent study demonstrated that both acute and chronic exposure to PS-NPs (100 nm; 10, 50, 100 µg/mL) induce vascular fibrosis and apoptosis, accompanied by increased expression of tiRNA-Glu-CTC. Overexpression of tiRNA-Glu-CTC in VSMCs promoted a shift from the contractile to the synthetic phenotype, characterized by downregulation of the contractile markers α-SMA, SM22α, and SMMHC and upregulation of the synthetic markers collagen I and vimentin, an effect regulated through its target Cacna1f [20]. PS-MPs and PE-MPs (564 μm and 632 μm) also promoted an osteogenic phenotype in VSMCs, increasing Runx2 and galectin-3 expression, which fosters inflammation and calcification [134–136].

Phenotypic switching is tightly linked to the migration of VSMCs. Quiescent contractile VSMCs exhibit low proliferative and migratory capacity, whereas the synthetic phenotype acquires enhanced migratory and proliferative properties [137]. Recent evidence identified the kinesin family member KIF15 as a key regulator in NPs-induced VSMCs migration. Experimental studies have demonstrated upregulation of KIF15 in mouse aortic VSMCs exposed to 50 nm PS-NPs. PS-NPs markedly enhanced the migratory capacity of these cells, whereas KIF15 knockdown attenuated this effect, implicating KIF15 as a critical target through which PS-NPs facilitate VSMCs migration [21].

Macrophage foam cell formation

Macrophage foam cell formation, driven by an imbalance in cholesterol uptake and efflux, is a hallmark of early atherogenesis [138]. It is promoted by dyslipidemia, oxidative stress, and inflammation, with emerging evidence underscoring the critical role of environmental pollutant exposure [139].

A growing body of research has implicated MNPs as novel accelerators of foam cell formation [23, 25, 140]. In murine atherosclerosis models, PS-MPs (0.5 μm; 1 µg/mL) treatment for 140 days promoted plaque development and dysregulated lipoprotein metabolism [141]. Likewise, PS-NPs (100 nm; 5 and 10 mg/kg for 90 days) enlarged aortic lesions, increased ROS and malondialdehyde (MDA) levels, and diminished catalase activity [25]. In vitro, co-treatment of macrophages with ox-LDL and PS-NPs (100 nm; 50, 100, 150 and 200 µg/mL) increased lipid droplet accumulation by enhancing LOX-1/CD36-mediated cholesterol uptake and suppressing ABCA1/ABCG1-mediated cholesterol efflux [25, 140]. Similarly, PET-MPs (1 μm; 10 and 50 µg/mL) upregulated SR-A1 and CD36 mRNA in THP-1 macrophages [142].

MARCO, another scavenger receptor, has been shown to mediate MNPs-induced foam cell formation. PS-NPs (50 nm) increased MARCO expression in aortic macrophages, promoting phagocytosis and lipid accumulation, which is related to increased serum levels of long-chain acyl carnitines [23]. Disruption of lysosomal function by PS-NPs (200 nm; 100 µg/mL) further contributes to foam cell formation, although precise molecular details remain to be fully clarified [143]. Collectively, these findings underscore the multifaceted role of MNPs in promoting macrophage foam cell formation through oxidative stress, scavenger receptor modulation, and impaired lipid homeostasis.

Molecular mechanisms underlying MNPs-induced cardiovascular toxicity

MNPs trigger cardiovascular toxicity through interconnected mechanistic pathways. Rather than acting in isolation, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, ER stress, inflammation, and cell death form an integrated network in which each component amplifies the others (Fig. 4). This section synthesizes evidence for these convergent mechanisms.

Oxidative stress

Oxidative stress is a central hub in MNPs-induced cardiovascular injury, driving endothelial dysfunction, myocardial damage, foam cell formation, and cell death [111, 131, 144]. It arises when ROS production, mainly generated by NOXs and impaired mitochondrial electron transport, exceeds the cellular antioxidant capacity [145].

MNPs exposure elevates intracellular ROS production and reduces antioxidant enzyme activities in endothelial cells, VSMCs, cardiomyocytes, and macrophages [111, 131, 144]. In vivo studies have revealed that both PS-MPs and PS-NPs induced oxidative stress through increased MDA levels and NOXs expression and reduced activities of key antioxidant enzymes such as GPx, SOD, and catalase [102, 146–148]. This finding aligns with additional research showing that PS, PS-COOH, and PS-NH₂ induce dose-dependent ROS production in RAW 264.7 macrophages [149], as well as in aortic endothelial cells and VSMCs [131, 144]. In cardiomyocytes, PP-MPs and PET-MPs reduced mitochondrial membrane potential, increased ROS generation, and induced apoptosis [101, 111]. Thus, oxidative imbalance should be viewed as a convergent driver that links MNPs-triggered NOXs activation and mitochondrial dysfunction, and primes downstream inflammation and cell death.

Mitochondrial dysfunction

Mitochondria are responsible for maintaining cellular energy homeostasis, functioning primarily to generate ATP through oxidative phosphorylation in the electron transport chain [150]. Dysregulation of mitochondrial metabolism not only impairs energy production but also promotes apoptosis, aberrant calcium signaling, and excessive ROS generation, which are closely associated with cardiovascular pathologies, including myocardial injury, myocardial dysplasia, and cardiac aging [151, 152].

Experimental evidence across species and models consistently supports mitochondrial dysfunction as a key mechanism of MNPs-induced cardiotoxicity. In mice, PS-MPs impaired mitochondrial energy metabolism by inhibiting the AMPK-PGC-1α pathway, which led to downregulation of the fusion- and fission-related genes TFAM, OPA1, MFN1, MFN2 and upregulation of the energy metabolism-related enzymes HK2, PKM2, PDHX, LDH [15]. In zebrafish, PS-NPs exposure suppressed mitochondrial activity and disrupted Notch and Wnt signaling, linking mitochondrial dysfunction to impaired cardiac development [153, 154]. In vitro, PS-MPs and PS-NPs induce mitochondrial fragmentation, loss of membrane potential, ATP depletion, and calcium overload, triggering intrinsic apoptosis [132]. Excessive mitochondrial Ca²⁺ drives ROS overproduction and mtDNA release via VDAC, activating cGAS-STING-dependent senescence [155].

Cardiac models, including HL-1 cardiomyocytes, human iPSC-derived cardiomyocytes, and organoids, reveal high mitochondrial vulnerability, with PS-NPs and PVC-NPs exposure impairing autophagic flux and opening mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP) [151, 152, 156].

ER stress

The ER maintains protein folding, lipid biosynthesis, and intracellular calcium homeostasis. Perturbations in these processes can trigger ER stress [157]. This stress is sensed by PERK, IRE1α and ATF6, which restore proteostasis by attenuating global translation, upregulating chaperones expression, and promoting ER-associated degradation. However, prolonged ER stress activates pro-apoptotic and pro-inflammatory cascades, leading to tissue injury [157]. In the cardiovascular system, excessive ER stress drives endothelial dysfunction, cardiomyocyte death, vascular inflammation, and pathological remodeling [158].

MNPs can provoke ER stress both indirectly via ROS generation that perturbs ER redox balance and protein folding [159], and directly by being internalized and causing ER swelling and physical disruption [160]. Experimental studies have demonstrated that PS-MPs exposure induces intracellular Ca²⁺ overload and the upregulation of the canonical ER stress markers GRP78, CHOP, eIF2α, and ATF4 [161]. Proteomic analyses confirmed enrichment of ER and autophagy pathways, and pharmacological inhibition of ER stress alleviated autophagic dysfunction and cellular injury, supporting a causal role for ER perturbation in cardiomyocyte toxicity [28, 161].

Beyond mature cardiomyocytes, ER stress also contributes to MPs-induced developmental cardiotoxicity. In developmental models, maternal PS-MPs and PS-NPs exposure induced fetal myocardial ER stress and suppressed cardiac transcriptional regulators TnnT2, Nkx2-5, Gata4, TBX5, and ACTN2, effects reversible by ER stress inhibitor 4-PBA [162, 163].

Inflammatory response

MNPs can disrupt immune homeostasis by eliciting pro-inflammatory cytokine production from both circulating immune cells and local cardiovascular cells [15, 164, 165]. Omics analyses identified immune, inflammatory, and cardiovascular-related pathways as major targets following MNPs exposure, underscoring a direct link between MNPs-induced inflammation and CVDs [166].

Endothelial cells, VSMCs, and macrophages serve as key mediators of this response. Across multiple studies, PS-MPs, PET-NPs, and environmentally aged mixed MPs (PS, HDPE, and LDPE) consistently upregulated adhesion molecules ICAM-1 and VCAM-1 and pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α in these cell types, promoting macrophage adhesion, vascular inflammation, and systemic effects that vary with particle size [131, 167–170].

MNPs also directly provoke transcriptional reprogramming in immune cells [96]. Neutrophils internalized PS-MPs and formed neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) that amplified inflammatory signaling [97]. In human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs), PVC-MPs and acrylonitrile-butadiene-styrene (ABS)-MPs suppressed histamine release while increasing IL-6 and TNF-α production [171]. The inflammatory response is polymer- and morphology- dependent, with NH2-modified MNPs activating the NLRP3 inflammasome to drive IL-1β and IL-8 release, while PET, polyacrylonitrile, and nylon particles trigger IL-8 independently of NLRP3 [172, 173]. Compared to spherical particles, irregular PVC-NPs robustly amplified IL-6, IL-10, and TNF-α release from human monocytes and dendritic cells [174]. Collectively, internalized MNPs act as danger signals to activate vascular and immune cells through NF-κB signaling, inflammasome activation, leukocyte recruitment, and cytokine release.

Cell death

MNPs induce multiple programmed and non-programmed cell death pathways following internalization. Organellar damage, including lysosomal destabilization and mitochondrial dysfunction, triggers ROS production and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) release, activating death signaling cascades that culminate in apoptosis, pyroptosis, or ferroptosis [19, 108, 148, 175]. These forms of cell death drive inflammation, homeostatic imbalance, and pathological tissue remodeling, representing the final common pathway of MNPs-induced cardiovascular toxicity.

Apoptosis, a non-inflammatory programmed death characterized by caspase activation, is widely triggered by MNPs in cardiovascular cells [22, 97, 111, 176]. PS-NPs and PP-MPs elevated intracellular ROS and Ca2+ levels, depolarized the mitochondrial membrane, and increased the ratio of pro-apoptotic Bax to anti-apoptotic Bcl-2, leading to cytochrome c release and caspase 9/3 activation [20, 108, 111]. This pathway is mediated by p38/MAPK and MAPK/Nrf2 signaling in cardiomyocytes and by AKT/ ERK1/2 activity suppression in endothelial cells [22, 111, 114, 124, 125]. Particle size influences severity, with smaller NPs provoking stronger apoptotic responses via TLR4 and NOX2 pathways [148, 176]. MNPs-induced mitochondrial dysfunction is key to triggering apoptotic death in cardiovascular cells.

Pyroptosis is an inflammatory programmed death driven by NLRP3 inflammasome activation and caspase-1-dependent gasdermin D (GSDMD) cleavage, resulting in membrane pore formation and IL-1β/IL-18 release [177]. Both PS-MPs and PS-NPs activated this cascade in rodent, avian, and human cardiovascular cells, with oxidative stress-mediated NF-κB and NLRP3 activation [15, 17, 108, 146]. Autophagy disruption via DKK3 suppression exacerbated pyroptosis, while DKK3 overexpression restored autophagy and mitigated pyroptosis [114]. These findings highlight pyroptosis as a critical inflammatory mechanism in MNPs-induced cardiovascular injury.

Ferroptosis is an iron-dependent form of programmed death characterized by lipid peroxidation and impaired redox balance. Exposure to PS-NPs and PS-MPs promoted ferroptosis in cardiomyocytes and endothelial cells by upregulating pro-ferroptotic ACSL4 and downregulating antioxidant enzyme GPX4, leading to ROS accumulation, mitochondrial membrane depolarization, and reduced cell viability [19, 178, 179]. The common mechanism involves MNPs-induced ROS generation, iron accumulation, and impaired redox balance, which together drive iron-catalyzed lipid peroxidation. These death pathways share upstream triggers including mitochondrial dysfunction, ER stress, and redox imbalance, and often co-occur to amplify tissue injury and inflammation.

In summary, several core mechanisms are likely polymer-agnostic, reflecting cellular stress responses to particulate exposure irrespective of chemical composition. These include excessive ROS generation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and apoptosis. In contrast, other mechanisms appear potentially polymer specific, influenced by distinct chemical compositions, additive content, and functional groups. For instance, PVC particles may exert toxicity largely through leached plasticizers such as DEHP [180], while NH2-modified particles preferentially activate the NLRP3 inflammasome via enhanced lysosomal disruption [173]. Similarly, PET and nylon particles can trigger IL-8 production independently of NLRP3, suggesting inflammasome-independent pro-inflammatory pathways unique to certain polymers [172]. These distinctions underscore the need for systematic comparative studies using environmentally relevant polymer types to accurately assess cardiovascular risk.

Discussion

Despite growing evidence of MNPs-induced cardiovascular toxicity, several challenges remain unresolved. Current techniques, including µFTIR, Raman, and Py-GC/MS, vary considerably in detection thresholds, size resolution, and polymer specificity, contributing to discrepancies in reported MNPs content in human tissues [181]. This methodological heterogeneity complicates cross-study comparisons and undermines accurate exposure assessment. Given the impracticality of large-scale quantification of MNPs in human tissues, alternative approaches such as measuring polymer specific metabolites or plastic derived additives in blood or urine may offer feasible exposure estimates.

Another major challenge is the predominant use of commercial PS spheres as model particles, despite PS not consistently representing the dominant polymer detected in human tissues [182]. This reliance may bias toxicological data, as distinct polymer physicochemical properties influence biological behavior and toxicity. Furthermore, translational gaps persist between artificial experimental conditions with supraphysiological doses and realistic human exposure. Future studies should prioritize environmentally relevant MNPs, conduct long-term exposures, and employ physiologically relevant levels reflecting human tissue concentrations. Given the inverse correlation between particle size and toxicity, systematic investigation of how size influences uptake, tissue penetration, and cardiovascular mechanisms is warranted.

MNPs rarely occur in isolation but serve as carriers for plasticizers, heavy metals, and endocrine-disrupting chemicals, potentially amplifying toxicity through synergistic interactions [183, 184]. Advancing standardized detection technologies and fostering interdisciplinary collaboration will be critical to understanding and mitigating these toxicological effects. Moreover, systematic investigation of the health consequences of combined exposure to MNPs and co-existing environmental toxicants is imperative, given their potential to exacerbate cardiovascular injury.

Current evidence linking MNPs to CVDs predominantly derives from detection in tissues from patients with advanced disease, which cannot establish causality. Such data cannot determine whether MNPs accumulation precedes and contributes to disease development or occurs as a consequence of pathological progression, nor can they fully account for confounding factors including lifestyle and environmental exposures. Establishing causality requires large-scale prospective cohort studies that enroll initially healthy individuals, quantitatively assess baseline MNPs exposure, and longitudinally track incident CVDs outcomes. Although age and sex are commonly considered, other critical confounders, including dietary habits, occupational exposures, and co-pollutant burdens, are frequently overlooked, introducing substantial residual bias. Thus, future studies should adopt more comprehensive designs and advanced analytical approaches to strengthen causal inference.

Based on the emerging association between MNPs and CVDs, a precautionary public health framework can be proposed across three domains. First, risk stratification should prioritize populations with higher exposure probability, such as plastic industry workers and those with increased biological susceptibility. Second, prevention strategies should combine public guidance to reduce personal exposure, such as avoiding heated plastics and using water filters, with systemic action to reduce plastic pollution at the source. These include promoting biodegradable materials, implementing policies to reduce single-use plastics, and strengthening waste management infrastructures. Third, regulatory policy should proactively establish MNPs safety standards in drinking water and food, mandate rigorous testing for polymers in medical and food-contact materials, and incentivize safer alternatives. These measures must be dynamically informed by emerging prospective cohort data and continuously refined as causal evidence evolves.

Conclusion and perspective

MNPs represent an emerging environmental threat to cardiovascular health, entering the human body through ingestion, inhalation, dermal contact, and medical procedures. Principal toxicity mechanisms include oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, ER stress, and inflammation, which promote various forms of cell death, potentially contributing to myocardial injury and fibrosis, endothelial dysfunction, phenotypic switching of VSMCs, and macrophage foam cell formation. Despite accumulating experimental and epidemiological evidence pointing to a potential association between MNPs and CVDs, significant knowledge gaps persist. Bridging these gaps demands accelerated development of standardized analytical techniques, comprehensive human exposure assessments, and prospective cohort studies to establish causal relationships and define acceptable risk thresholds. Concurrent efforts to identify early exposure biomarkers in blood and urine, together with coordinated international regulatory actions, are essential to prevent and mitigate MNPs contamination and its potential cardiovascular consequences.
