PHLDA1 knockdown exacerbates emphysema in COPD by attenuating proliferation of alveolar type II cells via modulating GLI1 nuclear translocation
Authors: Shuang Bai, Shuaifu Wang, Rui Ye, Di Wang, Mingxin Ma, Li Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The emphysematous phenotype is an important phenotype in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), with substantial morbidity and mortality. The mechanisms underpinning the role of alveolar type II (AT2) cells in alveolar repair within this phenotype remain poorly understood. This study aimed to elucidate the role of PHLDA1, a potential stemness regulator in AT2 cells, on emphysema development. Utilizing mice model, we performed a targeted knockdown of PHLDA1 in AT2 cells and subsequently exposed these mice to tobacco smoke to assess the resultant severity of emphysema and related alveolar damage. We manipulated PHLDA1 expression in AT2 cells line or primary mouse AT2 cells to examine its influence on AT2 stemness-related processes- differentiation, proliferation, and wound closure ability. The specific pathway of PHLDA1 mediated in AT2 cells, as well as its interaction with the GLI1 protein, was further investigated. Mice with reduced PHLDA1 expression developed the emphysema independent of smoking exposure. PHLDA1 knockdown in AT2 cells attenuated their proliferation via the Hedgehog pathway, impairing wound closure ability in the emphysematous phenotype. We also discovered a binding relationship between PHLDA1 and GLI1, where PHLDA1 modulates the nuclear translocation of GLI1, thus regulating the Hedgehog pathway and influencing the stemness and proliferation of AT2 cells. Our study suggests that PHLDA1 is a critical factor in the proliferation process of AT2 cells via modulation of GLI1 nuclear translocation. This regulation is essential to the pathogenesis of the emphysematous phenotype in COPD, signifying potential therapeutic targets for intervention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00018-026-06333-8.

Introduction

The emphysematous phenotype is a significant form of COPD, characterized by lung tissue destruction, hyperinflation of terminal alveolar airways, and alveolar septal destruction [1, 2]. Patients typically experience breathlessness and reduced exercise tolerance, with less prominent airway inflammation symptoms like coughing and expectoration, making conventional COPD treatments unsuitable. Furthermore, the mortality rate among patients with this phenotype are appreciably higher than other COPD phenotypes [3, 4]. Consequently, there is a lack of targeted treatments, necessitating urgent exploration of its patho-mechanisms. Most of the existing literature focuses on the emphysematous pathogenesis, primarily revolving around the mechanisms of tissue destruction [5, 6], however, the process of tissue cell self-repair has an equally imperative role in the progression of this disease and holds significant potential for scrutiny.

Alveolar Type II (AT2) cells, present within the lung parenchyma, are renowned for their capability to act as specialized stem cells [7], proliferating and differentiating into Alveolar Type I (AT1) cells to restore alveolar structure [8]. Previous findings indicate a decrease in the quantity and regenerative potential of AT2 cells in patients with COPD [9]. Moreover, investigations have revealed notable modifications in pathways pertinent to stem cell function, in COPD patients, such as WNT [10], Hedgehog [11], and Notch [12]. Therefore, an exploration into the pathological mechanism behind the reduced regenerative capacity of AT2 cells in emphysema patients can potentially unearth molecular targets of significance.

PHLDA1 has been identified as a stemness factor across multiple tissue cells [13–15], with its suppression reducing cell proliferation and sphere formation ability in intestinal epithelial cell lines [16]. Consequently, PHLDA1 has emerged as a critical molecular biomarker for investigating the stemness functions of diverse tissue cells [16–18]. In our prior study, PHLDA1 was decreased in AT2 cells of emphysematous COPD patients, and its deficiency induces AT2 cell apoptosis [19]. However, as a stemness factor, the specific role of PHLDA1 in AT2 cell proliferation remains unexplored. The Hedgehog pathway, integral to cell stemness and proliferation, is strongly associated with COPD, with notable single nucleotide polymorphism sites on the Hedgehog-interacting protein (HHIP) sequence in COPD patients [19–21]. It has also been noted that the genotype Hhip+/- mice are more predisposed towards developing emphysema [22]. Among lung cells, HHIP demonstrates its maximum expression in AT2 cells [23], and on inspection of lung tissue from COPD patients, it was found that 80% of AT2 cells exhibited positive HHIP expression [24]. The above results all confirm the close relationship between the Hedgehog pathway and COPD. The GLI family are terminal effectors of the Hedgehog pathway. While GLI2 and GLI3 are crucial for embryonic patterning, GLI1 is less critical for embryogenesis but becomes indispensable for postnatal tissue repair and stem cell maintenance. In order to investigate the role of PHLDA1 in AT2 cell proliferation, we focused on GLI1 [25, 26]. GLI1 is a paramount transcription factor [27, 28] in Hedgehog pathway. On activation, GLI1 enhances its nuclear entry leading to the activation of downstream cell stemness-related target genes such as Cyclin D, and this promotes the process of cell self-repair and proliferation [29]. Additionally, GLI1-expressing fibroblasts have been found to be highly responsive to SHH ligands and exhibit high proliferation during the early phase of alveolar development [30]. This suggests a strong correlation between GLI1 and proliferation, but its relationship with cell self-repair and proliferation in AT2 cells is yet to be investigated deeply.

Thus, this study knockdown the PHLDA1 in mouse AT2 cells, with intentional PHLDA1 intervention in MLE12 cells and primary mouse AT2 cells, in order to unveil the exact mechanism through which PHLDA1 influences the emphysema formation of COPD.

Materials and methods

AT2-specific knockdown of PHLDA1 in mice

Five-week-old male C57 mice (Beijing Huafukang Bioscience) were raised in SPF conditions. After one week, mice were anesthetized (100 mg/kg ketamine) and received AAV6 intervention via non-invasive tracheal intubation: 50uL adeno-associated viruses (AAV6)-shNC (control) or 50uL AAV6-shPHLDA1 (knockdown). AAV6-shPHLDA1 and AAV-shNC were custom-packaged by Hanbio Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). The short hairpin RNA (shRNA) targeting the sequence 5′-CGCCGAGATCACGTTGCAGAT-3′ was embedded within a microRNA-30 (mir-30) scaffold in an AAV6 shuttle plasmid. Viruses were produced using a triple-plasmid transfection system (pAAV-RC6, pHelper, and the shuttle plasmid) in 293T cells. The detailed vector map is included in Supplementary Fig. 1.

Smoking mouse model establishment

Post AAV6 intervention, mice divided into: shNC, shPHLDA1, shNC+Smoke, and shPHLDA1 + Smoke (4–8 mice/group). The smoke groups exposed to Hongmei cigarettes using a Hope-med 8052 device (2x/day, 60 min/session, 7 days/week). Mice were weighed and samples taken at 8-, 12-, and 20-weeks post exposure.

Arterial blood gas analysis

Arterial blood gas analysis was performed on mice following 20 weeks of smoke exposure. Mice were anesthetized via subcutaneous injection of ketamine (100 mg/kg). Blood samples were then collected from the abdominal aorta using a heparinized syringe (calcium-balanced lithium heparin as anticoagulant). Immediately after collection, samples were placed on ice and analyzed within 15 min using a GEM 3500 Blood Gas Analyzer (Instrumentation Laboratory, USA) to determine pH, partial pressure of oxygen (pO₂), partial pressure of carbon dioxide (pCO₂), sodium (Na⁺) levels, potassium (K⁺) and calcium (Ca⁺) levels.

Mouse lung function assessment

Pulmonary function parameters were measured non-invasively in conscious mice using the DSI BUXCO whole-body plethysmography system. Mice were placed in individual plethysmography chambers, and respiratory waveforms were recorded. Parameters including minute ventilation (MV), peak expiratory flow (PEF), mid-expiratory flow (EF50), Airway Resistance (Raw), functional residual capacity (FRC), etc. were derived from the recordings. All measurements were conducted and analyzed according to the manufacturer’s protocols.

Ethics approval and consent

All animal procedures were reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Shengjing Hospital, China Medical University (Shenyang, China; Approval No. 2023PS223K).

Primary mouse type 2 alveolar epithelial cell isolation

Primary mouse AT2 cells were isolated using the classic differential adherence method [31]. Lungs from six-week-old C57 mice was minced with scissors and dissected into 1mm3 pieces. The lung tissue was centrifuged and digested in 0.25% trypsin. Then, the cell suspension was filtered through a 100-µm mesh filter and then followed by a 40-µm mesh filter, and digested by 0.1% collagenase. Red blood cell lysis solution was added to remove remaining blood cells. Finally, cells resuspended in culture medium, incubated at 37 °C, and after 1-hour, non-adherent or weakly adherent cells were collected, the supernatant was then transferred to a new dish for the next round. The process is repeated sequentially for a total of six cycles, and the cells obtained after the last adhesion were identified as primary mouse AT2 cells. After 24 h of adherent culture, the cell type was confirmed using the AT2 cell marker SPC.

Immunofluorescence Microscopy

Immunofluorescence staining was performed on paraffin-embedded lung tissue sections as previously described, with modifications [32]. Briefly, sections were dewaxed and subjected to antigen retrieval. After blocking with 10% normal goat serum for 2 h at room temperature, sections were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. The following day, sections were washed and incubated with species-appropriate fluorescent secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. For immunofluorescence of cultured cells, MLE12 or primary AT2 cells were seeded onto coverslips in 24-well plates and allowed to adhere for 12 h. Where indicated, cells were treated with cigarette smoke extract (CSE 6% v/v) for 24 h. Cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min at room temperature. Subsequent blocking, primary antibody incubation, and secondary antibody incubation steps were identical to those described for tissue sections. For all samples, nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Images were acquired using a two-photon laser scanning confocal microscope (LSM 880, Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). Fluorescence intensity was quantified using Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software (Media Cybernetics, Rockville, MD, USA).

MLE12 cells cultivation

The mouse alveolar type II epithelial cell line MLE-12 was purchased from iCell Bioscience Inc. (Shanghai, China; Product No. iCell-m036). Cells were maintained in DMEM/F-12 medium (Procell, Wuhan, China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA) and cultured at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO₂. The cell line was authenticated by the supplier and its identity was verified in our laboratory by positive staining for the AT2 cell marker Surfactant Protein C (SPC). Cells were confirmed to be free of mycoplasma contamination prior to the experimental period.

Lentivirus transfection

Lentiviruses for knockdown and overexpression of PHLDA1, along with their respective controls were obtained from Hanbio Biotechnology. For knockdown, the same shRNA target sequence with AAV6 (5′-CGCCGAGATCACGTTGCAGAT-3′) was cloned into the pHBLV-U6-MCS-PGK-PURO vector, with expression driven by the U6 promoter. For overexpression, the full-length mouse PHLDA1coding sequence was cloned into the pHBLV-CMV-MCS-EF1-PURO vector, with expression driven by the CMV promoter. All lentiviruses were packaged using the third-generation psPAX2/pMD2.G system in 293T cells. The detailed vector map is included in Supplementary Fig. 1. For lentiviral transduction, MLE12 cells were infected at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 50. The infections were performed in medium containing 8 µg/mL polybrene. Specifically, cells were incubated with the lentivirus in half the standard medium volume for 4 h, after which the volume was restored with complete medium. The total infection duration was 24 h. Subsequently, the cells were cultured in fresh medium containing 8 µg/mL puromycin to select for transduced cells, followed the manufacturer’s instructions [33].

For primary cells, polybrene and puromycin were not utilized for transfection and selection. The efficiency of the transfection was evaluated using PCR and Western blotting after 24- and 48-hours post-transfection.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted and subsequently reverse transcribed into cDNA, using Takara reverse transcription kit (#RR047A) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Subsequently, the cDNA was amplified and detected using a SYBR Green PCR Kit (#RR820A) from Takara Bio (Kusatsu, Japan) on a Roche 480 Real-Time PCR System (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). The reaction conditions followed established protocols from our previous work [32]. The following primers were used: PHLDA1, forward 5’-CGCATCCGCATCCTCATCCTTAC-3’, reverse 5’-TGCTGGAGTTGGTACGGGTGAG-3’; GAPDH, forward 5’-CCTGGTATGACAACGAATTTG-3’, reverse 5’-CAGTGAGGGTCTCTCTCTTCC-3’.

CSE preparation

The preparation of CSE was carried out as delineated in a previous study [34]. Specifically, a HongMei cigarette (Hongta Tobacco (Group) Co., Ltd., Kunming, China) - consisting of 1.2 mg of nicotine and 15 mg of tar - passed through DMEM/F12 medium to obtain 100% CSE solution. The resulting smoke suspension was filtered through a 0.22-µm filter to eliminate bacteria and large particles. CSE was freshly prepared before every experiment, diluted to a working concentration of 6% with DMEM/F12 and was utilized within 30 min of its preparation.

EdU Incorporation Assay

Cell proliferation was assessed using the 5-ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine (EdU) incorporation assay. Briefly, MLE-12 cells were first transduced with lentivirus or corresponding controls. After a 48-hour transduction period, the medium was replaced. Cells were harvested, re-seeded into optical plates, and allowed to adhere for 24 h. Subsequently, the cells were pulsed with 10 µM EdU (BeyoClick™ EdU Cell Proliferation Kit, Beyotime, China) in fresh culture medium for 2 h at 37 °C under 5% CO₂ to label S-phase nuclei. After labeling, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature. The incorporated EdU was detected by incubating the cells with a reaction cocktail containing Alexa Fluor 594-azide for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. Cell nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 33,342. Fluorescent images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope (Nikon, Japan). The proliferation rate was quantified as the percentage of EdU-positive (red) nuclei relative to the total number of Hoechst-stained (blue) nuclei in multiple random fields per sample using Image-Pro Plus software (Media Cybernetics).

Cell scratch experiment

5 × 105 MLE12 cells were added to each well of the 6-well plate, and allowed to adhere for approximately 12 h until reaching 70–80% confluence. Using a 200 µL pipette tip, we inscribed horizontal lines across each well. The cells were replenished with serum-free medium and incubated. The scratch healing conditions were observed and photographed at 0 h and 24 h after scratching. Images were captured using an upright microscope (Nikon Ni) with a 200× objective and NIS-Elements F3.0 software (Nikon). The migration area was quantified using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA).

Co-immunoprecipitation

The immunoprecipitation process was carried out using a Dynabead Protein G Immunoprecipitation Kit (#10007D) from Invitrogen, based on previously described procedures [32]. Either an anti-PHLDA1 antibody (#ab133654) from Abcam (Cambridge, UK) (8 µg) or an anti-IgG antibody (#MA5–16384) from Invitrogen (Waltham, MA, USA) (8 µg) was combined with protein A/G beads in a 200 µL Ab-binding buffer. After washing, approximately 500 mg of total protein was introduced to the antibody binding beads, incubating for an hour at room temperature. Thereafter, elution and protein loading buffers were added in equal measure to each sample. The specimens were then denatured at a temperature of 70 °C for 10 min. This denatured protein was utilized for confirming the binding proteins.

Molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations

Molecular docking and dynamics simulations were conducted using AlphaFold3, PyMOL v2.3.4, and PRODIGY to predict and analyze protein interactions. A 100 ns molecular dynamics simulation was performed with GROMACS2024.1 using the AMBER FF19SB force field and the TIP3P water model in a cubic box, using 1.0 nm cutoffs for electrostatic and van der Waals interactions and the Particle Mesh Ewald method. Temperature and pressure were controlled by the V-rescale thermostat and Parrinello-Rahman barostat, respectively. Equilibration involved 200 ps of NVT and 100 ps of NPT dynamics. Analyses such as RMSD, RMSF, SASA, Gyrate, binding free energy, and free energy landscape were conducted using GROMACS modules, gmx_MMPBSA, and Python scripts.

Nucleoplasmic separation assay

Nuclear and cytoplasmic proteins from MLE12 cells were separated using a nuclear and cytoplasmic protein extraction kit (P0027) from Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology (Nantong, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions [35]. Cell pellets were treated with cytoplasmic protein extraction reagents A and B with PMSF, vortexed, lysed on ice, and centrifuged. The supernatant (cytoplasmic protein) was collected, and the pellet was washed and centrifuged. Nuclear protein extraction reagent was added to the pellet, vortexed, incubated on ice with vortexing intervals for 30 min, and centrifuged to yield the supernatant (nuclear protein).

Transfection of GLI1-overexpressing plasmid

MLE12 cells were seeded (2 × 10⁵ cells/well) and allowed to adhere overnight. Transfection of the GLI1 overexpression plasmid (or empty vector) was carried out using Lipofectamine 3000 at a plasmid concentration of 2 µg per well. The efficiency of GLI1 overexpression was assessed at 48 h post-transfection by measuring protein expression (Western blot with anti-GLI1 antibody).

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed using Prism 7 (GraphPad). Continuous data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Student’s t-test and Mann-Whitney U test compared means depending on data distribution, while One-way ANOVA or Kruskal-Wallis test was used for multi-group comparisons. Fisher’s exact test and chi-square test contrasted categorical data. The sample size (n) represents the number of biologically independent samples or animals per group, as indicated in the figure legends. Based on pilot data, sample sizes were determined via priori power analysis (α = 0.05, power = 0.9) using PASS 15.0 software (NCSS, LLC) to ensure adequate statistical power.

Results

Knockdown of PHLDA1 in type II alveolar epithelial cells contributes to emphysema formation

Mice with AT2 cell-specific PHLDA1 knockdown were created via AAV6 virus, confirmed by immunofluorescence showing reduced PHLDA1 in AT2 cells after 21 days. Immunofluorescence findings revealed a significant reduction in PHLDA1 expression within AT2 cells of mouse lung tissue 21 days after AAV6 administration (Fig. 1B, C). PHLDA1 protein levels were assessed by analyzing primary mouse AT2 cells, further corroborating the substantial decrease in PHLDA1 protein levels in AT2 cells of the shPHLDA1 groups (Fig. 1D, E).

Following 8 weeks of smoke exposure, smoked control mice (shNC+smoke) did not develop significant emphysema. However, mice with PHLDA1 knockdown in AT2 cells (shPHLDA1 + smoke) exhibited significant emphysema noticeable emphysema regardless of smoke exposure (Fig. 1F), with reduced mean alveolar numbers (MAN) and increased mean alveolar intervals (MLI) (Fig. 1H, I). After 20 weeks of smoke exposure, the shNC+smoke group showed substantial emphysema characterized by lower MAN and larger MLI (Fig. 1L, M); the arterial blood oxygen pressure in smoked mice with PHLDA1 knockdown (shPHLDA1 + smoke) was significantly lower compared to mice in the shPHLDA1 group. Additionally, the partial pressure of carbon dioxide was significantly increased and pH levels were significantly reduced in the shPHLDA1 + smoke group, while no notable changes were found in blood gas analysis of the smoked control mice (shNC+smoke) in comparison to the shNC group. Moreover, smoking resulted in a considerable reduction in mouse body weight (Fig. 1G, K). Subsequently, arterial blood gas analysis was performed on mice following 20 weeks of smoke exposure. Compared with the shPHLDA1 group, the shPHLDA1 + Smoke group exhibited significantly lower arterial partial pressure of oxygen (pO₂) and pH, along with significantly higher arterial partial pressure of carbon dioxide (pCO₂). Additionally, the arterial pO₂ in the shPHLDA1 + Smoke group was lower than that in the shNC+Smoke group (Fig. 1N-S).

The proliferation levels of AT2 cells in mice exposed to smoking for both 8 and 20 weeks (shNC+Smoke) were significantly diminished in comparison to those in the non-smoking group (shNC). Furthermore, AT2 proliferation levels in the shPHLDA1 group were markedly lower than those seen within the shNC group (Fig. 1T-V).

The emphysematous alveolar morphology observed after 12 weeks of smoke exposure was consistent with that seen at the 8-week time point. In addition, lung function was assessed in this 12-week cohort. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 2, compared with the control group (shNC), smoke-exposed mice (shNC+Smoke) displayed significant pulmonary function impairment, characterized by reduced minute ventilation and PEF, and increased FRC and expiratory time. Furthermore, AT2-specific PHLDA1 knockdown (shPHLDA1) recapitulated this obstructive pattern, resulting in decreased minute ventilation, PEF, and EF50, as well as increased airway resistance and expiratory time [36].

Assessment of AT2 and AT1 lineage marker expression after PHLDA1 modulation

To explore the specific functions of PHLDA1 in AT2 cells, we isolated primary AT2 cells from C57 mice (Fig. 2A) and transfected them with shPHLDA1 lentivirus, which resulted in a significant decrease in PHLDA1 expression in primary AT2 cells (Fig. 2B- D).

Immunofluorescence staining of the extracted primary AT2 cells revealed that, on the first day following isolation, they predominantly expressed the AT2 cell marker SPC (Fig. 2E, F). By the third day, both AT2 cell marker SPC and AT1 cell marker PDPN were expressed (Fig. 2E, G). By the fifth day, only the AT1 cell marker PDPN was expressed (Fig. 2E, H). However, there were no significant differences in the expression of these two markers between the shNC and shPHLDA1 groups at each time point (Fig. 2F-H).

PHLDA1 affects the proliferation of AT2 cells

To elucidate the influence of PHLDA1 on AT2 cell proliferation, we leveraged lentivirus to suppress and overexpress PHLDA1 in MLE12 cells (AT2 cell line), and verified the efficiency of knockdown and overexpression, respectively (Fig. 3A-F). Our findings revealed that the knockdown of PHLDA1(shPHLDA1) resulted in a substantial decrease in cell proliferation at 12 h, 24 h, and 48 h (Fig. 3G), compared with the shNC group. Accordingly, the positive proportion of intracellular PCNA was significantly reduced (Fig. 3I, J). CSE can significantly reduce the proliferation level of the MLE12 cells (NC + CSE vs. NC), however, overexpressing PHLDA1 after CSE treatment (PHLDA1 + CSE vs. NC + CSE) can reinstate the proliferation level decreased by CSE (Fig. 3H). Fluorescent staining endorsed that the ratio of PCNA-positive cells was significantly reduced after CSE treatment (NC + CSE vs. NC) within MLE12 cells, while the PCNA positive proportion significantly recovered after overexpressing PHLDA1(PHLDA1 + CSE vs. NC + CSE) (Fig. 3K, L).

To confirm that the reduced CCK-8 signal reflected impaired proliferation rather than cell death, we performed EdU assays. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 3, PHLDA1 knockdown significantly decreased the proportion of S-phase cells, confirming the CCK-8 findings.

PHLDA1 modulates the wound closure ability of AT2 cells

The scratch assay was used to examine the wound closure ability of MLE12 cells with PHLDA1 knockdown and overexpression. The results showed that following PHLDA1 knockdown, there was a notable reduction in the cell migratory capacity in the scratch assay (Fig. 4A, B). In contrast, the MLE12 cells demonstrated an amplified migratory capacity after the overexpression of PHLDA1 (Fig. 4A, C).

PHLDA1 binds to GLI1 in AT2 cells

Based on our previous published results on the mass spectrometric analysis of PHLDA1-binding proteins [32], we chose GLI1, a protein closely related to cell stemness and proliferation. Then, we verified the mutual binding relationship between PHLDA1 and GLI1, persisting even under CSE treatment conditions (Fig. 5A). Using immunofluorescence technology, we further proved the co-localization connection between PHLDA1 and GLI1. GLI1 was predominantly localized to the nucleus. In contrast, PHLDA1 was present in both the cytoplasm and the nucleus. Notably, within the nucleus, PHLDA1 co-localized with GLI1, as evidenced by the overlapping fluorescence signals (Fig. 5B). Then, to gain structural and energetic insights into the PHLDA1-GLI1 interaction, we performed computational analyses (Supplementary Fig. 4). Molecular docking predicted a stable complex with a calculated binding energy of -19.9 kcal/mol, suggesting strong affinity (Fig. 5C). Subsequently, all-atom molecular dynamics simulations were conducted to assess the stability of the docked complex and to analyze the energetic contributions of interfacial residues. The resulting per-residue binding energy profile identified several key amino acids within PHLDA1 that exhibited the lowest (most favorable) binding energies, highlighting potential hotspots for the interaction (Fig. 5D). Together, these computational results corroborate the biochemical evidence and suggest a stable, specific interaction between PHLDA1 and GLI1.

PHLDA1 affects nuclear translocation of GLI1

Moreover, we studied the nuclear import rate of GLI1 in CSE-treated MLE12 cells, and found that post-CSE treatment, the protein levels of GLI1 and PHLDA1 significantly dropped in the nucleus, leading to a substantial decrease in the nuclear-cytoplasmic ratio of GLI1. However, PHLDA1 protein levels within the cytoplasm remained relatively unchanged (Fig. 6A-E). This was further substantiated by immunofluorescence studies (Fig. 6F-G). After knock-downing PHLDA1, we observed a substantial reduction in both the nuclear level and nuclear-cytoplasmic ratio of GLI1 protein in MLE12 cells (Fig. 6H-J). Immunofluorescence studies also validated the above findings (Fig. 6K-L). Upon overexpressing PHLDA1 in the AT2 model treated with CSE we detected that PHLDA1 overexpression could partially counteract the CSE-induced decrease in the GLI1 nuclear-cytoplasmic ratio (Fig. 6M-O), a conclusion also supported by our immunofluorescence results (Fig. 6P-Q).

As GLI1 is a key transcriptional effector of the Hedgehog pathway, we examined key nodes within this pathway. To this end, we assessed the activity of its upstream inhibitors PKA and p-GSK3β, and measured the expression of Cyclin D1, a direct transcriptional target of GLI1 that drives cell proliferation. In MLE12 cells where PHLDA1 was knocked down, the levels of GLI1-targeted gene-CyclinD decreased, while the protein levels of p-GSK3B, PKA, and GLI1 protein unchanged (Fig. 7A-E). CSE treatment significantly decreased p-GSK3B and CyclinD levels, while simultaneously increasing PKA protein levels, which suggests that the Hedgehog pathway may be suppressed. In MLE12 cells overexpressing PHLDA1, the protein levels of CyclinD were considerably higher than those in the NC + CSE group, signifying a recovery in CyclinD levels decreased by CSE (Fig. 7F-J). These findings substantiate that PHLDA1 influence Hedgehog mainly through interacting with GLI1 to promote its nuclear translocation and Cyclin D transcription. While CSE inhibits the canonical Hedgehog pathway, PHLDA1 overexpression partially mitigates this suppression. The roles of p-GSK3β and PKA in this process remain unclear.

To validate the functional role of GLI1 in mediating the anti-proliferative effect of PHLDA1 deficiency, we performed a rescue experiment in MLE12 cells. Upon over-expression of GLI1, the decreased cell viability and reduced EdU incorporation rate caused by PHLDA1 knockdown were significantly restored (Fig. 8C- E). Consistent with these phenotypic changes, the downregulation of Cyclin D1 protein, was also substantially rescued by GLI1 overexpression (Fig. 8F, G). These findings demonstrate that forced activation of GLI1 is sufficient to counteract the proliferative impairment induced by PHLDA1 knockdown, functionally confirming that PHLDA1 modulates AT2 cell proliferation via GLI1.

Discussion

COPD, a highly heterogeneous disease, manifests varied clinical and pathological features across its phenotypes. Patients with the emphysematous phenotype face progressive lung tissue destruction. Traditional COPD treatments are less effective for this phenotype, leading to poor prognoses [37]. AT2 cells in COPD have the ability to proliferate and differentiate into AT1 cells, aiding in alveolar repair. Exploring AT2 cell mechanisms can reveal targets to decelerate emphysema progression. In this study, using AAVs, we knocked down PHLDA1 in mouse lung AT2 cells, discovering that PHLDA1 suppression contributes to the emphysematous changes regardless of cigarette smoke exposure. PHLDA1 is essential for maintaining AT2 cell stemness and proliferation, influencing the Hedgehog pathway via GLI1 nuclear translocation, thus playing a critical role in alveolar repair.

PHLDA1, a stemness-related molecule, has emerged in recent studies. It acts as a stemness factor in a variety of tissue cells [13–15], and has become a critical molecular marker for stemness function research [16, 18, 38]. However, its stemness impact on lung tissue cells and pertinent research remains blank. Our prior study confirmed that PHLDA1 decreased in AT2 cells of emphysematous COPD patients, with AT2 cells being the predominant cell type expressing PHLDA1 in lung tissue [32]. To investigate the functional consequence of this loss, we employed a targeted genetic approach in mice. Given its high tropism for alveolar epithelial cells, the AAV6 serotype has been extensively used for lung gene delivery. When coupled with an AT2 cell-specific promoter, it enables highly precise genetic manipulation within this cell population [39, 40]. In this study, we used AAV6 to intervene in mice, achieving specific knockdown of PHLDA1 in AT2 cells of lung tissue, effectively simulating the reduction of PHLDA1 protein levels in lung tissue of emphysema patients. We discovered that PHLDA1 suppression contributed to emphysema regardless of cigarette smoke exposure. Even after a smoking duration of 2 months, lungs of mice in smoke group (NC+Smoke) showed no apparent emphysematous changes, and there was no meaningful statistical difference in the MLI and MAN values between the mice exposed to air (NC). When the smoking duration escalated to 5 months, the mice in smoke group mice (NC+Smoke) exhibited clear emphysematous changes in lungs, and significant differences in MLI and MAN compared with mice exposed to air (NC). This result conclusively confirms the important role PHLDA1 plays in the development of emphysema in patients with the emphysematous phenotype. AT2 cells are recognized as specialized stem cells, mainly known for their proliferative function and the ability to differentiate into AT1 cells, thus facilitating the repair of lung tissue structure [41].Consequently, the current study sought to explore the role of PHLDA1 in the proliferation and differentiation of AT2 cells. To rigorously investigate this, we employed a complementary dual-model strategy. Primary mouse AT2 cells were used for their high physiological relevance in histological and differentiation-related analyses. For detailed, reproducible mechanistic studies on proliferation and signaling pathways, we utilized the MLE12 cell line—a well-established in vitro model that retains key AT2 cell characteristics [42, 43]. This approach balanced pathophysiological relevance with experimental tractability. Employing this strategy, we first assessed the impact of PHLDA1 on alveolar epithelial cell phenotype. Under the conditions tested, modulation of PHLDA1 did not lead to significant changes in the expression of AT2 marker SPC or the AT1 marker PDPN. This initial phenotypic assessment suggests that, within the scope of this study, the primary functional impact of PHLDA1 in AT2 cells may be more closely associated with proliferation rather than with a marked shift in lineage marker expression. In contrast, a pronounced effect was observed on cell proliferation. Using CCK8 and immunofluorescence for PCNA, we found significant proliferation reduction 12 h post-PHLDA1 suppression, similar to our previous results following 6% CSE treatment [32]. In this study, after 6% CSE treatment for 12 h, the cell proliferation levels markedly decreased (NC + CSE vs. NC). However, cell proliferation levels were remarkably recovered 24 h post PHLDA1 overexpression, when compared to cells treated with 6% CSE (PHLDA1 + CSE vs. NC + CSE). This implies that PHLDA1 overexpression could rescue the AT2 cell proliferation decline induced by cigarette smoke. Various prior studies have corroborated that PHLDA1 augments cell proliferation, particularly focusing on immortalized osteoblasts, intestinal cancer cell lines, and glioblastoma cells [16, 44, 45]. Consequently, maintaining an appropriate normal level of PHLDA1 protein in normal cells poses a thought-provoking question.

This function is distinct from, yet complementary to, its previously reported anti-apoptotic role mediated through interaction with 14-3-3ε in our prior study [32]. Rather than representing conflicting observations, these two lines of evidence collectively establish PHLDA1 as a multi-functional guardian of the AT2 cell compartment. In physiological conditions, PHLDA1 likely safeguards the AT2 cells through a “two-pronged” strategy: by promoting cell survival and by sustaining proliferative capacity. The coordinated regulation of cell proliferation and apoptosis by a single molecule is not uncommon. Key regulators such as PTEN [46], which constrains tumor growth by inhibiting proliferation and promoting apoptosis, and p53 [47], the archetypal determinant of cell fate through its control of the cell cycle, and apoptosis, exemplify this principle. In the context of emphysema, the observed reduction of PHLDA1 may therefore orchestrate a dual hit on AT2 cell homeostasis: increased susceptibility to apoptosis coupled with impaired proliferative renewal. This would severely compromise alveolar maintenance and repair, accelerating the development of emphysematous lesions. Our current study elucidating the proliferative arm of PHLDA1 function thus provides a necessary and novel extension to its known biology, offering a more comprehensive mechanistic understanding of AT2 cell failure in COPD pathogenesis.

Limited studies explore PHLDA1’s connection to cell repair. A study examining the degree of cell migration post PHLDA1 suppression in osteoblast cell lines found a significant decrease in osteoblast migration post suppression [44]. Our investigation, via suppressing and overexpressing PHLDA1, examined its role in AT2 migratory capacity in the scratch assay, and confirmed its facilitative effect on AT2 cell wound closure ability. However, its specific mechanisms warrant further research.

To investigate the precise function of PHLDA1 in AT2 cell proliferation, we analyzed the mass spectrometric analysis from our prior experiments to identify GLI1 [32], a key regulator of cellular proliferation, and subsequently confirmed the reciprocal binding interaction between PHLDA1 and GLI1. We also employed molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulation methods to validate the interaction between the two proteins and to identify potential binding sites. Given that GLI1 functions through nuclear translocation, we investigated PHLDA1’s influence on GLI1’s nuclear translocation. Our results confirmed that CSE treatment reduced both PHLDA1 protein levels and GLI1 nuclear translocation. After knocking down PHLDA1, the GLI1 nuclear translocation levels significantly decreased, which is consistent with the experimental results after 6% CSE treatment. In contrast, PHLDA1 overexpression restored GLI1’s nuclear presence (PHLDA1 + CSE vs. NC + CSE). Consequently, we asserted that PHLDA1 facilitates the nuclear translocation of GLI1 upon binding, enhancing AT2 cell stemness, and stimulating cell proliferation.

GLI1, a Hedgehog pathway transcription factor, impacts AT2 cell stemness and proliferation [48]. In existing research, the Wnt/β-catenin pathway also plays a pivotal role in preserving the stemness of AT2 cells and is a classical pathway governing the proliferation and differentiation of AT2 cells [49]. Nevertheless, there were study highlights that knockdown of PHLDA1 leads to decreased cell-matrix adhesion due to reduced collagen and lamin in binding, which in turn negatively impacts cell proliferation and inhibits cell migration. But knockdown of β-catenin or MYC does not significantly affect the transcription levels of intracellular PHLDA1, suggesting that PHLDA1 is not a downstream target of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway [16]. By knocking down PHLDA1 in MLE12 cells, it was observed that this manipulation did not impact the expression of other molecules within the Hedgehog pathway, but specifically influenced the expression of CyclinD, a downstream target gene of GLI1. Conversely, overexpression of PHLDA1 in CSE-treated cell models resulted in a significant increase in the levels of PKA, leading to the inhibition of Hedgehog pathway activation, decreased CyclinD transcript levels, and ultimately affecting the proliferation of AT2 cells. The upregulation of PHLDA1 in CSE-treated AT2 cells resulted in an increase in CyclinD expression levels compared to cells treated solely with CSE, while the expression of PKA decreased (PHLDA1 + CSE vs. NC + CSE). These findings suggest that PHLDA1 enhances proliferation in AT2 cells by modulating the translocation of GLI1 in the Hedgehog pathway, influencing the transcription of CyclinD and ultimately impacting cell proliferation. However, the mechanism by which PHLDA1 affects the protein levels of PKA requires further investigation.

Our study presents certain limitations. Firstly, our assessment of differentiation relied on the measurement of AT1/AT2 lineage marker expression levels. While this provides an initial phenotypic readout, it does not directly measure the rate of cellular fate conversion. Since we focus on the proliferation process in this manuscript, future studies employing lineage-tracing techniques or single-cell fate mapping will be required to definitively determine the role of PHLDA1 in the AT2-to-AT1 differentiation process. Secondly, while the MLE12 cell line is a valuable and widely used model that retains key AT2 markers and enables robust, reproducible mechanistic studies [42, 43], it is an immortalized line. As such, its differentiation potential may not fully recapitulate the behavior of primary AT2 cells in vivo or ex vivo. Therefore, primary cells were used for differentiation-related analyses while MLE12 cells were used for mechanistic studies related to proliferation and signaling pathways. Additionally, precise PHLDA1-GLI1 binding mechanisms are unclear. Notably, in our previous clinical cohort [32], PHLDA1 was downregulated in the emphysema phenotype, with greater radiological severity of emphysema; this further supports the potential significance of PHLDA1 loss-of-function in the progression of emphysema, as observed in the present study. Future studies with larger sample sizes are required to validate its utility as a biomarker.

Conclusion

In conclusion, PHLDA1 influencing the proliferation of AT2 cells through modulating GLI1 nuclear translocation in COPD phenotype, contributes to the emphysematous changes in COPD emphysematous phenotype.
