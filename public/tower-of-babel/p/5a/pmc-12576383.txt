Effect of Thermal Treatment on Nutritional and Physicochemical Properties of Sweet Pumpkin Flour and its Impact on Resistant Starch and Sensory Quality of Muffins and Noodles
Authors: Mingyo Ha, Felicia Irawan, Ha‐Eun Han, Sangmyung Jun, Dong‐Hwa Cho, Hyun‐Jung Chung
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis study aimed to investigate the effects of oven baking (180°C for 40 min), microwaving (8 min), and steaming (20 min) on the physicochemical properties, β‐carotene content, and in vitro digestibility of sweet pumpkin flours, along with the sensory profiles of muffins and noodles prepared with the treated flours. Among treatments, oven‐baked flour exhibited the highest β‐carotene content (477.6 ± 3.8 mg/100 g) compared to native flour (NF) (379.9 ± 0.2 mg/100 g). NF exhibited the highest RS content (13.2%), and although heat treatment slightly reduced resistant starch (RS) levels (11.4–12.9%), oven‐baked flour retained RS most effectively among the treated samples. Muffins and noodles incorporating oven‐baked flour showed higher RS content compared to control products (p < 0.05). Muffins and noodles prepared with oven‐baked flour showed lower grassy‐flavor intensity and more pronounced yellowish appearance than those with other treatments (p < 0.05). These findings suggest that thermal processing, particularly oven baking, enhances the nutritional and sensory properties of sweet pumpkin flour, supporting its use as a functional ingredient in bakery and noodle products.Keywords: muffins, noodles, pumpkin flour, RS, sensory quality, thermal treatment

Introduction

As Korean diets have gradually become westernized, there has been an increase in calorie intake due to the rising consumption of instant and fast foods. This dietary shift has contributed to the growing prevalence of various adult diseases, including obesity, diabetes, dyslipidemia, and heart disease, which have become significant social problems (Kim et al. 2018). In Korea, three out of ten people over the age of 65 and one in seven adults over the age of 30 have diabetes (Jung et al. 2021). Because of its growing prevalence, obesity has become one of the most pressing public health challenges of the 21st century and remains a major risk factor for diabetes. In response to these issues, consumer interest in functional foods has grown, which has led to the increasing popularity of low‐calorie diets that incorporate dietary fiber.

Dietary fiber escapes digestion and absorption in the small intestine and is recognized for its role in reducing the risk of chronic metabolic disorders (Walsh et al. 2022). RS, which functions similarly to dietary fiber by resisting digestion in the human body, serves as food source for beneficial micro biota and enhances the intestinal environment (Chung 2019). Functioning as a prebiotic, RS stimulates the proliferation of beneficial colonic micro biota, which metabolizes it into short‐chain fatty acids. RS exhibits several physiological functions, including lowering intestinal pH, increasing fecal bulk, regulating blood glucose levels, and enhancing insulin sensitivity (Chung 2019). As a result, RS is gaining attention as a low‐calorie, functional food ingredient that can effectively prevent diabetes and obesity (Chung 2019).

RS is classified into five types (RS1 to RS5) based on physical and chemical properties. RS1 is starch that is physically trapped within cell walls, making it inaccessible to digestive enzymes in the small intestine. RS2 refers to starch with a tightly packed, crystalline structure that naturally resists digestion. RS3 is a nongranular retrograded starch formed when starchy foods are cooked and then cooled (Walsh et al. 2022). RS4 is a starch that is artificially prepared through chemical modification to resist digestion, and RS5 is a type of RS known as the amylose–lipid complex. RS3, having high crystallinity and resistance to digestive enzymes even when processed at high temperatures, can be manufactured through a simple process without chemical modification. RS3 has been used as a food material by various companies (Chung 2019).

The intake of fruits and vegetables offers higher nutritional value than the intake of grains because they contain a considerable amount of dietary fiber and numerous bioactive compounds, such as flavonoids and carotenoids (Cerniauskiene et al. 2014). Beta‐carotene is known to reduce the risk of cancer, protect against heart disease, and help prevent skin diseases and vision disorders (Grassino et al. 2023; Liu et al. 2009). Sweet pumpkin (Cucurbita maxima D.) has attracted growing interest as a functional food in Korea. It is smaller than the traditional Korean pumpkin (C. moschata D.) but exhibits a relatively higher sugar content of 6–7 Brix (Lee et al. 2020). Moreover, sweet pumpkin contains significantly higher levels of vitamins, calcium, phosphorus, dietary fiber, β‐carotene, solid contents, essential amino acids, and unsaturated fatty acids compared to traditional pumpkins (Lee et al. 2020). However, incorporating raw sweet pumpkin into processed food products is challenging due to its grassy flavor, thereby reducing consumer acceptability. In addition, the direct use of raw sweet pumpkin is limited by its shelf life of 2–3 months (Lee et al. 2020). Therefore, it is essential to develop technical methods to preserve the functional qualities of sweet pumpkins while addressing these limitations. Until now, no studies have pursued a strategic approach to incorporate sweet pumpkins into processed foods.

The thermal treatment of flour made from food crops has been shown to reduce off‐flavors and off‐tastes while enhancing its dispensability and dissolution characteristic, making it suitable for the production of various ready‐to‐eat (RTE) food products (Chung et al. 2014). Processed foods containing sweet pumpkin offer enhanced nutritional, functional, and sensory values (Ghendov‐Mosanu et al., 2023). However, consumer acceptance is strongly influenced by sensory attributes such as aroma, flavor, and texture, and limited research has systematically linked flour‐level modifications to descriptive sensory outcomes in finished products. This study aimed to identify effective thermal processing methods for improving the functional, nutritional, and physicochemical characteristics of sweet pumpkin flour, including β‐carotene content, pasting properties, and in vitro digestibility. To link these changes in flour properties to product quality, a descriptive sensory analysis of muffins and noodles prepared with the treated flours was conducted. In addition, the RS content and texture properties of muffins and noodles containing thermally treated sweet pumpkin flour were examined to provide a theoretical basis for its application in processed foods.

Materials and Methods

Preparation of Thermally Treated Sweet Pumpkin Flour

Sweet pumpkin (cv. Candy), harvested in 2024, was provided by the Haenam Agricultural Technology Center (Jeonnam, Korea). The pumpkins were washed with tap water, and the stems and seeds were removed, leaving the skin intact. Each pumpkin was cut in half longitudinally and further sliced into uniform slices (approximately 50 mm thickness) before the thermal treatments, to ensure consistent moisture removal and even heat transfer. Three thermal processing methods were applied to evaluate the quality characteristics of sweet pumpkin flour. Oven‐baked sweet pumpkin flour (OB) was produced by baking in a deck oven (FDO‐7104B, Daeyoung Bakery Machinery Co., Seoul, Korea) at 180°C (top) /160°C (bottom) for 40 min. Microwave‐treated sweet pumpkin flour (MW) was prepared using a microwave (RE‐C21VB, Samsung Electronics Co., Seoul, Korea) at 2450 MHz and 700 W for 8 min. Steamed sweet pumpkin flour (ST) was obtained by placing the pumpkin into the metal basket of a vegetable steamer. The basket was placed in a steamer positioned above boiling water and maintained under steam for 20 min. The three thermal treatment conditions were selected based on preliminary experiments that identified factors yielding flours with comparable cooking characteristics. All treated pumpkins were refrigerated at 4°C for 24 h to induce the formation of RS3 by starch retro gradation (Li et al. 2024; Chakraborty et al. 2023; Feng et al. 2024). The sample was then air‐dried at room temperature for three days under controlled air circulation and at 40°C in a convection oven (WiseVen WOF‐155, Daihan Scientific Co., Gyeongbuk, Korea) for 24 h to diminish the color deterioration of pumpkin flour. The thermally treated pumpkin was then pulverized and passed through a 100‐mesh sieve. NF was prepared by drying and pulverizing the pumpkins without any thermal treatment or refrigeration.

Preparation of Muffins and Noodles Containing Treated Pumpkin Flour

Muffins were prepared according to Jeong et al. (2020). Supplementary Table 1 represents the muffin formulation with wheat flour (CJ Co., Seoul, Korea), waxy or normal rice flour (Tureban Co., Gyunggi‐do, Korea), margarine, sugar (CJ Co.), salt (Daesang Co., Seoul, Korea), eggs, baking powder (SL Food Co., Ltd., Seoul, Korea), and milk (Seoul Milk Co., Seoul, Korea). Control muffins were made with wheat flour. Pumpkin‐containing muffins were formulated with 50% wheat flour, 20% waxy rice flour, and 30% NF (N30) or 30% OB (P30). Gluten‐free pumpkin (GFP) muffins were prepared by adding 50% normal rice flour, 20% waxy rice flour, and 30% OB. In a preliminary sensory test of muffins formulated with three thermally treated pumpkin flours at substitution levels ranging from 20% to 50%, the N30 and P30 samples, which received relatively low sensory acceptance scores, were selected for further analysis. The prepared batter (60 g) was baked at 170°C for 27 min in a preheated baking oven (FDO‐7104B, Daeyoung Bakery Machinery Co.) and cooled to room temperature on a rack for 30 min.

Noodles were prepared according to the method described by Park et al. (2015). Ingredients used in the preparation of noodles are shown in Supplementary Table 2. P10, P15, and P20 noodles were prepared by replacing the wheat flour with 10%, 15%, and 20% (w/w) OB, respectively. Noodle formulation containing wheat flour alone served as the control sample, and N15 noodles were prepared by replacing the wheat flour with 15% NF (untreated). All ingredients were put into an electric kneader (Kenwood KMM020, JVCKenwood, Uithoorn, Netherlands) and kneaded at maximum strength for 20 min. The dough was put into a vinyl bag and aged at room temperature for 20 min. The aged dough was molded into a thickness of 3 mm using a noodle maker (Marcato Atlas Wellness Motor, Marcato, Italy) and then cut into strips (7 mm × 15 cm). The muffins or noodles from each formulation were prepared at least three times on different days with five muffins or noodles in each bath.

β‐carotene Content of Treated Pumpkin Flour

For the extraction of β‐carotene from sweet pumpkin flour, 2 g of flour was weighed into round bottom tubes, followed by the addition of 3% pyrogallol and 60% potassium hydroxide (KOH). After stirring at 120 rpm and 70°C for 30 min, 23 mL of 1% sodium chloride solution was added, and then hexane with ethyl acetate solution was added. After centrifuging at 7000 rpm for 10 min, the supernatant was collected in a round bottom flask, and the solvent was removed using a rotary vacuum evaporator. The evaporated sample was dispersed and diluted 10‐fold with ethanol. The β‐carotene content (mg/100 g) of sweet pumpkin flour was analyzed using an HPLC 1200 (Agilent Technologies Co. Ltd., Palo Alto, CA, USA) equipped with a UV‐visible detector operating at 450 nm (Health Food Code of the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety of Korea). Separation was carried out on a Zobax Eclipse SDB‐C18 column (46 × 150 mm, 5 m, 40°C). The mobile phase used for isocratic elution consisted of 70:30 of solution A (acetonitrile: methanol = 85: 15, v/v) and solution B (dichloromethane) with a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. All solvents and reagents were HPLC/analytical grade unless otherwise specified. β‐carotene standard (≥ 95% (HPLC), Sigma‐Aldrich, Burlington, Massachusetts, United States, Cas. 7235‐40‐7) was used for calculating the β‐carotene concentration.

Pasting Properties of Treated Pumpkin Flour

Pasting behavior was analyzed with a Rapid Visco Analyzer (RVA‐Tecmaster, Newport Scientific, Warriewood, Australia) following standard operating conditions. Slurries containing 12% flour (w/w, dry basis) were transferred to the RVA canister and heated from 50°C to 95°C at a rate of 6°C/min. The temperature was maintained at 95°C for 5 min, subsequently reduced to 50°C, and held for an additional 2 min. Throughout the analysis, the paddle speed was maintained at 160 rpm.

Color Properties of Flour, Muffins, and Noodles

The color coordinates lightness (L*), redness (a*), and yellowness (b*) for the flours, muffins, and noodles were assessed with a SpectraMagic NX colorimeter (Konica Minolta, Tokyo, Japan). For muffins, color measurements were collected separately from the crumb at the center and from the crust at the top surface. For noodle samples, color was assessed after cooking in boiling water for the designated time, followed by immediate cooling and draining to remove excess surface moisture.

RS Analysis of Flour, Muffins, and Noodles

The in vitro starch digestibility of sweet pumpkin flours, muffins, and noodles was evaluated according to the K‐RSTAR RS assay kit protocol (Megazyme International, Wicklow, Ireland) with minor modifications. Muffin and noodle samples were oven‐dried, ground to a powder, and passed through a 50‐mesh sieve. To ensure reproducibility, enzyme sources and activities were specified as follows. Pancreatic α‐amylase (from porcine pancreas, EC 3.2.1.1) and amyloglucosidase (AMG) (from Aspergillus niger, EC 3.2.1.3) were those supplied in the K‐RSTAR kit. An aliquot (100 mg) was mixed with 4 mL of pancreatic α‐amylase solution (10 mg/mL in 100 mM Tris–maleate buffer, pH 6.0) and AMG (300 U/mL) in a 25 mL conical tube, and agitated at 250 rpm for 16 h at 37°C. Subsequently, 4 mL of 99% ethanol was added, the mixture was vortexed, and centrifuged at 3000 × g for 10 min. The pellet was washed twice with 6 mL of 50% (v/v) ethanol under the same centrifugation conditions, then resuspended in 2 mL of 2 M KOH and stirred in an ice bath for 20 min. The suspension was neutralized with 8 mL of 1.2 M sodium acetate buffer (pH 3.8) and treated with 0.1 mL of AMG (3000 U/mL). After incubation at 50°C for 30 min and centrifugation, a 0.1 mL aliquot of the supernatant was reacted with 3 mL of GOPOD reagent at 50°C for 20 min, and absorbance was measured at 510 nm.

Total starch content was determined using the K‐TSTA kit (Megazyme International). Digestible starch (DS) was calculated as the difference between total starch and RS and expressed as a percentage of the sample weight.

Physical and Cooking Properties of Muffin and Noodle

Muffin height was measured at both the highest central point and the lowest side point at the midsection using a standard ruler. The volume (mL) of the muffin measured by the millet seed displacement method was divided by the weight (g) of the sample to determine the specific volume (mL/g).

The water absorption (g/g) and cooking loss (%) of noodles were analyzed by following the method of Chung et al. (2014) with a slight modification. The noodle sample (7 mm × 3 mm × 150 mm) was cooked in 200 mL of boiling water for 2 min, cooled in cold water for 2 min, placed on a sieve, and dried for 2 min. The water absorption was calculated from the weights of uncooked and cooked noodles.

The cooked noodles were removed from the flask and the remaining water was dried in a convection oven at 105°C as the solid fraction. The cooking loss (%) was calculated from the weights of the solid fraction and uncooked sample.

Textural Properties of Muffin and Noodle

The muffin containing sweet pumpkin flour was cut into a cube (20 mm × 20 mm × 20 mm). The textural properties of the muffins were measured using a texture analyzer (TA–XT plus, Stable Micro Systems Ltd., Surrey, UK) equipped with a square probe (20 × 20 mm). The conditions were as follows mode, texture profile analysis (TPA); test speed, 1.0 mm/s; strain, 50%. Hardness, adhesiveness, springiness, cohesiveness, and chewiness were automatically calculated as the textural parameters.

The elasticity of cooked sweet pumpkin noodles with a length of 15 cm was measured using a texture analyzer (TA–XTplus, Stable Micro Systems Ltd.) with an A/SPR probe. The data was obtained by pulling the noodles up at a speed of 1.0 mm/s with a 25 mm distance between the fixed probes. The experiment was conducted more than 10 times per sample.

Sensory Properties of Muffin and Noodle

Panelists Training

A descriptive sensory analysis was conducted to characterize the sensory attributes of muffins and noodles formulated with sweet pumpkin flour. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Chonnam National University (IRB No. 1040198‐191018‐HR‐105‐02). Twelve trained panelists (six females and six males, aged 22–35 years) who had previously completed screening for sensory acuity and familiarity with descriptive analysis participated in the evaluation. Prior to data collection, the panelists completed five training sessions (∼2 h/day) following ISO 8586:2023. During training, the panel developed operational definitions and reference anchors for the study attributes using matrix‐appropriate materials: yellowish appearance (standard yellow tile: Munsell 7.5Y 8.5/12), pumpkin odor (headspace of steamed sweet‐pumpkin purée), grassy flavor (chlorophyll/herb infusion), sweetness (2% sucrose solution), hardness (muffins and noodles made with wheat flour), and grainy texture (cornmeal in 1% maltodextrin). Replicated practice evaluations, feedback, and consensus discussions were used to align category boundaries and improve repeatability before formal testing.

Actual Testing

Samples of muffins and cooked noodles were coded with randomly assigned three‐digit numbers and served in a randomized sequence. Between each sample, panelists cleansed their palate with room‐temperature water to minimize carry‐over effects. The intensity of six attributes—yellowish appearance, pumpkin odor, grassy flavor, sweetness, hardness, and grainy texture—was rated on a 9‐point quantitative scale (1 = very weak, 5 = moderate, 9 = very strong). Detailed category descriptors and operational definitions for yellowish appearance, pumpkin odor, grassy flavor, sweetness, hardness, and grainy texture are provided in Supplementary Table 3.

Statistical Analysis

All experiments were conducted more than three times per sample, and the average value and standard deviation were calculated using the SPSS software program (SPSS 12.0; SPSS Inc., USA). The significance was verified with one‐way ANOVA for each variance, followed by Duncan's multiple range test, with significance set a p < 0.05.

Results and Discussion

Color Properties of Sweet Pumpkin Flour

The lightness (L*), redness (a*), and yellowness (b*) of the thermally treated sweet pumpkin flour are presented in Table 1. These samples had lower L*, a*, and b* values compared to untreated pumpkin flour (NF). Karadeniz et al. (2024) reported that L*, a*, and b* decreased as the heating time of pumpkin juice increased. Choi and Surh (2014) found that onion juice became darker as the heating condition intensified, explaining that the higher the treatment temperature, the more active the Maillard reaction and caramelization becomes, causing the color values (spectrophotometric at 420 and 520 nm) to increase due to browning. BeMiller and Huber (2008) reported that the L*, a*, and b* values decreased in the presence of large amounts of sugar due to caramelization. Fructose constituted 52.63% of the total soluble sugar content in sweet pumpkin (Wang et al. 2020). Therefore, in the present study, heating sweet pumpkin at a high temperature might accelerate the browning reaction. ST exhibited the lowest L* value (p < 0.05), while showing the highest a* and b* values among the heat‐treated sweet pumpkin flours (p < 0.05). The variation in cooking time and temperature among the different heat treatment methods used in this study might influence the browning reaction in the samples (Hwang et al. 2016).

β‐carotene Content of Sweet Pumpkin Flour

The β‐carotene contents of the sweet pumpkin flour prepared by different heat treatments are shown in Table 1. The β‐carotene contents of OB and ST were 477.6 and 441.2 mg/100 g, respectively, which were higher than that found in NF (p < 0.05). The increase may be explained by the disruption of β‐carotene crystals or protein–β‐carotene complexes within plant matrices during thermal processing, which facilitates solvent extraction during analysis (Qian et al. 2012; Hwang et al. 2016). MW had a lower β‐carotene content (p < 0.05) than OB, ST, and NF, which was consistent with its lower b* value among the samples (Table 1). This could be simultaneously attributed to structural deformation and loosening of the β‐carotene‐protein complexes induced by microwave treatment. Previous research suggests that the structure of the carotenoid‐protein complexes in the chloroplast could be denatured due to excessive molecular motion in the food matrix during microwave treatment (Hart and Scott 1995; Majer et al. 2017). Similarly, H. Y. Kim et al. (2017) reported that microwave heating might induce nutrient loss (vitamin E and folic acid) in paprika and pepper. This suggests that microwave treatment had the lowest efficiency in preserving β‐carotene stability among the three processing methods used in this study. Gallardo et al. (2025) reported 13.89 mg/100 g for freeze‐dried pumpkin flour, and Ninčević Grassino et al. (2023) indicated that the β‐carotene content in pumpkin products varied depending on species, ranging from 1.2 µg/g to 123.2 µg/g. By comparison, the β‐carotene content observed in this study is substantially higher than these reported ranges. This discrepancy can plausibly be explained by genetic background (species/cultivar), pedoclimatic conditions (growing region, climate, soil), and physiological maturity at harvest, as well as analytical methodology. Gavril et al. (2024) also documented wide between‐study variability across cultivars and processing routes. Overall, these comparisons support the technological relevance of the oven‐baking treatment for carotenoid retention in sweet pumpkin flour.

Pasting Properties of Sweet Pumpkin Flour

The pasting properties of sweat pumpkin flour prepared by different heat treatments are presented in Table 2. All thermally treated sweet pumpkin flours had lower pasting temperatures than NF. Pasting temperature refers to the point at which viscosity begins to increase as water is absorbed during the pasting process. The starch granules in thermally treated flours were partially swollen and gelatinized during heating, which is attributed to a weakened granular structure. This structural change facilitated water absorption at lower temperatures, as shown in Figure 1.

Because of the relatively low total starch content in NF (31.9%; Table 1) compared to cereal and tuber starches (70–90%), NF exhibited a distinct pasting viscogram characterized by low pasting viscosity and the absence of breakdown (Figure 1). The pasting viscogram of OB was comparable to that of NF (Figure 1). Martínez and Gómez (2017) reported that the starch particles on the surface of baked bread were not fully gelatinized. This result may be related to the oven heating process, which could hinder complete starch gelatinization due to the rapid evaporation of moisture in the hot air (Martínez and Gómez 2017). Lu et al. (2020) reported that the peak and final viscosities of waxy rice flour were significantly decreased by oven baking due to the decreased starch swelling resulting from the limited moisture availability. The similarity in the pasting viscograms between OB and NF suggested that the starch in OB samples was not fully gelatinized during oven baking due to the moisture loss through evaporation (Figure 1). However, the higher pasting viscosity observed in OB compared to NF may be attributed to partial gelatinization and restricted moisture absorption in the amorphous region (Lee and Yang 2020). The pasting temperatures of ST and MW were 57.2 and 56.5°C, respectively, exhibiting characteristics of cold‐viscosity, which facilitates a rapid increase in viscosity at relatively low temperatures (Table 1). Furthermore, ST and MW demonstrated overall higher viscosity compared to the pasting profiles of NF and OB (Figure 1). Similarly, Liu et al. (2019) reported an increase in peak viscosity and a decrease in pasting temperature when oat flour was subjected to steaming with hot water. This result can be attributed to the effect of steaming, which promotes the formation of water‐soluble compounds by enabling greater interaction between moisture and starch molecules, thereby enhancing starch solubility and moisture absorption capacity. (Horrungsiwat et al. 2016).

In Vitro Digestibility of Sweet Pumpkin Flour

The DS, RS, and total starch contents of sweet pumpkin flour prepared by different heat treatment methods are presented in Table 1. The total starch content of NF (31.9%) was increased by heat treatment, with ST showing the highest value (34.5%) among the samples (p < 0.05) (Table 1). This increase might be attributed to the dissolution of the amylose lipid complex, which released starch components during the heat process. (Kim et al. 2018). The elevated total starch content in ST could be associated with the breakdown of water‐soluble components, as the heat‐treated samples required the highest moisture content during processing.

The DS contents of all the thermally treated sweet pumpkin flours were higher than that of NF (Table 1). This increase could be attributed to the partial gelatinization of starch in raw sweet pumpkin during heat treatments, which enhanced the enzymatic digestion rate compared to NF (Kim et al. 2017). Among the heat‐treated sweet pumpkin flours, ST exhibited the highest DS content at 23.1% (p < 0.05), followed by MW and OB at 20.2% and 19.7%, respectively (Table 1).

The RS content of NF was the highest at 13.2% (p < 0.05), whereas the heat‐treated sweet pumpkin flours exhibited slightly lower RS content, ranging from 11.4% to 12.9% (Table 1). This reduction could be attributed to the transformation of some RS fractions into digestible forms through gelatinization during thermal treatment (Kim et al. 2017). When starch is heated with sufficient water, hydrogen bonds between starch chains are disrupted, transforming the starch structure into a continuous amorphous region. These structural changes facilitate the access of digestive enzymes to the food, thereby increasing its digestibility (Kim et al. 2017).

In this study, the moisture in the sweet pumpkin promoted gelatinization during the heat treatment, altering the starch structure and thus accelerating the digestion rate. Yuan et al. (2022) reported that the RS content in sweet pumpkin was significantly reduced by up to 80% when gelatinized in boiling water for 1 h. However, in the present study, the RS content in sweet pumpkin flour was decreased marginally (3–14%) by thermal treatment and subsequent cooling (Table 1). This result could be explained by the formation of RS3, which increased during storage of gelatinized starch at refrigeration temperature, where retro gradation effectively occurred (Rahman et al. 2007). Refrigeration immediately after heat treatment facilitated the formation of retrograded starch, which exhibited resistance to enzymatic digestion. Yuan et al. (2022) also reported that the RS content increased to 11.8–20.2% when gelatinized starch was aged at 4°C for 48 h. In the present study, the RS content in OB did not differ significantly from that in NF, suggesting that OB exhibited the highest RS retention efficiency among the heat‐treated flour samples. This finding indicates that oven baking, followed by subsequent cooling, is an effective method to maintain the RS content in sweet pumpkin.

Another possible explanation for the retention of RS content during heat treatment was associated with the high pectin content in the sweet pumpkin peel. Aziah et al. (2011) reported that the RS contents of peeled and unpeeled flour were 6.4% and 8.8%, respectively, attributing this difference to the presence of pectin (Jun et al. 2006). Pectin is a polysaccharide composed of α‐(1→4)‐linked galacturonic acid units, which increases viscosity during human digestion and influences the in vitro digestibility of starch by limiting amylase access through molecular interactions (Bai et al. 2020). Consequently, sweet pumpkin flours that included peel might contribute to the higher RS content observed in this study. These results suggest that the incorporation of sweet pumpkin flour can increase the RS content and improve the overall nutritional and functional properties of food products.

Color Properties of Muffins and Noodles

The color properties of muffins and noodles containing treated pumpkin flour are presented in Table 2. Color is a critical quality parameter influencing consumer perception and acceptability, particularly of baked products. Pellegrini et al. (2010) reported that color characteristics are important determinants of sensory quality in food products. The control muffin exhibited the highest lightness (L*) value among the tested samples (p < 0.05). In contrast, the incorporation of sweet pumpkin flour significantly decreased the L* value but increased the a* and b* values. This result could be attributable to the presence of carotenoid pigments, which are inherently abundant in sweet pumpkin. Lee and Lee (2013) also reported that the L* value of jelly decreased and the a* and b* values increased as the amount of sweet pumpkin flour incorporated into the jelly increased.

The N30 muffin exhibited the highest a* and b* values (Table 2), likely due to the chromatic properties of the NF used, as previously described in Table 1. The color parameters of the P30 and GFP muffins were generally comparable (Table 2), indicating similar visual appearances. The similarity in color attributes between GFP muffins (rice flour‐based) and wheat‐based counterparts suggests that gluten‐free formulations may be equally acceptable in terms of visual quality for commercial use.

The color values of the noodles prepared with sweet pumpkin flour are presented in Table 2. Noodles formulated with sweet pumpkin flour exhibited significantly lower L* values and higher a* and b* values than the control (p < 0.05). This result indicated that the incorporation of sweet pumpkin flour substantially affected the color characteristics of noodles. As the substitution level of treated sweet pumpkin flour increased, the L* values decreased, and the a* and b* values increased (Table 2). Previous studies also demonstrated that the addition of sweet pumpkin to processed food products such as noodles, mayonnaise, cream soup, and sausages resulted in a decreased L* value and increased a* and b* values (Jeong et al. 2021; Park et al. 2015). This result might be due to caramelization during thermal processing, which is promoted by reducing sugars. Fructose, the most reactive among common sugars, is abundant in sweet pumpkin along with sucrose and glucose (Wang et al. 2020). Heat treatment of sweet pumpkin flour likely enhanced browning reactions, contributing to the intensified color of the noodles.

Physical and Cooking Properties of Muffins and Noodles

The height and specific volume of the muffins are presented in Figure 2, and the physical appearance of the muffins is shown in Supplementary Figure 1A. The control muffin exhibited the highest central and side height values among the tested samples at 53.8 and 37.4 mm, respectively. The specific volume of the control muffin was also substantially higher than that of the P30 and GFP muffins. The results demonstrated that gluten content had a substantial impact on the physical properties of baked products, particularly height, volume, and specific volume. During dough development, the major wheat storage proteins—gliadin and glutenin—interact through hydration and mechanical mixing to form a viscoelastic gluten network. This network, stabilized by hydrogen bonding and disulfide linkages, contributes to the structural integrity and gas retention capacity of the baked matrix (Ortolan and Steel 2017). Therefore, the greater height and specific volume observed in the control muffins were presumably attributable to the formation of a well‐developed gluten structure derived from wheat proteins, which promoted expansion during baking.

In contrast, the GFP muffin, prepared without wheat flour, exhibited the lowest central height (p < 0.05) due to the replacement of gluten with waxy rice flour and sweet pumpkin flour Sabanis et al. (2009) demonstrated that the addition of dietary fibers, such as cellulose, rice bran, and oat hulls, reduced the volume and specific volume of baked products. Therefore, the lower specific volume observed in the P30 and GFP muffins might be attributed to the relatively high dietary fiber content of sweet pumpkin flour. Interestingly, the N30 muffin exhibited significantly greater height and specific volume than the P30 and GFP muffins (p < 0.05) (Figure 2A). Roman and Martinez (2019) also reported that bread containing RS exhibited reduced specific volume and increased density, as RS interfered with the formation of a starch network during baking due to the inability of damaged starch granules to maintain structural integrity. Similarly, the presence of RS in the P30 and GFP muffins might hinder network formation, resulting in denser air cell structures than the control and N30 muffins (Figure S1).

Cooking loss and water absorption are key parameters for assessing noodle quality after cooking. In particular, cooking loss reflects the extent of solid matter leaching into the cooking water and indicates the strength of the internal bonding within the noodle matrix (Lei et al. 2021). The cooking loss (%) and water absorption (g/g) of sweet pumpkin noodles are presented in Figure 2B, and representative images are shown in Figure S1B.

The cooking loss of noodles containing sweet pumpkin flour was higher than that of the control and increased proportionally with the level of sweet pumpkin flour substitution. Previous studies also reported that the addition of non‐wheat powders such as sweet pumpkin flour, quinoa powder, potato flour or starch, and dietary fiber increased cooking loss in comparison to wheat noodles (Kang et al. 2017; Lei et al. 2021; Park et al. 2015; Seol and Sim 2017). The extent of cooking loss is largely influenced by the robustness of the gluten framework present in wheat‐based doughs (Lucisano et al. 2012). The partial substitution with sweet pumpkin flour reduced the gluten content, thereby weakening the gluten network and increasing the leaching of soluble components during cooking. However, the cooking loss of the N15 noodles was not significantly different from that of the control. This result was attributed to the use of NF, which gelatinized during cooking and formed swollen starch granules that effectively retained moisture, thereby reducing cooking loss. Giuberti et al. (2015) reported that cooking loss above 12% reduced noodle quality. In the present study, the maximum cooking loss was 10.39%, suggesting that all samples prepared with sweet pumpkin flour were within an acceptable range. These findings supported the potential applicability of sweet pumpkin flour in commercial noodle production.

The water absorption of noodles containing sweet pumpkin flour was significantly higher than that of the control (p < 0.05) (Figure 2B). This was consistent with previous studies reporting increased water absorption in noodles supplemented with sweet pumpkin flour, quinoa powder, potato flour, or insoluble dietary fiber (Park et al. 2015; Kang et al. 2017; Seol and Sim 2017; Lei et al. 2021). Water absorption is affected by gluten content, starch properties, RS, and protein hydration (Bae et al. 2016; Kang et al. 2017). The RS and protein components of treated sweet pumpkin flour likely contributed to the increased water absorption observed in noodles containing sweet pumpkin flour.

Textural Properties of Muffins and Noodles

The textural properties of muffins containing sweet pumpkin flour varied significantly depending on the heat pretreatment applied to the sweet pumpkin flour (Table 3). The control muffin exhibited the highest hardness (1058.9 g, p < 0.05), whereas muffins containing treated sweet pumpkin flours showed reduced hardness, ranging from 1000.6 to 734.1 g. This reduction could be attributed to the absence or dilution of gluten, which is essential for dough elasticity, air cell formation, and structural expansion during baking (Shewry et al. 1995). The lack of gluten hindered proper gas retention, resulting in lower hardness and less developed crumb structures (Figure S1A). Among the treated samples, the P30 and GFP muffins showed greater hardness than the N30 muffins (p < 0.05), which was likely due to the presence of retrograded starch in OB. Retrograded starch is known to increase structural rigidity due to the formation of recrystallized starch granules during baking (Morgan et al. 1997; Every et al. 1998; Chen et al. 2018). In contrast, the N30 muffin, formulated with NF, likely underwent gelatinization during baking, resulting in reduced hardness due to lower crystallinity. Although both P30 and GFP muffins contained OB, the GFP muffin exhibited lower hardness than the P30 muffin, possibly due to the absence of gluten and the pregelatinized starch, which might interfere with the formation of a cohesive matrix with rice flour and pumpkin fiber.

Adhesiveness was significantly lower in the P30 and GFP muffins compared to the control and N30 muffins (p < 0.05) (Table 3). This could be attributed to the higher starch content, which increased the moisture requirement for gelatinization, thereby reducing stickiness. The control muffin exhibited the highest cohesiveness, presumably due to the superior moisture retention capacity of gluten. The N30 muffin also showed relatively high cohesiveness due to the gelatinization of native starch and increased water absorption during baking. Springiness and cohesiveness were highest in the control muffin and significantly decreased in muffins containing sweet pumpkin flour (Table 3). This result was consistent with previous findings that the addition of legume or rice flours reduced springiness and cohesiveness by disrupting the gluten network (Bae et al. 2012; Park et al. 2012; Li et al. 2017). The GFP muffin showed the lowest values, reflecting the absence of gluten and the weakened structural network. Chewiness was also the highest in the control muffin, corresponding to its greater hardness, springiness, and cohesiveness among all the muffins. Overall, the variations in textural properties could be attributed to differences in gluten content, the presence of retrograded starch, and their combined effects on the structural integrity of the muffins.

The tensile strength and breaking distance of noodles prepared with sweet pumpkin flour are presented in Table 3. Control noodles exhibited the highest tensile strength (54.62 g) and breaking distance (98.12 mm), reflecting the formation of a well‐developed gluten network formed during dough preparation (Dobraszczyk and Roberts 1994; Barak et al. 2014). In comparison, the N15 noodles showed the lowest tensile strength (29.79 g) and breaking distance (19.78 mm) among the tested samples (p < 0.05), likely due to starch swelling and gelatinization from NF during cooking, which may have disrupted the formation of a dense gluten–starch matrix and weakened structural cohesion (Sahai et al. 2001; Hatcher et al. 2002). The tensile strength and breaking distance of the noodles decreased significantly with the increasing substitution level of OB (Table 3). These reductions might be mainly attributed to gluten dilution caused by the incorporation of sweet pumpkin flour. This decrease in the tensile properties of noodles containing OB corresponded with the increased cooking loss (Figure 2B). Lii and Chang (1981) claimed that higher cooking loss led to increased leaching of solids, thereby reducing the mechanical resistance of cooked noodles. Nevertheless, in the present study, OB provided better structural integrity than NF, suggesting its greater suitability for noodle applications.

In Vitro Digestibility of Muffins and Noodles

The total starch content and in vitro digestibility characteristics of sweet pumpkin muffins and noodles are presented in Table 2. All muffins and noodles containing sweet pumpkin flour had lower total starch contents than that of the control samples, primarily due to the lower total starch content of sweet pumpkin flour (31.9–34.5%) compared to wheat flour (70–75%) (Ktenioudaki et al. 2015). Consequently, the substitution with sweet pumpkin flour led to a notable reduction in total starch content in both muffins and noodles. Among the muffins containing sweet pumpkin flour, the GFP muffin showed a higher total starch content than the P30 and N30 muffins (p < 0.05). This result could be attributed to the use of rice flour, which contains a higher starch content (76–86%) than wheat flour (Syahariza et al. 2013). Furthermore, the OB muffins exhibited a higher total starch content than those made with NF (p < 0.05), reflecting the higher starch content of OB due to processing effects (Table 1). Similarly, among all the noodle samples analyzed, the control noodles had the highest total starch content (85.1%), which decreased progressively with increasing sweet pumpkin flour substitution. At the same substitution level, N15 noodles showed a lower total starch content than P15 noodles, resulting from the higher total starch content in OB compared to NF.

The muffins containing sweet pumpkin flour exhibited lower DS content than the control (Table 2). The reduction in DS could be associated with the amylose content in flour. Wheat flour contains a higher amylose content (∼21.7%) than rice flour (∼18.1%) (Gaines et al. 2000), and increased amylose is known to promote starch retro gradation, thereby reducing enzymatic digestibility (Shah et al. 2016). As a result, the P30 and N30 muffins containing wheat flour showed lower DS content compared to the GFP muffin (p < 0.05), which was formulated without wheat flour. This finding is consistent with the structural properties of starch, as a higher amylose content enhances molecular compactness and limits enzymatic accessibility. Similarly, the DS contents in noodles containing sweet pumpkin flour (79.1–83.6%) were lower than in the control noodles (83.6%). The DS content of noodles declined marginally with the increasing substitution level of OB.

The RS contents in both muffins and noodles increased with the substitution of sweet pumpkin flour (Table 2). Among the tested samples, the N30 and P30 muffins, and P15 and P20 noodles showed higher RS contents than the other samples of muffins and noodles (p < 0.05), respectively. This might be attributed to the inherent RS content of both NF and OB (Table 1). Amylose in starch plays a key role in RS formation through the development of B‐type crystalline structures and recrystallization by hydrogen bonding between linear chains (Shah et al. 2016; Naseer et al. 2021). In particular, OB, treated by heating and retro gradation, likely contributed to a slight increase in RS content. Consequently, adding sweet pumpkin flour to muffins and noodles can confer several nutritional advantages, such as slowing starch hydrolysis, moderating postprandial glycemic, promoting satiety, and potentially influencing gut health positively (Chung 2019; Kim 2022).

Sensory Properties of Muffins and Noodles

The sensory attributes of muffins containing sweet pumpkin flour are summarized in Table 4. Muffins supplemented with sweet pumpkin flour received significantly higher scores for yellowness, pumpkin odor, and grassy flavor compared to the control (p < 0.05). These results suggested that sweet pumpkin flour enhanced the muffin's color and flavor characteristics. Among the treated samples, the N30 muffin was perceived as the most yellowish, which was attributed to the greater color intensity of NF compared to OB (Table 1). Panelists also perceived a stronger pumpkin odor and grassy flavor in the N30 muffin, likely due to the presence of volatile aroma compounds retained in NF. In contrast, the P30 and GFP muffins containing OB received significantly lower scores for yellowish appearance, pumpkin odor, and grassy flavor than N30 muffins (p < 0.05), possibly due to the reduction in pigment and volatile compounds during heat treatment.

Muffins containing pumpkin flour were assigned slightly higher scores for sweetness compared with the control (p < 0.05), which could be attributed to the presence of simple sugars such as glucose, fructose, and sucrose in sweet pumpkin (Wang et al. 2020). Regarding the texture attributes, the control and P30 muffins received marginally higher hardness scores than the GFP and N30 muffins (Table 4), consistent with the instrumental hardness results (Table 3). The highest score for grainy texture was received by the N30 muffin, which could be associated with the presence of pulverized peel particles. The N30 muffin had a lower overall acceptance than the control. This result could be associated with its strong grassy flavor, pumpkin odor, and grainy texture, which might be typically perceived as undesirable. These three attributes were notably reduced in the muffin formulated with OB (p < 0.05). These findings suggest that the thermal treatment of sweet pumpkin flour was effective in improving the sensory profiles of muffins.

The sensory evaluation results of sweet pumpkin noodles are shown in Table 4. The sensory attributes of the noodles showed a trend similar to that observed in the muffins. Scores for yellowness, pumpkin odor, grassy flavor, and grainy texture were significantly higher in noodles containing sweet pumpkin flour compared to the control (p < 0.05). The scores for these attributes were significantly lower in noodles containing OB than those containing NF and slightly increased with increasing substitution levels of OB (p < 0.05) (Table 4). The highest scores for sweet pumpkin odor and grassy flavor observed in N15 noodles might be attributed to the retention of volatile compounds in NF. However, the typical undesirable grassy flavor from plant‐derived compounds could be reduced by applying heat treatment, as observed in OB.

All sweet pumpkin noodles scored significantly higher in sweetness compared to the control, with no notable differences observed between OB and NF formulations. The hardness score decreased with the addition of sweet pumpkin flour (p < 0.05). This reduction corresponded with decreased tensile strength and breaking distance, as described in the mechanical texture results (Table 3). The decline in elasticity was attributed to gluten dilution, while the swollen, ungelatinized starch particles in NF further interfered with gluten network formation (Sahai et al. 2001; Hatcher et al. 2002). Nevertheless, the hardness of noodles containing OB was perceived as significantly higher than that of N15 noodles (p < 0.05). The hardness score decreased with the increase in OB substitution due to gluten dilution (Table 4). Park et al. (2015) also reported that consumer preference for noodles was improved by adding sweet pumpkin flour. Although a consumer acceptance test was not conducted in this study, the observed improvements in the sensory profile with sweet pumpkin flour suggest potential gains in consumer liking. In conclusion, oven‐based heat treatment of sweet pumpkin flour effectively reduced off‐flavors and improved noodle texture, supporting its application as a functional and commercially viable ingredient in processed foods.

Conclusions

Sweet pumpkin flour was treated by oven baking, microwaving, and steaming to develop a RTE ingredient with improved nutritional functionality and industrial applicability. OB exhibited enhanced β‐carotene retention, RS content retention, and improved color stability compared to NF. Muffins and noodles formulated with OB showed improved color and sweetness compared to their control counterparts. In particular, OB was perceived to reduce undesirable grassy flavor and improve the overall sensory profile, whereas muffins and noodles made with NF were characterized by stronger off‐flavors. These findings suggest that RTE sweet pumpkin flour produced by oven baking can serve as a functional ingredient in processed foods. Moreover, gluten‐free muffins demonstrated physicochemical and sensory qualities comparable to those made with wheat flour, indicating their potential for commercial application.
