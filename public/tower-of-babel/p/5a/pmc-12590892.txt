Chronic kidney disease in Romania: national adjusted prevalence, risk factors, and temporal trends in high-risk groups
Authors: Gabriel Ștefan, Octavian Andronic, Mihaela Daniela Balta, Ana Maria Alexandra Stănescu, Ștefan Busnatu, Viorel Jinga, Gabriel Mircescu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic kidney disease (CKD) is a growing global health concern, associated with substantial morbidity and mortality. In Romania, national-level data on CKD prevalence remain limited. Background

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is globally recognized as a significant public health challenge, affecting approximately 10% of the global adult population. Moreover, CKD is associated with substantial morbidity and mortality [1, 2]. CKD prevalence is rapidly increasing worldwide, driven primarily by population aging, diabetes mellitus (DM), arterial hypertension (HTA), and rising obesity rates [3, 4]. In Europe, CKD prevalence considerably varies, ranging from 3.3% to 17.3% across different countries, reflecting differences in population structure, comorbidity burden, laboratory assessment, and application of diagnostic or chronicity criteria [5]. While previous data from Romania indicated a CKD prevalence of 6.74% based on national estimates from the PREDATORR study (2012–2014), detailed and comprehensive epidemiological data remain scarce [6].

Understanding CKD epidemiology is crucial, as early detection and appropriate management can slow progression and significantly reduce cardiovascular and renal complications [7, 8]. Furthermore, targeted screening among high-risk individuals, particularly those with DM, HTA, or cardiovascular diseases (CVD), has been increasingly advocated internationally, demonstrating clinical benefit and cost-effectiveness [9–11].

The aim of this study was to evaluate the prevalence and the risk factors of CKD in Romania using two cohorts: a cohort from general population screened in 2007–2008 and a cohort of subjects at risk of CKD (with HTA, DM, or CVD) screened in 2023. In addition to estimating the standardized national prevalence of CKD, we retrospectively applied the screening criteria used in 2023 to the 2007–2008 general population cohort to assess the distribution and temporal trends in CKD in patients at risk of CKD.

Materials and methods

We analyzed two independent cohorts to evaluate the prevalence and risk factors of CKD in Romania. The first cohort – general population cohort - was derived from the 2007–2008 National Health Survey in Iassy region and includes adults with complete clinical and laboratory data. The second cohort – targeted cohort screened - included patients at risk for CKD, identified by a history of DM, HTA, or CVD, screened in 2023 by a structured program, in the Bucharest-Ilfov region.

General population screening study

Study design and population

This cohort was derived from the National Health Survey (NHS) conducted by the Romanian Ministry of Health in 2007–2008, in the Romanian general population. General practitioners performed standardized clinical and laboratory assessments, and data were entered into a central anonymized database. For this analysis, we selected data from Iassy region. From 176,586 records, we included adults (> 22 years) with complete clinical and laboratory (serum creatinine and proteinuria) data, yielding a final sample of 26,831 individuals.

Data collection

Collected data included demographics, smoking status, personal and family history of DM, CVD or kidney disease, body mass index, and blood pressure. Serum creatinine was measured using an enzymatic assay traceable to isotope dilution mass spectrometry, and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using the CKD-EPI 2009 formula. Proteinuria was measured either quantitatively (g/g creatinine) or qualitatively (dipstick), and classified per KDIGO into A1 (absent proteinuria, trace, or less than 15 mg/g creatinine), A2 (between 1 + and 2 + or 15 to 50 mg/g creatinine), and A3 (greater than 2 + or over 50 mg/g creatinine). Both CKD markers were measured once.

CKD definition

CKD was defined as eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m² and/or proteinuria ≥ A2, and risk was stratified according to KDIGO categories.

To evaluate temporal trends in CKD among individuals at risk of CKD, we retrospectively applied the 2023 targeted screening criteria (presence of HTA, DM, or CVD) to the 2007–2008 general population cohort. This allowed us to compare CKD burden in similar risk groups across both time points.

Prevalence standardization

To estimate the national prevalence of CKD, we applied direct standardization based on age, sex, and residency (urban/rural), using population data obtained from the Romanian National Institute of Statistics via the Tempo Online database (http://statistici.insse.ro:8077/tempo-online).

We calculated the expected number of CKD cases by multiplying subgroup-specific prevalence rates from our cohort by the corresponding subgroup sizes from national census data. Target population screening study

A cross-sectional, population-based screening program was conducted to evaluate the prevalence and risk stratification of CKD in Romania. The study was coordinated by the “Carol Davila” University of Medicine and Pharmacy through the “Împreună pentru Sănătate” Foundation, in partnership with AstraZeneca. The protocol was approved by the institutional ethics committee (“Carol Davila” University of Medicine and Pharmacy Ethics Committee for PExBCR study - “Program Expert de management al BCR în Medicina de familie” No 22/11/2022).

Participants and setting

The study was conducted in 2023 and involved approximately 100 general practitioners (GPs) from Bucharest who use the IcMED electronic medical record system, covering a target population of 150,000–225,000 adults from Bucharest-Ilfov region. All patients registered on participating GP lists were considered for evaluation.

Targeted screening strategy

A targeted screening approach was employed, focusing on patients with known DM, HTA, and CVD. These individuals were identified using ICD-10 diagnostic codes and selected automatically by a custom software integrated with the IcMED platform.

Screening procedure

The diagnostic process involved up to three structured clinical consultations:

Initial Visit (Consultation I): Blood pressure measurement, medication history, and referral for laboratory testing (serum creatinine to eGFR using CKD-EPI 2009 formula and urine albumin/creatinine ratio [ACR]).

Initial Visit (Consultation I): Blood pressure measurement, medication history, and referral for laboratory testing (serum creatinine to eGFR using CKD-EPI 2009 formula and urine albumin/creatinine ratio [ACR]).

Follow-Up Visit (Consultation II): Review of laboratory results, CKD risk stratification based on KDIGO guidelines, and initiation of lifestyle and pharmacologic interventions. Patients with suspected moderate-to-high risk CKD were either re-tested or referred to nephrology, as appropriate.

Follow-Up Visit (Consultation II): Review of laboratory results, CKD risk stratification based on KDIGO guidelines, and initiation of lifestyle and pharmacologic interventions. Patients with suspected moderate-to-high risk CKD were either re-tested or referred to nephrology, as appropriate.

Confirmation Visit (Consultation III): Conducted 3 months later for moderate-risk patients, this visit confirmed CKD diagnosis via repeat serum creatinine measurement, in line with the KDIGO definition requiring persistence over ≥ 3 months and finalized classification.

Data collection

Demographic and clinical and laboratory data were collected and recorded in the electronic platform: age, sex, blood pressure, comorbidities, antihypertensive and antidiabetic medications, eGFR, ACR, and primary kidney diagnosis. The expert software supported physicians by automatically calculating CKD risk class and issuing alerts for incomplete data or clinically significant findings.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the prevalence of CKD, defined as persistent reduction in eGFR (< 60 mL/min/1.73 m²) and/or high albuminuria (ACR ≥ 30 mg/g) confirmed in at least two separate assessments (≥ 3 months). Secondary outcomes included distribution in KDIGO-defined CKD risk categories (low, moderate, high, very high; KDIGO risk categories indicate the risk of CKD progression, based on eGFR and albuminuria) and their relationship with comorbidities.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables are presented as percentages, with comparisons performed using the χ² test. Continuous variables are displayed as means or medians with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI), depending on their distribution, and compared by either the Student’s t-test or the Kruskal-Wallis test, as appropriate.

Descriptive statistics were used to report CKD prevalence and risk category distributions by age and sex. Logistic regression models were employed to assess independent predictors of CKD among the target populations. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data analysis was conducted using SPSS statistical software.

Results

Chronic kidney disease in Romanian

CKD prevalence in the general population

The general population cohort included 26,831 individuals with a mean age of 57 years; notably, one-third were above the age of 65 years. The sex distribution was balanced, with a ratio near parity. Over half of the subjects resided in rural areas. Obesity was observed in a quarter of the cohort. Prevalence rates for DM, HTA, and CVD were 7%, 46%, and 11%, respectively (Table 1).

A total of 3,633 individuals in the general population cohort had CKD, corresponding to an unadjusted prevalence of 13.5%.

Standardized national CKD prevalence was calculated by applying age-, sex-, and residency-specific prevalence rates from the general population cohort to the corresponding Romanian population subgroups. The detailed distribution of these strata, including expected CKD cases and subgroup-specific prevalence percentages, is presented in Supplementary Table 1. CKD prevalence increased progressively with age across all demographic strata. Among adults aged 25–34, rates ranged from 1.1% to 3.0%, while in those aged 65–74 reached 13.9–25.1%, and in individuals aged 75 and above, exceeded 24% in all groups, peaking at 44.6% in urban women. Across most age strata, prevalence was higher in rural residents and in females, particularly in older age groups (Supplementary Table 1).

Based on direct standardization for age, sex, and residency, the adjusted national prevalence of CKD was 8.97%. Most individuals fell within the moderate (6.21%) and high (2.10%) KDIGO risk categories, while 1.00% were classified as very high risk. CKD was most frequently observed in the G3a-A1, G3a-A2, and G2-A2 categories (Fig. 1).

CKD risk factors in the general population

Subjects in the general cohort with CKD were generally older, more likely to live in rural areas, and less likely to smoke compared to their non-CKD counterparts. They also had higher BMI and a greater prevalence of obesity. Diabetes mellitus was more common in the CKD group. Additionally, this group had higher systolic and diastolic blood pressures, with a more significant proportion of HTA. Cardiovascular disease occurrences were notably higher among those with CKD (Table 1).

In the binomial regression analysis, CKD was linked to increased age, being of female sex, and having a higher body mass index. Diabetes mellitus raised the risk of CKD by 17%, while HTA and CVD were associated with 30% and 46% higher risks, respectively (Table 2).

Trends in CKD prevalence

To evaluate trends, we selected out of the 26,831 subjects included in the general population cohort 15,373 (57%) which met at least one of the target screening criteria (HTA, DM, or CVD). We used this at-risk of CKD subgroup for comparison with the 2,247 patients included in the target cohort screened in 2023.

Off note, the CKD definition differed: in the 2007–2008 cohort it relied on a single measurement of eGFR and proteinuria, whereas in the 2023 targeted screening cohort it required confirmation by repeat testing after three months.

Participants in the targeted screening cohort were older (median age 69 vs. 63 years), predominantly urban residents, and included fewer men compared to those in the general population cohort sample. The prevalence of HTA was lower (89% vs. 95%), whereas the prevalence of DM was higher in the targeted cohort than in the at-risk sample from the general population cohort (18% vs. 13%) (Table 4).

Discussion

This study provides the most comprehensive assessment to date of chronic kidney disease epidemiology in Romania, combining general population data from 2007 to 2008 with targeted screening in at risk of CKD individuals in 2023. The findings demonstrate a high and increasing burden of CKD, particularly among older adults and individuals with DM, HTA, or CVD.

The adjusted national prevalence of CKD in Romania was 8.97% (≈ 1.5 million persons), closely matching global estimates. The ISN Global Kidney Health Atlas (2023) reported that over 850 million people—around 10% of the world’s population—live with CKD, while the GBD 2017 study estimated a prevalence of 9.1%, rising to 13.4% in meta-analyses that included early stages (G1–G2) [1–3].

Romania’s adjusted CKD prevalence of 8.97% is comparable to European averages (9–12%) and slightly lower than recent U.S. estimates [4, 5]. CDC/NHANES data from 2017 to 2020 reported a prevalence of 13.9% among U.S. adults, with 11.5–16 million individuals in stages G3–G4 [4]. The higher U.S. rate may reflect methodological factors—such as the lack of confirmatory testing, which overestimates prevalence compared with guideline-based definitions requiring persistence—as well as population-level differences in obesity and DM [4]. Within Europe, prevalence ranges widely, from 3.3% in Norway to 17.3% in Germany, with Romania situated in the mid-range [5].

The PREDATORR study, conducted in Romania between 2012 and 2014, reported an age- and sex-adjusted CKD prevalence of 6.74%, which is slightly lower than the 8.97% estimated in our 2007–2008 cohort. This discrepancy likely reflects differences in study design, population structure, or CKD definitions, rather than a true change in disease burden over time [6]. For example, although CKD was defined as eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m² and/or albuminuria ≥ 30 mg/g, most participants were included in low or moderate KDIGO risk categories (G1-G2/A1), i.e. they had normal or mildly decreased eGFR without albuminuria. This appears inconsistent with the CKD definition used and suggests that the risk stratification may reflect the entire study population, not only those meeting CKD criteria, potentially leading to confusion in interpreting the reported prevalence [6].

Advanced age, sex DM, and HTA were the strongest independent predictors of CKD in the general population, consistent with prior research [9]. Age is a key driver of CKD burden. In our cohort, prevalence rose sharply after 65 years, with over one-third of Romanian CKD patients in this age group. This pattern mirrors European data, such as the Berlin Initiative Study (>60% of Germans ≥ 70 years old with eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m²) and Dutch data (>40% of those ≥ 85 years old with reduced eGFR) [7, 8].

In Romania, female sex and rural residency were also associated with higher CKD risk in univariate analysis, though the latter lost significance after adjustment. This suggests that social determinants, such as access to care and health literacy, may influence CKD risk more than geographic location alone [10, 12]. Moreover, the observed sex pattern is similar to European and US findings: CKD is more prevalent among women in terms of age-standardized prevalence, but men are more likely to progress to kidney failure and ESKD [1, 13, 14]. This could be due to faster CKD progression in men and under-recognition of CKD in women [13, 14].

In our study, obesity was more frequent among CKD patients, but its impact was mainly mediated through DM, HTA, and CVD, consistent with evidence that obesity amplifies CKD risk primarily in combination with metabolic comorbidities [15, 16].

In line with other studies, CKD prevalence in at-risk patients rose from 18% to 27% over ten years, reflecting aging, longer exposure to comorbidities, and greater chronic disease burden. Most patients were moderate risk by KDIGO criteria (KDIGO risk categories indicate the risk of CKD progression), but the proportion at very high risk increased from 7.7% to 10.5%, indicating later-stage diagnosis and underscoring the need for earlier detection. Part of this rise may also reflect improved GP awareness. Similar trends have been observed in Southeast Asia and Latin America, where aging and undetected risk factors contribute to advanced disease at presentation [12, 17].

Diabetes mellitus is the leading global cause of CKD and ESKD, with up to 40% of patients affected. Although annual CKD screening in DM is cost-effective and may cut progression by 40%, fewer than 40% of patients receive appropriate testing [18–22]. Similarly, HTA is common in CKD, with over 20% of hypertensives affected, and even high-normal blood pressure increases ESKD risk; and annual screening in this group is cost-effective [23–26]. Cardiovascular disease also raises CKD prevalence from 12% to 40%, and despite confounding by DM and HTA, the strong bidirectional link supports including CVD patients in screening programs [24, 27].

In our study, targeted screening more than doubled the efficiency of CKD detection, with 4 individuals screened per case identified versus 7 in the general population (OR 2.4; 95% CI 2.1–2.6). International evidence supports such risk-based strategies: a systematic review by Yeo et al. found CKD screening cost-effective in DM, HTA, or high-risk ethnic groups, with incremental cost-effectiveness ratios often below $30,000 per QALY [28]. Our 2023 program, focused on these groups, aligns with best practice guidelines and is likely cost-effective given the high prevalence and potential for early intervention [11]. By contrast, the 2024 KDIGO consensus emphasized that while CKD meets most WHO screening criteria, universal screening may not be cost-effective in low- and middle-income countries [11, 29]. Our findings therefore support prioritizing risk-based screening as the most efficient approach in Romania.

This study has several limitations. Its cross-sectional design precludes causal inference or assessment of CKD progression. The 2007–2008 general population cohort, drawn from the Iassy region, may not fully represent the Romanian population despite its large size and standardization. CKD diagnosis in this cohort relied on single measurements of creatinine and proteinuria, potentially overestimating prevalence, whereas the 2023 targeted cohort used repeat testing for confirmation, yielding more robust estimates. Thus, the apparent increase in CKD prevalence among at-risk populations should be interpreted with caution. Finally, differences in laboratory methods, GP awareness, diagnostic criteria, and population structure may complicate direct comparisons with each other and with studies such as PREDATORR.

Conclusions

This study provides the most comprehensive assessment of CKD epidemiology in Romania, confirming its major public health impact. National prevalence is ∼ 9%, consistent with European and global estimates, but rises to 27% in populations with DM, HTA, or CVD. The observed increase in prevalence and severity over time underscores the urgent need for earlier detection and proactive management. Our findings support expanding targeted screening in high-risk groups as a cost-effective strategy to reduce progression and complications.
