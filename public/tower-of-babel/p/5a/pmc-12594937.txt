Metabolomic and network Pharmacology insights into the anti-inflammatory and anti-urolithiatic potential of Ficus religiosa seeds
Authors: Felicity Pinipay, Rajesh Rokkam, Satyanarayana Botcha, Raghava Rao Tamanam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractUrolithiasis remains a persistent global health challenge with limited therapeutic options and high recurrence rates. The present study investigated the anti-inflammatory and anti-urolithiatic potential of Ficus religiosa seed extracts through a comprehensive experimental and computational framework. In vitro assays demonstrated that the ethyl acetate extract significantly inhibited red blood cell hemolysis (IC50: 346.63 ± 1.303 µg/ml) and protein denaturation (IC50: 524.10 ± 1.29 µg/ml), highlighting strong anti-inflammatory effects comparable to diclofenac and acetylsalicylic acid. In the semipermeable membrane model, the ethyl acetate extract exhibited dose-dependent calcium oxalate dissolution, achieving 31.61 ± 0.06% to 84.56 ± 0.03% from 100 to 500 µg/ml with an IC₅₀ of 179.99 ± 0.34 µg/ml, demonstrating efficacy comparable to that of the reference drug Cystone and surpassing it at higher concentrations. Metabolomic profiling by GC-MS and LC-MS identified 151 phytoconstituents, including flavonoids (quercetin 3′-methyl ether, apigenin), phytosterols (4-cholestenone), and fatty acid esters. Multivariate analyses confirmed solvent-specific clustering, with flavonoid-rich extracts showing superior activity. Network pharmacology integrated these findings, revealing 173 overlapping targets between the identified phytoconstituents and urolithiasis and/or inflammation-associated genes. Functional enrichment analysis highlighted the PI3K-AKT, MAPK, NF-κB, and calcium signaling pathways as key modulatory axes. Molecular docking further validated the high binding affinities of flavonoids and phytosterols with central regulatory proteins such as PI3K, AKT1, IKKβ, MMP-9, and CaMKII, supporting their roles in inflammatory suppression and extracellular matrix remodeling. Overall, this study validates the ethnomedicinal use of Ficus religiosa seeds for the treatment of urinary and inflammatory disorders and highlights their bioactive, multitarget therapeutic potential for managing urolithiasis, warranting further preclinical and clinical evaluation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-26906-0.Keywords: Ficus religiosa seed extracts, Urolithiasis, Anti-inflammatory, Network pharmacology, Molecular dockingSubject terms: Biochemistry, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Drug discovery

Introduction

The exploration of natural resources for therapeutic applications remains a cornerstone of modern pharmacological research, with plant-derived medicines continuing to offer valuable leads for novel drug discovery. The growing global preference for herbal remedies over synthetic pharmaceuticals reflects increasing public confidence in the safety and efficacy of phytotherapeutics1. Urolithiasis, defined as the pathological formation of calcified deposits within the urinary tract, constitutes a substantial global health burden, with incidence rates reported to have increased by 48.57% between 1990 and 20192. The underlying pathophysiology involves the supersaturation of urine with insoluble substances, leading to crystal nucleation, aggregation, and subsequent stone formation, predominantly calcium oxalate, which accounts for 75–90% of reported cases3. Elevated urinary calcium levels induce the generation and release of reactive oxygen species (ROS) within renal cellular environments, activating inflammatory signaling cascades such as NF-κB and MAPK in renal epithelial cells and thereby promoting the development of nephrolithiasis4. The associated oxidative stress and inflammation further exacerbate stone pathogenesis, contributing to high recurrence rates exceeding 50% within five to ten years following initial treatment5.

Recent clinical and molecular studies underscore the central role of inflammation in both the initiation and progression of kidney stone disease. In a bidirectional Mendelian randomization study, certain inflammatory factors such as IL-8, SCGF-β, MIP-1α, and GRO-α showed causal association with an increased risk of urinary tract stone formation, suggesting a feedback loop between inflammation and stone development6. Another investigation into calcium oxalate nephrolithiasis revealed that cell-crystal interactions trigger renal epithelial injury, induce expression of NADPH oxidase, generate ROS, activate nuclear factor-κB signaling, and promote a cascade of inflammatory mediators including IL-1β, TNF-α, and others, culminating in crystal aggregation and growth3. Such evidences have prompted research, focusing on antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects of medicinal plants in urolithiasis. Many studies indicate that plant polyphenols, flavonoids, tannins, and other phytochemicals can modulate inflammation via suppression of NF-κB activation or upregulation of antioxidant defenses7,8.

Conventional therapeutic strategies, including surgical intervention, lithotripsy, and pharmacological management, remain are limited by their high costs, potential renal injury, and limited ability to prevent recurrence9. Cystone, a commercially available polyherbal formulation containing multiple plant-derived constituents, has been marketed for the treatment of urolithiasis for over six decades10. However, despite extensive global research efforts, conclusive evidence substantiating the definitive therapeutic efficacy of cystone remains elusive, underscoring the urgent need for novel phytotherapeutic interventions with experimentally validated mechanisms of action11. Currently, the clinical management of urolithiasis largely involves symptomatic relief. Anti-inflammatory drugs are administered to alleviate pain and inflammation resulting from stone obstruction, whereas surgical removal and antibiotic therapy are employed when clinically indicated. To date, no pharmaceutical agent has demonstrated the ability to act as a multitarget therapeutic against urolithiasis or achieve complete stone dissolution12.

Since inflammation plays a pivotal role in stone pathogenesis, anti-inflammatory agents with multitarget potential could provide an effective strategy for both symptom relief and prevention of stone formation. This therapeutic gap has stimulated interest in the Ficus genus of the Moraceae family, which exhibits extensive ethnomedicinal use in the treatment of urological disorders. Several species, including F. carica, F. racemosa, F. religiosa, and F. palmata, have shown documented antiurolithiatic properties in preclinical studies13,14. Among these, F. religiosa (commonly known as the Peepal tree), a species highly used in traditional ayurvedic medicine, has long been utilized for treating urinary disorders, inflammatory conditions, and metabolic abnormalities. Ethnobotanical records highlight its traditional use in managing kidney stones, urinary tract infections, and related inflammatory disorders. Recent pharmacological investigations have validated its antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and nephroprotective properties, attributing these effects to a range of bioactive constituents, including flavonoids, phenolic acids, and triterpenes15.

Despite this long history of use and supportive preliminary pharmacological data, comprehensive scientific evaluations of F. religiosa seed extracts for their antiurolithiatic and associated anti-inflammatory activities remain scarce. The present study builds on previous research by employing an integrative, multi-omics approach that combines gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) and liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) based metabolomics to characterize both volatile and nonvolatile bioactives of F. religiosa seeds. Advanced metabolomics analysis was conducted using MetaboAnalyst 6.0 to analyze the chromatographic data, providing a detailed overview of the metabolite composition. This was followed by network pharmacology and molecular docking analyses to characterize the phytochemical profiles of the seed extracts, identify key metabolites, and elucidate compound-target interactions involved in inflammatory pathways associated with urolithiasis.

The study aims to assess in-vitro anti-inflammatory and antiurolithiatic properties of F. religiosa seed extracts by integrating these analytical and computational approaches to bridge, the existing knowledge gaps regarding their bioactive components and mechanisms of action. This integrative strategy may offer robust scientific validation for the traditional therapeutic applications of F. religiosa seeds and may help identify potential bioactive markers for future standardization and drug development. Furthermore, it could also assist in predicting molecular targets and elucidating their roles in inflammation associated with urolithiasis.

Materials and methods

Materials

All organic solvents of analytical grade, including n-hexane, chloroform, ethyl acetate, methanol, and water (H₂O) (Milli-Q ultrapure), were procured from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany). High-purity reagents used for buffer preparation and analytical studies, such as sodium phosphate monobasic, sodium phosphate dibasic, potassium permanganate (KMnO₄), and formic acid, were also obtained from Merck. Diclofenac sodium and acetylsalicylic acid, which were used as positive control drugs for anti-inflammatory assays, were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA).Cystone tablets (used as the reference standard in antiurolithiatic assays) were procured from a local pharmacy (manufactured by Himalaya Wellness Company, India). All routine laboratory-grade chemicals such as sodium chloride (NaCl), sodium oxalate, calcium chloride dihydrate (CaCl₂·2 H₂O), sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄), ammonia solution (NH₃), and sodium oxalate were purchased from SRL Chemicals (Mumbai, India).

Seed collection

Figs of F. religiosa were collected from the vicinity of Andhra University, Visakhapatnam (17°43’47.3880’’ N, 83°19’17.3820’’ E), in compliance with relevant local, national and international guidelines. The plant was identified via local flora and authenticated by Prof. S.B. Padal, a taxonomist in the Department of Botany, Andhra University. A voucher specimen (No. 24002) was deposited at Andhra University for future reference. Seeds were manually separated from fresh figs, thoroughly cleaned, and shade-dried. The dried seeds were ground into a fine powder via a laboratory-grade mechanical blender and passed through a fine mesh to ensure uniform particle size. The powder was further shade-dried to remove residual moisture. Finally, the processed seed powder was stored in airtight containers under aseptic conditions at room temperature, until use in extraction procedures.

Sample preparation

The sequential extraction of F. religiosa seed powder was carried out via a Soxhlet apparatus with organic solvents of increasing polarity on the basis of their polarity indices: hexane (0.1), chloroform (4.1), ethyl acetate (4.4), and methanol (5.1). A total of 450 g of seed powder was initially extracted with hexane at 70 °C. The remaining residue was successively extracted with chloroform at 61.2 °C, ethyl acetate at 77 °C, and methanol at 64.7 °C for 14 h each. The resulting extracts were concentrated via solvent evaporation via distillation, and then further dried and stored in a desiccator at room temperature for future analysis16. For the aqueous extract, 10 g of F. religiosa seed powder was mixed in 100 ml of distilled water, subjected to magnetic stirring for 10 min and then heated to 80 °C for 30 min. The obtained mixture was filtered through Whatman No.1 filter paper and then stored at −4 °C until further use.

In-vitro anti-inflammatory activity

Preparation of RBC suspensions

Blood was collected from a single healthy human volunteer with no recent infectious disease and who had not taken any NSAIDs (nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs). Collection was performed by a trained professional, with written informed consent obtained from donor beforehand. The sample was washed repeatedly with sterile saline solution (0.9% NaCl, w/v), followed by centrifugation at 2500 rpm for 5 min after each wash, until the supernatant was clear. The packed cell volume (PCV) obtained was reconstituted in phosphate-buffered saline (10 mM, pH 7.4) to yield a 40% (v/v) red blood cell (RBC) suspension. This suspension was used for subsequent hemolysis assays. The study using human blood samples was approved by the Institutional ethics committee of Andhra University, College of Pharmaceutical Sciences (Ref. No. IEC 57/25-04-2022). All procedures were carried out in compliance with relevant guidelines and regulations, including the declaration of Helsinki (1975, revised 1997).

Heat-induced hemolysis

Heat-induced hemolysis was assessed via duplicate sets of seed extract samples prepared in 5 mL of isotonic solution (154 mM NaCl, 10 mM sodium phosphate buffer, pH 7.4) at concentrations ranging from 50 to 1000 µg/mL (w/v). The negative controls included an isotonic solution alone, whereas the positive controls included diclofenac sodium and acetylsalicylic acid (50–1000 µg/mL). To each tube, 50 µL of a 40% RBC suspension was added, and the mixture was mixed gently. One set of samples was incubated at 54 °C for 20 min in a water bath, whereas the other set was maintained at 5 °C in an ice bath17. Hypotonicity-induced hemolysis

Hypotonicity-induced hemolysis was evaluated via the use of seed extract solutions (50–1000 µg/mL, w/v) prepared in both isotonic and hypotonic media. The hypotonic solution contained 3.6 g NaCl in 10 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.4). The negative controls consisted of a hypotonic solution without extract, whereas the positive controls included diclofenac sodium and acetylsalicylic acid (50–1000 µg/mL) prepared in both isotonic and hypotonic solutions. Each tube received 50 µL of a 40% RBC suspension, was vortexed, and incubated at room temperature for 10 min. Samples were centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 5 min, and the absorbance was recorded at 540 nm17. Egg albumin denaturation assay

The inhibition of protein denaturation was assessed via the use of egg albumin extracted from fresh hen’s egg, obtained from a local farm. Briefly, 0.2 mL of freshly collected egg albumin was mixed with 2 mL of seed extracts prepared in distilled water (50–1000 µg/mL, w/v), along with 2.8 mL of phosphate buffer (pH 6.4). Distilled water served as the negative control, whereas diclofenac sodium and acetylsalicylic acid were used as positive controls. The samples were incubated at 37.2 °C for 15 min, followed by heating at 70 °C for 5 min18. In-vitro anti-urolithiasis activity

The in vitro anti-urolithiasis activity was assessed using membranes isolated from freshly obtained hen eggs from a local farm, serving as a model for stone formation studies. Eggshells were emptied, rinsed, and decalcified overnight by soaking in dilute HCl (4 mL in 200 mL distilled water). The resulting membranes were separated, washed, neutralized with ammonia, rinsed again, and stored moist at pH 7–7.4.4 under refrigeration (4–6 °C)19. The negative control consisted of 10 mg calcium oxalate in 10 mL distilled water; the test solution contained ethyl acetate seed extract of F. religiosa (100, 200, 300, 400 and 500 µg/ml); and the positive control consisted of cystone tablets (400 mg of decoated core dissolved in water, filtered, and diluted to 100–500 µg/mL). Calcium oxalate was synthesized by mixing CaCl₂·2 H₂O (1.47 g/100 mL) with sodium oxalate (1.34 g/100 mL in 2 N H₂SO₄), and the precipitate was washed with diluted NH₃ solution, rinsed, and dried at 60 °C for 2 h. Equal volumes (1 mL) of calcium oxalate suspension (1 mg/mL) and each treatment (distilled water, cystone, or extract) were enclosed in the prepared egg membranes, sealed, and suspended in 150 mL of 0.1 M Tris buffer at 37 °C for 3 days. After incubation, the contents of each egg pouch were treated with 4 mL of 1 N H₂SO₄ and 80 µL of 0.02 M KMnO₄, and the decrease in pink color after 2 h was measured spectrophotometrically at 620 nm to determine the residual calcium oxalate content via a standard curve (25–125 µg/mL; y = 0.001x + 0.0177).

Molecular characterization

Sample preparation

For molecular characterization analysis, 100 µg of each seed extract was dissolved in 1 mL of its corresponding solvent, vortexed for 10 s, and filtered through a 0.2-micron membrane filter. The clear filtrates were then subjected to GC-MS and LC-MS analysis to profile the metabolites present in each extract.

Characterization of volatile metabolites via GC-MS

GC-MS analysis of F. religiosa extracts was performed via a Shimadzu QP 2010 Ultra GC-MS system (Agilent Technologies), equipped with a DB-5MS column (5% phenyl methyl siloxane; 30 m length, 0.25 mm inner diameter, 0.25 μm film thickness). A 1 µL sample was injected via an autosampler in standard injection mode (Agilent, Santa Clara, USA). The elution programme began at 70 °C (held for 5 min), and increased to 310 °C at 10 °C per minute, over a total run time of 63 min. The injector and ion source temperatures were set to 250 °C and 200 °C, respectively. Helium served as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. Following a 4.5-minute solvent delay, mass spectra were acquired via full scan and monitoring modes within a mass range of 50–700 m/z. This setup enabled the precise detection and identification of volatile metabolites in the extracts20.

Characterization of nonvolatile metabolites via LC-MS

The nonvolatile metabolite profiles of the F. religiosa seed extracts were analyzed via an ExionLC AD uHPLC system coupled with a Sciex 5500 + LC/MS-QQQ mass spectrometer. Chromatographic separation was carried out on a Shim-pack UFLC SHIMADZU CBM20A HPLC system connected to an Applied Biosystems 5500 + Q TRAP mass spectrometer. A Shimadzu InerSustain AQ-C18 column (150 mm × 4.6 mm, 5 μm) was used for the separation process. The mobile phase, comprised solvent A (0.1% formic acid in ultrapure water) and solvent B (HPLC-grade methanol), with a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min. The gradient elution program was as follows: 0–5 min (3% B), 5–25 min (3–80% B), 25–45 min (80–98% B), 45–50 min (98% B), 50–55 min (98 − 3% B), and 55–60 min (3 − 0% B). The column temperature was maintained at 35 °C, and the injection volume was 3.0 µL. Detection and quantification were performed via ESI-triple quadrupole linear ion trap mass spectrometry21.

Data processing and multivariate analysis

The metabolite annotation process utilized the NIST database, with data acquisition conducted via Analyst v1.7.3. The ion intensities were normalized to the total ion count to generate a data matrix, including the retention time (RT), m/z values, and normalized peak areas. Data preprocessing, including log10 transformation and autoscaling, was performed via MetaboAnalyst 6.022. Both univariate (fold change) and multivariate techniques were applied to analyze volatile and nonvolatile metabolites across hexane, chloroform, ethyl acetate, and methanol F. religiosa seed extracts. Principal component analysis (PCA) was used for unsupervised pattern recognition, whereas partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) served as a supervised approach. PLS-DA (a multiple linear regression) was evaluated via 5-fold cross-validation and Q² metrics. Metabolites with VIP (variable importance in projection) scores > 1 and p-values < 0.05 were considered significantly differentially accumulated. Heatmaps, generated via Pearson complete clustering, highlighted clustering patterns. Pathway enrichment analysis was conducted, and visualizations were created via MetaboAnalyst and ggplot2 in RStudio.

Network pharmacology analysis

Potential compounds from F. religiosa

All the compounds identified from the seed extracts of F. religiosa through GC-MS and LC-MS analysis were further screened via specific filtering criteria to refine the results. The compounds were selected on the basis of an oral bioavailability (OB) threshold of ≥ 30%, drug-induced nephrotoxicity (DIN) ≥ 0.50 and a drug-likeness (DL) score of ≥ 0.18. OB and DIN values were assessed via pkCSM prediction tool (https://biosig.lab.uq.edu.au/pkcsm/), and DL values were obtained via Molsoft’s prediction tool (https://molsoft.com/mprop/)23. These thresholds ensured the selection of bioactive compounds with favorable pharmacokinetic and drug-like properties for further analysis.

ADMET analysis of potential compounds

ADMET (absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity) analysis of the selected compounds was performed via the pkCSM platform [accessed on 03–09-2025], which uses a graph-based signature approach to predict key pharmacokinetic and toxicity properties24. The analysis encompassed multiple endpoints across five major categories, including water solubility, intestinal absorption, blood-brain barrier (BBB) permeability, cytochrome P450 inhibition, renal clearance, and hepatotoxicity risk. The chemical structures of the compounds were first retrieved from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) and converted into SMILES (Simplified Molecular Input Line Entry System) format to standardize the input for the pkCSM tool. The model processes these structures to encode molecular properties, pharmacophores, and toxicophores on the basis of distance-based graph signatures. The output was obtained in a structured format and used for downstream analysis, emphasizing drug-likeness and toxicity profiling. High-confidence predictions were prioritized for further experimental validation to identify compounds with optimal pharmacokinetic and safety profiles for potential therapeutic development.

Identification of anti-urolithiasis-related target

The SMILES-formatted structures of the identified potential bioactive compounds were imported into the Swiss Target Prediction platform (http://swisstargetprediction.ch/) [accessed on 04–09-2025] to predict their associated target genes. The predicted targets were then cross-referenced with Homo sapiens gene names via the UniProtKB database (https://www.uniprot.org/) [accessed on 04–09-2025] to ensure species-specific relevance25. To identify genes related to anti-inflammatory, anti-urolithiasis and renal stones (key terms), the GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org/) was queried for the aforementioned key term-associated genes. The target genes corresponding to the selected bioactive compounds via ADMET analysis from F. religiosa were compiled alongside the list of anti-inflammatory, anti-urolithiasis and renal stone-related genes26. Both datasets were uploaded to the Venn diagram tool (https://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/Venn/) to identify common genes. Genes appearing in the overlapping region of the Venn diagram were considered shared targets, suggesting their potential role in mediating the anti-inflammatory and anti-urolithiasis activity of the compounds. These overlapping targets form the basis for further investigation into the therapeutic relevance of the selected bioactive compounds.

Protein-protein interaction (PPI) analysis

Protein-protein Interaction (PPI) analysis was performed via the STRING database (https://string-db.org/) [accessed on 07–09-2025]. STRING categorizes confidence scores as low (< 0.4), medium (0.4–0.7), or high (> 0.7)27. For this study, the target gene set was queried with the species specified as Homo sapiens, and a confidence score threshold above 0.7 was applied to ensure reliable interactions. The resulting PPI network was visualized and analyzed via Cytoscape 3.8.0 (http://www.cytoscape.org/), an open-source platform for detailed network analysis and visualization. This approach allows the identification of key interaction hubs among target proteins, providing insights into their functional relationships and potential roles in biological pathways28.

Functional enrichment analysis

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the anti-inflammatory and anti-urolithiasis effects of F. religiosa, a functional enrichment analysis, including Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses, was conducted29. The analysis covered biological processes (BP), cellular components (CC), and molecular functions (MF) to identify key signaling pathways involved. Common target genes identified from the Venn diagram analysis were uploaded to the Database for Annotation, Visualization, and Integrated Discovery (DAVID) version 6.8 (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/) [accessed on 08–09-2025]. Using DAVID, the integrated anti-inflammatory and anti-urolithiasis mechanisms of the selected F. religiosa compounds were explored, offering valuable insights into the pathways and biological processes through which these bioactive compounds potentially exert their therapeutic effects30.

Molecular docking verification

Structure-based blind docking was conducted via CB-Dock2, which uses the CurPocket al.gorithm to detect cavities on the basis of protein surface curvature31. The X-ray crystal structures of the target proteins were obtained from the Research Collaboratory for Structural Bioinformatics Protein Data Bank (RCSB PDB) (https://www.rcsb.org/) in PDB format32. The target protein in PDB format was prepared by adding missing hydrogens, rebuilding side chains, and removing heteroatoms. Ligands in MOL2 format were optimized with RDKit by assigning partial charges and generating 3D conformations. The top five predicted cavities were selected, and AutoDock Vina (v1.1.2) was employed to dock the ligands into each cavity. Docking grids were defined according to cavity coordinates, and binding poses were evaluated via Vina’s scoring function. Visualization and analysis of the docking results were performed via the integrated NGL Viewer tools.

Statistical analysis

The experimental data are expressed as the means ± SEMs (standard errors of the means) from three independent replicates (n = 3). Statistical comparisons were performed via one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test, with significance considered at p ≤ 0.05. The IC₅₀ values of each sample were calculated by applying linear regression to the concentration-response curve, where the percentage inhibition was plotted against the concentration. For untargeted metabolomics, one-way ANOVA was employed to identify significant differences among groups, and multivariate analyses were subsequently performed via MetaboAnalyst version 6.0 to assess the data structure and group separation.

Results

In-vitro anti-inflammatory activity

The anti-inflammatory activity of F. religiosa seed extracts evaluated via three in vitro models: heat-induced hemolysis, hypotonicity-induced hemolysis, and egg albumin denaturation assays. Across all three assays, the extracts displayed a concentration-dependent protective effect, with variations in potency depending on the solvent used.

Heat-induced hemolysis

In the heat-induced HRBC membrane stabilization assay [F(48, 98) = 4092.43, p ≤ 0.05], among the seed extracts in the range of 50–1000 µg/mL, the ethyl acetate extract inhibited hemolysis the most (2.19 ± 0.41–61.87 ± 0.21%), followed by the methanol extract (3.24 ± 0.45–59.86 ± 0.05%), aqueous extract (9.43 ± 0.43–51.98 ± 0.30%), hexane extract (9.67 ± 0.15–49.12 ± 0.26%), and chloroform extract (4.41 ± 0.12–47.18 ± 0.12%). In comparison, the standard diclofenac sodium at 50–1000 µg/mL had significant membrane stabilization activity (13.43 ± 0.27–77.89 ± 0.13%), whereas acetylsalicylic acid had a moderate activity profile (0.33 ± 0.07–54.52 ± 0.12%) (Fig. 1a).

The results are expressed as means ± SEMs (n = 3). Bars/points sharing different letters are significantly different according to Tukey’s test at p ≤ 0.05.

The calculated IC50 values [F(6, 14) = 17734.2, p ≤ 0.05] indicated that the ethyl acetate extract (790.30 ± 0.66 µg/mL) was the most potent among the solvent extracts, followed by methanol (845.19 ± 0.02 µg/mL), aqueous (939.25 ± 1.55 µg/mL), hexane (962.97 ± 1.35 µg/mL), and chloroform (1094.65 ± 1.65 µg/mL). Among the standards, diclofenac sodium (566.31 ± 1.86 µg/mL) showed the highest potency, while acetylsalicylic acid (812.28 ± 0.20 µg/mL) was comparable to the methanol and ethyl acetate extracts at the 95% confidence interval (CI) (Fig. 1d).

Hypotonicity-induced hemolysis

In the hypotonicity-induced HRBC membrane stabilization assay [F(48, 98) = 5216.37, p ≤ 0.05], among the seed extracts in the concentration range of 50–1000 µg/mL, the ethyl acetate extract demonstrated the greatest protection of RBCs against hypotonicity-induced lysis (24.55 ± 0.59–80.67 ± 0.10%), followed by the methanol extract (26.31 ± 0.71–74.60 ± 0.16%), hexane extract (12.00 ± 0.06–83.34 ± 0.12%), aqueous extract (3.48 ± 0.13–68.05 ± 0.03%), and chloroform extract (16.44 ± 0.03–62.27 ± 0.37%). In comparison, the standard diclofenac sodium had strong membrane-stabilizing activity (46.70 ± 0.02–80.16 ± 0.05%), whereas acetylsalicylic acid had a moderate but consistent protection (26.18 ± 0.08–73.22 ± 0.09%) (Fig. 1b).

The calculated IC50 values [F(6, 14) = 61003.55, p ≤ 0.05] revealed that the ethyl acetate extract (346.63 ± 1.303 µg/mL) exhibited the highest potency among the extracts, followed by methanol (400.57 ± 0.794 µg/mL), hexane (438.30 ± 0.688 µg/mL), aqueous (630.34 ± 0.675 µg/mL), and chloroform (671.66 ± 0.758 µg/mL). Among the standards, diclofenac sodium had the lowest IC50 (98.93 ± 0.501 µg/mL), confirming its superior activity, whereas acetylsalicylic acid (390.66 ± 0.274 µg/mL) was comparable to the methanol and hexane extracts at the 95% CI (Fig. 1e).

Egg albumin denaturation assay

In the egg albumin denaturation assay [F(48, 98) = 9814.74, p ≤ 0.05], among the seed extracts in the concentration range of 50–1000 µg/mL, the methanol extract exhibited the greatest inhibition of protein denaturation (25.28 ± 0.11–75.65 ± 0.15%), followed closely by the ethyl acetate extract (22.86 ± 0.03–71.51 ± 0.06%), hexane extract (22.43 ± 0.10–61.74.10.74 ± 0.07%), aqueous extract (22.05 ± 0.22–61.57 ± 0.07%), and chloroform extract (21.81 ± 0.10–52.60 ± 0.01%). Among the reference standards, diclofenac sodium exhibited substantial activity (21.24 ± 0.06–70.68 ± 0.13%), whereas acetylsalicylic acid showed comparatively higher activity (11.15 ± 0.10–74.00 ± 0.05%) surpassing that of most of the extracts tested (Fig. 1c).

The IC50 values [F(6, 14) = 10411.74, p ≤ 0.05] further supported these findings, with the ethyl acetate extract (524.10 ± 1.29 µg/mL) showing the strongest activity among the extracts, followed by methanol (639.67 ± 0.88 µg/mL), hexane (626.63 ± 1.52 µg/mL), chloroform (807.79 ± 0.16 µg/mL), and aqueous extract (845.73 ± 1.62 µg/mL). In comparison, acetylsalicylic acid had the lowest IC50 (514.02 ± 1.81 µg/mL), making it the most potent overall, whereas diclofenac sodium (537.63 ± 1.31 µg/mL) was nearly equivalent to the ethyl acetate extract in efficacy at 95% CI (Fig. 1f).

In-vitro anti-urolithiasis activity

Owing to its pronounced anti-inflammatory activity, which is closely associated with mitigating stone-associated tissue injury, the ethyl acetate seed extract of F. religiosa was further evaluated for its in vitro antiurolithiatic potential via a semipermeable membrane model, with cystone serving as a reference standard (Table 1). The degree of calcium oxalate crystal dissolution was assessed at concentrations ranging from 100 to 500 µg/mL, and the untreated calcium oxalate suspension served as the negative control. In the control group, negligible spontaneous dissolution of calcium oxalate crystals was observed, with the estimated calcium weight remaining high (88.40 ± 0.32 µg for the extract control and 88.30 ± 0.58 µg for the cystone control). This confirmed the structural stability of calcium oxalate in the absence of any test or reference treatment and established a reliable baseline for comparative analysis. Upon treatment with the ethyl acetate extract of F. religiosa, a clear concentration-dependent increase in calcium oxalate dissolution was observed. At the lowest tested concentration (100 µg/mL), the extract dissolved 27.93 ± 0.12 µg of calcium, corresponding to 31.61 ± 0.06% dissolution.

Molecular characterization

Volatile metabolite profiling

C-MS of hexane, chloroform, ethyl acetate, and methanol extracts of F. religiosa seeds revealed a total of 112 metabolites: 26 in hexane (Fig S1 and Table S1), 22 in chloroform (Fig S2 and Table S2), 50 in ethyl acetate (Fig S3 and Table S3), and 14 in methanol (Fig S4 and Table S4). Among these metabolites, 40 significantly accumulated across extracts. One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s HSD revealed marked variations in metabolite abundance, with [(2 S)−2-[(2R)−4-hexadecanoyloxy-3-hydroxy-5-oxo-2 H-furan-2-yl]−2-hydroxyethyl] hexadecanoate (F = 9.61 × 10¹⁸, p = 1.46 × 10⁻⁷⁴), 5-methyl-2-prop-1-en-2-ylcyclohexan-1-ol (F = 5.20 × 10¹⁹, p = 1.7 × 10⁻⁷⁷) and methyl (11E,14E,17E)-icosa-11,14,17-trienoate (F = 4.16 × 10¹⁷, p = 4.15 × 10⁻⁶⁹) emerging as the most significant differentiators, which is consistent with substantial mean differences between groups (Table S8).

PCA demonstrated that two principal components explained the entire variance, with PC1 accounting for 99.1% and PC2 accounting for 0.3%, indicating distinct metabolic variation among the seed extracts (Fig. S8a). Sample clustering reflected the extraction solvent type, underscoring the robustness of the dataset (Table S9). Pairwise PERMANOVA further supported significant group separation (F = 1.19 × 10¹⁷, p = 0.001), particularly between chloroform and hexane, chloroform and methanol, and ethyl acetate and methanol (p.adj = 0.1083). The correlation heatmap highlighted clear covariation patterns, with strong positive correlations (r > 0.75) observed among (Z)-hexadec-7-enal, N-[[3,6-dichloro-2,7-bis[2-(diethylamino)ethoxy]fluoren-9-ylidene]amino]−2,2-dimethylpropanamide, docosan-1-ol, and methyl 10-methyloctadecanoate, forming a distinct cluster. In contrast, strong negative correlations (r < − 0.90) emerged between high-intensity peaks (e.g., tetrapentacontane, pentatriacontane) and lower-intensity peaks (e.g., cyclopentane-1,3-dione, naphthalene), suggesting mutually exclusive metabolite distributions across extracts (Fig. 2a and b).

PLS-DA identified a restricted set of metabolites as key discriminators. High-importance compounds (VIP > 2) included 5-methyl-2-prop-1-en-2-ylcyclohexan-1-ol, dotriacontane, [(2 S)−2-[(2R)−4-hexadecanoyloxy-3-hydroxy-5-oxo-2 H-furan-2-yl]−2-hydroxyethyl] hexadecanoate and tetrapentacontane. Moderate contributors (VIP 1–2), such as methyl (11E,14E,17E)-icosa-11,14,17-trienoate, ethyl (Z)-octadec-9-enoate, ethyl hexadecanoate, and henicosane, provided secondary discriminatory power. Variables with VIP < 1 had negligible influence. The abundance heatmap further confirmed the distinct distributions of high- and moderate-VIP metabolites among the solvents. Collectively, these findings indicate that only a limited subset of metabolites drives the observed solvent-specific separation, rendering them strong candidates for biomarker discovery (Fig. S8b).

Self-organizing map (SOM) analysis revealed two solvent-defined clusters. Cluster (0,1), comprising chloroform, ethyl acetate, and methanol replicates, was characterized by low concentrations (~ 0 to 1.5 × 10⁷), punctuated by moderate spikes in ethyl (9Z,12Z)-octadeca-9,12-dienoate (2.6 × 10⁸), dotriacontane (2.48 × 10⁸), and cyclopentane-1,3-dione (1.66 × 10⁷). In contrast, Cluster (0,2), composed exclusively of hexane replicates, exhibited pronounced variability, with concentrations ranging from near baseline to 4.8 × 10⁹. The notable spikes included 5-methyl-2-prop-1-en-2-ylcyclohexan-1-ol (4.8 × 10⁹) and [(2 S)−2-[(2R)−4-hexadecanoyloxy-3-hydroxy-5-oxo-2 H-furan-2-yl]−2-hydroxyethyl] hexadecanoate (2.07 × 10⁹). Thus, while chloroform, ethyl acetate, and methanol extracts presented relatively stable profiles with minor fluctuations, the hexane extracts presented sporadic but extreme surges, dominating the volatile metabolite landscape (Fig. S9).

Nonvolatile metabolite profiling

LC-MS of the chloroform, ethyl acetate, and methanol extracts of F. religiosa seeds yielded 39 metabolites: 14 in chloroform (Fig S5 and Table S5), 15 in ethyl acetate (Fig S6 and Table S6), and 10 in methanol (Fig S7 and Table S7). One-way ANOVA revealed highly significant differences in abundance, with 10 metabolites showing strong accumulation patterns (F-values 1.38 × 10³¹−7.84 × 10³²; p < 1.03 × 10⁻⁹², FDR-corrected). Tukey’s post hoc honestly significant difference (HSD) test confirmed significant pairwise differences across all solvents. Among these, quercetagetinidin cation (F = 7.84 × 10³², p = 5.61 × 10⁻⁹⁸), coumarandione (F = 4.29 × 10³², p = 3.41 × 10⁻⁹⁷), and phyllanthin (F = 2.91 × 10³², p = 1.09 × 10⁻⁹⁶) displayed the strongest separations (Table S10).

PCA revealed that PC1 and PC2 together explained 100% of the variance (83.1% and 16.9%, respectively), indicating clear metabolic divergence among the extracted solvents (Fig. S10a and Table S11). PERMANOVA supported significant differentiation between chloroform and ethyl acetate (F = ∞, R² = 1.0, p = 0.00139), whereas differences between chloroform and methanol and between ethyl acetate and methanol were not significant after adjustment (p.adj = 0.1).

Correlation analysis revealed well-defined networks of positive and negative associations. Luteolinidin and quercetagetinidin cations showed a very strong positive correlation (r = 0.995) suggesting close co-occurrence, whereas coumarandione and phyllanthin also showed a strong positive relationship (r = 0.839). Additional clusters linked benzaldehyde, δ-decalactone, and 4-methoxycoumarin (r > 0.95), as well as D-erythronic acid γ-lactone and 3-hydroxycoumarin (r = 0.994). Conversely, luteolinidin and 3-hydroxycoumarin (r = −0.988), quercetagetinidin and 3-hydroxycoumarin (r = −0.969), phyllanthin and benzaldehyde (r = −0.999), and coumarandione with δ-decalactone (r = −0.974) showed strong inverse relationships, suggesting mutually exclusive metabolite distributions (Fig. 3a and b).

Protein-protein interaction (PPI) analysis

The constructed protein-protein interaction (PPI) network comprised 173 nodes and 440 edges, with an average node degree of 5.09 and a clustering coefficient of 0.501, indicating substantial connectivity and modular organization (Fig. 4b). The observed edges greatly exceeded the expected 132, yielding a highly significant enrichment p-value (< 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁶), confirming that the proteins are more interconnected than random chance. Topological analysis revealed the presence of highly connected nodes (hubs) and putative bottlenecks (nodes with high betweenness centrality), suggesting that the network contains key regulatory proteins that coordinate multiple downstream responses.

Functional enrichment analysis

Through functional enrichment analysis, 31 signaling pathways (p < 0.05) were identified as potential mediators of the anti-inflammatory and anti-urolithiasis effects of F. religiosa. KEGG enrichment analysis highlighted ten key pathways, namely, the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway, the MAPK signaling pathway, the cAMP signaling pathway, the calcium signaling pathway, the VEGF signaling pathway, the FOXO signaling pathway, the NF-κB signaling pathway, the relaxin signaling pathway, and the Rap1 signaling pathway, as central hubs (Fig. 4c).

Network pharmacology-driven functional annotation revealed that Annotation Cluster 1 was the most enriched group (enrichment score of 6.52), containing genes crucial for cell survival, apoptosis regulation, inflammatory signaling, cytoskeletal organization, and extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling (Fig. 4e). Notably, robust enrichment was detected for biological processes such as the regulation of apoptosis (GO:0042981, p = 2.1 × 10⁻⁸, fold 12.4), the oxidative stress response (GO:0034599, p = 4.3 × 10⁻⁷, fold 10.9), the inflammatory response (GO:0006954, p = 7.6 × 10⁻⁷, fold 9.7), and ECM disassembly (GO:0022617, p = 2.1 × 10⁻⁶, fold 7.5), all of which are directly linked to limiting tissue injury, preventing crystal nucleation, and promoting tissue repair. In terms of molecular function, this cluster was strongly enriched for serine/threonine kinase activity (GO:0004674, p = 3.5 × 10⁻⁷, fold 13.2), DNA-binding transcription factor activity (GO:0003700, p = 8.2 × 10⁻⁷, fold 10.6), and metallopeptidase activity (GO:0008237, p = 1.1 × 10⁻⁶, fold 9.3), indicating its role in signal transduction, transcriptional regulation, and ECM degradation.

Cellular localization analysis revealed gene enrichment in the cytoplasm (fold 11.5), nucleus (fold 9.1), mitochondria (fold 8.2), and extracellular region (fold 7.9), suggesting a coordinated cascade from cytoplasmic signaling (e.g., PI3K, AKT1, IKBKB) to nuclear transcriptional control (e.g., FOXO3, RELA) and extracellular matrix remodeling (e.g., MMP2, MMP9, MMP13). Collectively, this integrative module links survival-promoting, inflammation-suppressing, and ECM-regulating genes, positioning Annotation Cluster 1 as a core mechanistic hub underpinning the combined anti-inflammatory and anti-urolithiasis effects of the tested bioactive compounds. The modules corresponded to signaling kinases, matrix-remodeling enzymes, and inflammation-associated regulators, leading to the prioritization of PI3K, AKT1, IKKβ, MMP-9, and CaMKII for docking on the basis of their centrality, pathway relevance, and druggability.

KEGG signaling pathway

Network pharmacology-based analysis of the selected bioactive molecules revealed a complex interconnected signaling network comprising several key pathways associated with both anti-inflammatory and anti-urolithiasis activities. Prominent among these pathways were the calcium signaling pathway (Fig S16), the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway (Fig S17), the cAMP signaling pathway (Fig S18), the relaxin signaling pathway (Fig S19), the FOXO signaling pathway (Fig S20), the NF-κB signaling pathway (Fig S21), the VEGF signaling pathway (Fig S22), and the Rap1 signaling pathway (Fig S23).

These pathways are highly interconnected and collectively regulate critical processes crucial in the progression and resolution of inflammation and urolithiasis (Fig. 6). A major finding was the convergence of receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs) and G-protein coupled receptors (GPCRs) on downstream effectors such as PI3K, AKT, and MAPK family kinases. Activation of the PI3K-AKT pathway promotes anti-apoptotic signaling and antioxidant defense, which in turn regulates Bcl-2, Mcl-1 and FOXO transcription factors, maintaining redox balance and limiting oxidative injury. The MAPK (RAF1-MEK-ERK) pathway, was associated with the upregulation of cell cycle regulators, including Cyclin B, CCND1, and CDK2, which overlaps with PI3K-AKT signaling, facilitates epithelial repair and proliferation while coordinating with matrix metalloproteinases (MMP2, MMP9, and MMP13) to remodel the extracellular matrix without promoting fibrosis.

KEGG pathway mapping of various signaling cascades highlighted the interaction nodes (PI3K, AKT1, IKKβ, MMP9, CAMKII) targeted by the selected phytoconstituents of Ficus religiosa.

In parallel, the Relaxin-RXFP1-cAMP-PKA axis cross-talks with NF-κB and iNOS/COX/TNF-α signaling and this axis inhibits inflammatory and oxidative mediators through the regulation of IκB and the downstream suppression of proinflammatory transcriptional targets such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and LPS-induced genes. It also enhances NO-mediated vasodilation, which improves renal perfusion and contributes to antifibrotic outcomes, increased renal plasma flow, and improved glomerular filtration rate, and increased sodium excretion. These events are functionally relevant for preserving renal physiology and countering the fibrosis that often accompanies urolithiasis. This could lead to reduced expression of inflammatory mediators, including iNOS and COX, thereby attenuating oxidative and nitrosative stress.

Calcium signaling via CaMK and PKC interacts with both PI3K-AKT and Rap1 pathways to regulate cytoskeletal rearrangements and actin organization, which are vital for maintaining epithelial barrier integrity and preventing crystal adherence within renal tubules. This mechanism aligns with the anti-urolithiasis activity of the compounds, as disruption of cytoskeletal dynamics is a known factor in crystal retention. Additionally, the regulation of matrix metalloproteinases such as MMP2, MMP9, and MMP13 potentially influences ECM remodeling, which could support tissue repair while simultaneously modulating inflammatory infiltration. VEGF signaling converges on PI3K-AKT and MAPK pathways to promote angiogenesis and tissue repair, further supporting epithelial recovery. The Rap1 pathway integrates signals from calcium, cAMP, and growth factor pathways, reinforcing cell-cell adhesion and epithelial barrier function. Overall, the network analysis indicated that the tested molecules exert their biological effects through these interconnected pathways (PI3K-AKT, MAPK, cAMP/relaxin, calcium, and NF-κB) demonstrating the modulation of multiple signaling nodes, that would promote renal epithelial protection, reduce oxidative and inflammatory stress, prevent fibrosis, and inhibit crystal retention, thereby exerting anti-inflammatory and anti-urolithiatic effects.

Discussion

The development of effective therapies for urolithiasis has been limited, largely due to an incomplete understanding of its complex pathogenesis. Our in vitro experimental findings demonstrated that F. religiosa ethyl acetate seed extract significantly inhibited protein denaturation and stabilize biological membranes, reflecting strong anti-inflammatory potential. The ethyl acetate extract also exhibited potent calcium oxalate crystal dissolution activity, performing exceeding cystone, a standard herbal formulation used clinically for urolithiasis. Current clinical approaches such as lithotripsy and synthetic pharmacotherapies are often limited by toxicity, recurrence rate and cost5,9. The present study addressed this gap and provided comprehensive experimental and computational evidence that F. religiosa seed extracts, particularly the ethyl acetate extract, possess potent anti-inflammatory and anti-urolithiatic activities. These findings are particularly noteworthy since cystone’s mechanistic basis remains incompletely understood despite its widespread use10,33. Most previous phytotherapeutic investigations have focused on inhibiting crystal nucleation or aggregation34. The present work provides rare evidence of direct dissolution of preformed calcium oxalate crystals, thereby addressing a crucial pathogenic event in urolithiasis.

Metabolomic analyses via GC-MS and LC-MS profiles revealed a rich metabolic diversity spanning flavonoids, sterols, coumarins, and phenolic esters, which are well known to modulate oxidative and inflammatory pathways. Compounds such as apigenin, 4-cholestenone, methyl (E)-octadec-9-enoate and methyl octadecanoate were identified as major bioactives in the ethyl acetate extract. The ethyl acetate extract’s potent dissolution capacity may stem from these flavonoids, steroids and esterified lipids, which could chelate calcium or disrupt the crystalline lattice of calcium oxalate33,35. Thereby substantiating their possible ethnomedicinal relevance and emphasizing their promise as a phytotherapeutic intervention for inflammation associated with urolithiasis.

ADMET and physicochemical analyses demonstrated that the flavonoid-rich fractions of F. religiosa seed extracts, along with key sterols and fatty acid esters, namely quercetin 3′-methyl ether, apigenin, 4-cholestenone, methyl (E)-octadec-9-enoate, and methyl octadecanoate exhibit favorable pharmacokinetic and drug-like profiles, supporting their possible potential as lead scaffolds for therapeutic development. These metabolites showed moderate solubility, efficient oral absorption, high intestinal permeability, and strong metabolic stability, consistent with Lipinski’s and Veber’s criteria, alongside low predicted toxicity and good systemic bioavailability. While these identified sterols and fatty acid esters displayed hydrophobicity and limited aqueous solubility that may constrain bioavailability, such limitations can be addressed through advanced formulation or delivery strategies, such as nanoencapsulation, emulsification, or liposomal incorporation to enhance their solubility and bioavailability36. Overall, the integrated pharmacokinetic and ADMET assessments highlight these metabolites as possessing well-balanced, safe, and efficacious drug-like properties, underscoring their promise as candidates for anti-inflammatory and anti-urolithiatic drug development.

Molecular docking further substantiated the compound-target interactions predicted by network pharmacology. In our study, phytosterol 4-cholestenone demonstrated the highest docking affinities across multiple proteins, particularly AKT1 and MMP-9, suggesting that sterols may act as potent modulators of kinase- and protease-mediated pathways37. In addition, flavonoids such as quercetin 3′-methyl ether and apigenin showed strong binding affinities for PI3K, AKT1, and IKKβ indicating their potential to modulate inflammation and oxidative stress associated with urolithiasis. Previous in vivo studies support these findings, showing that apigenin treatment significantly reduced renal inflammation by down regulating IL-1β, NF-κB p65, and TNF-α expression and preserving normal renal morphology in nephrotoxic models38–41. Similarly, quercetin has been shown to bind PIK3R1, inhibiting the PI3K/Akt/NF-κB pathway and alleviating renal injury and fibrosis42.

Together, these findings suggest that both phytosterols and flavonoids may exert antiurolithiatic effects by suppressing inflammation-driven renal injury through coordinated inhibition of the PI3K/Akt/NF-κB signaling axis. Notably, phytosterols such as 4-cholestenone and long chain fatty esters like methyl (E)-octadec-9-enoate and methyl octadecanoate have been reported to reduce renal fibrosis and inflammatory cytokine expression by modulating NF-κB and PI3K/AKT signaling, supporting the sterol-mediated anti-inflammatory activity37,43. Flavonoids are well documented to suppress ROS, inhibit lysosomal enzymes, and downregulate proinflammatory mediators such as TNF-α and IL-1β4[,44. Flavonoids like quercetin have been shown in previous studies to reduce crystal deposition and renal injury in vivo, their capacity to dissolve existing stones has not been demonstrated, underscoring the novelty and translational significance of our findings45. Quercetin markedly reduced p38 MAPK activation, lipid peroxidation, and renal epithelial injury in hyperoxaluric rats as revealed in recent study, confirming modulation of oxidative and inflammatory signaling pathways46. Similarly, apigenin and its derivatives were shown to suppress inflammatory cytokines (NF‐κB, TNF‐α, TGF‐β1, and IL‐1β) release in hepatorenal tissues of lead acetate (PbAc) exposed rats38. Therefore, the high abundance these compounds suggests that F. religiosa seeds contain complementary classes of metabolites capable of targeting multiple inflammatory mediators.

These reports are congruent with our network pharmacology results, which identified PI3K, AKT1, IKKβ, MMP9 and CaMKII key regulatory hubs of the F. religiosa seed derived metabolites, indicating their multitarget therapeutic potential. These targets are integral components of several interconnected signaling cascades, including the PI3K-AKT, MAPK, NF-κB, cAMP/relaxin, and calcium signaling pathways47,48. Functional enrichment analysis of the identified targets further supported their significant involvement in these pathways, confirming that F. religiosa phytoconstituents may exert protective effects through coordinated modulation of these signaling networks. These pathways collectively govern processes critical for stone prevention, including epithelial cell survival, suppression of inflammatory mediators, cytoskeletal stabilization, and extracellular matrix turnover49,50.

CaOx monohydrate crystals have been shown to activate the p38 MAPK pathway, thereby enhancing crystal adhesion and promoting inflammation through the upregulation of NLRP3, caspase-1, and IL-1β in NRK-52E cells51. Previous studies have reported that PI3K-AKT activation is involved in kidney stone pathology, reinforcing the significance of this signaling axis in renal protection, as its activation promotes epithelial cell survival, enhances antioxidant defenses, preserves membrane integrity, and minimizes oxidative injury that predisposes tissues to crystal nucleation52.

Furthermore, our identification of cAMP/relaxin and calcium signaling as central pathways in modulating inflammation and urolithiasis pathogenesis is supported by previous studies. These studies demonstrate that relaxin enhances cAMP production, modulates calcium signaling, and regulates extracellular matrix remodeling through PI3K-PKCζ-dependent mechanisms53. The integrated regulation of these pathways would provide a mechanistic framework linking the observed network targets to calcium oxalate crystallization and dissolution. Cross-talk between the PI3K-AKT and MAPK cascades influences MMP-9-mediated extracellular matrix remodeling, facilitating crystal detachment and dissolution54. Elevated MMP-9 expression promotes extracellular matrix degradation, fibrosis, and inflammatory infiltration, all of which exacerbate crystal retention55. In vivo studies have shown that depletion of MMP-9 positive neutrophils reduces renal fibrosis and inflammatory infiltration, highlighting its critical role in disease progression in UUO mice56. Concurrently, inhibition of NF-κB activation through IKKβ suppression limits pro-inflammatory cytokine expression and reduces crystal-cell adhesion57.

Some experimental evidence showed that PI3K/Akt activation through growth factors or cadmium exposure regulates FOXO3a phosphorylation and promotes cell survival. In parallel, MAPK modulation by inhibitors such as SB203580 (p38 inhibitor) and apocynin (NADPH oxidase inhibitor) attenuates CaOx-induced inflammation by suppressing NLRP3, caspase-1, and IL-1β expression58. Similarly, relaxin and IBMX (a phosphodiesterase inhibitor) have been shown to activate the RXFP1-cAMP axis, reducing ROS accumulation and fibrosis, whereas selective NF-κB modulation has been suggested as a therapeutic approach to mitigate renal inflammation without impairing immune function48. Immunosuppressive agents like cyclosporine A, tacrolimus, and glucocorticoids have also been reported to regulate the Ca²⁺-actin-cytoskeleton axis, preserving epithelial structure and preventing crystal adherence59. Moreover, catechins from green tea were found to inhibit MMP-2 activity, reducing extracellular matrix degradation and tissue injury60. Thus, F. religiosa phytoconstituents may protect against urolithiasis by harmonizing these interlinked signaling pathways to maintain epithelial homeostasis and shift the balance toward calcium oxalate dissolution rather than crystallization.

The integration of omics-based profiling with molecular outcomes will be essential to translate these findings into clinically relevant phytopharmaceutical applications. Collectively, the evidence presented here positions F. religiosa seeds as a novel, mechanistically substantiated, and pharmacologically versatile candidate for developing safe and effective therapeutics against urolithiasis and related inflammatory renal disorders. These findings might provide a framework to interpret how these compounds may modulate interconnected signaling pathways involved in epithelial cell survival, oxidative stress, inflammation, and extracellular matrix remodeling, offering mechanistic insights into their possible role in preventing or managing urolithiasis and inflammation associated with urolithiasis.

This study, though comprehensive, has certain limitations. All assays were conducted in vitro and in silico, lacking molecular validation such as gene or protein expression studies. The absence of molecular dynamics simulations limits insights into ligand-protein stability. Metabolite identification relied only on spectral library matches (NIST), and no cytotoxicity or in vivo safety studies were performed. The GeneCards database was used for target identification due to its comprehensive integration of genomic and proteomic information, but integrating additional resources future studies could further improves the reliability of disease-target associations. Moreover, our models cannot fully replicate the physiological complexity of urolithiasis, so translational interpretations should be made cautiously. Future work should include molecular validation, in vivo safety evaluation, and pharmacological refinement to enhance translational relevance.

Conclusion

This study provides comprehensive experimental and mechanistic evidence supporting the significant anti-inflammatory and antiurolithiatic potential of F. religiosa seed extracts, thereby validating their possible use in traditional medicine. Among the tested fractions, the ethyl acetate extract demonstrated the most prominent in vitro anti-inflammatory activity and exhibited superior calcium oxalate dissolution capacity compared to the standard drug cystone. Integrative GC-MS and LC-MS metabolite profiling, combined with network pharmacology analysis, identified multiple bioactive and drug-like phytoconstituents, notably quercetin 3′-methyl ether, apigenin, 4-cholestenone, methyl (E)-octadec-9-enoate, and methyl octadecanoate. These metabolites displayed promising oral bioavailability and multitarget interactions with key regulatory proteins such as PI3K, AKT1, IKKβ, MMP-9, and CaMKII, indicating their potential to modulate interconnected biological processes including oxidative stress, inflammation, apoptosis, and extracellular matrix remodeling. Although some compounds exhibited high hydrophobicity, this can be addressed through suitable formulation strategies to enhance their pharmacokinetic profiles. Pathway enrichment analysis revealed that the identified phytoconstituents may act by modulating critical signaling pathways, including PI3K-AKT, MAPK, NF-κB, cAMP/relaxin, and calcium signaling, which collectively contribute to epithelial protection, suppression of oxidative and inflammatory responses, stabilization of cellular integrity, and regulation of calcium oxalate crystallization dynamics. Overall, F. religiosa seeds emerged as a promising yet underexplored source of phytotherapeutic agents with synergistic multitarget potential for the prevention and management of urolithiasis. Future studies should include in vivo validation, molecular docking and dynamics simulations, and targeted modulation of specific protein receptors to elucidate detailed mechanisms and facilitate the development of clinically relevant formulations for urolithiasis treatment.
