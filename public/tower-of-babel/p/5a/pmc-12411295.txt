Potentially Inappropriate Medication Use in Patients With Parkinson's Disease: Analysis of Korean National Health Insurance Claims Data
Authors: Seon-Min Lee, Jieun Shin, Do-Young Kwon, Yu Jin Jung
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background and PurposePolypharmacy is common in Parkinson's disease (PD). This includes appropriate medications for PD-related symptoms and comorbid conditions, but also potentially inappropriate medications (PIMs). We aimed to determine the prescribing trends of PIMs based on the 2019 Beers Criteria in Korean patients with PD. MethodsThis retrospective observational study analyzed PD patients aged 40 years or older whose PIM prescribing patterns from 2014 to 2019 were included in the HIRA (Korean Health Insurance Review and Assessment Service) nationwide claims database in South Korea. The prevalence and usage were analyzed for both overall PIMs and PIMs that can exacerbate PD symptoms (PIMPDs). Additionally, the most frequently prescribed PIMs and PIMPDs were investigated.ResultsPIMs were used at least once in 38.3% of 257,142 patients with PD. The mean number of PIM items used per patient was 2.61. The PIM usage ratio, corresponding to the proportion of the total amount of PIMs to all medications per patient, was 19.7%. The proportion of patients taking PIMs among all PD patients decreased significantly up to 2018 but then increased in 2019, while the PIM usage ratio increased steadily up to 2018 and then decreased in 2019. Excluding the representative PD medications, the most frequently prescribed PIM was amitriptyline. PIMPDs were also commonly used, including levosulpiride and haloperidol.ConclusionsThe prevalence and usage of PIMs were high in Korean PD patients during 2014–2019, and the utilization of PIMPDs persisted throughout this period. These findings can be used to develop evidence-based guidelines for robust polypharmacy management strategies to ensure the safety of medications used by PD patients.

INTRODUCTION

Polypharmacy involving the concomitant use of at least five medications is a major and increasing concern in geriatric pharmacotherapy.12 In addition to polypharmacy, multiple comorbidities and physiologic alterations associated with aging itself in the elderly contribute to pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic changes that can increase the risk of adverse drug events (ADEs).12 A recent hospital-based evaluation found that 91% of patients experiencing ADEs were receiving polypharmacy, in contrast with 73% in the non-ADE group.3 To prevent ADEs and improve treatment outcomes, it is critical to thoroughly evaluate the benefits and risks of each prescribed medication in the elderly.

Medications whose potential risks outweigh their potential benefits in the geriatric population have been defined as potentially inappropriate medications (PIMs).4 The Beers Criteria are commonly used to detect PIMs in clinical practice, and these criteria have been periodically updated by the American Geriatrics Society since being originally developed in 1991.56 These criteria suggest rational drug prescribing to ensure the safety of these drugs and reduce ADEs.56 The risks associated with using PIMs in the elderly include cognitive impairment, delirium, falls, and fractures, as well as clinically significant harmful events including emergency department visits, hospitalizations, and even death.78 However, PIMs are frequently used in older individuals, with reports that at least 40% of geriatric patients being prescribed one or more PIMs.89

Parkinson's disease (PD) is a chronic, progressive neurodegenerative disease characterized by both motor and nonmotor symptoms and is associated with a high medication burden.10 The complex nature and complicated comorbidities of PD make polypharmacy common,11 which is related to an increased risk of falls in patients with PD.12 Polypharmacy includes the use of appropriate medications based on current evidence-based medicine (EBM) and the use of PIMs. A retrospective study of single-center hospitalized PD patients in the US found that the hospitalization duration was significantly longer in those who received PIMs than in the baseline PD patients.13 Moreover, despite recommendations to avoid certain medications in PD patients, a substantial number of such administrations still occurred.13

The intersection of polypharmacy and treatment outcomes, particularly in patients with PD, is an important area of clinical research. However, the use of PIMs has not been adequately quantified among PD patients, which makes it crucial to assess the current status of PIM use in patients with PD and establish guidelines to mitigate ADEs and improve patient treatment outcomes. Therefore, the objectives of this study were to characterize PIM prescribing patterns and quantify their use in Korean patients with PD based on the recently modified Beers Criteria. Furthermore, we aimed to determine the trends in and concerns about the use of PIMs that can exacerbate PD symptoms (PIMPDs) in South Korea.

METHODS

Data source

This retrospective, observational study analyzed nationwide claims data in the Korean Health Insurance Review and Assessment Service (HIRA) database. The HIRA database encompasses both inpatient and outpatient encounters, including diagnostic codes, prescription records, procedural data, and reimbursement claims.14 A customized, de-identified dataset was extracted in accordance with the study objectives to identify the PIM prescribing patterns in patients with PD.

Study patients and medications

This study included patients aged 40 years or older with a diagnosis of PD, as identified using the G20 code in the Korean Standard Classification of Diseases (KCD).15 The PIM prescribing patterns in this population were analyzed from 2014 to 2019.

The medications included in this study were selected based on the 2019 Beers Criteria, focusing on two primary categories: 1) PIMs that should generally be avoided in older adults and 2) medications that may exacerbate Parkinsonian symptoms (i.e., PIMPDs). Medications not explicitly listed in the Beers Criteria but belonging to the same pharmacologic class and approved for clinical use in South Korea were also included. These medications were identified using the Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) classification system16 as provided by the HIRA. The final medication list was cross-referenced with the HIRA reimbursement formulary and corresponding active-ingredient codes. Nonreimbursed medications, oral solutions, parenteral formulations, topical agents, and ophthalmic preparations were excluded, as were medications not approved for use in South Korea and those manufactured exclusively for export.

Levosulpiride is widely prescribed in South Korea as a gastroprokinetic agent, similar to metoclopramide, and is not included in the Beers Criteria due to the absence of any FDA-approved indication.1718 However, given its ability to block dopamine D2 receptors, which can exacerbate parkinsonism,1718 levosulpiride was classified as both a PIM and PIMPD in this study.

While benztropine and trihexyphenidyl are frequently used to manage PD, their potent anticholinergic effects warrant classification as “avoid” medications in the Beers Criteria due to their high risk of adverse effects in older adults.6 While not explicitly listed in the Beers Criteria, amantadine is classified as an “avoid” medication under the FORTA classification (category D)19 due to its association with increased risks of central nervous system (CNS) adverse effects, QT prolongation, and renal impairment in patients with PD.20 Amantadine was therefore included in the PIM category in the present study. Furthermore, bethanechol and propiverine, which are commonly used to manage urinary symptoms in PD, are not listed in the Beers Criteria. However, due to their strong anticholinergic properties and frequent clinical use in South Korea, they were also included in the medication list of the present study.

Antipsychotics have been categorized as PIMs in the Beers Criteria due to their high risk of extrapyramidal side effects. However, the 2019 Beers Criteria designate clozapine and quetiapine as exceptions for patients with PD, since there is evidence that they have a lower potential for exacerbating Parkinsonian symptoms.6 Accordingly, these medications were included in the overall PIMs but excluded from the PIMPDs category in this study.

In summary, 89 medications were classified as PIMs and 13 medications were classified as PIMPDs in this study, all of which were included in the final analysis (Supplementary Table 1 in the online-only Data Supplement).

Data measurement and definition

The primary outcomes of this study were 1) PIM prevalence, calculated as the proportion of patients prescribed at least one PIM relative to the total study population; 2) number of PIM items, calculated as the number of distinct PIMs prescribed per patient; and 3) PIM usage ratio, calculated as the proportion of PIMs relative to the total amount of medications prescribed per patient. These measures were similarly assessed for PIMPDs; that is, the PIMPD prevalence, number of PIMPD items, and PIMPD usage. The secondary outcomes included identifying the most frequently prescribed PIMs and PIMPDs. Additionally, annual changes in PIM and PIMPD prescribing patterns were evaluated.

Statistical analyses

Data preprocessing and descriptive statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.0.1, R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Temporal trends in the variables of interest were assessed using join-point regression analysis performed using software provided by the National Cancer Institute (https://surveillance.cancer.gov/joinpoint/). The annual percentage change (APC) was estimated to quantify the relative rate of change per year, rather than the absolute change. For example, if the estimated APC from the regression model is +10% and the proportion of the variable of interest in the previous year was 20%, the expected proportion for the following year would be 20%×(1+0.10)=22%. Join-point regression analysis was used to estimate the APCs for PIM and PIMPD prescribing patterns over time.

Ethics statement

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Daejeon St. Mary's Hospital (IRB approval No. DC-23ZIDI0001). The need to obtain informed consents from the included patients was waived by the IRB due to the retrospective design of the study.

RESULTS

Demographic characteristics

This study included 257,142 patients with PD whose demographic characteristics are described in Table 1. Most of the PD patients were female (n=167,046; 65.0%). The number of PD patients increased steadily from 2014 to 2019, and 38.3% of them (n=98,359) used PIMs at least once. The number of PD patients prescribed PIMs also increased over time, with 11.0% (n=10,913) having taken PIMPDs. Most of the PIM and PIMPD users were female (61.1% and 64.2%, respectively).

Trends in prevalence rates of PIMs and PIMPDs by sex and age

Table 1 lists the sex- and age-specific trends in the prevalence rates of PIMs and PIMPDs among patients with PD from 2014 to 2019. The PIM prevalence decreased significantly among both males (APC=-6.3%, p<0.001) and females (APC= -3.9%, p<0.001). The PIMPD prevalence was consistently higher in females than males throughout the study period. However, a significant decrease in the PIMPD prevalence was observed in females (APC=-5.8%, p<0.001), whereas the decrease was smaller and did not reach statistical significance in males (APC=-3.4%, p=0.234).

According to age group, the number of PIM users was the highest in those aged 70 years or older (75.3%), followed by those aged 60–69 years (17.7%), 50–59 years (5.7%), and 40–49 years (1.2%). From 2014 to 2019, the PIM prevalence decreased most significantly in patients aged 70 years or older (APC=-5.5%, p<0.001), followed in those aged 60–69 years (APC=-4.1%, p<0.001). The PIMPD prevalence decreased significantly in patients aged 50–59 years (APC= -6.7%, p<0.001) and 60–69 years (APC=-6.5%, p<0.001).

Number of PIM items in patients with PD

Table 2 lists the number of PIM items used in patients with PD. The number of PIM items used per patient was 2.61±2.57 (mean±standard deviation). From 2014 to 2019, multiple PIMs were prescribed to 52,737 PD patients (20.5%). For polypharmacy defined as the concomitant use of at least five drugs, the PIM polypharmacy rate was 5.6% in all of the PD patients over the 6-year study period. The annual polypharmacy rate for PIM items steadily decreased from 2014 to 2018 but then increased to a peak in 2019 (7.3%).

Prevalence and usage of PIMs and PIMPDs in patients with PD

The prevalence and usage of PIMs and PIMPDs are presented in Table 3. The PIM prevalence was 38.3%. This decreased significantly up to 2018 but then increased in 2019 (2014–2018: APC=-5.3%, p<0.001; 2014–2019: APC=-2.1%, p=0.446) (Fig. 1A). The mean PIM usage ratio was 19.7%; it increased steadily up to 2018 but then decreased in 2019. The prevalence of PIMPDs decreased significantly up to 2018 but then increased in 2019 (2014–2018: APC=-5.1%, p=0.021; 2014–2019: APC=0.5%, p=0.893) (Fig. 1B). The mean PIMPD usage ratio was 37.6%.

Frequently prescribed PIMs and PIMPDs

Table 4 presents the frequently used PIMs and PIMPDs in patients with PD during 2014–2019. Excluding representative PD medications such as amantadine, trihexyphenidyl, and benztropine, the most frequently prescribed PIM was amitriptyline, followed by dimenhydrinate. Benztropine prescriptions decreased after 2018, by one-third in 2019 relative to 2014. In contrast, there was an increase in quetiapine prescriptions of nearly threefold in 2019 relative to 2014. Additionally, the antiplatelet agent aspirin, the serotonin antagonist and serotonin-reuptake inhibitor trazodone, and the anticholinergic agent bethanechol were also commonly prescribed.

Levosulpiride and haloperidol were the most frequently prescribed PIMPDs in patients with PD. Metoclopramide and risperidone consistently ranked among the top five most-prescribed PIMPDs each year. Prescriptions of haloperidol showed a decreasing trend, while this had increased for risperidone, with its usage more than doubling in 2019 relative to 2014. Flunarizine was the fifth most frequently prescribed PIMPDs.

DISCUSSION

This study analyzed Korean HIRA claims data with the aim of identifying prescribing trends of PIMs in patients with PD. There was a substantial number of PIMs administered in Korean PD patients, including those with aggravating PD symptoms.

In this study, 38.3% of patients with PD received PIMs at least once between 2014 and 2019. This rate is comparable with the findings of a US study that utilized the 2015 Beers Criteria.21 Additionally, a recent meta-analysis of PIM prescribing patterns in individuals aged 60 years or older across several countries found that the global pooled prevalence of PIM use was approximately 36.7%,22 which closely aligns with the results of our study. The prescribing rate and usage of PIMs and PIMPDs were higher in females than in males. This is consistent with previous findings for PIM prescriptions in older adults, which suggest that females experience nonmotor symptoms such as depression, anxiety, and sleep disturbances more frequently than do males, leading to higher rates of the related prescriptions.2123 Additionally, the longer life expectancy of females and their higher healthcare utilization rates may contribute to this trend.24 These findings emphasize the critical need for greater attention and stricter management of medication prescriptions, particularly in elderly female PD patients. Our study also revealed an increasing trend in PIM and PIMPD prescriptions among older patients. These results emphasize the need to increase awareness about PIM and PIMPD use and implement systematic management strategies, particularly for older PD patients.

The annual trends in prescribing rates over the study period appeared as a decrease in PIM prevalence but an increase in the PIM usage ratio, and hence a divergent pattern. This suggests that while the number of patients receiving PIMs decreased, the per-patient use of PIMs increased. A similar trend was observed for PIMPDs, reflecting a shift in prescribing practices. These results are likely driven by the progressive nature of PD, which necessitates increasingly complex pharmacologic management since both motor and nonmotor symptoms increase over time. The decrease in PIM prevalence may reflect a positive shift in clinical practice, with physicians becoming more cautious about initiating new PIM prescriptions. However, the increased PIM usage ratio raises concerns about increasing ADEs associated with polypharmacy in patients with PD.

It was particularly notable that our analyses revealed that the proportion of PD patients meeting the criteria for polypharmacy used in this study (at least five concomitant PIMs) remained consistent at 5%–6% annually, underscoring the persistent challenge of managing the medication burden in this population. The ease of accessing healthcare services in South Korea in terms of cost and geographic proximity makes polypharmacy of particular concern. An analysis of Korean HIRA data for 2010–2019 revealed notable prevalence rates of polypharmacy (41.8% for periods >90 days and 38.1% for periods >180 days) and hypermedication (14.4% for periods >90 days and 9.4% for periods >180 days) among out-patients aged 65 years or older.25 There are intricate relationships between the number of prescribed PIMs and fatal events in the elderly, highlighting the critical need for policy interventions to reduce polypharmacy in this population.26 In particular, the area of PIM research and polypharmacy issues is an emerging challenge in patients with PD. PIM administration and its control significantly impact all aspects of the real-world outcomes in patients with PD, including their well-being, treatment guidelines, and social and economic perspectives.

Excluding representative PD medications such as amantadine, trihexyphenidyl, and benztropine, the most frequently prescribed PIMs identified in this study were tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs), including amitriptyline and nortriptyline, as well as the antivertigo agent dimenhydrinate. TCAs are probably frequently prescribed in PD patients due to the high prevalence of comorbid depression in this population.27 Additionally, TCAs are commonly used in older patients to manage neuropathic pain and insomnia.28 However, their potent anticholinergic properties pose significant risks of conditions such as confusion, cognitive impairment, and adverse parasympathetic effects that can increase the likelihood of severe cardiac complications.28 TCAs are also associated with increased risks of falls and fractures in older adults due to orthostatic hypotension, highlighting the need for careful monitoring.29 Consequently, the 2019 EBM guidelines for managing depression in PD patients indicate that there is insufficient evidence to support the safety and efficacy of amitriptyline, and so alternative medications such as selective serotonin-reuptake inhibitors and serotonin- and norepinephrine-reuptake inhibitors are recommended as safer options.30

Dimenhydrinate is a first-generation antihistamine that exerts its effects by blocking histamine H1 and cholinergic M1 receptors, and is mainly used to manage vertigo, motion sickness, nausea, and vomiting.31 However, the antihistaminergic and anticholinergic properties of dimenhydrinate may result in cognitive impairment in older adults and cause sedation in the CNS, thereby increasing the risks of falls and fractures.3132 PD patients frequently experience dizziness due to various underlying causes, which probably explains why dimenhydrinate is frequently prescribed in this population. However, it is crucial to evaluate the underlying cause of dizziness so that this medication is prescribed only when clinically justified.

Trihexyphenidyl and benztropine have long been used as antiparkinsonian medications, but the 2012 Beers Criteria classified these drugs as PIMs due to their anticholinergic ADEs and inadequate efficacy.6 Although these anticholinergic agents remained “clinically useful” in the 2018 EBM guidelines for managing motor symptoms of early or stable PD patients, their use should generally be restricted to young and cognitively intact patients.33 This can explain why benztropine prescriptions decreased after 2018, by one-third in 2019 relative to 2014. However, considering that trihexyphenidyl is still frequently prescribed, accurate evaluations of the appropriateness of all prescribed PD medications are necessary.

Another major objective of this study was to determine the PIMPD prescribing patterns, which represent a subset of PIMs known to worsen motor symptoms in patients with PD. The mean PIMPD prevalence was 11.0%, which was significantly higher than that of 8.7% found in a previous study investigating medications that may worsen motor symptoms in PD.21 These results emphasize the critical need for increased caution about prescribing practices, especially for PD patients, and to obtain high-quality evidence for improving medication safety in this population.

While there were some annual variations, levosulpiride and haloperidol were the most frequently prescribed PIMPDs. Levosulpiride is a widely prescribed prokinetic agent used to enhance gastrointestinal motility, and is a well-known dopamine D2 receptor antagonist that can exacerbate parkinsonism.17 A previous study identified levosulpiride as a significant contributor to parkinsonism, not only in patients with PD but also in elderly individuals.17 Its inclusion as the highest-ranking PIMPD in the present study highlights a persistent lack of awareness regarding its associated risks. Moreover, metoclopramide also consistently ranked among the predominant PIMPDs identified in our study. The findings of studies on managing gastrointestinal symptoms in PD patients have led to recommendations to use alternatives such as domperidone, which acts peripherally to block dopamine receptors, or mosapride, which targets serotonin receptors in the gastrointestinal tract, rather than centrally acting agents such as levosulpiride or metoclopramide.34 These results underscore the importance of careful management and the consideration of alternative medications when prescribing agents for managing gastrointestinal symptoms in PD patients.

Since psychiatric symptoms are prevalent nonmotor complications in patients with PD, antipsychotic medications—which usually act as antagonists of dopamine D2 receptors—are commonly used for PD psychosis, but they can worsen motor symptoms.35 Second-generation antipsychotics with weaker blocking effects on dopamine D2 receptors than first-generation antipsychotics have become treatment options for PD psychosis, but their efficacy and safety remain uncertain.36 Therefore, all antipsychotics must be used with great caution in PD patients because of the risk of ADEs, including increased mortality.37 We have revealed that both haloperidol, a first-generation antipsychotic agent, and risperidone, a second-generation antipsychotic agent, are frequently prescribed in Korean PD patients, and the use of haloperidol has shown a decreasing trend, while the use of risperidone has increased steadily. Both medications are well-known PIMs as well as PIMPDs, emphasizing the need for careful consideration when managing PD psychosis.

Current guidelines for PD psychosis recommend clozapine and pimavanserin as efficacious “clinically useful” treatment options.30 Although the evidence is insufficient, quetiapine is practically designated as “possibly useful” due to its efficacy and safety profile being similar to those of clozapine for the treatment of psychosis in PD.30 Since pimavanserin is not available in South Korea, quetiapine is used frequently in Korean PD patients. Based on the results of this study, the rate of prescribing quetiapine increased by about threefold in 2019 relative to 2014, which might reflect changes in the domestic healthcare system in South Korea. Due to specific characteristics of the Korean national health insurance system, the change directly affects the trends in drug prescribing patterns—the prescribing of quetiapine to PD patients with psychiatric symptoms began to be covered by Korean national health insurance in February 2018, and it seems likely that there was a dramatic change in quetiapine prescriptions around that time. Continuous education by domestic medical staff such as PD specialists as well as increased awareness among and active promotion to PD patients played major roles in the change in the indication for the medical insurance coverage of quetiapine.

The overall prescribing trends for flunarizine decreased, but it remained consistently ranked among the top five PIMPDs. Flunarizine is a calcium-channel blocker that decreases intracellular calcium levels and acts via diverse mechanisms, including histamine H1 receptor blockade and dopamine D2 receptor antagonism.38 While flunarizine is widely used for conditions such as migraine and vertigo, careful monitoring and cautious prescribing are essential, particularly for elderly patients and in those with compromised dopaminergic systems.38

The critical limitations of this study arise from inherent characteristics of the claims data, which were designed for the reimbursement of health insurance claims rather than for research purposes.14 The Korean national health insurance claims data are represented by the National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) data39 and the HIRA data.40 While these two data sources are similar, they exhibit several notable differences. HIRA data consist of five tables: general specifications, in-hospital treatment details, disease details, out-of-hospital prescription details, and nursing institution information.40 NHIS data include death records, some medical examination records, and socioeconomic variables of subjects such as income, in addition to all of the contents of the HIRA data.39

We selected the HIRA database since the research focus of this study was on prescribing trends. The first limitation of this study was PD being diagnosed only using KCD code G20. Unlike NHIS data, it was impossible to apply an additional rare and incurable disease registration code (V124) to define the inclusion criteria for PD using HIRA data. Furthermore, the inclusion of some PIMs among anti-Parkinson medications made it difficult to define the inclusion criteria using related medications in this study. The second limitation was that we were unable to fully exclude individuals with drug-induced parkinsonism (DIP) or atypical parkinsonism using the claims data. To minimize this potential misclassification, patients were selected when they had continuous and repetitive G20 code records within a defined period rather than only a single diagnosis record, in an attempt to reduce the probability of including transient DIP cases. The third limitation was the lack of detailed clinical information in the claims data preventing consideration of individual patient conditions and prescribing rationales. While certain PIMs may be inappropriate for older adults based on the Beers Criteria, they may be clinically necessary in specific cases, particularly in PD patients. However, the clinical judgments of physicians regarding PIM prescriptions could not be fully explained in this study. The Beers Criteria do not imply a one-size-fits-all prescribing restriction, and the concept of PIM itself is not fixed but can vary depending on various clinical conditions. Moreover, the claims data did not include nonreimbursable prescriptions or over-the-counter medications, potentially leading to underestimation of actual PIM use.14 Accordingly, rather than strictly judging the appropriateness of PIM or PIMPD lists, clinical considerations and flexibility of interpretation were warranted. Lastly, our findings were derived from the Korean healthcare system, which might restrict their generalizability to the global context. Although the Beers Criteria are widely used, their US-based framework might not fully reflect specialty-specific prescribing patterns, regional drug access, or the overall healthcare system in South Korea. Future studies should develop South-Korea-specific PIM lists that consider patient-level factors such as PD subtype, disease stage, and cognitive status. More-granular analyses by specialty and region may provide clinically meaningful insights for developing localized prescribing guidelines.

Notwithstanding these limitations, this study had several notable strengths. It performed one of the largest nationwide analyses of PD patients using the HIRA database, yielding comprehensive information about prescribing patterns across South Korea. Unlike single-year analyses, this study examined long-term prescribing trends over a 6-year period (2014–2019), enabling the identification of temporal changes in PIM and PIMPD use. Additionally, the inclusion of polypharmacy and PIMPD prescribing patterns in PD patients enabled more-comprehensive assessments of inappropriate medication use. These findings offer valuable insights to improve future prescribing guidelines and strategies for ensuring medication safety in PD management.

In conclusion, our retrospective big-data analyses have quantified the problem of PIM use in Korean PD patients. The prevalence and usage of PIMs remain high, with the utilization of medications that may worsen PD symptoms persisting. The increase in PIM prescriptions cannot be overlooked in the context of safe pharmacotherapy for PD patients, who commonly undergo polypharmacy. This suggests the importance of reviewing the benefits and risks of each medication as well as drug-drug interactions, and implementing appropriate prescribing practices. These findings can be used to develop evidence-based guidelines for robust polypharmacy management strategies to enhance the afety of medications in PD patients. Further research is needed to identify the key drivers of PIM prescriptions and their impact on patients with PD. Additionally, education programs and interventions are needed to optimize PIM prescribing practices.
