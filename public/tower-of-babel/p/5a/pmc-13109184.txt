Pharmacological, Natural and Emerging Therapies for Meibomian Gland Dysfunction: A Review
Authors: Jordan M Cooper, Ryan Mahmoud, Catherine J Jennings, Kalika Bandamwar, Michael T M Wang, Jennifer P Craig
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMeibomian gland dysfunction (MGD) is a leading cause of non-aqueous deficient dry eye disease. Meibomian glands are responsible for the production of meibum, a lipid-rich secretion that coats the surface of the tear film, inhibiting evaporation of the underlying aqueous tear fluid. MGD can arise due to numerous extrinsic and intrinsic factors that drive changes in meibum quality or obstruction of gland orifices and can result in tear film instability, hyperosmolarity, epithelial damage and downstream ocular surface inflammation. Despite the high disease prevalence, management is not standardised, with a wide range of pharmacological and naturally derived therapies available. Potential pharmacological agents include anti-inflammatories (antibiotics, immunosuppressants and topical integrin antagonists), perfluorohexyloctane and topical aqueous secretagogues. Natural therapies that may offer suitable long-term solutions include oral dietary supplements (polyunsaturated fatty acids, astaxanthin, bilberry, Chinese herbal formulations and Korean red ginseng) and naturally derived topical formulations (castor oil, manuka honey, tea tree oil, coenzyme Q10 and green tea extract). The rising prevalence of MGD has led to the development of several novel therapeutic strategies, expanding the range of management options available to clinicians. A thorough examination should precede the initiation of treatment to identify underlying pathophysiology and guide targeted interventions. Key Points

Meibomian gland dysfunction (MGD), considered the leading cause of dry eye disease (DED), is characterised by changes in meibum quality or outflow, which can drive tear film instability, hyperosmolarity and ocular surface damage.Despite high disease prevalence, MGD management is not standardised, with an extensive range of pharmacological and naturally derived therapies available to the clinician. This review provides an update of the current evidence on efficacy, duration of action and safety of existing and emerging treatments.

Introduction

Meibomian Gland Dysfunction Pathophysiology and Its Role in Dry Eye Disease

Meibomian glands, which are modified sebaceous glands in the upper and lower eyelids, produce meibum, a lipid-rich secretion that coats the surface of the tear film. The lipid layer that forms from the meibum is critical in stabilising the tear film. The resulting integrity of the lipid layer is fundamental to protecting the underlying aqueous tear fluid from excessive evaporation [1–3]. Meibomian gland dysfunction (MGD) is characterised by gland orifice obstruction and/or changes to meibum quality/quantity which may lead to long-term chronic structural changes. This compromises the lipid layer, resulting in localised evaporation and tear film hyperosmolarity, which can trigger the vicious cycle of dry eye disease (DED), leading to epithelial cell damage and resulting in chronic ocular surface inflammation [1, 4]. This damage compromises the ability of the ocular surface to support a stable tear film, promoting further loss of tear film and ocular surface homeostasis, leading to chronic symptoms of discomfort, impacted quality of vision and a reduction in quality of life [5, 6]. MGD has been described as hypersecretory, hyposecretory or obstructive in subtype, with the latter being the most common [7]. Obstruction typically results from hyperkeratinisation of the glands or lid margin and/or altered meibum composition, which increases meibum viscosity and melting point, and is often associated with anterior blepharitis. This leads to gland blockage and reduced secretion. Over time, meibum stasis raises intraductal pressure, resulting in meibomian gland dilation and ultimately atrophy [8–10]. MGD prevalence and severity increase with age and in chronic inflammatory diseases such as acne rosacea [11, 12]. MGD is recognised to be a leading cause of DED, a common, chronic, symptomatic condition that can affect any of the tissues and glands of the ocular surface [5, 6].

Assessment and Diagnosis of Meibomian Gland Dysfunction

Clinical MGD assessment involves evaluation of four key components: meibomian glands, lid margin, tear film/ocular surface parameters and symptoms [13, 14]. Meibomian gland parameters include gland expressibility, meibum quality and gland morphology, including gland dilation, truncation and dropout [15, 16]. Lid margin findings such as telangiectasia, capping, rounding and thickening, often correlate with meibomian gland parameters [14]. Lipid layer analysis provides useful insight into gland function. However, given the close association between MGD and DED, broader tear film and ocular surface assessments, including tear film stability, osmolarity and ocular surface staining, combined with symptoms, offer a more complete understanding of the disease and its impact [13]. Global consensus diagnostic criteria have been developed and refined with the purpose of reducing variability in disease prevalence estimates that arise in the absence of a standardised diagnostic algorithm [5, 17].

Contemporary Management of Meibomian Gland Dysfunction

With the prevalence of MGD recognised to be high, an increasing range of management options has become available in recent years, with treatment selection guided by underlying pathophysiology. Non-pharmacological approaches aim to improve meibum release and/or reduce orifice obstruction and commonly include heating therapies (e.g. patient-applied warm compresses, latent heat therapy, thermal pulsation therapy), lid margin debridement, eyelid hygiene and light-based treatments such as intense pulsed light (IPL) and low-level light therapy (LLLT) [18, 19]. Several topical and oral therapies, including antibiotics, immunosuppressants, tear stabilisers and polyunsaturated fatty acid supplementation, have shown promise in MGD management, however, they should be employed selectively on the basis of appropriate clinical indications [18, 19].

This review focuses on pharmacological, natural and emerging therapies for MGD, excluding interventions that primarily target DED, which frequently presents secondarily to MGD. In addition to outcomes from randomised controlled trials (RCTs), which represent one of the highest levels of evidence, other prospective studies including quasi-experimental controlled trials and pre–post intervention studies have been considered, providing valuable clinical insight [20]. Retrospective studies, observational research and reviews were excluded in support of methodological rigor and consistency. Relevant studies were identified through PubMed, Ovid MEDLINE and Google Scholar databases, using key search terms including “meibomian gland dysfunction”, “posterior blepharitis”, “management” and “treatment”, along with terms specific to individual treatments. Emerging therapies were identified through clinical trial registries, including ClinicalTrials.gov, Australian New Zealand Clinical Trial Registry (ANZCTR) and ISRCTN - The UK's Clinical Study Registry. The Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) levels of evidence framework was used to categorise studies [21] (Online Resource). Broadly, this defines level 1 as randomized controlled trials (RCTs), level 2 as trials of quasi-experimental design and level 3 as observational studies.

Pharmacological Treatments

Doxycycline and azithromycin are broad-spectrum tetracycline and macrolide antibiotics, respectively, traditionally prescribed to treat bacterial infections [47, 48], however, they are also commonly used in the treatment of rosacea [49], which has recognised associations with MGD [50]. Both antibiotics have been shown to reduce ocular surface inflammation through modulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines [49, 51–55], in turn demonstrating favourable effects on meibum quality and expressibility of meibomian glands, improving tear film stability and providing symptomatic relief.

Directly compared, azithromycin may be more effective than doxycycline in improving MGD signs and symptoms [30, 36], however, evidence is not consistent, with a number of studies reporting no significant difference in efficacy between the drugs [25, 29, 31]. Relative to doxycycline, azithromycin involves a shorter treatment course and results in side effects such as gastrointestinal upset, nausea and loss of appetite that are less severe and of shorter duration [25, 29–31, 35, 36]. In contrast, doxycycline is associated with photosensitivity and contraindications relating to teeth and bone development during pregnancy and in children [47].

Minocycline, another broad-spectrum tetracycline derivative, reduces inflammation by decreasing the formation of reactive oxygen species through neutrophil recruitment [56]. Improved meibum quality, reduced lid margin abnormalities and symptoms have been confirmed in two studies [44, 45]. Additionally, after 2 months of treatment, minocycline significantly reduced levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines in a level 1 study [45]. Oral minocycline appears similar to doxycycline in clinical effect, but future research directly comparing the antibiotics in controlled trials is needed to establish safety profiles [44, 45].

In the reported studies, oral azithromycin dosage was most commonly 1.5 g, administered over a 3–5-day period, frequently with 500 mg initially, followed by 250 mg once daily for 4 days [25, 29, 31, 41–43]. However, a number of studies report prescribing of 500 mg once daily for 3 days [30, 38–40]. In some instances, the treatment regime spanned 4 weeks, repeating the initial regimen weekly [30, 39, 40, 43]. The dosing rationale is frequently not mentioned in studies, although one study used the justification that a single 1 g dose translates to high tear fluid concentrations for at least 4 days and conjunctival levels for 14 days [35, 57]. No reported study has compared the relative effect of different azithromycin dosages, highlighting an area worthy of further research.

Varying dosing schedules are reported for oral doxycycline, with a single dose of 100 mg daily, for 3–16 weeks described across four studies [23, 27, 32, 37], and a stronger dose (200 mg daily) administered in eight studies, typically for a shorter duration of 4–6 weeks [22, 25, 29, 30, 33–35]. Four studies used tapered doses, most commonly commencing with a dose of 200 mg per day for 7–10 days, reducing to 100 mg daily from 2 weeks to 1 month [24, 26, 28, 31]. No specific dosing schedule demonstrated superior efficacy, however, side effects appeared to be dose dependent. A comparative case series reported that low dose doxycycline at 40 mg per day was as effective as 400 mg per day, with significantly fewer side effects, but did not assess MGD signs [58]. Such variable dosing is similarly observed with respect to oral minocycline [44–46].

In terms of treatment duration, oral azithromycin has been used safely for up to 12 weeks in adults [49, 59, 60], whilst oral doxycycline has been used safely for up to 12 months of ongoing administration in paediatric patients with ocular rosacea [61]. In prescribing for MGD, a maximum dosing duration of 4 weeks is reported for oral azithromycin [38] and 90 days for doxycycline [26]. The dosing duration of oral minocycline was approximately 2 months in all reported studies [44–46].

A limited number of studies have explored the longitudinal effects of oral antibiotics on MGD, warranting further research to draw more definitive conclusions. Azithromycin reportedly improved both signs of MGD and dry eye symptoms for up to 6 months post-treatment in a level 2 study [41]. Doxycycline has been reported in two level 1 studies to improve signs and symptoms for up to at least 3 months [26, 29]. A 12-week course of minocycline has shown similar beneficial effects up to at least 3 months post-treatment in a level 2 study [44].

Topical Antibiotics

Azithromycin, in topical form, demonstrates similar anti-inflammatory properties and therapeutic effects on MGD as in oral form, with a total of 16 studies investigating the effect of its topical application on MGD [22, 27, 32–34, 37, 39, 42, 43, 62–68] (Table 2). Although not globally available, topical azithromycin has become an increasingly popular therapeutic choice for MGD, shown in two level 1 studies following a 2-week course to outperform conventional management involving either preservative-free artificial tears or warm compresses, demonstrating a significantly greater short-term improvement in meibomian gland parameters [62, 67].

Evidence suggests that antibiotics applied topically, as opposed to orally, may have a superior safety profile and be more effective in improving MGD signs and symptoms [24, 33, 37, 42, 43], even where no differences in efficacy were apparent [22, 27, 32, 33]. Stinging and blurred vision on instillation are the most commonly reported side effects of topical azithromycin, typically subsiding after the first few days [22, 27, 32–34, 62, 64, 65, 67, 68]. Both topical azithromycin and oral doxycycline improve meibum properties, but lipid hydrocarbon chain order and phase transition temperature changes were found to be superior with topical azithromycin [69].

Topical azithromycin demonstrates lipid-regulating effects [64] with faster onset than oral doxycycline and greater efficacy after 3 weeks as reported in a level 1 study [37], although two level 1 studies with follow-up periods of 4 weeks or longer show minimal difference [33, 34]. Over a 1-month course, a level 2 study demonstrated topical azithromycin to be superior to oral doxycycline at 1 week, but with comparable effects on eyelid signs after 1 month of treatment and up to 1 month after treatment cessation [27]. Similarly, another level 1 study found more rapid effects with topical than oral azithromycin, with significant improvements in eyelid debris at 1-week post-treatment, however, similar improvements were reported at 5 weeks [43]. Tear film stability benefits with topical azithromycin appear to outlast improvements in other clinical ocular surface or MGD-associated parameters. In theory, this could be due to a sustained concentration of azithromycin within the ocular tissue for approximately 5 weeks after stopping treatment [22, 27, 43], or, not inconceivably, the regular topical vehicle application serving as a lubricant [70]. A 69% recurrence of symptoms was reported with topical azithromycin when used for only 6 days, compared with 25% with 1 month of oral doxycycline after 3 months, suggesting a need for longer treatment durations with topical azithromycin [32].

Evidence on concurrent prescribing of oral and topical antibiotics is limited. A level 1 study found both oral azithromycin and combined oral and topical azithromycin to be effective in improving symptoms and meibum quality, with the combination superior with respect to tear film stability and corneal staining [39]. Conversely, a similarly designed study with an additional topical azithromycin-only treatment arm reported comparable MGD improvement across all groups [42]. Inconsistent findings and limited evidence suggest further research is required to confirm any synergistic effect.

Topical Corticosteroids

Corticosteroids bind to glucocorticoid receptors, inhibiting pro-inflammatory mediator synthesis. However, corticosteroids also act on the inflammatory cascade in multiple other ways to reduce inflammation, by upregulating specific proteins that modulate enzymatic activity, prostaglandin and leukotriene synthesis and neutrophil function [71]. Oral corticosteroids are used widely to manage inflammatory conditions such as inflammatory bowel disease and asthma [72, 73], however, they also play a role in managing anterior segment and ocular surface inflammation [74]. Extended use is associated with a number of sight-threatening side effects including increased intraocular pressure, cataract formation and secondary infection [75].

Corticosteroids for ocular application can differ in corneal penetrability. Highly penetrating corticosteroids, such as prednisolone acetate, are readily transported through the cornea and can reach target structures within the anterior chamber, whilst less-penetrating agents such as fluorometholone or loteprednol etabonate are better suited to managing surface disease with reduced risk of adverse effects [76]. Topical corticosteroid use has been widely reported with confirmed efficacy in improving symptoms and general signs of DED, particularly in DED secondary to inflammatory conditions such as Sjögren’s disease or graft-versus-host disease [77, 78]. Use in MGD, where inflammation arises predominantly as a downstream sequela of disrupted tear film homeostasis, may be less applicable [6, 79].

Where the impacts of MGD trigger an acute inflammatory response at the ocular surface, a short course of pulsed corticosteroids may be used whilst the targeted therapy of an identified source deficiency takes effect [78]. However, evidence of efficacy remains limited, with only two studies exploring the effects of loteprednol etabonate on MGD [80, 81], and three studies evaluating fluorometholone [82–84] (Table 3).

In a level 1 and level 2 study, loteprednol etabonate 0.5%, applied four times daily for 2 months, significantly reduced both signs and symptoms associated with MGD, including MG expressibility, meibum quality and lid margin appearance [80, 81]. Both studies also reported a significant reduction in pro-inflammatory mediators [80, 81]. The three studies (two level 1 studies and a single level 2 study) evaluating fluorometholone 0.1% found similar benefits in meibum quality between 4 and 12 weeks [82–84].

Although corticosteroids reduce inflammation and improve MGD signs, their use as first-line therapy is not recommended due to potential side effects where safer alternatives exist [78]. Risk of symptom rebound after cessation further limits their suitability long term [85]. Comparative studies of different corticosteroids could help guide best clinical practice with regard to their use and applicability.

Topical Antibiotic/Corticosteroid Combination

Corticosteroids in combination with aminoglycoside antibiotics are available in commercial formulations. Four studies have investigated the effect of topical antibiotic–steroid combinations on MGD [86–89]; tobramycin combined with dexamethasone in three level 1 studies [86, 88, 89] and netilmicin combined with dexamethasone in a single level 2 study [87] (Table 4).

Of the three studies exploring topical tobramycin 0.3%/dexamethasone 0.1% ointment, two reported treatment for 4 weeks [86, 88] and one for 12 weeks [89]. Dosage varied across the studies. With all three robust study designs, improvements in both signs and symptoms associated with MGD were confirmed following 1 month of treatment [86, 88, 89]. Use, pre- and post-operatively in cataract surgery patients in one study, resulted in superior post-operative outcomes in both signs and symptoms compared with post-operative management only [88]. Given the recognised risk of DED exacerbation post-operatively, this study lends support to the concept of pre-operative MGD management [88, 90].

Netilmicin 0.3%/dexamethasone 0.1% topical gel (daily dosage unspecified) resulted in significant improvements in MGD signs and symptoms after 15 days relative to vehicle control [87]. This same combination has also been reported to reduce bulbar conjunctival hyperaemia and be better tolerated than the tobramycin/dexamethasone combination in a study looking at post-vitreoretinal surgery [91], however, this has not been tested in MGD.

Whilst antibiotic and topical steroids have been shown to be effective at both reducing inflammation and improving MGD signs, further research is warranted to confirm the benefits and risks of steroid/antibiotic combination therapy relative to those of the individual components or to other non-pharmacological means of managing MGD.

Steroid-Sparing Topical Immunosuppressants

Immunosuppressive agents reduce the inflammatory response in chronic DED, offering a safer alternative for long-term therapy than corticosteroids. The effects of topical immunosuppressants on MGD have been assessed in a number of studies, [24, 83, 89, 92–97] mostly evaluating cyclosporin A (CsA) [24, 83, 89, 92, 94–96], and less commonly tacrolimus [93, 97] (Table 5).

CsA is an immunosuppressive agent that targets T-lymphocytes, facilitating an anti-inflammatory response [98]. Topical CsA 0.05% has been shown to improve meibomian gland secretions, increase lipid layer thickness and reduce lid margin telangiectasia after as little as 4 weeks of twice daily application in three level 1 studies [92, 95, 96] and after up to 3 months in other studies of similar design [24, 94]. Clinical improvements in MGD signs can be substantial. A level 1 study reported an improvement in meibomian gland plugging of greater than 50% after 3 months of twice daily CsA application [94]. Similar improvements in gland plugging, as well as in reducing eyelid inflammation [89, 92, 95, 96], have been shown, with demonstration of improved secretion quality in most [89, 92, 96] but not all [95] studies.

Although CsA appears to yield significant improvements in both symptoms and signs of MGD, the effect on symptoms is variable, with several studies reporting no difference in effect between CsA and artificial tears [94–96]. Two level 2 studies reporting longer duration treatment reported symptom improvement at 12 weeks [92, 95], suggesting possible superiority over artificial tears or oral antibiotics [24, 92]. Variations in control groups and the short duration of follow-up may mask CsA’s long-term potential. Level 1 studies have shown that a notable improvement in MGD compared with either baseline or control may take up to 12 weeks from starting treatment [89, 95, 96], and symptomatic relief conceivably may take time, particularly given common reports of irritation on instillation, which tend to ease with time.

Tacrolimus (FK-506) is a macrolide agent with an immunosuppressive effect recognised to be several hundredfold greater than that of CsA [99–102]. Similarly to CsA, tacrolimus ultimately inhibits lymphocyte activation [103]. Level 1 studies describing use of tacrolimus 0.03% twice daily for 4 weeks report improvements in upper eyelid telangiectasia and corneal staining, but without a significant improvement in meibum quality [93, 97]. Both studies had a follow-up period of only 1 month, which may have been insufficient to observe MGD improvements, given the 3-month time frame demonstrated for efficacy of CsA [89, 92, 96].

Available evidence suggests that CsA reduces lid margin inflammation, gland expressibility and plugging. CsA does not conclusively influence the quality of meibomian gland secretion in the same way as antibiotics, and it can take time before patients notice an improvement in symptoms that is superior to that of artificial tears. Although more studies on tacrolimus are warranted, current evidence suggests that it demonstrates a similar effect on MGD as CsA, however, no direct comparison between the two immunosuppressants has yet been reported.

Topical Integrin Antagonist

Lifitegrast is an anti-inflammatory drug approved for treating DED. It is a lymphocytic function-associated antigen-1 (LFA-1) antagonist, inhibiting LFA-1 interaction with inter-cellular adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM-1). The LFA-1/ICAM-1binding pathway plays a key role in T-cell activation, adhesion and migration, promoting the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines involved in the cell-mediated inflammatory response. The potential effects of lifitegrast on MGD have been explored in a number of studies [104, 105] (Table 6).

One level 1 study comparing lifitegrast with thermal pulsation therapy found it to be more effective at reducing eyelid redness, likely attributable to the drug’s anti-inflammatory properties. Yet, whilst lifitegrast effectively reduced pro-inflammatory cytokine levels, it was found to be no more effective in managing MGD than thermal pulsation [104]. Lifitegrast use is associated with mild side effects. The level 1 study, described, reported no adverse events, however, 5.1% withdrew due to a lack of improvement [104]. In terms of patient preference, a retrospective review investigating patients who used both lifitegrast and CsA over the course of their disease, found a higher percentage of patients expressed preference for CsA [107].

Diquafosol has been linked to a rapid, transient increase in lipid layer thickness post-instillation [110, 112, 113]. Two level 1 studies have observed short-term improvements in lipid layer thickness immediately following diquafosol instillation [112, 113]. Conversely, even with ongoing treatment for 3 months, the lipid layer remains unaffected unless diquafosol is instilled immediately prior to assessment [110, 111, 115]. Only three level 2 studies have explored the effect of diquafosol on MGD-specific parameters over a longer period [110, 111, 114]. A significant improvement in meiboscore, meibomian gland plugging and lid margin telangiectasia after instilling diquafosol six times daily for 12 weeks was reported in one study [110]. Diquafosol has also been shown to slow meibomian gland loss in patients with glaucoma being treated with prostaglandin analogues over 48 weeks [114]. Another study reported no significant change to gland dropout over 12 weeks [111]. This highlights the need for more robust, long-term studies to draw definitive conclusions on the efficacy of diquafosol, particularly in regard to its possible protective effect on the meibomian glands, as assessments tend to be highly subjective.

Diquafosol has been compared with rebamipide, another aqueous secretagogue that primarily stimulates prostaglandin and mucus glycoprotein production, whilst inhibiting pro-inflammatory cytokines, reactive oxygen species and neutrophil activation [116]. Whilst rebamipide in oral form is commonly used for gastric disorders, the topical form may be used to treat DED [117–119]. It does not appear to have a significant effect on MGD, however, with no significant change in lipid layer thickness observed after 1 month of treatment [115], suggesting that rebamipide may impact the tear film through mechanisms other than lipid layer enhancement. Further long-term studies exploring the effects on meibomian gland parameters are needed to confirm any impact on meibomian glands.

Perfluorohexyloctane

Perfluorohexyloctane is a non-aqueous, semifluorinated alkane with both lipophobic and lipophilic properties [120]. Due to its unique amphiphilic structure, it is proposed to interact with the tear film by embedding its hydrocarbon segment within the lipid layer, whilst the fluorocarbon segment forms an air–liquid interface. The resulting thin lipomimetic film has been shown to enhance stability of the tear film [121, 122]. Perfluorohexyloctane has been shown to be an effective treatment option for DED, improving both symptoms and signs including tear film stability and ocular surface staining [123]. Given its unique pharmacodynamic properties, it has been investigated as a treatment for MGD in a total of seven studies [124–130] (Table 8).

Three level 1 trials have reported that perfluorohexyloctane, applied four to five times daily, is effective in improving lipid layer thickness in studies of up to 3 months in duration [124–126]. However, it does not appear to do this directly through supplementation, with one of the studies finding no significant change in lipid layer thickness immediately after instillation [125], suggesting that the long-term, sustained improvement may be due to other mechanisms such as a positive effect on meibum quality [128, 130]. Perfluorohexyloctane may potentially dissolve structurally altered lipids, and therefore has been postulated to reduce meibomian gland plugging and improve lipid secretion [128, 131]. Supporting this, improvements in gland expressibility, meibum quality, and lid margin appearance have been observed in two level 2 studies [128, 130]. Perfluorohexyloctane had no demonstrable effect on meibomian gland dropout over a 2-month treatment period in two level 1 studies [124, 129].

Given the limited evidence exploring clinical signs specific to MGD, further studies of robust design should explore relevant parameters over a longer time period to better understand the impact of longer-term perfluorohexyloctane application. Applied topically, perfluorohexyloctane exhibits a relatively good safety profile, with the most common side effects being transiently blurred vision [127, 128] and hypersensitivity [129] comparable to that of topical sodium chloride application [126, 129].

Nutraceutical Treatments

Nutraceuticals are derived from food sources and typically are available as dietary supplements aimed at providing medicinal or health benefits [132]. Topical formulations with naturally derived ingredients also represent a growing area of research relating to clinical application. This section summarises current scientific evidence on oral and topical natural therapies used in the treatment of MGD.

Polyunsaturated Fatty Acids (PUFA)

Omega-3

Omega-3 (n-3) polyunsaturated fatty acid (PUFA), predominantly alpha-linolenic acid (ALA), eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA), and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), are essential nutrients with known health benefits [133]. Unable to be synthesised by the body, they must be obtained through diet, particularly from fatty fish such as salmon and tuna, leafy vegetables, nuts, meat and eggs [133–135]. Marine-derived sources of omega-3 are readily absorbed by the body, unlike plant-based sources such as ALA, which requires conversion to EPA and DHA, which is less efficient [136].

The effect of omega-3 supplementation on MGD has been investigated in 12, predominantly level 1, studies [137–148] (Table 9), and is thought to modulate DED secondary to MGD through its role as a precursor to anti-inflammatory mediators [149], which supports improved meibum quality and meibomian gland expressibility [137, 140, 141, 143–147]. Seven studies have found omega-3 to be significantly more effective at improving meibomian gland quality and expressibility, in addition to other parameters, than either placebo or conventional therapy control [137, 140, 141, 143–146]. No differences in meibomian gland parameters were noted between treatment and placebo groups in four studies [138, 139, 142, 148]. A level 1 study reported that oral flaxseed oil, rich in omega-3, was more effective at reducing meibomian gland orifice blockage than the olive oil control, but that meibum quality, based on colour and fluidity, showed significant improvement in both groups [147].

Notably, of the seven comparative studies demonstrating superiority of omega-3, three described comparison with warm compresses, vitamin E and artificial tears, respectively, [137, 144, 145] and one did not specify the control [141]. Control oils varied across studies, with some using omega-6 rich oils such as sunflower or grapeseed oil. All studies that reported no significant difference chose a control with a higher ratio of omega-6 to omega-3 [138, 139, 142, 148]. Both studies using olive oil as a control were from the Dry Eye Assessment and Management (DREAM) study [138, 139]. The original 12-month level 1 study [138] reported no difference in meibography scores but did not assess any other meibomian gland parameters. The extension study [139], which looked at meibomian gland expression and meibum quality, reported no change associated with either omega-3 supplementation or the control following a further 12 months of treatment. However, given that the initial study did not assess these parameters, it is not possible to ascertain the initial effects of treatment.

Studies demonstrating positive benefit often included concurrent treatments of artificial tears, warm compress, lid massage and eyelid hygiene [137, 140, 143–147]. Only two studies, both level 1, explored the supplementary benefit of omega-3 over standard-of-care therapy, comparing the combined effects with either artificial tears or warm compress alone, and in both cases, found the combination to be more effective than conventional treatment alone [137, 145].

Exploring meibum quality after 90 days of omega-3 supplementation, one study explored meibum lipid composition with high-performance liquid chromatography and found no significant change in its composition. No other MGD signs were assessed [150].

Most studies involved administration of 1.2–2.2 g of omega-3 daily, with EPA typically ranging from 600 mg to 1680 mg and DHA from 480 mg to 1640 mg [140, 142, 143, 145, 148], although many studies failed to provide a detailed breakdown. Higher daily intake and longer durations did not consistently correlate with improved outcomes, potentially influenced by baseline omega-3 levels, which were not measured in these level 1 studies [139, 147]. It is plausible that those deficient in omega-3 may experience more benefit from dietary supplementation [136].

Evidence regarding efficacy of omega-3 in managing MGD is not robust. A 2020 systematic review and meta-analysis reported significant benefit to meibomian gland parameters [151] but attributed variability in outcomes to differences in control oils, baseline omega-3 levels and lifestyle factors. A level 1 trial described significant improvement only in severe MGD [137], suggesting that disease severity may influence the effect [137]. Isolating the impacts of omega-3 supplementation, in research, remains challenging due to dietary variations, making it difficult to ascertain the true effect of omega-3 in MGD.

Omega-6

Omega-6 (n-6) PUFAs, such as arachidonic acid or its precursor linoleic acid, are essential fatty acids that, similarly to omega-3 PUFAs, cannot be synthesised by the human body [135] and must be obtained through the consumption of meats, vegetable oils and other animal products [133, 152, 153]. Arachidonic acid, an omega-6 PUFA, gives rise to pro-inflammatory mediators, but studies show that increased intake does not necessarily elevate inflammatory markers [153]. Rather, the omega-3 to omega-6 fatty acid ratio is a more important determinant of inflammatory status [154].

Only two level 1 studies have evaluated omega-6 supplementation on MGD-specific parameters [155, 156] (Table 9). One RCT reported that daily supplementation with linoleic acid (28.5 mg) and γ-linolenic acid (15 mg) improved meibum quality and meibomian gland obstruction. Similar improvements together with a reduction in eyelid oedema and corneal staining were seen with daily eyelid hygiene using warm compresses, eyelid massage and cleansing. Combined dietary supplementation and lid hygiene further reduced tear meniscus foam [155]. The study did not compare effects between omega-3 and omega-6 supplementation, limiting conclusions regarding relative efficacy. The second RCT found evening primrose oil, containing predominantly omega-6 PUFA, improved contact lens discomfort compared with the olive oil control, but had no effect on lipid layer thickness or meibum quality; however, the baseline MGD status of participants was not reported [156].

Several studies have compared omega-3-with omega-6-rich controls comprising sunflower oil, grapeseed oil or linoleic acid. Of these studies, no difference in effect on MGD signs was observed between omega-3 supplementation and linoleic acid or grapeseed oil [142, 148], however, omega-3 was reported to be more effective in improving both signs and symptoms than sunflower oil [146]. Given that only a few studies have looked specifically at the potential health benefits of omega-6 in relation to MGD, further research is needed to better understand its association with meibomian gland function. As noted, wide variability in the range of supplements tested and the difficulty in isolating individual constituents presents challenges, and it is possible that the source of omega-6 could play a crucial role in the potential benefits conferred.

Combination Supplements

As described, the balance between omega-3 and omega-6 PUFA intake plays a critical role in maintaining optimal cellular levels and regulating inflammatory responses. A dietary omega-6 to omega-3 ratio of approximately 2:1 to 4:1 is considered ideal [135, 136, 154]. Whether the combination of PUFA, vitamins and other micronutrients in supplements and oils offers a potential synergistic effect on meibomian gland health remains to be elucidated. Three level 1 studies have evaluated the effect of combined supplementation on MGD signs and symptoms [157–159]. One study looked specifically at an omega-3 and omega-6 combination [157], and the other two studied commercially available dietary supplements [158, 159] (Table 9).

One study investigated a combination of omega-3 (1200 mg EPA, 300 mg DHA) and omega-6 (150 mg γ-linolenic acid), comparing it with a control containing coconut and olive oil. Whilst no change in meibography was observed over 3 months, the study reported a significant improvement in symptoms in both groups, with greater improvement in the treatment group, in those with an Ocular Surface Disease Index (OSDI) score greater than 52 at baseline [157]. This lends support to the theory that the magnitude of effect from dietary supplementation may be influenced by baseline disease severity. Individual components were not compared in this study, nor were any other meibomian gland parameters, making it difficult to ascertain the potential benefits of combining omega-3 and omega-6.

Of the two studies exploring commercially available dietary supplements, conducted by the same research group, one focused on Retaron® [158], and the other on Licofor [159]. Retaron® is an omega-3-containing supplement that includes a range of vitamins and other micronutrients such as β-carotene, lutein and zeaxanthin [158]. Licofor is a combined omega-3 and omega-6 supplement, and similar to Retaron®, contains lutein, zeaxanthin and a range of other micronutrients [159]. In both studies, all participants received concurrent treatment with warm compress therapy, artificial tears and antibiotic ointment. Improvements in meibum quality, meibomian gland expressibility and lid margin morphology were reported in both the treatment and control groups, weakening evidence of the supplements as effective MGD management options. A significant reduction in inflammatory cell density near the meibomian glands was also observed in the treatment group alone in both studies, however, this attribution to any one component of the supplement is not possible without further investigation [158, 159].

Other Oral Supplements

There is limited evidence published on several other oral supplements with regard to benefits in MGD. Studies that have identified possible improvements in clinical signs of MGD include astaxanthin [160], bilberry [161], Chinese herbal formulations [162] and Korean Red Ginseng [163] (Table 9).

Astaxanthin

Astaxanthin is a pigment found largely in crustaceans and salmon, amongst other seafood, which bestows a natural pink colour to certain seafoods. Importantly, it is a powerful antioxidant that may help to reduce oxidative stress and inflammation secondary to MGD [164]. Astaxanthin 12 mg daily has been reported in a level 2 study to significantly improve eyelid margin signs, meibomian gland expressibility and meibum quality, and also shows benefit in improving the broader signs and symptom of DED after 1 month of treatment [160] (Table 9).

Bilberry

Bilberry is a rich source of anthocyanins which give the bilberry its dark colour, and similarly to astaxanthin, is a powerful antioxidant that has potential to reduce oxidative stress and inflammation secondary to MGD [165]. A level 1 study evaluating 600 mg bilberry extract and 240 mg DHA-refined fish oil daily reported that compared with controls, the supplements produced no benefit in terms of the percentage of open meibomian gland orifices, but did result in a significantly greater improvement in symptoms and tear film stability (Table 9) [161]. This suggests that the supplement acted on the tear film through alternative mechanisms and not by influencing meibomian gland function. Again, the combined nature of the formulation prevents conclusions being drawn about bilberry’s effects in isolation.

Chinese Herbal Formulation

Dietary supplementation with a complex Chinese herbal formulation (Pinggan Yuyin Qingre, PGYYQR), containing a combination of antioxidants, anti-inflammatory agents and other bioactive ingredients with complementary therapeutic functions and other supportive ingredients, has been explored in a single level 1 study (Table 9) [162]. The study evaluated PGYYQR combined with artificial tear use, relative to artificial tears alone, and reported a significant improvement in meibomian gland secretion and eyelid margin morphology at 3 months compared with the control, with the improvement greatest in those with more severe MGD [162].

Korean Red Ginseng

Korean red ginseng is an herbal medicine, derived from the root of ginseng, that has documented immune enhancement, anti-oxidant and blood flow effects, amongst other benefits [166]. Ingestion of Korean red ginseng 3 g daily was tested in participants who required topical anti-glaucoma medication, in a single level 1 trial (Table 9) [163]. Whilst significant improvements in MGD quantity grade were observed in the treatment group only, no improvements in meibum quality were detected [163], suggesting a need for further research in this area.

Topical Natural Treatments

Several topical natural formulations have been investigated for DED, offering potential benefit from direct application to affected tissues. Topically applied natural ingredients that may improve clinical signs of MGD include castor oil [167, 168], Manuka honey [169–171], tea tree oil [172–174], coenzyme Q10 [175] and green tea extract [176] (Table 10).

Castor Oil

Castor oil has been identified as a potential treatment for anterior and posterior blepharitis on the basis of its reported antibacterial and antimicrobial properties. Furthermore, the main constituent of castor oil, ricinoleic acid, is a bipolar molecule potentially conducive to augmenting the tear film lipid layer [177]. Two level 1 studies have explored the effect of castor oil in MGD [167, 168].

A paired-eye RCT reported that 100% cold-pressed castor oil applied to the periocular area via a roller-ball applicator resulted in significant improvements in lid margin thickening, telangiectasia and crusting in treated eyes compared with the control eye at 4 weeks. No significant improvements in meibomian gland capping or expressibility, meibum quality or meibography grade were observed [167]. Improvements in symptoms as well as the listed clinical outcomes were attributed to a presumed benefit of castor oil in reducing signs of anterior blepharitis and eyelash crusting, including cylindrical dandruff, which is considered pathognomonic of Demodex infestation [178].

The other study explored a low-concentration homogenised castor oil eyedrop (2% castor oil with 5% polyoxyethylene castor oil as an emulsifier), reporting a significant improvement in meibomian gland obstruction score after application six times daily for 2 weeks [168]. This study did not look at any other MGD-specific parameters. No adverse events were reported in either study, suggesting favourable product tolerability [167, 168]. The limited evidence suggests that castor oil may benefit meibomian gland health, potentially through its antibacterial and antimicrobial properties, but further research is warranted.

Manuka Honey

Manuka honey (Leptospermum scoparium) produced from the nectar of the Manuka tree that is native to New Zealand and Australia has raised interest due to its potential antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory properties. A total of three level 1 studies have investigated Manuka honey in relation to meibomian gland health [169–171].

One study compared the use of Optimel 16% Manuka honey eye drops with conventional lubricants in patients with moderate-to-severe MGD. Both treatments significantly improved meibum quality and expressibility, and further improvements in symptoms, tear film stability and corneal staining were seen in the Manuka honey group only [169]. An earlier study reported improvement in meibomian gland expressibility when Manuka honey (98% gel and 16% drops) was added to conventional therapy (artificial tears, warm compress and lid massage) compared with conventional therapy alone. Specifically, the group using 98% Manuka honey gel showed superior changes in meibum expressibility over an 8-week treatment period, suggesting the possibility of a dose-dependent relationship, warranting further investigation.

Another RCT explored the effect of a non-commercialised, complexed methylglyoxal (MGO) Manuka honey microemulsion eye cream, thinly applied to the eyelids overnight over a 3-month period. Significant improvements were observed in symptoms and in several tear film parameters in the treatment group, including lipid layer grade. Improvements in meibomian gland expressibility or meibography grade were not observed [170].

Collectively, the evidence for topical Manuka honey application in MGD management remains limited, with the method of application and dose potentially playing a role in its efficacy. Tolerability, due to the mild transient stinging experienced on application, may also present a barrier to patient compliance [169–171].

Tea Tree Oil

Topical application of tea tree oil (TTO) to the eyelids in low concentrations, for management of anterior blepharitis, specifically Demodex blepharitis, has been investigated across numerous studies. In comparison, there are few studies exploring effects specifically in cases of MGD alone or reporting a clinical outcome measure for MGD. Of three level 1 studies identified, all consistently reported favourable outcomes for the management of MGD, demonstrating improvements in meibomian gland expressibility, orifice plugging and capping and meibum quality [172–174].

However, it is difficult to ascertain whether improvements were due to treating the co-existing anterior blepharitis rather than directly improving MGD. Further studies should investigate the effect of TTO lid hygiene, specifically in individuals without confounding signs of anterior blepharitis, to ascertain any primary effect in MGD. Despite its demonstrated efficacy, TTO has been associated with adverse effects in the form of ocular irritation and allergic reactions, and an in vitro study reported potential cytotoxic effects towards human meibomian gland cells [179, 180].

Coenzyme Q10

Coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10) is an endogenous, lipid soluble molecule involved in cellular functions, including energy production, and antioxidant defence. It is naturally synthesised by human cells but can also be found in foods such as fatty fish, animal products, nuts and seeds [181]. CoQ10 may be isolated as a supplement. A single level 1 study has explored the effects of a topical CoQ10 eye drop formulation with multiple constituents (crosslinked hyaluronic acid (XLHA) + CoQ10 eye drops (XLHA sodium salt 100 mg, CoQ10 100 mg, vitamin E TPGS 500 mg)). This formulation was tested against hyaluronic acid eyedrops, with both being applied four times daily for 3 months. The CoQ10 formulation was superior in improving MGD signs, symptoms and ocular surface staining, highlighting possible therapeutic benefit [175], although potential contributions from the other constituents limits the certainty of the benefits having arisen from CoQ10 itself.

Green Tea Extract

Green tea extract, rich in polyphenols, has antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties [182]. A single level 1 study investigated the effects of topical green tea extract in mild-to-moderate DED with MGD, over the course of 1 month, comparing it with artificial tears. Findings showed a significant improvement in meibum quality, tear film stability and symptoms in those treated with the topical green tea extract. No other MGD-specific parameters were investigated [176].

Heterogenous study outcomes and limited high-quality evidence is a common theme throughout much of nutraceutical and natural therapy research for MGD. Whilst a number of potentially promising natural therapies are being investigated, further research is needed in many areas to establish these as effective management options for MGD. Future studies require larger, standardised trials with longer follow-up periods to generate the robust evidence required to confirm the role of nutraceuticals in MGD management.

Emerging Therapies

The prominence of MGD has attracted development of novel therapeutic strategies to target various aspects of this multifactorial disease. This section summarises emerging pharmaceutical and nutraceutical treatments for MGD.

Pharmaceuticals

AXR-270 (AxeroVision/CS Pharmaceuticals)

AXR-270 is a selective glucocorticoid receptor agonist formulated as a topical eyelid cream. Initially developed by AxeroVision Inc and later acquired by CS Pharmaceuticals, AXR-270 was evaluated in a phase II clinical trial (NCT04469998) for MGD. Both 0.2% and 2% AXR-270 applied once daily for 22 days demonstrated improvements in signs and symptoms compared with vehicle [183]. Although positive outcomes were reported in the clinical trial registry, no results have been published in peer-reviewed literature. Commencement of a phase III trial has since been announced.

AZR-MD-001 (Azura)

Hyperkeratinisation of the meibomian gland ductal epithelium has been associated with obstructive MGD [1]. AZR-MD-001, a selenium sulphide-based ophthalmic ointment, is believed to exert keratolytic and lipogenic effects, potentially restoring meibomian gland function and meibum quality. Two phase II RCTs (NCT04314362 and NCT03652051) have reported favourable outcomes in the number of meibomian glands yielding liquid secretion (MGYLS), tear film stability and symptoms, following twice weekly, night time treatment with AZR-MD-001, with an apparent dose-dependent relationship up to 0.5% concentration [184–186]. AZR-MD-001 0.5% was found to have similar effects on MGYLS and symptoms over 6 months compared with the AZR-MD-001 1.0%, with fewer adverse events [186]. Two subsequent phase II clinical trials investigated twice weekly selenium sulphide topical ointment in contact lens wearers (NCT05548491 and NCT03972501), with results corroborating those of the earlier studies and further demonstrating improvement in contact lens discomfort and ocular surface staining [187].

AZR-MD-001 appears to be safe and well tolerated, with treatment-associated adverse events typically limited to ocular irritation/pain, lacrimation and ocular surface staining, and ranging from mild to moderate in severity [184]. The drug is currently under investigation in a phase III clinical trial (NCT06329791), which will look at the effects of 0.5% ophthalmic ointment, applied twice weekly, on MGYLS and OSDI symptom score [188]. Given that hyperkeratinisation of the lid margin represents a key etiological factor, AZR-MD-001 represents a promising, novel pharmacological approach to managing MGD.

CBT-006 and CBT-008 (Cloudbreak Pharmaceuticals)

CBT-006 and CBT-008 are novel therapeutics targeting potential cholesterol crystal deposits at the meibomian gland orifices. Both have been tested topically in phase II RCTs (NCT04884243 and NCT05261386), applying 2.5% CBT-008 or 10% CBT-006 three times daily over 3 months [189, 190]. Reported outcomes focus primarily on intraocular pressure as a safety measure.

HY-02 (Hovione Scientia Limited)

As discussed previously, oral minocycline has demonstrated efficacy in managing MGD through its anti-inflammatory properties [45]. HY-02, a proprietary topical minocycline ointment, was evaluated in a phase II clinical trial (NCT03888378) at concentrations of 0.3% and 1%, applied twice daily to the lid margins over a 12-week period. Minocycline’s lipophilicity is said to improve its affinity to the meibomian glands, supporting a targeted anti-inflammatory action. Additionally, it may inhibit lipase, reducing lipid degradation [191].

IL-1Ra (Anakinra)

Anakinra, an interleukin-1 receptor antagonist (IL-1Ra), was assessed for MGD management in a phase I/II trial (NCT00681109) at 2.5% and 5% concentrations [192]. After 3 months, corneal staining improved significantly in participants treated with 2.5% IL-1Ra only, whilst symptoms improved in both treatment groups. Meibum quality and gland expressibility did not change significantly in either group [192]. Exploratory biomarker analyses in evaporative DED revealed an inverse relationship between IL-1Ra levels naturally present in the tears, as well as tear film stability and tear lysozyme levels [193–195]. This suggests that the body naturally upregulates IL-1Ra in more severe DED, where it plays an important role in regulating inflammation secondary to MGD.

Nutraceuticals/Natural Treatments

Topical Vitamin D

Primarily, Vitamin D is synthesised in the skin with sunlight exposure, however, it may alternatively be obtained from foods such as fish and animal products. Topical vitamin D (1000 IU, or 25 µg/cc) is currently under investigation in a phase II/III randomised clinical trial (NCT05495958) for MGD, where it was administered every 6 h for 3 months with concurrent conventional therapy. Symptoms and tear film stability improved in those receiving vitamin D, but interestingly, meibomian gland expressibility declined significantly in both treatment and control groups [196]. The association between topical vitamin D use and gland expressibility was not discussed in the study. Two other studies have explored topical vitamin D as a drop or ointment, with both reporting significant improvements in clinical signs, including gland expressibility, meibum quality and lid margin telangiectasia over an 8-week treatment period [197, 198], with the drop form being more effective than the control [198]. Tolerability appears to be moderate, with some reports of visual blurring, minor conjunctival redness [198] and eyelid redness [197].

Lecithin

Sunflower lecithin supplementation is currently under investigation in a clinical trial (NCT06058559) evaluating the effect of 1200 mg lecithin softgel capsules daily for 90 days. The primary outcome measure is symptoms, however, the study will also assess meibomian gland function, tear film stability and inflammation [199]. Outcomes from this study may further inform the role of phospholipids in MGD management.

Whilst short-term findings have been reported for many of these emerging therapies, outcomes of long-term, robustly designed, clinical trials are needed to provide valuable insight into the role that these therapies will play in MGD management in future.

Conclusions

A wide range of pharmacological, nutraceutical and natural therapies are available for managing MGD, with several emerging options currently under investigation. Numerous high-level studies have evaluated the efficacy of these treatments. This review also makes mention of prospective studies with less rigorous design, recognising their value in contributing clinically relevant insights. Amongst pharmacological treatments, antibiotics, immunosuppressants and topical integrin antagonists (lifitegrast) largely target inflammation arising from a loss of tear film or ocular surface homeostasis, and variable efficacy is demonstrated across treatments. Topical and oral antibiotics, particularly tetracyclines and macrolides, as well as corticosteroids, appear to be effective, more consistently, in improving both signs and symptoms, whilst benefits of lifitegrast may be more limited. Other agents such as perfluorohexyloctane and topical aqueous secretagogues have been shown to improve lipid layer thickness, although both remain under investigation with regard to their full therapeutic potential and mechanisms of action in addressing MGD. Nutraceutical/natural therapies offer promising longer-term strategies for managing MGD with the potential to help mitigate the risk of disease development. PUFAs, obtained readily through common dietary sources, show benefits in improving MGD, although confounding factors limit the ability to draw definitive conclusions. A range of other oral and topical naturally-derived compounds show promise. However, their effect in MGD has generally been explored in only a handful of studies, making their clinical utility less well established. Several therapies are currently in development, with early findings of many suggesting beneficial effects on meibomian gland function, lid margin health and tear film/ocular surface parameters. These treatments predominantly address downstream inflammation or meibomian gland obstruction associated with poor meibum quality or hyperkeratinisation of the lid margin, with several providing more novel approaches to managing MGD. Given the wide array of available therapeutic options, the duration, dosage, safety profiles and mechanisms of action of each individual treatment should be considered prior to treatment initiation.
