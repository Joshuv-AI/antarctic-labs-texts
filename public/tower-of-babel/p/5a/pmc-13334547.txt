Eudragit S100-coated microbeads of thiolated chitosan/casein for pH-responsive colon-targeted delivery of 6-mercaptopurine
Authors: Huma Hameed, Syed Muhammad Ahmad, Shazia Akram Ghumman, Ali Irfan, Hafiz Shoaib Sarwar, Yousef A. Bin Jardan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
6-Mercaptopurine (6-MP) is a potent chemotherapeutic and immunosuppressive agent; however, its oral administration is limited by poor bioavailability and dose-dependent hepatotoxicity. This study aimed to develop and optimize a colon-targeted, pH-responsive delivery system for 6-MP using Eudragit S100-coated thiolated chitosan/casein microbeads (Eu-S100@TCh/CS) to enhance site-specific release and minimize systemic exposure. The microbeads were fabricated via emulsion cross-linking followed by solvent evaporation and optimized using a central composite design. Physicochemical characterization (FTIR spectroscopy, DSC/TGA, PXRD, and SEM) confirmed efficient drug incorporation in a molecularly dispersed state, thermal stability, and a uniform spherical morphology (541–672 µm). Swelling and dissolution studies demonstrated a clear pH-dependent behavior, with minimal swelling and negligible drug release at pH 1.2 and 6.8, and enhanced release (84.5% at 24 h) at colonic pH 7.4, corresponding to the dissolution of the Eudragit S100 coating and hydration of the polymeric core. Drug-release kinetics indicated Higuchi diffusion for uncoated beads (R2 = 0.9852; n = 0.624) and anomalous (non-Fickian) transport for the coated beads (R2 = 0.9994; n = 0.741), governed by combined diffusion and polymer relaxation mechanisms. Acute oral toxicity studies in rats revealed no significant alterations in the biochemical, hematological, or histopathological parameters, confirming systemic safety. In vitro cytotoxicity studies (MTT assay) showed enhanced antiproliferative activity of the 6-MP-loaded microbeads against HCT-116 cells compared to the free drug, attributed to improved solubility and sustained drug availability following release rather than whole-particle internalization. Blank microbeads exhibited high cell viability, confirming carrier biocompatibility. Overall, the Eu-S100@TCh/CS microbead system represents a rationally designed colon-targeted delivery platform with potential to improve local therapeutic efficacy and reduce systemic toxicity. Further studies are warranted to evaluate selectivity using normal colonic cell lines.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer represents one of the principal reasons for cancer-related morbidity and mortality and contributes significantly to the total number of cancers worldwide.1 Even after the progress made in the areas of chemotherapy and supportive therapy, survival rates continue to be unsatisfactory in the case of metastatic CRC, particularly due to the lack of effective targeting and dose-dependent systemic toxicity.2 Among various therapeutic modalities, 6-mercaptopurine has been one of the most promising antitumor compounds found to be effective in the case of CRC. However, the practical applicability of 6-MP turns out to be severely marred by several limitations associated with the drug, such as poor oral bioavailability, quick metabolism in the upper part of the gastrointestinal tract, a short half-life, as well as severe toxicity, particularly in the form of myelosuppression and hepatotoxicity, to name a few. The limitations mentioned above highlight the requirement for innovative approaches to drug delivery for protecting the drug 6-MP from degradation during transit through the gastrointestinal tract and releasing the drug exactly at the site of action in the colon.3

The selection of appropriate polymers is crucial for the development of a colon-targeted delivery system. The polymer can determine not only the structural stability and efficiency of drug encapsulation but also the site-specific release characteristics. TCh is a thiolated derivative of chitosan that exhibits strong mucoadhesive properties, permeation-enhancing ability, and local enzymatic degradation-inhibiting capacity. Thiol groups, via covalent bonds, enable TCh to form disulfide bonds with colonic mucins, thereby prolonging residence and consequently enhancing local drug concentration.4 Development of stable TCh microbeads using casein, a natural amphiphilic protein having good encapsulation and film-forming properties, resulted in better loading and protection of sensitive chemotherapy drugs such as 6-MP.5

In this formulation, TCh imparts strong mucoadhesive characteristics along with the property of permeation via covalent bonds with mucin, whereas casein (CS) is both a structural as well as a functional polymer. As a native, amphiphilic, biodegradable, protein-based compound, casein is a good carrier for drugs due to its efficient encapsulation ability, film-forming property, as well as inherent stability at gastrointestinal pH, which makes it especially well-suited for the purpose of protecting chemotherapeutic agents during their passage. Its susceptibility to enzymatic hydrolysis by colonic microflora is a characteristic feature that imparts controlled release to chemotherapeutic agents, which is a highly favorable property for colon-targeted drug-delivery systems. By emulsion cross-linking, TCh and CS form stable microbeads. To harness these advantages, we designed Eudragit S100-coated thiolated chitosan plus casein (Eu-S100@TCh/CS) mucoadhesive microbeads using an emulsion cross-linking method, where the cationic TCh interacted with the negatively charged casein to form stable microspheres. Subsequent coating with Eudragit S100 restricted drug release in gastric fluids and enabled selective colonic delivery.6Fig. 1 depicts the mechanism behind the formation of Eu-S100 coated 6MP-loaded TChCS beads.

Materials and methods

Materials

6-Mercaptopurine was received as a gift sample from Pharmedic Laboratories (Pvt) Ltd. Chitosan (Sigma Aldrich), casein (Sigma Aldrich), Eudragit S100 (Sigma Aldrich), sodium tripolyphosphate (Sigma Aldrich), and all other materials were of analytical grade.

Preparation of thiolated chitosan (TCh)

TCh was synthesized by following the reported methods.8 Briefly, chitosan (1% w/v in 1% v/v glacial acetic acid) was reacted with thioglycolic acid (1 g) using EDAC (50 mM) as a coupling agent. Hydroxylamine (50 mM) was added, and the pH was adjusted to 5.0 with 1 M HCl.7 The reaction proceeded for 3 h under stirring, after which the product was dialyzed (5 mM HCl, followed by 5 mM HCl with 1% NaCl, and again with 5 mM HCl), lyophilized, and stored at 4 °C.8

Preparation of thiolated-chitosan-casein beads loaded with 6-mercaptopurine (TCh/CS 6-MP beads)

The TCh/CS mucoadhesive beads were fabricated by emulsion cross-linking, as shown in Fig. 2. Initially, a 50 mg mL−1 stock solution of 6-MP was prepared and incorporated into a 1% (w/v) TCh/CS solution along with sodium tripolyphosphate (Na-TPP) as the ionic cross-linker. Different polymer-to-drug ratios were evaluated to optimize encapsulation and release performance. Magnetic stirring of the final mixture was performed at 500 rpm for 1 hour. There was spontaneous formation of TChCS mucoadhesive beads. Following that, the TChCS mucoadhesive beads were centrifuged and washed with petroleum ether followed by acetone. They were then dispersed in water and dried in a desiccator, which led to the formation of TCh/CS 6-MP beads, which were stored at 4 °C for later use.9,10

Schematic of the preparation of Eu-S100 coated TChCS beads loaded with 6-MP. [6-MP, 6-mercaptopurine; Na-TPP, sodium tripolyphosphate; Eu-S100, Eudragit S100; TCh, thiolated-chitosan; CS, casein].

Preparation of Eudragit S100 coated 6-MP loaded TChCS beads (Eu-S100@TCh/CS 6-MP beads)

Coating of TChCS mucoadhesive beads with Eu-S100 was done by the ionic crosslinking and solvent evaporation method shown in Fig. 2. The TChCS mucoadhesive beads and Eu-S100 solution were added to 400 mL of liquid paraffin containing 1% Tween 80. This solution was continuously magnetically stirred for 4 hours, centrifuged, and washed with petroleum ether. The Eu-S100@TCh/CS 6-MP beads were then stored in a tightly closed container at 4 °C for later use.11

Experimental design

To optimize the Eu-S100@TCh/CS 6-MP beads, we utilized a Central Composite Design (CCD) involving three factors at three levels. The three independent variables, TCh (%w/v), CS (%w/v), and Eu-S100 (%w/v), were selected based on preliminary one-factor-at-a-time (OFAT) experiments and a review of relevant literature, which demonstrated that these components exert the most significant influence on bead particle size and percentage yield. Specifically, TCh concentration governs cross-linking density and network integrity; CS concentration affects bead matrix viscosity and mucoadhesive properties; and Eu-S100 content modulates coating efficiency and enteric protection. The factor levels were established from preliminary trials: concentrations below the lower limit produced structurally unstable beads, while concentrations above the upper limit led to excessive aggregation or processing difficulties. Accordingly, TCh was studied at 1–2%w/v, CS at 0–0.5% w/v, and Eu-S100 at 0–2%w/v. Factors such as Na-TPP concentration, cross-linking time, and stirring speed were held constant at the optimized values identified in preliminary studies.

Design Expert Software (version 18, Minneapolis, MN, USA) was used to formulate a 17-run-based drug design. The concentrations of TCh (A), CS (B), and Eu-S100 (C) were chosen as the independent variables. The dependent parameters (responses) were the particle size (R1) and percentage yield (R2) (Table 1). Factors such as Na-TPP were kept constant. Response Surface Methodology (RSM) was applied to optimize the dependent variables. Each formulation was prepared and tested in triplicate. The resulting mean values were fitted into various mathematical models (linear, 2FI, quadratic, and cubic). The most significant model was then selected using the software-generated fit summary, which evaluated the adjusted and predicted R2, the p-value, and the residual sum of squares. The model whose predicted correlation coefficient was closest to the adjusted correlation coefficient with minimum PRESS value and p-value ≤0.2 was selected. Characterization

Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy analysis

The technique of FTIR analysis involves the preparation of samples and recording the spectra; they were performed by following a formerly defined method. The infrared spectra for 6-MP, TCh, CS, Eu-S100 and Eu-S100@TCh/CS 6-MP beads were recorded (4000–400 cm−1) with a Bruker Optics Alpha FTIR instrument and processed using OPUS spectroscopy software. The formulations were placed individually on the smart orbit sample plate, and the infrared spectra were recorded in the attenuated total reflectance mode.13

Differential scanning calorimetry and thermal gravimetric analysis (DSC/TGA)

The analyses via DSC and TGA of 6MP, TCh, CS, Eu-S100 and Eu-S100@TCh/CS 6MP beads were performed with the help of an auto Q20 V24.10 differential scanning calorimetry instrument (Universal V4.5A, TA instruments). The temperature was increased from 0 °C to 350 °C with a 10 °C min−1 heating rate. A nitrogen gas (purity 99.99%) atmosphere was used for all measurements, and the instrument was standardized against an indium reference material.14,15

X-ray diffraction

A diffractometer (XRD-6000, Shimadzu, Tokyo, Japan) was used, and patterns of powder X-ray diffraction were noted so that the assembly of the crystal of the sample could be determined. CuKα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) was used at an accelerating voltage of 40 kV and 30 mA over a temperature range from 4 °C to 70 °C.16

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

The surface morphology and structural characteristics of the optimized Eu-S100@TCh/CS 6-MP microbeads were examined using scanning electron microscopy (SEM; EVO LS10, Zeiss, Jena, Germany). A small amount of dried microbead sample was mounted on an aluminum stub using double-sided adhesive carbon tape and sputter-coated with a thin layer of gold to enhance surface conductivity. The samples were then analyzed under vacuum at various accelerating voltages, and micrographs were captured at different magnifications to assess the morphology, sphericity, and surface texture of the microbeads.17

Swelling behavior studies

To mimic the gastrointestinal environment, the swelling behavior of the formulated microbeads was investigated in phosphate buffer solutions at pH 1.2, 6.8, and 7.4. Uniform microbeads from each formulation were weighed precisely to obtain their initial dry weights (W0) and then soaked in their respective buffer media. In vitro drug release

The release of 6MP from the Ch/CS beads was assessed with the help of a bath shaker. A weighed amount of Eu-S100@TCh/CS 6MP beads was added to a glass beaker, with dissolution media (900 mL) of 0.1N HCl solution (pH 1.2) and phosphate buffer solution (pH 6.8 and pH 7.4). Following that, the beakers with the medium and the dissolution medium were placed in a water bath maintained at 37 ± 0.5 °C. A sample (5 mL) was taken from the beaker at 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, and 24 hours, and the volume was replenished with a similar volume of the respective buffers. In vivo toxicity studies

An acute single-dose oral toxicity study was performed to evaluate the safety of the optimized Eu-S100@TCh/CS 6MP microbeads before therapeutic assessment. Healthy Swiss albino rats of both sexes (male and female, n = 6 per group, body weight 180–200 g) were obtained from the animal facility of the University of Veterinary and Animal Sciences (UVAS), Lahore, Pakistan. All study procedures were reviewed and approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the University of Central Punjab (Ref. No. UCP/ORIC/TDF/App#09/2025) and were conducted in accordance with OECD acute toxicity guidelines and ARRIVE reporting standards. Animals were randomly divided into two groups (n = 6 each; equal distribution of males and females): a treatment group and a control group. All the rats were housed in controlled laboratory conditions (12 h light/dark cycle, temperature 22 ± 2 °C, relative humidity 55% ± 10%) with ad libitum access to standard pellet diet and water. The treatment group received a single oral dose of the optimized bead formulation, equivalent to 20 mg kg−1 body weight of 6-MP. This dose was selected from reported preclinical colorectal cancer studies to ensure sustained local drug exposure in the colon while minimizing systemic toxicity.23 The group that was not treated as part of the experiment was termed the control group. The animals were under daily surveillance for 14 days in order to check their overall condition, any possible toxicity or fatalities, changes in behavior, and their weights. After the experiment, blood (RBC, WBC, hemoglobin, platelet count), liver (AST, ALT) and kidney (urea, creatinine) biochemical markers were analyzed with an Olympus AU2700 Chemistry Analyzer (Olympus Optical, Tokyo, Japan) following IFCC guidelines. Major organs (heart, liver, kidney, spleen, and colon) underwent histopathological examination in order to identify any microscopic abnormalities. Tissues were fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin, paraffin-embedded, sectioned, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for microscopic evaluation.

Cytotoxicity analysis

The MTT cytotoxicity of the designed Eu-S100@TCh/CS 6-MP microbeads was tested using the HCT-116 human colorectal carcinoma cell line as the target. HCT-116 cells were isolated from the PCMD biobank, located at the International Center for Chemical and Biological Sciences (ICCBS), which is a part of the University of Karachi, Pakistan. The cells were kept in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) containing 10% calf serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and grown under standard conditions (37 °C, 5% CO2, humidified atmosphere). The cells were plated into 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well and incubated for 24 hours to make sure that they had adhered. The cells were then exposed to serial concentrations (50–400 µg mL−1) of the optimized Eu-S100@TCh/CS 6-MP microbeads. Blank Eu-S100@TCh/CS beads without the drug served as the negative control, while the free 6-MP was used as the positive control. After 48 hours of treatment, 20 µL of the MTT solution (5 mg mL−1) was added to each well and incubated for 4 hours. The formazan crystals were then dissolved in 150 µL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), and the absorbance was determined at 570 nm using a microplate reader. The percentage of cell viability was calculated in relation to the untreated control cells. Dose–response curves were constructed from the data, and the IC50 values were calculated by applying nonlinear regression analysis. It should be noted that the blank Eu-S100@TCh/CS beads demonstrated very low cytotoxicity, which, in turn, validated the safety of the polymeric carrier system. The reproducibility of the results was ensured by performing all the experiments in triplicate.24

Statistical analysis

All experimental data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical comparisons between groups were performed using a two-tailed Student's t-test for pairwise analysis and two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's post-hoc test for multiple comparisons. A probability value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.25

Results

Formulation optimization

The use of CCD (Continuous Combinatorial Drug Design) has become an integral part of the drug discovery process, better aligning the practical capability of the experiments to their design by optimizing and enhancing them. It overcomes the limitations found in classical optimization pathways. A 3-factor, 3-level CCD was employed in the present study to fabricate and optimize the microspheres. The factors were Ch (A), CS (B) and Eu-S100 (C), which had quantitative assessments related to their effects on the dependent responses: particle size (R1) and percentage yield (R2). Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC)

Differential scanning calorimetry was used to evaluate the thermal behaviour of the TCh/CS mucoadhesive beads, pure 6MP, and the Eu-S100@TCh/CS 6MP beads, and to probe possible drug–polymer interactions. The TCh/CS beads exhibited a broad endothermic event centered at 80.95 °C, which is attributed to loss of adsorbed/bound water, consistent with the hydrophilic nature of the biopolymeric matrix. A pronounced exothermic process was observed at 270 °C, consistent with thermal degradation of polymeric/amine-containing moieties in the TCh/CS system. The thermogram of pure 6-mercaptopurine showed a broad endothermic event centered at 183.3 °C and a sharper exothermic event near 211.2 °C. The endothermic transition is consistent with a melting-like phase transition for the crystalline drug, whereas the higher-temperature exotherm is assigned to subsequent thermal decomposition.

In the Eu-S100@TCh/CS 6MP beads, the drug-related thermal events were altered; the 6-MP associated endotherm was markedly reduced in intensity and shifted to a lower temperature (143.2 °C) relative to the pure drug, while the polymer-related exothermic degradation of the thiolated-chitosan component remained observable at 302 °C. The attenuation and shift of the 6-MP endotherm in the coated formulation indicate reduced drug crystallinity and/or physical dispersion of 6-MP within the polymeric matrix (and/or the Eudragit coating), rather than complete chemical degradation. The persistence of residual drug thermal features, together with polymer degradation signals, supports a picture in which 6-MP is primarily physically entrapped and engaged in secondary interactions (e.g., hydrogen bonding) with the TCh/CS matrix and the coating, rather than undergoing covalent modification. Overall, the DSC data indicate moisture loss and expected thermal degradation behavior for the TCh/CS beads, distinct melting and decomposition events for crystalline 6-MP, and a decrease in the intensity/shift of the drug's thermal transitions after formulation and coating, consistent with reduced crystallinity/amorphization and successful incorporation of 6-MP into the mucoadhesive beads with an outer Eudragit S100 layer, which can contribute to modified dissolution behavior at temperatures below the intact drug melting point, as shown in Fig. 5.

X-ray diffraction analysis

The XRD diffractograms comparing the crystalline characteristics of pure 6-mercaptopurine (6-MP), thiolated chitosan, casein, Eudragit S100, and the final drug-loaded microbead formulation are shown in Fig. 6. Pure 6-MP displayed a set of very sharp, strong, and easily distinguishable diffraction peaks, which means that the substance was highly crystalline. On the other hand, thiolated chitosan and casein essentially showed only one broad halo without any sharp reflections, confirming their amorphous structure. Eudragit S100 also presented a similar diffuse profile that was consistent with its polymeric nature. In the XRD pattern of the final microbeads containing 6-MP, the distinct crystalline peaks of 6-MP were lost and, in their place, a broad amorphous hump appeared that was similar to those of the polymeric components. The absence of distinct 6-MP peaks indicates that the drug completely left its crystalline form and was successfully converted into an amorphous or molecularly dispersed state within the TCh/CS blend coated with Eudragit S100. This change to the amorphous state is advantageous, as it generally leads to improved solubility and dissolution rates of poorly water-soluble drugs such as 6-MP.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

Fig. 7 shows the SEM images, illustrating the morphological characteristics of the optimized TCh/CS microbeads coated with Eudragit S100. From the low-magnification SEM image (left), it can be observed that the microbeads are spherical and discrete, thus confirming the efficiency of ionic gelation and successful stabilization during the formation of the particles. The bead surfaces appear slightly wrinkled and textured rather than smooth, which is expected from the interplay between the thiolated chitosan and casein during crosslinking, followed by surface coating with Eudragit S100. There is no indication of deformation, flattening or structural failure, which shows that the beads underwent the drying process without loss of structural integrity. From the higher magnification micrograph (on the right side), there is a tightly connected compact surface with linked polymeric regions. This confirms the existence of an even layer of Eudragit S100. There are no cracks, pores, or drug crystal surfaces, which shows the good encapsulation of 6-mercaptopurine without deposition on the surface. This agrees with XRD data, which indicates amorphization/molecular dispersion of the drug in the matrix. Such a compact surface morphology may contribute to reduced initial burst release and controlled, pH-dependent release behavior in the colonic environment.

Swelling behavior study

Uncoated TCh/CS microbeads showed immediate hydration and swelling when exposed to aqueous media, especially at mildly acidic and neutral intestinal pH. Because the matrix is based on hydrophilic thiolated chitosan and casein, water penetration begins quickly, leading to pronounced swelling and premature relaxation of the polymer network. As a result, uncoated microbeads readily absorbed fluid and began releasing the drug even in acidic medium (pH 2.0) or at intestinal pH 6.8, demonstrating no site-specific protection. In contrast, Eudragit S100-coated TCh/CS microbeads (Eu-S100@TCh/CS) exhibited negligible swelling at pH 2.0 and pH 6.8. Eudragit S100 is an anionic methacrylate polymer that is insoluble below pH 7.0. The coating acts as an impermeable barrier, preventing water penetration into the bead core. As a result, no matrix expansion or drug release occurs under gastric or upper intestinal conditions. Swelling and hydration of the TCh/CS core appear only when the pH exceeds 7.0 (colon-like conditions), where Eudragit ionizes (–COOH → –COO−), dissolves, and exposes the hydrophilic bead interior. Consequently, at pH 7.4, the beads show maximum swelling, accompanied by gradual drug release driven by polymer relaxation and diffusion. Two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post-hoc test showed significant effects of both pH and coating on swelling behavior (Fig. 8). Uncoated TCh/CS beads swelled progressively with pH, whereas Eu-S100-coated beads showed negligible swelling at pH 2.0 and 6.8 and maximum swelling at pH 7.4. The results confirm a strong pH-dependent swelling profile, consistent with colon-targeted performance of the coated formulation.

Effect of pH and coating on swelling (%) of TCh/CS microbeads at 24 h (mean ± SD, n = 3). Two-way ANOVA with Tukey's post-hoc test indicated significant effects of both pH and coating (***p < 0.0001 at pH 2.0 and 6.8; *p < 0.01 at pH 7.4). The Eu-S100 coating prevented swelling under acidic and intestinal conditions and enabled maximal hydration at colonic pH 7.4.

6-MP loading efficiency in Eu-S100@TCh/CS

The loading efficiency of 6-MP in the Eu-S100@TCh/CS beads was determined spectrophotometrically (absorbance measured at 316 nm). The different formulations showed a huge variation in drug incorporation, as indicated by the % E.E. values of 57.4% to 83.0%. The highest drug loading (83.0%) was found in Eu-S100@TCh/CS 6MP-5, while the lowest drug entrapment (57.4%) was recorded for Eu-S100@TCh/CS 6MP-4. It can be concluded that, in general, a higher concentration of the polymer matrix contributed to better incorporation of 6MP, but this improvement was not very consistent (notably in the case of Eu-S100@TCh/CS 6MP-4). The particles obtained with the emulsification method had lower drug incorporation compared to the other methods, which suggests that this technique is not optimal for the current system regarding drug loading. Casein-free formulations (or formulations with reduced amounts of casein), however, were found to enable better entrapment of the drug, indicating that the amount of casein present might influence drug encapsulation efficiency.

In vitro drug release

In vitro release of 6-MP from the uncoated (TCh/CS) and Eudragit S100-coated (Eu-S100@TCh/CS) microbeads was performed in sequential dissolution media mimicking gastrointestinal transit: pH 1.2, 6.8, and then pH 7.4. These release profiles are in strong agreement with the swelling behavior reported in Section 3.6 (Fig. 8), where the Eudragit S100 coating was shown to prevent bead hydration and matrix expansion at pH 1.2 and 6.8, thereby directly suppressing water penetration and drug diffusion under gastric and upper intestinal conditions.

During the initial period in acid medium at pH 1.2 (0–2 h), approximately 16% of the drug was released from the uncoated beads, whereas no detectable amount was released from the coated system, consistent with the negligible swelling of Eu-S100@TCh/CS beads at this pH. Upon changing the dissolution medium to pH 6.8 (2–6 h), a gradual increase up to 42% was recorded for the uncoated beads due to hydration and erosion of the hydrophilic matrix. The coated beads released only approximately 22% at 6 h, again reflecting their negligible swelling at this pH, as the Eudragit S100 layer remained largely intact and insoluble.

Upon transfer to pH 7.4 (6–24 h), dissolution of the Eudragit S100 coating triggered maximum swelling of the TCh/CS core, as demonstrated in Fig. 9. The increase in polymer mesh size upon swelling facilitated rapid water ingress and drug diffusion, resulting in a sharp increase in cumulative release. The coated beads reached 84.5% cumulative release at 24 h, while the uncoated system attained near-complete release (98.5%). This biphasic pattern confirms that the Eudragit S100 coating successfully suppressed premature drug loss in gastric and intestinal media and enabled pH-triggered colon-specific release.

In vitro cumulative drug release profiles of uncoated (TCh/CS) and Eudragit S100-coated (Eu-S100@TCh/CS) microbeads containing 6-mercaptopurine under simulated gastrointestinal pH conditions (mean ± SD, n = 3). The coated beads showed minimal release in acidic and intestinal pH (1.2 and 6.8), followed by rapid release at colonic pH 7.4, confirming pH-triggered dissolution of Eudragit S100 and colon-targeted drug delivery.

The drug release kinetics were fitted to mathematical models (Table 5). The Korsmeyer–Peppas model provided the best fit for the coated formulation (R2 = 0.9994, n = 0.741), where the release exponent n > 0.5 confirms anomalous (non-Fickian) transport, a release mechanism driven by the simultaneous contribution of drug diffusion and polymer matrix relaxation consequent to swelling. This mechanistically corroborates the observed swelling behavior at pH 7.4, where Eudragit S100 dissolution triggers TCh/CS core hydration, matrix expansion, and polymer chain relaxation, collectively governing the controlled and sustained colonic release of 6-MP. The uncoated system best fitted the Higuchi model (R2 = 0.9852) and Korsmeyer–Peppas model (R2 = 0.9891, n = 0.624), confirming Fickian diffusion as the primary mechanism from the freely hydrated polymer matrix.

Cytotoxicity assay

The in vitro cytotoxic activity of free 6-MP, optimized Eu-S100@TCh/CS 6-MP microbeads, and blank microbeads was assessed in HCT-116 colorectal carcinoma cells using the MTT assay. As shown in Fig. 11, both free 6-MP and Eu-S100@TCh/CS 6-MP microbeads exhibited a dose-dependent reduction in cell viability across concentrations ranging from 50 to 400 µg mL−1. At lower concentrations (50–150 µg mL−1), free 6-MP and Eu-S100@TCh/CS 6-MP microbeads showed moderate cytotoxic effects, with cell viability ranging between 75 and 90%. A more pronounced cytotoxic response was observed at higher concentrations. At 200 µg mL−1, viability decreased to approximately 65% for free 6-MP and 58% for Eu-S100@TCh/CS 6-MP microbeads. At the highest tested concentration of 400 µg mL−1, viability was reduced to 42% by free 6-MP and to 25% by Eu-S100@TCh/CS 6-MP microbeads, showing significantly enhanced cytotoxic potency compared with the free drug (p < 0.01). Conversely, blank microbeads exhibited virtually no cytotoxicity, as cell viability remained above 90% at all of the concentrations tested, confirming the safety and biocompatibility of the polymeric carrier system. IC50 values, determined from nonlinear regression of dose response curves, were: Free 6-MP: 365 µg mL−1 (R2 = 0.972); Eu-S100@TCh/CS 6-MP microbeads: 182 µg mL−1 (R2 = 0.985); Blank microbeads: >500 µg mL−1 (no significant cytotoxic effect). These findings demonstrate that Eu-S100@TCh/CS 6-MP microbeads significantly enhance the anticancer efficacy of 6-MP, while maintaining carrier safety.

MTT cytotoxicity assay of HCT-116 colorectal carcinoma cells treated with free 6-MP, optimized Eu-S100@TCh/CS 6-MP microbeads, and blank microbeads within the concentration range of 50–400 µg mL−1. Both free 6-MP and Eu-S100@TCh/CS 6-MP microbeads exerted a dose-dependent decrease in cell viability, where microbeads showed much higher cytotoxicity compared to the free drug at higher concentrations (p < 0.01). Negligible cytotoxicity (>90% viability at all doses) was observed for the blank microbead treatment, which confirmed the carrier safety. IC50 values: free 6-MP = 365 µg mL−1, Eu-S100@TCh/CS 6-MP microbeads = 182 µg mL−1, and blank microbeads >500 µg mL−1.

It should be noted that, given the microbead size range of 541–672 µm, whole-particle internalization by HCT-116 cells is not mechanistically feasible, as endocytic uptake pathways are operative only for sub-micron to low-micron range particles. The cytotoxic effect of the Eu-S100@TCh/CS 6-MP microbeads, therefore, arises from drug release into the culture medium, followed by cellular uptake of the liberated 6-MP via passive diffusion and transporter-mediated mechanisms. The superior cytotoxicity of the bead formulation over free 6-MP is attributable to the sustained local drug release, maintaining a higher drug concentration gradient at the cell surface, combined with the enhanced aqueous solubility of amorphous 6-MP, facilitating more efficient membrane permeation compared to crystalline free drug.

Discussion

The formulated Eu-S100@TCh/CS microbeads present an advanced delivery device with a view toward colon-specific delivery of 6-MP, where there is a need to overcome low oral bioavailability and systemic toxicity related to this medicinal agent. The physicochemical characterization confirms the successful formulation and amorphization of the poorly water-soluble 6-MP within the TCh/CS polymeric core, which is also reflected in its XRD pattern, through the disappearance of crystalline peaks. Such amorphization is known to be an important factor, enhancing the dissolution rate and potentially improving the therapeutic outcome of poorly water-soluble drugs that may come under the category of BCS Class IV medications, such as 6-MP.26 The SEM studies revealed a smooth, dense surface that strongly supports the presence of a complete and uniform layer of Eudragit S100, which is essential for protecting the core matrix in the harsh environment of the upper gastrointestinal tract. The stability of the microbeads was assessed through particle size consistency, percentage yield, and drug-release behavior across physiological pH conditions. While zeta potential is a well-established stability indicator for colloidal nanoparticulate systems, its relevance is limited for hydrogel-based microbeads of the size range reported here (541–672 µm); nonetheless, future studies may consider zeta potential measurements on intermediate nanoscale components, such as the Eu-S100 nanosuspension, to provide additional physicochemical insights.

The key success factor for this formulation is its pH-sensitive release profile, which is regarded as a characteristic feature for colon targeting. In the in vitro drug release profile, there is insignificant drug release in the initial 6 hours at both pH 1.2 and pH 6.8, imitating stomach and smaller intestine passage, respectively. This is a direct consequence of Eudragit S100, which is insoluble at pH values below 7.0, thereby creating a barrier for penetration.27 On shifting the samples to simulate the colon environment (pH 7.4), there is a rapid increase in dissolution of the coats. Burst release followed by sustained release is observed for 6-MP, reaching a cumulative release of 84.5% in 24 hours, showing a prominent triggering release profile. It is essential to ensure a concentrated drug level at a local site for colorectal cancer with minimum systemic drug intake and hence minimum toxicity, a strategy commonly followed in targeted cancer therapy.

The kinetic study of drug release has also helped to elucidate the release mechanism. The drug release profile was found to best fit the Korsmeyer–Peppas model, with a value of the release exponent ‘n’ of 0.741, indicating a non-Fickian or an anionic diffusion mechanism. This shows that the diffusion of the drug from a matrix is not a simple function of Fickian diffusion alone but also depends on the relaxation and erosion of the matrix due to the dissolution of the Eudragit-S100 coating.28 The controlled and sustained drug delivery profile, realized due to the combination of diffusion and matrix relaxation, is very effective and helpful because it provides therapeutic drug concentrations in the colon for a long period of time. The addition of TCh to the core not only helped to stabilize the core matrix but is also expected to provide mucoadhesive properties to the drug-delivery system, potentially prolonging its retention time within the colon, a property of TCh that has already been exploited in drug-delivery systems.29

Finally, the toxicity and increased therapeutic efficacy were demonstrated by the in vivo toxicity study and the in vitro cytotoxicity tests. In the acute oral toxicity study, the safety of the polymeric carrier system was well established, as it showed neither signs nor effects on the markers of essential organs of the animals tested.30 However, more importantly, the results of the MTT assays performed against the HCT-116 colon cancer cell lines showed a significantly increased anticancer efficacy, as the IC50 value of the coated microbeads was nearly twofold lower than the IC50 value of the free drug. The cytotoxicity study was conducted exclusively on HCT-116 colorectal carcinoma cells. Due to the non-availability of normal colonic cell lines locally and current logistical constraints, parallel evaluation on normal epithelial cells could not be performed. Future studies will include normal colonic cell lines (e.g., CCD-18Co or FHC) to establish a selectivity index and confirm the preferential cytotoxic activity of the formulation toward cancer cells over normal epithelium. The enhanced anticancer efficacy can be attributed to three synergistic mechanisms: (i) the amorphous/molecularly dispersed state of 6-MP within the polymer matrix improving post-release aqueous solubility and membrane permeability; (ii) the mucoadhesive thiolated chitosan component prolonging mucosal contact time at the colonic site, extending the absorption window; and (iii) polymer particle-mediated cellular internalization via caveolae/lipid raft-dependent endocytosis and micropinocytosis pathways, providing enhanced intracellular drug accumulation beyond what passive diffusion of free drug can achieve.31

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the development of a pH-responsive Eu-S100@TCh/CS microbead system for colon-targeted delivery of 6-mercaptopurine. The optimized formulation exhibited high entrapment efficiency, appropriate particle size, and robust structural integrity. A distinct pH-dependent biphasic release profile was achieved, with minimal release under gastric and intestinal conditions and sustained release at colonic pH, governed by swelling and anomalous (non-Fickian) transport. The system showed favorable mucoadhesion and cytocompatibility, while enhanced cytotoxicity against HCT-116 cells was attributed to improved solubility and sustained drug availability rather than particle internalization. Collectively, this platform represents a rationally designed colon-targeted delivery system with potential to enhance therapeutic efficacy and reduce systemic toxicity, warranting further investigation, including selectivity studies on normal cell lines.
