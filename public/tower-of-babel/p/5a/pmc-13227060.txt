Introduction

Fibromyalgia is a condition with multiple symptoms that extend beyond the musculoskeletal system.1 Individuals with this condition often experience widespread nociceptive pain, fatigue, non-restorative sleep, as well as somatic, cognitive, and affective symptoms, and a reduced quality of life.2–7 Fibromyalgia is considered to be among the top 3 most prevalent musculoskeletal disorders, which poses a substantial public health challenge.8 Patients often exhibit reduced work capacity, early retirement, and reliance on healthcare resources.9–12 While the exact pathophysiology of fibromyalgia remains unclear, converging evidence supports central and peripheral mechanisms.13–18

Pharmacological interventions demonstrate modest benefits. In randomized trials, only 10%-25% of patients achieve meaningful pain relief.19–21 Consequently, non-pharmacological strategies have gained prominence.22 Among these, transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS) has demonstrated encouraging outcomes.23–26 However, conventional TENS devices typically target 2-4 body regions, which may be inadequate given the widespread pain distribution characteristic of fibromyalgia.

Hence, the EXOPULSE Mollii Suit, a full-body garment, has been developed to address this limitation by concurrently stimulating up to 40 muscle groups through the utilization of low-frequency transcutaneous electrical currents. Preliminary findings suggest that a single 1-h session can provide immediate pain relief.27–29 However, Riachi et al.27 suggested an increase in pain score 24 h following a single session in the patients. Hence, the execution of randomized controlled trials comprising repeated sessions is imperative to derive reliable conclusions regarding the long-term effects of this intervention. A recent randomized controlled trial addressed this gap, demonstrating that 2 weeks of daily stimulation with the EXOPULSE Mollii Suit significantly reduced pain compared to sham.30 The study revealed that the intervention yielded additional benefits, particularly in the domains of fatigue, anxiety, and disease impact. In the subsequent open-label phase of the same work, sustained improvements were observed following 4 weeks of continuous stimulation, extending to depression ratings and quality of life measures. While these results are encouraging, further replication in independent cohorts is needed to substantiate the findings.

The primary objective of this study is to assess the effects of EXOPULSE Mollii suit on the fibromyalgia syndrome in terms of health status and functional disability by means of the Fibromyalgia Impact Questionnaire (FIQ). The secondary objective of this work is to assess the effects of this intervention on pain, fatigue, anxiety, depression, and quality of life.

Methods

Study population

This is a monocentric study conducted at Sheikh Shakhbout Medical City (SSMC), Abu Dhabi, United Arab Emirates, between January 2024 and Mai 2025. Patients were recruited among outpatients presenting to the rheumatology, pain management, and neurology clinics at SSMC. The stimulation sessions and assessments were carried out in the Neurology Division of SSMC.

Patients were recruited according to the following inclusion criteria: Adult patients aged between 18 and 75 years, a definite diagnosis of fibromyalgia according to the American College of Rheumatology (ACR) 2010 criteria set for at least 1 month,31 and the ability to understand verbal instructions. All patients met the ACR criteria requiring symptoms to persist for at least 3 months to receive the diagnosis, but the date of diagnosis was at least 1 month prior to enrollment. Patients were not included if they (1) had a pain level of <4 on the visual analog scale (VAS) over 1 week before inclusion, (2) were included in another research protocol during the study period, (3) were unable to undergo medical monitoring for the study purposes due to geographical or social reasons, (4) had a cardiac stimulator, a ventriculoperitoneal shunt, and/or intrathecal baclofen pump or other contraindications to using EXOPULSE Mollii suit, (5) were pregnant, (6) had change in their fibromyalgia pharmacological therapy in the last 3 months, (7) suffered from other somatic or psychiatric diagnoses other than anxiety and depression (eg, arrhythmias, uncontrolled epilepsy, other diseases causing osteoarticular and muscular pain), (8) had a body mass index above 35 kg/m2, (9) Prisoners (10) and if they were under juridical protection.

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the local ethics committee and performed in conformity with the Declaration of Helsinki. Patients signed the informed consent before participation. Subjects received no compensation for their participation. They were reimbursed for their transport costs.

Study design and interventions

The study was conducted in 2 phases. Phase 1 followed a randomized, controlled, double-blind, crossover design in which each participant was randomly allocated to receive both active and sham stimulation with the EXOPULSE Mollii suit (Figure 1). The duration of this phase was 12 weeks, encompassing 2 treatment blocks of 14 sessions each (7 sessions per week over 2 weeks), with each block separated by a washout period of at least 2 weeks. Two weeks after completing the second block, an open-label extension (phase 2) was proposed, consisting of 4 consecutive weeks of active treatment with daily sessions (7 per week), totaling 28 sessions. An illustration of the study design with the randomized controlled trial (phase 1) and the open-label trial (phase 2).

The EXOPULSE Mollii suit constitutes an assistive device that applies non-invasive electro-stimulation. It is a CE-labelled device that complies with the European regulation (EU) 2017/745. The system is composed of 3 elements: A jacket, pants, and a control unit. The garments are equipped with 58 electrodes, along with conductive wiring and connectors that facilitate communication with the control unit. The primary function of these devices is to deliver electrical pulses from the control unit to specific nerves and muscle groups throughout the body. The garments themselves are non-invasive, and they are classified as Class I according to Rule 1 based on Annex VIII of Regulation (EU) 2017/745. The control unit is a battery-powered electrical device that sends low-energy electric pulses (ie, Current 20 V, low frequency ∼20 Hz, pulse width of 25-175 µs) through connectors to the garments, which in turn transmit the pulses from the connectors to key nerves and corresponding muscle groups throughout the body. It is an active device and is classified as Class IIa according to Rule 9 based on Annex VIII of Regulation (EU) 2017/745.

Hence, the EXOPULSE Mollii suit consists of transcutaneously stimulating several body areas with a low-intensity electric current, aiming to reduce pain. This treatment method’s theoretical background primarily refers to the gate control theory, ie, stimulation of large proprioceptive fibers would inhibit the nociceptive information transmitted by small fibers.

A session of active intervention lasted 1 h using the abovementioned parameters. The control arm was a sham intervention through which the control unit was programmed to start stimulating for 1 min with the same parameters (as described above), after which it shut off automatically. The brief stimulation produces cutaneous sensations that mimic the active condition, aiming to achieve an effective blinding integrity.

Participants’ identification, randomization, and blinding

Each consented participant was given a unique 10-digit study number that was valid for the whole study duration. The software randomizer.org was used to randomize patients to receive active or sham stimulation during the first stimulation block phase 1. The patients were then crossed over to the opposite type of stimulation in the second block. Half of the patients received active followed by sham intervention after at least a 2-week washout period, and half of them received sham followed by active intervention. A research technician who is trained to use the EXOPULSE Mollii suit and not involved in any step of the study protocol (patients’ evaluation, performance of stimulation session, data collection, or analysis) programmed the control units for “active” and “sham” stimulations. All stimulation settings could not be modified by participants during home sessions, ensuring that parameters remained standardized for all participants. The Clinical Research Coordinator, study investigator, and patients were blinded to the stimulation condition.

Efficacy assessment

The primary endpoint of this study was the improvement of the FIQtotal score at the end of phase 1.32,33 The FIQtotal score is the sum of 10 items, each with a maximum score of 10, yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating a greater impact of fibromyalgia. Specifically, item 1 comprises 11 questions related to physical functioning that are scored and summed to yield a single physical impairment score. Individual items 2 to 10 assess the number of days the patient felt good, the number of days they were unable to work due to fibromyalgia symptoms, work difficulty, pain, fatigue, morning tiredness, stiffness, anxiety, and depression.

Besides the primary endpoint, the percentage of improvement in FIQtotal was calculated, aiming to compare with the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) of 14% established by Bennett et al.34

Several other secondary endpoints were considered. Pain was also assessed using several scales aiming to provide a global and complementary evaluation of this symptom. VAS consists of rating the symptom on a 10 cm horizontal line going from “no pain” to “worst pain imaginable”.35–37 It has good psychometric properties for assessing pain35,36 and has a high sensibility and sensitivity and a good discriminative power in patients with fibromyalgia.37 The Brief Pain Inventory (BPI) was also used to assess pain severity (4 items) and pain interference (7 items). BPI items are rated on a 10-point scale (numeric rating scale) running from 0 (no pain/does not interfere) to 10 (pain as bad as you can/interferes completely).38,39 In addition, the Pain Catastrophizing Scale (PCS) was employed (13 items).40 PCS is a valid and reliable scale that assesses the presence and severity of feelings or thoughts that emerge when experiencing pain. Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (not at all) to 4 (all the time). It is possible to calculate a total and 3 subscale scores (ie, helplessness, magnification, and rumination).40

Similar to pain, fatigue was evaluated using a VAS.25 Anxiety and depression were further assessed using the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS), a 14-item generic scale.41 It consists of 7 items assessing anxiety and 7 others assessing depression. Scores can range from 0 to 21 on each subscale, with higher scores indicating worse symptomatology.

The quality of life was evaluated using the Short Form 36 Health Survey (SF-36).42,43 SF-36 contains 36 items which assess the quality of life and yield 8 dimensions: Physical functioning, role limitations due to physical problems, role limitations due to emotional problems, pain, general health, fatigue/energy, social functioning, mental health.5 The score of each dimension ranges from 0 to 100, with higher scores implying better health status.

In addition, the patients were asked to evaluate their Clinical Global Impression (CGI) after each intervention.44 CGI consists of a 7-point scale ranging from “very much improved since the initiation of treatment” to “very much worse since the initiation of treatment” (from 1 to 7). After each stimulation block of phase 1, patients were asked to guess the type of stimulation received to assess the blinding integrity.

Furthermore, the study incorporated a comprehensive assessment questionnaire and systematic reporting of adverse events.

Study schedule

In instances where patients met the eligibility criteria during a routine disease monitoring visit, the investigator provided them with study information. During the screening visit, in accordance with standard care, the patient’s weight and height were documented to ascertain the appropriate EXOPULSE Mollii suit size. Subsequent to a period of reflection following the visit, patients were contacted to ascertain their willingness to participate. Those who consented to participate were scheduled for a baseline visit at the Division of Neurology, SSMC. During this visit, the eligibility criteria were reassessed, the trial was explained in detail, and informed consent was formally obtained. Subsequently, the investigator conducted a comprehensive clinical evaluation and a review of ongoing treatments. Ultimately, the research technician reviewed the randomization schedule and apprized the investigator of the group allocation (1 or 2). This determined the sequence of “active” and “sham” stimulation periods, as well as the corresponding control unit configuration for the patient. Patients received a training session on EXOPULSE Mollii suit usage. During this session, the participants were engaged in a face-to-face teaching session of the EXOPULSE Mollii suit application (30-60 min). They should show that they know how to wear the suit, start and stop the stimulation, attach and detach the control unit’s arms. Written instructions with pictures in print form and an electronic version were provided as backup. After this training period, patients received the suit for 2 weeks to perform their own stimulations for 1 h daily at home (a total of 14 stimulation sessions from Monday to Sunday). At least a 2-week washing-out period was allowed, after which the participants returned for evaluation. They returned the first control unit and received the one to use for the second stimulation block. The evaluation using the FIQ, BPI, VAS, PCS, HADS, and SF-36 was conducted immediately before the first session and immediately after the last session of the first stimulation block (active or sham, phase 1), as well as immediately before the first session and immediately after the last session of the second stimulation block (active or sham, phase 1). Patients were asked to assess their CGI and guess the type of stimulation received after the last session of each stimulation block of phase 1. Two weeks after phase 1, phase 2 started. An evaluation took place prior to the first stimulation session and consisted of FIQ, BPI, VAS, PCS, HADS, and SF-36. Afterwards, patients received a control unit dedicated to phase 2. The same questionnaires were used after the last stimulation session of phase 2 (FIQ, BPI, VAS, PCS, HADS, and SF-36), and patients additionally completed the CGI.

Sample size calculation and statistical analysis

The sample size estimation was conducted using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.645). A 2 × 2 within-subjects repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) was planned, with time (pre- and post-stimulation block) and stimulation condition (active vs sham) as factors for Phase 1. The calculation was based on a medium effect size, a 2-tailed alpha of 0.05, a statistical power of 80%, and the allowance for potential dropouts. This resulted in a required sample of 34 participants. No additional inclusions were planned to replace dropouts. For Phase 2, paired comparisons were performed using a within-subjects design, with time (pre- vs post-stimulation) as the repeated factor. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS (version 30.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). The normality of the data was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Given that not all variables satisfied the normality assumptions, non-parametric methods were employed.

For phase 1, Friedman’s test was implemented with Bonferroni-adjusted Dunn’s post hoc analysis taking into consideration 4 timepoints: (1) right before the first session of the first stimulation block, (2) right after the last session of the first stimulation block, (3) right before the first session of the second stimulation block, (4) right after the last session of the second stimulation block. The compared outcomes were as follows: FIQtotal and its subscales, BPItotal and its 2 subscales, VASpain, VASfatigue, PCStotal and its 3 subscales, SF-36 8 dimensions. Categorial data were analyzed using the Chi2 test (CGI and blinding questionnaire after the last session of the first stimulation block vs after the last session of the second stimulation block).

For phase 2, Wilcoxon’s signed-rank test was used to compare the outcomes that were assessed right before the first session and right after the last session of this open-label phase (FIQtotal and its subscales, BPItotal and its 2 subscales, VASpain, VASfatigue, PCStotal and its 3 subscales, SF-36 8 dimensions).

The analysis of FIQ individual items was provided as supplementary materials (Tables S1 and S2).

The magnitude of the effects was reported using Kendall’s W for Friedman’s test and r = Z/√N for Wilcoxon’s test, and was interpreted as small (< 0.3), medium (0.3-0.5), or large (≥ 0.5). The statistical significance was set at P < .05. The results are expressed as the mean±SD.

Results

Descriptive data

Thirty-three patients fulfilled the inclusion criteria. Twenty-two patients completed phase 1 (20 females, 2 males), and 20 completed phase 2. The mean age was 40.59 ± 10.59 years. Regarding relationship status, 6 were single, 15 were married, and 1 was divorced. The mean disease duration was 1.64 ± 1.29 years. The mean BMI was 27.34 ± 4.38 kg/m2. No serious adverse events were reported at any time. Table 1 summarizes the general characteristics of the study cohort. Figure 4 illustrates the participation flow chart.

Phase 1 outcomes

Regarding the primary outcome (FIQtotal), Friedman’s test for differences among repeated measures yielded a χ2 value of 11.62 (df = 3, P = .009). Post hoc analysis revealed a significant decrease in FIQtotal following the active condition (pre-active score: 66.06 ± 13.46 vs post-active score: 50.81 ± 23.22; Bonferroni-adjusted Dunn’s P = .010). No significant changes were observed after the sham condition (pre-sham score: 63.83 ± 17.62 vs post-sham score: 62.52 ± 14.38; Bonferroni-adjusted Dunn’s P = 1.000). These results are presented in Figure 5. The mean percentage of improvement in FIQtotal was 24.01%, which exceeds the previously established MCID of 14%.

Fibromyalgia Impact Questionnaire total score (FIQtotal) before and after each stimulation condition (active vs sham) in patients with fibromyalgia (phase 1). *P < .05.

The CGI did not significantly differ between both conditions (Chi2 P = .122): no change was reported by 54.55% in the active condition versus 81.82% in the sham condition, improvement was reported by 40.91% in the active condition versus 13.64% in the sham condition, and worsening was reported by 4.55% of patients in each condition. The number of patients who guessed or not the type of intervention did not significantly differ between active and sham conditions (correct guessing 68.18% in the active condition vs 90.91% in the sham condition; Chi2 P = .062).

Pain significantly decreased following the active intervention as per the PCS. Friedman’s test of differences among repeated measures rendered significant effects for the PCStotal (X2=19.32, df = 3, P < .001), PCShelplessness (X2=13.03, df = 3, P = .005), PCSmagnification (X2=13.36, df = 3, P = .004), and PCSrumination (X2=11.96, df = 3, P = .008). Post hoc analysis revealed significant decreases in each of PCStotal (pre-active score: 26.27 ± 10.96; post-active score: 17.64 ± 11.40, Bonferroni-adjusted Dunn’s P = .002), PCShelplessness (pre-active score: 11.82 ± 5.89; post-active score: 7.36 ± 5.64, Bonferroni-adjusted Dunn’s P = .014), PCSmagnification (pre-active score: 5.05 ± 2.89; post-active score: 3.09 ± 2.52, Bonferroni-adjusted Dunn’s P = .025), and PCSrumination (pre-active score: 9.41 ± 3.69; post-active score: 7.18 ± 4.29, Bonferroni-adjusted Dunn’s P = .017) following the active intervention. For these measures, post hoc analysis revealed no significant differences following the sham condition. Conversely, pain did not significantly change following any intervention according to BPI or VASpain.

With regards to SF-36, Friedman’s test of differences among repeated measures rendered X2 values of 17.03 (df = 3, P < .001) in the SF-36social functioning dimension. Post hoc analysis revealed a significant improvement in social functioning following the active condition (pre-active score: 50.00 ± 21.48; post-active score: 67.05 ± 26.03, Bonferroni-adjusted Dunn’s P = .003). No significant differences were observed following the sham condition.

Finally, regarding VASfatigue, HADSanxiety, and HADSdepression, the differences were either statistically non-significant or lost significance when running post hoc tests.

Phase 2 outcomes

Regarding the FIQtotal, a significant decrease was observed following 4 weeks of active intervention (P = .004). The mean percentage of improvement in FIQtotal was 30.96%, which further exceeded the previously established MCID of 14%, compared to the percentage obtained following the active condition of phase 1.

Following 4 weeks of active intervention, the CGI showed improvement in 75.00% of participants, compared to 40.91% in the active condition of Phase 1. No patient reported worsening, while 25.00% reported no change.

Moreover, significant analgesic effects were demonstrated across multiple measures: VASpain (P < .001), BPIpain severity (P = .002), BPIpain interference (P = .001), BPItotal (P = .008), SF-36pain (P = .002), PCShelplessness (P = .037), and PCSmagnification (P = .023).

Significant antifatigue effects emerged as well according to VASfatigue (P = .005) and SF-36energy/vitality (P = .002).

Furthermore, significant improvements in affective symptoms were observed according to HADSdepression (P = .012), HADSanxiety (P = .037), and SF-36emotional wellbeing (P = .014).

The improvement in symptoms and disease impact was paralleled by significant changes in quality of life measures as per the SF-36. Significant improvements were observed in SF-36social functioning (P = .015), SF-36general health (P = .004), and SF-36health change (P = .002), SF-36role limitation due to physical health (P = .007), SF-36role limitation due to emotional health (P = .031), SF-36energy/vitality (P = .002), SF-36emotional wellbeing (P = .014), and SF-36pain (P = .002).

Discussion

This study is the second randomized sham-controlled trial to evaluate the effectiveness of the EXOPULSE Mollii Suit in adult individuals with fibromyalgia. The study examined the effects of repeated daily sessions over 2 weeks (ie, each intervention block of phase 1) or 4 weeks (ie, phase 2). The results indicate the potential benefits of EXOPULSE Mollii Suit, as evidenced by a reduction in disease impact (primary outcome) and various pain metrics, alongside improvements in pain-related cognition, and some quality of life measures following Phase 1. Phase 2 revealed further enhancements, encompassing FIQtotal, additional measures of pain and quality of life, as well as other secondary outcomes.

The percentages of improvement in FIQtotal already exceeded the MCID early in phase 1, with a further increase after phase 2. These findings suggest that EXOPULSE Mollii Suit allowed a clinically significant decrease in disease impact.

The substantial decrease in disease impact and pain is consistent with the growing body of evidence that underscores the efficacy of neuromodulatory interventions in managing fibromyalgia pain and impact. The present results align with prior studies on the effects of the EXOPULSE Mollii Suit in patients with fibromyalgia (an open-label study27; a case report28; and 3 randomized crossover studies,29,30,46 respectively). Concerning the acute effects, Riachi et al.27 demonstrated a substantial decrease in VASpain scores both immediately following a single session and 24 h later. However, the results also indicated an increase in VASpain scores at 24 h compared to the immediate post-session period. Rubio-Zarapuz et al.29 only found a tendency toward pain decrease following a single session. These observations underscore the efficacy of repeating sessions to sustain and accumulate analgesic effects. Hence, sustained benefit obtained by repeating the treatment sessions is supported by the findings of 2 randomized controlled studies.30,46 Both studies, like the present report, reported enhanced analgesia with repeated treatment modalities (16-28 sessions).

Findings of the current trial confirm the potential of the EXOPULSE Mollii Suit on disease impact in patients with fibromyalgia. After 2 weeks of daily stimulation in Phase 1, a significant reduction in fibromyalgia impact and pain was observed. These effects were further enhanced after 4 weeks of continued treatment in Phase 2. This was paralleled by a subjective improvement that rose from phase 1 to phase 2. Improvements extended beyond pain to include several secondary outcomes supporting the importance of treatment duration in achieving optimal clinical outcomes. All these results also align with the findings of a previous randomized controlled trial (EXOFIB(1)), which demonstrated beneficial effects on pain, disease impact, fatigue, affective symptoms, and quality of life following EXOPULSE Mollii Suit use.30 An important area of convergence between EXOFIB1 and EXOFIB2 studies is observed in the domain of disease impact and pain-related outcomes. In both studies, significant pain relief (analgesia) was observed following the active interventions inPhase 1. Concerning pain-related cognitions, significant improvements in PCS subscores were evident after phase 1 in the current study, whereas similar effects in the previous study emerged only after phase 2. In addition, affective symptoms also showed distinct dynamics of response. While the reduction in HADSdepression rating was significantly observed in phase 2 of both studies, anxiety scores started to significantly decrease in phase 1 of the previous work, whereas it was not the case until phase 2 of EXOFIB2, as captured by HADSanxiety. This was also the case of fatigue measures, which started to significantly decrease in phase 1 of the previous work versus in phase 2 of the current work.

The difference in findings and dynamics between the studies likely reflects methodological and clinical factors. The initial study (EXOFIB1) included older patients with a longer disease duration than EXOFIB2. The disease duration has been found to significantly impact disease outcomes, influencing the intensity of symptoms, the effectiveness of treatments, and the patients’ overall quality of life.29 Consequently, the early identification and treatment of fibromyalgia may serve to modify the natural history of the disease, thereby underscoring the necessity for precise diagnoses to enhance patient care and optimize long-term prognoses. Cultural differences may have also influenced the results. The differences in sample sizes among the studies could also have contributed to the observed heterogeneities.

The clinical improvement in the reported measures was accompanied with an improvement of clinical outcomes in the activities of daily living (some quality of life domains). Here, it is helpful to consider the biopsychosocial model of fibromyalgia. The model includes sensory, immune, cognitive-emotional, and social factors that bidirectionally interact with each other and are involved in pain processing. Targeting one of these factors using neuromodulation could, in turn, improve the others, and vice versa.

Unlike TENS, which often encounters practical obstacles when treating widespread pain, the EXOPULSE Mollii Suit has a distinct advantage due to its full-body stimulation and user-friendly design. This makes it ideal for managing the diffuse symptoms of fibromyalgia. This is particularly significant given reviews that emphasize the limited and inconsistent effectiveness of pharmacological treatments, which benefit only a small subset of patients,19 and the increasing support for multimodal, patient-centered care approaches.22 The observed improvements in social and occupational functioning may reflect the added value of a non-invasive, home-based therapy that patients can self-administer, which could enhance compliance and treatment adherence.

From a mechanistic perspective, the clinical improvement may be driven by several mechanisms. With regards to pain, a previous TENS study suggests potential analgesic effects result from neurophysiological changes involving the peripheral nervous system as well as spinal and supraspinal descending inhibitory control of pain.47 The gate control theory explains how peripheral neuromodulation can induce analgesia by stimulating large proprioceptive fibers, which inhibit the transmission of information conveyed by small nociceptive fibers.48 In addition, muscular, immune, and vascular factors could also contribute at the peripheral level. Hence, the EXOPULSE Mollii Suit may alleviate fibromyalgia-related symptoms by helping to normalize the previously described abnormalities.

Additionally, recent studies suggest beneficial effects of the EXOPULSE Mollii Suit in terms of pain reduction, enhanced muscle oxygenation, and increased parasympathetic activity.28,29,46 Finally, central effects might have occurred through significant changes in cortical electrical activity and in cerebrospinal fluid metabolite concentrations.49 Such changes might involve the cerebral pain matrix dedicated to the sensory, cognitive, and emotional components of pain.18,50,51

This study has several strengths. First, its rigorous, double-blind, randomized, sham-controlled, crossover design limits biases related to placebo effects and interindividual variability. Including outcomes reported by both evaluators and patients with good psychometric properties provides a comprehensive evaluation of multiple symptom domains. Additionally, the follow-up open-label phase allows for the evaluation of the effects following 1 month of stimulation. However, limitations must also be acknowledged. The sample size was relatively small, particularly in light of the several dropouts that occurred and the absence of planned additional recruitment to replace them. This limitation, together with the monocentric nature of the trial, may limit the generalizability of the findings. The sample was predominantly female, reflecting fibromyalgia’s epidemiology but potentially limiting sex-based analysis. Furthermore, the study did not evaluate long-term outcomes beyond the 1-month open-label phase. In addition, despite the absence of significant disparities between the active and sham conditions as determined by the blinding procedure, some participants were able to discern the nature of the stimulation they received. The absence of a monitoring system precluded the verification of 2 key variables: The daily usage time of the suit and adherence to a once-daily stimulation limit. Also, the results of phase 2 should be interpreted in the absence of a sham condition. Finally, although the use of a washout period is a common practice to minimize carryover effects in studies involving multiple interventions in the same patients, a potential limitation is the possibility of residual effects despite the washout intervals between intervention blocks. To address these limitations, trials with larger samples, an improved device with built-in tracking and usage control features, an extended follow-up, and mechanistic correlates are needed.

Summary

The findings of the present study indicate that the utilization of the EXOPULSE Mollii Suit for a duration of 1 h daily over a period of 2 weeks is associated with a reduction in disease impact, pain, and related outcomes in adults diagnosed with fibromyalgia, with additional effects observed following a period of 4 weeks of daily stimulation. This approach holds promise in fibromyalgia, and its potential role in the therapeutic management of this debilitating condition warrants further investigation.
