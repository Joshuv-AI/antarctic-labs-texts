Plasmids of co-circulating Staphylococcus aureus serve as vehicles for antimicrobial resistance sharing across diseased host-species at short timescales
Authors: Kathryn R Piper, Ana Beatriz Garcez Buiatte, Stephanie S R Souza, Manuela Montoya-Giraldo, Letícia Roberta Martins Costa, Odion O Ikhimiukor, Marven Berlus, Elissa M Eckhardt, Nicole B Hansel, Nisalda Carreiro, Adrienne A Workman, Chrispin Chaguza, Robert Gibson, Isabella W Martin, Cheryl P Andam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe multi-host opportunistic pathogen Staphylococcus aureus causes various superficial and life-threatening diseases. Here, we aim to determine to what extent closely related, co-circulating S. aureus isolates from diseased humans and animals differ in their phenotypic traits, core genomes, and plasmids.ResultsWe selected 36 phylogenetically matched pairs of S. aureus from diseased humans and animals in New England, USA sampled from 2011 – 2020. These represented 24 sequence types (ST), with ST30 (12/72 genomes), ST5 (12/72 genomes), and ST8 (8/72 genomes) as being the most common. Core genome sequences were highly similar between paired isolates. We found no significant differences between human- and animal-derived isolates in hemolytic activity, growth rate, or biofilm formation under high-glucose conditions. Across all 72 genomes, we detected 24 antimicrobial resistance (AMR) genes, with mepA, tet38, blaZ, and fosB being most common. We identified a total of 30 complete circular plasmids in 56 hybrid genome assemblies, i.e., assembled from short- and long-reads data. A total of 19 plasmids (10 from animal isolates and nine from human isolates) carried genes conferring resistance to beta-lactams, aminoglycosides, macrolides, and heavy metals. Plasmids carrying the rep16 + rep19 replicon types exhibited the greatest diversity of genes associated with resistance against various agents, including two plasmids with ant(6)-Ia, aph(3")-IIIa, blaI, blaR1, blaZ, bcrA, bcrR, upp, msr(A), mph(C), sat4, graS, and graR genes. Clustering the plasmids based on sequence similarity calculated using Jaccard distances revealed nearly identical resistance plasmids present in isolates from different host species, including pairs that were not each other's closest phylogenetic relative.ConclusionsOur findings are consistent with plasmids and plasmid-borne AMR genes being shared across co-circulating S. aureus populations from different hosts over short evolutionary timescales, irrespective of their core genome relatedness. These findings can guide public and veterinary health strategies to limit the emergence and dissemination and spread of resistant clones.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12866-026-05381-1.Keywords: Staphylococcus aureus, Genome, Plasmid, Mobile genetic elements, Antimicrobial resistance

Background

The Gram-positive Staphylococcus aureus is a ubiquitous commensal and opportunistic pathogen that commonly inhabits the skin, nares and mucosal membranes of vertebrates [1, 2]. While it can be carried asymptomatically in humans, S. aureus also causes a wide spectrum of superficial and potentially life-threatening diseases, including skin and soft tissue infections, bone and joint infection, pneumonia, bacteremia, and endocarditis [3, 4]. Infections due to multidrug resistant and methicillin resistant S. aureus (MRSA) strains are particularly worrisome because of the high morbidity and mortality they cause [3, 4]. Resistant S. aureus strains present in normally sterile sites of the body (i.e., invasive) can lead to disease complications, relapse, or therapeutic failure due to limited antimicrobial options [5, 6]. In animals, diseases caused by S. aureus, such as mastitis in ruminants [7], bumblefoot (ulcerative pododermatitis) in chicken [8], and skin and soft tissue infection in dogs and cats [9], can have a significant impact on animal health and agriculture [10, 11].

Although S. aureus is a generalist species, some clonal lineages are predominantly abundant in either human or specific animal hosts [2]. Notable examples are clonal complex (CC) 97 in bovine [12], sequence type (ST) 385 in birds [13], and CC8, CC22, and CC30 in humans [14]. However, many lineages of S. aureus have been frequently transmitted between humans and animals throughout the species’ evolutionary history, mainly driven by agricultural industrialization, commercialization, and global trade [12, 14, 15]. Findings from genomic studies of S. aureus have revealed that transmission between mammalian hosts is often associated with the acquisition of genetic elements that enable the successful adaptation and infection in the new host [16, 17]. Examples of these genes include those associated with immune evasion, extracellular matrix adherence, antimicrobial resistance (AMR), and exotoxins [16–18]. Large-scale genomic analyses have broadened our understanding of the genetic basis and evolutionary processes shaping the generalist lifestyle of S. aureus [12, 14]. However, most of these studies operate at broad geographic and evolutionary scales, where the signal of plasmid exchange is hard to separate from long-term divergence and population structure. Much less is known about how plasmids and plasmid-borne AMR are distributed among co-circulating human and animal isolates from the same region over short timescales, precisely the setting with the greatest opportunity for between-host exchange. Two further gaps compound this: the diversity of animal-associated S. aureus plasmids remains poorly characterized and most prior studies rely on short-read sequencing data, which fragment plasmids across contigs and thus cannot resolve complete sequences (e.g., [19–21]).

In this study, we aimed to determine whether closely related S. aureus isolates from diseased humans and animals in the New England region (United States) differ in their phenotypic traits, core genomes, and plasmid-borne AMR gene content. We analyzed phylogenetically matched pairs of isolates, defined as closely related genomes that share a most recent common ancestor in the phylogenetic tree but originate from contrasting hosts (human versus animal). We found these isolates to be highly similar, including closely related or near-identical plasmids carrying AMR genes shared across host species. These findings indicate that S. aureus can carry similar plasmid-borne AMR genes across strains from different vertebrate hosts. Highly similar plasmids and bacterial strains shared between different host-species should be considered in public health efforts implemented to curb the burden of disease and dissemination of AMR.

Methods

Bacterial collection

We selected a subset of S. aureus from a previously published large collection of isolates from diseased human and animal sources in the New England region of the United States (n = 437 genomes) [18]. A single isolate was recovered from each human patient and animal.

The human-derived isolates came from patients diagnosed with bloodstream infection at Dartmouth-Hitchcock Medical Center, New Hampshire, USA. For the human isolates, the first significant blood culture isolate from each patient is routinely archived as part of the routine course of its clinical laboratory operations. Each isolate is designated a study number and all patient identifiers were removed. We did not collect nor use any patient protected health information. Ethical approval was granted by the Committee for the Protection of Human Subjects of Dartmouth-Hitchcock Medical Center and Dartmouth College (CPHS # STUDY00031572), which evaluated the study protocol as not human subjects research. All isolates are stored in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) solution at −80 °C.

The animal-derived isolates came from routine clinical specimens that were received from multiple veterinary practices in the states of Connecticut, New Hampshire, Maine, Massachusetts, and Vermont. All isolates were from animals with confirmed clinical infections. For the animal isolates, isolates were obtained as culture swabs from routine clinical specimen submissions to the New Hampshire Veterinary Diagnostic Laboratory (NHVDL), New Hampshire, USA. No live vertebrates were used; hence, the NHVDL was exempt from the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) approval process at the University of New Hampshire. All isolates are stored in glycerol solution at −80 °C.

From this large dataset [18], we identified 36 phylogenetically matched pairs of human- and animal-derived isolates (i.e., 72 isolates in total). Following the matched-pair sampling strategy of Farhat et al. [19], pairs were selected directly from the genome-based phylogeny by choosing, for each isolate, the contrasting-host isolate that minimized the phylogenetic distance between them, rather than ST or CC assignment. This approach is ideal to obtain fine-scale resolution of recently evolved differences between similar organisms, thus improving the power for genomic comparison while reducing the confounding effects of population structure [22].

Phenotypic tests

Hemolysis assay

Bacterial isolates were grown overnight on blood agar plate. For each isolate, a single colony was suspended in 1 ml tryptic soy broth (TSB) until it reached a turbidity comparable to 0.5 McFarland scale (~ 1.5 × 10^8 CFU/mL). A 200 uL volume of the cell culture was added to a 9.8 mL of 4% v/v sheep red blood cell in phosphate-buffered saline buffer. The solution was incubated for 1 h at 37 °C and 250 rpm. The solution was then centrifuged at 16,600xg for 10 min. A 1 mL volume of the supernatant was collected and the optical density (OD) reading was recorded at OD580 was recorded. For positive control, the same procedure was followed with 200uL of crystal violet and 9.8 mL of 4% v/v sheep red blood cells. The percentage of sample hemolysis was calculated by OD580/positive control OD580 X 100%.

Growth curve assay

Bacterial culture was streaked onto blood agar plate and incubated at 37 °C degrees for 18–24 h. A single colony from each isolate was selected and inoculated into a test tube containing 10 mL of TSB and incubated overnight at 37 °C. A 5 mL volume of the culture was inoculated into a conical flask with 250 mL of TSB. OD at 600 nm was recorded for hour zero and the flask was incubated at 37 °C. At each interval (1 h, 2 h, 4 h, 6 h, 8 h, 12 h, 24 h, 30 h, 48 h), the OD was recorded by aliquoting 1 ml of the suspension.

Biofilm assay

Bacterial isolates were grown overnight in trypticase soy agar with 5% sheep blood (BD BBL, Franklin Lakes, New Jersey, USA). A single colony was picked and suspended in 1 mL sterile distilled water until it reached a turbidity comparable to 0.5 on the McFarland scale (~ 1.5 × 10^8 CFU/mL). The suspension was then diluted 1:100 in brain heart infusion broth (BHI; BD Difco, Franklin Lakes, New Jersey) supplemented with either 0.0% or 5.7% glucose. A 200uL volume of the dilution was added in a flat-bottom 96-well polystyrene microtiter plate and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C. After incubation, excess cells were removed and the plate was washed 3X with 250uL 1 × PBS. A 0.1% solution of crystal violet in water was added to each well and incubated at room temperature for 15 min. The crystal violet was then removed, and each well was washed two times with 250uL of 1 × PBS. 125ul of 30% acetic acid was added to each well to solubilize the crystal violet. A 125uL volume of the solution was then transferred to a new plate and OD reading was recorded at 550 nm.

All OD readings were taken using the Accuris SmartReader 96 Microplate Absorbance Reader.

DNA extraction, library preparation and whole genome sequencing

All 72 isolates (i.e., 36 matched pairs) were primary clinical isolates archived at − 80 °C in DMSO or glycerol by the source laboratories at the time of their original isolation. For this study, isolates were retrieved from these archival stocks, subcultured once to recover viable cells, and re-stocked at − 80 °C in DMSO. No serial passaging was performed between original isolation and sequencing. Short-read (Illumina) sequence data for all 72 isolates were obtained from our previously published study [18] and were used both to select the matched pairs and for the short-read–based analyses described below. For the present study, the same 72 isolates were additionally sequenced with long-read (Oxford Nanopore Technologies [ONT]) technology to enable hybrid genome assembly and resolve complete plasmid sequences.

For long-read sequencing, isolates were retrieved from the archival stocks and subcultured in BHI broth (BD Difco, Franklin Lakes, New Jersey) at 37 °C for 24 h. High molecular weight DNA was extracted using the Quick-DNA HMW MagBead Kit (Zymo Research; Irvine, California) following manufacturer’s instructions and with the addition of 10 mg/mL recombinant lysostaphin (Biosynth; Louisville, Kentucky) reconstituted in 20 mM sodium acetate. DNA samples were prepared using the ONT SQK-LSK114 native barcoding kit. Sequencing was performed on the PromethION 2 Solo platform using a R10 version FLO-PRO114M and minKNOW v.24.06.10. Base calling was performed using the super-accurate base-calling model with barcode trimming enabled using Dorado v.7.4.12. ONT sequencing for 16 isolates failed, thus leaving 56 long-read genomes for all downstream analyses.

We used Qubit fluorometer (Invitrogen, Grand Island, New York, USA) to measure DNA concentration. ONT sequencing was carried out at SeqCoast Genomics (Portsmouth, New Hampshire, USA).

Genome assembly, sequence quality check and annotation

Short-read assemblies were obtained from our previously published study [18], whereby Illumina reads were assembled using shovill v.1.1.0 (https://github.com/tseemann/shovill) with the –trim setting enabled to trim adapter sequences. Shovill includes methods for subsampling read depth down to 150X, trimming adapters, correcting sequencing errors, and assembling using SPAdes v.3.14.1 [23]. For the long reads, adapters were removed using porechop v.0.2.4 (https://github.com/rrwick/Porechop). Filtlong v.0.2.1 (https://github.com/rrwick/Filtlong) was used to filter high quality reads, discarding those with 10% of the worst reads and reads shorter than 1 kb. Hybrid assembly of Illumina and ONT reads (the latter generated in this study) was done using unicycler v.0.5.0 [24].

The sequence quality of the 72 short-read draft genomes [18] and 56 hybrid-assembled genomes (Illumina and ONT) were assessed using CheckM [25] and QUAST [26]. For the short-read assemblies, we calculated genome completeness ranging between 99.55–99.66% and contamination ranging from 0.08–1.2%. These metrics were all within the genome quality standards recommended by CheckM. The short-read assemblies have < 200 contigs and an N50 > 40,000 bp. The number of contigs in the 56 hybrid assemblies ranged from 1–21.

In all, we used 72 short-read genomes (Supplementary Figure S1A-F and Table S1) and 56 hybrid genome assemblies (Supplementary Figure S2A-D and Table S2) for downstream analyses. Associated metadata of all isolates are shown in Supplementary Table S1. Genome sequences were annotated using Prokka v.1.14.6 [27] with default parameters.

Pan-genome estimation and phylogenetic tree reconstruction

We generated the core and accessory genes of the 72 short-read assemblies [18] with the moderate stringency mode using Panaroo v.1.2.2 [28]. Sequence alignment of individual genes was carried out using MAFFT [29]. Genes were classified based on their frequency across the genomes as core (present in 99–100% of genomes; n = 2,133), soft-core (95 to < 99%; n = 34), shell (15 to < 95%; n = 804), and cloud (< 15%; n = 893). There were 2,167 combined core and soft-core genes (comprising 56.08% of the pan-genome) and 1,697 accessory genes (comprising 43.92% of the genome). The 72 short-read assemblies have genome sizes ranging from 2.66–2.90 Mb (mean = 2.77 Mb) and an estimated 3,864 genes present in the pan-genome. Each genome carried between 2,394 and 2,684 genes (mean = 2,539).

A multi-sequence whole-genome alignment was generated based on consensus sequences of each isolate inferred after mapping the assembled contigs against a complete reference genome for a human S. aureus strain JP080 (GenBank accession: AP017922.1) [30] using Snippy v.4.6.0 (https://github.com/tseemann/snippy). From this alignment encompassing the 72 genomes, 115,380 single nucleotide polymorphism (SNPs) were extracted using SNP-sites v.2.3.2 [31]. These SNPs were then used to generate a maximum likelihood phylogenetic tree of the S. aureus isolates using IQ-TREE v.2.1.2 [32] with the general time-reversible (GTR) [33], Gamma distribution of rate heterogeneity, and 100 bootstrap replicates. The phylogenetic tree was visualized using the online platform Interactive Tree of Life v.6.8.1 [34].

For each pair of genomes, we calculated the genetic distance using SNP-dists v.0.8.2 (https://github.com/tseemann/snp-dists) based on the SNP distance calculated by Snippy from the core gene alignment. We also calculated the average nucleotide identity (ANI) for all genome pairs using fastANI v.1.34 [35]. We conducted a core genome multilocus sequence typing (cgMLST) analysis using chewBBACA v.3.4.2 [36]. Allele calling for the 72 S. aureus genome assemblies was performed against the public cgMLST scheme for the species [37], which comprises 1,716 loci. To mitigate bias from loci that were difficult to align or absent, the core genome was refined using the chewBBACA ExtractCgMLST module. A 95% presence threshold was applied, resulting in a customized scheme composed of 1,672 loci, which was subsequently used for the allele calling. For the visualization of cgMLST relationships, a minimum spanning tree was constructed from the allelic profile matrix using GrapeTree v.1.5.0 with the MSTreeV2 algorithm [38].

Association between accessory gene presence/absence and host source (human versus animal) was tested using Scoary v.1.6.16 [39], with the core genome maximum-likelihood phylogeny supplied to account for population structure. Significance was assessed after Benjamini–Hochberg correction for multiple testing (p < 0.05). This gene-level approach compared human and animal isolates as a whole, as the limited and unbalanced number of non-companion animal isolates precluded finer comparisons among animal species.

In silico determination of AMR and virulence genes and Sequence Types (STs)

Short-read assemblies and plasmid sequences were screened for the presence of genes associated with virulence using ABRicate v.1.0.1 (https://github.com/tseemann/abricate). We searched known virulence genes in the Virulence Factor Database (VFDB) [40]. The thresholds of 60% sequence coverage and 80% sequence identity were used when querying the database. Genetic determinants of AMR were screened using AMRFinderPlus v.4.0.22 [41] with minimum thresholds of 60% reference coverage and 80% sequence identity. The ST was determined using the program mlst v.2.19.0 (https://github.com/tseemann/mlst), which extracts seven housekeeping genes (arcC, aroE, glpF, gmk, pta, tpi, and yqiL) [42] from the sequence contigs. Allelic variation in these genes were then compared to previously characterized STs in the S. aureus PubMLST database [43].

Plasmid identification, typing and sequence analysis

Because complete plasmids cannot be resolved from short-read assemblies alone [19], plasmid identification and analysis were restricted to the 56 hybrid assemblies. For the identification of plasmid contigs, we employed two tools. First, we used mob-recon from the MOB-suite v.3.1.0 [44], which classifies plasmids based on the presence of known replicons, relaxase genes, conjugation genes, and similarity to reference plasmids, and therefore enabling the assignment of each plasmid as conjugative, mobilizable, or non-mobilizable. We then analyzed the same genomes with geNomad v.1.11.2 [45], which identifies plasmids using probabilistic models based on sequence composition and hallmark genes of mobile genetic elements (MGE). A contig was considered a plasmid when detected by both tools, whereas contigs identified by only one tool were included only if they exhibited at least one plasmid-associated hallmark gene and a circular topology, as bacterial plasmids are predominantly circular molecules and non-circular plasmids are relatively rare [46–48]. Plasmid replicon types were determined using the Plasmidfinder database accessed on January 2025 [49] implemented in ABRicate. Identified plasmid sequences were further compared against the Plasmid Database (PLSDB) v.2024_05_31_v2 [50] using the Mash screen strategy with a maximum p value of 0.1 and a minimum sequence identity threshold of 0.99. AMR genes were identified from plasmid sequences using AMRFinderPlus v.4.0.22 [41] with minimum thresholds of 60% coverage and 80% sequence identity. Pairwise distances between all the complete circular plasmids were calculated with Mash v.1.1 [51] (k = 21, sketch = 1,000) for downstream statistical analysis. Using these approaches, we identified a total of 30 complete circular plasmids from the 56 hybrid genome assemblies.

Because plasmids vary in size and gene content, we carried out clustering of the 30 complete and circular plasmids using Mge-cluster based on their sequence similarity [52]. Mge-cluster considers the entire plasmid sequence by identifying unitigs (k-mer = 31) and generating a de Bruijn graph based on these unitigs. Unitigs found with low variance in their presence and absence were filtered out and the remaining unitigs were used to calculate Jaccard distances from the presence/absence matrix. The resulting distance matrix was embedded into a t-SNE plot [53] and HDBSCAN [54] was then used to identify and visualize plasmid clusters. Plasmid sequences were annotated using Prokka v.1.14.6 [27]. Plasmids that carried one or more AMR genes were selected and compared with the other plasmids in their cluster using Clinker v.0.0.31 [55]. Additional AMR genes not identified by AMRFinderPlus but annotated by Prokka were counted to determine the number of AMR genes per plasmid and were included in Figs. 3 and 4, and Supplementary Table S3.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analysis were carried out using the ggstatsplot v.0.12.1 package [56] in R v.4.3.1 [57]. We checked for normality using the shapiro.test() function and homoscedasticity using the ncvTest() to run a Breusch-Pagan test. A Welch’s t-test was used for all normal and homoscedastic data. Mann–Whitney test was used for data that were neither normally distributed nor heteroscedastic. Wilcoxon signed rank test was used for comparisons of the number of accessory genes, AMR genes, virulence genes and phenotypic data. The Kruskal–Wallis test was used to compare ANI and SNP distances among assemblies from human isolates, among assemblies from animal isolates, and between human and animal isolates. Results were considered significant when p < 0.05.

To assess whether plasmid sequence divergence was associated with the phylogenetic relatedness of their host isolates or with the time elapsed between their isolation, and to determine whether such associations were independent of plasmid backbone identity, we compared plasmids that shared the same replicon type. For each pair of plasmids, we recorded the Mash distance between them (calculated as described above), the temporal distance between their host isolates (absolute difference in collection dates, in days), and the core genome SNP distance between the host isolates (computed as described above). Because the small number of plasmids per replicon type limited the power of group-wise tests, the pre-specified primary analysis was a pooled multiple regression on distance matrices (MRM) using the ecodist package v.2.1.3 [58] with 9,999 permutations across all plasmids with an identifiable replicon type. This model expressed plasmid Mash distance as a function of host core SNP distance, temporal distance, and a binary replicon-membership matrix (0 = same replicon, 1 = different) that accounts for the fact that plasmids of different backbones are intrinsically dissimilar. This was complemented by pooled partial Mantel tests of plasmid distance versus host core SNP distance (Spearman's rank correlation, 9,999 permutations) using vegan package v.2.7–3, conditioning separately on replicon membership and on temporal distance.

As exploratory analyses, we also tested these associations within each replicon type separately, restricting these tests to replicon types with an identifiable replicon and containing at least four complete plasmids; plasmids without an identifiable replicon type were not included in these group-wise tests. Associations were tested with Mantel and partial Mantel tests (Spearman's rank correlation) in the vegan package v.2.7–3 in R v.4.3.1, with 9,999 permutations (or complete enumeration when the number of possible permutations was smaller). Group-wise results are reported in Supplementary Table S6.

Results

Human- and animal-derived S. aureus share high genomic similarity

We compiled 36 pairs of phylogenetically matched S. aureus isolates derived from a previously published dataset of 437 short-read sequenced genomes from isolates in the New England region of the United States [18]. The isolates were collected between 2011 and 2020. Each matched pair consists of one isolate derived from a human source and another isolate from an animal source that share a recent common ancestor (Fig. 1A and Supplementary Table S1). The human isolates were obtained from patients diagnosed with bloodstream infections. The animal isolates came from animals with confirmed clinical infections. Animal isolates were obtained from cats (14), cow (1), dogs (18), horse (1), and rodents (2). The temporal distance between paired isolates ranged from 162 days (5.3 months) to 3,079 days (101.1 months), with a median of 1,152 days (37.8 months) (Supplementary Figure S3).

We identified a total of 24 STs in the 72 Illumina-sequenced short-read assemblies, with ST30 (12/72–16.67%), ST5 (12/72–16.67%), and ST8 (8/72–11.11%) being the most common. The STs were grouped into seven CCs, with CC5 (20/72–27.78%) being the most frequent, followed by CC8 (16/72–22.22%), CC30 (14/72–19.44%), CC1 (8/72–11.11%), CC45 (4/72–5.56%), CC15 and CC97 (2/72–2.78% each). Six genome assemblies were not assigned to any CC. We observed that eight pairs, although characterized by different STs, shared the same CC and showed small cgMLST allelic differences (43–439); these likely represent recently diverged STs (Fig. 1A and Supplementary Tables S1 and S4). We note that ST designation for S. aureus is based on allelic differences in only seven housekeeping core genes, thus it is not surprising to find such pairs.

As expected, given our matched-pair design, the core genomes of paired isolates were highly similar. We carried out a comprehensive pairwise comparison of the 72 genome assemblies using different genetic similarity metrics: Genome-wide ANI [35], SNP distance from the core gene alignment, and allelic distance based on cgMLST [37]. ANI refers to the mean nucleotide identity of orthologous gene pairs shared between two microbial genomes [35]. Genome-wide ANI values ranged from 97.07% to 100% (mean = 98.45%, median = 98.71%), with core SNP distances spanning 0 to 39,203 SNPs (mean = 20,734.60, median = 14,324). The pairs of interest were characterized by ANI values between 99.77% and 99.99% (mean = 99.91%, median = 99.92%) and SNP distances between 75 and 660 (mean = 268.64, median = 239.5) (Supplementary Figure S4AB and Table S4). No significant difference was observed in either genome-wide ANI or core SNP distances when comparing pairs originating from within the same sources (animal or human) to those from different sources (Supplementary Figure S4CD). Based on the cgMLST profiles, we constructed a minimum spanning tree, and the tree topology revealed a clear separation of pairs according to their CC assignment, reinforcing the observation that pairs with distinct STs belong to the same CC (Supplementary Figure S4E). The cgMLST allelic differences for these pairs ranged from 43 to 439 (mean = 167.14, median = 145.5) (Supplementary Figure S2E and Table S4).

We next examined the gene content of the 72 short-read Illumina-sequenced genomes (Supplementary Table S1 and Supplementary Figure S5). There was no significant difference in the number of accessory genes per genome between human- and animal-derived isolates (median animal = 364.92, median human = 380.22) (p = 0.07, Wilcoxon signed rank test) (Fig. 1B). To test whether any accessory gene distinguished human- from animal-derived isolates, we performed a pan-genome-wide association analysis using Scoary [39]. No accessory gene was significantly associated with host source after Benjamini–Hochberg correction for multiple testing (p < 0.05), consistent with the absence of a significant difference in the number of accessory genes per genome between hosts.

Across the 72 genomes, we detected the presence of 24 genes associated with AMR (Supplementary Table S5). There was no significant difference in the number of AMR genes per genome between human- and animal-derived S. aureus (median animal = 6.00, median human = 6.00; p = 0.25, Wilcoxon signed rank test) (Fig. 1C). We found an assortment of frequently detected and less common AMR genes in our dataset. Two AMR genes were identified in all genomes: mepA (multidrug) and tet38 (tetracycline). The fosB gene, related to fosfomycin resistance, was frequently identified in 55/72 genomes (76.39%; 27 animal, 28 human). The penicillin genes blaZ and blaI were detected in 51/72 genomes (70.83%; 25 animal, 26 human), frequently co-occurring with blaR1, and identified in 42/72 genomes (52.33%; 20 animal, 22 human). The methicillin resistance gene mecA was detected in 12/72 (16.67%) genomes (6 animal, 6 human), while its regulatory elements mecR1 and mecI were less frequent (3/72–4.17% each; 2 animal, 1 human). Aminoglycoside resistance determinants included genes encoding modifying enzymes, with ant(9)-Ia (spectinomycin resistance) being the most frequent (16/72–22.22%; 8 animal, 8 human), followed by aph(3')-IIIa (kanamycin/neomycin resistance) and ant(6)-Ia (streptomycin resistance), detected in 9/72 (12.50%) each (4 animal, 5 human). The same pattern was observed for sat4 (streptothricin resistance). The gene aadD1 (kanamycin/neomycin resistance) was less frequent, detected in 2/72 (2.78%—1 animal, 1 human). The methylase genes erm(A) and erm(C), conferring target modification and cross-resistance to lincosamide/macrolide/streptogramin, were detected in 16/72 (22.22%; 8 animal, 8 human) and 1/72 (1.39%) genomes (1 animal), respectively. Macrolide resistance genes msr(A) and mph(C) were identified in 8/72 (11.11%; 2 animal, 6 human) and 10/72 genomes (13.89%; 2 animal, 8 human), respectively. The lnu(G) gene, related to lincosamide resistance, was less frequent (2/72—2.78%; 1 animal, 1 human). Tetracycline resistance was mediated by tet(K) detected in 3/72 (4.17%) genomes (2 animal, 1 human) and tet(M) in 2/72 (2.78%; 1 animal, 1 human), in addition to the ubiquitous tet(38). We identified other rare determinants, including bleO (bleomycin resistance; 2/72—2.78%, 1animal, 1 human), and dfrG (trimethoprim resistance) and fusC (fusidic acid resistance) (1/72—1.39% each, 1 human).

Across the 72 genomes, we identified a total of 96 genes associated with virulence (Supplementary Table S1). The number of virulence genes per genome is slightly but significantly higher in human S. aureus (median = 76.94) compared to animal S. aureus (median = 74.56) (p = 1.66e-04, Wilcoxon signed rank test; Fig. 1D). A total of 43 virulence genes were present in all genomes, which have functions associated with hemolysis, immune modulation, biofilm formation.

Human- and animal-derived S. aureus exhibit phenotypic similarity

We sought to determine if the human- and animal-derived S. aureus differ in three phenotypic traits that are relevant to virulence: biofilm formation, hemolytic activity, and time to reach exponential phase of a growth curve (Supplementary Table S1 and Supplementary Figure S6). We did not find significant difference in the amount of red blood cells hemolyzed against the control between human-derived (mean = 19.41%) and animal-derived isolates (mean = 19.34%) (p = 0.88, Wilcoxon signed rank test; Fig. 2A). We also did not observe any significant difference in the growth rate of the isolates measured in terms of time it took to reach the exponential phase of a growth curve (p = 0.24, Wilcoxon signed rank test; Fig. 2B). Animal and human isolates took an average of 21 h and 18.67 h to reach exponential phase, respectively. Next, we measured biofilm formation on agar that either lacks glucose or enriched in 5.7% glucose, with the latter estimating the amount of glucose in the bloodstream. We found a significant difference in biofilm formation between human- and animal-derived isolates in the absence of glucose (median animal = 0.43, median human = 0.25) (p = 0.03, Wilcoxon signed rank test; Fig. 2C). However, we did not find significant difference between the two groups of isolates when grown in 5.7% glucose (median animal = 0.75, median human = 0.65) (p = 0.44, Wilcoxon signed rank test; Fig. 2D).

Numerous diverse plasmids are present, but vary in their distribution and gene content

Given the observed similarity between human- and animal-derived S. aureus in terms of their genomic and phenotypic features, we hypothesized that autonomously replicating MGEs may be a source of difference between them. We identified a total of 30 complete circular plasmids in 56 hybrid genome assemblies, i.e., assembled from short- and long-reads sequencing data (Supplementary Table S2). The 30 plasmids were present in 29 genomes (Supplementary Table S3). In all, we obtained 15 (from 34 genomes, 44.12%) plasmids from animal isolates and 15 (from 22 genomes, 68.18%) plasmids from human isolates. One human-derived S. aureus genome carried two plasmids, while 28 genomes contained a single plasmid (Fig. 3A). In animal isolates, 11 mobilizable plasmids and four non-mobilizable plasmids were identified, whereas in human isolates, 10 mobilizable and five non-mobilizable plasmids were detected (Fig. 3B). We did not detect the presence of plasmids in 27 S. aureus genomes.

We next examined the gene content of the 30 plasmids. Plasmid sizes ranged from 2,396 and 38,292 bp. Plasmids from animal S. aureus ranged from 3,125 and 36,354 bp (median = 20,731 bp) and plasmids from human S. aureus ranged from 2,396 and 38,292 bp (median = 20,403 bp), and the difference between them was not significant (p = 0.40, Mann–Whitney; Fig. 3C). In terms of the number of AMR genes present in each plasmid, we also did not observe significant difference between the two groups of plasmids (median = 3 each) (p = 0.79, Mann–Whitney; Fig. 3D).

Across the 30 plasmids, we identified the presence of eight replicon types and combinations thereof (i.e., multi-replicon plasmids [59]) based on the sequence variation of the gene encoding the replication initiation protein (Rep) (Fig. 3E). Six plasmids lacked the rep gene (two mobilizable and four non-mobilizable). Animal-associated plasmids contained seven replicon types or replicon combinations, while human-associated plasmids contained five. Common between the two groups were the rep types rep5a + rep16, rep20, rep16 + rep19, and rep13. There were rep types that were only found in each plasmid group: repUS5 + rep16, rep5a + rep16 + rep19, rep21 in plasmids from animal isolates, while rep20 + rep21 in human isolates.

Among the 30 plasmids analyzed, 11 did not harbor any AMR gene (5 with no identifiable replicon type, 1 with repUS5 + rep16, 2 with rep21, 2 with rep13, and 1 with rep20 + rep21) (Supplementary Table S3). In contrast, five distinct AMR gene combinations were identified among the remaining 19/30 (63.4%) plasmids, of which 10 (52.63%) were derived from animal isolates and nine (47.37%) from human isolates. Plasmids carrying the rep16 + rep19 replicon types exhibited the greatest diversity of AMR genes, including two plasmids with ant(6)-Ia, aph(3")-IIIa, blaI, blaR1, blaZ, bcrA, bcrR, upp, msr(A), mph(C), sat4, graS, and graR genes. Screening of plasmid sequences in the PLSDB plasmid database revealed that similar plasmids have been previously described in S. aureus and Klebsiella oxytoca from seven different continents, and they also carry a wide diversity of AMR genes. In contrast, the remaining plasmids with this replicon combination rep16 + rep19 (n = 3) carried a more restricted AMR profile consisting of blaI, blaR1, and blaZ. A single rep20 plasmid harbored blaI, blaR1, blaZ, msr(A), and mph(C), whereas the other rep20 plasmids (n = 4) contained only blaI, blaR1, and blaZ. These plasmids are similar to others identified globally in S. aureus, sharing comparable resistance profiles (Supplementary Table S5). Notably, two of these plasmids were classified as non-mobilizable. Finally, one rep5a + rep16 plasmid displayed the gene profile blaI, blaR1, blaZ, bcrA, bcrR, and upp, which was also observed in the single rep5a + rep16 + rep19 plasmid. This profile has been previously observed in isolates from North America, Asia, Europe, and Oceania, harboring a wide diversity of AMR genes (Supplementary Table S5). The remaining rep5a + rep16 plasmids (n = 6) carried the AMR genes blaI, blaR1, blaZ, and bcrR.

Collectively, the plasmid-borne AMR genes identified across the 30 plasmids comprised beta-lactam (blaI, blaR1, blaZ), aminoglycoside (ant(6)-Ia, aph(3")-IIIa), macrolide (msr(A), mph(C)), streptothricin (sat4), and bacitracin/cationic-peptide resistance genes (bcrA, bcrR, upp, graS, graR). All other AMR genes detected in these genomes were not located on any identified plasmid and were therefore likely chromosomal (Supplementary Table S5). These include the highly frequent mepA and tet38, the erm, tet, and fos genes, and mecA, the latter typically carried by the chromosomally integrated SCCmec element [60]. This locational assignment (i.e., chromosome versus plasmid) was based on the 56 hybrid (short- and long-read) genome assemblies, in which complete plasmids could be resolved; for the 16 isolates with short-read data only, the plasmid versus chromosomal location of AMR genes could not be determined.

Plasmid-borne AMR genes are shared by S. aureus from different hosts

We next sought to determine to what extent the plasmids from human- and animal-derived S. aureus are comparable in terms of their overall sequences. Using the 30 plasmid sequences from the 56 hybrid assemblies, we clustered the plasmids based on sequence similarity calculated using Jaccard distances (Fig. 4A). Six plasmid clusters were identified, of which five clusters each consisting of plasmids from S. aureus derived from human and animal sources. Six plasmids cannot be grouped into any of the six clusters, indicating that they have distinct sequences. Five clusters consisted of plasmids with varying replicon types or combinations thereof. Cluster 5 included plasmids with multi-replicon type consisting of rep5a + rep16. It is important to note that, among the 30 plasmids analyzed, 10 were identified across five genetically paired S. aureus isolates. While two isolate pairs exhibited similar plasmid profiles and were classified within the same clusters (8518a and 470sab; 19852b and 226sab), the remaining pairs harbored distinct plasmids (8114a and 406sab; 399sab and 1783b; 8581a and 230sab), underscoring that even closely related S. aureus strains can differ markedly in their plasmid repertoires.

We next sought to compare the genetic organization of plasmids that harbored at least one resistance gene (Fig. 4B-E). Comparisons were separated into groups based on cluster affiliation. All analyzed plasmids carried the cadmium resistance gene cadD. Some plasmid clusters were notable. In Cluster 3, we compared two plasmids from human S. aureus (222sab_p and 353sab_p) and one from an animal S. aureus (10095a_p), all of them classified as rep20 (Fig. 4B). Plasmids 222sab_p and 10095a_p were highly similar in terms of gene content and synteny. Although the genomes that carry them are not members of a phylogenetically matched pair, both belong to CC5 and harbored the blaI, blaR1 and blaZ resistance genes. Plasmid 353sab_p contained an additional region with the AMR genes msr(A), mph(C), and mercury resistance genes merA, merB and merR1, flanked by two IS6 family insertion sequences.

In Cluster 4, plasmids 4638_p (animal-derived S. aureus) and 211sab_p (human-derived S. aureus), both rep20 originating from CC5 genomes that are not members of a phylogenetically matched pair showed high similarity in terms of gene content and synteny (Fig. 4C). Both plasmids contained blaI, blaR1 and blaZ genes and an IS6 element.

Cluster 5 included six plasmids with AMR genes: three from animal S. aureus (10424_p, 1783b_p, 8518a_p) and three from human S. aureus (470sab_p, 406sab_p, 335sab_p) (Fig. 4D), and all were rep5a + rep16. Among these, only 8518a_p and 470sab_p were members of a phylogenetically matched pair (CC15). All six plasmids carried blaI, blaR1 and blaZ and bcrR. Two insertion sequences were present: ISNCY family in five plasmids and IS1182 family in one plasmid.

In Cluster 6, we compared six plasmids: four from animal S. aureus (10299a_p, 19852b_p, 7847c_p, 6696_p) and two from human S. aureus (226sab_p, 467sab_2cp) (Fig. 4E). Only plasmids 226sab_p and 19852b_p were members of a phylogenetically matched pair. Within this cluster were three plasmid configurations, with high similarity in terms of gene content and synteny between 226sab_p and 10299a_p, between 19852b_p and 467sab_p, and between 7847c_p and 6696_p. While all six plasmids carried blaI, blaR1 and blaZ, two plasmids (226sab_p and 10299a_p) carried additional AMR genes: aph(3″)-IIIa, sat4, ant(6)-Ia, bcrAB and uppP (bacitracin resistance), graSR (resistance to cationic antimicrobial peptides), msr(A), and mph(C), flanked by three IS6 family elements. Arsenic resistance genes (arsBC) and the transporter associated with bacitracin resistance (bcrR) were observed in plasmids 7847c_p and 6696_p. Four plasmids shared the same replicon type rep16 + rep19 (226sab, 467sab, 10299a, and 19852b), whereas two plasmids exhibited distinct replicon combinations: rep5a + rep16 (7847c) and rep5a + rep16 + rep19 (6696a).

In a matched pair of isolates sharing a recent common ancestor, the presence of a given plasmid in only one member is most parsimoniously explained by gain or loss by the lineages since their divergence and does not by itself indicate direct transmission between the sampled isolates. We tested whether plasmid sequence similarity reflected the relatedness of their host strains or the time between their isolation by comparing plasmids sharing the same replicon type (Supplementary Table S6). Plasmid distance was not associated with the time elapsed between isolates in any analysis (pooled MRM p = 0.18; partial Mantel r = 0.09, p = 0.23; all group-wise p > 0.16), indicating that plasmids of the same replicon type remained highly similar regardless of how many years separated the isolates carrying them. Within individual replicon types, a positive correlation between plasmid distance and host core-SNP distance was detected in only one of the three groups (rep5a&rep16; r = 0.90, p = 0.005), and was absent in the others. However, in the pooled analysis that accounts for replicon identity, plasmid distance was not significantly associated with host core-SNP distance (MRM p = 0.27; partial Mantel r = − 0.05, p = 0.58); the only significant predictor was replicon membership (MRM p < 0.001). Although the full model was significant (R2 = 0.27, p = 0.009), this fit was driven entirely by replicon identity rather than by host relatedness or sampling time. Thus, plasmid sequence similarity was determined by backbone identity rather than by the phylogenetic relatedness of the host or by the time of sampling.

Discussion

From healthcare clinics to farming systems and veterinary settings, the opportunistic S. aureus remains a formidable pathogen to control, in part due to its ability to rapidly acquire and disseminate AMR through MGEs [61, 62]. Here, we studied 30 plasmids from short- and long-read sequencing data of 56 phylogenetically matched S. aureus from disease cases. Our geographically narrow population of human- and animal-derived isolates, all sampled within a short evolutionary timescale (2011–2020), allowed us to study the genomic and plasmid relatedness without the confounding effects of large geographical distances and millions of years of evolution. We also postulate that their proximity to each other may provide greater opportunities for interaction and transmission.

We highlight two main findings. First, S. aureus isolates from diverse host-species exhibit notable genomic and phenotypic similarity, consistent with the generalist nature of this species [63, 64]. Although the genetic distances between isolates do not meet established thresholds for direct transmission [65, 66], the close genetic relatedness of these isolates, despite originating from different host species, suggests that ecological barriers between vertebrate hosts are highly permeable to S. aureus transmission and that host-switching events occur frequently enough to maintain genetic cohesion across the species [14]. Despite overall similarity, we observed subtle yet significant host-associated differences. Human isolates tended to harbor a broader repertoire of virulence genes, whereas animal isolates showed enhanced biofilm formation under glucose-limited conditions. These patterns may reflect rapid, host-specific adaptation over short timescales. Increased biofilm formation in the absence of glucose in animal isolates, compared to human isolates, may indicate adaptation to nutrient-limited or environmental niches, as biofilm production in S. aureus is strongly influenced by nutrient availability, and varies across genetic backgrounds and ecological contexts [67]. The expanded virulence gene repertoire in human isolates may be driven by selective pressures related to host immunity or clinical settings [2, 68].

Second, despite the range of variation in the plasmids present in our dataset in terms of size, gene content and replicon types, we found notable similarities between plasmids carried by S. aureus from different hosts. MGEs often exhibit evolutionary histories distinct from the bacterial cell that harbor them and can therefore rapidly disseminate clinically relevant traits across clonal lineages, ecological niches, and species boundaries [69]. The presence of highly similar plasmids across distinct host sources further supports the existence of a shared plasmid pool circulating between human and animal S. aureus populations [70]. Consistent with this, plasmid sequence similarity in our dataset was governed by replicon (backbone) identity rather than by the phylogenetic relatedness of the host or the time between sampling, a pattern more consistent with horizontal movement of plasmids than with vertical co-inheritance, although our data do not allow us to quantify transfer directly. On the other hand, the observation that closely related isolate pairs can harbor distinct plasmid profiles highlights the dynamic nature of plasmid gain and loss [71], consistent with frequent turnover and horizontal acquisition even among highly similar genomes. This genomic plasticity is further supported by the presence of multiple insertion sequences, including members of the IS6 family, which are known to facilitate gene mobilization and the assembly of complex resistance regions within plasmids [62, 72, 73]. Interestingly, despite this evidence of active genetic exchange, we did not identify fully conjugative plasmids in our dataset using MOB-suite. This pattern is consistent with previous observations in S. aureus, in which only an estimated 5% of plasmids carry conjugation gene clusters required for autonomous transfer and the majority of the non-conjugative plasmids (around 80%) depend on mobilization functions or helper elements for horizontal transfer [74–76]. This reliance on mobilization rather than autonomous conjugation, together with the presence of restriction-modification systems that impede foreign DNA uptake [77], may help explain why most plasmids in our study show greatest similarity to those previously identified in Staphylococcus species (in the PLSDB database), suggesting that, for most plasmids, a genetic barrier may constrain inter-species transfer. Indeed, the rep16&rep19 backbone was found among hundreds of related plasmids spanning all continents, indicating that some clinically important multidrug plasmids cross genus boundaries.

Our results on plasmid diversity and clustering are consistent with previous studies on AMR-bearing plasmids in S. aureus from various ecological niches. For example, the spd gene conferring resistance to the broad-spectrum aminocyclitol antibiotic spectinomycin is carried on a plasmid in MRSA ST398 isolated from both human and animal sources [78]. In France, an intriguing case is the rep7a plasmid family that carried the AMR genes str (streptomycin) and cat(pC221) (chloramphenicol) separately in distinct plasmids up until 2012 and mostly in equine isolates of S. aureus [21]. Yet the two AMR genes converged in the same plasmid backbone in 2013 and progressively replaced the single-AMR plasmids in ecologically diverse S. aureus [21]. Our study expands on these previous reports and we highlight the broad host-species range of AMR-carrying S. aureus plasmids. Our results indicate that human- and animal-associated S. aureus can each act as a reservoir of plasmid-borne AMR for the other. It is therefore critical that surveillance efforts of S. aureus go beyond simply identifying clonal lineages and genetic relationships based on core genes. When looking at short evolutionary timescales, S. aureus that are often indistinguishable in their core genomes and phenotypic features will considerably vary in plasmid gene content. Population-level analyses of plasmids, other MGEs and MGE-borne accessory genes in S. aureus will be critical in clarifying their roles not only in AMR transmission between hosts but also in host switching and host adaptation.

The nearly identical plasmids carrying multiple resistance genes in isolates sampled from different hosts, irrespective of whether the isolates are members of a phylogenetically matched pair or not, are notable and consistent with previous reports of plasmids as vehicles of AMR, heavy metal resistance, and virulence genes in clinical S. aureus [79–82]. However, the diversity of animal-associated S. aureus plasmids remains poorly investigated, and it is unclear if the distribution of such plasmids is shaped by the geography, host ecology or phylogeny of S. aureus in animals. In a previous study, it was reported that although plasmid-borne AMR and virulence genes have been linked to lineages, the ecological sources of strains were not examined [83]. Our matched human–animal design is therefore a useful starting point for future studies of animal-derived S. aureus plasmids. Given that plasmids can evolve faster than the host chromosome [84, 85], the persistence of nearly identical plasmids in isolates sampled years apart is striking. This stability may be maintained by ongoing selection from the AMR and heavy metal resistance genes these plasmids carry, by plasmid stabilization and maintenance systems [86, 87], and by the wide distribution of these plasmid backbones, some of which are shared across multiple species. Notably, the presence of heavy metal resistance genes is itself relevant, as arsenic, cadmium, and mercury can induce persister formation, which may favor survival under stress and the maintenance of AMR [21, 78–80]. Additional multidrug plasmids may be circulating in our S. aureus population beyond those captured here, and could be uncovered by additional long-read sequencing and broader surveillance. A variety of plasmids from different Staphylococcus species has been reported to spread across continents and persist for up to seven decades [88], and this should form the basis for extending our current work in the future. Plasmid transfer from outside the genus Staphylococcus is also worrisome, as had been reported in plasmid-encoded mecB-mediated methicillin resistance in S. aureus in a healthcare setting that likely originated in Macrococcus [80].

Our study is not without limitations. First, we do not have clinical data about the source patients of either the animal or human isolates, and as such are not able to consider patient outcomes of these isolates. Second, we grouped all animal species and animal diseases together, even though each is distinct and the selection pressures exerted by different hosts will certainly differ. Our animal isolates were also predominantly from companion animals, with only one cow, one horse, and two rodents, which precluded a robust comparison among animal groups. As companion animals share close contact with humans, this is consistent with the human-animal plasmid sharing we observed but may not extend to livestock or wildlife. Species-specific patterns of plasmid transmission therefore remain a priority for future studies with expansive and more balanced sampling. Third, we have a relatively small sample size of 72 isolates, which may not completely capture the genetic variation in S. aureus. Thus, other clonal lineages were not represented in our analyses. However, the matched-pair design and integration of genomic and phenotypic data provide high-resolution insight into recent evolutionary dynamics across hosts. Such approach will be useful in developing a broader and more systematic sampling scheme in the future.

Conclusions

In summary, our findings reveal that plasmids and plasmid-borne AMR genes can be shared across co-circulating S. aureus populations from different hosts over short evolutionary timescales, irrespective of their core genome relatedness. These findings can guide public and veterinary health strategies to limit the emergence and spread of resistant clones, while also informing our understanding of disease transmission and AMR dissemination in other multi-host bacterial pathogens. Importantly, they reveal that focusing solely on bacterial lineages underestimates the true dynamics of AMR spread, underscoring the need to integrate plasmid-level analyses into One Health surveillance efforts.
