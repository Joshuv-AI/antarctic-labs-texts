Early emergency department discharge for intermediate heart score patients presenting for chest pain
Authors: Amber Pawlikowski, Elizabeth Hubbard, Joel Krauss, Javier Valle, Jessica Doan, Susanne DeMeester, Bradley Hubbard
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractStudy ObjectiveThe use of the HEART score to risk stratify patients for short‐term major adverse cardiac events in the emergency department (ED) setting is well established. Although discharge to home for low‐risk HEART score patients is widely accepted as safe practice, there are limited outcomes data on moderate‐risk HEART score patients discharged to home. We investigated the safety of discharging moderate‐risk HEART score patients to home from the ED with established early cardiology follow‐up.MethodsWe performed a retrospective cohort analysis of patients presenting to the ED with chest pain from April 2020 through December 2020. Patients were evaluated in the ED and underwent serial conventional troponin testing and electrocardiogram (ECG). Clinicians calculated a HEART score and employed shared decision‐making with moderate‐risk patients (score 4–6), offering hospital admission versus discharge home with a formalized process for rapid cardiology follow‐up (within 2 business days). We assessed the frequency of acute myocardial infarction or death at 30 days and before cardiology follow‐up.ResultsDuring our study period, 2939 patient encounters were screened for chest pain. Of these, 333 of 547 eligible moderate‐risk HEART score patients were referred for rapid follow‐up. The median time to follow‐up appointment was 2.9 business days (interquartile range 1.3, 6.5), and 264 (79%) of patients kept their follow‐up appointment. One patient (0.3%) suffered death within 30 days, before cardiology follow‐up. There were no myocardial infarctions.ConclusionsThese results suggest that moderate‐risk HEART score patients may be considered for discharge from the ED with rapid cardiology follow‐up. Formalizing processes to facilitate these early evaluations may represent a viable alternative to hospital admission, without diminishing patient outcomes.Keywords: ACS, Cardiology, ED, HEART score, MACE

INTRODUCTION

Background

Chest pain is one of the most common presentations to the emergency department, accounting for an estimated 19.6 per 1000 visits annually. 1 Although <10% of these patients receive the diagnosis of acute coronary syndrome (ACS), 2 missed cases of ACS are a major cause for malpractice litigation in the United States, driving significant apprehension over the disposition of these patients. 3 To assist with disposition, clinicians often employ objective and validated risk scoring systems. The HEART score is a risk stratification score that categorizes patients into low, medium, or high risk for short‐term major adverse cardiac events (MACE) and has been endorsed for use by national and professional societies. 4 , 5 , 6 Low‐risk patients can be discharged to home safely, 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 whereas high‐risk patients are generally admitted for expedited evaluation. However, the optimal disposition and timing of evaluation for moderate‐risk patients remains unclear.

Importance

Moderate‐risk patients can represent over half of the patients evaluated using the HEART score. 12 In the absence of data to guide management for these patients, clinicians are forced to weigh the risks of adverse patient outcomes against unnecessary costs from increased length of stay and potentially inappropriate testing. With the impact of the COVID‐19 pandemic on health care use, resources, and staffing of inpatient services, these pressures were only heightened. 13 As a result, during the height of the pandemic, our institution developed a process to offer moderate‐risk HEART score patients rapid cardiology follow‐up to facilitate expedited evaluation and testing on an outpatient basis. The recent 2022 American College of Cardiology Expert Consensus Decision Pathway on the Evaluation and Disposition of Acute Chest Pain in the emergency department does offer discharge from the ED for some intermediate‐risk patients without additional cardiac testing but does recommend early outpatient follow‐up. 14 Early ED follow‐up of chest pain patients without high‐risk features of ACS has been shown to be safe, reduce admissions, and reduce unnecessary testing. 15

Goals of this investigation

In this analysis, we describe the feasibility and outcomes of discharging patients with moderate‐risk HEART scores to home rapid outpatient cardiology follow‐up from a community ED setting. We sought to evaluate the safety and efficacy of such a strategy for patients, evaluating time from ED discharge to cardiology follow‐up, acute myocardial infarction (AMI), or death before cardiology follow‐up and 30‐day AMI or death, as well as return to ED rates at 7 and 30 days.

METHODS

Study design and setting

We conducted a retrospective observational study examining patients presenting with chest pain at a community teaching hospital from April 2020 to December 2020. The hospital is a 537‐bed community teaching hospital with an average ED census of 80,000 visits annually. The ED is staffed by 43 emergency medicine board‐certified attending physicians and 22 advanced practice providers (APPs). Certain departments of the ED also incorporate rotating emergency medicine residents from a nearby academic medical center. Approximately 30% of ED patients are covered by Medicare and 23% by Medicaid. The hospital's cardiology division has 28 board‐certified cardiologists with 12 APPs all within the same hospital‐owned integrated practice. The center offers a full range of interventions for cardiac patients including diagnostic and interventional cardiology, as well as a robust cardiac surgery program.

The Bottom Line

This is a retrospective analysis of a proposed management approach to patients presenting to the emergency department with chest pain who have an intermediate HEART score. It may be possible for these patients to be discharged with close cardiology follow‐up and avoid unnecessary hospital admission.

Selection of participants

Patients were included in the study if they had a primary diagnosis of chest pain. Patients were excluded if they carried an alternate non‐coronary primary diagnosis (ie, aortic dissection, herpes zoster), as were patients who had ACS as documented by admission diagnosis or positive troponin values. Patients who were under 18 years of age, pregnant, or incarcerated were also excluded (Figure 1).

Interventions

Patients’ ED evaluation included a history and physical, serial ECGs, and 0‐ and 2‐hour conventional troponin I (TnI) testing, and calculation of HEART score per institutional chest pain pathway. Per institutional ED guidelines, patients were first ruled out for AMI with serial conventional TnI evaluations and ECGs at 0‐ and 2‐hours, using sex‐specific cutoff values (men: <0.1 ng/mL, women: <0.06 ng/mL). 16 , 17 Afterward, clinicians used the HEART score to aid in cardiac risk stratification. 5 , 6 Low‐risk patients (HEART score 1–3) were discharged home with primary care follow‐up for consideration of outpatient cardiology evaluation or testing, and high‐risk patients (HEART score 7 or higher) were admitted for inpatient evaluation. Through shared decision‐making, patients with a moderate‐risk HEART score (4–6) were offered either hospital admission or discharge home with rapid cardiology follow‐up. Those patients who agreed to outpatient follow‐up were included in the analytic cohort. Patients who were discharged to home but did not agree to rapid cardiology follow‐up, were not included in the primary analytic cohort, but their outcomes were tracked and analyzed separately.

Through collaboration between the cardiology and emergency departments, the Cardiology Rapid Follow‐up Clinic (CRFC) was created. The CRFC held additional outpatient appointments specifically for ED patients. As many of these patients would ultimately be seen in the cardiology clinic anyway, we felt this practice would streamline access as opposed to overburden outpatient capacity and so did not require the hiring of additional staff or the creation of a new physical space. ED attending physicians, residents, and APPs, engaged in shared decision‐making with patients and presented patients with the option of admission to the hospital versus rapid follow‐up in the newly established CRFC. This discussion was recorded in the medical record. If a patient elected for follow‐up in the CRFC, a phone call was made to the clinic, and patients were contacted by CRFC staff to schedule a follow‐up appointment within 2 business days. Patients received an appointment time before discharge from the ED. After hours and on weekends, a voicemail was left, and the patient was contacted the subsequent business day to establish an appointment with a goal of 2 business days after the initial ED visit. Patients did occasionally receive same‐day follow‐ups. The CRFC was staffed by cardiologists and cardiology APPs. ED physicians and and clinical teams as well as cardiology physicians and teams were educated on the process. Ultimate discretion for patient disposition relied on clinical judgment and shared decision‐making with the patient. Cardiac testing ordered at the CRFC was given priority scheduling so that the testing was optimally performed within 2 weeks of the visit.

Measurements

We performed a retrospective chart review to identify recurrent ED visits before scheduled cardiology follow‐up, as well as 7‐ and 30‐day incidence of ED return. Each patient's HEART score was either directly abstracted from ED documentation for scores assigned at the time of service or manually calculated by study investigators through chart audit.

Manual chart abstraction from the electronic medical record (EMR) was performed to identify ED visits at other affiliated and local institutions. State health records were reviewed for mortality using a secure analytic warehouse. Additional variables collected from the EMR included patient demographics, discharge disposition, 30‐day follow‐up imaging, testing, and procedures, 7‐ and 30‐day return to ED, and 30‐day AMI or death. AMI was defined by the Fourth Universal Definition of Myocardial Infarction. 18 All AMI and deaths were confirmed by chart review by the cardiologist. There was no direct patient interaction for the purpose of this research. Therefore, no consent documents were required. An institutional review board waiver of Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act authorization was obtained before study initiation.

Outcomes

The primary outcome of interest was either death or AMI within 30 days of discharge. We also evaluated AMI at both 7 and 30 days from ED evaluation and returns to ED at 7 days, 30 days, and before cardiology follow‐up. Additional outcomes including AMI and ED returns were selected to further characterize the short‐term trajectories of discharged patients. Finally, we also examined rates and timing of downstream cardiac testing (ie, stress testing and coronary angiography).

Data analyses

All study variables were reviewed for outliers and missingness before calculating summary statistics. The distributional assumption of normality was assessed for all variables under investigation. Demographic and comorbidity variables were summarized using mean and SD, median and interquartile range (IQR), or frequencies and proportions, depending on the variable type and distribution. Rates of primary and secondary outcomes are reported as proportions along with 95% confidence intervals, determined using Clopper–Pearson exact methods. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 28.0.1.1 (15) software.

RESULTS

Over the course of the study period, 333 patients were identified for inclusion in the analysis. Of the included 333 patients, 206 (62%) had HEART scores documented at the time of service, with 127 (38%) of scores retrospectively calculated by clinical reviewers from chart abstraction. The average HEART score of moderate patients discharged to home was 5.2. Full descriptive statistics can be found in Table 1.

Main results

Of the 333 patients discharged to home with moderate‐risk HEART scores and a plan for rapid cardiology follow‐up, there was 1 patient mortality (0.3%) within 30 days of ED evaluation and no AMIs. The mortality was a cardiac arrest in a patient with severe bioprosthetic aortic stenosis, end‐stage renal disease on dialysis, gastrointestinal bleeding, hemoptysis, and oxygen‐dependent chronic obstructive pulmonary disease with multiple chest pain admissions within the month. The patient was being evaluated for transcatheter aortic valve replacement and had been referred for rapid cardiology follow‐up but missed his appointment due to hospitalization. There were no AMI or deaths within 7 days of ED discharge. Return to the ED was infrequent, both within 7 days (n = 14, 4.5%) and within 30 days (n = 24, 7.2%). Of these ED return visits (45 individual events), 16 were repeat return visits. There were 29 (8.7%) unique patient return visits. Stress testing was performed in 114 patients (33.2%), performed at a median of 15 (IQR 9–19) days from ED discharge. Coronary angiography was performed within 30 days of discharge in 29 patients (8.7%), occurring at a median of 18 days (IQR 11–16) (Table 2, Figure 2).

Moderate‐risk patients without follow‐up

Although moderate‐risk HEART score patients were eligible for referral to CRFC, 214 (39%) did not receive rapid follow‐up. Reasons for appointments not being scheduled included patients declining appointments, patients selecting hospital admission, CRFC staff unable to contact the patient (ie, phone line disconnected, voice mailbox full, no answer), patients already being followed at a different cardiology practice, or patients presenting from out of state. In this group, there were 2 deaths at 30 days of follow‐up and no cases of AMI. One death was secondary to respiratory failure in the setting of stage IV lung cancer with superior vena cava syndrome and pneumonia, after the transition to comfort care. The second death was in an elderly patient with severe aortic stenosis who presented for evaluation of chest pain in the setting of atrial fibrillation. After conversion to sinus rhythm, his chest pain resolved, and he was discharged home. Unfortunately, he presented to the ED 3 days later with recurrent atrial fibrillation and cardiogenic shock, ultimately transitioning to comfort care.

LIMITATIONS

There are several study limitations. First, the retrospective nature restricted the investigators to the data that were documented in the patient's medical record. The study was performed at a single institution potentially limiting the applicability of applying to other institutions. This was not a randomized study and there was no control group given the circumstances of the COVID‐19 pandemic. Second, a significant proportion of patients (38%) did not have a documented HEART score at the time of initial clinical assessment, thus requiring a post hoc calculation based on chart review and raising the possibility of misclassification bias or overestimation of patient risk. However, when comparing the clinical and demographic characteristics of patients with and without retrospectively calculated HEART scores, the populations appeared similar (Table S1), limiting the likelihood of significant misclassification bias. Regarding overestimation of risk, although the clinical reviewers used a standard validated HEART scoring tool, we cannot exclude differences in interrater reliability. 19 However, prior analyses have suggested that in the presence of discordance between ED and cardiology calculations of HEART scores, cardiologists are more likely to assign lower scores than their ED counterparts. 20 As cardiologist investigators performed the majority of the chart reviews, it would be less likely for retrospectively calculated HEART scores to be systematically increased. Finally, this proportion of missing HEART scores is similar to rates previously published in like populations. 21 Third, a significant number of moderate‐risk HEART score patients did not follow up in our postdischarge clinic. Despite such a large number of patients not being seen in follow‐up, it did not appear to affect their overall outcomes. Finally, this study period included the initial phases of the COVID‐19 pandemic. It is possible that this could have affected decision‐making by the ED physicians and APPs and patients, such that care patterns and patient preferences were to avoid inpatient admission in favor of outpatient evaluation.

DISCUSSION

In this analysis, we found that rapid outpatient cardiology follow‐up was a reasonable and safe disposition for moderate‐risk HEART score patients presenting to the ED with chest pain. Of 333 patients with scheduled follow‐ups, there was a high rate of completed appointments, and these were performed in a timely fashion (within 7 days). Among these patients, there was a single major adverse cardiac event at 30 days. These data suggest that a formal process facilitating early cardiology follow‐up patients may be a viable strategy for moderate‐risk chest pain patients in the ED.

We chose to measure AMI and death as opposed to MACE with the combined outcome of stroke, coronary angiogram, or revascularization. The most meaningful outcomes for early ED discharge are missed AMI or death before follow‐up. These patients were being seen early after discharge, so coronary angiogram or revascularization within 30 days were not the outcomes we were most concerned about.

There have been multiple studies showing the safety of early ED discharge for low‐risk chest pain patients (as defined by a HEART score 0–3) and low high‐sensitivity troponins. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 Additionally, some studies have suggested that select moderate‐risk chest pain patients may not be at as high of a risk of cardiac events as previously reported. 22 Weinstock et al. presented a study of 45,000 patients presenting with chest pain. 23 The investigators examined patients with 2 negative troponins, non‐concerning ED vitals, and non‐ischemic ECG findings regardless of risk stratification and found a very low primary outcome rate (life‐threatening arrhythmias, inpatient ST–segment‐elevation MI (STEMI), cardiac or respiratory arrest, or death during hospitalization) of 0.18%. Sharp et al. examined 30‐day mortality or AMI in a prospective study using HEART score in patients presenting with chest pain. They found that patients with a HEART score of ≤5 fell below the 1% risk threshold of a 30‐day adverse event. 24 This cohort was 89% of their ED encounters for chest pain. These findings are similar to ours, with a mean HEART score of 5.2. In a retrospective study Spiegel et al. evaluated a composite of life‐threatening arrhythmia, inpatient STEMI, cardiac or respiratory arrest, or death in intermediate HEART score patients admitted to the hospital after a negative ED evaluation. 25 This study showed a low cardiovascular risk for inpatient intermediate‐risk HEART score if there was a non‐ischemic ECG. Although all these studies have similar outcomes to our patient population, they did not look at the outcomes of early ED discharge for intermediate‐risk patients.

Furthermore, not every patient discharged to CRFC underwent non‐invasive or additional cardiac testing. A prior analysis of health insurance data evaluated patients presenting to the ED with a diagnosis of chest pain and found the patients who did not undergo cardiac testing in the ED were no more likely to experience an AMI than those who did. 26 The researchers concluded that deferral of early non‐invasive cardiac testing was safe and reasonable. 26 Our findings add to these results, in concluding that not only is deferral of testing safe in selected patients, but the development of a CRFC may decrease unnecessary testing and decrease length of stay by diverting patients who are admitted for stress tests or undergo non‐invasive evaluation as part of an algorithm before cardiology evaluation.

Finally, a recent Canadian analysis of early ED discharge of intermediate‐risk chest pain patients produced similar findings to our study. 21 The investigators described similar ED return rates (6.1% vs. 7.2% at 30 days in our study) and no mortalities at 6 weeks (vs. an unpreventable death in our population), but with higher rates of ACS (5.4% as compared to 0% in our population at 30 days). However, they did not report their median follow‐up time.

In summary, our study and the previously published literature support larger investigations to evaluate the shared decision‐making early follow‐up algorithm for intermediate‐risk chest pain patients. Through establishing a formalized system for facilitating early cardiology follow‐up, we were able to safely manage a patient population that accounts for a significant proportion of ED visits on an annual basis. The process we developed allows us to offer moderate‐risk HEART score patients rapid cardiology follow‐up to facilitate expedited evaluation and testing on an outpatient basis. In the current era of health care, there is increased attention to resource use and value in health care delivery, only underscored by the impact of the COVID‐19 pandemic on hospital resources and staffing. These data suggest a meaningful opportunity to safely redirect select patients from a resource‐intensive settings like the ED and inpatient wards to the outpatient setting, potentially decreasing unnecessary testing and admissions and thereby contributing to health care value without diminishing quality.

In conclusion, our study suggests that the early discharge for some moderate‐ risk patients presenting to the ED with chest pain may be reasonable if early cardiology follow‐up can be ensured. Further investigation to evaluate this hypothesis more fully is needed.
