Left Bundle Branch Area Antitachycardia Pacing Improves Success Rate Compared to Right Ventricular Antitachycardia Pacing
Authors: Emmanuel Offei, Yuki Ishidoya, Douglas Smego, Martha Sofia Ruiz Castillo, Muhammad S. Khan, Kyoichiro Yazaki, Ankur Shah, Ava Yektaeian Vaziri, Matthias Lange, Annie M. Hirahara, Hui Li, Gregory J. Stoddard, Ravi Ranjan, Derek J. Dosdall
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDAntitachycardia pacing (ATP) therapy applies a sequence of pacing pulses to terminate ventricular tachycardia (VT). Clinically, left bundle branch area (LBBA) pacing has emerged as a novel way to induce synchronous ventricular activation.OBJECTIVESThe main aim of this study was to compare the efficacy and safety of ATP delivered to an LBBA lead and a conventional right ventricular (RV) lead.METHODSUsing a preclinical animal model (n = 7), pacing leads were implanted in the RV apex and LBBA and connected to implantable cardioverter-defibrillators. The left anterior descending artery was occluded for 2 hours to cause an ischemia-reperfusion injury. Four days following ischemia-reperfusion injury, VT episodes were induced using programmed pacing, and burst ATP therapy was delivered to the RV/LBBA leads for each VT episode. Activation sequences from an implanted basket catheter were determined for the pretherapy VT and ATP beats.RESULTSVT was induced 80 times, with a mean VT cycle length of 180.0 ± 30.0 milliseconds. ATP delivered to the LBBA terminated VT more often than RV ATP (70.2% vs 47.3%; P = 0.040). There was no significant difference in VT acceleration or ventricular fibrillation induction. The number of ATP pulses required for the activation sequences to correlate with the captured ATP pattern rather than the pretherapy VT pattern was lower for LBBA compared to RV ATP (4.1 vs 5.1 pulses; P = 0.047).CONCLUSIONSImproved performance of LBBA compared to RV ATP provides an incentive for future clinical studies in patients who are at risk of sudden cardiac death.

Acute myocardial infarction may lead to tissue damage and scar formation that cause conduction abnormalities in cardiac tissue. Conduction heterogeneity and unidirectional block can lead to re-entrant activity and the onset of ventricular tachycardia (VT) and ventricular fibrillation (VF). Patients who are at high risk for sudden cardiac death may be fitted with an implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) to deliver antitachycardia pacing (ATP) to leads implanted in the right ventricle (RV), conventionally, for treating VTs. About 800,000 people have ICD implants in the United States alone, with roughly 150,000 new ICD implantations performed annually.1 If ATP fails to terminate VT, high-energy shocks are delivered from the device to restore sinus rhythm. These high-energy shocks may damage cardiac tissue,2 increase mortality,3 and intensify psychological distress, anxiety, and depression compared to patients who do not receive shocks.4 Whereas clinical studies with RV ATP protocols demonstrate ATP success rates of >80%, depending on the programming,5 the success rate for fast VTs with rates >188 beats/min has been reported to be lower (52%-72%).6

Conduction system pacing delivered to the left bundle branch area (LBBA) has been shown to improve ventricular synchronicity and shows a survival benefit compared to standard RV or biventricular pacing.7,8 In this study, we hypothesize that ATP delivered to the LBBA will lead to increased capture of the ventricular myocardium during VT and thus to improved ATP efficacy and safety outcomes compared to RV ATP.

METHODS

PROTOCOL APPROVAL.

The experimental protocol conforms to the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals9 and was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of Utah.

LEAD AND ICD IMPLANTATION.

Lead and device implantation, ischemia reperfusion injury, VT induction, and ATP delivery are described in the Supplemental Appendix. In brief, normal mongrel dogs (n = 7, 30.0 ± 2.1 kg, 1 year old) were implanted with ICDs with leads in the RV apex and the LBBA (Supplemental Figure 1). Ischemia reperfusion injury was caused, and 4 days later, VT was induced, and ATP testing was performed.10

DATA ANALYSIS.

Electrograms from the endocardial recordings during ATP delivery were analyzed using custom software developed in MATLAB (MathWorks Inc). Five consecutive pre-ATP VT beats were analyzed for each VT event (VT beats). The beats when the ATP pulses were applied were also analyzed (ATP beats). The activation time for each beat was determined for all the electrodes using an algorithm that selected the minimum temporal derivative obtained from the raw electrograms. The activation time was picked manually whenever the algorithm failed. The characteristic capture pattern for the LBBA and RV pacing in each animal was selected from the activation maps created using the activation times (Figures 1A and 1B).

Purkinje activations were identified using bipoles created by subtracting recordings from adjacent unipolar electrodes. As previously reported, Purkinje activations were identified based on their morphology, timing, and amplitude.11 Purkinje activations were identified manually and marked as rapid, short-duration activations (about 1-2 milliseconds). Time intervals from the end of the last ATP pulse to the earliest Purkinje and myocardial activations were measured for successful ATP trials (n = 44).

STATISTICAL ANALYSIS.

Success rates for terminating VT with ATP delivered from the RV and LBBA leads were compared using a mixed-effects logistic regression model with a cluster-robust SE in Stata 17.0 (StataCorp). This model accounted for repeated measures and varying numbers of trials in each animal. Primary and secondary ATP success rates were compared using a Fisher exact test.

The correlation coefficients between the activation sequences of the ATP beats (Figure 1C) and the activation sequences from the LBBA and RV capture patterns were calculated using Spearman rank correlation test in MATLAB, as previously published.12 The same analysis was performed between the ATP beats and the VT beat for each VT episode. A linear regression was fitted to the ATP and VT coefficients for each VT episode. The intersection point (termed crossover point) for the 2 lines of best fit was identified (Figure 1D). A crossover point of 1 was assigned for linear regression intersections <1, and a crossover point of 8 was assigned for the linear fits’ intersections of >8. A mixed-effects linear regression model using a restricted maximum likelihood with Satterthwaite degrees of freedom approximation was created in Stata to compare the slope of the ATP and VT coefficients linear regressions for the LBBA and RV ATP episodes. For quantification methods of inter- and intraobserver variability, see the Supplemental Appendix.

RESULTS

THERAPEUTIC EFFICACY OF ATP.

One of the 7 dogs enrolled in the study was excluded because of a failure to resuscitate the dog from VF during the first VT induction attempt in the terminal study. The QRS duration during LBBA capture was narrower than during RV capture (70.6 ± 8.2 milliseconds vs 111.0 ± 11.4 milliseconds; P = 4 × 10 −4). Eighty episodes of VT were induced, and 1 episode was eliminated from the analysis because competing ATP pulses were delivered from the RV and LBBA lead locations. The mean VT cycle length for the 79 (41 for LBBA vs 38 for RV) analyzed episodes was 180.0 ± 30.0 milliseconds, and the VT cycle length was not significantly different between the LBBA and RV ATP trials.

The resumption of sinus rhythm characterized successful events (Figure 2A), and unsuccessful events had ongoing VT after the last ATP pulse was applied (Figure 2B). ATP trials at the LBBA were more effective than ATP at the RV (70.2 ± 10.2% vs 47.3 ± 11.3%; risk ratio: 1.44; 95% CI: 1.03-2.01; P = 0.040) (Figure 2C). In this model, the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was 0.19 (95% CI: 0.08-0.38).

Incidence of successful ATP terminating VT with a single (primary success) or multiple (secondary success) extra beats and negative outcomes of VT acceleration and VF induction were not statistically different between LBBA and RV ATP (Supplemental Figure 2) (see the Supplemental Appendix).

MYOCARDIAL CAPTURE WITH ATP.

For this analysis, 70 (37 for LBBA vs 33 for RV) of the 79 endocardial recordings were used. Eight were excluded due to poor signal quality, which made it impossible to determine the activation sequence accurately. The excluded episodes included 3 treated with LBBA ATP and 5 treated with RV ATP, and were unremarkable except for the noise that led to the exclusion. One was excluded due to a technical error, because we had no recording of the pre-ATP VT and the ATP beat for the first ATP pulse. Mean ICC values for interobserver reliability and intrarater reliability were high (Supplemental Table 1) (0.92 and 0.93; see the Supplemental Appendix for additional detail).

For both pacing locations, ATP coefficients increased in correlation with the LBBA/RV capture pattern as more ATP beats were added (Figures 3A and 3B). In contrast, for both pacing sites, the VT coefficients decreased in correlation with the number of VT beats as more ATP pulses were delivered (Figures 3C and 3D). A statistically significant positive effect existed between the pacing site and the slope of the lines of best fit for the ATP coefficients. The crossover point marked the point in time after which the ATP coefficients became larger in magnitude than the VT coefficients. Generally, LBBA ATP had a crossover point, 1 full beat earlier than the crossover point for RV ATP (4.1 vs 5.1; P = 0.047; ICC: 0.20; 95% CI: 0.04-0.57) (Figure 4A). The crossover point for the overall ATP outcome was shorter for successful vs failed ATP (3.7 vs 5.6 beats; P = 2.0 × 10 −4; ICC: 0.18; 95% CI: 0.04-0.56) (Figure 4B). Additionally, for both pacing locations, the crossover point for all successful cases was shorter in duration than for unsuccessful ones. For the LBBA, that was 3.3 vs 5.8 beats (successful vs. failure; P = 0.003; ICC: 0.20; 95% CI: 0.11-0.76) (Figure 4C), whereas the crossover point for the RV was 4.0 vs 5.7 (successful vs. failure; P = 0.008;) (ICC: 1.2 × 10−11; 95% CI: 1.2 × 10−11 to 1.2 × 10 −11) (Figure 4D). The interaction between the pacing site and unsuccessful events showed no statistical significance (P = 0.713; (ICC: 6.3 × 10-15; 95% CI: 6.3 × 10-15 to 6.3 × 10-15) (Figure 4E). For successful ATP trials, LBBA ATP had a crossover point that was 0.87 beats earlier than RV ATP, but this trend did not achieve statistical significance (P = 0.087; (ICC: 0.33; 95% CI: = 0.08-0.74) (Figure 4F).

TIMING FOR EARLIEST PURKINJE FIBER AND WM ACTIVATION.

On average, Purkinje activations were detected in 20 of the 56 bipoles created per VT episode. The activations consistently preceded working myocardium (WM) activations during sinus rhythm and VT (Figures 5A and 5B). In successful ATP episodes, Purkinje activations were detected earlier for LBBA ATP than for RV ATP (18.8 ± 5.4 milliseconds vs 39.8 ± 5.6 milliseconds; P = 8.4 × 10−8; (ICC: 0.65; 95% CI: 0.27-0.90) (Figure 5C). Similarly, myocardial activations were seen earlier for LBBA ATP than for RV ATP (25.7 ± 5.1 milliseconds vs 38.7 ± 5.4 milliseconds; P = 9.0 × 10−4; (ICC: 0.53; 95% CI: 0.17-0.86) (Figure 5D). Also, Purkinje activations were detected earlier than myocardial activations more frequently for the LBBA ATP than for the RV ATP therapy (84.7 ± 10.7% vs 41.8 ± 8.3%; P = 0.002; ICC: 0.05; 95% CI: 8.9 × 10−4 to 0.79) (Figure 5E).

DISCUSSION

MAIN FINDINGS.

This study compared the safety and therapeutic efficacy of ATP delivered in the RV to the LBBA. The primary findings include 1) LBBA ATP terminated VT with a higher success rate compared to RV ATP; 2) negative outcome rates of VT acceleration and VF induction from unsuccessful ATP delivery were not significantly different between LBBA and RV ATP; 3) LBBA ATP disrupts the VT activation pattern and captures the activation sequence in fewer beats than RV ATP; and 4) successful ATP delivered to the LBBA had a shorter pacing to Purkinje activation interval than RV ATP, suggesting that LBBA ATP spreads through the conduction system. This work provides valuable evidence supporting the use of LBBA ATP in a relevant preclinical VT model (see Central Illustration).

THERAPEUTIC OUTCOMES OF ATP DELIVERY TO THE LBBA AND RV.

The higher therapeutic efficacy rate of LBBA ATP therapy is consistent with LBBA ATP activating the Purkinje network for more extensive and synchronous myocardial activation, leading to improved ATP success rates for LBBA compared to RV ATP.

Recent studies have demonstrated that an LBBA lead with a shocking coil can be safely and effectively used in patients, eliminating the need for a shocking coil in the RV.13 The infarct zone in the left anterior descending artery perfusion bed is immediately adjacent to the RV apical lead location, whereas the LBBA lead was implanted in the spared septal tissue. The proximity of ATP delivery location has been shown to be associated with increased ATP success rates.14 Therefore, any bias in the success rate due to the proximity of the ATP delivery site would be for the RV apical lead, which accentuates the significance of our findings.

CROSSOVER POINT AND MYOCARDIAL CAPTURE RATE AS MARKERS FOR VT TERMINATION.

The results on the dynamics of ATP and VT coefficients during ATP reveal differences in the influence pacing sites have on these coefficients, which sheds some light on the mechanisms that underlie the therapeutic efficacy of ATP. Capture of the WM during VT through the pacing of the LBBA engaged the conduction system, causing the WM to be physiologically captured. Capture of the WM during RV ATP is not as rapid because the pacing occurs outside the natural conduction system.8 The abnormal electrical activation progresses through the ventricular muscle, and it yields a delayed activation of the WM that is made evident by the prolonged QRS duration15 during RV ATP. Our findings agree with those in published reports, where better electrical synchrony and, thus, more rapid WM activation are reported for LBBA pacing compared to RV pacing.16,17

A key finding from the work was the identification of the crossover point, a marker of the time point at which the activation pattern was more similar to the ATP activation pattern than to the VT activation pattern. The results from the crossover point demonstrate that depolarization wave fronts spread rapidly from the LBBA to capture the WM. The earlier crossover point for LBBA ATP is consistent with the superior synchronized activation of the WM for ATP delivery to the LBBA.

The results on the crossover point for the therapeutic outcomes (successes and failures) of each pacing location suggest that an early resetting of the VT circuit may influence ATP success. The temporal dynamics of beat progression during ATP delivery may be an essential marker for determining the likelihood of pacing success. Fewer beats were required for resetting the VT circuit when ATP was successful than when ATP failed. Several studies have shown that pacing the LBBA provides superior electrical resynchronization compared to traditional RV and biventricular pacing.8,17,18

INSIGHTS ON THE DIRECT CAPTURE OF THE PURKINJE SYSTEM DURING ATP DELIVERY.

Our results on the temporal dynamics of Purkinje fiber and WM activations provide novel insights into the mechanisms that underlie the effectiveness of ATP and pinpoint the importance of the pacing site in achieving termination of VT. The earlier Purkinje activations reported for LBBA ATP compared to RV ATP demonstrate that LBBA pacing provides more efficient engagement of the Purkinje system, facilitating earlier disruption of the re-entrant circuit. The earlier WM activation for LBBA ATP compared to that for RV ATP reflects a more synchronized depolarization of the myocardium, which likely contributes to the superior performance of LBBA ATP in interrupting the VT circuit. Other groups have also reported early capture of the conduction system and the WM during the pacing of the LBBA. Zhu et al19 reported a stimulus to left ventricular activation time of 48.70 ± 13.67 milliseconds. In their work in a canine model, Chen et al20 also reported a pacing stimulus to left ventricular activation time of 39.67 ± 1.53 milliseconds and an LBB potential to ventricular activation interval of 12.67 ± 1.15 milliseconds during LBBA pacing. These results imply a rapid capture of the conduction system and the WM during LBBA pacing, which agrees with our findings.

Moreover, the results on the higher incidence of Purkinje activations preceding myocardial activations when ATP was delivered to the LBBA than to the RV underscore the preferential engagement of the Purkinje fibers during LBBA pacing and suggest that LBBA ATP exploits the native conduction system more effectively than RV ATP. By activating the Purkinje fibers first, LBBA ATP ensures a rapid and organized spread of electrical activity to the WM, crucial for disrupting re-entrant VT circuits. RV pacing initiates a slower, less synchronous myocardial activation sequence, which may delay VT termination or reduce the success of ATP therapy.21,22

STUDY LIMITATIONS.

First, all the VT episodes in the work were induced from programmed electrical stimulation, which is characteristically different from the spontaneous VTs in humans that are easier to terminate.23 However, the side-by-side comparison of LBBA and RV ATP locations overcomes this drawback. Also, the VT cycle length in dogs is shorter (with a narrow excitable gap) than that in typical human VT episodes, making them more difficult to break than in humans, which is another limitation. Therefore, the study’s outcome will likely differ in humans, with a significant likelihood of more successful episodes than reported in the current work. The canine Purkinje system exhibits a similar sub-endocardial distribution to that of human hearts, whereas porcine hearts have Purkinje fibers that extend transmurally through the working myocardium. However, other differences in our chosen model, such as the fast VT rate, immaturity of the ischemic reperfusion injury, extensive collateral circulation, and single, uniform infarction size and region, may lead to variation from the human population. Future work investigating the effects of autonomic tone, mature infarcts in heart failure models, and more rigorous mapping of the LBBA and conduction system during ATP is warranted.

CONCLUSIONS

This study demonstrated that delivering ATP to the LBBA is more efficacious at terminating VT events compared to when ATP is delivered to the conventional RV apical site. Unlike RV ATP, LBBA ATP directly captures the Purkinje system and activates more myocardial fibers in a timely and synchronous manner, leading to a greater tendency for VT termination. The improved performance of ATP delivered to the LBBA compared to the RV provides further incentive for employing LBBA leads in cardiac resynchronization therapy and ATP delivery.
