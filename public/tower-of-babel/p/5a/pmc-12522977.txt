Determinants of urogenital tract infection symptoms among adolescent girls and young women in secondary schools and universities in Bukavu, Democratic Republic of Congo: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Raha Maroyi, Chirimwami Matabaro Espoir, Ushindi Muzikwa Chrispin, Mupenda Ngolombe Gloire, Mamy Matumwabiri Rolande, Balagizi Ganywamulume, Lampard Omari Mukanga, Musafiri Cirholo, Denis Mukwege
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGlobally, 40% of adolescent girls and young women suffer from urogenital tract infections (UGTIs) that affect their quality of life (QOL). Despite their significant health burdens, these conditions remain underreported and understudied, particularly in Low and Middle-income Countries (LMICs)This study aimed to examine the determinants of urogenital infection-related symptoms in adolescent girls and young adult women students.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted among adolescent girls and young women in ten secondary and university institutions from December 2022 to April 2023 in Bukavu, Eastern DRC. Using purposive sampling, 1,500 questionnaires were distributed with 1,048 completed responses from 1,200 returns. The variables included age, education level, origin, knowledge of infections, hygiene behaviors (self-medication, contraception, water intake, and sanitation), and urogenital infection symptoms. A logistic regression model was used to examine predictors of urogenital infections using Stata SE14.0.ResultsA total of 1048 adolescent girls and young women, aged between 12 and 30 years with a mean age of 20 years, were enrolled in the study. The prevalence of related urogenital infection symptoms was 12% among school-going adolescent girls and young women in secondary schools and universities in Bukavu town, eastern Democratic Republic of Congo. Several factors were significantly associated, notably information about UGTIs and their symptoms (AOR: 4.52; 95% CI: 2.45–8.22; p < 0.001). The use of rain, ground, river or lake water while bathing increased the risk of UGTI symptoms (AOR: 4.08; 95% CI: 2.10–7.95; p < 0.001). Adding disinfectants to water reduced this risk but did not cancel it (AOR: 1.37; 95% CI: 1.10–2.05, p = 0.034). Communal bathing vessels (AOR: 1.85; 95% CI: 1.17–2.94, p = 0.008), the habit of wearing clothing that permanently tightens the perineum(AOR: 2.12; 95% CI: 1.14–3.90, p = 0.016), and antibiotic self-medication (AOR: 2.03; 95% CI: 1.36–3.02, p < 0.001) were associated with UGTI symptoms.ConclusionUrogenital tract infections (UGTIs) are common among adolescent girls and young women in educational settings in Bukavu, DRC. Symptoms were determined by a lack of information on UGTIs, women’s hygiene practices, self-medication, and lifestyle habits. Comprehensive health education overhaul is needed to effectively address public health concerns.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12905-025-04057-8.

Introduction

Urogenital tract infections (UGTIs) are common health concerns among adolescent girls and young women, presenting significant clinical and public health challenges. These infections often cause physical discomfort, emotional distress, and interference with daily activities [1]. Beyond their immediate effects, UGTIs are linked to long-term consequences, including persistent pain, reproductive health issues, and heightened vulnerability to additional infections, highlighting their broader implications for women’s health [2]. Despite their substantial impact, preventive strategies, especially in low-resource or underserved areas, frequently lack consistency, evidence-based methods, or focused interventions for this high-risk group [3].

Globally, an estimated 40% of adolescent girls and young women aged 15–25 years experience UGTIs [4, 5]; however, the precise prevalence of these conditions in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) remains significantly under-documented owing to limited surveillance systems and barriers to healthcare access [6]. Despite the scarcity of comprehensive studies on disease burden among adolescent girls in LMICs, existing evidence suggests an imbalance in the impact within these regions. The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that 80–90% of the global prevalence of UGTIs is found in LMICs, reflecting disparities in socioeconomic factors, such as sanitation infrastructure, sexual health education, and healthcare availability [7]. This imbalance underscores the urgent need for improved epidemiological monitoring and targeted public health initiatives in resource-limited settings to quantify and address women’s health issues more accurately.

Adolescent girls and young women face heightened vulnerability due to a combination of biological, social, and structural factors. Their increased susceptibility is partly attributable to distinct anatomical and hormonal features, which further exacerbate health risks, and lack of awareness and education concerning sexual health and hygiene practices exacerbates their susceptibility. Several factors have been implicated in the occurrence of UGTIs, including menstrual and sexual hygiene practices, socioeconomic status, and sociocultural background [1, 8–11]. Furthermore, in the absence of well-developed health policies, population resilience often results in widespread reliance on self-medication, which can have serious short- and long-term consequences for women [8].

Education is a pivotal element in advancing adolescent welfare, as delineated in the framework of Baird et al. [12], who identified a direct correlation between public policy measures and improved academic performance. Drawing from recent achievements and new challenges in adolescent well-being, further research is necessary to consistently adopt an intersectional approach. Moreover, it is crucial to take steps to solidify advancements in sexual and reproductive health and expand these improvements to other aspects of adolescent health [11]. A comprehensive understanding of the determinants of these behaviors is essential for developing effective prevention strategies and health education programs tailored to this vulnerable population [11].

The prevalence of UGTIs in the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC) is still unknown, highlighting a critical gap in data. The widespread practice of self-medication with antibiotics, even for minor suspected infections, has been extensively documented. This behavior poses a significant risk of accelerating antimicrobial resistance and exacerbating preventable health complications, necessitating urgent intervention [13]. This aligns with Sustainable Development Goal 3, which seeks to ensure good health and well-being, and Goal 4, which emphasizes quality education for all, as both are critical in promoting the health and empowerment of young women [14].

Access to safe drinking water remains a major public health issue in many provinces of the Democratic Republic of the DRC, especially in South-Kivu and the city of Bukavu. Although alarms are regularly sounded to find a solution to this public health threat, concrete results are slow to materialize [15, 16]. Among the feared consequences, urogenital tract infections are an urgent problem, owing to their long-term effects. If unrecognized and/or untreated, these infections can negatively affect sexual and reproductive health. This study aimed to investigate the determinants of symptoms related to UGTIs among adolescent girls and young adult women attending school in Bukavu, DRC.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a cross-sectional survey of 1048 adolescents and young women adults over a 4-month period from December 12, 2022, to April 15, 2023. This study followed the STROBE standards for reporting cross-sectional observational studies [16].

Setting

The study was conducted in ten educational institutions at secondary and university levels in three communes of the city of Bukavu (Ibanda, Kadutu, and Bagira) in the eastern Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC). Seven secondary schools and three universities were selected based on their accessibility to the survey, after sharing and approving the protocol by different administrations.

Ethical consideration

The protocol was approved by the National Ethics Committee for Health (CNES/DP-SK/199PP/2022) in compliance with the Helsinki Ethical Principles for Medical Research [17]. The parents’ committee was informed of the research and the questionnaire. This committee is a representative body of parents, established in secondary schools. Consent was obtained from all participants, and minor participants provided oral assent alongside guardian consent prior to the submission of the questionnaires.

Participants

The sample used in this study was purposive and non-exhaustive. Of the 1,500 questionnaires distributed, 1,048 were completed out of the 1,200 returned. The estimated response rate was 80%.

The inclusion criteria were unmarried adolescent girls (< 18 years) and young women adults (≥ 18 years) who were present at their respective educational institutions on the day of the study and who provided consent to participate. Those who did not consent to participate were not accepted for the study.

A total of 1,048 adolescent girls and young women adults students were surveyed. 867 (82.9%) participants were enrolled in secondary schools and 181 (17.1%) were enrolled in universities.

Variables

This study focused on several independent variables including age in years, level of education (secondary or university), origin (town or suburb), information on UGTIs (yes, no), intimate genital hygiene (intimate bath frequency per day:1, 2, + 3; private vessels for intimate bathing usage: yes, no; intimate hygiene using: type of water (potable, rain, ground, river or lake); antiseptic, intimate bath with water: alone, disinfectant); antibiotic self-medication (yes, no); post-defecation hygiene practices (use of water, use of hygienic papers); hormonal contraception (yes, no); daily habit of wearing clothing that constantly tightens the perineum (yes, no); location for drying lingerie (out of sunlight, under direct sunlight), regular daily water intake (yes, no).

The dependent variable was UGTI symptoms. The reported symptoms include: vulvar pruritus, micturia, pelvic pain, leukorrhea, and pollakiuria. This variable was dichotomized (yes or no), where ‘no’ indicated the absence of all symptoms and ‘yes’ indicated the presence of at least one symptom.

Self-reported responses to questions about the symptoms of UGTIs were used to assess the participants’ understanding. These reports were cross-verified against the descriptions of clinical signs consistent with such infections. However, no objective evidence of confirmation by biological testing has been obtained to confirm or refute their presence at the time of occurrence.

Data collection

The questionnaire used in this study was designed within the framework of the study, tested, and validated by independent gynecologists who were not part of the research team. The questionnaire was distributed to secondary schools and universities that approved this study. Four doctors specializing in community surveys, together with institutional authorities, visited the targeted classes and auditoriums to explain the study’s interests, distribute the questionnaires, and collect them after completion. This was a self-administered and unsupervised questionnaire. The time given to hand in the questionnaire varied from 24 to 72 h.

This study focused on several explanatory variables including age, level of education (secondary or university), origin (rural or urban), knowledge of urogenital infections, intimate genital hygiene, assistance (use of antibiotics, hormonal contraception, water cure, and sanitation), and clinical signs of urogenital infections.

The dependent variable was self-reported symptoms of urogenital infections: vulvar pruritus, mictalgia, pelvic pain, leukorrhea, and pollakiuria.

Self-reported responses to questions about the symptoms of UGTIs were used to assess participants’ understanding. These reports were cross-verified against the descriptions of clinical signs consistent with such infections. However, no objective evidence of confirmation by biological testing has been obtained to confirm or refute their presence at the time of occurrence.

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Stata SE 14.0 (StataCorp LP, College Station, TX, USA). Quantitative variables with symmetrical distributions are summarized as mean ± standard deviation, while asymmetrical distributions are reported as medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs). Qualitative variables were presented as frequencies.

For comparative analyses, Pearson’s chi-squared test was used to assess proportions, and Fisher’s exact test was applied when the expected cell frequencies were ≤ 5. We first conducted univariate logistic regression to compute crude odds ratios (CORs) with 95% confidence intervals, retaining variables associated with self-reported symptoms of urogenital infections (p < 0.20) for further analysis. These candidate variables were subsequently included in a multivariable logistic regression model following the methodological approach described by CG Victoria, Sr Huttly, SC Fucks et M Olinto [18]. The final model provided adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals, demonstrating the strength of the association between independent variables and the outcome. A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

Results

According to the general distribution (Table 1), more than two-thirds of the participants (n = 732, 69.8%) were aged between 16 and 20 years with a mean age of 20.0 ± 3.2 years. The majority of respondents were from town (n = 625, 59.6%), and most were secondary school students (n = 867, 82.7%). In the current survey, over half of the participants (n = 717, 68.4%) reported having previously received information about UGTIs and their symptoms. 12% (n = 126/1048) of participants reported recognizing symptoms related to UGTIs (Fig. 1). The various symptoms reported included vulvar pruritus (n = 82/126, 64.1%), leukorrhea (n = 22/126, 17.5%), mictalgia (n = 14/126, 11.1%), pelvic pain (n = 7/126, 5.6%), and pollakiuria (n = 1/126, 0.8%) (Table 1).

We examined the association between participants’ characteristics and the reporting of urogenital tract infection symptoms and found a statistically significant correlation between the information and the reporting of UGTI symptoms (p < 0.001). The exclusive use of water during intimate bathing was significantly associated with the reporting of UGTIs (p < 0.001). The use of a mixture of disinfectants and water was also associated with the reporting of UGTIs (p = 0.028). Not using private vessels for intimate bathing was significantly related to the reporting of UGTIs (p = 0.011).

Post-defecation hygiene practices were related to the reporting of symptoms of UGTIs (p = 0.029). Wearing clothing that constantly tightens the perineum daily was also related to reporting UGTIs (p = 0.039). Participants who wore tight-fitting clothes (pants and/or underwear) many times were at a higher risk. Antibiotic self-medication was also linked to reporting UGTI symptoms (p < 0.001), as was the use of sanitary pads (p < 0.001) (Table 2).

Logistic regression enabled us to identify the factors associated with reporting UGTIs. There was no significant difference in reporting UGTIs between age groups, with p-values greater than 0.05 for each age group. Having information about UGTIs increased the risk of reporting them by 4.52 times (AOR: 4.52; 95% CI: 2.45–8.22; p < 0.001) compared to not having information about them. The use of water while bathing increased the risk of reporting UGTI symptoms by 4.08 times more than antiseptic use (AOR: 4.08; 95% CI: 2.10–7.95; p < 0.001). Adding disinfectants to water reduces this risk but does not eliminate it (AOR: 1.37; 95% CI: 1.10–2.05, p = 0.034). Not using private vessels increases the risk by 1.85 times (AOR: 1.85, 95% CI: 1.17–2.94, p = 0.008); wearing tight clothing around the perineum increases the risk by 2.12 times (95% CI: 1.14–3.90, p = 0.016); and antibiotic self-medication increases the risk by 2.03 times (95% CI: 1.36–3.02, p < 0.001) (Table 3).

Discussion

In this study of urogenital infections in adolescent girls and young women in the city of Bukavu, various factors were found to be associated with the condition, including a lack of knowledge about women’s UGTIs, certain habits such as the use of antiseptics during genital hygiene, antibiotic self-medication, non-use of private vessels for intimate bathing, and wearing clothing that permanently tightens the perineum.

A recent study showed that UGTIs in young women are a global concern [19]. Understandably, the proximity of the genital, genitourinary, and digestive systems, which is associated with cyclical physiological changes, is conducive to infection. Thus, several extrinsic influences were included.

Prevalence of urogenital tract infection symptoms

This study showed a prevalence of 12% of symptoms related to UGTIs among adolescent and young women in secondary schools and universities in urban areas. All of them were single. Regardless of the environment, the risk of contracting infections among teenage girls is high; therefore, information sessions are important based on participants’ self-reports, indicating that this clinical problem requires special attention [20]. A study in Kenya showed an elevated prevalence of 28% of adolescent rural schoolgirls aged 14–17 years who reported one or more reproductive tract infection (RTIs) symptoms with laboratory test confirmation [6]. In South Africa, 70.2% of young women had at least one laboratory-confirmed genital tract infection, although only 16.2% reported experiencing symptoms [21]. These studies show a disparity between subjective prevalence and objectivized prevalence by confirmatory tests, which demonstrates the importance of the problem.

Determinants of urogenital tract infection symptoms

Approximately 50% of women will have at least one UGTI in their lifetime, and despite antibiotic treatment, 20–30% of women with an initial UGTI will have a recurrent UGTI within 3–4 months of the initial infection [19]. Recurrent UGTIs have a considerable influence on health-related quality of life (QoL) and pose a health and economic burden [19], resulting in short-, medium-, and long-term consequences for women’s lives.

The respondents reported various signs of urogenital infections, including vulvar pruritus (n = 88, 64.3%), leucorrhea, mictalgia, pelvic pain, and pollakiuria. Adolescent girls and young adult women health is a critical aspect of public health that requires greater attention and integration into health policy. As this age group navigates the transition from childhood to adulthood, they face unique physical, emotional, and social challenges that have long-term implications for their wellbeing. Despite the importance of addressing specific health needs, these issues are often overlooked or insufficiently prioritized in existing health policies [22].

Our study identified several factors that contributed to the symptoms of UGTIs. Although the study population had a high general education level, lack of specific knowledge about UGTIs was a significant risk factor, increasing the likelihood of reporting UGTI symptoms by 4.52 times [AOR = 4.52 (95% CI: 2.45–8.22), p < 0.001] compared with ignorance of UGTIs; the use of water during intimate bathing increases the risk of reporting UGTI symptoms to 4.08 times more compared to the use of antiseptics [AOR: 4.08 (95% CI: 2.10–7.95), p < 0.001], the addition of additional antiseptics in the water does not cancel this risk but reduces it to 1.37 [AOR: 1.37 (95% CI: 1.10–2.05), p = 0.034]. From a logical perspective, the use of antiseptics exposes women to infections through the destabilization of the vaginal flora. This raises questions regarding the quality of the water used and how this population practices intimate hygiene. However, access to drinking water is a recurring problem for households in the city of Bukavu because water is mainly supplied by a single distribution company. The company’s sole supply source is located a few kilometers from the city and has become insufficient to serve the entire population due to the city’s rapid growth [23]. People sometimes resort to collecting rainwater or using river water to meet their needs. However, river water is sometimes polluted by sewage discharged upstream by households [23]. On the other hand, the population resorts to practices such as using groundwater, commonly known as “bizola” in Panzi, a peripheral part of the Ibanda municipality in Bukavu. This is a major cause of infant mortality and is linked to the use and consumption of groundwater. Another practice is to use saltwater from Lake Kivu in Nguba, in the city center, where social infrastructure for access to water is almost nonexistent [24]. The local media often reports this shortage, which is yet to be resolved. Consequences include waterborne diseases and urogenital tract infections [15, 16]. It is imperative to find an urgent solution to this societal problem, which endangers the health of the population.

University level was more strongly associated with the development and reporting of infections. This finding highlights the importance of increasing awareness and providing information. This is consistent with previous observations in Haiti on the lack of sexual and reproductive health education as responsible for health consequences and poor quality of life among young women in LICs [25]. In addition, a comprehensive review showed that in our century, certain barriers to accessing information have also been broken down by the media, especially among people with a certain level of education [26].

In particular, we found that general lifestyle factors, such as the non-use of private vessels, were independently associated with significantly increased odds of the outcome [AOR: 1.85 (95% CI:1.17–2.94), p = 0.008] relative to private vessel users; wearing clothing that constantly tightens the perineum increases the risk to 2.12 [AOR: 2.12 (95% CI: 1.14–3.90), p = 0.016], and antibiotic self-medication increased the risk to 2.03 [AOR: 2.03 (95% CI: 1.36–3.02; p < 0.001].

For adolescent girls and young women in low- and middle-income countries, menstrual health education is a real issue for adolescents girls and young women adults and an important factor that affects their health [25, 27, 28]. This finding highlights the importance of promoting awareness among young women in order to avoid consequences in adulthood [2]. On the eve of 2030, the World Health Organization (WHO) set a goal of ending sexually transmitted infection epidemics. Thus, new strategies propose a shared vision of eradicating epidemics and advancing universal health coverage, primary healthcare, and health security in the future, where everyone has access to high-quality, evidence-based, and people-centered healthcare [14]. A recent study proposed that a greater understanding of correct practices would enable women to be champions of their own intimate health [29].

The literature clearly indicates that general lifestyle factors, such as wearing tight-fitting garments, can increase the risk of urogenital tract infections [30]. In addition, the interdependence of perineal and vulvar microbiota, as well as the possibility of intestinal pathogens reaching the vulva and vagina, highlights the importance of perineal health. Intimate hygiene practices can vary greatly worldwide and among different age groups, and suboptimal habits, such as vaginal douching or the use of specific cleansers, might be linked to an increased risk of vulvar and vaginal disorders [29]. Bacterial vaginosis is of emerging importance because it reflects the dysbiotic state of the vaginal microbiome and increases susceptibility to other sexually transmitted infections (STIs) [31]. Therefore, the practice of antiseptic use among women is influenced by misinformation regarding menstruation. The menstrual cycle requires further study in the context of limited resources [32].

Self-medication with antibiotics by adolescent girls and young women was another issue in this study. This attitude is a factor in the dysbiotic state of the vaginal microbiome, leading to multidrug resistance, especially in conditions where sanitary standards are not respected. Antibioresistance to self-medication is a global problem [33].

Our research suggests that there is a substantial risk associated with this practice. Zeinabou et al. has conducted a clinical study on urinary tract infections in which 20% of positive confirmation tests had negative cultures due to extensive self-medication [34].

The rising prevalence of self-medication among youth is associated with greater familiarity with common medications, raising concerns about medication regulation [35].

This can be partly explained by the fact that many LICs have poorly regulated antibiotic prescription rules. It is also aggravated by the existence of an informal circuit for selling low-quality, inexpensive medicines that facilitate self-medication [36]. This dangerous practice underscores the importance of educating young adolescent girls about the health risks of antibiotic misuse.

These results have implications for the health of adolescent girls and young women adults in educational settings. The risk factors noted imply an increased awareness. Educational institutions should do more to make them understand that correct intimate care may contribute to improved genital and sexual health and overall well-being [29]. Early action in this population will help avoid public health consequences linked to urogenital infections, including PID, infertility, ectopic pregnancies, prematurity, and neonatal deaths, beyond the risk of adverse psychosocial and educational outcomes [37].

The WHO’s global health plan for STI control and the tool for boosting surveillance focus on four critical elements: case reporting, prevalence assessment, etiology, and antimicrobial resistance monitoring. Global STI control and prevention will help meet the sustainable development goals of universal access to sexual and reproductive healthcare services [14]. In addition, the of Baird at [12] al calls ultimately for transformative public policies that can significantly enhance the well-being of adolescents globally.

The results of this study represent the first step in a targeted fight against UGTIs. These findings have implications that can be applied at several levels. Notably, in-depth hygiene education is a pillar of life education at the school level. Second, education on the dangers of antibiotic misuse is urgently needed given the trend toward normalizing this dangerous practice. It is imperative to raise awareness among adolescent girls, young women, adolescents, and adults about the importance of using a private vessel for intimate bathing, wearing loose-fitting clothing, and using antiseptics in water. These results also constitute a milestone in which future research can verify the various hypotheses explored in this study. The results also provide a foundation for public health policy decisions and guidance for policies aimed at improving the health of adolescent girls and young women.

Limitations

The reported prevalence of UGTI symptoms may underestimate the actual extent of the problem, given the study’s non-biological approach. Therefore, biological confirmation is important to provide a true picture of UGTIs compared to the symptoms reported in the study population. Further research is needed to ascertain the attitudes, knowledge, and in-depth practice of menstrual hygiene.

Conclusion

This study conducted among adolescent girls and young adult women attending school in Bukavu highlights the issue of urogenital tract infections. The reported symptoms are determined by a lack of prior information about these infections, which appears to be a major contributing factor to symptom recognition.

Additionally, intimate hygiene practices and frequent wearing of tight clothing are identified as independent risk factors associated with the onset of symptoms. Self-medication with antibiotics raises concerns about the unregulated management of infections and the risk of bacterial resistance.

These results suggest the need for research based on biological screening in this population. Enhancing health education campaigns and initiatives to promote intimate hygiene practices are necessary to mitigate the health consequences of these conditions among adolescent girls and young women in educational settings.
