Green flame-retardant rigid polyurethane foam with cobalt hydroxystannate to improve the thermal stability, flame retardancy and smoke suppression properties
Authors: Xu Zhang, Zhaoqian Wang, Simiao Sun, Zhi Wang, Hua Xie
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe ecological benefits and concerns surrounding fossil fuels had led to increased interest in bio-based rigid polyurethane foam (RPUF). Nonetheless, due to its flammability, it had limited application in various fields. To solve this problem, a green bio-flame retardant, cobalt hydroxystannate (CoSn(OH)6), was prepared and compounded with montmorillonite (MMT) and chick feather protein (CF), and applied to RPUF, which not only realized the regeneration of resources, but also provided RPUF with better thermal stability, flame retardancy and smoke suppression properties. The experimental results showed that when 3 wt% CoSn(OH)6 was added, the RPUF (CF1/MMT3/Co3) had the greatest activation energy. In addition, the peak heat release rate (PHRR) and total heat release (THR) of CF1/MMT3/Co3 decreased by 12.73%, and 11.16% respectively, compared with no CoSn(OH)6. In addition, its Ds decreased by 28.9% and the light transmittance increased by 17.6% compared with the RPUF without CoSn(OH)6. At the same time, its peak smoke production rate (PSPR) and the total smoke release (TSR) decreased by 25% and 18%. And CF1/MMT3/Co3 also had the lowest fire risk evaluation index. This study presented possibilities for practical utilization of the RPUF substances founded on bio-based flame inhibitors.Keywords: Cobalt hydroxystannate, Rigid polyurethane foam, Thermal stability, Flame retardancy, Smoke toxicitySubject terms: Polymers; Design, synthesis and processing

Introduction

Rigid polyurethane foams (RPUFs) are popular in many fields because they have low electrical conductivity, high compressive strength, are lightweight, and are easy to prepare1–3. However, RPUFs had two fatal drawbacks. Firstly, due to their organic backbone and porous structure, they could release a significant amount of heat immediately upon ignition. Secondly, RPUFs produce large amounts of smoke and toxic gases when burned, causing significant environmental pollution4,5. Extensive research is imperative for the development and utilization of bio-based and halogen-free flame retardants to enhance the thermal stability, flame retardancy and smoke suppression performance of RPUFs while also considering the concepts of green environmental protection and sustainable development6–8. Long-lasting fire resistance can be facilitated by chemically adding fire-resistant elements to RPUF, such as silicon, nitrogen, or phosphorus9–11.

In order to protect the environment and conserve non-renewable resources, some efficient green flame retardants have been developed for flame retardant polymer materials. For example, graphene12,13 and nanoporous carbon14 are examples of flame retardants that can effectively improve the flame retardancy of polymer materials. Meanwhile, some biomass flame retardants and inorganic flame retardants have also attracted attention. For example, the biomass flame retardant, chicken feather protein (CF)15. It is a protein flame retardant composed of amino acids, and its structure contains a large number of C, N, H, O and S elements, so that non-combustible gases, such as N2, CO2, SO2 and H2O, may be produced during the combustion process16. These gases dilute the oxygen concentration around the substance, which inhibits the intensity of the combustion reaction and retards the flame spread. Montmorillonite (MMT) is the best representative of inorganic flame retardants because it is not only rich in Si elements, which improves the quality and stability of the carbon layer during combustion, but also produces less smoke and toxic substances during combustion, which is good for the environment17. It has been found that these flame retardants are more effective when used together with other flame retardants18,19.

In addition, with the continuous maturity of science and technology, the use of transition metal ions and tin inorganic compounds to prepare highly efficient, non-toxic and environmentally friendly inorganic flame retardants has also received great attention from flame retardant researchers. Xu et al. prepared a cobalt-zeolitic imidazolate framework-modified graphene (Co-ZIF-L@RGO) hybrid to study the effects of their interaction with intumescent flame retardants (IFR) on the flame retardant and thermal properties of thermoplastic polyurethane (TPU). The outcomes demonstrated that when a flame retardant with a mass fraction of 30% was added and the mass ratio of IFR to Co-ZIF-L/RGO was 27:3, the peak value of CO generation rate decreased from 0.0220 to 0.0042 g/s, and the suppression of toxic gases and smoke improved visibility and escape probability in fire20. Shi et al. prepared TPU/g-C3N4/CuCo2O4 nanometer composite materials by melt blending. It was found that when the mass ratio of g-C3N4/CuCo2O4 was 93:7 and the addition amount was 2%, the flame retardant effect was the best, and HRR and THR were 37.0% and 31.3% lower than those of pure TPU, respectively. In addition, the amount of combustible volatiles and CO produced was significantly reduced, while CO2 was increased21. Liu et al. prepared spherical cobalt hydroxystannate (CoSn(OH)6) and studied its flame retardant effect on flexible PVC. Compared to pure PVC, the LOI value of the 10 phr CoSn(OH)6-treated polyvinyl chloride sample (PVC/10) increased by 4%, and the THR and TSR volumes decreased by 18% and 50%, respectively22.

Zhang et al. modified flexible polyurethane foam (FPUF) with strontium stannate (SrSnO3) and APP. The outcomes demonstrated that the use of synergistic flame retardants successfully reduced the quantity of smoke produced following the addition of APP. The flame retardant and smoke suppression effects of FPUF could be successfully improved by the synergy of SrSnO3 and APP. The greatest results were obtained when SrSnO3 was at 5 wt% and APP was at 20 wt%23. Yu et al. prepared cerium stannate modified graphene nanosheets (GNS@Ce2Sn2O7, GCSO) and applied them to flame-retardant flexible PUF (FPUF) composites. The overall heat release and total smoke emission of FPUF composites were 42.2% and 75.1% lower, respectively, than those of pure FPUF. Furthermore, during combustion, harmful gases such CO2, CO, and NOx were greatly decreased. The GCSO's catalytic and barrier activities were attributed to these results, as they encouraged the development of high-quality residual carbon with thick, tiny and complete shapes24. Gao et al. prepared spherical nickel hydroxystannate (NiSn(OH)6, NHS) particles and added them to a polypropylene (PP) matrix along with pentaerythritol (PER) and expandable graphite (EG). The results showed that the PP composites (PP/2PER/28EG/2NHS) with 2phr PER, 28 phr EG and 2 phr NHS had passed the V-0 grade of UL-94, and 2PER/28EG/2NHS could promote the early thermal degradation of PP, slowed down the thermal degradation rate of PP, and improved the carbon-forming rate25. Sheng et al. prepared magnesium hydroxystannate [MgSn(OH)6, MgHS] and applied it to an intumescent flame-retardant polypropylene system. The addition of MgHS significantly reduced the generation of CO and improved the flame retardancy and thermal stability of the intumescent flame-retardant polypropylene system26.

Hydroxystannate had been used in flame retardant, such as zinc hydroxystannate and copper hydroxystannate, and had good flame retardant smoke suppression. Since Co, Cu and Zn elements were located in the same cycle and had similar electronic structures, it was speculated that CoSn(OH)6 also had a good flame retardant smoke suppression effect. There was less research on CoSn(OH)6 in the field of flame-retardant RPUFs. Additionally, based on previous research19, MMT and CF have good synergistic flame retardant effects. Thus, in accordance with the idea of green environmental protection, CoSn(OH)6 was synthesized and applied to the RPUF, enhancing its thermal stability, flame retardancy, and smoke toxicity in conjunction with CF and MMT.

Preparation of CoSn(OH)6

Figure 1 showed the flow chart of CoSn(OH)6 preparation. 100 ml of 0.10 mol/L CoSO4 solution was slowly added to the same volume of Na2SnO3 solution at the same concentration and rapidly mechanically stirred at 25 °C. After mixing, the mixed solution continued to be stirred for 1 h. The solution was poured into the high-pressure reactor and reacted at 100 °C for 6 h to obtain a pink precipitate. After filtering the pellet, the pellet was washed several times with deionized water and absolute ethanol until the pH value was neutral, and then dried in a vacuum oven at 80 °C for 12 h to finally obtain the sample CoSn(OH)6.

CoSn(OH)6 and RPUF preparation flow chart.

Preparation of flame retardant RPUF

Figure 1 showed the flow chart of RPUF preparation. Polyether polyol, chicken feather protein, MMT, CoSn(OH)6, foam stabilizer silicone oil, dibutyltin diformate, triethanolamine and deionized water were poured into a paper cup and mixed with an electric mixer to form a mixture. After adding the isocyanate to the cup, the liquid was vigorously mixed in an electric mixer for 7–11 s to cause foaming. To finish the foaming process, the resultant mixture was poured into the mold. In the current work, the "one-step method" of all-water foaming was used to prepare the modified RPUFs. Finally, CF1/MMT3/Co1, CF1/MMT3/Co2, CF1/MMT3/Co3, CF1/MMT3/Co4 and CF1/MMT3/Co5 containing CoSn(OH)6 (1 wt%, 2 wt%, 3 wt%, 4 wt% and 5 wt%), 2.5 wt% chicken feather protein and 3 wt% MMT were obtained.

Measurements and characterization

The crystal structure of CoSn(OH)6 were determined using an X-ray diffractometer (Smartlab 9 kW, Rigaku, Japan). The samples were tested with a scanning angle of 5°–90° and a scanning speed of 5°/min. The functional groups of CoSn(OH)6 were detected using Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (Thermo Fisher Scientific IS5). The scanning wavelength was 400–4000 cm−1. The thermal stability of different RPUFs at four heating rates (5, 10, 20 and 40 °C/min) was characterized by the DTG-60AH thermogravimetric (TG) analyzer. The condition was N2 and the mass was 3–5 mg. The temperature range was 30–800 °C. The flame retardant properties of two different radiation intensities (35 and 50 kW/m2) were studied by CONE calorimetry. The test time was 600 s and the sample size was 100 mm × 100 mm × 10 mm. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM, S4800, Hitachi, Japan) was used to observe the microscopic morphology of the RPUFs combustion residues. FTT smoke density chambers were used to characterize different RPUFs under flamed and flameless conditions, respectively. The temperature was 902 °C and the test time was 1200 s. The sample size was 70 mm × 70 mm × 10 mm.

Thermal weight loss analysis

Figure 4 showed the thermogravimetric analysis of five sample materials in a nitrogen atmosphere, and the data results were listed in Table S1. There were three phases to the RPUFs' pyrolysis process. The initial step involved the breakdown of the polyurethane molecular chain's hard portion. A significant number of polyols started to break down, releasing gaseous materials such as CO, CO2 and water vapor, and the quality loss was quickly stopped. The deterioration of its soft portion was the second stage. Mass loss persisted although relatively stable tiny molecule chains broke and disintegrated. The breakdown of a tiny quantity of residue was the third stage43,44. The initial phase of deterioration was 0–330 °C, and the stages of deterioration two and three were 330–630 °C and 630–800 °C, respectively. The initial decomposition temperatures of CF1/MMT3/Co1, CF1/MMT3/Co2, CF1/MMT3/Co3, CF1/MMT3/Co4 and CF1/MMT3/Co5 were 205.95 °C, 206.86 °C, 210.93 °C, 207.27 °C and 204.51 °C, respectively. The terminal temperatures of CF1/MMT3/Co1, CF1/MMT3/Co2, CF1/MMT3/Co3, CF1/MMT3/Co4 and CF1/MMT3/Co5 were 585.00 °C, 587.10 °C, 639.48 °C, 632.42 °C and 617.38 °C, respectively. By comparison, CF1/MMT3/Co3 had the maximum initial breakdown and termination temperatures Compared with RPUF-CF1/MMT319, the T5% of CF1/MMT3/Co3 decreased to 250.76 °C and the TP decreased to 328.05 °C. This was mainly due to the acidic substances produced by the decomposition of CoSn(OH)6 which encouraged the molecular chains of polyurethane to break down. This indicated that CoSn(OH)6 promoted the degradation of the composite material in the initial phase. The T50% of CF1/MMT3/Co3 was 352.37 °C, which was significantly higher than that of RPUF-CF1/MMT3 (345.25 °C)19, indicating that the thermal stability of CF1/MMT3/Co3 was high. At 800 °C, the carbon-like residue of CF1/MMT3/Co3 was 24.62%, which was significantly higher than that of RPUF-CF1/MMT3 at 23.91%. The above results showed that when 3 wt% CoSn(OH)6 was added, the RPUF composites' thermal stability at the elevated temperature was enhanced. From Fig. 4, the pyrolysis curves of the five RPUFs were seen to proceed in tandem into the area of high temperatures. The delayed heat transmission of the modified RPUFs was the source of this phenomenon. The main reason for the delay in heat transfer was the unique structure of hydroxybenzoates, the incorporation of hydroxybenzoates enhanced the thermal stability of the macromolecular chains within the matrix in RPUF, and therefore required a long period of high temperature for complete decomposition25. If the heating rate was too fast, the heat cannot be rapidly conducted to the inside of RPUF, which will lead to the incomplete decomposition of the RPUF. Therefore, in order to completely decompose RPUF, a higher heating temperature was required. In addition, the carbon residues of CF1/MMT3/Co3 were 23.15%, 23.1% and 21.91%, respectively, at heating rates of 10, 20 and 40 °C/min. Compared with RPUF-CF1/MMT319, the carbon residues increased by 1.67%, 0.03% and 0.5%, respectively. The temperatures of the initial thermal mass loss of CF1/MMT3/Co3 were 226.53 °C, 224.20 °C and 229.02 °C, respectively, at heating rates of 10, 20 and 40 °C/min. The terminal temperatures of CF1/MMT3/Co3 were 686.15 °C, 743.39 °C and 769.27 °C, respectively. In summary, the initial thermogravimetric temperature and terminal temperature of CF1/MMT3/Co3 were the highest.

At a temperature rate of 5 °C/min, the IPDT values of CF1/MMT3/Co1, CF1/MMT3/Co2, CF1/MMT3/Co3, CF1/MMT3/Co4 and CF1/MMT3/Co5 were 718.23 °C, 677.52 °C, 779.06 °C, 761.15 °C and 743.38 °C, respectively. In contrast, CF1/MMT3/Co3 had the maximum values of IPDT, evidencing that CF1/MMT3/Co3 was the most thermally stable when heated at a rate of 5 °C/min. In addition, compared with the IPDT value of RPUF-CF1/MMT3 (777.71 °C)19, the RPUF's IPDT value after adding 3 wt% CoSn(OH)6 increased by 1.35 °C. This also proved that the addition of 3 wt% CoSn(OH)6 in the RPUF-CF1/MMT3 system had some impact in enhancing the sample's thermal stability. At heating rates of 10, 20, and 40 °C/min, it was discovered that CF1/MMT3/Co3 likewise had the highest thermal stability by comparing the IPDT values of the modified RPUF. At the same time, compared with the IPDT of RPUF-CF1/MMT3 (720.71, 763.44 and 734.65 °C)19, the IPDT of CF1/MMT3/Co3 increased by 39.72 °C, 1.14 °C and 11.07 °C, respectively, at the three heating rates, after the addition of 3 wt% CoSn(OH)6.

Compared with the T5% of RPUF-CF1/MMT3 (278.40 °C, 298.11 °C and 324.76 °C)19, the T5% of CF1/MMT3/Co3 decreased to 265.18 °C, 280.37 °C and 294.35 °C, respectively. Compared with the TP of RPUF-CF1/MMT3 (353.03 °C, 367.72 °C and 390.27 °C)19, the Tp of CF1/MMT3/Co3 decreased to 343.92 °C, 360.35 °C and 381.78 °C, respectively. This suggested that during the initial test phase, CoSn(OH)6 encouraged the deterioration of composites. However, the T50% of CF1/MMT3/Co3 increased to 367.09 °C, 395.42 °C and 416.14 °C compared with the T50% of RPUF-CF1/MMT3 (359.71 °C, 385.35 °C and 412.98 °C)19, respectively. The enhanced thermal stability of the modified composites was demonstrated by the results. This was because transition metal Co ions had the effect of catalytic carbonization and could generate a carbon-based inorganic layer to prevent combustion. At 800 °C, the residual carbon content of CF1/MMT3/Co3 was 23.15 wt%, 23.1 wt% and 21.91 wt%, which was significantly increased compared with 21.48 wt%, 23.07 wt% and 21.41 wt% of RPUF-CF1/MMT3. The aforementioned findings demonstrated that the system's synergistic impact following the addition of CoSn(OH)6 enhanced the composites' thermal stability at elevated temperatures.

Pyrolysis kinetics analysis

The improved RPUFs' pyrolysis kinetic properties using the Flynn–Wall–Ozawa technique at different conversion rates were compiled in Table S3. As can be seen from Table S3, the E of the five modified RPUFs was 134.30, 135.44, 138.14, 136.06 and 135.76 kJ/mol. E of the five modified RPUFs was 0.95, 2.09, 4.79, 2.71 and 2.41 kJ/mol higher than RPUF-CF1/MMT3 (133.35 kJ/mol)19, respectively. This demonstrated that adding CoSn(OH)6 increased the material's thermal stability. The material's thermal stability was greatest when 3 wt% of CoSn(OH)6 was added. In addition, CF1/MMT3/Co3 was 0.67, 0.47, 1.54, 2.01 and 1.62 kJ/mol lower than RPUF-CF1/MMT3 when conversion rates were 10%, 20%, 30%, 40% and 50%. When conversion rates were 60%, 70%, 80% and 90%, CF1/MMT3/Co3 was 2.83 kJ/mol, 11.03 kJ/mol, 27.42 kJ/mol and 8.09 kJ/mol higher than RPUF-CF1/MMT319. This was consistent with the results of the thermogravimetric analyses, which showed that CoSn(OH)6 promoted the decomposition of the RPUFs in the initial phases of combustion. As the combustion progressed, CoSn(OH)6 catalyzed the formation of a dense protective layer of carbon, which in turn inhibited further decomposition of the combustible material.

The E of the modified RPUFs utilizing the Kissinger method were given in Table S4. The highest E of CF1/MMT3/Co3 was found to be 138.39 kJ/mol in Table S4. This indicated that CF1/MMT3/Co3 had the best thermal stability in the improved RPUF, which was in line with the estimations from the prior technique. Following the addition of CoSn(OH)6, the sample's E rose by 3.19, 3.72, 4.89, 2.29 and 1.58 kJ/mol in comparison to the E of RPUF-CF1/MMT3 determined using the present approach (133.50 kJ/mol), respectively.

Table S5 provided the E of the RPUFs utilizing the Starink technique at various conversion rates. It is possible to see from Table S5 that the E of CF1/MMT3/Co3 was 135.24 kJ/mol and the highest E among the five RPUFs. It could also be seen that when the conversion rate reached more than 60%, CF1/MMT3/Co3 increased significantly, which also proved the conclusion of the previous method. Compared with RPUF-CF1/MMT3 (130.01 kJ/mol)19, E of the five modified RPUFs increased by 1.36, 2.02, 5.23, 2.76 and 0.51 kJ/mol, respectively. The present E calculations were in agreement with the thermogravimetric analyses which concluded that the best thermal stability was achieved with the addition of 3 wt% CoSn(OH)6 to RPUF-CF1/MMT3.

CONE analysis

Figures 5, 6 and Table S6 displayed the modified RPUFs' heat release rate (HRR) and total heat release (THR) curves under 35 and 50 kW/m2, respectively. From Figs. 5, 6, it could be seen that RPUFs demonstrated the normal organic material combustion characteristics, with just one HRR observed during the whole combustion process. At a thermal radiation intensity of 35 kW/m2, the peak HRR (PHRR) of the five modified materials was 34.24, 36.40, 27.96, 34.58 and 34.13 kW/m2, respectively. The THR of the five modified materials was 2.04, 2.05, 1.91, 2.10 and 2.08 MJ/m2, respectively. It was preliminarily judged that the flame retardant effect of CF1/MMT3/Co3 was the better. In addition, under the condition of thermal radiation of 35 kW/m2, the PHRR and THR of CF1/MMT3/Co3 decreased by 4.08 kW/m2 and 0.24 MJ/m2, respectively, compared with the PHRR and THR of RPUF-CF1/MMT3 (32.04 kW/m2, 2.15 MJ/m2)19. THR was reduced for all five modified samples with CoSn(OH)6 addition. Compared with RPUF-CF1/MMT3, the THR of the five modified samples decreased by 0.11, 0.1, 0.24, 0.05 and 0.07 MJ/m2, respectively, which further proved that the addition of 3 wt% CoSn(OH)6 had a flame retardant effect on the RPUF-CF1/MMT3 system.

HRR (a) and THR (b) curves of the modified RPUFs at 35 kW/m2.

HRR (a) and THR (b) curves of the modified RPUFs at 50 kW/m2.

The modified RPUF's THR and PHRR values grew in tandem with the intensity of thermal radiation. This occurred because the changed RPUF's internal structure experienced a significant reaction from the increased radiation intensity, while more stable short molecule chains also reacted and burned entirely as the temperature rose45. When there was a thermal radiation intensity of 50 kW/m2, the PHRR and THR of the five modified materials were 47.68 kW/m2, 53.37 kW/m2, 30.76 kW/m2, 40.83 kW/m2, 35.35 kW/m2 and 2.24 MJ/m2, 2.21 MJ/m2, 2.07 MJ/m2, 2.24 MJ/m2 and 2.34 MJ/m2, respectively. With the lowest PHRR and THR, CF1/MMT3/Co3 exhibited the highest flame retardant qualities. This was consistent with the conclusion obtained at a thermal radiation intensity of 35 kW/m2. The PHRR of CF1/MMT3/CO3 was reduced by 11.42 kW/m2 compared with PHRR (42.18 kW/m2) and THR (2.38 MJ/m2) of RPUF-CF1/MMT319. The THR of RPUF after adding CoSn(OH)6 decreased by 0.14 MJ/m2, 0.17 MJ/m2, 0.31 MJ/m2, 0.14 MJ/m2 and 0.04 MJ/m2, respectively.

Figure 7a–e depicted the macroscopic morphology of the carbon residue following the combustion of the modified RPUFs. Figure 7a–e illustrated that the addition of CoSn(OH)6 resulted in the formation of a compact carbon layer on the RPUF surface due to CoSn(OH)6 decomposition. Upon addition of 1 wt%, 2 wt% and 3 wt% CoSn(OH)6, the pores and the amount of carbon layer fractures steadily diminished until none remained. However, some holes and cracks appeared again after adding 4 wt% and 5 wt% of CoSn(OH)6. This demonstrated that the combination of CoSn(OH)6, MMT and chicken feather protein led to an effective enhancement of RPUFs’ carbon formation capacity. Additionally, the uniformity of the carbon layer was increased, lowering the temperature of combustion and causing a denser layer of protection to emerge. The improved RPUF benefited from this layer's ability to prevent combustion. The results showed that adding 3 wt% CoSn(OH)6 produced the greatest outcomes. Consequently, CF1/MMT3/Co3 had the most effective flame-retardant properties.

The densest layer of carbon was critical in preventing free radicals from spreading as the foam burned, which effectively put an end to the chain reaction and acted as a flame retardant.

Smoke-toxic analysis

Smoke density

The sample emitted smoke as soon as it came into contact with heat radiation. The high density of material fumes compromised human health, while toxic fumes polluted the environment. Figure 8 and Fig. S1 depicted the improved RPUFs' transmittance and Ds curves under flameless and flame circumstances. As shown in Fig. 8, with flameless combustion, the sample burned rapidly and produced smoke within the first 0–200 s. However, after 200 s, the sample was depleted and smoke production peaked and then leveled off. At 1200 s, the Ds of the five samples were 31.49, 30.24, 22.88, 30.50, and 36.57. The light transmittance of the five samples was 57.74%, 59.01%, 67.09%, 58.74% and 52.84%, respectively. By comparison, CF1/MMT3/Co3 had the smallest Ds and the largest light transmittance, so CF1/MMT3/Co3 had the best smoke suppression performance. In addition, compared with RPUF-CF1/MMT3, the Ds of CF1/MMT3/CO3 was reduced by 28.9%, and the light transmittance was increased by 17.6%. When CoSn(OH)6 was introduced into the original system, the transition metal ions present in CoSn(OH)6 created inorganic oxides47. This procedure effectively stopped further burning and accelerated the formation of the carbon layer.

Smoke production rate and total smoke release

Many fatalities from big accidents are caused by toxic smoke from flames. The two primary measures used to evaluate the rate of smoke formation are the smoke production rate (SPR) and the total smoke release (TSR). These indicators help to determine the severity of the situation. Figs. S2 and S3 showed the SPR and TSR of the five RPUFs at thermal radiation intensities of 35 kW/m2 and 50 kW/m2. Fig. S2 showed that at a thermal radiation intensity of 35 kW/m2, the peak SPR (PSPR) for the five materials was 0.0072, 0.0085, 0.0054, 0.0073, and 0.0065 m2/s, and the TSR of the five materials were 49.89, 51.00, 44.57, 45.55 and 44.89 m2/m2. By comparison, when 3 wt% CoSn(OH)6 was added, the RPUF had the lowest SPR and the lowest TSR. It was proved that 3 wt% CoSn(OH)6 was the optimal ratio to suppress smoke production. Compared to the PSPR and the TSR of RPUF-CF1/MMT3, the PSPR of CF1/MMT3/Co3 decreased by 25% and TSR decreased by 18%.

At a thermal radiation intensity of 50 kW/m2 (Fig. S3), the PSPR of the five materials was 0.0099, 0.0130, 0.0062, 0.0086 and 0.0081 m2/s. PSPR of CF1/MMT3/Co3 was 29.5% lower than that of RPUF-CF1/MMT3 (0.0088 m2/s)19. The TSRs of the five materials were 55.41, 52.69, 50.98, 52.28, and 52.28 m2/m2. Compared with RPUF-CF1/MMT3 (59.71 m2/m2)19, it was reduced by 7.20, 11.76, 14.62, 12.44 and 12.44%, respectively. When 3 wt% CoSn(OH)6 was added, the smoke suppression performance of the material was the best.

Flame retardant mechanism

A schematic representation of the flame retardant mechanism was displayed in Fig. 9. The hydroxystannate in CoSn(OH)6 was flame retardant in both the gas phase and the condensed phase. In the condensed phase, the hydroxystannate produced during the decomposition of CoSn(OH)6 promoted the decomposition of the polyurethane molecular chain. At high temperatures, compounds such as Co2O3 and Co3O4 were formed. Condensed phase P–O–Si formed from the breakdown of MMT and chicken feather protein entered at the same time to generate carbon, minimizing the discharge of flue gases and flammable elements. Excellent catalytic carbonation characteristics were exhibited by transition metal ions throughout the flame retardant procedure. Transition metal catalytic carbonation was an important component of the flame-retardant metal compounds, as it captured free radicals in the polymer chain throughout the polymer combustion process and raised the crosslinked network's density49. In order to meet the goals of smoke suppression and flame retardancy, it may create a continuous, solid carbonaceous inorganic layer during burning, which served as a flame retardant in the condensed phase. The carbon layer can also effectively block the passage of heat to the interior. In addition, because of their strong catalytic activity, materials based on transition metals can prevent the release of harmful chemicals50.

Schematic diagram of flame retardant mechanism.

Cobalt ions have the ability to catalyze the oxidation of CO to carbon dioxide in the gas phase. Simultaneously, the breakdown of chicken feather proteins created ammonia and water vapor, which diluted flammable gasses and eliminated some of the heat. Ammonia can be utilized as a gas source during burning, which encourages the carbon layer to expand while preventing the transmission of heat and mass. In addition, CoSn(OH)6 flame retardants, in contrast to other inorganic flame retardants, can be applied in tiny amounts to significantly increase a substrate's flame retardancy without changing the polymer's mechanical characteristics51.

Conclusions

CoSn(OH)6 was synthesized and demonstrated by XRD and FTIR detection. A flame retardant system utilizing green, bio-based flame retardants was developed in the current study by incorporating CoSn(OH)6 into the chicken feather protein-based RPUF modified with MMT. The TGA results showed that CF1/MMT3/Co3 had the starting temperature of decomposition and end temperature, and had the largest residual carbon content. When the Flynn–Wall–Ozawa, Kissinger and Starink methods were used to calculate the E of the five RPUFs, it was found that CF1/MMT3/Co3 had the largest E. Compared with RPUF-CF1/MMT3, CF1/MMT3/Co3's thermal stability was enhanced.

At a thermal radiation intensity of 35 kW/m2, the CONE tests revealed that the PHRR and THR of CF1/MMT3/Co3 were 27.96 kW/m2 and 1.91 MJ/m2, respectively. At a thermal radiation intensity of 50 kW/m2, the PHRR and THR of CF1/MMT3/Co3 were 30.76 kW/m2 and 2.07 MJ/m2, respectively. CF1/MMT3/CO3 had minimal PHRR and THR. Furthermore, CF1/MMT3/Co3 exhibited the lowest Ds values (22.88 and 20.36) and the highest light transmittance (67.09 and 70.11%) under flameless and flame conditions. The fire hazard evaluation index calculations demonstrated that, under both thermal radiation flux conditions, CF1/MMT3/Co3 presented the smallest ToxPI, TSPI, FGI and THRI values, which corroborated the Ds conclusion.

In summary, the findings of this study indicated that incorporating CoSn(OH)6 into the RPUF that already contained MMT and chicken feather protein, could successfully inhibit flame diffusion while also preventing the flow of heat and the production of flammable gases. In light of this, the green bio-based flame retardant system presented a viable method for producing bio-based foam composites that are high-performing, long-lasting, fire-resistant, and smoke-resistant.
