Harmine-loaded PCL/PVA multilayer nanofibers for targeted pancreatic cancer therapy
Authors: Zeynab Zamanzadeh, Behzad Khansarinejad, Mahdieh Mondanizadeh, Shima Masodii, Hadi Karami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPancreatic cancer remains one of the deadliest malignancies because of late diagnosis, rapid metastasis, and poor response to conventional therapies. Localized drug delivery systems based on electrospun nanofibers may improve therapeutic outcomes by enabling sustained local drug release while minimizing systemic toxicity. This study developed harmine (HAR)-loaded multilayer nanofibers using polycaprolactone (PCL) and polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) and evaluated their physicochemical characteristics and anti-cancer effects against ASPC-1 pancreatic cancer cells.ResultsBilayer and trilayer HAR-loaded nanofibers were successfully fabricated with uniform, bead-free morphology. Morphology was characterized by FE-SEM, and drug incorporation was confirmed by FTIR analysis. The multilayer architecture demonstrated favorable swelling behavior, controlled degradation, and regulated water vapor transmission. Drug release studies showed an initial burst release (~ 16% within 6 h), followed by sustained HAR release reaching approximately 68% over 72 h. Blank nanofibers exhibited high biocompatibility (> 97% cell viability). Compared with free HAR, HAR-loaded nanofibers (NFs) significantly enhanced anti-cancer efficacy and achieved nearly complete suppression of viability in ASPC-1 cells after 72 h. Functional analyses demonstrated substantial inhibition of cell migration and colony formation. Molecular evaluation revealed significant apoptosis induction through upregulation of BAX and CASP3 and downregulation of BCL-2, with the PCL/PVAHAR formulation showing the strongest response (BAX ≈ 4.8-fold, CASP3 ≈ 3.9-fold, BCL-2 ≈ 0.35-fold; BAX/BCL-2 ratio ≈ 13.7). STRING analysis further supported the involvement of apoptosis-associated signaling pathways.ConclusionsHAR-loaded PCL/PVA nanofibers provide an effective localized drug-delivery platform with sustained release and enhanced anti-pancreatic-cancer activity compared with free HAR. These findings support the further development of this system as a potential implantable therapeutic strategy for post-resection pancreatic cancer management.Keywords: Harmine, Pancreatic cancer, Drug delivery system (DDS), PCL/PVA, Electrospinning, Nanofibers

Introduction

Cancer remains a major global health burden. Among all malignancies, Pancreatic cancer is one of the most aggressive and lethal cancers and is currently the third leading cause of cancer-related mortality in the United States [1]. Despite advances in cancer diagnosis and treatment, prevention, and treatment, patient outcomes remain poor due to late-stage detection, early metastasis, and resistance to conventional therapies [2]. Current therapeutic approaches, including surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy, provide limited long-term clinical benefit [3]. Therefore, the development of more effective and targeted therapeutic strategies remains an urgent priority.

Localized and implantable drug delivery systems (DDSs) have attracted increasing attention because they enable direct delivery of therapeutic agents to tumor sites, resulting in improved local drug concentration, controlled release, and reduced systemic toxicity [4]. Among these systems, electrospun NFs represent a promising platform owing to their high surface area, interconnected porosity, and tunable release characteristics [5]. Electrospun NFs have been widely applied in tissue engineering, wound healing, antimicrobial therapy, and cancer therapy, particularly through biodegradable implantable systems that improve patient compliance and eliminate the need for surgical removal [4–8]. Recent developments in coaxial and multifluid electrospinning have further expanded the ability to tailor nanofiber architecture and achieve immediate, sustained, biphasic, and stimuli-responsive drug release profiles [9]. Previous studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of electrospun NFs for sustained anticancer drug delivery, including paclitaxel-, fluorouracil-, docetaxel-, and curcumin-loaded formulations [10–14].

HAR, a natural β-carboline alkaloid isolated from Peganum harmala, has attracted interest as a potential anti-cancer agent [15]. HAR exerts anti-tumor effects by inhibiting cell proliferation, inducing apoptosis, and suppressing metastatic behavior through modulation of signaling pathways such as MAPK and PI3K/AKT and regulation of apoptosis-related genes, including BAX, CASP3, BCL-2 [16]. In pancreatic cancer models, HAR has been shown to inhibit tumor growth and invasion through regulation of epithelial–mesenchymal transition and angiogenic pathways [17]. However, clinical application of HAR is limited by poor aqueous solubility and short biological half-life, highlighting the need for localized and controlled delivery approaches to improve therapeutic efficacy [18].

PCL/PVA-based NF drug delivery systems provide a promising strategy for pancreatic cancer therapy by combining the mechanical stability of hydrophobic PCL with the hydrophilic characteristics of PVA to support drug encapsulation and sustained release [19, 20].

Although single-layer NFs have been extensively investigated, they may exhibit limitations, including burst release and insufficient spatial control of drug distribution. Recent improvements in hierarchical single-layer structures have enhanced their performance [21, 22], multilayer systems, particularly bilayer and trilayer (sandwich) architectures, offer additional advantages. These include improved regulation of degradation behavior, reduced burst release, protection of encapsulated agents, and more controlled release kinetics [23–25]. Building on these concepts, the present study utilizes a multi-layer structural design. The specific rationale for our multilayer configurations, particularly the trilayer (PCL/PVA/PCL) architecture, stems from the need to balance drug delivery kinetics with mechanical stability. While PVA is highly suitable for encapsulating HAR, non-crosslinked PVA is prone to rapid dissolution in aqueous environments, which can lead to an undesirable burst release. By employing hydrophobic PCL as the outer layers, the scaffold gains an essential mechanical backbone and a physical barrier that restricts rapid water penetration. This sandwich design protects the inner PVA layer, prevents its immediate degradation, and facilitates a controlled, sustained release of the therapeutic agent suitable for post-resection local implantation.

Therefore, the present study aimed to develop and characterize HAR-loaded multilayer PCL/PVA electrospun NFs and evaluate their anti-cancer activity against ASPC-1 pancreatic cancer cells. The effects of the developed formulations on cell viability, migration, colony formation, and apoptosis-related gene expression (BAX, BCL-2, and CASP3) were investigated to determine whether controlled HAR delivery could enhance anti-tumor efficacy through sustained release and apoptosis induction.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and materials

ASPC-1 pancreatic cancer cells (Pasteur Institute, Tehran, Iran) were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco/Invitrogen, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere [26].

Preparation of nanofibers (NFs)

PCL (80 kDa) and PVA (85 kDa) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (USA). DCM, DMF, HAR, ethanol, methanol, and acetic acid were obtained from Merck (Germany). A 10% PCL/PVA blend was prepared by dissolving 1 g of PCL powder in 10 mL of DCM/DMF (3:7 v/v) with agitation for 3 h. The DCM/DMF system was selected over acidic alternatives (e.g., acetic acid/formic acid) to prevent potential hydrolytic degradation of the PCL backbone, while DMF acted as a co-solvent to enhance the spinnability of the solution. For the PVA solution, to prevent ethanol evaporation and maintain a precise 10% (w/v) concentration, a two-step dissolution process was employed. First, 1 g of PVA was dissolved in 8 mL of distilled water at 80 °C under constant magnetic stirring. After complete dissolution and cooling to room temperature, 2 mL of ethanol was added to achieve a final water/ethanol ratio of 8:2 (v/v). Based on cytotoxicity results, each solution was mixed with the IC50 concentration of HAR and stirred for 2 h. Following the electrospinning process, to ensure the complete evaporation of any residual toxic solvents that could interfere with biological assays, all fabricated scaffolds were vacuum-dried at room temperature for 48 h prior to subsequent evaluations [27].

Electrospinning

Following solution preparation, each solution was electrospun into nanofibers using an electrospinning machine (Electroris, Fanavaran Nano-Meghyas, Tehran, Iran) equipped with a syringe pump. PCL was electrospun at 17 kV, 1 mL/h, 12 cm needle-to-collector distance, and 250 RPM collector rotation speed. Subsequently, to fabricate the multilayered scaffolds, a sequential electrospinning approach was employed. Specifically, without removing the initial layer from the collector foil, the polymer solution was immediately switched, and PVA was electrospun directly onto the existing PCL mat at 20 kV, 0.4 mL/h, 14 cm needle-to-collector distance, and 250 RPM collector speed. The higher voltage was applied to overcome the high viscosity of the PVA solution and the insulating effect of the underlying PCL layer. This sequential deposition ensured excellent interfacial contact between the layers, creating double- and triple-layer nanofibers with a mechanically robust PCL layer and a bioactive PVA layer [28]. For the fabrication of the multilayered scaffolds, 5 mL of the PCL solution and 5 mL of the PVA solution were sequentially electrospun. The rationale behind these specific volumes was to control the final thickness ratio of the layers. A larger volume of PCL was utilized to guarantee the mechanical strength and structural integrity of the supportive base layer, while the volume of PVA was selected to provide an optimal thickness for efficient HAR loading and controlled release, preventing excessive swelling while maintaining bioactivity. In vitro cytotoxicity (MTT assay)

The cytotoxic effects of free HAR and HAR-loaded PCL/PVA nanofibers on ASPC-1 cancer cells were evaluated using the MTT assay. ASPC-1 cells (7 × 103 cells/well) were seeded in 96-well plates and incubated overnight at 37 °C. Cells were then treated with free HAR and PCL/PVAHAR nanofibers (0-200 µM HAR equivalent), and incubated for 24, 48, and 72 h at 37 °C in 5% CO2. After incubation, MTT solution (10 µl) was added for 4 h at 37 °C. Formazan crystals were dissolved in 100 µl DMSO, and absorbance was measured at 570 nm using an ELISA plate reader. Cell migration assay with free-HAR and HAR-NFs

The migration and proliferation capacity of ASPC-1 cells were evaluated using an in vitro scratch wound assay. Briefly, 1 × 105 ASPC-1 cells were seeded in 24-well plates and cultured under standard conditions (37 °C, 5% CO2) until a confluent monolayer was formed. A linear scratch (wound) was created in each well using a sterile 100 µL pipette tip. The cells were then treated with free HAR and HAR-loaded PCL/PVA nanofibers at their respective IC50 concentrations. The wound area was monitored and photographed at 0 and 72 hours’ post-treatment using an inverted light microscope. The remaining scratch area was quantified using ImageJ software to evaluate the inhibitory effect of HAR formulations on cell migration [36].

Colony formation assay with free-HAR and HAR-NFs

To assess the colony-forming potential of ASPC-1 cells after HAR exposure, 500 cells were seeded in triplicate in a 24-well plate and incubated for 72 h at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Cells were then exposed to free-HAR and PCL/PVAHAR nanofibers at IC50 concentrations. After removing the medium, nanofibers, and drug, cells were incubated for 10 more days under the same conditions. Colonies were fixed with methanol: acetic acid (1:7) for 20 min at room temperature after washing twice with PBS. Following fixation, cells were stained with 0.5% crystal violet for 20 min at room temperature, washed four times with distilled water, and air-dried. Colonies were then counted visually [37].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9. Data are presented as mean ± SD. Group differences were analyzed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. GraphPad Prism 9 was used for statistical analysis and graph preparation. For all in vitro experiments, including cytotoxicity, migration, and qRT-PCR assays, biological replicates were defined as independent experiments performed on different days using distinct cell passages (n = 3). Technical replicates were defined as repeated measurements within the same experiment (e.g., three separate wells or test runs per biological sample). All reported values represent the mean of three independent biological replicates ± SD.

Results

Synthesis of PCL/PVA and analysis of characteristics

Figure 1 shows FE-SEM images of PCL/PVA and PCL/PVAHAR nanofibers and their diameter distributions. The nanofibers exhibit consistent, bead-free random fiber alignments. The absence of HAR aggregates on the NF surfaces indicates compatibility and miscibility between the PCL/PVA and HAR components. In the FT-IR spectrum of PCL/PVA nanofibers (Fig. 2), prominent bands at 1720 cm⁻¹ and 1100 cm⁻¹ correspond to carboxylic ester (C = O) and ether (C-O) groups, respectively. HAR ‘s FTIR spectrum shows characteristic peaks at 3400 cm⁻¹ (O–H stretching), 1600 cm⁻¹ (C = C aromatic stretching), and 1250 cm⁻¹ (C-N stretching). The PCL/PVAHAR nanofibers FTIR profiles show minimal spectral shifts and well-separated bands, confirming the successful incorporation of HAR into the NF structure.

Water vapor transmission rate (WVTR)

The WVTR is a critical parameter for implantable anti-cancer scaffolds, as it governs moisture and gas exchange within the local three-dimensional microenvironment post-tumor resection. As shown in Fig. 3A, incorporating HAR into a three-layer NF configuration reduces the composite nanomaterial’s WVTR compared to a two-layer configuration. This observation is attributed to the presence of the additional hydrophobic PCL layer in the trilayer structure, which acts as a more substantial barrier against vapor diffusion. Furthermore, although non-crosslinked PVA is inherently hydrophilic, the sandwich-like architecture ensures that the PVA core is shielded by PCL layers. This structural arrangement prevents the dissolution or structural collapse of the hydrated PVA during the test, allowing the mat to maintain its integrity while effectively regulating moisture permeability. It should be noted that maintaining an optimal WVTR is essential; excessively low permeability could lead to local fluid accumulation at the resection site, potentially disrupting the local tissue microenvironment.

Biodegradability of nanofibers

PCL nanofibers, while biocompatible, non-immunogenic, and mechanically strong, degrade slowly. To improve degradability, PVA was incorporated into PCL nanofibers. Degradation kinetics, assessed by mass loss in pH 7.4 PBS (Fig. 3B), revealed that PVA significantly accelerated the degradation of PCL/PVA nanofibers compared to PCL nanofibers alone. The addition of HAR, despite its hydrophobicity, did not substantially alter the degradation behavior of PCL/PVAHAR nanofibers compared to PCL/PVA.

In vitro drug release

Figure 3C shows an initial burst release of approximately 16% of the drug from the PCL/PVAHAR nanofibers within the first 6 h, indicating a surface-driven drug release mechanism that facilitates the rapid delivery of a therapeutic dose to tumor cells. This initial release is crucial for the targeted ablation of cancer cells. Subsequently, a controlled release of approximately 68% of the encapsulated drug occurred over 72 h. This sustained release targets and eliminates any remaining cancer cells, enhancing the overall therapeutic effectiveness. Although the uncrosslinked PVA layer is highly water-soluble, a massive burst release is prevented. The hydrophobic PCL layers effectively act as a physical barrier, limiting rapid water penetration and delaying the dissolution of the PVA layer, thereby facilitating this sustained drug release profile.

Swelling test

Adequate swelling of nanofibrous scaffolds is crucial for facilitating drug diffusion and mimicking the hydrated 3D extracellular matrix. In localized cancer therapy, enhanced water uptake allows the scaffold to effectively exchange fluids with the surrounding microenvironment, facilitating the release of therapeutic agents. Our study, illustrated in Fig. 4, demonstrates that nanofibers containing HAR reach water saturation by day seven. The inclusion of HAR may increases the NF hydrophilicity, which is highly beneficial for promoting a sustained drug release profile and maintaining a dynamic fluid exchange in the local microenvironment.

Electrospun NF swelling properties

It is worth noting that non-crosslinked PVA is inherently water-soluble. In the bilayer samples where the PVA layer was directly exposed to the PBS medium, the nanofibers rapidly lost their morphology and transitioned into a hydrogel state upon initial contact with the aqueous environment. However, the presence of the underlying hydrophobic PCL layer provided essential mechanical support, preventing the immediate macroscopic disintegration of the mat. The initial high swelling ratio observed in these samples is attributed to the rapid water uptake and gelation of the PVA layer on the PCL backbone. In contrast, the sandwich (trilayer) structure effectively shielded the PVA layer, controlling both the swelling rate and the structural integrity of the system.

Free-HAR cell cytotoxicity

The MTT assay evaluated the impact of HAR concentrations (0-200 µM) on ASPC-1 cells. Results, shown in Fig. 5, indicate that the IC50 of HAR is 50 µM after 24 h, demonstrating its efficacy in suppressing cell proliferation at varying concentrations.

Cytotoxicity of HAR-loaded nanofibers

The cytotoxic effects of free HAR and HAR-loaded NF formulations on ASPC-1 cells were evaluated by MTT assay after 24, 48, and 72 h (Fig. 6). Blank PCL/PVA nanofibers showed no significant cytotoxicity (> 97% viability at all-time points), confirming scaffold biocompatibility.

All HAR-loaded NF formulations—PCLHAR/PVA, PCL/PVAHAR, PCL/PVAHAR/PCL, and PVA/PCLHAR/PVA—demonstrated suppression of cell viability by 48 h and maintained this effect at 72 h. Free HAR (50 µM) showed only partial cytotoxicity, reducing viability to 65% at 48 h and 62% at 72 h, indicating possible drug degradation or cellular adaptation over time.

These results demonstrate that sustained release from NFs significantly enhances the cytotoxic efficacy of HAR compared to free drug, with all NF formulations achieving complete and durable eradication of ASPC-1 cells by 48 h.

Cellular migration

In a scratch wound assay, ASPC-1 cells treated with HAR-loaded PCL/PVA NFs exhibited significantly reduced migration and wound closure rates compared to controls group (Fig. 7). ImageJ quantification of the remaining scratch area at 0 and 72 h confirmed these findings, suggesting that the controlled release of HAR from the multilayer NFs effectively inhibited the migratory capacity of the ASPC-1 pancreatic cancer cells.

HAR-loaded nanofibers modulate apoptosis-related gene expression

To investigate the molecular mechanism by which HAR-loaded nanofibers exert anti-cancer effects, qRT-PCR was used to evaluate the mRNA expression of apoptosis-related genes (BAX, BCL-2, and CASP3) in ASPC-1 cells. Cells were treated for 72 h with 50 µM HAR (equivalent to the IC50 value) in seven groups: (1) untreated control; (2) blank PCL/PVA nanofibers; (3) 50 µM free HAR; (4) PCLHAR/PVA nanofibers; (5) PCL/PVAHAR nanofibers; (6) PCL/PVAHAR/PCL trilayer nanofibers; or (7) PVA/PCLHAR/PVA trilayer nanofibers. As shown in Fig. 8, both free HAR and HAR-loaded nanofibers significantly altered the expression of apoptosis-related genes compared to the control group, while blank PCL/PVA nanofibers showed no significant effect (p > 0.05), confirming scaffold biocompatibility. The PCL/PVAHAR formulation, with HAR incorporated in the hydrophilic PVA layer, exhibited the most potent pro-apoptotic effect, demonstrating the highest upregulation of BAX (approximately 4.8-fold, p < 0.001) and CASP3 (approximately 3.9-fold, p < 0.001), along with the most pronounced downregulation of BCL-2 (to approximately 0.35-fold, p < 0.01). Trilayer NF formulations also showed significant effects, with BAX upregulation ranging from 4.0 to 4.2-fold, CASP3 upregulation from 3.4 to 3.5-fold, and BCL-2 downregulation to 0.40-0.42-fold (p < 0.01). Free HAR treatment resulted in moderate changes, with BAX increased approximately 2.3-fold, CASP3 increased 2.1-fold, and BCL-2 decreased to 0.65-fold (p < 0.05). The BAX/BCL-2 ratio, a critical indicator of mitochondrial apoptosis, increased from 1.0 in control cells to approximately 13.7 in PCL/PVAHAR treated cells, compared to only 3.5 with free HAR treatment. These findings demonstrate that HAR-loaded nanofibers, particularly the PCL/PVAHAR formulation, are associated with apoptosis-related gene expression changes in ASPC-1 cells through modulation of the mitochondrial pathway, with significantly enhanced efficacy compared to free HAR.

Discussion

Pancreatic cancer remains a formidable therapeutic challenge due to its late diagnosis, aggressive metastasis, and resistance to conventional therapies, underscoring the need for improved treatment strategies [40]. Localized drug delivery systems (DDSs) have been proposed as a potential approach to overcome limitations of systemic chemotherapy by enabling sustained drug exposure at the tumor site while reducing systemic toxicity [41]. Among various DDS platforms, electrospun nanofibers offer advantages such as high surface area, tunable release kinetics, and structural adaptability for potential application in post-surgical cavities [42].

In this study, HAR-loaded PCL/PVA multilayer nanofibers were developed and evaluated for their in vitro effects on ASPC-1 pancreatic cancer cells. The results suggest that sustained release of HAR from the nanofibrous scaffold may enhance its pro-apoptotic and anti-metastatic effects compared with free drug administration.

The successful fabrication of HAR-loaded nanofibers with largely uniform and bead-free morphology was confirmed by FE-SEM, while FTIR analysis indicated successful incorporation of HAR without major chemical alterations (Figs. 1 and 2). These findings highlight the importance of structural design in nanofiber-based DDS. Differences observed among formulations, particularly PCL/PVA-HAR, suggest that layer sequencing may influence drug release behavior.

The outer PCL layer likely to contribute to structural stability and modulation of drug diffusion, whereas the hydrophilic PVA layer may facilitate initial swelling and improve drug accessibility. The incorporation of PVA increased scaffold hydrophilicity and swelling capacity (Fig. 4), which may support nutrient diffusion and waste removal in 3D environments, while also modifying degradation behavior compared to PCL alone (Fig. 3B). The in vitro drug release profile (Fig. 3C) showed a biphasic pattern, characterized by an initial burst release (~ 16% within 6 h), followed by a sustained and controlled release phase (~ 68% over 72 h).

This release behavior may offer an advantage over free HAR, which is rapidly cleared, by maintaining a more sustained local drug exposure. Such sustained release may be relevant for targeting highly proliferative cancer cells over time [43].

In cytotoxicity assays, HAR-loaded NF formulations showed a time-dependent increase in cytotoxic effects compared to free HAR. While free HAR at its IC₅₀ (50 µM) resulted in relatively stable viability over time, nanofiber-based delivery led to a greater reduction in cell viability at 72 h (Fig. 8). The most effective formulation (PCL/PVAHAR) reduced viability to approximately 19% at 72 h, suggesting enhanced anticancer activity under sustained release conditions (p < 0.0001). This effect may be associated with increased apoptosis, as reflected by changes in apoptosis-related gene expression.

At the molecular level, HAR-loaded NFs modulated apoptosis-related gene expression, including upregulation of BAX and CASP3 and downregulation of BCL-2 (Fig. 8). The observed shift in the BAX/BCL-2 ratio suggests activation of the mitochondrial apoptosis pathway [44]. The higher ratio observed in PCL/PVAHAR compared to other formulations and free HAR indicates that formulation design may influence the magnitude of apoptotic signaling. This effect may be related to sustained intracellular exposure to HAR and potential modulation of pro-survival pathways such as PI3K/AKT and MAPK, which have been previously associated with HAR activity [45].

In addition to cytotoxic effects, functional assays indicated that HAR-loaded NFs may suppress key metastatic behaviors, including cell migration (Fig. 7) and clonogenic survival (Fig. 8). These findings suggest that sustained drug exposure may contribute to prolonged inhibition of proliferative and metastatic potential. The reduction in colony formation may also indicate potential effects on long-term survival and clonogenic capacity of cancer cells.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, although sequential electrospinning was used to fabricate multilayer structures, cross-sectional SEM imaging was not performed to directly confirm layer architecture. Second, the study was limited to a single pancreatic cancer cell line (ASPC-1). Given the heterogeneity of pancreatic cancer, validation in additional cancer cell lines (e.g., PANC-1, BxPC-3) as well as non-cancerous pancreatic cells is required. Third, all experiments were performed in vitro; therefore, further studies using 3D tumor models and in vivo systems are necessary to better approximate physiological conditions. Fourth, degradation and drug release were evaluated in PBS, which does not fully replicate enzymatic or oxidative tumor microenvironments. Future studies under more physiologically relevant conditions are warranted. Finally, optimization of mechanical properties and translational design will be necessary to evaluate the potential clinical application of these nanofibrous systems.

Conclusion

In conclusion, a PCL/PVA-based nanofibrous drug delivery system was developed for sustained release of HAR and evaluated in vitro. The findings suggest that this delivery platform may enhance the anticancer activity of HAR by promoting apoptosis and reducing metastatic-related behaviors compared with free drug exposure. By enabling localized and sustained drug delivery, this approach may help achieve higher local efficacy while potentially reducing systemic exposure. Overall, the PCL/PVAHAR NF system represents a promising candidate for further preclinical investigation, particularly in the context of localized adjuvant therapy following tumor resection.
