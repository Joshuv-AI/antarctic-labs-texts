Lower fall and femoral fracture risks with semaglutide and tirzepatide compared with DPP-4 inhibitors in older adults with type 2 diabetes
Authors: Hsin-Yu Chen, Jheng-Yan Wu, Yi-Hsin Chu, Tse-Wei Chen, Chun-Feng Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThis retrospective cohort study evaluated whether semaglutide and tirzepatide reduce the risk of falls and femoral fractures in older adults with type 2 diabetes and overweight or obesity. The findings showed significantly lower risks of both outcomes compared with DPP-4 inhibitors.PurposeIncretin-based therapies may influence musculoskeletal health. This study evaluated the association of semaglutide and tirzepatide with femoral fractures and falls in older adults with type 2 diabetes (T2DM) and overweight or obesity.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study using the TriNetX database (2018–2025) included adults aged ≥ 65 with T2DM and BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2. Patients receiving semaglutide or tirzepatide were compared with those on dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4) inhibitors using 1:1 propensity score matching. Primary and secondary outcomes were 1-year femoral fracture and falls, respectively.ResultsAfter matching, 27,896 patients were included in the semaglutide/DPP-4 comparison and 12,808 in the tirzepatide/DPP-4 comparison. Femoral fractures were significantly lower with semaglutide (0.3% vs. 0.5%; HR 0.488; 95% CI 0.367–0.649; P < 0.0001) and tirzepatide (0.2% vs. 0.4%; HR 0.452; 95% CI 0.280–0.729; P = 0.0008) compared with DPP-4 inhibitors. Fall risk was also reduced for both semaglutide (3.6% vs. 5.4%; HR 0.663; 95% CI 0.612–0.718; P < 0.0001) and tirzepatide (3.6% vs. 5.7%; HR 0.664; 95% CI 0.591–0.747; P < 0.0001). Subgroup analyses showed consistent fall reduction across categories, while fracture reduction was more pronounced in patients with BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2.ConclusionIn older adults with T2DM and overweight/obesity, semaglutide and tirzepatide were associated with significantly lower risks of femoral fracture and falls compared with DPP-4 inhibitors, suggesting musculoskeletal benefits beyond glycemic control.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00198-026-08134-y.

Introduction

Hip fracture and falls represent some of the most serious health events affecting older adults [1]. These conditions are strongly associated with increased one-year mortality, long-term disability, loss of independent living, and a higher likelihood of institutionalized care, contributing significantly to increased healthcare expenditures and societal economic burden [2, 3]. Consequently, the prevention of falls and hip fractures has become a major priority in geriatric medicine and in the management of chronic metabolic diseases.

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) has consistently been associated with an increased risk of fractures [4]. Furthermore, T2DM frequently coexists with overweight and obesity, especially among older adults [5]. Excess adiposity, impaired balance, and diabetic complications may further increase the likelihood of falls and fracture events in this population [6, 7].

In recent years, incretin-based therapies have become increasingly important in the management of T2DM. Among these agents, semaglutide, a glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonist (GLP-1 RA), and tirzepatide, a dual glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP) and GLP-1 receptor agonist, have gained widespread use because of their potent glucose-lowering effects, substantial weight reduction, and demonstrated cardiovascular benefits [8, 9]. These medications are increasingly prescribed for older adults with diabetes and obesity. Emerging evidence also suggests that semaglutide may improve muscle function and reduce systemic inflammation [10]. Beyond metabolic effects, GLP-1 receptors are expressed in bone tissue, and experimental studies indicate that incretin signaling pathways may influence bone remodeling processes [11].

Despite these potential benefits, concerns remain regarding the skeletal effects of incretin-based therapies. The biological plausibility that GLP-1 receptor agonists provide direct protection against fractures in humans remains uncertain. Weight loss induced by GLP-1–based therapies may reduce skeletal mechanical loading and be accompanied by lean mass loss, potentially increasing frailty and fall risk in older adults [12, 13].

Given these uncertainties, real-world evidence is needed to clarify the relationship between incretin-based therapies and fracture-related outcomes in older adults with T2DM. However, evidence specifically addressing skeletal safety in older adults aged ≥ 65 years with obesity remains limited, as existing clinical trials have largely excluded frail or elderly individuals and have not simultaneously assessed both femoral fracture and fall risk in this vulnerable population. Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate the association between treatment with semaglutide or tirzepatide and the risk of femoral fracture and falls in older patients with type 2 diabetes and overweight or obesity using a large international electronic health record database.

Methods

Data source

This retrospective cohort study was conducted using the TriNetX Global Collaborative Network, a federated research platform that provides access to de-identified electronic health record (EHR) data from multiple healthcare organizations worldwide. The TriNetX database contains demographic information, diagnoses coded according to the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10), medications coded using RxNorm, procedures, and laboratory measurements coded using Logical Observation Identifiers Names and Codes (LOINC). The platform allows researchers to identify patient cohorts based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria and to perform statistical analyses using built-in analytical tools. All data within the TriNetX network are de-identified and comply with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) and the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR). Because the study used only aggregated and de-identified data, informed consent was waived. The study was approved by the Chi Mei Medical Center Institutional Ethics Committee (IRB: 11,503-E04).

Study design and population

This study was designed as a retrospective cohort study to evaluate the association between treatment with semaglutide or tirzepatide and the risk of femoral fracture and falls among older adults with type 2 diabetes mellitus. Patients were identified from the TriNetX database between January 1, 2018, and July 1, 2025. Eligible participants were adults aged 65 years or older with a diagnosis of type 2 diabetes mellitus (ICD-10: E11) and a body mass index (BMI) of 25 kg/m2 or greater.

The index date was defined as the date of the first prescription of one of the study medications during the study period. Patients were categorized into three exposure groups according to the medication prescribed at the index date: semaglutide, tirzepatide, or dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4) inhibitors. Patients in the semaglutide group were required to have continued semaglutide prescriptions during the 6- to 12-month period following the index date and no use of DPP-4 inhibitors or other GLP-1 RAs within one month before the index date or during the subsequent 12 months. Similarly, patients in the tirzepatide group were required to have ongoing tirzepatide use during the 6- to 12-month period after the index date and no exposure to DPP-4 inhibitors or other GLP-1 receptor agonists during the same exclusion window. The comparator cohort consisted of patients receiving DPP-4 inhibitors who continued treatment between 6 and 12 months after the index date and had no exposure to GLP-1 receptor agonists during the same period. DPP-4 inhibitors were selected as the active comparator for several reasons. First, as an established second-line glucose-lowering agent, DPP-4 inhibitors are frequently co-prescribed with or sequentially substituted by GLP-1 RAs in clinical practice, representing a clinically relevant comparator group. Second, this active comparator design reduces confounding by indication compared with an untreated control group. Third, while some evidence suggests DPP-4 inhibitors may exert modest skeletal effects, the overall fracture risk with this drug class is considered neutral in major meta-analyses, making them an appropriate reference for evaluating the incremental skeletal benefit of incretin-based therapies [14–16].

Patients were excluded if they had a diagnosis of type 1 diabetes mellitus (ICD-10: E10) or had a history within one year prior to the index date of falls (ICD-10: W00–W19), femoral fracture (ICD-10: S72), frailty (ICD-10: R54), or sarcopenia (ICD-10: M62.84). Patients with an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status of 4 or higher were also excluded to reduce potential confounding related to severe functional impairment.

Covariates

Baseline covariates were assessed during the one-year period preceding the index date. These variables included demographic characteristics, clinical comorbidities, medication history, and available laboratory data. Demographic variables included age, sex, race, and body mass index. Clinical comorbidities were identified using ICD-10 diagnostic codes and included major chronic conditions potentially associated with fracture or fall risk, such as hypertension, heart disease, arthritis, and cerebrovascular disease (CVD). Medication history included commonly prescribed antidiabetic agents and other medications that might influence fracture or fall risk. Baseline laboratory parameters available within the TriNetX database, including glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), were also included when available. These covariates were incorporated into the propensity score model to minimize potential confounding between treatment groups.

Outcomes

The primary outcome of interest was the occurrence of femoral fracture within one year following the index date. Femoral fractures were identified using the ICD-10 diagnostic code S72 (fracture of femur). The secondary outcome was the occurrence of falls, defined using ICD-10 codes W00–W19 (slipping, tripping, stumbling, and falls) during the same follow-up period. Patients were followed from the index date until the occurrence of the outcome, loss to follow-up, or the end of the one-year follow-up period, whichever occurred first.

Statistical analysis

To minimize confounding, propensity score matching (PSM) was performed using a 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching approach. Two matched cohorts were generated for comparisons of semaglutide versus DPP-4 inhibitors and tirzepatide versus DPP-4 inhibitors. The propensity score model included demographic variables, comorbidities, medication use, and laboratory parameters.

Baseline characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics. Continuous variables were expressed as means with standard deviations and compared using independent-sample t tests or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, while categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages and compared using chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests. Covariate balance after matching was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMDs), with values less than 0.2 indicating adequate balance.

Time-to-event analyses were conducted using Cox proportional hazards models to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Kaplan–Meier curves were constructed to illustrate cumulative incidence of outcomes. For the subgroup analyses, we repeated the Cox proportional hazards models and performed a test for interaction for each subgroup variable. All tests were two-sided, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Statistical figures were generated using R software. To evaluate the robustness of the primary on-treatment analysis, a supplementary intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis was conducted using the full propensity score–matched cohorts without requiring medication continuity during follow-up, allowing treatment discontinuation and crossover between exposure groups. Results of the on-treatment and supplementary ITT analyses are presented in Supplementary Table S2 and S3, respectively.

Outcomes

For the primary outcome, femoral fracture occurred in 70 patients (0.3%) in the semaglutide group and 145 patients (0.5%) in the DPP-4 inhibitor group. Semaglutide use was associated with a significantly lower risk of femoral fracture compared with DPP-4 inhibitors (HR 0.488, 95% CI 0.367–0.649, P < 0.0001, number needed to treat (NNT) = 372). Similarly, in the tirzepatide comparison cohort, 24 patients (0.2%) in the tirzepatide group and 56 patients (0.4%) in the DPP-4 inhibitor group experienced femoral fracture. Tirzepatide use was associated with a significantly reduced risk of femoral fracture (HR 0.452, 95% CI 0.28–0.729, P = 0.0008, NNT = 400). Subgroup analyses

Subgroup analyses were conducted according to age and BMI categories to evaluate the consistency of the associations between treatment groups and the risk of femoral fracture and falls.

For femoral fracture, the protective association of semaglutide remained significant among patients aged 65–79 years (HR 0.612, 95% CI 0.413–0.907, P = 0.0134). When stratified by BMI, the association was significant in patients with BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 (HR 0.513, 95% CI 0.339–0.775, P = 0.0012) but not in those with BMI 25–29.9 kg/m2 (HR 0.756, 95% CI 0.459–1.246, P = 0.2712). However, no significant interaction was observed between treatment effect and BMI category (P for interaction = 0.29). Similar patterns were observed in the tirzepatide cohort. Tirzepatide was associated with a lower risk of femoral fracture among patients aged 65–79 years (HR 0.561, 95% CI 0.339–0.93, P = 0.023) and among those with BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 (HR 0.414, 95% CI 0.237–0.726, P = 0.0015), whereas the association was not statistically significant in the BMI 25–29.9 kg/m2 subgroup (HR 0.571, 95% CI 0.273–1.192, P = 0.1307). Likewise, no significant interaction between treatment effect and BMI category was detected (P for interaction = 0.55). Discussion

In this large real-world study, the use of semaglutide and tirzepatide was associated with significantly lower risks of femoral fracture and falls compared with DPP-4 inhibitors in older adults with type 2 diabetes and overweight or obesity. These associations were generally consistent across subgroups defined by age and BMI. Although the protective effect on femoral fracture achieved statistical significance only among patients with BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 and failed to reach significance in those with BMI 25–29.9 kg/m2, the formal interaction testing yielded non-significant results (P for interaction = 0.29 for semaglutide and 0.55 for tirzepatide). These findings indicate a lack of true statistical heterogeneity, suggesting that the fracture-protective benefits of these incretin-based therapies generally extend across both the overweight and obese spectra.

The current evidence indicates that GLP-1 RAs do not increase fracture risk and may have neutral or modestly protective effects on bone in patients with type 2 diabetes [16–18]. A meta-analysis reported a pooled odds ratio (OR) of 0.71 (95% CI 0.56–0.91) for fracture among GLP-1 RA users compared with placebo or other anti-diabetic therapies, while semaglutide demonstrated neutrality (OR 0.87, 95% CI 0.39–1.95) [17]. Similarly, another recent meta-analysis found a pooled relative risk (RR) of 0.77 (95% CI 0.61–0.96) for fractures associated with GLP-1 RA use; estimates for semaglutide (RR 0.82, 95% CI 0.47–1.42) and tirzepatide (RR 1.12, 95% CI 0.44–2.85) likewise indicated no increased fracture risk [18]. Complementing these findings, real-world evidence demonstrated significantly lower odds of vertebral compression fractures (OR 0.83, 95% CI 0.79–0.87) and reduced need for vertebral surgical intervention among GLP-1 RA users compared with nonusers [19]. Collectively, these data support the skeletal safety of GLP-1–based therapies, including semaglutide and tirzepatide, and align with the fracture risk reduction observed in our cohort. The number needed to treat (NNT) for femoral fracture prevention was 372 for semaglutide and 400 for tirzepatide, reflecting the relatively low absolute incidence of femoral fracture (0.2–0.5%) in this predominantly younger-old cohort (mean age 69 years). While this NNT appears large in isolation, it should be interpreted alongside the substantially lower NNT for fall prevention (NNT 48–55), given that falls are the principal precipitating event for osteoporotic fractures. Considered together, the combined musculoskeletal benefit of these agents in older adults with T2DM and obesity may be clinically meaningful, particularly when weighed against their established metabolic and cardiovascular advantages.

Despite the observed clinical benefits, concerns remain regarding the potential for rapid weight loss induced by GLP-1 RAs to exacerbate lean mass loss and reduce bone mineral density (BMD) [20, 21]. Recent evidence suggests that the skeletal effects of GLP-1–based therapies may differ by diabetes status, with greater hip bone loss observed in individuals without diabetes but not in those with diabetes [22]. The relationship between T2DM and skeletal health is often described as the "diabetic bone paradox," where patients exhibit increased fracture risk despite having normal or even elevated BMD [23]. This discrepancy is largely attributed to impaired bone quality, altered body composition, and diabetic complications including frequent falls [9, 24]. Importantly, recent cross-sectional evidence from older hip-fracture patients demonstrated that the body composition phenotype characterizing T2DM is primarily driven by heightened visceral adiposity and central fat redistribution, rather than a reduction in appendicular muscle quantity [25].

Older individuals with obesity and T2DM exhibit preferential accumulation of central adiposity, particularly visceral adipose tissue [5, 26]. Visceral adipose tissue promotes systemic inflammation and bone marrow adiposity, shifting stem cell differentiation toward adipogenesis and enhancing osteoclast-mediated resorption. Meanwhile, the accumulation of advanced glycation end products (AGE) in bone leads to collagen cross-linking, matrix stiffening, and suppressed bone turnover via receptor for advanced glycation end products (RAGE) signaling [24, 27]. These mechanisms impair bone quality and contribute to increased fracture risk in diabetes despite preserved bone mineral density. Furthermore, central obesity profoundly alters biomechanical stability, shifting the body’s center of mass and increasing the impact force during falls [7, 28].

In this context, the potent effects of semaglutide and tirzepatide on body composition provide a possible mechanism for the risk reduction observed in our study. In the STEP 6 trial, abdominal visceral fat area was reduced by 40.0% in the semaglutide group compared with 6.9% in the placebo group (ETD − 33.2%, 95% CI − 42.1 to − 24.2) [29]. Rodríguez Jiménez et al. demonstrated that both subcutaneous and oral semaglutide significantly reduced visceral fat area after 6 months of treatment, with mean decreases ranging from 30.2 to 42.3 cm2 (P < 0.001) [30]. Although decreases in lean mass were also observed, the proportion of lean mass relative to total body mass increased [30, 31]. Tirzepatide appears to exert similar effects on body composition. In the SURPASS-3 MRI substudy, all doses significantly reduced VAT, abdominal subcutaneous adipose tissue (ASAT), and the VAT:ASAT ratio [32]. In addition, Heise et al. demonstrated that tirzepatide-induced weight loss was predominantly driven by fat mass reduction (− 9.6 kg vs. placebo, P < 0.001) rather than fat-free mass [33].

Beyond their metabolic effects, incretin-based therapies may influence skeletal homeostasis through multiple biological pathways. Experimental studies suggest that GLP-1 RAs can modulate bone remodeling by promoting osteoblastic activity while suppressing osteoclast-mediated bone resorption [34]. GLP-1 signaling has been shown to enhance osteogenic differentiation and bone formation through activation of pathways such as Wnt/β-catenin signaling, while also reducing osteoclast activity through calcitonin-mediated mechanisms [35–37]. In addition to these direct skeletal effects, GLP-1 RAs may improve the bone marrow microenvironment and systemic inflammatory status. Brady Park et al. reported that semaglutide enhances endogenous vascular repair and increases circulating vascular regenerative progenitor cells while simultaneously suppressing pro-inflammatory granulocyte precursors [38]. These changes may improve the bone marrow niche and support osteogenic differentiation of bone-derived mesenchymal stem cells [38]. Furthermore, GIP may provide additional skeletal benefits by inhibiting osteoclast activity and bone resorption while enhancing osteoblast survival through activation of the cAMP–PKA and PI3K–Akt signaling pathways [39].

Since falls represent a major cause of osteoporotic fractures in older adults, medication safety is particularly important in this population [1]. Although treatment with GLP-1 receptor agonists can lead to some degree of lean mass loss during weight reduction, particularly in older or frail populations, their metabolic and vascular protective effects may mitigate declines in muscle function [40]. Incretin signaling has been suggested to influence skeletal muscle physiology by improving insulin sensitivity, reducing chronic inflammation, and enhancing microvascular perfusion, which may help preserve muscle quality and mitochondrial function [10, 41]. In addition, GLP-1–based therapies are known to improve several comorbid conditions that are strongly associated with fall risk in older adults, including heart failure, peripheral arterial disease, knee osteoarthritis, and other cardiometabolic disorders [42–45]. These effects may contribute to improved physical performance and postural stability. From a clinical perspective, the magnitude of fall risk reduction observed with semaglutide and tirzepatide (HR 0.663–0.664) is broadly comparable to that reported with multifactorial fall prevention programs and exercise-based interventions in community-dwelling older adults [1], suggesting that incretin-based therapy may offer musculoskeletal benefits of clinically relevant magnitude in this population. These findings do not imply that GLP-1 receptor agonists should replace dedicated fracture prevention strategies such as antiresorptive therapy; rather, they suggest that for older adults with T2DM and obesity requiring glucose-lowering treatment, the selection of semaglutide or tirzepatide may confer ancillary musculoskeletal benefits that complement existing osteoporosis management. Prospective studies with bone density and fracture ascertainment as pre-specified endpoints are needed to confirm these observations and to determine whether these agents warrant consideration as part of integrated bone health strategies in this high-risk population.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered. First, this was an observational retrospective study, and residual confounding may remain despite the use of propensity score matching. Second, the database lacked detailed clinical information such as bone mineral density, body composition, physical activity, nutritional status, and detailed frailty assessments, which could influence fracture risk. Third, medication adherence, dosing differences, and duration of therapy could not be fully evaluated using prescription records alone. The requirement for continued prescriptions between 6 and 12 months after the index date constitutes an on-treatment rather than intention-to-treat analytical approach, which may introduce selection bias by preferentially retaining patients who tolerated treatment and had favorable clinical trajectories, potentially overestimating the treatment benefit. To address this limitation, a supplementary ITT analysis was conducted and is presented in Supplementary Tables S2 and S3; the direction of associations was preserved across both analytical frameworks, although effect sizes were attenuated under ITT conditions, particularly for tirzepatide and femoral fracture. Fourth, the one-year follow-up period may not fully capture the long-term skeletal effects of incretin-based therapies. Additionally, ascertainment bias may have affected the fall outcome, as patients using semaglutide or tirzepatide may have more frequent clinical contact, increasing the likelihood of fall documentation via ICD-coded records. Furthermore, despite propensity score matching, some residual imbalance remained in the tirzepatide cohort, particularly for HbA1c (SMD = 0.181) and systolic blood pressure (SMD = 0.142), which may have incompletely controlled for differences in diabetes severity and cardiovascular risk between groups. Finally, the absence of formal sensitivity analyses—such as restricting the cohort to patients not receiving concomitant antiresorptive therapy or varying the medication continuity criteria—limits the ability to fully assess the robustness of the observed associations, and such analyses should be considered in future work.

Future prospective studies and randomized clinical trials with longer follow-up periods are needed to confirm these findings. The use of GLP-1 receptor agonists in this population should still be approached with caution, and adequate protein intake and regular resistance or physical exercise should be encouraged to help preserve muscle mass and functional capacity during weight reduction.

Conclusion

In this large real-world retrospective cohort study, semaglutide and tirzepatide use was associated with lower risks of femoral fracture and falls compared with DPP-4 inhibitors among older adults with type 2 diabetes and overweight or obesity. These findings suggest that incretin-based therapies may provide additional musculoskeletal benefits beyond their established metabolic and cardiovascular effects. However, residual confounding, surveillance bias, and the reliance on ICD-coded fall ascertainment may have influenced these estimates. The independent contribution of weight loss magnitude to musculoskeletal outcomes warrants evaluation in future work.
