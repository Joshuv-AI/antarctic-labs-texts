The Relationship Between Nurses' Spiritual Well‐Being and Patient Privacy: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Serpil Su, Ayşe Kacaroğlu Vicdan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimThis study aimed to examine the relationship between nurses' levels of spiritual well‐being and their status of observing patient privacy.DesignA descriptive and cross‐sectional study was conducted.Methods and Data SourceThe sample comprised 199 nurses working in a university hospital. The study data were collected between 1 January and 28 February 2022, using a Descriptive Characteristics Form, the Spiritual Well‐Being Scale and the Patient Privacy Scale in Nursing. Ethics committee approval was obtained from a state university's Scientific Research Ethics Committee.ResultsIt was determined that the nurses' spiritual well‐being levels were above the medium level, and the patient's privacy was high. A statistically significant positive relationship exists between the Spiritual Well‐Being Scale and Patient Privacy Scale in Nursing scores. The regression model for the total scale showed an R 2 of 29.3%. A point increase in the Spiritual Well‐Being Scale score causes an increase of 0.424 units in the Patient Privacy Scale in Nursing score.ConclusionIt has been determined that as nurses' spiritual well‐being increases, they pay more attention to patient privacy.Implications for the Nursing Profession and Patient CareThis study contributes to the nurses' understanding of the importance of spiritual values in patient privacy and to pay attention to this issue in patient care.ImpactIn this study addressing patient privacy, it was found that nurses' spiritual well‐being is related to patient privacy. The conclusions impact future patients, nurses and managers.Reporting MethodThe study was compliant with the STROBE checklist.Patient or Public ContributionNo patient or public contribution.

Introduction

Today, privacy has come to the fore due to the development of science and technology, the increase in sensitivity to human rights, the creation of national and international legislation on patient rights, and the intense and unlimited penetration of the virtual world into all areas of life (Ak et al. 2021). Personal information's being in electronic form has facilitated data sharing, but it has also raised problems such as violation of privacy. Therefore, it is necessary to protect privacy (Park and Jeong 2021).

Privacy, one of the basic needs of individuals, can be defined as a universal value, the right to protection and a feature of private life (Demirsoy et al. 2021). Patient privacy covers the personal living space of patients. However, routine practices of healthcare personnel in hospitals or other areas where healthcare services are provided limit the personal sovereignty of patients (Özturk et al. 2014). The cultural, spiritual and religious values of nurses with the authority to enter the patient's private space (Hartigan et al. 2018) significantly impact the development of a sense of privacy (Ak et al. 2021). Therefore, there is a need for research that explains the impact of nurses' spiritual values and spiritual well‐being on patient privacy.

Background

Privacy is generally defined as a space where people can be alone, think and act as they wish, and decide where, when and under what conditions and to what extent they will communicate with others (Candan and Bilgili 2018). Privacy is a multidimensional concept that expresses a feeling about individuals' private space as well as their identities, personalities and freedoms and includes physical, social and psychological privacy (Kucukkelepce et al. 2021). In a different definition, privacy is referred to as one of the basic components of providing palliative care. In this definition, privacy is explained as protecting the dignity of the patient, increasing the closeness between the patient and the family, managing family pain and facilitating the implementation of social, cultural and spiritual rituals (Street and Love 2005). Privacy is generally seen as synonymous with the concept of confidentiality. Privacy is something that should be secret and remain secret. Confidentiality is the individual's control over the personal information that others will share. In other words, confidentiality is how privacy is achieved (Okumuş and Atilgan 2021).

Privacy, which is among the basic human rights, comes to be the foremost in the provision of health services. Confidentiality in healthcare services includes protecting and securing patient information (Ceylan and Çetinkaya 2020). The areas of living that patients have to explain for care and treatment or any other reason but do not want other people to know are defined as patient privacy (Candan and Bilgili 2018). In the process of diagnosis and treatment, healthcare professionals can access the secrets of a patient that they do not want to be known by anyone, and sometimes even the patient himself does not know (Ceylan and Çetinkaya 2020). Respecting patient privacy is also an ethical obligation for nurses, who must have the patient 24 h a day and the power to enter the patient's private space (Hartigan et al. 2018). Many national and international nursing organisations, such as the International Council of Nurses, the Canadian Nurses Association, the American Nurses Association and the Turkish Nurses Association, have drawn attention to patient privacy and stated that nurses are responsible for this issue (Öztürk et al. 2021). By paying attention to patient privacy, nurses protect patient dignity, a reassuring relationship is formed between patient and nurse and a safe environment is provided for achieving physical and mental health; thus, the quality of nursing care increases (Sakhaei et al. 2020; Demirsoy et al. 2021).

Protecting patient privacy is included in the spiritual dimension of nursing care. When nurses implement spiritual care initiatives, they primarily reflect their spiritual awareness and personal spirituality degrees to patient care (Taylor et al. 2018). The spiritual identities of nurses play an important role in shaping the behaviours they display in their lives, the attitudes they develop towards events and their perspectives on life (Ceylan and Çetinkaya 2020). Therefore, the spiritual well‐being of nurses affects their role in paying attention to the spiritual aspect of care (Biag and Angeles Jr 2021). Spiritual well‐being is a complex concept with many factors. Three important factors clearly define spiritual well‐being: a meaningful purpose in life, a strong personal value system, and a deep relationship with oneself and others (Ekşi and Kardaş 2017).

The dominant religion in Turkish society is Islam and devotion to religion is at the forefront (Kozani 2022). In Islam, it is argued that special importance should be given to the private sphere of a person. Privacy is protected by the concept of haram, which is forbidden even to approach (Köroğlu 2012). When the studies conducted with nurses belonging to the Islamic religion are examined, in the study of Musa (2017), it was stated that nurses with a high sense of spiritual well‐being are likelier to provide spiritual care interventions to patients. In addition, Musa's (2017) study revealed that nurses more often provide existential aspects of spiritual care, such as respect for privacy and dignity. In a study conducted in Taiwan, Wu et al. (2016) reported that there is a significant relationship between the spiritual knowledge levels of nurses and the spiritual care needs of their patients, and it was determined that nurses consider privacy and respect for dignity as the basic spiritual need of their patients.

Spiritual well‐being has individual, environmental, and social dimensions and directly affects nursing care (Biag and Angeles Jr 2021). In previous studies, nurses' spiritual well‐being was examined, but its relationship with patient privacy was not explained. For this reason, it has been recommended that studies be conducted that explain the effect of nurses' characteristics and values on protecting patient privacy (Kim et al. 2017).

The Study

Aim and Objective

This study aimed to examine the relationship between nurses' levels of spiritual well‐being and their status of observing patient privacy. From this study, there were three specific outcomes to be achieved: (1) determining the spiritual well‐being levels of nurses; (2) clarifying nurses' status of observing patient privacy; (3) revealing the relationship between nurses' spiritual well‐being and their status of observing patient privacy.

Methods

Design

This was a descriptive correlational study with a cross‐sectional survey design following the STROBE checklist.

Setting and Sample

This study was conducted in a state university hospital in Konya, Turkey. The hospital has a bed capacity of 1350 and has the largest patient population in the province. The study included nurses working in the internal medicine (nephrology, gastroenterology, oncology, etc.) and surgical (general surgery, orthopaedics, intensive care, etc.) clinics.

The number of people to include in the sample has been calculated using the website A priori Sample Size Calculator for Multiple Regression (alpha level of 0.05, effect size of 0.15, number of predictors is nine variables and desired statistical power level 0.95), and accessing at least 167 people was accordingly identified as the required sample size (Soper 2021). Considering the possibility that the research may lack certain data, the recommended sample size was increased by approximately 15%, which means 199 nurses were reached. Nurses aged ≥ 18 years who volunteered to participate in the study were included, while nurses on leave at the time of data collection were excluded from the study.

Measurements

The study data were collected using a Descriptive Characteristics Form, the Spiritual Well‐Being Scale (SWBS), and the Patient Privacy Scale (PPS) in nursing.

Descriptive Characteristics Form

This form was created by researching the literature on the subject (Özturk et al. 2014; Biag and Angeles Jr 2021). The form includes nine questions to determine descriptive characteristics such as age, sex, educational status, marital status, clinic work, duration of clinical experience, weekly working time, education on spiritual well‐being and receiving education on patient privacy.

SWBS

The scale was developed for adults to determine the process of understanding and living their lives with personal, social, environmental and transcendental aspects in line with the values and ultimate meanings of human beings. The SWBS was developed in Turkey by Ekşi and Kardaş (2017) to assess how adults' lives are compatible with their values and their understanding of ultimate meaning in personal, social, environmental, and transcendent terms. The scale consists of 29 items and consists of three sub‐dimensions, including ‘transcendence’ (items 1, 4, 5, 8, 9, 12, 13, 16, 17, 20, 21, 24, 25, 27, 29), ‘harmony with nature’ (items 2, 6, 10, 14, 18, 22, 28) and ‘anomie’ (items 3, 7, 11, 15, 19, 23, 26). The scale is a five‐point Likert‐type scale with response options ranging from 1 = Not suitable for me to 5 = Completely suitable. A minimum of 29 points and a maximum of 145 points can be obtained from the scale. While the high scores of ‘transcendence (minimum 15, maximum 75 points)’ and ‘harmony with nature (minimum 7, maximum 35 points)’ of the scale indicate positive well‐being (increases spiritual well‐being), high scores in the ‘anomie (minimum 7, maximum 35 points)’ subscale are interpreted as negativity (a negative concept that pushes people to unhappiness and loneliness). Anomie means that while the norms of society lose supervision and control over human behaviour, the individual is uncertain about how he or she should behave. The Cronbach's Alpha value of the scale is 0.886 (Ekşi and Kardaş 2017). In this study, the Cronbach alpha values were found to be 0.870 in the transcendence sub‐dimension, 0.835 in the harmony with nature sub‐dimension, 0.768 in the anomie sub‐dimension, and 0.848 in the total scale.

PPS in Nursing

The scale, developed by Özturk et al. (2014), was developed to determine whether nurses behave appropriately or take care to protect and maintain the privacy of patients hospitalised in the service/units of hospitals and whether there are privacy violations. The scale is a 5‐point Likert‐type scale with response options 5 = Totally agree, 4 = Agree, 3 = Undecided, 2 = Disagree, 1 = Strongly disagree. It consists of a total of 27 items. The content validity index of the scale is 0.91, Croncbach's Alpha value is 0.93, and the Spearman–Brown and Guttman coefficient is 0.85. The scale consists of the subscales of confidentiality of private life/personal information (Croncbach's Alpha 0.90), sexual privacy (Croncbach's Alpha 0.77), the privacy of those unable to protect themselves (Croncbach Alpha 0.82), physical privacy (Croncbach's Alpha 0.84), ensuring a favourable environment (Croncbach's Alpha 0.77). The total score on the scale ranges between 27–135 points. A score close to 135 obtained from the scale indicates that nurses pay attention to patient privacy, while scores close to 27 show that they do not pay attention (Özturk et al. 2014). In this study, the Cronbach's Alpha value was found to be 0.950.

Data Collection

Before the study data were collected, a preliminary application was made with 15 nurses who were not included in the sample, and the comprehensibility of the questionnaires was evaluated. The study data were collected using a questionnaire between 1 January and 28 February 2022. Before data collection, nurses were informed about the study, and their written consent was obtained. A questionnaire form was given to the nurses who volunteered to participate in the study, and the forms were collected by the researcher after they had been completed.

Data Analysis

The SPSS 22.0 package program was used to analyse the data. Kolmogorov–Smirnov was used to check whether the assumptions were met. The data appeared to be distributed normally. Sociodemographic characteristics of the participants, such as gender, education level, marital status, department, education on spiritual well‐being, and education on patient privacy, were analysed as frequencies and percentages. In analysing sociodemographic characteristics such as age, duration of working in the profession, weekly working time and scale scores, mean, standard deviation, minimum and maximum values were used. Cronbach's α internal consistency coefficient was used to assess the reliability of the scales. Differences in the levels of SWBS and PPS according to the descriptive characteristics (gender, marital status, department, receiving education on spiritual well‐being and receiving education on patient privacy) of the participants were analysed using a t‐test. The analysis of variance test was used to determine the differences between SWBS and PPS levels according to education status. The correlation between SWBS and PPS scores was analysed using the Pearson correlation coefficient. The effect of demographic variables on PPS measurement was investigated using multiple linear regression analysis, stepwise method, but results could not be reported due to the absence of significant factors. Due to the significant effect of only the SWBS measurement on the PPS measurement, the autocorrelation result (Durbin‐Watson) between the independent variables and the multicollinearity problem (VIF) value are not given. The effect of SBWS on the PPS scale score was evaluated by simple linear regression. The data were accepted as significant at the confidence interval of 95% (p < 0.05).

Ethical Considerations

For the implementation of the research, ethics committee approval was obtained from the Scientific Research Ethics Committee of a state university (No:15/96, Date: 03/11/2021), and institutional permission was obtained from the hospital where the research was conducted. Before the study, the purpose of the study was explained to the nurses, and their informed consent was obtained, stating that their participation in the study was in line with the principle of voluntariness. In addition, the nurses were informed that their information would be kept confidential and that they could withdraw from the study at any time. Permission was obtained from the authors to use the scales used in data collection.

Discussion

This correlational descriptive study was conducted to examine the relationship between nurses' spiritual well‐being levels and their status of observing patient privacy. According to the results obtained from the study, it was determined that the nurses' spiritual well‐being levels were above the medium level, their observing patient privacy was at a high level, and with the increase in the spiritual well‐being level, the patient privacy increased. No other study in the literature reveals the relationship between nurses' spiritual well‐being levels and their observance of patient privacy. In this respect, the results of the study were discussed in light of the relevant literature.

In this study, it was determined that the nurses' spiritual well‐being levels were above the medium level (121.48 ± 15.32). Similarly, in studies examining the spiritual well‐being levels of intensive care nurses, it was stated that the spiritual well‐being of nurses was above the medium level (Kutlu et al. 2020). In studies conducted with Muslim nurses, the spiritual well‐being levels of nurses were found to be moderate (Musa et al. 2019; Soleimani et al. 2019). In another study that examined the spiritual well‐being levels of Muslim nurses, it was stated that the spiritual well‐being levels of the nurses were high (Musa 2017). Spirituality and spiritual values have a special place in Islam (Soleimani et al. 2019); therefore, nurses' spiritual well‐being levels may be high in these studies conducted with Muslim nurses. It is stated that a patient's ability to understand, evaluate, and deal with their spirituality depends on the nurse's self‐awareness of their spirituality through self‐reflection (Jahandideh et al. 2018). Nurses' high level of spiritual well‐being indicates that their relationship with themselves, others, their environment, and God is of high quality. This suggests that the spiritual care provided by nurses to patients is sufficient (Biag and Angeles Jr 2021).

The anomie sub‐dimension score was found to be higher in single nurses than in married nurses. In the scale, the concept of anomie is stated as the negativity that pushes people to unhappiness and loneliness (Ekşi and Kardaş 2017). In this respect, it is thought that single nurses have high anomie scores because they live alone and lead a more individual life. In addition, in this study, it was found that anomie scores increased in young nurses. Considering the younger age of single nurses, these two findings support each other. On the other hand, studies show that married nurses have high levels of spiritual well‐being (Kim and Yeom 2018; Soleimani et al. 2019), which supports the study findings. In this study, it was determined that there was an increase in the spiritual well‐being levels of the older nurses. Kutlu et al. (2020) similarly found a positive relationship between age and spiritual well‐being in their study conducted with intensive care nurses. In the study of Kim and Yeom (2018), it was stated that the spiritual well‐being scores of nurses aged ≥ 30 years were higher. With the increase in age, commitment to social and cultural values increases, the meaning and purpose of life are understood, and the expectations of individuals from life become clear. Due to such reasons, it is thought that the spiritual well‐being levels of older nurses increased. On the other hand, in this study, the spiritual well‐being levels of nurses with years of experience were found to be high. These two findings are parallel.

A positive relationship was found between the working years of nurses and their spiritual well‐being levels. In the study of Kutlu et al. (2020), it was similarly stated that as the working years of nurses increased, their spiritual well‐being levels also increased. In the study of Kim and Yeom (2018), it was reported that nurses with > 5 years of nursing practice or intensive care experience had higher SWBS scores. A similar study revealed that nurses with 11–19 years of work experience had higher spiritual well‐being levels than nurses with less work experience (Wu and Lin 2011). In the literature, studies are showing that spiritual well‐being decreases with the increase in working years. Jahandideh et al. (2018) reported a negative relationship between nurses' work experience and SWBS scores. The reason for this difference may be related to the job satisfaction of nurses. In the literature, it is stated that one of the factors affecting the spiritual well‐being of nurses is job satisfaction (Soleimani et al. 2019). Additionally, in this study, it was determined that the level of spiritual well‐being decreased with the increase in the weekly working time. This finding is thought to be related to high workload and low job satisfaction. Because excessive weekly working hours increase the workload and cause problems such as stress, fatigue, burnout, decrease in job satisfaction, insomnia and physical diseases in nurses (Geniş et al. 2020; Varghese et al. 2023). All these problems negatively affect the spiritual well‐being levels of nurses (Al‐Osaimi et al. 2023; Amiri et al. 2023). In line with these results, it is thought that the spiritual well‐being levels of the nurses will be positively affected by the low number of patients per nurse, the sufficient number of nurses, and the fact that they will not have long working hours.

In this study, it was found that the nurses' observance of patient privacy was at a high level. Privacy is among the most important human rights that must be followed in institutions providing healthcare services, and nurses act in accordance with ethical principles to protect patient privacy. Thus, a trust‐based relationship is established between the nurse and the patient (Demirsoy et al. 2021). In a similar study conducted with paediatric nurses, it was seen that nurses observed patient privacy (Ceylan and Çetinkaya 2020). Akar et al. (2019) reported that nurses respect patient privacy. In a study that examined patient privacy from the perspective of patients and nurses, it was stated that while nurses' patient privacy scores were high, patients also stated that the nurses paid attention to privacy (Öztürk et al. 2021). Again, in similar studies conducted in Turkey, it is stated that nurses have positive attitudes and behaviours regarding patient privacy (Özata and Kubilay 2017). In studies examining patient opinions, patients stated that nurses pay attention to privacy (Akar et al. 2019; Öztürk et al. 2021). On the other hand, in a study conducted in Korea, it was stated that although nurses care about patient privacy, they do not pay much attention to patient privacy (Kim et al. 2017). This difference is related to the fact that when privacy is mentioned in Turkey, physical privacy comes to mind more (Ak et al. 2021). Almost all of the nurses in Turkey are Muslims, and it is considered a sin to see one's private areas in Islam (Ak et al. 2021). In addition, it is thought that nurses unintentionally gossip among themselves and share patient information and do not perceive this as a violation of privacy. In the study of Ceylan and Çetinkaya (2020), it was stated that nurses who exhibit gossipy behaviour take privacy less into consideration.

In this study, it was found that married nurses pay more attention to bodily privacy. Similar to this study, Kim et al. (2017) reported that married nurses pay more attention to patient privacy. In another study that examined the views of nurses and doctors on patient privacy, it was stated that single nurses were more sensitive to patient privacy (Demirsoy et al. 2021). Tanrıkulu et al. (2020) found that marital status was not effective in paying attention to patient privacy. The reason for the different study results may be due to the individual characteristics of nurses.

In this study, it was determined that older nurses attach more importance to patient privacy. We think that the reason for this is related to the fact that older nurses have more years of experience. This study found that nurses with more experience attach more importance to patient privacy. In a similar study conducted by Demirsoy et al. (2021), it was stated that nurses aged ≥ 48 years and nurses with ≥ 21 years of professional experience had higher patient privacy scores. In addition, there are studies showing that clinical experience is ineffective in observing patient privacy (Kim et al. 2017). This difference may be due to the change in working environments. With the increase in nurses' clinical experience in hospitals in Turkey, there is an approach such as working more in day shifts. In parallel with the low number of patients per nurse during the morning shift, the low workload may have increased the attention to privacy. Demirsoy et al. (2021) support this view as a result of the study. In this study, it was reported that nurses who do not work in the night shift pay more attention to privacy (Demirsoy et al. 2021). In a similar study, it was stated that the large hospital size, high number of patients and high workload make it difficult to provide patient privacy (Öztürk et al. 2021). Again, in this study, the fact that with the increase in weekly working hours, nurses pay less attention to bodily privacy and provide a safe environment is another finding that shows the effect of workload. Varol (2018) reported a negative relationship between long working hours and attention to patient privacy. The literature indicates that long working hours negatively affect professional behaviours in nurses (Bozkurt et al. 2019). We think that our study result is related to these factors and more studies are needed on this subject. In addition, in our study, nurses working in surgical clinics paid less attention to the privacy of patients who could not protect themselves. This may be due to the high patient turnover in surgical clinics.

In this study, a positive relationship was found between nurses' spiritual well‐being levels and their status of protecting patient privacy. Nurses' spiritual well‐being was found to be an important factor in predicting patient privacy. People's cultural and religious values are the two most important factors in developing a sense of privacy. The formation of the privacy boundaries of the individual takes place by being influenced by the cultural views and religious values of the society in which he lives. The Turkish society, where most people are Muslims, is also affected by cultural views and religious and spiritual values on privacy (Ak et al. 2021). The only study similar to ours was conducted on student nurses. In the study, the relationship between the spiritual attitudes of nursing students and their protection of patient privacy was examined, and it was stated that students with good spiritual attitudes protect patient privacy more (Sakhaei et al. 2020). Wu et al. (2016) reported an important relationship between nurses' spiritual knowledge levels and their patients' spiritual care needs and that nurses express respect for privacy and dignity as basic spiritual needs of patients (Wu et al. 2016). Again, it is stated in the literature that the spiritual characteristics of nurses strengthen the commitment to ethical principles, including privacy (Abbaszadeh et al. 2015). In addition, nurses with high anomie scores were found to have low levels of observing privacy. Assuming that these nurses are unhappy and lonely, it can be thought that they have difficulty observing patient privacy due to decreased job satisfaction.

Limitations

This study is one of the first to explore the relationship between nurses' spiritual well‐being and patient privacy. It is necessary to point out some limitations of this study. First, the surveys were voluntary. Hence, the results may not represent all nurses, as only those interested participated. Those interested in participating in the survey may have a higher level of spiritual well‐being and be more careful about patient privacy than those who do not respond. The most important limitation of the study is that it focused on a hospital in Turkey. Therefore, the results cannot be generalised to other cultures, religious beliefs, or Turkey. Nurses' social, cultural, and religious beliefs effectively achieved the results. Cultural differences should be taken into account. Another limitation is that the high average scores observed on both scales may indicate a potential ceiling effect that could limit variability and reduce the strength of the relationships. Since all variables are measured at a single point in time using self‐report instruments, the results may be subject to common method bias and inflate the observed relationships. Multiple statistical tests were conducted, which may increase the risk of Type I error. No formal correction was applied; therefore, findings should be interpreted with caution.

Conclusion

Patient privacy, an important concept that concerns all healthcare professionals, is an indicator of quality of care. In this study, it was found that nurses with high spiritual well‐being levels pay more attention to patient privacy. Nurses' spiritual well‐being is an important factor that is related to patient privacy. This result can guide nursing practices specific to spiritual well‐being and patient privacy. In addition, older nurses with more years of nursing experience are more careful about patient privacy. For this, training can be organised for young nurses who are new to the job. Case discussions that improve patient privacy behaviours can be held during these trainings.

In our study, it was observed that married nurses were more careful about bodily privacy than single nurses. For this reason, shifts can be arranged where married and single nurses work together. In this way, married nurses become role models for single nurses, and patients' physical privacy is better provided. In addition, nurses working in surgical clinics had some deficiencies in paying attention to the privacy of patients who could not protect themselves. In this regard, institutional policies can be created to reduce patient circulation in surgical clinics. Nurses should protect the rights of patients who cannot protect themselves, especially in surgical clinics. In this study, it was observed that spiritual well‐being levels decreased in nurses who worked more hours per week and they paid less attention to physical privacy and providing a safe environment. According to this results, managers should be sensitive about this issue, regulate nurses' working hours, and take measures to reduce workload. Additionally, studies can be conducted examining the effect of nurses' excessive weekly working hours on patient privacy.

Relevance to Clinical Practice

Observing the patient's privacy is very important in protecting human dignity and ethical approach. The results obtained from this study will guide nurses in increasing their awareness of spiritual and humanitarian values and transferring them to patient care. Additionally, these results may be useful in developing institutional policies to reduce deficiencies related to patient privacy. It is thought that in‐service training will be effective in increasing the sensitivity to respect for privacy and learning the legal dimension of the issue. In addition, the concept of patient confidentiality can be given more space in the nursing curriculum. In particular, its relationship with culture, spirituality, and religious belief should be emphasised and reflected in clinical practice. Educators should be role models for students in observing patient confidentiality in clinical practice. Thus, future nurses will be equipped to create a hospital environment that protects patients' privacy and implements practices that ensure privacy/confidentiality. Another suggestion is that nurses can be given training on the importance of socialisation and using methods such as meditation, yoga, and conscious awareness to increase their spiritual well‐being. Finally, since spirituality and privacy have different meanings and roles in different cultures and religious beliefs, it is recommended that the study be conducted in different cultures. Qualitative and experimental studies can be conducted to reveal the relationship between spiritual well‐being and patient privacy.
