The BrainDrugs-epilepsy study: A prospective open-label cohort precision medicine study in epilepsy
Authors: Maja R. Marstrand-Joergensen, Vibeke H. Dam, Kirsten Vinter, Cheng-Teng Ip, Kristian Reveles Jensen, Martin Balslev Jørgensen, Christina E. Hoei-Hansen, Brice Ozenne, Patrick M. Fisher, Gitte M. Knudsen, Lars H. Pinborg
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe BrainDrugs-Epilepsy Study is part of the established Lundbeck Foundation funded BrainDrugs research alliance (braindrugs.nru.dk). The study aims to identify biomarkers of treatment outcome as well as cognitive and psychiatric comorbidity in patients with a first epileptic seizure and epilepsy. Our goal is to provide a patient-level prediction of treatment response.MethodsIn a prospective cohort study design, we will consecutively include a total of 200 patients who have experienced their first epileptic seizure and 150 newly diagnosed patients with epilepsy (≥ 16 years old). We will systematically collect data at baseline and one, three, and five years after inclusion. Neuropsychiatric and neuropsychological assessments will be combined with electroencephalography (EEG) and neuroimaging tools such as Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) and Positron Emission Tomography (PET) brain scans using the synaptic vesicle glycoprotein 2A-binding radioligand [11C]-UCB-J. After completing the baseline investigations, patients diagnosed with epilepsy start anti-seizure medication (ASM) treatment with either lamotrigine or levetiracetam. Patients will be monitored every three months. For daily monitoring, we will use a mobile app. Our clinical endpoints are 1) ASM treatment response, 2) development of a second seizure/epilepsy diagnosis, 3) psychiatric symptoms, 4) cognitive disturbances, and 5) quality of life.DiscussionWe hypothesize that seizures, cognitive impairment, and psychiatric symptoms share common neurobiological mechanisms that can be identified using MRI, PET, and EEG. We will assess how these biomarkers change over time and in response to treatment, providing novel insights into disease trajectories in the first years of epilepsy.Clinical trial registrationNCT05450822. Retrospectively registered July 5, 2022 (https://clinicaltrials.gov/ct2/results?term=NCT05450822&Search=Search).

Introduction

For most patients, suffering from epilepsy has a significant impact on socioeconomic status and quality of life due to recurrent seizures and a high frequency of comorbidities, and even with the best possible combination of ASMs, one-third of patients do not attain sustained seizure freedom (Perucca, 2019; Kwan and Brodie, 2000; Chen et al., 2018a). For most patients, it is possible to predict the likelihood of achieving long-term seizure freedom within the first year based on their response to the first ASM (Chen et al., 2018a). Notably, in new-onset epilepsy patients, a history of psychiatric disease is significantly more frequent than expected from the background population (Boylan et al., 2004; Fiest et al., 2013; Friedman et al., 2009; Kim et al., 2018; Hesdorffer et al., 2006), and likewise, at the time of diagnosis, cognition is often impaired (Hermann et al., 2006; Taylor et al., 2010a; Witt and Helmstaedter, 2012). Importantly, knowledge of etiology, seizure, and/or epilepsy type does not allow the clinician to make significant predictions on the disease course of individual patients. Carefully designed prospective cohort studies are necessary to deconstruct factors critical to the course of epilepsy and identify measurable biomarkers relevant to prognosis and clinical endpoints. We hypothesize that premorbid factors (e.g., scores of psychometrics and neuropsychological testing) as well as morphological and functional biomarkers of brain network integrity using Positron Emission Tomography (PET), Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI), and electroencephalography (EEG) are abnormal already at the time of diagnosis and may serve as biomarkers for future treatment response.

We aim to establish cohorts consisting of patients who have experienced their first epileptic seizure (Cohort I) but do not fulfill the International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE) criteria for the diagnosis of epilepsy (Fisher et al., 2014) along with patients newly diagnosed with epilepsy (Cohort II-III). The ultimate goal of the study is to predict patient-level treatment response, epilepsy-related comorbidities and quality of life from various biomarkers, clinical measures, risk and protective factors that can aid treatment decisions in the clinical setting.

Identification of predictors of treatment response and comorbidities

Risk/protective factors

Epidemiological data suggest that factors such as etiology, family history of epilepsy, early age of onset, and number of seizures before diagnosis may determine a poor drug-response in epilepsy patients (Mohanraj and Brodie, 2013). Further evidence supports that coping style (Westerhuis et al., 2011), lifestyle (Johnson et al., 2019; Hauser et al., 1988), personality traits (Cano-López et al., 2019; Alonso et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2018; Rivera Bonet et al., 2019), stressful life events (Nimmo-Smith et al., 2016; Baldin et al., 2017), social relations (Beghi, 2019; Lin et al., 2020) and structural lesions (Bernasconi et al., 2019) are important for seizure frequency, treatment response and development of comorbidities.

Clinical measures (psychiatric symptoms and cognition)

Mood disorders (Kanner, 2013) and anxiety (Nogueira et al., 2017) are associated with drug-resistance and are most likely linked bidirectionally to epilepsy (Kanner, 2017a; Kanner et al., 2012; Nilsson et al., 2003) with shared underlying pathologies (Kanner et al., 2012; Kanner, 2017b). Depression (Boylan et al., 2004; Fiest et al., 2013; Friedman et al., 2009; Kim et al., 2018) and anxiety (Brandt et al., 2010; Hesdorffer et al., 2012) are frequent psychiatric disorders among epilepsy patients, affecting quality of life and seizure frequency, and psychiatric symptoms are associated with early mortality (Christensen et al., 2007; Fazel et al., 2013).

There is evidence of cognitive deficits in children (Hermann et al., 2006), in nearly half of newly diagnosed drug-naïve adult patients (Taylor et al., 2010a; Witt and Helmstaedter, 2012), and even before epilepsy is diagnosed (Taylor et al., 2010b; Helmstaedter and Witt, 2017).

It is speculated that epileptic seizures and comorbidities may share a common underlying brain condition, anatomical pathways, and networks (Hamid et al., 2011; Kanner, 2012; Adelow et al., 2012). Psychiatric, behavioural, and cognitive comorbidities may be more correctly considered constituent parts of epilepsy rather than comorbidity per se, defined as an association of etiologically unrelated conditions (Salpekar and Mula, 2018). This idea is supported by register studies and retrospective cohort studies demonstrating that epilepsy is associated with an increased incidence rate ratio of psychosis, anxiety, depression, and suicidality both before and after epilepsy diagnosis (Hesdorffer et al., 2006, 2012, 2016; Brandt et al., 2010). Moreover, there is a negative feedback loop, where psychiatric comorbidity is predictive of drug resistance (Nogueira et al., 2017; Hitiris et al., 2007) and reduced ASM adherence (DiMatteo et al., 2000) and drug-resistant epilepsy is negatively affecting both emotional well-being, cognitive and social function (Pauli et al., 2012).

Non-imaging biological measures

Inflammation and infections are involved in developing many types of epilepsies (Ravizza et al., 2011; Vezzani et al., 2011, 2016). Genetics could be potential biomarkers for treatment outcomes in epilepsy patients (Shazadi et al., 2014; Sánchez Fernández et al., 2019). Genetic mutations have been shown to influence both brain epileptogenic vulnerability and treatment efficacy (Noebels, 2015), and studies have reported microribonucleic acids (microRNA) as diagnostic biomarkers for epilepsy and drug-resistance (An et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2015a, 2015b). Furthermore, gut microbial biomarkers for treatment response are promising but require validation in larger cohorts and drug-naïve patients. Pilot studies report that gut microbiota shows distinct microbial signatures in drug-resistant epilepsy and that a ketogenic diet (Lindefeldt et al., 2019) and probiotic administration (Gómez-Eguílaz et al., 2018) lower seizure frequency.

Neuroimaging and electrophysiology

Two of the most commonly used ASMs include the sodium channel-blocking lamotrigine (LTG) (Leach and Brodie, 1995) and levetiractam (LEV), which targets the synaptic vesicle glycoprotein 2A (SV2A) (Lynch et al., 2004). SV2A is validated in both non-human and human studies as an indirect measure of synaptic density (Finnema et al., 2016), and recent PET studies report that severity of depressive symptomatology (Holmes et al., 2019), amnestic mild cognitive impairment (Vanhaute et al., 2020) and degree of cognitive dysfunction in Alzheimer's disease (Chen et al., 2018b) are negatively correlated with SV2A density. The radioligand [11C]-UCB-J binds to SV2A (Finnema et al., 2016), and PET [11C]-UCB-J studies have provided evidence of decreased synaptic density in epilepsy patients (Finnema et al., 2016, 2020). In vivo, investigations of how SV2A density is associated with cognitive and psychiatric comorbidities among newly diagnosed epilepsy patients have not yet been conducted. It is of particular interest to use [11C]-UCB-J PET to study the pharmacokinetics of the SV2A binding of LEV to improve our understanding of how synaptic density and SV2A occupancy correlate with treatment response, drug-resistance and adverse reactions. Additionally, a recent PET-MR neuroimaging study using [11C]-UCB-J showed cerebral blood flow decreased after Padsevonil intervention (Michel Koole et al., 2023). The primary regions of interests (ROIs) were chosen because of their importance in epilepsy, cognition, and emotional processing and include the neocortex, hippocampus, entorhinal cortex, fusiform gyrus, dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, ventrolateral prefrontal cortex, striatum, orbitofrontal cortex, anterior cingulate cortex, and amygdala.

For an overview of relevant brain regions, electrophysiology, and multimodal neuroimaging associated with epilepsy, see Supplementary Material.

Material and methods

The BrainDrugs Epilepsy Study will be conducted as an open-label, longitudinal, prospective cohort study (Fig. 1).

Participants

We aim to include 350 patients and 50 healthy subjects (HC) during the first three years of the study. All patients will be followed for five years. Patients will be recruited from either the epilepsy or first-seizure outpatient clinic via the Department of Neurology or the Department of Paediatrics and Adolescent Medicine at Copenhagen University Hospital, Rigshospitalet, Denmark. Enrolled patients can also be referred to these clinics from other parts of the Capital Region or Northern Zealand in Denmark to meet recruitment requirements. At the standard diagnostic evaluation at the outpatient clinic, patients will briefly be informed about the study and asked if a study investigator may contact them to invite them to the study. Healthy controls will be contacted by a study investigator from a locally established database of potential volunteers at the Neurobiology Research Unit and via advertisements online. If the patient or the healthy subject accepts the investigator will screen for in- and exclusion criteria and provide oral and written participant information and rights. Patients and healthy controls are given a minimum of 24 hours to read the information thoroughly, and only hereafter will they be permitted to submit their signed consent form. Participants have the unconditional right to withdraw from the study at any point. In addition, data from Danish health registries (e.g., the Danish National Prescription Registry, The Danish National Patient Registry, the Medical Birth Registry, and the Alcohol Dependency Registry) and electronic medical records will be used to characterize patients both retrospectively (e.g., information about birth complications) and prospectively (e.g., clinical status) during the study period.

In Cohort I, we will include 150 patients (≥16 years old) referred to clinical care after experiencing their first epileptic seizure but do not fulfill the diagnostic criteria for epilepsy. In Cohort II, we will include 200 newly diagnosed patients with epilepsy (≥16 years old). Patients in Cohort I diagnosed with epilepsy upon experiencing their second epileptic seizure will be included in Cohort II. Lastly, Cohort III will be a subset of approximately 45 adult patients (≥18 years old) from Cohort II with focal onset seizures who will undergo investigation with PET.

Inclusion of patients

Patients included in the study will be newly diagnosed with epilepsy according to the ILAE criteria (Fisher et al., 2014) or patients with a first epileptic seizure, between 16 and 55 years old for Cohort I-II and 18–55 for Cohort III. Patients in Cohort III must have an epileptogenic lesion on MRI, concordant with seizure semiology and/or EEG. Exclusion criteria are: life expectancy <10 years, known genetic syndromes, psychomotor retardation, disease associated with gross morphological brain changes such as brain tumour, major stroke or significant traumatic brain injury, body weight less than 40 kg, reduced kidney function (i.e., glomerular filtration rate (GFR) < 80 ml/min or 50 ml/min for patients 16–17 years old or ≥18 years old, respectively), moderately reduced liver function, cardiac conduction disorders (e.g., Brugada syndrome, long QT-syndrome), medication incompatible with study aims or causing interactions with the administered LEV or LTG therapy (e.g., SV2A binding agents, monoamine oxidase inhibitors, fluvoxamine, methotrexate, benzodiazepines, phenobarbital, carbamazepine, valproate, or regular use of other ASMs), contraindication for MRI (e.g., magnetic implants, pacemaker), inability to complete PET (Cohort III and HC only) or MRI scans (e.g., claustrophobia, issues with back pain), exposure to radioactivity >10 mSv within the last year or significant occupational exposure to radioactivity (Cohort III and HC only), pregnancy or lactation, pronounced visual or auditory impairments or severe intellectual disability, current or a significant previous alcohol or drug abuse, and allergies to substances used for the PET scan (Cohort III and HC only).

Inclusion of healthy controls

All HCs will be screened for neurological and psychiatric disorders by a trained clinician. Additionally, HCs will be screened with PSE and a self-report MDI and GAD-7 questionnaire. Cognitive functioning will be ensured by cognitive testing. HC should be ≥ 18 years and will have no history of or current psychiatric, neurological, or severe somatic illness. The exclusion criteria for HC are similar to the exclusion criteria for patients. HC will be age, sex, and educationally matched to the patients.

Investigative programs

Baseline assessments for healthy subjects and patients before treatment

Patients and healthy subjects will undergo an examination program (Fig. 1) that includes a neuropsychiatric interview and examination, neuropsychological tests and questionnaires, high density EEG (HD EEG), MRI brain scan (including T1, T2, fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR), DTI, arterial spin labeling (ASL), and rsfMRI), blood and urine samples, as well as gut microbiome samples (Cohort II-III). Patients in Cohort I-III will be invited for a study nurse interview with the setup of a mobile app. In addition to the examination program, patients in Cohort III and healthy subjects will undergo a [11C]-UCB-J PET brain scan followed by intravenous administration of LEV in a displacement paradigm. Various questionnaires will be sent with instructions to complete them online (time length to complete: approximately 2 hours). All meetings and examinations will be conducted in an outpatient clinical setting at Copenhagen University Hospital, Rigshospitalet, by a study physician or a trained research assistant. Well in advance, the participant will receive a personal schedule for the examinations. The aim is to carry out the examinations within a few days to minimize the inconvenience for the participant and, thus, maximize the retention of the study. Additionally, to increase retention, research assistants will assist patients with examinations whenever needed and systematically remind patients of upcoming appointments through telephone calls and via the mobile app. If a patient withdraws consent for a specific examination in the study, the patient will be given the option to continue with examinations for other outcomes.

Monotherapy with levetiracetam or lamotrigine

After completing the basic investigative program, patients in Cohort II will be randomized to receive monotherapy with either LTG or LEV. The randomization will be stratified by sex and age. The randomization will be conducted using computer methods by administrative research staff who are not otherwise involved in the study. Randomization only occurs after the patient is recruited and has completed baseline examinations, ensuring allocation concealment. It is well known that ASM can both trigger and worsen epilepsy-related comorbidities and alter brain functional networks. It is important to emphasize that the randomization in Cohort II is not carried out to test the effect of LEV or LTG but, to some extent, to control the effect of the ASM on EEG, MRI, and PET biomarkers and comorbidities. For this reason, investigators are not blinded to group allocation. Patients in Cohort III will start ASM treatment with LEV. A randomization procedure will not occur for Cohort III as the effect of LEV will be tested with [11C]-UCB-J PET.

After completing the basic investigative program, patients will enter a 4–7 weeks titration period receiving increasing doses of either 50 mg LTG or 250 mg LEV tablets (Cohort II) or increasing doses of 250 mg LEV tablets (Cohort III).

During weeks 5–30, patients will enter an evaluation period where, following standard treatment procedures, the daily dose can be increased by 50 mg LTG every week or 250 mg LEV twice daily every second week if seizures continue and if the patient does not report any clinically relevant adverse reactions. Reductions in dosage (25 mg LTG or 250 mg LEV) as per evaluation by the clinician will be made if the patient develops intolerable adverse reactions.

The dosing schedule will be maintained unless seizures reoccur or clinically significant adverse reactions attributable to the ASM therapy emerge.

Change in ASM therapy

In cases where the patient is experiencing unacceptable seizure control and/or intolerable adverse events or adverse reactions that cannot be managed by dose adjustment, LEV or LTG will be discontinued gradually over 2–4 weeks, and the patient will be shifted to a second-line treatment. After discontinuation, the patient will enter the 4–7 weeks titration period.

Drug treatment failure

A first-drug treatment failure is defined as discontinuing the ASM due to a lack of seizure control despite tolerating the medication at maximum doses (Kwan et al., 2010). If the medication controls the seizures to a reasonable degree but not entirely at the maximal therapeutic dose without causing adverse effects, an adjunct ASM will not be added; instead, the drug will be changed to the second-line treatment. These cases will also be considered a treatment failure in the present study.

Drug-resistant epilepsy

Drug-resistant epilepsy is defined when at least two different, well-tolerated, and appropriately chosen ASMs, either as monotherapy or in combination, have been used at suitable doses for a sufficient period without achieving sustained seizure freedom (Kwan et al., 2010). Seizure freedom is defined as three times the longest preintervention interseizure interval. Thus, the drug responsiveness is classified as “resistant” until sufficient time has passed for the individual patient (Kwan et al., 2010).

ASM compliance

Compliance will be ensured by asking the patient and monitoring ASM serum concentrations. ASM medication prescription and usage will also be extracted from national shared medication records. The duration of the ASM exposure and the reasons for discontinuation, will be noted.

Adverse events and reactions

Adverse events and reactions will be collected systematically using the LEAP scale. All patients with adverse events or reactions will be treated following standard clinical guidelines.

Follow-up assessments of patients

One, three, and five years after inclusion, all patients in Cohorts I-III will undergo an examination program similar to their baseline examination program (Fig. 1). Furthermore, patients in Cohort I-III will undergo clinical assessments every 3–6 months throughout the entire study period. During the clinical assessments, systematic data collection will take place, including information about seizure control, results of examinations (EEG, MRI), epilepsy etiology, cognitive and psychological symptoms, or other symptoms/diseases relevant to the study, as guided by SmartPhrases integrated into the electronic patient record. Patients fulfilling the diagnostic criteria of epilepsy during the observational period will be included in Cohort II. For patients in Cohort II-III, we also use a smartphone app that monitors symptoms daily.

Examination modalities

PET imaging

For HCs and patients in Cohort III, a [11C]-UCB-J PET scan will be conducted on a 3-T Siemens Biograph mMR hybrid PET/MR system (Siemens Healthcare, Erlangen, Germany). A catheter will be placed in the elbow vein to inject the tracer. Dynamic PET neuroimaging with [11C]-UCB-J with subsequent kinetic modeling will allow for the determination of SV2A binding. To measure the radiolabelled tracer's arterial input function, including its radiolabelled metabolites, blood samples will be drawn during the PET scan from an arterial catheter placed in the radial artery or possibly by venous sampling.

Intravenous administration of LEV during PET scans

A maximum dose of 30 mg/kg of the ASM LEV (not exceeding 2500 mg) will be administered intravenously during the PET scan at baseline and follow-up to block [11C]-UCB-J binding to SV2A (Rossano et al., 2020). Quantification of serum LEV concentrations will be performed.

Preprocessing and PET quantification

Dynamic PET will be acquired for 120 min. PET data will be reconstructed and motion corrected. Structural T1 and T2 MRI scans will be used for co-registration of the PET images. Attenuation correction will be done by using Magnetic Resonance Attenuation Correction (Ladefoged et al., 2023). PET images will be processed using PVElab (Svarer et al., 2005). Primary ROIs were chosen because of their importance in epilepsy, cognition, and emotional processing and include neocortex, hippocampus, entorhinal cortex, fusiform gyrus, dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, ventrolateral prefrontal cortex, striatum, orbitofrontal cortex, anterior cingulate cortex, and amygdala. PET images will be quantified using well-validated kinetic models. The outcome of PET studies is binding potentials, volume of distribution, and occupancy.

MRI imaging

Magnetic resonance brain imaging will be conducted using a 32-channel head coil on a Siemens 3-T Magnetom Prisma scanner. All participants will be screened for MR-compatibility. A urine sample will test for drug consumption and pregnancy. During the rs-fMRI scan, participants will be asked to keep their eyes closed without falling asleep. MRI data will be processed using, e.g., SPM12 software (https://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/, Wellcome Department of Cognitive Neurology, London, UK), BASIL in FSL (FMRIB software library, https://fsl.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/fsl/fslwiki/BASIL) and FreeSurfer (Athinoula A. Martinos Center for Biomedical Imaging, Boston, MA, USA) using the most recent scientific standards.

EEG

Electroencephalography will be recorded using a high-density electrode array, i.e., 256 evenly distributed scalp electrodes (HydroCel GSN, Magstim-EGI, Oregon, USA). Before EEG recording, the participant will receive 4 mg of melatonin. This will be followed by a 15-minute recording period (wake, with eyes closed) and a routine activation method: hyperventilation (3 min) followed by a 2-minute resting state recording (awake with eyes open). The rest of the recording will aim to achieve sleep. The co-registration of EEG positions will be performed using the GeoScan system (Magstim-EGI, Oregon, USA). Using the most recent scientific standards, EEG data will be pre-processed and analyzed in MATLAB and BESA software (BESA Gmbh, Gräfelfing Germany).

Identifying biomarkers relevant to the course of epilepsy is an area of research evolving rapidly. The scientific standards for analyzing EEG and neuroimaging data are expected to change during the study period. Thus, the detailed analysis plan for the different modalities will be based on an ongoing critical literature review; the analysis plans will be preregistered, e.g., on PROSPERO, and will be reported in detail in all articles based on the study.

Biomaterials

Blood biomarkers

Blood samples will be obtained from HC and patients to conduct standard biochemical screenings for undiscovered somatic conditions. Blood samples will also be collected at baseline and follow-up (patients) to monitor plasma neurotrophic factors (e.g., blood-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF)), the astrocyte marker S100B (Liang et al., 2019), for the determination of inflammatory markers known to be important in epilepsy and depression (e.g., IL-1A, IL-1B, IL-6, IL-8, IL10, IL-1 receptor antagonist IL-1RA, TNF-alpha, hsCRP, CD40, and complement system) (Vezzani et al., 2011; Zhang et al., 2014; Kanemoto et al., 2000, 2003; Salzmann et al., 2008; Jamali et al., 2010; Pollard et al., 2012; Dahl et al., 2014; Howren et al., 2009; Dowlati et al., 2010; Liu et al., 2012) and for blood purine concentrations (Beamer et al., 2021).

Limited genetics and gene expression

DNA from blood samples at baseline will be used for conducting relevant genotyping of epilepsy candidate genes (Myers et al., 2019), e.g., genes involved in GABA-ergic (Kauffman et al., 2008; Gambardella et al., 2003; Wang et al., 2008; Pernhorst et al., 2011; Haerian et al., 2016; Balan et al., 2013; Darrah et al., 2013), glutamatergic (Pfisterer et al., 2020), and dopaminergic (Rocha et al., 2012) neurotransmission; NMDA-receptors (GRIN2A/B), genes involved in glycosylphosphatidylinositol biosynthesis (PIGA, PIGV, PIGT); Vitamin B6 deficiency (ALDH7A1, PNPO); glucose transporter (SLC2A1); seizure suppressor gene PDYN (Stögmann et al., 2002); MTHFR gene predisposing for epilepsy (Scher et al., 2011); genes involved in cell-to-cell interactions, e.g., PCDH7 (Genetic determinants of common epilepsies, 2014), NRG (Zhu et al., 2016), and BDNF (Shen et al., 2016); integral membrane proteins (e.g., CLDN5), diagnostic biomarkers genes, MMP-2 and MMP-3 (Wang et al., 2016a, 2016b); for preventive treatment strategies (BDNF, TRKB) (Clossen and Reddy, 2017; Löscher, 2020); synaptic support proteins (e.g., SV2A, SYNGAP1, SNAP25); for adverse events (HLA-B*15:02, HLA-A*31:01, HLA-A*11:01) (McCormack et al., 2011; Man et al., 2007; Yang et al., 2018); and for drug metabolism (e.g., CYP2D6, CYP2C9, CYP2C19, UGT1A1, ABCB1, ABCC1) (Silvado et al., 2018). In addition, genotyping will be conducted on voltage-dependent chloride channel genes CLCN4 (He et al., 2021), candidate genes for predictive biomarkers (Gallek et al., 2016), for ion and water channels, including receptors (e.g., SCN1A, SCN2A, SCN3A, SCN8A, KCNA2, KCNT1, KCNQ2/3, KCNJ10, HCN1A, GABRA1, GRIN2A, ASIC1a, AQP4), for neuropeptide Y (Wickham et al., 2019) and genes of relevance for oligodendrocyte function and myelin components (Aston et al., 2005; Luo et al., 2015). Furthermore, blood samples from Cohorts I-III at baseline and follow-up will be used to assess epigenetic mechanisms such as microRNA from immunoreactive cells as an index of immune activation and neuroinflammation (Vezzani et al., 2011; Zhang et al., 2014; Kanemoto et al., 2000, 2003; Salzmann et al., 2008; Jamali et al., 2010; Pollard et al., 2012), for diagnostic biomarkers for epilepsy (e.g., microRNA (miR)-106 b-5p, miR-130a-3p, miR-146a-5p) (An et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2015a), for diagnostic biomarkers for drug-resistant epilepsy (e.g., miR-134, miR-194p, miR-301a-3p, and miR-30 b-5p) (Wang et al., 2015b; Leontariti et al., 2020), for the mTOR pathway regulators (e.g., TSC1/2, NPRL2/3, DEPDC5, AK3), for chromatin remodeling and transcription regulators (e.g., MECP2, ARX) (Ellis et al., 2020; Buono et al., 2004; Heuser et al., 2010; Lv et al., 2011), and for regulation of SCN2/3/8A, KCNQ3, GABRG2, GABRA1 and GABRB2/3 (Kobow and Blümcke, 2018).

Gut microbiome sample collection, transport, and storage

Feces sampling will be done for HCs and patients in Cohorts II-III using the paper hat of a stool collection kit at home. The stool collection tubes contain a buffer solution for preserving DNA at room temperature during storage and transport. The samples will be stored at −20 °C at a regional storage facility. Gut microbial DNA will be isolated using a suitable DNA stool kit. Shotgun DNA sequencing will be performed.

Statistical analyses

Model prediction performance within specific modalities

For Hypothesis 1 and 2, we will evaluate the predictive value of biomarkers within and across modalities using a logistic regression model (LR) and the machine learning method random forest (RF) to build epilepsy diagnosis, treatment response, and comorbidity probability scores from multiple biomarkers. Patients will be subdivided according to seizure localization and etiology prior to analyses. We will first assess model predictive performance (LR vs. RF) using broad clinical and non-imaging biological measures. Secondly, we will add cognitive and psychometric data. Thirdly, we will add EEG measurements. Subsequently, neuroimaging data from Cohort I-III (MRI) and Cohort III (PET) will be included. This allows for examinations of the added predictive value with and without neuroimaging data, which is both expensive and time-consuming. This training of classifiers follows a commonly used strategy already outlined in our parallel cohort study (Jensen et al., 2023). Models will be adjusted for age, sex, ASM treatment, seizure frequency, co-existing psychopathology and modality-specific relevant covariates such as injected mass/kg of the tracer (PET), scanner type (PET, MRI), education, dyslexia (cognitive data), sedatives, psychoactive medication, caffeine intake, sleep (MRI, HD EEG), antibiotics, laxatives, supplements, smoking habits, physical activity, other somatic diseases, type of diet (epigenetics, microbiota) etc.

For hypothesis 2, first, a binary version of the four seizure outcomes will be constructed and studied: 1) Seizure-free within six months after ASM titration and sustained seizure freedom for the rest of the study period (last observed seizure within the six months after starting ASM treatment); 2) Seizure-free more than six months after ASM titration and sustained seizure freedom for at least one year and until the end of the study period (patients in this category will be further subclassified according to time one-year seizure-freedom); vs. 3) Fluctuations with both seizure-freedom for at least one year and seizure-relapse) Never seizure-free for a year at the third- and fifth-year time point.

Using a threshold (e.g., 0.5), patients with an estimated probability greater than the threshold will be predicted to be diagnosed with epilepsy after a first seizure, to develop drug-resistance or epilepsy-related comorbidities, and otherwise not. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve will be used to assess the sensitivity and specificity of the classification across thresholds. The predictive performance of the epilepsy diagnosis, treatment response, and comorbidity score will be evaluated using the area under the curve (AUC) of the ROC curve and by calculation of the Brier score. The accuracy, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value at the threshold will be reported. We will use k-fold cross-validation to estimate model performance unbiasedly (Hastie et al., 2009; Varoquaux et al., 2017). This is a common data analysis strategy previously used in similar cohort studies (Köhler-Forsberg et al., 2020). We will control the family-wise error rate within each modality e.g., for EEG, rsfMRI, PET etc. by adjusting p-values and confidence intervals for multiple comparisons. Incorporating model assumptions and accounting for outliers in the context of using RF methodology is unnecessary. For LR, we will follow textbook state of the art when testing model assumptions and overall model fit and use Cook's distance to test for possible outliers. Handling of missing data will be dealt with as explained below in section 12.3.

Model prediction performance across modalities

To evaluate combinations of biomarkers that are not cohort specific LR and RF will be applied to identify biomarkers with a predictive value among all the existing biomarkers. The models will then be used to predict the chance of being diagnosed with epilepsy after the first seizure, treatment response, or epilepsy-related comorbidities. This strategy has previously been applied in a similar cohort study (Köhler-Forsberg et al., 2020). We will investigate the model performances (LR vs. RF). The adjustment of multiple comparisons as well as confounding and the assessment of the predictive performance of these strategies, will be carried out as described in the previous section.

Cerebral PET [11c]-UCB-J binding and treatment outcome

To address Hypothesis 3 and 4, we will use a Latent Variable Model (LVM) (Fisher et al., 2017) to assess the association between [11C]-UCB-J binding, SV2A occupancy, and clinical outcomes of interest. A single latent variable will be used to capture the shared correlation between the regional measurements (Fig. 2). For continuous outcome measures; we will report a percent change in regional [11C]-UCB-J binding and SV2A occupancy per unit increase or decrease in the outcome. For binary clinical outcomes, we will report the relative change in the average of [11C]-UCB-J binding and SV2A occupancy between groups (patients vs. healthy). The family-wise error rate for each clinical endpoint will be controlled by adjusting confidence intervals and p-values for the region-specific associations using the single-step Dunnett method (Dmitrienko and D'Agostino, 2013). The LVM will be adjusted for age, sex, and injected mass/kg of the tracer. Score tests will be used to detect model misspecification and identify additional parameters supported by the data. Model assumptions e.g. normality of residuals, will be tested by residuals plots or QQ-plots (Wood, 2017) and as already stated in section 12.1. [11C]-UCB-J binding and SV2A occupancy will be log-transformed to model a multiplicative (instead of additive) association. Estimates will be back-transformed when relevant, e.g., to report the ratio between the mean [11C]-UCB-J binding and SV2A occupancy in patients vs. healthy. The hypothesis of random drop-out and missing data will be assessed using LR modeling the probability of drop-out as a function of explanatory variables such as unacceptable adverse reactions, severe discomfort during participation, unacceptable seizure control, evidence of serious non-compliance, psychopathology, socioeconomic status, or education. If rejected, the inverse probability of censoring weighting (IPCW) will be used to handle censoring by weighting the contribution of patients with fully observed data in the LVM. Power calculation

We will use a machine learning approach to test Hypotheses 1 and 2 and no formal power calculations were performed, as the complexity of the studied data and predictive algorithms make it daunting to estimate statistical power under realistic assumptions. Nevertheless, this prospective cohort of first-seizure and newly diagnosed epilepsy patients (n = 350) is among the largest ever established. Given that the outcome variables are common in the patient group e.g. risk of a second seizure after a first seizure is approximately 30% within five years (Rizvi et al., 2017; Krumholz et al., 2015), one tone-thirdhird of all epilepsy patients do not become seizure-free with ASM treatment (Kwan and Brodie, 2000) and one-third of patients are expected to be diagnosed with a psychiatric disease (Kanner, 2022), a patient cohort of 350 is expected to be sufficiently large. The [11C]-UCB-J PET scans collected in Cohort III are expensive, time-consuming, invasive and not a part of the conventional diagnostic management of the patients. For this reason, we include fewer patients in Cohort III (n = 45). To test Hypothesis 3 and 4, a sample size, n = 45 and HC = 50, has a statistical power of 0.8 at a statistical significance threshold of p ≤ 0.025 to detect group differences with a Cohen's d effect size of 0.66 or greater. Note that the significance threshold for power analyses has been set at p ≤ 0.025 to control for the family-wise error rate when testing the two primary hypotheses.

Discussion

The aim of this study is to investigate biomarkers associated with treatment response, epilepsy-related comorbidities, and quality of life and to evaluate how these biomarkers change over time during disease progression with a structured and commonly used ASM treatment program in the crucial first years of epilepsy. Previous studies have aimed to identify biomarkers of drug resistance (Larivière et al., 2021; Dixit et al., 2016), comorbidities (Ravizza et al., 2017), as well as diagnosis (Pitkänen et al., 2016; van Vliet et al., 2017). Cohort studies have been conducted during the past 50 years (Shorvon and Goodridge, 2013), including the CAROLE study (De Zélicourt et al., 2000) and a 30-year retrospective cohort study (Chen et al., 2018a), often including patients with chronic epilepsy already treated with ASMs. Two ongoing studies are examining biomarkers in relation to epilepsy diagnosis (Adan et al., 2022) and if cognitive biomarkers can predict treatment response in newly diagnosed epilepsy patients (de Bézenac et al., 2019). We believe that our 5-year cohort study in first-seizure patients and newly diagnosed and drug-naïve epilepsy patients can significantly contribute with valuable knowledge to these two existing cohort studies and help inform the patient-level prediction of epilepsy diagnosis and treatment response. In addition, our cohort study will add novel investigations (PET imaging, MRI such as ASL and DTI, 256 channel HD EEG measurements, gut microbiome sampling, genetics, clinical psychiatric interviews, personality tests, extended neuropsychological testing, and questionnaires), that further can provide new insights into the disease trajectories and ASM mechanisms from the first epileptic seizure. The BrainDrugs-Epilepsy Study also significantly differs from the two studies mentioned above, as both first-seizure patients and newly diagnosed and drug-naïve epilepsy patients are included in the same study and, thus, get the same examinations. Furthermore, we also examine factors that can potentially modify the outcome when it comes to quality of life, e.g., level of education, personality traits, social support, etc. In addition, we chose to include patients not only with focal but also generalized epilepsy and adolescents down to 16 years of age.

To our knowledge, this is the largest cohort to date where machine learning algorithms will be applied to predict patient-level treatment response and epilepsy-related comorbidities using data collected from a longitudinal, prospective cohort study following newly diagnosed and drug-naïve patients (de Jong et al., 2021; Yao et al., 2019; Devinsky et al., 2016). Additionally, the BrainDrugs-Epilepsy Study will provide unique knowledge about how epilepsy develops as a progressive, dynamic brain network disease from the first epileptic seizure and how brain network biomarkers are affected by ASM treatment and comorbidities using the best clinical practice for prediction within neuroimaging (Varoquaux et al., 2017). We will examine how factors such as personality traits, education, socioeconomic conditions, etc. effect the course of epilepsy, including the development of comorbidities and quality of life. Moreover, the findings from this cohort study will contribute to an extensive network of related studies within epilepsy and depression (Jensen et al., 2023) (braindrugs.nru.dk). This will enable a comparison of biomarkers (EEG, MRI, PET, and non-imaging biological measures) between newly diagnosed patients with epilepsy and patients with a first depressive episode.

Progress to date

We opened the study for participant inclusions in February 2022. To date, 96 patients and 17 HCs have been recruited. The end of recruitment is expected to be February 2025 without an extension.

Ethics and consent to participate

The study protocol follows the Helsinki Declaration, and written informed consent will be collected prior to inclusion from all participating HCs and patients. The Committees on Health Research Ethics in the Capital Region of Denmark (reference number: H-21031962, date: 14.01.22) and the Danish Data Protection Agency (P-2021-664) have approved the study. All study participants will receive oral and written information about the study from a physician with specialized knowledge of the study project. Internal quality control and quality assurance of the study will be performed on an ongoing basis by sponsor, chief investigator, principal investigator, and study investigators, including randomization procedures and monitoring of the completeness of the datasets. A list of reasons for non-compliance and non-retention will be recorded throughout the study. Patients consent to give permission to study investigators, sponsor or sponsor's representatives, and the Regional Research Ethics Committee of the Capital Region to obtain information from collected data and documents, as well as the patient's medical record, that are required to complete the study and for quality control and monitoring as requested by law. The primary sponsor of the study is Gitte Moos Knudsen, professor, DMSc, MD neurology, and secondary sponsor is Lars H. Pinborg, professor, DMSc, MD neurology. Lars H. Pinborg is chief investigator, and principal investigator is Maja Rou Marstrand-Joergensen, MD. The study is retrospectively registered at clinicaltrials.gov (NCT05450822), date: July 05, 2022. Results from the trial and important protocol amendments will be communicated to all relevant parties and the public through publications in peer-reviewed international medical journals.

Availability of data and materials

During the recruitment phase, sensitive personal information in paper forms will be kept in a locked cabinet. Electronic information that can be traced to an identifiable person will be stored on password-protected computers behind secure firewalls in accordance with the applicable laws on the protection of personal data. Tissue samples (e.g., blood and gut microbiome samples) will be labeled with unique identification codes and stored safely within a research biobank in locked freezers in secured areas in the laboratory facility equipped with alarms. The identification key to the matching social ID numbers will be kept strictly confidential and restricted to authorized personnel only. Any excess material will be transferred to the CIMBI Biobank and will eventually be destroyed according to the CIMBI Biobank approval (2012-58-0004, 30–0291). Sponsor, the chief investigator, principal investigator, and study investigators will have access to the final dataset. Via the database, CIMBI data will be available for the research community upon request and after approval by the scientific board at Neurobiological Research Unit. Upon study completion, researchers may apply for access to the data: http://www.cimbi.dk/.
