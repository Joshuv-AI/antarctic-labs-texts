Meta-analysis of mortality burden due to Aluminum phosphide poisoning in India: A call for strong policy and decision making
Authors: Senthil Kumar, Ashok Kumar Pannu, Ranjit Immanuel James, Amol N Patil, Gagandeep Kwatra, Ashish Bhalla, Nusrat Shafiq, Samir Malhotra, Navneet Sharma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAluminum phosphide(AlP) insecticide is India's leading cause of poisoning-related mortality. Limited information is available about its mortality burden, common presentation type, and exposure(suicidal/accidental) characteristics. There exists no definite antidote for AlP poisoning management. The study was planned to systematically review the exact mortality burden, type of exposure- accidental/suicidal, commonest presentation in emergency department(ED), and gender difference in poison exposure, if any.MethodsWe conducted comprehensive search in Scopus, PubMed, Web of Science, and Embase, and cross-referencing relevant articles from 1982 to 31 January 2025. Prospective, retrospective, and cross-sectional Indian studies that provided information on mortality data, common clinical presentation, and exposure type-accidental/suicidal were included. Study details- time to reach ED, duration of hospital-stay, average dose consumed, gender distribution in poison exposure, and other relevant information, were also assessed. Random-effects meta-analysis with DerSimonian & Laird estimator, meta-regression, and subgroup analyses were conducted using R.ResultsForty-two studies involving 3449 Indian patients were included in the analysis. Pooled mortality prevalence was 54 %(95 % CI- 48-61 %) in the primary endpoint evaluation. In secondary endpoints analysis, it was observed that 94 %(95 % CI- 88-97) of the AlP poisonings were intentional(suicide), while accidental exposure accounted for 5 %(95 % CI- 2-12) of cases presenting to ED. Hypotension, with odds of 78 %(95 % CI- 64-88)(p < 0.01) was observed as common presentation type. Odds ratio for male-to-female gender among patients exposed to AlP was not significant, at 89 %(95 % CI- 61-129)(p = 0.86).ConclusionDeath of every second patient with AlP poisoning in ED room underscores the urgent need for stringent regulatory measures to control its access in India.Keywords: Aluminum phosphide, Phosphine, Poisoning, Mortality, India

Introduction

Aluminum phosphide (AlP), also known as “rice tablets,” is a widely used pesticide and insecticide. It is also used in fumigant and tablet forms to protect crops and grain stores from infestations. [1] Its effectiveness, potency, and low cost have led to its extensive use in Europe, the Middle East, and South East Asia. [2] It was registered as a pesticide in India almost five decades ago. [1] Both intentional and accidental poisonings from AlP have been reported in humans, requiring emergency department (ED) admissions. However specific information on most common type of presentation, mortality likelihood, common mode of exposure and age group remains unanswered till date. Management of AlP poisoning includes early interventions such as gastric lavage, oxygenation, and treatment of shock, but there is no specific antidote. [[3], [4], [5]] Despite its potential for fatality, AlP continues to be used in agriculture due to its easy availability, lack of residues on grains or food, and its gaseous nature, which prevents it from mixing with soil or other areas fumigated. [6] Moreover, AlP does not damage the ozone layer, nor does it persist in the atmosphere. However, it remains the common cause of agricultural poisoning in India, with steadily rising cases over the past four decades in the absence of a definitive therapy. [7,8]

AlP is rapidly absorbed into the bloodstream in the presence of a moist environment, such as gastrointestinal mucosa or moist skin, and releases highly toxic phosphine gas. The oxidant stress caused by phosphine gas affects cellular oxygenation, resulting in acid-base disturbances and tissue hypoxia. The severe toxidrome of AlP primly results from cardiotoxicity, manifesting as refractory hypotension, cardiac dysfunction, and a variety of ECG changes. [9] Other vital organs, such as the brain, kidney and liver, are also affected due to tissue hypoxia, leading to rapidly progressive multi-organ failure. [10] One tablet (three grams) of AlP releases one gram equivalent of phosphine gas, sufficient to cause fatal systemic toxicity. Tablets are more potent than the exposure gained via atmosphere and moisture. [11]

The outcome of treatment in the ED is influenced by factors such as clinical toxidrome, laboratory features, quality of medical care, and poison-related considerations such as the ingested dose, poison formulation, time of exposure, and time of arrival at the hospital. [12] Understanding exposure patterns and the mortality rate can help ED physicians and healthcare institutions devise effective management strategies. [13] Several studies from India have reported varying information on AlP poisoning mortality over the last four decades. [3] The overall mortality rate remains largely unknown. In the current study, we executed a meta-analysis to assess the overall mortality rate of AlP poisoning reported across various healthcare setups in India from 1982 to 2025. Additionally, we have examined the manner in which AlP exposure culminates in ED visits and respective outcomes.

Methods

To establish the robustness of the study's conduct, we followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) and MOOSE (Meta-analyses of Observational Studies) checklists (checklists).

Search strategy

Two independent reviewers conducted a comprehensive search of four databases: PubMed, Scopus, Embase and Web of Science (WoS) from the year 1982 to 31st January 2025. The search used MeSH terms such as ‘aluminum phosphide,’ ‘phosphine,’ ‘poisoning,’ ‘mortality’, and ‘India’. Only English-language literature was included. The detailed search strategy is provided in the supplementary file. Studies from each database were collated for analysis. We registered the study with inclusion-exclusion criteria and completed the PICOT information in PROSPERO study number CRD42024567362.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria for studies

Indian studies reporting information on clinically confirmed patients with aluminum phosphide poisoning diagnosis and presenting to the emergency department of hospital, of any gender, any age group, with survivor or non-survivor outcome status, were included. Studies reporting suspected or probable aluminum phosphide poisoning cases and those from outside India, animal studies, qualitative studies, policy papers, case reports were excluded. Morbidity assessment was not considered for the present analysis, as the nature of AlP poisoning, short stay in Indian hospital ED rooms and early outcome (dead/alive) reach was observed in the studies. (Supplementary file Table S1).

Study selection

We included country-specific(India) observational studies(Prospective, retrospective, and cross-sectional studies) and randomized clinical trials reporting mortality due to AlP poisoning in the ED. Exclusions were non-English-language studies, studies conducted outside India, studies not reporting relevant outcomes, reviews, commentaries, case reports, and editorials. Indian medical literature is reported mostly in the English language.

Data extraction

Three independent reviewers (SKR, RIJ, AKP) screened the abstracts and titles of the potential studies using PROSPERO-registered inclusion-exclusion criteria. The selected studies with respective details were compiled into a table. Data extracted included author names, publication years, total study population, number of AlP poisoning cases, age distribution, gender distribution, the number of patients who survived and died, manner of death, time of arrival at the hospital, and duration of hospital stay. Two independent investigators (GK and ANP) compiled this data, and a third investigator (SM) reviewed and corrected any conflicting information (Supplementary file Table S2). No author was contacted with a request for the raw data file. Unpublished research was not used in the present study.

Patient cohort and endpoints

Patient cohorts were from study reports mentioning AlP poisoning admissions to ED, management and outcome- death/survival. The primary endpoint of the study was mortality due to AlP poisoning. The secondary endpoints included the manner of poisoning (e.g. suicidal or accidental), the prevalence of hypotension in clinical presentation to the ED, and gender difference in AlP exposure, if any.

Risk of bias(ROB) assessment

We used the Newcastle Ottawa tool for the ROB assessment. Each study was assessed for any risk of bias associated with the study conduct and reporting of study outcomes. Two investigators performed ROB evaluations separately. Any conflict in scoring called for a third investigator to resolve the discrepancy.

Statistical analysis

We used R, an open-source, free software with associated packages like “readxl”, “meta”, “dmetar”, “metafor”, and “metasens”. The inverse variance method with the random effect method was used to calculate the pooled information of all the endpoints. Study heterogeneity was checked with the I2 test and prediction interval. We performed a sensitivity analysis to account for any biased studies affecting the overall outcome. A random effect regression model with Dersimonian and Laird statistics was used to derive overall effect information. Publication bias was addressed by the Doi plot method. The p-value <0.05 was assumed to be statistically significant.

Results

A systematic literature search identified 3506 research articles studying ED visits and admissions of AlP poisoning patients. Of these, 3316 articles were excluded due to being conducted outside India and duplicate entries. We screened four databases- PubMed, Embase, Scopus and Web of Science. Subsequently, 190 research papers were assessed for relevant numeric information required for the study endpoints. Sixty studies were excluded for reasons such as case reports, review articles or letters to the editor. Further screening removed 97 records out of 130 for not reporting outcomes of interest. We could retrieve 33 original research articles assessing AlP poisoning presentation and management in EDs, with an additional nine articles obtained through the back referencing, resulting in a final sum of 42 research papers eligible for meta-analysis(MA)(Fig. 1).

PRISMA flow chart.

These 42 studies were conducted over almost 40 years and included 3449 patients. The studies comprised 26 prospective cohort, 13 retrospective, three cross-sectional studies. Thirty-two studies were conducted between 2005 and 2024, while the remaining ten were published between 1982 and 2004. The average age of patient was 28.5 years. The average duration of hospitalization was 32 hr (S.D. 19.52 hrs). The latency between the consumption of AlP and reaching the hospital ED was 6.53 hr. The dose of AlP consumed by the study participants varied from 1.48 g to 6.56 g(Table 1).

The present study assessed the mortality among AlP poisoning patients presenting to ED services. The estimated prevalence of death among these patients was 54 % (95 % C.I.- 48–61)(Fig. 2). Secondary endpoint analysis showed that 94 % (95 %CI- 88-97) of AlP poisoning were intentional (suicidal), whereas accidental exposure accounted for 5 % (95 %CI- 2-12) of the patients (Fig. 3). The assessment also showed that hypotension was a common presentation, with an odds of 78 % (95 % CI 64–88) (p < 0.01)(Fig. 4A). Additionally, the study found no significant gender difference among the patients exposed to AlP, with an odds ratio (male: female) of 89 % with 95 % CI- 61-129) (p = 0.86) (Fig. 4B).

The doi plot method was used to assess publication bias. It showed an LFK index value of 0.45 (Supplementary file Figure SF2). The Baujat plot method assessed the study heterogeneity (Supplementary file Figure SF3).

Sensitivity analysis was conducted by removing one study at a time, which did not significantly affect the overall observation of a 54 % fatality rate (Supplementary file Figure SF4).

Two independent reviewers (AB and GK) checked the 42 studies for quality using the Newcastle Ottawa scale for the observational studies. Any dispute in scoring was resolved by consulting with the third reviewer (ANP). More than half of the studies (n = 29) demonstrated a score of six, eleven studies with a score of eight, and one study with a score of five and seven each (Supplementary file Table S3).

Discussion

The current study was carried out to investigate the country-specific prognosis and the poisoning determinants of AlP exposure, including the mode of exposure, the prevalence of hypotension in ED presentations, and any gender difference in AlP exposure. AlP has become a leading cause of both intentional and accidental poisoning. The absence of a specific antidote or chelating agent complicates the management of AlP toxicity, resulting in substantial mortality. Therefore, we focused on the mortality burden of AlP poisoning as our primary endpoint. AlP poisoning was not a significant concern in India before 1981, but the number of cases has steadily increased over the last four decades. Our study observed a pooled mortality prevalence of 54 % from the 42 included studies over this period. The reported mortality in the literature varies widely, ranging from 37 % to 100 %. In 1996, the death rate was approximately 20 %, increasing to almost 25 % by 2005. [8] Literature suggests that the consumption of three or more tablets of AlP carries a 100 % mortality risk, whereas consuming two tablets is associated with a worse prognosis. [1] Our study observed significant variability in the ingested dose, supporting a 54 % mortality risk as a mid-value in diverse data spread. Over the four decades, healthcare reforms in low- and middle-income countries like India have improved accessibility and the quality of healthcare infrastructure, potentially influencing these outcomes. [14,15]

The manner of poisoning is an important determinant in the AlP poisoning prognosis. [16] Intentional self-harm exposure can remain hidden until recognized by the ED team. [17,18] In contrast, accidental exposures, such as inhalational or topical routes, generally have evidence or history, aiding in early diagnosis and management. [19] Our analysis found that 94 % of AlP exposures were from Intentional self-harm. Suicide among farmers and the young generation is a significant mental health issue in India. [20,21] Both children and adults are known to be victims to pesticide poisoning in India. [22] Factors such as subclinical depression, anxiety, limited rural healthcare access, and hesitancy in psychiatry consultations contribute to this problem. [23,24] AlP is among the most accessed poisons in India, highlighting the need for strict regulatory supervision and control to limit its use. [8] Adolescent suicides and need for psychological support system was also felt need in Egypt during COVID 19 pandemic and lockdown. [18] Countries like France and the UK have implemented measures to prevent such poisoning by restricting access through regulations like the Pesticide Act 1998. [8,25,26]

The release of phosphine gas from AlP occurs immediately upon contact with water and hydrochloric acid in the gastrointestinal tract, rapidly absorbing it into the bloodstream and reducing oxyhemoglobin levels. Phosphine is toxic to vital organs such as the heart and brain, affecting tissue oxygenation, circulatory functions, acid-base balance, and neurological functions, though the exact mechanism remains unclear. [1,8] We observed hypotension as the most common presentation among the 3449 patients studied, with a pooled prevalence of 78 %. The prevalence of hypotension and refractory shock in other studies ranges from 76 to 100 %, corroborating that cardiovascular involvement is the principal toxicity in AlP poisoning. [3] Several agents, such as magnesium sulfate, N-acetyl cysteine, sodium bicarbonate, and vitamin C, have been studied for their potential utility in managing AlP poisoning, but with limited success. [[27], [28], [29]] Newer therapeutics, such as hyperinsulinemia-euglycemia therapy and extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, have shown promise in small-scale studies. However, large trials are lacking. Overall, there remains no specific antidote for AlP poisoning despite more than four decades of rising cases. [8,30] The study also evaluated whether there is any gender bias in AlP poisonings. We observed that the occurrence was almost identical in both genders, ruling out significant differences in consumption or access based on gender. However, Mathai et al. observed four times higher consumption by males compared to females in the Punjab state of India. [31]

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Heterogeneity among the included studies of one country data in terms of design, population, and clinical settings necessitates the validation of the findings. The lack of individual patient data precludes detailed analysis of patient-specific factors influencing outcomes. Variability in treatment protocols across studies could also influence results. Additionally, the focus on studies conducted in India limits applicability to Asian countries and other LMIC settings. Despite these limitations, this meta-analysis provides crucial insights into the mortality burden and clinical characteristics of AlP poisoning in India.

Conclusion

Given the high mortality prevalence, this meta-analysis highlights the urgent need for regulatory measures to control AlP accessibility and monitor its sale. The high incidence of suicidal poisonings especially in young population underscores the necessity for enhanced mental health services, especially in rural parts of India. Despite varied management approaches, no specific antidote for AlP poisoning remains, indicating a pressing need for further research into effective treatment protocols. Coordinated efforts from healthcare providers, policymakers, and researchers are essential to mitigate the impact of AlP poisoning and improve patient outcomes in India.
