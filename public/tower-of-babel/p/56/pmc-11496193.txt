Revisiting ALK (D5F3) immunohistochemistry: Insights into focal staining and neuroendocrine differentiation
Authors: Yeoun Eun Sung, Meejeong Kim
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundScreening for anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) rearranged non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) is crucial for identifying patients eligible for targeted therapy. The FDA‐approved ALK (D5F3) immunohistochemistry (IHC) assay, used with the OptiView Amplification Kit, demonstrates excellent sensitivity and specificity in detecting these patients. However, the clinical significance of resulting focal positivity remains unclear, and ALK (D5F3) expression unrelated to ALK fusion is observed in some cases of neuroendocrine differentiation. This study aims to validate these findings with molecular testing and contribute to the accurate interpretation of ALK (D5F3) IHC results.MethodsA total of 1619 patients diagnosed with NSCLC and neuroendocrine carcinoma were evaluated using ALK (D5F3) IHC. For cases with strong but focal expression and those with diffuse strong positivity in neuroendocrine differentiation, ALK fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) and/or next‐generation sequencing (NGS) tests were performed.ResultsSeven out of 1109 adenocarcinomas (0.6%) and six out of 289 squamous cell carcinomas (2.1%) exhibited strong focal ALK (D5F3) expression. Nine out of 209 neuroendocrine carcinomas (4.3%) showed homogeneously strong ALK (D5F3) expression. All these cases, including adenocarcinoma with neuroendocrine differentiation and combined small cell carcinoma, were negative for ALK fusions by FISH and/or NGS.ConclusionThis study demonstrates that strong but focal ALK (D5F3) immunostaining and strong expression in neuroendocrine differentiation may not indicate ALK fusion. By considering these findings, we can improve the accuracy of patient selection for targeted therapy by minimizing false‐positive interpretations of ALK (D5F3) staining.Keywords: anaplastic lymphoma kinase, false‐positive reactions, neuroendocrine tumors, non‐small cell lung cancer, staining

INTRODUCTION

Despite the development and application of numerous targeted therapies, lung cancer continues to have the highest rate of cancer‐related mortality. 1 Various oncogene driver mutations have been identified in non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), with anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) fusion being one of them. ALK fusion is a significant molecular event observed in approximately 5% of NSCLC cases, particularly in adenocarcinoma. 2 Identifying patients with ALK fusion is crucial, as they derive significant benefits from ALK tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs), which have demonstrated remarkable efficacy in this patient group. 3 , 4

Among the companion diagnostics for detecting ALK rearrangement, fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) was the first method to be clinically validated. In cases of ALK rearrangement, the FISH test shows separated red and green signals or red‐only signals. A positive result is determined when more than 50% of at least 50 evaluated tumor cells exhibit these signals. 5 However, due to the nature of the test, false negatives or false positives can occur. These issues include cases where the split signals are too close to accurately interpret, difficulty distinguishing between normal and tumor cells, and instances when the proportion of cells showing break‐apart or isolated red signals is near the cutoff threshold. 6 , 7

Another molecular method for detecting ALK fusion is next‐generation sequencing (NGS), which allows the parallel examination of millions or billions of DNA strands. NGS has the advantage of simultaneously detecting multiple driver gene mutations across a wide range. Previous studies have demonstrated that NGS exhibits high sensitivity and specificity for ALK fusion detection. 8 However, the turnaround time for NGS is over 2 weeks, which is longer than that of other tests, and it is more expensive, presenting certain limitations.

The Ventana ALK (D5F3) CDx assay (Ventana Medical Systems, Inc., Tucson, AZ), a U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA)‐approved ALK immunohistochemistry (IHC) assay, was developed to provide high analytical sensitivity and specificity, high concordance with FISH, and very high interobserver reproducibility through the use of a binary scoring algorithm. 7 The D5F3 clone detects the carboxyl terminus of the ALK protein, and numerous studies have demonstrated its excellent performance. 9 Additionally, the OptiView Amplification Kit (Ventana Medical Systems Inc.) is used in conjunction with the OptiView Detection Kit to increase sensitivity in identifying ALK positivity in focally positive cases. 9 With this amplification kit, any percentage of strong granular cytoplasmic staining is defined as ALK positive, and a binary scoring algorithm was established, 7 as reflected in the VENTANA ALK (D5F3) CDx Assay Interpretation Guide. 10 However, it is important to be aware of the pitfalls of ALK (D5F3) immunostaining, including the potential for false positives in tumor cells with neuroendocrine differentiation. 6 , 11 , 12

In this study, we aimed to determine whether the focal positivity of ALK (D5F3) is due to tumor heterogeneity, partial amplification leading to focal strong cytoplasmic staining instead of uniform amplification, or if it is unrelated to ALK fusion. We frequently observed partial staining in ALK (D5F3) immunohistochemistry conducted on squamous cell carcinoma, which rarely harbors ALK fusions. Additionally, while ALK (D5F3) staining observed in neuroendocrine differentiation is typically heterogeneous or presents in a checkerboard pattern, 9 our research team has occasionally encountered cases in neuroendocrine carcinomas such as small cell carcinoma, or in areas of neuroendocrine differentiation, where ALK (D5F3) staining shows homogeneous strong cytoplasmic staining similar to true positivity seen in NSCLC. Based on these observations, this study aimed to investigate the significance of partial ALK (D5F3) expression in NSCLC and the significance of diffuse strong positivity of ALK (D5F3) in neuroendocrine carcinoma or neuroendocrine differentiation using molecular testing. Furthermore, if necessary, we aimed to propose an enhanced algorithm to ensure accurate interpretation of ALK (D5F3) immunohistochemistry staining.

METHODS

Patient selection and sample collection

Patients diagnosed with adenocarcinoma, adenosquamous carcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma of the lung, and neuroendocrine carcinoma, including those of extrapulmonary origin, at Seoul St. Mary's Hospital, Korea, between 2018 and 2021 were included in this study. A retrospective chart review was conducted during the specified period, identifying cases diagnosed with lung adenocarcinoma, lung squamous cell carcinoma, and lung adenosquamous carcinoma. In instances where multiple samples were available from the same patient, only one representative specimen was selected. This resulted in the identification of 1124 cases of lung adenocarcinoma, 292 cases of lung squamous cell carcinoma, and 12 cases of lung adenosquamous carcinoma. Cases where ALK (D5F3) immunohistochemical staining could not be performed, including those with cytology specimens, very small sample size, or minimal malignant cell content (15 lung adenocarcinoma cases, 3 lung squamous cell carcinoma cases, and 0 lung adenosquamous carcinoma cases), were excluded. For neuroendocrine carcinomas, cases were identified by searching for diagnoses of neuroendocrine carcinoma, small cell carcinoma, and large cell neuroendocrine carcinoma, irrespective of the organ of origin. After excluding duplicate cases from the same patients, 227 cases were identified. Among these, 18 cases were excluded due to insufficient sample size for immunohistochemistry. Consequently, a total of 1619 patients were enrolled, with 54.0% (875 of 1619) of the samples being biopsy specimens and the remaining 46.0% (744 of 1619) being resection specimens. Formalin‐fixed paraffin‐embedded (FFPE) tissues from all specimens were utilized for immunohistochemical staining and molecular testing. Molecular tests were performed only on cases with sufficient tissue quantity and patient consent.

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Seoul St. Mary's Hospital of the Catholic University of Korea (KC22SISI0867).

Immunohistochemistry

Four micrometer‐thick sections were cut from the FFPE tissues of all specimens for immunohistochemical staining. The staining was performed according to the protocols provided by Ventana Medical Systems, Inc., and Roche Diagnostics International, Inc. One serial tissue section was stained with VENTANA anti‐ALK (D5F3) and another with the Rabbit Monoclonal Negative Control Ig antibody. The assay used the OptiView DAB IHC Detection Kit and OptiView Amplification Kit, and was run on the BenchMark ULTRA platform. Appendix tissue containing ganglion cells was used as a control.

For the interpretation of stained slides, the VENTANA ALK (D5F3) CDx Assay Interpretation Guide was referenced. 10 Nonspecific diffuse granular staining, light speckling in tumor cells, and no staining patterns were classified as negative. Homogeneous strong cytoplasmic staining was classified as positive, and focal strong cytoplasmic staining was categorized separately.

Fluorescent in situ hybridization

ALK FISH was performed on cases requiring validation. Two micrometer‐thick FFPE tissue sections were used to evaluate ALK genetic status. The Vysis ALK Break Apart FISH Probe Kit (Abbott, CA, USA) was used according to the manufacturer's protocol to detect ALK rearrangement. A minimum of 50 to occasionally 100 nonoverlapping cancer cell nuclei per specimen were counted based on localization in the corresponding hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)‐stained sections. The interpretation of FISH signals followed the criteria set by the CAP/IASLC/AMP guidelines related to ALK testing in lung cancer. Samples were classified as positive for ALK rearrangement if 15% or more of nuclei showed split signals (i.e., red and green signals were separated by ≥2 signal diameters) or single red signals (3′ ALK) were observed. 5 , 13 , 14

Next‐generation sequencing

To perform comprehensive genomic profiling, we utilized the Oncomine Comprehensive Assay v3 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). This assay is designed for the simultaneous analysis of DNA and RNA to detect single‐nucleotide variants (SNVs), insertions/deletions (indels), copy number variations (CNVs), and gene fusions across a wide range of cancer‐related genes.

FFPE tissue samples were used for NGS analysis. Each biopsy sample was sectioned to obtain 15–20 unstained slides, while each resection sample was sectioned to obtain two to four unstained slides, ensuring a minimum of 20% tumor content. DNA and RNA were simultaneously extracted from the sectioned slides using the Ion AmpliSeq Direct FFPE DNA/RNA Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific), following the manufacturer's protocol. The quality and quantity of the extracted nucleic acids were assessed using the Qubit Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) to ensure suitability for downstream applications.

Library preparation was performed using the Ion AmpliSeq Library Kit Plus (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For each sample, 10 ng of DNA and RNA were used to generate libraries. Libraries were quantified, pooled, and processed for clonal amplification using the Ion Chef System. Sequencing was performed on the Ion GeneStudio S5 System (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Sequencing data were analyzed using the Torrent Suite Software, with variant calling performed by the Ion Reporter Software. The assay detects SNVs, CNVs, gene fusions, and indels across 161 cancer‐associated genes. Both DNA and RNA analyses were conducted to confirm the presence of fusion events.

DISCUSSION

The ALK (D5F3) immunohistochemistry (IHC) assay, while not a molecular test, demonstrates high sensitivity and specificity in detecting ALK fusions, making it a valuable diagnostic tool in clinical settings as companion diagnostics. 9 , 14 , 15 , 16 This study evaluates the nonspecific expression of ALK (D5F3) IHC, which may not be associated with actual ALK fusions. The cancer types included in this study are adenocarcinoma, where ALK fusions are most commonly found and benefit the most from targeted therapies 2 , 17 , 18 , 19 ; squamous cell carcinoma, where ALK fusions are rarely reported and mostly negative 20 , 21 ; and neuroendocrine carcinoma, which primarily includes small cell lung cancer but also encompasses cases with extrapulmonary origins. Additionally, cases showing neuroendocrine differentiation, regardless of carcinoma type, were included.

According to current interpretation guidelines, 10 IHC results should be interpreted as negative if there is no staining, light speckling, or nonspecific diffuse granular staining (Figure S1), and positive if there is strong cytoplasmic staining, even if focal. In typical cases of adenocarcinoma with ALK fusion, a homogeneously strong cytoplasmic staining pattern is observed (Figure S1). However, rare cases show focal strong cytoplasmic staining.

The ALK (D5F3) immunohistochemistry, when used with the OptiView Amplification Kit (Ventana Medical Systems Inc.) alongside the OptiView Detection Kit, is known to improve the evaluation of ALK status in focally positive NSCLC specimens. 7 According to a previous study, 7 with the amplification kit, any presence of strong granular cytoplasmic staining in tumor cells was considered ALK positive, and a binary scoring system was established to standardize this evaluation. However, in our study, all cases with focal strong cytoplasmic expression were negative for ALK FISH and did not show ALK fusion on NGS. Instead, other driver gene mutations were detected. This suggests that even strong cytoplasmic staining, if focal, may not be indicative of ALK fusion status.

Previous studies have shown a 95% agreement among experts for ALK D5F3 IHC 22 with high sensitivity and specificity, typically above 95%, 9 although some studies reported specificities of 76%, 23 86%, 24 and 90.5%. 25 Wynes et al. 22 noted that cases with interobserver disagreement included variations in protein expression within the specimens. Lantuejoul et al. 23 reported 76% specificity, using a 10% cutoff for tumor cells, with positive IHC but negative molecular tests in cases where only 12%–25% of tumor cells were positive. These findings suggest that relatively low specificity may be due to focal expression cases. It is difficult to confirm further in other papers, but it is possible that the rare false‐positive cases are due to focal or heterogeneous expression.

The typical homogeneous strong cytoplasmic staining pattern in adenocarcinoma in this study was 6.4% (71 of 1109), whereas the focal strong cytoplasmic staining pattern was only 0.5% (6 of 1109) (Table 2). Of all the 77 positive cases, 6 cases (7.8%) were focal and had focal strong cytoplasmic staining, so according to current reading guidelines, these 6 cases should be considered ALK (D5F3) positive, suggesting the presence of ALK fusion as a companion diagnostic. However, as summarized in Table 3, NGS molecular testing, including ALK FISH and RNA analysis, was performed in these cases and all were negative for ALK fusion. The negativity of ALK FISH, another companion diagnostic for ALK fusion, the lack of ALK fusion observed by RNA‐based NGS, and importantly, the detection of driver gene mutations other than ALK in terms of oncogene addiction, strongly suggests that actual ALK fusion did not occur. Therefore, ALK (D5F3) expression in these six cases can be considered a false positive, that is, nonspecific expression unrelated to the ALK fusion event. Notably, one case was a CD56a‐positive adenocarcinoma with neuroendocrine differentiation, and RET fusion was detected by NGS. Neuroendocrine differentiation will be discussed later in the neuroendocrine carcinoma section.

In squamous cell carcinoma, ALK rearrangement is very rare but has been reported and validated using IHC and/or molecular testing (FISH or RT‐PCR). 20 , 26 , 27 In the present study, among 289 cases of squamous cell carcinoma, there was no homogeneously strong cytoplasmic staining in ALK (D5F3) immunostaining, and 6 (2.1%) cases of focal/partial strong cytoplasmic staining were observed (Table 2). In resection specimens, ALK (D5F3) immunostaining showed strong cytoplasmic staining in up to 30%–40% of the total tumor, and the rest were negative (Figure 1). These findings were relatively more widespread than the focal staining cases of adenocarcinoma, with one case being a biopsy and the other five cases being resection specimens. Based on the partial positive findings in the resection specimens (Figure 1g–l), it is predicted that the biopsy specimens of these tumors may appear diffusely positive in some cases. In five of the six cases, ALK FISH was performed on the area matching the IHC‐positive area and was negative. In addition, DNA‐ and RNA‐based NGS also showed no ALK fusion but revealed several mutations commonly found in squamous cell carcinoma, such as TP53 mutations (Table 3). Taken together, this suggests that focal/partial ALK (D5F3) expression in squamous cell carcinoma is likely to be a nonspecific immunostaining expression that is unrelated to ALK fusion events, that is, a false positive. Furthermore, the proportion of this expression type was higher in squamous cell carcinoma than in adenocarcinoma (2.1% vs. 0.5%, Table 2).

The OptiView Amplification Kit, which was introduced to increase the sensitivity of ALK (D5F3), allows for strong cytoplasmic intensity, making it a powerful tool for ALK (D5F3). The focal or partial strong expression seen in adenocarcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma may have been highlighted by amplification. Although tumor heterogeneity may exist in the oncogenes that contribute to tumor development, inferred from the cases of partial ALK (D5F3) expression included in this study, focal expression may suggest false‐positive or nonspecific immunostaining rather than tumor heterogeneity.

Neuroendocrine carcinoma, including small cell carcinoma and large cell neuroendocrine carcinoma, is characterized by TP53 and RB1 gene inactivation, 28 while ALK rearrangement, which is mainly observed in adenocarcinoma, is very rare. 29 , 30 Previous studies have reported ALK IHC expression unrelated to ALK fusion, typically showing focal or heterogeneous weak to moderate intensity staining. 6 , 11 , 12 In this study, 38 of 209 cases (18.2%) had partial expression, a higher proportion than in the previous literature, and 9 cases (4.3%) had diffuse strong cytoplasmic staining, which was different from the previous reference (Table 2, Figure 2a–c). The higher rate of partial or heterogeneous expression may be due to the fact that we used whole slides of biopsy or excision specimens in this study, or the antibody and staining protocol used may have been different from previous studies. ALK (D5F3) expression was observed not only in neuroendocrine carcinoma but also in adenocarcinoma, with CD56a‐positive neuroendocrine differentiation (Figure 1a–c).

As reported in previous studies, cases with focal expression were considered nonspecific staining, but the nine cases with diffuse strong positivity were further validated. Seven cases were small cell carcinoma, one case was combined small cell carcinoma with adenocarcinoma, and one case was an EGFR mutation‐positive case with suspected small cell transformation. In the combined small cell carcinoma case, strong staining was observed only in the small cell carcinoma component (Figure 2d–f). Seven cases were biopsy specimens, two cases were resection specimens, and all nine cases were negative for ALK FISH and negative for ALK rearrangement in the three cases where RNA‐based NGS was available. Although not all cases were accompanied by NGS, these results suggest that diffuse strong positivity in neuroendocrine carcinoma may not indicate ALK fusion and could be nonspecific staining. In addition, in the case of adenocarcinoma with neuroendocrine differentiation (Figure 1a–c), ALK FISH was negative and RET fusion was detected by NGS, indicating that ALK (D5F3) expression is not associated with ALK rearrangement.

The mechanism of the ALK immunoreactivity in neuroendocrine differentiation is not yet clearly understood, but it is likely to be true protein expression rather than a technical artifact. 12 ALK transcripts are clustered in distinct neuronal populations in the CNS, such as thalamic nuclei, olfactory bulb, mitral cells, and tufted cells. 31 ALK expression is also known to be found in neurons, glial cells, and endothelial cells. 32 For this reason, it can be inferred that ALK (D5F3) immunostaining is expressed in areas of neuroendocrine differentiation, rather than staining for ALK fusion protein, which is a methodological limitation of immunostaining compared with molecular testing.

Not all neuroendocrine carcinoma or neuroendocrine differentiation cases show ALK IHC expression, and no consistent patterns were found among positive cases. Diffuse strong positivity was observed in 7 of 174 cases (4.0%) of small cell carcinoma, 1 of 21 cases (8.3%) of combined small cell carcinoma, and 1 of 8 cases (12.5%) of suspected small cell transformation, but not in cases of extrapulmonary origin. Our literature search did not reveal any reports of ALK fusion or ALK (D5F3) positivity in extrapulmonary neuroendocrine carcinomas. This suggests that our study, which examines ALK immunohistochemistry in extrapulmonary neuroendocrine carcinoma, is among the few in this area and may hold significance. It also indicates the need for further investigation, including larger case series, to better understand the implications of ALK (D5F3) staining in these tumors. In addition, in NSCLC cases with neuroendocrine differentiation, that is, positive for neuroendocrine markers (CD56a, chromogranin, synaptophysin), 1 out of 34 cases (2.9%) showed diffuse strong positivity. Of these cases, only one case, POU2F3 type, was positive for only synaptophysin among the neuroendocrine markers, while the rest were positive for more than one neuroendocrine marker. TTF‐1 was positive in six cases and negative in three cases, and of the four cases for which small cell carcinoma subtype assessment was possible, one was ASCL1 dominant, two were NEUROD1 dominant, and one was POU2F3 dominant (Table 3). Overall, it is challenging to identify commonalities among neuroendocrine carcinomas with nonspecific ALK (D5F3) IHC expression, and it remains unclear which aspects of neuroendocrine differentiation are associated with the presence or absence of ALK (D5F3) IHC expression.

In addition to ALK, several other gene fusions play significant roles in oncogenesis, including ROS1 fusion, RET fusion, NTRK fusion, and MET exon 14 skipping. Among these genes, immunohistochemical markers correlated with fusion events are available for ROS1 33 and NTRK fusions, 34 although they have not been recognized as companion diagnostics. Additionally, RET fusion 35 and MET exon 14 skipping 36 are known to have limitations when using immunohistochemistry as a screening tool. Given that ALK (D5F3) is the only FDA‐approved companion diagnostic IHC assay to date, it would be valuable to explore whether similar false‐positive staining patterns, as observed in our study, occur with other fusion‐related IHC markers if they are developed and implemented in clinical practice. Such investigations could further enhance the accuracy and reliability of molecular diagnostics in oncology.

In this study, we evaluated ALK (D5F3) immunostaining using a representative block of biopsy and resection specimens of targeted NSCLC and NEC, and validated the results with ALK FISH and DNA‐ and RNA‐based NGS molecular tests. One of the strengths of this study is that the expression extent was more accurately assessed using whole slides compared with TMA in a previous study of NEC. 12 A limitation of this study is that it was a retrospective study using biopsy or surgical specimens, and ALK FISH and NGS were not performed in all cases due to limitations such as the lack of sufficient specimens in some cases. Furthermore, the study's single‐institution nature may limit the generalizability of the findings. The lack of a validation cohort may limit the reproducibility of our findings. Further studies should aim to replicate these findings in larger, multi‐institutional cohorts to enhance the reproducibility and robustness of the results. In addition, the mechanism behind nonspecific ALK (D5F3) IHC expression remains unclear.

As a companion diagnostic, ALK (D5F3) IHC can identify treatment candidates without molecular tests, but caution is needed with false positives. Our findings suggest that even strong focal positivity in adenocarcinoma should be validated by molecular testing. In addition, although ALK (D5F3) is not likely to be tested in NEC because of its significance in NSCLC, if the tumor is accompanied by neuroendocrine differentiation, ALK (D5F3) expression may be seen independently of ALK fusion events and may even be of strong diffuse intensity. Therefore, cases with ALK (D5F3) positivity and neuroendocrine features on H&E slides may require further confirmation with neuroendocrine markers and molecular testing. Based on the results of this study, new guidelines for interpreting ALK (D5F3) immunohistochemical staining can be proposed (Figure 3). By highlighting potential pitfalls in ALK (D5F3) IHC interpretation, we aim to contribute to more precise and effective use of ALK‐targeted therapies in clinical practice.
