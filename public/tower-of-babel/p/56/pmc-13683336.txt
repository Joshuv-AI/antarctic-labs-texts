A high-resolution near-surface meteorological forcing dataset for arid Xinjiang (3DVAR-MF-XJ)
Authors: Yang Xu, Liang Zhang, Hui Wang, Dongge Ning, Mengxin Bai, Xueping Cong, Zhixin Hao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHydroclimatic assessment in Xinjiang is constrained by sparse observations, complex terrain and systematic biases in widely used gridded and reanalysis products. Here we present the Three-Dimensional Variational Xinjiang Meteorological Forcing Dataset (3DVAR-MF-XJ), a 0.1° gridded near-surface dataset for 1961–2020 at hourly and daily resolution, including 2 m air temperature, relative humidity, 10 m wind speed, surface pressure, downward shortwave radiation and precipitation. 3DVAR-MF-XJ is generated using a hybrid framework in which regional modelling with three-dimensional variational data assimilation first produces dynamically consistent fields (3DVAR-XJ), followed by station-constrained bias correction on a common 0.1° grid. Evaluation against 30 withheld stations and independent benchmark products shows reduced mean and root-mean-square errors, higher correlations, and improved representation of precipitation over complex terrain and shortwave radiation at high elevations relative to ERA5, ERA5-Land and CN05.1. For precipitation, RMSE is reduced by 65.6%, 64.9% and 49.3% relative to ERA5, ERA5-Land and CN05.1, respectively. 3DVAR-MF-XJ provides a unified, well-documented forcing dataset for hydrological, ecological and land–atmosphere applications in arid Xinjiang.

Background & Summary

High-quality, temporally consistent near-surface meteorological fields remain limited across arid and semi-arid regions with complex topography1,2. From Central Asian Mountain–basin systems and the eastern flanks of the Andes to the lee of the Rockies and the margins of North African plateaus, sparse and uneven station networks, strong orographic forcing, and highly localized extremes systematically degrade widely used gridded and reanalysis products, particularly for precipitation, radiation, and near-surface winds2–4. Xinjiang, located in the core of the Eurasian continent, is a prominent example: the Altai, Tianshan and Kunlun ranges encircle the Junggar and Tarim basins to form a pronounced mountain–basin system, making the region an informative testbed for developing and evaluating high-resolution meteorological reconstructions5. Addressing the demands of water-resources management, oasis agriculture, ecosystem security and hazard assessment requires accurate and internally consistent characterization of key variables, including near-surface 2 m air temperature (Tav), humidity (Rhu), 10 m wind speed (Win), surface pressure (Prs), downward shortwave radiation (Swdown) and precipitation (Pre).

Existing assessments of hydroclimatic change over Xinjiang reach divergent conclusions, with some studies suggesting a general wetting tendency and others indicating emerging aridification in parts of the region6,7. These discrepancies arise from differences in spatial domains and analysis periods, source-dependent biases among station observations, reanalysis and merged products, and the use of distinct drought and wetness metrics and potential evapotranspiration (PET) formulations, especially in the treatment of humidity, wind and radiation8,9. Together, they highlight that robust diagnosis of dry–wet evolution cannot rely on a single variable, product or methodological choice. A multivariable, physically consistent and temporally continuous regional dataset is needed to provide a common, reliable baseline for characterizing hydroclimatic variability and change10.

Approaches for generating high-resolution meteorological datasets each involve trade-offs. Station-based interpolation directly leverages observations but is highly sensitive to network density, representativeness and inhomogeneities, leading to amplified uncertainties over complex terrain11,12. Data-driven and deep-learning surrogates can capture nonlinear relationships and are computationally efficient, yet they depend strongly on the coverage and quality of training data and may extrapolate poorly beyond the historical envelope1,13,14. Regional numerical modelling with data assimilation provides physically constrained, multivariate and spatiotemporally continuous fields, but performance depends on model configuration, parameterizations and boundary forcings, and remaining errors are often substantial over mountainous regions15–17. No single method therefore guarantees physical consistency, observational fidelity and spatial completeness simultaneously18, motivating hybrid strategies that couple dynamical constraints with observation-based corrections.

Modern reanalysis products offer an essential foundation for reconstructing near-surface conditions, but systematic evaluations reveal pronounced limitations over Xinjiang, including biases in precipitation frequency–intensity characteristics, high-elevation radiation, near-surface winds and extremes, as well as temporal inhomogeneities associated with observing-system changes and sparse station coverage19–21. When used as forcing data, these deficiencies can propagate into hydrological, ecological and crop models and affect drought and wetness monitoring, flood–drought transition analysis and risk assessments22. There is thus a clear need for a regionally tailored, high-resolution dataset that better represents complex terrain while remaining traceable to observations.

In this Data Descriptor, we present a hybrid framework that combines regional modelling with data assimilation and station-informed bias correction to produce a physically coherent, observation-constrained near-surface meteorological dataset for Xinjiang for 1961–2020. First, a regional modelling system is used to obtain spatially continuous and dynamically consistent fields. Station observations are then employed to estimate and apply gridded bias corrections on a common spatial framework, thereby reducing systematic and locally varying errors while preserving cross-variable physical relationships. The resulting dataset provides gridded fields of six key variables, including 2 m air temperature, relative humidity, 10 m wind speed, surface pressure, downward shortwave radiation and precipitation, at multiple temporal resolutions. It is designed as a unified, well-documented forcing dataset to support dry-wet indices, extreme-event analyses, moisture-transport diagnostics and a wide range of hydrological, ecological, agricultural and human-environment applications over Xinjiang’s complex terrain. The 3DVAR-MF-XJ dataset provides hourly and daily near-surface meteorological variables over Xinjiang at 0.1° spatial resolution for 1961–2020, which are provided in *.mat format (MATLAB).

Methods

Study area

The study domain covers the Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region of China, encompassing high mountain ranges, intramontane basins, deserts and oasis landscapes (Fig. 1). Elevation increases sharply from a few hundred metres in the Junggar and Tarim basins to over 8,611 m in the surrounding Altai, Tianshan and Kunlun ranges, creating strong surface heterogeneity and pronounced local climatic gradients. The region lies under the prevailing influence of the mid-latitude westerlies; in combination with orographic uplift, this produces a characteristic “wet mountains-dry basins” pattern in precipitation. Steep topographic gradients, heterogeneous land cover and especially sparse gauges over high-elevation and desert areas result in strong spatial and temporal variability in near-surface air temperature, humidity, wind, radiation and precipitation, and exacerbate sampling and representativeness errors23. These physical and observational features place strict demands on spatial resolution, temporal consistency and multivariable coherence of forcing data, underscoring the need for a dedicated high-resolution meteorological dataset tailored to Xinjiang’s complex terrain.

Data sources

Surface stations

We use daily records from the national surface meteorological network of China (version 3.0), comprising 105 stations within Xinjiang. Available variables include daily maximum, minimum and mean 2-m air temperature, precipitation, mean sea-level and surface pressure, mean wind speed, relative humidity and sunshine duration. Sub-daily observations at 02:00, 08:00, 14:00 and 20:00 local time are available for air temperature, pressure, wind and humidity, and precipitation is reported as two accumulations (20:00–08:00 and 08:00–20:00). The historical series have been homogenized and quality controlled by the data provider. All 105 stations were used within their actual observation periods, which are not identical across stations. If a station had no valid records in a given year, it was excluded for that year. Isolated missing or suspected values within otherwise available records were reconstructed using a two-step procedure: stepwise regression with neighbouring stations within 150 km, followed, when necessary, by same-day multi-year climatological infilling. This procedure was applied only to isolated missing values and was not used to extend records beyond the original observation period. Fewer than 0.1% of station records required infilling, so the effect on long-term statistics is expected to be negligible. Station metadata, including identifier, name, longitude, latitude, elevation and available period, are provided in the accompanying documentation file station_metadata_3DVAR_MF_XJ.xlsx. The processed station observations were used in data assimilation and station-constrained bias correction. For independent validation, 30 stations with broad spatial coverage across Xinjiang were withheld when generating 3DVAR-XJ-S and 3DVAR-MF-XJ-S and were used only as reference stations in the Technical Validation section.

Gridded and reanalysis products

The CN05.1 gridded dataset (0.25°, daily) is used as an independent China-wide benchmark to evaluate spatial patterns and climatological consistency; it is not ingested in either the assimilation or bias-correction procedures24. CN05.1 provides daily maximum, minimum and mean temperature, precipitation, mean pressure, mean wind speed, relative humidity and sunshine duration.

The ERA5 reanalysis (0.25°, hourly) supplies initial and lateral boundary conditions for the WRF model and serves as the background field for three-dimensional variational (3DVAR) assimilation25. Input variables include upper-air temperature, humidity, horizontal wind and geopotential height on 37 pressure levels from 1000 to 1 hPa, together with 2 m air temperature, total precipitation, 10-m wind speed, surface pressure, mean sea-level pressure, and soil temperature and moisture.

The ERA5-Land dataset (0.1°, hourly) is employed exclusively for independent evaluation26. For this purpose, we derive daily aggregates of 2-m temperature and dew point, precipitation, 10-m wind speed, downward shortwave and longwave radiation and surface pressure over the Xinjiang domain.

Satellite observations

We assimilate radiances from the Advanced Himawari Imager (AHI) aboard Himawari-8, operated by the Japan Meteorological Agency as part of the WMO Global Observing System. Because Himawari-8 became operational only during the later part of the study period, AHI radiances were assimilated only when available; for earlier years, the assimilation system relied on surface meteorological station observations and ERA5-based background fields. We use the infrared water vapor channels (bands 8–10) as constraints due to their reduced sensitivity to surface and cloud-top emission and enhanced sensitivity to tropospheric humidity, as well as their high-frequency full-disk sampling. These satellite observations are used only within the assimilation cycle to provide additional constraints on tropospheric moisture and cloud-related structures and are not used in the subsequent station-constrained bias-correction step.

Topography

Static topographic fields are derived from the ASTER GDEM v003 (Advanced Spaceborne Thermal Emission and Reflection Radiometer Global Digital Elevation Model), provided by NASA and JAXA, with a native spatial resolution of 30 m and near-global land coverage. The Xinjiang domain and surrounding regions are extracted and converted to the required model format to provide elevation and related terrain fields for the WRF configuration.

Land use/land cover

Land-use and land-cover information was specified according to data availability. For 1961–2000, when annual MODIS land-cover products were unavailable, the default static land-use fields from the WRF Preprocessing System (WPS) geographical input dataset were used to generate the lower-boundary land-surface fields for WRF. For 2001–2020, annual MODIS MCD12Q1 v061 land-cover data were reclassified to the WRF land-use categories, interpolated to the model grid and used to update land-use information year by year. This approach allows the post-2001 simulations to incorporate annually varying land cover while maintaining a consistent modelling framework over 1961–2020. The potential influence of this period-dependent treatment is discussed in the Usage Notes.

Regional modelling and data assimilation

To generate spatially continuous and dynamically consistent near-surface meteorological fields over Xinjiang’s complex terrain, we employ a regional dynamical downscaling and data assimilation framework based on the Weather Research and Forecasting (WRF) model coupled with the WRF Data Assimilation (WRFDA) system. The Advanced Research WRF (ARW) core is configured with two one-way nested domains (D01: 30 km, 149 × 145 grid points; D02: 10 km, 202 × 172 grid points) centered at 40 °N, 85 °E (Fig. 2), and 40 vertical sigma levels. The inner domain fully covers Xinjiang and adjacent regions. The model time step is 90 s, and outputs are archived at hourly intervals. To limit long-term drift and maintain consistency with the large-scale circulation, the integration is divided into consecutive 5-day segments. Each segment is initialized from ERA5, the first 24 h are treated as spin-up and discarded, and the subsequent 96 h are retained as analysis fields before reinitialization for the next segment.

WRF model domains (D01: 30 km; D02: 10 km) and locations of surface meteorological stations within D02.

Hourly ERA5 reanalysis fields at 0.25° resolution provide the initial and lateral boundary conditions and serve as the background for 3DVAR assimilation. The physical parameterization suite was selected on the basis of a separate WRF physics evaluation for arid Xinjiang, in which 34 combinations of physical parameterization schemes were tested for multi-decadal near-surface climate simulation and evaluated against surface meteorological observations27. The evaluation considered key near-surface variables, including air temperature, precipitation, wind speed, relative humidity, surface pressure and downward shortwave radiation, using standard error and correlation metrics. The present dataset therefore adopts the parameterization suite identified as the most suitable in that assessment. Full details of the tested combinations, evaluation metrics and selection results are provided in the companion preprint27.

Data assimilation is implemented using the WRFDA 3DVAR system. In this framework, the analysis is obtained by minimizing a cost function that balances departures from the background field against departures from assimilated observations. The relative contributions of the background and observations are controlled by the prescribed background-error covariance and observation-error statistics. We used the standard WRFDA 3DVAR background-error covariance configuration in the assimilation experiments rather than applying additional terrain-specific covariance tuning for Xinjiang. Accordingly, the assimilation step provides dynamically balanced multivariable fields constrained by available observations, while residual local errors may remain in topographically complex and observation-sparse areas.

Two observational streams were assimilated when available: (i) near-surface air temperature, relative humidity, wind speed and pressure from the national surface meteorological stations, and (ii) brightness temperatures from the Himawari-8/Advanced Himawari Imager (AHI) infrared water-vapour channels (bands 8–10) for the period when Himawari-8 observations were available. Station observations provide the primary and temporally consistent constraint on surface and boundary-layer fields throughout the study period, whereas satellite radiances provide additional constraints on tropospheric moisture and cloud-related structures during the satellite era.

The combined dynamical downscaling and multi-source assimilation procedure yields an hourly, multivariable analysis for 1961–2020 over Xinjiang, hereafter referred to as the Three-Dimensional Variational Xinjiang Reanalysis Dataset (3DVAR-XJ). 3DVAR-XJ was produced with the WRF-ARW model and the WRFDA 3DVAR system, with spatial interpolation implemented using ANUSPLIN and outputs consolidated on a uniform 0.1° (~10 km) grid. This intermediate product provides the physically consistent backbone for the subsequent station-constrained bias correction used to construct the final high-resolution forcing dataset. Because the background-error covariance was not specifically optimized for Xinjiang’s complex terrain, residual terrain-related and station-density-dependent uncertainties are addressed in the later correction step and evaluated with withheld stations in the Technical Validation section.

Station-constrained bias correction and final product generation

Building on 3DVAR-XJ, we apply a station-constrained bias-correction procedure using observations from 105 national surface meteorological stations across Xinjiang to derive the final high-resolution forcing dataset, 3DVAR-MF-XJ. For the independent validation experiment, 30 of the 105 stations were withheld from both the assimilation and bias-correction procedures, and parallel verification products excluding these stations (3DVAR-XJ-S and 3DVAR-MF-XJ-S) were generated to assess the performance of the workflow with fully independent reference observations. The final released 3DVAR-MF-XJ dataset, however, was generated using all 105 stations; therefore, withholding the 30 stations affects only the validation experiment and does not reduce the quality of the released product.

Following He et al.2, we use a unified framework in which additive variables are corrected using differences and multiplicative variables using ratios. The first two steps are identical for all variables: (i) interpolate 3DVAR-XJ fields to station locations to obtain station-space analyses; (ii) compute daily station-level discrepancies after quality control, defined as analysis minus observation for additive variables and analysis divided by observation for multiplicative variables. These discrepancies are then interpolated to the 0.1° grid using thin-plate spline interpolation (ANUSPLIN) to produce spatially continuous daily error or ratio fields, which are applied to correct the gridded analyses.

For additive variables (2 m air temperature, surface pressure, relative humidity and 10 m wind speed; Fig. 3a), daily gridded fields are obtained by subtracting the interpolated error fields from the corresponding 3DVAR-XJ daily analyses. Sub-daily series are reconstructed by preserving the native diurnal structure: for each grid cell and day, corrected hourly values are defined as the corrected daily mean plus the original hourly departures from 3DVAR-XJ. This removes systematic biases while retaining high-frequency variability and event-scale evolution.

Flowchart of the station-constrained bias-correction procedure from 3DVAR-XJ to 3DVAR-MF-XJ (Numbers in parentheses after a variable name, such as Difference (1), Difference (2) or Ratio (1), Ratio (2), indicate the first, second, etc. occurrence of the same type of field in the workflow; they represent different intermediate datasets computed at different steps, not identical data).

For downward shortwave radiation (multiplicative; Fig. 3b), daily gridded fields are corrected by dividing the 3DVAR-XJ daily values by the interpolated ratio fields. Hourly radiation is reconstructed by scaling the original 3DVAR-XJ hourly-to-daily fractions with the corrected daily mean, conserving the within-day distribution.

For precipitation (Fig. 3c), we enforce consistency at both daily and monthly timescales. First, a daily error field is constructed as for additive variables and applied to obtain bias-corrected daily precipitation, which is then disaggregated to hourly values using the original 3DVAR-XJ hourly fractions. In parallel, an independent monthly error field is derived from station–grid differences in monthly accumulations and used to obtain bias-corrected monthly totals. A monthly closure step is then applied: for each grid cell and month, the ratio between the bias-corrected monthly total and the temporal sum of the reconstructed higher-frequency values is used to rescale the hourly (and corresponding daily) series, ensuring exact agreement with the corrected monthly totals while preserving the temporal distribution.

Different correction forms were used because the six variables have distinct physical constraints and error structures. Additive correction was applied to variables whose errors are best represented as signed differences, including 2 m air temperature, surface pressure, relative humidity and 10 m wind speed. Multiplicative correction was used for downward shortwave radiation because it is a non-negative flux variable and relative errors better preserve the within-day radiation distribution. Precipitation was treated separately because of its intermittency, skewed distribution and accumulation constraint. Therefore, daily correction was combined with monthly closure to preserve event timing while ensuring consistency with corrected monthly precipitation totals.

Following this procedure, we construct 3DVAR-MF-XJ, an hourly 0.1° multivariable near-surface meteorological forcing dataset for 1961–2020 including 2 m air temperature, relative humidity, 10 m wind speed, surface pressure, downward shortwave radiation and precipitation. The corresponding verification product, 3DVAR-MF-XJ-S, supports independent assessment of the bias-correction strategy, with quantitative results presented in the Technical Validation section.

Evaluation design

We evaluate the performance of 3DVAR-XJ and 3DVAR-MF-XJ using withheld stations and independent gridded products with a set of standard statistics: mean error (ME), root-mean-square error (RMSE), Pearson correlation coefficient (CC), and the composite DISO metric. ME quantifies the signed systematic bias (negative for underestimation, positive for overestimation); RMSE measures the overall error magnitude; CC characterizes the strength of the linear association between estimates and observations. DISO is used as a complementary composite metric because it integrates bias, error magnitude and temporal consistency into a single distance-based score28. It combines CC, normalized RMSE and normalized ME in a three-dimensional space and measures the Euclidean distance from the ideal point, where CC = 1 and both normalized error terms equal 0. DISO is therefore useful for comparing multivariable meteorological datasets that may show trade-offs among ME, RMSE and CC. In this study, DISO is used together with the individual metrics rather than as a replacement for them. Data Records

All datasets generated in this study are provided in an open repository. The 3DVAR-MF-XJ product, covering Xinjiang at 0.1° horizontal resolution for 1961–2020 and available at hourly and daily temporal resolutions, has been deposited in Science Data Bank (ScienceDB)29. The data are distributed in MATLAB *.mat format. Hourly fields are stored as yearly files (for example, 1961.mat, 1962.mat, …, 2020.mat), each containing hourly 0.1° gridded meteorological variables. The hourly dimension follows Coordinated Universal Time (UTC), with 24 records per day corresponding to 00:00–23:00 UTC. Daily fields are stored in an aggregated file, daily.mat, which contains daily 0.1° gridded variables for the full study period. Grid information is provided in grids.mat, including the longitude and latitude of all valid grid cells. The dataset contains six near-surface meteorological variables; their names, descriptions, units and temporal resolutions are summarized in Table 1. All variables are stored on a common 0.1° grid consistent with the modelling configuration described in the Methods section. Detailed file structures and variable attributes are also provided in the repository documentation.

Technical Validation

We evaluate 3DVAR-XJ and 3DVAR-MF-XJ using an integrated strategy that combines: (i) independent validation at withheld stations; (ii) parallel comparison with ERA5, ERA5-Land and CN05.1; and (iii) multi-metric station-based assessment. Thirty national stations were selected as withheld stations to provide broad spatial coverage across Xinjiang and to represent different terrain and climatic settings, thereby allowing validation across the full Xinjiang domain. These stations were excluded from both assimilation and bias correction when generating the verification products 3DVAR-XJ-S and 3DVAR-MF-XJ-S and were used only as independent references. This design provides an independent test of the verification products in the sense that these observations were not used in their generation, although the validation remains constrained by the spatial coverage and representativeness of the national station network. The final released 3DVAR-MF-XJ product was generated using all 105 stations; therefore, withholding the 30 stations affects only the validation experiment and does not reduce the accuracy of the final released dataset.

All products are evaluated at the withheld stations. Skill is quantified using ME, RMSE, CC and DISO, as defined in the Methods. Smaller absolute ME, smaller RMSE and DISO, and larger CC indicate better agreement with observations. The station-based metrics were calculated from daily time series at each withheld station over the available validation period. To quantify the magnitude of improvement, we additionally calculated the percentage reduction in RMSE of 3DVAR-MF-XJ-S relative to the comparison datasets and the uncorrected 3DVAR-XJ-S product (Table 2). 2 m air temperature

3DVAR-MF-XJ-S closely reproduces the seasonal cycle (Fig. 4a), with only a slight summer warm bias and a domain-mean annual ME within about ±0.2 °C at the withheld stations. This is substantially smaller than for 3DVAR-XJ-S and the reference products, for which warm or cold biases persist through most seasons. CN05.1 is cold-biased year-round, while ERA5 and ERA5-Land are systematically warm-biased, especially in summer. Interannual series (Fig. 5a) show that 3DVAR-MF-XJ-S tracks observed variability without noticeable drift, whereas 3DVAR-XJ-S remains positively biased. Boxplots (Fig. 6a) confirm that 3DVAR-MF-XJ-S attains the highest CC, the lowest RMSE and DISO, and a median ME closest to zero with the narrowest inter-station spread, indicating the most accurate and spatially consistent temperature fields. 3DVAR-XJ-S reproduces variability but retains a systematic warm bias that motivates the bias-correction step.

Precipitation

The observed domain-mean precipitation is 116.7 mm. All products overestimate this value, but the magnitude of the wet bias differs substantially among datasets. 3DVAR-MF-XJ-S shows the smallest annual bias (ME ≈ 17.4 mm) and the narrowest inter-station spread, whereas 3DVAR-XJ-S, CN05.1, ERA5 and ERA5-Land exhibit much larger wet biases (Figs. 4b, 6b). The ME of 3DVAR-MF-XJ-S decreases from about 23 mm in 1961–1990 to about 12 mm in 1991–2020, suggesting improved consistency in recent decades while preserving interannual variability consistent with observations (Fig. 5b). 3DVAR-XJ-S captures the timing of wet and dry years but remains systematically too wet, indicating that dynamical downscaling and assimilation alone cannot fully remove precipitation errors. The remaining wet bias in 3DVAR-MF-XJ-S likely reflects the intrinsic difficulty of reconstructing precipitation in arid, topographically complex terrain, where precipitation is highly intermittent and spatially heterogeneous and where high-elevation and desert regions are only sparsely constrained by stations. Overall, 3DVAR-MF-XJ-S provides the best compromise between reduced precipitation bias and realistic temporal variability among the evaluated products.

10 m Wind speed

All products exhibit positive wind-speed biases over the 30 stations, but 3DVAR-MF-XJ-S is clearly closest to the observations. Its annual ME is 0.136 m s−1, with seasonal ME between 0.05 and 0.22 m s−1 and the tightest inter-station spread, indicating stable performance across complex terrain. CN05.1 is slightly worse (ME 0.142 m s−1) with more uniform but still positive biases. 3DVAR-XJ-S strongly overestimates wind speed (ME 0.549 m s−1), especially in spring–summer, and ERA5 and ERA5-Land also have larger positive biases (0.211 and 0.214 m s−1). Errors have increased in recent decades for ERA5, ERA5-Land and 3DVAR-XJ-S, whereas 3DVAR-MF-XJ-S remain stable. Boxplots (Fig. 6c) rank 3DVAR-MF-XJ-S highest, with CC around 0.75–0.92, the lowest RMSE and DISO, and the narrowest interquartile ranges, confirming the effectiveness of the station-constrained correction for wind.

Relative humidity

The observed regional mean RH is 51.08%. 3DVAR-MF-XJ-S shows a modest dry bias (ME −3.19%) while closely reproducing the seasonal cycle and interannual fluctuations, and it achieves the best combination of CC, RMSE and DISO (Fig. 6d). 3DVAR-XJ-S is more strongly dry-biased (−6.22%), indicating that assimilation alone does not fully correct humidity errors. CN05.1 has a small long-term bias (+0.35%) but lower CC and higher RMSE than 3DVAR-MF-XJ-S, suggesting less reliable temporal and spatial structure. ERA5 and ERA5-Land exhibit larger dry biases (−4.77% and −7.55%) and weaker skill. Thus, among all products, 3DVAR-MF-XJ-S offers the most balanced RH performance.

Downward shortwave radiation

The observed climatological mean downward shortwave radiation is 15.91 MJ m−2 d−1. All products tend to overestimate it, but with differing magnitudes. 3DVAR-MF-XJ-S has a small annual ME (0.334 MJ m−2 d−1), weak seasonal and decadal variation in bias, and the highest CC with the lowest RMSE and DISO (Fig. 6f), indicating a good balance between mean-state accuracy and temporal fidelity. 3DVAR-XJ-S overestimates more strongly (1.324 MJ m−2 d−1), particularly in spring–summer. CN05.1 has near-zero long-term ME (≈ −0.003 MJ m−2 d−1) but lower CC and higher RMSE, reflecting poorer representation of variability and extremes. ERA5 and ERA5-Land show substantial positive biases (1.438 and 1.201 MJ m−2 d−1) and the weakest composite performance.

Surface pressure

The observed regional mean surface pressure is 90.86 kPa. All products show small negative biases: ME of −1.281 kPa (ERA5), −0.906 kPa (ERA5-Land), −0.330 kPa (3DVAR-XJ-S) and −0.165 kPa (3DVAR-MF-XJ-S). Seasonal dependence is weak, and biases decrease over time, especially for 3DVAR-MF-XJ-S. Boxplots (Fig. 6e) indicate similar CC and RMSE among products, but systematically smaller ME and DISO for 3DVAR-XJ-S and 3DVAR-MF-XJ-S, with 3DVAR-MF-XJ-S exhibiting the smallest inter-station spread. This indicates improved long-term stability and spatial consistency relative to reference products.

The station-based validation demonstrates that 3DVAR-MF-XJ-S provides the most consistent performance across the six variables, with smaller ME, lower RMSE and DISO, and higher CC than the external comparison datasets and the uncorrected 3DVAR-XJ-S product (Fig. 6). The percentage reduction in RMSE further quantifies this improvement (Table 2). Relative to ERA5, ERA5-Land and 3DVAR-XJ-S, RMSE is reduced by 53.5–84.8%, 59.7–83.0% and 45.2–84.2%, respectively. For precipitation, a key variable for arid-region hydroclimatic applications, RMSE is reduced by 49.3–65.6% relative to the comparison datasets and the uncorrected product. These results indicate that the station-constrained correction improves the multivariable forcing dataset rather than only a single variable.

To complement the domain-averaged statistics and station-level boxplots, we further examined the spatial and sub-daily characteristics of the validation errors. Spatially, station-level ME and RMSE of 3DVAR-MF-XJ-S were mapped at the 30 withheld stations (Figs. 7 and 8). ME identifies the direction of residual bias, with positive and negative values indicating overestimation and underestimation, respectively, whereas RMSE quantifies local error magnitude. The maps show that validation errors are not spatially uniform but vary among variables and stations, consistent with the uneven station distribution and complex terrain across Xinjiang. These spatial diagnostics help identify areas where residual errors are relatively larger and where additional caution may be needed.

Because 3DVAR-MF-XJ provides hourly fields, we also evaluated its sub-daily performance using the available station observations at 02:00, 08:00, 14:00 and 20:00 Beijing Time. The comparison was conducted for variables with instantaneous or near-instantaneous sub-daily station records, including 2 m air temperature, 10 m wind speed, relative humidity and surface pressure. The corresponding 3DVAR-MF-XJ-S values were sampled at the same observation times and evaluated at the 30 withheld stations. Mean biases are small at the four observation times, generally within ±0.3 °C for air temperature, ±0.4 m s−1 for wind speed, ±2.5% for relative humidity and approximately 0.1 kPa for surface pressure. RMSE values were summarized as mean ± standard deviation across stations for each observation time (Table 3), providing a direct sub-daily assessment of the hourly product. Precipitation was not included because station precipitation is reported as two accumulation periods, 20:00–08:00 and 08:00–20:00, rather than as instantaneous observations at the four times. Downward shortwave radiation was also not included because comparable sub-daily station observations were not available.

Finally, to assess temporal stability and suitability for climate-change applications, we divided the validation period into 1961–1990 and 1991–2020 and recalculated ME and RMSE at the 30 withheld stations as mean ± standard deviation (Table 4). The split-period validation shows broadly consistent performance between the two periods, with generally lower RMSE values in 1991–2020 for most variables and nearly unchanged RMSE for downward shortwave radiation. These results indicate that validation performance does not deteriorate in the later period and support the use of 3DVAR-MF-XJ as a stable forcing dataset for hydrological, ecological and land–atmosphere applications over Xinjiang.

Usage Notes

3DVAR-MF-XJ provides 0.1° gridded meteorological fields over Xinjiang for 1961–2020 at hourly and daily resolution. It is designed for a wide range of applications, including drought and wetness assessment, land-surface, ecosystem and hydrological modelling, evaluation of regional climate simulations and event-scale diagnostics. The dataset is distributed as annual.mat files for hourly fields (1961.mat … 2020.mat) and as an aggregated daily file (daily.mat). Grid coordinates are stored in grids.mat. Variable names and units are documented in the Data Records section and in the repository metadata. Users should note that 3DVAR-MF-XJ is a physically consistent, station-constrained forcing dataset rather than a direct replacement for local point measurements. Surface station observations provide the primary constraint throughout 1961–2020, whereas satellite radiance constraints are available only during the Himawari-8 period. Land-use information is also period-dependent: static WPS land-use data are used for 1961–2000, whereas annual MODIS MCD12Q1 land-cover data are used for 2001–2020. These differences in observational and land-surface constraints may introduce some temporal inhomogeneity; the split-period validation in the Technical Validation section is therefore provided to help users assess temporal stability. Validation is also limited by the uneven distribution of stations, especially in high-elevation, glacierized and sparsely observed areas. In addition, relative humidity retains a modest dry bias, precipitation remains more uncertain in mountainous and sparsely gauged regions because of intermittency and strong spatial heterogeneity, and radiation-related applications over high-elevation terrain should consider the local validation results. Users should therefore consult the Technical Validation results for variable-specific performance characteristics and, where appropriate, perform additional checks over their sub-domain of interest.

Data availability

The 3DVAR-MF-XJ dataset generated in this study is publicly available from Science Data Bank (ScienceDB) at https://doi.org/10.57760/sciencedb.32106. The released dataset includes hourly annual files, the aggregated daily file, grid coordinates and repository documentation required to interpret and use the gridded product. ERA5 reanalysis data used for initial and lateral boundary conditions and as background fields for data assimilation were obtained from the Copernicus Climate Data Store. ERA5 hourly data on single levels are available at https://doi.org/10.24381/cds.adbb2d47, and ERA5 hourly data on pressure levels are available at https://doi.org/10.24381/cds.bd0915c6. ERA5-Land data used for product comparison were also obtained from the Copernicus Climate Data Store; the ERA5-Land hourly product is available at https://doi.org/10.24381/cds.e2161bac. The China National Ground Meteorological Station Daily Dataset (V3.0) used for model evaluation was provided by the National Meteorological Information Center, China Meteorological Administration (CMA), and is available from the data provider, subject to its access policy, via https://data.cma.cn/. The national surface meteorological station observations used for assimilation, station-constrained bias correction and validation were also obtained from CMA and are subject to the provider’s data-use restrictions; these original station time series are therefore not redistributed as part of the public 3DVAR-MF-XJ release. The CN05.1 gridded observation dataset used for comparison was obtained from the CN05 Grid Data source maintained by the Institute of Atmospheric Physics, Chinese Academy of Sciences, and should be accessed according to the provider’s data access policy. The dataset is described by Wu and Gao (https://doi.org/10.6038/cjg20130406). The ASTER GDEM v003 elevation data used for terrain preprocessing are publicly available from NASA Earthdata at https://doi.org/10.5067/ASTER/ASTGTM.003. The MODIS MCD12Q1 v061 land-use/land-cover data used for 2001–2020 are publicly available from NASA Earthdata at https://doi.org/10.5067/MODIS/MCD12Q1.061. The WRF Preprocessing System (WPS) geographical input data used for the pre-2001 static land-use fields were obtained from the official WRF users’ download page: https://www2.mmm.ucar.edu/wrf/users/download/get_sources_wps_geog.html.

Code availability

All custom scripts used to generate and validate the 3DVAR-MF-XJ dataset are openly available at https://github.com/apsiamsuer/apsiamsuer. Some station-interpolation and bias-correction steps depend on ANUSPLIN, a non-free thin-plate spline interpolation package; ANUSPLIN itself and executable-dependent station-interpolation scripts are therefore not redistributed without permission.
