Radiofrequency ablation with a conventional solid tip catheter in contact with an implantable cardioverter-defibrillator coil causing inadvertent ventricular fibrillation
Authors: Michael R. Manogue
Published: 2026

Key Teaching Points •When monopolar radiofrequency (RF) ablation with a conventional solid tip catheter is applied in physical contact with an intracardiac electrode such as a right ventricular implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) coil, accidental ventricular fibrillation can be induced. This interaction appears to be generalizable to all RF ablation procedures when in close enough proximity to an ICD electrode in contact with myocardium and is not likely exclusive to the lattice tip catheter platform.•In Biotronik ICDs, voltage switches designed to protect internal ICD circuitry of the device can oscillate between open and closed statuses when exposed to the RF electric field, which can functionally induce current at a 40-Hz frequency capable of capturing myocardium and inducting ventricular fibrillation. The RF current is diverted away from the internal circuitry of the cardiac implantable electronic device (CIED) to the housing of the device, resulting in artifact that saturates surface electrocardiogram signals, rendering them uninterpretable to the operator during and briefly after RF current application.•Ablation operators should carefully assess the proximity of RF ablation catheter tip to intracardiac electrode terminals of CIEDs during RF delivery, given the potential for serious arrhythmia induction. Cryoablation is an alternative energy source that avoids this potential complication.

Introduction

Catheter ablation using radiofrequency (RF) energy for both atrial and ventricular arrhythmias in patients with implantable cardioverter-defibrillators (ICDs) is common in clinical practice. With routine periprocedural reprogramming of the device, clinically significant interaction between RF delivery and ICDs is rare. In some cases, the ablation target may reside in close proximity to ICD electrodes that are in contact with cardiac tissue. Several published case series have described inadvertent ventricular fibrillation (VF) induction related to monopolar RF delivery with a 9-mm diameter lattice tip catheter in proximity to the ICD coil for both atrial and ventricular arrhythmia targets, with some investigators reporting the use of a conventional solid tip RF catheter as a successful alternative in such cases.1,2 This case report describes the repetitive induction of VF with RF ablation delivery using a conventional 3.5-mm solid tip RF ablation electrode near an RV ICD coil, proposes an underlying mechanism for this interaction, and suggests strategies to avoid this life-threatening complication.

Case report

A 44-year-old man with chronic ischemic cardiomyopathy sustained an ICD shock for premature ventricular contraction (PVC)-induced VF. He experienced palpitations that were correlated with monomorphic PVCs, which occurred despite β-blocker therapy. This PVC was felt to be the most likely trigger for the VF episode, and catheter ablation was planned. The patient had a single-chamber VDD ICD system, with an atrial sensing bipole and a single ICD coil implanted in a left pectoral position 8 months before the ICD shock (Acticor 7 VR-T DX ICD, Biotronik, Lake Oswego, OR) with normal chronic function.

PVCs were localized to the RV moderator band using a combination of activation mapping and pace mapping. At the selected ablation target site, a local bipolar signal was recorded 26 ms before the onset of the QRS complex on the PVC beat as well as a late potential during sinus rhythm (Figure 1A). Ablation was performed under direct integrated intracardiac ultrasound guidance with a CARTO 3 V7 mapping system (Biosense Webster, Irvine, CA) (Figure 1B). A zero-fluoroscopy approach was used in accordance with the expertise and practice patterns of the operator. Chronologically, the case was performed several years before submission, and because of that, images from the electroanatomic map are no longer available. Intracardiac bipolar electrograms (EGMs) were recorded with a band-pass filter from 30 to 500 Hz. Just before the onset of RF, there were a series of likely catheter-induced PVCs similar to the clinical PVC morphology followed by a pause and then a single demand ventricular paced QRS complex by the ICD at the programmed lower rate limit (40 beats/min). Next, there was nearly immediate signal saturation on the surface 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG). Simultaneous to the ECG signal being obscured by artifact, there was a high-frequency, irregular signal observed on the distal and proximal electrodes of the RF catheter, heralding direct contact of the RF catheter tip with the ICD coil during RF application (Figure 1C). The duration of this high-frequency signal was approximately 400 ms. RF energy was interrupted as soon as this was recognized. Once surface ECG signal recovered, VF was observed (Figure 1C), and the patient was emergently defibrillated with an externally delivered shock. Ventricular EGMs on the RF catheter show that VF was ongoing immediately after termination of the RF current before it was clearly discernible on ECG. The high-frequency signal on the ablation catheter and ECG saturation also coincided with the catheter inadvertently contacting the ICD coil, visible in real time on intracardiac echocardiography.

Initially, the cause of VF induction and ECG artifact was not immediately apparent. The possibility of a defective ablation catheter was considered initially, and the cables and dispersive electrode pad were exchanged. There was no visible problem with the RF catheter on inspection, and as an integrity test of the catheter and its associated electrical connections, the next RF application was delivered with the catheter tip positioned intentionally in the right atrial blood pool, not contacting cardiac tissue—this produced no ECG artifact and no abnormal signal on the RF catheter. The ICD was interrogated during the procedure and demonstrated normal sensing, pacing threshold/impedance, and shock impedance values. RF ablation was then applied with the catheter tip intentionally in contact with the atrial sensing electrode as visualized directly on intracardiac echocardiography, with simultaneous intracardiac EGM recordings, which showed high-frequency, irregular electrical noise on the atrial bipole that correlated with surface ECG artifact, similar to what was observed when the catheter inadvertently contacted the ICD coil while ablating (Figure 2A and 2B). This suggested that the ECG artifact observed during RF catheter/ICD electrode contact was related to current being diverted from an intracardiac ICD lead electrode to the ICD generator housing, leading to a voltage gradient across the skin, producing the ECG artifact. No intracardiac EGMs from the ICD were recorded during incidental catheter contact with the ICD coil since these were inherently accidental, unanticipated events. With this information, the presumed source of VF induction and ECG artifact was inadvertent contact of the RF catheter tip with the coil of the ICD while delivering RF ablation. Attempts were then made at ablating at the original target site on the RV moderator band with specific effort to avoid contact with the ICD lead during RF delivery, using a steerable sheath for catheter tip stability. However, because of unavoidable respiratory catheter motion, multiple subsequent ablation attempts entailed incidental RF electrode/ICD coil contact (Figure 2C), one of which caused an additional sustained VF episode requiring external defibrillation (Figure 2D). It was then determined that complete avoidance of catheter contact with the ICD lead would not be feasible at this target site. The ablation energy source was changed from RF to cryoablation energy, and an 8-mm tip cryo catheter was positioned at the target site (Freezor Max, Medtronic). A total of 8 lesions were delivered at the target site without ventricular arrhythmias with PVCs eradicated at the conclusion of the procedure.

ICD interrogation immediately after the procedure showed sensing, threshold, and pace impedance values in the normal range and consistent with established trends. Shock impedance before the procedure had averaged 80–99 Ω and remained in this range on remote monitoring trends chronically after the procedure. There was a subsequent spontaneous VF event approximately 6 months after the ablation procedure, resulting in delivery of a successful 40-J shock with an impedance of 95 Ω.

Discussion

RF ablation entails application of electric current to a catheter tip in contact with cardiac tissue and subsequent generation of resistive heating to destroy tissue and eliminate excitability. The frequency range of such RF current is 300–750 kHz, which does not result in cardiac tissue depolarization, given that its frequency range is far above the myocardial stimulation threshold, which is generally less than 100 Hz.3 This case illustrates an important life-threatening complication of RF ablation with a conventional solid tip catheter platform in patients with a transvenous ICD system, building on similar experiences reported with a lattice tip–type RF ablation electrode.1,2,4 An important distinction between this case and those reported with the lattice tip RF catheter relates to the apparent proximity requirements to induce ventricular arrhythmia. With a lattice tip RF ablation platform, VF has been observed with general proximity of the ablation catheter to the ICD coil, including targets in the atrium and opposite ventricular chamber, but without necessarily entailing direct physical contact.1,2,4 Presumably, the ability of the lattice tip RF ablation catheter to interact with the ICD coil at further distances relates to the larger electric field as a function of its comparatively larger catheter tip volume.

In this case, evidence of direct physical contact between the solid tip electrode and the ICD coil during RF application preceded each instance of ECG artifact and VF induction (confirmed using intracardiac echocardiography and intracardiac EGM tracings). No sustained ventricular arrhythmias or ECG artifacts were observed with any of the remaining RF applications delivered in close proximity but without evidence of direct contact with the ICD coil. Inadvertent VF induction thus also appears to be possible with a solid tip RF catheter in the monopolar ablation configuration if it makes physical contact with the ICD electrode during RF application. This supports the concept that VF induction with RF ablation in close proximity to the ICD electrode is likely a generalizable characteristic of monopolar RF ablation not specific to a single catheter platform, which carries important implications for strategies to prevent this complication. Possible underlying mechanisms have been suggested, including the concept of the ICD coil serving as an antenna to electrically couple the RF energy to the myocardium or induce voltage on the ICD electrode in a manner that produces depolarization, insulation breach resulting in compromised ICD lead integrity allowing current shunting into the system, or the possibility that tissue heating at the target site (independent of ICD electrode contact) induces VF.1,2,4

It is possible that VF induction in this case was a result of RF-induced automaticity at the target site, particularly given that PVCs from this site were the cause of clinical VF episodes. However, arguing against this as a proposed mechanism is the fact that of the 15 total RF ablation applications at the target site, 8 were associated with simultaneous catheter/ECG lead artifact as shown (2 of these induced sustained VF, 2 of them produced nonsustained VT, and the remaining 4 were not associated with any arrhythmia), and in none of the remaining RF applications applied to the moderator band target site did sustained arrhythmia or significant degrees of automaticity occur. It is also unlikely that an insulation breach or ICD system malfunction accounted for the induced arrhythmia, given that there were no acutely identified abnormalities on interrogation during the procedure, immediately after, or at the time of successful high-voltage shock delivered 6 months after. Furthermore, the similarities between this case and those already published with the lattice tip catheter platform support the interpretation of VF induction as a result of interaction between RF ablation and the ICD coil.

Direct coupling of RF current to an ICD coil alone would not be expected to capture myocardium, however, given that RF current is in the kilohertz range, which is a much higher frequency than what is capable of depolarizing myocardium. This implies that RF current interaction with the ICD coil in some fashion results in the frequency of the RF current being “reduced” into a range capable of capturing cardiac tissue to result in VF induction. To date, this phenomenon has only been reported in ICDs from a single manufacturer.

Biotronik transvenous ICDs are equipped with an overvoltage detection unit, which is a fixed, nonprogrammable feature designed to protect the internal ICD circuitry by preventing ingress of excess external voltage gradients that might be encountered clinically—during external defibrillation, for instance (T. Doerr, M.Sc., written communication, May 19, 2026). Each intracardiac electrode of the ICD system is equipped with its own voltage-gated switch. When excess external voltage is detected by the device, the switch is opened, diverting that voltage away from the internal processing components of the ICD to the common electrical ground of the system, which is the external housing of the ICD. After a 25-ms window, the switch is automatically closed to allow resumption of sensing, detection, and pacing therapy, which is temporarily disabled while the switch is open. In the presence of a continuously applied RF electric field in sufficient proximity to an ICD electrode, this switch can then be opened and closed in a cyclical fashion. The oscillating opening and closing of the circuitry protection mechanism modulates the input impedance at the ICD electrode at a frequency of approximately 40 Hz, which can result in sufficient current flow and localized voltage gradient to capture myocardial tissue, resulting in induction of VF (Figure 3). This oscillation of voltage at the ICD electrode when exposed to RF current has been confirmed by the manufacturer in unpublished bench testing (T. Doerr, M.Sc., written communication, May 19, 2026) and is the most probable mechanism of clinically observed instances of VF induction in this case and in those reported with the lattice tip catheter.Figure 3Schematic demonstrating the proposed mechanism of VF induction with RF ablation near a Biotronik transvenous ICD system. Whether this is a generalizable interaction of RF ablation with ICDs from other manufacturers is unknown, but it would seem prudent to exercise caution in similar circumstances with an ICD from other manufacturers. Further research from RF catheter manufacturers to quantify the electric field strength of their platforms and data from ICD manufacturers to determine the susceptibility of their systems to this form of interference would be useful to help determine the distance at which an operator might reasonably safely deliver RF current in proximity to an ICD electrode.

Conclusion

Monopolar RF current delivery when the ablation catheter tip is in physical contact with an RV ICD coil can cause inadvertent induction of VF. In Biotronik ICDs, this is likely due to the interaction of the RF electric field with the ICD circuit voltage protection mechanism, resulting in variations in input impedance to the ICD coil that produce localized voltage fluctuations in a frequency spectrum capable of capturing myocardial tissue and inducing VF. This case suggests that this clinically relevant interaction of this manufacturer’s ICD devices is generalizable to monopolar RF ablation with all catheter form factors, not just with a lattice tip platform. Electrophysiologists should be aware of this when applying RF energy in the vicinity of an ICD lead and take care at a minimum to avoid physical contact and limit physical proximity between the ablation catheter tip and intracardiac electrodes of a Biotronik ICD. For ablation targets in close proximity to an ICD electrode, cryo energy is an alternative that avoids this potential complication.
