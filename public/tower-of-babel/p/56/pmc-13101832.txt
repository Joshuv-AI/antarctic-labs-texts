Lateralization of facial recognition in Tibetan and Han individuals: Evidence from eye movement
Authors: Xingchen Guo, Jialin Ma, Yongxin Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In this study, eye movement technology was used to explore the lateralization characteristics of Han and Tibetan individuals when they recognized faces of individuals of their own and other ethnicities. In Experiment 1, the faces were divided into two areas of interest: the left and right sides. The results revealed that left lateralization occurred when participants of both ethnicities recognized faces of individuals of their own and other nationalities. In Experiment 2, the faces were divided into six areas of interest: the left and right eyes, the left and right sides of the nose, and the left and right sides of the mouth. The results revealed that the focus of left lateralization was the nose and mouth for the faces of Han individuals, whereas that for the faces of Tibetan individuals was the mouth. The results indicate that left visual lateralization occurs when the faces of Tibetan and Han individuals are recognized and that this lateralization differs across different ethnicities.

Introduction

Why do we always think that foreigners look the same? When watching foreign movies, the audience is often unable to accurately identify the protagonist. Is that Mary or Maria? Isn't she Mary? Why does everyone call her Maria? However, this problem does not exist when we watch domestic movies. This phenomenon is called the cross-race effect (CRE) in facial recognition. Malpass and Kravitz (1969) defined the CRE as the phenomenon in which individuals’ performance in recognizing faces of people of their own race is better than that in recognizing the faces of people of other races (Bothwell, Brigham, & Malpass, 1989; Ho & Pezdek, 2016). The CRE has been confirmed by multiple studies (Quinn, Lee, Pascalis, & Tanaka, 2015; Sun, Song, Bentin, Yang, & Zhao, 2013; Stahl, Wiese, & Schweinberger, 2010; Wiese, 2012; Xiao et al., 2015). In recent years, with the rise of eye-movement technology, researchers have begun to use this technology to explore the causes of the CRE. Eye-movement technology can directly reflect the visual processing of facial features and is widely used in exploring the visual processing mechanism of the CRE (Blais, Jack, Scheepers, Fiset, & Caldara, 2008; Hu, 2012; Kelly, Miellet, & Caldara, 2010; Liu, Jia, Liu, & Long, 2011; Wheeler et al., 2011).

Researchers usually divide faces into different areas of interest (AOIs) when the visual scanning patterns of cross-racial facial recognition are explored. Previous studies have shown that individuals focus primarily on three facial features—eyes, nose, and mouth—and that different gaze patterns for these three facial features often reflect differences in processing (Kelly et al., 2010). Therefore, some researchers have divided faces into three AOIs—eyes, nose and mouth—to explore the fixation characteristics of cross-racial facial recognition (Brielmann, Bülthoff, & Armann, 2014; Miellet, Vizioli, He, Zhou, & Caldara, 2013; Ma, Yang, & Li, 2022; Yüvrük, Tütüncü, & Amado, 2024). In Asian subjects, the fixation duration and fixation counts on the eyes and nose were significantly longer and greater, respectively, than those on the mouth (Goldinger, He, & Papesh, 2009; Kelly et al., 2010; Miellet et al., 2013). However, compared with those of the nose and mouth, the fixation duration and fixation counts of Western subjects on the eyes were significantly longer and greater, respectively (Brielmann et al., 2014; Wang et al., 2015). The reason for this difference is that Western subjects adopt a facial feature processing strategy that focuses more on the eyes (Brielmann et al., 2014). Compared with other facial features (such as the nose and mouth), individuals can obtain more information from the eyes; therefore, Caucasian individuals focus more on the eyes than other facial features. East Asian subjects adopt a holistic processing strategy that starts from the center and moves to the periphery of the face (Chua, Boland, & Nisbett, 2005; Ma et al., 2022). As the upper nose bridge between the eyes is the central area of East Asian faces (Ma et al., 2022; Tardif et al., 2017), East Asian subjects have longer fixation durations and greater fixation counts on the eyes and nose in the central region than on other facial features did.

Some researchers have divided faces into upper half and lower half AOIs. For example, Wheeler et al. (2011) divided faces into two AOIs (upper half and lower half), and Western subjects were asked to scan African and Western faces. The results showed that Western individuals fixated on the upper half of faces of individuals of their own race significantly more than on the faces of African individuals and on the lower half of faces of individuals of their own race significantly less than on the faces of African individuals did, although for both races, they focused more on the upper half of the faces than on the lower half. Wheeler et al. (2011) reported that this result is due to the difference in facial structure between Westerners and Africans. Although Western individuals prefer to process the eye region (the upper half of the face) to recognize faces, African faces have wider noses (the lower half of the face) than Western faces do, and this feature can attract more attention. Therefore, compared with Africans, Western individuals focus more on the upper half of faces of individuals of their own race and less on the lower half of faces of individuals of their own race.

Finally, some researchers divided faces into two AOIs (left and right) or six AOIs (left eye, right eye, left nose, right nose, left mouth and right mouth) to explore the left visual lateralization features of cross-racial facial recognition (Ma et al., 2022). Left lateralization of the face refers to the duration and number of scans on the left side of the face by individuals being significantly greater than those on the right side of the face. For example, in Experiment 1, Ma et al. (2022) divided faces into left and right AOIs and asked Chinese individuals to scan faces of their own race and Western faces without any task in mind. The fixation counts and durations on the left half of the face were significantly greater than those on the right half of the face, and race had no significant effect. In Experiment 2, Ma et al. divided facial features into six AOIs along the midline of the face (left and right eyes, left and right nose, and left and right mouth). The eyes and mouth, but not the nose, were the focus of left visual lateralization, and this left visual literalization was not affected by race. Ma et al. (2022) and others believed that this is because Chinese individuals (East Asians) adopt a processing strategy that moves from the facial center to the surrounding area. The facial center of both East Asians and Westerners is the nose; therefore the focus of lateralization is not the nose in Chinese and Western individuals. The eyes and mouth are not in the center of the face; thus the focuses of left lateralization are mainly these two areas. In summary, the cross-racial differences in facial recognition are related not only to processing strategies (for example, Westerners use feature processing, focusing more on the eye areas, whereas East Asians process from the center of the face to the surrounding areas) but also to facial structure (for example, a wide nose on the faces of African individuals increases the fixation on the lower half of the face).

Further studies revealed that the CRE also exists in facial recognition among subculture groups within the same race. The CRE can be observed when East Asian subjects are recognizing faces of individuals of their own ethnicity and other ethnicities (Camras et al., 2007; Liu et al., 2017; Ma, Yang, Luo, & Ding, 2020; Wang, Zhang, Liu, Liu, & Miao, 2019) that belong to the same race. For example, Yue (2018) adopted the learning-recognition paradigm and required Han subjects to learn the faces of Han and Tibetan individuals. The results revealed that the recognition accuracy of faces of individuals of their own ethnicity was significantly greater than that of faces of individuals of Tibetan individuals, which was considered the CRE. With the help of ethology and eye-movement technology, Ma, Zhang, and Li (2023) divided faces into three AOIs (eyes, nose and mouth) to explore the CRE in facial recognition and visual scanning patterns of Tibetan and Han individuals. The results revealed that the CRE was present when Tibetan and Han subjects recognized faces of individuals of their own and other ethnicities. Eye-movement results revealed that Tibetan and Han subjects focused more on eye areas when they recognized the faces of Han individuals and more on eye and nose areas when they recognized the faces of Tibetan individuals. Ma et al. (2023) reported that the difference was not due to the facial scanning pattern but to the structure of the faces of Tibetan and Han individuals. They reported that Tibetan and Han individuals both use the scanning pattern of processing from the facial center (the central region of the face refers to the geometric center point of the face) to the periphery. However, the facial centers of Tibetan and Han individuals differ because of their different facial structures; thus, the visual scanning patterns also differ. Specifically, the upper area of the nose between the eyes is the center of the faces of Han individuals; thus, Tibetan and Han individuals focus more on the eyes, which are part of the central area. Compared with Han individuals, Tibetan individuals have longer noses and facial shapes, and the central area on the face is the mid-ridge of the nose (which is located adjacent to the nose and eyes), as shown in Figure 1 (Ma et al., 2023; Ma, Huang, & Li, 2025). Therefore Tibetan and Han individuals focus more on the eyes and nose in the central area of the faces of Tibetan individuals. Thus Ma et al. (2023) reported that the differences in facial scanning patterns between Tibetan and Han individuals are caused by differences in facial structure.

However, the study by Ma et al. (2023) did not further explore whether there are differences in lateralization when Tibetan and Han individuals recognize faces of their own and other ethnicities. As mentioned above, the visual lateralization of facial recognition is also an important aspect when the visual processing features of cross-racial facial recognition are explored (Ma et al., 2022). Therefore, whether the CRE and visual lateralization features were present when Tibetan and Han individuals recognized the faces of individuals of their own and other nationalities was explored in this study. First, the study explored whether left lateralization occurred in Tibetan and Han individuals’ recognition of faces of individuals from different ethnic groups. Because Ma et al. (2022) reported that left lateralization of individual facial recognition is culturally universal, we assumed that both Han and Tibetan individuals would exhibit left lateralization when they recognized faces of individuals of their own and other ethnic groups and that left lateralization would not be affected by ethnicity. In Experiment 1, the faces were divided into left and right AOIs, as shown in Figure 2.

Left and right AOIs in Experiment 1.

Experiment 1 was conducted to explore the visual lateralization of Han and Tibetan individuals in recognizing faces of individuals of their own ethnicity and other ethnicities. When individuals recognize faces, they primarily scan three facial features: the eyes, nose, and mouth (Kelly et al., 2010). It remains unclear whether individuals exhibit similar lateralization patterns when recognizing these facial features. Therefore, in Experiment 2, the faces were divided into six AOIs (as shown in Figure 3: left eye, right eye, left area of nose, right area of nose, left area of mouth and right area of mouth) to further explore whether left lateralization occurred when Tibetan and Han individuals recognized different facial features. The lateralization of facial features may be affected by facial structure. As mentioned in the study by Ma et al. (2023), the facial center of the Han people is between the eyes, and individuals use a scanning pattern moving from the center to the periphery. Therefore there is no left lateralization when Tibetan and Han individuals fixate on the eyes in the central area of the Han face, and lateralization may be manifested mainly on the nose and mouth. The central area of the Tibetan face is the adjacent area between the nose and eyes, and left lateralization may be observed when individuals focus on the mouth but not on the nose and eyes in the central area. Therefore Hypothesis 2 was proposed: When Tibetan and Han individuals recognize the faces of Tibetan individuals, the focus of left lateralization is mainly on the mouth, whereas when they recognize the faces of Han individuals, the focus of left lateralization is mainly on the nose and mouth.

Experiment 1

Method

Participants

Eighty-one Han and Tibetan subjects were included in this experiment, with 39 Tibetan subjects (21.30 ± 3.27, 20 males) and 42 Han subjects (22.10 ± 3.27, 21 males). All Tibetan subjects were recruited from Tibetan autonomous prefectures in southern Gansu Province, China, whereas all Han subjects were recruited from areas in which Han people are concentrated and have fewer opportunities to interact with ethnic minorities. All the subjects were right-handed with normal or corrected-to-normal vision and provided informed consent.

Material

The facial images used in the experiment were selected from the “Facial-Expression Database of Chinese Han, Hui and Tibetan People” (FE-CHHT, Ma et al., 2020). A total of 96 facial images, including 48 Tibetan faces (24 males) and 48 Han faces (24 males), were included. All the faces are neutral and maintain consistent brightness and contrast. Photoshop CS6 was used to remove irrelevant elements such as hair and ears from all the faces, with a resolution of 640 × 480 pixels and color removal. All the faces were divided into left and right sides, two areas of interest are divided by the facial midline, as determined via the coordinate system provided on the Eyelink 1000 Plus display screen, as shown in Figure 2. The area of each face was approximately 8 cm², and the area of each AOI measured approximately 4 cm².

Equipment

Experimental Builder 1.4.0 software was used for the program, and eye-movement data were recorded by an EyeLink 1000 plus eye tracker. The sampling rate was 1000 Hz, and the line-of-sight error was accurate within 5°. The screen resolution was 1024 × 768 pixels, and the distance between the subject and the display screen was 65 cm. The visual angle of the screen was 15.5° × 11.7°, and the visual angle of the facial images was 9.85° × 7.5°.

Design

A design of 2 face ethnicities (Han face, Tibetan face) × 2 subject ethnicities (Han, Tibetan) × 2 AOIs (left half of face, right half of face) was adopted in this experiment. Subject ethnicity was the between-subjects variable, and face ethnicity and AOI (as shown in Figure 2) were the within-subjects variables.

Procedure

The experimental procedure was consistent with that of Ma et al. (2022). Instructions were displayed on the screen, asking the subjects to carefully study the 96 facial pictures presented randomly. The subjects were told that they would be tested on their observations later. In fact, there was no test; the purpose of the instructions was to ensure that the subjects scanned the faces intently. After the subjects read the instructions carefully, they could press any key to start the experiment. First, a fixation point “+” was presented on the screen for one second, and then a facial picture was randomly presented for five seconds. This was one trial, and the experiment included 96 trials in total. Eyelink 1000 Plus was used to record the subjects’ eye-movement data throughout the experiment.

Results

Repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) with 2 face ethnicities (Han face, Tibetan face) × 2 subject ethnicities (Han, Tibetan) × 2 AOIs (left half of face, right half of face) revealed that the main effect of facial AOI was significant for fixation duration (F(1, 79) = 9.52, p < .001, η² = .11), with a longer fixation duration on the left half of the face than on the right half of the face (see Table 1 for details). The interactions between facial ethnicity and AOI (F(1, 79) = 2.29, p = 0.13, η² = 0.03) and between subject ethnicity and AOI (F(1, 79) = 0.06, p = 0.80, η² = 0.01) were not significant. The interactions among facial ethnicity, subject ethnicity and AOI were not significant (F(1, 79) = 0.53, p = 0.47, η² = 0.01).

Experiment 2

Method

Participants

Sixty Han and Tibetan subjects were included in the experiment, with 30 Tibetan subjects (22.10 ± 2.46, 15 males) and 30 Han subjects (21.66 ± 2.90, 15 males). All Tibetan subjects came from Tibetan autonomous prefectures in southern Gansu Province, China, whereas all Han subjects were recruited from areas in which Han people are concentrated and have fewer opportunities to interact with ethnic minorities. All the subjects were right-handed with normal or corrected-to-normal vision and provided informed consent.

Material, equipment, and procedures

The experimental materials, equipment and procedures were the same as those in Experiment 1. The facial AOIs were divided according to Ma et al. (2022). the areas of the left and right eyes were both 0.85 cm², the areas of the left and right sides of the nose were both 0.95 cm², and the areas of the left and right sides of the mouth were both 0.8 cm².

Design

The experiment adopted a design of 2 face ethnicities (Han face, Tibetan face) × 2 subject ethnicities (Han, Tibetan) × 6 AOIs (left eye, right eye, left side of nose, right side of nose, left side of mouth, right side of mouth). Subject ethnicity was the between-subjects variable, and face ethnicity and AOI (as shown in Figure 3) were the within-subject variables.

Fixation counts on facial areas of interest

Repeated-measures ANOVA with 2 face ethnicities (Han face, Tibetan face) × 2 subject ethnicities (Han, Tibetan) × 6 AOIs (left eye, right eye, left nose, right nose, left mouth, right mouth) revealed that the main effect of facial AOIs was significant for the fixation count (F(5, 54) = 49.73, p < 0.001, η² = 0.39). The fixation counts on the left nose were significantly greater than those on the right nose; the fixation counts on the left mouth were significantly greater than those on the right mouth, and there was no significant difference in the fixation counts on the left eye and the right eye (see Table 2 and Figure 4A for details).

Additionally, the interaction between facial ethnicity and facial AOI was significant (F(5, 290) = 8.52, p < 0.001, η² = 0.10). After simple effect analysis, for the Han faces, the fixation counts on the left nose and mouth were significantly greater than those on the right nose and mouth were (F(5, 54) = 61.88, p < 0.001, η² = 0.81), and there was no significant difference in the fixation counts on the left and right eyes. For Tibetan faces, the fixation counts on the left mouth were significantly greater than those on the right mouth (F(5, 54) = 53.32, p < 0.001, η² = 0.78), and there were no differences in the fixation counts on the left and right eyes or nose (see Table 3 and Figure 5B for details). The interaction between subject ethnicity and AOI (F(5, 290) = 0.98, p = 0.43, η² = 0.02) and the interaction among face ethnicity, subject ethnicity and AOI (F(5, 290) = 1.09, p = 0.37, η² = 0.02) were not significant.

Discussion

In Experiment 1, the subjects were Han and Tibetan, and the faces were divided into two AOIs, left and right. Both Han and Tibetan subjects exhibited left lateralization when they recognized faces of individuals of their own and other ethnicities, and this lateralization was not affected by facial ethnicity or subject ethnicity; therefore, Hypothesis 1 was verified. To further explore the lateralization of Tibetan and Han individuals in recognizing faces of individuals of different ethnicities, in Experiment 2, the faces were divided into six AOIs: left eye, right eye, left nose, right nose, left mouth and right mouth. When Han and Tibetan subjects recognized faces of individuals of their own and other ethnicities, there was not only left lateralization, as confirmed in Experiment 1, but also different fixation preferences for specific facial features. The left lateralization of Tibetan and Han subjects’ recognition of the faces of Tibetan individuals manifested mainly on the mouth, whereas the lateralization of the faces of Han individuals manifested on the nose and mouth, which verified Hypothesis 2.

Left lateralization exists in the recognition of the faces of both Tibetan and Han individuals

The Tibetan and Han participants recognized faces of individuals of their own and other ethnicities with left lateralization, and this lateralization was not affected by the ethnicity of the subjects. The reasons are as follows. First, the left lateralization of visual processing may be the common processing bias of human beings and affects whether the individual is processing images (Verleger & Śmigasiewicz, 2015) or words (Asanowicz, Kruse, Åšmigasiewicz & Verleger, 2017; Davies-Thompson, Johnston, Tashakkor, Pancaroglu, & Barton, 2016). Facial images, as a type of visual information, are also subject to this rule. Second, compared with that of the right side of the visual field, the recognition of the left side is more accurate and is therefore a processing priority (Harris, Remington, & Becker, 2013). Individuals who focus more on the left side of the face can improve their facial processing efficiency (Wolff, 1933). Finally, the results of this study are supported by those of Ma et al. (2022), who reported that East Asian individuals exhibited left lateralization when they recognized faces of individuals of their own race and those of Western individuals and that this lateralization was not affected by ethnicity. Han and Tibetan individuals’ recognition of faces of individuals of their own and other ethnicities also exhibited left lateralization, and it was not affected by ethnicity. However, the results of this study are inconsistent with those of Bukowski, Dricot, Hanseeuw, & Rossion (2013), who reported that left-handed and right-handed subjects exhibited opposite lateralization: the right-handed subjects processed the left side of the face more than the right side, whereas the left-handed subjects processed the right side of the face more than the left side. Previous studies have shown that left-handed individuals process the right side of an image significantly more than the left side (Bulf, de Hevia, & Macchi Cassia, 2016). The subjects in this study were right-handed, while the subjects in Bukowski et al.’s study were left-handed, which may be the main reason for the inconsistency in the two research results. In addition, the discrepancies between these two studies may also be related to differences in facial stimuli and participant ethnicity. Previous research has identified significant cross-cultural differences in facial configuration (Ma et al., 2023) and visual scanning patterns of faces (Miellet et al., 2013) between Eastern and Western individuals. Therefore cross-cultural factors may represent another important explanation for the inconsistent findings across studies.

Differences in the lateralization of individuals’ recognition of Tibetan and Han facial features

The lateralization of Tibetan and Han individuals’ recognition of the faces of Han individuals manifested mainly in the nose and mouth, and there was no left lateralization in the recognition of the eyes; the lateralization of Tibetan faces manifested mainly in the mouth, and there was no left lateralization in the recognition of the eyes and nose. A study by Ma et al. (2022) confirmed that East Asian subjects use the scanning mode of moving from the facial center to the periphery when recognizing faces. Different positions of the facial center lead to different facial recognition scanning patterns. According to Ma et al.’s (2023) analysis of the structural features of the faces of Tibetan and Han individuals, the center of the face in Han individuals is located between the eyes, and the eyes are the main focus of the individual. When individuals recognize the faces of Han individuals, the main focus of left lateralization is not the eyes, but the nose and mouth since they are not included in the central area. However, the facial structures of Tibetan and Han individuals differ; specifically, the noses of Tibetan individuals are wider, and the center of their faces is lower (Wang, 2005). The center of the faces of Tibetan individuals is located in the middle bridge of the nose, including the nose and eyes, where lateralization is not manifested. The mouth is not part of the central area of the face in Tibetan individuals; therefore, the focus of lateralization is only the mouth. This explanation is also supported by Ma et al. (2022), who suggested that left lateralization in the recognition of different facial features both related to the pattern by which people scan faces and affected by facial structure, especially the facial center features. Left lateralization is not exhibited for all facial features, such as those located in the facial center, and it is manifested mainly in the facial features of noncentral areas.

Notably, Tibetan and Han individuals exhibit similar lateralization in facial recognition of individuals of the same ethnicity (Tibetan or Han) and do not differ across ethnic groups. This may be related mainly to the similar cultural backgrounds of the subjects. First, both Tibetan and Han individuals are East Asian. As confirmed by Kelly et al. (2010), East Asian individuals, including Tibetan and Han individuals, scan faces from the center to their surroundings. The same scanning mode leads to no differences because of subject ethnicity when individuals recognize faces. This view is also supported by Ma et al.’s (2023) research, which revealed that both Tibetan and Han individuals use the scanning pattern from the center of the face to the surroundings, which is not affected by the subject's ethnicity. Therefore the left lateralization of Tibetan and Han individuals’ facial recognition is not affected by the subject's ethnicity.

Research prospect

Although the study confirmed that both Han and Tibetan participants exhibit leftward lateralization when they recognize faces, this conclusion has been verified only within the Han and Tibetan groups. Whether individuals of other ethnicities also exhibit a similar leftward lateralization when recognizing faces requires further verification. Therefore future research is needed to further verify whether the lateralization of facial recognition among individuals is culturally universal or specific. In addition, leftward lateralization in facial recognition was primarily explored among right-handed individuals in this study. However, whether the leftward lateralization in facial recognition confirmed in this study is influenced by the subject's handedness (left or right) remains unclear. Further research is needed to verify this.

Conclusions

In summary, whether the faces of Han or Tibetan individuals are used, Tibetan and Han individuals exhibit left lateralization in facial recognition. The focus of lateralization of the faces of Han individuals is mainly the nose and mouth, whereas that of the lateralization of the faces of Tibetan individuals is the mouth.
