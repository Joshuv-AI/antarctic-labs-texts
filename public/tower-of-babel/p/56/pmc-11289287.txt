Impact of repositioning on brain injury following transcatheter aortic valve replacement with a self-expanding valve
Authors: Jiaqi Fan, Jun Chen, Gangjie Zhu, Yeming Xu, Stella Ng, Hanyi Dai, Dao Zhou, Abuduwufuer Yidilisi, Xinrui Qi, Xianbao Liu, Jian’an Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractRepositionable self-expanding valves allow for repositioning during deployment to achieve optimal valve placement. However, the risk of brain injury associated with repositioning, as detected by diffusion-weighted magnetic resonance imaging (DW-MRI), is unknown. Consecutive patients undergoing transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) with repositionable self-expanding valves and receiving DW-MRI before and within 7 days post-TAVR procedure were included. The primary outcomes were incidence, number, total volume, and volume per lesion of the cerebral ischemic lesion in DW-MRI after TAVR. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression assessed the association between repositioning and bigger total lesion volume (> 1 cm3 or > 0.5 cm3). Negative binomial regressions were performed to explore the association between repositioning and number of lesions. A propensity score matching was performed to adjust the potential confounders. Moreover, inverse probability of treatment weighted regression model with nonstabilized weights was used as sensitivity analysis. Among 243 included patients, repositioning was performed in 116 (47.7%) patients. The incidence of overt stroke (1.7% vs. 1.6%, p = 0.927) and silent stroke (86.2% vs. 85.8%, p = 0.932) were comparable between two groups. However, the number of new lesions (5.0 [2.0–9.0] vs. 3.0 [2.0–6.0], p = 0.048), and total lesion volume (275.0 [90.0–947.5] mm3 vs. 180.0 [50.0–440.0] mm3, p = 0.022) were significantly higher in the repositioned group. Moreover, the proportion of patients with lesion size greater than 0.5 cm3 was higher in the repositioned group (37.9% vs. 22.0%, p = 0.007). The similar results were observed after propensity score matching. In both multivariate regression model and sensitivity analysis, the repositioning was the independent predictor of number of lesions and bigger total lesion volume after TAVR. The utilization of the repositioning feature may increase the number and volume of silent brain infarcts in DW-MRI in patients who underwent TAVR. Introduction

Transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) has been the choice of severe aortic stenosis (AS) patients with low to high-risk profiles. The second-generation transcatheter self-expanding valve with advanced technology enables repositioning during deployment to achieve the desired valve placement. However, the additional maneuver with resheathing and repositioning during the TAVR procedure would be expected to relate to prolonged manipulations under the calcified aortic root structure, which hereby may be related to the increased risk for debris embolization1.

Previous studies demonstrated the comparable rates of stroke between cases using resheathing and repositioning capabilities, not only for disabling stroke but also for non-disabling stroke after TAVR2–6. However, Seeger et al. reported a larger number and higher total area of embolized debris captured by dual-filter cerebral embolic protection in the repositioning groups during TAVR4. Moreover, Devgun et al. recommended limiting the time spent manipulating devices in the aortic annulus during TAVR, which appears to contradict the practices of resheathing and repositioning7. Thus, operators face a dilemma in balancing these procedures to achieve optimal results.

Yet no study evaluated the impact of resheathing and repositioning on brain injury in DW-MRI following TAVR. Therefore, the objective of this present study was to evaluate the impact of resheathing and repositioning on cerebral ischemic lesions for patients treated with repositionable self-expanding devices.

Methods

Study design and population

This was a prospective study involving all consecutive patients undergoing TAVR with the repositionable self-expanding devices in the TORCH registry (NCT02803294, 16/06/2016). Exclusion criteria were: (1) undergoing TAVR with irretrievable valve; (2) implantation of incompatible metallic prosthesis or foreign body contraindicated to the DW-MRI examination, including pacemaker implantation; (3) aortic valve-related procedure performed in prior 3 months; (4) absence of DW-MRI examination due to patient refusal or overscheduling; (5) intolerance due to clinical situation; and (6) poor quality of imaging cannot be analyzed. After excluding irretrievable valve usage, contraindication to DW-MRI examination, and other reasons, a total of 243 patients were included from the Second Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine, from November 2017 to May 2022. TAVR was discussed and decided by the local heart team. Data were collected in the database of the TORCH registry, including the baseline characteristics, procedural information, and follow-up data.

Patients were divided according to the use of resheathing or repositioning into two groups: repositioned group and nonrepositioned group. A large proportion of patients were implanted with repositionable self-expanding valves, such as the Evolut Pro (Medtronic, Minneapolis, Minnesota), VenusA Plus (Venus Medtech, Hangzhou, China), VitaFlow II (Microport, Shanghai, China), and Taurus Elite (Peijia Medical, Suzhou, China)8. Information about partial resheathing or total recaptured of the valve into the delivery catheter was reviewed and recorded by two authors (G.J.Z and J.C.), and confirmed by the third dependent author (J.Q.F.). Multiple resheathing mentioned in this study was defined as resheathing performed 2 or more times during TAVR procedure. The brain DW-MRI was performed using a 1.5-T or 3-T whole-body MRI system (GE Signa, GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, Wisconsin). The imaging protocol comprised transversal T2-weighted turbo spin echo (1.5-T: repetition time/echo time = 4800/100 ms; 3-T: repetition time/echo time = 3300/80 ms) and transversal fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (1.5-T: repetition time/ echo time = 6000/120 ms; 3-T: repetition time/echo time = 1200/140 ms). DWI was performed with a spin-echo echo-planar pulse sequence (1.5-T: repetition time/echo time = 2921/78 ms, matrix = 128 × 256; section thickness = 5 mm; intersection gap = 1 mm; total acquisition time = 21.4 s; 3-T: repetition time/echo time = 3866/47 ms, matrix = 128 × 256, section thickness = 5 mm, intersection gap = 1 mm, total acquisition time = 46.3 s) with diffusion sensitization b-values of 0 and 1000 s/mm2. A new cerebral ischemic lesion was defined as a focal hyperintense area detected by the fluid-attenuated inversion recovery sequence, corresponding to a restricted diffusion signal in the diffusion-weighted imaging sequence, and confirmed by apparent diffusion coefficient mapping to rule out a shine-through artifact. The brain MRI for new ischemic lesions was analyzed by the 2 independent authors with the software and confirmed by the neurological physician by MRIcron software (NeuroImaging Tools and Resources Collaboratory, Columbia, South Carolina). The detailed information and description of brain MRI were presented in our previous study9. The primary outcomes were incidence, number, total volume, and volume per lesion of the cerebral ischemic lesion in DW-MRI after TAVR. Secondary outcomes were clinical outcomes, including mortality, overt stroke, and the other clinical events defined according to Valve Academic Research Consortium-3 criteria10. The mini–mental state examination (MMSE) was used to assess the cognitive function at baseline and 30-day follow-up in our study.

Statistical analysis

For this post hoc analysis, patients were divided into two groups according to the usage of the resheath or recapture feature of the self-expanding transcatheter heart valve (THV). Baseline characteristics, procedural information, and follow-up data were compared between the repositioned and nonpositioned groups. Categorical variables are presented as numbers (percentage) and compared with the chi-square test (for the observed count was < 5, the Fisher exact test was used). Continuous variables of normal distribution are shown as mean ± SD and compared by Student’s t-test. If the continuous data were skewed, median (interquartile range [IQR]) and Mann–Whitney U test were used. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression with backward stepwise method were used to assess the association between repositioning and total lesion volume more than 1 cm3 or 0.5 cm3. Univariate and multivariate negative binomial regression were performed to explore the association between repositioning and number of lesions among patients who received the repositionable self-expanding devices in TAVR. Clinically relevant variables or those variables with p value less than 0.1 were selected into the multivariate model. The highly correlated independent variables were excluded from the multivariate regression to avoid the problem of collinearity (The Pearson correlation coefficient for the variables “ascending aorta diameter at 4 cm” and “STJ diameter” was 0.77).

In the sensitivity analysis, we conducted a propensity score matching to adjust the confounders of age, sex, Society of Thoracic Surgeons (STS) score, atrial fibrillation, bicuspid aortic valve (BAV), and other echocardiographic and computed tomographic variables (baseline aortic regurgitation, aortic area, aortic perimeter, sino-tubular junction (STJ) height, right coronary artery (RCA) height, and left main (LM) height). The logistic regression was used to estimate the propensity score. One-to-one matching without replacement was performed and caliper value was 0.35. Moreover, we performed the inverse probability of treatment weighted regression model with nonstabilized weights to analyze association between repositioning and number of lesions, total lesion volume more than 1 cm3, and more than 0.5 cm3.

A value of p < 0.05 with 2-sided was considered statistically significant. Statistical analysis was performed using STATA software version 17.0 (Stata Corporation, College Station, TX, USA), and the figures were created in GraphPad Prism version 9.4.1 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, California).

Ethics approval and consent to participate

The study was approved by the medical ethics committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University and carried out according to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All patients provided written informed consent for TAVR and the use of anonymous clinical, procedural, and follow-up data for research.

Results

Two hundred and forty-three consecutive patients who received a brain DW-MRI examinations before and post-TAVR using a repositionable self-expanding valve were enrolled in the presented study, the patient flow chart is shown in Fig. 1. Nearly half of patients [47.7% (116/243)] required the repositioning mechanism. The mean age and median STS score of the repositioned group were significantly lower than the nonrepositioned group (age: 73 [69–79] years vs. 77 [72–81] years, p = 0.005; STS: 3.1 [1.8–4.9] vs. 4.0 [2.3–6.3], p = 0.019). The prevalence of BAV was significantly higher in the repositioned group (60.4% vs. 42.4%, p = 0.006). In addition, the area, perimeter of aortic annulus, STJ, and LM artery height were significantly higher in the repositioned group (p = 0.001–0.010). There was no significant difference between the two groups for other baseline characteristics and periprocedural data. The incidence of cerebral ischemic lesion was similar in the both repositioned and nonrepositioned groups (86.2% vs. 85.8%, p = 0.932). The number (5.0 [2.0–9.0] vs. 3.0 [2.0–6.0], p = 0.048), and total lesion volume (275.0 [90.0–947.5] mm3 vs. 180.0 [50.0–440.0] mm3, p = 0.022) of ischemic lesions detected in DW-MRI in the repositioned group was significantly higher, while the volume per lesion (57.1 [28.5–111.1] mm3 vs. 50.0 [22.5–85.0] mm3, p = 0.131) and maximum lesion volume (110.0 [50.0–310.0] mm3 vs. 80.0 [40.0–170.0] mm3, p = 0.054) were also slightly higher but with no statistical significance. Moreover, the proportion of patients with lesion size greater than 1 cm3 and 0.5 cm3 was higher in the repositioned group (lesion size greater than 1 cm3: 23.3% vs. 11.0%, p = 0.011; lesion size greater than 0.5 cm3: 37.9% vs. 22.0%, p = 0.007). In those patients with new cerebral ischemic lesion there were no significantly differences in different vascular border zones between these two groups. No patients died or had a myocardial infarction during the procedure of before discharge. The number of overt strokes was comparable between the two groups (1.7% vs. 1.6%, p = 0.927). Except for the post-procedural aortic valve area (AVA) which was higher in the repositioned group when compared with the nonrepositioned group (1.7 [1.4–2.0] cm2 vs. 1.5 [1.3–1.8] cm2, p = 0.004), the remaining echocardiographic parameters were similar between the two groups. In the univariate negative binomial analysis, age, STJ diameter, repositioning during the procedure, and BAV were associated with the number of the cerebral ischemic lesion in DW-MRI after TAVR. In the multivariable negative binomial analysis, only age, sex, BAV, and repositioning were statistically significant (p = 0.002, p = 0.014, p = 0.001, and p = 0.017, respectively). In the both multivariate regression model for total lesion volume > 0.5 cm3 and 1 cm3 in DW-MRI after TAVR, the repositioning was the independent predictor (for total lesion volume > 0.5 cm3: 2.173[1.209–3.904], p = 0.009; for total lesion volume > 1.0 cm3: 2.503 [1.236–5.067], p = 0.011). In the sensitivity analysis, there were 192 patients enrolled, consisted of 96 patients in both repositioned group and nonrepositioned group. The baseline characteristics, echocardiographic, and computed tomographic data of the patients in repositioned and nonrepositioned groups after propensity score matching are listed in Supplementary Table 1. The incidence of cerebral ischemic lesion was still similar in both repositioned and nonrepositioned group (85.4% vs. 84.4%, p = 0.840). The number (5.0 [2.0–9.0] vs. 3.0 [2.0–6.0], p = 0.024), and total lesion volume (250.0 [90.0–1075.0] mm3 vs. 165.0 [52.5–430.0] mm3, p = 0.022) of ischemic lesions detected in DW-MRI in the repositioned group was significantly higher, while the volume per lesion (55.8 [25.8–111.9] mm3 vs. 46.8 [23.1–87.1] mm3, p = 0.192) and maximum lesion volume (110.0 [50.0–340.0] mm3 vs. 85.0 [40.0–167.5] mm3, p = 0.073) were also slightly higher but with no statistical significance. Moreover, the proportion of patients with lesion size greater than 1 cm3 and 0.5 cm3 was higher in the repositioned group (lesion size greater than 1 cm3: 26.0% vs. 12.5%, p = 0.017; lesion size greater than 0.5 cm3: 37.5% vs. 19.8%, p = 0.007). The results of brain injury after propensity score matching are presented in the Supplementary Table 2. The inverse probability of treatment weighted regression model with nonstabilized weights demonstrated that the repositioning was independently predictor for number of lesions (p = 0.054), total lesion volume more than 1 cm3 (p = 0.019), and more than 0.5 cm3 (p = 0.007). The results of regression are presented in the Supplementary Table 3.

Discussion

The main finding of this study evaluating new cerebral ischemic lesions in patients undergoing TAVR with repositionable self-expanding devices were as follows: (1) repositioning (resheath or full recapture) was required in nearly half of the patients who received a repositionable self-expanding device in our final enrolled population; (2) the number and total lesion volume of new cerebral ischemic lesion was higher in the repositioned group compared with the nonrepositioned group, while no differences were observed in incidence, volume per lesion, and maximum volume of lesions between the two groups; (3) repositioning was the independent predictor of new cerebral ischemic lesion and bigger total lesion volume detected with DW-MRI.

To the best of our knowledge, the present study is the first to focus on the cerebral ischemic lesion in DW-MRI after TAVR for patients receiving the repositionable self-expanding THV. Prior studies reported that the repositioning rates varied between 21 and 41%3,5,6,11–17. However, in our study, nearly half of the patients required repositioning which is slightly higher than in prior reports. We suspected that the higher rates of repositioning in our study may be related to the higher prevalence of BAV with anatomical challenge in the aortic root structure.

It is expected that resheathing and repositioning would prolong manipulations in the surrounding of the calcified aortic valvar complex, hereby affecting stroke rates. Attizzani et al showed higher rates of nondisabling stroke with resheathing (3.9% vs. 2.0%, p = 0.09). Kefer and colleagues reported a 2% of stroke rate in resheathing group and 0.7% in the non-resheathing group, although with no statistical significance (p = 0.18). Seeger et al. analyzed the histopathologic and histomorphometric data of captured debris from filters using the dual-filter cerebral embolic protection and showed a much higher number of embolized debris (349 ± 326 vs 509 ± 495, p < 0.04) and larger debris area (15.56 ± 22.13 mm2 vs 38.9 ± 25.57 mm2, p < 0.01) in repositioning group. Nonetheless, some studies showed similar rates of stroke in the repositioning and no repositioning group6,15. In our study, we observed that the number and total volume of cerebral ischemic lesions in DW-MRI after TAVR were higher in the repositioned group. We hypothesize that prolonged manipulations at the level of the aortic valvular complex during TAVR may increase the risk of debris release and thus of small new cerebral ischemic lesions that can be detected with DW-MRI. In our study, we observed that the number of overt strokes as well as the MMSE assessed at baseline and 30-day follow up was comparable between the repositioned and nonrepositioned groups. However, it should be evaluated with long-term cognitive functional assessment.

In our study, we observed that the annulus area, annulus perimeter, STJ height, and LM height were larger in the repositioned group. This may be related to the younger, BAV patients. Of note, two previous studies failed to demonstrate an association of these anatomical variables with the need for THV repositioning3,6. In our study, we observed that patients requiring more THV repositioning companied with higher prevalence of BAV. Moreover, the post-dilatation, contrast used, duration of procedure were also more common in the repositioned group. Notably, we found that the ensuing post-TAVR echocardiographic AVA in the repositioned group patients was higher, which might potentially relate to the higher proportion of patients undergoing post-dilation during TAVR procedure.

We have identified four independent predictors for the number of new cerebral ischemic lesion detected with DW-MRI, age, sex, BAV, and repositioning. For the total lesion volume > 0.5 cm3, we identified the STJ diameters and repositioning were the two independent predictors, while for total lesion volume > 1 cm3, only the repositioning was. A previous meta-analysis reported some predictors of silent brain infarcts after TAVR and showed that a higher number of events adversely affect early neurocognitive outcomes18. Our previous data of DW-MRI in severe AS patients who received TAVR procedure demonstrated that BAV, THV type, age, left ventricular ejection fraction, post-dilatation, and procedure duration were the independent factors influencing the number of new cerebral ischemic lesions9. In the present study, we have identified repositioning as the independent predictor for both the number and bigger total lesion volume of new lesions in DW-MRI, but this may be related to different enrolled populations. In fact, we enrolled patients who received a repositionable self-expanding valve while our previous work included patients with severe AS that underwent TAVR with different types of devices.

The repositioning mechanism make it possible to achieve the desired valve placement. However, this rapier brings both benefit and harm. The clinical implication for the presented study was that resheathing and repositioning should be cautious as the repositioning may be associated with higher cerebral ischemic lesions after TAVR detected by DW-MRI.

The present study has several limitations. First, this is a single-center, nonrandomized study. Due to the characteristics of the participants, the results should be interpreted with caution when generalizing to other populations. Secondly, given that not all patients were suitable for an DW-MRI scan (e.g. patients with a metallic implant), our findings cannot be extrapolated to other populations. Third, the requirement of repositioning during the procedure was decided by the interventional cardiologist without expert consensus. Thus, future randomized controlled studies with rigorous design are warranted to explore the association between silent brain infarcts and repositioning during the TAVR procedure.

Conclusions

While the incidence of overt and silent stroke and the volume per lesion were similar in the repositioned and nonrepositioned groups, our data suggests that repositioning during TAVR was associated with both number and total lesion volume of silent brain infarcts in DW-MRI after TAVR.
