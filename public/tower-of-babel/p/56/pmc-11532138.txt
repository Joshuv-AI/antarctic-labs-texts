Developments, challenges and prospects in thermal-hydraulic research on accident tolerant fuel
Authors: Yiyi Li, Ersheng You, Tianmi Wang, Dianchuan Xing, Jianjun Xu, Haochun Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccident Tolerant Fuel (ATF) is a next-generation fuel technology developed to enhance the fundamental safety of light water reactors. Compared to existing standard fuel technologies, ATF technology can provide the same or better performance during normal operation, transient conditions, and accident scenarios of nuclear power plants, enhancing the ability of fuel elements to withstand severe accidents while reducing the cost of generating electricity over the licensed life of the plants. This paper introduces the definition, characteristics and thermal-hydraulic research objectives of ATF technology, systematically reviews the thermal-hydraulic research results of different ATF technologies in various aspects from the perspectives of near-term concepts (UO2-based composite fuels, coated Zr-based claddings, FeCrAl claddings) and long-term concepts (high-uranium-density UN fuels, FCM fuels, extruded metal fuels, SiC/SiC claddings), respectively. Mainly includes: improving fuel thermal conductivity, lowering fuel operating temperatures and stored energy, reducing fuel-cladding interactions, improving cladding-coolant reactions, reducing the production of flammable gases (e.g., hydrogen) and enhancing the retention of radioactive fission products. Finally, this paper points out the current shortcomings and challenges in the field of ATF thermal-hydraulic research and outlooks the potential future directions.Keywords: Accident tolerant fuel, Thermal-hydraulic research, Composite fuel, Coated cladding, FeCrAl cladding, SiC/

Introduction

Tight stockpiles and escalating prices of fossil energy are causing countries around the world to seek a balance between energy security and green development in the increasingly competitive clean energy industry. Nuclear power is a clean, low-carbon and efficient form of energy, it can effectively reduce greenhouse gas emissions and ease the pressure on energy delivery while realizing a reliable, sustainable and cost-effective supply of large-scale electricity. Against this backdrop, the global nuclear power market, which has always been conservative since the Fukushima nuclear accident in Japan in 2011 [1], is recovering rapidly and the share of nuclear power in the global energy market has increased year by year [2,3]. According to the International Atomic Energy Agency's Power Reactor Information System (PRIS), as of January 2024, there were 413 operational nuclear power reactors worldwide, with a total net installed capacity of 371,510 MWe. Nuclear power generation accounts for about 10 percent of the world's total electricity generation, and is about one-third of the world's clean energy generation.

However, the development and promotion of nuclear power must always be based on the premise and fundamental guarantee of safety [4,5]. Many countries attach great importance to the safety of nuclear power plant systems while accelerating the renewal of the nuclear power industry [[6], [7], [8]]. Currently, light water reactors (LWRs) are still the main type of nuclear reactors in operation worldwide, the accident at the Fukushima Daiichi nuclear power plant has further prompted efforts by nuclear energy promoters to ensure the safe operation and severe accident management of LWRs. Despite incremental improvements over the years, the UO2-Zr alloy standard fuel assembly of existing LWRs is approaching its inherent limits, which is susceptible to deterioration as nuclear operators become more demanding in terms of combustion consumption and system operating power requirements [9,10]. Iterations and upgrades of Generation III(+) LWRs systems require more robust systems to guarantee the safety and survivability of nuclear power plants under accident conditions. The U.S. Department of Energy urges prioritizing the development and qualification of meltdown-resistant accident tolerant fuels (ATFs) to improve the safety of current and future generations of LWRs [11,12]. Compared to current fuel assembly form, the ATF can tolerate active cooling losses in the reactor core for longer periods of time while maintaining or even improving fuel assembly performance during normal operation, transient operation, design basis accidents (DBAs), and beyond design basis accidents (BDBAs) [11,13]. The application of ATF systems can also reduce the cost of electricity over the licensed lifetime of commercial nuclear reactors, increasing the economic efficiency of nuclear power plants.

Referring to the deterioration of the UO2-Zr alloy fuel assembly under accident conditions [14,15] such as LOCA in Figs. 1–(a) [10], improvements from the ATF are made simultaneously from both the fuel and cladding sides. Figs. 1–(b) lists the objectives of the ATF thermal-hydraulic research, mainly including.

(1)Enhance the fuel thermal conductivity, reduce the operating temperature and stored energy of the fuel, diminish the fuel-cladding interaction. Thermal conductivity is critical to the response and behavior of fuel under normal, transient, and accidental operating conditions, controlling the conversion of thermal energy from fission to other energy sources such as electricity [16]. The relatively poor economic and low thermal conductivity of ceramic UO2 fuel pellets [17,18] are not favorable for the heat accumulated in the reactor core to be conducted outward, cutting down the thermoelectric conversion efficiency. At the same time, UO2 fuel pellets with low melting point and weak thermo-mechanical properties are prone to melt and migrate at high temperatures and even disperse into the coolant [19]. This can damage the reactor core structure and increase the probability of a nuclear accident.

(2)Improve the reaction between the cladding and the coolant, reduce the generation of flammable gases such as hydrogen, enhance the retention of radioactive fission products. As the direct loading of nuclear fuel, the cladding not only bears the responsibility of preventing the leakage of fission products, but also serves as an isolation barrier between the fuel and the coolant [20]. In a high temperature steam environment, rapid oxidation of Zr alloy cladding releases large amounts of heat and generates free hydrogen, where the heat further increases cladding temperature and accelerates the free hydrogen production [21]. Diffusion of free hydrogen into the unoxidized portion of the cladding will increase the potential for cladding embrittlement and failure. In the BDBA scenario, a large buildup of hydrogen inside the reactor vessel could potentially lead to a high-energy explosion similar to the Fukushima nuclear accident [22,23].

In response to the above research objectives, as shown in Fig. 2, this paper reviews a range of ATF technical concepts that have been proposed by the nuclear industry. Near-term concepts can be evaluated relying on existing data, models and methods, while the development of long-term concepts requires a large amount of new information. Both concepts advance in both fuel and cladding: near-term concepts mainly include UO2-based composite fuels, coated Zr-based cladding and the alternative FeCrAl cladding; longer-term concepts are essentially complete replacements for current fuel assemblies, including high-uranium-density UN fuels, fully ceramic microencapsulated (FCM) fuels, extruded metal fuels and SiC/SiC cladding. After reviewing the relevant thermal-hydraulic studies of these ATF technologies, this paper concludes by presenting some limitations and challenges that still exist in this field and outlooks potential future directions.

Description framework of different ATF technologies and their progress in thermal-hydraulic research.

Near-term ATF technical concepts

UO2-based composite fuels

Doping second-phase high thermal conductivity materials into UO2 pellets to make UO2-based composite fuels is currently the most desirable option to improve the thermal conductivity of reactor cores. It also offers advantages over conventional designs by reducing the rigidity of fuel pellets, minimizing the risk of cladding damage and promoting fission product retention. The U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Commission has approved the application of this program in boiling water reactors (BWRs), reactor licensees such as the Brunswick Nuclear Power Plant have also performed bulk loading of UO2-based composite fuel. Current combinations of composite fuels include UO2-BeO, UO2-SiC, UO2-Mo, UO2-diamond and UO2-CNT.

UO2-BeO

For many years, UO2-BeO has been selected as the fuel for the annular core research reactor at U.S. Sandia National Laboratories (SNL) [24]. With high levels of stability, melting point and thermal conductivity, BeO is currently considered the most chemically compatible dopant with UO2. At the same time, the price of BeO is moderate and is suitable for use in dispersed-phase fuel substrates or even reflectors and moderators in nuclear reactors [25]. Compared UO2 with UO2-BeO composite, Nishigaki et al. found that the addition of 20 wt%BeO to UO2 could increase the overall thermal conductivity by as much as 10–20 times [26]. Ishimoto et al. prepared UO2-BeO pellets and investigated the effect of different precipitation distributions of BeO on composite thermal conductivity [27]. They experimentally measured the thermal diffusivity by the laser flash method, according to which the thermal conductivity was evaluated in combination with the density and specific heat capacity. Results show that the thermal conductivity of the composite fuel increases with the increase of BeO content: at lower temperatures, the thermal conductivity of BeO-continuous pellets is higher than that of BeO-dispersed pellets, this difference decreases gradually with increasing temperature. When the temperature reaches 1100K, for the pellets composed of UO2 and 1.2 wt%BeO, the thermal conductivity of BeO-continuous composite and BeO-dispersed composite is about 25 % and 10 % higher than that of UO2, respectively. It should be noted that, such enhancements come at the expense of high temperatures of more than 2433K, which increases the cost and difficulty of the transition to commercial applications.

Latta et al. investigated the thermal conductivity of UO2-10 vol%BeO composite fuel by laser flash method, samples were prepared using both the green-granule method and the slug-bisque (SB) method to distinguish the effect of different processing techniques [28]. Compared to the single UO2, the doping of 10 vol%BeO is able to improve the fuel thermal conductivity by 56 % at low temperatures and 33 % at high temperatures. This also indicates that the type of preparation technique has no significant influence on fuel thermal conductivity. Ferreira et al. also produced a kind of UO2-BeO pellets with much stronger thermal performance by sol-gel and hydraulic methods, the improvement law of thermal conductivity by increasing the amount of BeO doping was also evaluated [29]. It can be measured that the thermal conductivity of UO2-BeO pellets can be increased by 8.1 %–125 % when the BeO doping amount is between 2 wt%∼14 wt%. Although the sintering temperature of UO2-BeO with the highest BeO doping is 50 °C lower than other schemes and the corresponding pellet samples do not reach the highest thermal conductivity value, the growth rate of thermal conductivity is still optimal relative to that of UO2 for the same conditions. This safer composite fuel is expected to improve the performance and extend lifespan of nuclear reactors, leading to greater economic benefits.

Kovalishin et al. considered the possibility of applying UO2-BeO in Russian VVER nuclear reactors [30]. The analysis shows that the introduction of BeO can affect the neutron multiplication factor of the fuel assembly by drastically reducing the fuel temperature. The UO2-BeO pellets can widen the limits of deposition energy inhomogeneity within the reactor core and enhance operational safety of the fuel assembly. Combined with the BeO-continuous structure, Garcia et al. doped the UO2 with 2.5 vol%, 5 vol%, 7.5 vol% and 10 vol% of BeO to enhance heat transfer [31]. Characterization of high-density samples reveals that the thermal conductivity of the composite fuel can be improved by about 10 % per 1 vol%BeO doping in the temperature range of 25 °C–250 °C, and 10 vol%BeO doping exhibits 100 % or exponential improvement in thermal conductivity.

Combined with the spark plasma sintering (SPS) technique, Li et al. reported for the first time the thermophysical property changes of high-density UO2-10 vol% BeO with a BeO-continuous structure from room temperature to the sintering temperature (and above) [32]. On the one hand, with 1500 °C as the critical point, coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE) of the composite fuel increases slowly with rising temperature in cold environments. Once the critical point is exceeded, the release of internal stresses and the further sintering will make CTE decrease rapidly when temperature increases. On the other hand, in Fig. 3, although the thermal conductivities of both composite and single UO2 show a decreasing trend with increasing temperature, thermal conductivity of the composite fuel is still improved by 45.3 % and 60.5 % at room temperature and 1600 °C, respectively. A significant enhancement of the thermal conductivity of UO2-10 vol%BeO can be clearly observed especially in high temperature range close to the accident conditions. This helps to reduce the thermal gradient inside the reactor core, lower the risk of fuel failure and greatly enhance the safety of the nuclear fuel system.

UO2-SiC

The thermal conductivity of single crystal SiC is 60 and 30 times higher than that of UO2 at room temperature and 800 °C, respectively [33]. SiC also has smaller neutron absorption cross section, lower decay heat, higher melting point and excellent dimensional stability of irradiation. Based on polymer impregnation pyrolysis (PIP) and SB processes, Solomon et al. and Sarma et al. attempted to fabricate UO2-SiC composite fuel by impregnating commercial SiC precursors into high-density UO2 [34,35]. Since the used PIP method needs multiple impregnations and relatively low processing temperature to avoid interactions between SiC and UO2 impairing thermal conductivity, this process only fills about 70 % of the open porosity of UO2. Subsequently, Singh et al. improved the PIP technique to produce UO2-SiC composite: they dispersed U3O8 in liquid allyl hydrogenated polycarbosilane (AHPCS), the pyrolysis of AHPCS at 900 °C produced a-SiC then U3O8 could be converted to UO2 by reacting with AHPCS in PIP pyrolysis cycle [36]. This updated scheme improves the distribution uniformity of uranium fuel source, also is expected to reduce the effect of neutron irradiation on composite pellets and be applied to the preparation of nuclear reactor fuel combined with further heat treatment.

Yeo et al. verified the effectiveness of preparing UO2-10 vol%SiC pellets within the 1400 °C–1600 °C range using SPS method [37]. Thermal conductivities of the UO2-10 vol%SiC and UO2 were measured at 100 °C, 500 °C and 900 °C, results in Fig. 4(a) display that the SPS-sintered composite fuel has a high density and a thermal conductivity that is 62 % higher than that of ordinary UO2. According to the XRD analysis and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) micrographs of UO2-10 vol%SiC, SPS sintering is beneficial in enhancing interfacial contact and mitigating the mutual reaction between two compositions at temperatures above 1370 °C. These characteristics are considered to be necessary for UO2-based composite fuels with enhanced thermal behavior. On this basis, Yeo et al. investigated the effect of SiC doping on thermal properties of UO2-SiC, such as thermal diffusion coefficient, specific heat capacity and thermal conductivity [39]. Mechanical mixtures of UO2 powder with β-SiC particles of different sizes and volume fractions were subjected to SPS processing for 5 min at temperatures ranging from 1350 °C to 1450 °C, and it was found that SiC particles with sizes taken as 0.6 μm, 1.0 μm and 9.0 μm are able to improve the thermal conductivity of composite pellet more effectively. SiC particles with larger sizes tend to cause microcracks or interfacial debonding within the composite, which in turn causes a reduction in thermal conductivity. In addition, the larger the SiC volume fraction, the more favorable it is to reduce the fuel density and improve the thermal conductivity. For SiC particle size of 1.0 μm, the overall thermal conductivity increases linearly with the increase of SiC volume fraction.

Li et al. also introduced SiC phase into the UO2 matrix by SPS method and studied the thermophysical characteristics of UO2-SiC below 1600 °C temperature [38]. The doping of SiC can inhibit the growth of grain size and the formation of equiaxed organization of UO2, which has a significant effect on enhancing fuel thermal conductivity after exceeding the critical sintering temperature of 1300 °C. From two empirical formulas for calculating thermal diffusion coefficient and thermal conductivity of UO2-SiC proposed combining temperature and SiC doping fraction, both thermal parameters increase with increasing SiC content and decrease with increasing temperature. In the sintered state at 1500 °C, thermal conductivity of the composite fuel can be increased by about 3 % for every 1 % increase in SiC doping. Meanwhile, the data in Fig. 4(b) show that the involvement of SiC can help reduce the CTE, which contributes to cut down the risk of pellet failure and improve the safety of nuclear reactor fuel under accident operating conditions.

UO2-Mo

Metallic materials with microcellular structure (similar to cell wall structures of plants) can be made to encapsulate UO2 particles and lead to uranium-based fuels with metallic second phase, where the continuous contact of highly thermally conductive phases can strengthen the fuel thermal conductivity more effectively [40,41]. The optional metallic materials should have sufficiently high melting point and high thermal conductivity as well as low neutron absorption cross-section, in order to prevent the lack of integrity of pellets under various operating conditions and to improve the economics of fuel cycle.

Among various options, Mo is the more suitable choice for the metallic second phase [43,44] and has been explored intensively. Using sintering annealing technique, Kim et al. prepared UO2-based samples containing Mo distribution by mixing UO2 pellets with 3 wt% or 5 wt% MoO3 powder [45]. The thermal conductivities of UO2 and Mo-containing composite samples were calculated by laser flash method in the temperature range of 298K–1673K, respectively. Results show that the thermal conductivity of the Mo-containing composite samples is higher than that of pure UO2 and increases with the increase of MoO3 powder fraction. UO2 particles were also optimized in the theoretical density range of 33%TD∼45%TD to obtain composite fuels whose density is not less than 97%TD and the Mo phase is continuous [46]. On this basis, in Fig. 5, Kim et al. further investigated the thermal properties of UO2-Mo composite fuel [42]. The conventional processing method was selected to press and sinter the mixture of UO2 and Mo powders. In the obtained UO2-Mo pellets, the density of UO2 particles is about 33%TD, the size of Mo powder and microcellular structure is about 300 nm and 150 μm–400 μm, respectively. Compared with UO2 fuel, thermal conductivity of UO2-Mo is significantly improved, and the continuous Mo channels are more conducive to improve thermal performance than the unconnected dispersed Mo particles. There is also a positive correlation between Mo volume fraction and thermal conductivity of UO2-Mo composite.

UO2-diamond

Diamond is the solid material with the highest thermal conductivity in nature [33], the internal sp3 hybridization orbitals give diamond stable chemistry, strong radiation resistance and small neutron absorption cross section. Therefore, diamond is also a desirable second phase, which can also result in composite fuels with superior heat transfer combined with UO2. Chen et al. used the SPS technique to mix UO2 powder with 0.25 μm nano-sized and 3 μm, 12 μm, 25 μm micrometer-sized diamond particles, investigated the thermal conductivity variation of UO2-5vol%diamond composite pellets under the conditions of 1300 °C–1600 °C and 5-min holding time [47]. During the preparation process, the poorer mixing and the lower density indicated that the particle diameter of 0.25 μm was not acceptable and microcracking also occurred in samples with particle diameter >12 μm. Diamonds with particle diameter of 3 μm were more uniformly distributed, which is more conducive to optimizing the fuel thermal properties. Also, a maximum sintering temperature of 1400 °C was recommended to obtain the higher density of pellets and to weaken the chemical reaction between UO2 and diamond. Compared to UO2, the thermal conductivity of this UO2-5vol%diamond composite is improved by 41.6 %, 38.3 % and 34.2 % at 100 °C, 500 °C and 900 °C, respectively. This suggests that diamond is a strong candidate for the second phase within UO2-based composite fuels.

UO2-CNTs

Carbon nanotubes (CNTs) have excellent thermal properties, the thermal conductivity of individual single-walled CNTs and multi-walled CNTs can reach up to 6600W/mK and more than 3000W/mK, respectively. CNTs also show good stability in vacuum at 2800 °C and in air at about 750 °C. Based on these characteristics, CNTs are widely studied for the fabrication of advanced nanocomposites. Subhash et al. worked on developing highly thermally conductive UO2-CNTs composite fuels whose neutron properties of UO2 are not significantly affected, the doped CNTs can improve fuel cracking and fission gas release due to high temperature, playing a crucial role in controlling thermal conductivity and stability of the fuel [48]. Using the rapid SPS sintering method, they fabricated UO2-CNT composite fuels with different volume fractions of CNT. Thermal conductivity of the UO2-5vol%CNT sample is improved by about 30 % compared to conventional UO2 fuel.

Coated Zr-based claddings

The most direct solution for designing ATF cladding is to coat the original outside Zr-based cladding with a thin layer of specialized materials [20]. Thin coatings can enhance the protection of fuel rods while reducing the impact on physical properties of the reactor core, which need to contain at least one of the Cr, Al, and Si elements to improve the resistance of claddings to high-temperature steam oxidation, corrosion, etc [13,49]. Currently, Cr is the most widely developed coating element, mainly including Cr coatings, CrAl coatings, CrN coatings and TiAlN coatings.

Cr coating

The most desirable properties of a coated Zr-based cladding should simultaneously express high thermal conductivity, corrosion resistance in coolant environments, oxidation resistance to high temperature vapors and neutron irradiation stability. Reviewing the existing coating technologies, Cr coatings have the best development prospects. The French Atomic Energy and Alternative Energy Commission (CEA) states that, when exposed to steam at 1000 °C for more than 4 h, Cr coating is able to significantly reduce the oxidation kinetics of Zry4 material, thus effectively preventing the massive hydrogenation and embrittlement of the cladding at high temperatures, as shown in Fig. 6(a). CEA also found that Cr coating can retard the generation of brittle ZrO2 and αZr(O) layer to improve the quench resistance and post-quench ductility of the cladding [50].

Based on Cr powder, in Fig. 6(b), Kim et al. developed a three-dimensional laser coating technique for Zr-based alloys and applied this technique to a Cr-coated Zry4 cladding with an axial length of 100 mm [51]. Although a few cracks appeared in Cr coating during the ring tensile test, the spalling never occurred. Because of the lower corrosion rate of Cr metal, the oxide layer on the surface of Cr coating is about 25 times thinner than that on the surface of pure Zry4 cladding in a high-temperature steam environment at 1200 °C. Hydrogen generation from the excess oxidation reaction is greatly reduced, the swelling and rupture resistance of Zry4 cladding under the effect of Cr coating is much more superior. Park et al. deposited Cr coating on the Zr substrate by cold spraying measure, the Cr-coated Zr alloys abandoned interdiffusion of alloying elements and showed good oxidation resistance under high temperature conditions [52]. In the LOCA scenario of Fig. 6(c), the Cr coating elevates the fracture temperature of Zr material, reduces the circumferential strain as well as the rupture size in expansion region, enhances mechanical strength and ductility. All these relevant properties contribute to the integrity and safety of fuel claddings. In addition, Westinghouse has demonstrated significant oxidation resistance as well as adequate mechanical properties under strain of Cr coating during normal operation and LOCA by covering ZIRLO Zr alloy with Cr coating using the optimized cold spraying method [53].

Evaluating the behavior of coating materials under irradiation is also necessary. Zinkle et al. speculated that the body-centered cubic (BCC) lattice can enable Cr metal to exhibit good dimensional stability when subjected to neutron irradiation at LWRs-relevant temperatures [55]. Wu et al. conducted ion irradiation experiments on the first generation of Cr-coated Zry4 with 20 MeV Kr8+ at 400 °C, with interface damage not exceeding 10dpa [56]. Results of high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) and EDS analyses show that the irradiated Cr/Zr interface has good chemical and microstructural stability.

CrAl coating

Kim et al. also selected the binary alloy of CrAl as coating to improve the oxidation and corrosion resistance of Zr-based cladding [57]. They investigated two processing methods, laser and arc ion, to enable good adhesion of the coating to Zr-based alloy, oxide dispersion strengthened (ODS) was also used to control the swelling and rupture openings of the cladding. In terms of corrosion behavior, there's no spalling or galvanic coupling corrosion between the CrAl coating and substrate interface. By optimizing the composition ratio of Cr and Al, stable oxides are formed so that the CrAl coating exhibits good oxidation/corrosion resistance. Due to the higher alloy strength and the good strength of ODS organization layer at high temperatures of CrAl, the mechanical properties of Zr-based cladding such as creep and wear resistance are also improved under the effect of CrAl coating. In the irradiation experiments, the irradiation intensity of Zr-based cladding with CrAl coating also reached 150 FPD, showing a good irradiation effect.

CrN coating

Based on the physical vapor deposition method, Daub et al. prepared CrN and AlCrN coatings with thicknesses of 2–4 μm on the surface of Zry4 alloy to study the corrosion resistance of different samples [58]. Considering the hydrogen embrittlement that occurs when the cladding is exposed to water containing dissolved hydrogen, they also analyzed the hydrogen permeability of different Cr-based coatings. There were three types of experimental environments: pure water at 350 °C for 24 h, CANDU conditions with PH = 10.5 at 300 °C for 30 days and superheated steam at 1000 °C–1100 °C. As shown in Fig. 7, the AlCrN coating has poor adhesion to the substrate and is prone to cracking. In contrast, the CrN coating has a more acceptable overall performance, allowing the cladding to show better corrosion resistance and less hydrogen absorption. Kuprin et al. prepared CrZr/Cr/CrN multilayer coating with a thickness of 7 μm through vacuum arc evaporation [59]. This multilayer coating effectively prevents the high-temperature oxidation of Zr-based alloys within 1 h at 660 °C–100 °C, the oxidation products CrO and Cr2O3 also reduce the oxygen permeability of the coating. As a control, the uncoated Zry4 alloy oxidized rapidly. At the highest test temperature of 1100 °C, the oxide layer thickness on the surface of multilayer coating is only 5 μm, which is about 1/50 of that of Zry4 alloy. For irradiation behavior, Nieuwenhove et al. studied in- and off-core experiments of the Halden reactor project [60]. CrN, TiAlN and ZrO2 were used as coating materials for small Inconel 600 cylinders and Zry4 samples in BWR and pressurized water reactor (PWR) experiments. The SEM results show that the CrN coating performs well and remains intact under irradiation. On this basis, CrN, TiAlN and AlCrN materials were further clad onto Zry4 samples under PWR conditions. Except for some localized cracking and disappearance, the CrN coating still responds optimally to irradiation and maintains more than 80 % integrity, while the TiAlN and AlCrN coatings disappear completely after irradiation.

TiAlN coating

The work of Daub et al. similarly confirms the good corrosion resistance and lower hydrogen permeability of TiAlN coating [58]. In addition, Alat et al. applied TiAlN ceramic coating to ZIRLO substrate using cathodic arc physical vapor deposition (CA-PVD) technology to improve the corrosion resistance in high-temperature water [61]. The titanium-bonded coating as an intermediate layer can strengthen the adhesion of TiAlN coating on ZIRLO substrate. After completing corrosion experiments in static pure water at 360 °C and 18.7 MPa for 3 days, it was found that the 600 nm-thick titanium layer combined with 12 μm-thick coating as well as substrate surface roughness of 0.25 μm lead to optimal performance of TiAlN ceramic coating. Compared to the average weight gain of 14.4 mg/dm2 for uncoated ZIRLO substrates, the presence of the optimized coating only results in a substrate weight gain of 1∼5 mg/dm2. It should be noted that, the depletion of Al in coating can lead to the emergence of boehmite phase, which to some extent weakens the corrosion resistance of a few TiAlN samples. Since removing Al content and depositing TiN helps eliminate boehmite phase in TiAlN coating, Alat et al. further deposited TiN/TiAlN multilayer coating with different structures on ZIRLO substrate based on the optimized thickness of titanium layer and surface roughness of substrate [62]. Temperature and pressure of the corresponding experiment were the same as the previous work but the duration was extended to 90 days. From the results, the top TiN layer with a thickness of about 1 μm is sufficient to prevent the migration of Al and hinder the formation of boehmite phase. Unlike other multilayer structures, the 8-layer TiN/TiAlN coating shows the best corrosion and oxidation resistance with no peeling or delamination. The weight gain of ZIRLO substrate under optimized coating coverage is about an order of magnitude less than the uncoated benchmark.

FeCrAl claddings

Ferritic steels with BBC crystal structure contain good resistance to stress corrosion cracking (SCC), which have been used as nuclear fuel cladding since 1951. Meanwhile, the excellent high-temperature steam oxidation resistance of FeCrAl cladding can significantly reduce hydrogen production and prolong the reaction time under DBA and BDBA conditions, the higher material strength also helps to reduce the cladding thickness and increase the fuel volume. In light of this, FeCrAl cladding is seen as the simplest and most economical alternative cladding technology. UO2-FeCrAl fuel assemblies can be used in existing LWR designs and infrastructures to improve reactor accident tolerance, which have a very complete analytical evaluation of severe accidents [63,64].

In the 1960s, the US General Electric Company announced a plan to apply FeCr, FeAl and FeCrAl alloys with high temperature oxidation resistance to high temperature reactors. In 2014, Terrani et al. formally proposed the application of advanced oxidation resistant ferroalloy to fuel cladding in LWRs and demonstrated the improvement of safety margin by ferroalloy cladding in severe accidents [66]. Besides, Raul et al. noted that (as shown in Fig. 8) in LWRs simulation environment, a thin and dense oxide is formed on the surface of commercial FeCrAl alloy (APMT), which is about 1/10 the thickness of the oxide on the surface of Zr alloy. Under severe accident conditions, the tolerance to superheated steam reactions of APMT is several orders of magnitude higher than Zr-based alloys, which produces less combustible hydrogen and helps to maintain a coolable geometry for a long time after LOCA occurs [65]. Nippon Nuclear Fuel Development, Co., Ltd. found that ODS-FeCrAl alloy in BWRs fuel cladding not only performs outstanding resistance to cooling water corrosion and high temperature steam, but also reduces the interaction between UO2 pellets and B4C, which means that ODS technology can be used for further development of ATF [67].

Field et al. investigated the irradiation resistance of FeCrAl alloy containing 10 wt%∼18 wt%Cr and 2.9 wt%∼4.9 wt%Al at 382 °C and 1.8dpa [68]. Results show that the microstructural characteristics of FeCrAl alloy under LWRs irradiation will affect the mechanical properties of the sample, where Fe-18Cr-2.9Al has the highest degree of irradiation-induced hardening. Therefore, it is necessary to optimize the composition of FeCrAl alloy to control the precipitation of Cr-rich α′ under neutron irradiation at typical LWRs temperatures. Unocic et al. compared the oxidation resistance of forged FeCrAlY alloy (Cr content <15 wt%, Al content ≥ 5 wt%) with commercial APMT in steam at 1200 °C–1475 °C and air [69]. When the Cr content is in the range of 10 wt%∼12 wt%, the oxidation behavior of FeCrAlY alloy is similar to that of APMT. Furthermore, influences of different component ratios in FeCrAl alloy on the oxidation properties of cladding at or above the melting point have also been studied [68,70].

Gamble et al. simulated the performance response of FeCrAl and Zry4 under the conditions of normal operating as well as oxidation and non-oxidation loss of coolant [71]. For the former, FeCrAl cladding can reasonably withstand thermal stress, the oxidation reaction kinetics with steam and hydrogen generation rate are also significantly improved. Meanwhile, the fuel-cladding mechanical interaction (PCMI) in FeCrAl are delayed compared to Zry4. When oxidation and non-oxidation loss of coolant occur, the bursting characteristics of FeCrAl and Zry4 are similar in Fig. 9(a), indicating that FeCrAl has the qualitative properties required for ATF materials. Moreover, the core characteristics of advanced BWR loaded with thin ODS-FeCrAl cladding were also evaluated [73]. By increasing the enrichment of UO2 fuel under the limit of 5 wt%, it has been confirmed that the reactor core containing ODS-FeCrAl has sufficient reactivity. The thermodynamic behavior of thin ODS-FeCrAl cladding is acceptable in both normal and transient operating conditions.

Long-term ATF technical concepts

High-uranium-density UN fuels

High-uranium-density fuels combined with inherently safe nuclear reactor design can help extend fuel cycles, reduce waste and provide higher power densities. At the same time, high-uranium-density fuels also widen the safety margin and mitigate the consequences of any DBA or BDBA occurrence. Uranium silicide (U3Si2、U3Si5) initially received a great deal of attention and was attempted to be used in lead test assemblies. However, Westinghouse made it clear in the latest study that U3Si2 is not suitable for continued iteration and development of nuclear reactor systems [74] and has shifted the effort focus to the development of UN fuels. Although the corresponding explanations or supporting evidence have not yet been publicly published, the potential causes are still well documented [75]: U3Si2 and U3Si5 do have significantly superior thermal conductivity and thermo-mechanical properties compared to UO2, but their relatively low melting temperatures result in nearly the same practical fuel melt margin. During the oxidation of fuel assemblies, they are prone to respectively increase the generation of hydrogen by a factor of 5 and 6, weakening the accident tolerance degree of fuels. In contrast, another alternative high-uranium-density fuel, UN, is able to exhibit higher thermal conductivity while maintaining a higher melting temperature, thus extending the fuel melt margin to more than twice that of UO2. This can compensate to some extent for the generation or even increase of hydrogen during the oxidation process. In addition to UN, other high uranium density fuels such as UC and UB2 have also been studied as alternatives to UO2, but UN has shown more acceptable performance under irradiation [76] and remains the most promising option for high-uranium-density fuels.

Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory (LLNL) initiated the LDRD program to develop improved UN fuel using in SSTAR reactors to support compact and long-lived reactor cores [79]. For LWRs, studies on the economic potential of alternative fuels have indicated that UN is a suitable option for the once-through fuel cycle [80], from which multiple researchers have been consistently active in implementing relevant development program. Feng et al. used MCODE to model the assembly of Hitachi's resource-renewable BWR and investigated the neutron multiplication ratio gain after switching from oxide fuel to higher density nitride fuel [77]. In Fig. 10(a), nitride fuels with densities of 95 % TD and 85 % TD at fixed burnup result in fissile fuel stock ratio of 1.17 and 1.15, respectively, which are improvements of about 10 % over the reference value of 1.04. Xu et al. proposed the possibility of replacing 5%-enriched UO2 fuel in GEN III and GEN III + commercial LWRs with UN, thermal safety margins of UN fuel in LWRs were evaluated under normal and accident conditions using a coupled CFD model in CASL program developed by the U.S. Department of Energy [81]. Zakova et al. on the other hand argued that replacing UO2 fuel with UN fuel will obtain higher heavy metal content in reactor core, as shown in Fig. 10(b), thus increasing the fuel residence time [78]. With the use of nitride fuel, fuel residence time is extended by about 1.4 years compared to UO2 fuel with the same enrichment, which implies that the possibility of one-month fuel replenishment time is increased by 1.4 %, showing a good economic efficiency.

FCM fuels

FCM fuels are also a key member of ATF alternative fuels in new generations of nuclear reactors, which has the advantages of high inclusion capacity for fission products, high thermal conductivity, as well as good stability under high temperature and irradiation. FCM fuels are columnar pellets formed from a large number of tri-isotropic fuel (TRISO) particles dispersed in a SiC matrix, which improves fission product retention by introducing one or more additional safety barriers. As a result, in an RIA accident, energy is selectively deposited at discrete locations, thereby enhancing the core's tolerance to an RIA accident. SiC matrix can show better stability and less mechanical changes under irradiation compared to conventional graphite matrices, so that the expansion and melting of fission parts can be immobilized and retained in inert medium.

Terrani et al. focused on the application of FCM fuel forms in LWRs [82]. The solidification of SiC substrates was achieved based on nano-infiltration and transient eutectic (NITE) phase techniques, which greatly reduces the dependence on oxide doped materials and acquires SiC substrates with higher density. As shown in Fig. 11, under the same conditions of core power and cladding temperature, the temperature profile of the FCM fuel pellet is obviously lower than that of conventional oxide fuel. Due to the restricted fuel expansion, the gap between the fuel and the cladding closes more slowly and no further deterioration of the heat transfer at the gap occurs. In LWRs, FCM fuels have also been found to exhibit adequate irradiation behavior. When the conventional Zr-based cladding fails and ruptures, SiC matrix allows the FCM fuel to maintain stable thermodynamic properties with a smaller decline rate facing with high-temperature water or steam [83]. Lee et al. used Al2O3 and Y2O3 as sintering additives to prepare SiC ceramics and FCM pellets by hot pressing sintering method, thermal properties of samples under different volume fractions of TRISO particle were tested and analyzed [84]. Due to the increasing lattice thermal conductivity of grain, thermal conductivity of SiC ceramics can improve with the growth of sintering additives content and sintering temperature. FCM pellets with different volume fractions of TRISO particle were sintered at 1850 °C, and it was found that the thermal conductivity of FCM decreases with the increase of particle volume fraction. Measured thermal conductivity of FCM pellets under different sintering conditions was also in good agreement with the predicted value obtained by Maxwell-Eucken model.

Extruded Metal fuels

For LWRs and water-cooled small modular reactors (SMRs), Lightbridge focused on developing a multi-blade and spiral-twisted extruded metal fuel rod [85]. In Fig. 12(a), the cross section of the fuel rod is cross-shaped, where the heat transfer area is about 35 % more than that of the cylindrical rod. Each rod is composed of Zr-Nb alloy cladding, U-Zr alloy fuel core and central Zr alloy displacer, which are bonded to each other by co-extrusion and metallurgical methods. The fuel core contains 50 % uranium and 50 % Zr, the thermal conductivity is much better than that of ceramic UO2 fuel. The design enrichment 19.75 % of U-235 in the core as a significant improvement over conventional 5 % increases core power, extends fuel cycle times and promotes fuel consumption up to 50 % or more of the current commercial limits. Moreover, the metallurgical combination of cladding and fuel allows the metal fuel rod to discard the traditional cladding air gap, while the displacer can be alloyed with combustible poisons, contributing to a longer life of reactivity control. As a joint result of shape, material and metallurgical bonding, the temperature on the operating centerline of this cross-section metal rod is about 1000 °C lower than that of conventional rods, greatly expanding the safety margin of core systems.

Extruded metal fuel rods: (a) cross section; (b) rod body with spiral twist.

The helically twisted design in Fig. 12(b) creates a self-spacing of 10 cm between the rods, eliminating the need for a spacing grid and reducing flow resistance while still meeting the seismic and vibration requirements. Therefore, when the coolant pumps inside the reactor system are operating at rated conditions, the reduced flow resistance to flow within the core can increase the coolant flow and reduce the pressure drop. With higher coolant flow rates and constant core temperature differentials, more heat inside the core can be transferred to the steam generator. This process has boosted power output by about 10 percent. In addition, the application of such metal fuel rods requires adjustments to the fuel cycle so that longer operating cycles can be realized. Modifications to the fuel pool are also necessary to accommodate the high enrichment of metal fuels and the initial high decay heat after removal from the reactor.

At the same time, extruded metal fuels are able to exhibit good fuel-cladding mechanical integrity. Alloys with roughly equal uranium and Zr content overcome the expansion problem of original fuels to some extent, showing excellent fission product retention and low stresses from the fuel to the cladding. The metallurgical bond between the cladding and the fuel is such that the cladding exists primarily as a barrier to the release of fission products rather than as a major structural component within the rod. A rupture of the cladding would result in only a small and localized exposure of the fuel to the coolant, mitigating the consequences of accidents. Furthermore, the significant reduction in the 238U content of extruded metal fuels greatly reduces the amount of plutonium produced during irradiation and is more proliferation-resistant than existing fuels. These inherent advantages allow extruded metal fuels to be used both in existing nuclear power plants and as a replacement for conventional nuclear fuel in SMRs without major modifications, directly improving the operational flexibility and economics of plants.

SiC/SiC claddings

Rigid SiC/SiC tubes formed by woven SiC fibers additionally impregnated with SiC are a key frontier hotspot for alternative cladding technologies. SiC/SiC composites show a high melting point and remain structurally intact at temperatures exceeding the melting point of UO2 together with small chemical activity and neutron absorption cross section. SiC/SiC not only has far superior high-temperature and oxidation resistance than coated Zr-based alloys or FeCrAl, also can significantly reduce the hydrogen production and prolong the response time in DBAs and BDBAs, which has been attempted to be used in claddings for LWRs since 2002 [[86], [87], [88]]. Currently there are two technological ways to fabricate SiC/SiC cladding: one is the isothermal chemical vapor infiltration (CVI) method. The high popularity of this method in countries such as the United States, France, South Korea, and Japan can be attributed to the high purity as well as the excellent crystallinity and radiation stability of the prepared SiC/SiC [89], while the density is relatively low (porosity of only 10 %–25 %) [90]. The other involves the NITE and the hot pressing process to provide denser SiC/SiC composites with good mechanical properties [91]. Shimoda et al. on the other hand suggested to use both CVI and NITE technologies to produce SiC/SiC in nuclear grade [92].

Based on the robust preparation technique, Koyanagi et al. performed neutron irradiation on different interfaces of monolithic SiC/SiC plates at 270 °C–310 °C and 8.7 dpa, torsional shear test and microstructure observation were also conducted simultaneously [93]. After irradiation, the SiC-based bonding layer has a more stable microstructure. In order to develop the application of SiC/SiC composites in LWRs, Koyanagi et al. also conducted neutron irradiation studies of CVI SiC/SiC composites reinforced with HNS, SA3, and SCS fibers in a high-flux isotope reactor under LWR coolant conditions (230 °C–280 °C and 2.0dpã11.8dpa) [94]. In Fig. 13(a), both HNS and SA3 fiber-reinforced composites show excellent resistance to neutron irradiation at a dose of 11.8dpa. After irradiation, the bending strength of the materials had no obvious change and still maintained the quasi-ductile fracture behavior, indicating that HNS and SA3 fiber-reinforced CVI SiC/SiC composites can be used as cladding materials in LWRs. In contrast, the mechanical degradation was more evident in the SCS fiber-reinforced composites and the fracture mode of material changed from quasi-ductile to brittle after irradiation. It can be considered that SCS fibers are not suitable for nuclear-grade cladding production. Using chemical vapor composited (CVC) and chemical vapor deposited (CVD) processes, Silva et al. synthesized and evaluated the chemical compatibility of different SiC samples with UO2 at 1100 °C, 1300 °C and 1500 °C [95]. In Fig. 13(b), SiC prepared by CVC had a slight reaction with UO2 at the first two lower temperatures while SiC prepared by CVD was essentially unchanged. At the highest temperature, the reactivity of both samples with UO2 was significantly higher and the SiC prepared by CVC was more reactive. Braun et al. also thermodynamically analyzed the UO2±x/SiC system, investigated the chemical compatibility of SiC and UO2±x using different experimental methods in temperature range of 1500K–1970K [96]. In the closed system, the reactivity of SiC and UO2±x was always in the trough up to 1668 K, implying that the melting threshold using SiC/SiC cladding will be much higher than that of Zr-based alloy cladding.

[94], Elsevier.; (b) Optical microscopic images of heat treatment of UO2/SiC components at different temperatures [95]. Source: figure reprinted with permission from Ref. [95], Elsevier.

In terms of high-temperature steam oxidation and quenching, Lee et al. evaluated the oxidation rate of SiC cladding in steam under LOCA conditions in LWRs [97]. Under the test conditions of atmospheric pressure, steam temperature of 1140 °C and steam flow rate of 1 m/s∼10 m/s, the oxidation rate of SiC was about three orders of magnitude lower than that of Zry4. Avincola et al. reported experimental results of steam oxidation and quenching of SiC/SiC samples at temperatures up to 2000 °C [98]. In the case of 1600 °C–1800 °C, the hydrogen amount generated by the samples was about 40 times less than that of Zr-based alloys. When heated to 2000 °C and quenched in water at 90 °C, the SiC/SiC remained in a coolable shape. In steam oxidation experiments at 1600 °C, the oxidation resistance was consistently good despite the appearance of bubbles on the surface of SiC/SiC composites, and the samples were able to provide additional safety margins to exceed the LOCA rating values. Besides, composite SiC/SiC is also an optional material for channels in BWRs and guide tubes in LWRs [99]. All of these results indicate that SiC composite cladding is expected to be the most influential ATF technology in BDBA scenarios if it can be successfully put into production.

Challenges and prospects

After the accident at the Fukushima nuclear power plant in Japan, increasing the accident tolerance of LWRs has become a serious topic in the nuclear industry. With the rapid increase in the number of nuclear power reactors, mainly LWRs, in recent years and the continuous climb of nuclear power generation in the share of global clean energy, more stringent requirements have been put forward internationally for the safe operation and severe accident management of nuclear reactors. ATF concepts was thus born. This paper presents the definition, characterization, and thermal-hydraulic research objectives of ATF concepts, reviewing the different types and dynamic development of ATF technologies in terms of near-term concepts (expected to be approved and deployed based on existing codes) and long-term concepts (still awaiting more developmental testing for licensing), respectively. Combining the analysis in this paper and the results of other scientific articles in ATF field, some recommendations for preferred ATF options can be given through considering the fuel and cladding aspects simultaneously. Based on the available data, test facilities and licensing approvals from nuclear authorities and in-service reactors worldwide, UO2-based composite fuels combined with coated Zr-based claddings remain the more mature near-term ATF scheme with better overall performance. Among them, UO2-BeO or UO2-SiC plus Cr coating is the most prominent, which have superior ability to improve the heat transfer and integrity of fuel rods as well as to reduce the generation of flammable gases. As for long-term ATF technology, from a stability perspective, high-uranium-density UN fuels or FCM fuels added with SiC/SiC claddings have excellent thermal, structural, and fission-product-inclusive properties in theory and limited experiments. In contrast, such long-term solutions still require a significant amount of new convincing data and information to reach the level of commercial use, which will cost a long lead time.

Nevertheless, ATF technologies, whether near-term or long-term, still contain shortcomings and limitations that require further improvement. Based on the current form of standard fuel systems, the existing problems and challenges in the ATF research field are also summarized below in terms of improvements and alternatives:

UO2-based composite fuels.

But the common problem is, doping other materials in UO2 matrix reduces the uranium amount in fuel, so a higher concentration of U-235 is needed to compensate for the loss of heavy elements, increasing the manufacture cost. Meanwhile, at the same core power, the specific power of UO2-based composite fuel is higher than that of UO2, which may shorten the operating cycle of the reactor.

Continuous explorations and refinements as well as the interpretation, evaluation, and application of related thermo-hydraulic properties with high-quality experimental data are critical to realizing the commercial-scale deployment and safety benefits of ATF technologies. The full development and adoption of ATF concepts not only improve the overall efficiency and economics of LWRs, but also provide a reference for other advanced nuclear systems.
