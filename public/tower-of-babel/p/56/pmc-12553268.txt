Ortho2Web: a workflow for disentangling the roles of hybridization and allopolyploidization in reticulation within Campanulaceae
Authors: Chao Xu, Zetao Jin, Siyu Xie, Hui Wang, Xiaohua Lin, Daikun Ma, Yu Zhang, Binbin Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPhylogenetic networks (Web of Life) depict the intricate evolutionary dynamics of organisms more accurately than simple bifurcating trees (Tree of Life), because processes such as hybridization and polyploidy connect lineages through reticulation. However, most existing workflows still examine evolutionary mechanisms separately. To provide an integrated view, we studied the bellflower tribe (Campanuleae), a lineage with a well‑documented reticulate past, and developed Ortho2Web, a workflow that teases apart hybridization and polyploidization within a single analytical framework.ResultsOrtho2Web employs multiple strategies to reduce the impact of non-biological factors, including (1) integrating multi-source genomic data to minimize sampling gaps, (2) generating complementary datasets to quantify phylogenetic signals, and (3) using tree-based orthology inference methods to mitigate the effects of paralogs. It then focused on clarifying the roles of biological factors, such as incomplete lineage sorting (ILS), hybridization/gene flow, and polyploidization. When applied to Campanuleae, the workflow produced a robust phylogenetic backbone and showed that early diversification was driven by interacting hybridization and allopolyploidization, while ILS contributed only marginally.ConclusionsBy integrating diverse data sources and analytical approaches in a modular, scalable framework, Ortho2Web offers a practical platform for inferring web-like phylogenies and elucidating the mechanisms of reticulate evolution. The Ortho2Web workflow is freely available at: https://github.com/PhyloAI/Ortho2Web.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12915-025-02426-1.Keywords: Campanuleae, Cytonuclear discordance, Introgression, Paralogs, Phylogenomics, Reticulate evolution

Background

The Tree of Life (ToL), a model originated from Darwin’s The Origin of Species [1], has long served as a conceptual and analytical framework for exploring the evolution and relationships among living and extinct organisms, such as the APG IV for the angiosperm backbone [2]. Central to this model is the assumption of a bifurcating phylogeny, where speciation events occur in a branching pattern [3]. However, with the increasing recognition of reticulation processes such as incomplete lineage sorting (ILS), hybridization, polyploidization, and introgression, this traditional view of evolutionary connectivity has been significantly challenged [4–6]. The recently proposed “Web of Life (WoL)” better captures the complex, network-like structure of evolution [7]. A growing body of genomic and/or phylogenomic studies across diverse taxa, including Bacterial and Archaeal lineages [8, 9], the vascular plant lineages [10, 11], birds [12], and mammals [13], have provided compelling evidence supporting the WoL framework.

Among plants, hybridization and allopolyploidization are particularly prominent drivers of reticulate evolution (as highlighted in recent reviews by Rothfels [4] and Stull [6]). These processes play a crucial role in enhancing genetic diversity and driving speciation, often complicating the traditional bifurcating model of evolution. Recent phylogenomic studies have proposed various workflows for detecting and estimating hybridization or polyploidization events individually, such as the hybridization-based workflow [7, 14] and the allopolyploidization-based workflow [15]. However, the combined effects of the processes on lineage diversification remain unexplored. Hybridization facilitates gene flow among species, potentially leading to introgression or hybrid speciation. When coupled with genome doubling, allopolyploidization can produce polyploid species with distinct evolutionary pathways. A joint analysis of these processes could offer a comprehensive understanding of the evolutionary dynamics within plant lineages.

This study uses the tribe Campanuleae as a model to explore the combined effects of hybridization and polyploidization and to develop a workflow for distinguishing these events. As the largest lineage in the family Campanulaceae, with over 620 species, Campanuleae has undergone extensive hybridization and polyploidy events [16–18]. Together with Cyanantheae and Wahlenbergieae, they form the subfamily Campanuloideae, characterized by radial floral symmetry and centered in the Holarctic region [19]. Since the description of Campanula, numerous “natural” infra-tribal classifications have been proposed based on morphological and karyological evidence [20–24]. These studies, though insightful, have been complicated by convergent evolution, cryptic species, and frequent reticulate evolution events [18, 25]. Genetic tools have helped clarify some of these evolutionary relationships. For instance, Eddie [26] used nuclear ribosomal internal transcribed spacer (ITS) sequences to show the polyphyly of Campanula, with Edraianthus and Phyteuma nested within it (Fig. 1a). Subsequent studies using plastid regions confirmed this polyphyly (Fig. 1b–g) [25, 27–31]. Subsequently, Xu and Hong [31] expanded on this, identifying four major clades (Fig. 1f, g) and 24 subclades in this tribe, although plastid markers, due to maternal inheritance, offered an incomplete picture [32]. Reticulate evolution, including hybridization, polyploidization, and ILS, has played a significant role in Campanuleae diversification [17, 18]. Integrating plastomes and 130 nuclear loci [18] revealed cryptic tetraploid and octoploid taxa in a Mediterranean Campanula clade, and showed that polyploid lineages often retain only one parent’s morphology. Cytological studies suggest that 13% of Campanuloideae are polyploid derivatives [16], emphasizing the significance of polyploidization in the evolutionary history of bellflowers and their relatives.

Next-generation sequencing (NGS) technology and new phylogenomic tools now enable the analysis of hundreds or thousands of nuclear genes, plastomes, and mitochondrial genes, leading to more accurate phylogenies. Techniques such as deep genome skimming (DGS) [33, 34] and target enrichment sequencing (Hyb-Seq) [35, 36] are improving our ability to uncover mechanisms underlying gene tree and cytonuclear discordance [37]. Using diverse genomic data helps address some limitations of single-marker approaches. With the increasing availability of genomic resources in public databases, including highly reusable data types [37] like DGS, whole-genome sequencing (WGS), and transcriptomic sequencing (RNA-Seq), we can leverage multiple lines of genomic evidence to investigate reticulation histories. In this study, we will integrate various genomic analyses into a single workflow, signaling a shift away from analyzing different data types separately.

While existing pipelines have facilitated conflict detection, they often rely on a single data source [38, 39] and analyze hybridization, polyploidy, and ILS separately [40]. As phylogenomic datasets grow in scale and complexity, researchers urgently need a unified workflow that can simultaneously integrate diverse data sources and disentangle evolutionary mechanisms.

In this study, we will integrate various genomic resources—Hyb-Seq, RNA-Seq, DGS, and WGS—to assemble plastid protein-coding sequences (plastid CDSs) and single-copy nuclear genes (SCN genes) for accurate phylogenetic inference. By utilizing datasets with distinct gene histories, we will assess cytonuclear discordance and identify potential ILS and hybridization events throughout the deep nodes of the phylogenetic backbone. Historical biogeographic analyses will be employed to infer when and where both recent and ancient hybridization and polyploidization occurred, contributing to the diversification of Campanuleae. Specifically, we aim to (1) propose a workflow that leverages multi-source genomic data for joint analyses of hybridization and polyploidization; (2) reconstruct detailed scenarios of the WoL in the early diversification of the bellflower tribe Campanuleae through integrative phylogenomic conflict and biogeographic analyses.

Results

SCN and plastid genes assembly and nuclear orthology inference

This study integrated multi-source genomic data, including 82 Hyb-Seq, eight RNA-Seq, three WGS, one Hi-C, and 44 DGS data, to conduct a comprehensive phylogenomic analysis (Additional file 1: Table S1). Nuclear assemblies generated through HybPiper yielded between 519 to 654 genes, highlighting some variation due to differences in sequencing coverage or quality among samples (Additional file 2: Fig. S1). Non-chimeric sequences recovered from HybPiper were used for orthology inference through four different methods, resulting in four distinct datasets: 1to1 with 445 genes, Monophyletic Outgroups (MO) with 645 genes, Rooted subTrees (RT) with 660 genes, and maximum inclusion (MI) with 669 genes. The final alignment of these four datasets, each comprising 134 taxa, contained 598,993, 864,819, 887,333, and 978,066 characters, respectively. Additionally, HybPiper was employed to assemble 79 plastid CDSs, and the recovery efficiency of these sequences was visualized as a heatmap (Additional file 2: Fig. S2).

Phylogenetic inference and gene tree discordance analyses

We generated 16 nuclear trees employed concatenation- and coalescent-based methods. These nuclear trees consistently supported the monophyly of the subfamily Campanuloideae and identified three distinct tribes: Campanuleae, Cyanantheae, and Wahlenbergieae (Fig. 2; Additional file 2: Figs. S3–S18).

The nuclear analyses identified four major clades within the bellflower tribe Campanuleae: clades α, β, γ, and δ. The relationships among the four clades were generally well-supported across both concatenation- and coalescent-based analyses. Clades α and β were consistently identified as sister groups, supported by robust statistical metrics (Fig. 3a; Additional file 2: Figs. S3–S28). These two clades represent the most species-rich lineages within Campanuleae, comprising approximately 500 species (including approximately 100 samples analyzed in this study), with nearly all members of the genus Campanula distributed within them. Clade γ was resolved as the sister group to clades α and β, encompassing four species from the Campanula and three species from Musschia [27, 31], making it the least species-diverse among the four major clades. In contrast, clade δ was consistently recovered as sister to the remainder of Campanuleae (Fig. 3a; Additional file 2: Figs. S3–S28). This clade includes approximately 15 species from the genera Jasione and Hesperocodon [31].

Clades α and β were further subdivided. Clade α was divided into six subclades (I–VI), while clade β was divided into three subclades (VII-IX) (Figs. 2 and 3a; Additional file 2: Figs. S3–S18). The relationships among the subclades within clade β were consistent across nuclear analyses, whereas clade α exhibited significant conflicts, likely reflecting its complex evolutionary history (Fig. 3a; Additional file 2: Figs. S3–S28). Clade α represents the most compositionally complex lineage within Campanuleae. This clade exhibits an interspersion of Campanula species among several taxonomically valid genera (e.g., Adenophora, Asyneuma, Legousia, Triodanis, and about ten others), exhibiting broad geographic distributions and remarkable morphological divergence—strong evidence for an intricate evolutionary history. In contrast, clade β shows a more coherent pattern: it is predominantly composed of Campanula species, including the core Campanula clade, where only a minority of embedded taxa belong to other recognized genera like Azorina, Edraianthus, Michauxia, and Trachelium [27, 31].

Despite some conflicting results, the overall monophyly of clade α was well-supported across nuclear datasets (Fig. 3a; Additional file 2: Figs. S3–S18). However, nodes within clade α exhibited discordance, with low concordance among gene trees and conflicting support scores, suggesting hybrid origins or ILS (Fig. 3a; Additional file 2: Figs. S3–S28). For example, subclades III and IV had strong support in both concatenation- and coalescent-based trees, whereas relationships involving subclades V and VI were less consistent, often indicating alternative topologies (Fig. 3a; Additional file 2: Figs. S3–S28).

Phylogenetic analyses of plastid CDSs showed considerable variation in the relationships among clade α subclades compared to the nuclear results (Fig. 3a; Additional file 2: Figs. S3–S18, S29–S32). For instance, the sister relationship between subclades V and VI was weakly supported in the plastid phylogeny, whereas nuclear analyses showed stronger support for different groupings (Fig. 3a; Additional file 2: Figs. S3–S36). Additionally, subclade IV was inconsistently placed as sister to a larger group consisting of subclades III, V, and VI in the plastid results, contrasting with more consistent nuclear placements (Fig. 3a; Additional file 2: Figs. S3–S18, S29–S32).

Cytonuclear discordance

The phylogenetic analyses were conducted across multiple datasets, including nuclear and plastid CDSs. These analyses employed both concatenation- and coalescent-based methods and consistently recovered congruent topologies at various taxonomic levels, with clade δ being the sole notable exception. However, within clade α, substantial discordance was detected, reflecting conflicting evolutionary signals between the nuclear and plastid data (Fig. 3a; Additional file 2: Figs. S3–S18, S29–S32). The summarized topologies inferred from various datasets and methods depicted the significant variations, more details refer to Fig. 3a and Additional file 2: Figs. S3–S18, S29–S32.

This cytonuclear discordance highlights the complexity of evolutionary processes within clade α. The divergent topologies inferred from nuclear and plastid datasets suggest that each genomic compartment reflects distinct aspects of the evolutionary history, likely influenced by factors such as hybridization or ILS.

Reticulate evolution of the four major clades in the tribe Campanuleae

To address the significant cytonuclear discordance observed among major clades of the tribe Campanuleae, we conducted an in-depth analysis of reticulate evolution. Using sequences from the MO dataset, we constructed a split network with Roella decurrens as the outgroup. The resulting network mirrored the relationships inferred from the nuclear phylogenetic tree. It effectively distinguished the four major clades within the tribe, with clades γ and δ showing particularly close affinity (Fig. 3b). The split network demonstrated significant levels of reticulation, highlighting the intricate evolutionary relationships among Campanuleae species (Fig. 3b). These findings underscore the substantial role that hybridization and other reticulate events have played in shaping the evolutionary trajectory of these clades.

Further analysis employing coalescent simulation (CoSi) and Mutation Calculation based on Coalescent (MuCCo) indicated that ILS alone could not account for the conflicts observed between gene trees and species trees (Fig. 4a, d; Additional file 2: Figs. S37, S38). However, we detected the presence of gene flow among tribe Campanuleae species, particularly in the clade α (Fig. 4b; Additional file 2: Fig. S39). To investigate potential hybridization events, we selected representative species from various subclades and conducted Species Networks applying Quartets (SNaQ) network analysis. Using the Campanuloideae 11-taxa dataset, we identified clade δ as a potential hybrid origin (Additional file 2: Fig. S40). Expanding the taxon sampling in clade δ to a 14-taxa dataset further supported the hybrid origin hypothesis, although the two proposed scenarios suggested different parental contributions (Fig. 4c; Additional file 2: Figs. S40, S41).

To explore potential allopolyploidy events, we utilized Polyploid Analysis (GRAMPA), which inferred that clade δ arose as an allopolyploid between clade γ and an unsampled or extinct lineage closely related to the tribe Campanuleae (Fig. 4d; Additional file 2: Fig. S42). Together, these results reveal the complex reticulate patterns that have shaped its evolutionary history.

Reticulate evolution of the six subclades in clade α

To resolve the extensive cytonuclear and gene tree discordance observed within clade α (Fig. 3a), we conducted a series of analyses targeting conflicting phylogenetic signals. The results from CoSi and MuCCo analyses consistently demonstrated that ILS was not the primary factor underlying the discrepancies in phylogenetic relationships (Fig. 4d; Additional file 2: Figs. S37, S38, S43). Instead, the findings suggested that hybridization and allopolyploidy were more likely contributors to these conflicts. SNaQ network analysis indicated a hybrid origin for subclade VI, with contributions from subclades V and II (Additional file 2: Fig. S44). Further analysis using GRAMPA confirmed the allopolyploid origin of subclade VI, identifying subclade V and the common ancestor of clade α as the parental lineages (Fig. 4d; Additional file 2: Fig. S45). Additional analyses, performed after sequentially removing subclades VI, I, and III to assess their specific contributions, provided deeper insights into allopolyploid events within clade α. Specifically, subclade I was determined to have an allopolyploid origin involving subclades II and V (Fig. 4d; Additional file 2: Fig. S46). After excluding subclade I, subclade III was identified as having an allopolyploid origin derived from subclades II and IV (Fig. 4d; Additional file 2: Fig. S47).

These findings underscore the significant roles of hybridization and allopolyploidy in shaping the evolutionary history of clade α, highlighting the complex reticulate evolution and multiple allopolyploidization events within this group. The recurrent identification of allopolyploid origins emphasizes the intricate evolutionary processes at play, contributing to the phylogenetic discordance observed across different datasets and inference methods.

Dating analyses, ancestral area, and chromosome number reconstructions

To estimate divergence times and diversification within the subfamily Campanuloideae, we performed analyses based on three distinct topologies: the maximum likelihood (ML) tree and ASTRAL tree from the MO dataset, and the ML tree inferred from plastid CDSs. All three analyses consistently indicated that the tribe Campanuleae originated in Europe-Southwest Asia during the early Eocene, approximately 54 to 56 million years ago (Mya) (Fig. 5a–d; Additional file 2: Figs. Most major clades diverged between the Eocene and Oligocene epochs. Clade α likely originated in Europe-Southwest Asia during the middle Eocene, as supported by all three phylogenies (Fig. 5a–d; Additional file 2: Figs. S48–S56). The six subclades of clade α diverged between 42 and 36 Mya, with plastid data suggesting this divergence may have occurred slightly earlier (around 44–38 Mya) (Fig. 5a–d; Additional file 2: Figs. S48–S56). This trend is further supported by the diversification rate analyses, which revealed a clear peak in speciation and net diversification rates during this epoch.

Clade δ originated through an allopolyploidization event involving two parental lineages: clade γ, which emerged in the early Eocene (51–50 Mya), and the MRCA of Campanuleae, which originated around 55–54 Mya (Fig. 5a–d; Additional file 2: Figs. S48–S56). The allopolyploidization event that led to clade δ likely occurred before its diversification, around 48–46 Mya. The hybrid origin of clade δ likely occurred during the late Miocene, when the MRCA of clade δ may have still been extant.

Our analyses indicate that clade α underwent rapid radiation, forming six subclades within a short period between the middle and late Eocene (42–36 Mya, Fig. 5e, f). Gene flow analysis using Dsuite showed little to no evidence of introgression among these subclades (Fig. 4b; Additional file 2: Fig. S39), suggesting independent diversification, with distinct evolutionary trajectories obscuring any potential recombination signals.

The diversification rates, based on both the nuclear MO and plastid CDS datasets, showed another increase from the middle to late Miocene (Fig. 5e, f). This abrupt diversification rise suggests a subsequent round of lineage radiation.

Chromosome evolution was modeled using ChromEvol, which identified LILINEAR_RATE_DEMI_EST as the best-fit model (AIC = 526). The inferred ancestral haploid chromosome number of the MRCA of subfamily Campanuloideae was n = 11, but were consistent (n = 9) across the tribe Campanuleae, as well as the corresponding MRCA of the other three tribes, i.e., Cyanantheae, Wahlenbergieae, and Campanuleae. Within Campanuleae, clades α and β retained the ancestral number of 9, while clade γ had 13 and clade δ had 9. Notably, subclade II exhibited an increased chromosome number of 15, while other subclades within clades α and β retained the ancestral count of 9 (green numbers in Fig. 2; Additional file 2: Fig. S57). These findings suggest that independent evolution of the major clades and subclades has obscured early genome duplication events.

Discussion

Ortho2Web: a workflow for disentangling hybridization and allopolyploidization using multi-source genomic data

Ortho2Web was developed to provide a comprehensive framework for disentangling the complex processes of hybridization and allopolyploidization, which are critical for understanding the evolution of reticulate lineages (Fig. 6). These processes play key roles in generating genetic diversity and driving speciation, yet they are notoriously difficult to differentiate using traditional methods, which often rely on analyzing individual data types in isolation. By integrating maternally inherited plastome data with biparentally inherited nuclear data, Ortho2Web overcomes these limitations. It leverages multi-source genomic data to perform phylogenomic conflict and historical biogeographic analyses, enabling the reconstruction of detailed evolutionary scenarios, both ancient and recent. However, we acknowledge that the accuracy of these reconstructions can still be limited by sampling bias and data quality across public genomic repositories.

Overview of the Ortho2Web Workflow. The workflow begins with DNA/RNA extraction and Next Generation Sequencing (a), followed by the design of single-copy nuclear (SCN) gene references and assembly of plastome and nuclear SCN genes (b). In the step of orthology inference (c), a phylogenetic tree illustrates the divergence of two gene copies, Gene_a and Gene_b, resulting from an ancestral duplication, with orthologs in samples 1_a, 2_a, and 3_a (Gene_a) and paralogs between samples 1_a and 1_b. Data cleaning steps using tools like trimAl, Spruceup, and TreeShrink prepare the data for phylogenetic inference, where two methods—concatenation-based and coalescent-based approaches—are applied (d, e). Phylogenomic conflict is analyzed using phyparts and Quartet Sampling (f, g), and a phylogenetic network is inferred to estimate hybridization events (h). Gene flow is analyzed through SNP calling and gene flow testing (i), while gene trees are mapped onto the species tree to explore potential duplication events, distinguishing between autopolyploidy and allopolyploidy using the GRAMPA method (j). The optimal ploidy model is applied to estimate ploidy levels (k), and tree-to-tree distances between empirical gene trees and the ASTRAL species tree are compared to those from coalescent simulations (l). Finally, historical biogeographic analysis includes dating, ancestral area distribution reconstruction, and diversification rate analysis (m)

The causes of phylogenetic discordances have been thoroughly examined and divided into non-biological and biological factors [41]. Among the non-biological factors, global taxonomic sampling and genome-level SCN gene approaches have effectively reduced taxon and locus sampling gaps, minimizing stochastic deviations [42]. With the rapid advancement of NGS technologies, researchers in fields such as plant taxonomy, evolutionary biology, and horticulture have generated vast amounts of genomic data from species worldwide. Much of this data is now available in public repositories like the Sequence Read Archive (SRA), National Genomics Data Center (NGDC), and European Nucleotide Archive (ENA).

The Ortho2Web workflow comprehensively explores public genomic repositories to fill sampling gaps, particularly for globally distributed lineages. While pipelines such as HybPiper or Phyluce are limited to a single data source—most often target enrichment data [38, 39]—Ortho2Web accepts all major NGS outputs: WGS, DGS, RNA-Seq, and Hyb-Seq (Fig. 6a). Instead of switching among Trinity for RNA-Seq, SNP-based workflows for WGS, and HybPiper for Hyb‑Seq and DGS, Ortho2Web uses one platform‑independent script set that assembles nuclear genes from all data types in a single, streamlined step (Fig. 6b).

Ortho2Web is fully modular: sequence retrieval, SCN gene assembly, orthology inference, and downstream phylogenomic analyses can be run independently or in parallel. Users can split large datasets into smaller manageable subsets, reducing run time and memory use while improving the reproducibility of hybridization network inference and polyploidy detection. These efficiencies make Ortho2Web a practical choice for large, species-rich lineages in which computational cost is often the main barrier to comprehensive phylogenomics.

In addition, contentious relationships in phylogenomic studies are often driven by a handful of genes, where tiny subsets of large data matrices can undermine support and alter inferred topologies [43]. Ortho2Web addresses this by employing a tree-based orthology inference approach, generating four nuclear datasets (1to1, MO, MI, and RT) to quantify phylogenetic signals, mitigating the effects of hidden orthologs and paralogs (Fig. 6c) [41, 43]. We also developed cleaning strategies to remove low-quality bases and sequences (Fig. 6d). By utilizing multi-source genomic data and establishing standardized analytical pipelines (Fig. 6), we have significantly reduced the influence of non-biological factors on topological conflicts, allowing researchers to focus on biological factors through extensive nuclear and plastid gene analyses.

Phylogenetic incongruences can stem from ILS, hybridization/introgression, polyploidization, or horizontal gene transfer (HGT) [44–47]. While HGT is primarily observed in bacteria and is less relevant in plant systematics [48–50], ILS and gene flow are more likely contributors to the discordances observed in plant lineage. We employed two methods developed by the PhyloAI team—MuCCo and CoSi—to examine ILS in reticulate evolution [15], offering complementary insights into specific phylogenetic nodes.

Introgressive hybridization, a key driver of species diversification, has been extensively studied for its role in facilitating gene flow [51–53]. To assess gene flow patterns in Ortho2Web, we first performed a D-statistics analysis, a widely used method that provides a comprehensive overview of introgression among major clades (Fig. 6h). Our experience showed that well-sampled major clades with different species had minimal impact on introgression detection. This approach allowed us to identify non-neutral gene flow, offering insights into ancient genetic exchanges. Additionally, tools like PhyloNetworks [54, 55] and PhyloNet [56] have enhanced our ability to detect specific hybridization events, though their high computational demands can be limiting (Fig. 6i). Combining multiple analytical tools is essential for constructing accurate phylogenetic networks [57, 58]. Given their strong performance in hybridization estimation, we proposed a multi-step strategy in our Ortho2Web workflow, generating multiple data subsets at various taxonomic levels to explore network patterns. Our results indicate that using different data subsets can result in significantly different inference outcomes when applying tools like SNaQ or PhyloNet. To ensure reliable results, we recommend generating multiple subsets with different representative species from the targeted lineages. Notably, limiting each subset to fewer than 15 samples not only helps manage the substantial computational demands but also increases the reproducibility of the analyses. Additionally, we suggest comparing results from both D-statistics and SNaQ/PhyloNet, and excluding hybridization events inferred by only one method to ensure robust conclusions. Given the similarity between hybridization and allopolyploidization, we identified the inferred hybridization events as potential allopolyploidization occurrences. We then conducted GRAMPA analysis to determine whether these events involved genome doubling following hybridization (Fig. 6h–j).

We conducted further analyses to explore the role of genome doubling and polyploidization in reticulation. Ploidy estimates, sourced from literature and databases like (Chromosome Count Database, CCDB), provided preliminary insights into the historical occurrences of polyploidization and WGD within the focal lineage. Subsequent analyses focused on detecting paralogs and mapping gene duplication events onto phylogenetic trees to further corroborate polyploidization/WGD events. Our experience suggested that the predesigned “SCN genes” should include some duplicated genes to effectively detect duplications. In parallel, we used GRAMPA to scan phylogenetic nodes for potential polyploid lineages, identifying both autopolyploidy and allopolyploidy occurrences. By integrating these phylogenomic and experimental analyses in this workflow, we aim to provide a comprehensive assessment of the role of polyploidization in the evolution of the studied lineages.

A study using Arabidopsis thaliana as a representative species proposed a genome-based pipeline to assess whether phylogenetic relationships are strictly tree-like or exhibit reticulate patterns [40]. This pipeline employs a quartet-based, parallelized framework to evaluate gene tree discordance arising from ILS and uses non-parametric tests to detect gene flow and deviations from a strictly bifurcating evolutionary history. Ortho2Web extends this concept to broader taxonomic scales. By integrating nuclear and plastid data from WGS, RNA-Seq, and Hyb-Seq sources, Ortho2Web can elucidate reticulation at the deep phylogeny (e.g., family, tribe, and genus) rather than within single populations. In addition to visualizing topological conflict, Ortho2Web includes dedicated modules that disentangle hybridization, allopolyploidization, and ILS at deeper phylogenetic scales.

In summary, Ortho2Web is built to grow. As bioinformatics advances, the workflow can readily incorporate new tools from our PhyloAI team and beyond the wider community. This extensibility establishes a robust foundation for studying complex evolutionary histories and for teasing apart how reticulate processes shape the diversification of biological systems, with implications for both evolutionary biology and related scientific disciplines.

Exploring the reticulate phylogenetic backbone of the bellflower tribe Campanuleae

The evolutionary history of the bellflower tribe Campanuleae is far more intricate than a simple bifurcating tree. Traditional phylogenetic models often fail to capture the complexities inherent in evolutionary processes, particularly when reticulate events such as hybridization and polyploidization are involved [5, 6]. In this study, we utilized the Ortho2Web workflow to elucidate the network-like evolutionary history within Campanuleae, revealing evidence of reticulation that complicates the traditional understanding of lineage relationships. Discrepancies in the phylogenetic placement of clade δ (Fig. 3a; Additional file 2: Figs. S3–S18) further suggested a more intricate evolutionary pattern, prompting us to hypothesize that the relationships among these clades may not be strictly bifurcating.

One of the primary challenges in reconstructing the evolutionary history of Campanuleae was addressing tree incongruence, a pervasive issue in molecular phylogenetics [41, 45, 59, 60]. While non-biological factors, such as insufficient taxon sampling or errors in sequence alignment, can contribute to incongruence [41], our application of the Ortho2Web workflow implemented a series of strategies to mitigate these factors. These strategies included the use of a multi-source genomic dataset, meticulous orthology inference, and rigorous sequence alignment procedures. Consequently, it became apparent that biological factors, particularly ILS and hybridization, were the more plausible explanations for the observed phylogenetic discordance. Both ILS and hybridization are well-documented sources of phylogenetic incongruence across the tree of life [61, 62], and our analyses implicated these processes in the evolutionary history of Campanuleae.

To disentangle the contributions of ILS and hybridization, we employed a combination of coalescent-based ILS analyses, network analyses, and polyploidy assessments within the Ortho2Web framework (Fig. 6h–l). The MuCCo and CoSi analyses indicated that ILS alone could not account for the observed reticulate patterns (Fig. 4a, d; Additional file 2: Figs. S37, S38). Further investigation using PhyloNetwork and the GRAMPA reconciliation algorithm provided compelling evidence for hybridization and polyploidy events (Fig. 4c, d; Additional file 2: Figs. S40–S42, S44–S47). Specifically, our results supported an allopolyploid origin for clade δ, arising from the MRCA of the tribe Campanuleae and that of the clade γ (Fig. 4d; Additional file 2: Fig. S42). This event likely occurred in Europe and Southwest Asia during the early Eocene (Fig. 5a–d; Additional file 2: Figs. S48–S56), a period characterized by significant geological transformations, such as the formation of the Alpine mountain chain and the closure of the Tethys Sea—factors that may have facilitated the diversification of the tribe.

Within clade α, comprehensive taxon sampling identified 15 clades, which were subsequently grouped into six subclades (I–VI, Figs. 2 and 3a). Significant discordances between nuclear and plastid datasets indicate multiple instances of hybridization and allopolyploidization. For example, conflicting phylogenetic placements of subclade VI between nuclear and plastid datasets suggest complex hybrid origins and differential inheritance patterns (Fig. 3a). Subclade VI likely originated as an allopolyploid, with the maternal lineage derived from the MRCA of subclade V, while the paternal lineage remains uncertain, with differing results from various analyses (Fig. 4d; Additional file 2: Figs. S44, S45). These conflicting results imply that subclade VI may have arisen from hybrid swarms involving whole-genome duplication (Fig. 4d). Similarly, subclades I and III were inferred to have allopolyploid origins involving other subclades, underscoring a complex reticulate evolutionary network within clade α (Fig. 4d; Additional file 2: Figs. S44, S45). Despite maintaining a gametophytic chromosome number of n = 9 (Fig. 2; Additional file 2: Fig. S57), the MRCAs of subclades I, III, and VI exhibit genomic characteristics indicative of an allopolyploid origin. This pattern may result from chromosomal diploidization following hybridization or from polyploid genome stabilization [63], both of which can preserve the ancestral gametophytic chromosome number. The variation in gametophytic chromosome numbers (n = 8, 10, 14, 15, etc.; Fig. 1) within clade α likely reflects the combined effects of multiple evolutionary processes, including post-polyploid chromosomal adjustments [64], structural rearrangements [65], and aneuploidy [66]. Collectively, these mechanisms underscore the dynamic nature of chromosome evolution in clade α.

Additionally, discrepancies between nuclear and plastid data regarding the ancestral areas of clade α suggest that the true evolutionary history may be more nuanced (Fig. 5a–d), necessitating the integration of multiple genomic resources to capture a comprehensive picture. Our historical biogeographic analyses further supported that the diversification of the six subclades of clade α was likely driven by rapid radiation during the Middle to Late Eocene, approximately 42–36 Mya (Figs. 2 and 5). This period coincided with significant climatic and tectonic shifts in Europe and Southwest Asia, including the collision of tectonic plates and the formation of the Alpine mountain chain. These geological events, coupled with a cooler yet still warm global climate, likely created new ecological opportunities that facilitated the rapid diversification of clade α. Although nuclear and plastid data provide different perspectives on the ancestral areas of clade α, the Ancient Mediterranean region, encompassing Europe and Southwest Asia, likely served as the center for early diversification.

The observed chromosome number variation along the terminal branches of clade β indicates that both ancient and recent polyploidization events may have shaped its evolutionary dynamics [27]. However, our network analyses found no statistically significant evidence of reticulate evolution, most likely due to the limited taxon samplings (Fig. 4). The high species richness, wide geographic range, and significant morphological diversity of Campanula [27, 31] greatly challenged the comprehensive sampling of this lineage, reducing the power to detect recent polyploidization and hybrid speciation events. Consistent with recent findings, our results underscore that sparse sampling can hide reticulation signals [7, 15]. Future studies should expand sampling to cover evolutionarily significant taxa and combine chromosome ploidy data with biogeographic approaches. Such integrated methods would reliably reveal how often—and where—polyploidization and hybridization have shaped the diversification of clade β.

The significant cytonuclear discordance observed in our dating analyses, ancestral area reconstruction, and diversification rate analyses (Fig. 5; Additional file 2: Figs. S48–S56) underscores a critical point: reliance on a single genomic resource is insufficient for capturing the full complexity of biogeographic evolution in potentially reticulate lineages [34, 41, 67]. Crucially, the spatial distribution of plastid haplotypes—shaped by seed dispersal, landscape barriers, and historical refugia—can differ from the geographically mixed nuclear lineage patterns generated by pollen flow and recombination. Rather than treating one history as more “accurate” than another, we interpret each signal as complementary. The plastid and mitochondrial genomes elucidate the maternal evolutionary history, whereas the nuclear genome conveys the biparental evolutionary pathway, together revealing reticulate processes such as hybridization, introgression, and allopolyploidy.

To place each genome‑specific history in its geographic context, we integrated a historical-biogeography module into Ortho2Web (Fig. 6m). We link every taxon to vetted GBIF and herbarium records with ≤ 10 km accuracy, then group the points into six regions that correspond to the major floristic provinces of the tribe Campanuleae. The dated tree generated by Ortho2Web is exported in BioGeoBEARS‑ and RASP‑ready formats, allowing users to infer ancestral ranges with no extra formatting. In our case study, this spatial layer shows that the earliest allopolyploid event (clade δ) arose in Europe and Southwest Asia during the early Eocene—a pattern that would have been missed without a geographic perspective.

Recent studies consistently report earlier divergence times inferred from nuclear genes compared to plastomes, likely due to differences in substitution rates and evolutionary constraints in the two genomes [7, 15]. These discrepancies highlight the necessity of integrative, multi-dimensional approaches in phylogenomic research to ensure a more comprehensive understanding of evolutionary histories and processes. Integrating multiple data sources, historical biogeographic analyses, and geological evidence—such as paleoclimatic data, tectonic plate movement, and fossil records—is essential to bridge knowledge gaps, validate findings, and generate nuanced insights into the complexities of lineage diversification and adaptation [5, 6, 41, 68]. Such multidimensional analyses are essential for reconstructing the spatial drivers of lineage diversification and adaptation in reticulate clades.

The evolutionary history of Campanuleae underscores the role of reticulation, driven by hybridization, polyploidy, and complex geological and climatic changes, in shaping the diversification of this intriguing plant group. By integrating multi-source genomic data with advanced phylogenomic methods, we were able to reconstruct a more comprehensive and accurate depiction of this tribe’s evolutionary past. This study highlights the necessity of employing a multifaceted approach to understand the complexities of lineage diversification, particularly in groups where reticulate evolution plays a prominent role.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the Ortho2Web workflow provides a robust framework for unraveling the complex interplay of evolutionary processes within reticulate lineages, such as hybridization and polyploidization. By applying Ortho2Web to the bellflower tribe Campanuleae, we have demonstrated its ability to clarify the critical roles of these processes in driving species diversification, particularly during the early diversification of lineages. Our results underscore the utility of incorporating orthologs into a web-like phylogenetic framework to gain deeper insights into reticulation-driven evolutionary dynamics. Moreover, the flexibility and scalability of Ortho2Web render it broadly applicable to other biological systems characterized by complex reticulation events.

The Ortho2Web workflow has certain limitations that should be acknowledged early on. Its performance is highly dependent on the availability and quality of genomic data, which can vary considerably across taxa, potentially compromising the accuracy of the inferred relationships. Additionally, the computational demands of the workflow may pose challenges, particularly for researchers with limited access to high-performance computing resources. Despite these constraints, we anticipate that Ortho2Web will significantly advance studies focused on hybridization, polyploidization, and ILS, and serve as a valuable tool for the research community, enabling deeper exploration of the complexities inherent in phylogenetic networks beyond conventional bifurcating models.

Methods

Taxon sampling, DNA extraction, and sequencing

In this study, we utilized the taxonomic system proposed by Lammers [16, 17], which provides a comprehensive overview of generic and species delimitation and is widely accepted within the Campanulaceae community. Mansion [27] subdivided Campanula sensu lato into 17 distinct clades based on chloroplast petD group II intron sequences. Our sampling strategy included representatives from all 17 major clades of Campanuleae, providing a comprehensive nuclear and plastid phylogenetic framework for the bellflower tribe. Specifically, we analyzed 134 accessions, comprising 116 ingroup individuals, representing 110 species out of ca. 620 species across all 17 clades within Campanuleae (Additional file 2: Table S1) [27]. We also sampled 18 outgroup species to assess the potential polyphyly of Campanuleae (Fig. 1g) [31] and to inform the subsequent dating analysis.

Our approach integrated multi-source genomic data from diverse sequencing technologies, including DGS, WGS, Hyb-Seq, and RNA-Seq. To reduce sequencing costs and fill sampling gaps, we extensively utilized public databases such as SRA from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI), and this strategy has been successfully utilized in recent case studies, such as Liu et al. [5, 7, 15, 33, 34]. For this study, our PhyloAI team newly sequenced 134 samples, with 90 generated via Hyb-Seq and 44 via DGS. Notably, four samples (Adenophora palustris, A. petiolata, A. remotiflora, and A. triphylla) were sequenced twice using both Hyb-Seq and DGS to increase coverage, and data generated from both methods for each sample were combined for subsequent analyses. Additionally, we incorporated four publicly available samples downloaded from SRA.

For DGS and Hyb-Seq sequencing, total genomic DNAs were extracted from silica-gel dried leaves and, where applicable, herbarium/museum specimens. The extraction was performed using the modified CTAB (mCTAB) method [69] at the Institute of Botany, Chinese Academy of Sciences (IBCAS). For DGS sequencing, we used the NEB Next Ultra DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB, USA), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Sequencing was performed on the DNBSEQ-T7 and BGISEQ-500 Sequencing Systems (Novogene, Beijing), yielding paired-end reads of 2 × 150 bp for 35 accessions and 2 × 100 bp for five accessions, respectively (Additional file 1: Table S1). For Hyb-Seq, library preparation was performed using the Fast Library Prep Kit (IGeneTech, Beijing). Subsequent solution-based hybridization and target enrichment were conducted with the TargetSeq One® Kit at the iGeneTech facility in Zhejiang, China. Sequencing was performed using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform, yielding paired-end reads (2 × 150 bp) for 82 accessions (Additional file 1: Table S1). For RNA-Seq, we extracted total genomic RNAs from silica-gel dried leaves via the mCTAB method. The libraries were prepared using the NEBNext® Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (USA). Sequencing was conducted on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 system, resulting in paired-end reads of 2 × 150 bp for eight accessions (Additional file 1: Table S1). All raw reads (134 accessions) sequenced for this study have been deposited in the NCBI SRA under the BioProject PRJNA895940 and PRJNA974960.

Raw reads processing, and nuclear SCN and plastid sequence assembly

In recent studies, our PhyloAI team has developed and refined approaches to integrate multi-source genomic data for phylogenomic analyses [5, 7, 15, 33, 34]. Here, we followed the established pipeline for processing and assembling raw reads. Low-quality reads and base calls were trimmed, and adaptor sequences were removed using Trimmomatic v. 0.39 [70]. The quality of the processed reads was assessed using FastQC v. 0.11.9 (https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/). To screen for putative single-copy genes, we utilized the transcriptome of Adenophora polyantha (SRA accession: SRX8528008) with MarkerMiner v. 1.0 [71], which resulted in 659 SCN gene sequences as references. We used nextflow hybpiper-nf [39, 72] to assemble the 659 SCN gene sequences, summarize gene recovery statistics, and retrieve sequences, which may include paralogs, for all recovered genes. This process produced an unaligned multi-FASTA file for each gene.

Given the history of gene rearrangements and diverse repeat sequences in the Campanulaceae plastome [73], we focused on assembling only the protein-coding sequences (hereafter referred to as plastid CDSs) for plastid phylogenetic inference. We extracted 79 plastid CDS sequences from three chloroplast genomes using Geneious Prime [74]: Adenophora remotiflora (GenBank accession: KP889213), Campanula takesimana (GenBank accession: KP006497), and Trachelium caeruleum (GenBank accession: EU090187). These 79 plastid CDSs served as the reference for assembly. We also employed nextflow hybpiper-nf [39, 72] to assemble the plastid CDSs.

Orthology inference and data matrices generation for nuclear SCN genes

Due to the high prevalence of allopolyploid species documented within the tribe Campanuleae (as noted in the CCDB), we employed multiple methods to accurately identify paralogs and distinguish potential orthologs from homologs. Using multiple methods helps address the challenges of accurately distinguishing these gene types, as paralogs can complicate phylogenetic analyses by introducing misleading signals if not properly accounted for. Additionally, we quantified the distribution of phylogenetic signals and generated four distinct data matrices with specific subsets of SCN genes to ensure accurate phylogenetic inference [43]. We adopted the orthology inference method developed by the Ya Yang Group [75, 76], which has been integrated into HybPiper v. 2.0 under the module “paragone-nf” [72]. Using this approach, we generated four nuclear datasets: one-to-one orthologs (1to1), MO, RT, and MI. These methods are designed to minimize the influence of paralogs in phylogenetic inference. The 1to1 algorithm retained unique homologs, effectively eliminating potential paralogs. The MO approach identified and pruned paralogs by using homologs with monophyletic groups of non-repeating outgroups, involving rerooting and pruning paralogs from root to tip. In instances without an outgroup, only sequences without duplicated taxa were included. The MI algorithm iteratively extracted the subtree with the highest number of taxa without taxon duplication. The RT method removed paralogs by extracting ingroup clades and cutting paralogs from root to tip; similarly, sequences without duplicated taxa were retained in the absence of an outgroup. For both methods, we set a minimum threshold of 25 ingroup taxa. We generated four datasets from these analyses to use as the basis for phylogenetic inference, i.e., the 1to1, MO, RT, and MI datasets.

Cleaning of nuclear and plastid sequences

To address low-quality sequences, we implemented a series of processing steps based on established methods [5, 33, 34]. First, we used MAFFT v. 7.505 [77] to align each SCN sequence, employing the Smith-Waterman algorithm and the “–maxiterate 1000” parameter to optimize alignment quality. The resulting alignments were then trimmed using trimAl v. 1.2 [78] to remove poorly aligned regions by excluding columns containing gaps in more than 20% of sequences or with a similarity score below 0.001.

Next, we cleaned the sequences further using Spruceup v. 1.0 [79], which detected and removed outlier sequences with a window size of 50 and an overlap of 25. Alignments before and after Spruceup processing were concatenated and split using AMAS v. 1.0 [80] to standardize the dataset structure and ensure uniformity across alignments, which is crucial for accurate downstream analyses. To prevent issues in phylogenetic inference, sequences shorter than 250 bp were excluded using a Python script from Liu et al.[34]. The cleaned sequences were then used to infer gene trees with RAxML v. 8.2.12 [81], using the “-f a” option and 200 bootstrap (BS) replicates for clade support evaluation. To enhance the accuracy of species tree inference, TreeShrink v. 1.3.9 [82] was employed to remove excessively long branches, which could introduce errors. After these processing steps, the sequences were designated as “clean nuclear genes” and organized into four data matrices: 1to1, MO, RT, and MI.

For plastid sequences, we compiled a dataset of 79 plastid CDSs, referred to as the “plastid CDS dataset”. The processing of plastid sequences largely followed the steps used for nuclear genes, except that we did not remove short sequences or long branches. This was because plastid sequences are more conserved and exhibit lower variability, making such removals unnecessary for accurate phylogenetic inference.

Accurate phylogenetic inference using multiple methods

To estimate accurate phylogenies and assess topological discordance, we employed both concatenation- and coalescent-based methods. The gene trees, refined by TreeShrink, were used to estimate species trees with ASTRAL-III [83], which follows the multi-species coalescent model (MSC) for accurate species tree estimation. ASTRAL-III computed branch lengths in coalescent units, representing the expected number of coalescent events per branch, thus providing a measure of discordance among gene trees. To improve accuracy, branches with low support (≤ 10) were collapsed using phyx [84].

We used the clean nuclear genes for both ML and Bayesian inference (BI) tree inference. PartitionFinder2 [85, 86] was used to determine the most suitable partitioning schemes and molecular evolution models. These partitioning schemes and models were then used in ML tree estimation using IQ-TREE2 v. 2.2.0.3 [87] with 1000 SH-aLRT and Ultrafast Bootstrap replicates, as well as RAxML v. 8.2.12 [81] with the GTRGAMMA model and 200 BS replicates, balancing computational efficiency and clade support reliability.

BI analysis was performed using MrBayes 3.2.7a [88], running Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) analyses for 50 million generations. Stationarity was confirmed when the average standard deviation of split frequencies fell below 0.01. Trees were sampled every 1000 generations, with the initial 25% of samples discarded as burn-in. The remaining trees were used to generate a 50% majority-rule consensus tree.

Gene tree and species tree discordance analyses

In our study, the quartet sampling (QS) and phyparts analyses were performed to assess discordance and evaluate the robustness of our phylogenetic hypotheses. Based on the 20 phylogenetic trees generated from the five datasets (nuclear 1to1, MO, RT, and MI, and plastid CDSs) and four methods (three concatenation-based: IQ-TREE2, RAxML, and MrBayes and one coalescent-based: ASTRAL-III), we identified four distinct topological structures (IQTREE tree based on CDSs dataset, RAxML/MrBayes/ASTRAL trees based on CDSs dataset, IQTREE/RAxML/MrBayes trees based on four nuclear datasets, and ASTRAL species trees based on four nuclear datasets). These topologies were selected for further analysis because they represent different combinations of datasets and analytical approaches that highlight various aspects of phylogenetic conflict and tree resolution.

We used phyparts to calculate unique, conflicting, and concordant bipartitions within individual orthologs across the phylogeny [89]. These partitions help identify concordance and conflict among orthologs, allowing us to better understand the sources and extent of gene trees and specified topology discordance. To address issues with missing taxa in orthologs, we conducted both quick concordance (-a 0) and full concordance (-a 1 -s 50) analyses (https://bitbucket.org/dfmoralesb/target_enrichment_orthology/src/master/). The conflict analysis generated a pie chart for each node, segmented into five sections, representing different proportions of orthologs: those supporting the clade (blue), those supporting the main alternatives (green), those supporting the remaining alternatives (red), the uninformative (dark grey), and missing data (light grey). The blue section indicates strong support for the clade, while the green and red sections represent varying levels of conflict from alternative relationships. The dark grey and light grey sections reflect data limitations, providing context for potential uncertainties. Additionally, we computed “internode certainty all” (ICA) scores on the concatenation-/coalescent-based tree using the set of ortholog trees [90] and visualized the results using a phyparts script by Morales-Briones et al. [76].

To complement phyparts, we used quartet sampling (QS) [91] to analyze discordance in large, sparse, and genome-wide datasets. With sequences and specific topologies as input files, QS can effectively handle alignment gaps and distinguish between strong conflict and weakly support branches, providing a detailed understanding of discordance. QS calculates three scores for each internal branch: quartet concordance (QC), which measures the level of support for the branch; quartet differential (QD), which indicates the strength of conflicting signals; and quartet informativeness (QI), which assesses the amount of phylogenetic information available for the branch. The results of the QS analysis were visualized utilizing the plot_QC_ggtree.R script (https://github.com/ShuiyinLIU/QS_visualization). These metrics offer complementary insights into the nature of discordance.

Inference of global split networks

Split networks are useful for visualizing inconsistencies in a dataset. In a split network, ancestral species are not represented by specific nodes; instead, parallel edges denote splits derived from the data, with their lengths indicating the importance of each split. For the tribe Campanuleae, we constructed a split network using SplitsTree v. 4.19.0 [92], based on aligned SCN sequences from the MO dataset. The MO dataset was selected because it provides reliable orthologs, offering a strong foundation for inferring the split network. Given the substantial divergence within the dataset, we applied split decomposition to uncorrected P distances and used the NeighborNet method to achieve higher resolution. We then used the EqualAngle network construction algorithm to infer the split network.

Multiple methods for testing the effect of ILS

Gene tree discordance can arise from independent evolutionary events such as ILS, hybridization, or allopolyploidization, or a combination of these factors. To differentiate among these causes, we conducted a series of analyses. We applied the Step-by-Step Exclusion (SSE) [7] approach to distinguish the effect of each factor and performed two analyses to assess the role of ILS. The CoSi analysis, available at https://github.com/PhyloAI/Coalescent-Simulation-Analysis, was used to test ILS globally, while the MuCCo analysis, available at https://github.com/PhyloAI/Mutation-Calculation-based-on-Coalescent-Model, tested ILS at specific nodes [15].

We began with the CoSi analysis to determine if ILS could explain gene tree conflicts, as this method provides a broad initial assessment of discordance across the phylogeny. In essence, when coalescent-based simulated gene trees show strong congruence with observed gene trees, this supports ILS as the primary cause of gene tree discordance. This approach has proven effective in recent studies [7, 15, 34, 76, 94]. We first estimated the species tree using ASTRAL-III [83] and converted it into an ultra-metric tree. Using this ASTRAL ultrametric species tree, we simulated 10,000 gene trees under the MSC model with the “sim.coaltree.sp” function in the R package Phybase v. 1.5 [95]. We then compared the distribution of tree-to-tree distances between simulated and empirical gene trees using the DendroPy v. 4.5.2 Python package [96]. The overlap between simulated and empirical gene tree distributions, visualized in a column chart, indicated how well the coalescent model explained gene tree discordance and whether ILS was a plausible factor.

In the MuCCo analysis, we assess the influence of ILS by estimating the population mutation parameter “theta” [46]. We first transformed the branch lengths of the species tree from coalescent units to mutation units. This transformation was performed using the alignment matrix and a predetermined species tree topology with RAxML v. 8.2.13 [81]. Theta values for each internal branch were calculated by dividing the branch length in mutation units (as estimated by RAxML) by the corresponding branch length in coalescent units from the ASTRAL species tree. A theta value of greater than 0.1 indicates a significant level of ILS, suggesting a high degree of genetic variation that cannot be explained by the coalescent model alone, implying additional evolutionary processes such as ILS.

SNP calling and gene flow analyses

We utilized the genome of Adenophora triphylla var. japonica (accession number: GCA_022495995.1) from GenBank as the reference for SNP calling. The reference genome was indexed using BWA v. 0.7.17 [97], SAMtools v. 1.19 [98], and Picard v. 3.1.1 (http://broadinstitute.github.io/picard/) to ensure efficient read alignment, variant calling, and quality control in subsequent analyses.

For SNP calling, we employed BWA v. 0.7.17, which uses a Burrows-Wheeler Transform (BWT)-based algorithm to align sequencing reads to the reference genome. SAMtools v. 1.19 was then used to convert the aligned reads into BAM format. The BAM files were subsequently sorted by chromosomal position for consistency. Next, Picard v. 3.1.1 was applied to mark duplicate reads introduced during library amplification, which helps reduce biases in downstream variant calling and improves the accuracy of SNP identification.

To identify SNPs, we used GATK4 v. 4.5.0.0 [99] with the “HaplotypeCaller” and “CombineGVCFs” commands, which allowed us to call variants from multiple samples and combine them into a single VCF file. To minimize false-positive SNPs, we filtered the combined VCF file using GATK4 and VCFtools [100], excluding SNPs with a quality score below 30 or quality depth below 2. A quality score below 30 often indicates low confidence in the variant call, while a quality depth below 2 suggests insufficient sequencing coverage, both of which could lead to unreliable SNP identification. Additionally, VCFtools was used to extract SNPs from specific genomic regions, omitting sites with over 50% missing data or quality scores below 30.

To evaluate gene flow within Campanuloideae, we used the “Dtrios” function from the Dsuite package [93] to calculate the f4-ratio across various species combinations. To control the false discovery rate, we applied the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) correction using the “p.adjust” function in R. The results were visualized using the Ruby script “plot_d.rb”, which effectively illustrated gene flow patterns, including specific relationships and introgression events among species.

Phylogenetic network estimation

In this study, we employed network-based approaches to investigate the early diversification of four major clades (α-δ) and nine subclades (I–IX) within the tribe Campanuleae. To reduce the computational demands, we selected a subset of 11 species (the Campanuloideae 11-taxa dataset) that represented the broader diversity within the major clades, allowing us to test for potential ancient reticulate evolution events. The dataset included Lobelia seguinii as the outgroup, Codonopsis lanceolata from the tribe Cyanantheae, Wahlenbergia undulata from the tribe Wahlenbergieae, and eight species from the tribe Campanuleae: Hanabusaya latisepala and Campanula prenanthoides (clade α), C. asperuloides and Edraianthus serbicus (clade β), C. peregrina and C. lactiflora (clade γ), and Hesperocodon hederaceus and Jasione montana (clade δ). Extensive hybridization was observed within clade δ, prompting us to expand the sampling of this clade by adding three more species, resulting in a 14-species dataset (the Expanding 14-taxa dataset).

Within clade α, we grouped all 74 samples (representing 71 species) into six monophyletic clusters (subclades I–VI) based on 16 nuclear and four plastid trees. Grouping these samples into monophyletic clusters is significant because it helps clarify the evolutionary relationships within clade α and provides a framework for understanding the role of gene flow in the early diversification of this clade. To assess the impact of gene flow during the early diversification, we generated a dataset of seven species (the clade α seven-taxa dataset), with each subclade represented by one species: C. prenanthoides (subclade I), Peracarpa carnosa (subclade II), C. hawkinsiana (subclade III), Homocodon brevipes (subclade IV), C. cymbalaria (subclade V), and Hanabusaya latisepala (subclade VI).

To estimate species networks, we applied the SNaQ approach, as described by [54]. This method, integrated into the PhyloNetworks package [55] in Julia, uses maximum pseudolikelihood estimation to infer species networks while accounting for ILS through the coalescent model and incorporating horizontal gene inheritance via reticulation nodes. The use of pseudolikelihood reduces the computation burden compared to full likelihood calculations and allows for efficient estimations at the quartet level, facilitating parallelization [54]. For phylogenetic network analysis, we followed the protocol provided by Solís-Lemus, available at https://github.com/crsl4/PhyloNetworks.jl/wiki. We used h values ranging between 0 and 6, conducting 50 independent runs for network inference.

Allopolyploidy analysis

To identify parental lineages involved in the formation of auto- and allopolyploids, we used the Gene-tree Reconciliation Algorithm with Multi-labeled trees (MUL-trees) for GRAMPA, adapted to work with MUL-trees [101], which relates to changes in overall ploidy level (x). Given the similarity between hybridization and allopolyploidization, we tested hybridization events inferred by SNaQ using GRAMPA to determine whether these events were followed by genome doubling.

Since GRAMPA is limited to inferring one WGD event at a time, we used the method proposed by Morales-Briones et al. [76] to successively investigate WGD events along the phylogenetic backbone. Specifically, we defined the relevant clades as “-h1” and “-h2” and performed a reconciliation search using all MO ortholog trees against the MO species tree estimated with ASTRAL-III [83]. Once a polyploid clade was identified through GRAMPA analysis, we removed it from the species tree and repeated the analyses on the remaining clades. This iterative process continued until no further polyploid clades were detected.

Dating analysis and ancestral area reconstruction

Historical biogeographic analyses, including dating and ancestral area reconstruction, can provide insights into the timing and locations of both recent and ancient hybridization and polyploidization events. The earliest known macrofossils of the Campanulaceae family are seeds of Campanula paleopyramidalis, discovered in Miocene deposits from ca. 17–16 Mya in Nowy Sacz, Carpathians, Poland [102–104]. These fossilized seeds have been used as a key calibration point in several studies, including those by [25, 27–29, 105, 106]. This species is closely related to the extant Campanula pyramidalis. We used this fossil as the MRCA of a clade comprising C. carpatica, C. pulla, C. rainerii, and Favratia zoysii. Additionally, we incorporated two secondary calibration points to constrain deeper nodes within Campanulaceae: the crown clade of Campanulaceae, dated between 72.24 and 52.66 Mya, and the crown clade of the subfamily Campanuloideae, dated between 63.52 and 44.58 Mya [107].

To investigate the impact of conflicting topologies on dating analyses, we used the MCMCTree package in PAML v. 4.9j [108] to estimate divergence times in Campanulaceae based on multiple topologies (concatenation- and coalescent-based trees) and datasets (plastome and nuclear). We began by determining the nucleotide substitution rate and Hessian Matrix using MCMCTree, applying the independent rates clock model and the GTR substitution model. Each dataset underwent two independent runs of MCMCTree, each initiated with different random seeds. The initial 1,000,000 generations of each MCMC were discarded as burn-in, and sampling was conducted every ten generations, yielding a total of 500,000 samples. These samples were analyzed using Tracer v. 1.7.1 [109] to confirm convergence and ensure that the effective sample size (ESS) for all parameters exceeded 200. The results from both runs were visualized using FigTree v. 1.4.4 (https://github.com/rambaut/figtree) and cross-validated by comparing parameter estimates and divergence times to ensure convergence.

We utilized BioGeoBEARS v. 1.1.2 [110] within RASP v. 4.2 [111] to reconstruct the ancestral area of the bellflower tribe. Based on the current distribution of Campanulaceae [16, 112, 113], we divided the geographic range into six regions: (A) Europe, (B) North Asia, (C) Africa, (D) North America, (E) Southeast Asia combined with Australasia, and (F) Southwest Asia. We used three estimated trees to assess the effect of topological discordance on ancestral area reconstruction, specifying a maximum of four areas to retain biologically meaningful results. Six models provided by BioGeoBEARS v.1.1.2 were utilized to estimate the biogeographic history, and the corrected Akaike information criterion (AICc) values were compared to determine the optimal model for reconstructing the ancestral area of Campanuleae.

Chromosome number reconstructions

To examine the potential impact of polyploidization on changes in chromosome numbers, we estimated the ancestral chromosome numbers for each lineage. Haploid chromosome numbers (n) which reflecting gametophytic stages were obtained from the Chromosome Count Database (CCDB) [114] and the Campanulaceae monograph by Lammers [16]. Using the ASTRAL-species tree, we employed ChromEvol v. 2.2 [115] to infer the ancestral haploid chromosome number using a likelihood-based approach, as it allows for more accurate modeling of chromosome number evolution compared to similar methods. Species lacking chromosomal data are denoted with “X”. When a species had multiple chromosome counts, we used the most frequently observed count for analysis. The analysis was conducted using the parameter “_mainType = All_Models,” which included ten evolutionary models, with “_simulationsNum 1,000,000” to enhance precision. The best-fit model was selected using the AIC values.

Diversification analyses

To access changes in speciation and extinction rates over time within the tribe Campanuleae, we used the RevBayes v. 1.2.1 [116] using the episodic birth–death (EBD) model [117]. To visualize these rate changes, we used RevGadgets v. 1.0.0 [118]. Both nuclear genes (MO datasets) and plastid CDSs datasets were analyzed to compare diversification rates between the nuclear and plastid genomes.
