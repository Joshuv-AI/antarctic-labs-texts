Therapeutic targeting of CD47 using a tri-specific NK cell engager to inhibit multiple myeloma
Authors: Ratchaneewan Sumankan, Prin Sungwan, Natthanich Boonsatit, Seiji Okada, Aussara Panya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Multiple myeloma (MM), a hematological malignancy, remains an incurable disease due to the development of resistance to the treatment; thus, there is an urgent need for new and effective therapeutic strategies, particularly for patients who do not respond to standard therapies. High levels of Cluster of Differentiation 47 (CD47) expression have been reported in MM and are associated with disease progression. CD47 acts as a cancer immune escape mechanism by binding to SIRPα protein, resulting in inhibiting phagocytosis of macrophages and NK cell activity. Therefore, blocking the CD47 signaling pathway has emerged as a promising strategy for cancer immunotherapy. In this study, we confirmed that MM cells have high CD47 expression. We generated and characterized a tri-specific killer engager targeting CD47, namely TriKE-CD47, that targets both CD47 on MM cells and CD16 on NK cells. Additionally, it incorporates an IL-15 moiety to enhance NK cell proliferation. TriKE-CD47 treatment promoted a remarkable proliferation of NK cells overexpressing CD16 (N6 cells). Co-culturing MM cells with N6 cells, primary NK cells, and monocyte-derived macrophages in the presence of 200 ng of TriKE-CD47 significantly improved NK cytotoxicity and macrophage phagocyte activities against MM cells. Notably, the efficacy of TriKE-CD47 was directly correlated with CD47 expression levels on the target cells reflecting the specificity of TriKE-CD47 to target antigen. Furthermore, TriKE-CD47 effectively suppressed tumor growth in MM xenograft mice models. Taken together, these findings strongly supported that TriKE-CD47 could be a potential therapeutic for MM patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00262-026-04431-x.

Introduction

Multiple myeloma (MM) is an incurable plasma cell malignancy that is characterized by the impairment of B cell development in the bone marrow. These abnormal plasma cells can produce an excessive antigen-specific immunoglobulin, also known as paraprotein (M-protein), which can be detected in the blood and urine of the patients [1]. This disease can cause bone marrow failure or osteolytic lesions, leading to other organ dysfunctions, for instance, hypercalcemia, renal impairment, anemia, and immune paresis with resulting infection [2]. Currently, there are various standard treatments for MM patients to extend the outcome and overall survival of the patients, for instance, traditional chemotherapy, alkylating agents, immunomodulatory drugs, proteasome inhibitors, monoclonal antibodies, CAR-T therapy, and bispecific antibodies, etc. [3] [4] [5] [6]. However, MM remains an incurable disease due to drug resistance that can develop in MM patients, and it is associated with relapsed and refractory, which is caused by cytogenetic abnormalities [7]. Thus, the effective treatment for MM patients should be investigated.

Interestingly, the previous report has shown that drug-resistant MM patients have high Cluster of Differentiation 47 (CD47) expression levels, especially in bortezomib-resistant [8]. CD47 is an integrin-associated protein (IAP), which overexpressed in many cancer cell types [9]. Overexpression of CD47 has been reported in MM cells and those correlated with disease progression and poor survival of MM patients [10]. Mechanistically, CD47 has been shown to regulate cancer cell proliferation, apoptosis, and migration [10] [11]. Moreover, it plays an important role in the immune surveillance evasion of cancer cells by targeting the signal regulatory protein-α (SIRPα), which is expressed on macrophages, leading to the activation of the “don’t eat me” signal and the inhibition of phagocytosis of macrophages [12]. Therefore, the blockade of the CD47 signaling pathway to enhance anti-tumor activity has been proposed as the target for cancer immunotherapy, including MM. Magrolimab and AO-176 serve as key clinical examples of anti-CD47 antibody therapies for MM. Magrolimab facilitates tumor clearance primarily through macrophage-mediated opsonization and phagocytosis; however, its clinical utility is limited by dosing challenges associated with on-target red blood cell (RBC) binding. Another agent, AO-176, is an anti-CD47 IgG2 antibody with distinct characteristics, including preferential binding to tumor cells and negligible binding to red blood cells (RBCs). In addition, AO-176 has been reported to induce phagocytosis and immunogenic cell death (ICD) through the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), thereby potentially eliciting a broader adaptive immune response. AO-176 has been evaluated in phase I/II clinical trials to assess its safety profile and clinical outcomes. Despite these advances, maintaining a favorable safety-efficacy profile remains challenging, supporting further development of next-generation multispecific agents to improve efficacy [13] [14].

Cancer immunotherapy, which harnesses the patient’s own immune system to target and eliminate cancer cells, is a promising treatment strategy that has significantly improved therapeutic efficacy and specificity, thereby extending patient survival and outcomes. Natural killer (NK) cells are innate immune cells that provide effective cytotoxic activity to combat malignant cells [15]. Nowadays, NK cell-based immunotherapies represent a promising alternative approach due to their favorable safety profiles, which minimize the risk of graft-versus-host disease (GvHD) and are associated with rare instances of cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and neurotoxicity [16] [17] [18]. With their HLA-unrestricted recognition, NK cell therapies offer an off-the-shelf approach that holds great promise for treating multiple myeloma (MM) patients.

In this study, we generated a tri-specific killer engager targeting CD47, referred to as TriKE-CD47. TriKE-CD47 is composed of three functional components: (1) a single-chain variable fragment (scFv) that recognizes CD47 on tumor cells; (2) a human interleukin-15 (IL-15) moiety that promotes NK cell proliferation; and (3) a single-domain anti-CD16 antibody (sdAb) that binds CD16 on NK cells. Together, these elements enable TriKE-CD47 to enhance the anti-tumor activity of NK cells through antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC). We confirmed CD47 expression in nine MM cell lines using flow cytometry. We then evaluated the ability of TriKE-CD47 to bind directly to CD47-expressing MM cells and CD16-expressing NK cells. The functional activity of TriKE-CD47 was assessed by measuring its capacity to enhance NK cell-mediated ADCC and macrophage-mediated antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis (ADCP). Furthermore, our study demonstrated that TriKE-CD47 significantly boosted the anti-tumor activity of NK cells, resulting in effective tumor control in a xenograft mouse model.

Materials and methods

Cell cultures and media

The K562 cells and MM cell lines, including KMS-11, RPMI8226, KMS-12-PE, KMM-1 (JCRB Cell Bank, Osaka, Japan), MM.1S, MM.1R, JJN3, U266, and NCI-H929 (These cells were kindly provided by Dr. Hiroto Ohguchi at the Institute of Resource Development and Analysis, Kumamoto University) were cultured in RPMI-1640 (FUJIFILM Wako, Osaka, Japan) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). The CD16-overexpressing NK cell line, namely N6 (provided by Prof. Shuzo Matsushita, Kumamoto University, Japan), was grown in RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10% FBS and interleukin-2 (200 U/ml). The cells were cultured in the incubator at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2.

Control normal B cells were obtained from the separation of cord blood mononuclear cells (CBMC) using Pancoll Human (PAN Biotech GmbH, Aidenbach, Germany). The study protocols were approved by Kumamoto University ethics committee (Approval no.: RINRI 2753).

Determination of CD47 expression

To determine the CD47 expression, MM cells (1 × 106 cells) were stained with anti-CD47-FITC (Clone 2D3, eBioscience Inc. San Diego, CA). Control normal B cells from CMBC were used as the control and stained with anti-CD47-FITC to determine CD47 expression on CD19 + B cells. The stained cells were analyzed with FACS analysis (BD Bioscience, San Jose, CA). The collected data were analyzed with FlowJo Version 10 software (Tree Star, San Jose, CA).

Generation and production of TriKE-CD47 protein

Generation and production of TriKE-CD47 protein was previously described [19]. Briefly, the lentivirus vector harboring TriKE-CD47 encoding sequences was generated by incorporating the gene encoding for ScFv specific to CD47 (derived from Magrolimab), human IL-15, and anti-human CD16 sdAb (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/nuccore/EF561290), each separated by linker peptides and tagged with C-Myc and 6 × His for detection and purification. An RFP gene was included for stable cell selection. The vector was used to produce lentivirus and transduce HEK293T cells, followed by puromycin selection. Expression of TriKE-CD47 was confirmed by fluorescence microscopy and flow cytometry. For protein production, selected HEK293T cells were cultured in serum-free Optimem (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA), and culture supernatants were collected for purification.

Purification of TriKE-CD47

TriKE-CD47 was purified using a His GraviTrap™ column (Cytiva, USA). The column was equilibrated twice with 10 ml of binding buffer (50 mM NaH2PO4, 300 mM NaCl, 5 mM imidazole), after which the culture medium containing TriKE-CD47 was applied to allow binding via the His-tag. The column was washed with 10 ml of washing buffer of the same composition, and the bound protein was eluted in three 1.5 ml fractions containing 100-, 200-, or 300-mM imidazole (in 50 mM NaH2PO4 and 300 mM NaCl). Imidazole and excess salt were removed by dialysis using an Amicon Ultra-4 concentrator (Sigma, Ireland). Protein purity and identity were verified by Coomassie Brilliant Blue R-250 staining and immunoblotting with an anti-6 × His antibody (Invitrogen). Protein concentration was determined using Bradford reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

The binding activity of TriKE-CD47

The binding activity of TriKE-CD47 to CD47 on MM cell lines and CD16 on N6 cells was investigated. The MM cells, including RPMI8226 and KMS-12-PE (1 × 105 cells per reaction), were incubated on ice for 1 h with various concentrations of TriKE-CD47 (0.78 ng-12.8 μg per reaction). The N6 NK cells were incubated with 10 ng/μL Magrolimab (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA USA) for 1 h to block CD47 prior TriKE-CD47 incubation. After the indicated time, the cells were stained with anti-HIS conjugated APC at a dilution 1:50 (Clone J095G46, BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA). The binding activity was detected by a CytoFLEX Flow Cytometer (Beckman Coulter, Indianapolis, IN, USA), and the acquired data were analyzed using CytExpert software (Beckman Coulter, USA) and FlowJo 10.0 software (FlowJo, Ashland, OR, USA). The values of positive cell proportion were used to calculate the half-maximal effective concentration (EC50) using nonlinear regression analysis of GraphPad Prism Ver.6 software (GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA).

Hemagglutination assay

For the hemagglutination assay, the 5% v/v of whole blood was prepared by diluted with PBS and seeded into a 96-well U-shaped plate. Serial dilutions of TriKE-CD47 or Magrolimab (from 25, 50, 100, 200 ng) were added and incubated with human RBC for 4 h at room temperature. Hemagglutination was identified by the presence of non-settled RBCs appearing as a diffuse haze, whereas non-agglutinated RBCs formed distinct punctate red dots at the bottom of the well.

Apoptosis assay

PBMCs were isolated and seeded in a 96-well plate with 1.5 × 105 cells/well and treated with 0, 100, or 200 ng of TriKE-CD47 for 24 h. Then, the cells were harvested and stained with Annexin V-allophycocyanin (APC)/propidium iodide (PI) solution (Immunotools, Friesoythe, Germany). Flow cytometry was conducted using a CytoFLEX Flow Cytometer (Beckman Coulter, Indianapolis, IN, USA). The percentages of living (AnnexinV − /PI −), early apoptosis (AnnexinV + /PI −), and late apoptosis (AnnexinV + /PI +) cells were analyzed using the FlowJo Version 10 software.

Cell proliferation assay

The cell proliferation was measured using tetrazolium dye methylthiotetrazole (MTT) assay (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO). Briefly, the N6 cell line was seeded at 5 × 103 cells/well in 96-well flat bottom plate (Corning Incorporated, NY, USA) and cultured with IL-15 or TriKE-CD47 for 0, 24, 48, and 72 h. After the indicated time, MTT solution was administered to each well at a final concentration of 0.5 mg/ml. Subsequently, the plate was further incubated for 4 h at 37 °C, 5% CO2 incubator, and 100 μl of 0.08 N HCl/isopropanol was added to dissolve the formazan crystals. Then, the optical density (OD) of MTT was measured at dual-wavelength 570 nm/655 nm using a microplate reader (iMark; Bio-Rad Laboratories Inc, CA, USA).

Isolation and expansion of NK and monocyte-derived macrophages

PBMC isolation was approved by the Human Ethics Committee of Kumamoto University, Japan (Approval No. RINRI 2753), and informed consent was obtained from all participants. PBMCs from healthy donor blood were isolated by density-gradient centrifugation using Pancoll (PAN Biotech, Germany). The method for human NK cell isolation and expansion was modified from our previous protocols [20] [21] [22]. In brief, human NK cells were isolated from freshly obtained PBMCs of healthy donors using an NK Cell Isolation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec) according to the manufacturer’s protocol based on the MACS technique. The primary NK cells were then cultured in RPMI 1640 supplemented with 10% FCS, 200 U/mL IL-2, and 100 U/mL IL-15 under standard cell culture conditions (37 °C, 5% CO2). Subsequently, MHC class I deficient K562 cells were co-cultured with NK cells at an effector-to-target (E:T) ratio of 10:1 on days 3 and 7. Appropriate volumes of fresh growth medium were added to the co-culture system during the induction and expansion phases. NK cell purity was confirmed using an antibody cocktail consisting of anti-human CD56-BV421, anti-human CD16-PE, and anti-human CD3-APC antibodies (BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA). The human monocyte-derived macrophages (MDMs) were prepared according to previously described protocols [20] [23]. Briefly, freshly isolated human PBMCs were plated in 10-cm tissue culture dishes (Corning Incorporated, Corning, NY, USA) and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 1 h in RPMI 1640 containing 1% FCS to allow monocyte adherence. Subsequently, non-adherent cells were removed and the dishes were gently washed with PBS. The adherent monocytes were further cultured in RPMI 1640 supplemented with 1% FCS and 100 ng/mL recombinant human macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF) (Morinaga Milk Co., Tokyo, Japan) for 7 days to induce macrophage differentiation.

Antibody-dependent cell cytotoxicity (ADCC)

RPMI8226, KMS-12-PE, or NCI-H929 cells were labeled with CFSE (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA) and seeded into 96-well U-bottom low-evaporation plates. Cells were incubated with 200 ng of TriKE-CD47 for 15 min at room temperature. N6 cells or human expanded NK cells were then added at effector-to-target (E:T) ratios of 0:1, 5:1, 10:1, and 20:1. Co-cultures were incubated for 18 h at 37 °C and 5% CO2. After incubation, cells were stained with Ghost Dye 780 (1:1000; Tonbo Biosciences, San Diego, CA) to assess target-cell death (CFSE⁺/Ghost Dye 780⁺) by flow cytometry (BD Bioscience, San Jose, CA). The target-cell death was compared between untreated and TriKE-CD47 treated condition at equal E:T ratios.

Antibody-dependent cell phagocytosis (ADCP)

The target cells, including RPMI8226 and KMS-12-PE, were labeled with CFSE in the presence or absence of 200-ng TriKE-CD47 for 15 min at room temperature in 96-well U-bottom low evaporation plate. Primary human monocyte-derived macrophages (MDMs) were seeded into the co-culture at the indicated concentration and further incubated for 2 h at 37 °C, 5% CO2. The cells were stained with anti-CD11b-BV421 (Clone M1/70). BioLegend (San Diego, CA). The phagocytic activity was measured by flow cytometry. The phagocytosed target cells were defined as CD11b + CFSE + cells. The phagocytosed target cells were compared between untreated and TriKE-CD47 treated condition at equal E:T ratios.

MM xenograft mouse model

BALB/c Rag-2/Jak3 immunodeficient (BRJ) mice were housed and monitored at the Center for Animal Resources and Development (CARD), Kumamoto University, following institutional animal care guidelines. All procedures were approved by the Kumamoto University Animal Ethics Committee (Approval No. A2023-099) and performed as previously described [24]. KMS-12-PE cells (8 × 106 per mouse) were subcutaneously injected into both flanks (5 mice per group). Three days later, N6 cells (7 × 106 per mouse) were peritoneally transplanted, followed by daily intraperitoneal injections of TriKE-CD47 (50 μg/mouse). Body weight and tumor volume were monitored every three days. Mice were killed when tumors exceeded 1 cm, and tumor weights were measured. The experiment was terminated on day 13 because tumor volume in the control group exceeded 1 cm3. According to standard ethical guidelines for subcutaneous tumor models measured by calipers, tumor volume should not exceed 1.5 cm3.

The width and length of both left and right tumors were measured using calipers, and tumor volume was calculated according to the formula below. The reported data represent the mean tumor volume derived from both tumor sites in each mouse.

Equation: Tumor volume = ½ × width2 × length.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism Ver.6 software (GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). The data from at least three independent experiments were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Turkey’s multiple-comparison test. The p-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Generation of the recombinant TriKE-CD47 protein

We generated a lentiviral vector (pCDH) encoding a tri-specific killer engager targeting CD47, termed TriKE-CD47 (Supplementary Fig. 2A). TriKE-CD47 comprises an anti-CD47 scFv for targeting myeloma cells, human IL-15 to promote NK cell proliferation, and an anti-CD16 sdAb to activate NK cell cytotoxicity via ADCC. The construct was fused in-frame with RFP (Supplementary Fig. 2B). Stable expression was confirmed by RFP detection using flow cytometry and fluorescence microscopy (Supplementary Fig. 2C–D). TriKE-CD47 protein was purified using His GraviTrap™ and concentrated with a concentrator column. Its purity (> 90%) and approximate molecular weight (~ 75 kDa) were verified by Western blot and Coomassie Brilliant Blue staining (Supplementary Fig. 2E–F, Supplementary Fig. 3).

The RPMI8226 with high CD47 expression and KMS-12-PE with moderate CD47 expression were used to demonstrate the binding activity of TriKE-CD47 on MM cell lines. The cells were incubated with various concentrations of TriKE-CD47. Moreover, to evaluate the binding site of TriKE-CD47 for CD47 protein, the competition assay was performed using humanized monoclonal anti-CD47 antibody called Magrolimab (Hu5F9-G4). The results represented that under the competitive condition with Magrolimab, the binding ability of TriKE-CD47 (200 ng) was completely decreased, suggesting that TriKE-CD47 binds to CD47 at a similar epitope as Magrolimab (Supplementary Fig. 4). Taken together, the results suggested that TriKE-CD47 efficiently bound to either CD47 on MM cells or CD16 on NK cells.

TriKE-CD47 causes no hematological toxicity

To evaluate the potential hematological toxicity, we first characterized the baseline surface expression of CD47 on healthy red blood cells (RBCs) and T lymphocytes. RBCs exhibited detectable but relatively low CD47 expression compared with MM cell lines. Consistent with this, binding analysis showed that TriKE-CD47 binds to CD47 on RBCs at low levels, with substantially reduced binding relative to MM cells, likely reflecting the lower surface density of CD47 on RBCs (Fig. 3A). Hematological safety was further investigated using a hemagglutination assay. The results showed that TriKE-CD47, at concentrations ranging from 25 to 800 ng, did not induce RBC agglutination. In contrast, clear agglutination was observed with magrolimab at 25 ng (Fig. 3B). Furthermore, treatment with TriKE-CD47 (100–200 ng) did not result in a significant depletion of total RBC counts (Supplementary Fig. 7), suggesting that neither RBC agglutination nor lysis occurred following treatment.

To address the potential cytotoxic effects on normal cells, we performed apoptosis assays using PBMCs to evaluate the impact of TriKE-CD47 treatment. CD47 surface expression on T cells was first assessed and found to be markedly lower than that observed in the MM cell line RPMI8226 (Supplementary Fig. 8). Consistent with this lower expression, binding analysis demonstrated only minimal TriKE-CD47 binding to T cells, indicating limited engagement due to the relatively low surface density of CD47 (Fig. 3C). To further determine whether TriKE-CD47 could promote NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity against normal T cells, PBMCs (containing T cells, NK cells, monocytes, and residual RBCs) were treated with TriKE-CD47 at 100 and 200 ng. After 24 h of incubation, apoptotic profiling via Annexin V/PI staining indicated no significant decrease in T cell viability or induction of apoptotic cells compared to untreated controls (Fig. 3D). Collectively, these data indicate that TriKE-CD47 has a favorable safety profile, with minimal effects on normal cells, likely due to the low baseline expression of CD47.

TriKE-CD47 efficiently induces NK cell proliferation

TriKE-CD47 incorporates the IL-15 moiety, which plays a crucial role in NK cell proliferation and survival. To examine the ability of TriKE-CD47 to stimulate NK cell proliferation, N6 cells were incubated with TriKE-CD47 or IL-15 at the concentrations of 25, 50, and 100 ng/ml for 0, 24, 48, and 72 h. The results revealed that TriKE-CD47 treatment significantly stimulated N6 cell proliferation in a time-dependent manner, at a level comparable to IL-15 treatment (Fig. 4). We further performed a proliferation assay using the CD16-negative NK cell line KHYG to evaluate IL-15-driven NK cell expansion. The results demonstrated that TriKE-CD47 treatment effectively promoted KHYG proliferation at a level comparable to recombinant IL-15 (Supplementary Fig. 5). TriKE-CD47 potently induces N6 cytotoxicity against MM cells

Engagement of CD16 on NK cells by antibodies targeting tumor-associated antigens induces antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) against cancer cells. To assess the ability of TriKE-CD47 to enhance ADCC, we conducted cytotoxicity assays using RPMI8226 (high CD47), KMS-12-PE (moderate CD47), and NCI-H929 (low CD47) cell lines. After co-culturing MM cells with N6 cells at different E:T ratios in the absence or presence of TriKE-CD47 for 18 h, the cancer cell death was determined. The results represented that N6 cells directly killed MM cell lines in an E:T ratio-dependent manner. The cytotoxicity observed in RPMI8226, and KMS-12-PE showed significantly higher compared to that in NCI-H929 (Fig. 5A-C). At the E:T ratio of 10:1, TriKE-CD47 increased the cell death of RPMI8226 from 24.4 (untreated) to 36.5% (Fig. 5A), KMS-12-PE from 11.3 (untreated) to 52.9% (Fig. 5B), whereas NCI-H929 did not cause significant change (from 10.4 to 12.7%) (Fig. 5C). Notably, TriKE-CD47 treatment alone did not affect the MM cell viability (Supplementary Fig. 6). TriKE-CD47-mediated ADCP activity through the activity of human monocyte-derived macrophages (MDMs) against myeloma cells

Antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis (ADCP) mediated by CD16 relies on the anti-tumor activity of macrophages to eliminate cancer cells. We assessed the effect of TriKE-CD47 on enhancing the phagocytosis of macrophages by co-culturing RPMI8226 and KMS-12-PE with human monocyte-derived macrophages (MDMs) at different E:T ratios in the presence or absence of TriKE-CD47. Discussion

CD47 represents a promising therapeutic target in MM. In this study, we found that MM cells exhibit markedly heterogeneous but frequently elevated the CD47 expression compared with normal CBMC cells (Fig. 1), consistent with previous reports showing that cancer cells express approximately twice as much CD47 as normal peripheral blood B cells [25]. CD47 overexpression has been associated with advanced stages of MM. Previous study has demonstrated that CD47 expression markedly increases during disease progression, including the transition from monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS) to MM [26] [27]. Specifically, myeloma cells derived from patient bone marrow exhibit higher CD47 expression compared with non-myeloma cells, with elevated CD47 levels observed in approximately 73% of MM patient samples relative to normal cell populations [28]. Moreover, patients with high CD47 expression have significantly shorter progression-free and overall survival than those with low expression, indicating a strong association between CD47 upregulation, disease progression, and poor prognosis [10]. In addition to its overexpression in MM cells, the CD47–SIRPα interaction contributes to tumor immune evasion by delivering a “don’t eat me” signal that suppresses macrophage phagocytosis and impairs NK cell responses [11] [29]. Therefore, blocking this pathway represents a promising therapeutic strategy in cancer treatment.

We generated a tri-specific killer engager (TriKE-CD47) designed to target CD47. TriKE-CD47 exerts its anti-tumor activity through three major mechanisms: (1) targeting CD47-expressing MM cells via an anti-CD47 scFv; (2) binding directly to CD16 on NK cells through an anti-CD16 sdAb to stimulate antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC); and (3) enhancing NK cell proliferation through an IL-15 moiety. The ADCC-mediated activity of TriKE-CD47 using N6 cells and expanded human NK cells from healthy donors was assessed (Figs. 5 and 6). The N6, which is V158 variant of FCGR3A CD16-expressing KHY-G1 cell line, was used as a proof-of-concept model to provide a consistent and highly reproducible system for evaluating TriKE-mediated NK cell activation and cytotoxicity. While N6 cells do not fully recapitulate the heterogeneity of primary human NK cells, they are well suited for mechanistic studies focusing on CD16-dependent engagement. Flow cytometric analysis was performed to compare CD16 surface expression between N6 cells and primary human NK cells, demonstrating comparable expression levels between the two models. These findings support the suitability of the N6 cell line as a translational model for studying CD16-mediated responses in primary NK cells (Supplementary Fig. 9). Our results showed that the highest cytotoxicity of N6 cells was observed in RPMI8226 and KMS-12-PE, which express high and medium expression levels of CD47, respectively. In contrast, TriKE-CD47 had no effect on NCI-H929, which exhibits a lower CD47 expression level than RPMI8226 and KMS-12-PE (Fig. 5). To address physiological relevance, we also performed key experiments using expanded primary human NK cells, which demonstrated comparable trends in cytotoxicity to kill RPMI8226 and KMS-12-PE (Fig. 6), supporting the relevance of the findings obtained with the N6 model.

The expression level of antigen on the cell surface is expected as a crucial factor which directly impacts on the activity of antibody and affects the overall efficiency [30]. Likewise, the basal expression of CD47 is a crucial factor in TriKE-CD47 treatment since its potency depends upon CD47 expression levels on the MM cell surface. However, the ADCC assays performed using both N6 and primary NK cells, demonstrated a consistent trend in which KMS-12-PE cells appeared more responsive to TriKE-CD47-mediated cytotoxicity than RPMI8226 cells, despite RPMI8226 exhibiting slightly higher CD47 expression (MFI: 1067 vs. 972, respectively) (Fig. 1). Although RPMI8226 exhibited a lower EC50 value than KMS-12-PE cells (4.314 vs. 5.620), which suggesting greater binding accessibility and more rapid engagement of TriKE-CD47 (Fig. 2), TriKE-CD47 showed greater binding intensity in KMS-12-PE cells compared with RPMI8226 at equivalent concentrations, as reflected by higher MFI values. These findings indicate that TriKE-CD47 binding and functional activity may correlate more closely with the magnitude of TriKE-CD47 accumulation on the cell surface and the stability of protein–protein interactions, rather than with CD47 expression levels alone.

Moreover, intrinsic differences in baseline sensitivity to NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity may also contribute to the observed effects. Tumor cell heterogeneity is known to influence susceptibility to NK cell killing and overall cytotoxic responses. In our experiments, RPMI8226 cells exhibited greater baseline sensitivity to N6-mediated cytotoxicity, as evidenced by E:T ratio-dependent cell death even in the absence of TriKE treatment, compared with KMS-12-PE cells. Consequently, although TriKE-CD47 significantly enhanced cytotoxicity in RPMI8226 cells, the relative magnitude of enhancement appeared less pronounced than in KMS-12-PE cells, particularly at higher E:T ratios. This finding is consistent with the human-expanded NK cells as well (Fig. 6).

Alternatively, CD16-mediated ADCP is another mechanism that can also induce through CD16 expression on macrophages [31] [32]. Therefore, we assessed the effect of TriKE targeting CD47 on ADCP property using MDMs from healthy donors. As shown in Fig. 7A-D, the results indicated that TriKE-CD47 had the potential to promote ADCP against CD47-expressing MM cells. The in vivo studies were conducted using BRJ mice, a severely immunodeficient strain lacking functional B cells, T cells, and NK cells, while retaining mouse peritoneal macrophages, and the complement levels in BRJ mice are low. To specifically assess CD16-mediated cytotoxicity, CD16-expressing N6 cells were adoptively transferred into BRJ mice following multiple myeloma cell engraftment. This experimental design enabled the evaluation of TriKE-CD47 activity in the presence of defined CD16-positive effector cells. Furthermore, we demonstrated that TriKE-CD47 treatment with N6 cells dramatically suppressed tumor growth in the MM xenograft mice model, as shown in Fig. 8. Notably, no overt toxicity was observed within the limitations of this murine model, as evidenced by the absence of body weight loss [33]. To further determine whether mouse macrophage-mediated mechanisms contributed to the in vivo anti-tumor effects, we examined the impact of TriKE-CD47 on thioglycolate-elicited mouse peritoneal macrophages. In contrast to the robust ADCC observed with N6 NK cells, TriKE-CD47 did not induce mouse macrophage-mediated phagocytosis (Supplementary Fig. 10). These findings suggest that antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis (ADCP) is unlikely to play a significant role in the observed in vivo anti-tumor activity in this model. Together, these findings support CD16-mediated NK cell engagement as the dominant mechanism of action in vivo, while highlighting the potential for additional macrophage-mediated effects in human immune settings (Supplementary Fig. 11) [34] [23] [35]. However, further investigation to quantify the proportion of NK cells or other immune cells within tumor tissues would provide important evidence as to whether TriKE-CD47 enhances the recruitment or enrichment of immune cells in the tumor microenvironment. Moreover, the therapeutic performance of CD47 TriKE could be further strengthened by additional in vivo studies incorporating multiple dosing regimens to generate more robust efficacy data, as well as head-to-head comparisons with standard-of-care treatments in appropriate preclinical models.

TriKEs offer several advantages over conventional monoclonal antibodies. Their smaller size allows for increased biodistribution, enabling them to penetrate tumors more effectively, which is especially beneficial in the treatment of solid tumors [18] [36]. In addition, TriKEs are engineered using humanized or fully human components, which are expected to minimize immunogenicity and facilitate rapid modular design against diverse tumor antigens. Due to their reduced molecular size, TriKEs are anticipated to have shorter half-lives than full-length IgG antibodies, resulting in faster systemic clearance that may lower the risk of prolonged off-target exposure but may also necessitate more frequent dosing or continuous infusion strategies [36] [37] [38]. Notably, incorporation of an IL-15 moiety into TriKE constructs has been shown to enhance NK cell survival, proliferation, and functional persistence in vivo, which may partially compensate for shorter systemic exposure and improve pharmacodynamic durability. Nevertheless, optimal dosing regimens and therapeutic windows for TriKE-CD47 will require pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic evaluation in future preclinical and clinical studies [16] [39].

Therapeutic monoclonal antibodies targeting CD47, Magrolimab, have been explored to inhibit cancer cell activity and enhance macrophage-mediated phagocytosis in hematological and solid tumors [9] [40] [41] [42]. However, clinical trials revealed adverse effects, including anemia, hemagglutination, and thrombocytopenia, due to CD47 expression on normal cells, particularly red blood cells (RBCs) [43]. Administration of a priming dose of Magrolimab (1 mg/kg) reduced hemoglobin via macrophage-mediated phagocytosis of aging RBCs, which then stimulated reticulocyte maturation. Interesting, newly synthesized RBCs were resistant to higher doses (30 mg/kg), indicating that adverse effects were age dependent. Accordingly, blood transfusions could mitigate these effects [9] [41]. Unlike conventional bivalent anti-CD47 IgG antibodies, TriKE-CD47 incorporates distinct design features that may mitigate hematologic and normal cell toxicity. The anti-CD47 component of TriKE-CD47 is a monovalent scFv, which limits cross-linking of CD47 on RBCs and reduces RBC binding and hemagglutination [39] [41]. Furthermore, TriKE-CD47 lacks a conventional IgG Fc region, eliminating Fc-mediated mechanisms such as Fcγ receptor bridging and complement activation via C1q, rendering TriKE-CD47 incapable of mediating complement-dependent cytotoxicity (CDC) [44]. Together, these features suggest that TriKE-CD47 is expected to improve the safety profile compared with conventional anti-CD47 antibodies. The additional advantage is that they can drive potent NK cell function and expansion by directly engaging CD16 on NK cells and incorporating IL-15 [45].

Our study demonstrated that TriKE-CD47 treatment did not induce RBC agglutination or enhance NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity against normal T cells, as assessed by hemagglutination and apoptosis assays, indicating an absence of detectable hematologic toxicity (Fig. 3). Nevertheless, although CD47 is constitutively expressed on various normal cell types, our findings indicate that the level of expression is a critical determinant of TriKE-CD47 activity. While PBMCs serve as a representative normal cell population, notably, we acknowledge that CD47 expression may be upregulated under certain inducible or pathological conditions. Therefore, monitoring CD47 expression levels may be important for evaluating potential side effects. Importantly, further investigation is warranted to define the therapeutic index of TriKE-CD47 in MM patients, including establishing CD47 expression thresholds that may predict clinical benefit and optimize patient selection. Moreover, the absence of observed toxicity in our xenograft mouse model likely reflects, at least in part, species-specific limitations. The anti-CD47 and anti-CD16 components of TriKE-CD47 were designed to recognize human antigens and are not expected to cross-react efficiently with murine CD47 or murine CD16. Therefore, the lack of toxicity observed in this model should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of clinical safety. Nevertheless, these findings provide preliminary insight into the potential off-target and direct adverse effects associated with TriKE-CD47 administration. However, several limitations should be considered. While we assessed CD47 expression on T cells and red blood cells (RBCs) and observed no evidence of hematologic toxicity, other CD47-expressing normal cell populations—such as platelets, monocytes, and hematopoietic stem cells—were not evaluated. In addition, the absence of a CD47-negative control limits the ability to definitively attribute the observed effects to CD47-specific targeting. Future studies incorporating CD47-deficient models, such as CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knockout or siRNA knockdown systems, will be instrumental to more comprehensively define target specificity and functional dependence. In addition, to further delineate the safety profile, immunohistochemical analysis utilizing tissue microarrays (TMAs) encompassing a broad panel of normal human tissues will be performed. This high-throughput approach will enable systematic mapping of TriKE-CD47 binding across multiple organ systems and provide a spatial assessment of potential ‘on-target, off-tumor’ interactions within the complex architecture of healthy tissues. Such studies are critical to validate binding specificity and to better understand the safety implications of CD47 targeting. Finally, further investigation is required to determine the optimal clinical dosing strategy for TriKE-CD47 to maximize NK cell-mediated therapeutic efficacy while minimizing potential systemic adverse effects.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we demonstrated that CD47 expression is elevated in a subset of MM cell lines but remains at a low-level expression in normal cells. TriKE-CD47 induced cell death through both ADCC and ADCP activities. Furthermore, TriKE-CD47 potentially inhibited MM tumor growth in vivo. Our study supports preclinical evidence for using TriKE targeting CD47 as a promising effective therapeutic strategy for treating MM patients.
