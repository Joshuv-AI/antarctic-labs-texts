Anaplastic lymphoma kinase–positive primary CNS anaplastic large cell lymphoma in a pediatric patient: illustrative case
Authors: Duygu Dolen Burak, Cafer Ikbal Gulsever, Alperen Poyraz, Gokcen Unverengil, Gulcin Yegen, Gulcan Erbas, Tugrul Cem Unal, Altay Sencer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDAnaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK)–positive primary CNS anaplastic large cell lymphoma (ALCL) is an extremely rare pediatric malignancy. Its radiological appearance often mimics infectious or glial lesions, complicating diagnosis and delaying treatment.OBSERVATIONSThe authors report the case of a 10-year-old immunocompetent female who presented with absence seizures and vomiting. Brain MRI revealed a left frontal lesion with peripheral contrast enhancement and diffusion restriction, suggestive of a cerebral abscess. She was started on antibiotics and underwent resection. Histopathology confirmed ALK-positive primary CNS ALCL. The patient was treated with NHL-BFM (non-Hodgkin lymphoma–Berlin-Frankfurt-Münster) chemotherapy, the ALK inhibitor lorlatinib, and focal cranial radiotherapy (18 Gy/22 fractions). She remains in complete remission at 18 months. A systematic review of 15 pediatric ALK-positive CNS ALCL cases revealed male predominance, supratentorial location, and inconsistent treatment protocols. Methotrexate-based chemotherapy was most common treatment; the role of radiotherapy and surgery varied.LESSONSALK-positive primary CNS ALCL should be considered in the differential diagnosis of pediatric intracranial masses with atypical imaging. Early histological confirmation is essential. Primary CNS lymphoma (PCNSL) is a rare subtype of non-Hodgkin lymphoma that primarily affects the brain, spine, CSF, and eyes. Anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK)–positive anaplastic large cell lymphoma (ALCL) is an exceptionally uncommon variant, with only a few reported cases, mostly in immunosuppressed patients. Because of its rarity, clinical recognition and diagnosis remain challenging.1–3

Symptoms of ALK-positive PCNSL vary depending on tumor location and may include motor deficits, headaches, seizures, sensory disturbances, and behavioral changes. While some laboratory markers in blood and CSF may assist in diagnosis, none are specific. Neuroimaging can provide clues, but given the overlap with other CNS pathologies, a definitive diagnosis requires histopathological confirmation.4,5

We present a case of ALK-positive primary T-cell lymphoma in an immunocompetent pediatric patient that initially mimicked a cerebral abscess. In addition to discussing this case, we provide a literature review of this rare entity. This report highlights the diagnostic difficulties and the need for further investigation into optimal management strategies for ALK-positive PCNSL.

Study Description

Informed consent was obtained from the patient’s legal guardians before including the case details in this study. This study consists of both a case report and a systematic review, following the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines to ensure a structured and transparent research process.

Systematic Literature Review

A systematic literature search was conducted to investigate previously reported cases of ALK-positive primary CNS T-cell lymphoma. The databases searched included PubMed, Scopus, Google Scholar, and Web of Science. The search terms used were “anaplastic lymphoma kinase,” “ALK,” “primary central nervous system lymphoma,” “primary CNS lymphoma,” “primary CNS T-cell lymphoma,” “T-cell lymphoma,” and (“CNS” OR “central nervous system”).

The initial search yielded 81 articles from the PubMed, Scopus, Google Scholar, and Web of Science databases. After removing duplicates (n = 7), non–English-language studies (n = 8), and records excluded for other reasons (n = 5), 61 articles remained for screening. Following title and abstract screening, 35 articles were excluded, including 16 that were not ALK-positive, 9 involving nonpediatric cases, and 10 excluded for other reasons. A total of 26 full-text articles were sought for retrieval, all of which were successfully obtained (0 reports not retrieved). After full-text assessment, 11 studies were excluded due to methodological issues or full-text irrelevance. Ultimately, 15 studies met the inclusion criteria, all of which were case reports.

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA guidelines, ensuring a rigorous and transparent selection process. Illustrative Case

A 10-year-old female patient presented to the pediatric emergency department with sudden-onset absence seizures and vomiting. She had no prior medical history, and her neurological examination was unremarkable, with no signs of meningeal irritation or fever. Vital signs were stable on admission. There was no history of weight loss, night sweats, lymphadenopathy, or other constitutional symptoms suggestive of malignancy. Family history was noncontributory, with no known cancer or immunodeficiency. Laboratory investigations, including complete blood count, C-reactive protein, erythrocyte sedimentation rate, and blood cultures, were all within normal limits. The CBC did not reveal any abnormalities suggestive of either an infectious or oncological process. The absence of systemic signs of infection or laboratory markers of inflammation contributed to diagnostic uncertainty. Contrast-enhanced cranial MRI revealed a 27 × 21 × 22–mm peripheral contrast-enhancing mass with restricted diffusion in the left frontal lobe (Fig. 2). Based on these findings, she was admitted to the neurosurgery department with a preliminary diagnosis of a cerebral abscess. Thoracic and abdominal CT scans showed no abnormalities, and empirical antibiotic therapy with meropenem and vancomycin was initiated. Although the patient was clinically stable, the well-circumscribed lesion with mass effect warranted early surgical intervention. Surgery was performed within 48 hours of admission. While the initial diagnosis favored an abscess, surgical drainage is generally indicated in such cases to reduce intracranial pressure and obtain microbiological confirmation. Given the superficial location of the lesion in the noneloquent left frontal lobe, and the radiological impression of a localized, encapsulated process, the surgical team opted for gross-total resection rather than aspiration or biopsy. This approach served both therapeutic and diagnostic purposes, providing effective decompression and timely acquisition of tissue for histopathological evaluation. Postoperative MRI confirmed gross-total resection (Fig. 2).

Histopathological analysis revealed a hypocellular lymphoproliferative lesion. Immunohistochemistry showed positive expression of ALK, CD30, CD3, and EMA, while CD2, CD5, and CD7 were negative. The Ki-67 proliferation index was 50%, indicating high cellular proliferation. In situ Epstein-Barr virus RNA testing was negative. These findings confirmed the diagnosis of ALK-positive primary CNS T-cell lymphoma, specifically ALK-positive T-cell ALCL (Fig. 3). A whole-body PET scan showed no systemic involvement, including no spinal cord infiltration. Postoperative bone marrow aspiration and CSF cytology were performed to evaluate for possible systemic lymphoma; both were negative for malignant infiltration, supporting the diagnosis of PCNSL. CSF analysis was negative for atypical cells, and immunodeficiency screening was normal. The patient received NHL-BFM (non-Hodgkin lymphoma–Berlin-Frankfurt-Münster) chemotherapy in combination with the ALK inhibitor lorlatinib and radiotherapy (18 Gy/22 fractions). Follow-up cranial and spinal MR images showed no progression (Fig. 4), and she has remained tumor free for 18 months. Discussion

ALK-positive ALCL is an exceptionally rare primary CNS tumor, predominantly affecting males from early childhood to young adulthood. It primarily involves the supratentorial region, with occasional extension to the dura and bone. Because of its rarity and nonspecific clinical presentation, it is often misdiagnosed as a cerebral abscess, leading to the initiation of empirical antibiotic therapy before a definitive diagnosis is established. However, histopathological confirmation remains essential, and treatment primarily consists of chemotherapy and radiotherapy, with resection playing a limited role except for obtaining a diagnosis.6,7

ALK-positive primary CNS ALCL is classified as a miscellaneous lymphoproliferative disease of the CNS. It is considered an aggressive malignancy, with both primary and systemic ALK-positive ALCL carrying a high risk of recurrence. However, ALK-positive ALCL generally has a better prognosis than its ALK-negative counterpart, although the overall survival rates remain poor. Prognostic evaluation relies on two established scoring systems. The International Extranodal Lymphoma Study Group score evaluates age, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group Performance Status, lactate dehydrogenase levels, CSF protein concentration, and deep brain involvement to predict the 2-year survival rate. The Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center score considers only age and Karnofsky Performance Status to predicting the median overall survival.2,8 Despite advances in treatment, resection is generally not advised due to a lack of supporting evidence. The Bonn protocol, a first-line treatment approach, has demonstrated comparable efficacy to more invasive regimens.7

In our case, the tumor was located in the anterior frontal region, a relatively favorable location, without evidence of bone or dural involvement. Given the MRI findings suggestive of a cerebral abscess, the patient initially received empirical antibiotic therapy. However, gross-total resection was ultimately performed, aiding in both symptom relief and histopathological confirmation of ALK-positive primary CNS ALCL. Since rare CNS lymphomas are frequently associated with underlying immunodeficiency, testing was conducted, but no abnormalities were detected. Following chemotherapy and radiotherapy, the patient has remained tumor free for 18 months, reinforcing the potential benefit of a multimodal treatment approach.

While our systematic review focused exclusively on ALK-positive primary CNS ALCL, it is important to acknowledge broader cohort studies that have addressed CNS involvement in pediatric ALCL regardless of ALK status. Notably, Attarbaschi et al.9 reported 17 CNS ALCL cases among 75 children with PCNSL. However, ALK expression was not specified, and therefore the study could not be included within our review parameters, which were restricted to molecularly confirmed ALK-positive cases. Nonetheless, this work contributes to the broader understanding of disease burden and highlights the heterogeneity of CNS ALCL presentation and prognosis.9 Our case complements these findings by offering detailed clinical, imaging, and histopathological documentation of a confirmed ALK-positive CNS ALCL case with sustained remission following multimodal therapy.

In recent years, the use of high-dose chemotherapy (HDC) followed by autologous stem cell rescue (ASCR) has been explored as a radiation-sparing approach in pediatric CNS lymphomas, particularly to avoid neurocognitive and endocrinological sequelae associated with cranial radiotherapy. Although no standardized guidelines exist for ALK-positive CNS ALCL due to its rarity, several centers have reported treating young patients successfully with chemotherapy-only regimens, reserving radiotherapy for refractory or residual disease.10,11 In our case, radiotherapy was incorporated as consolidation therapy following complete remission due to the initial radiological impression of a high-grade CNS mass, the need for early disease control, and multidisciplinary consensus at the time of diagnosis. However, we acknowledge that non–radiation-based strategies, including HDC/ASCR, merit further investigation in prospective studies to assess long-term neurotoxicity and relapse rates in this subset of patients.12

There remains no established consensus on the optimal treatment strategy for ALK-positive primary CNS T-cell lymphoma. However, an overwhelming majority of reports in the literature favor chemotherapy and radiotherapy, as lymphoma is highly responsive to these modalities. A male predominance has been observed in reported cases. The tumor is predominantly supratentorial, occurring in 93% of cases, with headache being the most common presenting symptom, reported in 50% of patients. Additional symptoms are largely dependent on tumor location and edema-induced mass effect. The leading causes of mortality include sepsis, chemotherapy-related toxicity, and radiotherapy complications. All authors emphasize the critical importance of early diagnosis and timely treatment initiation to improve overall survival and clinical outcomes.10,12–24

Despite the growing number of reported cases, ALK-positive primary CNS ALCL remains poorly understood, and treatment approaches remain inconsistent. The rarity of this lymphoma poses challenges in conducting large-scale clinical trials, making retrospective analyses and case series the primary sources of available data. Future studies should focus on optimizing treatment regimens, evaluating novel targeted therapies, and improving prognostic stratification to enhance patient outcomes. Given the high risk of recurrence, long-term follow-up is essential to monitor disease progression and treatment efficacy. Our case adds to the literature by highlighting a presentation that was radiologically indistinguishable from a cerebral abscess in an immunocompetent child with no systemic symptoms or laboratory abnormalities suggestive of infection or malignancy. The diagnostic confirmation through gross-total resection, use of lorlatinib as part of a successful multimodal therapy, and 18-month remission outcome, in combination with a systematic review of the existing literature, underscore the unique clinical and educational value of this report.

In conclusion, ALK-positive primary CNS ALCL is an exceptionally rare and aggressive CNS malignancy. While chemotherapy and radiotherapy remain the cornerstone of treatment, resection is essential for obtaining a definitive pathological diagnosis and may aid in disease management, although it is not considered curative. Because of the limited number of reported cases, the optimal treatment strategy remains uncertain, and further research is needed to determine the most effective therapeutic approach. This case underscores the importance of a multidisciplinary approach in diagnosing and managing PCNSL. Additional case reports and larger studies are crucial to establishing a standardized treatment consensus and improving long-term clinical outcomes.

Observations

This case highlights how ALK-positive primary CNS ALCL may initially mimic common neurosurgical pathologies such as cerebral abscesses, especially in immunocompetent children without prior malignancy. The lesion’s radiological features—peripheral contrast enhancement and diffusion restriction—led to an initial misdiagnosis, underscoring the overlap between malignant and infectious CNS lesions. Gross-total resection enabled early histopathological diagnosis, which was pivotal in guiding therapy. Despite the lack of systemic disease, the case required a multidisciplinary treatment plan including chemotherapy, targeted ALK inhibition with lorlatinib, and focal cranial radiotherapy. Sustained remission was achieved, supporting the feasibility of multimodal therapy in these patients.

Lessons

Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for rare CNS lymphomas when confronted with atypical or nonresponsive intracranial lesions, even in the absence of systemic involvement. Early neurosurgical intervention, although not curative, may be diagnostic and provide timely tissue for immunophenotypic and molecular analysis. The integration of ALK inhibitors may offer an additional therapeutic benefit, although further research is required. For young patients, radiation-sparing strategies, including HDC/ASCR, should be considered when appropriate, to minimize long-term neurocognitive effects. Given the rarity and variability in presentation and outcomes, continued reporting of such cases is essential to improve clinical recognition and establish standardized management protocols.
