Direct Determination of Apparent Particle Density and Porosity in Silica Nanoparticles by Size-Resolved Mass–Volume Analysis
Authors: Soheyl Tadjiki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Porous silica nanoparticles are widely used in catalysis, separations, and nanomaterial applications, yet their internal structure is typically inferred from ensemble measurements that assume structural uniformity across particle populations. Here, we determine the apparent particle density and porosity of silica nanoparticles on a fraction-resolved basis by independently measuring nanoparticle mass and geometric volume. Particle effective mass is obtained from centrifugal field-flow fractionation (CFFF) retention behavior, while particle volume is determined from transmission electron microscopy measurements of narrowly fractionated particle populations. The analysis was applied to silica nanoparticles spanning 100–700 nm, including mesoporous particles, nominally nonporous nanoparticles, and NIST-traceable size standards. Apparent densities range from approximately 1210 to 2150 kg m–3, corresponding to porosities from near-dense silica to highly void-rich frameworks. Nominally monodisperse 160 and 200 nm samples resolve into distinct buoyant-mass-defined subpopulations that are internally monodisperse yet apparently structurally distinct. These results demonstrate that external particle diameter does not uniquely define internal architecture and that significant structural heterogeneity can exist within nominally monodisperse silica nanoparticle populations. The mass–volume approach provides a direct method for determining the apparent particle density and porosity of nanoparticles without relying on ensemble assumptions and enables fraction-resolved characterization of porous nanomaterials.

Introduction

Porous silica nanoparticles are widely used as catalyst supports, separation media, adsorbents, dielectric materials, and drug delivery carriers. In each of these applications, performance depends on more than particle diameter. Instead, it is governed by the internal structure. Pore volume, wall thickness, connectivity, and framework density affect diffusion rates, mechanical stability, loading capacity, and interfacial reactivity. The relevant material parameter is not size alone but the distribution of solid and void within each particle.

In practice, nanoscale porosity is commonly assessed by using bulk characterization techniques. Nitrogen adsorption, for example, provides specific surface area and total pore volume averaged over the entire sample, but it requires relatively large quantities of material and assumes structural uniformity across the particle population. As a result, these measurements do not reveal whether porosity varies systematically with particle size nor do they establish the true mass associated with an individual particle of a given external diameter. As synthesis strategies generate hierarchical pore networks, partially condensed frameworks, hollow interiors, or radial density gradients, ensemble characterization becomes increasingly inadequate for describing the structure of individual particles.

From a materials standpoint, density is a direct structural descriptor. For silica, deviations from the bulk density of fully condensed amorphous SiO2 reflect incomplete condensation and internal voids. Two particles with identical geometric diameters may differ in apparent particle density. These differences depend on precursor chemistry, hydrolysis–condensation kinetics, templating efficiency, or postsynthetic treatment. External size alone does not uniquely define internal architecture.

Quantitative determination of nanoscale porosity requires an independent measurement of particle mass and geometric volume. Electron microscopy directly measures particle size and morphology with nanometer resolution. This allows reliable determination of the geometric volume for narrowly distributed fractions. Determining the nanoparticle mass is much more challenging. In centrifugal field-flow fractionation (CFFF), particles separate under a controlled centrifugal field. Their retention behavior gives an effective (buoyant) mass. Collecting narrow fractions and analyzing them by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) enable independent measurement of particle mass and volume. Apparent density follows as the ratio of measured mass to measured volume. This approach does not assume that bulk equivalence.

This framework has been used for engineered silver nanoparticles, where measured densities were much lower than those of bulk silver due to synthesis-induced porosity, highlighting the method’s structural sensitivity.

Because fractionation precedes density calculation, the density can be determined as a function of particle size. Any reduction in the solid fraction due to incomplete framework condensation, internal voids, or hierarchical pore development is directly reflected in the measured density. Density serves as an integrated structural parameter. It links the synthesis to the internal architecture.

In this work, we apply a mass–volume approach to porous silica nanoparticles to directly determine apparent particle density and porosity. Particle effective mass is obtained from CFFF retention data, while geometric volume is determined from TEM size measurements of narrowly fractionated particle populations. Apparent particle density is calculated from the ratio of mass to volume, and porosity is determined from the deviation of this density from that of fully condensed silica. This size-resolved analysis characterizes the internal structure of porous silica nanoparticles without relying on ensemble averaging and links framework density to synthesis conditions. To examine how internal structure varies across different classes of silica nanoparticles, we analyzed mesoporous particles, nominally nonporous nanoparticles, and NIST-traceable size standards spanning diameters from 100 to 700 nm.

Experimental Section

Chemicals

Sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) and sodium azide were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA. Deionized water was obtained from a Synergy water purification system, EMD Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA, and had a resistivity of 18 MΩ·cm. The CFFF carrier solutions were made by adding 0.5 g of SDS and 0.2 g of sodium azide to 1 L of deionized water. The solutions were filtered through a 0.2 μm filter prior to use. The pH of the solution was measured at 7.5.

Silica Samples

Six commercially available silica nanoparticle samples were examined to represent materials spanning nominally dense and mesoporous architectures. The 100 and 160 nm samples were obtained from nanoComposix (San Diego, CA, USA) and supplied as nonporous silica nanoparticles. These materials were included as reference samples to establish the upper bound of the apparent particle density for nominally dense silica frameworks. The 200 S and 500 S samples were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA) and are mesoporous silica particles synthesized via templated routes. The 500 D and 700 D samples were obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA) and are NIST-traceable silica particle size standards. These materials are certified with respect to geometric diameter and were included as size-defined reference materials across larger particle dimensions.

CFFF Separation of Silica Samples

The CFFF instrument used in this study was manufactured by Postnova Analytics GmbH, Landsberg am Lech, Germany. Channel dimensions were as follows: tip-to-tip channel length, L = 90.1 cm; channel breadth, b = 2 cm; channel thickness, w = 0.0131 ± 0.0005 cm; channel volume, V 0 = 2.31 ± 0.09 mL; and channel radius, r = 15.1 cm. A constant field in the range of 3.8–149 g was used to separate the silica samples. The channel flow rate was in the range of 1–2 mL/min. The channel flow rate was measured during the course of the separation for every 16 s using a flow meter (Olsen Advanced Electronics, Salt Lake City, Utah). The flow meter was connected to a balance (Cole-Parmer, USA) to measure the carrier solution weight as it was collected from the detector waste line. The rate of weight change per time was then converted to volumetric flow rate by assuming a density of 997 kg m–3 for the carrier solution. A mean value of the measurements was acquired and used as the average channel flow rate in the calculation of effective mass. The CFFF system was connected to a UV–vis detector (PN3211, Postnova Analytics GmbH, Landsberg am Lech, Germany) for the detection of the silica samples. The detector was set to a wavelength of 254 nm.

The retention time for each collected fraction was determined from the midpoint of the selected collection time slice. Collection windows were chosen near the maximum of each elution peak to minimize overlap between adjacent populations and to obtain narrowly distributed fractions for subsequent TEM analysis. For samples exhibiting bimodal elution behavior, separate fractions were collected independently from the early- and late-eluting peak regions. Effective buoyant mass was calculated from the measured retention parameter using the Brownian-mode CFFF relationship described in the Theory section.

Effective-mass measurements were performed in triplicate for each sample. Uncertainties in the effective mass were determined from the variation in retention time corresponding to the peak maximum across the three replicate separations.

The calculated particle masses and densities are also subject to potential systematic uncertainties associated with the CFFF separation processes. Factors such as incomplete particle relaxation, wall interactions, aggregation during separation, sample recovery losses, and deviations from ideal Brownian mode retention behavior could influence the measured retention times and therefore the calculated buoyant masses. Although no evidence of such effects was observed in the present study, their potential contribution cannot be completely excluded and should be considered when interpreting the reported density and porosity values.

TEM Size Analysis of Collected Fractions

A 5 μL aliquot of the fractions collected from the CFFF experiment was dropped on a 200-mesh carbon-coated copper grid (Electron Microscopy Sciences, Hatfield, PA) and air-dried at room temperature. The procedure was repeated three times to ensure sufficient particle surface coverage for imaging. The grids were imaged using an FEI Tecnai T12 transmission electron microscope (FEI, Hillsboro, OR, USA) operated at an accelerating voltage of 120 kV.

TEM images were analyzed using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). For each collected fraction, more than 100 individual particles were analyzed to determine the particle diameter distribution. Only isolated particles without visible overlap or aggregation were selected for analysis. Particle projected area was measured from the TEM micrographs assuming the projection of a circular disc. Particle diameter was then calculated from A=π4d2 where A is the measured projected area and d is the calculated particle diameter. Particle volume was subsequently calculated assuming spherical geometry. Uncertainty in particle diameter corresponds to the standard error of the mean obtained from the analysis of more than 100 particles per fraction.

Uncertainty in particle volume was propagated from the uncertainty in the mean TEM diameter using the spherical volume relationship. The uncertainty in apparent particle density was then calculated by propagating the uncertainties in particle mass and volume. Particle volume was calculated from the mean TEM diameter of each collected fraction, assuming spherical geometry. Because particle volume scales with the cube of particle diameter, the calculation of volume from the mean diameter rather than from the full diameter distribution may introduce bias when particle size distributions are broad. However, the fractions analyzed in this study were selected from narrow CFFF fractions and exhibited relatively narrow TEM size distributions, thereby minimizing this effect. Nevertheless, the reported densities and porosities should be interpreted within an approximation.

Discussion

The mass–volume analysis reveals substantial variation in apparent density across the examined silica nanoparticles, spanning approximately 1210–2150 kg m–3. These values correspond to porosities ranging from near-dense silica (φ ≈ 0.04) to highly void-rich frameworks (φ > 0.8), demonstrating that nominal particle diameter alone does not define internal structure. Even among commercially available materials with specified size distributions, significant variation in apparent particle density and solid fraction is observed.

The present approach measures porosity indirectly through the reduction in apparent particle density relative to fully condensed silica. As a result, the calculated porosity should be interpreted as an apparent internal void fraction rather than as a direct measure of accessible pore volume. The method does not distinguish between open pores accessible to the carrier liquid, closed internal pores, framework hydration, trapped solvent, or other structural features that reduce apparent density. Consequently, the reported porosity values represent an integrated measure of density reduction arising from all internal low-density regions within the particle. Independent validation by gas adsorption or pycnometric measurements was not performed. Such measurements generally require substantially larger sample quantities than are available from the narrowly fractionated populations collected by CFFF and therefore cannot be readily applied on a fraction-resolved basis. The reported porosity values should therefore be interpreted within the assumptions of the present CFFF–TEM mass–volume model.

For silica, the bulk density of fully condensed amorphous SiO2 represents the upper limit of apparent particle density. Deviations from this value reflect incomplete siloxane network formation and/or internal void space. The 100 nm particles supplied as nominally nonporous silica exhibit densities approaching 2150 kg m–3, corresponding to low but measurable porosity. This indicates that even materials described as nonporous may contain residual internal void fractions or incomplete condensation at the nanoscale.

The NIST-traceable 500 D and 700 D size standards exhibit apparent densities of approximately 1540 and 1295 kg m–3, respectively. These materials are certified with respect to geometric diameter but not apparent particle density. The measured values are consistent with a substantial internal void fraction at larger particle sizes, underscoring that size certification does not imply structural homogeneity or full framework condensation. The pronounced reduction in density with increasing size among these standards suggests that internal structure evolves independently of geometric scaling.

The relatively low apparent densities inferred for the NIST-traceable size standards should be interpreted with appropriate caution. These materials are certified with respect to particle size rather than density or porosity, and independent validation of the apparent porosity values was not performed. Consequently, the reported values should be viewed as estimates derived from the assumptions of the present CFFF–TEM mass–volume model rather than as independently confirmed measures of pore volume.

The mesoporous 200 S and 500 S materials exhibit lower densities overall, consistent with templated pore formation and a reduced solid fraction. However, the most instructive behavior is observed in the 160 and 200 S samples, each of which resolves into two buoyant-mass-defined subpopulations. CFFF separation combined with TEM confirmation is consistent with the presence of internally monodisperse, yet structurally distinct fractions. For 200 S, the early-eluting fraction (200 S1) exhibits a lower density and higher porosity than the late-eluting fraction (200 S2). In contrast, the 160 nm material shows the opposite trend: the larger fraction (160–2) is less dense and more porous than the smaller fraction (160–1).

This inversion demonstrates that the density does not scale monotonically with particle size. In one material, larger particles correspond to a greater void fraction; in the other, larger particles correspond to a higher solid fraction. The relationship between size and density therefore depends on the specific synthesis pathway rather than on geometric scaling alone.

From a formation perspective, such a behavior is consistent with differences in condensation kinetics and templating evolution. Particle growth may proceed either through continued framework densification or through templated expansion that preserves or amplifies the pore volume. Variations in micelle organization, precursor concentration, nucleation timing, aging conditions, or postsynthetic treatment can generate discrete structural populations within nominally monodisperse materials.

Although the observed bimodality is consistent with structurally distinct particle populations, alternative explanations cannot be completely excluded. Factors such as particle aggregation, differences in surface chemistry, residual synthesis components, nonideal fractionation behavior, or channel-related effects could also contribute to differences in retention behavior. Additional orthogonal characterization is required to unambiguously establish the origin of the observed subpopulations.

Importantly, these structural differences would not be apparent from the diameter measurements alone. Particles of comparable geometric sizes can possess substantially different internal solid fractions. By independently resolving buoyant mass and geometric volume, the present approach quantifies framework density on a size-resolved basis and reveals structural heterogeneity that would otherwise remain obscured by an ensemble characterization.

Taken together, the results establish apparent density as a quantitative descriptor of framework condensation and void fraction in porous silica nanoparticles. The wide porosity range observed confirms that these materials span a broad structural spectrum, from near-dense silica to highly porous frameworks. The CFFF–TEM mass–volume approach therefore enables direct linkage between synthesis conditions and internal architecture at the fraction-resolved level.

An independent comparison with adsorption or pycnometric measurements was beyond the scope of the present study and will be addressed in future work.

Conclusions

Size-resolved mass–volume analysis reveals substantial variation in apparent particle density and porosity across commercially available silica nanoparticles, including nominally nonporous materials and NIST-traceable size standards. Measured densities span a broad range, corresponding to frameworks that vary from near-dense silica to highly void-rich architectures. The presence of discrete buoyant-mass-defined subpopulations within the 160 and 200 S samples demonstrates that particles of comparable external diameter can possess distinct internal solid fractions. Moreover, the opposite size–density trends observed within these materials indicate that framework condensation and particle growth are not universally coupled and evolve in a synthesis-dependent manner.

These findings establish apparent density as a quantitative, size-resolved descriptor of framework condensation in porous silica nanoparticles. By independently determining particle mass and geometric volume, the CFFF–TEM approach enables direct assessment of internal architecture without reliance on ensemble averaging or bulk assumptions. The reported porosity values represent apparent porosity within the assumptions of the CFFF–TEM mass–volume model. This methodology provides a general framework for fraction-resolved structural characterization of porous nanomaterials.
