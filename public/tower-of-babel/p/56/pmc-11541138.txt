The Optimal Variation Range of Posterior Condylar Offset Associated with Positive Clinical Outcomes of Primary Total Knee Arthroplasty: A Retrospective Analysis
Authors: Yanlin Zhong, Zengfa Deng, Cheng Gu, Ming Li, Yan Kang, Weiming Liao, Zhiqi Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ObjectivePosterior condylar offset (PCO) and anterior condylar offset (ACO) exert an influence on the sagittal alignment in total knee arthroplasty (TKA). However, there is no common consensus that the variation range of posterior condylar offset (PCO) is associated with patient‐reported outcome measures (PROMs) and the optimum variation range of PCO. This study aims to investigate the correlation between PCO and the PROMs of primary TKA for osteoarthritis (OA) and find out the optimal variation range of the PCO.MethodsIn this study, we performed a radiographic analysis of 106 patients (112 knees) with primary TKA. Patients were divided into two cohorts (A and B) according to the Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis index (WOMAC). Correlations between the sagittal parameter and WOMAC were investigated using univariate and multivariate analysis. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was used to establish the cut‐off value for the optimal variation range. We then further investigated how different variation range affects the WOMAC subscale score and forgotten‐joint‐score‐12 (FJS‐12).ResultsUnivariate analysis revealed a correlation between the variation range of PCO (p < 0.01), ACO (p < 0.01) and PROMs. Multivariate analysis showed that only PCO was associated with PROMs. In the ROC graph, the cut‐off value of the variation range of PCO is 2.85 mm (AUC = 0.66, Youden index = 0.26). The WOMAC functional ability score of the group outside the PCO variation range of 2.85 mm significantly increased compared to the group within the range.ConclusionIn this study, PCO variation was significantly associated with clinical outcomes in TKA and the optimal PCO variation range was within 2.85 mm. Maintaining the PCO variation within 2.85 mm could enhance functional recovery and patient satisfaction.

Introduction

Total knee arthroplasty (TKA) is an effective treatment for most end‐stage knee osteoarthritis cases in patients with severe multicompartmental osteoarthritis and severe deformity. 1 It can significantly relieve joint pain, correct joint deformities, and improve joint function. Restoration of limb alignment is critical for ensuring functional ability and long‐term effects. 2 Compared with coronal and rotational alignment in TKA alignment research, 3 sagittal alignment has received less attention. However, sagittal alignment plays a crucial role in the extension and flexion of the knee and patient‐report outcome measures (PROMs) after TKA. 4 , 5 The majority of studies focus on anterior condylar offset (ACO) and posterior condylar offset (PCO). Both of them are key to function and ability after TKA. 5 , 6 Excessive reduction in ACO will lead to notching, while excessive increase will lead to overstuffing. A previous study found that overstuffing and notching of the patellofemoral joint after TKA could have a negative impact on knee flexion and that patients could be bothered by knee pain. 7 , 8 As far as PCO is concerned, it plays an essential role in motion range and joint stability after TKA. 9 , 10 The result of computational simulation revealed that the force on PCL, patellofemoral joint and quadriceps muscles will increase with the change of PCO in cruciate‐retaining (CR) TKA, 11 which could lead to knee pain and restricted knee movement. Han and Kang 12 reported that a larger of PCO correlated with a lower flexion angle if the tibial slope angle was more than 3°. In contrast, PCO was positively correlated with knee movement, if the tibial slope angle ≤3°. On the other hand, femoral sagittal bowing exerts a great influence on the entry point of the intramedullary rod and the location of femoral prosthesis. It is one of risk factors for periprosthetic fracture after TKA due to the malposition of the intramedullary rod. 13 However, there is still controversy about the association between femoral sagittal parameters and clinical outcomes. Additionally, the optimal variation of PCO is still under debate. The primary aim of this study was to evaluate the association between clinical outcome and femoral sagittal parameters. The secondary aim was to find out the optimal variation of PCO.

Methods

Study Design

The study was approved by the ethics committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat‐sen University (No. 2021‐571). The data used in this study was obtained from the Joint Surgery Center database of the hospital. The sample inclusion criteria include a diagnosis of end‐stage KOA of the knee joints (Kellgren–Lawrence classification grade IV), underwent primary TKA and no combined spine or hip disease. The exclusion criteria are as follows: traumatic, gouty, or suppurative arthritis; history of hip, spine, or ankle surgery; revision knee arthroplasty; prior fractures; neuromuscular conditions; or other reasons such as loss to follow‐up. Total 106 patients (112 knees) were evaluated in our study and a minimum follow‐up of 1 year (Figure 1).

Flow chart for enrolled patients.

Patient‐reported outcome measures (PROMs), including the Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis index (WOMAC) and forgotten‐joint‐score 12 (FJS‐12) were evaluated before the surgery and 1 year after. Radiography was performed before and after the surgery.

Surgimap software (Nemaris Inc., New York, NY, USA) was used to measure the following parameters: PCO, ACO and anterior bowing of the femur before and after surgery (Figure 1). PCO is measured between the posterior aspect of the femoral condyle and a projection of the posterior femoral condyle (Figure 2). ACO was the distance between the front edge of the femur cortex and the front aspect of the anterior femoral condyle (Figure 2). The femur bowing angle was defined as the angle between the line 5 and 10 cm from the distal end of the femur on the anterior femoral cortex and the line 10 and 15 cm from the proximal end of the femoral head. 14 (Figure 2).

Measurement of posterior condylar offset, anterior condylar offset and Femur bowing angle.

According to the WOMAC score, patients were divided into two groups (Group A, WOMAC Score: <15 points; Group B, WOMAC Score: ≥15 points). Variables were selected based on the previous study and clinical experience. 15 , 16 , 17 Risk factors or comorbidities associated with TKA were identified, and a univariate and multivariate analysis of all parameters were conducted to measure the difference between the two cohorts. Also, the optimal variation of PCO were analyzed.

Statistical Analysis

SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Co., Armonk, NY, USA) was applied to analyze the data. Univariate analysis was performed to assess the differences in related variables between the group with group A and the group B (the t test was used for continuous variables, whereas the χ 2 test was used for categorical variables). Variables with a p value <0.05 in univariate analysis were included in the multivariate analysis and the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves was applied to find out the cut‐off value. The t‐test was used to compare the within and outside cut‐off value on the WOMAC scores for pain, stiffness, difficulty in functional ability, and the FJS‐12 score. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Result

Univariate and Multivariate Analysis

The baseline information, including demographic and clinical data, and radiographic data including ACO (preoperative and postoperative), PCO (preoperative and postoperative) and the general and variation of ACO and PCO, are clearly listed in Table 1. Our univariate analysis showed the significant difference in flexion contracture (5.69° ± 7.16° vs 2.70° ± 4.65°, p < 0.01), ACO variation (0.90 ± 0.59 mm vs 1.53 ± 1.62 mm, p < 0.01), PCO variation (1.89 ± 1.55 mm vs 3.42 ± 3.16 mm, p < 0.01). Multivariate analysis showed PCO variation was correlated with WOMAC scores (p < 0.01) (Table 2).

Identifying the Optimal Variation Range of PCO

The optimal variation range of PCO was assessed in the study. In the ROC curve, the maximum Youden index was 0.26, and the cut‐off value was 2.85 mm (Figure 3). Combined with the results of univariate and multivariate analysis, we concluded that the optimal variation range of PCO was 2.85 mm. It is shown that when PCO variation was within 2.85 mm, the WOMAC score tended to be favorable (<15 points).

Receiver operating characteristic curve for the change of posterior condylar offset predicting Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis index score after total knee arthroplasty.

Comparison of WOMAC Subscale Score and FJS‐12 at Cut‐off Value

To investigate the difference in clinical outcome for changes in PCO of within 2.85 mm and beyond 2.85 mm, we compared the WOMAC subscale scores and the FJS‐12 score. The WOMAC functional ability score of the group outside the PCO variation range of 2.85 mm significantly increased compared to the group within the range. (p < 0.01) (Table 3).

Discussion

Main Findings of the Study

Our current results indicated that appropriate PCO variation is associated with positive clinical outcomes but failed to show the association between ACO, femur bowing and clinical outcomes. We also established the optimal variation range is within 2.85 mm.

Role of PCO and the Significance on Clinical Outcome in TKA

Although the variation range of PCO was observed for a long time, there has recently been a specific interest in determining their function and PROMs in TKA. A 3‐year retrospective study of 970 TKAs showed that there is no significant difference between PROMs and PCO variation. 18 Similarly, Geijsen et al., 19 failed to identify an association between PCO and outcome measures on the outcome of 116 patients. However, the results of Clement et al.'s study determined the association between PCO and postoperative PROMs, 6 which is similar to our study result. What is more, our study established the optimum variation: 2.85 mm. A retrospective study based on 38 patients revealed that 2 mm of the variation range of PCO is considered optimal, 10 but the sample amount is limited. Although Kang et al.'s 11 biomechanical study supports the opinion, clinical data and PROMs were lacking in the study. In addition, Khow et al., 20 also considered tibia. They demonstrated the range of PCO should be within 1.5 mm if the posterior tibial slope (PTS) was within 2°–8°. Moreover, it is preferable to reduce PCO by 1.0 mm when PTS is under 2° and restore PCO at 0 mm when PTS is beyond 8°. Previous studies revealed that maintaining the PCO is crucial for ROM after TKA, 21 but the study did not support the opinion. 19 , 22 It is known that there is some margin in the measurement of ROM, which fails to reflect the function of knee after TKA. In this study, we discovered that beyond the variation range would affect the WOMAC function score, although ROM improved to varying degrees compared to the preoperative.

The potential impact of ACO and anterior bowing of femur in TKA

The correlation between ACO, anterior bowing of femur and PROMs was not proven with our results. Thus, the optimal range of variation of ACO was not found. The study by Matz et al., 23 also shows that anterior femoral offset was not significantly associated with WOMAC scores (p = 0.681) or subscale scores, regardless of whether ACO increased, maintained or decreased. Furthermore, although the prevalence of notching is only 1.1% and none of the patients among 200 patients with notching suffered periprosthetic fracture during the 2‐year follow‐up, 24 , 25 ACO variation and femoral bowing increase the risk of notching. 13

Limitations

First, it was a short‐term retrospective study and the sample was insufficient. Further study is necessary to verify with a larger sample size. Second, the measurements were based on two‐dimensional radiographs, which ignores cartilage thickness, CT and MRI examinations may be more accurate.

Conclusion

In this study, PCO variation was significantly associated with clinical outcomes in TKA and the optimal PCO variation range was within 2.85 mm. Maintaining the PCO variation within 2.85 mm could enhance functional recovery and patient satisfaction.
