Insufficient erythrocyte-derived S1P: A pathogenic driver and diagnostic biolipid for tumor progression
Authors: Zhenjiang Li, Weilun Huang, Zhouzhou Yao, Juncheng Wang, Wenhao Xiao, Qi Guo, Changhan Chen, Tingting Xie, Zhiyu Yang, Qingyang Liu, Rodney E Kellems, Mengzhi Wu, Yang Xia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryErythrocytes are the sole oxygen (O2) transporters and highly sensitive hypoxia responders, yet their active role in tumor development remains unclear. Here, we identify a compensatory erythrocyte adaptation to hypoxia that enhances O2 release in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC). Untargeted metabolomics revealed a 13-metabolite erythrocyte signature with diagnostic potential, with sphingosine emerging as a central metabolic node altered across erythrocytes, plasma, and tumors. Erythrocytes from patients with HNSCC exhibited metabolic reprogramming characterized by increased sphingosine and sphingosine kinase 1 (SPHK1)-dependent sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P) production, together with loss of the S1P transporter major facilitator superfamily domain-containing protein 2B (MFSD2B), resulting in systemic sphingolipid imbalance. Genetic ablation of erythrocyte-specific SPHK1 disrupted O2 and S1P release, impaired angiogenesis, and promoted an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, thereby accelerating tumor growth. These findings establish erythrocytes as active metabolic regulators of tumor progression via SPHK1/S1P signaling.Subject areas: Immunology, Cancer

Introduction

Cancer is the second leading cause of death worldwide, with incidence and mortality rates continuing to rise, posing a serious threat to global public health.1 In 2020, there were approximately 19.3 million new cancer cases and 10 million cancer-related deaths worldwide.2 These numbers underscore the urgent need to address cancer as a critical public health issue. Due to a lack of early diagnostic biomarkers, high recurrence rates, metastasis, and therapy resistance, head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) ranks as the seventh most common cancer worldwide, and its incidence continues to rise in recent years, with a five-year survival rate below 50% for advanced-stage disease.2 The primary treatment options for HNSCC involve a combination of surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy,3 while emerging cancer immunotherapy has also become an important approach for treating HNSCC.4,5,6 However, due to its insidious onset and nonspecific early symptoms, HNSCC is often diagnosed at locally advanced stages.7 In addition, the high rate of local recurrence at the primary site and frequent lymph node metastasis further contribute to the poor prognosis of patients with HNSCC.7,8 Currently, no effective early screening strategies for HNSCC have been established,8 making the identification of novel diagnostic and therapeutic biomarkers crucial for improving patient outcomes.

A critical feature of solid tumors, including HNSCC, is the development of hypoxia within the tumor microenvironment (TME), driven by oncogenic drivers and cancer hallmark features.9 Hypoxic conditions reprogram cancer cell metabolism through key regulators such as hypoxia-inducible factors (HIFs),10 promoting malignant behaviors such as proliferation, migration, invasion, and angiogenesis, and contributing to therapeutic resistance.9,11 Tumor cells have a unique ability to survive under hypoxic stress by altering their metabolic activity, notably through the Warburg effect,12 which facilitates rapid cell proliferation and survival. This metabolic reprogramming, including changes in lipid and amino acid metabolism, has become a hallmark of cancer.12,13

Tumor cells are more adept at adapting to hypoxic conditions than other cellular components within the tumor microenvironment.13In addition to affecting tumor cells, hypoxia impairs immune effector cell function and disrupts immune homeostasis, making it a significant barrier to effective cancer treatment.11,14 Current strategies targeting tumor hypoxia mainly focus on enhancing oxygen delivery, improving tumor blood flow, generating oxygen in situ, and reducing oxygen consumption by cancer cells.9,11,15This makes hypoxia a major challenge and a promising therapeutic target in the optimization of current cancer treatment strategies.11

Erythrocytes, the sole cellular orchestrators of systemic O2 delivery, are exquisitely sensitive to hypoxia and serve as master regulators of tissue oxygenation and metabolic adaptation.16 Unlike other cell types, erythrocytes employ a unique HIF-independent hypoxia-sensing mechanism—driven by glucose metabolic reprogramming—to dynamically modulate O2 release and combat tissue hypoxia.17,18 Despite their vital role in oxygen delivery, erythrocytes’ metabolic plasticity in response to tumors and their involvement in cancer progression remain underexplored.19,20,21,22

Here, we bridge this critical knowledge gap through an integrative human-murine discovery platform, combining precision RBC functional profiling, deep multi-compartment metabolomics (RBCs, plasma, tumor, and peritumoral tissues) in a well-characterized HNSCC cohort, and genetically engineered erythrocyte-specific mouse models across two distinct cancer types. Our findings redefine the tumor-induced erythrocyte hypoxic and metabolic-response axis that operates independently of classical HIF signaling to promote O2 delivery, highlighting erythrocyte-specific metabolic signatures for non-invasive cancer detection and monitoring and genetic proof-of-concept evidence of the importance of erythrocyte sphingolipid metabolism in counteracting tumor hypoxia, immune escape, and its progression.

Results

Enhanced erythrocyte O2 release capacity in patients with head and neck squamous cell carcinoma and its strong links with tumor progression

To investigate functional alterations of RBCs in patients with HNSCC and evaluate their potential utility for early diagnosis, we enrolled a well-characterized human cohort, including both patients with HNSCC and healthy volunteers (Table 1). Peripheral blood samples were collected from both groups (Figure 1A). The O2 tension at which hemoglobin is 50% saturated with O2 reflects erythrocyte O2-release capacity. To explore functional changes in erythrocytes from patients with HNSCC, we used a blood oximeter (TCS Hemox-Analyzer) to measure differences in P50 between the RBC samples from patients with HNSCC and healthy controls. The results demonstrated that the P50 of HNSCC erythrocytes was significantly higher than that of healthy controls (Figures 1B and 1C), precisely defining a reduced hemoglobin-O2 affinity and enhanced O2 release in patients with HNSCC.

Anemia may potentially affect the P50 measurements obtained by oximetry. To exclude the influence of RBC and hemoglobin concentration on P50 values and to compare inflammatory indicators, we performed complete blood count (CBC) analysis in both groups. CBC analysis revealed no significant differences in RBC count (Figure 1D), hemoglobin concentration (Figure 1E), or platelet count (Figure 1F) between the two groups. These findings imply that the observed changes in P50 are unlikely to be primarily attributed to differences in RBC number or lifespan.

Next, we conducted correlation analyses between P50 and tumor progression by stratifying the patients according to T stage (T1+T2 VS. T3+T4). We found that those with advanced-stage disease (T3+T4) exhibited significantly higher P50 values (Figure 1G). Similarly, when patients were grouped based on the mean depth of invasion (DOI), those with higher DOI also showed elevated P50 values (Figure 1H). These findings further support the notion that erythrocyte O2-releasing capacity is enhanced with tumor progression.

Erythrocyte metabolic reprogramming reflects disease progression in HNSCC and serves as a diagnostic biomarker

Given the well-known link between erythrocyte function and metabolic reprogramming, we hypothesize that metabolic alterations occur in erythrocytes during HNSCC, contributing to the observed changes in P50 (Figure 1I). Thus, to investigate erythrocyte metabolic alterations associated with HNSCC, we collected erythrocyte and plasma samples from 44 pairs of patients with HNSCC and matched healthy controls (Figure 1A), as well as tumor and peritumoral tissues from 13 patients with HNSCC (Figure 2A). Untargeted metabolomic profiling was then performed on all samples. Initially, to seek the potential circulating differential metabolic profiling, partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) was performed based on both RBC and plasma metabolic profiles, respectively. We identified distinct separations between the HNSCC and control groups in both RBCs and plasma (Figures 2B and 2C). Heatmap and volcano plot analyses identified numerous differentially abundant metabolites (DAMs) between the two groups in both compartments (Figures 2D–2G). Next, the intersection of the DAMs from both RBC and plasma datasets revealed 13 shared metabolites, including lactate and sphingosine (Figure 2H). To evaluate the combined diagnostic potential of these metabolites, we performed receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis based on the integrated metabolic profile of the 13 shared metabolites. All selected metabolites demonstrated an area under the curve (AUC) value of 0.997, indicating high predictive accuracy for distinguishing HNSCC from healthy controls (Figure 3A).

Moreover, to explore dynamic changes in these metabolites during HNSCC progression, we performed Mfuzz clustering. Three distinct patterns emerged: Cluster 1 metabolites exhibited a continuous decrease, Cluster 2 metabolites showed progressive accumulation, and Cluster 3 metabolites displayed a biphasic pattern, initial elevation followed by a decline (Figure 3B). These patterns were illustrated by representative metabolites (Figures 3C–3F): lactate (Cluster 1), sphingosine and S1P (Cluster 2), and cadaverine (Cluster 3). In summary, these findings indicate that RBCs undergo significant metabolic reprogramming during tumor progression. The metabolic profiles of RBCs and plasma not only reflect the state of tumor progression but also hold potential as predictive biomarkers for HNSCC progression.

Increased sphingosine and SPHK1 activity combined with the downregulation of the S1P transporter MFSD2B in erythrocytes underlies impaired sphingolipid imbalance in HNSCC

To investigate the relationship between RBC metabolic reprogramming and HNSCC progression, we performed Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis. Sphingolipid metabolism was most significantly enriched across RBCs, plasma, and tumor tissues (Figure 4A). Notably, sphingosine—a key sphingolipid intermediate—was identified as the only commonly, and most significantly changed metabolite across all three compartments based on liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) profiling (Figure 4B). Given the established role of RBCs in adapting to hypoxia through S1P-associated pathways,17,18 we specifically examined S1P-related metabolic changes in our untargeted metabolomics dataset. The results revealed significantly elevated levels of both sphingosine and S1P in HNSCC RBCs (Figures 4C and 4D). SPHK1 is the primary enzyme responsible for S1P production in erythrocytes. To evaluate SPHK1 enzymatic activity in RBCs from patients with HNSCC and healthy controls, we performed activity assays and found that SPHK1 activity was significantly elevated in the HNSCC group (Figure 4E). Furthermore, stratification by pathological TNM (pathological tumor-node-metastasis) stage revealed that SPHK1 activity was highest in stage II patients (Figure 4F). Targeted LC-MS quantification confirmed these findings. To our surprise, while S1P levels did not significantly differ between tumor and peritumoral tissue (Figure 4G), RBCs from patients with HNSCC showed markedly higher S1P levels; Oppositely, plasma S1P was significantly decreased compared to controls (Figure 4H). This inverse relationship was further supported by correlation analysis, which revealed a strong negative correlation between RBC and plasma S1P levels in the HNSCC group, a pattern absent in controls (Figure 4I). Thus, these findings revealed that elevated SPHK1 activity coupled with increased sphingosine underlie the increased S1P levels in erythrocytes and are a potential molecular mechanism for increased O2 release during tumor progression.

Because approximately 50% of plasma S1P originates from RBCs,23 we hypothesized that impaired S1P export could contribute to the observed plasma depletion. MFSD2B, the primary RBC transporter for S1P, was previously identified as a key player in this process.24 Supporting this possibility, Western blot analyses demonstrated significantly reduced expression of MFSD2B in RBCs from patients with HNSCC compared to controls (Figure 4J). Thus, we identified that the downregulation of MFSD2B in RBCs appears to impair S1P efflux, contributing to plasma S1P deficiency in HNSCC (Figure 4K). This dysregulation may impact key tumor-associated processes, including angiogenesis and immune cell trafficking, thereby contributing to HNSCC pathogenesis.

Erythrocyte-derived S1P counteracts tumor progression by promoting O2 delivery, angiogenesis and immune cell infiltration in TME

Determining whether a reduction in RBC-derived S1P contributes to tumor progression in humans is challenging. To address this, we conducted in vivo experiments using mouse models. Specifically, we established a subcutaneous HNSCC tumor-bearing model in eSphk1−/− mice and control mice (Sphk1f/f) (Figure 5A). Our observations revealed that tumors in eSphk1−/− mice grew significantly faster and reached greater weights compared to those in control mice (Figures 5B–5D), indicating that eSphK1 plays an important role in combating tumor development.

To define the molecular bases underlying the beneficial role of eSphK1 in tumor progression, we first measured SPHK1 enzymatic activity and P50 values. Both were significantly reduced in the knockout group (Figures 5E and 5F), indicating that eSphK1 is essential to promote O2-releasing capacity in response to tumor development. Next, we evaluated the effect of erythrocyte SPHK1–S1P deficiency on tumor hypoxia, assessed using hypoxyprobe (pimonidazole, which forms protein adducts in hypoxic cells).25 Hypoxyprobe staining results showed that tumors borne by eSphk1−/− mice had significantly reduced oxygenation levels compared with those in control mice, indicating increased intratumoral hypoxia (Figure S1).

To characterize hematological changes between the erythrocyte-specific knockout and control groups, we performed CBC analysis. The results showed no significant differences in peripheral blood parameters, including hemoglobin, RBC counts, and platelet counts, between the two groups. However, a potential reduction in white blood cell (WBC) count was observed in the conditional knockout group (Figure 5G).

Next, to clarify the effects of eSphk1−/− on immune regulation in the tumor-bearing model, we performed flow cytometric analysis and immunofluorescence staining on tumor tissues. The results showed a significant reduction of intratumoral CD4+ T cell infiltration in the eSphk1−/− group (Figure 5H), suggesting impaired immune cell chemotaxis, which may compromise antitumor immune responses. Given the importance of S1P in angiogenesis,26,27,28 we quantified the angiogenesis by CD31 immunofluorescence staining in these two groups. We found that a decreased microvascular density in tumors from the eSphk1−/− group compared to controls (Figure 5I).

To quantitatively define the contribution of erythrocyte-derived SPHK1 to systemic and tumor-associated sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P) pools, we performed targeted mass spectrometry analyses of S1P levels in erythrocytes, plasma, and tumor tissues from control and eSphk1−/− mice. In tumor-bearing mice, erythrocyte S1P levels were significantly reduced in eSphk1−/− mice compared with control littermates, and their circulating plasma S1P was also decreased accordingly (Figure S2). This indicates that the loss of SPHK1 in red blood cells affects S1P levels both within erythrocytes and in plasma. In contrast, quantitative analysis showed that S1P levels within mEER tumor tissues did not differ significantly between eSphk1−/− and control mice. This indicates that tumor-intrinsic S1P remains largely intact despite the loss of erythrocyte-derived S1P, suggesting that the observed phenotypic differences are unlikely to be driven by changes in tumor cell-derived S1P.

Altogether, we provided a genetic proof-of-concept evidence that erythrocyte SPHK1 is essential to counteract HNSCC tumor progression by promoting O2 and S1P release, leading to enhanced oxygenation, angiogenesis, as well as immune cell infiltration in the tumor microenvironment (Figure 5J).

Lastly, to define the broad protective role of eSphK1 in tumor progression, we generated an independent orthotopic tumor transplantation model using Py8119 cells to mimic breast cancer growth in its native microenvironment (Figure 6A). Similarly, we found that eSphK1 deletion led to severe tumor progression, featuring a large tumor size, less angiogenesis, and more immunosuppression (Figures 6B–6G). Collectively, these results demonstrate that diminished RBC-derived S1P contributes to an immunosuppressive and angiogenesis-deficient microenvironment, thereby promoting HNSCC progression.

Discussion

In this study, we identify a previously unrecognized role of erythrocyte metabolic reprogramming in driving HNSCC progression. Through integrated metabolomics profiling, functional O2 kinetics, and genetic manipulation, we demonstrate that RBCs in patients with HNSCC undergo significant metabolic rewiring, marked by enhanced O2 release capacity and dysregulated sphingolipid metabolism. Notably, we discovered a compartment-specific S1P imbalance, with S1P accumulating intracellularly in RBCs while being depleted in plasma due to defective S1P export mechanisms in patients with tumor. Using genetically engineered eSphk1−/− mice, we provide direct evidence that RBC-derived S1P is a key regulator of tumor biology. Loss of erythrocyte S1P production resulted in accelerated tumor growth due to i) disrupted hypoxia adaptation with impaired O2 release, confirming RBCs as active modulators of tumor oxygenation; ii) collapse of antitumor immunity with reduced T cell infiltration; and iii) suppressed angiogenesis linking RBC metabolism to vascular remodeling. Overall, our findings establish erythrocytes as dynamic metabolic hubs that govern tumor progression through S1P-mediated crosstalk. RBC-specific metabolic signatures serve as a liquid biopsy for innovative diagnoses and innovative therapeutic opportunities by reclassifying RBCs from passive to active participants in cancer biology.

A major discovery is the significantly elevated RBC P50 values in patients with HNSCC, indicating enhanced O2-releasing capacity. Hypoxic conditions not only promote metabolic reprogramming and increased aggressiveness of tumor cells, but also regulate the expression of inflammatory cytokines through pathways such as HIF-1.10 Tumor cells adapt more effectively to hypoxic conditions in the microenvironment by undergoing metabolic reprogramming that supports their survival and proliferation.12,15,29 Persistent hypoxia impairs immune cell function, leading to immunosuppressive signaling and tumor progression.30,31,32 RBCs respond to local hypoxia by increasing O2 release, thereby elevating O2 tension in the TME and mitigating tumor advancement. As hypoxia persists throughout tumor progression, RBC P50 levels tend to increase accordingly, reflecting a sustained compensatory effort to counteract the hypoxic burden. Therefore, the elevation of RBC P50 not only serves as a marker of tumor hypoxia but also participates in the regulation of immune and inflammatory responses through adaptation to the hypoxic microenvironment.

Previous studies have explored modulating hemoglobin-O2 affinity as an anticancer strategy. For instance, myo-inositol trispyrophosphate (ITPP), an allosteric effector of hemoglobin, shifts the O2 dissociation curve to promote O2 delivery to tissues and alleviate tumor hypoxia.33,34 Similarly, compounds such as myo-inositol hexakisphosphate (IHP),35efaproxiral (RSR13),36,37 and resveratrol38,39 have shown comparable effects. Taken together, our findings suggest that increased RBC P50 may represent not only a compensatory response to local tumor hypoxia, but also a key component of systemic immunometabolic remodeling. It may serve as an indirect indicator of disease severity and hold promise as a novel biomarker for risk stratification and personalized therapeutic targeting in HNSCC.

Untargeted metabolomics, including LC-MS-based analysis, is widely used to explore cancer metabolism,39,40,41,42,43,44,45,46 but RBC metabolic alterations in tumor settings remain understudied. Our metabolomic analysis identified substantial metabolic reprogramming in RBCs and plasma from patients with HNSCC, revealing 13 metabolites that were significantly altered, predominantly involved in glycolysis and sphingolipid metabolism. These metabolites demonstrated strong diagnostic potential, highlighting RBC metabolic signatures as minimally invasive biomarkers for early diagnosis and risk assessment in HNSCC.

S1P is a bioactive lipid molecule derived from the metabolism of sphingolipids in the cell membrane, with potent signaling regulatory functions.40,41 It is not only widely present both intracellularly and extracellularly but also mediates various biological processes by binding to specific G protein-coupled receptors (S1P Receptors 1–5), such as cell proliferation, migration, angiogenesis, lymphocyte homing, and regulation of inflammatory responses.40,41

The synthesis of S1P is primarily catalyzed by SPHK1 and SPHK2, with SPHK1 dominating in most tissues and cells, while SPHK2 is mainly localized to the mitochondria, endoplasmic reticulum (ER), and cell nucleus.42,43 Our quantitative metabolomic analysis showed that S1P levels were elevated in RBCs from patients with HNSCC, whereas plasma S1P levels were significantly reduced. Our previous findings demonstrated that S1P promotes glycolysis in red blood cells (RBCs) by enhancing the 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate (2,3-BPG) shunt, also known as the Rapoport-Luebering pathway, thereby increasing the O2-releasing capacity of RBCs in response to tissue hypoxia.17,18 In the present study, although non-targeted metabolomics could not distinguish between 1,3-BPG and 2,3-BPG, increased levels of the upstream metabolite glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (G3P), along with decreased levels of the downstream metabolites 3-phosphoglycerate (3-PG) and 2-phosphoglycerate (2-PG), indicated enhanced activity of the Rapoport-Luebering pathway (Table S1). Combined with the observation of decreased P50 in eSphk1−/− mice compared to controls, these results suggest that increased intracellular S1P levels in HNSCC lead to increased RBC O2 releasing capacity in HNSCC.

SPHK1 and MFSD2B are two essential proteins that regulate S1P synthesis and export in RBCs, respectively, and together influence circulating S1P levels.24,44 Previous studies have reported elevated plasma S1P levels in several cancers, such as lung cancer,45 breast cancer,46 and liver cancers.47 The variation in plasma S1P levels may be attributed to the differential effects of distinct tumor types on SPHK1 activity and MFSD2B expression in RBCs. MFSD2B is a lipid transporter that mediates S1P release from RBCs and platelets.24 In homeostasis, the transporter MFSD2B supplies most blood S1P and supports vascular function.48 Studies in Mfsd2b−/− mice have shown that RBCs accumulate S1P intracellularly, while plasma S1P drops to 42–54% of wild-type levels.24 These findings align with our results in patients with HNSCC, where MFSD2B protein expression was significantly downregulated on the RBC membrane.

SPHK1 has been extensively studied in tumors, where its elevated activity promotes cancer cell proliferation, invasion, metastasis, and immune evasion.49 In addition, tumor cells within the TME can synthesize and actively export S1P through SPHK1/2, while endothelial and immune cells also contribute to S1P production and release.40,41 Pharmacological targeting of the SPHK1–S1P axis has shown therapeutic promise in several cancers, such as HNSCC,50 breast cancer,49,51 and melanoma.52,53 However, most of these studies involve systemic SPHK1 inhibition, which may mask the potentially beneficial effects of RBC-derived S1P on tumor regulation. Our data demonstrates that SPHK1 activity is significantly increased in RBCs from patients with HNSCC. During the progression of HNSCC, tumor proliferation can lead to varying degrees of laryngeal obstruction, resulting in hypoxia within the body. To eliminate the influence of airway obstruction, we utilized a subcutaneous mouse model of HNSCC tumor and an orthotopic mouse model of breast cancer in this study. The results demonstrated that eSphk1−/− mice exhibited a significant reduction in P50, accompanied by accelerated tumor growth. These findings suggest that the RBC SPHK1-S1P axis responds to tumor hypoxia, independent of the mechanical obstruction of the airways.

Using both subcutaneous and orthotopic tumor transplantation models in eSphk1−/− mice, we showed that the RBC-specific deletion of SPHK1 reduced S1P export, leading to decreased immune cell infiltration and angiogenesis in tumors. These changes ultimately accelerated tumor progression. S1P guides T cell migration by forming concentration gradients, and its signaling is essential for the tumor homing of both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells.48,54,55 In the eSphk1−/− mice, we observed a reduction in tumor-infiltrating CD45+ immune cells in both tumor types examined. Previous studies reported no significant difference in tumor S1P levels between tumor-bearing Sphk1−/− mice and controls,56 indicating that the phenotypes observed are mainly driven by erythrocyte-specific SPHK1 loss rather than S1P changes in TME. The mEER cells are murine-derived HNSCC cell lines containing HPV16 E6/E7 and HRAS.57 Notably, mEER tumor-bearing mice exhibited a marked decrease in CD4+ T cells, which may be associated with the known role of human papillomavirus (HPV) in promoting CD4+ T cell accumulation within the HNSCC immune microenvironment.58 In addition, S1P can influence the activity of certain cytokines, such as tumor necrosis factor α (TNF-α).59 A recent review highlights that the SphK1/S1P/S1PR axis mediates cancer resistance to TNF-α-induced apoptosis.60 This is not entirely consistent with our findings that circulating S1P is reduced in eSphk1−/− mice, accompanied by decreased immune cell infiltration and angiogenesis within tumors. This discrepancy may be related to the reciprocal regulatory mechanisms between S1P and TNF-α and suggests that erythrocyte-derived SPHK1/S1P may influence tumors through unique mechanisms. These findings link red blood cell S1P metabolism to tumor immune evasion and offer new insights into the systemic immunoregulatory mechanisms of cancer. These findings underscore a protective role of RBC-derived S1P in cancer, which appears to stem not only from its regulation of O2 release capacity, but also from its extracellular functions in modulating immune cell chemotaxis and tumor vascularization.

Finally, based on our findings in patients with HNSCC and tumor-bearing mouse models, we propose a working model (Figure 7). RBCs play a dual role in tumor progression by contributing both to O2 delivery and to S1P, thereby modulating the tumor microenvironment. The level of RBC-derived S1P is tightly regulated by two key proteins: SPHK1, which governs intracellular S1P synthesis, and MFSD2B, which mediates S1P export. Together, these proteins maintain the intracellular-extracellular S1P balance. The work model highlights two potential mechanisms by which RBC-derived S1P contributes to tumor progression: (A) Intracellular S1P modulates P50 levels, thereby influencing tumor hypoxia. In patients with HNSCC, RBCs exhibit increased O2-release capacity (higher P50) to compensate for local hypoxia within the tumor microenvironment. This compensatory mechanism persists even at advanced disease stages but is eventually overwhelmed by excessive hypoxia caused by rapid tumor proliferation, leading to decompensation. In eSphk1−/− mice, reduced SPHK1 expression results in lower RBC P50, diminished hypoxia compensation in the TME, and accelerated tumor progression. (B) Extracellular S1P released from RBCs enters circulation, where it regulates angiogenesis and the chemotaxis of immune cells, thereby modulating anti-tumor immunity. In addition, RBCs undergo marked metabolic reprogramming. Beyond the significantly altered sphingolipid metabolism, other potential metabolic pathways may also contribute to tumor progression (Table S1). A selected panel of overlapping metabolites identified in both RBCs and plasma demonstrates strong potential for diagnostic application.

These findings broaden our understanding of the role of RBCs in cancer biology and suggest erythrocyte-directed diagnostics and therapies to overcome tumor hypoxia and immune evasion.

In conclusion, our discoveries broaden our understanding of the functional relevance of RBCs in cancer biology by challenging the long-term dogma of RBCs as passive O2 carriers. Our studies have revealed multiple key innovative discoveries: 1) erythrocyte SPHK1/S1P directly modulates O2 delivery and tumor progression; 2) erythrocyte metabolic reprogramming as a systemic driver of cancer-associated sphingolipid dysregulation; 3) Erythrocyte-based metabolites and targeted therapy as a groundbreaking approach to overcome tumor hypoxia and immune evasion. Thus, our findings fundamentally redefine the role of erythrocytes in cancer biology, introducing erythrocyte-directed diagnoses and therapy and opening multiple transformative research avenues, including pan-cancer RBC biology across malignancies; molecular triggers underlying tumor-derived signals that reprogram RBC SPHK1/MFSD2B and developing RBC-specific sphingolipid modulators for O2 delivery enhancers and immune-potentiating agents.

Limitations of the study

This study provides valuable insights into the role of erythrocyte-derived S1P in tumor progression, but several limitations must be acknowledged. While we have shown that erythrocyte-derived S1P regulates tumor growth by modulating oxygenation and immune responses, the precise molecular mechanisms remain underexplored. Further studies are needed to clarify how S1P interacts with specific S1P receptors in the TME and how this interaction influences immune cell function and angiogenesis. In our study, we observed that loss of SPHK1 in erythrocytes significantly alters their oxygen-release capacity, which we believe influences tumor oxygenation. Although current data provide an initial framework, we require establishing a stronger causal relationship; future investigations incorporating metabolic flux tracing and compartment-specific S1P quantification will be required to clarify these mechanistic relationships. Finally, although informative, animal models do not fully recapitulate the heterogeneity of the human TME, and future studies using patient-derived organoid co-culture systems will be essential to validate direct interactions between red blood cells and tumor cells in a more.

Experimental model and study participant details

Human subject study

This study included 44 patients diagnosed with head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC group), all of whom were confirmed by histopathological examination. None had undergone local surgical resection, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, or any other anti-tumor treatment prior to initial therapy at the time of enrollment. Concurrently, 44 age- and sex-matched healthy individuals were recruited as the normal control (NC) group. Control participants with a history of malignancy or other significant systemic disorders were excluded from the study. Human studies were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Xiangya Hospital, Central South University (202203081) and Hunan Cancer Hospital and The Affiliated Cancer Hospital of Xiangya School of Medicine, Central South University (KY2021109). All participants provided written informed consent prior to participation.

Animal experiments

Erythroid-specific SphK1 knockout (Sphk1f/f;EpoR-Cre+) mice were initially acquired from Dr. Richard L. Proia at the National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases, NIH (Bethesda, MD). Both Sphk1f/f;EpoR-Cre+ and littermate control Sphk1f/f mice on a C57BL/6 background were housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions. All protocols involving animal studies were reviewd approved by the Experimental Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of Central South University (Ethics Number: CSU-2024-0248).

The mEER and Py8119 cell lines are both mouse-derived tumor cell lines.57,61The mEER cell line was kindly provided by the research groups of Prof. Xin Zhang(Central South University Xiangya Hospital). and Py8119 was kindly provided by postdoctoral researcher Aji Huang (Central South University Xiangya Hospital). Py8119 cells were cultured in RIPM1640 medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Thermo). mEER cells were maintained in culture medium as described in the literature.57 All cells were incubated in a humidified incubator (37°C, 5% CO2) and routinely passaged at 80–90% confluence using 0.25% trypsin-EDTA.

At 6–8 weeks of age, mice received subcutaneous inoculations of tumor cells at two distinct anatomical sites. For HNSCC modeling, 4×105 mEER mouse HNSCC cells suspended in 0.1 mL PBS into the right flank. Simultaneously, breast carcinoma modeling was initiated by injecting 5×105 Py8119 cells suspended in 0.1 mL PBS into the second mammary fat pad of each animal. Tumor dimensions were measured every 3 days using digital calipers, and volume was calculated as (length × width2)/2. Mice were euthanized at the endpoint for sample collection.

Red blood cell and plasma Isolation

Whole blood is collected from experimental animals or humans and treated with an anticoagulant. The blood is then centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 5 min, and the plasma, RBCs, and the intermediate layer (containing leukocytes and platelets) were separated. The plasma and red blood cells were aliquoted and stored for subsequent use.

Method details

P50 measurement

Oxygen dissociation curves were generated using a Hemox Analyzer (TCS Scientific Corp., New Hope, PA, USA) under standard conditions (pH 7.4, 37°C). P50, the partial pressure of oxygen at which hemoglobin is 50% saturated, was calculated using software provided by the manufacturer. Measurements were conducted in triplicate for each sample. This parameter was used to reflect the oxygen-releasing capacity of RBCs.

Untargetted metabolomics analysis

Metabolomic profiling was performed on the samples using an ultra-high-pressure liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (UHPLC-MS) system. Specifically, a Dionex UltiMate 3000 UHPLC system coupled with a Q Exactive HF mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was employed for the analysis. Samples were analyzed using a 5-min gradient elution program. Briefly, metabolites were separated on a Kinetex C18 column (150 × 2.1 mm, 1.7 μm; Phenomenex, 00F-4475-AN). Chromatographic conditions were as follows: flow rate of 0.45 mL/min, column temperature set at 45°C, autosampler temperature at 4°C, and injection volume of 10 μL. In the positive ion mode, mobile phase A consisted of 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile, and mobile phase B consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water. In the negative ion mode, mobile phase C was 5% acetonitrile in water containing 1 mM ammonium acetate, and mobile phase D was 95% acetonitrile in water with 1 mM ammonium acetate. The mobile phase gradient elution protocol was executed according to the previously described chromatographic conditions.62 All samples were randomly ordered and analyzed separately under both positive and negative ion modes. To assess system stability and quality control, QC samples were injected every ten runs. Mass spectrometer settings were as follows: data acquisition in full scan (Full MS) mode with a resolution of 70,000, scan range of 65–900 m/z, maximum injection time of 200 ms, and microscan number of 2. The automatic gain control (AGC) target was set to 3 × 106. The electrospray ionization (ESI) voltage was set to 4.0 kV for both positive and negative modes. Sheath gas flow was set at 45, auxiliary gas at 15, and sweep gas at 0, all using nitrogen. Raw data files were converted to mzXML format using RawConverter (Scripps Research Institute).Metabolite feature extraction was carried out using the EI-Maven software (v0.12.0, Elucidata).63 Standardization and statistical analysis were performed using Metaboanalyst software v6.0 (http://www.metaboanalyst.ca).64

Targeted measurement of S1P

Following sample extraction, S1P quantification was performed using LC-MS/MS with stable isotope-labeled internal standard calibration. A deuterated internal standard (d7-labeled Sphingosine-1-phosphate, 99% purity) was added to the extraction solvent (methanol:acetonitrile:water [5:3:2, v/v]) at 0.225 μM for metabolite normalization. Subsequent steps followed the untargeted metabolomics detection protocol as outlined above. Raw data files were converted to mzXML format using RawConverter (Scripps Research Institute).Metabolite feature extraction was carried out using the EI-Maven software (v0.12.0, Elucidata).63 For S1P quantitation, chromatographic peak integration was performed based on stable isotope-derived mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) differences, with peak area selected as the quantitative metric for statistical analysis.

SPHK1 activity assay

SPHK1 activity in RBCs was measured using a modified version of a previously described method.17,65 Briefly, RBCs were lysed in buffer containing 50 mM HEPES (pH 7.4), 15 mM MgCl2, 0.05% Triton X-100, 10 mM KCl, and a protease inhibitor cocktail. The lysates were then incubated with 5 μM NBD-sphingosine (Avanti Polar Lipids, #810205) in a total volume of 100 μL reaction buffer consisting of 50 mM HEPES (pH 7.4), 15 mM MgCl2, 0.05% Triton X-100, 10 mM KCl, 10 mM NaF, 1 mM ATP, and 1% fatty acid–free bovine serum albumin (BSA). The reaction mixture was incubated at 37°C for 30 min. To terminate the reaction and extract the lipids, 100 μL of 1 M potassium phosphate buffer (pH 8.5) and 500 μL of chloroform/methanol (2:1, vol/vol) were added. Samples were vortexed and centrifuged at 15,000 rpm for 5 min. Subsequently, 100 μL of the aqueous phase was transferred to a 96-well fluorescence microplate and fluorescence was measured at 489 nm excitation and 535 nm emission wavelengths. Quantification of NBD-S1P levels was performed using a standard curve generated with authentic NBD-S1P.

Immunofluorescence staining

To assess microvessel density, tumor sections were stained with an anti-CD31 antibody (Proteintech, 28083-1-AP). Sections were stained overnight at 4°C with anti-CD31, antibody in PBS with 0.2% Triton X-100. Following washing in PBS for 5 min, sections were incubated with fluorescent-conjugated secondary antibodies (Abcam, Alexa Fluor 488, ab150077). Sections were then mounted on slides, dried at room temperature, and covered with antifade mounting medium with DAPI (4′,6-Diamidino-2-Phenylindole, Beyotime, P0131). Fluorescence images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope (Nikon) at 400× magnification. Regions of interest (ROIs) were selected, and the fluorescence intensity within these ROIs was quantified using ImageJ software.

Hypoxyprobe stainingss

To evaluate tissue hypoxia, a method adapted from prior literature was employed.17 Mice were administered Hypoxia probe-1 (Hypoxyprobe Omni Kit, Inc) intraperitoneally at a dose of 50 mg/kg, 30 min before euthanasia. Tissue Sections were rehydrated, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS for 20 min, blocked with 3% BSA in PBS and incubated in anti-hypoxia probe antibody (raised in rabbit, Hypoxyprobe, Inc, Burlington, MA, 1:100 in 3% BSA-PBS) at 4° C overnight. Goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) Alexa Fluor 594-conjugate antibody (Abcam, ab150080) was used as a secondary antibody at room temperature. After washing, slides were mounted using an antifade mounting medium containing DAPI (4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole, Beyotime, P0131). Images were captured at 200× magnification using a Nikon fluorescence microscope. The fluorescence intensity and area within selected regions of interest was quantified with ImageJ software.

RBC membrane protein extraction

Red blood cell samples (100 μL) are mixed with 900 μL of ice-cold ddH2O containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors and incubated on ice for 10 min to induce hypotonic lysis. The mixture is then centrifuged at 1,000g for 5 min at 4°C to collect the membrane pellet. The pellet is resuspended in 900 μL of ice-cold ddH2O with the same inhibitors and centrifuged again at 20,000g for 10 min at 4°C. This wash step is repeated 3–5 times using pre-chilled PBS until the supernatant becomes clear. The final pellet is lysed in RIPA buffer, and the extracted protein solution is aliquoted and stored at −80°C until further use. Finally, protein concentration is determined using a BCA protein assay kit.

Western Blot analysis of MFSD2B

Protein concentration was determined by BCA assay. Equal amounts of protein were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore Sigma) at a constant current of 260 mA for 90 min. The membrane was blocked with 5% non-fat milk dissolved in TBST at room temperature for 60 min. The membrane was then incubated with the MFSD2B primary antibody (Invitrogen, PA5-21050) overnight at 4°C. Afterward, the membrane was incubated with the secondary antibody (Abiowell, AWS0002) at room temperature for 60 min. β-actin (Proteintech, 66009-1-Ig) served as loading control. The membrane was then incubated with ECL reagents (ECOTOP, EK-5008) for chemiluminescent detection, and images were captured using a chemiluminescence imaging system (Tanon-5200Multi). Signal intensity was quantified using ImageJ software.

Flow cytometry of mice tumor samples

Fresh tumor samples harvested and minced enzymatically digested. Single cell suspensions were generated by digestion with collagenase V (1 mg/mL, Sigma-Aldrich) and DNase I (0.1 mg/mL, Sigma D5025) for 30 min at 37°C and filtration through 70 μm cell strainer. After three PBS washes (10 min), cells were stained with Zombie Aqua viability dye (BioLegend) for 20 min at room temperature in the dark, followed by two washes with ice-cold FACS buffer. Fluorescently labeled anti-mouse antibodies (CD3, CD45, CD4 and CD8, all of which were procured from BioLegend) were incubated with cells for 30 min at 4°C under light-protected conditions, with parallel isotype controls. Stained cells were analyzed using flow cytometry (NovoCyte Advanteon, Agilent, USA), and data were processed in FlowJo (Tree Star).

Quantification and statistical analysis

Data are presented as Mean ± SD. Differences between two groups were assessed using Student’s t test, while comparisons involving more than two groups utilized one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test. A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The unsupervised clustering was performed using the R package Mfuzz in Hiplot Pro (https://hiplot.com.cn/). Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 9.
