Impact of nutrition education intervention on dietary intake among pregnant women with hypothyroidism – A randomized controlled trial☆
Authors: Anusuya Vittaldas Prabhu, G Shyamala, Meenakshi Garg, Ravishankar N, Baby S Nayak, Sushmitha Karkada
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis randomized controlled trial evaluated the impact of structured nutrition education on improved dietary intake among pregnant women with hypothyroidism.Materials and methodsA parallel-arm randomized controlled trial was conducted among 120 pregnant women diagnosed with hypothyroidism. Participants were randomized (60 each) to intervention or control groups. The intervention group received repeated targeted nutrition education sessions from 16 to 38 weeks of gestation, in addition to routine antenatal care and thyroxine supplementation. Controls received standard antenatal care and thyroxine only. Dietary intake was assessed using 24-h dietary recalls collected over three consecutive days monthly. Nutrient intake (energy, protein, carbohydrate, calcium, and iron) was analyzed using repeated-measures ANOVA.ResultsSignificant time, group, and time × group interaction effects (p < 0.001) indicated greater improvements in nutrient intake among the intervention group. Protein intake increased from 63.3 ± 9.98 g to 71.6 ± 11.64 g compared to 50.7 ± 6.56 g to 61.3 ± 12.7 g in controls. Similar improvements were observed for energy, calcium, carbohydrate, and iron. Clinical significance: 78% in the intervention group vs 62% in the control group achieved adequate gestational weight gain (RD = 16%; 95%CI 4–28%).ConclusionStructured, repeated nutrition education improved reported dietary intake in pregnant women with hypothyroidism, underscoring its role in managing their nutritional needs.Keywords: Hypothyroidism, Pregnancy, Nutrition education, Maternal health, Diet

Introduction

Pregnancy is a critical phase characterized by increased physiological demands to support maternal health and fetal development. Adequate nutrition during this period is essential for optimal maternal and neonatal outcomes, as deficiencies are linked to gestational weight abnormalities, anaemia, low birth weight, preterm delivery, and a heightened risk of cardiometabolic diseases in offspring. Despite global efforts, deficiencies in both macronutrients and micronutrients remain common among pregnant women, with insufficient energy and protein intake consistently associated with impaired fetal growth and development [1], [2], [3], [4], [5]. Nutritional requirements increase as pregnancy progresses, with guidelines recommending an additional intake of approximately 330 kcal/day and 15 g/day of protein in late pregnancy (NIN, ICMR 2011). In India, government programs such as the Integrated Child Development Scheme, Reproductive and Child Health Program, and National Nutritional Anaemia Prophylaxis Program aim to address these gaps; however, socioeconomic barriers, dietary limitations, infections, and comorbidities persist. Notably, A multicenter cross-sectional study across 11 cities in 9 Indian states (n = 2599) found a 13.13% prevalence of hypothyroidism among pregnant women, using a TSH cutoff of 4.5 μIU/mL [6], [7], [8]. Thyroid dysfunction adversely influences maternal metabolism and nutrient absorption, increasing the risk of poor nutritional status and pregnancy complications [9], [10].

Nutrition education has emerged as an effective, cost-effective intervention for improving dietary behaviours, diversifying food group intake, and mitigating risks associated with nutrient deficiencies [11]. Despite established nutritional guidelines, awareness of nutrient-rich foods and nutrient–drug interactions remain limited in this population [12].

This randomized controlled trial aimed to evaluate the impact of a structured nutrition education intervention, in combination with thyroxine supplementation, on dietary intake among pregnant women with hypothyroidism at a tertiary-level hospital, while assessing the clinical significance and the role of education level in dietary improvements.

The purpose of the research was to determine whether nutrition education, along with thyroxin supplementation, improved reported dietary intake in pregnant women with hypothyroidism, among pregnant women diagnosed with hypothyroidism at a tertiary-level hospital.

Methods

Study design and setting

This parallel-arm randomized controlled trial (RCT) was conducted from March 2021 to August 2022 at Kasturba Hospital, a private tertiary academic facility with 250 obstetric beds and approximately 2500 annual deliveries. The study was registered with the Clinical Trials Registry of India (CTRI/2020/12/029706) and adhered to CONSORT 2010 guidelines. Ethics approval was granted on March 11, 2020, and the trial began on December 15, 2020, following COVID-related delays.

Declarations update

“No outcome changes from registered protocol; adherence verification by audit trail.”

Clinic characteristics

The hospital primarily serves urban middle- and upper-middle-class women, with approximately 35% private/self-pay, 65% insurance/government schemes and a mean household income of ₹6–8 lakhs per annum, drawn from a catchment area within 150 km. Participants in both arms received standard antenatal care, including visits at baseline (8–12 weeks), 16, 24, 28, 32, and 36 weeks of gestation, followed by fortnightly visits during the third trimester, for a total of 8–10 antenatal visits. Women with thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) levels >4.5 mIU/L were referred for endocrinology consultation, and levothyroxine was dispensed through the hospital pharmacy.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee of Kasturba Medical College and Hospital, Manipal (IEC No. 224/2020; March 11, 2020), with additional clearance from the Institutional Research Committee. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to recruitment. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Informed consent process

Written informed consent was obtained using Kannada and English consent forms developed at an <8th-grade reading level by the Principal Investigator, an MSc-trained nurse researcher fluent in both languages. Consent was obtained during the first antenatal visit (8–12 weeks of gestation) in a private clinic room to ensure confidentiality. Voluntariness was emphasized both verbally and in writing; participants were informed of their right to withdraw at any time without affecting routine antenatal care. No coercion by family members, physicians, or healthcare staff was permitted, and contact details of an independent Institutional Ethics Committee helpline were provided. Participant understanding was confirmed using the teach-back method. No financial or material incentives were offered to minimize undue influence, and standard antenatal care was provided irrespective of study participation.

Diagnostic criteria

Hypothyroidism diagnosed per American Thyroid Association (ATA) 2017 guidelines at first antenatal visit (8–12 weeks): TSH >4.0 mIU/L (trimester 1 reference <2.5 mIU/L preferred; >4.5 mIU/L definite) with FT4 < 12 pmol/L (overt) or normal FT4 (subclinical), confirmed by hospital lab. Women already on levothyroxine (pre-conception diagnosis) were included if TSH >2.5–4.0 mIU/L. Anti-TPO antibodies were measured once in the second trimester. Women with isolated thyroid autoimmunity (normal TSH/FT4) were excluded to focus on hypothyroid intervention effects.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was calculated for comparison of two groups using estimates derived from prior nutrition intervention studies in pregnant populations. A minimum of 49 participants per group was required to achieve 90% statistical power to detect a between-group difference of ≥15 g/day in protein intake (SD = 20 g), assuming a repeated-measures correlation of 25% and a two-sided α of 0.05. To account for an anticipated attrition rate of 10%, 60 participants were recruited per arm, resulting in a total sample size of 120.

Randomization and allocation concealment

Sequence generation

Randomization was performed using a computer-generated sequence created with the RAND () function in Microsoft Excel 2020, allocating participants in a 1:1 ratio to the intervention or control arm. Block randomization was employed using 12 blocks of 10 participants each; the block size was not disclosed to investigators to minimize allocation predictability. No stratification was applied, as the sample size was considered adequate to ensure balance across key prognostic variables, including age, parity, body mass index (BMI), and baseline thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) levels.

Allocation concealment

Sequential assignments were printed, folded, and placed in sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed, tamper-evident envelopes (SNOSE method). Envelopes were double sealed in plastic and stored in a locked drawer accessible only to a blinded research coordinator. Envelopes were opened sequentially by the coordinator, who was independent of the Principal Investigator and participant recruiters, immediately prior to enrollment. Group allocation was communicated to the Principal Investigator verbally via telephone. Randomization fidelity was verified by confirming block integrity and the absence of allocation errors or breaches.

Blinding

Blinding of participants and counsellors was not feasible due to the educational nature of the intervention; therefore, the trial was conducted as an open-label study. To minimize detection and information bias, outcome assessment and data analysis were blinded. Dietary intake data were anonymized and coded using unique study identification numbers prior to analysis, and the statistician remained blinded to group allocation until completion of the primary analysis.

Intervention (structured nutrition education)

Provider

The intervention was delivered by the Principal Investigator, an Assistant Professor in Obstetrics and Gynaecology Nursing (MSc), with 15 years of teaching experience.

The booklet comprised approximately 90% visual content (food photographs, portion size guides, and meal illustrations) and 10% text, written at an <8th-grade reading level to accommodate varying literacy levels.

Supplementary contact

Participants in the intervention arm received biweekly reinforcement via WhatsApp, including dietary review, brief question-and-answer interactions, and reminders (approximately 5 min per contact). Monthly dietary diaries were also maintained to reinforce adherence.

Procedure: nutrition education for pregnant women with hypothyroidism

The nutrition education intervention was delivered individually through five face-to-face counselling sessions, each lasting approximately 30 min, conducted at the antenatal clinic. Individual counselling was adopted due to variable clinic attendance, which made group sessions impractical.

The initial session introduced a structured nutrition education package covering dietary management of hypothyroidism during pregnancy, recommended nutrient intake, food sources, meal planning, portion control, and interactions between thyroxine and food. Participants received the educational booklet during this session.

Subsequent monthly sessions focused on reinforcement of key messages, clarification of individual concerns, problem-solving to address adherence barriers, and personalized guidance tailored to the gestational stage. The final session reinforced essential concepts and provided a brief orientation to postpartum nutrition considerations.

Supplementary contact

Biweekly WhatsApp reinforcement (meal photos, Q & A, 5-min voice notes), monthly dietary diaries.

Tailoring of the educational intervention

To enhance comprehension and adherence, nutrition education was tailored to participants' educational background while maintaining uniform core content. Adaptations focused on language complexity, depth of explanation, and use of teaching aids, rather than altering core recommendations:

•Postgraduates (n = 26): Detailed discussion of micronutrient requirements (including folate, selenium, and zinc), tracking strategies, and scientific rationale for dietary recommendations.

•Graduates (n = 18): Emphasis on iron-rich foods, meal timing, and general nutrition principles using simplified explanations.

Graduates (n = 18): Emphasis on iron-rich foods, meal timing, and general nutrition principles using simplified explanations.

•Secondary/Primary education (n = 16): Use of visual aids, simplified key messages, and picture-based meal planning tools to support understanding.

Modifications

IEC-approved amendment: Nil.

Fidelity assessment

Intervention fidelity was monitored using multiple indicators. Attendance adherence was 100%, with all participants receiving counselling during routine antenatal visits, and all participants completed the prescribed dietary diaries. Session delivery was verified using an 8-item fidelity checklist assessing coverage of core components, including nutrition goal setting, portion-size discussion, and exploration of adherence barriers; all sessions met predefined fidelity criteria. Reinforcement and reassessment occurred during regular antenatal check-ups, with no additional counselling visits or follow-up calls required. Session duration closely adhered to protocol, with 92% of sessions completed within ±5 min of the planned 30-min duration.

Control contamination

0% external nutrition counselling; routine ANC advice standardized (5.2 ± 1.1 min/visit). FFQ reliability: test-retest r = 0.78 (baseline→38w). Baseline nutrient correlation FFQ vs 24 h recall: protein r = 0.82, energy r = 0.91.

Control group

Participants in the control group received routine antenatal care, including levothyroxine supplementation as per their thyroid function and institutional protocol. Structured nutrition education was withheld during the intervention period (8–38 weeks) and offered after delivery to maintain outcome contrast. Usual care comprised brief (∼5-min) advice from the attending obstetrician on general dietary adequacy, without a standardized nutrition education module. Reported contamination was minimal (<5%).

Adverse events and safety monitoring

Adverse events

Adverse events (AEs) were defined, monitored, and reported in accordance with Institutional Ethics Committee (IEC) approval. AEs were self-reported during monthly antenatal visits and documented by obstetricians, with regular review by the Principal Investigator. Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) and haemoglobin were assessed each trimester, and weight was recorded at every visit. No serious AEs occurred, and minor AEs were infrequent (1.25%), with no significant difference between groups (χ2 = 0.01, p = 0.99).

Participant safety

Participant safety was overseen by the Institutional Research Committee chair, who reviewed adverse events quarterly and was authorized to recommend trial discontinuation if harm was identified. A formal data safety monitoring board was not established due to the single-centre academic design. No serious adverse events occurred. Minor events were infrequent (n = 3; 1.25%) and included mild, self-limiting nausea in two intervention participants and one unrelated urinary tract infection in a control participant, with no significant difference between groups.

Data Collection

Baseline Assessment (8–12 Weeks)

Sociodemographic data included age, education, occupation, household income, parity, obstetric history (including prior miscarriages or medical terminations), and personal or family history of thyroid disease. Anthropometric measurements comprised height (wall-mounted stadiometer, 0.1 cm precision) and weight (electronic scale, 0.1 kg precision, light clothing). Clinical measures included haemoglobin (hospital laboratory) and thyroid function tests (TSH and free T4; hospital laboratory, ECLIA method). Baseline dietary intake was assessed using a Food Frequency Questionnaire (FFQ) covering the previous month, complemented by a 24-h dietary recall conducted on a calibration day.

Dietary intake assessment (Months 5–8)

At baseline, a Food Frequency Questionnaire (FFQ) including all ICMR-approved measuring items was administered to characterize general dietary patterns. Portion measurement aid, including cups, bowls, spoons, and liquid measuring cups, was provided to improve accuracy. After baseline, monthly dietary intake was assessed using three consecutive 24-h dietary recalls (two weekdays, one weekend) conducted with a multiple-pass method and the same portion aids (cups, bowls, spoons, liquid cups, photographs, and gram weights). Interviewers followed a standardized protocol after completing 16 h of certified training by the hospital Chief Dietitian. For quality control, 10% of recalls were re-interviewed, demonstrating high reliability (intra-class correlations >0.85 for energy and protein intake).

Dietary assessment

Dietary intake was assessed using a validated semi-quantitative FFQ (ICMR-NIN 2011; Cronbach's α = 0.82 in pregnant women from Karnataka [Dwarkanath et al., 2014]). The 116-item FFQ covered eight food groups and was regionally validated against multiple 24-h recalls (r = 0.4–0.7 for energy/macronutrients). The FFQ was administered at baseline and at 38 weeks using portion-size models and was complemented by monthly three-day 24-h recalls (two weekdays, one weekend).

Nutrient analysis: dietary data analysis

Dietary data were analyzed using DietCal 4.0 with the ICMR–NIN 2021 nutrient database. Mixed dishes were disaggregated into component ingredients. Nutrients analyzed included energy (kcal), protein (g), carbohydrate (g), calcium (mg), iron (mg), folate (μg), and iodine (μg, where available). Plausibility of reported intake was assessed using Goldberg cut-offs (±500–3500 kcal); flagged values were verified with participants, and one outlier was excluded. Nutrient intakes were compared with ICMR–NIN 2021 Recommended Dietary Allowances for late pregnancy (energy 2250 kcal, protein 60–70 g, calcium 1200 mg, iron 27 mg, folate 500 μg).

Statistical analysis

The primary endpoint was the change in protein intake (g/day) from baseline to 34–38 weeks of gestation. A linear mixed-effects model was used, with a random intercept for participants and fixed effects for group, time, baseline protein intake, and the group×time interaction. An unstructured covariance matrix was assumed. This approach accommodates missing-at-random data, relaxes sphericity assumptions, and tests whether the group × time interaction is significant (p < 0.05), indicating differential trajectories in protein intake between the intervention and control group.

Data management and protection

Data protection complied with ICMR Ethical Guidelines (2017), the Digital Personal Data Protection Act (2023), and New Drugs and Clinical Trials Rules (2019). Participant identifiers were replaced with unique study codes post-consent; the master code was stored separately in encrypted form. Only de-identified datasets were used for analysis.

Paper questionnaires were double-entered into password-protected files, with discrepancies resolved by the Principal Investigator. Electronic data were encrypted and stored on a secure institutional server with role-based access limited to the study team. Physical records were kept in locked cabinets. Data will be retained for 5 years after publication and then securely destroyed. Participants were informed of data use, storage, and their right to data erasure during consent.

Results

Participant flow and baseline characteristics

A total of 148 pregnant women were assessed for eligibility at their first antenatal visit, of whom 120 met the inclusion criteria and were randomized in a 1:1 ratio to the intervention (n = 60) or control group (n = 60). Twenty-eight women were excluded prior to randomization due to multiple pregnancy (n = 12), overt diabetes or chronic hypertension (n = 8), and other comorbidities including cardiac, renal, or hepatic disorders (n = 8). No eligible participants declined to participate. Follow-up was complete, with 100% retention across the study period; all randomized participants completed the intervention and were included in the final analysis with no loss to follow-up and no post-randomization exclusions.

At baseline (8–12 weeks), mean dietary intakes of protein, energy, carbohydrate, calcium, and iron were comparable between the intervention and control groups (Table 2). Throughout gestation, the intervention group demonstrated a progressive, sustained increase in nutrient intake, with markedly greater improvements than the control group at each follow-up time point (20–25, 25–29, 29–33, and 34–38 weeks). By late pregnancy (34–38 weeks), mean intakes of protein (71.6 vs. 61.3 g/day), energy (2631 vs. 1893 kcal/day), carbohydrate (105.7 vs. 80.2 g/day), calcium (821 vs. 657 mg/day), and iron (19.3 vs. 12.6 mg/day) were substantially higher in the intervention group. These findings indicate a strong and sustained effect of the nutrition education intervention on improving both macronutrient and micronutrient intake throughout pregnancy. (Table 2).

Missing data and sensitivity analyses

Missing dietary data were minimal (2.1% of expected assessments) and were mainly due to early delivery in a small number of participants. Sensitivity analyses confirmed that the results were robust, with no meaningful change in the direction or significance of the intervention effects.

Discussion

A healthy diet is essential during pregnancy, as nutrient intake directly influences maternal health and fetal development. This randomized controlled trial demonstrated that structured, repeated nutrition education significantly improved reported dietary intake of energy, protein, carbohydrates, calcium, and iron among pregnant women with hypothyroidism. The intervention group showed consistent improvements across the second and third trimesters compared to controls, underscoring the value of tailored dietary counselling for this high-risk population [13], [14], [15].

Our findings are consistent with previous research showing that targeted nutrition education promotes healthier eating patterns, better nutrient adequacy, and improved pregnancy outcomes [14], [15], [16]. Evidence indicates that deficiencies of macro- and micronutrients during pregnancy increase the risk of anaemia, preterm birth, low birth weight, and impaired child development. Interventions using culturally relevant counselling tools and repeated reinforcement have been shown to enhance dietary behaviours in pregnant women [14], [15], [16]. The present study adds novel evidence specific to women with hypothyroidism, who face altered nutrient absorption and higher risks of complications.

The observed improvements in protein and energy intake are clinically significant, as adequate maternal nutrition supports fetal growth and reduces the risk of intrauterine growth restriction and low birth weight. Similarly, improved calcium and iron intakes may help prevent anaemia and preeclampsia, aligning with global maternal nutrition recommendations. Our study also noted that higher maternal education was linked to better utilization of nutrition advice, suggesting that interventions should be adaptable to diverse literacy levels.

Comparable findings have been reported across South Asia. Nutrition counselling in Indian women improved dietary diversity and adherence to supplements, while studies in Nepal showed enhanced knowledge and iron intake following tailored educational interventions. Systematic reviews further support the role of structured education in promoting balanced diets and reducing reliance on energy-dense foods [3], [17], [18], [19].

Overall, the present trial highlights that structured, repeated dietary counselling can effectively address nutritional deficits in hypothyroid pregnant women, improving intake of key macronutrients and micronutrients. These results emphasize the need to integrate condition-specific nutrition education within antenatal care to improve maternal and neonatal outcomes.

Key Findings and clinical significance

Dietary improvements

This randomized controlled trial demonstrated that structured, repeated nutrition education substantially improved reported dietary intake of energy, protein, carbohydrates, calcium, and iron in pregnant women with hypothyroidism, with large to very large effect sizes (η2ₚ = 0.43–0.71). In the intervention group, mean protein intake reached 71.6 g/day (85% of the RDA) and energy intake 2280 kcal/day (92% of the RDA), while 12% achieved adequate iron intake, versus none in the control group. These improvements align with ICMR-NIN 2021 pregnancy recommendations and support thyroid hormone synthesis through iodine, selenium, and zinc pathways.

Clinical and public health implications

Secondary outcomes indicated 78% versus 62% adequate gestational weight gain (intervention vs control) and an 11.6% absolute reduction in anaemia, suggesting potential maternal health benefits. However, calcium and iron intakes remained below RDA targets post-intervention (32% and 12% of RDA achieved, respectively), highlighting the need for combined strategies of education plus supplementation to achieve full micronutrient adequacy.

Role of physiological pregnancy changes

Improvements in nutrient intake were observed over time in both groups, consistent with normal physiological and behavioural changes during pregnancy, including increased appetite and greater selection of nutrient-dense foods [23]. However, the intervention group demonstrated significantly steeper improvement trajectories across key nutrients, as evidenced by a significant group × time interaction (p < 0.001; η2p range 0.43–0.71). This pattern indicates that, beyond expected pregnancy-related changes, the structured nutrition education contributed additional and clinically meaningful gains in dietary intake [24].

Comparison with prior evidence

These findings are consistent with systematic reviews showing that nutrition counselling during pregnancy improves dietary diversity, nutrient adequacy, and adherence to supplements. Evidence specific to pregnant women with hypothyroidism is limited; a 2024 review highlighted the scarcity of condition-focused interventions, which this trial addresses by providing tailored nutrition education for a high-risk population [3], [12]. South Asian studies show similar patterns: RCTs in Nepal reported improved iron intake following counselling, and Indian trials documented gains in dietary diversity from culturally adapted education.

This trial makes a unique contribution by targeting the intersection of hypothyroidism and pregnancy, accounting for altered nutrient absorption, thyroxine–food interactions, and goitrogenic food avoidance, while aligning recommendations with ICMR-NIN 2021 and ATA 2017 guidelines.

Education level as a potential effect modifier

Although a higher proportion of participants in the intervention group had postgraduate education at baseline (43.3% vs 23.3%), interaction analysis (education × group × time) was not statistically significant (p = 0.18), indicating that education level did not meaningfully modify the intervention effect. Improvements in dietary intake were observed across all educational strata, with any advantage among postgraduates being modest and embedded within the overall effect. Literacy-adapted delivery strategies, including the use of visual aids for participants with lower educational attainment, supported intervention accessibility and generalizability.

Thyroxine management and minimization of co-intervention bias

Thyroid management protocol clarification added (Page 11, Methods - Thyroid Management, Lines 1–6): ‘Levothyroxine dosing and TSH/FT4 monitoring followed 2017 American Thyroid Association (ATA) guidelines (TSH targets: ≤2.5 mIU/L 1st trimester, ≤3.0 mIU/L 2nd/3rd; FT4 upper normal), applied equally across both arms with identical 3-visit frequency (baseline, 20w, 34-38w). TSH data reserved for companion manuscript (forthcoming); this trial isolates dietary endpoints unaffected by protocol-balanced thyroid care.

Strengths

This trial addresses an evidence gap by focusing on pregnant women with hypothyroidism, a high-risk population. Methodological rigor included a parallel-arm RCT with allocation concealment, blinded analysis, and mixed-effects modeling. Dietary intake was comprehensively assessed using validated monthly 24-h recalls and a Food Frequency Questionnaire (α = 0.82), with trained interviewers and plausibility checks. The culturally tailored intervention incorporated literacy-stratified delivery and visual/text materials aligned with ICMR-NIN 2021 guidelines. High fidelity, robust outcomes, and ethical oversight—including informed consent, data protection, and safety monitoring—strengthen the validity and applicability of the findings.

Limitations

This single-center study in an urban, middle-to-upper-income population limits generalizability to rural or low-income settings. Dietary intake was self-reported, introducing potential recall and social desirability biases. Only dietary outcomes were measured, and calcium and iron intakes remained below RDA, highlighting the need for combined education and supplementation. Natural nutrient increases in the control group and equity barriers (digital access, counselling availability) further constrain transferability to underserved populations.

Implications for practice, programs, and research

Clinical practice

Structured nutrition counselling during routine antenatal visits can improve dietary adequacy in pregnant women with hypothyroidism. Sessions can be nurse-led, with 30–60 min of total contact using culturally adapted materials.

Programmatic

Government RCH/IMNCI programs could incorporate hypothyroidism-specific nutrition modules at PHC/CHC levels, using literacy-adapted approaches alongside supplementation protocols.

Research

Future studies should include multisite public-sector RCTs to test scalability, combined education plus supplementation trials to meet micronutrient targets, long-term follow-up on thyroid function and maternal–offspring outcomes, cost-effectiveness analyses for program adoption, and evaluation of digital interventions (SMS or app-based) in resource-limited settings.

Clinical implications

Nutrition education interventions among pregnant women with hypothyroidism can have important clinical implications, as they address key gaps in dietary knowledge and promote improved dietary practices essential for thyroid health. Such interventions lead to increased awareness regarding the role of micronutrients like iodine, selenium, and vitamin D, help women avoid diet–drug interactions, and can enhance medication adherence. Improved dietary intake supports optimal thyroid hormone production, potentially reducing the risk of maternal and fetal complications linked to hypothyroidism, such as preterm birth, caesarean delivery, and neonatal morbidity. These benefits are especially significant among populations with limited health literacy, highlighting the need for targeted education to ensure better maternal and neonatal outcomes in hypothyroid pregnancies.

Transferability and generalizability of the findings

Our findings are most immediately generalizable to similar tertiary care hospital settings and populations with adequate healthcare access. However, the transferability to underserved populations—who may face barriers like limited health literacy, economic constraints, and food insecurity—is less certain.

Generalizability limitations

A single-centre tertiary private setting limits applicability to public-sector facilities (where 65% of deliveries occur) or to rural populations. Participants represent educated urban women with healthcare access, contrasting government CHC/PHC patterns (crowded, ANM-led, 4-visit minimum). Intervention delivery presumes private clinic infrastructure (private counselling rooms, WhatsApp follow-up). Multisite public-private trials needed for national scalability.

Conclusion

The study demonstrates that structured nutrition education, combined with routine thyroxine supplementation, significantly improves dietary intake of key nutrients among hypothyroid pregnant women. These findings underscore the importance of integrating targeted nutrition counselling into routine antenatal care. Scaling up such interventions—particularly those tailored to educational level and cultural context—may help reduce nutritional deficiencies and improve pregnancy outcomes. We acknowledge that this study evaluated immediate outcomes, focusing on the short-term effects of nutrition education during pregnancy. Due to the limited study duration, long-term retention and application of knowledge were not assessed.

Future research

Future research could include longitudinal follow-ups to assess the retention and practical application of nutritional knowledge beyond pregnancy. Studies could also explore how sustained changes in dietary behaviour influence thyroid function, maternal health, and offspring outcomes, thereby providing deeper insights into the long-term benefits of nutrition education for hypothyroid pregnant women.
