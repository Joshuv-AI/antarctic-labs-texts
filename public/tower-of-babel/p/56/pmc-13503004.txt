Improving surgical patients’ safety in African hospitals: exploring healthcare professionals’ experiences of Phase 1 of the ‘5Rs to Rescue’ programme
Authors: Charles Fadipe, Rowan Duys, Gillian J Bedwell, Pierre Barker, Godfrey Barabona, Bruce M Biccard, Ryan Ellis, Adam Hewit-Smith, Fitsum Kilfe, Marianne E McPherson, Busisiwe Mrara, Ezile Ninise, Rupert M Pearse, Rafael Shayo, Timothy J Stephens, APPRISE Investigators, Reece Hinchcliff
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe mortality rate of surgical patients in Africa is twice the global average. Pan-African studies have identified that this excess mortality is driven by deaths arising from postoperative complications. Preventable deaths can be reduced with enhanced monitoring in the postoperative period. This study reports on the first of a two-phase Quality Improvement (QI) programme, ‘5 Rs to Rescue’, designed to reduce failure to rescue for surgical patients in Africa.MethodsUsing a QI approach, we co-produced a failure to rescue intervention with stakeholders across four African countries (Ethiopia, South Africa, Tanzania, and Uganda). These initial interventions were as follows: (i) Risk assessment using a validated tool, (ii) Recognition of patient deterioration using an early warning system (EWS), (iii) Response to patient deterioration (using locally defined protocols, (iv) Reassessment using an EWS, and (v) Reflection on care provided. These were tested and refined in clinical practice at nine hospitals through QI approaches. We interviewed frontline staff, project leaders, and QI advisors at the end of Phase 1 to learn from their experience to refine the intervention prior to Phase 2. Deductive thematic analysis was used, sensitized by constructs from the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR).ResultsWe interviewed 11 frontline staff, seven national project leads and five QI advisors. From the CFIR-based coding, two cross-cutting, higher-order themes were developed to describe the participant’s experience. Theme 1 centred around the substantial relational work involved in leading the 5Rs project. Surgeons reported the recurrent communication required to engage, train, and motivate all the necessary stakeholders. Surgical and nursing hierarchies were poorly linked in many settings, initially hampering these relational efforts. Theme 2 centred on the complexity of the 5Rs tools being introduced into complex, and already overstretched systems. A long period was required for nurses and doctors to become familiarized with the proposed ‘5Rs’ tools. Although their value was broadly accepted, not all tools were a good fit for all the project settings. Potential solutions to overcome barriers identified in Phase 2 included a longer QI programme, more emphasis on involving nurse leaders and selection of a less complicated ‘EWS’ for detecting patient deterioration.ConclusionDeveloping a multifaceted intervention that fits within complex, resource-limited contexts is a major challenge for efforts to improve patient care. This Phase 1 evaluation has provided actionable learning, targeting specific barriers, to be incorporated into an improved failure to rescue QI programme being developed in Phase 2.Keywords: quality improvement, perioperative care, surgery, Africa, low- and middle-income country

Introduction

Surgical mortality in Africa is twice that of the global average, with ∼600 000 deaths annually, despite patients being comparatively younger with less co-morbid disease [1, 2]. A leading contributor to this excess mortality is failure to rescue (FTR), defined as a potentially avoidable death following the failure to recognize or adequately respond to clinical deterioration [3]. The African Surgical Outcomes Study (ASOS) documented that FTR accounts for the majority of post-surgical deaths in Africa, with 95% of deaths occurring in the postoperative period [1]. Post-surgical mortality can be reduced with enhanced monitoring to detect complications and deterioration combined with effective management of identified complications in the postoperative period [3]. However, most hospitals in African countries lack routine systems for enhanced postoperative monitoring and timely intervention, and previous large-scale efforts to address this issue have not achieved sustained reductions in mortality [4, 5].

Targeting FTR through the implementation of early warning systems (EWSs) and rapid response protocols have been increasingly used in high-income countries to predict clinical deterioration and inform timely escalation of care [3, 6]. These systems and tools have demonstrated potential to improve the detection of at-risk patients and reduce FTR rates, although the evidence remains inconclusive [7, 8]. A recent attempt to reduce FTR through increased postoperative surveillance in African hospitals failed to show any mortality benefit [4]. Lack of a thoroughly pre-tested intervention and implementation and delivery of that intervention within a short, randomized controlled trial dictated timeframe were identified as two causes of intervention failure [5]. This experience, in part, has prompted the design of a multi-phase, quality improvement programme (QIP) to develop, test, and evaluate FTR tools in African hospitals. In brief, in a QIP, clinical teams participate in a structured process to identify best practice, apply specific improvement methods to implement this, collect and share data, and learn about ways of achieving improvement from the experiences of others [9]. Quality improvement (QI) interventions are invariably complex, with many interacting components, a large number of discretionary behaviours or actions required among those receiving the intervention, and flexibility in tailoring implementation [10]. In an African context, there is evidence that appropriately adapted QI approaches can be effective at improving patient care and outcomes [11–13]. However, the requirements for an effective FTR-focused QIP in Africa and how to adapt existing approaches to the surgical context in African hospitals remain poorly understood, and there is limited evidence on how to design, implement, and evaluate such interventions in diverse, resource-constrained settings.

Between February 2023 and August 2024, the first phase of the ‘5Rs to Rescue’ QIP to reduce post-operative FTR was delivered across nine hospitals from four African countries using a Demonstration Quality Improvement Collaborative design [9]. This qualitative study was designed to explore and characterize the experiences of healthcare professionals leading and participating in the ‘5Rs to Rescue’ QIP. We report on the key learnings from this study and how this has informed the design of the intervention and evaluation of the subsequent phase of the project.

Materials and methods

The 5Rs to Rescue improvement programme

This was the first of a two-phase project designed to develop (Phase 1) and then evaluate (Phase 2) interventions to reduce post-surgical FTR in nine hospitals in Ethiopia, South Africa, Tanzania, and Uganda [14]. We used a QI design to develop, adapt, and test the implementation of a scalable model of postoperative care. The aim was to develop the activities and associated tools for reliable delivery of the clinical activities associated with risk identification, recognition of deterioration, clinical response to deterioration, and regular review of system performance (the ‘5Rs’). Based upon existing evidence [15], and experience of the study team and in collaboration with clinicians in African hospitals and the Institute for Health Improvement (IHI), we developed or adapted a set of FTR interventions for early-stage testing and adaptation. This process involved a series of online workshops with investigators and frontline staff from the study hospitals to present and discuss possible tools and interventions and culminated in an in-person 3-day event held in Addis Ababa to reach consensus on the initial 5Rs clinical intervention and learn about QI methods.

The clinical intervention focused on five strategies or tools to improve post-operative monitoring for high-risk surgical patients (see also Fig. 1): (i) Risk assessment using a previously validated tool (African Surgical Outcomes Study Risk Calculator, known as the ASOS score) [1, 16], (ii) Recognition of patient deterioration using an EWS (either National Early Warning Score or Vital Signs Directed Therapy) [17, 18]; (iii) Response to patient deterioration (using locally defined protocols), (iv) Reassessment following intervention to manage deterioration using an EWS, and (v) Reflection on care provided following a patient’s deterioration and/or death (at regular, interprofessional mortality review meetings).

Outline of the 5Rs to Rescue interventions. ASOS [1, 16]; NEWS2, National Early Warning Score 2 [18]; VSDT, Vital Signs Directed Therapy [17].

We used a traditional QI collaborative approach in nine hospitals to develop the implementation model [9]. The activities and tools that supported the clinical processes were developed, tested, and improved using routine QI methods (Model for Improvement and Plan Do Study Act testing) [9, 19], supported by in-person visits from QI coaches. Webinars provided a venue for cross-learning for participating sites.

Study design, data collection, and participants

We used a purposive sampling approach to recruit participants across the hospitals in the four countries, with sampling based on country, profession (nurse, doctor, improvement advisor) and level of involvement in the QIP [IHI improvement advisor, QIP leader (national), QIP leader (local), or QIP recipient (hospital staff)]. We were guided by the concept of information sufficiency/power in determining our sample size [20]. Interviews were structured using a question topic guide informed by evidence and theory and reviewed and agreed by the investigator group (Supplementary File) [21]. Interviews were either one-on-one (local QIP leaders and hospital staff) or small group [one group of hospital staff (three nurses), one for improvement advisors, and one for national QIP leads], conducted online or in-person over a 3-week period in April 2024 (by T.S., C.F., R.D., and G.J.B.). Interviews lasted between 30 and 75 min depending on the number of participants. Consent from participants was obtained and interviews audio recorded and transcribed. Ethical approval was granted by the Human Research Ethics Committee at the University of Cape Town (HREC200/2024), with local approvals obtained in each country as necessary.

Data analysis

Two investigators (C.F. and T.S.) used thematic analysis [22], initially with a primarily deductive approach sensitized by Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) constructs [23–25]. We used the CFIR constructs to provide an initial analysis structure, and then moved through an interpretive approach using the data meetings to refine and develop themes through iterative discussion. Disagreements were resolved by discussion and agreement across the investigator group at the data meetings, leading to refinements in how data were aligned with the CFIR constructs and the meaning and interpretation of these with regards to the 5Rs project [26]. Following this, the analyses were developed into higher level themes, staying close to the meaning of the CFIR constructs whilst presenting their inter-relatedness in describing the experience of participants [22]. These were reviewed, refined, and agreed by the investigator group at a final data meeting.

Research team positionality

The 5Rs investigator group comprised physicians and allied health professionals either working in one of the four 5Rs project countries (R.D., G.J.B., G.B., B.B., A.H.S., F.K., B.M., E.N., R.S.) or the UK (C.F., T.S., R.P.). All have a strong professional interest in reducing excessive mortality after surgery in an African context. C.F. and T.S. are two clinicians and researchers working in the UK, part of the 5Rs investigator group, but with primarily UK-based clinical experience. T.S. is also an experienced qualitative researcher and implementation scientist. None had strong views about how to achieve the desired improvements in patient care, hence the iterative, QI design project to identify and test novel interventions for this complex and longstanding problem.

Results

We interviewed local QIP leaders and recipients, totalling three surgeons, one anaesthesiologist, one physician, and six nurses from QIP hospitals in Ethiopia, South Africa, and Uganda (see Table 1 for a summary of key information about each). We were unable to interview any hospital staff in Tanzania due to lack to local research permissions, which we recognize as a limitation in our dataset. We also interviewed seven national project leads (all anaesthesiologists or physicians, also based at QIP hospitals) and five QI advisors (all physicians or nurses) from the Institute for Healthcare Improvement from these countries and Tanzania. Data were deductively coded and themed using 12 constructs that spanned four of CFIR domains (Intervention, Inner Setting, Individuals, and Implementation Process; see Table 2 for CFIR constructs and indicative quotations). There was substantial commonality in the interview data regarding the key barriers to, and facilitators of, the 5Rs Improvement Programme across study sites. Further, the application of the CFIR constructs to the real-world, situated experiences of participants demonstrated a high degree of inter-relatedness, rather than representing discrete or stand-alone themes. To represent this commonality and inter-relatedness, two higher-order themes were generated to summarize and integrate the key learning from this first phase of the project.

Theme 1: There was more relational work involved in leading the 5Rs project than expected

Theme 1 crossed the boundaries of the CFIR Inner Setting, Individuals, and Implementation Process domains. There was agreement across local QIP leads that although this relational work was highlighted upfront by the IHI, the amount of time and energy it took was unexpected. Even when FTR was recognized as an issue, surgeons leading the QIP in each hospital reported the substantial time needed to communicate with multiple colleagues on multiple occasions for key messages to be internalized and understood. Planning for change and engaging with staff was seen as important, but substantial work that did not always fit into already full clinical work schedules. This work needed to have begun well before the start of the QIP and ideally led by several senior surgeons rather than only one. Where this did not happen, it was seen as a barrier to timely progress.

Another common finding was that surgical and nursing hierarchies are not well linked, making this relational work more difficult. When seniors from both hierarchies (surgical and nursing) collaborated, communication and engagement was reported as easier. Some surgeons also reported working hard to empower nurses (and sometimes junior doctors) to see their vital role in preventing FTR. This not only required relational and motivational work but also required surgeons and their juniors to respond timeously to reports of aberrant vital signs from nurses, underscoring the importance of the enhanced postoperative monitoring. Senior surgeons were required to engage and motivate their juniors; they reported that exerting power dynamics through the use of the professional hierarchy, was of some use but alone, was an insufficiently effective method of behaviour change.

Theme 2: The 5Rs tools are complex interventions being introduced into complex systems

Theme 2 crossed the boundaries of all four CFIR domains and centred around the long period required for nurses and doctors to be familiarized with and trained on the proposed ‘5Rs’ tools. The co-produced interventions were designed to work in resource-constrained settings but nevertheless required teams to work in different ways. There appeared little question about the potential value of the tools, rather the problems were about the burden of process or behaviour change in already busy work contexts. Nurses in most hospitals had never used an EWS before, nor were many familiar with identifying patients as high-risk who then needed enhanced postoperative monitoring. In some hospitals, postoperative monitoring was very limited at the start of the QIP project, so changing to regular, complete sets of vital signs and calculating an EWS score was viewed as a complex, and burdensome task. With familiarity over time, many nurses reported changing their view but noted this was a steep learning curve, helped in hospitals where senior nurses and surgeons actively coached the staff on EWS use. However, even where buy-in and learning was achieved, some concerns remained about the reliability of EWS score calculations and one site in South Africa began trialling a simpler approach before the end of Phase 1.

Participants described differing challenges for integration of the chart into ward routines, depending on whether observations were charted on paper (five hospitals) or electronically (three hospitals). In paper systems colour printing was seen as an expense hospital management was not always supportive of, leading to lengthy negotiations and sometimes, charts printed in grayscale rather than in colour as they were designed to be. Electronic systems required a different set of negotiations to change existing online charts, which was also reported as challenging and led to nurses having to record observations on both the new paper chart and electronic record in some cases.

Another key challenge was the frequency of staff rotation from the intervention wards, leading to a loss of expertise and the need to repeat training regularly. Teams also reported uncertainty about who was responsible for some new tasks, such as who would complete the ASOS Surgical Risk Calculator score. In sites where scoring was led by the anaesthesia team, the score was reported to be done more consistently but challenges arose in the communication of the risk score to the ward teams. Conversely in sites where scoring was led by the surgeons, this was reported to be less consistently completed. This improved as the project progressed but highlighted the need for well-planned coordination between different teams ahead of commencing implementation of any new tools into a complex system.

Many participants stated the fifth R (reflection on care provided) was very helpful, allowing teams to learn together from cases without fear of blame. However, achieving this learning (rather than blame) culture required a paradigm shift and intensive work by surgical and nursing seniors to foster collaborative and constructive reflective discussions.

Applying the learning for Phase 2

We identified actionable insights for Phase 2 of the 5Rs to Rescue QIP. The clinical components of the QIP were generally well accepted by frontline staff, with their advantage relative to current care being clear. The key changes (and which CFIR constructs they address) are as follows: (i) a substantially longer set-up and preparation period to facilitate more extensive preparation and training on the new tools that will be used (Innovation complexity; Relational connections; Compatibility; Culture, Recipient-centredness; Access to Knowledge & Information); (ii) further emphasis from the inception of the QIP on the relational work needed for coordination between interprofessional teams. This includes but is not limited to empowering nurses by involving them in leadership positions within the QIP (Relational connections; Communication; Motivation; Engaging); (iii) as the complexity of the chosen EWS systems was highlighted, the next iteration of 5Rs to Rescue will use a simpler EWS, graphically indicating patients at risk on a project specific observation chart using a ‘join the dots’ process but with no numerical scoring system (Relative Advantage; Intervention Complexity; Compatibility; Work Infrastructure); and (iv) widespread introduction of the daily team huddles to foster teamwork, improved communication and reflection on practice (Relative Advantage, Relational Connections; Communication; Individual Capability; Reflecting and Evaluating).

Discussion

Summary of principal findings

In this study, we identified the complexities and challenges of developing and testing interventions to reduce surgical FTR in hospitals in Africa. Despite co-producing the initial 5Rs intervention with local teams, and regular, in person expert support from IHI improvement advisors, local project leads reported multiple challenges. Although some practical issues varied, these issues were largely shared across the nine 5Rs hospitals. These centred around the large amount of ongoing relational work involved to engage and train frontline staff to help them understand and ‘fit’ new interventions into existing, already overstretched workflows. Even though engagement activities were identified as important from the outset by the IHI team, the burden of this work was unexpected by local project leads. From this theory-based evaluation, we were able to refine and adapt the initial 5Rs intervention to address the challenges identified. This adapted intervention will be evaluated in a cluster-randomized trial in 20 sites in the four participating countries.

Strengths and limitations

The strengths of this study include the theory-based approach and the range of professional roles of participants. Limitations include the hospitals involved that were either large tertiary or referral hospitals, meaning findings may not be generalizable to smaller hospitals. Our purposive sample was relatively small, although we were guided by the concept of information sufficiency. We were unable to interview frontline HCPs from Tanzania, who may have had different experiences.

Interpretation within the context of the wider literature

There are few multi-centre, multi-component studies focussed on reducing FTR for surgical patients in low- and middle-income countries. One exception is the ASOS-2 study, a pan-African trial of a simple post-operative surveillance intervention [4]. The concurrent CFIR-based process evaluation identified poor intervention fidelity as the likely reason that this trial failed to demonstrate a mortality benefit [5]. Key influences on intervention delivery or non-delivery were within the Inner Setting and Implementation Process domains, highlighting the need to foster a supportive context for change and engaging staff in the improvement process. Such staff engagement must also foster strong interprofessional collaboration to be truly effective, with nurses and doctors (especially at mid and senior leadership levels) discussing, planning, and implementing the 5Rs programme together from the outset. This may be challenging but is vital, given the documented power dynamics and imbalances between nurses and doctors in an African context [27, 28]. Another influence was the perceived lack of intervention advantage amongst some investigators that likely hampered its widespread adoption. Research to reduce FTR for other patient groups in low- and middle-income countries has found broadly similar challenges. An evaluation of the implementation of paediatric EWS in hospitals across Latin America found that efforts to understand frontline staffs’ motivation to and readiness for change were vital for success. They also identified that the work implementation leaders had to undertake to address staff needs in order to achieve successful change [29]. A CFIR-based evaluation of the same paediatric EWS implementation found the complexity of the scoring system to be problematic, requiring significant and time-consuming changes to the local systems (incl. documentation, protocols and processes) for implementation to be successful. This study also reported the significant burden of relational work required to engage and train staff to adopt a new practice which is perceived as difficult [30]. A study of obstetric early warning scores in hospitals in Nigeria also found that staff responded positively to the score, understanding its value to detect deterioration but similarly report that staff rotations and generally high patient-to-nurse ratios were significant barriers to adoption [31].

Implications for policy, practice, and research

Our findings demonstrate the requirement of early-stage testing of QI interventions and programmes when testing in novel contexts, even when the core QI (and clinical) interventions have been used elsewhere. This finding fits with both the Model for Improvements’ Plan Do Study Act approach and the NIHR/MRC Complex intervention framework [10]. The value of formal, theory-based, evaluation of each testing and development phase, to inform iterative intervention development and to maximize learning, both for the project itself and the wider evidence-base, is also highlighted. Relatedly, as QI approaches are more frequently used across Africa, research alongside QI programmes will be required to understand the contextual and cultural adaptations to existing techniques that enhance effectiveness. The commonality of our findings suggests that the human and social experience of doctors and nurses on surgical wards working together to improve care may often share several key features, even when these wards sit within varied meso-level contexts and macro-level health systems. This fits with both a micro-systems perspective [32, 33], and the view that QI is a sociotechnical process [34]. One avenue of enquiry should be around strategies and models to improve communication, coordination, and interprofessional working between doctors and nurses, as a key ‘social’ challenge to QI programme effectiveness. Further work is also required to understand and mitigate the challenges of routine, major nursing staff rotations, which was a common feature of all participating hospitals and poses a real risk to the sustainability of any new intervention implementation and routinization. As in this study, such research is best led and conducted by African clinicians and academics to ensure that developments are both from an African perspective and that they fit into African healthcare contexts [35].

Conclusion

This qualitative study of Phase 1 of the 5Rs to Rescue programme has provided several actionable insights to improve the tools and QI processes required to reduce FTR in African hospitals. During Phase 2 of the QIP the substantial amount of relational work to engage colleagues will be emphasized, key interventions simplified and significantly more time allocated to embedding new processes into complex hospital systems.
