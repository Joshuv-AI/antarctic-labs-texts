Presenter: Dr Charles D. Fraser

Dr Ryan Davies(Dallas, Tex). So, I think it's an interesting paper because I think a lot of us do things as one-offs. And it's good to see a series of patients with sort of a consistent approach to how to reconstruct in transplants. And the other thing I think that's valuable is thinking about warm ischemic time because total ischemic time is what's available in the databases. We spend a lot of time thinking about it, but I think along with your group, I tend to feel the warm ischemic time is actually the thing that's the most deleterious to the heart function. I think in your series there are the cold ischemic—the total ischemic times I think we’re closer to 4 to 5 to 6 hours on some of these cases, but the heart seemed to do well. And I think that's largely because you minimized warm ischemic time. I think the challenge with overall data is often that total ischemic time includes all sorts of things about the length of the operation and how long bleeding, and all sorts of stuff—so I think minimizing warm ischemic time and giving them growth is a valuable goal. With that in mind, the first question I have is have you actually measured the growth of the arterial segments at all directly on any linear basis?

Dr Charles D. Fraser(Aurora, Colo). We haven't because we didn't really think about a prospective imaging protocol. For the pulmonary arteries, it's a little easier because we have right heart caths very consistently, and so we have more right heart cath data. But after the 1-year mark where the patients do get left heart caths, we don't have consistent data, so we're relying on their growth, their echocardiogram data to kind of deduced growth but don't have a definitive protocol.

Dr Davies. It'd be nice to find out. In terms of warm—this is not necessarily directly into your paper. But in terms of minimizing warm ischemic time, I tend to prefer to do transplants with left atrium and the aorta and do everything else with a new heart beating. Is that how your group tends to do it?

Dr Fraser. It's case by case. I think typically our strategy is left atrium, inferior vena cava, aorta, and then the pulmonary artery is dependent on the complexity at the time of the aorta. So, I think sometimes we will, and sometimes if we feel it's an oversized heart that's going to make that difficult, we'll just at least get the pulmonary artery started before taking the clamp off.

Dr Davies. That's great. And then the last question I had was really thinking about taking this idea of minimizing the implant technical challenge of actually putting the heart into a little bit of a—I think there's more and more hyper ventricular assist devices (VADs) being performed. And VADs to get single-ventricle patients to transplant—I think we've started to lean toward actually doing more and more reconstruction at the time of the VAD, so I would probably rather do a Norwood VAD than a [inaudible] VAD because then when the patient gets to the transplant, you're just plugging the heart in for the most part as opposed to taking vents down and reconstructing the arch at the time of transplant. Have you guys tried to do that at all to really minimize the work at the transplant?

Dr Fraser. I don't think we've done a hybrid VAD at our center, but we definitely address—in particular the pulmonary arteries at the time of VAD and we've done that recently I think, so it's something that we consider case by case when we're assessing for transplant.

Dr Davies. And great work.

Dr Fraser. Thanks.
