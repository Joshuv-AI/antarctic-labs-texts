Are we Measuring Knee Proprioception Correctly? A Case‐Control Study of Joint Reposition Sense and Reflex Latencies for Measuring Proprioception in Knee Hemophilic Arthropathy
Authors: Pilar Alberola Zorrilla, Rafael Escamilla Ugarte, Sofía Pérez Alenda, Ana Chimeno Hernández, Daniel Sánchez Zuriaga
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsKnee proprioception deficits have been found in several types of knee injuries using different measurement techniques. In some cases, such as hemophilic arthropathy, such deficits have been largely inferred from the results of gait and postural stability tests. Hemophilic arthropathy is supposed to produce balance and neuromuscular control impairments associated to knee proprioception deficits. However, there is a lack of studies focused specifically on the measurement of joint proprioception in this group of patients. The aim of this case‐control study was to analyze local knee proprioception through joint reposition sense error and reflex muscle latencies in subjects with different degrees of hemophilic arthropathy.Methods11 healthy subjects and 13 patients with hemophilic arthropathy (7 with a lower grade and 6 with a higher grade arthropathy) participated in the study. Knee proprioceptive acuity was measured by means of: (1) the repositioning error between two repetitions of complete extension and mid‐flexed knee positions, and (2) the onset latencies of rectus and biceps femoris reflex activations during sudden knee loading. This study has been presented by the STROBE guidelines.ResultsNo statistical differences between groups were found either in joint repositioning error or onset latencies of reflex activation. The present study shows that knee proprioception could be preserved on patients with hemophilic arthropathy. It is possible that past measurements of knee proprioceptive acuity in these and other groups of patients have not been specific enough.ConclusionOur results suggest the necessity of a reevaluation of the association of some types of knee injuries with knee proprioceptive alterations.

Introduction

Position and movement sense are two of the main components of proprioception. Receptors on the joint capsule and articular ligaments are crucial to integrate movement and position proprioceptive information [1]. These receptors are also the afferent arm of ligamento‐muscular reflexes [2], which are important to keep joint stability against sudden loads [3]. Such responses of the motor pathway show the complex interplay between the sensory pathways and the motor efferent systems, and are included in a wider definition of the proprioceptive system which comprises sensoriomotor interactions [4]

Proprioceptive systems in the lower limb have a critical role in motor behavior during complex tasks such as walking or balance [5]. Among all load‐bearing joints, proprioceptive acuity in the knee has been probably the most studied. Reflex control of knee movements depends on sensoriomotor mechanisms that are initiated and modulated by mechanoreceptor afferents. Several types of mechanoreceptors have been found on the joint capsule, periarticular tendons and cruciate and collateral ligaments, such as Ruffini corpuscles, involved primary on stretching and movement sensations, and free nerve endings, which are mostly involved in the perception of pain and joint inflammation [6, 7]. Proprioceptive impairments have been described after cruciate ligament injury [8], articular cartilage damage [9] or knee osteoarthritis [10]. However, measurement methodologies for proprioception are highly variable among previous studies, as well as the results obtained [11, 12].

The measurement of joint reposition acuity, which quantifies the ability of the subject to reproduce previous positions, is one of the most usual techniques for the specific assessment of joint proprioception [13]. The reproduction of knee flexion angles is altered in anterior cruciate ligament injuries [14] and knee osteoarthritis [15]. Knee repositioning sense has shown a high reliability to discriminate patients with proprioceptive abnormalities in knee osteoarthritis [16]. Knee osteoarthritis and anterior cruciate ligament rupture patients with a greater loss of joint reposition sense have higher levels of pain and worse physical function scores than patients with better proprioception [17, 18]. Proprioceptive acuity is restored following the reconstruction of anterior cruciate ligament injuries [19]. This suggests a possible relationship between knee proprioceptive acuity and the degree of impairment of joint health and function.

The measurement of reflex latencies after a sudden stimulus is also useful as an objective assessment tool for knee proprioception, one which provides information about the specific efferent motor responses associated to the sensoriomotor system [4]. The measured delay in reflex response of the corresponding muscle has been considered a measure of knee proprioceptive ability [20]. It has long been known that patients with anterior cruciate ligament deficiencies have alterations in the reflex latencies of the knee muscles [21]. Such alterations have also been found in patients with patellofemoral pain [22], and have been proposed as an objective assessment of the efficacy of conservative treatment and the need for surgery [20].

One pathology affecting the knee which has traditionally been associated with deficits in proprioception is hemophilic arthropathy. Chronic hemophilic arthropathy is associated to important alterations of articular structures specially on knees, elbows and ankles, such as chronic inflammation and hypertrophy in the synovium, destruction of joint cartilage, iron deposition on joint surfaces (hemosiderin) and changes in bone tissue [23]. In the knee joint, Pförringer [24] showed histological alterations in the cruciate ligaments after hemarthrosis. The cruciate ligaments are important proprioceptive structures, which have an afferent function in ligamento‐muscular reflexes of the hamstring and quadriceps muscles [25]. Nevertheless, specific evidence about proprioceptive alterations in patients with hemophilic arthropathy is scarce. There is only an isolated study by Hilberg et al. [26], which found possible alterations of knee joint repositioning sense in these patients. Cruz‐Montecinos et al. studied alterations in the neuromuscular control strategies on daily living activities of patients with hemophilic arthropathy, such as gait [27], balance [28] or dual tasks [29]. However, these are all complex tasks, which involve more than one joint and would not only be affected by the hypothetical loss of proprioceptive acuity. The same authors warn that their results are not entirely correlated to the severity of the arthropathy due to the implication of more than one affected joint and the presence of persistent pain [27]. It would seem that more specific evidence is needed to confirm the presence of knee proprioceptive alterations in hemophilic arthropathy, and their possible relationship to the degree of knee joint health and functional status impairment in these patients.

The aim of this study is thus the analysis of local proprioception at the knee, assessing joint reposition sense error and the onset latencies of muscular reflex activation. These assessments will be performed in patients with different degrees of hemophilic arthropathy, and their results compared to healthy control subjects.

Methods

Participants

This study was a case‐control study comparing healthy participants and people with hemophilic arthropathy, conducted in 2024. Participants signed a written consent after they were informed about the study, which was approved by the University Ethics Committee on Human Research and included data and photographs publishing permissions (assigned study number UV‐INV_ETICA‐2515070). This study was performed in line with the principles of the STrengthening the Reporting of OBservational studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines (Supporting Information S1).

A convenience sample of thirteen adult male patients with knee hemophilic arthropathy and severe hemophilia A participated in the study. They all had received prophylactic treatment in the 16 h before the experiment. Eleven male adult healthy subjects with no history of knee pathology formed the control group.

Exclusion criteria were: (1) neurological illness with muscle spasticity or hypotonia, (2) any significant previous surgery of the knee, which excluded the assessment of the affected joint, (3) use of artificial cardiac pacemakers, which may be interfered by the electrogoniometer, (4) vascular, rheumatic or any other kind of disease that may restrict the performance of sudden loading tests. Additional exclusion criteria for the arthropathy group were: (5) knee hemarthrosis during the 4 months before the measurements, (6) knee range of motion below 25°.

Instrumentation

Angular displacement of the knee in the sagittal plane was recorded using a Liberty 240/16 electromagnetic motion capture system (Polhemus Inc., Colchester, VT, USA). This apparatus uses a low frequency magnetic field generated by an electromagnetic source, on a plastic platform at knee level for each participant. One sensor attached to the dorsal skin of the foot between both ankle malleoli provided positional data, with a sampling frequency of 240 Hz.

Electromyographic (EMG) activity was recorded by an EMG100C Biopac module (Biopac Systems Inc., Goleta, CA, USA), using pregelled disposable silver‐silver chloride (Ag/AgCl) surface disk electrodes. Rectus and biceps femoris EMG electrodes were located following the recommendations of the Surface Electromyography for Non‐invasive Assessment of Muscles (SENIAM) project [30] (Figure 1). A reference electrode was placed on the fibular head. The distances between electrodes, their relationship with the underlying muscle fibers, the processing of the electromyographic signal and the synchronization techniques with the knee angular displacement recordings have been described elsewhere [31].

Outcome Measures

All participants completed the International Physical Activity Questionnaire Short Form (IPAQ‐SF) [32]. Knee joint health status was evaluated with the Hemophilia Joint Health Score (HJHS). HJHS has been shown to significantly differentiate between disease severity groups [33], with an estimated cutoff point between mild and severe knee hemophilic arthropathy of 6 [34, 35]. Considering these cutoff values, the median of HJHS knee scores was calculated for the arthropathy subjects of the study, and subjects with HJHS below the median (with a lower grade of arthropathy, HJHS between 1 and 5, n = 7) were compared to those above (with a higher grade of arthropathy, HJHS between 9 and 14, n = 6).

Participants remained seated with their back supported in a comfortable starting position, with both feet hanging freely over the border of the seat. They were strapped to their seats at the most proximal level of the thighs, to avoid any involvement of the hip joint. In the group with knee hemophilic arthropathy, joint repositioning acuity and reflex latencies were measured on both legs. On the control group, tests were performed on the dominant leg, and the most affected leg on hemophilic arthropathy participants according to HJHS was used for comparisons. The preferred leg to kick a ball determined lower limb dominance [29]. Subjects were blindfolded during both tests, and additionally given headphones with relaxing music in the sudden release intervention, to remove visual and auditory cues [3] (Figure 2). All tests were repeated twice. The values of the two repetitions were averaged for all calculations, and used to calculate the within‐day reliability of the measurements.

Starting position. (A) Participants remained seated with a backrest, strapped at the most proximal level of the thighs, and blindfolded during both tests. (B) Muscular reflex activation test. The leg (1) is in full relaxed extension using a strap around the ankle (2), held by an adjustable weight (3) through a pulley system (4).

Joint position sense was measured by assessing the participants' ability to reproduce two positions. Participants began the test in their seated starting position with legs hanging freely over the edge of the seat at approximately 90° of knee flexion, sometimes less if they had limitations in their knee flexion range. They were asked first to extend their knees as far as they comfortably could. This movement, from free‐hanging flexion to full extension, enabled the participants to gauge a mid‐flexed position, at the point they felt had covered “halfway” the range from free‐hanging flexion to full knee extension. The fully extended and “halfway” positions were maintained for 3 s and repeated twice.

At the starting position of the muscular reflex activation test the participants' leg was kept in full relaxed extension using a strap around the ankle, held by an adjustable weight through a pulley system [36] (Figure 2). The participants were asked to relax their lower limb muscles as much as possible, so that their leg rested completely on the strap hanging from the pulley. After a variable period of time, the weight load was released resulting in a sudden and unexpected drop of the leg, which caused a reflex peak of activation of rectus and biceps femoris muscles.

Data Processing

Two experienced researchers, who were blinded about the origin of the data (control/hemophilia groups), analyzed all the variables independently to calculate the interobserver reliability of the measurements.

The position of the leg and the EMG activation of rectus and biceps femoris during the joint reposition and sudden release tests were calculated from graphic representations (Figures 3 and 4). The mean absolute difference in degrees of flexion between successive attempts at reproducing the “halfway” and fully extended positions was used as a measure of repositioning error. During the sudden release test, the onset of movement was identified from the abrupt movement of the leg sensor from its baseline starting position. From the EMG recordings, the onset latency was determined as the time between the sudden displacement of the leg and the onset of reflex activity. This onset was defined by visual inspection of the EMG curves as the start of the first peak in EMG activity, following the perturbation, that exceeded the peak amplitude of the baseline activity [3].

An example of the graphs for the sudden release/reflex activation test. The thin curve represents knee flexion motion and the thick curve rectus femoris EMG activation. MATLAB 2020b (MathWorks Inc., Natick, MA, USA) was used for all data analysis.

Statistical Analysis

The low number of subjects in each group made difficult the assumption of data normality. Accordingly, a nonparametric Mann–Whitney U test was performed to compare control subjects with arthropathy patients as a whole. The Kruskal–Wallis one‐way analysis of variance test was used to show any significant differences in the variables between control subjects and high and low severity arthropathy subjects. Finally, the nonparametric Wilcoxon test was used to compare both knees on the hemophilic arthropathy group.

An a priori analysis of statistical power and effect size was made, estimating effect size by means of Cohen's d. Statistical power was calculated using the G*Power 3.1.9.7 software (Heinrich‐Heine‐Universität, Düsseldorf, Germany).

The within‐day and interobserver reliabilities of all the measurements were evaluated by means of the (2,1) intra‐class correlation coefficient (ICC) [37].

An α level below 0.05 was considered significant for all analyses. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 28.0 for Windows (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

A priori calculations of effect size were based on the results of published work which compared similar dependent variables (knee repositioning error) in participants with and without knee hemophilic arthropathy [26]. The resulting Cohen′s d was 1.015, which with the sample sizes of our convenience samples was equivalent to a statistical power of 0.8 for an alpha level of 0.05 and a critical t value of 1.721.

Interobserver ICC values were excellent for all the joint repositioning error variables, with values ranging from 0.96 (95% confidence interval 0.92–0.98) to 1 (95% confidence interval 1–1). Interobserver ICC values were also good to excellent for all the reflex latencies variables, with values ranging from 0.92 (95% confidence interval 0.81–0.97) to 0.98 (95% confidence interval 0.95–0.99). Within‐day ICC values were excellent for the degrees of knee flexion of the extended and half‐flexed positions, with values ranging from 0.96 (95% confidence interval 0.90–0.98) to 0.99 (95% confidence interval 0.98–1).

Within‐day ICC was 0.76 for the repositioning error in extension, with confidence interval values ranging from moderate to excellent (95% confidence interval 0.50–0.91). The within‐day ICC for the halfway repositioning error was 0.47, with confidence interval values ranging from poor to moderate (95% confidence interval 0.14–0.76). When the values for the halfway repositioning error were calculated separately for the arthropathy and control groups, patients with hemophilic arthropathy showed an ICC of 0.77, with poor to excellent confidence interval values (95% confidence interval 0.21–0.93), whereas the reliability for the control subjects was poor (ICC = 0.35). Within‐day ICC was 0.76 for the reflex latency of biceps femoris, with confidence interval values ranging from poor to good (95% confidence interval 0.42–0.90). The within‐day ICC for the rectus femoris reflex latency was 0.62, with confidence interval values ranging from poor to good (95% confidence interval 0.10–0.84) [38].

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study that performs a specific analysis of knee proprioception in patients with different hemophilic arthropathy grades, using both joint repositioning error and reflex onset latencies of muscle activity without any added loads, and testing the reliability of the measurements. The results of the present study did not show differences in either joint repositioning errors or reflex onset latencies between patients with a lower and higher grade of hemophilic arthropathy and healthy controls, or between both knee joints in the hemophilic arthropathy group. This could indicate the preservation of proprioception in this population, and its non‐affectation independent of the degree of arthropathy.

Joint Repositioning Errors

The values of repositioning error we found in our study for all groups were similar to the ones reported for healthy control subjects in studies about knee proprioception in osteoarthritis (2 ± 2 degrees of flexion) [16] or anterior cruciate ligament injury (3 ± 1 degrees) [14].

Proprioception has generally been thought to be affected in subjects with knee hemophilic arthropathy. Joint repositioning sense was specifically studied in patients with hemophilic arthropathy by Hilberg et al. [26], but their work has several issues. Their tests were performed on a pedal, which could have biased the proprioceptive information due to the pressure exerted on the device. Hilberg et al. used pre‐stablished angles to be reproduced by the subjects, whereas in our study participants gauged by themselves which position they considered to be mid‐flexion [39]. The evaluation of preferred ranges may be a better way of assessing proprioceptive acuity, as it avoids potential biases caused by the participants' understanding of the specific angle they were asked to reproduce. Previous studies using also preferred ranges of knee flexion found good reliability for joint reposition acuity measurements, in patients with knee osteoarthritis [40].

Hilberg et al. [26] recorded neither limb dominance nor any standardized score for the level of hemophilic arthropathy. Despite having a similar sample size to the present study and also using non‐parametric statistical tests, their results were reported as mean and standard deviation, instead of median and interquartile range. The authors performed multiple significance tests (8 measurements per subject), but only found significant differences in two ranges of motion (0⁰ and 100⁰ in the left knee). Given that they did not apply any adjustment of the significance level to avoid the accumulation of type I error, the relevance of these isolated significant differences is questionable. On the other hand, their posturography variables did not show statistically significant between‐group differences, unlike the single‐leg balance test. However, the single‐leg balance test involves the entire lower limb and vestibulocochlear elements, so its results may not relate specifically to knee joint proprioception. Besides knee proprioceptive acuity, knee‐related postural stability deficiencies have been explained by several factors, such as knee pain [41] or decreased muscle strength in knee osteoarthritis patients [42]. Lower limb balance or posturographic alterations do not necessarily equal knee proprioceptive impairment, although some previous studies [9] have assimilated one to the other.

In our study, participants with a higher grade of arthropathy consistently maintained a greater flexion angle in the joint position test. This could be explained by the presence of flexion contractures, which are common after repeated bleeding episodes and subsequent immobilization [43]. The presence of contractures in hemophilic arthropathy is another reason that supports the use of preferred flexion ranges in this type of studies, rather than a specific predetermined range whose achievement could be limited by the patient′s contractures.

Reflex Latencies

In the present study the onset latency of reflex muscle contractions during a sudden stimulus showed no statistical differences between groups. As for the values of reflex latencies after a sudden perturbation, it is difficult to find studies comparable to ours, since the type of perturbations applied varies greatly. In practically all the studies, perturbations are applied with the patient standing and with the lower limb under load, making it difficult for the muscle to be completely relaxed. Our reflex latency values for the quadriceps and hamstrings are similar for example to those found by Shultz et al. [44] in the male population after a sudden knee perturbation, with latencies ranging from 95 to 100 ms for the quadriceps and 60–80 for the hamstrings. However, it is difficult to extrapolate these results to ours, since the perturbation was applied in a different direction and with the lower limb under load.

Our results show long‐latency reflexes, ranging from 50 to 150 ms [45, 46]. These longer latencies, unlike short latencies that may serve a protective function, could be involved in the generation of neuromuscular synergies [47]. Cruz‐Montecinos et al. analyzed neuromuscular synergies in complex tasks such as gait [27], postural control [28] or unipedal balance with dual tasks [29] on patients with knee hemophilic arthropathy. Their studies on gait demonstrated a reduced complexity of neural control in hemophilic subjects, but these alterations were not related to the severity of the arthropathy measured by the HJHS scale. Moreover, measurements in our study were more specifically focused on knee proprioception than those of Cruz‐Montecinos et al. Balance measurement results frequently show poor test‐retest reliability and weak construct validity between tests [48]. Gait is an activity that involves variable loading and requires multi‐articular mobility with cyclic muscle activation. The changes that these patients show in their patterns of muscle activation, motion and loading may be related to other factors apart from an altered knee proprioception, such as pain and years of arthropathy [27]. Moreover, unlike our study, Cruz‐Montecinos tests were all performed in weightbearing conditions, and it has been suggested that the results from weightbearing tests on the knee could be confounded by many factors apart from proprioceptive deficits, such as lack of muscle strength, knee pain or lack of balance in standing [11], or even by differences in weight or body mass index between the study groups, which could alter the loading forces acting on the knee joint. Thus, the presence of alterations in neuromuscular coordination during complex weight‐bearing activities, such as walking, does not necessarily imply an alteration in knee proprioception, both in hemophilic arthropathy and in other joint disorders, such as knee osteoarthritis [10].

Explanation of Results

Given that repositioning error and muscle reflex latencies are mediated by ligamento‐muscular proprioceptive structures, our results could indicate the maintenance of knee proprioception in patients with hemophilic arthropathy. Cruciate ligaments are the knee joint structures with the highest number of mechanoreceptors, and have therefore been considered the most important in the maintenance of proprioception [6]. In vitro studies have shown histological alterations in the cruciate ligaments after hemarthrosis, with an 8 to 16 weeks recovery period depending on the associated synovial damage [24]. In the 4 months before our study, none of the participants with hemophilia had suffered any hemarthrosis in the knee. Any potential damage to proprioceptive joint structures due to hemarthrosis may well have been overcome by the time of our measurements. This may not be the case in other studies that have found alterations in specific measures of knee proprioception in pathologies such as injuries with surgery of the anterior cruciate ligament [8] or knee osteoarthritis [40]. In these studies, the patients' injuries were recent and showed active symptoms.

In our study, all the participants in both arthropathy groups were physically active. This could also contribute to the good proprioceptive abilities demonstrated by these subjects, as it has been shown that physical exercise improves neuromuscular control [10, 49] and even in some cases direct proprioception measurements [50]. However, the results of therapeutic exercise aimed to knee proprioceptive training have been studied mostly in knee osteoarthritis patients, and are still contradictory [10, 51].

The present study shows that knee proprioception could be preserved on patients with hemophilic arthropathy, despite the traditional assumption that these patients show knee proprioceptive alterations. It is possible that past measurements of knee proprioceptive acuity in these and other groups of patients have not been specific enough. Our results suggest the necessity of a reevaluation of the association of some types of knee injuries with knee proprioceptive alterations.

Limitations and Future Research

The main limitation of this study was the sample size, which makes it difficult to draw any definitive conclusions from our results. Nevertheless, a priori calculations showed an estimated statistical power of 0.8 calculated from our sample sizes and the effect size of the study by Hilberg et al. [26], the most similar to ours. This implies that our study has enough statistical power to detect the same differences that Hilberg et al. [26] found, if these differences were indeed present. In fact, Hilberg et al. [26] and other previous similar studies [3, 19] used even smaller sample sizes. The present study is as powerful statistically as most previous studies on the subject, and shows specific knee proprioception measurements carried out using two different techniques.

Conclusions

Our results are at least suggestive that the widespread belief that patients with knee hemophilic arthropathy have joint proprioception deficits, a belief based on the studies discussed in our work and which significantly influences the rehabilitation treatment of these patients, could be mistaken. More studies with larger groups of participants would be necessary to further study the possible preservation of knee proprioceptive acuity in patients with hemophilic arthropathy.
