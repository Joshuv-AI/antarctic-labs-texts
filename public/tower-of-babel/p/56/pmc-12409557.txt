AGRL-DSE: Adaptive Graph Representation Learning on a Heterogeneous Graph for Drug Side Effect Prediction
Authors: He Tan, Xiangmin Ji, Chen-Zhen Xu, Xiaoyu Zhao, Jie Hou, Mao Liu, Yan Ren
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Identifying side effects is crucial for drug development and postmarket surveillance. Several computational methods based on graph neural networks (GNNs) have been developed, leveraging the topological structure and node attributes in graphs with promising results. However, existing heterogeneous-network-based approaches often fail to fully capture the complex structure and rich semantic information within these networks. Furthermore, the oversmoothing problem in GNNs remains a major challenge. In this study, we propose AGRL-DSE, a novel adaptive graph representation learning framework designed to enhance node-feature learning for predicting drug side effects. First, we construct a heterogeneous graph with intra- and interlayer connections to represent similarities and associations between drugs and side effects, capturing hidden topological relationships in heterogeneous contexts. Second, we integrate three GNN modules in AGRL-DSE, graph convolutional network (GCN), graph sample and aggregation (GraphSAGE), and graph attention network (GAT) at the graph, node, and edge levels, respectively, with the aim of capturing semantic information at different levels in graph data in a hierarchical manner, gradually extracting and enhancing the features of the graph. Additionally, we introduce an adaptive layer attention mechanism that dynamically assigns weights to each layer’s features to achieve adaptive fusion of multilevel features, thereby automatically adjusting the contribution of each layer to the final embedding. Experimental results demonstrate that AGRL-DSE outperforms state-of-the-art predictive models in both hot- and cold-start scenarios, highlighting its superiority and generalizability. AGRL-DSE’s ability to capture complex relationships and provide deeper insights into drug–side effect interactions could transform drug evaluation, monitoring, and prescription, leading to better health outcomes and more efficient drug development processes.

Introduction

Drug side effects, or adverse drug reactions (ADRs), are harmful and unexpected outcomes that occur when patients take medication. ADRs not only hinder the development of new drugs but also lead to the removal of marketed drugs. Hence, the safety concerns associated with ADRs have attracted considerable attention. Early and accurate identification of drug side effects is crucial throughout the drug life cycle. However, traditional toxicity testing and clinical trials face challenges such as high cost, significant time consumption, and small sample sizes. Consequently, computational methods have emerged as key innovations to support effective drug development and ensure reasonable drug use.

Recently, machine learning and deep learning have become the most widely used computational methods for predicting drug side effects. These techniques utilize various types of data and databases to learn about drug side effects from past experiences. Traditional machine learning methods, such as random forest, support vector machines (SVM), and logistic regression, use drug properties and labeled ADRs in relevant databases to build models and make predictions. For example, Ding et al. introduced a drug–side effect association predictor using graph-based semisupervised learning, which constructs multiple kernels from the drug and side effect spaces and linearly weighs them using the multiple kernel learning (MKL) algorithm. Cakir et al. applied a random forest method with iterative feature selection to predict common side effects caused by chemotherapy drugs. Galeano and Paccanaro integrated the SIDER 4.1 database, standardized the associations into five frequency classes, and predicted the drug–side effect frequency using non-negative matrix factorization. In subsequent research, Galeano and Paccanaro proposed the geometric self-expressive model (GSEM) to learn globally optimal self-representations for predicting unknown side effects of drugs with limited clinical trial data. Although traditional machine learning methods have yielded promising results, they struggle to extract deep latent features, have limited interpretability, and cannot be used in cold-start experimental scenarios, which restricts their applicability. Additionally, critical information, such as the chemical structure of drugs, is often underutilized.

Deep learning-based methods have gained significant traction, with many researchers incorporating deep learning techniques into drug-related association networks. Compared to traditional machine learning, deep learning offers a clear advantage: it can automatically learn potential relationships between nodes in a network using diverse information. , This makes data processing more efficient in drug development, leading to significant successes. For example, Wang et al. used a multilayer perceptron (MLP) model with two hidden layers to predict adverse drug events based on textual information and 17 drug chemical and physical properties. This approach achieved an area under the curve (AUC) of 84.4%, outperforming other machine learning methods such as Gaussian naïve Bayes (NB), SVM, and probability matrix factorization (PMF). Das et al. proposed a deep neural network (DNN) for predicting ADEs based on 17 drug molecule properties, drug functions, and chemical 1D structures. Xu et al. introduced HSTrans, which treats drugs and side effects as sets of substructures, using a transformer encoder for unified substructure embedding and a convolutional neural network (CNN) in interaction modules for association prediction. Graph neural networks (GNNs) have demonstrated advanced performance in several tasks by learning topological graph structures and transferring features between nodes and edges. − Their hierarchical frameworks can overcome the limitations of traditional GNNs in heterogeneous graphs. , Recently, GNNs have been successfully applied to predict drug side effects. Zhao et al. integrated drug features and introduced a multiview data model, MGPred, using an attention mechanism to extract association information. Xu et al. proposed an encoder-decoder framework, DSGAT, to predict drug–side effect associations and frequencies. This framework employed graph attention networks (GATs) to extract drug features from molecular graphs and side effect features from similarity graphs. Jin et al. proposed the A3Net network model, which combines graph attention learning, cross-attention interaction, and feature aggregation into a unified framework to explore intrinsic relationships. Despite these advances, current studies face significant limitations in capturing feature information on drugs and side effects from spatial neighborhoods, as well as their interaction information. Existing methods fail to deeply integrate various connection relationships or neighbor topologies of targets and nodes and still suffer from the oversmoothing problem.

In this paper, we introduce a novel and effective adaptive graph representation learning framework, AGRL-DSE, designed to improve drug–side effect prediction. The advantages of our study are as follows:

First, a heterogeneous graph is constructed containing drug attributes, semantic information about side effects, and association relationships to capture hidden topological information between drugs and side effects. Our heterogeneous network incorporates both intra- and interlayer connections, differing from existing methods that treat them separately, thus offering new insights into potential drug–side effect interactions.

Second, the graph learning process includes multiple graph neural network modules, which leverages the graph convolutional network (GCN), graph sample and aggregation (GraphSAGE), and graph attention network (GAT) at the graph, node, and edge levels, respectively, with the aim of capturing semantic information at different levels in graph data in a hierarchical manner. This process gradually extracts and enhances the features of the graph within a heterogeneous network of drug side effects.

Third, we employ an adaptive layer attention mechanism, which dynamically assigns weights to each layer’s features to achieve adaptive fusion of multilevel features, rather than using simple averaging or concatenation. This mechanism can automatically adjust the contribution of each layer to the final embedding, thereby enhancing the flexibility and expressiveness of the model and alleviating oversmoothing problem.

Finally, we conduct comprehensive comparative experiments using the SIDER and FAERS data sets. AGRL-DSE outperformed other models in both hot- and cold-start scenarios, demonstrating its superior performance and generalizability.

Methods

Data Set

In this study, we used the benchmark data set from Galeano and Paccanaro, which integrates the SIDER database version 4.1. This data set contains 37,441 frequency classes, including 759 drugs and 994 adverse effects. Side effect frequencies were categorized into five classes: very frequent (frequency = 5), frequent (frequency = 4), infrequent (frequency = 3), rare (frequency = 2), and very rare (frequency = 1). The distribution of these frequency classes is as follows: 11.12% very frequent, 47.46% frequent, 26.92% infrequent, 11.29% rare, and 3.21% very rare. We represent the frequency of side effects using a rating matrix M, where a nonzero value indicates a known frequency for a drug–side effect pair, and 0 indicates no information. The rating matrix is sparse, with only 4.97% nonzero elements.

To further evaluate the model’s robustness and generalizability, we also used the FDA Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) as an independent test set. FAERS, the largest spontaneous reporting system, collects ADR reports from healthcare professionals, manufacturers, and patients, with data updated quarterly. In this study, we used FAERS data from the third quarter of 2014 to the first quarter of 2022, which included 1073 drugs, 893 side effects, and 141,752 interaction associations.

AGRL-DSE Framework

We propose a novel framework called AGRL-DSE (Figure ) to improve drug side effect prediction. A heterogeneous graph is constructed through the drug denoising similarity matrix, side effect denoising similarity matrix, and drug–side effect associations, which represent similarities and associations using both intra- and interlayer connections. We introduce a novel graph representation learning framework that stacks different types of graph neural network layers (GCN, GraphSAGE, and GAT) to hierarchically capture semantic information at different levels within the graph data, gradually extracting and enhancing the features of the graph. The relationship between layers is progressive. Specifically, the heterogeneous graph is first passed into the GCN module, which aggregates information from all adjacent nodes, extracts structural features of the graph, and captures its topological relationships. Next, the output of the GCN module is then passed as input to the GraphSAGE module, which further refines node features through sampling and aggregation, effectively collecting feature data from the local neighborhood of nodes to generate informative node representations. Then, the output of the GraphSAGE module is passed to the GAT module, which dynamically adjusts the importance of neighboring nodes through an attention mechanism, captures multiscale topological information in heterogeneous graphs, and enhances the representation of key relationships. Through this hierarchical design, semantic information at different levels in graph data is captured in layers. Additionally, to coordinate the contributions of different layer features, adaptive layer attention assigns different weights to various convolution layers, enhancing feature representations and improving the model’s predictive performance. AGRL-DSE enhances the modeling of complex topological relationships through the complementarity of heterogeneous modules while alleviating the problem of oversmoothing. Finally, the adjacency matrix is reconstructed using an inner product operation, and prediction scores are calculated using a sigmoid function.

Construction of the Drug Side Effect Heterogeneous Graph

Chemical–Chemical Similarities

The chemical structure of each drug can be depicted as a graph, where the vertices correspond to the atoms and the edges denote the chemical bonds. We obtained Simplified Molecular Input Line Entry Specification (SMILES) data for each drug from DrugBank and PubChem and used the open-source chemical information package RDKit to calculate feature vectors for each drug. For a given drug d i (i = 1,2, ···, m), its graph representation can be denoted as D i = (F i , A i ), where m is the number of drugs, Fi∈Rui×M and Ai∈Rui×ui are the feature matrix and the adjacent matrix of the drug d i, respectively. Here, u i is the number of atoms in drug d i and M is the number of the atom features. The atom features are represented as a 109-dimensional multi-hot vector (M = 109), which includes chemical and topological properties, with each attribute encoded as a one-hot vector. The atom features are then constructed by concatenating multiple one-hot vectors. After converting drugs into feature vectors, we used the Jaccard similarity measure to calculate pairwise similarities between drugs. The drug similarity can be represented by an m × m square matrix S d, where S d denotes the similarity between drug d i and drug d j .

To reduce the influence of noise on node representation learning, we constructed a k-nearest neighbor (KNN) graph based on the similarity matrix. The adjacency matrix of the drug KNN graph is denoted as Ad∈Rm×m . The entry A ij d of A d is defined as Aijd={1,ifdj∈Νk(di)0,otherwise 1 where N k (d i ) represents the first k neighbors corresponding to drug d i , which are the k drugs with the highest similarity to drug d i , excluding d i .

Side Effect Semantic Similarities

Side effect descriptors, available from the Adverse Drug Reaction Classification System (ADReCS), integrate data from sources such as DrugBank, PubChem, and SIDER. Each side effect has a unique identifier (ID) and may be associated with multiple tree numbers. Side effect descriptors are represented as directed acyclic graphs (DAG). We used the DAG-based semantic similarity approach proposed by Wang et al. to calculate the similarity between side effects. Each side effect s i (i = 1,2, ···, n) is represented by DAG­(s i ) = (N(s i ), E(s i )), where N(s i ) is the vertex set that stores the nodes representing the side effect and its ancestor side effect descriptors and E(s i ) stores the edges connecting parent nodes to child nodes. Side effects with more common ancestors are assumed to have higher semantic similarities. We computed the semantic similarities between side effects of s i and s j based on this DAG structure.

Similar denoising is applied to side effect similarities, resulting in a square matrix S s of size n × n, where S ij s represents the similarity between side effects s i and s j . Each entry A ij s in A s is defined as follows: Aijs={1,ifsj∈Νk(si)0,otherwise 2 where N k (s i ) represents the first k neighbors corresponding to the side effect s i , and N k (s i ) does not include s i .

Heterogeneous Network

We constructed a heterogeneous network based on drug–side effect associations, drug–drug similarities, and side–side effect similarities. First, we denote drug–side effect associations as a binary matrix A∈Rm×n , where m and n denote the number of drugs and side effects, respectively. Each element A ij ∈ {0,1} in the matrix corresponds to a specific drug d i or side effect s j . If drug d i is associated with side effect s j , then A ij = 1; otherwise, A ij = 0.

We define the pairwise drug similarities as the similarity matrix S d, with S ij d as its (i, j)­th entry, and the pairwise similarities between side effects were denoted as the similarity matrix S s, with S ij s as its (i, j)­th entry. To accelerate computational efficiency and improve the rate of model convergence, we normalized the similarity matrices using SdS=Dd−1/2SdDd−1/2 and SsS=Ds−1/2SsDs−1/2 , where D d = diag­(Σ j S ij d ) and D s = diag­(Σ j S ij s). Finally, we define the heterogeneous network using the adjacency matrix. AH=[SdSAATSsS] 3

We then initialized the drugs and side effects embeddings using H(0)=[0AAT0] . A penalty factor μ is introduced to control the contribution of similarities during the propagation process of the GNN. Specifically, the input graph G is expressed as G=[μSdSAATμSsS] 4

Model Architecture

GNNs have gained significant attention due to their ability to analyze complex graph-structured data. They have been widely applied in drug discovery and related fields. , For heterogeneous networks, such as those representing drug–side effect associations, drugs, side effects, and their mutual relationships are naturally modeled using a graph structure. In this study, we propose an AGRL-DSE framework for drug side effect prediction at the graph, node, and edge levels, incorporating GCN, GraphSAGE, GAT, and adaptive layer attention.

GCN Module

GCNs are variants of graph-based convolutional neural networks that effectively extract global feature information and capture the topological spatial structure of nodes in a graph. They can be categorized into two types: spatial-domain-based and spectral-domain-based. Spatial-domain GCNs define a graph convolution operator based on node spatial relationships, simulating the convolution operation in local regions of the graph structure. Spectral-domain GCNs map the graph structure to the frequency domain space using the graph Fourier transform, performing convolution in the time domain by multiplying in the frequency domain.

The GCN layer used in AGRL-DSE is a spectral-domain-based GCN. Specifically, taking the adjacency matrix G corresponding to the drug–side effect heterogeneous graph structure as input, the propagation rule of the GCN is formulated as H(1)=σ(D−1/2GD−1/2H(0)WGCN) 5 where W GCN is the layer-specific trainable weight matrix; H (0) is the initialization of the drug and side effect embeddings; H (1) is the embedding of nodes at the first layer; D = diag­(∑ j G ij ) is the degree matrix derived from G; and σ represents the nonlinear activation function.

GraphSAGE Module

Traditional GCNs require all nodes to participate in training to obtain node embeddings. Hence, they can only learn information from adjacent nodes and cannot quickly generate embeddings for new nodes. Consequently, GCNs are not well-suited for generalizing to unknown vertices. To better capture the structural and semantic information on each node, we introduce GraphSAGE, which enhances the model’s ability to generalize to new nodes by learning more spatial information.

GraphSAGE is an inductive representation learning method for GNNs. Unlike other GNN frameworks that rely on a fixed graph, GraphSAGE aggregates feature information from a node’s local neighborhood by sampling a fixed-size set of neighbors, known as local neighborhoods. This method generates low-dimensional node embeddings that capture both structural and semantic information, enabling efficient generalization to new nodes.

The core idea of GraphSAGE is to sample a fixed-size set of neighbors rather than considering all direct neighbors, as is done in GCNs. This reduces computational complexity, facilitating more efficient training of large-scale graph data. For each node u, its local neighborhood is denoted as N(u). The GraphSAGE layer in our sample and aggregate module is defined as Hu(2)=σ(WSAGE.MEAN({Hu(1)}∪{Hv(1),∀v∈N(u)})) 6 where the mean aggregation function is applied. W SAGE is the weight matrix, v represents the neighbors of the target node u, H u (1) refers to the previous node representation, and σ is the nonlinear activation function.

GAT Module

GAT networks adaptively extract feature representations by assigning different importance to neighboring nodes. By leveraging an attention mechanism, GATs learn different weights for adjacent node features, focusing more on local feature extraction. This mechanism enables GATs to capture multiscale topological information and various connections, which are often represented in heterogeneous networks. As a result, GATs can learn semantically rich node embeddings.

The graph attention module assigns different attention coefficients to different edges in the graph network. Let N denote the number of nodes in the graph, and let H represent the node feature matrix with dimensions N × F, where each node has a feature vector of dimension F. The input to the graph attention layer is H, and the new-node feature vector has dimension F ’. The process is as follows: First, for all input nodes, a weight matrix W is trained with a dimension of F ’ × F representing the relationship between the input features F and the output features F ’. Then, the self-attention mechanism is applied to each node, with the attention coefficient defining the importance of node v’s features to node u: euv=LeakyReLU(aT[WHu(l)∥WHv(l)]) 7 where H u (l) represents the feature vector of node u and a is the attention weight. ∥ denotes concatenation, which applies a LeakyReLU in the end, and then normalizing and converting e uv to a probability output α uv through a softmax function: αuv=softmaxv(euv)=exp(euv)∑k∈Ν(u)exp(euk) 8

Finally, the graph convolution output of each node is obtained by Hu(3)=σ(∑v∈Ν(u)αuvWGATHv(2)) 9 where W GAT is the shared weight matrix and σ represents the nonlinear activation function.

Adaptive Layer Attention

The embeddings at different layers capture different types of structural information in the heterogeneous network. To adaptively weigh the features from different layers, we employed a layer attention mechanism. Adaptive layer attention enables the model to adjust the feature importance at each layer, which is particularly useful for capturing relationships and information at different scales. In drug–side effect prediction, features from different layers may represent different levels of biological information and associations. By integrating these features effectively, the model can enhance its ability to capture complex relationships.

Optimization

In the data set with m drugs and n side effects, drug–side effect pairs are positive, while other pairs are negative. The positive and negative sets are denoted as y + and y –, respectively. The drug–side effect prediction is treated as a binary classification problem. As there are considerably fewer known associations than no drug–side effect observed associations, we adopted a weighted cross-entropy loss function: Loss=−1m×n(λ×∑(i,j)∈y+log⁡aij′+∑(i,j)∈y−log(1−aij′)) 12 where (i, j) denotes the pair for drug d i and side effect s j , and λ = |y –|/|y +|, |y +|, and |y –| are the numbers of instances in y + and y –, respectively. The weight factor λ imposes the importance of observed associations to reduce the influence of data imbalance.

The Adam optimizer is used to minimize the loss function. It updates the network weights in an iterative manner based on the training data. To prevent overfitting, we incorporated node dropout and regular dropout into the model. Additionally, a cyclic learning rate is employed during optimization, with the learning rate oscillating between maximum and minimum values to ensure a balance between training speed and accuracy.

Results and Discussion

Parameter Settings

AGRL-DSE was implemented using the TensorFlow framework and executed on an NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU. The training parameters were set as follows: the total number of training epochs was 6000, the embedding dimensionality was 128, and the initial learning rate of the optimizer, denoted as lr, was set to 0.01. To mitigate overfitting, we applied a node dropout rate of 0.6 and a regular dropout rate of 0.4. Additionally, the penalty factor μ in the heterogeneous network was selected from {2,4, 6, 8}, with AGRL-DSE achieving the highest AUC and mean average precision (MAP) at 4.

Evaluation Metrics

To objectively assess AGRL-DSE’s performance, we employed 5-fold cross-validation. The model was evaluated using four key metrics: MAP, AUC, Precision@N, and Recall@N. The first two metrics assess accuracy, whereas the latter two evaluate top N recommendation performance: Precision@N=TPN 13 Recall@N=TPT 14 where TP is the number of positive samples in the topN recommendations and T is the number of positive samples in the test set.

To evaluate prediction accuracy for frequency estimation, we used root mean squared error (RMSE) and mean absolute error (MAE): RMSE=1t∑i=1t(yi−ŷi)2 15 MAE=1t∑i=1t|yi−ŷi| 16 where t is the total number of tested side effect frequency pairs, y i is the observed value, and ŷ i is the predicted value.

Comparison with Other Methods

To assess AGRL-DSE’s effectiveness, we compared it against several state-of-the-art drug–side effect association prediction models, including XGBoost, Galeano’s model, MGPred, DSGAT, A3 Net, and HSTrans. To ensure a fair comparison, each model was trained using the optimal parameters specified in the original papers.

Table and Figure present the performance comparison. AGRL-DSE outperformed all other models across most evaluation metrics, achieving a MAP of 0.246 and an AUC of 0.945. The next-best models, HSTrans and A3Net, obtained MAP values of 0.232 and 0.238 and AUC values of 0.925 and 0.926, respectively. DSGAT and Galeano’s model performed worse, while MGPred had the lowest AUC and MAP. These results suggest that AGRL-DSE is more effective at identifying drug–side effect associations. However, MGPred achieved the best performance in frequency prediction accuracy of drug–side effects, with AGRL-DSE ranking second, outperforming XGBoost, Galeano’s model, DSGAT, A3Net, and HSTrans. This suggests that while MGPred predicts frequencies accurately, it may also predict false associations, limiting its applicability.

Notably, while AGRL-DSE had lower Precision@1 and Recall@1 compared to some models, it maintained strong performance in Precision@15 and Recall@15, indicating superior ability to capture drug–side effect associations across a broader candidate set.

AGRL-DSE’s superior performance can be attributed to three key factors. First, the constructed heterogeneous network provides flexible representations of the node and edge types, facilitating the extraction of various types of information in complex relational networks. Second, unlike methods such as MGPred, DSGAT, A3Net, and HSTrans, which use single-feature extractors, AGRL-DSE integrates multilayer semantic information from graphs, nodes, and edges. Finally, we introduced adaptive layer attention that effectively assigns different weights to different embedding layers, improving feature integration at the node level, and enhancing overall performance. In addition, AGRL-DSE still has better prediction performance in AUC compared with knowledge graph embedding-based approaches. AGRL-DSE can dynamically integrate multilayer semantic information through adaptive attention, ensuring a more comprehensive representation of both global and local topological relationships, further enhancing its predictive capabilities. Our comprehensive framework, AGRL-DSE, is a powerful tool for capturing the spatial characteristics of drugs and their side effects.

Cold-Start Analysis

In drug development, new drugs often lack previous training data, leading to a cold-start problem. Originally identified in recommendation systems, this issue arises when there is insufficient data for accurate predictions. To evaluate AGRL-DSE’s performance in cold-start scenarios, we randomly divided 750 drugs into 10 groups, each containing 75 drugs as the test set and the remaining 675 drugs as the training set. Ablation Experiments

Ablation experiments were conducted to evaluate the contributions of different heterogeneous network inputs and structural components to the overall performance of AGRL-DSE. The full AGRL-DSE model incorporates all components.

First, to assess the impact of drug–drug and side effect similarities in the heterogeneous network for association prediction, we performed ablation experiments with different input configurations. Specifically, AGRL-DSE-ds represents a network using only drug–side effect associations. AGRL-DSE-dsd incorporates both drug–side effect associations and drug–drug similarities. AGRL-DSE-dss includes drug–side effect associations along with side effect similarities. Table presents the experimental results. The inclusion of drug and side effect similarities improved prediction accuracy, enhancing AGRL-DSE’s overall performance. The drug–side effect association network alone also achieved strong prediction scores, demonstrating its effectiveness. However, removing drug or side effect similarities did not improve predictions, highlighting the importance of multimodal information in predicting drug side effects, particularly in drug cold-start scenarios.

In this section, we focus on elucidating the interpretability of drug and side effect embeddings in our method. The primary objective is to decipher the biological significance embedded within the representations produced by our model.

In the association prediction task, we trained AGRL-DSE and Galeano’s methods separately using all frequency information in the frequency matrix, generating embedded representations of drugs and side effects. In order to investigate the interpretability of drug embeddings in relation to target information, we designed a binary classification task based on ATC codes (including anatomical, therapeutic, pharmacological, and chemical categories) to analyze the biological significance of drug embeddings from the perspective of clinical activities. Specifically, we use the similarity of drug embeddings as predictive labels to evaluate whether a pair of drugs shares the same ATC code. Figure a shows the AUC histograms of these predictions, indicating that AGRL-DSE outperforms Galeano’s method in all categories. These findings emphasize that drug embedding reflects biological significance, such as clinical activities.

Case Study

To illustrate AGRL-DSE’s practical capabilities, we first examined the top 20 predicted drug–side effect pairs. We validated these predictions using data from ADReCS and Drugs.com (which provides accurate and independent information on more than 24,000 prescription drugs, over-the-counter medicines, and natural products) and published literature. We confirmed 8 of the top 10 pairs and 15 of the top 20. The supplementary file provides detailed results. Most predicted side effects were supported by evidence, demonstrating AGRL-DSE’s robust predictive power.

To further evaluate the application value of AGRL-DSE, we conducted a detailed analysis using allopurinol and paroxetine as examples. In the SIDER data set, allopurinol has the fewest side effects, while paroxetine has the most side effects. Our focus is on the top five side effects with the highest drug prediction scores. Figure shows these results, indicating that our method is effective in predicting side effects even for drugs with few known side effects, highlighting the robustness of the method. It is worth mentioning that in the ″ground-truth″ data set, allopurinol is not associated with headaches, but our model accurately identified this association. This discovery is consistent with postmarket monitoring data provided by the authoritative pharmacovigilance resource Drugs.com. According to case reports from the U.S. Food and Drug Administration, headaches are a side effect of allopurinol (https://www.drugs.com/sfx/allopurinol-side-effects.html). This finding indicates that AGRL-DSE is able to successfully detect potential side effects that were previously unrecognized.

Conclusions

This study presents AGRL-DSE, a novel and effective graph representation learning framework for predicting drug-related side effects. Specifically, we encoded and integrated the semantic information on diverse connection relationships between drug and side effect nodes. The heterogeneous graph we constructed includes both intra- and interlayer connections. AGRL-DSE integrates the GCN, GraphSAGE, and GAT modules to learn topological spatial relationships and semantic information in a heterogeneous graph of drug side effects. It extracts contextual topological relationships and captures both global and local node features. To account for the varying contributions of embeddings across different layers, we employed an adaptive layer attention to adaptively capture multiscale information and obtain deeper-level feature representations. This approach provides more comprehensive insights into drugs and side effects, enhancing the model’s predictive accuracy and generalization ability. Experimental results demonstrate that AGRL-DSE outperforms existing methods in predicting drug side effects, offering a valuable reference for clinical trial screening.

However, our study has some limitations. First, the interaction of GCN, GraphSAGE, and GAT modules in the current model is unidirectional and fixed, which may limit the adaptability of the model to different tasks and data. Second, while AGRL-DSE performs well in cold-start scenarios, its improvements remain limited, as cold-start cases still primarily rely on drug similarity information. Finally, the long-tail distribution of drug side effects can lead to data set imbalance. Common side effects, such as headaches or nausea, occur more frequently than rare effects, such as severe allergic reactions. There are significant differences in the frequency of drug side effects in the real world, which may lead to the insufficient prediction ability of the model for rare side effects and then affect the generalization performance of the model in real applications. In the future, we will explore the multiview learning method, by integrating a variety of drug characteristics (such as chemical structure, gene expression data, and drug targets) and dynamically fusing feature vectors from different perspectives, to improve the prediction performance in the cold-start scenario, so as to further enhance the generalization ability of the model for new drugs. Then, we plan to introduce a contrastive learning strategy to optimize node embedding by enhancing the contrastive learning of positive and negative samples, so that it can more accurately capture the similarities and differences between drugs and side effects and further improve the representation ability of the model.
