Task-relevant information availability shapes eye movements and perceptual judgment confidence
Authors: Thibault J Desbordes, Nadia Alahyane, Alain Guillaume
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHumans continuously decide where to look to gather task-relevant information. While affective rewards such as money are known to bias gaze direction, it remains unclear whether non-affective informational value can similarly shape oculomotor decisions. Here, we modulated the availability of task-relevant visual information at saccade targets by probabilistically varying its presentation duration, in a perceptual judgment task performed by human participants. Results showed that participants developed implicit biases, increasingly avoiding an experimentally introduced low-information region. These learned preferences were associated with longer saccade latencies toward non-preferred regions, similar to patterns observed with affective reward learning. However, saccade peak velocity remained unchanged across locations. Perceptual accuracy was not influenced either. When participants’ confidence ratings reliably distinguished correct from incorrect responses, confidence was higher for preferred regions, suggesting a dissociation between perceptual and metacognitive performance. These findings demonstrate that the probability of accessing easily usable information can be implicitly learned to guide eye movement decisions, much like reward. Moreover, subjective confidence can be linked to learned preferences, without modulation of perceptual performance. Our results highlight that informational value, independent of affective cues, shapes oculomotor decision-making and post-perceptual judgment confidence.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-13814-6.Keywords: Implicit learning, Selection history, Decision making, Metacognition, SaccadesSubject terms: Cognitive neuroscience, Oculomotor system, Reward, Human behaviour

Introduction

Several times per second, we choose where to direct our gaze in order to acquire task-relevant information–pieces of knowledge that will guide subsequent behavioral actions. Among the mass of information that characterizes our visual world, how do we select where, or what to look?

The attentional selection of a specific item or location in a visual scene, without or with eye movements, has been conceptualized as a balance between bottom-up (stimulus-driven) and top-down (goal-directed) factors1–3. However this classical top-down and bottom-up dual drive scheme for target selection has been challenged and a third drive has been introduced referred to as selection history4. History or past experience of previously obtained rewards (reward-based learning) or statistical regularities present in the environment (statistical learning) can further shape exploration patterns5–12. In the present study, we ask whether the probability to obtain exploitable visual information required to perform a perceptual task influences saccade decisions.

In the context of saccadic eye movements, reward-based learning and its consequences on oculomotor control has often been studied in relation to motivational and affective rewards such as water or juice for non-human primates13–15 and money, game points, or social rewards for humans16–20. These studies revealed that saccade preferences are consistently biased toward higher-value items (see21 for review). Our study set out to address two important limitations of this body of work. First, acquiring information relevant to the task at hand is arguably a more natural and ubiquitous driver of saccadic eye movements than obtaining explicit rewards such as money or points (see e.g.22). Second, in everyday vision, we are typically confronted with multiple visual options23, and we freely select the target we believe is most likely to provide useful information for the task at hand.

Regarding information gain versus affective reward, the extent to which decision-making processes based on informational value resemble those driven by more concrete affective rewards remains unclear. For instance, Collins24 asked participants to make saccades toward laterally presented photographs of faces while performing a global categorization task, in which they were required to determine, at the end of the session, whether there had been more male or female target faces. By varying the probability of obtaining information after a saccade on each side, she found that participants exhibited a side preference in free-choice trials with two simultaneously presented items only when information availability was maximally contrasted (0% vs. 100%), but not when the probability differences were more gradual (e.g., 25% vs. 75%). Additionally, Wolf and Schütz25 found that, unlike in experimental paradigms that involve motivational rewards, saccade latencies were not modulated by the information obtained following the saccade (see also26). Overall, these results suggest a distinct influence of informational outcomes and motivational rewards on oculomotor control.

Regarding the free choice nature of behavior, most studies tested the effect of affective reward on saccade choices. Usually two stimuli were simultaneously presented (one left and one right) and the participant had to make a saccade toward the target of their choice. Results showed that saccade choices evolve to maximize reward acquisition (monetary rewards:16,17,27–29; point-based rewards:30). Beyond that, recent work on free choice highlights multiple influences on decision-making, such as expected value and action planning31, as well as spatial and identity-related properties of possible targets23. Zajkowski et al.32 further suggested that reward probability and participants’ spontaneous preferences collectively influence behavior in free-choice tasks.

The spatial nature of oculomotor integration suggests a priority map organization2,9,33. In the context of reward-based learning, Chelazzi et al.18 argue that reward processing leads to alterations in spatial priority maps. Belopolsky6 proposed a unified priority map that integrates statistical learning, reward-based learning and emotional influences within the oculomotor system. Although not explicitly addressed in these studies, a key feature of these maps is the presence of lateral interactions, which facilitate information integration and refine representations34,35. If one specific higher-value location, resulting from statistical or reward-based learning, can spatially extend its influence to neighboring locations, this would suggest the presence of lateral interactions within the priority map. However, to our knowledge, only one study36, which explored the effect of statistical learning on the spatial distribution of covert attention, suggests that this might be the case.

Beyond the influence of affective reward on subsequent target selection for gaze orientation, the characteristics of eye movements themselves have been shown to reflect preferences21. In non-human primates, Takikawa et al.13 demonstrated that saccades toward rewarded locations exhibit shorter latencies and higher peak velocities (see also15,37,38). Similar effects have been observed in humans for saccades directed toward targets associated with monetary rewards19, game-related rewards39, and socially-relevant stimuli40 (see41 for review).

Reward-based and statistical learning also affect perceptual processes. In covert attention tasks, decreased visual perception has been observed when statistical learning links a given location to a high probability of distractor appearance36,42. This finding aligns with numerous demonstrations of enhanced perception at attended locations (see43,44 for reviews): statistical learning appears to facilitate attentional allocation to beneficial locations, thereby influencing perceptual accuracy. Also noteworthy is the study by Cheng et al.45, whose results revealed that foveal visual perception was slightly, but significantly, enhanced for the perceptual task associated with a higher monetary reward.

Finally, a key determinant of behavioral control that accompanies each perceptual decision is confidence in its correctness46–48. Confidence reflects the perceived likelihood that the decision was correct in the absence of external feedback. Whether these confidence judgments can be shaped by different types of learning–reward-based, statistical, or information-related–has, to our knowledge, never been assessed. A related question is whether confidence ratings accurately reflect changes in perceptual performance, or whether they could be dissociated from accuracy during the course of learning. Previous results indeed suggest that perceptual judgments and confidence in these perceptual judgments may be dissociable processes49 (see50 for a review).

In the present study, we designed a spatially structured binary saccade free-choice task in which participants selected a location and made a saccadic eye movement to it in order to access task-relevant information. Following the saccade, participants completed a perceptual judgment on the foveated item and provided a confidence rating for their perceptual decision. We probabilistically manipulated the spatial distribution of information made available to perform the perceptual task, by varying its presentation duration. We examined whether learning about the likelihood of accessing easily usable task-relevant information could influence saccadic decisions–similarly to how affective rewards have been shown to shape choice behavior. We further examined whether this information-based learning exhibited a spatially organized, map-like structure, and whether it modulated saccadic parameters. Finally, we tested whether the information-based learning influenced post-saccadic perceptual accuracy and confidence ratings.

Results

We designed a two-alternative forced-choice task in which participants were peripherally presented with two diametrically opposite random noise patches (Fig. 1A and B). Participants were told to freely choose one of the two targets to orient their gaze toward and that, upon saccade offset, a Gabor patch would replace the chosen target. They were then informed that they would have to judge the orientation of the Gabor patch, and that its presentation time could vary and sometimes be very short. Participants knew that in each trial, the orientation was identical for both targets–referred to as ’the orientation of the trial’–either vertical (0∘) or slightly tilted to the right (+6∘) (Fig. 1A). They were simply asked to do their best in the discrimination task, with no instruction to randomize or vary their saccade choices. Unbeknownst to the participants, we reduced the ease with which they could get the orientation information by increasing the probability of a very short presentation time in a specific region of the visual field (counterbalanced across participants), referred to as the altered region. Trials were categorized as either ’easy trials’ or ’difficult trials’. ’Difficult trials’ occurred with 80% probability in the altered region and with only 20% probability in other locations (Fig. 1D). By randomly varying the presented target pairs (Fig. 1B) and using this probabilistic approach (Fig. 1D), we minimized the likelihood that participants would detect this manipulation. After each saccade, participants discriminated the orientation of the foveated item without receiving external accuracy feedback (Fig. 1C) and rated their confidence in their perceptual decision (Fig. 1E). An experimental session consisted of 120 trials, each pair of items (Fig. 1B) was presented 30 times. We conducted two experiments, each corresponding to a different level of difficulty in ’difficult trials’ (Fig. 1C). In Experiment 1, difficult trials corresponded to an extremely low information availability condition: the Gabor was presented for only 16.67 ms (4 frames, n = 64 participants). In Experiment 2, difficult trials corresponded to a Gabor presentation duration of 50 ms (12 frames, n = 32) which still remained challenging and with reduced information availability (see Materials and Methods). In ’easy trials’, the orientation discrimination was comfortable, with Gabors on-screen for 250 ms (60 frames). This experimental protocol allowed us to investigate: 1) whether implicit learning of information availability variations across visual space influenced overt target choices; 2) potential spatial interactions within the informational map by analyzing learning effects on locations near the altered region (’close’ region, Fig. 1D); the impact of information availability learning on 3) saccadic parameters, 4) perceptual judgment performance and 5) confidence ratings in these perceptual judgments. Detailed experimental procedures and statistical analyses are described in the Materials and Methods section.

Task-related information availability guides saccade decisions

To examine the effect of task-related information availability on saccade choices, we focused on Critical trials (60 trials per participant, Fig. 1D). These trials included two locations with low information availability (80% probability of difficult trial occurrence), corresponding to the altered region (AR), while the two opposite locations contained high information availability, representing the non-altered region (NAR). Results revealed that task-related information availability influences participants binary saccade choices. In Experiment 1 (Fig. 2A), where visual information was available for only 16.67 ms in 80% trials in the AR, the median choice rate for this AR was significantly below 0.5 (median = 0.24, IQR = 0.41), indicating a strong influence of information manipulation on choice behavior (χ2(1, n = 64) = 7.70, P = 0.006, Φ = 0.35). In Experiment 2 (Fig. 2B), where the altered region corresponded to 80% trials for which information was available for 50 ms, we also found a significant below-chance choice rate for the AR (median = 0.38, IQR = 0.42; χ2(1, n = 32) = 4.10, P = 0.043, Φ = 0.35). These results thus suggest implicit learning of the locations where there is a higher probability of obtaining sufficient information to perform the task. The smaller reduction of choice rate for AR in Experiment 2 is to be associated with the lower learning pressure due to the longer presentation time of information in the AR (50 ms versus 16.67 ms). To provide further support of the implicit nature of this information-based learning, at the end of the experimental session, we asked participants whether they noticed anything particular in the spatial distribution of various presentation durations. The majority of participants noticed nothing. In Experiment 1, only 3 out of 64 participants reported that they had noticed modulation in the information duration distribution, and 2 of them indeed correctly reported the location of the AR. In Experiment 2, only 2 out of 32 participants reported sensing the manipulation (1 with certainty). Importantly the possible awareness of the probabilistic manipulation of information presentation duration was not reflected in choice rates. In Experiment 1, the 3 participants had a choice rate for AR of 0.25, 0.51, 0.08, respectively. In Experiment 2, values were 0.55 and 0.80.

An important feature of our study was that participants were free to choose the target to look at, on every trial. We did not encourage them or instruct them to vary their choices or to explore different locations. Moreover the experiment was kept rather short, with 120 trials, half of which were Critical trials, namely trials in which there was an altered region with a 80% probability of obtaining a lower information amount. This resulted in a rather large variability in behaviors adopted by participants. Indeed, some participants explored one or the other alternative (AR or NAR) during the first trials to finally more often select the NAR. Others adopted a much more biased strategy, either toward the AR or toward the NAR. Note that we use here the ‘biases strategy’ terminology because some participants performed only a few number of visits toward one of the two Critical areas. In both Experiments 1 and 2, we even had one participant who always selected the AR with hence a choice rate for AR of 1. The largest number of participants with saccade directions biased toward the NAR in both Experiments could be explained by the fact that participants did not find any reason to alter their initial spatial bias, whereas those with initial biases toward AR had pressure to evolve in their choices. Finally, it is also possible that for some participants, difficult trials were seen as a challenge, which brought them to develop a preference for the altered region. Noteworthy is the observation that participants with very high AR choice rates were not those who identified the manipulation (see above).

As the AR location was fully counterbalanced across participants, the observed significant decrease in choice rate for both Experiments 1 and 2 is difficult to attribute solely to diverse stable spatial biases–i.e., without invoking an evolution of choice rate over trials consistent with a learning process. Nevertheless, to more robustly assess whether such a learning process occurred, we conducted a mixed-effects logistic regression analysis to evaluate systematic changes in choice behavior over the course of the experimental session. For each Experiment, separate models were fitted to the choice data from the Critical and Control target pairs. These models included fixed effects for the display index (capturing the temporal evolution of choice behavior throughout the session), pair identity (reflecting differences between the two pairs), and the interaction between these two factors. Participants were included as a random factor, with both random intercepts and random slopes for the display index (see Materials and Methods for details).

For the Critical pairs in Experiment 1 (Fig. 2C, left panel), we found a significant main effect of the display index factor (b = -0.046, SE = 0.012, Z = -3.716, P < .001): the likelihood of choosing the AR option decreased by 4.7% with each successive trial (Odds Ratio = 0.955, 95%CI = [0.933, 0.979]). The pair identity factor and the interaction between the two factors were non-significant (b = -0.010, SE = 0.164, Z = -0.061, P = 0.951 and b = 0.010, SE = 0.010, Z = 0.975, P = 0.330, respectively). The fixed intercept of the regression was significant (b = -0.591, SE = 0.242, Z = -2.445, P = .014): at trial zero (i.e., at the first presentation of a Critical pair), across all target pairs and with pair identity controlled for in the model, the estimated odds indicate that participants were 1.805 times less likely to choose the AR than the NAR (OR = 0.554, 95%CI = [0.345, 0.889]).

In Experiment 2 (Fig. 2D, left panel), no clear evidence of learning was observed (Fig. 2E). The main effect of the display index factor was non-significant (b = -0.030, SE = 0.018, Z = -1.718, P = .086) with a 3.05% decrease in the likelihood of choosing the AR region per trial (OR = 0.970, 95% CI = [0.938, 1.004]). The main effect of the pair identity factor was not significant (b = 0.048, SE = 0.219, Z = 0.218, P = 0.827) but the interaction between the two factors was close to the significance threshold (b = 0.026, SE = 0.014, Z = 1.919, P = .055). The intercept was not significant (b = -0.471, SE = 0.289, Z = -1.630, P = .103). Given the near significant interaction, we performed post hoc simple slope analyses using dummy coding of the two Critical pairs. There was a significant effect of display index for the first Critical pair encountered (b = -0.043, SE = 0.019, Z = -2.278, P = .023; OR = 0.958, 95% CI = [0.923, 0.994]), but only a marginal effect for the second one (b = -0.032, SE = 0.019, Z = -1.731, P = .084; OR = 0.969, 95% CI = [0.934, 1.004]).

Taken together, these mixed-effects logistic regression analyses strengthen the hypothesis of a genuine learning process: there was a significant main effect of the display index factor for both Critical pairs in Experiment 1 and this effect was significant for one of the two pairs in Experiment 2. These observed changes in choice behavior over time suggest a progressive integration of the spatial distribution of information availability by participants.

The significant intercept observed in Experiment 1–suggesting a preference for the NAR option from the very first Critical pair presentation–may reflect a bias introduced by the overall trend in the data, rather than a true initial preference. Indeed, a separate chi-square test on the first trial alone revealed no significant bias, suggesting that the intercept significance may result from the overall fit being pulled downward by the rest of the data: 28 AR vs. 36 NAR choices; (χ2(1, n = 64) = 1.00, P = 0.32). In Experiment 2 the distribution was perfectly balanced with 16 AR vs. 16 NAR choices.

Overall, despite the observed behavioral variability, our findings demonstrate that, at the population level, the probability of finding easily usable task-related information led to an implicit learning, decreasing the probability of choosing the locations where task-relevant information was accessible for a much shorter time.

Informational availability learning alters an informational map

The design of the experiment allowed to check for a spillover of the information availability in the AR to nearby regions. For this analysis, we focused on Control trials (Fig. 1D). In these trials, both pairs of potential targets contained gabors presented for 250 ms in 80% cases. For each pair of targets, one was closer to the AR (‘close’ targets) while the other was farther (‘far’ targets). We found no significant shift in choice rates for close regions in Experiment 1 (χ2(1, n = 64) = 0.781, P = 0.377, Φ = 0.11; Fig. 2A, right) nor in Experiment 2 (χ2(1, n = 32) = 0.227, P = 0.634, Φ = 0.08; Fig. 2B, right). This was confirmed by the mixed-effects logistic analysis which resulted in no significant effects, either main effects (Experiment 1: display index b = 0.002, SE = 0.006, Z = 0.334, P = 0.739, pair identity b =-0.258, SE = 0.134, Z = -1.933, P = 0.053; Experiment 2: display index b = -0.006, SE = 0.006, Z = -0.915, P = 0.360, pair identity b = -0.140, SE = 0.181, Z = -0.769, P = 0.442), interaction (Experiment 1: b = -0.003, SE = 0.008, Z = -0.374, P = 0.708, Experiment 2: b = -0.001, SE = 0.011, Z = -0.111, P = 0.911) or regression intercept (Experiment 1: b = -0.174, SE = 0.111, Z = -1.567, P = 0.117, Experiment 2: b = -0.146, SE = 0.108, Z = -1.358, P = 0.174). However, regression analyses revealed strong relationships between choice rates for Close regions and choice rates for AR (t(61) = 15.08, P < 0.001, R2 = 0.61 for Experiment 1, Fig. 2E; t(28) = 15.24, P < 0.001, R2 = 0.58 for Experiment 2, Fig. 2F). This indicates that, despite having 80% easy trials with comfortable information access in these Close locations, saccade choices were significantly linked to the preferences of participants, be they the result of biased strategy or of learning (see Discussion). Overall, these results, obtained from two separate participant groups, support the hypothesis of an informational map in which nearby regions are connected through lateral interactions.

We next studied whether the probability of obtaining usable task-relevant information was associated with saccadic parameters (latency, peak velocity and landing error), post-saccadic perceptual judgment accuracy and confidence in these perceptual decisions. We used regression analyses to evaluate participant-wise the relationship between these variables and the choice rate for AR in the Critical trials. This choice rate was taken as a global indicator of the spatial preference developed by participants. We did not postulate a causal link between choice rate and the other parameters, but simply assessed whether a relationship existed between them. Choice rate and other dependent variables could be seen as different markers of participants’ preferences resulting from initial spontaneous bias and/or learning of probability of information distribution (see Discussion). For each variable of interest, we computed for each participant the difference between mean values in AR and in NAR regions, and in Close and Far regions. For participants who tended to adopt a relatively biased strategy (see above), we had very few data points in a given region (either AR or NAR, depending on the choice rate value). The method we then adopted was to remove from regression analyses data from participants with a choice rate included in [0, 0.1] or [0.9, 1] intervals in order to get more reliable evaluation for each parameter (see Materials and Methods). Nevertheless, data points for these participants are kept illustrated in the graphs (in grey whereas the other data points are represented in blue) to show that our selection method did not influence the observed trends.

Saccadic latency modifications follow choices rate changes

Saccade latency, time taken to select one of the two targets and to initiate a saccade toward it, is illustrated in the middle panels of Fig. 3A for Critical pairs and Fig. 3B for Control pairs. Saccade latency was slightly longer in Experiment 2 than Experiment 1 but the difference was not significant (t(61) = 1.77, P = 0.082). To examine whether saccadic latency was related to information availability, differences in latency between NAR and AR (Fig. 3A) were computed for each participant. A negative value hence indicated that a participant took longer to initiate a saccade toward the AR when this alternative was chosen. There was no significant difference in latency between AR and NAR (Experiment 1: mean difference = -3.2 ms, t(38) = 0.84, P = 0.408; Experiment 2: mean difference = -12.2 ms, t(21) = 1.70, P = 0.103; only blue data points considered). Nevertheless, these difference values between the two Critical regions (NAR/AR) were linked to the choice rate of the participant (Fig. 3A). In Experiment 1, the regression model suggested that for every 0.5 increase in choice rate, there is an expected increase of 25 ms in latency difference between the two AR and NAR areas (t(38) = 2.22, P = 0.032, R2 = 0.12). For Experiment 2, the model suggested an increase of 44 ms in latency difference between the two areas for every 0.5 increase in choice rate (t(21) =2.33, P = 0.030, R2 = 0.21). Noteworthy here is that the exclusion of grey data points (participants with choice rate equal to or below 0.1 or with choice rate equal to or above 0.9) did not influence these relationships. Complementary analyses on the absolute latency values reinforced these results and showed that saccade latency was shorter for preferred regions (Supplementary Figure 1, FigSI-1)). We then examined whether differences in saccade latency in Close versus Far regions may be related to choice rate for AR (Fig. 3B). We found no significant regressions (t(38) = 0.62, P = 0.541 for Experiment 1 and t(21) = 1.10, P = 0.285 for Experiment 2).

Overall, these results show that saccade initiation time is modulated by saccade preferences. Contrary to previous works showing that saccade peak velocity is modulated by monetary or social reward (see Introduction), we found no effect of information availability on saccade peak velocity in the present study (Supplementary Figure 2, FigSI-2). We also examined whether information availability could be related to saccadic landing error, defined as the distance between the saccade endpoint and the center of the noise patch. There was no relationship between choice rate and landing error and no difference in landing error between AR and NAR trials (Supplementary Figure 3, FigSI-3). Finally, we tested for a potential speed-accuracy trade-off–i.e., whether longer saccade latencies were associated with smaller landing errors. This was not the case for AR or NAR trials (Supplementary Figure 4, FigSI-4).

Mean accuracy in perceptual judgment is not impacted

After participants made a saccade to the target of their choice, the saccade target changed into a gabor patch, requiring them to discriminate the orientation of the grating (vertical or tilted, Fig. 1A, Fig. 1E). As stated in the Introduction section, when tested in covert attention conditions (i.e., without eye movemens36,42), learning based on selection history (statistical learning) influenced accuracy of perceptual decision, with a better accuracy for the high probability target location. Here participants made eye movements toward the target of interest. We examined whether Gabor orientation discrimination accuracy was related to learning based on information availability. Our analyses focused on difficult trials, the easy trials (250ms Gabor presentation) being not discriminant (see Materials and Methods). In other words, for both Critical and Control trials in Experiments 1 and 2, we specifically considered the trials where the chosen location presented a gabor with short 16.67 or 50 ms durations.

Figure 4 illustrates results on perceptual judgment accuracy. In Critical pairs, mean accuracy was significantly above chance level (t-test against constant value of 0.5; Experiment 1: t(39) = 2.59, P = 0.014; Experiment 2: t(22) = 6.15, P < 0.001; Fig. 4A, middle panels). As expected, accuracy was significantly higher in Experiment 2 than in Experiment 1 (t(61) = 4.73, P < 0.001). A similar pattern was observed for Control pairs (Fig. 4B, middle panels) except that mean accuracy in Experiment 1 was not significantly different from 0.5 (t(39) = 3.71 x 10-8, P > 0.999). Whether the gabor grating was presented for 16.67 or 50 ms, perceptual judgment accuracy was not significantly different between NAR and AR (t(38) = -0.74, P = 0.47 for 16.67 ms and t(21) = -0.64, P = 0.53 for 50 ms), or between Close and Far regions (t(38) = -1.20, P = 0.24 for 16.67 ms and t(21) = 0.81, P = 0.43 for 50 ms). There was no difference in accuracy between Critical and Control pairs (Experiment 1: t(78) = 1.515, P = 0.134); Experiment 2: t(44) = -0.533, P = 0.597). Moreover, perceptual judgement acccuracy was not related to choice rates (Critical pairs: for Experiment 1, t(38) = -0.16, P = 0.874; for Experiment 2 t(21) = 0.40, P = 0.694; see Fig. 4A leftmost and rightmost panels; Control pairs: for Experiment 1, t(38) = -0.20, P = 0.843; for Experiment 2 t(21) = -1.23, P = 0.233; see Fig. 4B). In sum perceptual judgment accuracy is not modulated by saccade preferences.

Confidence in perceptual decision is impacted when discriminant

After having judged the orientation of the Gabor without external accuracy feedback, participants had to rate their confidence for their response (on a scale ranging from 1, ’not confident at all’, to 7, ’totally confident’). We investigated whether mean judgment confidence could change despite the absence of changes in perceptual judgment accuracy (see Fig. 4). Here also, only difficult trials, i.e., trials where the chosen locations contained a Gabor that was presented for 16.67 or 50 ms were considered for the analysis (Fig. 5). Indeed confidence was always close to 7 for ’easy trials’ (see Materials and Methods). Mean confidence scores were significantly lower for the most difficult Experiment 1 than for the Experiment 2: t(61) = -5.54, P < 0.001 for Critical pairs and t(61) = -5.80, P < 0.001 for Control pairs (Fig. 5, middle panels).

Perceptual judgment confidence in difficult trials. Same format as in Fig. 3. Confidence scores were higher in Experiment 2 than in Experiment 1 (middle panels). Mean confidence differences between NAR and AR (Critical pairs, A) and between Far and Close (Control pairs, B) were not significantly different from zero. Individual confidence differences were significantly associated with choice rate for AR in Critical pairs of Experiment 2 (right panel in A). Insets show a comparison of confidence scores for correct and incorrect trials (i.e., metacognitive accuracy): confidence scores discriminated correct from incorrect responses in Experiment 2 only.

In Experiment 1, there was no significant difference in confidence scores between NAR and AR (mean difference = 0.11, 95% CI = [-0.077 0.305]; t(38) = 1.206, P = 0.24). No relationship was found with choice rates (t(38) = -0.702, P = 0.487). In Experiment 2, there was no significant difference in confidence scores between NAR and AR (mean difference = -0.25, 95% CI = [-0.644 0.134]; t(21) = 1.361, P = 0.188). However, there was a strong relationship between these confidence differences and the choice rate (t(21) = 2.932, P = 0.008). Thus, despite the reduced choice rate for AR seen in both Experiments 1 and 2, we found a relationship between choice rate and confidence differences in Experiment 2 only. Two differences between these two Experiments should be noted. First, the longer Gabor presentation duration of 50 ms fostered higher confidence in perceptual decision (Fig. 5, middle panel). Second, the 16.67 versus 50 ms Experiments may differ in terms of metacognitive efficiency, i.e., the capacity of metacognition to be discriminant or, in other words, to reliably differentiate between correct and incorrect perceptual judgments. The insets in each graph (Fig. 5A) show that metacognitive accuracy was very low in Experiment 1 whereas it was largely better in Experiment 2. Indeed, confidence ratings were similar between correct and incorrect judgments in Experiment 1 (t(39) = 1.2, P = 0.237). On the contrary, in Experiment 2, confidence score was higher when perceptual judgment was actually correct compared to when it was incorrect (t(22) = 5.03, P < 0.001). Overall, these results indicate that judgment confidence, when discriminant, can be modulated by saccade preferences. No significant relationship was found for the Control pairs (Fig. 5B, t(38) = -1.42, P = 0.165 for Experiment 1 and t(21) = 1.08, P = 0.292 for Experiment 2).

Discussion

We investigated how the probability of obtaining task-relevant information could bias saccadic eye movements in free choice situations. Four possible pairs of diametrically opposite targets were alternately presented to participants who had to choose one of the two targets. Upon saccade offset, the chosen target changed into a Gabor patch grating and participants judged its orientation, followed by a rating of their confidence in their perceptual judgment. Unbeknownst to participants, two specific adjacent locations were associated with reduced Gabor patches presentation time, leading to lower probability of providing easily exploitable task-relevant information. Without awareness, participants significantly reduced their saccade choices toward locations where visual information was available for a very short time. The probability of choosing nearby locations was strongly related to participants’ preferences regarding the low-informational region. Saccade latency was linked to participants’ preferences with faster latency for preferred locations, but saccadic peak velocity remained unchanged. We also examined how the probability of obtaining easily usable information impacts post saccadic events. The accuracy of perceptual judgments performed after the saccade was not related to participants’ preferences. Conversely, when confidence in these perceptual judgments was predictive of correct versus incorrect perceptual decisions, confidence scores were significantly related to the probability of choice, suggesting a dissociation between perceptual accuracy and perceptual decision awareness.

Determinants of saccade choices

In the present study, which exposed participants to free choices, we did not provide any specific instructions other than doing their best to perform the task. In other words, we did not encourage them to vary their choices or to explore different options. Our results revealed a combined influence of idiosyncratic spatial preference biases and learning of the probability of finding exploitable task-relevant information.

Indeed, some participants exhibited extreme choice rate values, either favoring the NAR or the AR (Fig. 2). These extreme values are difficult to attribute to a learning effect, given the limited opportunity to explore the alternatives, and instead likely reflect spatial preference biases. Such spontaneous and enduring preferences across multiple free-choice trials have already been observed in studies including trials associated with equal reward probabilities32. In our study, participants likely started with the assumption of equal probabilities, as no indication was provided regarding the probabilistic distribution of task-related information. Additionally, Vogel et al.51 demonstrated that different strategies can be adopted in free-choice tasks: some participants pay little attention to the task or the environment, making choices that result in extreme choice rate values similar to those observed in our study, while others are more inclined to follow the task structure and are thus more prone to learning. Finally, it is also possible that the very concept of free choice, without specific instructions, was difficult to handle for some participants, leading them to more easily adopt an initial preference bias. This preference likely applied to a large part of the visual scene rather than a single target position, as extreme choice rate values (for AR or NAR) were associated with spread values that align perfectly with more median choice rate values (Fig. 2E and F). Note nevertheless that an initial preference bias may not have been present in all participants and that the present study was not specifically designed to investigate such possible biases.

Despite the variation of the location of the AR across the visual field and across participants, we did find a significant decrease in choice rate for the altered, lower information, region (Fig. 2A and B). This decrease was proportional to the magnitude of the availability of the task-related information (i.e., information present for 16.67 ms or 50 ms). Additionally, we were able to show a decrease in AR choices over the course of the session–clearly in Experiment 1 and to a lesser extent in Experiment 2 (see Fig. 2C and D). Moreover, most participants did not notice the probability manipulation. These results demonstrate the emergence of implicit learning of exploitable task-related information probability distribution, potentially overlaying any previously discussed idiosyncratic preference biases, if present. This may explain why we observed more extreme choice rate values for the NAR than for the AR: participants with an initial preference for NAR would have their behavior reinforced, whereas a preference for AR would be counteracted by the information probability distribution. Adding further complexity, the scarcity of task-related information in the AR may not have had the same effect on all participants. While most tended to avoid this region (as indicated by the mean choice rate value being below 0.5), some participants instead developed a preference for it. This behavior could correspond to participants who were challenged by the paucity of visual information, thereby the difficulty of the perceptual task, and thus made a greater effort to explore this low-information region.

Interestingly, Zajkowski et al.32 reported that, in a free-choice task, preferred options in equally rewarded situations were accompanied by shorter reaction times. This pattern was also evident in our results (see Fig. 3 and FigSI-Fig. 1): preferred regions–whether reflecting a learned preference, a stable bias or a combination of both (see above)–were selected with saccades showing shorter latencies than those directed to the other region. These shorter saccade latencies were not accompanied by lower saccade accuracy, excluding any speed-accuracy trade-off. Grey points in Fig. 3 correspond to data points of participants with extreme choice rate values and hence, with too few data points for one region to be included in the linear regression analysis due to insufficient reliability in estimating the mean. These participants nevertheless presented results–although caution is warranted due to the limited data–that generally followed the overall trend consisting in lower saccade latency for their preferred option. Importantly, if we similarly consider grey data points in Fig. 5, our study also suggest that these spontaneous preferences were accompanied by greater confidence in perceptual judgments (see below for further discussion on confidence).

Overall, we propose that a potential framework to explain our results involves a preference-based process that guides saccade choice behavior. This preference process could initially be influenced by the possibility (not necessarily for all participants) of an initial or rapidly adopted bias. Then, learning of the informational probability distribution may modulate this preference process. This modulation varies in degree across participants, and can potentially override an initial bias if present. Critically, saccade reaction time and confidence ratings in perceptual judgment would ultimately be determined by this preference mechanism, as participants with extreme choice rate values–and presumably no learning–still followed the general trend. We suggest that this type of preference-based process, which integrates different influences such as initial spatial biases and learned value (e.g., informational), could correspond to a unified attentional map like the one proposed by Belopolsky6. In the following section, we further discuss the implicit learning component.

Information versus motivational reward

Extensive research has shown that the probability of receiving an affective reward, such as money or game points, influences decision-making in free-choice tasks10,16,17,27,28,30. Here, we tested whether the availability of task-relevant information, probabilistically manipulated and independent of affective value, could similarly guide behavior. This question stems from growing evidence that affective rewards and information-based drives are dissociable, both empirically (see Introduction) and within theoretical frameworks such as active inference52,53 or active sensing54. Supporting this distinction, neuroimaging and electrophysiological studies have implicated partially distinct neural systems for reward value and information value processing55,56 (but see57).

Despite these differences, our findings suggest a functional convergence between the two. Just as affective rewards shape behavior, we observed that the availability modulation of task-relevant information biased saccade selection, suggesting that information can act as a value signal. This parallel was reinforced by the modulation of saccade latency–another well-established marker of value-based behavior control–although peak velocity remained unaffected (see FigSI-Fig2). This latter point is notable given that saccadic peak velocity is often interpreted as a proxy for motivational vigor19,40,58,59 (see41 for review). The fact that saccade velocity was not impacted in our study may reflect a lower salience or incentive weight of visual task-related information compared to affective rewards (see also26).

The current parallel drawn between affective reward mechanisms and informational factors, based on the present results, is in line with recent proposals suggesting that vision itself can serve as an oculomotor reward60, thereby emphasizing the cognitive contributions to saccadic control. The broader literature also highlights how the brain assigns value to information per se61–63, which is consistent with our interpretation. In the present task, performance (see middle panels of Fig. 4) was tightly coupled to the duration of available information, suggesting that success may have served as an internal reinforcement signal64–66.

One avenue for future research is to investigate whether learning about information-related availability observed here generalizes to different eye movement tasks, such as more complex visual search tasks or, conversely, to more reactive saccades (see e.g.,67). Interestingly concerning the latter, express saccades - saccades with extremely short reaction time68 - are thought to bypass value representations altogether69: thus, they should not be influenced by the information-related value learning observed here. Overall, these transfer studies could offer important insights into the mechanisms by which value, whether affective or informational, shapes visuomotor behavior.

Priority map and lateral interactions

Probability learning, whether based on reward6,18 or spatial regularities (referring to statistical learning9,11), is proposed to influence a priority map that contributes to attentional target selection. Lateral interactions represent a hallmark of these maps, facilitating information integration and the refinement of representations34,35. In the context of statistical learning in a covert attention paradigm, Wang and Theeuwes36 showed that regularities in distractor location can be learned across eight locations, and that the status of one location can influence neighboring locations. These findings support the idea of a learning-modifiable map that integrates lateral interactions. In our study, the spillover we observed from our informational availability distribution to nearby regions provides further support for this hypothesis of a priority map including lateral interactions.

Post-saccadic perceptual accuracy

In an additional singleton paradigm, participants are typically asked to find a specific target shape while a distractor may also appear36,42. This task is usually performed while gaze remains at fixation, requiring thus covert attention. If a distractor appears more frequently at a specific location, its influence on response times decreases, and interestingly, error rates in other locations also decrease. This suggests that covert attention orientation is facilitated toward the low-probability distractor locations, which more often contains the task-relevant item. We asked whether such modulation of accuracy would be seen with overt attention; in our study a saccade was executed, and the target was on the fovea during the orientation discrimination task. Cheng et al.45 showed that in foveal condition, monetary reward enhanced perceptual accuracy. Here we did not find such an accuracy benefit for the preferred location. Perhaps informational availability reflects lower saliency compared to affective reward in foveal vision. This lower salience may have been insufficient to influence perceptual processes. This interpretation is also consistent with the absence of peak saccade velocity modulation discussed above.

Confidence in perceptual judgment

After performing a perceptual judgment, we can evaluate the confidence we have in this perceptual decision. This confidence rating reflects our belief about the likelihood that the perceptual decision was correct46–48. In the present study, despite the absence of any modulation of perceptual accuracy, we found a relationship between participants’ preferences and their confidence ratings across different locations of the visual scene in Experiment 2 (50 ms, see Fig. 5A): confidence ratings were higher for the preferred locations, even though mean perceptual accuracy rates were similar for both AR and NAR locations (see above).

One question is why is this relationship found for the Gabor 50 ms presentation-Experiment 2, but not in the 16.67 ms-Experiment 1? One possibility may be that implicit learning links with confidence only when it is discriminant, and hence useful. This was the case in Experiment 2 for which we found that confidence ratings were different when perceptual judgments were actually correct versus incorrect (see inset in Fig. 5A). On the contrary, when participants had to judge gabor grating orientation presented for only 16.67 ms, their confidence was rated similarly whether they actually gave a correct judgment response, or not.

This pattern of results for confidence modulation, in Experiment 2 but not in Experiment 1, is difficult to reconcile with studies emphasizing the role of uncertainty in confidence ratings70,71. Indeed, uncertainty concerning the AR should be similar for 16.67 ms and 50 ms, or even greater at 16.67 ms. Zylberberg et al.72 suggested a role of preference biases in confidence ratings. Here also, we showed preferences biases in both Experiments 1 and 2, but confidence varied only in Experiment 2. Further work is required to clarify these discrepancies.

Importantly, this modulation of perceptual judgment confidence occurred without any change in perceptual accuracy (see above). Confidence modulation without accuracy modulation was found in several studies49,73 (see50 for a review). Rahnev and Denison50 proposed two explanations for this dissociation: either perceptual and confidence responses rely on distinct processing streams, or a supplementary stage selectively determines confidence. Our results at least support that confidence does not only rely on perceptual evidence. A final question can be raised: does confidence change because preferences (and thus choices) evolve, or conversely, is the modulation of confidence the cause of evolving choices? We believe that the fact that in Experiment 1 we observed changes in choices without any alteration in confidence supports the first alternative. Indeed, one would have observed changes in confidence along with the learned choice modulation in Experiment 1 if changes in confidence were causal to the evolution of preferences.

Overall, our results suggest that saccade spatial preferences, related to information availability distribution learning or initial biases, could influence confidence in perceptual judgment.

Conclusion

Our present study reveals that the probability of finding easily exploitable information, just like the probability of obtaining affective reward (e.g., money or game points), is implicitly integrated to guide saccade decisions in free-choice situations. Resulting saccade preferences are compatible with a spatial priority map integrating lateral interactions and modulating saccade initiation time. By examining perceptual processes after these saccadic decisions, we also show that these developed preferences introduce a dissociation between the accuracy of perceptual judgment and confidence in these perceptual decisions, when confidence ratings are discriminant between correct and incorrect perceptual judgments; accuracy was not modified but confidence was higher for preferred locations. Overall, the present study discloses strong links between available information distribution, oculomotor decision and perceptual judgment confidence.

Limitations of the study

While our findings provide strong evidence that informational availability can implicitly shape saccadic choices and metacognitive confidence, several limitations should be considered. First, the relatively short duration of the experimental session (120 trials per participant) may have constrained the extent of learning and limited our ability to fully characterize its time course. Future studies with longer exposure could better describe learning dynamics. Second, a subset of participants exhibited strong spatial biases, potentially masking or interacting with learning effects. While we accounted for this in our analyses, additional controls or adaptive designs might help distinguish learning from idiosyncratic preferences. Finally, our results concerning metacognitive confidence were significant only under specific task conditions (i.e., when confidence ratings were discriminant), suggesting that the relationship between information-based learning and metacognition may depend on task difficulty or perceptual clarity. The present study provides substantial evidence that information-based learning can shape both eye movement behavior and confidence judgments; future studies could build on these findings to further delineate the underlying mechanisms and boundary conditions.

Materials and methods

Participants.

Ninety-six volunteers participated in the study (ages 18 to 46 years, mean = 19.6 years, SD = 3, 82 females) and received course credits for their participation. They were undergraduate students from the Institute of Psychology of Université Paris Cité. All participants had normal or corrected-to-normal vision and no known neurological disorders. All were naïve to the objectives of the study and to the experimental condition they were in. They gave informed written consent prior to the experimental session. Sixty-four participants participated in Experiment 1 and the remaining 32 participants participated in Experiment 2. These two Experiments differed in the duration of the presentation of the Gabor patch for the discrimination task (see below, and Fig. 1). The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Université Paris Cité (IRB number: 00012023-9) and conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations, as well as the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Apparatus.

Participants sat in a dimly lit room with their head stabilized with a chin and forehead rest. A ProPixx DLP Projector (VPixx Technologies; resolution 1024×768 pixels, refresh rate 240 Hz) was used to project the experimental stimuli onto a matt white screen (effective display size of 240×180 cm) placed at a viewing distance of 153 cm. The position of the right eye was recorded using an EyeLink 1000+ infrared recording system (SR Research Ltd.) equipped with a 35-mm lens at a sampling rate of 1000 Hz. Synchronization between the eye tracker and the visual experimental display was achieved by custom Python 3 scripts relying on the PyLink package74 along with PsychoPy75. The gaze estimation accuracy was ensured by a 9-points calibration procedure prior to the recordings. Calibration was performed again during the experiment when necessary. To control visual stimuli based on oculomotor behavior, saccadic eye movements were detected online using a velocity threshold optimized for each participant76. Saccade onsets were detected when reaching the thresholds of 40.2±12.8∘/s horizontally and 46.6±14.3∘/s vertically (Mean ± SD) across trials and participants.

Stimuli.

Visual stimuli were random noise and circular Gabor patches (1.14x1.14∘ size) presented on a grey background. The Gabor gratings had a spatial frequency of 4.39 c/d and their orientation was either 0∘ (vertical) or +6∘ (tilted) from vertical (Fig. 1A).

Procedure and task.

At the start of each trial, a fixation cross was presented for a duration of 1300 ms. Subsequently, two noise patches were simultaneously displayed on either side of the central fixation cross, forming one of four possible pairs of stimuli (along 4 possible directions: 0∘-180∘, 45∘-225∘, 90∘-270∘, 135∘-315∘; Fig. 1B). Together, the two items of these pairs were positioned along an imaginary circle with an 8∘ radius (Fig. 1D) and represented two potential targets. Participants were asked to select one of the two potential targets and make a saccade toward the chosen one. At the end of the saccade (Fig. 1C), the target noise patch changed into a Gabor patch grating. Participants were then required to judge the orientation of the grating: either vertical or tilted. They were told that on each trial, the orientation of the gabor patch grating at the two opposite positions was the same, whichever the location they chose. They were thus encouraged to freely choose the location they preferred to complete the orientation judgment task for that trial, and do their best to judge the orientation. No specific instruction was provided to vary their choices or their visual exploration.

Following its presentation, the grating was masked again by a noise patch for a duration up to 300 ms after the saccade end. Then a screen was displayed to ask for the orientation perceptual response (Fig. 1E). Participants responded by pressing either the upward (for vertical orientation) or the rightward (tilted orientation) arrow key on a keypad with their right hand (forced-choice response). They were then asked to self-report their confidence in this perceptual judgment on a 7-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (’not confident at all’) to 7 (’totally confident’). Arrow keys allowed to displace a cursor on the presented scale, and the space bar was used to validate the chosen value with their left hand (Fig. 1E). A 600 ms dark grey screen indicated the end of the trial.

To investigate the influence of information availability on saccade choices required to perform the discrimination task, we manipulated the duration of the Gabor presentation probabilistically (see below). The presentation duration was either long (250 ms - 60 frames, easy trials) or very brief (16.67 ms - 4 frames in Experiment 1, or 50 ms - 12 frames in Experiment 2, difficult trials, Fig. 1C). Easy trials allowed subjects to comfortably inspect Gabors and judge their orientation. In difficult trials, information availability was strongly reduced and challenging, with a higher degree for the 16.67 ms duration77 (see their Figure 2a). This 16.67 ms duration was validated by pilot tests, which resulted in orientation accuracy judgments at or just above chance level. We initially ran the 16.67 ms Experiment (= Experiment 1). Following our observations of information-based learning, in order to evaluate this learning in a less constrained but still challenging context, we carried out a second Experiment including a Gabor presentation time of 50 ms (= Experiment 2).

Unbeknownst to participants, the distribution of easy and difficult trials was probabilistic and spatially dependent. For each participant, there was a specific region where information access was reduced and more challenging, called the ‘altered’ region. In this altered region (AR), two adjacent positions (e.g., 0∘ and 45∘ positions in Fig. 1D) were associated with 80% probability of having difficult trials (Gabor presentation duration = 16.67 ms in Experiment 1, or 50 ms in Experiment 2) and 20% probability of having easy trials (Gabor presented for 250 ms). The opposite positions (e.g. 180∘ and 225∘ in Fig. 1D) represented the non-altered region (NAR). In this NAR region, 80% trials were easy whereas 20% were difficult. These two target pairs were referred to as Critical pairs. They allowed to determine whether, and to what extent, the lower probability of obtaining easily usable information in the AR would affect saccade choices. The remaining two target pairs, referred to as Control pairs, corresponded to a 20% probability of a difficult trial and 80% of an easy trial on each side, a proportion of easy and difficult trials similar to that of the NAR. For each of these Control target pairs, one target was closer to the AR while the other was farther. These Control trials allowed us to investigate whether the lower probability of accessing usable information in the AR would influence saccade choice in close versus far locations. The eight possible locations of the altered region (0∘-45∘, 45∘-90∘, 90∘-135∘, 135∘-180∘, 180∘-225∘, 225∘-270∘, 270∘-315∘, 315∘-0∘) were counterbalanced across participants.

In both Experiments, each stimuli pair was presented 30 times, for a total of 120 trials per participant. The order of trials was randomized for each participant. Trials were interrupted and immediately repeated if 1) a saccade occurred before 80 ms following the stimuli pair presentation; 2) no saccade was produced within 3000 ms; or 3) if the detected saccade was not directed toward one of the two targets (+/- 45∘ spatial tolerance). In these cases, warnings appeared on the screen: “too fast”, “too long”, “landing error”, respectively.

Data analysis.

Participants were engaged in binary choices. To examine whether the decrease in task-related information presentation duration in the altered region impacted saccade choice, we calculated a choice rate for each participant for Critical trials. Choice rate was defined as: number of saccades directed to the AR / (number of saccades toward the AR + number of saccades toward the NAR). We then assessed whether the choice rate deviated from a 50/50 distribution by using chi-square tests. The choice rate for the Control pairs was similarly analyzed to investigate saccade preference for Close versus Far regions.

To characterize the evolution of choice behavior over the course of the experimental session, we conducted mixed-effects logistic regression analyses. For each experiment, separate models were fitted to data from the Critical and Control pairs. Each model included two fixed factors: (i) display index, corresponding to the presentation number of a given pair (ranging from 1 to 30, coded as 0 to 29); and (ii) pair identity, to account for any difference between the two pairs (coded as -0.5 and +0.5 with -0.5 corresponding to the first encountered by the participant; see Fig. 1D). In addition, models included the participant as a random factor, allowing for the modeling of two random effects: a random intercept, capturing baseline choice behavior at the first display index regardless of the pair, and a random slope for display index, capturing individual differences in how choice behavior evolved over time. P-values for fixed effects were obtained using Wald (Z) tests, comparing each estimated coefficient to zero.

To investigate a potential spillover of the information-based learning obtained from the AR to adjacent regions (Close regions, Fig. 1D), we conducted linear regression analyses testing, participant-wise, a relationship between the choice rate for the altered region (AR) and the choice rate for the close regions.

To examine the effect of information availability on saccadic parameters and post-saccadic tasks, we computed differences in saccade latency, perceptual judgement accuracy scores, and judgment confidence ratings between the NAR and AR for each participant. Positive difference values denoted higher values for responses in the NAR compared to the AR. The choice rate (across the 60 Critical trials) was used as an index of spatial preferences for each participant. We assessed whether there was a relationship between the choice rate and each of these difference values by using regression analyses. Similar analyses were performed by considering differences between Close and Far regions. Complementary analyses are reported in Supplementary Material for absolute values of saccade latency, saccade peak velocity and saccadic landing error. For saccade latency and peak velocity, values more than 1.5 times the interquartile range above the third quartile or below the first quartile, were considered as outliers and removed. For perceptual accuracy and confidence scores, we selected only the difficult trials, that is, trials where the chosen location presented a gabor for 16.67 or 50 ms. Indeed, for the easy task (gabor presentation of 250 ms), scores were not discriminant (mean accuracy for easy trials = 97.5% (Experiment 1) and 97.3% (Experiment 2), mean confidence for easy trials = 6.28 (Experiment 1) and 6.31 (Experiment 2)).

For all these linear regression analyses, we removed data from participants whose choice rate was either (< or = 0.1) or (= or > 0.9). Indeed, with these extreme choice rate values, there were too little data (in the AR or NAR) to obtain reliable difference values for our regression analyses. Although not included in regression analyses, we illustrated the data points of these participants for comparison (in grey symbols in Fig. 3-5). In each regression analysis, we first centered (removing the mean) the choice rate distribution to evaluate a significant difference from zero for the mean of the regressed parameter across all participants (y-intercept) and then, we examined the relationship between the choice rate and difference values. With these simple linear regression analyses, R2 corresponded to the effect size. Overall significance was set at alpha = 0.05.
