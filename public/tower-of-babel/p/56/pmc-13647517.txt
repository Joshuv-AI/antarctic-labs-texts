S2P-modified PLGA bifunctional nanodrug: inhibiting vascular senescence and foam cell formation for atherosclerosis treatment
Authors: Cilin Zhao, Xiaolan Luo, Yajuan Fu, Huixia Yang, Jiaqi Yang, Feifei Yu, Xiaoyan Dong, Huiping Zhang, Yinju Hao, Bin Liu, Yideng Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The progression of atherosclerosis (AS) is closely related to endothelial senescence and abnormal lipid metabolism in macrophages. Although metformin (Met) can exert vascular endothelial protective effects by inhibiting endothelial cell senescence, the drug exhibits limited capacity to regulate lipid metabolism. In contrast, evolocumab (Evol), a PCSK9-specific inhibitor, can lower LDL-C levels to reduce foam cell formation. This study takes pathological endothelial cells and macrophages as core targets. Leveraging the complementary between Met-mediated anti-endothelial senescence and Evol-regulated lipid metabolism, S2P peptide-modified PLGA nanocarriers (S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs) were constructed to achieve co-delivery of the two drugs, further enhancing the dual-targeting function of the nanomedicine. In vitro studies showed that S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs effectively reduced oxidative damage, inhibited vascular aging and enhanced macrophage cholesterol efflux. In vivo studies showed that this nanodrug significantly alleviated aging damage, lipid accumulation, and inflammatory responses, synergistically reduced plaque burden, enhanced plaque stability, and exhibited a good safety profile. In conclusion, this dual-cell targeted synergistic regulation strategy provides an effective approach for the treatment of AS.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12951-026-04659-w.

Introduction

Atherosclerosis (AS) is the primary pathological basis of cardiovascular diseases [1]. Its pathogenesis involves multiple risk factors and complex pathophysiological processes, including oxidative stress, vascular aging, endothelial dysfunction, lipid accumulation, and chronic inflammation. Their interactions collectively contribute to plaque formation and potential rupture [2–4]. Homocysteine (Hcy), as an intermediate metabolite of methionine, accelerates endothelial cell senescence, enhances lipid accumulation and phenotypic conversion of macrophages, and is causally implicated in the progression of AS lesions as an independent risk factor [5–7]. Therefore, regulating endothelial cell senescence and promoting macrophage lipid efflux to alleviate inflammatory responses holds promise for restricting the pathological progression of AS.

Hyperhomocysteinemia (HHcy) induces oxidative stress in endothelial cells attached to the vascular endothelium, leading to the production of large amounts of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [8]. ROS induces DNA damage, activates cell cycle checkpoints, and triggers cell cycle arrest, ultimately driving cells into a senescent state [9]. Additionally, senescent endothelial cells release senescence-associated secretory proteins (SASP), further impairing endothelial function and promoting AS [10]. Therefore, intervening in vascular aging holds significant clinical value for inhibiting AS. To date, metformin (Met) is the first drug in clinical trials to demonstrate an age-targeted effect [11]. This drug mitigates endothelial senescence and enhances cellular function through multiple mechanisms. Beyond adenosine monophosphate (AMP)-activated protein kinase (AMPK)-dependent pathways, it also activates AMPK-independent pathways - including DNA damage response, MAPK signaling, and nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) pathways - thereby slowing the progression of cardiovascular diseases [12]. However, gastrointestinal side effects caused by long-term use and low bioavailability have led to under utilization of metformin [13]. Consequently, there is an urgent need to develop novel strategies to modulate endothelial cell senescence to enhance the targeting, bioavailability, and stability of Met, while providing personalized treatment options to improve the management of AS.

Apart from endothelial cells, macrophages extensively phagocytose oxidized low-density lipoproteins via surface scavenger receptors, leading to lipid accumulation and activates M1 macrophages to produce inflammatory cytokines, promoting plaque progression and instability [14–16]. As a regulatory protein involved in lipid metabolism, Proprotein convertase subtilisin/Kexin type 9 (PCSK9) plays a key role in Hcy-induced AS [17, 18]. On one hand, Hcy upregulates PCSK9 expression to suppress ABCA1/ABCG1-mediated cholesterol efflux, thereby facilitating lipid accumulation in macrophages [19]. On the other hand, PCSK9 exacerbates atherosclerotic progression by promoting M1 macrophage polarization and amplifying inflammatory responses [18]. Therefore, inhibiting PCSK9 expression mitigates lipid accumulation and suppresses inflammatory responses, thereby conferring anti-atherosclerotic effects. Evol, a specific PCSK9 inhibitor, effectively suppresses foam cell formation and mitigates inflammatory responses and has been clinically approved for the treatment of cardiovascular diseases [20]. However, this medication may be associated with adverse events, including headaches, muscle spasms, and limb pain [21]. Thus, reducing the dosage while maintaining efficacy and improving therapeutic outcomes remains a critical challenge in the clinical management of AS.

This study overcomes the limitations of Met monotherapy by combining with Evol, thereby synergistically enhancing the therapeutic efficacy in AS treatment. This is achieved by suppressing endothelial cell senescence, promoting macrophage lipid efflux, and inducing phenotypic switching to mitigate inflammatory responses. However, achieving synchronous co-delivery of the two drugs to elicit synergistic effects remains an unresolved challenge. Nanomedicine, with its capabilities of precise targeted drug delivery and immunotherapy, has been widely used for the treatment of AS [16, 22]. Among various nanocarriers, poly (lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) has garnered significant attention in the biomedical field as a U.S. FDA-approved drug carrier. This recognition is attributed to its exceptional biocompatibility, controllable biodegradability, and favorable safety profile [23]. Herein, we developed a bifunctional nanomedicine that synergistically exerted anti-inflammatory effects for AS treatment by inhibiting endothelial cell senescence and foam cell formation. Specifically, PLGA polymers were utilized to co-deliver Met and Evol. Surface modification with the S2P peptide (CRTLLTVRKC), which selectively binds to stabilin-2, a receptor highly expressed on plaque macrophages and endothelial [24–25], enabled the construction of targeted nanomedicine (designated as S-P@(Met+Evol) NPs). The conceptual design and engineering of this nanotherapeutic platform were guided by the following considerations: (i) Met exerts anti-aging effects via AMPK-independent pathways - including DNA damage response, MAPK signaling, and NF-κB pathways- thereby protecting vascular endothelial function; (ii) Evol exerts lipid-lowering and anti-inflammatory effects by inhibiting PCSK9, thereby enhancing macrophage cholesterol efflux through ABCA1 and ABCG1 upregulation and promoting phenotypic switching; (iii) S2P peptide-conjugated PLGA nanoparticles exhibit specific targeting capacity by selectively recognizing stabilin-2 receptors on the surface of diseased endothelial cells and macrophages. In vitro and in vivo studies demonstrated that S-P@(Met+Evol) NPs exhibited superior targeting efficiency, robust anti-aging activity, modulated cholesterol metabolism, and potent anti-inflammatory properties - thereby markedly reducing aortic plaque burden and providing a promising therapeutic strategy for AS (Fig 1).

Materials and methods

Reagents and materials

Metformin and BCA Protein Quantification Kit were provided by Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd (Beijing, China). Evolocumab was provided by Amgen Manufacturing Limited (USA). Mal-PEG-NH2 was purchased from Penson Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). S2P peptide (CRTLTVRKC) was provided by Qiangyao Biotechnology Co., Ltd. DAPI, homocysteine, and Oil Red O were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich Company (MO, USA). Ox-LDL was provided by Yiyuan Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Guangzhou, China). Senescence-Associated β-Galactosidase and amplex red cholesterol and cholesterol ester detection kit was provided by Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology (China). Anti-γ-H2AX, anti-p-p38MAPK and anti-p-p65NF-κB antibodies were purchased from Abcam Shanghai Trading Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Anti-p53, anti-p21, anti-ABCG1, anti-ABCA1, anti-CD80, anti-CD206, and anti-PCSK9 antibodies were purchased from Triple Eagle Technologies (IL, USA). ELISA kits were purchased from Shinbo Sheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (China).

Preparation of S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs

S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs were prepared using a dual-emulsion solvent evaporation technique. A 3% ultrasonic treatment was applied to emulsify a 2 min mixture of 1 mg Met and 0.5 mg Evol in aqueous solution, which was then added dropwise to 5 mg PLGA to form a water-in-oil (W/O) primary emulsion. The mixture was then transferred to 10 mL of 1% polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) solution and homogenized for 2 min at 800 rpm/min. Secondary emulsification was performed using 6% ultrasonication for 2 min, yielding a water-in-oil-in-water (W/O/W) emulsion. Organic solvents were evaporated by continuous stirring at 600 rpm/min for 12 h. Centrifuge at 10,000 rpm for 10 min to collect the P@(Met + Evol) NPs precipitate for later use. Mix PLGA NPs, EDC, Sulfo-NHS, and Mal-PEG-NH₂ to achieve nanoparticle polyethylene glycolation. First, suspend PLGA NPs, EDC, and Sulfo-NHS in 5 mL phosphate-buffered saline (pH 7.0) and stir at room temperature for 1 h to form the ester. Next, dissolve Mal-PEG-NH₂ in 1 mL phosphate-buffered saline (pH 7.0). Finally, the Mal-PEG-NH₂ mixture was added to the above solution and gently stirred at room temperature. The thiol group of S2P peptide reacted with Mal-PEG-PLGA nanoparticles in the phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) at a 1:1 (M/M) molar ratio. The mixture was stirred overnight at 200 rpm at room temperature to achieve coupling between the S2P peptide’s thiol group and the Mal functional groups of PEG surrounding the nanoparticle surface. Finally, centrifuge at 10,000 rpm for 15 min to obtain S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs.

Representation of S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs

Dispersed PLGA NPs, P@(Met + Evol) NPs, and S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs dispersions were spotted onto copper grids coated with carbon film, dried at room temperature, and analyzed using TEM. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) was employed to determine the particle size distribution and zeta potential of the NPs. FTIR spectroscopy analysis was conducted using an FTIR8300 series spectrometer (Shimadzu, Japan). High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) was used for the quantitative determination of metformin.

The release behavior of S-P@(Met+Evol) NPs

The drug release profiles of S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs in vitro were evaluated by monitoring the drug concentration in 5 mL PBS (pH 6.4 and pH 7.4). Specifically, 1 mL of the drug-loaded formulation was sealed in a dialysis membrane and immersed in PBS (pH 6.4 and pH 7.4), with continuous stirring in a 37℃ water bath. The concentration of Evol in the dialysate at 0, 2, 6, 12, 24, 48, and 72 h was determined using a BCA protein assay kit, and the cumulative release amount of Evol was calculated according to the standard curve. The concentration of Met was measured at the same time points using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, Japan) at a wavelength of 231 nm, and the cumulative release amount of Met was calculated according to the standard curve.

In vitro blood compatibility

Whole blood was collected from C57 BL/6 mice, plasma was separated, and the remaining cells were washed with PBS, then centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min at 4℃ until the supernatant clarified, and the RBC precipitate was collected. Subsequently, 25 µL of RBC + PBS (negative control), distilled water (positive control), different concentrations of Met+Evol, P@(Met + Evol) NPs, and S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs were added (total volume 500 µL). The mixture was incubated at 37℃ for 6 h. After centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 10 min at 4℃, 200 µL of the supernatant was aspirated and placed in a 96-well plate, and the absorbance at 562 nm was measured. Meanwhile, the morphology of red blood cells was observed under an inverted fluorescence microscope.

Cell culture

Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs), macrophage cells (RAW264.7), and vascular smooth muscle cells (VCSMs) were obtained from the Chinese Academy of Sciences Cell Bank/Stem Cell Bank. Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Sijiqing), 1% streptomycin, and penicillin. Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO₂.

In vitro cytotoxicity evaluation of S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs

Cytotoxicity assessment of S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs using RAW264.7, HUVECs, and VCSMs. After seeding 1 × 10⁴ cells per well in a 96-well plate and incubating for 24 h, add different concentrations of S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs to the wells and incubate for an additional 24 h. Apply MTT reagent according to protocol, incubate at 37℃ for 30 min, then measure absorbance at 562 nm.

Cell uptake and escape behavior

RAW264.7 and HUVEC cells were cultured in 12-well plates. First, confocal microscopy was used to detect the uptake efficiency of Ce6-labeled S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs by both cell lines. Subsequently, lysosomes were labeled with lysosome-specific fluorescent probes (e.g., LysoTracker), and fluorescence colocalization analysis was employed to evaluate the lysosomal escape capacity of the nanoparticles.

Mechanisms for the treatment of atherosclerosis

SA-β-Galactosidase staining

HUVECs (1 × 10⁴ cells/well) treated with 100 µM Hcy were cultured in 12-well plates at 37℃ in a 5% CO₂ incubator for 24 h. Supernatant was removed, and cells were treated with Met + Evol and S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs for 12 h, followed by three washes with PBS. Add β-galactosidase staining fixative and fix at room temperature for 15 min. Remove the cell fixative and wash three times with PBS. Subsequently, add the staining working solution and incubate overnight at 37℃. Finally, remove the working solution, add 1 mL of PBS, and observe under an optical microscope.

Evaluation of antioxidant activity

HUVECs and RAW264.7 cells (1 × 10⁴ cells/well) were treated with 100 µM Hcy in 12-well plates and cultured for 4 h at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ incubator to induce intracellular ROS production. Cells were then treated with Met+Evol and S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs for 2 h, followed by incubation with the DCFH-DA probe (10 µM) for 30 min. Intracellular green fluorescence signals were visualized using a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM). Mitochondrial membrane potential in RAW264.7 cells was assessed using the JC-1 Mitochondrial Membrane Potential Assay Kit. Fluorescence signals were visualized and quantified via confocal laser scanning microscopy.

Evaluation of anti-inflammatory effect

HUVECs and RAW264.7 cells were cultured in 24-well plates. After 2 h pretreatment with Met + Evol and S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs, cells were co-incubated with 100 µM Hcy for 24 h. Supernatants were collected, centrifuged at 12,000 rpm at 4℃ for 30 min, and SASP factor and inflammatory factor levels were detected according to the ELISA kit protocol.

Cell cycle assessment

HUVECs were cultured in 6-well plates with 100 µM Hcy for 24 h. After 12 h of treatment with Met + Evol and S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs, cells were washed three times with PBS, collected, and stained using the V-FITC/PI cell cycle detection kit. Apoptosis levels were then measured by flow cytometry, with results analyzed using FlowJo software.

Cell migration assessment

HUVECs were cultured in 12-well plates with 100 µM Hcy for 24 h. Treatment with Met + Evol and S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs was performed for 12 h. Scratch assays were observed at 0 h and 24 h using an inverted microscope, and results were analyzed with Image software.

Cytoskeletal assessment

HUVECs (1 × 10⁴ cells/well) treated with 100 µM Hcy were cultured in 12-well plates at 37℃ in a 5% CO₂ incubator for 24 h. Supernatant was removed, and cells were treated with Met + Evol and S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs for 12 h. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min and permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 15 min. Wash with PBS. Incubate cells with Alexa Fluor 568-labeled Phalloidin (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) for 30 min in the dark to specifically label F-actin. Subsequently, stain cell nuclei with DAPI for 10 min. After staining, wash thoroughly and image cells using CLSM.

Ox-LDL uptake assessment

RAW264.7 cells were treated with 100 µM Hcy in 12-well plates for 24 h. Following the same protocol as above, cells underwent 2 h of drug pretreatment before co-incubation with 80 µg/mL ox-LDL for 48 h. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min, stained with 0.3% ORO for 1 h, and washed with 60% isopropanol. Finally, images were captured under an inverted microscope, and plaque areas were analyzed using software.

Cholesterol and cholesterol ester detection assay

RAW264.7 cells were treated with 100 µM Hcy in a 24-well plate for 24 h. After adding Met + Evol or S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs for 12 h, collect cells, discard the supernatant, and aspirate residual liquid. Add an appropriate proportion of Beyolysis™ Buffer A for Metabolic Assay. After gentle pipetting, collect cells into centrifuge tubes, centrifuge to obtain the supernatants, and perform detection according to the kit instruction.

Immunofluorescence

HUVECs and RAW264.7 cells were treated with 100 µM Hcy in 24-well plates for 24 h. Supernatant was removed, and cells were treated with Met + Evol and S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs for 24 h. After treatment, cells were washed three times with PBS. Fix cells with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min, then wash three times with PBS. Permeabilize cells at room temperature with immunofluorescence mounting medium for 15 min. Block non-specific binding sites by adding goat serum (1:20 dilution) for 1 h. Add primary antibodies against γ-H2AX, ABCA1/G1, CD80 and CD206, incubating overnight at 4℃. Wash three times with PBS, add fluorescent secondary antibodies, and incubate at room temperature for 2 h. Wash three times with PBS, stain nuclei with DAPI, and capture images using CLSM.

Western blotting analysis

Protein expression levels were quantitatively assessed across experimental groups. Total proteins were extracted using RIPA lysis buffer, with protein concentration determined via the BCA method. Protein samples were separated by SDS-PAGE gel electrophoresis and transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were then blocked with 5% nonfat milk at room temperature for 2 h, followed by three 5 min washes with TBST. Primary antibodies targeting p38 MAPK, p-p38 MAPK, p65 NF-κB, p-p65 NF-κB, γ-H2AX, p53, p21, ABCG1, PCSK9 and GAPDH were incubated overnight at 4 °C. After three washes with TBST (5 min/wash), HRP-labeled secondary antibodies were added and incubated at room temperature for 2 h. Wash three times with TBST for 5 min each. Develop the PVDF membrane using chemiluminescent developing solutions.

High-throughput transcriptome sequencing analysis of cells

Collect HUVECs and RAW264.7 cells from the Control group, Hcy group, and S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs group. Centrifuge to obtain cell pellets for high-throughput transcriptomic sequencing. (Guangzhou Gedi Bio Co., Ltd., Guangzhou, China).

In vivo pharmacodynamic studies

Pharmacokinetic analysis and in vivo targeting studies

Six-week-old C57BL/6 mice were administered P@Ce6 NPs and S-P@Ce6 NPs (5 mg/kg) via tail vein injection. At predetermined time points, 50 µL blood was collected from the orbital venous plexus, centrifuged to obtain supernatant, and analyzed for Ce6 fluorescence using an IVIS spectral imaging system (Lumina XR). In vivo targeting experiments: 12 h after tail vein injection of Ce6, P@Ce6 NPs, and S-P@Ce6NPs (5 mg/kg) into 6-week-old male ApoE-/- mice, the aorta and major organs were harvested and imaged using the IVIS Spectra System to detect Ce6 fluorescence.

Feeding and treatment protocol for atherosclerotic mice

Male C57BL/ApoE-/- mice (seven weeks old) were fed a high-methionine diet (HMD) for 12 weeks to induce atherosclerotic plaque formation. Male ApoE-/- mice aged 7 weeks were fed HMD for 4 weeks, followed by tail vein injections of PBS, Met (0.5 mg/kg), Evol (5 mg/kg), Met + Evol (0.5 mg/kg + 2.5 mg/kg), and S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs (0.5 mg/kg + 2.5 mg/kg) twice weekly.

Pathological examination of atherosclerotic lesions and aortic tissue

Whole aortas from ApoE-/- mice were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde until transparent. Longitudinally sectioned aortas were stained with ORO to detect lipid-rich plaques. Frozen sections of the aortic root were stained and quantified with 3% ORO. Histological examination was performed using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and Masson’s Trichrome Staining. Immunohistochemistry detected relevant antibodies in aortic root sections (ABCA1: 1:200; ABCG1: 1:200). ROS within plaques was assessed via dihydroethyltriamine (DHE) staining. To evaluate the in vivo effects of S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs, aortic root sections were stained with anti-p38MAPK (1:500), anti-p65NF-κB (1:500), p53 (1:500), anti-p21 (1:500), anti-γ-H2AX (1:500), anti-CD80 (1:500), and anti-CD206 (1:500) antibodies. Images were acquired using CLSM.

Vivo biosafety assessment

Serum samples were collected from each group of mice and analyzed using a blood biochemistry analyzer to measure relevant indicators. Major organs such as the heart, spleen, lung, and kidney were collected from each group of mice and prepared into paraffin sections for H&E staining.

Serum metabolomics analysis

Blood samples from the HMD group and S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs group of ApoE-/- mice were collected, centrifuged to separate plasma, which was then used for metabolomics sequencing (Hangzhou Jingjie Bioengineering Co., Ltd., Hangzhou, China).

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 10.0 and reported as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). One-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to evaluate the significance of the data. The significance levels were defined as: *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001, ns, not significant (P > 0.05).

Result and discussion

The combined application of Met and Evol inhibits endothelial cell senescence and macrophage foaming

Homocysteine induces endothelial cell senescence, impairs macrophage cholesterol efflux and phenotypic switching, and thereby synergistically accelerates the progression of AS [6, 17, 18]. Therefore, we used HUVECs and RAW264.7 as in vitro models to explore the therapeutic potential of Met plus Evol in Hcy-induced AS. The safe concentrations of Met and Evol for cells were first determined using the MTT assay. The results showed that in two cell models, cell viability of all groups remained above 80% under low-dose treatment (2.5 µM Met + 1.25 nM Evol). High-dose Evol monotherapy reduced cell viability to near or slightly below the 80% safety threshold, indicating mild cytotoxicity, whereas the high-dose combination group maintained cell viability above 80% without additive toxic effects. Therefore, the low-dose combination of 2.5 µM Met and 1.25 nM Evol, with favorable biocompatibility, was selected as the optimal ratio for subsequent experiments (Fig. S1A and B). To evaluate the biological effects of Met, Evol, and their combination, cellular senescence, lipid deposition, and ROS levels were assessed in the respective cell models. Considering the key role of senescence marker p21 in cell cycle arrest during aging and in response to DNA damage [26]., we firstly investigated the effect of Met on this protein in Hcy treated HUVECs. Detection results showed that compared to the model group, Hcy-induced p21 levels were reduced by 29% and 48% in the 2.5 µM and 5 µM Met treated cells, respectively (Fig. 2A). These findings demonstrate that Met can effectively inhibit p21 expression. Subsequently, we investigated the effect of Evol treatment on ox-LDL clearance in RAW264.7 cells. ORO staining revealed abundant red lipid droplets within RAW264.7 cells following 24 h co-incubation with Hcy and ox-LDL, indicating significant foam cell formation. Compared to the model group, the staining intensity of intracellular lipid droplets was reduced by 58% and 77% in the 1.25 nM Evol and 2.5 nM Evol treatment groups, respectively (Fig. 2B). These results indicate that Evol can effectively inhibit lipid accumulation in RAW264.7 cells. In addition, DCFH-DA staining revealed the enhancement of green fluorescence in both RAW264.7 macrophages and HUVECs in the Model group. Treatment with either Met or Evol alone reduced ROS production, and the combination of the two agents (especially at higher concentrations: 5 µM Met + 2.5 nM Evol) showed the highest more pronounced effect, with ROS levels approaching that of the Control group (Fig. 2C − E). Based on the above results, to balance the dual objectives of minimizing potential toxicity and ensuring therapeutic efficacy, the concentrations of Met and Evol were adjusted to 2.5 µM and 1.25 nM, respectively, for subsequent experiments. The therapeutic efficacy of the drug combination was further evaluated using SA-β-Gal staining (Fig. 2F) and ORO staining (Fig. 2H). Compared to the model group, the proportion of SA-β-Gal positive cells was reduced by 53.45% (Fig. 2G), while the percentage of ORO-stained positive area was decreased by 64% (Fig. 2I). These results demonstrated that the combination group exhibited superior efficacy over the monotherapy group in both inhibiting endothelial cell senescence and reducing macrophage lipid accumulation. In summary, the combination of Met and Evol efficiently inhibited Hcy-induced senescence in HUVECs and foam cell formation in RAW264.7 cells, thereby exerting dual protective effects.

Preparation and characterization of S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs

Based on the above findings, the Met-Evol combination was selected for nanoparticle formulation development to facilitate the advancement of a subsequent targeted therapeutic system. PLGA NPs, with high biocompatibility and potential for surface functionalization, have emerged as ideal carriers in drug delivery including atorvastatin and curcumin for targeted AS therapy [11, 27]. In this study, we prepared PLGA nanoparticles co-loaded with Met and Evol using a double-emulsion evaporation method. To achieve targeted delivery, we modified the nanoparticle surface with S2P peptide, as previously discribed [24] (Fig. 3A). TEM images revealed that S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs exhibited a morphologically uniform, well-dispersed spherical structure (Fig. 3B-D). Firstly, using DLS analysis revealed average sizes of 229 nm P@(Met + Evol) NPs (Fig. 3E). Subsequently, the effect of S2P peptide concentrations on particle uniformity was analyzed by DLS. It was found that S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs with uniform size distribution, good stability, and dispersion was obtained at the 1:2 molar ratio of S2P to PLGA-COOH (Fig. 3F). DLS analysis revealed average sizes of 257 nm for S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs (Fig. 3G), with corresponding zeta potentials of -35 mV and − 20 mV (Fig. 3H). To balance material utilization, subsequent experiments adopted a molar ratio of S2P to PLGA-COOH at 1:2. UV-Vis analysis revealed an absorption peak of the peptide bond at approximately 210 nm for both the S2P peptide and S2P-PLGA NPs (Fig. 3I). To systematically verify the successful modification and chemical stability of S2P, Met, and Evol in the nanoparticles, free S2P, Met, Evol, and S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs were characterized by FTIR. Free S2P exhibited characteristic absorption peaks at 1650 cm⁻¹ (amide I band, C = O stretching vibration) and 1550 cm⁻¹ (amide II band, N-H bending vibration) (Fig. 3J). Free Met showed a characteristic peak at 1274 cm⁻¹ corresponding to C-N stretching vibration (Fig. 3K), while free Evol showed a characteristic C = O stretching vibration peak at 1641 cm⁻¹ (Fig. 3L). All these characteristic peaks were clearly detected in the spectrum of S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs, with no significant peak shifts, and no new strong peak or disappearance of characteristic peaks were observed. These results confirmed that S2P, Met, and Evol were all successfully incorporated into the nanoparticles, and their chemical structures remained stable during the preparation process without degradation or irreversible chemical changes, providing reliable structural support for subsequent biological applications. To verify the loading of Met in nanoparticles, qualitative analysis was first performed using ultraviolet-visible absorption spectroscopy. As shown in Figure S2A, free Met exhibited a characteristic ultraviolet absorption peak at 231 nm. This peak clearly detected in S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs, and the absorbance markedly higher than that of blank S-PLGA NPs, preliminarily confirming the effective encapsulation of Met within the nanocarriers. HPLC results showed stable peak profiles and satisfactory separation of Met, further verifying drug loading at the qualitative level (Fig. S2B). After background subtraction, both UV–visible spectroscopy and HPLC quantitative analysis steadily captured specific absorption signals at 231 nm (Fig. S2C, Table S1). The highly consistent quantitative data obtained by the two methods eliminated methodological errors and fully validated the accuracy and repeatability of the quantification results. Meanwhile, BCA assay results showed the successful incorporation of Evol into the nanoparticle system (Fig. S2D, Table S2). In conclusion, both Met and Evol were efficiently loaded into nanoparticles, with encapsulation efficiencies of 71.4 ± 2.6% and 61.68 ± 0.5%, and drug loading contents of 7.14 ± 0.26% and 3.08 ± 0.02%, respectively (Fig. 3M). These findings provided a solid structural foundation for subsequent in vitro and in vivo biological studies. Furthermore, the stability of S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs under physiological conditions was evaluated by DLS at different temperatures [22]. The results showed that the particle size increased slightly only in PBS at room temperature, while no significant changes were observed under other conditions (PBS at 4℃, and 10% FBS-supplemented medium at both 4℃ and room temperature) (Fig. 3N and O, Fig. S2E and F). These findings exhibited good stability nanoparticles under refrigerated storage and in serum-containing physiological environments. In addition, to further evaluate the stability of S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs, we studied pH-dependent release behavior, demonstrating a clear trend of targeted drug delivery to lesion sites. To further evaluate the stability of S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs, we investigated the pH-dependent behavior via dialysis to demonstrate the clear trend of targeted drug delivery to lesion sites. The results showed that under simulated physiological conditions (pH 7.4), after 72 h of incubation, the cumulative release rates of Met and Evol reached 47% and 45%, respectively. In contrast, under simulated AS weak acidic condition (pH 6.4), the release of both drugs was accelerated, with cumulative release rates reaching 77% and 50% at 72 h (Fig. 3P and Q). These results collectively indicated significant advantages of nanomedicine in physical stability and storage stability. In summary, we have successfully engineered an S2P-modified PLGA delivery system characterized by uniform particle size, high drug loading efficiency, pH-responsive release behavior, and superior stability, laying a solid therapeutic foundation for targeted treatment of AS.

S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs uptake, escape and targeting capabilities

Efficient cellular internalization and in vivo escape capacity are indispensable prerequisites for the advancement of nanomedicines in drug delivery and therapeutic applications [28]. In this study, Hcy was employed to induce HUVECs and RAW264.7 cells, thereby establishing in vitro models. Fluorescence imaging results demonstrated that P@Ce6 NPs were successfully internalized by both normal HUVECs and RAW264.7 cells, whereas S-P@Ce6 NPs significantly reduced fluorescence intensity, indicating its capacity to evade uptake by normal cells. However, S-P@Ce6 NPs displayed significantly stronger red fluorescence signals than P@Ce6 NPs in senescent cells and inflammatory cells, indicating that the S2P peptide markedly enhanced the targeted delivery efficiency of nanoparticles to senescent endothelial cells and inflammatory macrophages (Fig. 4A&B). To validate the critical role of S2P peptide in targeted delivery, competitive assay was performed: cells were preincubated with free S2P peptide for 2 h prior to S-P@Ce6 NPs treatment. Results showed that this pretreatment significantly reduced intracellular fluorescence intensity, indicating competitive blockade of the S2P peptide’s target binding sites (Fig. 4A&B). S2P blocks the targeting process by competitively binding to the Stabilin-2 receptor, thereby confirming the central role of the S2P-Stabilin-2 axis in specific recognition-mediated mechanisms [29]. Collectively, these results demonstrate that S-P@Ce6 NPs possess dual functionality: they evaded non-specific endocytosis by normal cells, while actively targeting Stabilin-2 over expressing inflammatory macrophages and senescent endothelial cells, thereby providing an efficient delivery strategy for precision therapy of AS.

To thoroughly investigate the internalization mechanism of S-P@Ce6 NPs into cells, we employed a combination of endocytosis inhibitors (β-cyclodextrin: caveolae-mediated endocytosis inhibitor; chlorpromazine: clathrin-mediated endocytosis inhibitor; colchicine: phagocytosis inhibitor) and immunofluorescence imaging to systematically characterize the cellular uptake pathways [28]. Results showed that the uptake rates of S-P@Ce6 NPs were reduced by 43%, 57%, and 83% in the respective inhibitor-treated groups. Quantitative analysis further confirmed that the clathrin and phagocytosis-mediated endocytic pathways were primary internalization mechanism (Fig. S3 A&B).

To further evaluate the endosomal escape capability of S-P@Ce6 NPs following internalization by RAW264.7 and HUVECs cells, S-P@Ce6 NPs were co-cultured with these cells and visualized via CLSM. Results showed that LysoTracker-labeled lysosomes (green fluorescence) colocalized with S-P@Ce6 NPs (red fluorescence) at 6 h post-internalization in RAW264.7 (Fig. 4C) and 2 h post-internalization in HUVECs cells (Fig. 4D). Pearson’s correlation coefficient (PCC) was calculated to quantify the colocalization degree between S-P@Ce6 NPs (red fluorescence) and LysoTracker- labeled lysosomes (green fluorescence). Results showed that S-P@Ce6 NPs (red fluorescence) exhibited high colocalization with the lysosomal marker LysoTracker (green fluorescence) at 6 h, with colocalization coefficients of 55.66% in HUVECs and 73.33% in RAW264.7 cells—confirming their successful escape from endosomal/lysosomal retention and release into the cytoplasm. Over time, the colocalization coefficients gradually decreased, reaching 63% in RAW264.7 and 43% in HUVECs cells at 8 h which further validates the lysosomal escape capability of S-P@Ce6 NPs and lays the foundation for their drug release and functional performance.

Subsequently, we established a Transwell co-culture system (upper chamber: Hcy-aged HUVECs; lower chamber: RAW264.7 cells) to mimic the AS plaque microenvironment and systematically evaluated the uptake efficiency of S-P@Ce6 NPs by both HUVECs and RAW264.7 cells. Results revealed that S-P@Ce6 NPs exhibited stronger red fluorescence signals in both cell types compared to unmodified P@Ce6 NPs (Fig. S3C and S3D). These findings suggest that S2P peptide modification can simultaneously enhance nanoparticle targeting recognition of Stabilin-2, uptake efficiency by plaque cells, and endosomal escape performance [28]. This provides further robust support for its application in AS therapy. Encouraged by these findings in vitro, we further investigated the in vivo characteristics of S-P@Ce6 NPs. This study systematically analyzed their half-life in animals using pharmacokinetic method [27]. Following tail vein injection of P@Ce6 NPs and S-P@Ce6 NPs, we measured the fluorescence signal intensity of both formulations in blood samples. Results demonstrated that the in vivo half-life of S-P@Ce6 NPs was significantly prolonged compared to P@Ce6 NPs (1.369 h vs. 1.044 h, Fig. 4E), confirming that PEG modification enhances nanoparticle stability in vivo by prolonging circulation time [29]. Similarly, we further validated the targeting performance of S-P@Ce6 NPs using IVIS imaging: Compared to sole Ce6, P@Ce6 NPs exhibited an approximately 1.36-fold increase in fluorescence intensity in the aortic region; S-P@Ce6 NPs further enhanced this signal by 1.5-fold relative to P@Ce6 NPs (Fig. 4F), with a concurrent significant reduction in hepatic fluorescence signals (Fig. S3F&G). These findings indicate that S-P@Ce6 NPs achieve targeted enrichment in atherosclerotic plaques by specifically binding to stabilin-2 receptors, highly expressed on senescent endothelial cells and inflammatory macrophages, via surface-modified S2P peptide.

S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs inhibit HUVECs senescence

To systematically evaluate the anti-HUVECs senescence of S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs, we employed Hcy-induced HUVECs as an in vitro aging model and conducted a series of molecular-level and functional validation experiments. Hcy-induced ROS overload drives endothelial cell senescence through a dual mechanism: on one hand, it activates the MAPK pathway (such as p38) to transcriptionally upregulate p53/p21 expression and arrest the cell cycle; on the other hand, it activates NF-κB to enter the nucleus and initiate transcription of SASP factors like IL-6 and TNF-α, ultimately accelerating the senescence process [30]. In the Hcy-induced model group, strong intracellular ROS (green fluorescence) markedly increased proportion of SA-β-Gal positive cells (blue) and substantially elevated of DNA damage marker γ-H2AX (red fluorescence). Concurrently, G1 phase arrest in the cell cycle and the secretion levels of proinflammatory factors IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α were significantly upregulated. In contrast, S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs treatment caused ROS fluorescence intensity decrease by 62.80% (Fig. S4A and B); γ-H2AX fluorescence intensity decreased by 78.50% (Fig. 5A and B); SA-β-Gal positivity rate dropped by 71.36% (Fig. 5C and D); Flow cytometry revealed that drug treatment alleviated G1 phase arrest and increased the proportion of cells in the S phase (Fig. 5E and F). Western blot results further validated the inhibition of S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs on the expression of p53, p21, γ-H2AX, p65NF-κB, p-p65 NF-κB, p38MAPKand p-p38MAPK proteins (Fig. 5G–I). Additionally, S-P@(Met+Evol) NPs downregulated IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α by 51%, 53%, and 55%, respectively (Fig. 5J–L). These results demonstrated that S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs exert anti-aging effects by downregulating ROS levels through the coordinated involvement of MAPK signaling pathway and NF-κB pathway. Subsequently, the effects of S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs on cytoskeletal homeostasis and cell migration, which were impaired in senescent cells [31], were evaluated. The results revealed the disorganized cytoskeletal microfilament and low wound healing rate in Hcy-induced HUVECs. However, after treatment with S-P@(Met+Evol) NPs, the actin microfilaments of the cytoskeleton were arranged in an orderly manner (Fig. 5M), and the wound healing rate was increased by 34.97% at 24 h (Fig. 5N and O).These findings demonstrate that S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs treatment attenuates ROS and DNA damage, alleviates cycle arrest in senescent HUVECs, and restricts the pathological proliferation within plaques, thereby inhibiting AS progression.

To elucidate the molecular mechanism by which S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs alleviate Hcy-induced senescence in HUVECs, we performed transcriptome sequencing (RNA-seq) to analyze alterations in gene expression profiles. Figure 6A showed a schematic diagram summarizing the protective mechanism of S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs against HUVEC senescence. Meanwhile, significant difference of expressed genes were found between the control group, model group, and S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs group (Fig. 6B). Venn diagram analysis revealed 401 common genes among the three groups (Fig. 6C). Volcano plot analysis further revealed that compared to the model group, 772 genes were significantly upregulated and 486 genes were significantly downregulated in the control group. Compared to the model group, 728 genes were significantly upregulated and 511 genes were significantly downregulated in the S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs group (Fig. 6D and E). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis indicated that MAPK, NF-κB and TGF-β signaling pathways, which closely associated with aging, were significantly regulated following S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs treatment (bubble plot, Fig. 6F). GSEA results further confirmed that differentially expressed genes in these pathways were significantly enriched in the same group (Fig. 6G), suggesting the drug may intervene in Hcy-induced endothelial cell senescence by targeting and inhibiting the aforementioned pro-inflammatory and pro-senescence pathways. Among these, oxidative stress, as a key upstream trigger, activates the MAPK and NF-κB pathways, further amplifying cellular aging phenotypes and inflammatory responses [30]. In addition, the TGF-β signaling pathway induces cell cycle arrest, accelerates cellular senescence, and promotes the onset and progression of age-related diseases [2, 32]. This study demonstrated that S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs suppressed abnormal activation of inflammatory and stress pathways by synergistically regulating aging gene networks through multiple targets, providing novel insights and strategies for the prevention and treatment of age-related diseases.

S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs modulate cholesterol efflux and phenotypic conversion in RAW264.7 cells

Using the RAW264.7 cells as an in vitro model, we evaluated the effects of S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs on cholesterol efflux and phenotype transformation of macrophages. It is known that excessive uptake of LDL by macrophages and the subsequent transformation into foam cells constitute a key event in the early atherosclerotic lesion [33]. First, we established a cellular model by co-stimulating RAW264.7 cells with Hcy and ox-LDL to investigate the regulatory effect of S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs on lipid uptake. ORO staining showed that red lipid droplet in S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs-treated cells reduced 76.61% compared to the model group (Fig. 7A). Cholesterol efflux is a crucial step in maintaining lipid homeostasis [34]. Subsequently, we co-incubated RAW264.7 cells with Hcy to investigate the cholesterol efflux-promoting effect of S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs and found that the model group exhibited only faint red fluorescent signals, whereas S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs treatment enhanced the red fluorescence intensity of ABCA1 and ABCG1 by 92% and 62%, respectively, compared to the model group (Fig. 7B and C). Western blot further confirmed that the S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs significantly upregulated expression of ABCA1 and ABCG1, alongside downregulated PCSK9 expression (Fig. 7D), compared to the model group. The detection results of intracellular cholesterol and cholesterol ester levels showed that, compared to the model group, the cholesterol content was markedly decreased in the S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs-treated group. Specifically, the total cholesterol level decreased by 32.26%, the free cholesterol level by 47%, and the cholesterol ester level by 32.34% (Fig. S5A - S5C). This result indicated that S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs reduced foam cell formation. Mechanistically, these nanoparticles inhibited PCSK9 while upregulating ABCA1 and ABCG1, thereby facilitating cholesterol efflux.

Excessive accumulation of cholesterol esters in foam cells disrupts mitochondrial membrane integrity, leading to electron transport chain (ETC) dysfunction and a surge in reactive ROS production [35]. This study evaluated the effect of S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs on mitochondrial membrane potential (Δψm) in macrophages. The results showed that J-aggregates within healthy mitochondrial matrices exhibited red fluorescence in the control group, whereas Hcy-induced model macrophages displayed green fluorescence, reflecting the significant reduction in Δψm. Following treatment with S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs, RAW264.7 cells showed enhanced red fluorescence and diminished green fluorescence (Fig. 7E and S5D), and intracellular ATP levels in the treated group were significantly higher than those in the model group (Fig. S5E). These findings demonstrated that the S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs effectively restored Δψm in inflammatory macrophages and improved mitochondrial energy synthesis. Subsequently, intracellular ROS levels were detected using the DCFH-DA fluorescent probe. The results showed that compared to the model group, the ROS fluorescence intensity in the S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs group was reduced by 91% (Fig. 7F). These findings demonstrated that S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs restore mitochondrial function by regulating mitochondrial membrane potential and enhancing ATP synthesis, thereby exerting a potent ROS-scavenging capacity.

Excessive ROS production drives macrophage toward pro-inflammatory M1 polarization, thereby accelerating the progression of AS [36]. Therefore, the regulatory effect of S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs on macrophage polarization was evaluated via immunofluorescence staining. RAW264.7 cells were co-cultured with Hcy to establish an in vitro model. The results showed that, compared to the model group, the fluorescence intensity of the M1 marker CD80 in the S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs group was decreased by 73.69% while the fluorescence intensity of the M2 marker CD206 was 11-fold higher than that in the model group (Fig. 7G). In addition, PCSK9 induces the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α and IL-6), thereby exacerbating M1 macrophage-mediated inflammatory responses [37]. ELISA assay showed that compared to the model group, the pro-inflammatory factors TNF-α and IL-6 in the S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs group were reduced by 60% and 89%, respectively, whereas the level of the anti-inflammatory factor IL-10 increased 1.5-fold(Fig. 7H-J). The above results confirmed that S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs can promote macrophage phenotypic polarization to exert anti-inflammatory effects.

Effects of S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs on plaque progression in ApoE-/- mice

Based on prior in vitro findings, we further evaluated the therapeutic efficacy of S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs in ApoE-/- atherosclerotic mice. The treatment regimen is shown in Fig. 9A. To evaluate vascular functional changes, intima-media thickness (IMT) and hemodynamic parameters were assessed using a small animal ultrasound imaging system [38]. Results showed a significant increase in IMT and accelerated blood flow velocity in the aortic roots of model group, indicating vascular structural remodeling and associated functional impairment. In contrast, the S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs group demonstrated a marked reduction in IMT and normalized blood flow velocity in their aortic roots, suggesting effective amelioration of vascular dysfunction and structural remodeling (Fig. S6A-C). In vivo efficacy assessment revealed that mice in the S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs treatment group exhibited significant reduction in body weight compared to the model group (Fig. 9B). Gross anatomical examination of the aorta demonstrated diffuse plaque distribution and enlarged plaque areas in the aortic arch region of the model group, whereas mice in the treatment group showed reduced plaque numbers on the aortic surface (Fig. 9C). ORO staining further confirmed that the total aortic plaque burden in the model group was 87% greater than that of the normal control group, confirming the successful establishment of the atherosclerosis model (Fig. 9D and H). To further investigate the in vivo therapeutic effects of S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs, we performed ORO staining on the mouse aortic root tissues and found that the plaque area in the S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs treatment group reduced by 64.39% compared to the model group (Fig. 9E and I). These findings indicated that S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs exerted effective inhibition on plaque progression along both longitudinal and transverse axes. H&E staining analysis demonstrated a 49.57% reduction in necrotic core area within plaques in the S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs group compared to the model group (Fig. 9F and J), suggesting the mitigation of necrotic inflammatory responses in plaques. In addition, we performed Masson trichrome staining to detect collagen content, a parameter for reflecting plaque stability [39]. The results showed that compared to the model group, the collagen content in the plaques of the S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs group increased by 2.6-fold (Fig. 9G and K). This finding indicated that S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs promoted fibrous cap formation, enhanced plaque stability, and reduced the risk of rupture. Collectively, these data demonstrated that S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs can effectively inhibit AS plaque progression and enhance plaque stability. Anti-atherosclerotic therapeutic effects and mechanisms of S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs in ApoE-/- mice

Then, in order to explore the molecular mechanism for inhibiting atherosclerosis progression in ApoE⁻/⁻ mice, we assessed oxidative stress levels in aortic tissue. DHE staining for ROS detection revealed that the red fluorescence intensity in plaques of the model group was significantly increased. After treatment with S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs, the red fluorescence intensity decreased by 62.7% compared to the model group (Fig. 10A). Meanwhile, γ-H2AX staining revealed that the intensity of yellow fluorescence signals decreased by 64.28% in the S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs -treated group compared to the model group (Fig. 10B), indicating their protective effect against DNA damage. Further immunofluorescence staining was performed for core markers of cellular senescence. Results showed that after treatment with S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs, the fluorescence signal intensities of p53 (green) and p21 (red) within the plaque were reduced by 64.56% and 72.65%, respectively, compared to the model group (Fig. 10C&D). In summary, S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs exert crucial anti-senescence effects in the process of AS by reducing ROS levels, alleviating DNA damage, and inhibiting the expression of p53 and p21.

Subsequently, we investigated the in vivo efficacy of S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs, including promoting cholesterol efflux in macrophages, regulating macrophage phenotypic polarization, and remodeling the plaque immune microenvironment. Immunohistochemistry staining showed that the proportion of ABCA1-positive staining in the aortic root of the S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs treatment group was 8.46-fold higher than that of model group (Fig. 10E), and the proportion of ABCG1-positive staining was 2.34 times higher than that of model group (Fig. 10F). These results indicated that S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs alleviate lipid accumulation and foam cell formation by upregulating the core cholesterol efflux proteins ABCA1 and ABCG1.

In addition, immunofluorescence staining showed that compared to the model group, the proportion of CD80 positive macrophage in the aortic root region of mice in the S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs treatment group significantly reduced by 76.9% (Fig. 10G), while the proportion of CD206 positive macrophage significantly increased by 7.25-fold (Fig. 10H). ELISA assay showed that compared to the model group, the serum levels of pro-inflammatory factors TNF-α and IL-6 in the S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs treatment group reduced by 54% and 76%, respectively, while the level of the anti-inflammatory factor IL-10 increased by 73% (Fig. 10I-K). Collectively, these findings demonstrated that S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs exerted comprehensive effects of anti-aging, lipid-lowering, and anti-inflammatory activities to cooperate inhibiting AS c progression, highlighting the promising therapeutic potential for AS disease. S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs improve metabolic dysregulation induced by HMD

Next, we analyzed the change of serum metabolite profiles in the mice aiming to elucidate the protective mechanism of S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs against AS. PCA of the metabolomics data revealed significant differences in metabolites between the two groups (Fig. S7A). Volcano plot analysis revealed a distinct metabolic profile in S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs treated samples, with 131 significantly upregulated and 94 significantly downregulated metabolites relative to the model group, indicating nanoparticle-induced metabolic reprogramm (Fig. S7B). Cluster-based heatmap analysis of metabolite categories revealed marked differences in the abundance of multiple metabolite classes including lipids and analogues, organic acid derivatives, between the S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs group and the model group (Fig. S7C). This suggests potential link to key pathological processes of lipid deposition, inflammatory responses, and oxidative stress in AS. KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that metabolites significantly altered by S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs were primarily enriched in lipid metabolism, amino acid energy metabolism, inflammation, and oxidative stress pathways, including the FOXO signaling pathway, sphingolipid metabolism, oxidative stress, and ABC transporter pathways (Fig. S7D). The following metabolites can promote the progression of AS by synergistically interfering with multiple metabolic signaling pathways. Sphingosylphosphocholine (SPC), enhances migration in human aortic smooth muscle cells (SMCs) [40, 41]. Lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC) upregulates the expression of inflammatory chemokines in endothelial cells, exacerbates oxidative stress, and suppresses endothelial cell migration and proliferation [42]. Glycerol-3-phosphate (G3P) exacerbates impairment of cholesterol reverse transport via competitive inhibition [43]. Carnitine can act FOXO1 and enhance the protective effect of β-oxidation [44, 45]. Pyruvate-driven lactate accumulation serves as a key mediator of mitochondrial and cellular dysfunction [46]. Prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) drives M2 macrophage polarization and suppresses proinflammatory cytokine secretion [47]. Serine, the initial substrate for sphingolipid biosynthesis, promotes ceramide production [48]. These metabolites collectively form a molecular network governing AS progression. This study demonstrates that S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs significantly downregulate G3P, PGE2, LPC, SPC, serine, and pyruvate (Fig. S7E-K), while concurrently upregulating carnitine levels (Fig. S7L). In summary, this study revealed that S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs inhibited AS progression by regulating key metabolites in the aforementioned pathways. However, its specific mechanism of action requires further in-depth investigation.

Biocompatibility and biosafety of S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs

We conducted a series of experiments to evaluate the biosafety of nanodrug for clinical translation [49]. MTT assay employed to evaluate the effect of S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs on the viability of various cell lines. The results demonstrated that the viability of all cells remained above 80% (Fig. S8A-C), indicating no significant cytotoxicity. Hemolysis experiments evaluated the blood compatibility of Met + Evol, P@(Met + Evol) NPs, and S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs to assess their biocompatibility. Morphological images revealed the intact biconcave discoid structure without observable rupture or shrinkage for red blood cells following co-incubation with nanoparticles, indicating negligible hemolytic activity (Fig. S8D-F). Given the rapid developmental cycle and high genetic homology to human, zebrafish embryo was employed as a model to assess the developmental toxicity of nanoparticles [50]. Morphological assessment revealed normal embryonic development in Met + Evol, P@(Met + Evol) NPs, and S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs groups, without overt abnormalities such as pericardial edema, spinal curvature, or tail malformation (Fig. S8G). Body length quantification demonstrated no significant difference between the three experimental groups and the blank control (Fig. S8H). The heart rate of embryos in the S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs group was 130 ± 5 beats·min⁻¹, with no significant difference compared with the control group (120 ± 2 beats·min⁻¹) (Fig. S8I). Collectively, these findings indicated that S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs possess favorable biocompatibility.

The subsequent study focused on evaluating the in vivo biosafety of S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs in ApoE-/-mice. Following a 12-week treatment protocol, major organs of the mice were harvested for H&E staining. The results demonstrated neat arrangement of myocardial fiber in the S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs group, with clear red pulp/white pulp boundaries in the spleen, intact alveolar architecture in the lung, and normal glomerular and tubular morphology in the kidney. Liver tissue staining revealed no drug-related pathological changes (e.g., edema, inflammatory cell infiltration, or necrosis) in the S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs group (Fig. S9A). These findings confirmed that S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs were non-toxic to major organs. Subsequently, the harvested plasma supernatants were subjected to hematological analysis. Plasma analysis revealed that routine blood parameters such as red blood cell count, platelet (PLT) levels, and hemoglobin (HGB) concentration were all within the physiological reference range (Fig. S9B-F), excluding the risk of hematological toxicity. Clinical biochemical tests indicated that levels of ALT, AST (liver function), UREA, and CREA-M (renal function) were maintained within normal ranges (Fig. S9 G-J), suggesting that S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs have no significant impact on liver and kidney function. In addition, compared to the model group, the level of high-density lipoprotein (HDL) increased in the treatment group (Fig. S9K). Meanwhile, the levels of low-density lipoprotein (LDL), total cholesterol (TC) and triglyceride (TG) in the treatment group markedly decreased relative to the model group (Fig. S9L-N). These results confirmed that the nanodrug can effectively ameliorate dyslipidemia and exert a favorable systemic lipid-lowering effect. Additionally, significant reduction of Hcy levels in plasma was found in the same group (Fig. S9O), indicating that S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs ameliorate hcy-induced. Overall, S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs exhibited no significant in vivo toxicity over the 12-week treatment period, while efficiently reduced lipid and homocysteine levels. These findings provided critical evidence supporting the safety of their clinical translation.

Conclusion

This study successfully constructed an S2P peptide-mediated active targeting nano-delivery system, S-P@(Met + Evol) NPs, and confirmed its dual-target synergistic efficacy against AS: (i) Inhibition of the MAPK/NF-κB/p53 axis to alleviate endothelial cell DNA damage, reverse cell cycle arrest, and delay vascular aging; (ii) Upregulation of ABCA1/ABCG1 cholesterol efflux proteins inhibits foam cell formation and induces macrophage polarization from pro-inflammatory M1 to anti-inflammatory M2, thereby reducing inflammatory factor levels in plaques. (iii) S2P peptide modification enables specific recognition of AS plaques, enhancing lesion drug enrichment and in vivo circulation duration. This nanodrug provides a novel strategy for the development of anti-AS nanomedicines through synergistic intervention in the core pathological network of AS: aging-lipid accumulation-inflammation.
