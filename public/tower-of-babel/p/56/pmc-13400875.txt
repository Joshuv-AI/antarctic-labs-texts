Under nutrition and associated factors among pregnant women residing in productive safety net program beneficiary and nonbeneficiary households in West Belesa District, northwest Ethiopia
Authors: Yeshiwas Ayale Ferede, Agerie Mengistie Zeleke, Jember Ayelgne Beyene, Ayanaw Dinku Abebe, Worku Chekol Tassew
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMalnutrition affects approximately 462 million pregnant women globally. Despite initiatives like the productive safety net program (PSNP) in Ethiopia, evidence comparing maternal undernutrition between PSNP beneficiary and nonbeneficiary households remains limited. This study aimed to compare undernutrition and its associated factors among pregnant women in West Belessa District, Central Gondar Zone.MethodsA community-based comparative cross-sectional study was conducted from April 1 to April 30, 2025, among 617 randomly selected pregnant women. Data were collected through face-to-face interviews, and nutritional status was assessed using mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC). Data entry was done using EpiData 3.1 and analyzed with STATA 14. Bivariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses identified factors associated with undernutrition, with significance set at P<.05. Model fitness was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test.ResultsThe overall prevalence of undernutrition was 44.4% (95% CI: 40.5–48.5). Factors significantly associated with undernutrition included poor nutritional knowledge (AOR=5.73, 95% CI: 3.9–8.4), no prenatal dietary advice (AOR=1.89, 95% CI: 1.27–2.83), poorest wealth index (AOR=3.24, 95% CI: 1.75–6.0), and no habit of eating snacks (AOR=1.47, 95% CI: 1.04–2.16).ConclusionUndernutrition among pregnant women in West Belessa District remains high. Improving nutritional knowledge, promoting dietary counseling during antenatal care, and addressing socioeconomic disparities are essential to reduce maternal undernutrition.Key words: Ethiopia, pregnant women, productive safety net program, under nutrition, West Belessa

AJOG Global Reports at a Glance

Why was this study conducted?

To assess the prevalence of undernutrition and identify associated factors among pregnant women living in Productive Safety Net Program (PSNP) beneficiary and non-beneficiary households in West Belesa District, Northwest Ethiopia, where evidence remains limited.

Key findings

Undernutrition remained a significant public health problem among pregnant women. The study identified important sociodemographic, maternal, dietary, and household-related factors associated with undernutrition, with differences observed between PSNP beneficiary and non-beneficiary households.

What does this add to what is known?

This study provides comparative evidence on maternal undernutrition among PSNP beneficiary and non-beneficiary households and highlights the need for targeted nutrition interventions integrated with social protection programs to improve maternal nutritional status.

Background

Adequate nutrition during pregnancy is essential to support fetal growth and maintain maternal health. Pregnant women require optimal nutrients and dietary diversity to meet their increased physiological demands and ensure positive pregnancy outcomes.1 Undernutrition refers to an insufficient intake of calories and essential nutrients necessary for maintaining normal body function and health.2 It develops from the combined effects of increased nutritional demands and inadequate dietary intake during pregnancy.3 Maternal undernutrition is a major contributor to maternal morbidity, mortality, and adverse birth outcomes, including low birth weight, preterm birth, and perinatal complications.4 The mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC) is a well-recognized, simple, and reliable anthropometric measure for assessing maternal nutritional status during pregnancy.5

Globally, an estimated 462 million women, including pregnant women, are affected by undernutrition.6 The majority of the 795 million undernourished people worldwide live in low- and middle-income countries, with pregnancy being a particularly vulnerable period.7 Pregnant women in low-resource settings continue to experience high rates of undernutrition, which is a key predictor of adverse birth outcomes.8 Africa bears a disproportionate share of this burden.8 Maternal undernutrition is an underlying cause of approximately 3.5 million maternal deaths and disabilities annually in low- and middle-income countries.2 Beyond its health consequences, undernutrition also has significant economic implications, as it reduces productivity, impairs cognitive development, and perpetuates intergenerational cycles of poverty.5

In Ethiopia, maternal undernutrition remains a major public health concern. Approximately one in five pregnant women is undernourished, with the prevalence being 68% higher among rural women compared to their urban counterparts.9 Maternal undernutrition increases women’s susceptibility to infections, risk of miscarriage, poor fetal growth, low birth weight, and infant morbidity and mortality. Furthermore, it leads to long-term, irreversible cognitive, motor, and health impairments in children.4

The nutritional status of women before and during pregnancy is influenced by their knowledge, attitudes, and dietary practices.7 Evidence from various studies indicates that maternal undernutrition is associated with illiteracy, large family size, advanced maternal age, short birth intervals, low dietary diversity, and household food insecurity. Other contributing factors include lack of extra food intake during pregnancy, inadequate dietary counseling, poor sanitation, and limited decision-making power.

To address food insecurity and improve nutritional outcomes, Ethiopia has implemented several nutrition-specific interventions.10 In addition, the productive safety net program (PSNP), a nutrition-sensitive intervention, was launched to support chronically food-insecure households through cash or food transfers aimed at improving household food security and preventing asset depletion.11 The fourth phase of the PSNP, implemented in 2015, aims to improve food security and nutrition among rural households vulnerable to food insecurity.2 Beneficiaries receive monthly transfers equivalent to 15 kg of grains and 4 kg of pulses, adjusted for inflation, with cash rates determined by local market prices.1 As part of its livelihood component, the program provides skill-based training through local institutions and farmer training centers.3 The PSNP directly and indirectly influences household food consumption, school enrollment, health service utilization, nutrition, asset accumulation, and livelihood development.4 The revised 2015 PSNP also began providing temporary direct support and social service linkages for pregnant and lactating women in food-insecure households to improve maternal and newborn nutritional outcomes.

Despite these initiatives, limited evidence exists regarding the prevalence of undernutrition and its determinants among pregnant women in PSNP beneficiary areas. Moreover, there is an information gap concerning differences in maternal undernutrition between PSNP user and nonuser households in Ethiopia, particularly in the West Belessa District. Therefore, this study aimed to assess and compare the prevalence of undernutrition and identify associated factors among pregnant women from PSNP user and nonuser households in the West Belessa District, northwest Ethiopia. The findings of this study are expected to inform program implementers and policymakers in designing effective interventions and policies to reduce undernutrition among pregnant women and serve as a valuable reference for future research in the area.

Methods and materials

Study design and period

A community-based comparative cross-sectional study was conducted from April 1 to April 30, 2025.

Study area

The study took place in West Belessa District, located in the Central Gondar Zone of the Amhara National Regional State, northern Ethiopia. The district lies approximately 212 km from Bahir Dar, the regional capital, and 84 km from Gondar town, at 12°45′N latitude and 37°45′E longitude. West Belessa District comprises 32 kebeles, of which 19 are PSNP beneficiaries, and 13 are nonbeneficiaries.

According to the 2016 population estimate, the district has a total population of 173,566, with a density of 127,777 persons per square kilometer. Using a 3.37% conversion factor, data from the District Health Office indicated that approximately 5850 pregnant women reside in the area, of whom 3474 are from PSNP beneficiary households.

Agriculture and livestock rearing are the main sources of livelihood in the district. However, since crop production depends solely on rainfall, the area frequently experiences food shortages. The district encompasses both highland and lowland areas, with the lowland zones being more prone to drought and food insecurity. PSNP implementation in West Belessa began in 1997, and all kebeles have since benefited from the program. Currently, the PSNP operates in two forms: cash transfers for households with able-bodied members who participate in public work activities such as road construction and soil or water conservation, and food transfers for households without physically capable members.

Source population

The source population consisted of all pregnant women residing in PSNP beneficiary and nonbeneficiary households in the West Belessa District.

Study population

The study population included all pregnant women from PSNP user and nonuser households living in the selected kebeles of West Belessa District during the study period.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

All pregnant women who had resided in the study area for at least 6 months and belonged to PSNP user or nonuser households were included in the study. Pregnant women who were severely ill or had diagnosed mental disorders during the data collection period were excluded.

Sample size determination

The sample size was calculated using the double population proportion formula for comparative cross-sectional studies. Pregnant women from PSNP user households were considered the exposed group, while those from nonuser households were considered the unexposed group.

The proportion of maternal undernutrition among non-PSNP households (43.1%) was taken from a previous study.12 Since no prior study was available for PSNP users, the prevalence among PSNP households was assumed to be 15% higher (58.1%),13 considering the greater vulnerability of food-insecure households.

Using a 95% confidence level, 80% power, 1:1 ratio of exposed to unexposed, 5% margin of error, design effect of 1.5, and 10% nonresponse rate, the final sample size was 617 participants (309 PSNP users and 308 nonusers).

Sample size calculations for additional significant variables (age at first marriage, dietary diversity, and latrine availability) were performed using OpenEpi version 7. However, the largest calculated sample size (617) from the primary objective was retained as the final sample size.

Sampling procedure

A multistage sampling technique was employed. First, kebeles were stratified into PSNP beneficiary and nonbeneficiary groups. From these strata, 10 kebeles were randomly selected (6 PSNP beneficiary and 4 nonbeneficiary).

Using household registration lists maintained by health extension workers, eligible households with pregnant women were identified. Participants were then selected using computer-generated simple random sampling. In households with more than one eligible pregnant woman, one participant was selected using a lottery method.

Operational definitions

Under nutrition: Pregnant women with MUAC <23 cm were classified as undernourished; MUAC ≥23 cm indicated normal nutritional status.14

Adequate diet diversity: Consumption of at least 5 out of 10 food groups in the previous 24 hours.14

Nutritional knowledge: Assessed using five dichotomous questions (0=does not know; 1=knows). Scores above the mean indicated good knowledge.15

Coffee drinking; Classified as nondrinker, sometimes drinker (1 cup/d for 1–3 days/wk), or daily drinker (3 cups/d).16

Prenatal dietary advice: Defined as receiving advice on increased meal frequency, balanced diet, and fruit/vegetable consumption.17

Wealth index Constructed using principal component analysis (PCA) and categorized into five quintiles.18

Data collection tools and procedures

Data were collected using an interviewer-administered structured questionnaire adapted from validated literature. The tool included sections on sociodemographic characteristics, maternal and obstetric factors, dietary practices, and environmental sanitation. Ten trained BSc nurses collected the data under the supervision of two health officers.

MUAC was measured on the nondominant arm at the midpoint between the acromion and olecranon processes using a non-stretchable tape. Measurements were recorded to the nearest 0.1 cm. Two measurements were taken, and the average value was used for analysis.

Data quality assurance

The questionnaire was pretested on 5% of the sample in kebeles not included in the main study. Data collectors and supervisors received two days of training. Completed questionnaires were reviewed daily for completeness and consistency. Continuous supervision and regular feedback sessions were conducted throughout the data collection period.

Data processing and analysis

Data were entered into EpiData version 3.1 and exported to STATA version 14 for analysis.

Descriptive statistics were computed to summarize participant characteristics. Bivariate logistic regression was conducted to identify candidate variables (P<.20) for multivariable analysis. Multicollinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF=1.02).

Multivariable logistic regression was performed to identify independent predictors of undernutrition. Adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals were reported. Statistical significance was declared at P<.05. Model fitness was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test (P=.2391), indicating good model fit.

Result

Sociodemographic characteristics of the study participants

A total of 598 pregnant women were interviewed, with a response rate of 96.9%, of which 299 (96.7%) were from PSNP user households and 299 (97%) from non-PSNP mothers. Most of the respondents (50.5% from both groups) were in the age group of 25–34 years. The mean age of the participant was 30.9 with a standard deviation of 0.27. More than half of the respondents from both groups were housewives (56.5% from PSNP user HHs and 66.5% from non-PSNP user HHs); 87 (29%) and 55 (18.4%) of the study participants from PSNP user and non-PSNP user households were unable to read and write, respectively (Table 1).

Environmental related characteristics of the study participant

Nearly half (44.8%) of the respondents from PSNP user and 47.2% from non-PSNP user HHs had piped to neighbor drinking water during the data collection period. A total of 181(60.5%) respondents from PSNP user and 206 (68.9%) from non-PSNP user HHs had no latrine during the current pregnancy. A total of 141(47.2%) respondents from PSNP user and 163(54.5%) from non-PSNP user HHs had the practice of treating drinking water by water agar during the current pregnancy.

Prevalence of undernutrition of the study participant

The overall prevalence of undernutrition among pregnant women in the district was 44.48% (95% CI: 40.5–48.5), whereas it was 45.1% (95% CI: 40.2–47.3) and 43.81% (95% CI: 40.34–50.25) among pregnant women in PSNP user and non-PSNP user households, respectively. Though the study indicated a slight increase in the prevalence of undernutrition among pregnant women residing in PSNP user households, the difference was not statistically significant (P=.742).

Factors associated with undernutrition

In multivariable logistic regression analysis, habit of eating snacks, nutritional knowledge, prenatal dietary advice, and wealth index were independently associated with undernutrition (P<.05).

Pregnant women with poor nutritional knowledge were 5.73 times more likely to be undernourished compared to those with good knowledge (AOR=5.73; 95% CI: 3.9–8.42).

Women who did not receive prenatal dietary advice were 1.89 times more likely to be undernourished compared to those who received advice (AOR=1.89; 95% CI: 1.27–2.83).

Pregnant women in the poorest wealth quintile had 3.24 times higher odds of undernutrition compared to those in the richest quintile (AOR=3.24; 95% CI: 1.75–6.00).

Women who did not have a habit of eating snacks were 1.47 times more likely to be undernourished compared to those who consumed snacks (AOR=1.47; 95% CI: 1.04–2.16).

Other variables, including extra meal intake, latrine availability, gestational age, and minimum dietary diversity, were not statistically significant in the adjusted model (Table 4).

Discussion

This study assessed the prevalence of undernutrition and its associated factors among pregnant women from PSNP user and non-user households in West Belessa District, 2025. The overall prevalence of undernutrition was 44.4% (95% CI: 40.5–48.5). Independent predictors of undernutrition included absence of snack-eating habits, lack of prenatal dietary advice, poor nutritional knowledge, and low household wealth index. No significant difference was observed between PSNP user and nonuser households.

The prevalence found in this study is consistent with a previous study conducted in a rural community in southern Ethiopia, which reported a prevalence of 41.2%.19 However, it is notably higher than findings from several other studies conducted across Ethiopia, 16.2% in Gondar hospital20 and 14% in Gondar town,12 19.8% in Dessie town,21 31.8% in Central Refit Valley,12 9.2% in Wondogenet District southern Ethiopia,22 21.8% in Silte zone southern Ethiopia(15),19.5% in Eastern Ethiopia,17 and 24% in humanitarian setting in Ethiopia.23 The discrepancy could be attributed to differences in sociodemographic characteristics, geographical variation, seasonal food availability, cultural food taboos, and the relatively limited nutritional intervention coverage in West Belessa District.

Furthermore, the prevalence observed in this study exceeds the pooled estimates reported from several African and Asian countries. For instance, lower rates of maternal undernutrition have been documented in South Sudan (18.9%), Kenya (19.3%), Madagascar (23.5%), Nigeria (9%), China (10%), India (11.8%), and Sri Lanka (5%–15%).12,24, 25, 26, 27, 28, 29 Such differences may be explained by the variation in MUAC cutoff values, differences in nutritional policies and interventions, and sociocultural distinctions affecting dietary practices across settings.

This study also compared the magnitude of undernutrition between PSNP user and nonuser households. The prevalence among PSNP users was 45.1%, while it was 43.8% among nonusers; however, the difference was not statistically significant (P=.742). Although PSNP aims to improve food security, the lack of a significant difference could reflect subjectivity in program targeting, where some households that should have been included in the program might have been excluded. Additionally, rain-dependent agriculture, recurrent drought, and limited livelihood diversification in both PSNP and non-PSNP Kebeles might have minimized observable differences in nutritional outcomes. Moreover, both groups received targeted supplementary feeding for pregnant women with MUAC <23 cm, and the similarity in nutritional knowledge about extra meals during pregnancy across groups could further explain the lack of statistical difference.

In examining the associated factors, snack-eating habits showed a significant association with maternal undernutrition. Pregnant women who did not consume snacks between meals were more likely to be undernourished compared to those who did, consistent with findings from the Guma District.17 Regular snack consumption contributes to improved energy balance and nutrient intake during pregnancy, supporting better maternal nutritional outcomes.

Prenatal dietary advice also demonstrated a significant protective effect. Pregnant women who did not receive dietary advice were more likely to be undernourished compared to those who received guidance during antenatal care. This finding underscores the importance of integrating nutrition education and counseling into routine antenatal services, ensuring that women understand the importance of adequate and balanced dietary intake throughout pregnancy.

The wealth index was another important determinant of maternal nutritional status. Pregnant women from the poorest households were more likely to experience undernutrition compared to those from the richest households. This aligns with studies from Eastern Ethiopia,17 Gumay District, Jimma Zone, South West Ethiopia,30 and rural Bangladesh.31 Poverty is closely linked with food insecurity, hidden hunger, and micronutrient deficiencies, all of which contribute to increased vulnerability to undernutrition.

Finally Nutrition knowledge of pregnant women was significantly associated with maternal undernutrition. It was negatively associated with maternal undernutrition. The odds of undernutrition among pregnant women with poor nutritional knowledge were higher than those of their counterparts. This finding is similar to a previous study.21 Poor nutritional knowledge often leads to inadequate dietary practices, resulting in insufficient nutrient intake and, consequently, undernutrition.

Strengths and limitations

The study’s comparative community-based cross-sectional design is a major strength, allowing direct comparison between PSNP user and nonuser households. Use of standardized tools, composite indices, and house-to-house data collection enhanced the validity and reliability of the findings.

Limitations include the absence of laboratory assessments for nutrition-related conditions, reliance on self-reported data (which may introduce recall and social desirability bias), and the exclusive use of quantitative data, which may limit insights into behavioral and contextual determinants of maternal nutrition.

Conclusion and recommendations

Undernutrition among pregnant women was found to be highly prevalent in the study area, with no significant difference observed between those from PSNP user and non-user households. Key determinants of maternal undernutrition included meal snacking practices, nutritional knowledge, prenatal dietary counseling, and household wealth status.

To address this persistent challenge, multisectoral and coordinated actions are essential. The Ethiopian Federal Ministry of Health, Amhara Regional Health Bureau, Central Gondar Zone Health Department, West Belessa District Health Office, and partner organizations should prioritize sustained nutrition education and behavior change communication targeting pregnant women and their families. Strengthening prenatal dietary counseling, promoting frequent meal snacking, and implementing economic empowerment initiatives to improve household wealth are critical steps toward improving maternal nutrition and pregnancy outcomes.
