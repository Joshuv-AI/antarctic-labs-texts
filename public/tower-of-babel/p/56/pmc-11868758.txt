A unique case of protein-losing enteropathy as a complication of segmental arterial mediolysis
Authors: Nedaa Skeik, Rebecca Windschitl, Naureen Qadri, Jesse Manunga
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractProtein-losing enteropathy (PLE) can be caused by erosive and nonerosive gastrointestinal diseases, mesenteric lymphatic obstruction, and increased central venous pressure. Superior mesenteric artery pathology (SMAP), including dissection or occlusion is another very rarely reported etiology. Management of PLE includes treatment of the underlying cause in addition to a low-fat, high-protein, medium-chain triglyceride diet to improve nutrition. The management of SMAP and its complications, including PLE, necessitates a multidisciplinary approach involving vascular surgeons and gastroenterologists. Treatment options include conservative measures, medical management, and, in some cases, revascularization. We report a unique case of SMAP-induced PLE leading to a fatal outcome. We further conducted a literature review with the aim of summarizing the existing literature on this topic and providing intrigued readers with the most relevant and up-to-date information on this topic in hopes of improving the care of patients presenting with this unique entity. To our knowledge, our patient represents the only case in the literature where segmental arterial mediolysis is the underlying cause of superior mesenteric artery dissection and occlusion leading to PLE.Keywords: Superior mesenteric vasculopathy, Segmental arterial mediolysis, Protein-losing enteropathy

Case report

A 47-year-old man with a past medical history of hypertension presented with a 5-day history of epigastric abdominal pain associated with nausea and vomiting. Vital signs revealed a blood pressure of 140/86 mm Hg, pulse of 97, respiratory rate of 33, and temperature of 97.9°F. Pertinent examination findings included a mildly distended and tender abdomen. Initial laboratory workup revealed venous lactic acid 7.4 mmol/L (0.7-2.1 mmol/L), white blood cells: 19.9 ×10∗3 μ/L (4-11.0 × 10∗3 μ/L), aspartate aminotransferase 62 U/L (17-59 U/L), alanine aminotransferase 64 U/L (<50 U/L), and C-reactive protein: 36.4 mg/dL (<0.5 mg/dL). The remainder of the laboratory tests, including a hepatic panel and lipase, were within normal limits. Computed tomography angiogram (CTA) of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis confirmed proximal superior mesenteric artery (SMA) dissection followed by total occlusion with concurrent multifocal small bowel thickening concerning for early ischemic changes (Fig). He immediately underwent exploratory laparotomy, which confirmed extensive dissection of the SMA and patchy ischemic findings involving the distal duodenum without frank necrosis. SMA embolectomy with patch angioplasty was performed the next day. A follow-up CTA of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis performed 4 days later showed patent SMA with postoperative changes and remaining dissection flaps. With additional medical management, including intravenous anticoagulation and blood pressure control, the patient's bowel function and oral intake had improved significantly. He was discharged 23 days later on apixaban 5 mg twice daily and aspirin 81 mg daily for proposed segmental arterial mediolysis (SAM) and thromboembolic mesenteric ischemia.

Three days later, he was readmitted for worsening large volume (2-3 L/d) diarrhea. Laboratory results were significant for hemoglobin of 10.1 g/dL (13.5-17.5 g/dL), albumin of 1.4 g/dL (4.0-4.9 g/dL), total protein of 3.9 g/dL (6-8 g/dL), and lactate of 2.5 mmol/L (0.5-2.0 mmol/L). A radiograph revealed evidence of bowel perforation. He emergently underwent right hemicolectomy with primary anastomosis. Right colon biopsies showed extensive focally transmural ulceration, negative for inflammatory bowel disease or neoplasm. An ischemic etiology was presumed given clinical context and intraoperative findings. Push endoscopy and colonoscopy revealed a nonbleeding, cratered gastric fundus ulcer, two large proximal jejunal ulcers, and ulceration of the transverse colon and anastomosis. Biopsies were positive for possible ischemia and negative for cytomegalovirus. A magnetic resonance lymphangiogram revealed normal central conduction lymphatic anatomy and flow. Further workup revealed an increased alpha-1-antitrypsin (A1A) of 91 mg/dL (<54 mg/dL). Based on persistent diarrhea and imaging and laboratory findings, the patient was diagnosed with protein-losing enteropathy (PLE), and was managed with total parenteral nutrition (TPN). Despite best efforts with TPN, diuresis and albumin, four antidiarrheal agents, and a diet high in protein (2-3 g/kg), low in fat, and high in medium-chain triglycerides, his diarrhea and PLE continued to worsen for 5 more months. At that time, a CTA of the abdomen demonstrated interval development of proximal subtotal SMA occlusion. An upper endoscopy showed midjejunal stenosis, and a colonoscopy revealed congested granular mucosa and an ileocolonic anastomosis with friable mucosa concerning for mesenteric ischemia. As patient's symptoms continued for 3 more days despite conservative therapy, he underwent successful and uncomplicated mesenteric angiogram with balloon angioplasty and stenting of main trunk of the SMA and right colic branch. Based on symptomatic indications including recurrent abdominal pain, there had been multiple follow-up CT studies performed at 7, 16, 15, 14, 2, 7, 10, and 2 days after the second stenting had been performed revealed a patent native SMA with a moderate stenosis of the SMA stent and mildly diminished flow distally. The celiac and inferior mesenteric arteries were widely patent. He continued to require recurrent hospitalizations for small bowel obstruction, numerous infections, and persistent PLE despite extensive treatment that included appropriate antithrombotic therapy with apixaban 5 mg twice daily and aspirin 81 mg daily. The decision was made to transition to comfort care and patient expired 8 months after his initial presentation. The patient had consented to have his information used for research, including for this case report.

Discussion

The literature was reviewed systematically to identify and compare known cases of PLE in the setting of SMA pathology (SMAP) by searching public PubMed and Google Scholar databases for full-text articles published during any time. Key words included “superior mesenteric arterial dissection,” “celiac artery dissection,” “segmental arterial mediolysis,” “protein losing enteropathy,” “hypoalbuminemia,” and “hypoproteinemia.” A total of 45 articles were identified, out of which 41 were excluded owing to the subject not having a diagnosis of PLE (n = 20), the cause of PLE not being vascular in nature (n = 2), the article not being a case report (n = 17), and the article not being available as full text (n = 2). All five patients initially presented with abdominal pain.1, 2, 3, 4 In addition, three patients presented with nausea and vomiting,1,4 and one with hematochezia.1 CTA was used in four cases for the initial diagnosis, all of which reported SMA dissection and occlusion with thrombus.1,3, 4, 5, 6 The remaining case used contrast-enhanced CT scan, which reported complete SMA occlusion without confirmed dissection.2 For the diagnosis of PLE, one case used technetium-99m-labeled human serum albumin scintigraphy,2 two used A1A clearance,3 and the remaining two cases presumed PLE owing to lack of other causes of hypoalbuminemia.1,4 Our case was the only one with the diagnosis of SAM as the main etiology for the SMA dissection and occlusion. Furthermore, we are the only authors to report the use of a magnetic resonance lymphangiogram to rule out hypoalbuminemia from lymphatic leakage.

Of the five cases, endoscopy was used in three cases to evaluate for anatomical abnormalities and capsule endoscopy was used in two.1, 2, 3, 4 Although four patients experienced ulceration or erosion,1,3,4 three were presumed secondary to ischemia,1,3 and only one had confirmed necrosis on biopsy.4 Revascularization interventions for dissection and occlusion have varied. Two cases did not undergo vascular intervention owing to a mild presentation.2,4 Two of the remaining three patients who underwent revascularization had thrombectomy and stenting, one of whom had underwent initial patch angioplasty.1 The remaining patient underwent SMA arteriotomy and intimectomy.3 Nutritional support was an important aspect of treatment, using TPN in four cases.1, 2, 3 The importance of protein-rich nutrition was also emphasized in three cases, either using a goal of two times the standard diet or 2 g/kg/d.2,4 Although three patients had long uneventful recoveries,1,2,4 one patient suffered a myocardial infarction after revascularization and one patient died.3

PLE is characterized by hypoproteinemia and hypoalbuminemia from intestinal losses which can be associated with edema, effusions (pleural or pericardial) malnutrition, ascites, immunodeficiency, and electrolyte imbalances. Diagnosis of PLE is made with A1A clearance (=A1A × stool volume/serum A1A) greater than 27 mL/24 hours in addition to ruling out other causes of protein loss.7 As one study showed, technetium-99m-labeled human albumin scintigraphy also has a high sensitivity for diagnosing PLE.3 Proposed mechanisms include increased mucosal permeability without erosions or ulcerations, intestinal loss of lymphatic fluid, and inflammatory exudation.8 Our patient developed intractable gastrointestinal ulcers caused by SMA dissection with presumed thromboembolic mesenteric ischemia, similar to the other cases summarized in the Table. Although most patients in our literature review did have ulceration or erosion,1,3,4 mucosal necrosis was only confirmed by biopsy in one case.4 Crozet et al3 proposed that, even without mucosal necrosis, blood supply in such patients can decrease to the point at which it causes intestinal dysfunction, malabsorption, and chronic postischemic enteritis.

It can be difficult sometimes to confirm a diagnosis of PLE in the postoperative period, especially in cases with short bowel syndrome. Our patient started to have evidence of PLE even before any large bowel resection. Furthermore, the patient only underwent hemicolectomy with no small bowel resection. Although our patient met the diagnostic criteria for PLE based on presentation and A1A clearance, the diagnosis of short gut syndrome was less likely because he had ≥200 cm of functional small bowel that remained in place. Based on the multiple subsequent CT studies after the second revascularization, which showed appropriate mesenteric blood flow through the widely patent celiac and inferior mesenteric arteries in addition to some blood flow through the SMA, we believe that acute mesenteric ischemia was very less likely to explain the patient's continuous PLE directly, which most likely was related to mucosal dysfunction after the initial ischemic insult.

The treatment of PLE includes a multidisciplinary approach addressing both dietary support and treatment of the underlying cause. Dietary support is a crucial part of management, with sources recommending two times the normal amount of daily protein intake, or approximately 2 to 3 g/kg/d, in addition to a diet high in medium-chain triglycerides and low in fat and long-chain triglycerides.2,7 Malabsorption in patients with PLE also increases the likelihood of developing fat-soluble vitamin deficiencies, which should be replaced as a part of treatment.7 As considered in our patient, controlling fluid balance with a combination of diuretics and albumin can help to treat edema from hypoproteinemia.2 Some patients require symptomatic management with antidiarrheals. Like other case reports detailed, our case was extremely difficult to manage because our patient failed different medical and nutritional therapies. Controlling the underlying cause of PLE, including revascularization for an ischemic etiology, is an important aspect of the management. Despite revascularization procedures, TPN, albumin, and antidiarrheal agents, our patient's symptoms continued to worsen, leading to significant deconditioning and eventual death.

The current proposed criteria for the diagnosis of SAM include dissection and/or aneurysm with or without organ infarction in mesenteric, and or renal arteries with the exclusion of fibromuscular dysplasia, vasculitis, or a connective tissue disorder.9 Based on our patient's age, gender, and presentation, including SMA dissection and occlusion with no history or clinical or imaging evidence of other vasculopathies, the diagnosis of SAM was presumed.

To our knowledge, our patient represents the only case of SAM in the literature that caused SMA dissection and occlusion leading to PLE. Consistent with previously reported criteria, the diagnosis of SAM was based on his age, gender, arterial involvement, and the exclusion of other mimicking arteriopathies, such as localized vasculitis of the gastrointestinal tract.9,8

Conclusions

We present a unique case of SMA dissection and occlusion as a rare cause of PLE that led to our patient's fatal outcome. To our knowledge, our patient represents the only case in the literature with presumed SAM as the main underlying cause for PLE. Prompt diagnosis and appropriate multidisciplinary management is crucial to address complications and improve outcomes for patients with SMAP-induced PLE. Treatment may include medical and nutritional management in addition to revascularization procedures when indicated. Future research is necessary to understand the mechanism behind SMAP-induced PLE fully and standardize treatment approach.
