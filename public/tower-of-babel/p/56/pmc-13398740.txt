Clinical Features and Outcomes of Chronic Otitis Media Complications: A 20‐Year Retrospective Study at a Tertiary Hospital
Authors: Viraporn Atchariyasathian, Nipit Tangchitnob
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveChronic otitis media (COM) is a major public health concern, particularly in developing countries. Although complications have decreased with modern antibiotics, they still cause significant morbidity and mortality. This study analyzed the clinical features, bacteriological profiles, and treatment outcomes of COM complications at a tertiary hospital over 20 years.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective review of medical records from 55 patients with COM complications at Songklanagarind Hospital (a total of 4364 patients, 2004–2023). Complications were classified as intracranial or extracranial. Data included demographics, clinical presentations, bacteriology, and clinical outcomes.ResultsThe overall complication rate was 1.26%, with intracranial complications at 0.21% (9 patients) and extracranial complications at 1.05% (46 patients). Common symptoms included otorrhea (85.5%) and otalgia (61.8%). Extracranial complications primarily included facial nerve palsy (60.0%), skull base osteomyelitis (14.5%), and subperiosteal abscesses (12.7%). Intracranial complications included brain abscess (5.5%), subdural empyema (5.5%), and lateral sinus thrombosis (3.6%). The predominant pathogens were Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Staphylococcus aureus, and Proteus mirabilis; however, no resistant strains were detected. Intracranial complications showed higher mortality (11.1% vs. 2.2%) and morbidity (22.2% vs. 19.6%) rates than extracranial complications.ConclusionAlthough COM complications remain uncommon, the substantial morbidity rates warrant early recognition and aggressive management. The absence of resistant strains suggests that the current empirical antibiotic protocols were effective in our setting.

Introduction

Chronic otitis media (COM) can lead to serious complications when the infection extends beyond the middle ear into the temporal bones and adjacent structures. These complications are traditionally classified into two categories: extracranial complications (EC) and intracranial complications (IC). EC include mastoiditis, facial nerve paralysis, labyrinthitis, and petrous apicitis, whereas IC include meningitis, brain abscess, and lateral sinus thrombosis. The reported incidence of COM complications varies substantially, ranging from 0.3%–10.5%, with higher rates observed predominantly in developing countries [1, 2, 3, 4]. Although complications of COM have shown a declining trend, with rates decreasing from 28.1% to 19.1% between 1994 and 2013 in a Nigerian tertiary center study, their severity and potential negative outcomes remain concerning, particularly in regions with limited healthcare access [4]. A 1993 study from Thailand reported a relatively low complication rate of 0.66% (92/13,948 patients); however, the associated mortality rate was notably high (18.6%). Morbidity rates were also substantial, reaching 27.9% for IC and 14.3% for EC [1]. These findings emphasize the importance of early diagnosis and appropriate intervention, whether through targeted antibiotic therapy or surgical management, to prevent adverse outcomes.

The current management of COM complications presents the following challenges: First, the declining incidence has resulted in reduced clinical exposure among physicians, potentially affecting diagnostic accuracy and timely interventions. Second, the widespread use of antibiotics in chronic suppurative otitis media has contributed to the evolution of pathogen resistance patterns [5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10]. Of particular concern in developing countries and Asia is the emergence of resistant organisms, including methicillin‐resistant Staphylococcus aureus and fluoroquinolone‐resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa [7, 8, 9, 10]. These changes in antimicrobial susceptibility patterns may influence the clinical presentation, distribution, and severity of COM.

Considering the potential evolution of the epidemiology, microbiology, and clinical characteristics of COM complications coupled with the limited contemporary data from Thailand, there is a clear need for updated research in this area. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the current patterns of COM complications, including its clinical presentation, microbiological profiles, and treatment outcomes. Understanding these aspects is crucial for identifying risk factors, improving diagnostic approaches, and optimizing therapeutic strategies to reduce the morbidity and mortality associated with COM complications.

Methods

We conducted a retrospective review of medical records from patients with COM complications at Songklanagarind Hospital between 2004 and 2023. Complications were classified as intracranial or extracranial. Patients with combined IC and EC were grouped with the intracranial category to emphasize clinical severity because ICs are more severe and require urgent medical attention, and to avoid double counting in statistical analysis.

The inclusion criteria were: 1.Patients with a confirmed diagnosis of COM (based on clinical history, otoscopic findings, and imaging findings).2.Presence of at least one EC or IC of COM (e.g., mastoiditis, facial nerve palsy, labyrinthitis, meningitis, brain abscess, or lateral sinus thrombosis).

Data were extracted from electronic medical records using a standardized collection form. The following parameters were retrieved: Demographic information: Age, sex, and underlying disease. Clinical presentation: Symptoms at presentation (otorrhea, hearing loss, pain, vertigo, headache, and neurological symptoms). Complications: Type of complications categorized as EC (mastoiditis, subperiosteal abscess, facial nerve palsy, labyrinthitis, petrous apicitis, and skull base osteomyelitis [SBO]) and IC (meningitis, subdural empyema, epidural abscess, cerebritis, brain abscess, and lateral sinus thrombosis). Microbiological data: Results of middle ear swabs, pus samples from abscesses, and tissue cultures, when applicable (including pathogen identification and antibiotic sensitivity profiles). Outcomes: Clinical resolution, morbidity (defined as persistent neurological deficits or incomplete recovery of organ function following complications), and mortality (defined as death due to COM).

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the demographic and clinical characteristics of the study population. Categorical variables (such as type of complication and underlying disease) were expressed as percentages and frequencies, while continuous variables (such as age) were reported as mean ± standard deviation. Comparative analysis between groups was conducted using chi‐square tests for categorical variables and t‐tests for continuous variables. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Ethical Considerations

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Faculty of Medicine, Prince of Songkla University (approval number: 62‐227‐13‐1). The requirement for obtaining informed consent was waived owing to the retrospective nature of the study. All data were anonymised during the collection and analysis. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and local regulatory requirements.

Results

Incidence and Demographics

During the 20‐year study period (2004–2023), 55 patients with complicated COM were identified among the 4364 patients with COM, yielding an overall complication rate of 1.26%. IC occurred in 9 patients (0.21%) and EC in 46 patients (1.05%). See Table 1.

The study population included 34 men (61.8%) and 21 women (38.2%), with ages ranging from 1 to 94 years (mean age: 50.3 years). Overall, 23 patients (41.8%) presented with underlying comorbidities, with diabetes mellitus being the most common (n = 16; 29.1%), followed by hypertension (n = 10; 18.2%) and hyperlipidemia (n = 5; 9.1%). Cholesteatoma was observed in 22 patients (40.0%).

When comparing the IC and EC groups, several trends were noted (Table 2). The IC group had 66.7% males, while the EC group had 60.9% males. Underlying comorbidities, such as diabetes mellitus (44.4% in IC vs. 26.1% in EC) and hypertension (22.2% in IC vs. 17.4% in EC), were present in both groups, with diabetes being notably more common in the IC group. Cholesteatoma was present in 33.3% of the IC group and 41.3% of the EC group. Among patients with IC, 7 patients (77.8%) underwent canal wall down mastoidectomy, and 2 patients (22.2%) underwent canal wall up mastoidectomy. Similarly, in patients with only EC, canal wall down mastoidectomy was performed in 27 patients (58.7%) and canal wall up mastoidectomy in 19 patients (41.3%). Overall, canal wall down mastoidectomy was the predominant surgical approach in both groups, though the proportion was higher in the IC group (77.8% vs. 58.7%).

Profile of Complications

The predominant EC were facial nerve palsy (n = 33, 60.0%), followed by SBO (n = 8, 14.5%), and subperiosteal abscess (n = 7, 12.7%). Notably, SBO occurred exclusively in patients with COM without cholesteatoma, whereas subperiosteal abscesses were significantly associated with cholesteatomatous COM (p = 0.034).

For clinical presentation, the common symptoms were otorrhea (47 cases, 85.5%), otalgia (34 cases, 61.8%), and facial palsy (32 cases, 58.2%). Patients with IC demonstrated higher rates of headache, altered consciousness, and neurological deficits compared to those with EC (Figure 1). Treatment Outcomes

For treatment outcomes, complete recovery was achieved in 42 patients (76.4%), while overall morbidity and mortality occurred in 11 (20.0%) and 2 (3.6%) patients, respectively (Table 4). When comparing the intracranial (IC) and extracranial (EC) groups, morbidity was observed in 22.2% of IC patients and 19.6% of EC patients. Mortality occurred in 11.1% of IC patients (1/9) and 2.2% of EC patients (1/46), suggesting a trend toward higher adverse outcomes in the IC group.

Fatal Cases of COM Complications

Among the 55 patients in our cohort, two cases (3.6%) resulted in mortality, highlighting the potentially life‐threatening nature of COM complications.

Case 1: IC Leading to Multiorgan Failure

A 69‐year‐old male with COM without cholesteatoma and significant comorbidities, including type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease, presented with facial nerve palsy and progressive neurological deterioration. Imaging revealed a 1.6 cm × 1.8 cm rim‐enhancing brain abscess with adjacent temporal lobe edema (Figure 2) and internal carotid artery (ICA) thrombosis (Figure 3). Despite radical mastoidectomy, targeted antimicrobial therapy, and neurosurgical consultation, the patient developed multiorgan failure, including recurrent NSTEMI, hospital‐acquired pneumonia, and acute kidney injury, ultimately resulting in death from complications of COM‐associated intracranial infection, ICA thrombosis, and subsequent sepsis.

Axial T2‐weighted axial MRI of the temporal bone demonstrating pathological hyperintense signal (solid white arrow) within the right internal carotid artery (ICA), definitively indicating thrombus formation. Case 2: EC With Progressive SBO

A 56‐year‐old male with hypertension and poorly‐controlled type 2 diabetes presented with right COM without cholesteatoma complicated by complete facial nerve paralysis (House–Brackmann Grade VI). Initial management included right canal wall down mastoidectomy with facial nerve decompression, followed by culture‐directed antibiotic therapy. Despite aggressive treatment, the patient developed extensive SBO with radiological evidence of disease progression showing a 3.2 cm × 1.0 cm × 1.3 cm rim‐enhancing hypodense lesion with osteolytic destruction of the right petrous apex, clivus, jugular foramen, and occipital condyle (Figure 4). The condition rapidly deteriorated to septic shock and respiratory failure requiring mechanical ventilation. The patient's status progressively declined despite multiple antimicrobial regimens (Ceftazidime, Clindamycin, Meropenem, and Colistin), leading to death from uncontrolled infection.

Discussion

In this study, among 4364 patients with COM, the incidence rate of complications was 1.26% (55 patients), comprising IC at 0.21% (9 patients) and EC at 1.05% (46 patients). Compared to the previous study by Kangsanarak et al. [1] in Thailand, we observed an increase in the overall complication rate (from 0.65%) and EC rate (from 0.45%), whereas the IC rate showed a slight decrease (from 0.24%). This trend aligns with the 22‐year study by Wu et al. [11], demonstrating a gradual increase in EC and a decline in IC. Various factors may explain this epidemiological shift, including enhanced accessibility to otolaryngology specialists, improved diagnostic capabilities, earlier detection of EC, and the advent of newer‐generation antibiotics that potentially prevent the progression to IC.

The mean age of 50.3 years in our cohort differs notably from previous studies by Kangsanarak et al. [1], Sharma et al. [3], and Yorgancilar et al. [12], which reported higher incidence rates in patients under 30 years. This demographic shift likely reflects the trend of an aging population and aligns with the findings of Im et al. [13], indicating increased surgical interventions for COM in patients aged 51–70 years after 2007. This older age distribution may be attributed to age‐related immunosenescence and an increased prevalence of comorbidities, particularly diabetes mellitus, which predispose older patients to more severe infections.

Facial nerve palsy emerged as the predominant EC (60.0%), consistent with the findings of Kangsanarak et al. [1], but higher than the 30% approximate reported in other studies [14]. In contrast, Prasad et al. [15] and Verma et al. [16] identified postauricular abscess as the most common EC, which occurred in only 12.7% of patients included in our study. SBO emerged as the second most frequent complication in our series. Although historically underdiagnosed because of diagnostic limitations, SBO is increasingly being recognized with the advancement of imaging technologies and the growing population of older adults and immunocompromised patients [17, 18].

All eight SBO cases in our cohort occurred exclusively in patients with COM without cholesteatoma (24.2% vs. 0%, p = 0.016), suggesting a distinct pathophysiological mechanism involving chronic infection progression along vascular channels and fascial planes of the skull base, particularly in immunocompromised hosts. This striking presentation pattern may be attributed to more virulent pathogens, insidious spread of infection, and repeated episodes of inadequately treated COM leading to diagnostic delays compared to the more localized, pressure‐induced bone erosion typically seen with cholesteatomatous disease.

The presenting symptom profile in our study (otorrhea, 85.5%; otalgia, 61.8%; and facial palsy, 58.2%) differed from that in previous reports that emphasized fever, otalgia, and headache as the primary manifestations [1, 3]. The association of headache altered consciousness and neurological deficits with IC emphasizes the importance of maintaining a high clinical suspicion for these complications.

Consistent with the literature, P. aeruginosa and S. aureus were the predominant pathogens [19, 20, 21, 22]. Notably, no fluoroquinolone‐resistant P. aeruginosa or methicillin‐resistant S. aureus were identified, although emerging resistance to aminoglycosides and cephalosporins (both 7%) was observed. While these findings suggest that the current empirical antibiotic choices remain effective, the relatively low culture positivity rate (47.3%) and small sample size warrant cautious interpretation.

The overall mortality rate of 3.6% falls within the reported range of 2%–10% for COM with complications and represents a significant improvement from historical rates [16]. This reduction likely reflects advances in medical management, improved microsurgical techniques, and the predominance of EC in our cohort. Despite appropriate surgical management, both fatal cases in our study progressed to severe systemic complications. This highlights that while surgery remains a cornerstone of treatment, patient outcomes are influenced by numerous factors, including comorbidities, timing of intervention, and virulence of pathogens.

Certain limitations should be considered when interpreting our findings. First, the relatively small sample size, particularly in the IC group, which limited formal statistical comparisons. Second, the single‐center nature of the study may affect generalizability. Third, the low culture positivity rate may underestimate antimicrobial resistance patterns.

Despite the relatively low incidence of complications and improved mortality rates, the significant morbidity associated with COM complications emphasizes the need for the following: 1.Early recognition and intervention, particularly in older adults and immunocompromised patients.2.Regular monitoring of local antimicrobial susceptibility patterns.3.Heightened awareness of atypical presentations, especially in patients with risk factors.

Conclusion

In our cohort of 4364 patients with COM, the most common complication of COM was an EC. This study found no resistance of P. aeruginosa or S. aureus to commonly used antibiotics. Although the incidence of complications is relatively low and the mortality rates of COM with complications have improved with advances in medical and surgical treatments, morbidity remains a significant concern.
