Abortion and its association with antiretroviral therapy among young women living with HIV in northern Uganda: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Edward Kumakech, Deo Benyumiza, Marvin Musinguzi, Wilfred Inzama, Ebong Doryn, James Okello, Lydia Kabiri, Jasper Watson Ogwal-Okeng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAbortion is a critical reproductive health issue among young women living with HIV (YWLHIV). Despite the widespread use of the antiretroviral therapy (ART) for women of reproductive age, its impact on abortion prevalence remains unclear. We set out to determine the prevalence of abortion among the YWLHIV receiving TLD-based ART regimen in northern Uganda and its association with the ART regimen and duration alongside other key socio-demographic, reproductive health, lifestyle and facility access– related factors.MethodsA cross-sectional study of YWLHIV who reported at least one pregnancy in northern Uganda. Using an interviewer-administered questionnaire, participants were asked about their abortion history, ART regimen and duration, contraceptive use, parity, male partner’s HIV status, and access to community-based family planning resources. Descriptive statistics for abortion prevalence, Chi-square test, Fisher’s Exact test, bivariate and multivariate Poisson regression analyses for the associations between these variables and the occurrence of abortion were used. The 5% significance level and 95% confidence intervals were considered.ResultsWe analyzed data of 268 YWLHIV who reported conceiving at least one pregnancy. The abortion prevalence was 20.9% (95% Confidence Interval (CI) of 16.2% − 26.1%). No significant association was found between the abortion experience and ART regimens nor duration. The significant predictors for abortion included awareness of public health facilities that provide family planning services, parity, sero-concordant HIV-positive partnerships, and modern contraceptive use.ConclusionThis study found a substantial abortion prevalence of 20.9% among the YWLHIV in northern Uganda. There was no significant association between the occurrence of abortion and ART regimens nor duration. Key predictors of abortion included awareness of public health facilities that provide family planning services, parity, use of contraceptive methods and the male partner’s HIV positive status. To reduce abortion, creation of awareness of public health facilities that provide family planning services, contraceptive use, and couple-focused HIV testing and status disclosure are recommended.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12981-025-00777-x.Keywords: Abortion, Women, HIV, Antiretroviral therapy, Uganda

Introduction

Globally by 2023, there were estimated 1.9 million adolescent girls and young women (AGYW) aged 15–24 years living with HIV compared with 1.2 million adolescent boys and young men aged 15–24 years [1]. Seventy-seven per cent of the AGYW who acquired HIV in 2023 live in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) including almost two thirds (60%) in Eastern and Southern Africa [1]. In Uganda by 2022, there were 119,000 AGYW living with HIV [2].

Maternal HIV infection and or its poor management have been associated with an increased risk for adverse reproductive health outcomes including abortions or miscarriages, preterm deliveries and low birth weights [3–5]. The global and SSA pooled rates for miscarriages and abortions among young women living with HIV (YWLHIV) are scarce. In South Africa, 20% of pregnancies among YWLHIV resulted in loss [6]. Similarly in Uganda, 54% of the pregnancies resulted into abortions (both spontaneous and non-spontaneous) [7]. The increased risk of miscarriages or abortions among YWLHIV were linked to the high viral loads, low CD4 count, delayed antiretroviral therapy (ART) initiation, poor immune function, increased susceptibility to infections, tobacco smoking, alcohol consumption, and marijuana use in studies conducted in the United States (US) and South Africa [3, 6, 8]. Women with suboptimal ART regimens, high viral load and or low CD4 counts face higher miscarriage and abortion rates possibly due to the inadequate viral suppression and immune suppression [6]. Other risk factors linked to the increased risks for miscarriages or abortions among YWLHIV included the lack of marital life, lack of fertility desires, high number of living children, non-use of modern contraceptives, unintended pregnancy and the use of abortion pills particularly misoprostol [9]. Miscarriages or abortions can lead to physical complications, emotional distress like grief, worries, depression, anxiety, and social isolation due to stigma [10]. Miscarriages or abortions can also lead to heavy bleeding, anemia, infections, and or maternal death [11].

To reduce the adverse health outcomes associated with HIV infection including miscarriages or abortions, the World Health Organization (WHO) recommend pregnant women living with HIV (PLHIV) to use ART, primarily combination ART (cART) [12]. The cART typically consists of two nucleoside/nucleotide reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NRTIs)—the ‘backbone’—and a third antiretroviral agent, Efavirenz (EFV). The most frequently used NRTI is tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (TDF), which is most often coformulated with another NRTI, emtricitabine (FTC) or lamivudine (3TC) as a convenient once per day medication [13]. Despite the above interventions, previous studies in SSA continue to indicate a high proportion of the YWLHIV (53.0%) experiencing miscarriages and abortions [7]. Some previous studies suggested a potential link between cART regimen especially those containing protease inhibitors (PIs) and the increased risk of miscarriages or abortions [14, 15], particularly when the cART is initiated before or early in the pregnancy. In one global systematic review and meta-analysis, the authors concluded that tenofovir/emtricitabine cART is likely to increase the risks for stillbirth, early neonatal death and even early premature delivery compared with zidovudine/lamivudine cART, but the certainty is low when they are not coprescribed with lopinavir/ritonavir [16]. Other studies indicated no significant differences in the miscarriage or abortion rates between YWLHIV on cART and those not on cART [17, 18].

The above conflicting literature warrants further research on the associations between cART and miscarriage or abortion rates more so in the new era of fixed-dose combination (FDC) ART regimens. The cART regimen containing tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (TDF), lamivudine (3TC) and dolutegravir (DTG) also known as TLD is a new FDC regimen that was introduced in Uganda in 2018 [19]. This followed the WHO’s recommendation for dolutegravir-based regimen as the preferred first-line treatment due to their superior efficacy and tolerability [20, 21]. Initially, access to the TLD regimen in Uganda and other countries was restricted among women of childbearing potential due to concerns over potential premature births, small gestational age, and neural tube defects [22–24] and miscarriages or abortions [25]. However, subsequent safety evidence led to its expansion to all adults, including women of childbearing age in line with the updated WHO guidelines [19].

Prior to the national rollout, a pilot study conducted in Uganda found that YWLHIV largely preferred the TLD because of its small pill size, once daily dosing, and absence of other previous efavirenz (EFV)-like side effects [26]. In related studies in Uganda, the superior viral suppression ability of the TLD regimen were also confirmed [27, 28].

Since the switch from the EFV-based cART to the TLD in 2018, limited research has examined the associations between the TLD regimen and miscarriage or abortions in SSA settings. Yet, miscarriages or abortions remain a significant concern in SSA. This study therefore set out to determine the abortion rate and the associated factors among Ugandan YWLHIV who were receiving the new TLD regimen. The findings will help address the current evidence gap on the association between the TLD regimen and miscarriages or abortions in SSA.

Methods

Conceptual framework

In examining the association between the ART regimen and the occurrence of abortion, this study considered several potential confounding and explanatory variables. These were selected based on their theoretical relevance and the literature [6, 7], as per the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) recommendations for reporting confounding variables.

HIV-related characteristics considered included the duration since HIV diagnosis, disclosure of HIV status to the male sexual partner and whether the woman was in a sero-discordant relationship. Markers of duration since HIV diagnosis such as viral load and CD4 count specifically were indicated as a risk factor for abortion among the YWLHIV in previous studies in the US and SSA [3, 6, 29, 30].

ART-specific characteristics considered included the specific ART regimen used by the participants particularly the duration of TLD only and the previous non-TLD plus the TLD regimens. The type of ART regimen particularly the use of non-protease inhibitors has been associated with the increased risk of abortion among the YWLHIV in previous studies [3, 31]. Also considered was the women’s duration on ART for a longer duration or conceiving on ART was associated with an increased risk for adverse pregnancy outcomes including abortions [15].

Reproductive and obstetric health characteristics considered included the woman’s age at sexual debut, and whether the pregnancy was planned or unintended and the use of modern contraceptives. Lack of fertility desires and unintended pregnancy has been implicated for abortion among the YWLHIV in a previous study [32]. Other reproductive history considered included the parity and or the number of living children which were indicated to influence the risk of abortion consistent with the previous studies [5, 31, 33].

Lifestyle factors considered included the health risk behaviors such as tobacco smoking, alcohol consumption, and drug use. Previous studies have showed significant association between smoking and the occurrence of abortion among the YWLHIV [3, 8, 34]. Alcohol use was also associated with an increased risk for abortion among the YWLHIV in a previous study in the US [3].

Awareness of and access to community-based family planning resources variables considered included the distance to the nearest health facility and awareness of and access to the various community-based healthcare resources that provide family planning services such as health centres and hospitals along with exposure to the mass media. In a previous study conducted in South Africa, access to community-based support by the lay community health workers was associated with a decreased risk for stillbirth among YWLHIV [35]. Furthermore, lack of access to sexual and reproductive healthcare including contraceptive methods have been implicated for abortion among the YWLHIV in previous studies [32].

Study design

This study employed a cross-sectional design [36]. The cross-sectional design was used for analysis of the data from the YWLHIV population in Lira district and Lira city at a single point in time, providing efficient snapshots of the abortion prevalence, risk and protective factors. The design was cost-effective and quick to implement.

Study area and setting

This study was conducted across six public health facilities in Lira District and Lira City, Northern Uganda, selected out of 42 total facilities in the region. The participating sites included one regional referral hospital, one primary healthcare center (HC IV), and four dispensaries (HC IIIs) located in both urban and rural areas. These facilities were specifically chosen because they maintained functional ART clinics with complete, up-to-date registries of the YWLHIV (aged 15–24) receiving treatment - criteria that other local facilities couldn’t meet. Located about 342 km north of Kampala, this strategic selection ensured representation across different healthcare levels while maintaining data reliability for studying HIV care access in northern Uganda’s diverse settings.

Study population

The target population comprised 504 YWLHIV aged 15–24 years registered and receiving ART at the six public health facilities by the time of data collection from 20th November 2022 (Fig. 1). Of these, 273 with a positive history of having conceived pregnancy at least once in their lifetime were eligible to participate in the study. Of the 273, five were severely ill to withstand the study procedures and therefore were excluded from the study, leaving an accessible population of 268 YWLHIV (Fig. 1).

Flowchart showing the participant’s recruitment process.

Sample size determination

The initial sample size for the larger cross-sectional study on the female-controlled dual protection method use among the YWLHIV in Uganda [37] was determined using the Kish Leslie (1965) formula, assuming a 50% prevalence, a 95% confidence level, and a 0.05 margin of error, giving a minimum required sample size of 384 participants. This was further adjusted for a non-response rate resulting in a final target sample size of 423 YWLHIV.

For the present subgroup analysis focusing on the prevalence of abortion and its association with antiretroviral therapy, we analyzed data from the subset of 268 women who reported having had at least one pregnancy in their lifetime out of the original 423 participants. This subgroup was appropriate because the abortion outcome could only be meaningfully assessed among women who had ever been pregnant.

Sampling and participant recruitment strategies

Stratified random sampling was employed to ensure representativeness across facility levels [38]. The three strata used were the three health facility types where the ART clinics were based namely referral hospital, health center level four (or primary healthcare center) and health center level three (or dispensary). The ART clinic registers from each facility type which has the list of the YWLHIV aged 15–24 years attending the facility type served as the sampling frames for the stratified random sampling. A proportionate to size numbers of participants were selected randomly within the strata. With the assistance from the healthcare providers, selected participants found present at the ART clinics seeking healthcare were identified and enrolled into the study. Similarly, with the assistance from the community health workers, selected participants not present at the ART clinics during the study visits were called by phone to come to the ART clinics or were physically visited at their homes to participate in the study. The ART clinics operate every Monday to Friday from 8:00 am to 5:00 pm.

Data collection tools and procedures

Data were collected from November 20, 2022 to April 30, 2023 using a structured questionnaire (supplementary material 1). The tool was adapted from previous studies conducted in Uganda [7] and South Africa [6], and pretested among 10% of the initial 423 sample size of the YWLHIV at Lira University Hospital. Face–to-face interviews were conducted by six trained midwife-research assistants with healthcare background in English or Langi. The face-to-face interviews lasted approximately one hour. Interviews were held in private rooms within the ART clinic facility.

Study variables

Outcome variable

Lifetime number of abortions was the outcome variable. In this study, abortion was defined as the self-reported expulsion of the products of conception before term and it encompassed both spontaneous (miscarriage) and induced terminations. Accordingly, participants were asked about the number of times they experienced abortions in their lifetime. This data was later dichotomized into women who did not experience any abortion (coded as 0) and those who experienced 1 + abortions (coded as 1) for estimation of the abortion prevalence.

Exposure variables

The explanatory variable assessed was the participant’s duration of exposure to non-TLD plus TLD– based ART regimen or TLD– only based ART regimen. This was calculated from the year the participant was initiated on ART. The participant who initiated ART before 2018 were regarded to have been exposed to non-TLD plus TLD– based ART regimen (coded as 0) while those who initiated ART from 2018 onwards were regarded to have been exposed to TLD-based regimen only (coded as 1). Additional ART - related variables assessed was the participant’s daily pill burden which was assessed by asking how many medicines from the ART clinic she usually gets prescribed and given to take on daily basis.

Confounding and explanatory variables

Other potential confounding and explanatory variables assessed included the participant’s socio-demographic characteristics (age, urban or rural address, marital status, educational level, and monthly income), reproductive history (age at sexual debut, gravidity, parity and number of living children), and sexual health indicators (male partner’s HIV status disclosure, discordance, and fertility desires).

Access to community-based FP resources was also assessed by asking whether the participants were aware of and had ever accessed FP information, education or services from the community resources such as the community health workers also known as the Village Health Teams (VHTs) in Uganda, private medical centers or clinics, public health centres and hospitals, ART clinics, pharmacies or drug shops, community-based organizations (CBOs), and non-governmental organizations (NGOs) that provide family planning information, education, services or methods. They were also asked whether they were aware of and had ever accessed the FP information or education from the mass media particularly the radio, television, posters, or the billboards. Participants reporting awareness and access were considered to be aware of and had ever accessed the FP resources; those who had neither heard of nor accessed the resources were considered unaware and never accessed the community-based FP resources.

Data management and analysis

The data were double-entered in the EpiData 3.1 (EpiData Association, Odense, Denmark), validated, and exported to the IBM Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the abortion rates and the participant’s characteristics, awareness of and access to the community-based FP resources. Associations were assessed using the Chi-square or Fisher’s Exact tests.

To identify the populations at higher risk of abortion (stratified prevalence), we conducted comparative analyses using Chi-square or Fisher’s Exact tests across several key demographic and behavioral factors. These analyses specifically examined differences in abortion prevalence between: multigravida women (those who have gotten pregnant multiple times) vs. prime gravida women (first-time pregnancies); multiparous women (those who had given birth before) vs. nulliparous women (those who had never delivered); alcohol consumers vs. non-consumers; modern contraceptive users vs. non-users and women with intended pregnancies vs. those with unintended pregnancies.

For each comparison, we calculated and reported the abortion rates within each subgroup, along with the corresponding Chi-square (χ²) or Fisher’s Exact test values and their statistical significance (p-values). This approach helped to pinpoint high-risk groups.

As sensitivity analysis, factors showing significant associations at bivariate analysis were first assessed for multicollinearity before entry into the multivariate regression analysis. The multi-collinearity diagnostics (Table 1) strongly suggest multicollinearity between the woman’s parity and number of living children and therefore parity was removed from the multivariate regression model shown in Table 3. Accordingly, the HIV discordance status shows a very strong association with the male partner’s HIV status (Phi = 1.414, p = < 0.001) and so HIV discordance status was also removed from the multivariate modeling shown in Table 3.

Independent predictors were then identified using the modified Poisson regression with robust error variance. Each of the 11 factors shown in Table 1 were first individually entered into the modified Poisson regression to generate the unadjusted measure of associations, the crude prevalence ratios (cPR). The above 11 factors included alcohol consumption, awareness of VHTs who provide FP, awareness of public health facilities that provide FP, awareness of ART clinics that provide FP, number of living children, dual protection method use, modern contraceptive use, short-acting contraceptive use, intendedness of the recent pregnancy, the initial HIV status of the woman and that of the male partner. These were later jointly entered into the modified Poisson regression to generate the adjusted measure of associations, the adjusted prevalence ratios (aPR). The measures of the associations were reported alongside their corresponding 95% confidence intervals. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Ethical considerations

The ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Gulu University Research Ethics Committee (GUREC) under the approval number GUREC-2022-309. Informed consents were obtained from the participants aged 18 and above. Although the participants aged 15–17 years were sexually active, living with HIV, receiving antiretroviral therapy and some were married, were regarded as emancipated minors, they also provided assent in addition to informed consents from their adult parents or legal guardians. Consent was provided through written signatures or thumbprints. Research assistants facilitated the consent process to ensure participants fully understood the study, its procedures, risks, benefits, and their rights to withdraw from the study without any penalties.

The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and other relevant ethical guidelines. Participants were compensated with 10,000 Ugandan shillings (approximately three U.S. dollars) for their time and participation in the study.

Results

Participant’s socio-demographic characteristics

We recruited 268 YWLHIV aged 15–24 years with the mean age of 22.5 ± 1.9 years. The demographic profile of the participants reveals a predominantly young population, with the majority (92.2%) aged between 19 and 24 years. Most participants identified as Catholic by religion (44.0%).

In terms of education, a significant portion (62.3%) had completed primary education, 31.0% had attained secondary or tertiary education, and 6.7% had no formal education. A large majority (82.8%) earned income from non-salary sources, while only 17.2% were salaried. Regarding income levels, 56.0% earned less than the equivalent of US$24 per month, while 44.0% earned US$24 or more.

Most of the participants (61.2%) were married. The same proportion 61.2% reported sexual debut before the age of 18. The majority lived in urban areas (79.1%). Access to health facilities was generally good, with 90.3% of participants living within 5 km of a health facility.

In relation to HIV, just over half (53%) had been diagnosed with HIV for more than four years. A slightly higher proportion (56%) had been on ART for four years or less, compared to 44% who had been on ART for more than four years. With regard to the newer TLD-based ART regimen, 52.6% had been on it for four years, while 47.4% had used it for three years or less.

Prevalence of abortion

The prevalence of abortion among the study participants was 20.9%, with a 95% Confidence Interval (CI) of 16.2–26.1%. This means that out of the total 268 participants, 56 women reported having experienced one or more abortions (20.9%), while 212 women (79.1%) did not. The number of abortions ranged from 0 to 3 with a mean of 0.26 ± 0.552. Among the 56 women who reported abortion, 45 (80.3%) had one, 9 (16.1%) had two, and 2 (3.6%) had three abortions.

High-risk groups for abortion

The study revealed significant differences in abortion rates across various demographic and behavioral factors. Women who had been pregnant multiple times showed substantially higher abortion rates (32.0%) than those experiencing their first pregnancy (18.3%), with this difference being statistically significant (χ² = 4.59, p = 0.032). And even more pronounced disparity emerged when examining parity, as women who had never given birth had dramatically higher abortion rates (60.0%) compared to those who had previously delivered (13.0%), a difference that was highly significant (χ² = 47.23, p < 0.001).

Behavioral factors also showed significant associations with abortion prevalence. Women who consumed alcohol exhibited nearly double the abortion rate (39.4%) of non-drinkers (18.3%), a statistically significant difference (χ² = 6.57, p = 0.010). Similarly, contraceptive use patterns revealed that women using modern birth control methods had higher abortion rates (25.8%) than non-users (13.8%), with this difference reaching statistical significance (χ² = 5.66, p = 0.017).

Perhaps most strikingly, pregnancy intention showed one of the strongest relationships with abortion outcomes. Women whose pregnancies were unintended had abortion rates more than three times higher (40.0%) than those who had planned their pregnancies (11.7%), a difference that was highly statistically significant (χ² = 8.73, p = 0.003).

The findings regarding the associations between factors and the occurrence of spontaneous abortion is shown in Table 2. Several factors were found to be significantly associated with the experience of abortion. This included alcohol consumption, awareness of and access to the family planning services from the VHTs, public health centres or hospitals, and ART clinics, parity and the number of living children, the use of dual protection methods (such as condoms combined with another modern contraceptive method), and modern contraceptive use. Additional significant factors included woman’s experiences of unintended pregnancy, the woman’s initial HIV status, and the HIV discordance status of the couple.

Factors not associated with abortion

The study found numerous factors across four key domains that showed no statistically significant associations with abortion (all p-values > 0.05 unless noted).

Socio-demographics: No significant relationships were found for age (p = 0.163), religion (p = 1.000), education level (p = 0.675), income sources/amount (p = 0.121–0.842), marital status (p = 0.822), or urban/rural residence (p = 1.000).

Health behaviors/access: Neither substance use (p = 0.349), healthcare proximity (p = 0.588), contraceptive access perceptions (p = 0.113–0.496), long-acting reversible contraceptives (LARCs) use (p = 0.881), condom use (p = 1.000), nor contraceptive access failures (p = 0.466) were significant predictors.

Reproductive history: Pregnancy count (gravidity, p = 0.055), fertility desires (woman’s p = 0.920; partner’s p = 0.121), sexual debut age (p = 0.542), or years sexually active (p = 0.344) showed no associations.

HIV-related factors: HIV diagnosis duration (p = 0.316), transmission mode (p = 0.631), status disclosure (p = 0.264–0.522), partner’s status (p = 0.064), ART regimens and duration (p = 0.260–0.478), or daily pill burden (p = 0.349) were unrelated. Age at HIV diagnosis also showed non-significant association (p = 0.109) (Table 2).

Predictors of abortion

The predictors of abortion are shown in Table 3. After adjusting for other variables in the multivariate model, five factors remained statistically significant. Firstly, women who were not aware of public health facilities that provide FP services were nearly three times more likely to have an abortion compared to those who were aware of such services from these facilities. Secondly, women who had no children were almost three times more likely to report a history of abortion compared to those who had at least one child. Thirdly, women who were not using modern contraceptives had over five times the likelihood of experiencing an abortion compared to those who used the modern contraceptives (APR = 5.53, 95% CI of 1.23–24.74, p = 0.025). Furthermore, women whose male partners were also HIV positive were significantly more likely to experience an abortion compared to those who were unsure of their partner’s status (APR = 2.70, 95% CI of 1.01–7.23, p = 0.048). Nevertheless, alcohol consumption, the use of short-acting contraceptives (SACs), awareness about VHTs and ART clinics that provide family planning services, and the women’s initial HIV status before their current male partners were among the non-significant predictors of abortion.

Discussions

We found 20.9% prevalence of abortion among the YWLHIV in northern Uganda. This 20% abortion prevalence in northern Uganda is consistent with the 20% abortion prevalence reported from a previous study conducted in rural South Africa [6] but just about half of the 54% prevalence reported in a previous study conducted in Central and Eastern Uganda [7]. The persistent high abortion rates among YWLHIV in such SSA settings highlight the need for improved access to contraception, family planning counseling and integration of HIV and reproductive health services.

We found no association between the participant’s exposure to TLD only, non-TLD plus TLD ART regimens and their experience of abortion. This study instead found that abortion among YWLHIV was primarily influenced by healthcare-seeking preferences—particularly access to family planning services—and individual risk behaviours, rather than being directly associated with ART. Participants demonstrated a strong desire to access family planning services as a preventive measure against adverse reprodictive health outcomes such as abortion. However, the study also revealed that most abortions were not medically indicated but rather based on personal choice. This contradictory result and its lack of direct linkage with ART represents a significant insight and underscores the need to strengthen the integration of family planning within ART programs. This finding is consistent with the previous studies including the two conducted in Kenya that reported no association between ART regimens and abortion [17, 18]. It is also consistent with a previous study conducted in the US that reported no association between the use of combination ART regimens and abortion [5]. The absence of a direct link suggests that factors beyond ART influence abortion risk among the YWLHIV. Simply expanding ART access or adherence efforts is insufficient. Effective reduction in abortions may require an integrated sexual reproductive health and HIV care.

The study also revealed that YWLHIV who lacked awareness and or access to the public health facilities offering family planning services were more likely to experience an abortion than those who were informed about such facilities. This finding concurs with a prospective cohort study conducted in the US that found that providing access to free birth control to women reduces unplanned pregnancies and abortions [39]. This underscores the importance of strengthening family planning education and service delivery through the professionally trained health workers.

The nulliparous YWLHIV were found to be more likely to experience abortions than the primiparous or multiparous ones. This inverse relationship contrasts with findings from a previous study conducted in the US which reported an increased risk of abortion from higher parity [5]. The discrepancy between these studies may reflect differences in access to reproductive health services. An implication of this finding is that nulliparous YWLHIV may require more targeted reproductive health support to reduce the risk of abortion.

The study revealed that the use of dual protection methods such as the female or male condoms alongside a modern contraceptive was associated with an increased risk for abortions. This finding is consistent with a previous study conducted in the US which also found that the use of dual protection method particularly condom was associated with an increased risk of abortion in YWLHIV [5]. An implication of this finding is that health education efforts should go beyond simply promoting the dual protection methods. They should focus on factors promoting their effectiveness such as ensuring correct and consistent use of both the condoms and the modern contraceptives.

Furthermore, we also found that the modern contraceptive use was significantly associated with a decreased risk of abortion. This finding suggests that effective contraception plays a critical role in preventing unintended pregnancies, which are a major precursor of abortion. An implication of this finding is that improving access to and education about modern contraceptive methods should be a priority in HIV care targeting the YWLHIV.

The study revealed that YWLHIV whose male partners were also HIV-positive faced a significantly higher risk of abortion compared to those whose male partners had an unknown HIV status. This finding concur with a global review that indicated that women in concordant HIV-positive partnerships were more likely to experience abortion [40]. This finding suggests that HIV counseling and reproductive health services need to address not just the individual woman’s reproductive health status but also the couple’s status, including their shared HIV status.

Study strengths

This study demonstrates several notable strengths. The use of a conceptual framework allowed for the collection of data on a wide spectrum of socio-demographic characteristics, HIV-related, ART– related, reproductive and obstetric health- related, lifestyle - related, and awareness and or access to the family planning services - related factors. The use of the face-to-face interviews to administer the questionnaire allowed the research team to clarify participants’ responses and collect high-quality and reliable data.

Study limitations

The study had some limitations. First, the researchers did not collect data on the gestational age at the time of abortion, the specific circumstances preceding the abortion (such as stress, exposure to violence, or prior use of medications, chemicals, or contraceptives to induce the abortion) nor the clinical characteristics (such as associated symptoms). Consequently, the researchers were unable to classify the abortions into specific categories, such as unsafe, medical, spontaneous, induced, threatened, missed, incomplete, complete or septic abortions. Future research should aim at collecting detailed information on the timing, circumstances and clinical features of abortion to enable more precise classification and analysis.

Secondly, the study did not collect data on the clinical HIV- and ART-related variables such as the participants’ most recent CD4 cell count, viral load, WHO clinical staging of the HIV disease, history of opportunistic infections, history of sexually transmitted diseases, adherence to ART, presence of ART-related side effects nor whether ART was initiated before or during the pregnancy that ended in abortion. These factors have been documented in the previous studies as risk factors for abortion among the YWLHIV [6, 9, 18, 31, 34] and their omission limits the depth of the current analysis.

Additionally, the study did not gather data on other clinical factors that could influence the risk of abortion including the participant’s history of stillbirths, antenatal care attendance (number and timing of the visits), pregnancy spacing or any pre-existing health conditions such as hypertension or diabetes. Information was also lacking on infections that influence abortion like sexually transmitted infections (STIs), urinary tract infections (UTIs) or malaria during pregnancy as well as nutritional indicators such as body mass index, mid-upper arm circumference, or hemoglobin levels. Beyond clinical variables, the study also did not capture environmental and household factors that might impact abortion rates such as exposure to environmental stressors (for example, extreme heat, toxic substances or displacement due to flooding or other climate-related events) or household food insecurity which can affect maternal nutrition and stress levels.

More so, the definition of abortion was based on the self-reported expulsion of the fetus before term not based on a formal diagnosis by a physician, nurse or midwife. To ensure accuracy in the reporting, women were probed about the months when abortion occurred and only those that happened within the first five months of pregnancy that is to say before 20 weeks of the pregnancy were considered as abortion.

Also by asking the women whether they intended their recent pregnancy may have introduced social desirability bias in the reporting of the number of abortions they experienced. To minimize this risk, the women were also asked supplementary and complementary questions like how many times they have ever gotten pregnant (gravidity), their number of childbirths (parity) and their number of living children and these were triple checked to make sure they matched.

These gaps highlight the need for subsequent research efforts to incorporate a broader set of clinical, environmental and social variables to better understand their associations with specific types of abortion among the YWLHIV.

Conclusions

This study found a substantial prevalence of abortion (20.9%) among the YWLHIV in northern Uganda, consistent with rates reported in other SSA contexts but lower than some regional estimates in Uganda. Association analysis revealed no significant associations between the type or duration of ART regimens and abortion experience.

Key factors associated with an increased risk for abortion included having limited awareness about public health facilities that provide family planning services and the non-use of modern contraceptive methods. Moreover, women in sero-concordant HIV-positive partnerships had a higher likelihood of abortion.

To reduce abortion rates among the YWLHIV, creation of awareness and access to the public health facilities that provide family planning services, contraceptive use, and couple-focused HIV testing and status disclosure are recommended.
