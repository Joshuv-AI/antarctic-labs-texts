Revisiting PIV in lung cancer: bridging prognostic insights with clinical applicability
Authors: Huazhen Wu, Jia Liao, Lishan Hu, Siwen Li
Published: 2026

To the editor,

We read with great interest the recent article by Hong et al. [1], titled “Pan-immune-inflammation value in lung cancer: prognostic significance and implications for therapeutic guidance—a systematic review and meta-analysis” (World Journal of Surgical Oncology, 2025). The authors conducted a comprehensive meta-analysis and convincingly demonstrated that elevated pan-immune-inflammation value (PIV) is associated with poorer overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) in lung cancer patients. This work stands out for its robust methodology, comprehensive subgroup analyses, and timely focus on immunotherapy—a treatment domain in urgent need of reliable biomarkers.

Despite these strengths, we wish to raise three additional limitations that were not fully addressed and may inform future studies. First, the authors dichotomized PIV into “high” and “low” groups using varying thresholds across studies, but did not investigate PIV as a continuous variable. This limits our understanding of whether a dose-response relationship exists—that is, whether incremental increases in PIV consistently correlate with worse prognosis, or if there are saturation or threshold effects. Dichotomization can also lead to loss of information and potential misclassification. Furthermore, the prognostic value of dynamic PIV trends over time—such as early changes during therapy—may offer better insights into tumor-host interactions and treatment monitoring [2]. Future research should model PIV continuously, apply restricted cubic splines to explore non-linear associations, and assess serial PIV values in prospective cohorts to better understand its temporal behavior and prognostic kinetics.

Second, while the meta-analysis convincingly demonstrates an association between higher baseline PIV and poorer long-term survival, its potential predictive role in relation to treatment response remains uncertain. In clinical practice, oncologists often require biomarkers that not only stratify prognosis but also help anticipate early treatment benefit, particularly in the context of immunotherapy and targeted therapy. Future studies that directly link PIV to short-term endpoints such as objective response rate (ORR), disease control rate (DCR), or early progression under specific systemic regimens would be valuable to determine whether PIV functions purely as a prognostic marker or also carries predictive information that might inform therapy selection and adaptation [3, 4].

Third, although PIV captures the complex interplay between inflammation and immunity, it is still a non-specific biomarker, and its incremental utility beyond existing molecular and clinical parameters remains unclear. The study does not compare PIV to established tumor-specific biomarkers (e.g., PD-L1, TMB), nor does it examine its integration into composite models with clinical staging or performance status [5]. As precision oncology moves toward multi-dimensional decision tools, it is crucial to assess whether PIV adds independent and synergistic value. Future studies should use statistical methods such as net reclassification improvement (NRI), concordance indices, and decision curve analysis to quantify whether including PIV improves risk stratification, especially when combined with existing prognostic frameworks.

In conclusion, this meta-analysis significantly advances our understanding of PIV as a prognostic marker in lung cancer. To maximize its clinical relevance, future research should explore continuous and longitudinal applications of PIV, connect it with treatment response data, and assess its added value within composite biomarker frameworks. We commend the authors for their important contribution and hope these suggestions will support continued progress in the development of clinically actionable prognostic tools.
