Factors associated with adherence to muscle-trengthening activity guidelines among adolescents at different educational stages in China
Authors: Xiaolu Zha, Xiao Ma, Yan Tang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study aimed to examine the associations between guideline-concordant muscle-strengthening activity (MSA) and multiple factors, including anthropometric measures, demographic characteristics, behavioral patterns, and sociocultural influences, across different educational stages among Chinese school-aged children.MethodsUtilizing data from the 2019 Shanghai Student Physical Fitness Index Survey, we analyzed a representative sample of 81,264 school children (aged 9–17 years; 49.6% female) and their parents. Participants reporting ≥ 3 days of MSA per week were classified as meeting the MSA recommendations. Student-reported measures included gender, grade level, MSA frequency, moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA), peer and parental support, and anthropometric data for BMI calculation. Parent-reported measures encompassed family income and parental MSA/MVPA participation. Binary logistic regression was employed to identify factors associated with meeting the MSA recommendations.ResultsThe analysis revealed that 51.2% of Shanghai school children met MSA recommendations, but participation rates varied significantly by grade level. In the elementary school model, student MVPA (school days: OR = 1.77, 95% CI:1.74–1.81; weekends: OR = 1.84, 95% CI:1.76–1.93), parental MSA (OR = 1.14, 95% CI:1.11–1.16), peer support (OR = 1.07, 95% CI:1.06–1.09) and family income (30,0001–10,0000) (OR = 1.08, 95% CI:1.0-1.18) were significantly associated with guideline-concordant MSA, explaining 72.5% of the variance. The middle school model additionally identified parental support as a significant predictor (junior middle: OR = 1.01, 95% CI:1.00-1.01; junior high: OR = 1.02, 95% CI:1.01–1.02), accounting for 68.6% and 70.8% of the variance, respectively.ConclusionsOur findings advance the understanding of MSA behavior among Chinese youth and its grade-specific determinants. The results suggest that effective interventions should adopt a multi-level approach, engaging not only the children themselves but also their parents and peers to promote healthier, more active lifestyles.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24959-4.

Introduction

Muscle-strengthening activities (MSA), encompassing both structured resistance training (RT) and recreational pursuits, are crucial for promoting health in school-aged youth [9, 24]. Global health authorities, such as the World Health Organization (WHO), recommend that youth aged 5–17 engage in muscle-strengthening exercises at least three days per week to enhance physical fitness and prevent chronic diseases [5]. The policy initiatives aim to establish MSA as a priority in both global and national health agendas.

Despite policy efforts, muscular strength levels among school-aged youth have shown a declining trend [2, 11]. Population-based MSA surveys indicate low MSA adherence among adolescents in countries like the United States(49.5%) [13, 20], more alarmingly, in China, where only less than 40% of youth were reported to meet recommendations [23, 26, 28]. These figures highlight the urgency of identifying the determinants of MSA to inform effective interventions.

It is of great importance to identify the influential factors of student MSA behavior for proposing an effective intervention and promoting our understanding of the benefit of MSA participation. Of the work that has been done, associations have been found between adolescents’ MSA and demographic characteristics, including gender, grade and income [23, 27, 28], weight status [26]; biological variables, including muscular fitness [21, 24]; psychosocial variables, including peer/parent support [22, 28], RT self-efficacy and RT motivation [24]; behavioral variables, including sleep duration [24] and moderate to vigorous intensity physical activity (MVPA) [1, 28]. However, there are still many gaps in the current literature. Firstly, most large-scale data come from Western countries, such as Australia [24] and the United States [21, 22], and there is a lack of evidence from the special context of major developed cities in China with extremely competitive education and its own culture. Secondly, two studies form China consider adolescents as a totally homogenous population and fail to explore the dynamic changes of influencing factors in different educational stages (i.e., primary, junior high, and senior high school) [23, 26, 28]. With the increase of academic burden and physiological changes due to aging, it is expected that the determinants of MSA would be greatly different. Finally, even though the impact of sociocultural factors has been recognized [28], the relationship between these factors and individual behaviors as well as anthropometric profiles(e.g., body mass index category) has not been fully clarified in large-scale epidemiological studies among China.

Given the importance of MSA and the undiscovered influential factors, this study analyzed data from the 2019 Shanghai Student Physical Fitness Index Survey to investigate school-aged student MSA participation in China. The study aims to (1) investigate the various factors related to MSA participation among school-aged children (9–17 years) in a major developed city in China, stratified by gender and grade level and (2) examine the associations between demographic(e.g., age, gender, grade level and family income), anthropometric(e.g., BMI), behavioral(e.g., MVPA), and sociocultural factors(e.g., support) with adherence to MSA guidelines, and to explore whether these associations differ by grade level.

Methods

Study design and participants

The data for the current study were derived from the 2019 Shanghai Student Physical Fitness Index Survey, conducted between November 21, 2019, and January 3, 2020, utilizing a multistage stratified cluster random sampling method to ensure representativeness across diverse student populations. Organized by the Shanghai Municipal Education Commission, the survey achieved full participation compliance from all targeted schools. Data collection was carried out through the Children and Youth Physical Fitness Questionnaire (Student/Parent Version), a validated instrument with established reliability and validity [17, 29]. This questionnaire has been widely adopted in prior research on youth physical activity and health outcomes [18, 28]. Data collection was conducted on-site at each participating school during designated physical education classes. A team of trained health professionals and postgraduate students in public health administered the questionnaires and conducted all anthropometric measurements according to a standardized protocol to ensure data quality and consistency. The study population included students from Grade 4 of elementary school to Grade 3 of secondary school (aged approximately 9–18 years) and their corresponding guardians, ensuring a comprehensive assessment of familial and individual factors influencing physical fitness behaviors.

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Shanghai University of Sport, and permission to conduct the study was obtained from the teachers and principals of the participating schools. All the participants, including students and their parents, were informed of aims, procedure, and the results announcement of the present study and signed a verbal consent before their participation. The final analysis included 81,264 students inserts from the total of 93,253 student samples. The data clean procedure is shown in Fig. 1 for details.

Procedures

The data collection process consisted of three main phases. Initially, basic demographic information (gender, age, grade, and student ID) was systematically recorded in a secure database, with each entry assigned a unique numerical identifier for data management purposes. Subsequently, students completed a comprehensive four-page questionnaire during class time, which assessed their physical activity patterns and collected additional demographic data. Concurrently, legal guardians (including but not limited to biological parents) completed a parallel four-page questionnaire at home, providing information regarding family characteristics and their support for the child’s physical activity, resulting in the exclusion of 14 unmatched student questionnaires from the final analysis. Throughout this process, trained research personnel administered the survey and provided necessary clarification to participants.

Measures

Demographic variables

Demographic characteristics were assessed through student self-reported questionnaires, capturing age, gender, and grade level. Family socioeconomic status was evaluated using parent-reported per capita annual income, categorized into four tiers: <9,000 RMB, 9,000–30,000 RMB, 30,001–100,000 RMB, and > 100,000 RMB, following established methodology [7].

Anthropometric measuring

Student height (accurate to 0.1 cm) was measured without shoes, and their body weight (accurate to 0.1 kg) was recorded. These measurements were taken using a portable device (GMCS-IV; Jianmin, Beijing, China). Each adolescent’s BMI was calculated using the formula: weight (kg) divided by height (m) squared. Weight status classification followed International Obesity Task Force criteria [8] dichotomized into ‘not overweight’ (including thin and healthy weight) and ‘overweight/obese’ (encompassing overweight, obese, and morbidly obese categories).

Behavioral variables

Parent and student participation in MSA was assessed using a validated item: “During the past week, on how many days, Monday through Sunday, did you participate in muscle-strengthening activities such as push-ups, sit-ups, or weightlifting?” Responses were recorded on a 0–7 day scale. Adherence to World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines was defined as ≥ 3 days per week for adolescents and ≥ 2 days per week for adults, consistent with age-specific recommendations [5].

Parent and student engagement in MVPA was assessed using two items: (1) In the past week, on how many days did you do at least 60 min of MVPA Monday through Friday? (Responses: 0 = none, 1 = 1 day, 2 = 2 days 3 = 3 days, 4 = 4 days, 5 = 5 days); 2) How many days in the past week did you do at least 60 min of MVPA on weekends? (Responses: 0 = none, 1 = 1 day, 2 = 2 days. MVPA was operationally defined as activities causing substantial increases in breathing and heart rate, encompassing physical education classes, sports training, and daily activities (e.g., hiking, cycling, brisk walking).

Sociocultural variables

Parental support was evaluated through a 9-item scale assessing various dimensions of support from the parental questionnaire. Peer support for students was evaluated using a 4-item scale assessing from the student questionnaire. The full list of questions is available in Appendix1.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 26.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). The analytical approach comprised three sequential stages. Initially, descriptive statistics were computed for all study variables, with subsequent stratification by gender (boys vs. girls) and educational level (elementary, junior middle, and junior high schools). Subsequently, bivariate associations between study variables were examined using Pearson correlation coefficients for normally distributed data and Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients for non-parametric data.

Comparative analyses were conducted using appropriate statistical tests: chi-square tests were employed to examine gender differences in meeting MSA recommendations, while one-way ANOVA was utilized to assess variations in MSA across different school years. Furthermore, logistic regression analyses were performed to evaluate the associations between meeting MSA recommendations and multiple predictors, including MVPA, demographic factors (gender, body mass index), and interpersonal factors (social support). These analyses were conducted separately for different grade groupings, with results expressed as odds ratios and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (OR, 95% CI).

Effect size interpretation followed established guidelines [10], with the following thresholds: correlation coefficients of 0.20, 0.50, and 0.80; R-squared values of 4%, 25%, and 64%; and odds ratios of 2.0 (or 0.50), 3.0 (or 0.33), and 4.0 (or 0.25) representing small, medium, and large effects, respectively. The significance level was predetermined at p < 0.05. for all statistical tests.

Results

Among the 81,264 participants, 40,924 (50.4%) were boys and 40,340 (49.6%) were girls. The distribution by grade level was as follows: 27,335 (33.6%) in elementary school (4th −6th graders), 35,269 (43.4%) in middle school (7th −9th graders), and 18,660 (23.0%) in high school (10th −12th graders). Analysis revealed that 32.2% of students engaged in MVPA daily during school days, compared to 23.6% on weekends. Regarding weight status, 44.2% of participants maintained a healthy weight range. The sample’s socioeconomic characteristics indicated that 24.8% of families reported an annual per capita income exceeding RMB 100,000. Discussion

This is the first study to investigate factors influencing adherence to MSA guidelines among Chinese school children across various grade levels. Initial analyses identified significant associations between MSA adherence and most demographic, behavioral, and sociocultural factors, with the notable exception of family income. Importantly, multivariable modeling revealed that the predictive factors displayed grade-specific patterns, suggesting that developmental stage moderates the determinants of MSA. These findings address critical gaps in adolescent health promotion literature by shedding light on how MSA behavioral evolve across transitions through secondary education, thereby enhancing our understanding of factors related to MSA participation among a large sample of Chinese youth.

Our results indicated that adherence to MSA guidelines declined progressively across educational stages: 55.8% of elementary students, 54.8% of middle school students, and 37.7% of high school students. Compared to international benchmarks, Shanghai’s high school students showed lower MSA adherence than U.S. (49.35%) [20] and Canadian (53.7%) [12]. Similarly, middle school rates (54.8%) lagged behind U.S. data (84.4%) [21]. While cross-national elementary school data remain scarce, Shanghai’s elementary students exhibited the highest MSA adherence rate (55.8%) across all grades, suggesting potential cultural or structural differences in early exercise engagement. This decline likely reflects systemic challenges within China’s education system, including academic overload (reducing time for physical activity), lack of parental/peer support for exercise, and underdeveloped fitness habits. Furthermore, China’s cultural prioritization of academic success may devalue physical activity, necessitating policy interventions that align educational goals with health promotion (e.g., incorporating MSA into school curricula).

A key finding of our study was emergence of a significant grade-related gender disparity in MSA adherence. While boys demonstrated progressively higher compliance rates than girls, this gap became a significant factor only in junior middle and high school. This finding aligns with similar observations in Western youth [2, 11, 12, 24] and suggests that adolescence is a critical period for these disparities to develop. We propose that this adolescent-specific gender gap, which contrasts with findings in our elementary school students and in some adult populations [3], is sensitive to developmental stages. Early biological maturation in girls has been associated with reduced physical activity, potentially mediated by negative self-concept [14]. Notably, BMI z-score showed a modest yet significant association with gender, with healthy-weight students having 39–48% higher odds of meeting MSA guidelines in secondary education. This suggests that body image perceptions may mediate gender-based participation disparities. These findings have direct implications for interventions. Programs should adopt developmentally tailored, gender-sensitive strategies that address both biological and sociocultural factors. For adolescent girls, specifically, interventions could integrate body-positive curricula (e.g., workshops on healthy body image and media literacy) to counteract negative self-perceptions linked to early maturation. Furthermore, schools and sports clubs should prioritize gender-sensitive physical education policies, such as offering a diverse range of activity choices alongside traditional sports and ensuring safe, non-competitive environments for all students.

Our finding show a significant association between MVPA and MSA adherence which was consistent with prior studies [24, 25, 28]. There was only a weak to moderate correlation between MSA and schooldays MVPA in elementary, middle, and high school, as well as MSA with weekend MVPA. Our results, which indicate a stronger association with school-day MVPA in elementary and high school cohorts, underscore the effective role of the school environment in promoting physical activities [16]. These insights suggest that to increase MSA, interventions should not only target MSA directly but also promote overall MVPA, particularly through school-based programs [24]. To improve measurement accuracy in the future, device-based methods (e.g., accelerometers) could be used to better distinguish between MVPA and MSA components, thereby refining our understanding of this relationship.

In addition, this study provides new evidence on the distinct roles of social support in promoting MSA at different developmental stages. While existing literature confirms a positive correlation between social support and MSA [28], our study extends this understanding by revealing how the relative importance of parental versus peer support varies with age. Specifically, the relative importance of parental versus peer support varies significantly between educational phases. During elementary school years, peer support emerges as the predominant factor influencing MSA engagement, supports the view that “peer influences were more important than parental support in total physical activity [15]”. In contrast, secondary school students demonstrate responsiveness to both parental and peer support systems. Furthermore, our analysis also highlights a significant intergenerational effect, where a parent’s MSA adherence is a strong predictor of their child’s adherence, particularly during the elementary school years. This intergenerational transmission of physical activity behaviors can be theoretically explained through mechanisms of role modeling, observational learning, and value internalization [4]. This suggests that role modeling and active parental participation are more influential than verbal encouragement alone. This finding underscores the importance of implementing multi-level, family-centered intervention strategies. Practical recommendations include: (1) enhancing home-based MSA resources through provision of basic equipment (e.g., resistance bands, dumbbells); (2) improving community accessibility to fitness facilities; and (3) developing joint parent-adolescent physical activity programs. These strategies can help optimize MSA engagement across different developmental stages and promote long-term health.

Study limitations and strengths

A key strength of this study is its large, representative sample spanning a wide age range, which enabled robust tracking of MSA prevalence trends across developmental stages. To our knowledge, this is among the first studies to analyze associations between MSA adherence, demographic/interpersonal factors, and grade-specific trends, providing robust evidence for stage-targeted interventions. Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the regional focus on Shanghai limits generalizability to similar urban contexts [6, 17]. Second, the cross-sectional design of the study is its primary limitation, as it prevents us from establishing causal relationships between the identified factors and MSA adherence. Although we found strong associations, we cannot definitively conclude that peer support, for example, directly causes higher MSA participation; the relationship may be bidirectional. Future research using this same cohort will enable us to track changes in MSA behavior over time and, crucially, to assess causality and the long-term impact of these variables, which will be essential for designing timely and effective interventions. Third, the use of a single proxy variable for SES is a limitation and emphasize the need for future research to employ more comprehensive measures (e.g., an index combining income, parental education, and occupation). Furthermore, while self-reported MSA assessments align with global surveillance standards [12, 20, 22, 24], their subjective nature and low validity in adolescents weaken causal conclusions [24, 28]. Existing tools inadequately measure critical MSA dimensions—including intensity (e.g., % 1RM), temporal patterns (e.g., session duration), and modality (e.g., equipment type)—necessary for evaluating health outcome associations.

Practical implications

While this study advances insights into MSA adherence, future research should prioritize developmental-stage-specific intervention frameworks to address grade-level disparities. In elementary school, MSA interventions may be universally applicable, as gender and BMI differences minimally influence participation at this developmental stage. However, gender disparities emerge in adolescence, with girls increasingly perceiving MSA as male-dominated—a trend linked to gendered self-efficacy and societal norms [19]. Therefore, targeted strategies should prioritize (1) gender-inclusive MSA programming to counter stereotypes in middle school, (2) tailored BMI-category initiatives (e.g., resistance training for underweight students, joint-loading modifications for obese youth), and (3) peer-led advocacy to normalize MSA across body types. Across all grade levels, peer support systems—such as buddy training or group challenges—should be leveraged to sustain MSA engagement.

Conclusion

This study addresses a critical gap in public health research by examining MSA adherence across educational stages, a topic previously underexplored in youth populations. Key correlates of MSA adherence included total MVPA, parental MSA modeling, and peer/parental support across all grades, while gender and BMI emerged as significant predictors exclusively in middle school cohorts. Future studies should employ longitudinal or experimental designs to establish causal pathways between identified correlates and MSA adherence. Finally, interdisciplinary investigations are needed to identify novel determinants, as the current three model explained just over half the variance in MSA guideline recommended.
