Prevalence and factors associated with malaria amongst under-five children in Senga Hill District, Northern Province, Zambia, a community-based cross-sectional study
Authors: Arthur shaba-santu, Lavel Moonga, Shoheil Ogata, Kyoko Hayashida, Martin Chitolongo Simuunza
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionMalaria is an urgent and high-priority public health issue. The persistent burden of malaria among under-five children in Zambia, with particular focus on Senga Hill District, remains a huge Challenge. This population subgroup continues to carry a disproportionate share of malaria morbidity and mortality, and therefore, research that identifies local prevalence and determinants of infection is highly relevant for malaria control and elimination strategies.MethodsA community-based cross-sectional study was conducted among under-five children in Senga Hill District from November 2022 to August 2023. A total of 216 under-five children were subjected to a Care Start™ Malaria Rapid Diagnostic Test, which can detect histidine-rich protein 2 of Plasmodium falciparum, and Plasmodium lactate dehydrogenase of P. vivax was used to diagnose malaria. At the same time, a structured questionnaire was administered to the parents or guardians to collect data on hypothesised risk factors for the disease. Bivariate analysis and binary logistic regression analysis was used to identify risk factors associated with malaria.Results and ConclusionIn this study, the overall malaria prevalence was 31% (95% CI = 26, 38%). Subsequent data analysis revealed that correct hanging of an ITN reduced the odds of being positive for malaria by 88% (OR = 0.012, 95% CI 0.00.05, 0.304). Having two insecticide treated nests (ITNs) and indoor residual spraying (IRS) done in the last spraying season reduced odds of being malaria positive by 86% (OR = 0.0.14, 95% CI 0.040, 0.510) and 58% (OR = 0.42, 95% CI 0.21, 0.85), respectively. Further, being a male child had increased odds of being positive by 204% (OR = 3.04, 95% CI 1.52, 6.07). These results show that the prevalence of malaria among the under-five children in Senga Hill was high. Thus, all concerned bodies, including the community should strengthen ownership and encourage correct use of ITNs and acceptance of IRS service acceptance to stop the transmission of the malaria.Keywords: Prevalence, Malaria, Risk factors, Under-five children, Mosquitoes, Zambia

Introduction

Under-five children are the most susceptible to malaria and make up 61% of all deaths from the disease globally [1]. According to data from the World Malaria Report, 2020 [1], nearly every minute, a child under 5 years old dies of malaria. Many of these deaths are preventable and treatable. In 2020, there were 249 million malaria cases globally that led to 608,000 deaths in total. Of these deaths, 76% were children under 5 years of age. Children who survive malaria may experience long-term effects from the illness [2]. Frequent bouts of fever and sickness limit play, social engagement, and educational opportunities, suppress appetite, and ultimately hinder growth [3]. Poorer households are known to have higher rates of child mortality, with malaria accounting for a significant share of these deaths [3].

In resource-limited environments, like those found in most African countries and Zambia in particular, malaria is a significant contributor to poverty [2]. Some population groups are at considerably higher risk of contracting malaria and developing severe disease, including infants, mobile populations, and travellers [3]. Furthermore, new-borns, under-five children, pregnant women, and HIV/AIDS patients are significantly more likely to get malaria and develop severe illness [3]. Malaria has an impact not only on health status but also on homes and daily life. Severe episodes of the disease can cause modest developmental and cognitive abnormalities in children as well as long-term neurological sequelae [4, 5]. Families may also suffer significant financial implications [6].

Malaria prevention and control in Zambia commenced in 1952. Since then great progress has been achieved. However, malaria still kills more under-five children than any other diseases. It affects more than 4 million Zambians annually [1], causing 30% of outpatient visits resulting into about 8000 deaths each year [1].

Risk is highest in the wetter, rural, impoverished areas of Luapula, Northern, Muchinga, North Western, Western, Copperbelt, and Eastern provinces [8]. According to the Zambia Demographic Health Survey (ZDHS) data, malaria is one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality in the Sanga Hill District, affecting both adults and children under five [7]. The incidence risk for all ages in the three years 2018, 2019, and 2020 was 405, 478, and 439/1000 population, respectively [7]. The incidence risk for the under-five children rose over the same period from 823/1000 population in 2018 to 860/1000 population in 2019 and 853/1000 in 2020 [7]. Hence, knowing the prevalence of malaria and its associated risk factors is of paramount importance in designing and scaling up appropriate intervention programs. Currently, there is scarcity of information as no such studies have been previously done in the study area (Senga Hill District) [8].

Materials and methods

Study area

Senga Hill District is one of the twelve administrative districts of Northern Province, of Zambia. The district is approximately 175 kms from Kasama, the provincial headquarters. It has a population of about 126,308 people, with a population growth of 2.8 per annum and an estimated 27, 470 under-five children [9]. The district is divided into 31 Catchment areas with Mambwe Mission being the central catchment area. The study was conducted in four catchment areas (community level) of the study district, namely Senga and Nondo (peri-urban catchment areas), Mambwe and Mpande (rural catchment areas). The four targeted catchment areas were selected using simple random sampling.

Research design and sampling

This study was cross-sectional in design. Under five children who had resided in the area for more than 6 months, and their parents/guardians consented, were included in the study.

The sample size was estimated at 216 participants using the single population proportion formula of Dobson formula [10]. The assumptions were a malaria prevalence for Senga Hill of 85% (Based on RDT report in HMIS) [11], a 95% confidence level, a 5% margin of error and 10% non-response rate. The number of under-five children in each catchment area was proportionally allocated to each catchment area. (Table 1).

Data on under five population was obtained from the health care clinic, and then research data was gathered at the community level in the four respective catchment areas of the District, using a simple random sampling technique to select the study participants.

A questionnaire was used to obtain data from guardians or parents following consent. Information on socio-demographic characteristics, insecticide-treated nets (ITN) condition, availability and utilization, indoor residual spraying (IRS) coverage, presence of stagnant water within 500 m of household, child outdoor stay beyond 19:00 h Central African Time, housing condition, and travel outside the district and health information about malaria risk was collected. The questionnaire was pre-tested to determine the quality of data to be collected. The pre-test was done at Chikunta Catchment area (not part of the study sites), which has similar population characteristics to the targeted catchment areas of the study.

A blood sample was aseptically collected into a capillary tube from each child regardless of malaria signs and symptoms. The blood sample test was done by a medical laboratory professional (laboratory technologists). The blood sample were tested immediately using Care Start™ Malaria RDT (malaria parasitological test) which detect histidine-rich protein 2 (HRP2) of Plasmodium falciparum and plasmodium lactate dehydrogenase (pLDH) of Plasmodium vivax according to the manufacturer’s instructions [12].

Data quality assurance

For data quality assurance, field assistants (data collectors) received training to ensure that they shared a consistent knowledge of how to gather the necessary demographic data. Secondly, the laboratory technologists collected blood samples and performed RDTs. A discussion was held regarding the application of standard operating diagnostic procedures. The team members cross-checked each filled questionnaire to ensure that it was accurate, consistent, and comprehensive. Where necessary, corrective action was performed (not clear writing, heavy rubbing, figures written outside the answer box were all addressed). Additionally, basic codes were used to organize/mark all of the sampled houses.

Data analysis

Data was entered into MS Excel [13], then transferred to SPSS-V20 software for analysis [14]. Frequencies, proportions, and summary statistics were then generated for each variable. Chi-Square (χ2) test was used to determine the association between categorical variables. All variables that had p-values less than or equal to 0.250 in the bivariate analysis were included in the binary logistic regression model (to control the potential confounders) using a backward Stepwise (Likelihood Ratio) method. The omnibus test for model coefficients and the Hosmer and Lemeshow test was used to assess whether the model-fit the data. All statistics were considered significant at p ≤ 0.050.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics of the study population

A total of 216 under-five children were enrolled in the study (Table 2). Slightly more than half (55%) of the study participants were female with a mean age of 2.38 months. Children were relatively evenly distributed across age categories, with 27.3% aged < 12 months, 27.3% aged 12–23 months, 25.5% aged 24–35 months, and 19.9% aged 36–47 months (Table 2). The largest proportion of participants resided in Senga catchment (40.3%), followed by Mambwe (20.4%), Nondo (20.4%), and Mpande (19.0%).

Most households (40.7%) had only one under-five child. Nearly half of households reported having two sleeping spaces (48.1%), while 45.4% reported having three or more sleeping spaces. Regarding mother or caregiver characteristics, the majority were married (84.7%), had up to primary education (59.3%), and reported receiving a monthly income below K200 (47.7%), indicating generally low socioeconomic status. Knowledge of malaria transmission was high, with 81.0% of caregivers correctly identifying mosquitoes as the vector for malaria transmission. In addition, 73.1% of caregivers reported seeking treatment for febrile illness within 24 h of onset of symptoms, reflecting relatively good health-seeking behaviour at the household level, whilst about 19% of caregivers still had misconceptions of the mode of malaria transmission (Table 2).

Prevalence of malaria among under-five children

The overall prevalence of malaria among the under-five children in Senga Hill District was 31.9% (95% CI = 25.78, 38.61), indicating a substantial burden of infection. Malaria prevalence varied across age groups. Children in the age group 12–23 months had the highest prevalence of malaria, followed by the age group < 12 months, while those aged 24 to 35 months had the lowest malaria prevalence (Table 3). This age gradient, though not statistically significant, suggests increased vulnerability during infancy and early childhood, a critical period for targeted malaria prevention and case management.

Bivariate analysis identified several factors significantly associated with malaria infection, among them, the male gender was strongly associated with a positive malaria RDT test result (χ2 p = < 0.001). Further, preventive interventions demonstrated a malaria protective effect: children from households that reported correct use of correctly hung insecticide-treated nets (ITNs) had significantly lower malaria prevalence compared to those with incorrect ITN use (χ2, p < 0.001). Similarly, households that reported receiving indoor residual spraying (IRS) in the previous transmission season had significantly lower malaria prevalence (χ2, p = 0.018). In contrast, the age category of the child, number of sleeping spaces per household, and educational status of the caregiver were not significantly associated with malaria prevalence (p > 0.050). Similarly, environmental and behavioural factors, including a child staying outdoors after 19:00 h, and overgrown vegetation within 500 m of the household, showed no statistically significant association with malaria prevalence (p > 0.050) (Table 3). Furthermore, caregiver knowledge of malaria transmission and prevention, as well as cultural or community-related factors influencing health-seeking behaviour, were not significantly associated with malaria prevalence (p > 0.050), suggesting that knowledge alone may be insufficient to reduce infection risk without effective implementation of preventive measures.

Risk factors of under-five children being positive for malaria.

The Omnibus Test for Model Coefficient was significant (P < 0.001) and the Hosmer and Lemeshow test was not significant (p = 0.853), indicating that the model adequately fit the data. Four variables were found to be significant predicators of malaria in under-five children in Senga Hill District, when the effects of all the other variables were taken care of. These were correct use of ITNs and having IRS services for mosquitoes in the previous spraying season were independently associated with reduced odds of malaria infection, underscoring the effectiveness of vector control interventions when properly implemented (Table 4). Conversely, male gender was associated with increased odds of malaria infection compared to female under-five children. Interestingly, households with two ITN were associated with reduced odds of malaria infection compared to households with no ITN. Other variables were not significant predictors of malaria infection in under-five children in Senga Hill district.

Discussion

Malaria is a serious public health challenge in tropical and subtropical regions, including Zambia, where transmission persists despite sustained implementation of vector control interventions. It is important that the drivers of the disease at the local level are understood to optimize the implementation of preventive and control strategies. This study assessed the prevalence of malaria and identified key risk factors among under-five children in Senga Hill District, providing evidence to inform district-level malaria control planning. Among the 216 children sampled in this study, the study observed malaria a high prevalence among the under-five children, indicating a substantial burden of infection in the study area. Although this prevalence was lower than that reported in neighbouring districts such as Chiengi (48.1%) and Puta (61.3%) in Luapula Province, Zambia [15], the disease burden remains epidemiologically significant. Differences in prevalence could be attributed to variation in study design (community-based versus facility-based), ecological conditions, and local implementation intensity of malaria control interventions [16–18]. The current prevalence, however, was higher than that reported from Choma district, Southern Province, Zambia, that ranged from 2.1% to 3.9% [19], further highlighting substantial geographical heterogeneity in malaria burden within Zambia, underscoring the need for risk-stratified interventions rather than uniform national approaches.

Consistent with prior evidence, the risk of malaria infection was higher among households without ITNs. This finding is consistent with earlier studies in Zimbabwe [20] and Ethiopia [21], reinforcing the protective role of ITNs when available and used. Despite ITN usage being largely dependent on access [22], household dynamics and intra-household allocation of ITNs influence utilisation patterns, particularly in resource-constrained settings. This demonstrated increased odds of ITN usage among children from households with good ITN supply compared to those with limited ITN supplies. Cultural norms that prioritise adult household heads for ITN use may inadvertently increase exposure risk among young children, despite their biological vulnerability [23].

In this study, households that reported having two ITN had reduced odds of malaria in under-five children compared to those whose households reported not having an ITN. Results are consistent with studies, including a cohort study from Kenya in which ITN use was associated with a 44% relative reduction among under-five children [24] and a study from Tanzania in which the estimated protective efficacy of ITN use was 27% among children under 5 years old [25]. Nevertheless, the percentage of children using ITNs was still below the World Health Assembly [26] and the Ministry of Health (Zambia) target of 80% for an acceptable level of protection. Low rates of bed net usage reported by communities in the tropics are attributed primarily to lack of sufficient nets to cover all household members [27] but also to heat discomfort associated with poor airflow caused by bed nets [28]. Further, the utilisation of ITN per household in countries like Zambia and Senga Hill in particular, is crucial for malaria control. Despite the universal access policy leading to high ITN ownership, the effectiveness of ITNs in reducing malaria cases is influenced by various factors, including household size (Larger households may have more ITNs, but still fail to ensure adequate protection for all members, particularly under-five children [41].

Households that reported incorrect use (Hanging of ITNs) and/or use of worn-out ITNs had an increased odds of having a child being positive for malaria compared to households reported correct use or not worn-out ITN. These results are consistent with recent findings from other studies (western Kenya) that have reported lack of protective effect for ITN use and coverage that could be attributed to the state and conditions of the ITNs [29]. This result contrasts with a research conducted in Uganda, which found that odd of being malaria positive were 1.33 times higher in children who had used an ITN had than those of children who did not use one [30]. However, the findings in our study are in line with earlier research conducted in the East Shewa zone of the Oromiya regional state and southern Ethiopia [31, 32], that showed that, households that reported incorrect use and/or use of worn-out ITNs had an increased odds of being positive for malaria amongst under-five children. Additionally, this is consistent with existing literature on ITNs reducing malaria transmission. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis of 11 studies in Ethiopia found that the use of ITNs was associated with lower odds of malaria [33]. Based on the findings, targeted interventions such as correct hanging and utilisation of ITNs should be prioritised among under-five children. It is also important to draw lessons from successful experiences in other regions, such as China [35]. By studying and adapting successful malaria control programs, sub-Saharan countries like Zambia (Senga Hill District in particular) can enhance their efforts to combat malaria effectively by developing a community-based program that involves a multifaceted approach.

Indoor residual spraying demonstrated in the last 12 months had significant decreased odds of having a malaria positive under-five child compared to those whose households that did not receive the service within the same period, consistent with previous studies done in Nchelenge district, Northern Zambia [36] and a secondary analysis of Zambia Malaria Indicator Survey 2018 (MIS) [36]. The odds of contacting malaria for children who slept in households that have been sprayed with an insecticide in the last 6 months were 58% less when compared to those that slept in non-treated households [37]. This finding supports similar evidence presented by Loha et al. [37] that IRS significantly reduced the incidence of falciparum malaria. Combating malaria based on IRS is associated with the willingness of households to accept the spraying of residual insecticides during the spraying campaigns [37] [42]. Chemicals used in indoor residual sprays kill and repel mosquitoes. As a result, the residents of the home are shielded from mosquito bites and the risk of contracting malaria. This is in line with a Ugandan study that found indoor residual spray to be protective and that children who lived in untreated homes were more likely to have malaria [38].

In this study, gender was independently associated with increased malaria prevalence, with male children having a high odds of being positive for malaria compared to female children. The annual incidence of malaria in Zambia for males was estimated to be 447 cases per 1000 population, while for females this was 413 cases per 1000 population in the year 2020 [39]. The malaria inpatient case fatality rate (CFR) reported among children was at 7.2/1000 population and 6.0/1000 population for male and female children, respectively [39] [40]. Therefore, the results from our study are consistent with the statistics presented in these reports. These results are further consistent with earlier research conducted in Nsanje District in Malawi, which also showed that there were more malaria cases in males (51.4%) than in females (48.6%) [40]. However, another study in Zambia, specifically on health-seeking behaviour, did not find any significant differences between boys and girls for common childhood illnesses [41]. A potential explanation could be the differences in population proportions, with the female gender having a higher proportion compared to males [42], and that females are more likely to use ITNs than males.

Conclusion

This study collectively revealed that malaria among under-five children in Senga Hill District remains high (31.94%.) and is strongly influenced by sex-specific risk patterns and potential ineffective utilisation of vector control interventions, rather than caregiver level of education. The utilisation factors identified were non-availability of ITNs, incorrect and inconsistent use of ITNs, and inadequate coverage of IRS services. These findings highlights the need for strengthening community uptake, behavioural compliance and correct utilisation of ITNs. Sustainable IRS coverage remains crucial, suggesting the need for programmatic audits of ITN distribution, use patterns, and household targeting strategies. It is therefore urgently required to reinforce integrated vector control strategies and to tailor interventions to household dynamics in Zambia’s high transmission districts.
