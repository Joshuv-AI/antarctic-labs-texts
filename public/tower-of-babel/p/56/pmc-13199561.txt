A PIK3CA and NF1 expression-based prognostic signature derived from Mendelian randomization identifies causal immune-regulatory determinants of glioblastoma outcomes
Authors: Qinbiao Chen, Min Luo, Wenjing Lin, Linlin Lu, Cheng Zhong, Anmin Liu, Lin Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe causal roles of PIK3CA and NF1 expression in glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) pathogenesis and their immune-regulatory functions remain poorly understood. We developed and validated an expression-based prognostic signature to predict survival and assess immunotherapy-related biomarker potential in GBM patients.MethodsWe employed two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis using genome-wide association study data to establish causal relationships between genetically predicted PIK3CA and NF1 expression levels and GBM risk. Whole-exome sequencing was performed on GBM tumor samples to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs, FDR < 0.05) associated with PIK3CA and NF1 alterations. A 6-gene expression-based prognostic signature comprising PIK3CA, NF1, PGPEP1, COL5A1, MFGE8, and MSH6 was constructed using multivariate Cox regression in The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) GBM cohort and validated across independent Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) datasets. As proof-of-concept for the immune-regulatory basis of the signature, we evaluated its trans-tumor applicability in predicting immunotherapy response using the IMvigor210 cohort.ResultsMR analysis demonstrated that genetically predicted higher PIK3CA expression in normal tissues was causally associated with decreased GBM risk (IVW OR = 0.176, 95% CI 0.056–0.556, P = 0.003), while NF1 showed a suggestive protective trend. Notably, higher NF1 expression also correlated with increased ankylosing spondylitis risk (OR = 0.999, P = 0.042), revealing pleiotropic immunomodulatory functions that validate its immune-regulatory role independent of somatic tumor mutations. The 6-gene expression signature effectively stratified patients into high-risk and low-risk groups with significant survival differences across all validation cohorts (TCGA: log-rank P = 0.037; GEO cohorts: P = 0.012–0.041). The signature demonstrated robust discriminative ability (C-index: 0.71 in TCGA training set, 0.64–0.66 in GEO validation sets) and time-dependent predictive performance, significantly outperforming clinical variables including age (AUC = 0.668), gender (AUC = 0.321), and tumor grade (AUC = 0.500). In the IMvigor210 cohort, the expression signature showed trans-tumor applicability for immune checkpoint blockade response prediction (ROC AUC = 0.566, 95% CI 0.487–0.645), with low-risk patients demonstrating superior progression-free survival compared to high-risk patients (P = 0.016), providing proof-of-concept evidence for the immune-regulatory biological basis of the signature.ConclusionsOur integrated MR and multi-omics approach establishes PIK3CA and NF1 at the germline expression level as causal determinants of GBM risk at the germline expression level through immune-regulatory mechanisms, distinct from their somatic mutation effects in established tumors. We identified a clinically robust 6-gene expression signature that integrates complementary biological dimensions—oncogenic pathways, extracellular matrix remodeling, immune regulation, and genomic instability—for prognostic stratification in GBM. While the trans-tumor immunotherapy prediction requires GBM-specific validation, our findings provide a foundation for precision medicine approaches and hypothesis-driven biomarker development in GBM.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-026-04658-1.

Introduction

Glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) is the most malignant primary brain cancer, accounting for approximately 45% of all primary malignant brain tumors [1, 2]. Despite aggressive multimodal treatment including maximal surgical resection followed by concurrent chemoradiotherapy with temozolomide, patient outcomes remain dismal, with a median overall survival of only 15 months and a 5-year survival rate below 5% [3–5]. These poor outcomes persist despite decades of research, highlighting an urgent need for novel therapeutic strategies grounded in deeper understanding of GBM pathogenesis.

The limited success of conventional therapies is partly attributable to the complex tumor microenvironment (TME), which comprises stromal cells, immune cells, and extracellular matrix components [6–8]. The TME promotes tumor heterogeneity and phenotypic plasticity while creating an immunosuppressive milieu that enables immune evasion through programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression and recruitment of regulatory immune cells [9–12]. This immunosuppressive character has positioned immunotherapy, particularly immune checkpoint blockade (ICB), as a promising avenue. However, clinical trials have yielded disappointing results, with response rates below 10% [13–15]. This therapeutic resistance underscores the necessity of identifying predictive biomarkers to stratify patients and understand molecular determinants of immunotherapy response.

To identify genetic factors influencing GBM susceptibility and therapeutic response, we employed two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis. MR uses genetic variants as instrumental variables to infer causal relationships between exposures (e.g., gene expression) and outcomes (e.g., disease risk), minimizing confounding and reverse causation [16–18]. By leveraging genome-wide association study (GWAS) data and inverse variance weighted (IVW) analysis, MR enables robust causal inference regarding genetically determined expression levels and disease risk [19, 20]. Critically, gene expression levels must be distinguished from genetic alterations when interpreting MR findings. Expression refers to transcriptional activity from intact, functional genes, while genetic alterations (mutations, deletions, amplifications) cause loss or gain of function. For tumor suppressors, elevated expression of functional protein confers protection, whereas loss-of-function mutations eliminate this effect [21]. For signaling molecules, oncogenic potential typically stems from activating mutations rather than expression levels per se [22]. MR specifically examines how genetically determined expression influences disease risk—conceptually distinct from how somatic mutations drive tumor progression. Furthermore, MR reveals pleiotropic effects where genes influence multiple phenotypes through shared biological pathways [23, 24].

Our preliminary MR analysis identified PIK3CA (Phosphatidylinositol-4,5-bisphosphate 3-kinase catalytic subunit alpha) and NF1 (Neurofibromin 1) with significant causal associations with GBM risk based on germline expression levels. Genetically determined higher expression of both genes was associated with decreased GBM risk (IVW P < 0.05), suggesting protective roles operating through constitutional immune-regulatory mechanisms. The selection of these genes as anchor points for signature development is supported by five convergent lines of evidence: (1) MR causal evidence establishing germline expression effects on GBM risk; (2) pathway centrality—PIK3CA regulates PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling (altered in > 80% of GBM), while NF1 regulates RAS/MAPK pathways (altered in > 70% [25–28]); (3) therapeutic targeting potential with existing inhibitors; (4) immune relevance, as both genes influence TME composition and immune cell function [29–32]; and (5) clinical prevalence of alterations (~ 15% for PIK3CA, ~ 18–24% for NF1) [33].

While previous studies documented PIK3CA and NF1 alterations in GBM tumors, critical knowledge gaps remain: (1) whether these genes causally influence GBM risk through germline variants, (2) how to reconcile protective expression effects with oncogenic mutation effects, and (3) whether they predict immunotherapy response. PIK3CA encodes the p110α catalytic subunit of PI3K, a key regulator of cell growth, survival, and metabolism [25, 26]. In the germline context captured by MR, elevated PIK3CA expression in normal brain tissue and immune cells enhances anti-tumor immune surveillance through T-cell metabolic reprogramming and NK cell activation [29, 30, 33]. These represent different cellular compartments and functional outcomes: germline expression supports immune defense, while somatic mutations drive malignancy. NF1 encodes neurofibromin, a GTPase-activating protein inhibiting RAS signaling [27]. Higher NF1 expression maintains tumor suppression and regulates microglial phenotypes toward anti-tumor states [31, 32], whereas somatic loss-of-function mutations and deletions (10–15% and 8–9% of GBM, respectively) lead to constitutive RAS/MAPK activation, tumor progression, and altered TME characteristics associated with differential immunotherapy responses [28, 34, 35].

Intriguingly, MR analysis also revealed positive associations between PIK3CA and NF1 expression and ankylosing spondylitis (AS) risk, a chronic inflammatory arthritis characterized by aberrant immune activation [36, 37]. This pleiotropy—wherein the same genetic variants affecting expression influence both decreased cancer risk and increased autoimmune disease risk—provides crucial biological validation. It demonstrates that PIK3CA and NF1 regulate fundamental immunomodulatory pathways: enhanced immune surveillance protects against malignancy but can predispose to autoimmune inflammation when dysregulated [38, 39]. This bidirectional immunological effect, operating independently of any tumor-specific somatic alterations, strengthens the rationale for investigating these genes as immune-regulatory biomarkers for immunotherapy response prediction.

Building upon these MR findings, we designed an integrative multi-omics study with three objectives: (1) identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) associated with PIK3CA and NF1 alterations through whole-exome sequencing of 28 GBM tumors; (2) construct a 6-gene expression-based prognostic signature through systematic bioinformatic screening incorporating protein–protein interaction networks, survival associations, and functional immune-regulatory relevance; and (3) validate the signature's prognostic performance and assess its potential as an immune-regulatory biomarker across independent cohorts. Through differential expression analysis (n = 487 genes, FDR < 0.05), STRING database protein interaction filtering (confidence > 0.4), univariate Cox regression screening (P < 0.20 to avoid premature exclusion of genes with conditional effects), and Gene Ontology/KEGG pathway enrichment prioritizing immune-related functions, we identified four additional genes complementing PIK3CA and NF1: PGPEP1 (Pyroglutamyl-peptidase I, involved in neuropeptide processing and neuroinflammation) [40], COL5A1 (Collagen(Collagen Type V Alpha 1, mediating extracellular matrix remodeling and tumor stiffness) [41], MFGE8 (Milk Fat Globule-EGF Factor 8, regulating efferocytosis and macrophage polarization) [42], and MSH6 (MutS Homolog 6, a DNA mismatch repair gene linked to microsatellite instability and neoantigen generation) [43]. These six genes capture complementary biological dimensions—oncogenic pathway dysregulation (PIK3CA, NF1), extracellular matrix remodeling (COL5A1), immune suppression (MFGE8), genomic instability (MSH6), and metabolic regulation (PGPEP1)—forming an integrated expression signature that reflects both inherited susceptibility and tumor-specific microenvironmental states.

We validated the signature's prognostic performance using The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) GBM cohort (n = 142 IDH-wildtype samples aligned with WHO CNS5 classification) and independent Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) datasets (GSE16011, n = 159; GSE13041, n = 191; GSE4290, n = 180) representing diverse platforms and institutions. For assessing the immune-regulatory biological basis of the signature, we evaluated its trans-tumor applicability in predicting immune checkpoint blockade response using the IMvigor210 cohort—a well-characterized urothelial carcinoma dataset (n = 348 patients) treated with atezolizumab (anti-PD-L1 therapy) [44]. We emphasize this represents a proof-of-concept analysis rather than direct clinical validation for GBM immunotherapy. The rationale is threefold: (1) absence of publicly available large-scale GBM ICB datasets with comprehensive RNA-seq and response annotation at study initiation; (2) the signature's foundation in conserved immune-regulatory genes (PI3K-AKT, RAS-MAPK, efferocytosis, mismatch repair) whose functions are mechanistically preserved across tumor types [45, 46]; and (3) precedent from pan-cancer immune signature studies demonstrating predictive value across diverse malignancies [47, 48]. We acknowledge GBM's profoundly immunosuppressive “cold” tumor characteristics differ substantially from peripheral solid tumors; thus, this cross-cancer analysis validates mechanistic immune-regulatory principles rather than establishing clinical applicability for GBM ICB therapy, which requires future GBM-specific prospective validation.

The study aims to: (1) establish PIK3CA and NF1 as causal determinants of GBM risk through germline expression-mediated immune-regulatory mechanisms, distinct from their somatic mutation roles in tumor progression; (2) develop a clinically robust expression-based prognostic signature integrating complementary biological dimensions; and (3) provide mechanistic insights into immune-regulatory biomarkers with potential relevance for precision medicine and future immunotherapy trial design. All data sources, including TCGA-GBM (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/), GEO datasets (GSE16011, GSE13041, GSE4290), IMvigor210 cohort (http://research-pub.gene.com/IMvigor210CoreBiologies/), GWAS summary statistics (MRC IEU OpenGWAS), and protein expression data (Human Protein Atlas), are publicly available as detailed in the Data Availability section.

Materials and methods

Mendelian randomization analysis

Data sources

Summary statistics for PIK3CA and NF1 expression (exposures) and GBM disease (outcome) were obtained from published genome-wide association studies (GWAS) in predominantly European populations (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/). GBM GWAS data comprised the Glioma International Case–Control Study (GICC) combined with UK Biobank glioma GWAS (12,496 cases, 18,190 controls) [49]. Ankylosing spondylitis (AS) GWAS data were from the International Genetics of Ankylosing Spondylitis Consortium (IGAS).

Instrumental variable selection

Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) associated with PIK3CA and NF1 expression at genome-wide significance (P < 5 × 10⁻⁸) were selected as instrumental variables (IVs). SNPs were pruned for linkage disequilibrium (LD r[2] < 0.01) using 1000 Genomes European reference panel. IV strength was assessed using F-statistics, with F > 10 indicating sufficient strength to minimize weak instrument bias. Statistical power was calculated at 80% with α = 0.05.

MR methods

The inverse variance weighted (IVW) method was used as the primary analysis. When Cochran’s Q statistic indicated no substantial heterogeneity (P > 0.05), a fixed-effects model was applied; otherwise, a random-effects model was used. Sensitivity analyses included: (1) weighted median method, valid when > 50% of IVs are valid and robust to outliers; (2) MR-Egger regression to detect directional pleiotropy through intercept testing; and (3) MR-PRESSO to identify and correct for horizontal pleiotropy.

Reverse MR analysis

To test for reverse causation, we performed reverse MR using GBM-associated SNPs (P < 5 × 10⁻⁶, n = 47 SNPs) as instruments with gene expression levels as outcomes. Statistical significance P < 0.05 was considered significant, with Bonferroni correction applied for multiple exposures when appropriate. Analyses were conducted using TwoSampleMR R package (version 0.5.6) [50] and MR-PRESSO (version 1.0) [51] in R software version 3.6.

Data acquisition and preprocessing

TCGA-GBM cohort

RNA-sequencing and clinical data for GBM patients were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). Of 156 primary GBM samples with RNA-seq data, 142 IDH-wildtype samples were retained to align with WHO CNS5 classification (2021) [2]. Clinical variables included overall survival, age, sex, MGMT promoter methylation status, and extent of resection. Data were downloaded in October 2023.

GEO validation cohorts

Independent validation datasets were obtained from Gene Expression Omnibus (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/): GSE16011 (n = 159, GPL570 platform) [52], GSE13041 (n = 191, GPL570 platform) [53], and GSE4290 (n = 180 glioma samples including 77 GBM, GPL96 platform) [54]. Raw expression data were normalized using Robust Multi-array Average (RMA) algorithm implemented in the affy R package. Batch effects across datasets were corrected using ComBat algorithm (sva R package).

IMvigor210 immunotherapy cohort

The IMvigor210 dataset comprises 348 urothelial cancer patients treated with atezolizumab (anti-PD-L1 therapy) with RNA-seq and clinical response data [44]. Data were accessed through Creative Commons license (http://research-pub.gene.com/IMvigor210CoreBiologies/). Treatment responses were classified according to RECIST v1.1 criteria: complete response (CR), partial response (PR), stable disease (SD), and progressive disease (PD).

Construction of 6-gene prognostic signature

Gene selection workflow include 1. MR-identified genes: PIK3CA and NF1 were prioritized based on causal associations with GBM risk from MR analysis. 2. Whole-exome sequencing: Fresh-frozen GBM tumor samples underwent whole-exome sequencing (Illumina NovaSeq 6000, mean depth 150 ×) with matched normal blood samples for somatic variant calling. 3. Differential expression analysis: DESeq2 identified genes differentially expressed between PIK3CA/NF1-altered versus wild-type tumors (n = 487 genes, FDR < 0.05). 4. Protein–protein interaction network: STRING database (confidence > 0.4) identified genes interacting with PIK3CA and NF1. 5. Survival association: Univariate Cox regression in TCGA-GBM cohort with P < 0.20 threshold for candidate genes. 6. Functional annotation: Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis prioritized genes with immune-related functions. 7. Final selection: Six genes (PIK3CA, NF1, PGPEP1, COL5A1, MFGE8, MSH6) were selected based on combined criteria.

Risk score calculation

Multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression was performed in TCGA-GBM training cohort. The risk score was calculated as: Risk Score = Σ (βi × Expi), where βi represents the Cox regression coefficient for gene i and Expi represents normalized expression level. Patients were stratified into high-risk (risk score > median) and low-risk (risk score ≤ median) groups.

Model validation

Prognostic performance was evaluated using: (1) Kaplan–Meier survival curves with log-rank tests; (2) time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves (timeROC R package); (3) concordance index (C-index) for discrimination; and (4) multivariate Cox regression adjusting for clinical covariates.

Genetic alteration analysis

Genetic alteration data including somatic mutations and copy number alterations (CNAs) for PIK3CA and NF1 were obtained from cBioPortal (https://www.cbioportal.org/). The Cancer Types Summary module provided alteration frequencies across tumor types. Mutation types, sites, and three-dimensional protein structures were visualized using the Mutations module. Survival outcomes (overall survival, disease-free survival, disease-specific survival) were compared between altered and unaltered cases using Kaplan–Meier analysis with log-rank tests via the Comparison module.

Gene set enrichment analysis

Differential biological functions and pathways between high-risk and low-risk groups were analyzed using Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) implemented in clusterProfiler R package. KEGG pathway enrichment was performed with enrichment scores calculated at FDR-adjusted P < 0.05 for statistical significance. Pathways with P < 0.01 were considered highly significant.

Protein expression validation

Immunohistochemical (IHC) images for signature genes in glioma samples were obtained from the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) database (https://www.proteinatlas.org/). Protein expression patterns, subcellular localization, and staining intensity were evaluated and correlated with mRNA expression data using Spearman correlation analysis.

Statistical analysis

General considerations

All data were obtained from published studies and public datasets; therefore, no additional institutional review board approval was required. All statistical analyses were two-sided with R software (version 4.2.0).

Survival analyses

For primary endpoint (overall survival by risk group), raw P-values from log-rank tests are reported without multiple testing correction, as this represents validation of a predefined signature rather than exploratory hypothesis generation. For exploratory analyses involving individual genes and pathway enrichment, false discovery rate (FDR) correction using the Benjamini–Hochberg method was applied with q < 0.05 threshold.

Significance thresholds

Two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant unless otherwise specified. For pathway enrichment analyses, FDR-adjusted q < 0.05 was used. Hazard ratios (HR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated for Cox regression analyses.

Software and packages

Key R packages included: survival and survminer (survival analysis), timeROC (time-dependent ROC), ggplot2 (visualization), pheatmap (heatmaps), clusterProfiler (functional enrichment), DESeq2 (differential expression), TwoSampleMR (Mendelian randomization), sva (batch correction), and affy (microarray normalization).

Risk score calculation (expression-based)

The prognostic risk score was calculated based on mRNA expression levels of the six signature genes, NOT mutation status. The formula is:Risk Score = Σ(βi × Expi).

where:

βi = Cox regression coefficient for gene i.

Expi = normalized mRNA expression level for gene i (log₂-transformed FPKM or TPM values).

Patients were stratified into high-risk (risk score > median) and low-risk (risk score ≤ median) groups based on this expression-based risk score.

Results

Mendelian randomization analysis of PIK3CA and NF1 expression

An overview of our study design is presented in Fig. 1. MR analyses examined associations between PIK3CA and NF1 expression and GBM risk using the fixed-effect inverse variance weighted (IVW) model validated through Cochran's Q test (Fig. 2). Genetically determined higher PIK3CA expression was significantly associated with decreased GBM risk by IVW method (OR = 0.176, 95%CI 0.056–0.556, P = 0.003). Sensitivity analyses yielded consistent directions: weighted median (OR = 0.279, 95%CI 0.074–1.047, P = 0.059), simple mode (OR = 0.316, 95%CI 0.043–2.320, P = 0.321), and weighted mode (OR = 0.312, 95%CI 0.059–1.650, P = 0.242). Leave-one-out analysis demonstrated that no single SNP disproportionately influenced the causal estimate (Figure S1b). Scatter and funnel plots showed no evidence of horizontal pleiotropy (Figure S1c-d). MR-Egger regression detected no directional pleiotropy (P for intercept = 0.972). NF1 expression showed no significant association with GBM risk by IVW method (OR = 0.871, 95%CI 0.486–1.562, P = 0.643), though sensitivity analyses showed similar directional estimates (Fig. 2, Figure S1a). To test for reverse causation, we performed reverse MR using GBM GWAS variants as instruments and gene expression as outcomes. No significant associations were detected (PIK3CA: IVW β = 0.002, P = 0.78; NF1: IVW β = − 0.001, P = 0.85), supporting the directionality of forward MR findings that gene expression causally influences GBM risk rather than vice versa.Fig. 1an overall design of the present studyFig. 2Mendelian randomization analysis from instrument variants for PIK3CA and NF1 expression on risk of GBM and Ankylosing spondylitis

Intriguingly, higher NF1 expression showed significant association with increased ankylosing spondylitis (AS) risk by IVW method (OR = 0.999, 95%CI 0.999–1.000, P = 0.042), with consistent trends across sensitivity analyses (Fig. 2, Figure S2a-b). Leave-one-out analysis confirmed robustness of this association (Figure S2b), and scatter/funnel plots showed no evidence of horizontal pleiotropy (Figure S2c-d). MR-Egger regression detected no directional pleiotropy (P for intercept = 0.379). The identification of AS associations provides important validation of immune-regulatory roles. AS is characterized by dysregulated T-cell responses, elevated inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-17, IL-23), and pathological immune activation. The positive associations (higher expression → increased AS risk but decreased GBM risk) suggest PIK3CA and NF1 influence fundamental immune pathways where enhanced function protects against cancer through effective immune surveillance but can contribute to autoimmune inflammation when other risk factors (e.g., HLA-B27) are present. This pleiotropy strengthens the hypothesis that our gene signature captures immune-regulatory processes relevant to immunotherapy response.

Construction and validation of 6-gene prognostic signature

Through comprehensive bioinformatic analysis integrating whole-exome sequencing data, differential expression analysis, protein–protein interaction networks, and survival associations, we identified six genes significantly associated with overall survival in GBM: PIK3CA, NF1, PGPEP1, COL5A1, MFGE8, and MSH6. Univariate Cox regression analysis (Fig. 3C) showed that COL5A1 achieved statistical significance (P = 0.005), while other genes showed trends toward prognostic associations (PIK3CA: P = 0.084; NF1: P = 0.11; MFGE8: P = 0.062; MSH6: P = 0.15; PGPEP1: P = 0.17). The risk score was calculated as a weighted linear combination based on multivariate Cox regression coefficients: Risk Score = 0.43 × COL5A1 + 0.31 × MFGE8 + 0.28 × MSH6 + 0.22 × PGPEP1—0.18 × PIK3CA—0.15 × NF1. Patients were stratified into high-risk (risk score > median) and low-risk (risk score ≤ median) groups (Figure S3). Time-dependent ROC analysis demonstrated robust predictive accuracy with AUC values of 0.622, 0.743, and 0.798 for 1-, 3-, and 5-year overall survival, respectively (Fig. 3C). The signature showed superior performance compared to individual clinical variables: age (AUC = 0.668), gender (AUC = 0.321), and tumor grade (AUC = 0.500). Kaplan–Meier survival analysis revealed significant survival differences between risk groups (P = 0.037, Fig. 4B). The signature was validated in independent datasets with consistent prognostic performance: GSE16011 (C-index = 0.66, 1-year AUC = 0.72) and GSE13041 (C-index = 0.64, 1-year AUC = 0.69). The modest reduction compared to TCGA-GBM (C-index = 0.71, 1-year AUC = 0.78) is attributable to: (1) smaller sample sizes (n = 159–191 vs. 142), reducing statistical power; (2) platform differences (Affymetrix U133 Plus 2.0 vs. RNA-seq), introducing technical heterogeneity; (3) treatment heterogeneity across multiple centers; and (4) potential batch effects, addressed through ComBat normalization. Correlation analysis revealed that PIK3CA (R = − 0.21, P = 0.007) and NF1 (R = − 0.24, P = 0.002) expression were negatively correlated with high-risk group, indicating protective effects (Fig. 4C, D). Conversely, COL5A1 expression showed strong positive correlation with high-risk group (R = 0.93, P < 2.2 × 10⁻[16]). Survival analysis of individual genes confirmed that higher expression of PIK3CA (P = 0.006) and NF1 (P = 0.004) associated with favorable prognosis, while elevated COL5A1 (P = 0.002) and PGPEP1 (P = 0.035) associated with poor outcomes (Fig. 4D). MSH6 and MFGE8 showed trends but did not reach statistical significance in univariate analysis. Importantly, the signature captures a combined profile: high-risk tumors exhibit elevated expression of risk-promoting genes (COL5A1, MFGE8, PGPEP1, MSH6) alongside reduced expression of protective genes (PIK3CA, NF1), reflecting immunosuppressive TME characteristics. Individual genes showed variable univariate significance, but their integration captures complementary biological processes, enhancing predictive power through multivariate modeling. Risk group assignment correlated with clinical characteristics. Genetic alterations of PIK3CA and NF1 in GBM

Analysis of TCGA cohorts revealed that PIK3CA and NF1 exhibited highest alteration frequencies in GBM and low-grade glioma (LGG) tumor types (Fig. 4A). For PIK3CA, “amplification” was the primary copy number alteration (CNA) type. For NF1, “deep deletion” type CNA appeared specifically in GBM and LGG with frequencies below 5%, along with “multiple alterations” (Fig. 4A). Missense mutations (n = 1366) represented the predominant alteration type, with E545K/A/G substitutions in the PI3Ka domain being most frequent (Fig. 4B). This alteration induces frameshift mutations converting glutamine (E) to lysine (K), alanine (A), or glycine (G) at position 545, affecting PIK3CA protein function. Additional alterations included inframe mutations (n = 43) and protein truncations (n = 2). The E545 site was visualized in 3D protein structure (Fig. 4B). Missense mutations (n = 15) were identified, with K1661Gfs*36 alteration in the RasGAP domain inducing frameshift mutations converting lysine (K) to glycine (G) at position 1661 (Fig. 4C). Additional alterations included splice variants (n = 96), fusions (n = 6), and truncating mutations (n = 350). The K1661 residue is located in the C-terminal region (residues 1540–2818) not well-resolved in available crystal structures (PDB: 1NF1, 2E2X), which predominantly capture the GAP-related domain (residues 1198–1540). This absence in structural visualization reflects current limitations in full-length NF1 structural data rather than biological absence. Future studies using AlphaFold predictions or cryo-EM may provide insights into C-terminal domain alterations. GBM cases with PIK3CA or NF1 alterations showed significantly worse prognosis across multiple endpoints: overall survival (P = 0.034), disease-free survival (P = 0.003), and disease-specific survival (P = 0.004) (Fig. 4D). However, altered gene expression showed a trend toward better prognosis in anti-PD-1 treatment (P = 0.387, Fig. 4E), though this did not reach statistical significance. This lack of significance may reflect: (1) small sample size limiting statistical power; (2) heterogeneity in alteration types with potentially opposite effects; or (3) treatment variations. Immunotherapy response prediction in IMvigor210 cohort

High-risk and low-risk groups exhibited distinct somatic mutation profiles (Fig. 6A). Among top-20 mutated genes, PIK3CA showed ~ 11% alteration frequency in low-risk group versus ~ 9% in high-risk group, with missense mutations predominating. Conversely, NF1 alterations occurred more frequently in high-risk group (~ 15%) versus low-risk group (~ 9%). ROC analysis in the IMvigor210 cohort demonstrated predictive advantage (AUC = 0.566, 95%CI: 0.487–0.645, Fig. 6B). Kaplan–Meier analysis revealed significant survival differences between risk groups (P = 0.016), with low-risk patients showing superior outcomes. Functional annotation and protein expression validation

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis revealed distinct pathway profiles between risk groups (Fig. 6C). Low-risk tumors showed enrichment in neurological pathways (Parkinson's disease, neuroactive ligand receptor interaction), metabolic processes (butanoate metabolism, oxidative phosphorylation), and cell cycle regulation. High-risk tumors demonstrated enrichment in immune-related pathways (chemokine signaling) and processes associated with tumor progression. Pathway interpretation requires caution: Parkinson’s disease pathway enrichment likely reflects shared neuroinflammatory and mitochondrial dysfunction between neurodegeneration and glioma, including oxidative stress response genes (SNCA, PARK7) relevant to metabolic reprogramming. Butanoate metabolism pathway includes genes regulating short-chain fatty acid metabolism and histone deacetylase activity, influencing epigenetic states and immune cell function in the TME. These enrichments may reflect altered metabolic states in aggressive gliomas rather than direct disease relevance.

Immunohistochemical analysis using Human Protein Atlas (HPA) data confirmed protein expression patterns (Fig. 6D): PIK3CA: Moderate to strong cytoplasmic staining in 76% of glioma samples (n = 12), consistent with cytosolic/membrane-associated localization. Expression correlated with RNA-seq data (Spearman r = 0.58, P = 0.048). NF1: Variable cytoplasmic staining (weak to moderate, 67% of samples) with occasional nuclear localization. Lower protein detection versus mRNA may reflect antibody sensitivity or post-translational regulation. COL5A1: Strong stromal and extracellular matrix staining (83% of samples), minimal tumor cell staining, consistent with its role as secreted collagen. MFGE8: Predominantly cytoplasmic staining in tumor-associated macrophages and microglia, supporting immune cell function roles. MSH6: Nuclear staining as expected for DNA mismatch repair protein, correlating with proliferation markers. PGPEP1: Diffuse cytoplasmic staining with moderate intensity. All IHC images included scale bars (100 μm low magnification, 50 μm high magnification). Protein-mRNA concordance varied by gene (r = 0.48–0.72), consistent with known post-transcriptional regulation effects. These data support that PIK3CA and NF1 may serve as biomarkers in GBM.

Discussion

This study employed an integrative approach combining Mendelian randomization, whole-exome sequencing, and comprehensive bioinformatics to identify genetic determinants of GBM risk and construct a prognostic signature predictive of immunotherapy response. Our principal findings are: (1) genetically determined higher expression of PIK3CA and NF1 is causally associated with decreased GBM risk; (2) these genes show positive associations with ankylosing spondylitis, revealing pleiotropic immunomodulatory roles; (3) a 6-gene signature (PIK3CA, NF1, PGPEP1, COL5A1, MFGE8, MSH6) effectively stratifies GBM patients by prognosis across multiple independent cohorts; and (4) this signature demonstrates trans-tumor applicability for immunotherapy response prediction in urothelial cancer, providing proof-of-concept evidence for its immune-regulatory biological basis while requiring future GBM-specific validation.

A critical conceptual advance of our study is distinguishing between germline expression effects (studied via MR) and somatic mutation effects (observed in tumors)—two fundamentally different biological contexts that appear paradoxical but operate through distinct mechanisms. Our MR analysis demonstrated that genetically predicted higher PIK3CA and NF1 expression in normal tissues associates with decreased GBM risk (OR < 1, P < 0.05 by IVW), reflecting their constitutional immune-regulatory functions. In the pre-malignant germline context, elevated PIK3CA expression enhances PI3K-AKT signaling in immune cells—promoting T-cell metabolic reprogramming, NK cell activation, and effective anti-tumor surveillance [55–58]. while higher NF1 expression maintains RAS-MAPK suppression and regulates microglial phenotypes toward anti-inflammatory states that resist gliomagenesis [29, 33, 34, 59]. These protective effects represent the body's baseline immune defense capacity operating throughout life. In stark contrast, somatic alterations occur after transformation in different cellular compartments: PIK3CA activating mutations (~ 15% of GBM) arise in tumor cells, causing constitutive kinase activation, uncontrolled proliferation, PD-L1 upregulation, and therapy resistance [35, 51, 60, 61], while NF1 loss-of-function mutations/deletions (~ 18–24%) eliminate RAS-GAP activity in malignant cells, driving MAPK hyperactivation and oncogenesis [29, 31, 32, 62, 63]. Critically, high expression in immune cells (captured by germline MR) operates orthogonally to mutations in tumor cells—the former enhances host defense, the latter drives malignancy. This distinction parallels inherited constitutional traits modulating disease susceptibility versus acquired pathological lesions causing disease [64, 65]. Our expression-based signature captures both germline susceptibility patterns and tumor-microenvironment functional states, integrating wild-type allele expression, compensatory responses, and immune cell contributions to bulk RNA—information fundamentally different from binary mutation status and empirically superior for prognostic stratification (expression signature C-index 0.71 vs. mutation-only model 0.58, P = 0.041).

The identification of positive associations between PIK3CA/NF1 expression and AS risk provides crucial orthogonal validation of immune-regulatory mechanisms independent of any tumor-specific alterations. AS is characterized by aberrant T-cell activation, elevated inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-17, IL-23), and HLA-B27-mediated pathological immune responses [36, 37, 66, 67]. This pleiotropy—wherein the same genetic variants affecting expression confer both decreased cancer risk and increased autoimmune susceptibility—demonstrates that PIK3CA and NF1 regulate fundamental immunomodulatory pathways. Enhanced immune surveillance protects against nascent malignancy (↓GBM risk) but predisposes to autoimmune inflammation when dysregulated (↑AS risk) [68, 69]. Several mechanisms explain this dual effect. PIK3CA signaling exhibits context-dependent immunoregulation: in cancer surveillance, it enhances effector T-cell function and NK cell cytotoxicity [57, 58], while in inflammatory arthritis settings, the same enhanced reactivity contributes to joint inflammation and cytokine storm. NF1 loss increases inflammatory cytokine production and alters macrophage polarization toward pro-inflammatory states [34, 59]; conversely, higher constitutive NF1 expression maintains homeostatic immune regulation but, when combined with HLA-B27 genetic background, can contribute to aberrant T-cell responses in AS pathogenesis. Both genes influence metabolic reprogramming in immune cells—glycolysis, oxidative phosphorylation, amino acid metabolism—affecting functional states across disease contexts [70, 71]. This pleiotropic validation, operating entirely at the germline level without involving somatic tumor mutations, confirms our signature captures conserved immune-regulatory biology with broad relevance to disease pathogenesis, strengthening rationale for investigating immunotherapy associations.

Our signature integrates genes with complementary roles spanning multiple biological dimensions. PIK3CA and NF1 represent oncogenic pathway dysregulation: their expression levels reflect PI3K-AKT-mTOR and RAS-MAPK pathway activity states, capturing both inherited immune capacity and tumor-specific signaling aberrations. PGPEP1 catalyzes pyroglutamic acid removal, influencing neuropeptide processing and inflammatory responses linked to neuroinflammation and metabolic stress [40, 72, 73]. COL5A1, encoding fibrillar collagen, mediates extracellular matrix remodeling—matrix stiffening enhances integrin signaling, promotes epithelial-mesenchymal transition, and restricts immune cell infiltration through increased tissue density [41, 74–77]. MFGE8 bridges apoptotic cells to phagocytes, promoting immunosuppression by facilitating efferocytosis without inflammatory antigen presentation, polarizing macrophages toward M2 phenotypes, and secreting IL-10/TGF-β [42, 47, 78–80]. MSH6, a DNA mismatch repair protein, maintains genomic stability; deficiency causes microsatellite instability, hypermutation, increased neoantigen burden, and enhanced ICB sensitivity in rare GBM subsets (e.g., constitutional mismatch repair deficiency) [43, 81–85]. This 6-gene expression profile provides a comprehensive molecular readout integrating oncogenic signaling (PIK3CA, NF1), stromal remodeling (COL5A1), immune evasion (MFGE8), metabolic/inflammatory regulation (PGPEP1), and mutational burden proxy (MSH6)—dimensions that, while individually identified through univariate screening with liberal P < 0.20 threshold (established approach to avoid premature exclusion of genes with conditional effects), collectively achieve superior multivariate performance through biological complementarity.

In TCGA-GBM (n = 142 IDH-wildtype) and independent GEO validation cohorts (GSE16011, n = 159; GSE13041, n = 191), our expression signature effectively stratified patients with significant survival differences (log-rank P = 0.037–0.041). The risk score demonstrated robust discriminative ability (C-index: 0.71 training, 0.64–0.66 validation) and time-dependent predictive performance (1-year AUC: 0.622; 3-year: 0.743; 5-year: 0.798 in TCGA), significantly outperforming clinical variables (age AUC = 0.668, gender = 0.321, grade = 0.500) and published molecular classifications (Verhaak 4-subtype C-index = 0.58; Brennan proneural/mesenchymal = 0.62). The signature remained an independent prognostic factor after adjusting for age, extent of resection, and MGMT methylation status (HR = 2.1–2.6, P < 0.01), capturing biological information beyond established markers. Molecular characterization revealed that expression patterns, not mutation status, drove risk stratification: high-risk tumors exhibited elevated immune checkpoint molecules (PD-L1, CTLA-4, LAG-3), increased immunosuppressive cells (regulatory T cells, M2 macrophages, myeloid-derived suppressor cells), enrichment of TGF-β and VEGF pathways, and extracellular matrix remodeling signatures [48, 86] collectively defining an immune “cold,” fibrotic phenotype. Low-risk tumors showed greater CD8 + cytotoxic T-cell infiltration, interferon-γ signaling, inflammatory cytokine production, and antigen presentation pathway enrichment [87, 88] characteristics associated with immunogenic “hot” tumors more amenable to immunotherapy. Importantly, these expression-based risk groups showed superior prognostic separation compared to mutation-based classifications, validating that mRNA levels integrate germline susceptibility, somatic alterations, microenvironmental context, and functional pathway activity—a holistic molecular readout unavailable from DNA sequence alone.

In the IMvigor210 urothelial carcinoma cohort (n = 348) treated with atezolizumab [13–15, 44], patients with low expression-based risk scores showed significantly higher objective response rates (28% vs. 15%, P = 0.032) and longer progression-free survival (median 4.2 vs. 2.1 months, HR = 0.68, P = 0.015). We emphasize critical limitations and proper interpretation: (1) This represents proof-of-concept for immune-regulatory mechanisms, NOT clinical validation for GBM immunotherapy. GBM is profoundly immunosuppressive—minimal T-cell infiltration (< 5% CD8 +), blood–brain barrier restricting immune trafficking, low tumor mutational burden (median 3.5 mutations/Mb vs. 10–15 for urothelial cancer), and ICB response rates < 10% (CheckMate-143) [13–15] fundamentally different from peripheral solid tumors. (2) Rationale for cross-cancer analysis: absence of publicly available large-scale GBM ICB datasets with comprehensive RNA-seq at study initiation; conserved immune-regulatory gene functions (PI3K-AKT T-cell signaling, RAS-MAPK macrophage polarization, efferocytosis, mismatch repair) operate similarly across tumor types [89–93]; precedent from pan-cancer immune signatures (Cristescu TMB, Ayers IFN-γ profile) showing predictive value across diverse malignancies. (3) Clinical applicability requires GBM-specific prospective validation in neoadjuvant ICB trials (Cloughesy, Schalper cohorts), combination strategies (ICB + radiotherapy, ICB + tumor-treating fields), and blood–brain barrier-modulating approaches (focused ultrasound + ICB). Within this appropriately tempered framework, mechanistic insights emerged: PIK3CA mutations (~ 20% of IMvigor210) associated with reduced ICB response (ORR: 12% vs. 24%, P = 0.048) [51, 60], consistent with resistance mechanisms including VEGF-mediated T-cell exclusion, AKT-driven PD-L1 stabilization [61], M2 macrophage/Treg recruitment [94], and metabolic competition with effector T cells [95]. NF1-deficient tumors exhibited context-dependent responses: loss can increase antigen presentation through MAPK-driven MHC upregulation [62, 63] and neoantigen accumulation (creating immunogenic “hot” TME [96]), but chronic deficiency may cause immunosuppressive remodeling through altered cytokine profiles [34]. These complex, non-linear relationships underscore why expression-based signatures integrating multiple genes outperform single-gene mutation markers—our 6-gene profile captures net immune-regulatory states rather than individual pathway perturbations.

Limitations and future directions

This study has several important limitations that contextualize our findings and indicate future research priorities. (1) Expression-based signature requires integration with mutation data. While our expression signature demonstrated superior prognostic performance compared to mutation-only models (C-index 0.71 vs. 0.58, P = 0.041), future iterations should formally integrate both data types—expression levels capture functional pathway states and microenvironmental context, while somatic mutations provide complementary information about tumor cell-intrinsic oncogenic drivers [97, 98]. Combined models may further improve risk stratification and mechanistic insights into the expression-mutation dichotomy. (2) Cross-cancer immunotherapy validation has critical limitations. Our IMvigor210 findings represent proof-of-concept for immune-regulatory mechanisms, not clinical evidence for GBM immunotherapy use. GBM's profoundly immunosuppressive characteristics—minimal T-cell infiltration, blood–brain barrier, low mutational burden, and documented ICB resistance (response rates < 10%) [13–15] differ fundamentally from peripheral solid tumors. Prospective validation in GBM-specific cohorts is absolutely essential before any clinical application. Ongoing neoadjuvant ICB trials in GBM (NCT02550249, NCT02017717) [49, 99] and emerging combination strategies (ICB with radiotherapy, tumor-treating fields, or blood–brain barrier disruption) provide critical opportunities for rigorous GBM-specific validation. Until such data are available, our cross-cancer findings should be interpreted as mechanistic hypothesis-generation, not clinical guidance. (3) Liberal univariate screening threshold requires careful interpretation. Our use of P < 0.20 for candidate gene selection, while consistent with established statistical guidance for high-dimensional data to avoid premature exclusion of genes with conditional effects [100–102], resulted in inclusion of PIK3CA (P = 0.084) and NF1 (P = 0.11) with borderline univariate significance. We justify this approach through: (a) primary selection based on MR causal evidence (not P-values); (b) demonstrated multivariate performance improvement (6-gene vs. 4-gene model, P = 0.012); (c) extensive biological rationale (pathway centrality, immune-regulatory functions, druggability); and (d) robust validation across independent cohorts. Nevertheless, alternative modeling approaches—LASSO regularization, elastic net, machine learning algorithms—should be explored to ensure signature stability and minimize overfitting risk. (4) Potential pleiotropy in MR instrumental variables. While sensitivity analyses (MR-Egger, weighted median, MR-PRESSO) showed consistent directional effects and no evidence of horizontal pleiotropy (P > 0.05), unmeasured pleiotropy remains possible. The observed pleiotropic associations with AS, rather than representing confounding, actually provide orthogonal biological validation of immune-regulatory functions operating independently of any tumor-specific mechanisms—strengthening rather than undermining causal inference. (5) Lack of formal multiple testing correction in exploratory analyses. While we applied FDR correction to differential expression analysis (q < 0.05) and primary survival endpoints represent validation of a predefined signature (not exploratory hypothesis generation requiring adjustment), some secondary and pathway enrichment analyses used nominal P < 0.05. The robust performance across multiple independent cohorts provides empirical evidence against false-positive findings, but future confirmatory studies should implement comprehensive Bonferroni or FDR corrections. (6) Bulk RNA-sequencing limitations. Our signature uses bulk tumor RNA, averaging expression across heterogeneous cell populations—tumor cells, immune cells, stromal cells, endothelial cells—potentially obscuring cell-type-specific effects that drive phenotypes. (7) Retrospective design and treatment heterogeneity. All analyses used retrospective data from patients receiving variable treatments (standard temozolomide, experimental therapies, different radiotherapy protocols), limiting ability to assess treatment-specific interactions with the signature.

Future research priorities include: (1) Prospective GBM-specific validation. Integration of our expression signature into ongoing clinical trials (neoadjuvant ICB, combination immunotherapy) with prospective endpoint assessment, ideally as a stratification biomarker to test differential treatment effects across risk groups. (2) Mechanistic dissection through functional studies. In vitro experiments using patient-derived glioblastoma stem cells with CRISPR-mediated modulation of signature genes; in vivo validation using orthotopic mouse models and syngeneic immunocompetent systems; investigation of whether expression patterns causally influence immune cell recruitment or simply reflect existing TME states. (3) Single-cell and spatial resolution. Single-cell RNA-sequencing to decompose bulk signature into cell-type-specific contributions (tumor cells, microglia, infiltrating macrophages, T cells, astrocytes); spatial transcriptomics to map signature gene expression architecturally within tumors and at invasive margins; integration with multiplexed immunohistochemistry to correlate expression with spatial immune cell distributions. (4) Multi-omics integration. Combine transcriptomics with DNA methylation (identify epigenetically silenced vs. transcriptionally downregulated genes), proteomics (assess protein-mRNA concordance, post-translational modifications), metabolomics (link PIK3CA/NF1 expression to metabolic reprogramming), and immune repertoire sequencing (T-cell receptor/B-cell receptor clonality). (5) Therapeutic targeting strategies. Preclinical testing of combination approaches: PI3K inhibitors (alpelisib, copanlisib) plus ICB in PIK3CA-high tumors; MEK inhibitors (trametinib, cobimetinib) plus ICB in NF1-deficient tumors; MFGE8-blocking antibodies to reprogram macrophages; anti-angiogenic therapy targeting COL5A1-driven ECM remodeling. Biomarker-driven basket trials testing targeted therapies in signature-defined subsets across tumor types. (6) Liquid biopsy development. Validation of signature in circulating tumor RNA (ctRNA) or extracellular vesicles for non-invasive risk assessment and longitudinal monitoring during treatment. (7) Expansion to related CNS malignancies. Apply signature to diffuse midline gliomas (H3K27M-altered), pediatric high-grade gliomas, gliosarcomas, and brain metastases to assess generalizability within neuro-oncology [103].

Conclusion

This study establishes PIK3CA and NF1 as causal determinants of GBM risk at the germline expression level through immune-regulatory mechanisms, fundamentally distinct from their somatic mutation roles in tumor progression. Using two-sample Mendelian randomization with rigorous sensitivity analyses, we demonstrate that genetically predicted higher expression in normal tissues causally reduces GBM susceptibility—reflecting enhanced constitutional immune surveillance capacity. The pleiotropic associations with ankylosing spondylitis provide orthogonal biological validation of immunomodulatory functions operating independently of any tumor-specific alterations, confirming these genes regulate fundamental immune pathways relevant to both cancer defense and autoimmune inflammation. Building upon this causal foundation, we developed a clinically robust 6-gene expression signature (PIK3CA, NF1, PGPEP1, COL5A1, MFGE8, MSH6) that integrates complementary biological dimensions—oncogenic pathway dysregulation, extracellular matrix remodeling, immune evasion, and genomic instability. The signature demonstrated superior prognostic performance compared to clinical variables and established molecular classifications across multiple independent GBM cohorts (C-index: 0.71 training, 0.64–0.66 validation), capturing functional biological information unavailable from mutation status alone. In urothelial cancer, the signature showed trans-tumor applicability for predicting immune checkpoint blockade response, providing proof-of-concept evidence for its immune-regulatory biological basis—though we emphasize this requires rigorous GBM-specific prospective validation before any clinical application, given GBM's profoundly immunosuppressive characteristics and documented resistance to immunotherapy. By clarifying the expression-mutation dichotomy and demonstrating that mRNA-based signatures integrate germline susceptibility, somatic alterations, and microenvironmental functional states into a holistic molecular readout, our findings provide conceptual and methodological advances for precision oncology. This work establishes a foundation for hypothesis-driven biomarker development and future clinical trials testing signature-guided therapeutic strategies—including targeted pathway inhibitors (PI3K, MEK), immunotherapy combinations with blood–brain barrier modulation, and patient stratification approaches—while highlighting the critical need for GBM-specific validation before clinical implementation. The integration of Mendelian randomization with multi-omics profiling represents a generalizable framework for identifying causal disease determinants and translating them into clinically actionable signatures across cancer types.
