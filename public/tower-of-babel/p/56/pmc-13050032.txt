Efficient recovery of chlorogenic acid and rutin from Ficus erecta leaves using liquid carbon dioxide-ethanol–water extraction with and without ultrasonic irradiation
Authors: Tanjina Sharmin, Minami Kanazawa, Kazusa Mishima, Sahena Ferdosh, Agus Saptoro, Hussein Znad, Md Zaidul Islam Sarker, Salim Mustofa, Toshihiro Takeshita, Mikio Ouchi, Kenji Mishima
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract

Introduction

Phenolic compounds such as chlorogenic acid (CGA) and rutin play important roles in human nutrition due to their strong antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and vasomodulatory properties. CGA contributes to glucose metabolism and cardiovascular protection, while rutin exhibits capillary-strengthening and antioxidative effects [1], [2], [3], [4]. Both compounds occur widely in edible plants and are increasingly used as functional ingredients in foods and nutraceuticals. Ficus erecta (Inubiwa), a fig species native to East Asia including Japan, has traditionally been consumed for its medicinal and nutritional benefits. Recent phytochemical studies have confirmed that its leaves contain significant amounts of CGA and rutin, suggesting strong potential as a value-added source of phenolic compounds. However, systematic investigations of environmentally friendly extraction strategies for this plant remain limited.

As polar compounds, CGA and rutin are commonly extracted using aqueous–organic solvent mixtures (e.g., methanol, acetone, or ethanol) to improve solubility and yield [5]. However, conventional solvent-based extraction methods suffer from several drawbacks, including high solvent consumption, limited selectivity, complex downstream purification, and potential degradation of heat-sensitive compounds. In addition, the use of organic solvents poses safety and environmental concerns due to their flammability, toxicity, incomplete removal, and generation of hazardous waste.

To address these environmental and safety concerns, green extraction technologies using carbon dioxide (CO2) have been developed. CO2, in either supercritical or liquid form, is widely recognized as a non-toxic and recyclable solvent [6], [7], [8], [9]. Liquid CO2 extraction operates under relatively mild temperature (≈15–25 °C) and moderate pressure conditions, preserving thermolabile compounds while minimizing solvent residues. Because CO2 is nonpolar, co-solvents such as ethanol and water are often introduced to increase polarity and enhance the extraction of phenolic compounds. Mixed CO2–ethanol–water systems therefore offer a promising food-grade extraction platform [8], [9].

Ultrasonic irradiation is another green process intensification technique that enhances extraction through acoustic cavitation, generating microjets and localized shear forces that disrupt plant cell structures and improve mass transfer [10], [11]. The combination of ultrasound with liquid CO2 has been explored as a hybrid extraction strategy to enhance the recovery of phenolics and essential oils [10], [11], [12], [13], [14], [15]. However, the effectiveness of ultrasound under CO2 conditions depends strongly on plant matrix structure, porosity, and solute–solvent thermodynamic interactions. In systems where extraction is primarily solubility-limited rather than diffusion-limited, cavitation-induced disruption may not significantly increase yield. Therefore, the mechanistic contribution and practical limits of ultrasound-assisted liquid CO2 extraction remain to be systematically clarified.

Despite the traditional use of Ficus erecta leaves and their high phenolic content, systematic investigations of green extraction strategies for this plant remain scarce. In particular, the combined influence of solvent composition and ultrasonic irradiation on the extraction behavior of individual phenolic compounds has not been clarified. To the best of our knowledge, this study represents the first systematic investigation of compound-specific ultrasound effects on chlorogenic acid and rutin extraction from Ficus erecta leaves using a liquid CO2–ethanol–water mixed solvent system.

In this work, the effects of solvent composition and ultrasonic irradiation on the extraction performance of CGA and rutin were investigated using liquid CO2. Morphological changes in the leaf matrix during extraction were analyzed using FE-SEM to assess structural disruption caused by solvent penetration and cavitation. In addition, thermodynamic analysis based on vapor–liquid equilibrium (VLE) calculations and Hansen solubility parameter (HSP) modeling was performed to establish a predictive relationship between solvent composition and extraction yield. This combined experimental and modeling approach provides mechanistic insight into compound-specific extraction behavior and supports the development of a sustainable, food-grade extraction strategy for the valorization of Ficus erecta as a source of bioactive phenolics.

Materials and methods

Plant Material and Reagents

Ficus erecta leaves were collected from Fukuoka University (Fukuoka, Japan), air-dried at room temperature, and ground into fine powder using a freezer mill (Model 6750, SPEX CentriPrep Co., NJ, USA). The moisture content of the air-dried Ficus erecta leaves was not directly measured; however, under similar ambient drying conditions plant materials typically retain approximately 5–10 wt% residual moisture, and all samples were dried and processed under identical conditions to ensure experimental consistency. Chlorogenic acid (CGA, >97%), and rutin (>95%) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (Japan), and Tokyo Chemical Industry Co. (Japan), respectively. Analytical grade ethanol and methanol (>99.7%) were purchased from Wako for extraction and HPLC analysis. High-purity CO2 (>99%) was supplied by Fukuoka Sanso Co., Ltd.

Conventional aqueous-ethanol extraction

Conventional aqueous-ethanol extraction was performed to optimize the extraction efficiency with different ethanol–water mixtures (0% to 100% v/v). The solvent-to-solid ratio (30 mL / 0.1 g) and extraction time (30 min) were selected based on preliminary time-course experiments conducted at conducted under excess solvent conditions (100 mL / 0.1 g), which showed that the extraction yields of chlorogenic acid and rutin reached equilibrium within approximately 30 min (Fig. S1). For all experiments, the total extraction time for each experiment was 30 min. Ficus erecta leaf powder (0.1 g) was extracted with 30 mL of solvent in a thermostated water bath (Eyela SB-24, Tokyo Rikakikai Co., Ltd.) equipped with a shaker (Eyela SS-8) and a heating circulator (Eyela T-80), maintained at 298.15 K with a precision of ± 0.1 °C. Extraction was carried out for 30 min and then filtered and analyzed by HPLC to quantify CGA and rutin.

Liquid CO2 extraction Setup and Procedure

A schematic diagram of the liquid CO2 extraction system is illustrated in Fig. 1. A detailed description of the apparatus is given previously [8], [9], [10], [11], [12], [13], [14]. In brief, it consists of a stainless-steel extraction cell (∼150 cm3; 34 mm i.d., 165 mm length; Toyo Koatsu Co., Ltd., Hiroshima, Japan) equipped with a titanium ultrasonic horn connected to an ultrasonic processor (VC-750, Sonics & Materials Inc., UK). The processor operates at 20 kHz with a maximum power of 500 W and amplitude of 61 μm. During ultrasound-assisted extraction, sonication was applied at 25% amplitude (15.3 μm), in pulsed cycles (5 s ON / 10 s OFF) for 125 s at 8 MPa and 298.15 K. High performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) analysis

CGA and rutin were quantified by a reverse phase HPLC-UV method [14]. Separation was performed using a TSK-GEL ODS-80Ts column (150 × 4.6 mm, Tosoh Co., Tokyo, Japan) at 40 °C. The mobile phase consisted of methanol and 0.1% acetic acid in water with a gradient profile (Table 3). Flow rate was 1.0 mL/min. Detection was carried out at 335 nm using a UV detector (875-UV, JASCO). Concentrations were calculated from calibration curves prepared with standard solutions. Calibration curves were constructed using external standard solutions, yielding correlation coefficients (R2) of 0.994 for CGA and 0.9996 for rutin. The LOD and LOQ were calculated based on the standard deviation of blank responses and the slope of the calibration curve. Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscopy (FE-SEM) analysis

Surface morphology was examined using FE-SEM to evaluate the structural changes in Ficus erecta leaf powder before and after extraction. Samples were dried under vacuum, mounted on aluminum stubs using conductive carbon tape and sputter-coated with a thin platinum layer (approximately 4 nm thickness) using a JEOL JFC-1600 auto fine coater (JEOL Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) to improve electrical conductivity and minimize charging effects during imaging.

Microstructural observations were performed using a JEOL JSM-IT800 FE-SEM system (JEOL Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) under high-vacuum conditions at accelerating voltages of 10, or 15 kV under high vacuum. Images were acquired at magnifications ranging from 550 × to 2,000 × to assess surface disruption, pore formation, and cell wall integrity. Five sample conditions were analyzed: (a) untreated, (b) solvent-extracted, (c) solvent plus ultrasound-treated, (d) CO2-extracted, and (e) CO2 plus ultrasound-treated samples.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate (n = 3), and the results are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical analyses were conducted using Microsoft Excel 365 (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA, USA) with the Data Analysis Toolpak add-in. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to evaluate differences among treatment groups. When significant differences were detected, Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) post hoc test was applied for multiple comparisons. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results and Discussion

Identification of major polyphenols in Ficus erecta leaves

The presence of chlorogenic acid (CGA) and rutin in Ficus erecta leaf extracts was confirmed by HPLC analysis through comparison with authentic standards. As shown in Fig. 2, the chromatographic peaks corresponding to CGA and rutin were detected at retention times of approximately 24 and 34 min, respectively. Conventional aqueous-ethanol extraction

Alcoholic solvents such as methanol and ethanol are commonly used to extract polyphenols from plant materials. Binary mixtures of alcohol and water are particularly effective, as small additions of water enhance the polarity of the solvent system, thereby improving the extraction of phenolic compounds. To identify the optimal ethanol–water ratio, aqueous-ethanol extraction experiments were conducted using mixtures ranging from 0% to 100% ethanol (v/v). As shown in Fig. 3, CGA and rutin yields were strongly dependent on ethanol fraction. CGA extraction increased with ethanol concentration, reaching 7.5 ± 0.51 mg g-1 dry powder at 50% ethanol, and remained high at 60% ethanol (6.78 ± 0.5 mg g-1). However, further increases in ethanol concentration reduced CGA yield to 2.54 ± 0.3 mg g-1 at 70% ethanol and 0.74 ± 0.19 mg g-1 at 80% ethanol. Rutin exhibited maximum extraction at 60% ethanol (9.54 ± 0.64 mg g-1), followed by 7.86 ± 1.7 mg g-1 at 70% ethanol and 6.69 ± 0.37 mg g-1 at 80% ethanol. Extraction efficiency declined sharply above 90% ethanol. This trend agrees with previous reports showing that moderate ethanol concentrations (approximately 30–50% v/v) often maximize phenolic extraction efficiency by providing an optimal polarity balance for compounds such as chlorogenic acid [25]. Previous studies have reported CGA concentrations of approximately 4–12 mg g-1 in Ficus species and other phenolic-rich leaves extracted using aqueous ethanol systems [26].Fig. 3Effect of ethanol concentration on the extraction yield of CGA (■) and rutin (□) from Ficus erecta leaves at 298.15 K and atmospheric pressure.

Effect of ethanol concentration on the extraction yield of CGA (■) and rutin (□) from Ficus erecta leaves at 298.15 K and atmospheric pressure.

Since extraction time and agitation conditions were identical for all experiments, mass-transfer conditions were comparable across solvent systems. The presence of a distinct maximum at intermediate ethanol fractions (50–70%) indicates that extraction was predominantly solubility-limited rather than diffusion-limited. The optimal solvent composition likely reflects a balance between polarity and hydrogen-bonding capacity, which enhances solute–solvent compatibility. At lower ethanol fractions, reduced organic character limits solubilization of moderately polar phenolics, whereas at higher ethanol fractions, diminished water content reduces hydrogen-bonding interactions and solvent affinity for highly polar compounds such as CGA and rutin. Thus, solvent polarity tuning rather than diffusion resistance appears to govern extraction efficiency under the present experimental conditions.

Liquid CO2 extraction using ethanol as co-solvent with or without sonication

Liquid CO2 extraction (298.15 K, 8 MPa) with 50% ethanol as co-solvent was evaluated with and without ultrasonic irradiation and compared to conventional 50% aqueous–ethanol extraction (Fig. 4). Ultrasound significantly enhanced conventional extraction, increasing CGA and rutin yields to 16.04 ± 1.10 and 12.3 ± 0.16 mg g-1, respectively, consistent with cavitation-induced mass-transfer enhancement reported for ultrasound-assisted extraction of phenolic compounds from plant matrices [10], [27]. Liquid CO2 extraction further increased CGA yield to 25.5 ± 1.1 mg g-1 (p < 0.01), and to 31.6 ± 0.37 mg g-1 (p < 0.001) when combined with ultrasound. In contrast, rutin yield showed no statistically significant difference between CO2 extraction without ultrasound (14.54 ± 0.16 mg g-1) and with ultrasound (16.5 ± 0.44 mg g-1; p > 0.13), exhibiting a clear plateau despite additional structural disruption.Fig. 4Effect of sonication (amplitude 25%, 125 s) on the extraction yield of CGA (■) and rutin (□) from Ficus erecta leaves with and without liquid CO2 extraction at 298.15 K and 8 MPa. FE-SEM micrographs (Fig. 5) confirm extensive pore formation and tissue fragmentation under CO2 and CO2 + ultrasound treatments, indicating that matrix accessibility was largely maximized. The absence of further rutin enhancement therefore suggests that its extraction is not diffusion-limited but governed by solute–solvent equilibrium. As a highly polar glycosylated flavonoid containing multiple hydroxyl groups and a rutinose moiety, rutin exhibits strong hydrogen-bonding interactions with polar ethanol–water mixtures. Consequently, rutin’s solubility in the CO2–ethanol system is determined mainly by the polarity balance of the ethanol–water co-solvent. Once equilibrium solubility is reached, additional cavitation does not increase recovery. Similar compound-dependent behavior has been reported in ultrasound-assisted extraction of plant phenolics, where the degree of enhancement depends on compound solubility and matrix diffusion limitations [28]. In contrast, the continued increase in CGA yield suggests that part of the compound remains diffusion-restricted within intracellular or bound domains and can therefore benefit from cavitation-induced structural disruption. These results demonstrate compound-specific extraction control: ultrasound enhances recovery under mass-transfer-limited conditions, but its contribution diminishes once extraction becomes solubility-controlled.Fig. 5FE-SEM images showing the surface morphology of Ficus erecta leaf powder:(a) Untreated raw sample; (b) Sample after ethanol (50% v/v) extraction; (c) Sample after ethanol extraction with ultrasonic irradiation; (d) Sample after liquid CO2 extraction; (e) Sample after liquid CO2 extraction with ultrasonic irradiation.

Residual mass after extraction with and without ultrasound

To further evaluate matrix-level effects, residual mass after extraction with and without ultrasonic irradiation was compared for Perilla (reported previously in ref 12) and Ficus erecta under identical CO2–ethanol–water conditions (Table 2).

Under non-ultrasonic conditions, residual mass was 98.26% for Perilla and 94.28% for Ficus erecta. After ultrasonic irradiation, the residual mass for Perilla decreased slightly to 97.32%, whereas Ficus erecta showed essentially no change (94.25%). These results indicate only a small reduction in residual mass for Perilla under ultrasonic treatment, while the bulk matrix of Ficus erecta remains largely unchanged. It should be noted that residual mass reflects overall matrix removal, whereas the enhanced CGA yield observed under ultrasonic conditions indicates selective release of intracellular compounds rather than substantial additional removal of bulk biomass. Because each experiment was performed once, statistical significance testing was not applied, and the results are interpreted as descriptive comparisons.

SEM observations of Ficus erecta in the present study (Fig. 5) reveal extensive pore formation and internal cavity development after CO2 extraction, indicating substantial solvent permeation even without ultrasonic irradiation. By comparison, SEM images reported for Perilla under comparable conditions [12] show a more organized lamellar microstructure that undergoes pronounced deformation upon ultrasonic treatment. Although these structural differences were not quantified by morphometric or porosity analysis, the combined residual mass data and qualitative morphology suggest that Perilla presents greater diffusion resistance than Ficus erecta.

Collectively, these findings indicate that ultrasound enhances extraction primarily in matrices where mass-transfer resistance is significant, as observed for Perilla. In contrast, the limited ultrasonic effect in Ficus erecta supports the interpretation that extraction under CO2–ethanol conditions is not strongly constrained by structural accessibility but instead governed by compound-specific solubility limitations, as discussed in Section 3.3.

Phase behavior of the CO2–Ethanol–Water system

To interpret the extraction behavior of chlorogenic acid (CGA) and rutin, the phase behavior of the CO2–ethanol–water solvent system was analyzed. Depending on pressure and composition, this ternary system can exist as either a single phase or a gas–liquid two-phase system, which directly influences solvent composition and extraction performance. Therefore, vapor–liquid equilibrium (VLE) calculations were conducted at 298.15 K and 8.0 MPa to determine the phase state of the solvent mixtures.

Phase equilibrium was described using a γ–φ thermodynamic framework in which the vapor phase was modeled with the Peng–Robinson equation of state (PR EOS) combined with the Wong–Sandler/Huron–Vidal mixing rule, while liquid-phase non-ideality was represented using the NRTL activity-coefficient model [[17], [18], [19], [20], [21], [24]]. Detailed expressions for fugacity coefficients, activity coefficients, mixing rules, and parameter values are provided in the Supporting Information S2 (eqs S1–S14).

The calculated phase diagram (Fig. 6) indicates that the solvent compositions employed in this study lie within a gas–liquid two-phase region under the applied conditions. Formation of a two-phase system was also visually confirmed in the high-pressure cell during extraction. This phase behavior influences the effective solvent composition surrounding the solute and therefore impacts extraction performance.Fig. 6Tie lines of ternary VLE composition of the CO2–EtOH–H2O system (298.15 K, 8 MPa).

For the EtOH:H2O = 1:1 system (Fig. 7a), both compounds exhibited a noticeable increase in extraction yield with increasing CO2 fraction. The predicted yields closely followed the experimental data, capturing the non-linear enhancement associated with CO2 addition. As CO2 was introduced, the Hansen solubility distance (Ra) between the target phenolics and the mixed solvent gradually decreased, indicating improved solute–solvent affinity and enhanced dissolution under pressurized conditions.

For the EtOH:H2O = 3:2 system (Fig. 7b), absolute extraction yields were lower, and a weaker CO2-dependent enhancement followed by a mild plateau was observed. At this higher ethanol fraction, the solvent mixture becomes less polar and deviates further from the optimal polarity required for dissolving highly polar phenolics such as CGA and rutin. Consequently, the reduction in Hansen solubility distance (Ra) upon CO2 addition is smaller, leading to limited improvement in solute–solvent affinity and an early saturation of extraction yield. Nonetheless, the model successfully reproduced this behavior, indicating that solvent polarity balance and CO2 entrainer effects jointly influence extraction performance in this composition range.

Across all solvent compositions, incorporation of the CO2 volume fraction term (ϕ3) was essential to account for the entrainer effect of liquid CO2. The fitted parameter a3 was positive for CGA under both ultrasound and non-ultrasound conditions (Table 6), indicating a strong sensitivity to CO2 addition, whereas the corresponding effect for rutin was comparatively smaller. This observation is consistent with the mechanism discussed in Section 3.3, where CGA extraction is influenced by CO2-induced solvent effects and mass transfer, whereas rutin extraction is governed primarily by the polarity balance of the ethanol–water co-solvent.

These kinetic effects increase extraction rates but are not explicitly captured by the equilibrium-based thermodynamic model. Nevertheless, ultrasound does not significantly alter intrinsic solvent–solute interactions, and the HSP–VLE framework remains valid for predicting equilibrium extraction behavior. The proposed estimation method relies solely on molecular structure through Hansen solubility parameters and does not require additional physical property data, making it industrially practical. For extraction processes, a prediction error of 25% or less is generally considered within the acceptable range. Although one data point showed a maximum deviation of 33%, the remaining data were within this range, indicating that the model provides a reasonable predictive capability. Taken together, these results demonstrate that the HSP-based thermodynamic model provides a practical framework for predicting phenolic extraction behavior in CO2–ethanol–water solvent systems.

Conclusions

Liquid CO2 extraction using ethanol–water as a co-solvent provides an efficient and environmentally friendly approach for recovering phenolic compounds from Ficus erecta leaves. Compared with conventional aqueous–ethanol extraction, the CO2-based system significantly improved CGA recovery while maintaining comparable rutin yields. A compound-specific ultrasound effect was observed under optimized CO2 extraction conditions, indicating a transition from diffusion-controlled to solubility-controlled extraction behavior. FE-SEM analysis confirmed substantial structural disruption of the leaf matrix under CO2 and ultrasound treatments. Thermodynamic analysis combining vapor–liquid equilibrium (VLE) modeling and Hansen solubility parameter (HSP) correlations successfully described the relationship between solvent composition and extraction yield. The proposed model reproduced experimental trends with deviations generally below 25%, indicating good predictive capability for mixed CO2–ethanol–water systems.

However, the present model primarily considers equilibrium solute–solvent interactions and does not explicitly account for kinetic effects such as cavitation-induced mass transfer. In addition, scale-up factors such as mass-transfer limitations and solvent recycling efficiency require further investigation. Future work should therefore integrate kinetic modeling and process-scale evaluation to further optimize sustainable CO2-based extraction processes for phenolic bioactives.
