Terbium(III)-Doped Silica Surface of Fe(II) Spin Crossover Nanoparticles as a Multifunctional Interface
Authors: Nikolia Lalioti, Maria Katsikini, Fani Pinakidou, Labrini Sygellou, Kyriacos Georgiou, Sotirios Christodoulou, Francesco D’Acapito, Radim Mach, Ondrej Malina, Vassilis Tangoulis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Spin-crossover (SCO) nanoparticles (NPs) of the ID coordination polymer [Fe­(HTrz)2(Trz)]·(BF4), where HTrz = 1H-1,2,4-triazole, have been synthesized with integrated luminescence properties, focusing on the role of dopant trivalent Terbium (Tb) ions. Using a reverse micelle method, three types of nanoparticles were synthesized: pristine NPs (NP1), silica covered (NP2), and Tb-doped (NP3) NPs. Structural and morphological analyses, including TEM, STEM-XEDS, FT-IR, P-XRD, XPS, and XAFS spectroscopy, confirmed the successful incorporation of Tb ions into the silica matrix of NP3 and revealed their bonding environment with SiO4 tetrahedra and nitrate ions. Magnetic susceptibility measurements demonstrated hysteretic SCO behavior in all nanoparticle types, with NP3 exhibiting the highest thermal hysteresis width and a shift in critical temperatures, indicating enhanced cooperativity and modified magnetic properties due to the presence of Tb ions. Temperature-dependent photoluminescence (PL) measurements of NP3 showed hysteresis during thermal cycling, attributed to SCO transitions between high- and low-spin states. The study highlights the synergy between SCO and luminescence properties, paving the way for the development of multifunctional materials for applications in optoelectronics, sensors, and thermometry. These findings provide valuable insights into the design and optimization of advanced SCO-luminescent nanomaterials.

Introduction

Multifunctional materials that combine spin crossover (SCO) and luminescent properties are an emerging class of advanced materials with significant potential for applications in optoelectronics, sensors, thermometry, and elastocaloric refrigeration. − The SCO phenomenon, observed in certain transition metal complexes [e.g., Fe­(II)], involves a reversible switch between low-spin (LS) and high-spin (HS) states triggered by external stimuli such as temperature, pressure, or light. This transition is accompanied by changes in magnetic, structural, and electronic properties, making SCO materials highly versatile.

Integrating luminescence, the emission of light from a material, into SCO systems adds an optical dimension to their functionality. Luminescence is highly sensitive to the electronic and geometric structure of the complex, which changes during the SCO transition. This interplay creates a synergy between SCO and luminescence, enabling the tracking of spin states through variations in light emission. The luminescence mechanism typically involves radiative or nonradiative energy transfer, driven by spectral overlap between the luminophore’s emission and the SCO complex’s electronic absorption. During spin transitions, luminescence intensity is modulated, often strengthening in the HS state due to reduced quenching by the LS state. Despite the challenges in achieving SCO-luminescence synergy, innovative strategies like the use of luminescent ligands, doping luminophores, and designing nanomaterials have enabled the creation of bifunctional systems. , ,−

The synergy between SCO and luminescence is achieved by integrating luminophores into SCO systems, either through doping, grafting, or hybrid core–shell structures. SCO nanoparticles have been fabricated using techniques like reverse micelle synthesis, with silica commonly used as a matrix due to its porous nature and compatibility with luminophores. [Fe­(NH2Trz)3]·(tosylate)2 NPs where NH2Trz = 4-amino-1,2,4-triazole are doped with Rhodamine-110 and exhibit luminescence modulation through radiative energy transfer, with emission intensity increasing during LS-to-HS transitions. Similarly, dansyl-functionalized silica nanoparticles of [Fe­(HTrz)2(Trz)]·(BF4) show luminescence modulation that tracks SCO behavior, with energy transfer mechanisms such as Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET) playing a key role. ,

Hybrid SCO nanomaterials are created by chemically grafting luminophores like pyrene, cyanine dye, Tb­(III) complex, and Cadmium Telluride (CdTe) quantum dots onto [Fe­(HTrz)2(Trz)]·(BF4)@SiO2 SCO NPs. The luminescence mechanism in the pyrene-SCO composite involves a nonradiative energy transfer between the excimer and the iron site due to the mechanical strain induced by spin transitions. For the case of Tb­(III)–SCO composite, the luminescence effect is related to a partial reabsorption mechanism according to which emitted photons from the Tb­(III) complex are reabsorbed by the SCO NP due to its low extinction coefficient in the LS state and the large distances between the Fe­(II) – Tb­(III) sites. Radiative energy-transfer mechanisms likely drive luminescence modulation in CdTe–SCO nanocomposites, although the magnitude of modulation varies with the distribution and encapsulation of the quantum dots. The luminescence intensity of the CdTe quantum dots was found to decrease reversibly during the spin transition from the HS to the LS state, attributed to spectral overlap between the CdTe emission and the LS-state absorption of the SCO complex.

Finally, SCO thin films are fabricated using methods such as spin-coating, sequential assembly, or vacuum deposition. − These films integrate SCO complexes with luminophores to achieve luminescence modulation. Bilayer films of [Fe­(HB­(tz)3)2], where tz = 1,2,4-triazol-1-yl, and Ir­(ppy)3, where ppy = 2-phenylpyridine, show reversible luminescence switching due to UV absorption by the LS state of the SCO complex. Similarly, polyfluorene-based SCO films exhibit fluorescence enhancement during the LS-to-HS transition, driven by spin-transition-induced structural changes. SCO thin films fabricated with [Fe­(dpp)2]·(BF4)2 (where dpp = 2,6-di­(pyrazol-1-yl)­pyridine) and doped with chlorophyll-α or acridine orange, , exhibit reversible luminescence switching. In these systems, luminescence is regulated by spin-state transitions, which alter the energy levels responsible for electron transport. These films have been integrated into optoelectronic devices, such as organic light-emitting devices (OLEDs).

In this study, we aim to investigate the interplay between spin-crossover (SCO) behavior and luminescence in nanoparticles doped with trivalent Tb ions. Building on previous advancements in SCO-luminescent systems, we focus on the synthesis and characterization of silica-coated SCO nanoparticles integrated with Tb ions to enhance their multifunctional properties. By employing techniques such as reverse micelle synthesis, structural characterization, and spectroscopic analysis, we explore the impact of Tb doping on the magnetic, structural, and optical properties of the nanoparticles. The morphological characteristics of the nanoparticles, including shape and size, were investigated by Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM). X-ray Absorption Fine Structure (XAFS) and X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS) were employed to determine the bonding environment and oxidation state of Fe and Tb. It should be noted that XAFS has been applied to Fe-triazole SCO NPs to investigate changes in the Fe bonding environment and electronic structure upon switching of the Fe spin state. − The heating-induced spin state transition and its effect on the magnetic and optical properties were studied with DC magnetic susceptibility measurements and optical luminescence.

Experimental Section

Materials and Methods

All manipulations were performed under aerobic conditions using reagents and solvents (Alfa Aesar, Sigma-Aldrich, Serva) as received. The ligands 1H-1,2,4-triazole (Htrz), the tetraethoxysilane (TEOS), and (3-aminopropyl)­triethoxysilane (APTES) were purchased from Alfa-Aesar, while Fe­(BF4)2·6H2O, Tb­(NO3)3·6H2O, and n-hexanol from Sigma-Aldrich. Triton X-100 and cyclohexane were obtained from Serva and used without further purification. The deionized water used for synthesis was deoxygenated by simultaneous sonication and argon bubbling for 1 h.

Elemental analyses (C, H, N) were performed at the University of Patras (Greece) using in-house facilities. IR spectra (4000–400 cm–1) were recorded using a PerkinElmer 16PC FT-IR spectrometer with samples prepared as KBr pellets. The powder X-ray diffraction (P-XRD) measurements were performed at room temperature on a Malvern Panalytical X’Pert PRO diffractometer with focusing Kα1 geometry. Polycrystalline samples were loaded into 1 mm borosilicate glass capillaries, and the X-ray tube was operated at 45 kV and 40 mA. The incident-beam side (Cu Kα1 radiation, λ = 1.54056 Å) is equipped with a focusing X-ray mirror, a 0.5° fixed divergence slit, 0.5° antiscatter slits, and 0.04 rad Soller slits. In contrast, on the diffracted-beam side, the system was configured with 0.04 rad Soller slits and a PIXcel1D detector with antiscatter shielding. Four scans were performed in Debye–Scherrer mode, with a step size of 0.0066° on a spinning stage (∼300 rpm) over a 2θ range of 4.0–90.0°. No radiation damage was observed even after 5 h of measurement; therefore, all scans were merged to increase counting statistics.

The TEM study was performed utilizing/using a FEI CM20 TEM operating at 200 kV. TEM specimens were prepared by drop casting a 3 μL droplet of nanoparticle suspension in acetone on a carbon-coated Cu TEM gridthe size of the particles was determined by “manual counting” using the ImageJ software (https://imagej.net). High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) images and elemental distribution maps were acquired using a FEI Titan transmission electron microscope operating at 60–300 kV.

The direct-current (DC) magnetic susceptibility measurements were performed on powder samples using a physical properties measurement system (PPMS, Quantum Design) at temperatures ranging from 2 to 300 K with a rate of 0.5 K min–1 under an applied DC magnetic field of 1000 Oe. The experimental data were corrected for the sample holder’s diamagnetism and signal, and Pascal constants were used for the diamagnetic corrections.

Differential Scanning Calorimetry (DSC) measurements were carried out in a N2 (g) atmosphere using a DSC instrument (Q100, TA Instruments, USA). Aluminum hermetic pans were used to encapsulate 5–7 mg of the sample. The pans were purged with nitrogen at 50 mLmin–1, and liquid nitrogen was used for cooling.

The XAFS measurements were conducted at the LISA beamline of the European Synchrotron Radiation Facility (ESRF). Spectra were recorded at the Fe–K and Tb-L3 edges in both the transmission and fluorescence yield modes. Powder sample pellets were prepared by mixing with cellulose in appropriate proportions to minimize self-absorption and achieve proper transmittance. A 4-element energy-dispersive fluorescence Si drift detector (ARDESIA) enabled energy discrimination of the Fe fluorescence signal by setting Regions Of Interest (ROI). This was necessary because the Tb L3 edge is located approximately 400 eV above the Fe–K edge. Where no edge overlap occurred, transmission mode was preferred due to its superior signal-to-noise ratio. A Fe foil was used to correct monochromator-induced energy shifts. Measurements were conducted at low temperatures (80–100 K) using a liquid nitrogen cryostat to minimize damping of the XAFS signal due to thermal disorder. Background subtraction, photoelectron scattering path construction, and fitting of the Extended XAFS (EXAFS) spectra were performed using the ATHENA, FEFF8, and FEFFIT packages, respectively. − X-ray Fluorescence (XRF) spectra were recorded using the same setup to determine the Tb/Fe atomic ratio.

The surface analysis measurements were performed in a UHV chamber (P ∼ 5 × 10–10 mbar) equipped with a SPECS Phoibos 100–1D-DLD hemispherical electron analyzer and a nonmonochromatized dual-anode Mg/Al X-ray source for XPS. The XP Spectra were recorded with Al Kα at a photon energy of 1486.6 eV, and an analyzer pass energy of 15 eV, resulting in a Full Width at Half Maximum (fwhm) of 0.85 eV for the Ag 3d5/2 line. The analyzed area was a 7 × 10 mm2 rectangle. For spectra collection and analysis, including fitting, the commercial software SpecsLab Prodigy (by Specs GmbH, Berlin) was used. The atomic ratios were calculated from the intensity (peak area) of the XPS peaks, weighted by the corresponding relative sensitivity factors (RSFs), and considering the analyzer’s transmission function. The samples were in powder form, and for XPS measurements, they were pressed into pellets.

A temperature-dependent PL measurement was performed using a Horiba Fluorolog Spectrophotometer System utilizing a xenon lamp. The powder sample was placed between two thin (500 μm) glass coverslips, sealed, and mounted on a Janis cryostat. The temperature was controlled and monitored by an Oxford Instruments ITC, which cycled between 295 and 400 K. The sample was photoexcited using a xenon lamp filtered to emit at 500 nm, with the PL being scanned and collected on a photodetector over the spectral range from 520 to 570 nm with a 0.5 nm resolution.

Synthesis of NPs

[Fe­(HTrz)2(trz)]­(BF4)·0.7H2O·0.3 Acetone (NP1)

An aqueous solution of Fe­(BF4)2·6H2O (1.00 mmol) in 0.5 mL of deionized H2O was added to a solution containing Triton X-100 (1.8 mL), n-hexanol (1.8 mL), and cyclohexane (7.5 mL). The resulting mixture was stirred for 30 min, during which a clear water-in-oil microemulsion formed. A similar procedure was applied to HTrz (4 mmol) in 0.5 mL of deionized H2O. Both microemulsions were combined quickly, and the mixture was stirred for 48 h, after which acetone was added to break the microemulsions. The precipitated nanoparticles were isolated by centrifugation at 6000 rpm, washed several times with EtOH, then with acetone, and finally dried under vacuum. Results and Discussion

General Synthetic Aspects and Morphological Characterization

Nanoparticles of the SCO polymer [Fe­(HTrz)2(Trz)]·(BF4) (NP1), the silica-covered NP2, and the Tb­(III)-doped NP3 were prepared by the reverse micelle method at ambient conditions. Two separate aqueous solutions were initially prepared in ambient conditions. An aqueous solution of Fe­(II) salt and an aqueous solution containing the stoichiometric amount of the ligand (HTrz), where [Fe]/[HTrz] = 1/4. Each of these was mixed with a previously prepared solution of Triton X-100 (surfactant), n-hexanol (cosurfactant), and cyclohexane (solvent). To prepare stable microemulsions, the two mixtures were shaken with a vortex system (2 min) and vigorously stirred for 30 min. The two microemulsions were mixed by adding the ligand to the metal and stirring for several hours to allow for micellar exchange. The above procedure was repeated several times to confirm the reproducibility of NP1 synthesis. TEM measurements revealed spherical NPs with a mean size of 32 nm (Figures , and S3).

TEM images of NP1, NP2, and NP3 SCO NPs. TEM-HAADF of NP3, and (c) Individual STEM-XEDS mapping of elemental Fe (red), Tb (green), and an overlay of elemental Fe (red), Tb (green), and Si (blue).

For NP2 and a [Fe]/[HTrz] ratio of 1/3, a two-step hydrolysis/condensation of TEOS/APTES was performed, following an experimental protocol established by our group for effective silica coating of the SCO NPs. According to this protocol, TEOS is added to the aqueous phase (first step). 24 h after the micellar exchange, the microemulsions are treated with APTES, followed by the addition of TEOS 30 min later, with further stirring for 24 h (second step). The final NPs exhibit nonhomogeneous SiO2 coverage, as confirmed by other groups, , while the average NP2 size is 65 nm (Figures , and S3). When TEOS/APTES are introduced into the nanodroplets the effective saturation is changed: the silane hydrolysis/condensation processes alter the internal water volume. Under these altered conditions, the 1:4 [Fe]/[HTrz] ratio led to flocculation/aggregation, whereas a 1:3 ratio restored controlled nucleation and produced uniform particles. The actual nature of bonding between the SiO2 shell and Fe/Htrz SCO core is hydrogen bonding or/and physical encapsulation (van der Waals and electrostatic interactions). These interactions are sufficient to stabilize the core@shell structure and can influence spin-crossover properties through subtle changes in the local environment.

In the doping scenario, we modified the NP2 synthesis protocol by adding a percentage of Tb salt to the aqueous phase containing the ligand HTrz. The synthetic pathway for NP3 is illustrated in Scheme . TEM images reveal that nanoparticles with an average size of 37 nm (Figures , and S3). Additionally, STEM-XEDS mapping shows that the Tb ions are evenly distributed throughout the area of the individual NPs (Figure ). At the same time, the SiO2 coating exhibits a nonuniform distribution, similar to that observed with NP2. The Fe/Tb ratio, which is equal to 0.045 is confirmed by EDS and XAFS/XRF analysis (Figures S1 and S2). To understand the reaction mechanism of the Tb salt in the reverse micelle experiment, a blank sample (BS) was synthesized following the protocol of NP3, omitting the addition of the Fe­(II) salt.

Structural and Chemical Characterization

Figure S4 shows the FT-IR spectra of NP1-NP3 and BS. In the cases of NP2 and NP3, similar features are exhibited, which correspond to the sharp peaks of the NP1 SCO material overlapped with the characteristic peaks of the SiO2 coating. Several characteristic bands are observed at around 523, 634, and 873 cm–1, which are attributed to the bending vibrations and out-of-plane stretching vibrations of the coordinated triazole ligand. Meanwhile, the bands at around 1221, 1453, and 1496 cm–1 correspond to the ring stretching. The bands at 483 and 742 cm–1 could be assigned to two different bending vibrations of the Si–O–Si group. In the case of NP3, a characteristic band at 1385 cm–1 is also observed, revealing the inclusion of NO3 – anions in the NPs. This was further confirmed by the FT-IR spectrum of BS, which showed the same band, along with the characteristic vibrations of the Si–O–Si group. In the same spectrum, the characteristic bands of the coordinated triazole ligand are absent, revealing no direct bonding of the Tb ions with the triazole ligand.

P-XRD measurements have been carried out and presented in Figure S5. The diffraction patterns for NP1, NP2, and NP3 are similar and correspond to polymorph II of the coordination polymer [FeII(Htrz)2(trz)]·(BF4). The Le-Bail method was employed to obtain the lattice parameters, and the results are shown in Table S1. For the case of NP3, no significant changes in the lattice parameters are monitored, indicating that the Tb ions do not replace host Fe­(II) ions, in accordance with the FT-IR study, or occupy interstitial sites in the lattice. It should be noted that the P-XRD diffractogram of the BS sample reveals an amorphous silica phase.

XPS and XAFS Study

The surface-sensitive chemical speciation of NP1-NP3 and BS was investigated by XPS. Figure S6 shows the C 1s peak from NP1-NP3 and BS samples, which is deconvoluted into three components at binding energies 285.0 ± 0.1 eV assigned to aliphatic carbon, C–C­(sp3), at 285.7 ± 0.1 eV, and at 286.6 eV assigned to N–C­(sp2) and N–C­(sp3) bonds, respectively. Similarly, the N 1s peak, shown in Figure S6, is analyzed into three components centered at 399.6 ± 0.1, 400.5 ± 0.1, and 402.0 ± 0.1 eV, representing pyrrolic (triazole), imine-like N (-N = ) bonded with Fe atoms and protonated amino groups (NH3 +), respectively. , Moreover, the N 1s spectra of the reference samples HTrz ligand, APTES, and NP1 were measured and are also shown in Figure S6. It is clear that the N 1s components at 400.5 and 399.6 eV are present in the APTES and triazole reference samples, whereas the peak component at ∼ 402.2 ± 0.1 eV is present in NP2, BS, and traces on NP3 denoting protonated amino group (NH3 +) components in their silica surface. A peak at a binding energy of ∼ 407 eV is present in the BS sample and is attributed to nitrate anions (NO3 –), consistent with FTIR results. However, this peak was not detected in the NP3 sample due to the low concentration of Tb, which was found to be 0.2% based on % relative atomic concentrations derived from the peak areas of C 1s, Fe 2p, N 1s, O 1s, Si 2p, and Tb 3d, divided by the relative sensitivity factors and the analyzer’s transmission function (Table S2). The intensity of the protonated amino group (NH3 +) component is higher in the BS sample. Figure S7 shows the Si 2p peak from the NP2, BS, and NP3 samples. The peak at 103.4 eV is assigned to the SiO2 network in the APTES and TEOS components. Figure S7 also shows the Fe 2p doublet from NP2 and NP3 with spin–orbit splitting of 13 eV, and the Fe 2p3/2 position is at 708.8 eV, assigned to Fe­(II). In the same figure, the Tb 3d XPS core-level spectra of the NP3 and BS samples are shown. As the concentration of Tb in both samples is very low (0.2% in NP3 and 0.8% in BS), the intensity of the Tb 3d peaks is also low, rendering the accurate determination of the binding energy difficult. Furthermore, since the Tb 3d5/2 peak overlaps with the C KLL Auger peak, only the Tb 3d3/2 peak can be analyzed. The binding energy is located at ∼ 1275.4 eV (3d3/2) and is ascribed to the Tb­(III) state in both NP3 and BS. However, in NP3, a shoulder at a higher binding energy, ∼ 1278 eV, is ascribed to a higher oxidation state of Tb (i.e., Tb­(IV) chemical state), which could indicate a superficial TbO x , x ∼ 2, on the NP3 surface. The relative atomic ratio of Tb: Fe in sample NP3 is 0.11. This ratio is higher than that derived by XRF spectroscopy (Tb/Fe = 0.045) and the nominal (0.04) due to the different information depth of XPS (less than 8 nm).

To further elucidate the chemical environment of the Tb ions, XAFS measurements were performed. The X-ray Near Edge Structure (XANES) spectra, recorded at the Fe–K and Tb-L3 edges, of the NP1, BS, and NP3 samples are shown in Figure . The spectra were subjected to subtraction of the pre-edge line and normalization to the edge jump. The position of the white line in the Tb-L3-edge XANES spectra (7519.5 eV), which corresponds to 2p3/2 → 5d transitions, is characteristic of Tb­(III) species. , Slight differences in white line intensity and spectral features around 7535 eV between the two spectra, indicate a slightly different bonding environment of Tb in the two samples. In contrast, the addition of Tb in NP3 does not considerably affect the Fe–K-edge XANES spectrum, revealing that no notable changes in the bonding geometry of Fe occur, in accordance with the XRD characterization. The inset in the bottom panel of Figure shows the magnification of the two low-intensity peaks observed below the Fe–K absorption edge. The single peak at 7112.7 eV, denoted as “a”, is assigned to 1s→3d transitions of Fe­(II) in the low-spin state. Its low intensity is characteristic of the octahedral coordination of Fe­(II). The peak denoted as “b” located at 7116.0 eV could be attributed to 1s→4p + LMCT (Ligand to Metal Charge Transfer) shakedown processes.

The χ­(k) spectra, obtained by subtracting the atomic background and transforming to k-space, were fitted in both k- and R-space using appropriate models. In the case of the Fe–K-edge spectra, the structure of Fe that is octahedrally bonded with 6 triazole rings to form chains [Figure a] was used as a starting model (produced from the XRD data). Since the atomic numbers of N and C differ only by 1, the photoelectron scattering paths were constructed using only C atoms. The bonding of Fe with the planar triazole rings results in a considerable number of multiple scattering paths [they are shown in Figure a]. The distances that correspond to the multiple scattering paths were fixed according to the distances of the related single scattering paths, considering a rigid triazole ring. The fitting results are listed in Table S3, which show that the introduction of Tb into the NP3 nanoparticles does not affect the Fe bonding configuration. The Tb-L3-edge χ­(k) spectra and the corresponding FT amplitudes, shown in Figure b, reveal substantial differences between the BS and NP3 samples. The BS exhibits lower FT amplitude and less structure than NP3, indicating that the Tb ions are trapped in the SiO2 matrix. Magnetic and Optical Study

DC magnetic measurements were performed in the temperature range of 200 to 400 K and an external DC field of 1000 Oe. Three thermal cycles were performed at 1 K/min, and the third is shown in Figure in the form of mass susceptibility multiplied by the temperature vs temperature. Because the Tb ion’s chemical environment is complex, a reliable molecular weight or formula for NP3 could not be defined. Thus, magnetic results will be presented as mass susceptibilities for all compounds. The SCO NPs reveal a hysteretic behavior with critical temperatures T 1/2↑ = 375 K, T 1/2↓ = 345 K (ΔΤ = 32 Κ) for NP1, T 1/2↑ = 380 K, T 1/2↓ = 337 K (ΔΤ = 43 Κ) for NP2 and T 1/2↑ = 393 K, T 1/2↓ = 348 K (ΔΤ = 45 Κ) for NP3 (Figure ). The critical temperatures were calculated from the first derivative of the temperature-dependent susceptibility data and are shown in the same Figure. The cooperativity of spin transition in NP2 is significantly enhanced when nanoparticles are encapsulated within a silica shell, achieving a maximum thermal transition width of approximately 43 K. This phenomenon indicates that the silica shell effectively transmits elastic vibrations generated during the low-spin to high-spin (LS–HS) transition to adjacent nanoparticles, thereby increasing the efficiency of interparticle interactions and amplifying cooperativity. The nonzero value of χMT at low temperatures for the case of NP1 and NP2 is related to the fact that even at low temperatures, not all Fe­(II) centers convert to the low-spin state. This is attributed to surface effects and incomplete cooperativity in smaller nanoparticles, where surface atoms or defects may remain in the high-spin state due to reduced elastic interactions or altered local environment.

NP3 was excited at 500 nm resonantly which is correlated with the 7F J →5D4 transition of Tb. Interestingly, at higher-energy excitations, the NP3 did not exhibit any absorption or emission features, which is consistent with the complexation mechanism proposed within the silica shell. Despite the lack of nanoantenna effect which usually lead to high absorption cross-section and enhance photoluminescence, resonant excitation revealed the characteristic emission at lower energy transitions of Tb 5D4 → 7FJ (J = 6–4) at 543, 584, and 611 nm, respectively (Figure ). It should be pointed here that several of these emission peaks are overlapped with the absorption band of the [Fe­(HTrz)2(Trz)]·(BF4) in the LS state. In contrast, the absence of absorption bands in the high-spin (HS) state of the SCO system promotes the emergence of Tb emission peaks. Therefore, the most intense 5D4-7F5 transition (543 nm), is near the absorption maximum of the SCO system at 530 nm. These spectral characteristics facilitate the modulation of emission intensity. PL peak intensity at 543 nm for NP3 was measured as temperatures cycled from 295 to 400 K. Figure shows that PL intensity clearly depends on sample temperature, changing in response to transition between the complex’s spin states. While heating, PL has been initially reduced before it starts to monotonically increase at approximately 370 K, with the maximum PL value being reached at 400 K. During the cooling process, a step-like reduction of the peak PL intensity is observed at around 340–350 K resulting in a hysteric-loop behavior of the PL during the thermal cycling experiment (Figure ). For comparison reasons the thermal hysteretic behavior of the magnetic susceptibility of NP3 is also shown in the same Figure. These findings underscore the reversible modulation of emission by spin-state transitions. This behavior can be attributed to the SCO between the HS and LS electronic states of Fe­(II) that occurs during thermal cycling of the sample. The maximum PL enhancement at 543 nm was found to be only 6% and is related to the overall quenching of Tb photoluminescence due to the nonradiative decay mechanism caused by the interactions between the hydroxyl groups and the adsorbed water molecules or silanol groups present in the silica framework. Interestingly, changes in luminescence intensity during spin crossover phenomenon were possible to be recorded and two primary mechanisms are proposed: a) Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET) involving nonradiative energy transfer between the donor Tb­(III) complex (Figure b) and acceptor, the SCO complex and b) reabsorption of emitted photons by the SCO complex, given the low extinction coefficient of [Fe­(HTrz)2(Trz)]·(BF4), which results from the forbidden d–d transition in the low-spin (LS) state. Further experiments involving temperature dependent luminescence lifetime measurements, as well as Monte Carlo theoretical investigation are under way to clarify the luminescence mechanism.

Conclusions

This study reports the synthesis and characterization of spin-crossover (SCO) nanoparticles (NPs) of the 1D coordination polymer [Fe­(HTrz)2(Trz)]·(BF4) exhibiting both magnetic and luminescent properties, with a particular emphasis on the influence of trivalent Tb ions. The nanoparticles were synthesized via a reverse micelle method, yielding three distinct types: pristine (NP1), silica covered (NP2), and Tb-doped (NP3) NPs. TEM and STEM-XEDS analyses confirmed that the distribution of Tb ions in NP3 was homogeneous. The silica shell was identified as nonuniform, and the Tb/Fe atomic ratio was quantified using XRF and EDS spectroscopy and found to be 0.045.

XPS and XAFS analysis further elucidated the coordination environment of Tb, indicating its interaction with SiO4 tetrahedra and nitrate ions in NP3. EXAFS fitting results revealed that Tb­(III) ions formed mixed mono- and binuclear complexes within the silica matrix, accompanied by considerable structural disorder.

Magnetic susceptibility measurements demonstrated the largest thermal hysteresis width (ΔT = 45 K) for NP3 and a shift of the critical temperatures to higher values. The presence of a silica shell enhanced cooperativity during spin transitions. At the same time, the incorporation of Tb ions further modulated the magnetic properties, most likely due to interactions between the nanoparticle core and the Tb-doped silica shell.

Temperature-dependent photoluminescence (PL) measurements of NP3 revealed a hysteresis loop during thermal cycling, attributable to spin-crossover transitions between high-spin and low-spin states. Modulation of PL intensity was influenced by cooperative effects and internal stresses within the material system, underscoring the synergy between SCO and luminescence properties.

In conclusion, the incorporation of Tb ions into silica-coated SCO nanoparticles enhanced their multifunctional properties, including modulation of luminescence and spin-crossover behavior. These findings contribute to the development of advanced materials for applications in optoelectronics, sensing, and thermometry. Further investigation of the interactions between dopant ions and SCO systems is expected to provide deeper insights for optimizing these bifunctional materials.
