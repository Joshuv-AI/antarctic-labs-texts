Intra-arrest systemic thrombolytic therapy during cardiopulmonary resuscitation: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Soheil Dehghani, Shinichiro Ohshimo, Dean Giustini, Bernd W Böttiger, Nikolaos Nikolaou, Keith Couper, Tommaso Scquizzato, Brian Grunau
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSystemic thrombolysis has been proposed as a routine treatment for cardiac arrest, given potential benefits in treating fibrin formation and/or in treating the etiology of the cardiac arrest (e.g. ST-elevation myocardial infarction and pulmonary embolism [PE]). This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to evaluate the effect of intra-arrest thrombolysis on survival and neurological outcomes in patients with cardiac arrest.MethodsWe searched MEDLINE, EMBASE, CENTRAL, and the Web of Science from inception until September 30, 2025 for randomized controlled trials evaluating systemic thrombolytic therapy versus standard care during cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR). The primary outcome was survival to hospital discharge, and the secondary outcomes were favorable neurological outcome at hospital discharge, return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC), and survival to hospital admission. We performed meta-analyses using a random-effects model. We evaluated bleeding complications and examined Utstein-based subgroups. We evaluated risk of bias using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 tool and evidence certainty using GRADE.ResultsWe included three RCTs, which enrolled a total of 1318 participants. Thrombolysis did not improve survival (RR 0.86; 95% CI 0.65–1.14) or favorable neurological outcome (RR 0.99; 95% CI 0.69–1.40) at hospital discharge. No benefit was observed for ROSC or survival to admission. Thrombolysis increased the risk of any intracranial hemorrhage (RR 6.96, 95% CI 1.59–30.47). Among cases with bystander CPR, thrombolysis led to decreased survival; other subgroup analyses were neutral.ConclusionsAvailable evidence demonstrates that routine intra-arrest thrombolysis in unselected patients does not improve hospital-discharge survival, however does increase bleeding risk.Keywords: Intra-arrest thrombolysis, Fibrinolytic therapy, Out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA), Cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR), Neurological outcomes, Bleeding complications

Introduction

Cardiac arrest (CA) is a leading cause of mortality worldwide, accounting for an estimated 3.7 million deaths annually.1 In the United States, more than 350,000 out-of-hospital cardiac arrests (OHCA) are reported each year, with only approximately 10% of emergency medical service (EMS)-treated cases surviving to hospital discharge.1, 2 Comparable survival rates have been reported across Europe, as well as in other regions worldwide.3, 4 Cardiac arrest may result from a broad range of causes, including acute coronary syndromes (ACS) or other primary cardiac disorders, pulmonary embolism (PE), drug toxicity, intracranial hemorrhage, and other vascular emergencies.5 Identifying the precise etiology during resuscitation is inherently challenging, and treatment is typically initiated empirically without diagnostic certainty.6

Several etiologies of CA, namely ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) and PE, include thrombolysis as a potential treatment option. Thus, thrombolysis has been considered as a potential empiric treatment for CA, especially if a PE is suspected.7 PE may account for 2–10% of OHCA cases and is associated with high mortality.8, 9 Current international resuscitation guidelines recommend consideration of systemic thrombolysis for confirmed or strongly suspected PE-related cardiac arrest, based on non-randomized studies, pathophysiological rationale, and extrapolation from non-arrest populations.10, 11 STEMI, involving a complete blockage of a coronary artery causing ST elevation on ECG, is also a precipitant of OHCA.12, 13 Although primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) remains the gold-standard reperfusion strategy,14, 15 thrombolytic therapy remains an alternate viable therapeutic.16, 17 Beyond treating specific etiologies of cardiac arrest, thrombolysis has also been proposed as a potential treatment option due to its potential to treat intravascular clotting and fibrin formation that may develop during cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR),18 which may impede cerebral microcirculation and lead to ischemia.19, 20

We performed a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) to investigate the potential benefit of routine thrombolytic therapy during CPR for both OHCA and in-hospital cardiac arrest (IHCA). This review informed the International Liaison Committee on Resuscitation (ILCOR) consensus on science and treatment recommendations.2

Methods

Review oversight and reporting standards

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted under the guidance of the ILCOR Advanced Life Support (ALS) Task Force, within the framework of ILCOR’s ongoing systematic evidence evaluation.21 The review follows the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) statement,22 and the protocol was prospectively registered with the Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; CRD420251121194). All methods were specified a priori.

Eligibility criteria

The research question was developed using the PICOST framework (Population, Intervention, Comparator, Outcomes, Study design, Timeframe):

•Population: Adults or children of any age with either IHCA or OHCA who were receiving active conventional CPR.

Definitions

Systemic thrombolytic therapy was defined as the administration of any fibrinolytic agent (e.g., alteplase, tenecteplase, reteplase, streptokinase, urokinase) via a systemic route (e.g., intravenous, intraosseous, etc.) during ongoing CPR (intra-arrest). Survival to hospital discharge was defined as the patient being discharged alive from the index hospitalization, irrespective of neurological status. ROSC was defined as a palpable pulse for any period of time. Favorable neurological outcome was defined as Cerebral Performance Category (CPC) 1–2 or equivalent.23 Bleeding complications were categorized according to study-specific definitions and grouped when possible.

Information sources and search strategy

A health sciences librarian (DG) developed comprehensive search strategies in MEDLINE (Ovid) and translated them for EMBASE (Ovid), and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL) from inception to September 30, 2025. The strategies were peer-reviewed by the study team.

To increase search sensitivity, we searched the Web of Science Core Collection, reviewed references of included studies and relevant reviews, and consulted content experts to identify additional eligible publications. Our initial searches were re-run immediately prior to the final analysis to capture recently-published studies. Gray literature was identified through extended searching in the references of our relevant seed papers and in the Web of Science.

We also used Undermind.ai (an artificial intelligence [AI]-powered search tool)—to identify any additional citations missed by the above search methods and to verify our search yields.24

Study selection

References were uploaded (DG) into EndNote to detect and remove duplicate records. Unique records were imported into Covidence for screening. At least two members of the study team (SD, SO, NN, BG) independently screened each title and abstract against the pre-specified eligibility criteria, excluding studies that did not meet inclusion requirements. Disagreements within pairs were resolved by discussion, and if consensus could not be reached, a third reviewer adjudicated.

Full-text articles considered potentially eligible were then assessed independently, again by a minimum of two reviewers. Disagreements within pairs were resolved by discussion, and if consensus could not be reached, a third reviewer adjudicated. Reasons for exclusion at the full-text stage were recorded, and the overall study selection process was illustrated in the PRISMA flow diagram.

Data extraction

A standardized data extraction form was developed and piloted in Microsoft Excel. Two pairs of reviewers (BG, SD) independently extracted data in duplicate, with discrepancies resolved through discussion and, when necessary, arbitration by a third reviewer. Extracted items included study characteristics (first author, year of publication, country, study design, sample size), population characteristics (adult vs. pediatric, in-hospital vs. out-of-hospital cardiac arrest), arrest characteristics (setting, initial rhythm, witnessed status), CPR variables (bystander CPR, time to CPR initiation, time to ROSC), intervention details (thrombolytic agent, dose, route, timing relative to CPR), comparator details, and all outcomes of interest. Extracted datasets were entered into Review Manager (RevMan Web, Cochrane Collaboration) for meta-analysis.

Risk of bias assessment

Two reviewers (KC, TS) independently assessed each trial using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool25 for randomized trials across the domains of randomization process. Disagreements were resolved by discussion to reach consensus.

Synthesis of results

For all binary outcomes (e.g., ROSC and survival measures), we calculated risk ratios (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) using a random-effects model. When essential data required to compute effect estimates were missing or unclear, we attempted to contact study authors for clarification.

Between-trial heterogeneity was quantified using Chi2-based Cochran’s Q test and the I2 statistic, with I2 values above 50% considered evidence of substantial inconsistency. Owing to the anticipated variability in trial populations and interventions, random-effects models were prespecified and used for primary analyses. Primary meta-analyses used the Mantel–Haenszel (M–H) method under a random-effects model with DerSimonian–Laird (DL) estimator for between-study variance (τ2). Forest plots were generated to present effect estimates from individual studies alongside pooled results.

Subgroup analyses were planned a priori to examine potential effect modification by clinically relevant factors, including presenting rhythm (shockable vs. non-shockable), witness status (witnessed vs. unwitnessed), provision of bystander CPR (present vs. absent), and OHCA vs IHCA. RevMan’s chi-square (Q_between) test for interaction was used for the test of subgroup differences.

Sensitivity analyses were pre-specified to assess the robustness of findings and included exclusion of studies at high overall risk of bias, exclusion of trials with major protocol deviations, exclusion of studies that used intracoronary administration, repeating analyses under a fixed-effect model, omission of studies with imputed data, and leave-one-out analyses.

Statistical analyses were conducted using Review Manager (RevMan Web, Cochrane Collaboration). Statistical significance was defined using a two-sided alpha level of 0.05. The PRISMA 2020 checklist, as well as the templated data collection form, extracted data, and data used for analysis, can be found in the Supplement.

Results

Study selection (PRISMA)

Our search strategy (Supplementary Figs. 1–4) identified 637 records from MEDLINE (n = 237), CENTRAL (n = 191), Embase (n = 112), and Web of Science (n = 97). After removal of 72 duplicates (71 through Covidence and one manually), 565 unique records remained for screening (Fig. 1). Of these, 20 full-text articles were retrieved and assessed for eligibility, of which seventeen were excluded. The AI-facilitated search did not identify any missed studies. Ultimately, three RCTs enrolling 1318 patients were included in this systematic review.

PRISMA 2020 flow diagram.

Study characteristics

The three included RCTs, Abu-Laban et al. (2002; Canada; n = 233),26 Fatovich et al. (2004; Australia; n = 35),27 and Böttiger et al. (2008; Europe; n = 1050),28 were parallel-group, double-blind, placebo-controlled trials evaluating systemic fibrinolysis during ongoing CPR. While sharing similar methodological designs, they differed in setting, timing of intervention, and enrolled populations (Supplementary Table 1).

Abu-Laban et al. conducted a single-center RCT which enrolled 233 adult patients with cardiac arrest in the out-of-hospital or Emergency Department (ED) settings who had >1 min of pulseless electrical activity (PEA), and randomized to alteplase IV 100 mg (over 15 min) or placebo. The primary endpoint was survival to hospital discharge. Fatovich et al. performed an ED-based pilot trial, enrolling OHCA who were transported to the ED, to tenecteplase 50 mg IV vs placebo. The primary outcome was ROSC, but other clinical outcomes were also reported. Böttiger et al. performed the multicenter “TROICA Trial” across 66 EMS systems in 10 European countries, enrolling adult patients with witnessed OHCA of presumed cardiac origin, with participants randomized to Tenecteplase IV or placebo. The study also notes that “treatment with open-label thrombolytic therapy rather than randomization into the trial was permitted for cases in which pulmonary embolism was suspected to be the cause of the cardiac arrest.”.29, 30 The primary outcome was 30-day survival, with additional secondary outcomes reported. Abu-Laban et al. and Böttiger et al. reported data on bleeding events.

Risk of bias assessments can be seen in Supplementary Figs. 5 and 6. For clinical outcomes, risk of bias was assessed as low for Abu-Laban et al. and Böttiger et al., and high for Fatovich et al. (risk of bias arising from randomization process due to concerns regarding the process of allocation concealment and important baseline differences between study groups). For safety outcomes, risk of bias was assessed as moderate for Abu-Laban et al. and Böttiger et al., and high for Fatovich et al.

Primary outcome: survival to hospital discharge

All three RCTs (n = 1299) reported survival to hospital discharge. The pooled estimate did not detect an improvement in survival with intra-arrest thrombolysis compared with placebo (RR 0.86, 95% CI 0.65–1.14), with no evidence of statistical heterogeneity across trials (I2 = 0%; Fig. 2).

Analysis examining the outcome of survival to hospital discharge.

Secondary outcomes

Three RCTs (n = 1266) evaluated survival to hospital discharge with favorable neurological outcome. Not all included trials reported favorable neurological outcome for all randomized patients, resulting in a reduced pooled sample size compared with other outcomes. The pooled analysis did not detect an improvement with intra-arrest thrombolysis compared with placebo (RR 0.99, 95% CI 0.69–1.40; I2 = 0%; Fig. 3).

Analysis examining favorable neurological outcome at hospital discharge.

Results were consistent across all secondary outcomes (ROSC, survival to hospital admission, and 24-h survival): thrombolytic therapy did not demonstrate evidence of benefit compared with standard care (Fig. 4 and Supplemental Figs. 7, 8).

Analysis examining outcome of return of spontaneous circulation.

Safety outcomes

Pooling of bleeding outcomes across trials was not feasible due to heterogeneity in definitions. Böttiger et al. reported that “any intracranial hemorrhage” occurred in 14 (2.7%) vs. 2 (0.4%) of those treated with thrombolysis and placebo, respectively (RR 6.96, 95% CI 1.59–30.47; Fig. 5). Symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage occurred in 4 (0.8%) vs 0 (0%) cases (RR 8.93, 95% CI 0.48–165.45; Supplemental Fig. 9). Major non-intracranial hemorrhage occurred in 40 (7.7%) vs 33 (6.4%) cases (RR 1.21, 95% CI 0.77–1.88; Supplemental Fig. 10), most commonly gastrointestinal or airway bleeding. “Symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage” and “major non-intracranial hemorrhage” were defined as bleeding complications in those respective locations “that were considered life-threatening or fatal or that led to hemodynamic compromise requiring intervention”.

Analysis examining safety outcome of “Any intracranial hemorrhage”.

In Abu-Laban et al., two patients (1.7%) in the alteplase arm experienced a “major bleed” vs. no patients (0%) in the placebo arm (RR 4.96, 95% CI 0.24–102.16; Supplemental Fig. 11). Abu-Laban et al. defined cases as having a “major bleed” if “it was overt and associated with either a decrease in the hemoglobin of at least 2.0 g per deciliter or a need for transfusion of 2 or more units of blood, or if it was retroperitoneal or intracranial.”.26, 31

Fatovich et al. did not report systematic safety data, although no fatal hemorrhage was described.

Subgroup analyses of the primary outcome

Among patients who received bystander CPR, thrombolytic therapy was associated with a lower likelihood of survival compared with placebo (RR 0.55, 95% CI 0.35–0.87; Supplementary Fig. 12). No difference was observed among patients without bystander CPR (RR 1.11, 95% CI 0.78–1.59). We did not detect a difference when stratified by initial shockable (RR 0.80, 95% CI 0.60–1.06) or non-shockable (RR 1.04, 95% CI 0.44–2.46) rhythms (Supplementary Fig. 13). We did not detect a difference among those with witnessed arrests (RR 1.17, 95% CI 0.31–4.44); unwitnessed arrests were too infrequent for reliable estimation (Supplementary Fig. 14). There was insufficient data to examine subgroups of OHCA and IHCA.

Sensitivity analyses

In the pre-specified sensitivity analyses, exclusion of the only study judged to have high overall risk of bias (Fatovich et al.) did not materially change the effect estimate of the primary analysis (RR 0.87, 95% CI 0.66–1.15) (Supplemental Fig. 15). We did not identify any studies with major protocol deviations, intracoronary thrombolytic administration, or imputed data, and therefore these planned sensitivity analyses were not performed. Leave-one-out analyses yielded consistent results.

Discussion

In this systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials including 1318 participants from three RCTs27, 28, 32 intra-arrest thrombolysis did not improve ROSC, survival, or favorable neurological outcomes at hospital discharge. However, thrombolysis resulted in an increased incidence of bleeding. Overall, available evidence indicates that empiric thrombolysis treatment during undifferentiated cardiac arrest does not confer clinical advantage but increases the risk of bleeding complications.

Although limited by risk of Type II error, we explored several subgroups to assess whether any population appeared to benefit from thrombolysis. All subgroup analyses were consistent with the main analysis, with the exception of the subgroup of patients who received bystander CPR where thrombolytic-treated patients had a lower proportion who survived to hospital discharge. Given the large number of comparisons, this result may be spurious and must be interpreted with caution. The mechanism underlying this finding remains uncertain, however the hypothesis that early compression-supported circulation might enhance the benefits of fibrinolytic delivery was not supported by the results. Among patients with initial shockable rhythms, the point estimate was suggestive of worse outcomes, however this did not reach statistical significance. Given that shockable rhythms often reflect acute coronary syndromes, this pattern may relate to underlying coronary pathology but should be interpreted cautiously.

Safety remains a major limiting factor for intra-arrest thrombolysis. In the TROICA trial,28 the incidence of intracranial hemorrhage was nearly sevenfold higher among patients receiving tenecteplase than among those receiving placebo, although the absolute number of events was low. Earlier single-center studies15 suggested an acceptable safety profile, but their limited sample sizes precluded reliable assessment of rare complications. Further, given that most cases died (with an overall median length of stay of less than one day),26 the majority likely did not undergo assessment for bleeding complications, and thus it is possible that many patients with bleeding complications were missed which would result in an underestimate of the bleeding complication incidence.

Major international guidelines recommend considering thrombolytic medications for cases in whom pulmonary embolism is suspected or confirmed.33, 34 Notably, the TROICA trial, which contributed the majority (n = 1050) of patients to the pooled analyses, allowed clinicians to exclude patients suspected to be due to PE.28 In contrast, PE was one of the main rationales cited for performing the Abu-Laben et al. study, however patients were not individually risk stratified for this diagnosis.26 Overall, it is not possible to know how many cases with PE were included in this systematic review, given the lack of post-mortem investigation results.

Thrombolytic therapy as a treatment for cardiac arrest has not demonstrated benefit, despite the existing biological rationale. In cardiac arrests caused by acute coronary thrombosis or pulmonary embolism, persistent vascular obstruction may sustain the arrest state, and systemic fibrinolysis could theoretically restore perfusion even under low-flow conditions.7, 35 Even beyond cases of STEMI and PE, experimental and translational work has shown that fibrinolytic administration during mechanical CPR can transiently improve microvascular flow and myocardial perfusion pressure.30, 36, 37 Furthermore, cardiac arrest is associated with a prothrombotic and inflammatory response characterized by platelet activation and impaired endogenous fibrinolysis, which may promote microvascular obstruction and limit reperfusion.38, 39, 40 In this context, exogenous fibrinolysis could counteract these effects and enhance tissue perfusion. However, pharmacokinetic and safety limitations may constrain these theoretical benefits. Drug delivery during CPR is severely impaired due to low cardiac output and hepatic metabolism, delaying therapeutic onset.41 In addition, systemic fibrinolysis may exacerbate endothelial injury and cerebral hemorrhagic transformation, consistent with the increased incidence of intracranial hemorrhage reported in contemporary trials.7

Our study findings can be compared to observational investigations, some of which have suggested potential benefit. Böttiger et al.42 observed higher rates of return of spontaneous circulation and ICU admission after intra-arrest administration of rt-PA with heparin, while Lederer et al.43 reported improved 24-h survival and hospital discharge in a retrospective analysis. Similarly, Kürkciyan et al.44 and Ruiz-Bailén et al.45 also described better outcomes when thrombolytics were given during arrests secondary to massive PE. However, these early observational reports lacked randomization and frequently enrolled patients with confirmed or presumed thrombotic causes of arrest. As a result, their findings may have overestimated treatment benefit due to selection bias and confounding by indication. Subsequent translational and re-analyses have further delineated the limitations of intra-arrest thrombolysis. Stadlbauer et al.46 found higher hospital admission rates after thrombolysis but no improvement in discharge survival once adjusted for confounders, indicating that early hemodynamic gains did not translate into durable benefit. Renard et al.47 similarly reported increased hospital admissions without better neurological outcomes.

Limitations

The certainty of evidence is limited by the small number of randomized trials and their methodological heterogeneity. Early single-center studies were underpowered, and even the larger multicenter trial was stopped early for futility, reducing statistical power to detect modest effects. Overall, the pooled estimates are heavily influenced by the TROICA trial, which enrolled the majority of patients and accounted for most observed survivors. Variability in thrombolytic agents, dosing regimens, and timing of administration further limits comparability. Cardiac arrest includes a heterogeneous patient population of multiple etiologies; it is possible that certain phenotypes may benefit from thrombolytic therapy. The included trials in this review were conducted between 1998 and 2006; it is unclear how the result may differ if these trials were repeated. Given that contemporary resuscitation techniques demonstrate higher quality of CPR,48, 49 a contemporary thrombolytic trial may show a higher incidence of bleeding complications, or may show increased efficacy of thrombolytics given enhanced drug delivery. Despite our comprehensive search strategy, we may have missed eligible studies.

Conclusions

In this systematic review and meta-analysis of 1318 participants from three randomized trials, routine administration of intra-arrest thrombolytic therapy did not improve survival to hospital discharge or neurological outcomes in cardiac arrest and was associated with an increased risk of intracranial hemorrhage.
