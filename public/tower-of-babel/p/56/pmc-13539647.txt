Constipation outcomes in children with and without autism spectrum disorder: Insights from a tertiary motility service
Authors: Umair Khalid, Rishi Bolia, Joshua Bunt, Ray Lang, Nikhil Thapar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesAutism spectrum disorder (ASD) is commonly associated with gastrointestinal comorbidities, particularly chronic constipation. Despite high clinical burden and frequent tertiary referrals, comparative data describing clinical characteristics, management and outcomes between children with and without ASD—and across ASD severity levels—remain limited. This study compared constipation outcomes at 1 year and the most recent follow‐up between children with and without ASD and assessed whether autism severity influenced prognosis.MethodsWe retrospectively analysed children with chronic constipation managed through a tertiary motility service and enrolled as of 31 January 2024. Demographics, comorbidities, adherence, interventions and outcomes were extracted. Subgroup analyses examined outcomes across ASD severity levels. Statistical comparisons used Chi‐square, Mann–Whitney U and Kruskal–Wallis tests, with significance defined as p < 0.05.ResultsAmong 196 children, 74 (37.7%) had ASD and 122 (62.2%) did not. The ASD cohort had more males (62.2% vs. 45.9%, p = 0.038), lower medication adherence (56.8% vs. 76.2%, p = 0.006) and lower constipation resolution at last follow‐up (11% vs. 25%, p = 0.036). Attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) was more prevalent in the ASD group (50% vs. 13.9%, p = 0.0001). Across ASD severity levels, outcomes, compliance, and therapy needs were broadly similar, though toileting compliance tended to be lowest in Level 3.ConclusionsChildren with ASD demonstrate lower treatment adherence and poorer constipation resolution compared with non‐ASD peers. Outcomes across ASD severity levels are largely similar. Targeted, multidisciplinary strategies are needed to address adherence and optimise outcomes in this high‐risk group.Keywords: functional constipation, paediatric neurogastroenterology and motility, treatment adherence

INTRODUCTION

Constipation is a common gastrointestinal (GI) complaint in children, affecting 10%–30% of the paediatric population, with many meeting Rome IV criteria for functional constipation. 1 Among children with autism spectrum disorder (ASD), the burden is markedly higher, with prevalence estimates between 22% and 50%. 2 , 3 , 4 Functional constipation is the most frequently reported GI issue in ASD and contributes to pain, behavioural challenges and reduced quality of life for children and families. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7

Children with ASD experience functional GI disorders at two to three times the rate of neurotypical peers, with constipation the most prevalent. 2 , 3 , 4 , 8 , 9 Emergency department visits and inpatient admissions are more common, reflecting greater severity and suboptimal outpatient management. 10 , 11 Pathophysiology is multifactorial—altered motility, serotonin signalling, microbiome changes and immune dysfunction have been proposed. 12 , 13 Communication differences often mask classic symptoms, with behavioural cues such as irritability or aggression substituting for reported pain. 14 , 15 Megarectum, withholding and encopresis are frequently observed and can significantly impact wellbeing, perpetuate social isolation and is distressing for families. 6 , 16 , 17 The severity of constipation in ASD may correlate with specific autistic traits, including rigid‐compulsive behaviours, non‐verbal status and sensory sensitivity. 18 , 19 Data from the Autism Treatment Network suggest that children with higher levels of repetitive or obsessive‐compulsive behaviours are more likely to experience mixed bowel symptoms, including severe constipation and faecal incontinence. 18 Food selectivity and restricted diets—common in autistic children—have also been identified as contributing factors, mediating the link between autistic traits and constipation severity. 20

Diagnostic delays are common. 14 , 21 Constipation is often under‐recognised in children with ASD due to limited verbal communication, lack of age‐appropriate self‐report tools and clinician uncertainty about how behaviours map onto GI symptoms. 14 , 21 Sleep disturbances, behavioural escalation and self‐injurious behaviour may be misattributed to psychiatric comorbidity, when they reflect underlying GI distress. 22 A recent review also identified inconsistency in diagnostic approaches across studies, with underuse of standardised criteria such as the Rome IV or Bristol Stool Scale in autistic populations. 23

Healthcare disparities compound the issue. Children with ASD are more likely to receive late referrals, less likely to undergo diagnostic testing, and more often prescribed psychotropics instead of targeted constipation therapy. 10 , 24 First‐line treatments such as polyethylene glycol (PEG) and stool softeners are effective but frequently difficult to administer due to sensory aversion or poor adherence. 25 , 26 Behavioural toileting and dietary modification show variable success, with limited high‐quality data in ASD. 26 , 27 A subset requires escalation to antegrade continence enemas (ACE), nasogastric cleanouts, or surgical intervention for refractory cases. 16 , 28 At our tertiary paediatric motility clinic, referrals for ASD have increased substantially (22.7% in 2020 to 47.5% in 2023). Many present with severe long‐standing constipation. Despite this, little evidence exists on outcomes for this population in specialist settings or whether autistic children respond differently to standard constipation treatments. This study addresses this gap with the primary aim to compare short‐term (1‐year) and most recent follow‐up constipation outcomes in children with and without ASD attending a tertiary paediatric motility clinic. The secondary aim will be to explore the relationship between autism severity and constipation outcomes.

METHODS

Ethics statement

This study was approved by the Children's Health Queensland Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC reference number: HREC/2025/QCHQ/120688). Given the retrospective medical record review design, a waiver of individual patient consent was granted in accordance with the National Statement on Ethical Conduct in Human Research (2007, updated 2018).

Study design

This is a single‐centre retrospective cohort study conducted at the Queensland Children's Hospital (QCH) Gastroenterology Motility Clinic. This study occurred at a tertiary referral centre, and medical records were reviewed for all eligible patients attending the motility clinic between 31 January 2019 and 31 January 2024.

Participants

Participants included all patients aged 0–18 years of age with a diagnosis of functional constipation as per the Rome IV criteria referred to the motility clinic during the study period. This includes all eligible patients attending the motility clinic as of 31 January 2024, and who had a minimum follow‐up of 12 months. The exclusion criteria for this study included those who had an organic cause for their constipation, prior GI surgery (excluding gastrostomy), insufficient data in the medical records or lacked a 12‐month follow‐up in clinic or those who had a prior surgical intervention (for constipation) prior to referral.

Variables and outcomes

Variables included demographics, clinical history, ASD diagnosis and severity, comorbidities, management strategies, and outcomes at 1 year and most recent follow‐up. Autism severity was classified according to the DSM‐5 support levels where available in the clinical record. These levels reflect the degree of support required for social communication and restricted or repetitive behaviours: Level 1 (‘requiring support'), Level 2 (‘requiring substantial support') and Level 3 (‘requiring very substantial support'). 29 In some cases, ASD diagnosis and severity were recorded from an outside provider or caregiver report rather than confirmed at our institution. The primary outcome was constipation status at 1 year and most recent follow‐up, categorised as resolved (no symptoms and no laxative use), controlled (symptom resolution with ongoing laxatives) or persistent (ongoing symptoms despite therapy). The secondary outcome was the relationship between autism severity and constipation outcomes, accounting for confounders such as social situation, adherence, multidisciplinary involvement, laxative use and surgical escalation.

Procedures

Constipation management in the motility clinic followed a standardised protocol using a multidisciplinary team (MDT) approach. Initial therapy focused on optimising an osmotic laxative, usually PEG, with a stimulant added if symptoms persisted. Allied health supports were engaged early, particularly continence physiotherapy, with additional input from dietitians and occupational therapists. Children with persistent symptoms despite optimised medical therapy and adherence were referred for surgical consideration. This structured approach ensured consistency of care across the cohort.

Data collection

Data were extracted from electronic medical records into a secure, de‐identified database. Variables collected for both groups included demographics, clinical features, anthropometry, therapies, comorbidities, medications, compliance, specialised motility investigations and social factors (including child protection involvement and non‐nuclear family status). Compliance with toileting and medication was based on parental report and clinical documentation, rather than through standardised questionnaires.

Statistical analysis

Results are presented as frequencies (percentages) for categorical variables and as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) or ranges for continuous variables. For comparisons between children with and without ASD, continuous variables were analysed using the Student t‐test or Mann–Whitney U‐test after testing for normality of distribution, and categorical variables were analysed using the chi‐square test. For comparisons across ASD severity levels, continuous variables were analysed using Kruskal–Wallis testing after assessment for normality of distribution, and categorical variables were analysed using the chi‐square test. For all analysis, a two‐tailed p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 30.0 (IBM Corp.).

RESULTS

A total of 389 patients were screened, of whom 196 met criteria for analysis. This included 74 children with ASD and 122 without ASD. Twenty‐five children were referred with a pre‐existing Malone antegrade continence enema (MACE)/Chait device (not included in the study), 40% of whom had ASD. Patient characteristics

In the ASD cohort, the median age at recruitment was 11.0 years (range 3–16), with 62.1% male participants. The median age of constipation onset was 2.0 years (range 0–14) and referral to the motility service was 8.0 years (range 1–16). In the non‐ASD cohort, the median age at recruitment was also 11.0 years (range 1–18), with 45.9% male participants, and ages of constipation onset (2.0 years [range 0–14]) and referral (8.0 years [range 1–16]) were comparable. There were no significant differences between groups for faecal or urinary incontinence (Table 2). Autism severity in the ASD group was Level 1 in 10 children (13.5%), Level 2 in 41 children (55.4%) and Level 3 in 23 children (31.1%) (Table 3). Comorbidities were frequent: 50.0% of the ASD cohort also had attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) compared with 13.9% of children without ASD (p = 0.0001). Social risk factors such as non‐nuclear family structure or child protection services involvement were identified in 20.3% of children with ASD and 11.5% without ASD (p = 0.09). At the initial consult, the most commonly used osmotic laxatives were PEG (82.4% ASD vs. 81.1% non‐ASD) and lactulose (1.35% vs. 3.3%). Stimulant laxatives included bisacodyl (2.7% vs. 6.6%), senna (1.35% vs. 2.5%), sodium picosulphate (13.5% vs. 22.9%) and prucalopride (2.7% vs. 0%).

Comparison of ASD and non‐ASD outcomes

Short‐term outcomes (1‐year follow‐up)

Constipation outcomes at 1‐year follow‐up were broadly similar between the ASD and non‐ASD groups (Table 2). Rates of resolution without laxatives, symptom control with laxatives and persistent constipation did not differ significantly between groups at this time point. Patterns of laxative prescribing were similar between ASD and non‐ASD groups, with comparable use of osmotic and stimulant laxatives. However, medication adherence differed significantly, with lower compliance in the ASD cohort (56.8% vs. 76.2%, p = 0.006), whereas toileting compliance was similar. Multidisciplinary involvement, including physiotherapy‐led continence support, was common in both groups. At follow‐up, the most frequently used osmotic laxative was PEG (74.3% ASD vs. 78.7% non‐ASD), with lactulose used less often (5.4% vs. 0%). Stimulant laxatives included bisacodyl (18.9% vs. 9.0%), senna (5.4% vs. 1.6%), sodium picosulphate (16.2% vs. 26.2%), and prucalopride (1.35% vs. 2.45%).

Outcomes at most recent follow‐up

At the most recent follow‐up, autistic children demonstrated lower rates of constipation resolution and higher persistence of symptoms. The median follow‐up duration was 826 days (range 365–1946) and did not differ significantly between groups (Table 2). Resolution without laxatives was sustained in 11% of the ASD cohort compared with 25% of the non‐ASD cohort (p = 0.036). Symptom control with laxatives was similar in both groups, while persistent constipation was more common in ASD. At the most recent follow‐up, medication adherence improved compared with the 1‐year follow‐up (80.6% vs. 68.9%). Within groups, adherence increased in both cohorts: 78% in the ASD group (previously 56.8% at 1 year) and 82% in the non‐ASD group (previously 76.2%). This included two patients who underwent gastrostomy placement specifically for medication adherence during the study period.

Medication prescribing patterns also evolved over time. Use of osmotic laxatives decreased from 84% at initial follow‐up to 58.7% at most recent follow‐up, while stimulant laxative use increased from 27.6% to 42%. The proportion of patients on stimulant therapy at last follow‐up was significantly higher in the ASD group compared with the non‐ASD group (43/74 [58%] vs. 38/122 [31.1%], p = 0.003).

Specialised motility investigations were undertaken in 24.3% (n = 18) of children with ASD and 33.6% (n = 41) without ASD (p = 0.2), including nuclear medicine (NM) transit studies, anorectal manometry and colonic manometry. Among those who underwent NM studies, 11.8% of ASD children had normal transit, 35.3% had slow colonic transit, and 58.8% had anorectal retention, compared with 15.8%, 34.2%, and 50.0% in the non‐ASD group, respectively (p = 1.0). A total of 18 patients with slow transit constipation on the colonic transit study underwent colonic manometry (ASD—6, non‐ASD—12). There was no difference in abnormal colonic patterns (33.3% [2/6] vs. 25% [3/12], p = 1.00] between both the groups. Abnormal colonic motility pattern included left‐sided colonic dysmotility (ASD = 2, non‐ASD = 3).

Anorectal manometry was performed in 42 patients. Sixteen studies were performed under anaesthesia and demonstrated a normal rectal anal inhibitory reflex. Among the remaining studies (n = 26), nine (34.6%) had abnormalities which included pelvic floor dyssynergia (n = 4), rectal hyposensitivity/increased rectal compliance (n = 4) and anal hypertension (n = 1). There was no difference in manometric abnormalities in those with and without ASD (5/16 vs. 4/10, p = 0.69).

Escalation to surgical procedures such as MACE or Chait cecostomy was required in 8.1% of ASD patients and 7.4% of non‐ASD patients (p = 1.0). The Malone/Chait flushes worked well for all patients. One patient in each group experienced crampy pain with flushes which responded by titrating the flush glycerol level. Of the five children who managed with transanal irrigation, three had ASD.

Comparison by ASD severity

Within the ASD cohort, outcomes were compared across severity levels (Table 3). At both 1‐year and at the most recent follow‐up, the outcomes were comparable across all severity groups. Surgical escalation was uncommon and did not differ significantly across severity groups. Trends suggested worsening incontinence with increasing ASD severity, but none were statistically significant. Similarly, medication and toileting compliance were comparable across groups.

DISCUSSION

The proportion of children with ASD in our cohort (37.7%) is considerably higher than estimates in the general paediatric population, where ASD prevalence is approximately 1%–2%. 30 Children with ASD have a higher prevalence of functional GI disorders, particularly constipation and are more frequently referred to specialist services due to complex behavioural, dietary and adherence challenges. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 9 In this retrospective cohort of children with chronic constipation managed in a tertiary motility clinic, those with ASD had significantly poorer outcomes at most recent follow‐up compared with non‐ASD peers. Medication adherence was significantly lower in children with ASD. Medication adherence improved over time in both groups, increasing from 68.9% at 1 year to 80.6% at the most recent follow‐up; likely reflecting ongoing clinical engagement and optimisation of treatment regimens during follow‐up. Outcomes did not differ significantly by ASD severity, although trends suggested greater toileting challenges and incontinence in higher severity. Overall, ASD was associated with a more persistent constipation course, but severity of autistic traits did not independently predict outcome.

Our results align with prior studies showing a disproportionately high prevalence of functional constipation and related GI symptoms in children with ASD. 2 , 3 , 4 , 8 , 9 , 30 Registry and population‐based studies consistently report autistic children to be two to three times more likely to experience chronic constipation than neurotypical peers. 3 , 4 , 9 A greater healthcare burden has also been noted, with higher rates of emergency presentations and hospitalisations for constipation. 10 , 11 Specialist clinic studies, such as Seidler et al. 16 and Shruti et al., 28 emphasise the complexity of this subgroup, often requiring escalation to advanced bowel management or surgical intervention.

However, our findings suggest that while escalation occurred at similar rates in ASD and non‐ASD children, autistic patients had poorer resolution and lower adherence, highlighting adherence as a key mediator of long‐term outcomes. For some children with ASD and severe constipation, adherence to oral medications remains a major barrier despite multidisciplinary support. Innovative strategies have been explored, including gastrostomy tube (GT) placement to facilitate medication and fluid administration. In a small single‐centre series of nine autistic children with functional constipation, GT placement improved stool consistency, reduced straining and enhanced quality of life, with no major complications reported. 31 Although not a first‐line option, GT placement may be viable in carefully selected patients with profound feeding or adherence difficulties.

The lack of significant differences by ASD severity level contrasts with some prior reports linking rigid‐compulsive traits and repetitive behaviours to constipation and mixed bowel symptoms. 5 , 18 , 19 This may reflect the limitations of clinical ‘severity levels’ in capturing behavioural phenotypes that are most relevant to GI outcomes. Our findings suggest that autism itself, rather than severity classification, is the dominant predictor of risk for refractory or persistent constipation.

Previous studies have primarily focused on prevalence and symptom reporting rather than longitudinal outcomes, limiting understanding of prognosis. 4 , 8 , 9 Our study adds to the literature by demonstrating that even in a specialist motility service, children with ASD remain less likely to achieve sustained constipation resolution, echoing findings from Coe et al., who noted more complex, persistent and refractory presentations in autistic children. 6 Similarly, Seidler et al. found that children with neurodevelopmental disorders had poorer outcomes following bowel management programs, 17 consistent with our observation that persistence of constipation at the most recent follow‐up is more common in ASD despite access to structured care. Together, these findings highlight the unique contribution of our study in providing longitudinal outcome data, helping to bridge a critical gap in the current literature.

Constipation in ASD is likely multifactorial. Proposed biological mechanisms include altered gut motility, gut–brain axis dysregulation, microbiome differences, immune and serotonin signalling abnormalities 12 , 13

Behavioural and sensory features are also relevant. Food selectivity and restricted diets, common in ASD, can reduce fibre and fluid intake and contribute to constipation. 20 We noted many children with ASD were subjectively described as picky or selective eaters, although few had a diagnosed eating disorder. Rigid‐compulsive behaviours and sensory sensitivities can interfere with toileting routines and reduce acceptance of laxatives or enemas. 5 , 18 , 19 Together, these overlapping biological and behavioural contributors likely explain the reduced treatment responsiveness observed in autistic children despite access to multidisciplinary specialist care.

An additional notable finding was the high prevalence of ADHD in autistic children, with half of the ASD group also carrying this diagnosis. This is clinically relevant given that ADHD is independently associated with increased risk of both constipation and faecal incontinence, with affected children attending more medical visits for these conditions than their peers. 32 The overlap of ASD and ADHD may therefore represent a subgroup at particularly high risk for refractory or persistent constipation, as both conditions are linked to behavioural challenges in adherence and toileting, as well as possible shared neurobiological pathways influencing gut–brain signalling. 12 , 18 , 32

The significantly lower medication adherence in the ASD cohort highlights an important modifiable factor. First‐line agents such as PEG are effective in most children, 25 but barriers including taste, texture and sensory aversion may limit adherence in ASD. 26 , 27 Strategies to improve adherence include offering alternative formulations, simplifying regimens, using visual supports, and caregiver coaching. Multidisciplinary care is also critical: early involvement of dietitians, occupational therapists and continence physiotherapists may improve outcomes. 26 , 27 Structured escalation protocols—including stimulant laxatives, ACE and timely inpatient cleanouts—should help prevent crisis‐driven presentations. 10 , 16 , 28 Finally, the broadly comparable baseline characteristics between groups suggest that differences in outcomes are unlikely to be explained solely by demographic or clinical factors at presentation. Instead, factors such as treatment adherence, behavioural challenges and differences in treatment implementation may contribute to the observed disparities.

This study has several limitations. The retrospective, single‐centre design may limit generalisability, and reliance on medical record documentation introduces the potential for underreporting of symptoms, compliance and behavioural factors. Classification of ASD severity was based on clinical documentation rather than validated scales. Subgroup analyses by severity level were underpowered due to small sample sizes. Furthermore, children referred to a tertiary motility service are likely to represent more severe or refractory cases, limiting applicability to community populations. Additional limitations include the absence of controlled analyses evaluating the effect of social determinants of health, role of anxiety and applied behaviour analysis therapy.

Future research should prioritise prospective, multicentre cohorts to validate these findings and incorporate patient‐ and parent‐reported outcomes such as quality of life and treatment burden. Trials targeting adherence—through caregiver training, digital reminders and sensory‐adapted toileting—are needed. Comorbid neurodevelopmental conditions are an additional priority. Nearly half of the ASD cohort also had ADHD, which is independently associated with constipation and faecal incontinence. 32 This overlap may identify a subgroup at particularly high risk of persistent constipation, given links with executive dysfunction and adherence. Future studies should stratify outcomes by comorbidity to guide tailored interventions.

CONCLUSION

Children with ASD and chronic constipation demonstrate lower rates of resolution at the most recent follow‐up and reduced medication adherence compared with neurotypical peers, despite similar short‐term outcomes. ASD severity did not significantly influence outcomes, suggesting that autism itself is the principal risk factor for persistence. These findings highlight the importance of adherence‐focused, multidisciplinary management strategies in this high‐risk group. Future research should explore targeted interventions to support adherence, as well as the impact of coexisting neurodevelopmental conditions such as ADHD on GI outcomes.
