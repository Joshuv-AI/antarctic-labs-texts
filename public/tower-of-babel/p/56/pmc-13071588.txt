Comparative tuberculosis risk in healthcare and educational workers: occupational synergy
Authors: Yun-Hee Lee, Hyung Woo Kim, Ju Sang Kim, Young-Joon Park, Gahee Kim, Yujin Kim, Gyuri Park, Jun-Pyo Myong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHealthcare workers (HCWs) face higher tuberculosis (TB) risk from occupational exposure. Whether their risk exceeds that of nonclinical institutional workers when matched on key factors is unclear. We compared HCWs with educational workers in South Korea while controlling for age, sex and TB infection (TBI).MethodsA retrospective cohort was conducted using national TBI screening data linked with the Korean National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) and TB Surveillance System. After 1:1 propensity score matching on age, sex and interferon-γ release assay (IGRA), 203 959 HCWs and 203 959 educational workers were included. Cox proportional hazards models estimated adjusted hazard ratios (aHRs) for TB incidence for at least 2 years. Additive interaction between IGRA positivity and healthcare employment was assessed.ResultsTB incidence was highest in untreated IGRA-positive HCWs (245.5 per 100 000), whereas untreated IGRA-positive educational workers had a lower incidence of 77.0 per 100 000. Among IGRA-positive participants, untreated HCWs had a significantly higher risk of developing TB than untreated educational workers (aHR 2.18, 95% CI 1.56–3.06). Even IGRA-negative HCWs demonstrated elevated risk compared with IGRA-negative educational workers (aHR 1.76, 95% CI 1.10–2.83).ConclusionHCWs are at elevated risk of TB incidence, even after adjusting for infection status and demographics, most likely reflecting ongoing occupational exposure during follow-up. The simultaneous presence of two risk factors, employment in healthcare and IGRA positivity, substantially increased the incidence of TB. These findings highlight the need for targeted infection control, improved TBI treatment adherence and early detection strategies in healthcare settings.

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) infection (TBI) is a condition where the immune system responds to Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB) antigens without signs of active TB [1]. According to previous studies, approximately 25% of the global population has TBI [2]. The World Health Organization (WHO) consolidated guidelines (2024) recommend omitting the term “latent” and using TBI, acknowledging that TBI is not a stable but variable condition. TBI represents a spectrum of infection states that may progress to disease, particularly among high-risk groups such as children, immunocompromised individuals and recent contacts of infectious TB patients [3]. Approximately 5–10% of individuals with TBI develop active TB, with about half progressing within the first 2 years after infection [4]. The duration of TBI varies from several months to several decades.

South Korea's TB burden remains high compared with other high-income countries [5]. WHO and Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency (KDCA) reported 19 540 TB cases in 2023 (38 per 100 000) and 35.2 per 100 000 in 2024; despite this decline, incidence remains high for Korea's socioeconomic status [6–9]. To address this issue, the Korean government has provided TBI screening since 2016 for individuals in welfare, educational and medical institutions, as well as out-of-school youth, first-year high school students, postpartum care centres and correctional facilities [10].

Healthcare workers (HCWs) are at increased risk of TBI and disease in medium- to high-incidence countries, whereas in low-incidence settings their risk is similar to or lower than that of the general population [11, 12]. In South Korea, frequent unexpected nosocomial TB exposures further increase risk [13]. A meta-analysis of 21 studies from 16 countries found that HCWs had a 37% TBI prevalence (OR 2.27, 95% CI 1.61–3.20) and an annual active TB incidence of 97 per 100 000 (incidence rate ratio (IRR) 2.94, 95% CI 1.67–5.19), with up to a 24-fold higher risk compared with the general population [14]. Consistently, the WHO consolidated guidelines (2024) identify HCWs as a priority group for TBI screening and preventive treatment [3].

Our previous study showed increased TB risk among HCWs [15] but this was limited by insufficient adjustment for confounders such as age and sex [16, 17]. Therefore, this study examines whether TB risk is higher among HCWs than the general population, represented by educational institution workers, while appropriately controlling for potential confounders.

Methods

Study design and data source

This retrospective cohort study used data from healthcare and educational workers in South Korea who underwent interferon-γ release assay (IGRA) in the national TBI screening project between March 2017 and December 2020, with follow-up to April 2021 [10, 18]. QuantiFERON-TB Gold In-Tube (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) was used for IGRA, and results were interpreted as per the manufacturer's guidelines [19]. Using individual identity numbers, the TBI screening database from the KDCA was linked to the database from the Korean National Health Insurance Service (NHIS). The NHIS database, which includes medical information for approximately 97% of the Korean population, provides data on participants’ underlying disease, clinical characteristics and other relevant information [20], ensuring high reliability and accuracy (NHIS-2020-1-503). The Korean National TB Surveillance System database was also used to check TB status and history [18]. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Incheon St. Mary's Hospital, the Catholic University of Korea (IRB no. OC19ZESE0023).

Study participants

Between 2017 and 2020, a total of 428 448 HCWs and 352 696 educational workers who underwent TBI screening were enrolled in the present study. Among these participants, those without data in the NHIS database (n=42 993 and 4995, respectively), those who had not undergone IGRA testing (n=7713 and 1927, respectively), and those with a history of TB (n=2667 and 1553, respectively) were excluded, and participants under 20 or over 70 years old were excluded (n=3641 and 1820, respectively) in consideration of their working age. In addition, participants who had started treatment for TBI were excluded because they could affect the course of TBI (n=22 774 and 25 113, respectively). Because participants received IGRA at different times, observation periods varied. To allow valid comparisons, we included only those with at least 2 years of follow-up; hence, only participants enrolled on or before 30 April 2019 were analysed (data available until April 2021). A total of 264 024 HCWs and 305 978 educational institution workers included in the analysis were matched 1:1 using propensity score matching (PSM). The final study participants included 203 959 HCWs and 203 959 educational institution workers. The number of IGRA-negative cases in the two groups was 183 704 and 183 721, respectively, and the number of IGRA-positive cases was 20 255 and 20 238, respectively (figure 1).

Independent variables

We classified IGRA results in the KDCA database as either positive or negative, with positivity defined according to the manufacturer's guideline cut-off (≥0.35 IU·mL−1 for QuantiFERON-TB Gold In-Tube). We also recorded the follow-up period, which began on the date of the first IGRA test and ended on the earliest of the following: the date TB was reported, the date of death or 30 April 2021, depending on the participant. Based on the information in the NHIS database, we identified age, sex, place of residence, income level and Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) at the time of enrolment [21]. Income levels were reclassified into five groups based on the 20th percentile and place of residence was categorised as Seoul (“Capital”), “Metropolitan city” or “Rural area”.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were compared before and after matching by categorising participants into IGRA-negative and IGRA-positive groups and then comparing healthcare and educational workers. Categorical variables were analysed with frequency and chi-squared tests, continuous variables with means and t-tests, and standard mean difference (SMD) was calculated for age, sex and IGRA results (SMD≤0.2 indicated adequate matching). TB incidence per 100 000 was compared across groups, and univariate and multivariable Cox models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs). IGRA-negative educational workers served as the reference. The cohort was further stratified by IGRA result and institution type, with models adjusted for unmatched factors including income, residence and CCI score. Statistical interactions were assessed on the additive scale using the relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI), the attributable proportion (AP) and the synergy index (SI) [22, 23]. Detailed formulas and derivations are provided in the supplementary material.

Additionally, cumulative incidence curves were used to estimate and visualise the cumulative incidence of TB, and differences between groups were assessed using the log-rank test. The statistical significance level was set at 0.05, and all analyses were conducted using SAS Enterprise Guide v.7.1 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA) and R v.4.0.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Using the formula described in the Methods section, the calculated RERI was 161.34. This indicates that the absolute risk in the group that was both IGRA-positive and employed at healthcare facilities (245.47 per 100 000) was 161.34 higher than the sum of the absolute risk increases from each exposure alone (77.02–5.94 and 13.05–5.94). Additionally, the AP was calculated to be 0.674, indicating that approximately 67.4% of the excess risk observed in the group that was both IGRA-positive and employed at healthcare facilities, relative to the reference group (IGRA-negative educational workers), can be attributed to the interaction between the two exposures. The SI was also notably high at 3.06, further supporting a substantial additive interaction, meaning the combined presence of IGRA positivity and employment in healthcare greatly amplified the risk.

Discussion

In the age, sex and IGRA-matched cohort, HCWs had a higher TB risk than education workers, consistent across IGRA status. Among IGRA-positive individuals, HCWs had a 2.18-fold higher risk of progression to TB than education workers.

Our previous study showed a higher standardised incidence ratio (SIR) for HCWs than education workers relative to the general population [15]. Unlike that indirect SIR-based comparison, this study directly compared the two groups using a PSM model. Even after matching for age, sex and IGRA, HCWs had a higher TB risk, suggesting that occupational or environmental exposure significantly contributes to this risk. Although the proportion of IGRA-positive individuals was higher among educational workers than among HCWs before exclusions and matching, the rates were similar after applying the exclusion criteria and PSM, and the risk of progression to active TB remained significantly greater in the HCW group, which may reflect ongoing occupational exposure to MTB in healthcare settings.

HCWs are well known to be at increased risk of TB. According to a meta-analysis observing the TB incidence among HCWs, the risk of new onset TB among HCWs was higher than that of the general population, with the magnitude varying by TB incidence setting. The IRR was 2.42 (95% CI 1.20–3.64) in low-incidence countries, 2.45 (95% CI 1.05–3.84) in intermediate, and 3.68 (95% CI 2.89–4.48) in high-incidence countries, with an overall pooled IRR of 2.97 (95% CI 2.43–3.51) [24]. This occupational risk was most evident in medium-to-high-incidence settings. Risk factors include age, direct patient contact, BCG vaccination, job type and employment duration [25, 26]. A systematic review (2005–2017) reported that HCWs in low- and middle-income countries remain at high risk of TBI due to frequent exposure [27], largely from inadequate infection control and limited research capacity [28]. By contrast, high-income countries such as the US have effective infection control policies, with low TB incidence among HCWs except for immigrants from high-burden regions [29].

Most previous studies reported higher TBI prevalence or TB incidence among HCWs than the general population, without considering TBI status. In contrast, our large-scale study directly compares the risk of progression from TBI to active TB between HCWs and education workers, a proxy for the general population. Even with the same IGRA-positive status, HCWs had a twofold higher risk of progression compared with the control group. This likely reflects frequent unexpected TB exposures in Korean healthcare settings [13]. Since recent infections carry a greater progression risk [30], their presumed higher proportion in HCWs may contribute, though IGRA cannot distinguish recent from remote infections [31]. Furthermore, the significantly elevated TB incidence observed among HCWs, even within the IGRA-negative group, warrants further consideration. This finding suggests that ongoing exposure in clinical settings may lead to new infections during the follow-up period, resulting in progression to active disease despite an initial negative IGRA result. According to a meta-analysis of 13 studies conducted between January 2002 and September 2020, the incidence gap between IGRA-negative and IGRA-positive individuals was considerably narrower in high TB burden countries compared with low-burden settings. This may reflect new infections during follow-up in high-burden areas, causing TB even in initially negative individuals and reducing test sensitivity [32].

In Korean healthcare settings, unexpected TB exposure is common due to delayed isolation, especially when patients are admitted outside pulmonology or infectious disease departments [33], indicating low clinical suspicion. Strategies are needed to reduce such exposure. To reduce unexpected TB exposure in clinical settings, several strategies may be considered. The first and most critical intervention is for clinicians to maintain a high index of suspicion and to obtain early chest radiography in suspected cases. Once chest radiography is performed, the integration of artificial intelligence (AI)-based chest radiography interpretation systems may further facilitate earlier detection and diagnosis of active TB cases, thereby minimising diagnostic delays and transmission within healthcare facilities. According to the Korean TB guidelines (fifth edition), HCWs in designated medical institutions should undergo periodic TB screening, including chest radiography. However, the exact frequency is determined by each institution based on risk assessment rather than a fixed national interval [34]. The 2021 WHO guidelines recommend computer-aided detection as an alternative to human interpretation [35]. A recent study developed a deep learning algorithms for TB diagnosis [36], showing AI's potential to reduce diagnostic delays [37].

Strengthening TBI screening and treatment in HCWs is essential, as active TB often results from re-activation [38]. Yet only 32% completed treatment in our previous study [19], with rates varying by age and occupation. Tailored strategies such as age-specific counselling, better access and occupation-based education are needed to improve adherence. In Korea, the national TB guidelines (fifth edition) recommend preventive treatment for IGRA-positive HCWs working in category 1, 2 and 3 medical institutions, as well as for individuals with recent IGRA conversion within 2 years or radiological evidence of self-healed TB lesions [34]. In practice, however, reluctance among physicians to prescribe preventive treatment to otherwise healthy adults has been reported as a significant barrier to the implementation of TBI management in this group. Therefore, addressing this reluctance is essential to improve the uptake and effectiveness of TBI preventive treatment among HCWs. Furthermore, institutional policies must also support systematic follow-up to improve completion rates and strengthen TB control in healthcare settings. Moreover, environmental interventions such as adequate ventilation, early isolation of suspected cases and use of protective equipment are essential to reduce occupational exposure and complement screening and treatment efforts.

This study has several limitations. As a retrospective study, establishing causality is difficult, and because it was conducted solely in a Korean population, it did not account for diverse ethnic groups, limiting generalisability. In addition, variables such as smoking, alcohol use, employment duration and use of personal protective equipment were not included. Another limitation is that we did not assess the correlation between the quantitative intensity of IGRA results and the risk of TB. Previous studies have demonstrated that quantitative TBI test results can be strong predictors of progression to active TB [39]. As our database only provided dichotomised IGRA results (positive/negative), this aspect could not be evaluated. Last, concerns remain regarding whether educational institution workers can truly represent the general population, as IGRA is not routinely performed in individuals without risk factors. Before PSM, the IGRA positivity rate was higher among educational workers, likely reflecting their older age and lower income distribution (supplementary tables S1 and S2). In addition, previous studies in Korea have reported that older age and low income are risk factors for TBI [40]. To address these baseline differences, we applied PSM for age, sex and IGRA status, which balanced the two groups and allowed a valid comparison during follow-up. By doing so, we aimed to minimise bias and evaluate occupational risk more appropriately through the observed progression to active TB.

Despite these limitations, this study has strengths in using a large and reliable dataset and in employing a rigorous design with a well-matched control group based on age, sex and IGRA results. Moreover, it extends previous findings by demonstrating that HCWs with TBI have a significantly higher risk of progressing to active TB not only compared with the general population, but also when matched with a control group under the same conditions. These findings highlight the need for interventions beyond TBI screening, including preventive measures and treatment strategies tailored for HCWs.

In conclusion, this study confirmed that HCWs have a higher risk of TB incidence even when compared with a control group matched for age, sex and IGRA results, emphasising that this excess risk is likely attributable to ongoing occupational exposure during the follow-up period rather than intrinsic medical factors. Furthermore, by demonstrating that the simultaneous presence of two risk factors, employment in healthcare and IGRA positivity, substantially increases the risk of TB, our findings highlight the need for tailored infection control policies that integrate occupational characteristics with TBI status, including the adequate prescription and surveillance of preventive treatment. This approach could enable the effective allocation of limited healthcare resources and facilitate the identification of high-risk groups, thereby advancing policies aimed at proactively preventing TB transmission and disease onset.
