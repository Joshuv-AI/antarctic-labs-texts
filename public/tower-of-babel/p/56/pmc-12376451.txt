Pulmonary and neurobehavioral efficacies of AVR-48, a TLR4 modulator, in a preterm lamb model of bronchopulmonary dysplasia
Authors: Suchismita Acharya, David Riley, Adam Dayoub, Eesha Acharya, Andrew Rebentisch, Maggie Simmons, Sydney Bowen, Joanna Beachy, Elaine Dawson, Emily Major, Michael Borton, Jakob Van Boerum, Mar Janna Dahl, Vineet Bhandari, Suhas G. Kallapur, Dale J. Christensen, Kurt H. Albertine
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAVR-48 is a small molecule that modulates toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) activity, changing macrophage phenotype from pro- to anti-inflammatory and increasing the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10. Treatment with AVR-48 via intraperitoneal injection effectively prevented hyperoxia-induced pathology in a newborn mouse model of bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD).ObjectiveTo evaluate the early and late-stage efficacy of AVR-48 in preventing BPD and associated complications in a mechanically ventilated preterm lamb model that mimics human BPD.Design and methodsPreterm lambs were delivered at 128 days (d; about 85% gestation) following the administration of maternal antenatal steroids, intubated, given surfactant, and managed with invasive mechanical ventilation for seven days, followed by three days of noninvasive respiratory support. Either vehicle or AVR-48 was administered to the preterm lambs via intravenous bolus infusion six hours after birth and continued for seven days (every 12 h). Equivalent early (10 d) and late-stage (90 d) endpoints were selected to model human clinical outcomes at 36 weeks and 12–18 months post-natal age, respectively. Survival, growth, pulmonary pathology, respiratory system function, cardiovascular function, and neurobehavioral parameters were evaluated at both early and late stages. The forced oscillation technique was used to assess the resistance and reactance of the respiratory system. Blood samples were collected to determine the pharmacokinetics of AVR-48 and to measure levels of inflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokines. Lung homogenates were analyzed for TLR4, cleaved caspase-3, p53, PCNA, VEGF, VEGF-R2, and other biomarkers using immunoblot and RT-PCR.ResultsCompared to the vehicle, AVR-48 at 3.0 mg/kg significantly reduced respiratory severity scores, increased lung compliance, decreased lung resistance, and preserved alveolar formation without inflammation or fibrosis at 10d. In the 90d study, AVR-48 improved respiratory system mechanics, alveolar formation, and neurodevelopmental outcomes compared to vehicle controls. A decrease in the pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6) and an increase in the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 in lamb plasma collected between days 1 and 10 were noted. Lung tissue showed decreased TLR4 protein and inflammatory cytokines at 90d.ConclusionAVR-48 is a promising novel candidate drug for further development in preventing BPD and has the potential to reduce the associated adverse neurodevelopmental sequelae in preterm neonates.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12931-025-03337-z.

Introduction

Bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) is the most common morbidity in infants born prematurely, affecting ~ 30% of infants born < 32 weeks of gestation. BPD is diagnosed clinically by the need for supplemental oxygen (O2) at 36 weeks post-menstrual age (PMA) in premature infants [1]. Follow-up studies demonstrated that BPD is a major determinant of airway hyperreactivity and adverse neurodevelopmental sequelae in surviving preterm infants, which significantly impact treatment costs and the provision of health care during the lifetime of those born prematurely [2–4]. Despite multiple clinical trials, safe and effective therapies specifically directed at preventing BPD and associated adverse long-term pulmonary and neurodevelopmental sequelae remain elusive [1, 5]. Glucocorticoids are commonly used in the evolving or established phase of BPD and can improve short-term pulmonary outcomes [6], but concerns for adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes significantly limit their use.

The pathogenesis of BPD includes injury from invasive mechanical ventilation, exposure to hyperoxia, sepsis, and genetic predisposition. Excessive inflammation and activation of myeloid and other immune cells, as well as their innate immune receptor toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4), in the lung also play crucial roles in the pathogenesis of BPD [7–9]. Earlier, we demonstrated that a novel small molecular weight (344 Da) chitin derivative, AVR-48 ((2S,3R,4R,5S,6R)−4,5-dihydroxy-6-(hydroxymethyl)−2-(4-nitrophenoxy)tetrahydro-2 H-pyran-3-yl)acetamide), decreased LPS-induced inflammation in human ex vivo model of peripheral blood mononuclear cells [10], and in a hyperoxia-induced mouse model of BPD in vivo [11]. Compound AVR-48 is a reversible inhibitor of TLR4, with the advantage of modulating immune responses rather than completely suppressing TLR4 signaling. This immunomodulatory effect helps maintain immune system in balance and reduces the risk of broad immunosuppression, which can occur with irreversible or non-selective TLR4 inhibitors. The distinctive binding of AVR-48 to both the TLR4 and scavenger receptor CD163 was previously published by our group in mouse spleen-derived [10] and human cord blood-derived (unpublished) monocytes/macrophages. AVR-48 facilitates the polarization of macrophages into an intermediate phenotype, upregulating the anti-inflammatory cytokine interleukin-10 (IL-10) while downregulating the pro-inflammatory cytokines tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), IL-1β, and IL-6. Along with its anti-inflammatory activity, AVR-48 demonstrated antimicrobial activity through macrophage-mediated phagocytosis and intracellular bacterial killing, key features necessary for neonates with an underdeveloped immune system to fight infections [10]. Additionally, AVR-48 treatment resulted in the upregulation of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) in the BPD mouse model, thereby promoting vascular development.

Building on the success of murine models, in vitro, and ex vivo experiments [10–12], the present study demonstrates the effectiveness of AVR-48 in preventing key aspects of BPD, using an established preterm lamb model of evolving human BPD, including prematurity, mechanical ventilation, and exposure to supplemental oxygen [13]. Here, we examined several key outcomes related to BPD pathology, including functional, structural, molecular, and neurobehavioral assessments.

Materials and methods

AVR-48 ((2S,3R,4R,5S,6R)−4,5-dihydroxy-6-(hydroxymethyl)−2-(4-nitrophenoxy)tetrahydro-2 H-pyran-3-yl) acetamide) was synthesized by AyuVis Research (Fort Worth, TX, USA) with a purity of greater than 95%.

Formulation and dosing of AVR-48

AVR-48 (3.0 mg/mL) was formulated in 0.9% sterile saline and filtered through a 0.2-micron syringe filter into 10 mL serum vials ready for dosing. The efficacy dose was determined in a pilot study by stepwise escalating the AVR-48 dose concentrations (Supplemental Figure S1). The dose selection was based on our prior report on the mouse pup BPD model, where 10 mg/kg was found to be the most effective dose [11]. For the pilot study, we tested 0 (n = 2), 0.1 (n = 1), 0.3 (n = 1), 1.0 (n = 2), and 3.0 (n = 3) mg/kg/dose of AVR-48. The vehicle control groups received 0.9% sterile saline every 12 h (h). The demographics of the lambs used in studies are listed in Supplemental Table S3.

Study design and timeline of the preterm (PT) lamb model

Fig. 1 illustrates the timeline for the two-endpoint study involving two groups of PT lambs. The first endpoint involved PT lambs managed by IMV for 7 days, followed by extubation to noninvasive respiratory support (NRS) for up to 3 days of life (day of life; DOL7-10). At that time, terminal tissues were collected. The second endpoint utilized the same management protocol, followed by weaning the lambs from respiratory support and housing them in the lamb intensive care unit as former PT (FPT) lambs for 2 months of corrected postnatal age (3 months chronological age, DOL 90). The rationale for the 2-month corrected postnatal age for FPT lambs is that when sheep are weaned from milk, it is equivalent to about 12–18 months for humans. Methods for the FPT lamb model have been reported [13, 14]. Full-term reference lambs, introduced on day 30 of life (DOL 30) of the preterm (PT) lambs, were used as a positive control in the 90-d study. Term reference lambs were introduced into the study at 2–3 days of age (chronological), corresponding to the time point when former preterm (FPT) lambs reached 30 days of life (DOL). This aligns with the ‘delivery’ point shown in Fig. 2 for the term lambs. From that point, daily weight measurements were recorded for the next 60 days. The term reference lambs were co-housed with the FPT lambs to ensure a consistent postnatal environment. To clarify, DOL 1 for term reference lambs corresponds to approximately DOL 30 for the FPT lambs.

Study design

A randomized, blinded, placebo-controlled study design was used for both endpoints. For each endpoint study, one group of PT lambs was treated with vehicle (saline, administered intravenously, iv, over approximately 10 min) for 7 days and thus served as the control. Another group of PT lambs was treated iv with AVR-48 for 7d. Either treatment was started at 6 h of postnatal life to allow the PT lambs to stabilize after cesarean-section delivery and resuscitation physiologically and to permit initiation of ventilator-induced lung injury. We relied on visual identification of biological sex at the time of delivery in the absence of an ultrasound unit in our lamb intensive care unit. We tracked sex as a biological variable; however, the study was unable to assess differences in outcomes based on sex due to the small sample sizes. Details of surgical preparation and management of lamb groups are described in the supplemental section.

Respiratory supports during DOL 1–7 and DOL 8–10

The lambs were placed on a heated NICU bed, with ventilator settings of 60 breaths/min, 0.32-second inspiratory time, PIP of 21 cmH2O, and PEEP of 8 cmH2O. Target expiratory tidal volume ranged from 5 to 7 mL/kg. PT lambs were ventilated for 7 days.

Transition to former PT lambs began on days 7–8. Pentobarbital was discontinued, and the ventilator circuit was separated from the endotracheal tube. FiO2 was increased to 100%, and spontaneous breathing and oxygen saturation were monitored. Once stable, a 3.0–3.5 mm uncuffed nasal tube was inserted, reaching the mid-length of the nasal cavity. The ventilator was switched to high-frequency oscillation (HFO) mode with an amplitude of 20–25 cmH2O, HFO frequency of 8 Hz, and a 1:1 I:E ratio. Conventional breaths were set at 10 breaths/min with 1.0-second inspiratory time and 25 cmH2O PIP for sigh breaths. Oxygenation was targeted at 88–94% saturation (PaO2 60–80 mmHg), and ventilation was aimed at a PaCO2 of 45–60 mmHg. Lidocaine was used to reduce discomfort from the nasal tube. Noninvasive support was maintained for 3 days, with occasional reintubation and weaning as necessary due to ineffective respiration or apnea episodes.

Respiratory system mechanics assessments with methacholine challenge

Respiratory mechanics were assessed in early-stage (DOL1-10) and late-stage (DOL10-90) studies. In the early stage, respiratory mechanics were recorded every 5 min while PT lambs were intubated and mechanically ventilated. Dynamic lung compliance and resistance were averaged every 12 h. After extubation (DOL7-10), lambs transitioned to noninvasive support, and respiratory mechanics were measured using forced oscillometry (FOT) with a Tremoflo N-100 device and a small canine mask. FOT was calibrated before use and operated according to the manufacturer’s instructions. This instrument, validated in full-term and preterm human infants [15], enables the measurement of respiratory system mechanics in uncooperative subjects (i.e., not intubated and breathing spontaneously) by applying a pressure stimulus at the airway opening and measuring the resulting flow.

FOT measurements were taken at 1 month corrected postnatal age (1moC, DOL60) and 2 months (2moC, DOL 90) for FPT lambs, with term lambs serving as controls. Lambs were supported in a veterinary sling and given Lorazepam (0.1 mg/kg) to calm them before testing. FOT measured resistance and reactance in four steps: baseline, bronchodilator (albuterol), bronchoconstriction (methacholine challenge), and bronchodilator. Airway responsiveness was assessed during stepwise increase in nebulized methacholine (Provocholine; Pancap, Inc., Markham, Ontario, Canada), following the ATS 1999 guideline [16] and reported by our group for FPT and term reference lambs. Methacholine concentration was doubled at each step, starting at 0.06 mg/mL and increasing up to 4.00 mg/mL. Oxygen saturation, heart rate, and blood pressure were continuously monitored. The entire procedure lasted 45–60 min.

Neurobehavioral assessments

The lambs underwent three neurobehavior tests: (1) curiosity (novel objects), (2) socialization (non-reflective vs. reflective surfaces), and (3) learning and memory (maze with milk reward). These tests were adapted from other studies that assessed the neurobehavior of sheep [17–22]. The tests were conducted over two weeks, starting with habituation on days 1–2. This involved 10-minute sessions in the test room, followed by 30-minute rest periods in the restroom, and was repeated the next day to familiarize the lambs with the test environment, tender, and equipment.

Each test trial lasted 10 min, with a 30-minute rest between trials. The trials were video-recorded using the Noldus System (EthoVision XT 17) to analyze the lamb’s activity and behavior, including movement direction, speed, and periods of inactivity. The testing room was divided into 9 zones to monitor these factors. A ceiling-mounted camera recorded each test, and lamb activity was documented using the Noldus software.

Curiosity test (day 3)

Four trials were conducted in this study. In trials 1 and 2, two objects—a brown and a blue bowl were placed on the floor. In trial 3, the brown bowl was replaced with a novel pink pitcher, and in trial 4, a yellow ball was added alongside the blue bowl and pink pitcher. The test measured the time spent investigating each object.

Socialization test (days 4–5)

Two trials were performed each day in this study. Two metallic surfaces (one reflective, one non-reflective) were attached to the wall, and the test measured the time spent investigating each surface.

Learning and memory test (day 6)

Two trials were conducted in this study. A maze was set up with a milk bottle as the reward at the end. A tender stood at the maze’s corner holding the milk, and the lamb was timed based on how long it took to reach the reward.

Terminal tissue collection

Our group reported earlier on the methods for terminal tissue collection [13, 14, 23–25]. Briefly, at the end of the early-stage study (DOL 10) or late-stage study (DOL 90 for FPT lambs; DOL60 for term reference lambs), the lambs were given ketamine (10–20 mg/Kg, intramuscularly) followed by face mask inhalation anesthesia with 1.0-2.5% isoflurane with O2. Lambs were intubated and ventilated with a tidal volume of 5–7 mL/kg, and given heparin (1000 U, iv). The lambs were given Beuthanasia solution (0.25 mL/kg, IV; Intervet Inc., Madison, NJ) followed by potassium chloride (10 mEq, iv; Hospira, Lake Forest, IL).

For morphological analyses, the entire left lung was insufflated with formalin and sampled using systematic, uniform, random methods for quantitative histology [26]. Morphometric and stereologic methods quantified radial alveolar count, secondary septal volume density, and the thickness of distal airspace walls [24]. Alveolar capillary growth was quantified by using a combination of immunohistochemistry to identify endothelial cells in lung tissue sections and quantitative histology. For capillary surface density measurement, we used a recombinant anti-CD34 antibody (ab81289, Abcam; Waltham, MA) as a marker for capillary endothelial cells. Negative immunostain controls were the omission of the primary antibody, the omission of the secondary antibody, and the substitution of the primary antibody with a species-matched, irrelevant antibody. Epithelium lining the air spaces was counterstained blue. We used a computer-assisted true-color imaging system (Bioquant Life Science, Nashville, TN) to quantify the surface density of alveolar capillaries and the overlying epithelium.

For molecular analyses, the right caudal lobe was cut into 1 cm³ pieces (~ 1 g each), using systematic, uniform, and random sampling methods. The lung pieces were obtained from peripheral tissue, devoid of visceral pleura, central airways, vessels, and connective tissue. Homogenates of snap-frozen tissue pieces were used for quantitative real-time RT-PCR and immunoblot analyses. Primer-probe sets for PCR are summarized in Supplemental Table S1. Expression of mRNA was normalized to GAPDH. Primary antibodies for immunoblot analyses are summarized in Table S2. For immunoblot analysis, we used cleaved caspase 3 (EnzoADI-AAP-113, Enzo Life Sciences, Inc., Farmingdale, NY), PCNA (ab2426, Abcam, Cambridge, MA), and VEGF-R2 (sc-393163, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX) proteins. Positive controls for immunoblot were the respective native proteins. Protein abundance was normalized using Memcode (PI24585, Fisher Scientific, Pittsburgh, PA).

ELISA and BCA assays

The lamb BAL fluid samples (10d and 90 d terminal studies) were centrifuged to remove the cell pellets. The supernatant was used to assess total protein using the Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (Cat# 23227) from Thermo Fisher Scientific, Rockford, IL, which was read using a BioTek Synergy H1 microplate reader (BioTek, Winooski, VT). The serum and bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) fluid samples were used to quantify cytokine and soluble CD163 (sCD163) concentrations. The ELISA experiment used commercially available kits [ovine IL-1β (cat # ELO-IL1β), IL-6 (cat # ELO-IL6), RayBiotech, Peachtree Corners, GA, sheep IL-10 (cat # E12817Sh-96), Lifeome Biolabs, Oceanside, CA, sheep CD163 (cat #MBS9364870), MyBioSource, Inc. San Diego, CA] and each was quantified using a BioTek plate reader. We used GraphPad Prism V. 10.4 for data analysis and plotting.

Pharmacokinetic study

The pharmacokinetics (PK) of AVR-48 were determined using plasma from the PT lambs following twice-daily IV infusion (10 min) of AVR-48 every 12 h for 7 days. Tested doses were 0 (n = 2), 0.1 (n = 1), 0.3 (n = 1), 1.0 (n = 2), 3.0 (n = 3) mg/kg. PK evaluation for AVR-48 was only performed for DOL1 (12 time points between 0 and 12 h after the initial dose; the volume of plasma samples through DOL2-7 was inadequate for PK evaluation). Quality-controlled plasma bioanalytical data were transferred from the Bioanalytical group to the PK scientist of the Biopharmaceutics Department of Pharmascience Inc., Montreal, Canada. Samples were analyzed, using Phoenix WinNonlin software, version 8.1 (Certara, USA), using a previously standardized HPLC/LC/MS method.

Statistical assessments

Results are presented as mean ± SD. We used GraphPad Prism for statistical assessments. The physiological results were analyzed using ANOVA and a mixed model due to some missing data values on days 6 and 7. Quantitative histological results were analyzed by an unpaired t-test. Molecular analyses were performed by using the Mann-Whitney U test. We accepted p ≤ 0.05 for identifying statistical differences.

Results

Pharmacokinetics of AVR-48 after intravenous (iv) dosing in preterm lambs

For dose comparison, AVR-48 was administered at 0.1, 0.3, 1.0, and 3.0 mg/kg/dose intravenously every 12 h for 7 days, with changes in concentration plotted at different time points (Supplemental Fig. S1). Pharmacokinetics (PK) evaluation for the effective dose of AVR-48 (3.0 mg/kg) was performed from 0 to 12 h after the initial dose (Fig. 1A). The maximum plasma concentration (Cmax) occurred at 0.25 h for all doses tested (Figs. 1 A and S1). The theoretical Cmax (concentration extrapolated to 0 h; C0) for AVR-48 (3.0 mg/kg) was followed by a bi-exponential decline in drug levels, indicated by its half-life (T1/2), estimated at 0.64 ± 0.24 h (Table 1). Exposure to AVR-48 (AUC(0-t)) increased dose-dependently over the tested dose range (Supplemental Fig. S1A). The volume of distribution (Vd) for AVR-48 was estimated at 668.4 ± 129.0 mL/kg, and clearance (CL) was estimated at 791.6 ± 277.7 mL/hr/kg. The effective dose of AVR-48 (3.0 mg/kg/dose) resulted in a Cmax of 3885.7 ± 1458.7 ng/mL (10.73 µM), followed by a linear decline in drug concentration (Fig. 1A) in lamb plasma. For comparison, the Cmax of AVR-48 in the plasma of mouse pups at the effective dose of 10 mg/kg administered intraperitoneally was 1981.77 ng/mL (5.7 µM) (11). Therefore, a 3.0 mg/kg dose was expected to achieve the desired efficacy in preterm lambs. Dose-dependent effects of AVR-48 on lung outcomes

AVR-48 treatment was administered for 7 days during the invasive mechanical ventilation (IMV) period, followed by 3 days of noninvasive respiratory support without AVR-48. Over the 10-day study period, we observed dose-related improvements in respiratory parameters in preterm lambs treated with AVR-48 at doses of 0.1, 0.3, 1.0, and 3.0 mg/kg (Supplemental Fig. S1D–F). Notably, the 3.0 mg/kg dose resulted in significant improvements in the respiratory severity score (RSS) and lung structure. Furthermore, quantitative histology showed that the radial alveolar count (RAC) was statistically higher in the AVR-48-treated PT lambs compared to the vehicle-treated lambs (Fig. S1E). Conversely, distal airspace walls were statistically thinner in the AVR-48-treated PT lambs compared to the vehicle lambs (Fig. S1F). The cumulative functional and structural improvements observed in the preterm lambs’ lungs, along with the achieved therapeutic plasma concentrations from the pharmacokinetic study [Cmax = 3885.7 ± 1458.7 ng/mL (10.73 µM)], determined 3.0 mg/kg/dose as the effective dose of AVR-48, supporting its use in further validation experiments.

AVR-48 improved survival, growth, and lung outcomes in the early and late stages

For the early-stage study, almost 100% of PT lambs survived in both groups (Fig. 2A). For the late-stage study, survival significantly declined from d10 to d90 for the FPT vehicle lambs (40%, 4/7) compared to the FPT AVR-48 lambs (80%, 1/5) (Fig. 2A). This result demonstrates that AVR-48 treatment significantly increased the survival of the FPT lambs. Supplemental Table S3 summarizes the reasons for early termination. Besides the increase in survival, the body weight increased significantly (p < 0.05; p < 0.01) in FPT AVR-48 lambs compared to FPT vehicle lambs (Fig. 2B). AVR-48 improved long-term neurobehavioral outcomes in the late-stage study

Brain injury in survivors of preterm birth and those in the NICU is linked to poor neurodevelopmental outcomes later in life, with worse outcomes associated with a higher degree of chronic lung injury [29]. BPD in very preterm infants without severe brain injury is strongly associated with increased risks of mortality and neurological disabilities at 3 years of age [30]. Preterm lambs with chronic lung disease develop non-cystic, non-hemorrhagic diffuse brain damage [31].

We adapted tests as part of the late-stage study for curiosity behavior, using novel objects of different colors and shapes; socialization, comparing a nonreflective (dull) surface with a reflective surface (mirror); and learning and memory, utilizing a maze with a reward (Fig. 8) [22]. Apoptosis, proliferation, and permeability markers in lung tissue homogenates after AVR-48 treatment

Analysis of lung homogenates for changes in apoptosis and proliferation molecular markers related to PT lung development showed that on DOL10, the lung tissues of AVR-48 treated PT lambs had significantly lower levels of cleaved caspase-3 (Fig. 10E) compared to vehicle lamb lungs, indicating reduced cellular apoptosis and supporting the increased RAC results seen in lung morphometric analysis (Fig. 5E). No significant differences were observed between vehicle and AVR-48 treated lamb lung samples when analyzing mRNA levels of p53, caspase-3, c-Myc, and TGF-β (Figs. 10A-D) or protein levels of PCNA (Supplemental Fig. 10 F). In the 90-day study samples, P53 (an anti-apoptotic marker) showed higher levels (although not significanE p = 0.15) in AVR-48 treated lamb lungs and was significantly higher in term reference lamb lungs compared to FPT vehicle lambs (Fig. 9G). Additionally, there were no significant differences in the mRNA and protein levels of other apoptosis and proliferation markers between AVR-48-treated FPT lambs and term reference lambs. This suggests complete recovery from respiratory distress and normal lung development after AVR-48 treatment (Figs. 10H–L), supporting earlier findings of improved lung mechanics and compliance at 90 DOL (Fig. 7). Furthermore, we examined the mRNA and protein levels of the ligand VEGF-A and its receptor VEGFR2, which are involved in BPD pathology and promote angiogenesis and vascular permeability in the lungs. In the early-stage study at 10 days, we observed a significant decrease in VEGF-A protein levels, a marker of capillary permeability and angiogenesis, in the AVR-48 PT group compared to the vehicle PT group (Supplemental Fig. S16F).

Discussion

In the preclinical dose-range finding pharmacokinetic (PK) and efficacy studies, the mechanically ventilated preterm lambs were used to determine that, an intravenous dose of AVR-48 (3.0 mg/kg every 12 h) effectively decreases BPD pulmonary pathologies and achieves plasma concentrations consistent with the therapeutic levels of AVR-48 observed in our previously published mouse model of BPD. At this dosage, early treatment with AVR-48 during ventilator- and oxygen-induced lung injury in preterm lambs resulted in functional improvements, as evidenced by significantly better respiratory gas exchange parameters and lower respiratory severity scores (RSS), which are used clinically. RSS, calculated as a function of FiO2 and mean arterial pressure (MAP), is predictive of severe BPD in humans. An RSS ≥ 3.0 at postnatal day 14 and an RSS ≥ 3.6 at postnatal day 21 in preterm infants correlated with severe BPD or death [33]. Pulmonary function tests showed substantially reduced small and large airway resistances and reactances in the AVR-48 treated preterm lambs compared to those treated with the vehicle. Long-term survival and growth were significantly better in the FPT AVR-48 lambs compared to FPT vehicle lambs and were comparable to term reference lambs. Notably, while 100% survival was reached in both treated and untreated preterm lambs during the mechanical ventilation period (DOL 1–7) and the subsequent oxygen mask support (DOL 8–10), a significant (p < 0.05) drop in survival (down to 40%) was observed in vehicle- treated lambs during the unsupported period (DOL 10–90). This trend is frequently observed in the preterm BPD lamb model [13, 14] and closely mirrors the clinical outcomes in premature neonates experiencing severe respiratory distress. In contrast, AVR-48 treatment significantly increased survival to about 80%, with only one unrelated death recorded. These AVR-48-treatment-related improvements were further associated with enhanced indices of alveolar formation at both early (DOL 10) and late (DOL 90) time points. No notable difference in lung mechanics was found at DOL 60 (1 month corrected) between the AVR-48 treated FPT lambs and either vehicle-treated or term reference lambs, potentially due to measurement accuracy limitations related to using the TremoFlo device in smaller, less compliant preterm lambs than in the 2-month corrected PNA lambs. Altogether, these results demonstrate that AVR-48 prevented the pathophysiology and histopathology of the “new bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD)” [34] in a clinically relevant large-animal model. Remarkably, these improvements were also associated with enhanced long-term neurobehavioral and cognitive outcomes.

Glucocorticoids are commonly used in the evolving or established phase of BPD and can improve short-term pulmonary outcomes [6]. Experimental studies in monkeys, lambs, and mice have shown that both prenatal and postnatal exposures to glucocorticoids decrease the lung mesenchyme, cause thinning of the alveolus-capillary barrier, and increase the potential lung gas volume, as well as increase the synthesis and secretion of surfactants [35, 36]. However, these beneficial effects are counterbalanced by the formation of fewer, larger alveoli due to the inhibition of alveolar secondary septation [35, 36]. A significant clinical concern with the use of glucocorticoids is adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes and cerebral palsy caused by these steroids [6], limiting the usefulness of glucocorticoids in treating BPD. Another concern with glucocorticoids is the decreased somatic growth demonstrated in neonatal mouse models of BPD [35]. In contrast with the glucocorticoids, the beneficial effects of AVR-48 on respiratory gas exchange, alveolar formation, and respiratory system mechanics were achieved, including the formation of alveolar secondary septa. Similarly, postnatal growth was improved in FPT lambs treated with AVR-48 compared to FPT vehicle controls. Importantly, AVR-48 treatment did not adversely affect neurodevelopmental outcomes assessed at DOL 90 (2 months corrected age), a time point approximating a human toddler aged 12–18 months, when neurobehavioral assessments can be performed with greater accuracy than at DOL 60 (1 month corrected age). While neurobehavioral assessments in preterm lambs are not directly comparable to human developmental evaluations (e.g., Bayley Scales of Infant Development), and similar developmental trajectories should not be assumed, the data remain informative. Despite the limited sample size, at least one neurobehavioral outcome showed significant improvement by DOL 90, with no adverse effects observed across other measures following AVR-48 treatment.

Persistent inflammation is a significant precursor to BPD, and clinical studies suggest that interrupting the overt inflammatory response in preterm infants during early life could lead to improvement and possible reversal of the harmful effects of hyperoxic exposure on lung development that cause the BPD phenotype. TLR4 is involved in the development of BPD [37]. In fetal mice, administering lipopolysaccharide (LPS), a potent TLR4 activator, disrupts lung development by impairing alveolarization, causing pathological changes similar to BPD. In contrast, these abnormalities are absent in TLR4 knockout mice, and treatment with TAK-242, a classic TLR4 blocker, reduces BPD-like lung pathology in hyperoxic neonatal mouse models [38]. Unlike TAK-242, AVR-48 acts as a modulator of TLR4 rather than a direct blocker. In the late-stage study, we observed a significantly lower TLR4 protein level in lung tissue (DOL 90) after AVR-48 treatment. Conversely, in the early-stage study (10 days), there was no change in TLR4 protein levels compared to vehicle-treated lung samples. As expected, AVR-48 markedly decreased pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6) and elevated the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 at specific times during the 10-day respiratory support period. Notably, vehicle-treated lambs showed a significant rise in IL-1β during the early postnatal period (6–10 h of life), followed by increased IL-6 levels during later stages (4–6 days of life). This sequential cytokine increase may reflect the time-specific responses of an immature neonatal immune system and merits further study. Clinical research indicates that preterm (PT) infants who develop BPD experience an initial rise in IL-10 levels during the first 0–3 days of life, followed by a decline between days 3–21 [39]. Our results in vehicle-treated PT lambs follow this pattern, with higher IL-10 levels during the first 10 h of life and a gradual decrease over the next 7 days. Notably, AVR-48 treatment maintained higher IL-10 levels from days 5 to 7, indicating enhanced immunomodulatory effects. While circulating cytokine levels may not fully reflect local inflammation in the lungs, these findings support a systemic immunomodulatory role for AVR-48, consistent with previous results in murine models [11].

The cell surface expression of CD163 on alveolar macrophages is decreased in preterm infants with BPD [40]. Transcriptomic profiling of BPD versus non-BPD preterm infants showed that those with severe BPD had a loss of function in scavenger receptors (CD163, CD204) and mannose receptors (CD206), which predicts the disease [41]. CD163 is released into the circulation as soluble sCD163 through the cleavage of its extracellular domain by matrix metalloproteases in response to oxidative stress [42] or through TLR4 activation following inflammatory stimuli [43, 44]. Along with changes in these cytokines, we found a higher concentration of plasma sCD163 in the first four days of life in vehicle-treated preterm lambs, indicating increased inflammation. As the lambs received AVR-48, the sCD163 levels gradually declined.

Our study has translational strengths. The major strength lies in using the PT lamb model, which emulates the clinical context of preterm birth followed by mechanical ventilation with oxygen-rich gas for respiratory distress in a neonatal intensive care setting, including treatment with exogenous surfactant, caffeine, and prophylactic antibiotics. Our model recapitulates the pathophysiology and histopathology of evolving BPD [45], long-term developmental endpoint equivalence at 36 weeks PMA in humans (term gestation in sheep), and weaning from milk at 12–18 months in humans (2 moC in sheep). Our model also recapitulates challenges with inadequate early nutrition and subsequent poor weight gain, recurrent respiratory tract infections, diminished respiratory system mechanics, increased airway hyperreactivity [15, 46], and neurodevelopmental impairment. Lastly, male sex is an independent risk factor for developing BPD [46, 47], which is more frequent for male lambs [13, 14].

Our study also has limitations. The model is non-lethal and involves fetal lambs surgically delivered at about 85% of gestation, which corresponds to the saccular stage of lung development, roughly equivalent to 28 weeks of gestation in humans. Induced prenatal inflammation or infection is not present in our model [46, 47]. Additionally, the duration of mechanical ventilation and exposure to AVR-48 was for 7 days. A more extended period of ventilation and treatment might be necessary to assess the long-term molecular benefits of AVR-48. Other limitations include the smaller sample size in the late-stage study, which may have reduced the statistical power to detect differences in specific neurobehavioral outcomes.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides new insights into the physiological, morphological, biochemical, and neurobehavioral effects of AVR-48 on pulmonary and neurodevelopmental outcomes in preterm lambs during early and late postnatal life. AVR-48 treatment significantly enhanced survival and growth, with sustained pulmonary benefits along with positive long-term neurobehavioral and cognitive results. These findings support AVR-48 as a promising therapeutic candidate for preventing bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) in preterm infants and reducing its related neurodevelopmental issues.
