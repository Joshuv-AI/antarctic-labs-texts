Protective Human Papillomavirus Type 16/18 Vaccine–Induced Neutralizing Antibody Levels Remain Elusive
Authors: Matti Lehtinen, Tiina Eriksson, Tiina Petäjä, Katja Harjula, Kaisa Heikkilä, Mari Hokkanen, Julia Butt, Birgitta Michels, Peter Sehr, Martin Müller, Ville Pimenoff, Tim Waterboer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe studied human papillomavirus antibodies in a subset of a cohort comprising 16 988 women who received human papillomavirus (HPV) 16/18 vaccination as adolescents, among whom 50 cervical high-grade squamous cell intraepithelial lesions (HSILs) were diagnosed by 28 years of age. No differences in neutralizing HPV16/18 antibody levels between the HSIL cases and 1:2 matched vaccinated controls with or without persistent cervical HPV infection were observed. As compared with the HPV16/18, cross-neutralizing HPV33 antibody levels were significantly increased in the HSIL cases with concomitant cervical and serum sampling. However, during 16 years of follow-up, protective vaccine–induced, HPV16/18 cross-neutralizing (clade A9/A7 specific) antibody levels remained elusive.Keywords: antibody, cervical neoplasia, human papillomavirus, neutralization, vaccination

Human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccines comprise virus-like particles (VLPs), which, with repetitive epitopes, are excellent immunogens and provide high vaccine efficacy (VE) against infections with HPV16/18 and against high-grade squamous cell intraepithelial lesions (HSILs) [1–3]. HPV vaccine–induced neutralizing antibody levels are sustained for >10 years [4]. Bivalent vaccine–induced antibodies especially are cross-reactive and protect against persistent infections with clade A9 HPV types 31/33/33/35/52/58 and clade A7 HPV type 45 [2, 4, 5], but the definition of protective antibody levels is pending.

For hepatitis B virus (HBV) vaccine, a protective vaccine–induced antibody level (10 mIU/mL) was defined >25 years ago [6]. This has proven to be highly useful when assessing the number of HBV vaccine doses needed [7]. Attempts to reduce the number of HPV vaccine doses have become topical. A 1-dose regimen provides sustainable 10-year antibody response, but undisputed proof on the noninferiority of induced antibody levels or VE is lacking [8, 9].

We studied antibody levels following administration of 3 bivalent HPV16/18 vaccine doses in adolescent females. During 16 years of follow-up, vaccinated cervical cases (with HSIL or persistent cervical nonvaccine HPV infection) and their matched controls without anogenital HPV infections were identified at 18–28 years of age [10, 11].

METHODS

Participants

Two overlapping trials approved by the Pirkanmaa Hospital District Ethical Review Board, Tampere, Finland—a community-randomized trial on the impact of gender-neutral vs girls-only HPV vaccination strategies in adolescents (NCT00534639 [10]), and thereafter an individually randomized noninferiority trial comparing low- with high-intensity cervical screening in 22- to 28-year-old vaccinated women (NCT02149030 [11])—were conducted following an informed consent between 2007–2014 and 2010–2024, respectively, among 16 988 and 6959 HPV16/18 vaccinated females.

During 2007–2010, almost all (99.4%) participants of the community-randomized trial obtained the bivalent HPV16/18 vaccine at 0, 1, and 6 months between ages 12 and 15. Throughout cervical screening visits at ages 18/22/25/28, the participants showed an excellent 85% overall compliance [11]. Serum samples were collected at the 2 last screening visits at ages 25 and 28.

Pseudovirion-Based Neutralization Assay

We analyzed neutralizing antibodies using a high-throughput pseudovirion-based neutralization assay (HT-PBNA) for HPV6/11/16/18/31/33/35/39/45/52/58/59 [4]. The assay uses pseudovirions (PsV) comprising HPV L1/L2 proteins, which encapsidate a Gaussia-luciferase reporter plasmid. Following production in HEK293TT cells and purification by ultracentrifugation in an Optiprep gradient, the PsV particles infect HeLaT cells, allowing transduction of the reporter gene. Consequent Gaussia-luciferase activity is measured by luminescent reaction.

PsV infection is blocked by neutralizing antibodies, and transduction of the reporter gene is reduced. Serum dilutions ranging from 1:40 to 1:180 000 and concentrations inhibiting 50% of the PsV infection (EC50 values) were calculated from the median of the triplicates. EC50 values >40, 83, or 100 by a given HPV type were defined as neutralization positive.

Study Design and Statistical Analysis

To understand possible differences in both vaccine-type and non-vaccine-type neutralizing antibody responses in the HPV16/18 vaccine recipients, we matched (1:2) all 50 women who had developed HPV-associated HSIL by age 28 (Group A) with women who, postvaccination, developed persistent cervical infection with an identical HPV type (Group B) or with vaccinated control women (Group C) devoid of cervical HPV infections by the time Group A women were diagnosed with the cervical HSIL. Matching was for age at vaccination and similar history of sampling visits.

In our previous work, adolescent women who had received the bivalent HPV16/18 L1 VLP vaccine did not have detectable cervical HPV16/18 L1 antibodies, provided they had >3.3 times lower serum HPV16/18 antibody levels than those who had cervical antibodies [12]. Geometric mean titers (GMTs) of HPV16/18 antibody levels in vaccinated controls (Group C) were 51.251 EC50 and 5.186 EC50, respectively. We assumed that lack of detectable antibodies in dilutions corresponding to ∼3.3-fold lower vaccine-induced serum antibody levels—16.357 EC50 and 1.475 EC50—would preclude the detection of corresponding cervical HPV16/18 antibodies (and were used as a priori defined cutoff levels).

HPV type–specific seroprevalence (percentage of neutralizing antibody positives among vaccinees) was assessed with IBM-SPSS Software (Armonk, New York, USA). Two sets of GMTs, 1 GMT for all and 1 GMT exclusively for vaccinees with detectable cross-neutralizing antibodies, were used. A nonparametric Mann-Whitney U test was used.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

All study participants (100%) had detectable neutralizing HPV16/18 antibodies (Tables 1 and 2) up to 16 years postvaccination, during which time no cervical HPV16/18/31/45 infections were observed. No significant differences in HPV16/18/33/45 antibody levels were found between 50 vaccinated women with HSIL associated with HPV33/35/39/51/52/56/58/59/66/68 (Group A [11]) vs 99 vaccinated women who had developed persistent HPV infection with the same nonvaccine HPV types (Group B) (Tables 1). In Groups A and B, both the neutralizing HPV16 and HPV18 antibody levels were similar, irrespective of the sampling history.

The numbers of vaccinees who had serum HPV16 or HPV18 antibody levels below the a priori defined cutoff levels suggestive of possible lack of detectable cervical antibodies [12] were low. The proportions of individuals below the cutoff levels were materially equal among the different groups irrespective of their cervical infection and sampling history: persistent cervical nonvaccine type infection (Group B), 8/59; and healthy controls (Group C), 8/60. The lack of below-the-cutoff-level cases was true also irrespective of the concomitant sampling in all groups: (A) 2/17, (B) 5/33, and (C) 4/34.

The state-of-the-art method for identification of neutralizing antibodies to a wide variety of HPV types [4] and the unique study setting [10, 11] were strengths of our study. We studied matched cases and controls, who after receiving HPV16/18 vaccination as adolescents attended 16 years of clinical follow-up, with regular cervical sampling and serum sampling at ages 18/22/25/28 and 25/28, respectively. During this ∼16 years of postvaccination follow-up, the participants (A) were diagnosed with cervical HSIL (cases), (B) were diagnosed with persistent cervical infection with the same HPV type (infection controls), or (C) remained HPV infection and lesion free (healthy controls). This enabled accurate evaluation of both the bivalent vaccine–induced neutralizing and cross-neutralizing antibody responses and infection-boostered neutralizing and cross-neutralizing antibody responses by HPV type. The latter evaluations took into account also the natural history of cervical nonvaccine type HPV infections. While both comprehensive matching and long-term follow-up were strengths of our study, the stratification of the data set into different HPV clades, and in particular by clade A9 HPV types, gave limited numbers for the subgroups.

As compared with healthy controls, we observed a statistically significant 7-fold increase of cross-reactive neutralizing HPV33 antibody levels in HSIL cases with concomitant cervical HPV DNA sampling and serum sampling history (Table 1). The increased presence of lesional HPV16 (amount of HPV16 DNA and virions) and related antibody responses have been reported to be associated with increased risk of cervical carcinoma in situ/HSIL [2, 13]. Hence, it is plausible that an increased presence of lesional HPV33 in the HSIL cases, as compared with cervical HPV33 infection in the infection controls, might have boostered the HPV33 L1 antibody response in the former. Corresponding phenomena were not observed for HSIL cases associated with cervical HPV type 35/58 or HPV type 39/45 infections, which all have shorter clearance times, and shorter times to express (and induce related antibody responses to) the viral L1 protein, than HPV type 33–associated HSIL [2].

We have previously observed that total serum HPV52 VLP L1 antibodies tend to be lower in the vaccinees with cervical HSIL than without [14]. On the contrary, increased HPV52 VLP antibody levels following vaccination with the bivalent HPV16/18 vaccine have been reported from the Netherlands [15].

While we found logarithmic differences in the levels of neutralizing vaccine-type vs non-vaccine-type clade A9 HPV antibody levels in vaccinated women, the levels of cross-reactive antibodies needed for protection against infections with the latter remained elusive. Even more so, our results show that the bivalent HPV16/18 vaccine–induced cross-neutralizing antibody responses are not totally protective against cervical clade A9 types other than HPV16/31 [2–4, the present study]. Furthermore, probable boostering of the vaccine-induced cross-reactive neutralizing antibody levels following concomitant cervical infection with HPV type 33 and associated HSIL was observed. For infections and lesions associated with clade A7 HPV types, the numbers were too small for comparable evaluations.

We were not able to identify protective vaccine–induced clade A9– or clade A7–specific neutralizing or cross-neutralizing antibody levels. The comparable HBV vaccine–induced protective antibody level of 10 mIU/mL has for decades guided boostering of HBV vaccine–induced immunity [6, 7]. There are fundamental differences between the immuno-pathogenesis of the 2 viral infections: systemic hepatitis B virus infection and mucosal HPV infection that may eventually preclude the identification of protective vaccine–induced antibody level against the latter. This is unfortunate as the HPV vaccine–induced protective antibody level could be beneficial for monitoring reduced HPV vaccine dosing. The bivalent vaccine–induced antibody levels are ∼10 times higher than those induced by the quadrivalent (or nonavalent) vaccine [4]. Thus, findings from this rigorously designed matched study with long-term follow-up suggest that the identification of protective serum HPV vaccine–induced antibody levels may remain elusive [14]. A thorough understanding of (the mechanisms of) HPV vaccine–induced protection warrants further investigation.
