Mechanism and function of GPR3 regulated by a negative allosteric modulator
Authors: Geng Chen, Jana Bláhová, Nico Staffen, Harald Hübner, Nadja Nunhöfer, Chen Qiu, Peter Gmeiner, Dorothee Weikert, Yang Du, Jun Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Allosteric modulators have gained substantial interest in current GPCR drug discovery. Here, we present a mechanism of allosteric modulation involving the dimerization of GPR3, a promising drug target for metabolic diseases and central nervous system disorders. We show that GPR3 forms constitutive homodimers in live cells and reveal that the inhibitor AF64394 functions as a negative allosteric modulator (NAM) specifically targeting dimeric GPR3. Using cryogenic electron microscopy (cryo-EM), we determine the structures of the AF64394-bound GPR3 dimer and its dimer-Gs signaling complex. These high-resolution structures reveal that AF64394 binds to the transmembrane dimer interface. AF64394 binding prevents the dissociation of the GPR3 dimer upon engagement with Gs and restrains transmembrane helix 5 in an inactive-like intermediate conformation, leading to reduced coupling with Gs. Our studies unveil a mechanism of dimer-specific inhibition of signaling with significant implications for the discovery of drugs targeting GPCRs capable of dimerization.

Introduction

G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) represent the largest family of membrane proteins and are involved in nearly all physiological processes1. Consequently, they are targeted by more than 30% of marketed small-molecule drugs. While traditional drug discovery efforts have predominantly focused on developing molecules addressing the orthosteric ligand binding site, allosteric modulators can provide significant therapeutic advantages by binding to structurally distinct regions of the receptor and fine-tuning the biological responses of the natural signaling pattern2. Recent advances in structural biology have uncovered multiple allosteric sites within the GPCR superfamilies and significantly improved our understanding of how these allosteric ligands interact with their targets3–5. However, the potential for allosteric modulation of orphan GPCRs, whose endogenous ligands have not yet been identified, remains largely underexplored, due to the scarcity of tool ligands and limited functional knowledge.

G protein-coupled receptor 3 (GPR3), a family A orphan GPCR, has emerged as a promising therapeutic target due to its roles in metabolic regulation and central nervous system (CNS) function. Recent studies have demonstrated GPR3’s involvement in adipose thermogenesis in brown and beige fat6, glycolysis stimulation7, and the inhibition of obesity and liver pathogenesis through its activity in Kupffer cells8. These findings position GPR3 as a potential target for treating metabolic disorders such as obesity and diabetes. Additionally, GPR3 is broadly expressed in neurons across various brain regions and has been implicated in promoting neurite growth, regulating neuronal cell survival, and mediating neuropathic pain and addiction9–13. Importantly, GPR3 expression levels are elevated in a subset of patients with Alzheimer’s Disease (AD), and GPR3 has been identified as a key modulator of γ-secretase activity and amyloid beta (Aβ) production14–17. These findings highlight the therapeutic potential of developing GPR3 inverse agonists or negative allosteric modulators (NAMs) for the treatment of AD. Notably, a small molecule, AF64394 (Fig. 1a), was previously identified as an inverse agonist of GPR3 that can inhibit receptor-mediated cAMP accumulation18, and a recent study suggested the existence of multiple druggable binding sites based on competition experiments with an AF64394-derived fluorescent ligand, AF64394, and the GPR3-agonist diphenyleneiodonium chloride (DPI)19. We and others have previously reported the cryo-EM structures of the GPR3-Gs complex, revealing a lipid-like ligand bound to a hydrophobic orthosteric pocket, which contributes to GPR3’s high constitutive activity20–22. In this work, using cryo-EM and live-cell BRET assays, we show that GPR3 can form constitutive dimers. Notably, AF64394 specifically engages the dimeric form of GPR3, binding to a unique allosteric pocket located at the dimer interface, primarily constituted of transmembrane helix 5 (TM5). The binding of AF64394 restrains TM5 from adopting a fully active conformation, preventing the dissociation of the GPR3 dimer upon Gs engagement. This results in a loosely coupled and unstable dimer-GPR3-Gs complex and diminished signaling efficacy. These findings reveal a mechanism for inhibition of GPR3 signaling by a NAM and open avenues for the future rational design of lead compounds and drugs toward this highly promising target7,17,24, e.g., in Alzheimer’s Disease and metabolic disorders.

Results

AF64394 is a negative allosteric modulator targeting dimeric GPR3

To elucidate how AF64394 binds to GPR3 and inhibits receptor activity, we sought to determine the structure of the AF64394-bound GPR3 complex. Using a cell-based signaling assay (cAMP GloSensor), we confirmed that AF64394 can inhibit GPR3 activity with a potency of ~240 nM in HEK293T cells transfected with GPR3. In control experiments conducted without GPR3 co-transfection, AF64394 neither affected basal cAMP levels nor reduced the cAMP accumulation induced by stimulation of endogenous β-adrenergic receptors with isoprenaline (Fig. 1b). This indicates that the AF64394-induced decrease in intracellular cAMP concentrations in the GPR3 cells is receptor-dependent. To facilitate inactive GPR3 structure determination with cryo-EM, we initially replaced the ICL3 with a thermostable glycogen synthase domain of Pyrococcus abyssi (PGS) as a fiducial marker for particle alignment25. The engineered GPR3-PGS fusion protein was expressed and purified in monodisperse form in the presence of AF64394 (Supplementary Fig. 1a). Unexpectedly, rather than adopting the monomeric form observed in previous GPR3-Gs complexes22, the AF64394-bound GPR3-PGS complex displayed as a dimer, as shown by the 2D classifications (Supplementary Fig. 1b–d). Three-dimensional reconstruction yielded a final map at 3.1 Å resolution, which enabled the unambiguous assignment of the majority of residues for GPR3 and the fused PGS protein (Supplementary Fig. 1e–j and Supplementary Table 1). Interestingly, clear electron densities were observed for two AF64394 molecules (Fig. 1c), which were symmetrically located at the transmembrane dimer interface between the two GPR3 protomers (hereafter named ProtA and ProtB) (Fig. 1d, e, Supplementary Fig. 1g). The canonical orthosteric binding pocket of both protomers was still occupied by the lipid-like endogenous agonist, as observed in previous GPR3-Gs complexes22. We tentatively modeled the agonist with OEA based on the density shape (Supplementary Fig. 1k) and our previous functional studies22. These results suggest that, instead of an orthosteric inverse agonist as proposed by previous simulation and docking studies23, AF64394 is a negative allosteric modulator (NAM) specifically targeting dimeric GPR3, therefore revealing a mechanism of allosteric regulation that has not yet been observed in other family A GPCRs.

Structure analysis of the dimeric GPR3-PGS showed that the two PGS molecules in the dimer exhibit some interactions (Supplementary Fig. 1l). To exclude the possibility that the fused PGS protein promotes dimerization of GPR3 and affects AF64394 binding, we expressed and purified the AF64394-bound GPR3 without the PGS fusion. Cryo-EM analysis showed that the receptor can still form a stable dimer in the presence of AF64394 (Supplementary Fig. 2a–d). Receptor dimerization seemed to help particle alignment during data processing, and we finally obtained a map at 3.4 Å resolution without an additional marker, which allowed us to unambiguously model the majority of residues for GPR3 and the NAM AF64394 (Fig. 1d, e, Supplementary Fig. 2e–i, Supplementary Table 1). These data demonstrated that the dimerization of GPR3 is PGS-independent. While PGS fusion did not perturb the overall dimer structure and AF64394 binding, we observed a noticeable difference in ICL2 conformation between the two structures, which is likely caused by PGS fusion (Supplementary Fig. 2j). We therefore used the non-fusion map and model for the following analysis.

Structural basis of AF64394 binding and GPR3 dimerization

The identified allosteric binding pocket for AF64394 is formed by TMs 3 and 4 of ProtA, and TM5 of ProtB, with the second symmetric binding site formed in an analogous manner (Figs. 1d and 2a). It should be noted that membrane-facing pockets involving TMs 3, 4, and 5 have been identified as conserved allosteric binding sites in other Family A GPCRs2, however, the AF64394 binding pose is unique due to the involvement of a second protomer within a GPCR dimer (Fig. 2a, Supplementary Fig. 3a–c). AF64394 can be subdivided into three chemical groups: the [1,2,4]-triazolo-[1,5-a]-pyrimidine core scaffold (R1), the phenyl group (R2) and the 4-(3-chloro-5-isopropoxybenzyl) group (R3) (Fig. 1a). The R1 moiety and the secondary amine linking R1 and R3 exhibit multiple hydrogen bond/polar interactions with Q2115.56 and R2185.63 in TM5 of ProtB, while the R2 and R3 chemical moieties mainly contact with the TM3 and 4 of ProtA through hydrophobic and van der Waals interactions (Fig. 2a, Supplementary Fig. 3d). Consistent with the structural model, disruption of the polar receptor-ligand contacts by the Q2115.56A mutation significantly reduced the potency of AF64394 (77-fold, Supplementary Table 2), and the R2185.63A mutation completely abolished the inverse agonist activity of AF64394, resulting in a weak enhancement of cAMP accumulation (Fig. 2b). Two mutations in TMs 3 and 4 underpin the contribution of hydrophobic interactions with the R2 and R3 moieties to the binding of AF64394, as its potency decreased 10-fold and 5-fold, respectively, when L1363.52 and M1564.45 were replaced by alanine (Fig. 2b). Increasing the available space at the top of the binding pocket by the L1283.44A mutation improved the potency of AF64394 about 6-fold (Supplementary Fig. 4a), although it should be noted that this receptor mutant exhibited decreased surface expression (Supplementary Fig. 4b). In contrast, the mutations R1524.41A, V1554.44A, and L1594.48A did not substantially influence the inhibition of GPR3 signaling by AF64394 (Supplementary Fig. 4a, b, Supplementary Table 2). Interestingly, we observed clear lipid densities in the membrane-facing side of AF64394, which may further contribute to the binding of AF64394 (Supplementary Fig. 3e, f). The GPR3 dimer interface is built by both hydrophobic and polar interactions primarily through residues from TM5 of each protomer, starting from extracellular N1935.38 to intracellular Q2155.60 (Fig. 2c). Additionally, the extracellular residues L2686.56 and L2696.57 in TM6 undergo hydrophobic and van der Waals interactions with the residues V1965.41 and N1935.38 in TM5 (Fig. 2d). Moreover, the conserved residue Y1353.51 of the DRY motif from the intracellular side of TM3, together with Q2115.56 and Q2155.60 of TM5, form an extensive polar network connecting the two GPR3 protomers (Fig. 2e). Sequence alignment reveals that most of the TM5 residues constituting the GPR3 dimer interface are not conserved within Family A GPCRs, even for GPR6 and GPR12, two closely related orphan receptors (Supplementary Fig. 5). This suggests that the observed dimer interface is likely unique for GPR3. Indeed, a structural comparison confirms that the GPR3 dimer is distinct from other reported Family A GPCR dimers observed by either cryo-EM or X-ray crystallography (Supplementary Fig. 6). In addition to the direct interactions between the two protomers, we observed lipid molecules located at the interface, forming hydrophobic interactions with the receptors (Supplementary Fig. 3h, i), which may support the formation of dimers.

Constitutive homo-dimerization of GPR3 in live cells

The extensive protein-protein interactions between the two protomers appear to play a central role in stabilizing the dimer and may contribute to the specificity of GPR3 dimerization. These structural features suggest that GPR3 may constitutively form homodimers, and that AF64394 recognizes and binds to the pre-formed allosteric pocket in the dimerized GPR3. Consistent with this notion, perturbation of the dimer interface by introducing the sterically demanding mutations V1965.41W and I2005.45W in the upper part of TM5 reduced AF64394’s potency for the inhibition of cAMP accumulation more than 10-fold (Supplementary Fig. 7a). Although the single mutations Y1353.51A and Q2155.60A did not dramatically influence the functional activity of AF64394, its potency was reduced when both mutations were combined (Supplementary Fig. 7a), likely due to the perturbation of the hydrophilic network at the bottom of the dimer interface.

To validate a constitutive dimerization of GPR3, we tried to express the full-length receptor in insect cells in the absence of AF64394 to see if we could obtain purified dimeric receptors without AF64394 bound to the interface. However, we were unable to get monodisperse protein samples under the same expression and purification procedure, as shown by the gel filtration profile (Supplementary Fig. 7b). We speculate that the dimeric receptor without AF64394 bound to the interface is not stable enough to persist through detergent solubilization. We then performed cell-based BRET saturation assays to probe whether GPR3 homodimerizes within the cell membrane of live cells. To this end, we transfected HEK293T cells with a constant amount of GPR3 with a C-terminal fusion of Renilla luciferase (GPR3-Rluc8) as the BRET donor and variable amounts of GPR3 with a C-terminal fusion of mVenus (GPR3-mVenus) as the BRET acceptor (Fig. 3a), in analogy to previously described protocols26. Based on our cryo-EM structure, the C-terminal parts of the two GPR3 protomers are apart from each other and face opposite directions of the dimer interface (Fig. 1d), so the fused BRET sensors are unlikely to affect the dimerization. As expected, we observed a saturation hyperbola for the BRETratio with increased expression of GPR3-mVenus, indicating constitutive homodimerization of GPR3 in live cells (Fig. 3b). In contrast, the co-expression of GPR3-Rluc8 with D2SR-mVenus and measurement of BRET under the same experimental conditions did not result in a substantial, saturable BRET signal, indicating the specificity of the BRET signal observed for GPR3 homodimers (Fig. 3b). The saturation profile for GPR3 homodimerization did not change upon the addition of a saturating concentration of AF64394 (10 µM) (Fig. 3b), suggesting that the presence of AF64394 is not required for GPR3 dimerization. Interestingly, we observed a slight increase in the inter-receptor BRET signal in additional BRET time-course experiments upon addition of 10 µM AF64394 (Fig. 3c). Additional BRET saturation experiments with mutated GPR3-RLuc8 and GPR3-mVenus constructs were performed to probe the contribution of individual residues to GPR3 homodimerization. The mutations Y1353.51A, Q2115.56A, and Q2155.60A all led to a reduction of the maximum BRETratio compared to wildtype, indicating a smaller fraction of GPR3 homodimers formed (Fig. 3d, e). This agrees with these residues being directly involved in the polar network at the bottom of the dimer interface. The combination of the mutations did not further enhance the effect (Fig. 3d, e), but when all three residues were simultaneously replaced by alanine, a substantial reduction of receptor expression on the cell surface was observed (Supplementary Fig. 4b), highlighting the importance of the polar network for proper GPR3 function and trafficking. In contrast, the R2185.63A mutation, a residue that was shown to be crucial to the functional activity of AF64394, hardly affected the BRET ratio (Fig. 3d, e), indicating that it is not important for the constitutive homodimerization of GPR3. Similar to the results observed with wtGPR3, the BRET saturation profiles of the studied receptor mutants were not significantly influenced by 10 µM of AF64394 (Supplementary Fig. 7c).

AF64394-bound GPR3 dimer exhibits inhibited Gs activation

Previous biochemical and functional studies on other purified GPCRs have shown that receptor dimerization can decrease G protein activation efficacy, in part because only one protomer can be activated and couple to a G protein within a GPCR dimer27. Indeed, our structure suggested that the GPR3 dimer can only couple to Gs via a two-to-one stoichiometry. There would be severe steric clashes between the two Gαs subunits if both promoters simultaneously engaged with Gs (Supplementary Fig. 8a). Recent cryo-EM structural studies of GPR3-Gs complexes also indicate that the receptor mainly couples to Gs in a monomeric form in the absence of AF6439420–22, suggesting that the homodimeric receptors observed in live cell may undergo dissociation upon Gs coupling. We, therefore, speculate that the inverse agonism of AF64394 may partly function by preventing this activation-associated dissociation, which reduces the amount of receptors that can effectively interact with Gs. Indeed, we found that the AF64394-bound dimer in purified detergent micelles was very stable, remaining associated as assessed by size exclusion chromatography (SEC) for almost 24 h, even in AF64394-free buffer (Supplementary Fig. 8b).

To directly examine the function of the AF64394-bound dimeric GPR3, we performed an in vitro GTP-turnover assay using purified receptor and Gs. AF64394-bound dimeric GPR3 exhibited significantly weaker efficacy and slower kinetics in Gs activation compared to monomeric GPR3 (Fig. 4a, b), consistent with the NAM effect of AF64394 observed in the cellular signaling assay. A statistically significant GTP-turnover mediated by the dimer was only observed at higher receptor/Gs concentrations (1 μM) (Fig. 4a) or longer incubation time (>90 min) (Fig. 4b). These data suggest that the AF64394-bound dimer can still couple to Gs, but in a manner distinctive from the coupling between the monomeric GPR3 and Gs, leading to a less productive signaling complex. Structural basis for allosteric inhibition of GPR3 signaling by AF64394

To understand the molecular mechanism of AF64394’s NAM effect on GPR3, we compared the structures of the AF64394-bound dimeric GPR3 and the previously determined fully active monomeric GPR3 coupled to Gs protein (hereafter monoGPR3Gs)22. Because the dimer structure is symmetric, we only used one of the protomers for comparison. Interestingly, the overall receptor conformation in the NAM-bound dimer structure was quite similar to that of the monoGPR3Gs, with a root-mean-square-deviation (RMSD) of 0.492 Å (Supplementary Fig. 10a). The lipid-like agonist adopts a similar binding pose and residues constituting the orthosteric pocket are in nearly identical conformation (Supplementary Fig. 10b). In addition, the conserved toggle switch and the core triad motif display active-like conformations (Supplementary Fig. 10b, c). Despite these similarities, there were notable differences between the two structures in the intracellular G protein binding domain. First, the intracellular end of TM6 in the NAM-bound dimer GPR3 displayed a slight inward movement (~2 Å) compared to monoGPR3Gs (Supplementary Fig. 10d). Second, the extended helix at the intracellular side of TM5 observed in monoGPR3Gs was not resolved in the dimer structure (Supplementary Fig. 10e), likely due to the conformational flexibility without interaction with Gαs. Additionally, the conserved residues Y2135.58 adopt inactive-like side chain conformations in the dimer structure. This contrasts the active conformations observed in the monoGPR3Gs, where Y2135.58 forms a hydrogen bond interaction with the conserved R1343.50 (Supplementary Fig. 10f). Finally, we observed a distinct ICL2 conformation in the GPR3 dimer structure, which exhibits a more disordered loop conformation as opposed to the the short helix conformation in the Gs coupled active state (Supplementary Fig. 10d). The distinct ICL2 conformation in the dimer likely represent an inactive state as it could have steric clashes with the α5 helix of Gαs (Supplementary Fig. 10g). Similar conformational differences of ICL2 between inactive and active states were also observed in other GPCRs such as the β2-AR29.

Because of both active-like and inactive-like structural features, the conformation captured in our dimer structure may represent a pre-active intermediate state, which is likely due to the constant binding of the lipid-like orthosteric agonist. Notably, compared to other GPCR structures that were determined in the presence of agonist alone, the agonist-bound GPR3 exhibited the largest TM6 outward movement (Supplementary Fig. 10h). These results indicate the unique receptor conformational dynamics and activation mechanism of GPR3. Interestingly, our recently published apo-state model of monomeric GPR322 also exhibited an active-like conformation with only a minor inward shift of TM6 intracellular end compared to the monoGPR3Gs structure (Supplementary Fig. 10i). In addition, an AlphaFold2-predicted model of inactive monomeric GPR3 closely resembled our model calculated using MD and metadynamics methodology (Supplementary Fig. 10i). These observations stand in contrast to other GPCRs where receptor activation requires substantial conformational changes of a series of intra-molecular micro-switches starting from the orthosteric pocket to the intracellular G protein binding site, most notable for the large outward movement of TM6 (10–15 Å)30. Together, these results suggest that, unlike most other GPCRs, the inactive and active states of GPR3 have small conformational differences and that the intermediate conformation observed in our dimer structure likely reflects the agonist-bound state in the monomeric form.

While the active-like structural features of agonist pre-occupied GPR3 may lead to more efficient G protein coupling and high constitutive activity, they did not provide a clear explanation for the NAM effect of AF64394. To elucidate the structural basis for the dampened signaling efficacy of AF64394-bound dimeric GPR3, we determined the structure of the AF64394-bound GPR3 dimer in complex with Gs (Fig. 4c, d). Despite its weaker functional activity, we were able to assemble the AF64394-bound dimer-GPR3-Gs complex in vitro (Supplementary Fig. 11a). However, the complex formation efficiency was not as good as that of the monomer-GPR3-Gs complex. Nonetheless, this complex sample enabled us to obtain a map at a nominal resolution of 3.83 Å, with higher local resolution at the dimer interface and the interaction interface with Gs (Supplementary Fig. 11b–h). Of note, from the same dimer-GPR3-Gs dataset, we also isolated a small particle population and reconstructed a low-resolution map that displayed an inactive-like Gs located under the TM domain (Supplementary Fig. 11i). This may represent a pre-coupled state as the α-helical domain of Gα was in a closed conformation as observed in GDP-bound G protein. Interestingly, a similar inactive G protein coupling mode has been reported in the dimeric mGluR2-Gi complex31.

Our dimer-GPR3-Gs structure confirmed the two-to-one stoichiometry between the dimeric receptor and G protein (Fig. 4c, d), which is similar to the recently reported dimeric Apelin receptor-Gi complex32 and class C GPCR-G protein complex structures33,34. In fact, the two copies of AF64364 bound at the interface between the two protomers established the same interactions with TMs 3,4, and 5 as observed in the absence of Gs (Supplementary Fig. 11j). The stability of these interactions and the overall stability of the complex was confirmed by molecular dynamics simulations of the dimeric Gs signaling complex (3 × 2 µs) revealing little fluctuations of the two ligand-bound protomers (Fig. 4e, Supplementary Fig. 12a, Supplementary Table 3). After removal of the second protomer, both AF64394 molecules rapidly dissociated from their binding sites in MD simulations with the remaining monomeric GPR3 (Fig. 4f, g). These results substantiate the hypothesis that AF64394 can well stabilize a preformed GPR3 dimer, but not stably engage with monomeric GPR3 at the same site, although we cannot fully exclude the possibility that AF64394 would bind to monomeric GPR3 in a different way.

The overall architecture of the dimer-GPR3-Gs complex is similar to the monomer-GPR3-Gs complex, both presenting a typical GPCR-Gs interaction mode with the Gαs C-terminal α5 helix inserted into the intracellular cavity of the 7TM domain (Fig. 4h). However, there are notable differences between the two complex structures. First, the intracellular end of TM6 displays a small inward displacement (~2 Å) (Fig. 4i) and the conserved Y2135.58 in TM5 remains in an inactive-like conformation in the Gs-coupled protomer of the dimeric signaling complex (hereafter ProtBG) (Fig. 4j). These structural features are similar to those that are observed in ProtB of the dimer alone (Supplementary Fig. 10a–f). This is likely due to protein-protein interactions between the two protomers (mainly through TM5) and the direct interactions between AF64394 and TM5, which restrain the conformational changes of TM5 (Fig. 2). As a consequence, R1343.50 is unable to form a direct hydrogen-bond interaction with Y2135.58 as observed in the monoGPR3Gs structure because of the restrained side chain conformation of Y2135.58 (Fig. 4j). This difference persisted in molecular dynamics simulations of the dimeric GPR3 Gs complex and monoGPR3Gs, where we observed distances 8.2 Å for ProtBG of the dimeric complex and 3.7 Å for monoGPR3Gs, respectively, in the main simulation frames (measured between the hydroxyl group and Cζ of R1343.50, Supplementary Fig. 12b, c). In addition to the receptor part, the heterotrimeric Gs in the dimer-Gs complex exhibited a looser coupling mode compared to the tight coupling observed for the monomer-GPR3-Gs complex. Specifically, the α5 helix exhibits a small (~1.5 Å) downward shift in the dimer-Gs complex (Fig. 4j). This movement positions the αN helix ~7 Å farther away from the receptor, accompanied by a 5–6 Å translation of the Gβγ subunits (Fig. 4k). In agreement with the lower stability of the receptor-G protein complex observed during complex preparation for cryo-EM, our MD simulations indicated that the αN helix of the Gαs subunit showed a more dynamic behavior and sampled a broader conformational space in simulations of the dimeric GPR3 Gαs complex compared with simulations of monoGPR3Gs (Supplementary Fig. 12d). Such a looser coupling mode might be caused by the restricted conformation of TM5 and the lack of the hydrogen bond between Y2135.58 and R1343.50 in the AF64394-bound dimer, which in turn led to the instability of the complex and weaker efficacy in Gs activation as compared to the tight monomer-GPR3-Gs complex (Fig. 4a, b).

Discussion

In this study, we determined the cryo-EM structures of an AF64394-bound GPR3 dimer with or without Gs coupling. In combination with functional assays, live cell dimerization analysis, as well as evidence from previous structural studies on monomeric GPR3-Gs complexes, we propose a model for the allosteric mechanism by which AF64394 negatively modulates GPR3 signaling (Fig. 5). In the absence of the NAM, GPR3 exhibits a dynamic equilibrium between the dimer and monomer species (Fig. 5a). This is supported by BRET saturation assays showing that GPR3 can constitutively form homodimers in the cell membrane (Fig. 3). Upon Gs coupling, GPR3 undergoes conformational changes from the agonist-bound intermediate state to the fully active state, featured by the inward movement of Y2135.58 in TM5 (Fig. 5a). As the dimer interface is primarily mediated through TM5, the structural changes in TM5 likely weaken the protein-protein interactions between the two protomers, which lead to the dissociation of the dimer. Therefore, the majority of the receptors signal as a monomer, which can form a stable tight complex with Gs and lead to high signaling efficacy (Fig. 5b). In line with this notion, recent structural studies from three different groups revealed that GPR3 couples to Gs mainly in a monomeric form20–22. This is further supported by the reduced tendency to form homodimers in live cells in the presence of the positive GPR3 modulator CF3O-DPI (Supplementary Fig. 9). We propose that the dimerization state of GPR3 provides a regulatory mechanism for the signaling process in vivo as the receptor is activated by a persistently bound endogenous lipid agonist once expressed in cells, lacking the spatial and temporal control by binding of extracellular ligands typical for conventional GPCRs.Fig. 5Proposed model for the negative allosteric inhibition of GPR3 signaling by AF64394.a GPR3 bound to the lipid agonist is in an equilibrium between monomeric and dimeric states. b G protein engagement leads to a rearrangement of residues within TM5, dissociation of the dimer, and efficient coupling and signaling of the Gs protein. c AF64394 binds to dimeric GPR3, restricts the conformational changes of residues in TM5, and d prevents the dissociation of the dimer upon engagement of the G protein, leading to a loosely coupled and low-efficiency signaling complex.

Proposed model for the negative allosteric inhibition of GPR3 signaling by AF64394.

a GPR3 bound to the lipid agonist is in an equilibrium between monomeric and dimeric states. b G protein engagement leads to a rearrangement of residues within TM5, dissociation of the dimer, and efficient coupling and signaling of the Gs protein. c AF64394 binds to dimeric GPR3, restricts the conformational changes of residues in TM5, and d prevents the dissociation of the dimer upon engagement of the G protein, leading to a loosely coupled and low-efficiency signaling complex.

The NAM AF64394 specifically recognizes and binds to the dimer species, engaging in extensive interactions with both protomers (Fig. 5c). The direct interactions between AF64394 and TM5, as well as the protein-protein interactions involving TM5 of both protomers restrict the TM5 conformation at the intermediate state, which prevents efficient and tight coupling of Gs followed by dissociation of the dimer. This in turn results in an unstable, loose dimer-Gs complex and ultimately an inhibited signaling efficacy (Fig. 5d). It should be noted that there is neither structural information for the agonist-bound GPR3 monomer in the absence of Gs nor the agonist-bound GPR3 dimer (without ICL3 fusion) in the absence of AF64394 (Fig. 5a). The receptor conformations in these states may be distinct from what we have observed for our AF64394-bound structures and it cannot be excluded that canonical antagonism with displacement of the lipid-like orthosteric agonist is also possible for GPR3. Still, our cryo-EM AF64394-GPR3 complexes provide the structural basis for the chemical optimization of the scaffold and enable structure-based design for GPR3 modulators, serving as potential future therapeutic agents for AD or metabolic disease.

While it has been well established that family C GPCRs function as obligate dimers, and can be targeted by various allosteric modulators35, family A GPCR were traditionally considered to function as monomers36, and the existence and functional relevance of family A GPCR dimerization remains a subject of debate37. However, a growing body of evidence from biochemical experiments, BRET- or FRET-based studies, and high-resolution microscopy indicate that family A GPCRs can form dimers under certain conditions38. This has been notably demonstrated for opioid receptors39, adrenergic receptors40,41, chemokine receptors42, as well as many other druggable targets like dopamine43–45 and muscarinic receptors46. Recent cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) studies of rhodopsin47 and the apelin receptor32 have provided direct structural evidence for dimer formation in family A receptors. It has been reported that receptor dimerization can influence ligand binding, signal transduction, and intracellular trafficking, therefore providing possibilities for the development of more potent and selective compounds with reduced side effects48–50. Hence, significant efforts have been made toward the discovery of ligands that target family A GPCR dimers. One important strategy is the design of bivalent ligands that probe putative dimeric receptors36,48,51, however, most of the evidence for such ligand-induced dimerization comes from BRET/FRET assays, and the structural basis and molecular details remain elusive. Another powerful approach to induce protein dimerization is the use of molecular glues, which have been demonstrated in many other protein systems52. Recent GPCR-transducer complex structures suggest that some allosteric modulators act like molecular glues, stabilizing receptor-transducer complexes to regulate downstream signaling53. Despite these advances, by far, there has been no conclusive evidence that family A GPCR dimers can be directly targeted by small molecule allosteric compounds. Our study provides structural and functional evidence demonstrating that an allosteric small molecule specifically binds to a dimeric Family A GPCR and alters its functional outcome. Further studies will need to explore whether this represents a general allosteric mechanism applicable to other family A GPCRs with the potential of forming dimers.

Methods

Compounds and plasmids for cell-based assays

AF64394 was obtained from Hycultec GmbH, Germany (Cat. No. HY-12664). CF3O-DPI was synthesized according to a literature procedure28 with the details provided in the methods section below. For both molecules, the chemical probe requirement of biochemically/biophysically determined target engagement with affinities <100 nM (Ki/KD) in vitro has not been reported; however, both molecules are the most potent GPR3 modulators of their classes, and have previously been shown to exhibit GPR3 activity in cells at concentrations <1 µM18,28. Wildtype GPR3 in pcDNA3.1 was obtained from the cDNA resource center (cDNA.org). At the N-terminus, a cleavable HA-signal peptide followed by a FLAG-tag54 was added using polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and Hifi-Assembly according to the manufacturer’s instructions (New England Biolabs). For BRET saturation experiments, the FLAG-tagged GPR3 was fused to a 24-amino acid linker (ATGLRSRAQASNSAVDGTAGPVAT) followed by the RLuc8-coding DNA or a GGGAS-linker followed by the mVenus-coding DNA, as this design was successful for saturation experiments with D2R and NTSR126. Single point mutations (Y135A, Q211A, G215A, R218A) or combinations thereof were introduced into the aforementioned FLAG-tagged constructs employing one-step site-directed mutagenesis55 with the respective primers (Supplementary Table 4). For the generation of additional receptor mutants (L136A, R152A, V155A, M156A, L159A, V196W, I200W), we used the previously described FLAG-GPR3-10xHis construct22, which carries ten additional histidine residues at the receptor’s C-terminus, as the PCR template instead. The sequence identity of all constructs was verified using Sanger sequencing (Eurofins Genomics).

Chemical synthesis of CF3O-DPI28

CF3O-DPI was synthesized in a three-step procedure involving a Suzuki coupling, a Sandmeyer-type reaction, and an oxidative ring closure28. For the first step, 2-bromoaniline (70 µL, 0.62 mmol) and (4-(trifluoromethoxy)phenyl)boronic acid (163 mg, 0.79 mmol) were dissolved in 1,2-dimethoxyethane (0.5 mL). Subsequently, potassium carbonate solution (1.0 mL, 2.0 mmol) and bis(triphenylphosphine)palladium chloride (4.0 mg, 5.8 μmol) were added, and the mixture was heated at 80 °C overnight. After cooling to room temperature, the reaction was extracted with ethyl acetate (3x), and the combined organic layers were dried over Na2SO4. The solvent was removed under vacuum, and the crude product was purified by column chromatography (isohexane – ethyl acetate 9:1) to yield 4’-(trifluoromethoxy)-[1,1’-biphenyl]-2-amine as a yellow oil (117 mg, 72%); 1H-NMR (400 MHz, CDCl3) δ 7.53 – 7.46 (m, 2H), 7.41 (dd, J = 8.0, 1.4 Hz, 1H), 7.22 (ddd, J = 8.0, 7.4, 1.6 Hz, 1H), 7.16 – 7.08 (m, 2H), 6.96 – 6.86 (m, 2H).

In the following step, 4’-(trifluoromethoxy)-[1,1’-biphenyl]-2-amine (168 mg, 1.09 mmol) was dissolved in THF (1 mL) and cooled to 0 °C, followed by the addition of 4.0 M HCl (0.2 mL). A solution of NaNO2 (0.09 g, 1.31 mmol) in water (1.0 mL) was added dropwise, and the mixture was stirred at 0 °C for 30 min before a solution of KI (0.450 g, 2.73 mmol) in water (1 mL) was added dropwise. Stirring was continued at room temperature for 1 h. The reaction was stopped by the addition of saturated Na2S2O3 solution and the aqueous mixture was extracted three times with ethyl acetate. The combined organic layers were dried over Na2SO4, and the solvent was removed under reduced pressure to obtain 2-iodo-4’-(trifluoromethoxy)-1,1’-biphenyl as a yellow oil without further purification (169 mg, 70%); 1H-NMR (400 MHz, CDCl3) δ 7.87 (dd, J = 7.9, 1.6 Hz, 2H), 7.63 (dd, J = 8.0, 1.5 Hz, 1H), 7.41 – 7.35 (m, 1H), 7.21 (ddd, J = 8.0, 7.4, 1.6 Hz, 2H), 7.00 (ddd, J = 7.9, 7.4, 1.5 Hz, 2H).

In the final step, 2-iodo-4’-(trifluoromethoxy)-1,1’-biphenyl (70 mg, 192 μmol) was dissolved in dry CH2Cl2, flushed with N2 and cooled to 0 °C, before mCPBA (77%, 20 mg, 115 μmol) and CF3SO3H (16.3 mg, 192 μmol) were added dropwise to the reaction. The reaction was stirred at room temperature for 3 h, before it was extracted with ethyl acetate (3× 30 mL). The combined organic layers were dried over Na2SO4. After evaporation of the solvent, the crude product was purified using column chromatography (CH2Cl2:MeOH 99:1) and preparative RP-HPLC (Zorbax Eclipse XDB-C8, 21.2 mm × 150 mm, 5 μm, flow rate 12 mL/min, CH3CN and H2O/0.1% TFA: 10% for 1 min, 10–60% in 20 min, 60–95% in 3 min, 95–10% in 2 min, 10% for 2 min) to obtain 3-(trifluoromethoxy)dibenzo[b,d]iodol-5-ium trifluoroacetate as a white solid (17.8 mg, 25%); HRMS calculated for C13H7F3IO: 362.9488 [M + H]+, found 362.9488 [M + H]+; 1H NMR (400 MHz, DMSO-d6) δ 8.60 (d, J = 8.7 Hz, 1H), 8.51 (dd, J = 8.0, 1.4 Hz, 1H), 8.26 – 8.30 (m, 2H), 7.94 – 7.83 (m, 2H), 7.75 (ddd, J = 8.5, 7.3, 1.4 Hz, 1H); 13C NMR (DEPTQ, 151 MHz, DMSO-d6) δ 148.4, 141.1, 140.4, 131.3, 130.8, 130.6, 128.2, 127.4, 123.9, 122.7, 122.5; purity >95% by HPLC (Agilent 1200, Zorbax Eclipse XDB-C8, 4.6 mm × 150 mm, 5 μm, flow rate 0.5 mL/min, binary solvent system CH3CN and H2O/0.1% TFA: 3–85% CH3CN in 26 min, 85–100% in 2 min, 100% for 2 min, 100–3% in 2 min, UV detection at 254 nm).

Cell culture

HEK293T cells (gift from the Chair of Physiology, FAU Erlangen-Nürnberg) were cultivated in DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum, 100 µg/mL penicillin, 100 µg/mL streptomycin, and 0.5 mg/mL L-glutamine at 37 °C and 5 % CO2 on 10 cm culture dishes. Cells were not further authenticated but were regularly confirmed to be free from mycoplasma contamination using the MycoalertPlus detection kit (Lonza).

Bioluminescence resonance energy transfer (BRET)

Homodimerization of GPR3 was investigated using BRET saturation experiments with GPR3-Rluc8 and GPR3-mVenus in analogy to previous protocols56,57. HEK293T cells were detached from the culture dishes and diluted to a concentration of 250,000 cells/mL in growth medium. 300,000 cells were transfected in suspension using 200 µL PBS (Gibco, without Mg2+/Ca2+) containing 1 µg of DNA mix composed of 5 ng or 25 ng of GPR3-Rluc8, 0–600 ng of GPR3-mVenus and 375–995 ng of ssDNA (Sigma-Aldrich) and linear polyethyleneimine (PEI, 25 kDa) at a 1:3 DNA to reagent ratio. Saturation curves for GPR3-D2SR interaction were determined under the same transfection conditions, but using D2SRmVenus26 as the BRET acceptor instead. For time-course experiments, HEK293T cells were transfected with 5 ng of GPR3-Rluc8, 20, 100, or 300 ng GPR3-mVenus, and 695-975 ng of ssDNA per 300,000 cells. 100 µL/well of the transfected cell suspension was consecutively transferred to a white bottom 96-well plate (Greiner) pretreated with poly-D-lysine (MP Biomedicals), with each transfection condition at least in duplicates. Plates were then incubated for 48 h at 37 °C and 5 % CO2. On the day of the assay, the cell medium was removed and cells were equilibrated with PBS (DPBS with Ca2+ and Mg2+ for time-course experiments) for 45 min at 37 °C. Direct mVenus-fluorescence was measured with an excitation filter of 497–15 nm and an emission filter of 535–30 nm with Clariostar plate reader (Reader control version 5.40, Clariostar Mars data analysis version 3.30, BMG LabTech, Germany) prior to incubation with the ligand (10 µM) dissolved in PBS containing 0.3% DMSO or the respective vehicle at 37 °C for 10 min incubation at 37 °C. Afterwards, coelenterazine-h (Hycultec, 5 µM final concentration) was added and incubation was continued for 20 min in the dark at 37 °C. BRETratios were calculated from the simultaneous measurement of emission at 535–30 nm and 475–30 nm with a Clariostar plate reader equipped with the respective filter set. The netBRET values were calculated by subtracting the BRET ratio obtained in the absence of a fluorescent acceptor protein. Data were analyzed using nonlinear regression for saturation binding in GraphPad Prism version 6.0 for Windows (GraphPad Software, USA), where netBRET was plotted against the ratio of the separately determined mVenus to Rluc8 counts (data analyzed in Microsoft Excel 2019 or 2021). For time-course experiments, coelenterazine-h was added first (10 µM final), and basal BRET was monitored for 20 min, before ligands (10 µM final) or vehicle control were added. BRET was then monitored for an additional 60 min. Data were analyzed as treatment-induced change in BRET (ΔBRET, subtraction of rawBRET of vehicle-treated cells) in GraphPad Prism Version 10.4 for Windows (GraphPad Software, USA) and are shown as mean ± s.e.m. of three independent experiments.

cAMP accumulation

GPR3-mediated accumulation of cAMP was determined with the GloSensor cAMP assay (Promega, Madison, USA) in analogy to the manufacturer’s instructions58. HEK293T cells were transfected with 0.25 µg of the plasmid encoding wtGPR3, FLAG-GPR3, FLAG-GPR3-10xHis, or the respective receptor mutants, 0.5 µg Glo-22F cAMP plasmid (Promega), and 2.25 µg of a mock pcDNA3.1 plasmid employing Mirus TransIT-293 (Peqlab) transfection reagent in a 3:1 reagent to DNA ratio. After 24 h of incubation at 37 °C, 5 % CO2, cells were detached, and 15,000 cells/well diluted in culture medium, were transferred into white bottom 384-well plates (Greiner) coated with poly-D-lysine (MP Biomedicals). Cells were incubated for a further 24 h at 37 °C, 5 % CO2, before the growth medium was replaced with Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS, Capricorn) containing GloSensor cAMP Reagent (3%, Promega) followed by 1 h incubation at room temperature in the dark. After incubation, AF64394 dilutions in HBSS (from 3 mM stock in DMSO) or vehicle control were added. Experiments with wtGPR3 were conducted in the general presence of 1% DMSO. Plates were incubated for a further 30 min at room temperature in the dark before luminescence was determined with the Clariostar plate reader (Reader control version 5.40, Clariostar Mars data analysis version 3.30, BMG LabTech, Germany), and data were analyzed using the algorithms for 4-parameter nonlinear regression in GraphPad Prism version 6.0 or 10.4 for Windows (GraphPad Software, USA).

Receptor surface expression

Assessment of receptor surface expression was performed using an Enzyme-Linked Immuno Sorbent Assay (ELISA) directed against the N-terminal FLAG tag. HEK293T cells were grown to 60–80% confluency in culture medium. Cells were transfected as described above for the GloSensor cAMP assay and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C, 5 % CO2. Cells were detached using culture medium and 250,000 cells/well were transferred into clear, 24-well plates (Greiner) coated with poly-D-lysine (MP Biomedicals) followed by incubation for 24 h at 37 °C, 5 % CO2 in 600 µL of complete growth medium. On the day of the assay, all incubation steps were carried out at room temperature. First, the cell medium was removed and cells were fixed with 400 µL of 4% paraformaldehyde (RotiHistofix, Carl Roth, Germany) for 10 min, followed by a washing step with wash buffer (600 µL, 150 mM NaCl, 25 mM Tris, pH = 7.5) for 2 min. Wells were treated with blocking buffer (1600 µL, 3% skim milk in wash buffer) for 1 h, before incubation with the primary antibody (mouse anti-FLAG, F3165, Sigma-Aldrich, lot # SLCG2330, 1:4,000 in blocking buffer, 400 µL) for 1 h. Following two washing steps (600 µL) for 2 min and further blocking step (1600 µL) for 1 h, to each well was added rabbit anti-mouse IgG-peroxidase-coupled secondary antibody (A9044, Sigma-Aldrich, lot # 034M4761, 1:20,000 in blocking buffer, 400 µL) and plates were incubated for 1 h. After two washing steps for 2 min, substrate buffer (400 µL, 35 mM citric acid, 66 mM Na2HPO4, 3 mM o-phenylendiamine, 0,05 % H2O2) was added. After 15 min, the reaction was stopped by adding 400 µL of 1 M H2SO4, and the solutions were transferred into a clear flat-bottom 96-well plate (150 µL/well, quadruplicates). Absorbance was measured at 492 nm with the Clariostar plate reader (Reader control version 5.40, Clariostar Mars data analysis version 3.30, BMG LabTech). Data were normalized in GraphPad Prism version 6.0 (GraphPad Software, USA) relative to the signals from HEK293T cells transfected with a FLAG-tagged dopamine receptor (FLAG-D3R, =1.0, positive control) and non-tagged wild-type GPR3 (=0.0, negative control), which were used in each experiment, and compared to the expression of wild-type FLAG-tagged GPR3.

Expression and purification of GPR3 dimer

GPR3 was expressed in insect cells using the Bac-to-Bac baculovirus expression system. Briefly, WT Homo sapiens full-length GPR3 (NM_005281.3) was cloned into pFastbac1 vector. For the PGS fusion construct, the ICL3 of GPR3 (from R222 to T242) was replaced by PGS protein. To increase protein expression and stability, an N-terminal HA signal peptide, followed by the Flag epitope, a 3C protease site, and a BRIL fusion protein were inserted before the GPR3 sequence. The construct was transformed into DH10bac cells, and the recombinant bacmid was extracted to produce baculovirus for GPR3 expression in Sf9 (Spodoptera frugiperda, gift from B.K. Kobilka laboratory, Stanford University, USA, not further authenticated) cells. Sf9 cells were infected with the baculovirus at a density of 4 × 106 cells per ml and were collected after 48 h.

For expression and purification of dimer GPR3 (with or without PGS fusion), 1 μM inverse agonist AF64394 was added during expression. Cells were collected after 48 h and then lysed in the lysis buffer 10 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 1 μM AF64394, 1 mM EDTA, 4 mg/ml iodoacetamide, 2.5 μg/ml leupeptin, 0.16 mg/ml Benzamidine for 1 h at room temperature. Then the cells were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min to collect the membrane. The membrane was solubilized in a buffer of 20 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, 10 μM AF64394, 1% LMNG (NG310 Anatrace), 0.1% CHS (CH210, Anatrace), 4 mg/ml iodoacetamide, 2.5 μg/ml leupeptin, 0.16 mg/ml Benzamidine for 2 h. Extracted GPR3 dimer protein was further purified by G1 flag resin (GenScript, L00432) affinity chromatography and finally eluted by elution buffer 20 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, 1 μM AF64394, 0.003% LMNG, 0.0004% CHS, 0.001% GDN, 100 μM TCEP, 200 µM flag peptides. The eluted protein complex was concentrated and loaded onto a Superdex 200 increase 10/300 GL column and peak fractions were collected and concentrated for further electron microscopy analysis.

Expression and purification of Gs heterotrimer

Gs heterotrimer was expressed in Trichoplusia ni Hi5 insect cells (Invitrogen). Human Gαs was cloned in pFastbac1 vector, and N-terminal 6×His-tagged rat Gβ1 and bovine Gγ2 were cloned into pFastBac-Dual vector, and the viruses were prepared the same as GPR3. The cells were infected with both Gαs and Gβγ virus at a ratio of 10:1 at 27 °C for 48 h. The Gs heterotrimer was purified as previously reported. Briefly, cells were lysed in hypotonic lysis buffer, and the membrane was collected by centrifuging. Then it was resuspended in a solubilization buffer containing 1% sodium cholate for 1 h at 4 °C and centrifuged again to remove the cell debris. The supernatant was loaded onto Ni-NTA resin and after flow through the resin was extensively washed. During the wash steps, the detergent was changed from 1% sodium cholate to 0.01 % LMNG. Then the Gs protein was eluted with buffer containing 250 mM imidazole and treated with lambda phosphatase at 4 °C overnight. Note that all the buffers need to be supplemented with GDP and MgCl2 to maintain G protein activity. The next day Gs protein was concentrated, fast frozen in LN2, and stored at −80 °C.

Dimer-GPR3-Gs complex assembly and purification

The purified dimer GPR3 (1 mg) was incubated with Gs (2 mg) in a buffer composed of 20 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, 1% LMNG, 25 uM GDP, and 10 mM MgCl2 on ice for 2 h, then adding apyrase and incubated on ice overnight to remove GDP. The next day, the mixture was diluted in a buffer of 20 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, 0.01% LMNG, 0.003% GDN, 0.001% CHS, and loaded onto anti-FLAG G1 affinity resin. The resin was extensively washed and the detergent concentration was decreased to 0.003% LMNG with 0.001% GDN during the wash step. The complex was eluted with the 20 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, 0.003% LMNG, 0.001% GDN, 0.004% CHS, and 200 μM flag peptide. The eluted protein was finally loaded onto Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 size exclusion column (GE) against the running buffer composed of 20 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, 0.003% LMNG, 0.001% GDN, 0.004% CHS, and 100 μM TCEP. The monomeric complex peak was collected and concentrated to 3.2 mg/ml for electron microscopy experiments.

Cryo-EM sample preparation and data collection

The amorphous alloy film49 (CryoMatrix nickel titanium alloy film, R1.2/1.3, Zhenjiang Lehua Electronic Technology Co., Ltd.) was glow discharged at Tergeo-EM plasma cleaner. 3 μL purified protein sample was applied onto the grid and then blotted for 4 s with a blotting force of 0 and quickly plunged into liquid ethane cooled by liquid nitrogen using Vitrobot Mark IV (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

Cryo-EM data were collected at the Kobilka Cryo-EM Center of the Chinese University of Hong Kong (Shenzhen), on a 300 kV Titan Krios Gi3 microscope. The raw movies were recorded by a Gatan K3 BioQuantum Camera at the magnification of 105,000, and the pixel size is 0.83 Å. Inelastically scattered electrons were excluded by a GIF Quantum energy filter (Gatan, USA) using a slit width of 20 eV. The movie stacks were acquired with the defocus range of −1.2 to −2.0 micron with a total exposure time 2.5 s fragmented into 50 frames (0.05 s/frame) and with a dose rate of 20.6 e/pixel/s. The semi-automatic data acquisition was performed using SerialEM.

Cryo-EM data processing and model building

All cryo-EM data was processed in cryoSPARC V4.4.1. As shown in Supplementary Figs. 1, 2 and 11, image stacks were collected and subjected for patch motion correction and patch CTF estimation. Particles were auto-picked by blob picker from a subset of 500 micrographs, and then subjected to 2D classification to generate the 2D template. Particles were picked based on the template and subject to several rounds of 2D classification and Ab-initio reconstruction. The best subset of particles from Ab-initio were subjected to non-uniform refinement and local refinement, yield a 3.07 Å map for dimer GPR3-PGS fusion, a 3.42 Å map for dimer WT_GPR3 and a 3.83 Å map for GPR3 Dimer-Gs, respectively. Local resolution map was generated by Local Resolution Estimation in cryoSPARC.

The active GPR3 structure (PDB ID 8X2K)22 was used as the initial model for all the structures. All models were docked into the EM density map using Chimera59 followed by iterative manual building in Coot-0.9.260 and refinement in Phenix 1.18-386161. The final model statistics were validated by Molprobity62.

GTPase-GloTM assay

The dimeric GPR3 and dimeric GPR3-Gs complex used for GTPase-GloTM assay were purified as described above and frozen at −80 °C before use. The GTPase reaction was initiated by adding protein sample (concentration 0.25 µM to 1 µM, see Fig. 4a, b) in 5 µL reaction buffer (20 mM HEPES, 100 mM NaCl, 0.02% LMNG, 1 mM MgCl2, 5 µM GTP, 5 µM GDP) in a 384-well plate. The GTPase reaction was incubated at room temperature (22–25 °C) for 0.25–2 h (incubation time as indicated in Fig. 4a, b). After incubation, 5 µL reconstituted 1xGTPase-GloTM Reagent (Promega) was added to the completed GTPase reaction, mixed briefly, and incubated with shaking for 30 min at room temperature (22–25 °C) to convert the remaining GTP into ATP. Then 10 µL Detection Reagent (Promega) was added to the system and incubated in the 384-well plate for 5–10 min at room temperature (22–25 °C) to convert the ATP into luminescent signals. Luminescence intensity was quantified using a Multimode Plate Reader (PerkinElmer EnVision 2105) luminescence counter. Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism version 9.0 (GraphPad Software, USA).

Molecular dynamics simulations

Simulations of monoGPR3-Gs were based on the published structure of GPR3 in complex with its endogenous ligand OEA and Gs protein (PDB: 8X2K)22. The cryoEM structure of the dimerGPR3-Gs complex described herein served as the initial model for simulations of both the dimerGPR3-Gs complex and after omission of ProtA and Gs Protein for the simulations of monoGPR3 bound to two AF64394 molecules. Gβ and Gγ subunits of the heterotrimeric G protein were removed from the original structure for simulation of the ternary complexes of monoGPR3-Gs and dimerGPR3-Gs. Unresolved residues in ICL3 and G Protein were modeled using Maestros Prime Module (Schrodinger, v2024-3)63. Terminal residues were capped with an acetyl group or N-methylamide respectively. Palmitoyl groups were attached to Cys313 of GPR3 and Gly2 and Cys3 of Gs. All titratable residues were left in their dominant protonation state at pH 7.4 as predicted by PROPKA (Schrodinger, v2024-3), except for D862.50 and D1333.49. Both conserved residues have been reported as protonated in active state Class A GCPRs64. Initial coordinates and topology were generated using the tleap module of the AMBER24 program package65. Energy minimization was performed using the PMEMD module of AMBER24, starting with 500 steps of the steepest descent algorithm, followed by 4500 steps of the conjugated gradient algorithm. Before inserting the complexes into a solvated and pre-equilibrated membrane of dioleoyl-phosphatidylcholine (DOPC) lipids using the GROMACS tool g_membed66, the monoGPR3-Gs system was oriented to the orientation of proteins in membranes (OPM)67 model structure of β2AR (PDB-ID 3SN6)68, or in case of the dimeric structure to a OPM model generated by the PPM3.0 Webserver69. Replacement of water in the simulation box by sodium and chloride ions resulted in a neutral 0.15 M NaCl solution to provide physiological conditions. Box dimensions were 14 × 14 × 16 Å3 (total number of atoms: 318,971; 79,367 water molecules and 520 lipids) for the monoGPR3-Gs simulations, 15 × 15 × 15.5 Å3 (total number of atoms: 362,735; 89,083 water molecules, 592 lipids) for the dimerGPR3-Gs simulations and 8 × 8 × 10 Å3 (total number of atoms: 65,306; 13,141 water molecules and 154 lipids) for monoGPR3 bound to two AF64394 molecules. OEA and AF64394 were geometry optimized by Gaussian 1670 using the B3LYP functional and the 6–31 G(d) basis set. Charges were calculated using the HF functional and the 6–31 G(d) basis set. Atom point charges were fitted according to the RESP procedure71 and assigned using antechamber65. The general AMBER force field (GAFF2)72 was used for all ligands, the Lipid14 force field73 for every lipid except for OEA. For every protein residue ff14SB74 was applied. The SPC/E water model75 was used. All simulations were performed using GROMACS 2021.476. Each simulation system was energy minimized and equilibrated in the NVT ensemble at 310 K for 1 ns followed by the NPT ensemble at 310 K and 1 atm for 1 ns with harmonic restraints of 10.0 kcal·mol−1 on the protein and ligands. In the NVT ensemble, the V-rescale thermostat was used. In the NPT ensemble the Berendsen barostat, the V-rescale thermostat, a surface tension of 22 dyn·cm−1, and a compressibility of 4.5 × 10−5 bar−1 was applied. Further equilibration in the NPT ensemble at 310 K and 1 atm for 25 ns included restraints on protein backbone and ligand atoms which were reduced stepwise every 5 ns to values of 10.0, 5.0, 1.0, 0.5 and ultimately 0.1 kcal·mol−1·Å−2. Production simulations were performed for a duration of 2 µs with 3 independent repeats for each system. To constrain the bond lengths involving hydrogen atoms, the LINCS algorithm was used77. All simulations were performed under periodic boundary conditions. The Cutoff for Lennard-Jones interactions and short-range electrostatic interactions was set to 12.0 Å. Long-range electrostatics were computed using the particle mesh Ewald (PME)78 method with a fourth-order interpolation scheme and fast Fourier transform (FFT) grid spacing of 1.6 Å. A continuum model correction for energy and pressure was applied to long-range van der Waals interactions. The time step for the integration of the equations of motion was 2 fs. Trajectory analysis was performed using UCSF Chimera v1.1859 and CPPTRAJ79. Plots were created using Matplotlib 3.6.380. Initial and final configurations as well as input files of the molecular dynamics simulations for the monomeric and dimeric GPR3 complexes, are provided as ‘Additional Supplementary Data’ files.
