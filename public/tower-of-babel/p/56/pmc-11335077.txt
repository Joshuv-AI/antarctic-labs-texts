Comparing Plasma Donor–derived Cell-free DNA to Gene Expression in Endomyocardial Biopsies in the Trifecta-Heart Study
Authors: Philip F. Halloran, Jeff Reeve, Martina Mackova, Katelynn S. Madill-Thomsen, Zachary Demko, Michael Olymbios, Patrick Campbell, Vojtech Melenovsky, Timothy Gong, Shelley Hall, Josef Stehlik
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background.Plasma donor–derived cell-free DNA (dd-cfDNA) is used to screen for rejection in heart transplants. We launched the Trifecta-Heart study (ClinicalTrials.gov No. NCT04707872), an investigator-initiated, prospective trial, to examine the correlations between genome-wide molecular changes in endomyocardial biopsies (EMBs) and plasma dd-cfDNA. The present report analyzes the correlation of plasma dd-cfDNA with gene expression in EMBs from 4 vanguard centers and compared these correlations with those in 604 kidney transplant biopsies in the Trifecta-Kidney study (ClinicalTrials.gov No. NCT04239703).Methods.We analyzed 137 consecutive dd-cfDNA-EMB pairs from 70 patients. Plasma %dd-cfDNA was measured by the Prospera test (Natera Inc), and gene expression in EMBs was assessed by Molecular Microscope Diagnostic System using machine-learning algorithms to interpret rejection and injury states.Results.Top transcripts correlating with dd-cfDNA were related to genes increased in rejection such as interferon gamma-inducible genes (eg, HLA-DMA) but also with genes induced by injury and expressed in macrophages (eg, SERPINA1 and HMOX1). In gene enrichment analysis, the top dd-cfDNA-correlated genes reflected inflammation and rejection pathways. Dd-cfDNA correlations with rejection genes in EMB were similar to those seen in kidney transplant biopsies, with somewhat stronger correlations for TCMR genes in hearts and ABMR genes in kidneys. However, the correlations with parenchymal injury-induced genes and macrophage genes were much stronger in hearts.Conclusions.In this first analysis of Trifecta-Heart study, dd-cfDNA correlates significantly with molecular rejection but also with injury and macrophage infiltration, reflecting the proinflammatory properties of injured cardiomyocytes. The relationship supports the utility of dd-cfDNA in clinical management of heart transplant recipients.

INTRODUCTION

Plasma donor–derived cell-free DNA (dd-cfDNA) has emerged as a noninvasive screening test for rejection in patients with organ transplants, providing an opportunity to avoid some screening endomyocardial biopsies (EMBs) when the dd-cfDNA is below a predetermined threshold. Many studies have established the relationship between plasma dd-cfDNA in heart transplant recipients and histologic rejection in EMBs.1-9 Elevated dd-cfDNA correlates with histologic antibody–mediated rejection (ABMR) and T cell–mediated rejection (TCMR), although the relationship of dd-cfDNA to histologic TCMR has been weaker than for ABMR.8 Dd-cfDNA elevation early after heart transplant is also believed to reflect allograft injury.8–10

In addition to histology correlations, there is interest in defining the relationship between dd-cfDNA and the molecular changes in transplanted organs. Genome-wide gene expression can now be assessed using the Molecular Microscope Diagnostic System (MMDx), which measures transcripts with 99% reproducibility11 and interprets disease states by machine learning–derived algorithms to assign automated archetypes.12,13 MMDx strongly agrees with histology but with discrepancies,12,13 likely attributable to the intrinsic variability in histology14 or to sampling variation, particularly since institutional review boards limited MMDx to 1 or 2 EMB pieces in clinical trials. The relationship between dd-cfDNA and the molecular states in kidney transplant biopsies has been documented in the Trifecta-Kidney study (ClinicalTrials.gov No. NCT04239703), in which plasma dd-cfDNA taken before kidney transplant biopsies correlated strongly with ABMR and moderately with TCMR and injury.15,16 Dd-cfDNA in Trifecta-Kidney correlated more strongly with molecular rejection than with histologic rejection,16–18 and predicted molecular ABMR in the biopsy more strongly than donor-specific antibodies (DSA), reflecting the extent of DSA-negative ABMR.18,19

To define the relationships between plasma dd-cfDNA and the molecular states in heart transplant EMBs, we developed the Trifecta-Heart (ClinicalTrials.gov No. NCT04707872)—an investigator-initiated, multicenter, international prospective study to correlate plasma dd-cfDNA measurements taken just before biopsy with the molecular rejection and injury states in transplant EMB and with DSA. The present analysis 18 mo after study initiation was performed to examine the relationship between plasma dd-cfDNA and gene expression in the EMBs and compare these results to the relationships in kidney transplant biopsies in the published Trifecta-Kidney study.15,16 The goal of this analysis is to identify the top molecules in transplant EMBs correlating with plasma dd-cfDNA. Note that this analysis does not attempt to estimate the diagnostic accuracy of dd-cfDNA for molecular ABMR and TCMR, or the relative sensitivity of dd-cfDNA for molecular versus histologic rejection. Those analyses await the completion of the study. Trifecta-Heart is not primarily directed at the relationship of dd-cfDNA to histologic rejection, which as noted earlier has been extensively documented in other studies.3,8

MATERIALS and METHODS

Study Population

The population included 105 heart transplant recipients undergoing 180 standard-of-care EMBs performed for clinical indications or per screening protocol. A total of 43 biopsies were excluded from study, 7 biopsies because of insufficient material for microarray processing and additional 36 biopsies, where plasma dd-cfDNA measurements were not available because blood samples were not drawn or were unsuitable for analysis. The present study, therefore, includes 70 heart transplant recipients with 137 consecutive EMBs with available gene expression and %dd-cfDNA results and compares these results to the published findings in 604 kidney transplant biopsies in the Trifecta-Kidney study15,16 (Figure 1).

Trifecta-Heart study design (A). Workflow for collection and processing of the 180 biopsies for molecular analysis (the MMDx) and concurrent blood samples collected for dd-cfDNA (the Prospera test) in the first Trifecta-Heart study (B). From a total of 180 collected samples, 137 EMBs (from 70 patients) with MMDx results and 137 blood samples with dd-cfDNA results were compared in the first paired analyses. dd-cfDNA, donor-derived cell-free DNA; EMB, endomyocardial biopsy; MMDx, Molecular Microscope Diagnostic System.

Sample and Data Collection

The study participants consented at the time of standard-of-care EMBs. At the time of the EMB, 1–2 biopsy pieces were immediately stabilized in RNALater and shipped to the Alberta Transplant Applied Genomics Centre (http://atagc.med.ualberta.ca) at ambient temperature for RNA extraction and processing as previously described.20 Blood samples were collected for dd-cfDNA testing in two 10 mL DNA Streck tubes using 20 or 21-gauge needles, drawn before the EMB procedure on the same day to avoid concerns about dd-cfDNA release by bioptome injury to the myocardium. Blood was shipped to Natera Inc for analysis. As per standard of care, histologic and clinical data and local DSA assessments were recorded at each center and were available for this study. The study was not permitted to request additional local tests and the centers did not agree to provide biopsies for central histology reading because neither are standard of care.

The Molecular Microscope Diagnostic System

Gene expression was measured using PrimeView microarrays (Applied Biosystems, 49 495 probe sets). All molecular diagnoses were made without knowledge of histology, DSA, clinical data, or dd-cfDNA results. MMDx reports were sent to the participating centers, usually within 2 working days of receiving the biopsy. Automated output from ensembles of machine-learning algorithms, both supervised (classifiers) and unsupervised (archetypal analysis12,13) were used to assess rejection, parenchymal injury, and atrophy-fibrosis and generate an automated MMDx report. All reports were signed out by an expert reader.

The Prospera dd-cfDNA Test

The Prospera test uses massively multiplexed targeted PCR to identify 13 392 single nucleotide polymorphisms21 to determine the proportion of dd-cfDNA circulating in plasma. For analytic purposes in this study, we used the cutoff of %dd-cfDNA<0.15%, which was formally validated and interpreted as a low risk of rejection by Natera Inc.6 The results were reported to the center several days to weeks after the blood draw and were not used to influence the decision to biopsy or change patient management.

Statistical Analyses

All analyses were performed by using R (version 4.2.3). Beeswarm boxplots were used to present the relationship between MMDx groups and %dd-cfDNA. Dunnett’s post hoc test was used to show differences between the molecular rejection categories and nonrejection. Top genes positively correlated with %dd-cfDNA were determined by Spearman coefficient correlations (SCC). Gene Ontology (GO) terms were derived from the top 200 genes positively correlated with %dd-cfDNA. Nonparametric smoothed splines were used to represent time course analyses, generated using the “modss” function in the “npreg” package.22 For comparison between %dd-cfDNA in the 137 EMBs used here and the 604 biopsies from the Trifecta-Kidney study,15 SCC were applied for top probe sets and rejection and injury transcript sets. The probe sets listed as the top 20 for ABMR and TCMR activity in kidney were selected in a published reference set of 1208 kidney transplant biopsies.23 The probe sets listed as the top 20 for ABMR and TCMR activity in hearts were those that were most positively correlated with TCMR and ABMR archetype scores in a new extended reference set of 3230 EMBs.20

RESULTS

The study cohort consisted of 137 EMBs from 70 patients (median age 53 y, 34% female) enrolled in Trifecta-Heart at 4 vanguard centers. EMBs were taken between 14- and 8285-d posttransplant (median = 288 d; Table 1) and included 37 for clinical indications and 90 for protocol/surveillance biopsies.

Given the clinical importance of plasma atrial natriuretic peptide B measurements,24 we also recorded the expression of the atrial natriuretic peptide A and B genes (NPPA and NPPB; Table 2). The expression of both of those genes was strongly correlated with %dd-cfDNA (SCC = 0.24, respectively, SCC = 0.34) and was increased in ABMR, TCMR, and mixed rejection. In contrast, the expression of 2 genes critical for myocardial contractile function such as myosin heavy chain beta (MYH7) and alpha (MYH6) were both strongly negatively correlated with %dd-cfDNAs in EMBs. We previously showed that many transcripts (HTs) highly expressed in normal hearts have reduced expression in injured hearts,25 a change we interpret as reflecting dedifferentiation of injured cardiomyocytes.

The GO terms for top 200 genes positively correlated with %dd-cfDNA were related to leukocyte infiltration/inflammation and immune responses, including IFNG-inducible pathways, for example, the “antigen processing and presentation” pathway and the “response to IFNG” pathway (Table 3). Many of the pathways included genes expressed in macrophages for example, HMOX1, ICAM1, and CLEC7A in the “leukocyte activation involved in the immune response” pathway.

Comparing Correlations With %dd-cfDNA With Transcripts Sets in Heart Transplants Versus Kidney Transplants in Trifecta-Kidney (ClinicalTrials.gov No. NCT04239703)

Compared with previous kidney studies,15,16 the top dd-cfDNA-correlated genes in EMBs had more macrophage and injury genes but fewer natural killer (NK) cell–related genes (Table 2). To explore these differences, we compared the correlation of plasma dd-cfDNA with expression of rejection and injury transcript sets in EMBs in Trifecta-Heart versus kidney transplant biopsies in Trifecta-Kidney (Table 4—note that in many cases, P-values in kidneys are more significant than in hearts because of the larger sample size rather than higher correlations). Macrophage-related transcript sets had the highest correlations with dd-cfDNA in hearts, much higher than in kidneys. Genes inducible by IFNG had high SCCs in hearts and kidneys, slightly higher in hearts. ABMR-related transcript sets, and TCMR-related transcript sets correlated strongly with dd-cfDNA in hearts and kidneys, with slightly higher correlations with ABMR in kidneys and with TCMR in hearts. Injury-induced transcript sets correlated more strongly with dd-cfDNA in hearts, and normal parenchymal transcripts (whether annotated in hearts [HTs] or kidneys [KTs]) showed stronger negative correlations with dd-cfDNA in hearts, that is, dd-cfDNA was increased when expression of normal parenchymal transcripts was decreased, that is, dedifferentiation of the donor tissue.

DISCUSSION

This first analysis of the Trifecta-Heart study at 18 mo explored the relationship of dd-cfDNA measurements immediately before EMBs to the molecular findings in the EMBs. As expected, the mean %dd-cfDNA levels were higher in the early months posttransplant, rose again at later times, and were higher in indication biopsies. Again, as expected, the %dd-cfDNA was significantly higher in biopsies with molecular TCMR, ABMR, and mixed rejection compared with biopsies with NR, echoing our earlier kidney findings.15,16,19 The top genes correlating with %dd-cfDNA in EMBs included many genes inducible by injury and expressed in macrophages, indicating that both cardiomyocyte injury and rejection can contribute to the elevation of %dd-cfDNA. The expression of the atrial natriuretic peptide A and B genes (NPPA and NPPB) in EMBs was strongly correlated with increased plasma %dd-cfDNA, suggesting injury-induced upregulation of both genes in endomyocardium.24,26 The MYH7 and MYH6 genes, which are critical to cardiomyocyte function and associated with mutation-inherited cardiomyopathies,27 negatively correlated with %dd-cfDNA in EMBs, a phenomenon we interpret as injury-induced dedifferentiation. ABMR-associated genes were significantly correlated with %dd-cfDNA in both hearts and kidneys. TCMR-associated genes also correlated with %dd-cfDNA in hearts and in kidneys. The TCMR genes correlated more strongly with %dd-cfDNA in hearts than in kidneys, but this relationship awaits definitive analysis when the full Trifecta-Heart study is completed. Injury-induced genes, macrophage-expressed genes, and loss of parenchymal transcripts were also highly associated with increased %dd-cfDNA in hearts than in kidneys. The key lesson is that the dd-cfDNA-molecular biopsy relationships are very similar in heart and kidney, but the differential diagnosis of high dd-cfDNA in heart EMBs should give consideration to injury as well as rejection, probably reflecting the higher susceptibility of cardiomyocytes to injury compared with nephrons.

The correlations of dd-cfDNA with macrophage genes in hearts presumably reflect the ability of injured cardiomyocytes to recruit recipient macrophages, since the dd-cfDNA is being released by the injured donor heart tissue, not by recipient macrophages. Macrophages play an important role in cardiac injury.20,25,28–34 We could speculate that macrophages are recruited by release of organelles such as mitochondrial fragments from the damaged cardiomyocytes.32,34 It will be of interest to see how these molecular injury states in the donor tissue, with injury-induced genes, injury-suppressed genes (dedifferentiation), and macrophage infiltration, correlate with the long-term function and survival of the hearts, and whether they predict a higher likelihood of the late atrophy-fibrosis phenotype20,25 or cardiac allograft vasculopathy.35

This analysis found that plasma dd-cfDNA was increased in TCMR, reflecting indicating effects of the TCMR host inflammatory process on the donor tissue. The relationship of plasma dd-cfDNA to TCMR has often been found to be more complex than the relationship to ABMR both in molecular kidney TCMR15,16 and histologic heart TCMR (ACR).8 This will be of interest because we have found in kidney that the dd-cfDNA release in TCMR has a complex relationship to the TCMR molecular activity.15 We will further explore the details of the relationship of dd-cfDNA to the molecular TCMR process in the heart as more cases accrue in Trifecta-Heart because these associations could provide clues to the mechanisms of cardiomyocyte injury in TCMR. (The diagnostic performance of dd-cfDNA as a screen for TCMR activity awaits the definitive analysis on study completion.) Some disagreement between molecular and histologic TCMR assessments is expected, given biopsy sampling error and pathology inter-reader variability.36 One source of disagreement may be the fact that histology sometimes identifies injury as TCMR: we previously reported in the INTERHEART study that early molecular injury in hearts is sometimes mistaken for TCMR by histology.37 The MMDx system also has potential errors in TCMR assessment for example, mistaking a focal Quilty lesion for TCMR, particularly when it is interpreting only a single EMB piece. We now recommend that at least 2 EMB pieces should be sent for MMDx assessments to avoid sampling errors.

The top 20 transcripts correlating with plasma dd-cfDNA in kidney transplant biopsies were all strongly correlated with %dd-cfDNA in hearts, emphasizing the sharing of molecular processes between these 2 organ transplant types. The only minor exception was NK-cell gene GNLY, which was somewhat more strongly associated with ABMR in kidneys although still strongly correlated in both. Moreover, compared with kidney transplants, %dd-cfDNA correlated equally strongly with gene sets and classifiers representing ABMR and TCMR. The biggest difference between EMBs and kidneys was in gene sets reflecting recent parenchymal injury, dedifferentiation, and macrophage infiltration, which were more associated with parenchymal injury in EMBs than in kidney biopsies, presumably reflecting the greater vulnerability of the cardiomyocyte to injury compared with the nephron and the unique ability of the cardiomyocyte to recruit macrophages.

The striking similarity of dd-cfDNA correlations with the TCMR and ABMR activity genes, whether selected in hearts or kidneys, confirms the shared mechanisms operating in rejection in both organs.

However, dd-cfDNA reflects how the donor tissue responds to these shared immunologic mechanisms. The strong correlations between TCMR genes and dd-cfDNA in hearts is consistent with the known fragility of the cardiomyocyte to injury. We hypothesize that immunologic TCMR mechanisms in hearts induce more severe injury to the delicate cardiomyocyte than the same mechanisms operating on the nephron15 and that these injuries have long-term implications for heart function and survival.38 It will be of interest to explore the details of the correlations of dd-cfDNA signals within the TCMR biopsies and the later changes that correlate with dysfunction and survival. Thus, we suspect that the immunologic TCMR mechanisms operating in hearts are very similar but the responses of the cells releasing dd-cfDNA—the cardiomyocyte and the nephron and their associated structures—are very different, with long-term implications for the future health of the organ.

A limitation of this first analysis is the small number of biopsies with definite TCMR and ABMR MMDx diagnoses, which prevents estimates of diagnostic performance but has more than sufficient power to explore statistical associations between dd-cfDNA and molecular TCMR, ABMR, and injury pathways and the similarities with kidney transplants. The considerable variation in the dd-cfDNA among biopsies with the same rejection state is similar to previous experience in Trifecta-Kidney, reflecting the intrinsic variation known in dd-cfDNA,39 in molecular activity within rejection states, in the effects of the host rejection mechanisms on the donor tissue, and of course sampling error in EMBs. We now recognize that molecular rejection of lower intensity is found in many biopsies that fall below the diagnostic thresholds, in biopsies we classify as “Minor rejection” and even “NR.”28 The definitive analysis of Trifecta-Heart must take such variation and subthreshold rejection activity into account, as we define the diagnostic performance of dd-cfDNA and centrally measured DSA for molecular TCMR and ABMR states in comparison with histological TCMR and ABMR states. These issues will be a strong focus in the complete Trifecta-Heart analysis.

This Trifecta-Heart analysis provides insights into the clinical interpretation of plasma dd-cfDNA monitoring to patient care and the differential diagnosis of dd-cfDNA elevation in heart transplant patients. The current use of dd-cfDNA is as a screening test that when abnormal will lead to biopsy, but technical advances may distinguish dd-cfDNA released by TCMR versus ABMR versus injury without a biopsy. Clinicians are looking for reassurance that screening by dd-cfDNA will reduce protocol biopsies without missing clinically important and potentially treatable conditions such as TCMR and ABMR and the differential diagnosis of abnormal results. Trifecta-Heart offers reassurance in this respect at the molecular level and guidance for the differential diagnosis of elevated dd-cfDNA, considering the time posttransplant and including TCMR, ABMR, and other sources of cardiomyocyte injury. The close relationship between dd-cfDNA levels and the molecular events on the heart support the utility of dd-cfDNA in clinical management of heart transplant recipients, and the promise of dd-cfDNA follow-up as a guide to interpretation of response to therapy.
