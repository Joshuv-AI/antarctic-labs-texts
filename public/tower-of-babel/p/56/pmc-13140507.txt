Placental pathology in gestational diabetes mellitus: Associations with neonatal outcomes and postpartum dysglycemia
Authors: Chutima Chavanisakun, Siriwan Tangjitgamol, Chadakarn Phaloprakarn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionGestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is associated with adverse outcomes in both pregnancy and the postpartum period. Although placental pathology has been extensively studied in relation to neonatal complications, its relevance to maternal health after delivery remains less well defined. This study aimed to compare gross and histologic placental features between GDM and normoglycemic pregnancies according to the Amsterdam Placental Workshop Group Consensus Statement. It also evaluated associations between specific placental lesions and both neonatal outcomes and maternal dysglycemia at 6 weeks postpartum in the GDM cohort.Material and MethodsThis prospective observational cohort study included 200 women with GDM and 100 normoglycemic women who delivered at term. Placental examination comprised gross and histologic evaluation of lesions classified according to the Amsterdam criteria, including maternal and fetal vascular malperfusion, as well as commonly reported non‐classified lesions such as chorangiosis and delayed villous maturation (DVM). Neonatal outcomes assessed were large‐for‐gestational‐age status, low 1‐min Apgar scores (<7), and neonatal hypoglycemia. Postpartum maternal dysglycemia at 6 weeks was assessed using a 75‐g, 2‐h oral glucose tolerance test and classified as prediabetes or type 2 diabetes mellitus. Associations were evaluated using logistic regression analysis.ResultsPlacental lesions were significantly more common in GDM than in normoglycemic pregnancies. These included maternal vascular malperfusion (67.5% vs. 39.0%, p < 0.001), fetal vascular malperfusion (49.0% vs. 22.0%, p < 0.001), chorangiosis (51.0% vs. 11.0%, p < 0.001), and DVM (17.0% vs. 8.0%, p = 0.034). DVM was independently associated with large‐for‐gestational‐age infants (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] = 2.54, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 1.03–6.24) and low 1‐min Apgar scores (aOR = 4.59, 95% CI = 1.08–19.63). Chorangiosis was independently associated with maternal dysglycemia at 6 weeks postpartum (aOR = 2.33, 95% CI = 1.07–5.07), an association primarily driven by prediabetes (aOR = 2.74, 95% CI = 1.22–6.15). Gross placental measurements did not differ significantly between groups.ConclusionsGDM is associated with various placental abnormalities, including Amsterdam‐classified and non‐classified lesions. DVM was associated with adverse neonatal outcomes, whereas chorangiosis was associated with postpartum dysglycemia. Systematic placental examination may aid in identifying women at risk of future metabolic disease.Keywords: Amsterdam criteria, dysglycemia, gestational diabetes mellitus, gross pathology, histology, neonatal outcomes, placenta

Key message

Placental lesions, including delayed villous maturation and chorangiosis, are clinically relevant, with delayed villous maturation linked to adverse neonatal outcomes and chorangiosis predictive of postpartum dysglycemia.

INTRODUCTION

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is a common metabolic disorder of pregnancy, characterized by glucose intolerance first recognized during gestation. 1 The hyperglycemic intrauterine environment in GDM disrupts placental structure and function through morphological and histologic changes, 2 , 3 , 4 leading to impaired maternal–fetal nutrient and gas exchange, 2 which may affect pregnancy outcomes and long‐term maternal and neonatal health.

Assessing placental pathology is integral to understanding the impact of GDM on pregnancy outcomes. To standardize diagnostic criteria, the Amsterdam Placental Workshop Group introduced a classification system in 2016 that categorizes key placental lesions into maternal vascular malperfusion (MVM), fetal vascular malperfusion (FVM), acute chorioamnionitis, and villitis of unknown etiology. 5 , 6 In line with this classification approach, previous studies have reported a higher prevalence of FVM and MVM in placentas from pregnancies complicated by GDM. 7 , 8 , 9 Notably, a study conducted in Japan using the antenatal 75‐g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) and the International Association of Diabetes and Pregnancy Study Group diagnostic criteria 1 demonstrated that the severity of FVM correlated with the degree of dysglycemia during pregnancy. 7 In parallel, research from the United States showed that MVM lesions were associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes. 9 Taken together, these findings suggest that maternal and fetal vascular pathologies may represent complementary pathways through which GDM disrupts placental function and contributes to complications in both mothers and newborns.

Placental examination provides valuable insights into pregnancy outcomes; however, its clinical utility is often limited because pathological findings are typically assessed retrospectively. 10 Beyond immediate pregnancy complications, emerging evidence suggests that placental pathology may predict long‐term maternal health outcomes. For instance, MVM lesions in normoglycemic pregnancies have been associated with adverse cardiovascular profiles a decade postpartum. 11 However, whether placental abnormalities in GDM pregnancies, which have been previously linked to the degree of antenatal dysglycemia, 7 can predict postpartum metabolic disorders remains largely unexplored. Identifying such associations could enable prospective risk stratification and targeted prevention strategies.

To address this gap, we conducted the Placenta in Gestational Diabetes Mellitus (PL‐GDM) study, analyzing the gross and histologic features of placentas from GDM and normoglycemic pregnancies using the Amsterdam criteria. We aimed to identify placental differences between both groups and evaluate the associations of placental lesions with adverse neonatal outcomes and postpartum dysglycemia in the GDM cohort.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

Study design and population

We conducted the PL‐GDM study, a prospective observational cohort, at a university hospital in Bangkok, Thailand, between December 2021 and May 2024. The PL‐GDM study was an umbrella project comprising several substudies on placental pathology in GDM pregnancies. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrollment.

Prior analyses of the PL‐GDM cohort examined umbilical venous blood flow and birthweight‐to‐placental weight ratios in relation to placental pathology and perinatal outcomes. 12 , 13 Extending these findings, the current substudy investigated associations between placental pathology and neonatal outcomes and, for the first time, postpartum maternal dysglycemia. The inclusion and exclusion criteria have been described in detail elsewhere. 13 Participants were classified as having GDM or normoglycemia. Although some countries adopt a 75‐g OGTT based on the International Association of Diabetes and Pregnancy Study Group criteria, GDM in Thailand is predominantly diagnosed using a 100‐g, 3‐h OGTT with Carpenter and Coustan thresholds. 1 This approach is recommended by national obstetric guidelines and represents the standard diagnostic protocol at our institution. 14

Sample size

The sample size was calculated based on data from a prior study, 15 which reported a mean placental weight of 734 g in GDM pregnancies compared to 668 g in normoglycemic pregnancies. Assuming a significance level of 0.05 and a power of 90%, a minimum of 60 participants per group was required. To ensure adequate statistical power, the main PL‐GDM study included 200 women with GDM and 100 women with normoglycemic pregnancies.

Placental gross and histologic examination

Placentas with attached umbilical cords were collected immediately after delivery and fixed in 10% buffered formalin. Gross examination was performed according to standard protocols, as previously described. 12 In brief, the placental membranes and umbilical cord were trimmed flush with the placental disc. The trimmed placentas were weighed using a calibrated digital scale (PULISITE model PTX‐5102; Provision Scientific, Bangkok, Thailand). Placental diameter and circumference were measured using a measuring tape, whereas placental thickness was recorded at the midpoint of the placental disc using a ruler. Placental volume was determined using the water displacement method, whereby the placental disc was enclosed in a plastic bag and fully submerged in a water‐filled container. The volume of displaced water was then measured in a beaker, with 1 mL corresponding to 1 cm3 of placental tissue.

Gross placental lesions, including hematomas, infarcts, and umbilical cord abnormalities were systematically documented before histopathologic sampling. Representative tissue sections were obtained from the cord, membranes, and three full‐thickness placental sites (central disc, cord insertion, and disc margin), with additional sections taken from any abnormal‐appearing areas. Tissues were paraffin‐embedded, cut at 4 μm, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin. All slides were reviewed by an experienced pathologist (C.C.), who was blinded to the clinical data, with discrepant cases adjudicated by a second pathologist (S.T.).

Placental lesions were classified using the Amsterdam criteria, focusing on MVM and FVM lesions, with additional evaluation for chorangiosis and delayed villous maturation (DVM). MVM included infarcts, retroplacental hemorrhage, decidual arteriopathy, and accelerated villous maturation; FVM included thrombosis, avascular villi, intramural fibrin deposition, villous stromal‐vascular karyorrhexis, stem vessel obliteration, and vascular ectasia. 5 , 7 Chorangiosis was defined as the presence of ≥10 capillaries in ≥10 terminal chorionic villi across ≥10 non‐infarcted areas in at least three low‐power microscopic fields of the placenta. 16 Diagnosis of DVM required a monotonous population of ≥10 terminal villi with central capillaries and reduced vasculosyncytial membranes involving ≥30% of a full‐thickness section. 5

Data collection

Data collection included maternal clinical data, placental gross and histologic findings, neonatal adverse outcomes, and postpartum dysglycemia (in the GDM group).

Maternal clinical data included age, parity, family history of diabetes, pre‐pregnancy body mass index (BMI), BMI at delivery, gestational weight gain, gestational age (GA) at delivery, and mode of delivery for all participants. For participants with GDM, additional data included glucose levels from the antenatal 100‐g OGTT, GA at GDM diagnosis, and treatment modality. When pharmacologic intervention was indicated, insulin served as the sole therapeutic option, as oral hypoglycemic agents were not utilized for GDM management at our institution.

Neonatal outcomes assessed were large‐for‐gestational‐age status (birth weight above the 90th percentile for GA based on Thai neonatal birth weight nomograms), 17 low 1‐min Apgar scores (<7), and neonatal hypoglycemia, diagnosed according to the criteria of the American Academy of Pediatrics. 18

Postpartum glucose tolerance was assessed at 6 weeks using a 75‐g, 2‐h OGTT. Results were classified as normal glucose tolerance or dysglycemia, which included prediabetes and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). Prediabetes was defined as a fasting plasma glucose level of 100–125 mg/dL (5.6–6.9 mmol/L) or a 2‐h plasma glucose level of 140–199 mg/dL (7.8–11.0 mmol/L), whereas T2DM was defined as a fasting plasma glucose level ≥ 126 mg/dL (≥7.0 mmol/L) or a 2‐h plasma glucose level ≥ 200 mg/dL (≥11.1 mmol/L). 1

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 28.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were assessed for normality and compared using Student's t‐test or Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate. Categorical variables were compared using the χ 2 test. Multivariable logistic regression was used to examine associations between placental lesions, neonatal outcomes, and postpartum dysglycemia. The models were adjusted for potential confounders. For associations with adverse neonatal outcomes, adjustments included maternal age, pre‐pregnancy BMI, insulin use, GA at delivery, and mode of delivery. For associations with postpartum dysglycemia, adjustments included maternal age, pre‐pregnancy BMI, family history of diabetes, number of abnormal values on the 100‐g OGTT, GA at GDM diagnosis, and insulin use. A p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Compared with the normoglycemic group, women with GDM were significantly older, more likely to have a family history of diabetes, and had higher pre‐pregnancy and delivery BMIs (Table 1). Additionally, they delivered at a slightly earlier GA and had a higher rate of spontaneous labor.

With respect to placental gross findings, no significant differences were observed between groups in diameter, circumference, thickness, volume, or weight, nor in the presence of hematomas or infarcts. In contrast, histologic examination revealed a significantly higher prevalence of MVM, FVM, chorangiosis, and DVM in the GDM group compared with normoglycemic controls (all p < 0.05; Table 1).

Associations of placental lesions with maternal 100‐g OGTT results during pregnancy and adverse neonatal outcomes

In the GDM group, the mean GA at diagnosis was 21.6 ± 8.7 weeks (median 26 weeks; interquartile range 13–29 weeks). During the diagnostic 100‐g OGTT, abnormal fasting glucose values were observed in 81 participants (40.5%), while abnormal 1‐h, 2‐h, and 3‐h glucose values were observed in 173 (86.5%), 169 (84.5%), and 103 (51.5%) participants, respectively. Most participants (n = 108; 54%) exhibited two abnormal glucose values, followed by three (n = 58; 29%) and four abnormal values (n = 34; 17%). When examining associations between placental lesions and metabolic parameters, no significant associations were identified between abnormal glucose values or insulin use during pregnancy and the evaluated placental lesions, including MVM, FVM, chorangiosis, or DVM. In contrast, GA at GDM diagnosis was significantly associated with chorangiosis. Specifically, women diagnosed with GDM at or before 24 weeks of gestation had a higher prevalence of chorangiosis than those diagnosed later (Table 2).

Associations of placental lesions with postpartum dysglycemia

A total of 172 participants with GDM (86.0%) completed a 75‐g, 2‐h OGTT at 6 weeks postpartum. The clinical characteristics of these 172 participants and the 28 participants who were lost to follow‐up did not differ significantly, except that the prevalence of chorangiosis was lower in the returning group than in the non‐returning group (47.1% vs. 75.0%, p = 0.006). The associations between placental lesions and postpartum dysglycemia among the 172 participants who completed follow‐up are shown in Table 4. No significant relationships were observed between MVM, FVM, or DVM and any form of postpartum dysglycemia (prediabetes or T2DM). In contrast, chorangiosis was significantly associated with a higher prevalence of overall dysglycemia (46.9% vs. 27.5%, p = 0.008; unadjusted OR 2.33, 95% CI 1.24–4.40), driven primarily by an increased prevalence of prediabetes (42.0% vs. 23.1%, p = 0.008; unadjusted OR 2.41, 95% CI 1.25–4.66).

DISCUSSION

In this prospective cohort substudy of the PL‐GDM study, placental histologic abnormalities, including MVM, FVM, chorangiosis, and DVM, were significantly more prevalent in the GDM group compared with normoglycemic controls. Importantly, specific placental lesions showed differential associations with clinical outcomes that have distinct neonatal and maternal implications. Among women with GDM, DVM was associated with adverse neonatal outcomes, whereas chorangiosis was associated with early postpartum dysglycemia. These findings are particularly relevant in Thailand, where GDM affects approximately 17–22% of pregnancies. 19 , 20

From a pathophysiological perspective, these associations are biologically plausible. Experimental models have demonstrated that maternal hyperglycemia induces endothelial dysfunction through oxidative stress and inflammatory pathways. 21 This pro‐inflammatory intrauterine environment promotes thrombosis, tissue hypoxia, and dysregulated angiogenesis, resulting in placental vascular lesions. 22 , 23 Consistent with these mechanisms, previous studies applying the Amsterdam criteria or conventional pathology have reported higher rates of FVM, MVM, chorangiosis, and DVM in GDM pregnancies, 7 , 8 , 9 findings that align with our results.

Despite the higher prevalence of histologic abnormalities, placental size did not differ between GDM and normoglycemic pregnancies. This finding is consistent with prior reports 24 , 25 and may be explained by the favorable metabolic profiles in our cohort. Most women with GDM achieved good glycemic control, with 77% meeting the glucose targets (fasting glucose <95 mg/dL [<5.3 mmol/L] and 2‐h postprandial glucose <120 mg/dL [<6.7 mmol/L]) recommended by the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists without requiring insulin therapy. 1 Although the median pre‐pregnancy BMI in the GDM group (26 kg/m2) was slightly higher than that reported in previous Thai studies (22–25 kg/m2), 19 , 20 the majority of participants in our cohort had pre‐pregnancy and delivery BMIs <30 kg/m2 (77% and 69%, respectively) and gestational weight gain <20 kg (81%). Given that placental enlargement in GDM has been linked to obesity (BMI >30 kg/m2), excessive weight gain (>20 kg), and poor glycemic control, 26 , 27 , 28 the absence of placental size differences in our well‐controlled cohort is not unexpected.

Within the GDM group, we found no association between the degree of hyperglycemia and any placental lesion. This contrasts with a prior study linking the number of abnormal glucose values to FVM severity 7 and may reflect differences in OGTT protocols (100‐g vs. 75‐g), maternal BMI, ethnicity, and comorbidities. The role of insulin therapy in placental pathology remains debated. While insulin therapy has been hypothesized to influence placental function through improved glycemic control or potential direct vascular effects, 29 our data do not support an independent effect on placental vascular development. This lack of association may reflect the generally good glycemic control achieved in our study, suggesting that when glucose targets are met, the mode of treatment may be less critical than the adequate metabolic regulation.

Instead, the timing of metabolic exposure appeared to be more critical. Chorangiosis was significantly associated with GDM diagnosis at or before 24 weeks of gestation, suggesting that early gestational hyperglycemia exerts greater influence on placental vascular development than its severity or subsequent treatment. Placental vasculogenesis and angiogenesis occur predominantly during early to mid‐gestation, with key processes largely completed by approximately 24 weeks. 30 Chronic metabolic disturbances during this critical developmental window may result in sustained hypoxic signaling and compensatory capillary hyperplasia, manifesting histologically as chorangiosis. 16

We next examined the associations between specific placental lesions and neonatal outcomes. Among women with GDM, DVM showed the strongest association with adverse neonatal outcomes, particularly LGA infants and low 1‐min Apgar scores. Mechanistically, maternal hyperglycemia induces fetal hyperinsulinemia, driving excessive fetal growth and increased metabolic demand. 21 In response, the placenta undergoes adaptive structural remodeling, 2 including villous immaturity with expansion of terminal villi, characteristic of DVM, to enhance nutrient exchange. 8 , 31 , 32 This adaptive response is supported by a prior analysis of the PL‐GDM cohort demonstrating a progressive increase in DVM prevalence across higher umbilical venous blood flow categories. 12 Elevated umbilical venous blood flow reflects enhanced fetoplacental perfusion, facilitating increased transplacental nutrient delivery to sustain accelerated fetal growth. This mechanism likely underlies the observed association between DVM and LGA infants in our study.

Beyond its role in fetal overgrowth, DVM may also compromise neonatal outcomes through its structural characteristics. 5 Excessive trophoblast proliferation and reduced vasculosyncytial membranes impair gas exchange, increasing vulnerability to perinatal hypoxia. 33 This mechanism may explain the association between DVM and low 1‐min Apgar scores observed in our cohort and is consistent with previous reports linking DVM to adverse neonatal outcomes, including stillbirth and congenital anomalies. 34 , 35 , 36

More importantly for long‐term maternal health, our findings reveal a novel association between placental pathology and postpartum metabolic dysfunction. Chorangiosis was independently associated with dysglycemia at 6 weeks postpartum. This finding extends prior evidence linking placental vascular lesions to long‐term maternal cardiovascular risk 11 and identifies placental hypoxic lesions as potential markers of early metabolic vulnerability. As a histologic marker of chronic placental hypoxia characterized by capillary hyperplasia, 16 chorangiosis is associated with sustained hypoxic and inflammatory signaling. 37 , 38 , 39 These pathways overlap with mechanisms promoting insulin resistance and glucose dysregulation, 40 suggesting chorangiosis may reflect underlying maternal metabolic vulnerability persisting beyond pregnancy rather than solely fetal adaptation.

From a clinical standpoint, this association offers a clinically actionable opportunity for early risk stratification. Unlike the retrospective nature of neonatal outcome assessment, postpartum metabolic screening can be intensified and targeted based on placental findings. Although the Amsterdam classification system is widely accepted for standardized placental pathology reporting, 5 , 6 it does not formally include chorangiosis or DVM within its primary diagnostic categories (MVM, FVM, acute chorioamnionitis, and villitis of unknown etiology). Our findings suggest that incorporating routinely observed lesions such as chorangiosis and DVM alongside Amsterdam‐classified lesions may enhance postpartum risk assessment and support targeted interventions for T2DM prevention in women with GDM.

The strengths of this study include its prospective design, standardized placental pathology assessment, inclusion of a normoglycemic control group, and high postpartum follow‐up rate. These features allowed a robust evaluation of the relationships between GDM, placental pathology, neonatal outcomes, and postpartum dysglycemia.

This study has several limitations. First, nearly half of women with GDM (48%) were diagnosed at ≤24 weeks of gestation; although women with known diabetes were excluded and HbA1c was assessed in those with markedly elevated GCT values (≥200 mg/dL [≥11.1 mmol/L]), undiagnosed pre‐existing T2DM could not be completely excluded. Second, glycemic management was limited to diet or insulin therapy because metformin was not included in institutional guidelines; consequently, our findings apply specifically to diet‐controlled or insulin‐treated GDM. Third, aspirin prophylaxis was not administered, as this cohort was considered low risk for preeclampsia, which restricts generalizability to populations receiving aspirin prophylaxis and precludes assessment of its effects on placental vascular lesions. Fourth, neonatal assessment relied solely on Apgar scores, as umbilical cord blood pH was not routinely available, limiting objective assessment of perinatal hypoxia. Finally, the relatively small number of adverse neonatal outcomes may have limited statistical power. Additionally, selection bias cannot be excluded, particularly given the higher prevalence of chorangiosis among women lost to postpartum follow‐up.

CONCLUSION

This study confirms that placental abnormalities, including MVM, FVM, chorangiosis, and DVM, are more prevalent in pregnancies complicated by GDM. Extending this knowledge, we demonstrate that specific placental lesions are differentially associated with clinical outcomes. DVM was independently associated with LGA infants and low Apgar scores, whereas chorangiosis was associated with early postpartum dysglycemia and was more common in women diagnosed with GDM at ≤24 weeks of gestation. These findings underscore the clinical value of placental pathology for evaluating both neonatal outcomes and maternal metabolic risk. Incorporating both Amsterdam‐classified lesions and routinely observed but non‐categorized abnormalities, such as chorangiosis and DVM, into postpartum assessment may enhance risk stratification and support targeted T2DM prevention in women with GDM.
