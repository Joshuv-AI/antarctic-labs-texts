Innovative approach to synthesizing polyhydroquinoline and dihydropyrimidine derivatives from alcohols by g-C3N4/PANI/Cu2+ nanocomposite
Authors: Abbas Moradi, Hossein Ghafuri, Sharif Kaamyabi, Zeinab Tajik, Peyman Hanifehnejad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study introduces an innovative and sustainable approach for synthesizing polyhydroquinoline and dihydropyrimidine derivatives directly from alcohols via in situ oxidation to aldehydes, using a nanocomposite catalyst composed of graphitic carbon nitride (g-C₃N₄), polyaniline (PANI), and copper ions (Cu²⁺). The g-C₃N₄/PANI/Cu²⁺ catalyst exhibited excellent catalytic performance, affording yields of 63–84% under relatively mild and environmentally acceptable conditions. Comprehensive characterization by Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR), energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS), X-ray diffraction (XRD), and field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM) supported the successful formation and structural stability of the nanocomposite. Mechanistic insights suggest that cooperative interactions among g-C₃N₄, PANI, and Cu²⁺ species facilitate the in situ oxidation of alcohols and subsequent multicomponent condensation, leading to heterocyclic frameworks. Overall, this methodology provides an efficient and sustainable catalytic pathway for synthesizing biologically relevant heterocycles with potential applications in pharmaceuticals and materials science.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-44478-5.Keywords: g-C3N4/PANI/Cu2+, Nanocomposite, Polyhydroquinoline, Dihydropyrimidine, Aromatic alcoholSubject terms: Chemistry, Materials science

Introduction

Heterocyclic compounds constitute a fundamental class of molecules in both natural and industrial systems due to their diverse structural features and broad biological activities1,2. Many biomolecules, including DNA, vitamins, and enzymes, contain heterocyclic frameworks, and more than 90% of newly developed pharmaceuticals incorporate heterocyclic motifs with antibacterial, antifungal, antiviral, anticancer, and anti-inflammatory properties3,4. Consequently, the development of efficient and sustainable methods for heterocycle synthesis remains an important objective in modern organic chemistry5.

Conventional catalytic systems for heterocycle synthesis often rely on harsh reaction conditions, expensive reagents, and limited catalyst recyclability. In recent years, nanocomposite catalysts have emerged as attractive alternatives owing to their high catalytic efficiency, tunable physicochemical properties, and improved environmental compatibility6–8. Nevertheless, many reported nanocomposites still suffer from drawbacks such as complex preparation procedures, high material cost, or limited structural stability9–11.

Graphitic carbon nitride (g-C₃N₄) has attracted considerable attention as a sustainable catalytic platform because of its unique electronic structure, high thermal stability, and metal-free composition12–14. However, its relatively low surface area and limited number of accessible active sites often restrict its catalytic performance15. Therefore, structural modification and functionalization strategies are commonly employed to enhance the activity of g-C₃N₄-based systems16.

Among various modification approaches, the incorporation of conductive polymer matrices—particularly polyaniline (PANI)—is highly effective. PANI can suppress agglomeration, improve electrical conductivity, and facilitate charge transfer within the composite framework, thereby enhancing catalytic efficiency17–20. In addition, coordination of transition-metal species such as Cu²⁺ with nitrogen-rich supports can introduce highly active redox centers that promote oxidative transformations21. These advantages render such hybrid systems highly attractive; however, the development of efficient heterogeneous systems capable of promoting tandem oxidative multicomponent reactions under mild aqueous conditions remains limited.

Multicomponent reactions (MCRs) represent powerful and atom-economic strategies for constructing complex heterocyclic scaffolds in a single operation. These processes are well aligned with green chemistry principles, especially when performed in environmentally benign solvents such as water22–24. Furthermore, tandem oxidative processes (TOPs), in which alcohols serve as in situ precursors of aldehydes, provide a more sustainable alternative to conventional aldehyde-based protocols by simplifying reaction handling and reducing environmental impact25,26.

In this work, we report the design and application of a g-C₃N₄/PANI/Cu²⁺ nanocomposite as an efficient heterogeneous catalyst for the one-pot synthesis of pharmacologically relevant polyhydroquinoline and dihydropyrimidine derivatives directly from aromatic alcohols. The synergistic interaction between the nitrogen-rich g-C₃N₄ framework, conductive PANI matrix, and Cu²⁺ redox centers enables efficient tandem oxidation–condensation under mild aqueous conditions, providing a green and practical route to valuable heterocyclic compounds.

Experimental

Reagents and apparatus

All chemicals were purchased from Merck and Sigma-Aldrich (Germany) and used without further purification. Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectra were recorded on a Tensor 27 spectrometer (Bruker, Germany). X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were obtained using a Dron-8 diffractometer (Russia). Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) measurements were performed on a Numerix DXP-X10P analyzer (USA). Field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM) images were recorded using a TESCAN MIRA-III microscope (Czech Republic). Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra were acquired on a Varian Inova 500 MHz spectrometer (Agilent Technologies, USA).

Preparation of bulk g-C₃N₄ and exfoliated g-C₃N₄ nanosheets

Melamine (2 g) was thermally polymerized at 550 °C for 4 h in a muffle furnace with a heating rate of 2.5 °C min⁻¹ to obtain a yellow powder of bulk g-C₃N₄. The resulting material was finely ground using a ball mill. Subsequently, bulk g-C₃N₄ (1.0 g) was dispersed in concentrated H₂SO₄ (10 mL) and stirred at 90 °C for 5 h to achieve partial exfoliation. After cooling to room temperature, ethanol (100 mL) was added, and the mixture was stirred for an additional 2 h. The obtained suspension was then ultrasonicated in 30 mL of a water/isopropanol mixture (1:1 v/v) to complete the exfoliation process. Finally, the exfoliated g-C₃N₄ nanosheets were collected by filtration, washed thoroughly with deionized water, and dried at 60 °C under vacuum27.

Preparation of g-C₃N₄/PANI and g-C₃N₄/PANI/Cu²⁺ nanocatalysts

40 mg of g-C₃N₄ nanosheets were dispersed in 1 mL of 1 M H₂SO₄ and sonicated for 1 h in a round-bottom flask. Subsequently, aniline (0.07 mL, 0.77 mmol) was added to the dispersion and stirred at room temperature for 30 min until complete dissolution was achieved. Separately, ammonium persulfate (0.05 g) was dissolved in 5 mL of 1 M H₂SO₄ and then added dropwise to the above mixture under continuous stirring at 0–5 °C for 8 h to initiate the oxidative polymerization of aniline. The resulting dark-green precipitate was collected by filtration, thoroughly washed with deionized water and ethanol, and dried at 60 °C for 12 h to yield the g-C₃N₄/PANI composite28.

For the synthesis of the g-C₃N₄/PANI/Cu²⁺ nanocatalyst, Cu(NO₃)₂·6 H₂O (80 mg) was dissolved in deionized water (10 mL) and added to the as-prepared g-C₃N₄/PANI composite (40 mg). The mixture was stirred at room temperature for 12 h to allow coordination between Cu²⁺ ions and the nitrogen sites of polyaniline. Finally, the product was filtered, washed several times with deionized water, and dried at 60 °C under vacuum29.

General procedure for the synthesis of polyhydroquinoline derivatives from alcohols

In a typical reaction, benzyl alcohol (1.0 mmol), ethyl acetoacetate (1.0 mmol), dimedone (1.0 mmol), and ammonium acetate (1.0 mmol) were mixed with g-C₃N₄/PANI/Cu²⁺ nanocatalyst (10 mg) and tert-butyl hydroperoxide (TBHP, 1.5 equiv, 1.5 mmol, 0.2 mL of 70% aqueous solution) in 3 mL of deionized water. The reaction mixture was refluxed at 80 °C for 4 h under magnetic stirring. The progress of the reaction was monitored by thin-layer chromatography (TLC). Upon completion, the catalyst was separated by simple filtration, washed with water and ethanol, and dried for reuse. The organic layer was extracted with ethyl acetate, and the combined extracts were concentrated under reduced pressure. The resulting crude product was purified by recrystallization from ethanol to afford the desired polyhydroquinoline derivatives in good yields. General procedure for the synthesis of dihydropyrimidine derivatives from alcohols

In a typical one-pot reaction, benzyl alcohol (1.0 mmol), ethyl acetoacetate (1.0 mmol), urea (1.0 mmol), g-C₃N₄/PANI/Cu²⁺ nanocatalyst (10 mg), and tert-butyl hydroperoxide (TBHP, 1.5 equiv, 1.5 mmol, 0.2 mL of 70% aqueous solution) were mixed in 3 mL of deionized water. The reaction mixture was refluxed at 80 °C for 4 h under magnetic stirring. The progress of the reaction was monitored by thin-layer chromatography (TLC). Upon completion, the catalyst was separated by simple filtration, washed with water and ethanol, and dried for reuse. The organic products were extracted with ethyl acetate, and the combined organic layers were concentrated under reduced pressure. The crude solid was purified by recrystallization from ethanol to afford the desired dihydropyrimidine derivatives in good yields. Results and Discussion

Synthesis and characterization of g-C3N4/PANI/Cu2+

The preparation of g-C₃N₄/PANI/Cu²⁺ proceeds through four consecutive steps (Fig. 1). First, bulk g-C₃N₄ was obtained by calcination of melamine in a muffle furnace. Second, the bulk material was acid-treated with H₂SO₄ (10 mL) and diluted with ethanol (100 mL), followed by ultrasonication in a water/isopropanol mixture (1:1 v/v) to afford exfoliated g-C₃N₄ nanosheets. Third, g-C₃N₄/PANI was produced via in situ oxidative polymerization: the nanosheets were dispersed in H₂SO₄ (1 mL, 1 M), aniline (0.07 mL) was added, and an aqueous solution of ammonium persulfate (0.05 g in 1 M H₂SO₄, 5 mL) was introduced dropwise at 0–5 °C; the reaction was maintained for 8 h. The dark-green solid was collected by filtration, washed thoroughly with deionized water and ethanol, and dried at 60 °C for 12 h. Finally, g-C₃N₄/PANI/Cu²⁺ was obtained by mixing the as-prepared g-C₃N₄/PANI composite with copper(II) nitrate hexahydrate (Cu(NO₃)₂·6H₂O) in deionized water at a 2:1 (w/w) composite: Cu-salt ratio. The mixture was stirred at room temperature for 12 h, followed by filtration, washing, and drying. This ratio was selected to achieve uniform Cu²⁺ coordination with the nitrogen sites of polyaniline and g-C₃N₄ while avoiding surface oversaturation or Cu aggregation, ensuring high dispersion and catalytic activity.

Schematic synthesis of g-C3N4/PANI/Cu2+ catalyst.

The chemical structure of the synthesized materials was investigated by FT-IR spectroscopy (Fig. 2). For pure g-C₃N₄ (Fig. 2a), a broad absorption band in the 3200–3500 cm⁻¹ region is attributed to N–H stretching vibrations of terminal amino groups together with O–H stretching of adsorbed moisture. The absorption band at 1651 cm⁻¹ corresponds to the C = N stretching vibration, while the peaks at 1300–1500 cm⁻¹ arise from the aromatic C–N and C–NH–C stretching modes within the heptazine units. The prominent band at 792 cm⁻¹ corresponds to the characteristic breathing mode of the s-triazine/heptazine units of g-C₃N₄. Additional weaker bands observed at lower wavenumbers (704 and 618 cm⁻¹) can be attributed to out-of-plane deformation and skeletal vibration modes of the CN heterocyclic framework30,31. In the spectrum of PANI (Fig. 2b), the peaks at 1557 cm⁻¹ and 1480 cm⁻¹ are assigned to the stretching vibrations of quinoid (C = N) and benzenoid (C = C) rings, respectively, confirming the emeraldine form of polyaniline. The absorption band observed in the 1250–1300 cm⁻¹ region is attributed to C–N stretching vibrations of the aromatic amine units in the polymer backbone. A broad band in the 3200–3400 cm⁻¹ region can be assigned to N–H stretching of secondary amine groups. The band at 790 cm⁻¹ is attributed to the out-of-plane bending vibration of aromatic C–H bonds. Additionally, the band observed at 697 cm⁻¹ can be assigned to aromatic C–H out-of-plane deformation of the substituted benzene ring. The weak absorption around 500 cm⁻¹ is related to C–N deformation or ring skeletal vibrations32,33. In the FT-IR spectrum of the g-C₃N₄/PANI/Cu²⁺ nanocomposite (Fig. 2c), the characteristic bands of both g-C₃N₄ and PANI are retained, indicating that the polymerization and composite formation did not destroy the main frameworks. The bands in the 1300–1650 cm⁻¹ region are associated with C = N and C–N stretching vibrations from both components, and the ring-breathing vibration near 795 cm⁻¹ remains visible. Notably, slight shifts and broadening of the C = N and C–N bands compared with the parent materials are observed, which are consistent with interactions between Cu²⁺ species and nitrogen-containing sites of PANI and g-C₃N₄. In addition, a weak absorption band appearing in the low-wavenumber region (505 cm⁻¹) is observed for the g-C₃N₄/PANI/Cu²⁺ nanocomposite, which is consistent with the presence of copper species in the hybrid structure. Overall, the FT-IR results support the successful formation of the g-C₃N₄/PANI/Cu²⁺ nanocomposite.

FT-IR spectra of the g-C3N4 nanosheets (a), PANI (b), and g-C3N4/PANI/Cu2+ (c).

FT-IR spectroscopy is useful for identifying functional groups; however, it does not provide distinct peaks for confirming the presence of metallic species such as Cu. Therefore, energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) was performed to verify the elemental composition of the synthesized nanocomposite (Fig. 3). The EDS spectrum clearly shows the presence of carbon (C), nitrogen (N), oxygen (O), sulfur (S), and copper (Cu) elements in the g-C₃N₄/PANI/Cu²⁺ nanocomposite. The strong peaks of C and N indicate the presence of the g-C₃N₄ and PANI frameworks, while the weak but distinct Cu signals further support the successful incorporation of Cu species into the composite structure. The detected sulfur (≈ 0.46 at%) most likely originates from the use of sulfuric acid and ammonium persulfate during the polymerization process. Given its very low content, the presence of sulfur is not expected to significantly influence the main FT-IR features of g-C₃N₄. The characteristic vibrational bands of the heptazine framework remain clearly observable, indicating that the minor sulfur residue does not account for the observed FT-IR signals. According to the semi-quantitative EDS results, the atomic percentages of C, N, O, Cu, and S were approximately 72.3%, 13.9%, 12.8%, 0.6%, and 0.5%, respectively. To obtain a more accurate estimation of the copper content, inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES) was carried out, and the Cu loading in the composite was found to be 1.5 wt%.

EDS analysis of g-C3N4/PANI/Cu2+.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was performed to investigate the crystal structure and phase composition of the synthesized materials (Fig. 4). For pure g-C₃N₄ (Fig. 4a), two characteristic diffraction peaks appear at 2θ = 12.2° and 27.45°, which correspond to the (100) and (002) planes, respectively. The peak at 12.2° is attributed to the in-plane structural packing of tri-s-triazine units, while the strong diffraction at 27.45° is assigned to the interlayer stacking of conjugated aromatic systems, confirming the formation of g-C₃N₄ nanosheets with graphitic-like structure. The XRD pattern of PANI (Fig. 4b) exhibits broad diffraction peaks centered at 2θ = 20.8° and 25.3°, which correspond to the periodicity parallel and perpendicular to the polymer chain, respectively, confirming the semi-crystalline nature of polyaniline34. In the case of the g-C₃N₄/PANI/Cu²⁺ nanocomposite (Fig. 4c), the diffraction peaks of both g-C₃N₄ and PANI are retained, but with a slight broadening and decrease in intensity. No separate peaks corresponding to crystalline Cu or CuO phases are observed, suggesting that the Cu²⁺ ions are highly dispersed and successfully incorporated into the polymer–carbon nitride matrix rather than forming distinct crystalline domains. These observations collectively confirm the successful formation of the g-C₃N₄/PANI/Cu²⁺ nanocomposite and the preservation of the main structural features of both components.

XRD patterns of g-C3N4 nanosheets, PANI, and g-C3N4/PANI/Cu2+.

The surface morphology and microstructure of the synthesized samples were investigated by field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM) (Fig. 5). In the FE-SEM image of pure g-C₃N₄ (Fig. 5a), stacked and plate-like nanosheets with irregular edges are clearly visible, reflecting the layered graphitic structure of carbon nitride. The nanosheets appear partially agglomerated due to van der Waals interactions between layers. After incorporation of PANI and Cu²⁺ (Fig. 5b), the morphology changes significantly: the composite shows a rougher and more granular texture compared to pristine g-C₃N₄. The PANI matrix uniformly coats the g-C₃N₄ nanosheets, forming a continuous interconnected network that prevents layer restacking and increases the accessible surface area. These morphological changes confirm the strong interfacial interaction among the components and demonstrate that the g-C₃N₄/PANI/Cu²⁺ nanocomposite possesses a heterogeneous and porous surface favorable for catalytic reactions.

FE-SEM images of g-C3N4 nanosheets (a) and g-C3N4/PANI/Cu2+ (b).

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was employed to further investigate the morphology and structural features of the synthesized g-C₃N₄/PANI/Cu²⁺ nanocomposite (Fig. 6a-c). The TEM images reveal aggregated, sheet-like structures with wrinkled and irregular edges, characteristic of layered graphitic carbon nitride (g-C₃N₄). The presence of partially restacked nanosheets is observed, which can be attributed to strong π–π interactions and van der Waals forces during solvent evaporation and drying. The nanosheets exhibit regions of variable contrast, with thinner and semi-transparent edges surrounding relatively darker central domains, further confirming the layered nature of the material. No distinct or well-defined Cu-based crystalline nanoparticles are detected within the observed regions. The absence of visible metallic aggregates suggests that copper species are highly dispersed within the nitrogen-rich g-C₃N₄/PANI matrix rather than forming separate crystalline domains. Additionally, a slightly thickened and amorphous surface texture can be observed on the nanosheets, which may be attributed to the successful incorporation of polyaniline (PANI) onto the g-C₃N₄ framework. These observations are consistent with the XRD results, which show no detectable diffraction peaks corresponding to Cu-related crystalline phases, and with ICP analysis indicating a relatively low Cu loading (1.5 wt%).

Catalyst application for the synthesis of polyhydroquinoline and dihydropyrimidine derivatives

The catalytic activity of the g-C₃N₄/PANI/Cu²⁺ nanocomposite was evaluated in the synthesis of polyhydroquinoline and dihydropyrimidine derivatives directly from aromatic alcohols. Optimization of the reaction parameters was performed to achieve the highest catalytic efficiency, including the effects of oxidant type, catalyst loading, temperature, solvent, and reaction time. To establish the optimal conditions, two model reactions were investigated:

Model reaction 1 (Fig. 7): synthesis of polyhydroquinoline derivatives from 4-chlorobenzyl alcohol (1 mmol), dimedone (1 mmol), ethyl acetoacetate (1 mmol), and ammonium acetate (1 mmol).

Model reaction 1.

Model reaction 2 (Fig. 8): synthesis of dihydropyrimidine derivatives from 4-chlorobenzyl alcohol (1 mmol), ethyl acetoacetate (1 mmol), and urea (1 mmol).

Model reaction 2.

The reaction progress was monitored by TLC, and the final products were isolated by recrystallization. In the absence of any catalyst, no product formation was observed regardless of the oxidant used (Table 1, Entries 1–3), confirming the essential role of the catalyst. Similarly, the reaction did not proceed without an oxidant (Table 1, Entry 4), highlighting the necessity of an oxidative step for in situ conversion of alcohols to the corresponding aldehydes—an intermediate required for the subsequent multicomponent condensation. Upon introducing 10 mg of g-C₃N₄/PANI/Cu²⁺ catalyst, the reaction proceeded efficiently, yielding the desired products. Among various oxidants (O₂, H₂O₂, and TBHP), TBHP provided the best results (Table 1, Entries 5–7). The superior performance of TBHP can be attributed to its controlled and selective oxidation ability under mild conditions, whereas O₂ tends to cause overoxidation of the aldehyde intermediates, and H₂O₂ often leads to the formation of carboxylic acids. The effect of reaction temperature was also evaluated (Table 1, Entries 8–10). Increasing the temperature from room temperature to 80 °C enhanced the reaction yield due to the higher collision frequency and activation of catalytic sites. However, temperatures above 80 °C did not provide further improvement and could promote by-product formation. The solvent effect was next examined using water, acetonitrile, DMF, and DMSO (Table 1, Entries 11–14). Water was found to be the optimal solvent, offering the highest yield and selectivity. Its polarity and ability to form hydrogen bonds facilitate solubilization of polar intermediates and stabilize transition states, in line with green chemistry principles. Varying the catalyst amount (5, 10, and 15 mg) demonstrated that 10 mg was optimal (Table 1, Entries 15–17). Lower catalyst amounts reduced the number of available active sites, while higher loadings decreased selectivity, possibly due to overoxidation of intermediates. Finally, the reaction time was studied at 4, 6, and 8 h (Table 1, Entries 18–20). The highest yield was achieved after 4 h, while prolonged reaction times led to a decrease in product formation, likely due to the oxidation of intermediate aldehydes to carboxylic acids. Based on these results, the optimal reaction conditions were determined as follows: 10 mg catalyst, TBHP oxidant, H₂O as solvent, 80 °C reaction temperature, and 4 h reaction time. To further evaluate the practical applicability and catalytic efficiency of the optimized protocol, the model reaction was performed on a 5 mmol scale under identical conditions using proportionally increased catalyst loading. The isolated yield remained comparable to that obtained at the 1 mmol scale, confirming the robustness and scalability of the catalytic system. In addition, based on the Cu loading determined by ICP analysis (1.5 wt%), the turnover number (TON) and turnover frequency (TOF) were calculated. TON values of approximately 331 and 318 were obtained for model reactions 1 and 2, corresponding to TOF values of 82.6 and 79.4 h⁻¹, respectively, indicating efficient catalytic performance. Calculation details are provided in the Supporting Information. To further identify the role of each catalyst component, control experiments were conducted with g-C₃N₄, PANI, g-C₃N₄/PANI, and Cu(NO₃)₂ under identical conditions (Table 2). Among them, the g-C₃N₄/PANI/Cu²⁺ nanocomposite exhibited the highest catalytic activity, suggesting the synergistic effect between the g-C₃N₄ support, conductive PANI matrix, and Cu²⁺ active sites. To further clarify the role of the PANI component, a comparative experiment was performed using single-atom/ionic Cu(II) supported on g-C₃N₄ (Cu@C₃N₄) under the optimized conditions. Compared with g-C₃N₄/PANI/Cu²⁺, the Cu@C₃N₄ catalyst afforded lower yield within the same reaction time while maintaining comparable selectivity. This result indicates that although g-C₃N₄ effectively stabilizes Cu–Nₓ active sites, the presence of the conductive PANI phase facilitates faster Cu²⁺/Cu⁺ redox cycling and TBHP activation, leading to enhanced catalytic efficiency (Table 1, Entry 21). In addition, the reaction was examined under solvent-free conditions (Table 1, Entry 22). Under these conditions, a significant decrease in yield was observed (17% and 13% for reactions 1 and 2, respectively). The marked reduction in efficiency highlights the crucial role of water in facilitating effective heterogeneous catalysis. The absence of an aqueous medium likely limits efficient interfacial contact between the solid catalyst, TBHP, and the alcohol substrate, thereby reducing the efficiency of the tandem oxidation–condensation process.

The g-C₃N₄/PANI/Cu²⁺ nanocomposite catalyst exhibited excellent activity and broad substrate compatibility in the synthesis of both polyhydroquinoline and dihydropyrimidine derivatives from a variety of aromatic alcohols under the optimized conditions (Tables 3 and 4). For the polyhydroquinoline derivatives (Table 3), different substituted benzyl alcohols were examined. In general, aromatic alcohols bearing electron-donating substituents such as –CH₃ and –OCH₃ gave higher yields (84% and 79%, respectively), whereas those containing electron-withdrawing groups such as –NO₂ and –CN provided slightly lower yields (70–73%). This trend can be explained by the electronic influence of substituents on the oxidation of alcohols to aldehydes and the subsequent condensation step: electron-donating groups enhance the electron density on the benzylic carbon, facilitating oxidation and accelerating aldehyde formation, while electron-withdrawing groups make the alcohol less reactive toward oxidation. All products showed melting points consistent with reported literature values34–41, confirming the successful synthesis and high purity of the compounds.

Overall, these results confirm that the g-C₃N₄/PANI/Cu²⁺ nanocatalyst not only promotes the oxidation–condensation tandem sequence efficiently but also exhibits excellent tolerance toward various functional groups. The mild reaction conditions, use of water as a green solvent, and high yields underline the potential of this method for sustainable synthesis of heterocyclic compounds.

To assess the efficiency and practicality of the developed method, the results obtained from the model reactions for the synthesis of polyhydroquinoline and dihydropyrimidine derivatives were compared with previously reported systems that employ aldehydes as starting materials (Tables 5 and 6). As summarized in Table 5, most of the reported protocols for polyhydroquinoline synthesis involve aldehydes and require either elevated temperatures, prolonged reaction times, or the use of organic solvents. For example, the use of VDDAP or heteropolyacid-based catalysts [(NH₄)₂.₅H₀.₅PW₁₂O₄₀] typically demands high temperatures or long reaction times to achieve yields of 70–80%. In contrast, the g-C₃N₄/PANI/Cu²⁺ nanocatalyst in this work enabled the same transformation starting directly from aromatic alcohols under mild and greener conditions, affording comparable or even better yields (78%) without the need for preformed aldehydes. A similar trend was observed for the synthesis of dihydropyrimidine derivatives (Table 6). Previously reported catalysts such as SBA/PS/PPA, PPF–SO₃H, or SMF/Na₂Zn(OH)₄ generally relied on aldehydes as substrates and required refluxing in ethanol or solvent-free conditions at 80 °C to achieve yields of 74–80%. The present g-C₃N₄/PANI/Cu²⁺ system achieved comparable yields (75%) within 4 h, while using aromatic alcohols as more stable and less toxic precursors. These results demonstrate that the proposed tandem oxidation–condensation approach effectively replaces aldehydes with readily available alcohols, maintaining high product yields while avoiding the use of unstable, volatile, and sometimes toxic aldehyde intermediates. Furthermore, the use of water as a green solvent and the mild reaction temperature emphasize the environmental and operational advantages of this method. Therefore, the developed g-C₃N₄/PANI/Cu²⁺ catalytic system provides a sustainable and efficient alternative to aldehyde-based multicomponent reactions for the synthesis of valuable heterocycles.

Mechanism

The plausible mechanism for the g-C₃N₄/PANI/Cu²⁺-catalyzed synthesis of polyhydroquinoline and dihydropyrimidine derivatives is depicted in Fig. 9, while detailed pathways are provided in the Supporting Information (Figure S5 and figure S6). The tandem process begins with Cu-mediated oxidation of benzyl alcohol to the corresponding aldehyde in the presence of TBHP, a transformation that has been widely reported in the literature59. In this step, TBHP is proposed to be activated by Cu²⁺ sites through a Cu²⁺/Cu⁺ redox cycle. The resulting aldehyde subsequently undergoes a multicomponent condensation with dimedone, ethyl acetoacetate, and ammonium acetate (or urea) to afford the desired heterocyclic products60. The enhanced catalytic performance is likely related to the synergistic interaction. Substituent effects observed in Tables 3 and 4 are consistent with a benzylic radical pathway, where electron-donating groups accelerate the oxidation step, while electron-withdrawing groups slightly decrease the reaction rate.

Proposed mechanism for the in-situ oxidation of benzyl alcohols and subsequent multicomponent condensation.

Recycling experiments

The recyclability and stability of the g-C₃N₄/PANI/Cu²⁺ nanocatalyst were systematically investigated through consecutive catalytic cycles for the synthesis of both polyhydroquinoline and dihydropyrimidine derivatives (Fig. 10). After each reaction, the catalyst was separated by simple filtration, thoroughly washed with water and ethanol to remove any adsorbed organic residues, and dried at 60 °C for 12 h before reuse. As shown in Fig. 10, the catalyst retained excellent catalytic activity even after five successive cycles, with only a moderate decrease in yield from ~ 76% to ~ 61% for polyhydroquinoline and from ~ 75% to ~ 59% for dihydropyrimidine derivatives. The gradual decline in efficiency can be attributed to partial surface fouling and possible minor loss of active sites due to mechanical handling during recovery.

Evaluation of the g-C3N4/PANI/Cu2+ nanocatalyst Reusability in the synthesis of polyhydroquinoline 2a and dihydropyrimidinone 2b.

To confirm the structural stability of the catalyst after multiple uses, FT-IR, XRD, FE-SEM, and EDS analyses were performed on both the fresh nanocomposite and the catalyst recovered after the fifth catalytic cycle (Figs. S60–S63). The FT-IR spectra (Fig. S60) showed no significant change in the characteristic bands of g-C₃N₄ and PANI, indicating that the chemical integrity of the composite framework was preserved. The XRD patterns (Fig. S63) revealed that the major diffraction peaks corresponding to g-C₃N₄ and PANI remained intact, although with slightly reduced intensity, suggesting minor structural relaxation or surface disorder after repeated use. The FE-SEM images (Fig. S62) further supported this observation, showing that the overall morphology of the composite was largely retained, though a slight aggregation of particles occurred after several runs. The EDS analysis (Fig. S61) confirmed that the elemental composition remained nearly unchanged, and the Cu signal was still clearly detectable, verifying that the metallic component was not significantly lost during the catalytic process. Quantitative ICP-OES measurements corroborated this finding, showing that the Cu(II) loading decreased only slightly from 1.5 wt% (fresh catalyst) to 1.3 wt% (after the fifth run).

These results collectively demonstrate that the g-C₃N₄/PANI/Cu²⁺ nanocomposite possesses remarkable structural robustness and reusability. The combination of strong metal–nitrogen coordination, conductive polymeric support, and π–π interactions within the g-C₃N₄/PANI framework effectively prevents Cu leaching and structural collapse during catalytic cycling. Hence, the developed catalyst is not only efficient and green but also sustainable for multiple reuses in practical applications.

Conclusion

In this study, an efficient and environmentally benign catalytic system based on g-C₃N₄/PANI/Cu²⁺ nanocomposite was successfully synthesized and characterized using various analytical techniques. The prepared nanocomposite exhibited excellent catalytic activity for the one-pot multicomponent synthesis of polyhydroquinoline and dihydropyrimidine derivatives, using aromatic alcohols as starting materials instead of the corresponding aldehydes. The developed methodology represents a green and sustainable route, as it eliminates the need for pre-synthesized aldehydes, minimizes toxic by-products, and employs water as a safe and eco-friendly solvent. The tandem oxidation–condensation process efficiently converts alcohols to the corresponding heterocycles under mild conditions, affording high yields within short reaction times. Moreover, the catalyst demonstrated remarkable stability and recyclability, retaining most of its catalytic performance even after five consecutive runs, without significant structural degradation or Cu leaching. The strong metal–support interaction and the synergistic effect between Cu²⁺, PANI, and g-C₃N₄ are key factors contributing to its durability and high efficiency. Overall, this work introduces a versatile and robust nanocatalyst for green organic synthesis, offering a practical and sustainable alternative to traditional aldehyde-based multicomponent reactions. The modular design of the g-C₃N₄/PANI framework also provides an adaptable platform for further modification with other transition metals, paving the way for the development of multifunctional catalytic systems capable of promoting diverse organic transformations and complex cascade reactions.
