Mendelian randomization analysis reveals causal associations between HLA gene expression, inflammatory biomarkers, and non-small cell lung cancer risk
Authors: Zexin Gu, Tianyuan Ma, Cuicui Li, Hanxu Tang, Jianing Liu, Weiwei Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNon-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) represents a major global health burden with complex genetic etiology. While observational studies have identified numerous potential risk factors, establishing causal relationships remains challenging due to confounding and reverse causation.MethodsWe conducted a comprehensive Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis to investigate causal relationships between circulating biomarkers, lung tissue gene expression levels, and NSCLC risk. The analysis employed three complementary MR methods: inverse variance weighted (IVW), MR Egger, and weighted median approaches. Genetic instruments were selected from 10 to 26 single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) significantly associated with exposure variables. We validated key findings through quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis in A549 and H1299 NSCLC cell lines. We examined multiple biomarkers including inflammatory mediators, immune-related proteins, and particularly focused on the HLA region within the major histocompatibility complex.ResultsAmong circulating biomarkers, thymic stromal lymphopoietin demonstrated a statistically significant positive association with increased NSCLC risk (OR: 1.240, 95% CI: 1.073–1.433, p = 0.003). Gene expression analysis identified 18 genes with significant causal associations (FDR < 0.05), revealing a complex pattern of protective and risk-associated effects. qRT-PCR validation in A549 and H1299 cell lines confirmed differential expression patterns, with protective genes (HLA-DQB2, GMPPA) showing lower expression and risk-associated genes (VDR, P4HTM) showing higher expression in cancer cells compared to normal bronchial epithelial cells. Notable protective genes included HLA-DQB2 (OR: 0.917), HLA-DRB9 (OR: 0.875), and GMPPA (OR: 0.697), while risk-associated genes included VDR (OR: 1.825), P4HTM (OR: 1.520), and multiple HLA variants. Regional association analysis of the HLA-DQB2 locus revealed extremely high significance levels (p-values > 10^-135) within the MHC region on chromosome 6.ConclusionsThis MR analysis provides robust genetic evidence for causal relationships between immune system regulation, inflammatory pathways, and NSCLC development, with experimental validation supporting the functional relevance of identified genetic associations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-026-04772-0.

Introduction

Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounts for approximately 85% of all lung cancer cases and remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide. Despite significant advances in targeted therapy and immunotherapy, the overall prognosis remains poor, with five-year survival rates below 20% for advanced stages [1–3]. Understanding the causal mechanisms underlying NSCLC development is crucial for improving prevention strategies, early detection methods, and therapeutic interventions.

Traditional epidemiological studies have identified numerous potential risk factors for lung cancer, including environmental exposures, lifestyle factors, and genetic predisposition. However, establishing causal relationships from observational data is challenging due to confounding variables, reverse causation, and unmeasured factors that may influence both exposures and outcomes [4–6]. These limitations have hindered the translation of epidemiological findings into effective preventive and therapeutic strategies.

Mendelian randomization (MR) has emerged as a powerful analytical approach that leverages genetic variants as instrumental variables to infer causal relationships between exposures and outcomes. By utilizing the random assortment of genetic variants at conception, MR analysis mimics the design of a randomized controlled trial, thereby minimizing confounding and eliminating reverse causation [7–9]. This approach is particularly valuable for investigating the causal roles of biomarkers and molecular pathways that are difficult to manipulate experimentally in human populations.

The immune system plays a fundamental role in cancer surveillance and tumor development. The major histocompatibility complex (MHC), located on chromosome 6p21, contains the highest density of disease-associated genes in the human genome. Within this region, human leukocyte antigen (HLA) genes encode cell surface proteins essential for antigen presentation and immune recognition [10–12]. Genetic variations in HLA genes have been associated with various cancers, including lung cancer, but the causal nature of these associations remains unclear.

Inflammatory mediators and immune-related biomarkers represent another category of potential causal factors in lung cancer development. Chronic inflammation creates a microenvironment conducive to tumorigenesis through mechanisms including DNA damage, promotion of angiogenesis, and suppression of immune surveillance [13–15]. However, the complex interplay between inflammatory pathways and cancer development requires careful investigation to distinguish causal relationships from mere associations.

In this study, we conducted a comprehensive MR analysis to investigate the causal relationships between circulating biomarkers, lung tissue gene expression levels, and NSCLC risk. Our analysis focused particularly on immune-related genes and inflammatory mediators, employing multiple complementary MR methods to ensure robust causal inference. The findings provide genetic evidence for the causal roles of specific molecular pathways in lung cancer development and offer insights into potential therapeutic targets for precision medicine approaches.

Methods

Study design and data sources

This study employed a comprehensive Mendelian randomization (MR) approach to investigate the causal relationships between various biomarkers, gene expression levels, and non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) risk [16]. The analysis utilized multiple complementary MR methodologies to ensure robust causal inference and minimize potential biases inherent in observational studies.

Mendelian randomization framework

The primary analytical framework consisted of three core MR methods: inverse variance weighted (IVW), MR Egger, and weighted median approaches. The IVW method served as the primary analytical approach, providing causal estimates by meta-analyzing individual genetic instrument effects. MR Egger regression was employed to detect and adjust for potential horizontal pleiotropy, while the weighted median method offered additional robustness by providing valid estimates even when up to 50% of genetic instruments were invalid.

Data sources and study populations

Summary-level data for NSCLC were obtained from the largest available genome-wide association study (GWAS) meta-analysis, including data from the Transdisciplinary Research in Cancer of the Lung (TRICL) consortium, the International Lung Cancer Consortium (ILCCO), and OncoArray studies. The NSCLC GWAS comprised 29,266 cases and 56,450 controls of European ancestry. Exposure data for circulating biomarkers were derived from large-scale proteomic studies with sample sizes ranging from 3301 to 21,758 participants. Gene expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) data were obtained from the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) project version 8, specifically utilizing lung tissue samples (n = 515) to ensure tissue-relevant predictions for pulmonary oncogenesis. All participating studies received ethical approval from their respective institutional review boards, and all participants provided written informed consent.

Genetic instrument selection and validation

The primary analytical framework consisted of three core MR methods: inverse variance weighted (IVW), MR Egger, and weighted median approaches. The IVW method served as the primary analytical approach when all genetic instruments were assumed valid, providing the most statistically efficient causal estimates by meta-analyzing individual SNP-outcome effects weighted by the inverse of their variances. Genetic instrumental variables were pruned for linkage disequilibrium at a threshold of r2 < 0.01 to ensure independence. Instrument strength was assessed using F-statistics and explained variance (R2), with all F-statistics > 10 indicating no weak instrument bias. This method assumes that all genetic variants affect the outcome exclusively through the exposure (no horizontal pleiotropy), making it most powerful under ideal conditions but potentially biased if this assumption is violated. MR Egger regression was employed to detect and adjust for potential horizontal pleiotropy, relaxing the no-pleiotropy assumption and providing valid estimates even in the presence of directional pleiotropy, though with reduced statistical power and requiring the InSIDE (Instrument Strength Independent of Direct Effect) assumption. The weighted median method offered additional robustness by providing valid estimates even when up to 50% of genetic instruments were invalid, serving as a middle ground between the efficiency of IVW and the robustness of MR Egger.

Our decision-making framework prioritized IVW estimates when MR Egger intercept tests indicated no significant pleiotropy (P > 0.05) and results were consistent across methods. When pleiotropy was detected, we prioritized MR Egger and weighted median results and discussed the implications of pleiotropic bias on causal inference.

Biomarker and gene expression analysis

The study examined multiple categories of exposures including circulating biomarkers (CUB domain-containing proteins, eotaxin levels, interleukin-24, latency-associated peptide transforming growth factor, protein S100-A12, SIR2-like protein 2, thymic stromal lymphopoietin, and urokinase-type plasminogen activator) and lung tissue gene expression levels. Gene expression analysis focused particularly on the HLA (Human Leukocyte Antigen) region within the major histocompatibility complex, given its critical role in immune system function and cancer development.

Circulating protein exposures were obtained from plasma proteomic GWAS and included immune- and inflammation-related biomarkers biologically relevant to lung tumor microenvironment. Lung-specific gene expression exposures were derived from lung tissue eQTL data, representing local transcriptional activity relevant to pulmonary oncogenesis. This dual exposure strategy allowed evaluation of both systemic inflammatory status and tissue-specific regulatory effects on NSCLC risk, enhancing biological interpretability of causal inference.

Statistical analysis and sensitivity testing

Statistical significance was assessed using odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals. Multiple testing correction was applied using the false discovery rate (FDR) method across the entire study with a threshold of FDR < 0.05. To control for type I error across different hypothesis tiers, stratified FDR correction was additionally performed within each exposure category (circulating biomarkers, eQTL-based expression, and lung tissue-level expression), and conservative Bonferroni correction was employed as a sensitivity analysis to account for the total number of independent tests. Comprehensive sensitivity analyses were conducted through scatter plots, forest plots, and funnel plots to evaluate potential pleiotropy, heterogeneity, and the validity of MR assumptions. Regional association analysis was performed to examine the genetic architecture surrounding significant loci, particularly focusing on the HLA-DQB2 gene region on chromosome 6. Steiger directionality testing was performed to confirm causal direction from exposures to NSCLC and exclude reverse causation.

Horizontal pleiotropy was assessed using the MR Egger intercept test. Additionally, the MR-PRESSO global test was conducted to detect and correct for potential outliers caused by pleiotropic variants; no outlying SNPs were identified in the present analysis. Steiger filtering was applied to verify the causal direction and exclude reverse causation, ensuring that genetic variants primarily influenced the exposure rather than the outcome.

To address the extensive linkage disequilibrium (LD) and complex haplotype structure within the human leukocyte antigen (HLA) region, we used a stringent LD clumping threshold (r2 < 0.001 within 10,000 kb) to select independent genetic instruments, specifically reducing confounding from long-range LD in the major histocompatibility complex (MHC) region. Furthermore, we applied Summary Mendelian Randomization (SMR) integrated with the HEIDI test to distinguish putative causal gene signals from LD-driven artifacts. Given the complex LD architecture of the HLA region, we interpreted associations within the MHC locus with caution, acknowledging that formal fine-mapping and colocalization analyses would be required in future studies to pinpoint the exact causal variants.

Heterogeneity across genetic instruments was evaluated using Cochran’s Q statistic and corresponding p-values derived from the inverse variance weighted (IVW) model. Significant heterogeneity (P < 0.05) indicated potential violation of the MR assumptions, and results were interpreted with caution in the presence of high heterogeneity.

Data visualization and interpretation

Results were presented through multiple visualization approaches including forest plots for overall effect estimates, volcano plots for genome-wide significance patterns, regional association plots for localized genetic effects, and comprehensive multi-panel figures combining scatter plots, forest plots, and funnel plots for detailed sensitivity analysis. This multi-faceted analytical approach ensured thorough evaluation of causal relationships while maintaining statistical rigor and biological interpretability.

Cell culture and experimental validation

To validate the functional relevance of genes identified through MR analysis, we performed quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) experiments in NSCLC cell lines and normal control cells. Two well-characterized NSCLC cell lines were utilized: A549 (adenocarcinoma, ATCC CCL-185) and H1299 (large cell carcinoma, ATCC CRL-5803). Normal human bronchial epithelial cells (BEAS-2B, ATCC CRL-9609) served as the normal control. A549 and H1299 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco) at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO₂. BEAS-2B cells were cultured in bronchial epithelial cell growth medium (BEGM, Lonza) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

RNA extraction and qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from cells using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), with A260/A280 ratios between 1.8 and 2.0 considered acceptable. First-strand cDNA was synthesized from 1 µg total RNA using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Takara, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, USA) on a QuantStudio 3 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). The reaction system (20 µL total volume) contained: 10 µL SYBR Green Master Mix, 0.4 µL forward primer (10 µM), 0.4 µL reverse primer (10 µM), 2 µL cDNA template, and 7.2 µL nuclease-free water. The cycling conditions were: 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 1 min. Melting curve analysis was performed to verify amplification specificity.

We selected six representative genes for validation based on their statistical significance and biological relevance: three protective genes (HLA-DQB2, HLA-DRB9, GMPPA) and three risk-associated genes (VDR, P4HTM, HLA-DQB1). Primer sequences were designed using Primer-BLAST (NCBI) and are listed in Supplementary Table S1. GAPDH was used as the internal reference gene. Each experiment was performed in triplicate with three independent biological replicates. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2-∆∆ct method, with BEAS-2B cells serving as the calibrator. Statistical analysis of qRT-PCR data was performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0. Differences in gene expression between NSCLC cell lines and normal control cells were assessed using one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). For quantitative real-time PCR validation, gene-specific primers were designed and synthesized. The primer sequences used were as follows: HLA-DQB2 forward primer 5’-CTGGAGCGACTTCACACAGAT-3’, reverse primer 5’-GGTAGCAGCGGTAGAGTTGG-3’; HLA-DRB9 forward primer 5’-TGTCATTTCTTCAACGGGACG-3’, reverse primer 5’-CCGGATCCTTCGTGTCTCTG-3’; GMPPA forward primer 5’-AGCTGCTCAACATCGTGAAGA-3’, reverse primer 5’-TGGCTTCTCCAGGTCATTCTC-3’; VDR forward primer 5’-CCTGAGGTTTCGGTCAATGTG-3’, reverse primer 5’-CATCATGCGGAGAACAGGTAGG-3’; P4HTM forward primer 5’-GGAGACGGAAGTTTTCCTGGT-3’, reverse primer 5’-ACACACTGGACTCGTTGAACT-3’; HLA-DQB1 forward primer 5’-GTGCGTCTTGTGAGCAGAAG-3’, reverse primer 5’-GCAATCCACAGTCACCACAG-3’; GAPDH (reference gene) forward primer 5’-GTCTCCTCTGACTTCAACAGCG-3’, reverse primer 5’-ACCACCCTGTTGCTGTAGCCAA-3’. All primers were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China) and validated for specificity through melting curve analysis.

Discussion

The predominant involvement of HLA genes in our MR analysis underscores the fundamental role of immune system regulation in NSCLC susceptibility. The HLA region, spanning approximately 4 megabases on chromosome 6p21.3, represents one of the most polymorphic and evolutionarily conserved regions of the human genome [17–19]. This genetic diversity reflects the evolutionary pressure to maintain effective immune responses against diverse pathogenic threats while avoiding autoimmunity.

Our findings reveal a complex pattern of causal associations within the HLA region, with some genes conferring protection (HLA-DQB2, HLA-DRB9, HLA-DQA2) while others increase cancer risk (HLA-DQA1, HLA-DQB1, HLA-DRB5). This apparent paradox can be understood through the functional specialization of different HLA molecules. Class II HLA genes, which dominated our significant associations, encode proteins responsible for presenting extracellular antigens to CD4 + T cells, thereby initiating adaptive immune responses. The differential effects observed may reflect varying efficiencies in tumor antigen presentation or distinct roles in immune tolerance mechanisms.

The protective effect of HLA-DQB2 (OR: 0.917) is particularly noteworthy given its extremely high statistical significance (p-values > 10^-135) in regional association analysis. HLA-DQB2 exhibits limited expression compared to other HLA-DQ genes and shows distinct peptide-binding preferences. Its protective role may relate to enhanced presentation of tumor-associated antigens or more effective activation of anti-tumor immune responses. The strong linkage disequilibrium observed in this region suggests that multiple causal variants may contribute to the overall protective effect, reflecting the complex evolutionary history and functional interdependence of HLA genes.

The identification of thymic stromal lymphopoietin (TSLP) as a significant risk factor (OR: 1.240) provides important insights into the role of inflammatory mediators in lung cancer development. TSLP is an epithelial cell-derived cytokine that plays crucial roles in allergic inflammation and immune regulation. Elevated TSLP levels have been associated with various inflammatory conditions, including asthma and atopic dermatitis, which share common pathophysiological pathways with certain aspects of tumorigenesis.

The causal relationship between TSLP and NSCLC risk may operate through several mechanisms. TSLP promotes the differentiation of naive T cells toward Th2 phenotypes, potentially creating an immunosuppressive microenvironment that favors tumor growth [20–22]. Additionally, TSLP can directly stimulate epithelial cell proliferation and survival, processes that may contribute to malignant transformation when dysregulated. The pulmonary epithelium, being the primary site of NSCLC origin, may be particularly susceptible to TSLP-mediated effects.

Interestingly, other inflammatory biomarkers examined in our study, including interleukin-24, eotaxin, and various immune-related proteins, did not demonstrate significant causal associations with NSCLC risk. This selectivity suggests that not all inflammatory pathways contribute equally to lung cancer development and highlights the importance of distinguishing between causal mediators and mere disease markers.

Beyond the HLA region, our analysis identified several non-immune genes with significant causal associations. The vitamin D receptor (VDR) gene showed the strongest risk association (OR: 1.825), which may seem counterintuitive given the generally protective effects attributed to vitamin D. However, this finding likely reflects the complex regulatory roles of VDR beyond vitamin D metabolism. VDR functions as a transcription factor that regulates numerous genes involved in cell proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis. Certain VDR variants may alter these regulatory functions in ways that promote tumorigenesis, particularly in the context of chronic inflammation or environmental exposures.

The strong linkage disequilibrium observed within the HLA region presents both opportunities and challenges for causal inference. While this genetic architecture has been shaped by balancing selection to maintain immune diversity, it complicates the identification of specific causal variants. Our regional association analysis suggests that multiple variants within the MHC region may contribute to NSCLC susceptibility, possibly through additive or epistatic effects.

Beyond the HLA region, the vitamin D receptor (VDR) gene showed the strongest risk association (OR: 1.825), which may seem counterintuitive given vitamin D’s generally protective effects. Our experimental validation confirmed that VDR is markedly overexpressed in NSCLC cell lines (3.27-fold in A549 and 3.89-fold in H1299 cells), supporting the genetic evidence for its risk-associated role. This finding likely reflects VDR’s complex regulatory roles beyond vitamin D metabolism. VDR functions as a ligand-dependent transcription factor regulating over 200 target genes. The counterintuitive risk association may result from several mechanisms:

First, certain VDR genetic variants may alter transcriptional activity independently of vitamin D binding, shifting VDR’s program toward pro-proliferative gene expression. Second, VDR overexpression in cancer cells may reflect compensatory upregulation in response to local vitamin D depletion within the tumor microenvironment, where elevated VDR without sufficient ligand could exert dominant-negative effects. Third, context-dependent effects of VDR in different cellular compartments may explain the paradox—while vitamin D-VDR signaling in immune cells promotes anti-tumor immunity, VDR activation in tumor cells may have opposing effects depending on cellular context and oncogenic mutations present. These findings suggest that simplistic vitamin D supplementation strategies may not uniformly prevent lung cancer.

Similarly, our validation of P4HTM overexpression in NSCLC cells (2.74-fold in A549 and 3.18-fold in H1299) provides experimental support for its causal risk association (OR: 1.520). P4HTM is involved in collagen modification and extracellular matrix remodeling, and its upregulation may facilitate tumor progression through enhanced collagen deposition. The correlation between MR-derived odds ratios and expression changes observed in our validation experiments (Pearson r = 0.93 for risk genes, r = 0.89 for protective genes) provides strong evidence for biological validity of our genetic findings.

HLA variants may affect antigen-processing efficiency and CD4⁺ T-cell activation in NSCLC [23, 24], while tumor metabolic remodeling can suppress immune surveillance and antigen presentation capacity [25–27]. VDR and P4HTM may further influence immune–metabolic regulation and stromal-mediated immunosuppression within the tumor microenvironment [28–30].

The validation of GMPPA as a protective factor (OR: 0.697), with corresponding 68–73% reduced expression in NSCLC cells, is also noteworthy. GMPPA participates in N-glycosylation pathways essential for proper protein folding and cell surface receptor function. Reduced GMPPA expression may impair glycosylation of immune checkpoint molecules or antigen presentation machinery, potentially compromising immune surveillance mechanisms.

The causal relationships identified have important implications for therapeutic development and precision medicine. Patients carrying protective HLA alleles (particularly HLA-DQB2, HLA-DRB9) may show enhanced responses to checkpoint inhibitors, as these therapies depend on effective antigen presentation. Conversely, patients with risk-associated HLA profiles may benefit from combination strategies augmenting antigen presentation, such as HLA-matched tumor antigen vaccines.

For TSLP, our findings provide genetic support for therapeutic development of TSLP-blocking antibodies in lung cancer prevention, particularly in high-risk populations with elevated TSLP levels. For VDR, rather than supporting general vitamin D supplementation, our findings suggest that selective VDR modulators targeting specific cellular compartments might offer therapeutic advantages. Our findings could also contribute to polygenic risk scores incorporating HLA variants, VDR genotype, and other genetic factors to improve risk stratification for personalized screening strategies.

Increasing evidence suggests that genetic susceptibility to lung cancer interacts with environmental exposures, particularly smoking, with significant additive interactions observed between polygenic risk scores and smoking status in prospective cohort analyses [31]. Similarly, long-term exposure to air pollutants such as PM₂.₅ has been shown to increase lung cancer risk, especially among individuals with high genetic susceptibility, indicating joint or amplified effects of environmental and inherited risk factors [32–34]. Notably, immune-related loci within the HLA region may mediate these gene–environment interactions, as smoking-induced immune alterations overlap with pathways enriched for lung cancer genetic risk variants, supporting an immune-mediated interaction framework [35–37].

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, single-variant Mendelian randomization analyses may not fully capture gene–gene or gene–environment interactions, and our study did not formally evaluate gene–environment or gene–lifestyle effects that may contribute to NSCLC susceptibility. Second, the exclusive use of European-ancestry cohorts limits generalizability, particularly given population-specific variation in highly polymorphic HLA loci. Third, MR estimates reflect lifetime genetic exposure and may not directly translate to short-term pharmacological interventions. Fourth, tissue-specific expression patterns and intra-tissue heterogeneity were not comprehensively modeled. Fifth, experimental validation was limited to two NSCLC cell lines and may not fully recapitulate the tumor microenvironment. Finally, our study focused on disease risk rather than progression or survival, and future research should incorporate functional validation, multi-ethnic replication, prospective cohorts, and polygenic risk modeling.

Conclusions

Using Mendelian randomization, this study identified potential causal associations between several genetically predicted proteins including HLA variants, TSLP, VDR, and GMPPA and NSCLC susceptibility, implicating immune and inflammatory pathways. These findings require multi-ethnic replication and functional validation to confirm their biological and translational significance.
