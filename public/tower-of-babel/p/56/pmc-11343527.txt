Management of Ulcers and Lymphorrhea in Lower Limbs after Charles Procedure via Supermicrosurgery
Authors: Zhegang Zhou, Longbiao Yu, Fanbin Meng, Jingjing Wen, Aikang Li, Yingfeng Xiao, Shengxiang Wan, Hui Zeng, Fei Yu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Summary:An 80-year-old female patient was admitted to the hospital due to recurrent lymphedema, ulcers, and lymphorrhea in the right lower limb for 7 years. The physical examination revealed changes after the Charles procedure below the right knee joint plane, with a 7 × 7 cm2 lymphorrhea area in the lower one-third plane of the anterior tibia area. Continuous lymphatic exudation and scattered ulcers could be seen. The dorsal artery of the foot could not be reached due to the thickening and fibrosis of the dorsal skin of the foot. The peripheral blood circulation was favorable. The glycated hemoglobin test revealed that blood sugar was not high and could be controlled. The color Doppler ultrasound of lower limb blood vessels revealed no obvious stenosis of arteries, with normal venous return. In addition, no varicose veins or deep venous thrombosis were observed. Based on these findings, the patient was diagnosed with primary lymphedema combined with lymphorrhea after the Charles procedure, complicated with diabetes. After admission, the patient underwent lymphaticovenous anastomosis on the right lower limb under general anesthesia. During the follow-up of 2 months after surgery, it was found that the lymphorrhea was alleviated, the ulcer basically healed, and the swelling on the right lower limb decreased. At the follow-up of 6 months after surgery, the lymphorrhea was eliminated and the ulcer was healed. There were 1-cm reduction in leg and back circumference and 10% volume reduction. The lymphedema quality-of-life score of this patient was 57 points after surgery.

Lymphedema is local tissue edema caused by the obstruction of lymphatic return and abnormal increase of fluid in tissue gaps. This disease can induce lipid deposition, fat sclerosis, and connective tissue proliferation, as well as skin thickening and roughness in severe cases. Patients with severe lymphedema may have skin as tough as rubber, which is called elephantiasis.1 Lymphedema can be divided into primary lymphedema caused by heredity, sporadic, or delayed factors and secondary lymphedema caused by tumor, trauma, infection, or other factors. This disease can exert a serious impact on the health and quality of life of patients. Lymphaticovenous anastomosis (LVA) is an effective procedure for the treatment of primary underdeveloped lymphedema, primary proliferative lymphedema of the lower limbs, and secondary lymphedema of the limbs. It is one of the main surgical procedures for treating lymphedema.2 Chronic lymphedema combined with ulcers should also be managed with more attention. In clinical practice, conservative treatment combined with progressive continuous compression treatment is commonly used for the treatment of limb ulcers. For complex cases, surgical treatment is required.

CASE REPORT

An 80-year-old female patient was admitted to the hospital due to recurrent lymphedema, ulcers, and lymphorrhea in the right lower limb for 7 years. The patient had experienced lymphedema in the right lower limb for 40 years and received the Charles procedure 30 years ago to treat the lymphedema. She had achieved a favorable recovery after the Charles procedure. However, the patient developed recurrent lymphedema 7 years ago, with persistent and increasing ulcers and lymphorrhea in the lower limb. The effect of conservative comprehensive swelling reduction and wound care was not satisfactory, and the affected area continued to expand. Besides, the patient had a history of diabetes for 15 years. The physical examination revealed changes after the Charles procedure below the right knee joint plane, with a 7 × 7 cm2 lymphorrhea area in the lower one-third plane of the anterior tibia area. Continuous lymphatic exudation and scattered ulcers could be seen. The dorsal artery of the foot could not be reached due to the thickening and fibrosis of the dorsal skin of the foot. The peripheral blood circulation was favorable (Fig. 1). The glycated hemoglobin test revealed good blood sugar control. The color Doppler ultrasound of lower limb blood vessels revealed no obvious stenosis of arteries, with normal venous return. In addition, no varicose veins or deep venous thrombosis were observed. Based on these findings, the patient was diagnosed with primary lymphedema combined with lymphorrhea after the Charles procedure, complicated with diabetes.

Patient condition at admission. Preoperative ulcer and lymphorrhea in the right calf, resulting in limited walking.

In terms of the design of the surgical incision, indocyanine green lymphangiography was performed before surgery, but effective functional lymphangiography was not obtained. Therefore, the incisions on the dorsum of the foot, medial side of the lower limb, lateral lower side of the knee joint, and medial thigh were made based on the common anatomical distribution of lymphatic vessels, and lymphatic vessels in scar tissues were also detected. The diameter of lymphatic vessels in this patient was about 0.2–0.4 mm, and the end-to-end anastomosis was performed on lymphatic vessels and veins near the proximal end of adjacent veins during surgery. After admission, the patient underwent LVA on the right lower limb under general anesthesia. During surgery, three incisions and four lymphatic vessels were anastomosed. The incisions were located on the foot, the upper and inner sides of the calf, the outer and lower edges of the patella, and the inner side of the calf (these areas had not undergone Charles procedure), all of which were treated through end-to-end anastomosis. The diameter of the anastomosed lymphatic vessels was approximately 0.2–0.4 mm, and that of the veins was 0.4–0.6 mm. The anastomosis was performed using 11-0 microsutures.

The patient received treatment with an elastic pressure bandage after surgery, which was replaced by a low tension pressure bandage one week after surgery, accompanied by manual drainage. Only antibiotics were administered to the patient before surgery, and no antibiotics were administered after surgery.

During the follow-up 2 months after surgery, it was found that the lymphorrhea was alleviated, the ulcer basically healed, and the swelling on the right lower limb decreased by about 10% (Fig. 2). During the follow-up 6 months after surgery, the lymphorrhea was eliminated and the ulcer was healed (Fig. 3). There were 1cm reductions in leg and back circumference, and 10% in volume reduction (circumferential diameter measurement method). The lymphedema quality-of-life score3 of this patient was 60 points before surgery and 57 points after surgery.

DISCUSSION

In this report, the patient with lymphedema was rated as International Society of Lymphology (ISL) grade 3,4 which posed challenges to the treatment. Currently, lymphaticovenous anastomosis (LVA) and autologous vascularized lymph node transfer (VLNT) are considered effective procedures for the treatment of lymphedema. Liposuction would be performed first in patients complicated with adipose hyperplasia. Subsequently, LVA or VLNT can be performed to reconstruct lymphatic circulation. The patient suffered from severe elephantine limbs caused by lymphedema for 40 years, and she had undergone the Charles procedure for effective volume reduction.5 The affected skin and subcutaneous tissue were removed through skin-fiber-fat resection.

Although chronic lymphedema and ulcers are less prevalent compared with venous ulcers in the limbs, they are also serious complications.6 Chronic lymphedema and lower limb ulcers7 can be divided into four stages. In the first stage, there are eczematous ulcerative lesions in the lymph node, accounting for approximately 2% of patients; In the second stage, there are confirmed ulcerative lesions, with approximately 4% of patients; In the third stage, about 3% of patients with elephantiasis develop deep sinus ulcers in the limbs; in the fourth stage, approximately 4% of patients have ulcers that appear after resection.8 In the second and third stage, patients could be successfully treated with a combination of reconstructive and reductive procedure (VLNT + Charles). This combination could prevent the recurrencies.9,10Correspondingly, there are different treatment methods for lesions at the four stages. In the fourth stage, ulcers may occur after resection surgery, such as the Charles procedure, in patients with ISL stage 3 lymphedema. Among the four stages, patients with lymphedema in the fourth stage after resection surgery have the highest treatment difficulty index due to the involvement of lymph nodes and relevant complications. For patients with lymphedema in the fourth stage, ulcer debridement and layered skin transplantation are necessary. However, conventional ulcer healing methods cannot achieve favorable outcomes. In some cases, the ulcer can heal up after the implementation of conservative treatment combined with subsequent thickness-based skin transplantation for a certain period. However, the relapse of this disease may occur in a later stage. This can be mainly attributed to the continuous outflow of lymph, which leads to skin maceration and ulcer formation.

In summary, this patient had recurrent lymphedema, scattered ulcers, and lymphorrhea in the lower limb after the Charles procedure. The lymphatic vessel vein anastomosis achieved favorable outcomes in ulcer healing and lymphorrhea elimination after surgery. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first clinical report on the treatment of recurrent lymphedema with LVA. These findings are expected to provide reference for the treatment of ulcers and lymphorrhea in lower limbs after Charles procedure and studies with larger cohorts are needed in the future.
