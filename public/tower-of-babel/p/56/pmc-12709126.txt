Unusual cause of myocardial infarction in a 3-year-old: role of cardiovascular magnetic resonance in diagnosis—a case report

Introduction

Myocardial infarction (MI) is of an exceptionally rare occurrence in young children, with most cases linked to congenital or inflammatory causes. We describe a 3-year-old girl with an unusual presentation of embolic MI secondary to dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) and highlight the pivotal role of cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) imaging in achieving an accurate diagnosis. The aim is to emphasize the importance of advanced imaging in paediatric cardiology, especially in rare presentations of myocardial injury.

Case presentation

A 3-year-old girl, born at 39 weeks via uncomplicated vaginal delivery, presented with symptoms of lethargy and breathlessness. Approximately 10 days prior to this acute presentation, she had an episode of periorbital swelling, which was initially managed with antibiotics for presumed cellulitis. Her medical history is notable for episodes of pica. Family history reveals that two of her siblings also have pica and have previously received iron transfusions, while the other siblings are healthy. Additionally, her father experienced a stroke but has not undergone any investigations to determine its cause. She had been active and showed no previous signs of fatigue, chest pain, or palpitations.

On clinical examination, she exhibited persistent tachycardia and a systolic murmur. Chest radiography revealed cardiomegaly, and transthoracic echocardiography confirmed a diagnosis of DCM with severely reduced left ventricular (LV) systolic function, showing an ejection fraction of 16% as assessed by Simpson’s biplane method. There was no LV outflow tract obstruction. Additional findings included LV thrombi (Figure 1), a small-to-moderate pericardial effusion, and normal origin and proximal course of the right and left coronary arteries, including the typical bifurcation of the left coronary artery. No hyperechoic papillary muscles were observed.

Echocardiography images: (A) short-axis view and (B and C) apical four-chamber view showing thrombi in the left ventricle (arrows).

Laboratory investigations revealed elevated and progressively rising troponin levels (Figure 2), consistent with ongoing myocardial injury. N-terminal pro B-type natriuretic peptide level at the time of acute presentation was 17 183 ng/L (nanograms per litre), normal range < 400 ng/L. Haematological analysis showed microcytic anaemia, with a haemoglobin level of 77 g/L (grams per litre), normal range 110–140 g/L, and a mean corpuscular volume (MCV) of 64 fL (femtolitres), normal range 73–86 fL. The patient’s lipid profile and metabolic screen were within normal limits. Furthermore, workup for DCM, including assessment of selenium and carnitine levels, yielded normal results.

Chart showing elevated and progressively rising troponin levels, indicative of ongoing myocardial injury (ng/L: nanograms per litre).

Myocarditis was initially considered as a diagnosis due to the elevated troponin levels. However, 72 h after admission, evolving electrocardiographic (ECG) changes, including ST-segment elevation in the inferolateral leads (Figure 3), prompted further investigation with CMR. Cardiovascular magnetic resonance imaging was performed under general anaesthesia; findings showed no myocardial oedema or inflammation, ruling out active myocarditis (Figure 4). Instead, it revealed a transmural MI in the left ventricular apical segments (Figure 5), along with subtle mid-wall fibrosis in the basal and mid-septal areas. Additionally, severely dilated left and right ventricular volumes (Table 1), global hypokinesia, akinetic apical segments, and significantly reduced ejection fraction (EF) of both ventricles [LV EF 15%, right ventricular (RV) EF 20%] were observed (video). Moderate functional mitral regurgitation was also noted (33%), calculated as the difference between the LV stroke volume and the aortic forward flow. These findings were consistent with an embolic MI affecting the distal left anterior descending (LAD) artery territory, most likely originating from the LV thrombi, in the context of severe DCM. She had genetic testing, which showed a variant of uncertain significance on PKP2 gene.

During her 22-day stay in the intensive and high-dependency care units, the patient received intravenous inotropic support, diuretics, fluid restriction, and iron infusion to manage severe iron-deficiency anaemia. Anticoagulation therapy for LV thrombi was initiated using a heparin infusion, with dosing adjusted to maintain an anti-Xa level of 0.3–0.5 IU/mL (international units per millilitre). Follow-up imaging confirmed resolution of the thrombi. The patient was subsequently transitioned to rivaroxaban, with plans to complete a 3-month course followed by dose reduction to a prophylactic level.

As her clinical status improved, she was transferred to the general ward and later discharged with a medication regimen that included diuretics, enoximone (a selective phosphodiesterase inhibitor), an angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor, an anticoagulant, and oral supplements. She had a follow-up appointment in the cardiomyopathy clinic 1 month after her initial discharge. She is thriving and remains free of cardiac-related symptoms. Investigations revealed a persistently and severely dilated left ventricle (LV) with severely impaired function, which remained stable compared to her discharge echocardiogram. Her medication regimen remained unchanged. Screening referrals were made for her first-degree relatives, and she was scheduled for another follow-up in 1 month.

Discussion

Myocardial infarction in a young child is a diagnostically complex condition that presents significant challenges, and identifying the underlying cause is particularly difficult in the absence of coronary abnormalities.2 Paediatric MI often lacks traditional coronary risk factors and is frequently associated with non-specific symptoms, making diagnosis through conventional methods more challenging.

In this case, initial investigations suggested myocarditis, a common condition that can mimic MI. However, CMR imaging provided essential clarification by revealing a transmural MI pattern, thereby distinguishing ischaemic injury from myocarditis. The use of late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) imaging was pivotal in accurately characterizing myocardial damage and identifying its aetiology.3–5 While myocarditis typically exhibits epicardial or mid-wall enhancement, sparing the subendocardium, MI is characterized by subendocardial or transmural enhancement.5,6 These distinctive imaging findings enabled a precise diagnosis and guided targeted management, thereby avoiding unnecessary invasive procedures such as coronary angiography. The decision not to proceed to coronary angiography was made following a multidisciplinary team discussion. Considering the patient’s young age and the presence of an established infarct with severely impaired left ventricular function, it was concluded that the risks associated with invasive angiography outweighed its potential benefits.

Myocarditis often resembles MI in both its clinical presentation and initial diagnostic findings, with findings such as ST-segment elevation or arrhythmias on ECG, elevated troponin levels, and normal echocardiographic results in less severe cases.7 These non-specific features underscore the importance of CMR in resolving diagnostic uncertainty. Beyond detecting myocardial injury, CMR offers detailed insights into the distribution, extent, and nature of the damage, which are critical for determining the underlying cause. In this case, CMR confirmed the diagnosis of embolic MI secondary to severe DCM, shifting the clinical focus away from active myocarditis and preventing potentially invasive or inappropriate treatments.

Conclusion

This case underscores the diagnostic complexity of myocardial injury in children and the indispensable role of CMR in distinguishing between aetiologies such as myocarditis and MI. In this patient, CMR accurately identified an embolic MI and ruled out active inflammation, guiding targeted management with anticoagulation and heart failure therapies.
