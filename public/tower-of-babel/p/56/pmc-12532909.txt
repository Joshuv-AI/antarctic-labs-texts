Factors related to postpartum length of stay in women with pre-eclampsia: a systematic review
Authors: Aline Santana Goes, Alex Santana Oliveira, Rafaella Oliveira Santos Silva, Francielly Lima da Fonseca, Cinara Rejane Viana Oliveira, Divaldo Pereira de Lyra Junior, Alfredo Dias de Oliveira-Filho
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo conduct a systematic review in order to identify risk factors for the length of hospital stay in postpartum women with pregnancy-induced hypertensive disorders.MethodsA systematic search was performed in the databases CINAHL, Cochrane Library, Embase, Latin American and Caribbean Health Sciences Literature (LILACS) (via VirtualHealth Library—VHL), PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science for articles published until November 2024. To identify the articles, “Medical Subject Headings” (MeSH) descriptors and keywords for “Preeclampsia”, “Length of stay” and “Postpartum” were used. The descriptors were adapted for each database and combined using Boolean operators (OR and AND). Cohort and case–control studies were included if: presented a population of patients with hypertensive disorders during pregnancy, addressed length of stay as an outcome and were published in English, Portuguese or Spanish.ResultsThe initial search retrieved approximately 2,580 articles, eight of which met the inclusion criteria. The main risk factors for increased length of hospital stay were ascites (p < 0.001), cesarean delivery (p < 0.0001), drug-related problems (p = 0.0001), diastolic blood pressure above 100 mmHg (p = 0.006), intrauterine growth restriction (p = 0.0001), preeclampsia severity (p < 0.001), Congestive Heart Failure (p < 0.001) and Ventricular Tachycardia (p = 0.001). The included studies had a cohort design, with the general methodological quality considered good.ConclusionsThis review identified multiple factors influencing postpartum length of stay in women with preeclampsia, highlighting cesarean delivery and drug-related problems as key contributors. The findings underscore the importance of tailored interventions and local assessments to optimize hospital management and improve maternal outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-025-07312-w.Keywords: Preeclampsia, Length of stay, Cesarean delivery, Drug-related problems, Postpartum women

Introduction

Hypertension during pregnancy is the leading cause of maternal–fetal morbidity and mortality [1] and is categorized into four types: 1) preeclampsia/eclampsia; 2) gestational hypertension; 3) chronic hypertension; and 4) preeclampsia/eclampsia variants combined with hypertension [2]. Among these, preeclampsia is particularly noteworthy. It is a hypertensive disease that typically appears in pregnant women around the twentieth week of pregnancy and affects at least 5% of pregnancies worldwide [3].

Preeclampsia, considered the third leading cause of maternal mortality globally and the primary cause in Brazil [4], is characterized according to the National High Blood Pressure Education Program (NHBPEP) as having a blood pressure ≥ 140/90 mmHg [5, 6], along with symptoms such as headache, visual blurriness, abdominal pain, and abnormal laboratory test results, including thrombocytopenia, elevated liver enzymes, renal impairment, pulmonary edema, and visual and cerebral disturbances. Proteinuria may or may not be present [7].

Therefore, the definitive treatment for preeclampsia is believed to be delivery, regardless of the chosen delivery method [8]. However, there are controversies because in some cases, the hypertensive condition persists in these patients. The increased use of antihypertensive and pain relief drugs may increase the risk of a drug-related problem (DRP) for patients, consequently leading to prolonged hospital stays for these patients [9–11].

However, a significant part of the available evidence is derived from limited observational studies, which are prone to biases. Moreover, factors identified as significant predictors of length of stay in single studies or specific hospitals may reflect local medical practices rather than generalizable trends, or they might simply represent anecdotal observations [12].

Consequently, analyzing results from multiple studies on factors influencing length of stay could provide more robust evidence to guide future research and enhance clinical practice in managing these patients effectively. Therefore, this review aims to identify the risk factors associated with postpartum length of stay in women with preeclampsia.

Methods

This systematic review was carried out in November 2024 following the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines [13]. The study protocol was registered in PROSPERO under registration number CRD 42022300635.

Data source and search strategy

To address the research question “What are the factors influencing in the postpartum length of stay in women with pre-eclampsia?” We conducted a comprehensive literature search across multiple databases, including the CINAHL, Cochrane Library, Embase, Latin American (via Virtual Health Library—VHL), PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science databases. We utilized Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and text words for “Pre-Eclampsia,” “Length of Stay,” and “Postpartum” to identify relevant articles. The search strategy was adapted for each database and combined using Boolean operators (OR and AND). No filters were used to restrict time in the search strategy. The complete search strategy for all the databases can be found in SUPLEMENTARY 1.

Study selection

We included cohort and case‒control studies that (a) involved patients with hypertensive syndromes during pregnancy; (b) reported length of stay as an outcome; and (c) were published in English, Portuguese, or Spanish.

The exclusion criteria for this systematic review were (a) articles indexed in multiple databases; (b) articles lacking abstracts or full texts; and (c) conference proceedings, letters to the editor, comments, qualitative studies, mixed-method studies, literature reviews, integrative reviews, scope reviews, systematic reviews with or without meta-analyses, overviews of systematic reviews with or without meta-analyses, book chapters, dissertations, and theses.

Two independent reviewers (A.S.G. and A.S.O.) manually screened the search results and evaluated the titles, abstracts, and full texts. The references of the selected articles were reviewed to identify additional relevant studies. Discrepancies between reviewers were resolved by a third reviewer (R.O.S.S.).

The Ryyan QCRI Program was used to assess titles and abstracts, ensuring a higher quality review process. This free web application, developed by QCRI (Qatar Computing Research Institute), aids systematic review authors in organizing their work, collaborating efficiently, managing time, and quickly including relevant articles.

Data extraction

The extracted information included the following: authors, year of publication, country of origin, study design, duration, location, participant characteristics, and risk factors associated with postpartum hospitalization duration in women with preeclampsia.

It is important to emphasize that the length of Postpartum Length of Stay refers to the period between birth and discharge of the mother and, in some cases, the newborn. It is an essential metric in assessing the quality of maternal and neonatal care and is influenced by several factors, including the type of delivery (vaginal or cesarean section), the presence of maternal and neonatal complications, and pre-existing clinical conditions [19].

Quality assessment

The Newcastle‒Ottawa Scale (NOS) [14] was used to assess the quality of the studies, focusing on the selection of cohorts, comparability of study groups, and adequacy of outcome assessment. This scale assigns up to nine stars to studies of the highest quality. Two independent reviewers (A.S.G. and A.D.O.F.) conducted the validity assessment. Any discrepancies were resolved through consensus between the two reviewers.

Study selection

The initial search across selected databases yielded 2,580 studies. Among these, eight studies [10, 15–18, 20–22] met the inclusion criteria. Figure 1 provides an overview of the selection process, detailing the number of articles excluded at each stage of this systematic review.

Characteristics of the studies

The selected studies spanned three continents: Asia (n = 3) [16, 18, 20], Africa (n = 1) [15], and America (n = 4) [10, 17, 21, 22]. These studies employed cohort [10, 15–18, 20–22] designs. The sample sizes ranged from 98 to 256,010 patients. All studies were conducted in local reference maternity hospitals, three of which were teaching maternity hospitals. Four studies focused on patients with severe preeclampsia [15, 17, 18, 20], while one study addressed eclampsia [16].

The patients in the selected studies had a mean age ranging from 23.4 to 35.5 years [10, 15–18, 20–22] and a minimum gestational age of 33.18 weeks, with the majority reaching full-term pregnancy at 37 weeks or more [10, 15–18, 20]. Two studies did not specify the gestation time [21, 22]. Further details of the study characteristics are detailed in Table 1.

This review identified multiple risk factors associated with prolonged postpartum hospitalization in women with preeclampsia, encompassing clinical, biochemical, and sociodemographic aspects. The findings are presented based on the type of risk factor, integrating evidence from studies with varying methodological designs and levels of confidence. Below, the key findings are organized by risk factor, providing a structured approach to the data interpretation. The factors identified in this review are represented in the following framework in Fig. 2.

Framework the risk factors for length of stay in women with preeclampsia

Ascites

One significant risk factor for prolonged postpartum hospitalization was the presence of ascites, as reported in a cohort study by Mbonyizina et al. (2019), which included 112 patients diagnosed with severe preeclampsia. Ascites was identified in 60 patients (cases). Women with both antepartum and postpartum ascites experienced longer hospital stays (7.7 ± 0.52 vs. 5.4 ± 0.39 days, p < 0.001). Other factors, such as severe hypertension and altered biochemical parameters, were also identified, potentially contributing to the increased length of stay.

Cesarean delivery

Three studies 10,17,20 identified cesarean delivery as a potential risk factor for prolonged postpartum hospitalization. In the study by Amorin et al. (2014), the cesarean delivery rate was 68.2%, with an average hospitalization of 8 days for cesarean births compared to 5 days for vaginal births. Prolonged hospitalization was attributed to complications such as postpartum hemorrhage and increased blood pressure. Goes et al. (2021) also noted cesarean delivery as a factor increasing hospitalization, but this association was not statistically significant after adjusting for confounding factors. Li et al. (2022) reported an average hospitalization of 5.8 days for cesarean births, compared to 3.4 days for vaginal births. Prolonged hospitalization may result from complications and patient-related factors, such as comorbidities and the severity of preeclampsia. While these studies did not directly examine local practices, hospitals with limited resources may influence hospitalization duration.

Drug-Related Problems (DRPs)

Another risk factor identified in the study by Goes et al. (2021) was the presence of drug-related problems (DRPs), which significantly increased the length of stay from 4.4 (SD = 3.3) to 5.4 (SD = 3.6) days (p = 0.0001). DRPs were defined according to the Pharmaceutical Care Network Europe (PCNE) classification system as any event or circumstance involving pharmacotherapy that interferes or may interfere with desired health outcomes. In this study, DRPs were categorized into three main domains: indication problems (e.g., lack of prescription despite a clear indication), effectiveness problems (e.g., the treatment does not control adequately blood pressure), and safety problems (e.g., adverse drug reactions or inappropriate dosages). Patients exhibiting DRPs related to effectiveness showed a significantly increased risk of prolonged hospitalization, with an odds ratio of 3.73 (95% CI: 1.87–7.46). Similarly, those with DRPs related to blood pressure control—a subset of safety problems—had an odds ratio of 2.73 (95% CI: 1.43–5.19). These findings highlight the critical need for effective pharmaceutical care to minimize DRPs and their impact on postpartum recovery.

Altered biochemical parameters

In the study by Moroy et al. (2007) [16], risk factors related to changes in biochemical parameters were examined. The 98 patients were divided into two groups: short-term hospital stay (n = 33, 1–3 days) and long-term hospital stay (n = 65, four days or more). Among the patients analyzed, 3.03% of the short-term group (hospitalization ≤ 3 days) and 15.38% of the long-term group (hospitalization > 3 days) had a history of treatment with antihypertensive drugs before delivery (p = 0.049). Specific laboratory abnormalities, including elevated fibrinogen (p = 0.048), urea (p = 0.05), alanine transaminase (ALT) (p = 0.035), and aspartate transaminase (AST) (p = 0.040), were significantly correlated with hospital stays exceeding 3 days. Furthermore, the long-term hospitalization group exhibited diastolic blood pressure levels above 10 mmHg (p = 0.006). These findings suggest that these factors may not only indicate a prolonged length of stay but also reflect the underlying severity of the disease, as biochemical and hematological alterations are characteristic of more severe cases of preeclampsia.

Severity of preeclampsia

In the study by Xue et al. (2023)18, the severity of preeclampsia was also considered a factor affecting the length of hospital stay for pregnant women. The researchers found that women with severe or early-onset preeclampsia tended to have a longer hospital stay (5.7 days) and a greater risk of developing recurrent postpartum hypertension, severe preeclampsia (odds ratio 4,111, 95% CI: 1,236 a 13,271, p = 0,016), and early-onset preeclampsia (odds ratio 3,539, 95% CI: 1,049 an 11,472, p = 0,034) than women with mild or late-onset preeclampsia (4.5 days).

In study by Li and colleagues (2022) the severity of preeclampsia played a role in the length of hospital stay. Women with severe or early-onset preeclampsia had prolonged hospital stays of 5.4 to 5.6 days, which were significantly longer than those with mild (4.1 days) or late-onset preeclampsia (4.4 days) (p = 0.0001 or p < 0.0001, respectively).

Intrauterine Growth Restriction (IUGR)

In a study by Li and colleagues (2022) [20], intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR) emerged as a significant factor contributing to prolonged hospital stays for pregnant women with preeclampsia. Women with preeclampsia complicated by IUGR had a significantly longer duration of hospitalization (5.3 days) than those without IUGR (4.3 days) (95% CI: p = 0.0001, -0.98 [-1.48, -0.49]). Increased severity of placental dysfunction in IUGR cases requires closer maternal monitoring and management. Additionally, neonates with IUGR often require specialized care, such as admission to a Neonatal Intensive Care Unit, contributing to prolonged maternal hospitalization. These factors underscore the greater clinical complexity of preeclampsia with IUGR and the need for extended observation [20].

The type of delivery also influenced hospital stay duration, with 34 women with preeclampsia having a vaginal birth (average days: 3.4) and 174 women with preeclampsia undergoing cesarean section (average days: 5.8). Furthermore, the severity of preeclampsia played a role in the length of hospital stay. Women with severe or early-onset preeclampsia had prolonged hospital stays of 5.4 to 5.6 days, which were significantly longer than those with mild (4.1 days) or late-onset preeclampsia (4.4 days) (p = 0.0001 or p < 0.0001, respectively).

Congestive Heart Failure (CHF)

The study by Elkattawy et al. (2024) investigated the impact of CHF on women with preeclampsia and found CHF to be a significant factor associated with prolonged hospital stays. Patients with CHF had a longer mean length of stay (6 days vs. 4 days, p < 0.001) and were at increased risk for severe complications such as cardiac arrest (a OR 4.635, p = 0.004), ventricular tachycardia (a OR 17.487, p < 0.001), and pulmonary embolism (a OR 6.987, p < 0.001) [21].

Ventricular tachycardia

The study by Elkattawy et al. (2024) identified ventricular tachycardia as a rare but significant complication in preeclamptic patients, occurring in 0.04% of cases. Patients with ventricular tachycardia had a substantially prolonged hospital stay (7.16 days vs. 4.13 days, p = 0.001) and were more likely to experience severe outcomes, including cardiac arrest, postpartum hemorrhage, and the need for invasive procedures such as pacemaker implantation and heart catheterization. Predictors of ventricular tachycardia included pre-existing conditions such as congestive heart failure, atrial fibrillation, and obstructive sleep apnea [22].

Assessment of methodological quality

The studies included in this review were primarily cohort and case–control studies, with an average score of eight stars, indicating good methodological quality. However, there was notable heterogeneity among the studies, including variations in design and sample size. This heterogeneity may decrease the comparability of results both within and between studies, which should be taken into account when interpreting the findings. Strengths and limitations

This study has both strengths and limitations. Notably, this inaugural review delved into the risk factors influencing extended postpartum stays among these patients, and meticulous efforts were undertaken to mitigate data heterogeneity. The inclusion of case‒control and cohort studies, which are known for their robustness in identifying risk factors, adds strength to the analysis. Furthermore, the study quality assessment utilized a widely recognized tool tailored for this purpose. The review’s inclusion of articles published not only in English but also in Portuguese or Spanish broadens its scope, and comprehensive searches across major scientific health literature databases were conducted.

It is important to note that the conditions and severity levels varied across the included studies, which may influence the comparability of findings. While some studies focused on severe preeclampsia cases requiring intensive monitoring, others included patients with milder forms of the condition. This heterogeneity in patient profiles and clinical settings highlights the need for caution when interpreting aggregated results, as variations in baseline health status, treatment protocols, and resource availability may impact the observed outcomes.

Discussion

The results of this systematic review indicate that several factors are associated with prolonged postpartum hospital stay in women with preeclampsia. These include cesarean delivery, severity of preeclampsia, congestive heart failure and ventricular tachycardia, ascites, biochemical parameters, and intrauterine growth restriction. These results highlight the multifactorial complexity of this condition and the need for an integrated approach to the management of women with preeclampsia, considering clinical variations and postpartum care. Thus, by understanding and addressing these factors, health care providers can optimize patient care, reduce hospital admissions, and improve outcomes for women with preeclampsia.

The studies included [10, 15–18, 20–22] was conducted in developing countries, where the incidence of preeclampsia and eclampsia among women is generally higher. This increased incidence may be attributed to greater exposure to risk factors or challenges in accessing prenatal care [23, 24]. Despite originating from countries with similar developmental levels, important characteristics such as the average age of patients and gestational age, were comparable to those of studies conducted in developed countries focusing on preeclampsia [24]. This similarity allows for greater generalization of the findings observed in our study.

Several risk factors contribute to difficulties in blood pressure control and are associated with a significant increase in postpartum hospitalization time. These factors include cesarean delivery, severity of preeclampsia, the presence of DRPs, prepartum ascites, high diastolic blood pressure (above 100 mmHg), intrauterine growth restriction and some biochemical parameters. While exact percentages varied across studies, the collective impact of these factors suggests that, when present together, they can extend hospital stays by more than 20%. This estimate was derived by aggregating the findings from the included studies, with adjustments for the cumulative effect of multiple risk factors on the length of stay. However, when accounting for number of supporting studies and methodological aspects such as sample size calculations, final sample size, population variability, and increased hospitalization time, the primary risk factors associated with prolonged stays were (a) cesarean section delivery rather than vaginal birth, (b) severity of preeclampsia and (c) the presence of at least one DRP [10, 17, 20].

Complications related to cesarean section and severity of preeclampsia, such as postpartum hemorrhage, the need for blood transfusion, and significant elevations in blood pressure, contribute substantially to prolonged postpartum hospitalization, as reported by Amorim et al. (2015) [17]. Although the study focused on surgical and hemodynamic complications, it did not explore the impact of intensive use of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) for postoperative pain management, which may be relevant. Previous studies indicate that up to 80% of patients undergoing cesarean section use NSAIDs, which, in addition to relieving pain, may be associated with adverse effects such as increased risk of hemorrhage and renal alterations in vulnerable patients [25, 26].

While NSAIDs are supported by strong evidence, as highlighted by Zeng et al. (2016) [27], who demonstrated significant reductions in pain scores and opioid consumption across 22 randomized clinical trials, their potential to interfere with antihypertensive therapy has been recognized. This interference is particularly concerning in women with preeclampsia receiving antihypertensive drugs such as nifedipine, captopril, and furosemide, as inadequate blood pressure control could exacerbate complications and delay recovery [17, 28, 29, 30].

The relationship between cesarean delivery and prolonged hospitalization is further influenced by local healthcare contexts [31]. Differences in discharge protocols, availability of postnatal care, and postoperative recovery strategies significantly impact hospitalization duration. For instance, facilities with limited resources or stricter discharge criteria may prolong hospital stays to ensure patient stability, while those with robust outpatient follow-up systems may transition monitoring to home settings, thereby reducing hospital time [17, 23, 24]. Regional disparities in access to healthcare services and cultural norms surrounding cesarean deliveries also play a role, affecting both clinical decision-making and resource allocation [22]. These factors highlight the importance of context-specific analyses to better understand and address extended hospitalizations.

While cesarean delivery initially appeared to be a risk factor for extended hospitalization in Goes et al. (2021), this association lost significance in multivariate analysis. Instead, the presence of at least one DRP emerged as the primary determinant of prolonged postpartum hospitalization. DRPs, particularly those affecting blood pressure control, demonstrated a stronger influence on hospital stays, underscoring the critical need for effective pharmaceutical care to mitigate these issues [10].

A significant proportion of patients exposed to DRPs experienced challenges in achieving blood pressure control with antihypertensive drugs, leading to prolonged hospitalization until blood pressure reaches safer levels, minimizing the risk of complications such as stroke or organ damage. Safety-related DRPs, characterized by adverse drug reactions demanding additional management and observation time, also contributed to extended hospital stays. In the study by Goes et al. (2021), 11.3% of patients experienced at least one adverse drug reaction. Additionally, the majority of patients in the study were prescribed more than five drugs, a factor predictive of increased length of hospitalization [32, 33].

In addition, biochemical parameters such as fibrinogen, urea, ALT, and AST, essential for monitoring and controlling preeclampsia, may also influence the duration of hospitalization [16]. Changes in these parameters, which can serve as indicators of clinical severity in patients and can lead to liver and kidney dysfunction and coagulation, respectively, may contribute to an increased length of hospital stay [34, 35]. Subsequent studies could help identify the predictive role of these parameters on hospitalization time. Ascites, which has an incidence of approximately 21% in women with severe preeclampsia and is linked to higher mean systolic and diastolic blood pressure, and intrauterine growth restriction (women with severe preeclampsia or preeclampsia complicated by IUGR experience a slower recovery from clinical symptoms, leading to prolonged hospital stays) may also play a significant role in determining the timing of hospital discharge [15, 20, 36].

However, current evidence on these three potential risk factors has limitations in terms of sample size and selection bias, for example; suggesting the need for further studies to verify their association with increased length of stay [37]. Other risk factors have also been investigated recently, such as cardiac heart failure and ventricular tachycardia, which pose potential risk factors for prolonged hospital stays in women with preeclampsia due to the increased need for intensive monitoring and management of complications such as cardiac arrest and pulmonary embolism [21]. CHF and preeclampsia are also associated with endothelial dysfunction and heightened cardiovascular risk [38–41].

Conclusions and implication

This systematic review highlighted the complexity of factors influencing postpartum length of stay in women with preeclampsia, encompassing clinical complications such as ascites and congestive heart failure, as well as disease severity and DRPs. While cesarean delivery and pharmacotherapeutic issues emerged as recurring factors, the heterogeneity of clinical practices and hospital settings suggests that tailored interventions based on local assessments are crucial to optimizing hospital stay duration and clinical outcomes. Thus, this review provides valuable insights for improving care guidelines and for developing future research that explores integrated maternal care strategies adapted to the realities of different healthcare systems.
