Strain-induced faceting of Ti4O7 for active chlorine electrosynthesis
Authors: Kuanchang He, Wei Li, Jinxing Ma, Jianghu Cui, Yuan Kang, Kui Yang, Qian Liu, Min Zhang, Sihao Lv, Faliang Cheng, Defeng Xing
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Electrosynthesis of bulk chemicals such as active chlorine depends on the most reactive crystal facets, yet these facets are often thermodynamically disfavored during crystal growth. Here, we present a faceting strategy that integrates 3D printing with electric field inducement to reorient triclinic Ti4O7, realizing a dominant facet transition from (1 − 2 0) to high-energy (0 2 − 2) by storing and releasing strain energy to promote the preferential growth of crystal. Such transition trigger active site switching from O on pristine (1 − 2 0) facet to Ti on the reoriented (0 2 − 2) facet, greatly boosting the active chlorine generation rate to a comparable level (0.19 mg·min−1·cm−2) to benchmark dimensionally-stable anodes while suppressing parasitic water activation. A flow-by reactor reaches high active chlorine generation rates of 0.33–0.35 mg·min−1·cm−2 within 2.9–8.9 s, outperforming industrial dimensionally-stable anodes. This strain-induced faceting approach establishes a general paradigm for controllable crystal reorientation and underscores the potential of 3D printing to expand facet engineering for advanced catalytic systems.

Introduction

Active chlorine (AC, encompassing HClO and ClO−) is primarily generated via chlorine electrosynthesis in the chlor-alkali process and widely used in sterilization, chlorine-based chemical manufacturing, and water treatment1–3. However, current practices face major challenges: high energy consumption (>150 TWh annually)3,4; anode degradation with metal ion leaching in corrosive electrolyte (5 M Cl−, pH <3)5; activity-selectivity trade-offs in commercial dimensionally stable anodes (DSAs) caused by the competing oxygen evolution reaction (OER)2,6–8; and high cost of Ru/Ir-based DSAs3. Addressing these issues requires the development of noble-metal-free anodes that combine high activity with selectivity for efficient AC production.

Noble-metal-free Ti4O7 anodes with high OER potential (>2.5 VSHE)3,9–11 offer a promising route by decoupling chlorine evolution (CER) from OER. However, sluggish Cl− adsorption and activation on the naturally exposed (1 − 2 0) facet limit their applications8. Prior efforts to improve material activity have focused on morphology modulation, defect engineering, and elemental doping8,12. Specifically, elemental doping introduces heteroatoms to create abundant active sites and tune reactant/intermediate adsorption/desorption for high activity and selectivity. However, recent studies show that heteroatom doping (e.g., Fe, Ru) of Ti4O7 anodes not only enhances CER activity8,12, but also promotes water activation on the dominant (1−2 0) facet13,14, thereby compromising CER selectivity. Recent progress in chlorine electrosynthesis suggests that the design of noble-metal-free CER catalysts is evolving beyond simple composition screening toward active site engineering and interfacial electronic structure modulation15,16. These advances underscore the need for tailoring local coordination environments and surface reaction pathways to design Ti4O7 anodes with high CER selectivity and negligible side reaction influence.

Crystal facet engineering provides such an avenue by tuning atomic density, coordination, and electronic structure17–19 to regulate catalytic efficiency, selectivity, and performance20–23. Reorientation of specific facets has delivered enhanced selectivity in other systems, such as propylene epoxidation on Cu2O (1 1 0)24. We envision that crystal facet engineering offers a feasible approach via eliminating unfavorable facets and stabilizing high-activity planes to enhance CER and mitigate water activation; however, oriented crystallization is typically restricted to high-symmetry crystals grown under conditions (e.g., high temperature/pressure)25–28. Thus, imparting low-symmetry Ti4O7 facets with high activity and selectivity for CER is an appealing yet formidable challenge.

Herein, this study presents a strain-induced faceting strategy that integrates three-dimensional (3D) printing with electric field (EF) inducement to reorient triclinic Ti4O7. The resulting 3D-ordered architecture stores and releases strain under external field, reorientating toward the high-energy (0 2 − 2) facet. The 3D Ti4O7-EF anode exhibits AC generation rates comparable to DSAs (0.19 vs. 0.20 mg·min−1·cm−2). Combined theoretical and in situ Raman studies reveal active sites shift from O atoms on the (1 − 2 0) facet to Ti atoms on the (0 2 − 2) facet, lowering chloride activation barriers and suppressing water activation. Finally, we demonstrate a flow-by reactor based on 3D Ti4O7-EF for on-site AC generation, showcasing applications in sterilization, chemical synthesis, and pollutant remediation.

Results

Fabrication and characterization of 3D Ti4O7-EF

The 3D Ti4O7-EF anodes were fabricated following the schematic shown in Fig. 1a. Initially, raw Ti4O7 was homogeneously mixed with polyurethane resin to create an ink, which was then extruded to form Ti4O7 scaffolds using 3D printing. These scaffolds underwent thermosetting (70 °C) and electric field induction (20 V·cm−1) to yield 3D Ti4O7-EF anodes. To examine the morphologies and compositions of 3D Ti4O7-EF and raw Ti4O7, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and elemental mapping were performed. As shown in Fig. 1b and Supplementary Figs. 1, 2, no major difference was observed in physical structure or elemental distribution of Ti4O7 after the fabrication process, indicating that the procedure had negligible influence on its structural and compositional characteristics. Notably, a crystal-facet reorientation toward (0 2−2) in 3D Ti4O7-EF was observed compared to raw Ti4O7 with a naturally exposed (1−2 0) facet, as revealed by X-ray diffraction (XRD) (Fig. 1c)29,30. High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM), i.e., the lattice spacing, and selected area electron diffraction (SAED) confirmed the faceting of low-symmetry Ti4O7 (Fig. 1d, e and Supplementary Fig. 3)9. To probe the electronic structures changes after faceting process, we compared X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) signals in 3D Ti4O7-EF and raw Ti4O7. As displayed in Fig. 1f–i, Ti 2p showed a negative shift of ~0.5 and 0.5 eV in 2p1/2/2p3/2 doublet of 3D Ti4O7-EF compared to 464.7 and 459.0 eV in raw Ti4O7. Similar shifts were also observed in O 1 s spectrum. Peaks assigned to Ti−O−Ti (530.4 eV) and Ti−OH (532.1 eV) in raw Ti4O7 moved to lower binding energies of 530.0 and 531.9 eV in 3D Ti4O7-EF. A new peak of C − OH at 533.0 eV was attributed to the inert resin. To demonstrate the ∆ε-dependent facet orientation, we calculated (Fig. 2b, Supplementary Figs. 4–6, and Notes 1–5) and displayed the Gibbs free energy of the most representative facets when h was set as the original ink diameter (160 μm) in triclinic Ti4O7 as a function of ∆ε (Eq. 1)31–35. Figure 2c collectively shows these G curves, revealing three distinctive regions. (1) When ∆ε remained low (0 < ∆ε < 0.007%), these curves largely overlapped, indicating a mixture crystalline planes. Among them, (1−2 0) exhibited the lowest G to be a slightly more dominant facet, consistent with common observations in natural Ti4O7 crystals, indicating the leading role of γhkl in crystal morphology regulation; (2) higher ∆ε (0.007 <∆ε < 0.027%) would render (1 0−4) facet energetically favored in Ti4O7. This suggests a leading-role switch from γhkl to Ehkl in crystal growth, proving the possibility of high-index facets being preferentially exposed compared to the naturally exposed one with sufficient strain energy input and release24,34–36; (3) As ∆ε surpassed 0.027%, (0 2−2) facet emerges and stabilizes as the main crystal plane. To evaluate the influence of the diameters of Ti4O7 ink (160 ± 30 μm in reality verified by the SEM image) on crossover points in thermodynamic model, G-∆ε curves were replotted with a physically reasonable h range (~160 ± 50 μm) bracketed by the measured diameter distribution and broader bounds (Supplementary Figs. 7‒8 and Note 6). The crossover points moved to 0.008 and 0.032% at h = 110 μm, while those were 0.006 and 0.023% at h = 210 μm. The physical justification of faceting after strain energy release under these ∆ε values is provided in Supplementary Note 7.

To validate the above theoretical prediction, we measured the ∆ε of 3D Ti4O7-EF during the faceting process. The strain distribution in 3D Ti4O7-EF was quantified using geometric phase analysis (GPA)36,37. GPA revealed a gradual decrease in the compressive in-plane strain (ε) throughout the 3D Ti4O7-EF structure, declining from −0.046 ± 0.002% in the internal area (Fig. 2d1, e and Supplementary Figs. 9, 10) to −0.022 ± 0.003% in the middle region (Fig. 2d2, e) and −0.011 ± 0.002% at the surface (Fig. 2d3, e). We infer that strain energy release was initiated at the surface, and a ∆ε of 0.035% between the Ti4O7 surface and internal area represents the effective strain release, implying that (0 2−2) facet was dominant in 3D Ti4O7-EF based on the G-∆ε thermodynamic models with h = 110, 160, and 210 μm. The strain release value (∆ε at 0.035%) stayed in the regime favoring (0 2−2) facet over the full plausible h interval, and therefore it can be concluded that the thickness of Ti4O7/resin ink did not significantly influence the thermodynamic preference toward (0 2−2) facet in 3D Ti4O7-EF. This theoretical result was confirmed by the XRD results (Fig. 1c), verifying the theoretical feasibility of crystal growth for high-index facet dominance in low-symmetry crystals via 3D printing technology and EF inducement.

To investigate the distinct roles of 3D printing and electric field inducement in strain storage and Ehkl release, we prepared 3D Ti4O7 (without EF inducement), non-3D Ti4O7/resin-EF (without 3D printing), and non-3D Ti4O7/resin anodes (without 3D printing and EF inducement) (Supplementary Figs. 11–15). GPA analyses revealed their uniform strain distributions: 3D Ti4O7 exhibited a compressive strain of −0.022 ± 0.002% to −0.026 ± 0.002% (Fig. 2e and Supplementary Fig. 16), whereas the non-3D Ti4O7/resin-EF and non-3D Ti4O7/resin samples displayed only minor strain values of −0.008 ± 0.001% to −0.010 ± 0.008% (Fig. 2e and Supplementary Fig. 17) and −0.004 ± 0.002% to −0.005 ± 0.004%, respectively (Fig. 2e, Supplementary Fig. 18). These results demonstrate the strain accommodation capacity of the 3D-ordered structure, which facilitates higher strain distribution density38. The necessity of 3D-ordered structure was further experimentally and theoretically investigated, and finite element analysis results demonstrated that 3D scaffold had a stronger capacity for strain storage (Supplementary Figs. 19–23, Note 8, Tables 1–2)39. The intrinsic flexibility of 3D Ti4O7-EF is important during the resin thermosetting process, as it allows the 3D scaffold to elastically deform and conform to the volumetric shrinkage of the resin, thereby efficiently accommodating and storing the resulting mechanical energy as a persistent strain field40. Furthermore, we found that the electric field doubled the stored strain from −0.026 ± 0.002% (3D Ti4O7) to −0.046 ± 0.002% (3D Ti4O7-EF), underscoring its role in amplifying strain storage in a periodic structure. We attributed this enhancement to the combined effects of dielectric polarization and electromechanical coupling in the 3D-ordered architecture (Supplementary Fig. 24 and Note 9), and electric field-assisted dislocation multiplication within 3D Ti4O7-EF particles. The dislocation multiplication was validated by the subcritical-treatment 3D Ti4O7-EF-1h with a dominant (1 − 2 0) facet, displaying the lattice rotation and the formation of a dislocation-type boundary after EF inducement (Fig. 2f, Supplementary Fig. 25, and Note 9)41,42. The other Ti4O7/resin anodes in planar structures can only undergo straining on their outer surfaces (Supplementary Fig. 26 and Note 10), leading to inefficient strain storage38,43–45.

Electric field inducement also promotes strain release through dislocation slip and annihilation for dislocation-type grain boundary migration, atomic rearrangement, and crystal reorientation processes, and ultimately results in deformation banding on the surface (Supplementary Note 11)46–51. It should be noted that the pronounced surface-to-internal strain gradient of 3D Ti4O7-EF was inferred to a post-transformation strain state associated with facet rearrangement (Fig. 2f, Supplementary Figs. 25, 27, Note 12). Cross-sectional focus ion beam-scanning electron microscopy (FIB-SEM) analysis further revealed a strain relief region beneath the deformation bandings in 3D Ti4O7-EF52, demonstrating that the bandings were associated with the release of Ehkl in crystal (Fig. 2g, Supplementary Figs. 28‒29, Note 13)50. By contrast, similar bandings were absent in non-3D Ti4O7/resin-EF (Supplementary Fig. 30). This result suggested that energy release only occurred in 3D Ti4O7-EF, and thus the crystal morphologies of 3D Ti4O7, non-3D Ti4O7/resin-EF, and non-3D Ti4O7/resin samples were governed by γhkl, exposing a mixture of crystalline planes with low γhkl values, akin to those on natural Ti4O7 crystals (Supplementary Note 14), as confirmed by the XRD results (Supplementary Fig. 13). Thus, 3D printing provides the reservoir for strain storage, while the electric field serves as a dual role in strain storage amplification and effective release, a synergy essential for high-index facet dominance in low-symmetry Ti4O7.

Enhanced CER performance using 3D Ti4O7-EF

To evaluate the direct impact of faceting on electrocatalysis, we conducted electrochemical tests (4 M NaCl, pH 1) on the 3D Ti4O7-EF and compared its CER performance against that of raw Ti4O7 and a commercial DSA (RuO2/IrO2/Ti). As shown in Fig. 3a, the linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) results revealed that 3D Ti4O7-EF exhibited a markedly higher CER activity than raw Ti4O7 yet comparable to DSA, as evidenced by a pronounced increase in anodic current response. Specifically, the onset potentials (Eonset, defined at 1 mA·cm−2) were only 1.35 and 1.36 VRHE for 3D Ti4O7-EF and DSA, respectively, both significantly lower than that of raw Ti4O7 (1.60 VRHE)5. The corresponding Tafel slopes of 3D Ti4O7-EF and DSA (181 and 145 mV·dec−1) were also much smaller than that of raw Ti4O7 (324 mV·dec−1) (Fig. 3b). Furthermore, the overpotentials required to achieve 10 mA·cm−2 (η10) were 116 mV for 3D Ti4O7-EF and 100 mV for DSA, in sharp contrast to 586 mV for raw Ti4O7 (Fig. 3c). We excluded the contribution of resin for CER in 3D Ti4O7-EF due to the inferior electrochemical performances of other Ti4O7/resin anodes (Supplementary Figs. 31, 32 and Notes 15‒16). These results demonstrate that the CER activity of 3D Ti4O7-EF rivals that of state-of-the-art DSA, highlighting the beneficial role of faceting in enhancing reaction kinetics. The DSA-comparable activity and selectivity of 3D Ti4O7-EF was verified by the AC generation rates and corresponding Faradaic efficiencies, showing a similar AC generation rate (0.19 vs. 0.20 mg·min−1·cm−2) and Faradaic efficiency (54.5 ± 2.9% vs. 58.7 ± 3.3%) to DSA, while those of raw Ti4O7 were only 0.02 mg·min−1·cm−2 and 6.0 ± 0.1% (Fig. 3d–f, Supplementary Fig. 33, and Note 17). The application feasibility of 3D Ti4O7-EF under industrially relevant current densities (150–700 mA·cm−2) was then investigated. Drastic AC yields reaching 432.5 ± 12.5 to 1989.7 ± 45.4 mg·L‒1 in 5 min with corresponding AC generation rates at 1.23–5.68 mg·min‒1·cm‒2 and Faradaic efficiencies ranged from 44.3 ± 2.8% to 50.6 ± 3.4% were observed (Supplementary Fig. 34 and Note 18). The slight decline in Faradaic efficiencies demonstrated that 3D Ti4O7-EF preserved a preference for CER than side reactions under industrial-scale conditions. The operational stability of 3D Ti4O7-EF and the mechanical stability of the resin were also demonstrated (Fig. 3g and Supplementary Fig. 35, Note 19). SEM images, XPS, and LSV analysis confirmed its stability, as revealed by the robustness of 3D architecture, negligible change of Ti 2p and O 2p XPS spectra during CER, and stable electrocatalytic activity (Supplementary Figs. 36–38 and Note 20). Mechanism of preferential chloride activation on the (0 2−2) facet

We subsequently explored the origin of high CER performance upon faceting. This started by comparing available active site numbers in 3D Ti4O7-EF and raw Ti4O7 using double-layer capacitances (Cdl) and electroactive surface area (ESA). Results in Supplementary Fig. 39 and Note 21 show that the switching unexpectedly lowered Cdl (from 3.42 to 0.92 mF·cm−2) and ESA (from 325.14 to 108.38 cm2·cm−2), indicating reduced active site density in the dominant (0 2 − 2) facet. This led us to believe that enhanced catalytic site activity could well compensate for the density loss and cause overall better performance. To this end, we measured catalyst turnover frequency (TOF) and found 3D Ti4O7-EF showing a TOF value of 0.01–0.17 s−1 at overpotentials of 30–100 mV53. This was substantially higher than that of raw Ti4O7 (1.08–2.47 × 10−3 s−1) and other high-performing CER catalysts (Ir1O6: 3.8 × 10−3 s−1, and Fe-Ti4O7: 3.6 × 10−3 s−1), indeed proving the enhanced intrinsic activity in 3D Ti4O7-EF (Supplementary Fig. 40)8,54.

To rationalize such enhancement, we referred to density functional theory (DFT) calculations (Supplementary Figs. 41–42, Note 22), which suggested a clear active site shifting from O on (1−2 0) facet to Ti on (0 2 − 2) facet. As of how this active-site migration altered overall CER kinetics, we further examined the energetics of major reaction steps by calculating their Gibbs free energy profiles. Typically, the CER process follows the classical Volmer–Heyrovsky mechanism via *Cl pathway (Supplementary Figs. 44–48 and Note 23–25), involving a Cl− adsorption step (* + Cl− → *Cl + e−, Volmer step) and a Cl2 formation step (*Cl + Cl− → Cl2 + e− + *, Heyrovsky step)57. Our results (Supplementary Fig. 49) demonstrate that the distinct rate-determining steps (RDS) of (1−2 0) facet exhibited the highest ∆G (1.60 eV at U = 0 VRHE) at the Volmer step, implying thermodynamically hindered Cl− adsorption to the site, while (0 2−2) facet with the Heyrovsky step as RDS (1.56 eV at U = 0 VRHE) displayed a lower ∆G (1.16 eV) at the Volmer step, indicating faster Cl− adsorption. Consistently, Cl− adsorption experiments confirmed that the (1−2 0) surface exhibited weaker Cl− binding, validating the above theoretical prediction (Supplementary Fig. 50, Note 26). Furthermore, an energy barrier closer to 0 eV for the following Cl− activation (−0.20 eV at U = 1.36 VRHE) on (0 2−2) facet was observed (0.24 eV on (1−2 0) facet) (Fig. 4c). These results imply that the (0 2−2) facet offers a more favorable pathway for chloride adsorption and activation for catalytic performance.

Concurrent with strengthened chlorine activation, suppressed competitive H2O adsorption and activation on Ti of (0 2−2) facet was also identified after excluding the possible participation of O site (Supplementary Figs. 51–52, Notes 27–29, and Video S1). In contrast to the preferential H2O activation observed on the (1−2 0) facet, the (0 2−2) facet unfavored the endothermic adsorption of H2O and its dissociation, as evidenced by a higher Gibbs free energy barrier for *OH formation and an RDS shift from *OOH formation to *OH formation (Fig. 4d, Supplementary Figs. 53–54, and Note 30)58–60. We then extend and construct the Pourbaix diagram for thermodynamically stable configurations identifications under different pH and applied electrode potential (USHE)5,8,61,62. On (1−2 0) facet, *O is quite stable in a wide pH range, but the generation of *Cl and *OCl is limited. On (0 2−2) facet, *Cl is stable under acidic and neutral conditions, providing available application scenarios for 3D Ti4O7-EF (Supplementary Fig. 55 and Note 31). Thus, we demonstrated the preferential Cl− activation on (0 2−2) facet and H2O activation on (1 − 2 0) facet (Fig. 4e), which was also verified by the lower LUMO−HOMO energy gaps of (0 2−2)–Cl and (1−2 0)–H2O systems (Supplementary Fig. 56 and Note 32), indicating facilitated charge transfer for the respective adsorbates. These theoretical findings were experimentally validated by LSV measurements and probe molecule tests using nitrobenzene (NB, •OH quenching agent), acetate (•Cl quenching agent), and 1,4-dioxane (1,4-D, •OH and •Cl quenching agent) (Fig. 4f, Supplementary Figs. 57–59 and Notes 33–34)63–67.

The electronic profiles after Cl− and H2O adsorption provided additional insights to explain the swapped reaction priority following the active site shifting. The changes of total density of states (TDOS) values near the Fermi level (EF)68 correlate to electronic response of catalyst after adsorption, reflecting selectivity toward different adsorbates on catalysts. Results revealed higher responses of Cl− and H2O on (0 2 − 2) and (1 − 2 0) facets, respectively (Fig. 4g, Supplementary Fig. 60, and Notes 35). The interfacial electron redistribution revealed stronger electronic coupling between Cl and Ti atom on the (0 2−2) facet (Supplementary Figs. 61 and Notes 36), which was also confirmed by the off-domain orbital overlaps at EF in the partial density of states (PDOS) spectra. As shown in Fig. 4h, i, higher hybridization between Cl 2p and Ti 3 d orbitals on (0 2−2) facet than that between Cl 2p and O 2p orbitals on (1 − 2 0) facet was exhibited69. These electronic features are consistent with the stronger Ti–Cl and weaker (Ti–)O–Cl bonding intensities observed in the in situ Raman spectra (Fig. 4a, b).

Broad Application and Future Prospects

Having established the mechanistic origin of its high catalytic activity, we next investigated the practical applicability of 3D Ti4O7-EF in scalable chlorine electrosynthesis and environmental remediation scenarios. Its efficacy for rapid chlorine electrosynthesis was first assessed under batch conditions using simulated saline water with NaCl concentrations ranging from 50 to 500 mM, as well as real seawater (~380 mM Cl⁻) (Supplementary Fig. 62, Note 37, and Table 3). Subsequent tests demonstrated the efficient degradation of Rhodamine B (RhB, a model organic contaminant) with the apparent reaction rate constants (kobs) at 0.5–4.78 min−1, much higher than that without NaCl (0.005 min−1), which indicated the involvement of chlorine radicals (•Cl) generated in saline water (Supplementary Figs. 63–67 and Notes 38–42). Comprehensive evaluations of influencing factors (current density and pH), total organic carbon (TOC) removal rates, and energy consumption, together with economic analysis and aquatic safety assessments, confirmed the catalytic feasibility, cost-effectiveness, and environmental compatibility of the 3D Ti4O7-EF-based system for water decontamination (Supplementary Figs. 68–74, Notes 43–48, and Table 4). Specifically, 3D Ti4O7-EF exhibited rapid RhB degradation at low current densities and a working pH range of 3–9, with final TOC removal at 83.5 ± 3.3%–91.1 ± 1.4%. In terms of aquatic safety, low toxic byproduct formation and efficient common pathogen control were observed. Chlorate (ClO3−) and perchlorate (ClO4−) formation was limited, reaching for only 0.02%–2.42% and 0.03‰–0.20% of feed chloride, respectively. The in situ generated AC (~10.8 mg·L−1 within 1 min) achieved 6.66 and 6.22-log killing of E. coli in 300 mM NaCl and seawater. Overall, the economical 3D Ti4O7-EF (estimated areal cost at 103.0 $·m−2) operated at lower energy consumptions (0.13–0.83 kWh·m−3) provides an efficient solution for contaminant abatement.

To further explore its practical potential, a custom flow-by reactor incorporating 3D Ti4O7-EF as the anode was fabricated (Fig. 5a–c), with its physical assembly shown in Supplementary Fig. 75. As depicted in Fig. 5d and Supplementary Fig. 76, direct AC electrosynthesis yielded concentrations of 10.9–60.9 mg·L−1 and 10.4–63.4 mg·L−1 in 500 mM NaCl saline water and real seawater, respectively, at retention times (RT) of 2.9–8.9 s under 4–10 mA·cm−2 in single-pass mode. To distinguish the geometric mass transport effect from the facet effect in flow-by reactor for AC generation, 3D Ti4O7 having the same geometric structure but without EF inducement was employed to reassemble the reactor. 3D Ti4O7-embedded flow-by reactor produced 1.9–6.1 mg·L−1 AC with the calculated Faradaic efficiencies and AC generation rates at 2.5%–6.4% and 0.03–0.04 mg·min−1·cm−2, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 79). These results showed that the two electrodes sharing the same 3D geometry and reactor configuration with only different dominant crystal facets, exhibited nearly one order of magnitude performance difference, demonstrating the decisive role of high-energy (0 2 − 2) facet in enhancing intrinsic CER activity and selectivity.

Furthermore, rapid RhB degradation of 96.2–99.2% was achieved at low EE/O values of 0.1–0.3 and 0.1–0.4 kWh·m−3 (Supplementary Figs. 80, 81 and Video 2). The influence of chloride and coexisting anions on CER selectivity was discussed, and results indicated that SO42− did not exhibit significant impact on anodic chloride oxidation (Supplementary Fig. 82 and Note 52). The negligible cations (e.g., Mg2+/Ca2+) precipitate formation on cathode surface and channels in flow-by reactor after seawater electrolysis was analyzed (Supplementary Figs. 83–86 and Note 53). Computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulations suggested that velocity field in the reactor was relatively uniform, and the streamlines largely extended throughout the reactor without pronounced stagnation zones or locally closed recirculation regions. The faster electrolyte renewal and stronger convective mass transfer with increasing flow velocity and shorter RTs were probably favorable for fast removal of cathodic in situ-generated OH−, thereby alleviating the local high pH and weakening the precipitation of Mg2+/Ca2+ species, which were corroborated via XPS analysis. These results collectively demonstrate the high efficiency of AC generation and pollutant removal at short RTs in single-pass operation, underscoring the potential of this system as a scalable electrosynthesis platform for producing reactive chlorine species in practical electrochemical applications. Finally, successful upscaling of 3D Ti4O7-EF to dimension of 14 × 14 × 0.4 cm (Fig. 5f, g) further confirms its feasibility for on-demand implementations in sterilization, industrial processes (e.g., chlorine-based chemical manufacturing), and large-scale water treatment (Fig. 5h)70–72.

Discussion

Conventional crystal facet engineering to modulate catalyst activity and selectivity usually requires harsh conditions, such as high temperature and pressure, posing practical challenges for low-symmetry crystals. Herein, in this study, we present a strain-induced faceting strategy that combines 3D printing technology and EF inducement to reorient crystal facet toward (0 2 − 2) facet with high CER reactivity in low-symmetry Ti4O7. The printed 3D-ordered structure can store and release the strain energy for crystallization reorientation under electric field. Notably, the chlorine electrosynthesis on (0 2 − 2) facet achieves performance levels akin to DSAs (0.19 vs. 0.20 mg·min−1·cm−2). We discovered that the active site transition from O on (1 − 2 0) facet to Ti on (0 2 − 2) facet enhanced the orbital hybridizations between the active sites and Cl species, leading to thermodynamically favorable CER and suppressed H2O activation on (0 2 − 2) facet. A 3D Ti4O7-EF embedded flow-by reactor achieved AC production rates of 0.33–0.35 mg·min−1·cm−2 within 2.9–8.9 s. This research not only opens a pathway for employing faceting strategies in electrode development for efficient catalysis but also underscores the potential of utilizing non-noble metal anodes in a wide range of practical applications.

Methods

Chemicals, materials, and water samples

High-purity (>99%) Ti4O7 nano-powder was provided by Zhejiang Huiji metal materials Co., Ltd. (China). Thermoset polyurethane resin (ADT-DIW-S01L) was purchased from Shenzhen Adventuretech Co., Ltd. (China). Commercial stable anode (DSA, 1 × 1 cm) was obtained from Shuertai Industrial technology Co., Ltd. (China). Sodium chloride (NaCl), RhB, sodium sulfate (Na2SO4), sodium hydroxide (NaOH), sulfuric acid (H2SO4), 1,4-D, tert-butyl alcohol (TBA), sodium hypochlorite (NaClO), sodium chlorite (NaClO2), sodium chlorate (NaClO3), sodium perchlorate (NaClO4), acetate, sodium thiosulfate (Na2S2O3), benzoic acid (BA), and NB were obtained from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (China). The 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline-N-oxide (DMPO) used in •Cl and •OH detection was provided from Sigma-Aldrich Chemical Co., Ltd. (USA). LB (Luria-Bertani) agar and LB broth were obtained from BKMAMLAB, Co., Ltd. (China). All chemicals applied in the experiments were analytical grade or higher. All stock solutions were prepared with ultrapure water (~18.2 MΩ cm) from a Milli-Q system IQ 7000. E. coli (ATCC25922) was purchased from Biobw Co., Ltd. (China). Actual seawater samples were taken from Weiyuan Island (113°64’E, 22°81’N) in Dongguan, Guangdong, China.

Synthesis of Ti4O7 ink

The raw Ti4O7 powder was first ball milled in a ZrO2 (4 and 5 mm at a ratio of 1:1) ball crusher (Emax, Retsch, Germany) for 20 min. 30 g well-ground Ti4O7 powder was added into 5.8 g thermoset polyurethane resin, which was subsequently transferred into a planetary mixer (Mianyang Shinuo Technology Co., Ltd., China). The mixture was stirred at 800 r·min−1 for 2 min, and then cooled down to room temperature (25 °C). Finally, liquid-phase Ti4O7/resin ink (83.8% Ti4O7/16.2% resin) was obtained for the subsequent 3D printing process.

Fabrication of 3D Ti4O7-EF anodes

Based on custom-designed printing models from AutoCAD software, all Ti4O7 scaffolds with grid-shaped structures were generated through a direct ink writing (DIW) system (ADT-3D-LB-Printer-0050, Shenzhen Adventuretech Co., Ltd., China). As an extrusion-based technique, the DIW system employed the prepared ink to deposit layer-by-layer for grid-shaped scaffolds (3D Ti4O7 anode). Specifically, the grid-structure of the 3D Ti4O7 anode (diameter = 3 cm, thickness = 0.2 cm) was produced via building alternating layers of parallel lines with a uniform spacing at 1 mm between lines, and other printing parameters are shown in Supplementary Table 6. Afterwards, 3D Ti4O7 was solidified through the thermosetting process at 70 °C in an oven for 2 h. Each 3D Ti4O7 weighed ~1.5 g. The 3D Ti4O7 anodes were subjected to electric field (EF) inducement under 20 V·cm−1 in 200 mM Na2SO4 for 6 h to obtain 3D Ti4O7-EF.

Fabrication of 3D Ti4O7, non-3D Ti4O7/resin-EF, and non-3D Ti4O7/resin anodes

To explore the synergistic effect of 3D printing and EF inducement in the faceting process, 3D Ti4O7, non-3D Ti4O7/resin-EF, and non-3D Ti4O7/resin anodes were also fabricated. The 3D Ti4O7 anode was synthesized using the same method as the 3D Ti4O7-EF, but without the application of EF inducement. For the non-3D Ti4O7/resin anode, the prepared liquid-phase Ti4O7/resin ink was coated onto a carbon cloth, and the resultant Ti4O7/resin/carbon cloth underwent the same thermosetting process. The non-3D Ti4O7/resin-EF anode was obtained using non-3D Ti4O7/resin with EF inducement. Each non-3D Ti4O7/resin-EF, and non-3D Ti4O7/resin anode weighed ~1.5 g.

Fabrication of SPS Ti4O7-EF anodes

To synthesize SPS Ti4O7-EF, 1.5 g well-ground Ti4O7 powder was loaded into a cylindrical mold (diameter = 3 cm). The thermal-pressing process started before the system was evacuated to less than 1 Pa. The heating conditions (with pressure maintained at 5 MPa until cooling) was as follows: 0–3 min, 25 °C to 50 °C; 3–6 min, 50 to 200 °C; 6–10 min, 200–700 °C; 10–16 min, 700 to 1100 °C; 16–31 min, kept at 1100 °C; 31–150 min, quick cooling to less than 50 °C under vacuum9,73. The synthesized SPS Ti4O7 was subject to EF inducement to obtain SPS Ti4O7-EF.

Characterization

Surface morphology images were obtained via scanning electron microscope (Hitachi, Regulus 8100, Japan). Detailed inner structures and element distribution mapping images were analyzed by HRTEM, SAED, and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) via JEM-F200 combined with Ultim Max 80 (JEOL, Japan). Crystallography characterizations were evaluated through XRD (X’ Pert Pro MPD, Panalytical Co., Holland) with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5418 Å) via D8-Advance diffractometer (Bruker, Germany). Raman spectra were collected by a micro-Raman spectroscopy system (inVia-Renishaw, UK) with a 532 nm laser excitation source. The surficial elemental oxidation states were determined via XPS with Al Kα radiation at 1486.8 V using an ESCA Lab 250Xi instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). In situ Raman spectra were recorded using a confocal Raman system (LabRAM HR-Evolution, Horiba, Japan) with a 633 nm laser excitation source.

Reasons for replacing 532 nm laser excitation source with 633 nm laser excitation source during in situ Raman spectra collection

Based on the aforementioned Raman characterizations of all anodes (Supplementary Fig. 26) at 532 nm laser excitation source, we first monitored in situ Raman signals of 3D Ti4O7-EF and raw Ti4O7 under the same laser excitation. However, substantial electrogenerated intermediates may accumulate on 3D Ti4O7-EF during electrochemical tests, inducing undesired, pronounced fluorescence backgrounds under 532 nm laser excitation that overlapped the characteristic Raman signals. To this end, we then employed a laser excitation source with a higher wavelength at 633 nm to minimize the interference74. Notably, negligible fluorescence signals were observed on the surfaces of both 3D Ti4O7-EF and raw Ti4O7, offering optimal conditions for reliable Raman signal collection. Consequently, 633 nm laser excitation source was selected for in situ Raman spectra experiments in this study.

Spin-polarized DFT calculations were performed by using the Cambridge Serial Total Energy Package (CASTEP) code with projector-augmented wave (PAW) in Materials Studio 202479. Batch and flow-by experimental setup

Batch experiments were conducted in a 120-mL undivided cylindrical electrochemical reactor. The setup included an anode 3D Ti4O7-EF with a RuO2/IrO2/Ti cathode, all installed with a fixed inter-electrode gap of 2 cm. For chlorine electrosynthesis, 50–500 mM NaCl solutions and real seawater were used. NaCl solutions (pH = 6.76 ± 0.08) were prepared while using. Experiments were performed in as-prepared solution (100 mL) under an applied current of 20 mA·cm−2. Additionally, for RhB removal experiments, RhB (0.5 mM) was prepared in either 50–500 mM NaCl solutions or real seawater. All solutions were stirred using a magnetic stirrer (IKA-RCT, Germany) at a rate of 800 r·min−1. Experimental investigations for RhB removal mechanism were carried out using various quenching agents including phenol (50 mM), TBA (300 mM), acetate (300 mM), Na2S2O3 (300 mM), and BA (30 mM). Flow-by electrooxidation experiments were conducted in a 5-mL customized apparatus. The flow-by cell consisted of five sets of parallel 3D Ti4O7-EF (3.4 × 3.4 × 0.2 cm) anodes (Fig. 4c) and RuO2/IrO2/Ti (3.4 × 3.4 × 0.1 cm) cathodes with a gap of 0.3 cm between each anode and cathode. For chlorine electrosynthesis, 500 mM NaCl solution or real seawater was used under an applied current density of 4‒10 mA·cm−2 in a single-pass mode with different RT of 2.9–8.9 s. Furthermore, RhB (0.3 mM) was used as the model contaminant to evaluate the contaminant removal capability of the flow-by cell. Water samples were fed using a peristaltic pump (LongerPump Co., Ltd., BT600−2J, China) at flow rates of 35.2‒80 mL·min−1. Considering the extended reaction between AC and RhB, which may influence the analysis of RhB removal after single-pass treatment, water samples (10 mL) were collected and immediately spiked with Na2S2O3 (50 mM) to quench the reaction.

Analytical methods

NB and 1,4-D concentrations were measured using high-performance liquid chromatography with an ultraviolet detector (HPLC-UV, UltiMate 3000, Thermo Fisher Sci., USA). HPLC-UV analysis was carried out at a temperature of 30 °C, employing a C18 column (Hypersil GOLD, Thermo Fisher Sci., USA, 250 × 4.6 mm). The determination of NB and 1,4-D was conducted at wavelengths of 254 and 190 nm9,81. The blend of acetonitrile and acetate solution (65:35, v/v), and anhydrous acetonitrile and ultrapure water (12:88, v/v) were applied as mobile phases for NB and 1,4-D analyses, respectively. Separations were systematically executed at a constant pump flow rate of 1.2 mL·min−1. Acetate concentrations were determined through combustion ion chromatography (Thermo Fisher Scientific, ICS-2100, American) using AS11-HC column (Thermo Fisher Scientific, ICS-2100, American). The mobile phase consisted of ultrapure water and 40 mM potassium hydroxide (20:80, v/v). The flow rate was set at 1 mL·min−1. RhB concentrations were measured by ultraviolet−visible (UV−vis) spectroscopy (Thermo Fisher Scientific, EvolutionTM 220, American) at a wavelength of 554 nm82. Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR, Bruker, EMXplus, Germany) analyses were conducted using 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline N-oxide (DMPO) as a spin trapping reagent for •OH and •Cl tests. Total organic carbon (TOC) concentrations were analyzed using an analyzer (multi-N/C 3100 TOC/TN, Analytik Jena, Germany). The concentrations of various ions in real seawater and generated electrochemical byproducts, including ClO3− and ClO4− were characterized by combustion ion chromatography (Thermo Fisher Scientific, ICS-2100, American).

Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) methods for •Cl and •OH detection

The •Cl and •OH generation was evaluated using spin trapping and EPR detection83. Prior to EPR, spin trapping was utilized to preserve •Cl and •OH by the DMPO agent. DMPO can transform •Cl and •OH into DMPO − X and DMPO − OH adducts. The DC power was immediately switched off after spin trapping for 60 s, and 1 mL of solution sample was transferred into an ampoule and sealed by the cap. Concentrations of DMPO − X and DMPO − OH adducts in solution samples were evaluated using the EPR spectrometer, which was connected to a computer for data recording.

Disinfection experiment setup

Escherichia coli (E. coli) was employed in the disinfection experiments, which was first cultured in an LB agaric solid medium overnight at 37.5 °C in a constant-temperature oscillator (SFP-250, JingHong Instrument, China). The E. coli was then inoculated into an LB broth for logarithmic growth, followed by centrifugation at 1677 × g for 5 min and removal of the supernatant. The bacterial pellet was resuspended in 20 mM Na2SO4, 300 mM NaCl, or real seawater to the final concentrations of E. coli at 107 colony-forming units (CFU) mL−1. During the disinfection experiments using 3D Ti4O7-EF, 0.1 mL solutions were collected at the given time intervals and then spread evenly onto the surface of fresh LB agar solid media. COMSOL multiphysics simulation

Computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulations were conducted using COMSOL Multiphysics 6.3, which adopted the MUMPS sparse direct solver with finite element discretization and the backward differentiation formula (BDF) for time stepping. The mesh applied in the reactor consisted of four domains, 15,638 edges, 5248 boundaries, and 10,420 vertices, with 10 void domains. The solution in the reactor was regarded as a normal Newtonian fluid with a constant density and was defined as incompressible flow; thus, the incompressible form of the Navier−Stokes equations was adopted (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{D\rho }{{Dt}}=0$$\end{document}DρDt=0). The Navier−Stokes equations were employed to describe the kinematic equation followed by the laws of mass conservation, momentum conservation, and energy conservation.
