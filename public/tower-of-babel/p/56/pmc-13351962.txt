Endoscopic management of a duplicate nerve root—case report and technique
Authors: Sahil Garg, Patrick Young, Imran Noorullah, Nathan Lehman, Arianna Gill, Sanjitpal Gill, Michael Gallizzi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNerve root anomalies (NRAs), including duplicate and conjoined exiting nerve roots, are uncommon lumbar anatomic variants that increase the risk of neural injury during foraminal and extraforaminal surgery. Fully endoscopic descriptions remain limited, with only a small number of prior reports. We present a case and technique note on the endoscopic management of a duplicate exiting nerve root with a concomitant far-lateral disc herniation through a transforaminal approach and build on the existing literature to describe a structured, reproducible intraoperative sequence for safe decompression.Case DescriptionA 69-year-old woman presented with right lower-extremity radicular pain and progressive foot drop following a far-lateral disc herniation at L4–L5. She underwent a right L4–L5 far-lateral endoscopic microdiscectomy via a transforaminal approach. A Type IIB duplicate right L4 exiting nerve root was identified on meticulous preoperative imaging review by the operating surgeon (not described on the radiology report) and confirmed intraoperatively within the neural foramen. A structured intraoperative sequence, adapting commonly taught endoscopic approaches but not formally published termed “rotating neural blockade”, was employed, in which each limb of the duplicated root was sequentially protected, allowing for safe discectomy and foraminal decompression without neural injury. The patient’s radicular pain and dorsiflexion weakness improved postoperatively.ConclusionsThis case represents one of the first reports of fully endoscopic transforaminal management of a duplicate exiting nerve root and describes a reproducible intraoperative framework for safe decompression in the setting of a Type IIB NRA.Keywords: Endoscopic spine surgery, transforaminal approach, lumbar microdiscectomy, duplicate nerve root, case report

Highlight box

Key findings

• The “rotating neural blockade” technique involves rotating the bevel of the endoscope such that it creates a physical barrier between the disc and the neural elements during far-lateral decompression, sufficiently allowing surgeons to proceed with adequate discectomies. Including this step into a surgeon’s routine technique may help increase consistency of safe surgical practices in all cases. This may prevent iatrogenic nerve root injury in general.

What is known and what is new?

• For a new surgeon still refining their endoscopic technique, an intraoperatively identified nerve root anomaly may lead to intraoperative technical difficulty or neurologic injury during docking, with limited fallback to open approaches given the constrained anatomy of the far-lateral foramen.

• This case adds to a small but growing body of endoscopic reports of anomalous lumbar nerve roots and contributes a structured, named intraoperative sequence, the “rotating neural blockade”, that formalizes bevel rotation as a reproducible technical step for safe endoscopic decompression in the presence of a duplicate exiting root.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Including the “rotating neural blockade” technique into endoscopic decompression minimally invasive spine surgeries may increase consistency of safe practices in all cases. Additionally, this may prevent iatrogenic nerve root injury in general.

Introduction

Lumbar disc herniation (LDH) is the most common indication for decompressive lumbar surgery (1,2). LDH occurs in 5–20 per 1,000 people annually, peaks between ages 30–50 years, and is twice as common in males (3). Most patients improve with non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), physical therapy, and non-operative care. A subset require surgical decompression, most commonly involving discectomy (4). Open discectomy was historically the standard approach, but minimally invasive techniques using tubular retractors or endoscopic techniques have increasingly evolved, reducing iatrogenic injury to the paraspinal muscles, ligaments, and posterior osseous elements implicated in postoperative back pain and instability (5-7).

Operative planning is complicated when a lumbosacral nerve root anomaly (NRA) is present. Although rare, recognition of an NRA is essential because inadvertent injury to a nerve may cause severe and permanent disability (8). NRAs are most often identified on preoperative magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), but differentiation is difficult because pathological elements themselves may obscure visual appreciation of neural elements (8). In a cadaveric series of 100 specimens, the incidence of adjacent conjoined lumbosacral nerve roots was 14%, most commonly at L5–S1 (9). In practice, two classification systems are commonly utilized. Cannon’s original schema categorizes intradural spacing deformities as Type I, extradural anastomotic deformities as Type II, and extradural spacing deformities as Type III (10). Neidre and MacNab subsequently refined this schema as follows: Type IA, two intradural roots sharing a common sheath; Type IB, a nerve root exiting at a right angle to the dural sheath; Type IIA, an extradural anomaly in which one foramen is unoccupied; and Type IIB, foramina occupied with an additional (duplicate) root exiting through one foramen (11). Intradural-extradural anastomoses are grouped as Type III.

The surgical goals in NRA cases, decompression and mobilization without neural injury, are implied, but operative techniques to avoid injury are not standardized (12). Reports of fully endoscopic management of duplicate or conjoined exiting nerve roots remain few in number compared to open and microscopic techniques but include recently reported cases of Type IIB and Type IA conjoined nerve roots (7,12-16). We add to this small endoscopic series the case of a 69-year-old woman with a right-sided Type IIB duplicate exiting nerve root and a concomitant far-lateral disc herniation, treated with endoscopic microdiscectomy at L4–L5 via a transforaminal approach. We also describe a structured, reproducible intraoperative sequence, consistent with prior reports but carefully described here, which we term “rotating neural blockade”, that applies established principles of endoscopy in a defined order to sequentially protect the duplicated root during discectomy and foraminal decompression. We present this article in accordance with the CARE reporting checklist (available at https://jss.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jss-2026-1-0037/rc).

Case presentation

A 69-year-old female patient presented to the Orthopedic Spine practice of The Steadman Clinic for evaluation and treatment of right low back and right leg pain with progressive right foot drop. The patient had a chronic history of mild low back pain, however, her symptoms significantly worsened approximately 8 weeks prior to her clinic visit after a day of skiing. The patient developed severe pain in the right anterior thigh and right anteriomedial shin, along with progressive weakness of right foot dorsiflexion that was significantly affecting gait, requiring the patient to emphasize lifting the right foot through her stride to prevent tripping. They tried a course of physical therapy for 8 weeks and over-the-counter anti-inflammatories including naproxen, ibuprofen, and acetaminophen, with only mild transient relief of her symptoms. The patient rated her pain as severe and debilitating, stating that her symptoms caused significant disability in her day-to-day activities, with most of her day spent in bed due to pain. The patient denied incontinence of bowel or bladder and had no saddle anesthesia. She had a known history of non-operatively managed grade 1 anterolisthesis at L3–L4 and on imaging had been found to have transitional anatomy comprising of a patent S1–2 intervertebral disc space. She had no prior spine surgery and no significant past medical history. MRI films and X-ray (XR) films are included for reference (Figures 1-6). The pathological and operative level is referred to throughout this manuscript as right L4–L5.

During preoperative clinical examination, the patient was found to have weakness of the right tibialis anterior and extensor hallucis longus (EHL), scoring as 3− out of 5 and 4+ out of 5, respectively, using the standardized Oxford strength measurement scale. All other lower extremity muscle groups were measured as 5 out of 5 strength. She exhibited 2+ patellar reflexes bilaterally, 2+ bilateral Achilles reflexes, and negative bilateral clonus with downgoing toes on Babinski testing. The patient had an exquisitely positive right-sided straight leg raise and a negative left-sided straight leg raise. She demonstrated an antalgic gait with limping on the right leg and was unable to heel walk on the right foot. Sensory examination revealed painful, radiating numbness in the right L4 distribution with profound numbness of the right anterior shin and part of the dorsal foot. The patient’s clinical vignette and findings indicated a right sided L4 radiculopathy. These findings were also supported using a lumbar MRI scan (Figures 1-4). The aforementioned long-standing pathology was also visualized in addition to a right L4–L5 far-lateral disc herniation with a 5 mm right subarticular/foraminal extrusion and free fragment extending 9 mm cephalad, resulting in severe right subarticular recess narrowing and severe right L4–L5 foraminal stenosis with compression of the exiting right L4 nerve root (Figures 1-4).

Due to the underlying progressive neurological weakness, positive clinical findings correlated with MRI, and an elective decision to proceed with surgery, this patient was selected as a candidate for a right L4–L5 far-lateral microdiscectomy.

During the surgical procedure, dissection was performed to access the far-lateral disc herniation. Upon exposure of the neural elements, it was confirmed that this patient had a duplicate right L4 exiting nerve root in the right L4–L5 neural foramen (Figure 7). Both nerve root branches were found to be compressed by the far-lateral disc herniation. This anatomical variation had been identified on preoperative imaging review and was not described in the radiological report. Careful consideration was given during the decompression to protect both neural elements, with careful identification and preservation of each duplicated nerve root branch during the discectomy. These specific intraoperative details are described in the discussion below.

De-rotating the bevel of the endoscope to visualize adequate decompression and removal of disc material.

Postoperatively, this patient had an initial period of nerve root irritation with significant pain in the immediate postoperative period. She was managed with over-the-counter NSAIDs for pain control and enrolled in outpatient physical therapy, which she continued for 2 months. The recorded TA had improved to 3 immediately post operation. Later, improving to 4 at two-week follow-up and 4+ at the three-month follow-up with regained ability to perform all desired daily functions (Table 1). Their sensory examination showed significant improvement from the preoperative state with marked reduction in paresthesia intensity. At the three-month postoperative follow-up visit, the patient reported doing well with complete resolution of her pain and radicular symptoms. She had a successful return to walking, golfing, and favorite activities. The patient was pleased with surgical outcome and had returned to work in a full-time capacity.

Discussion

For the practicing surgeon, NRAs are likely to be present in multiple cases across a career. In a landmark retrospective study by Postacchini et al., the authors examined 2,123 subjects who underwent contrast lumbar imaging and identified a prevalence of 2.17% or 46 cases (17). In prospective studies, estimated prevalence rates of 6–14% are cited (18). While intraoperative management of such anatomy has been well described using tubular microdiscectomy techniques, endoscopic descriptions have only recently begun to appear and remain few in number (7,17-20). The inability to utilize instruments in an endoscopic system that a surgeon may have used in residency (such as kerasins and micro-pituitaries) and a larger surgical corridor, may present a challenge for those early in the learning curve of endoscopy. For a new surgeon still refining their endoscopic technique, an intraoperatively identified NRA may lead to significant intraoperative technical difficulty or neurologic injury during docking, with limited fallback to open approaches given the constrained anatomy of the far-lateral foramen. In many instances, such anatomic anomalies are unknown until intraoperative exposure (14). Recent reports have begun to describe endoscopic management of anomalous lumbar nerve root anatomy, but the literature remains limited (15,16).

This patient also demonstrated lumbosacral transitional anatomy, with a patent S1–S2 disc space. While the literature linking lumbosacral transitional vertebrae to NRAs is limited, vigilance for concurrent NRAs may be warranted when segmentation variants are identified on preoperative imaging.

More recently, Mobbs and Lui reported two cases of fully endoscopic management of conjoined nerve roots, including a 63-year-old patient in whom a Type IIB conjoined L5 nerve root sharing a common dural sleeve was identified intraoperatively during endoscopic decompression for L4–L5 lateral recess stenosis (16). Their report establishes important precedent that the magnification and high-definition visualization of endoscopic spine surgery can support safe identification and decompression of anomalous lumbar nerve roots, even when the anomaly is not appreciated on preoperative imaging (Figure 4). The present case extends this small body of literature in several respects. First, the operative pathology was a far-lateral disc herniation rather than lateral recess stenosis, requiring a fully transforaminal approach through Kambin’s triangle and direct navigation of the duplicated exiting root within the foramen itself (Figures 1-3). Second, the duplicate root in this case represented a true bifid Type IIB anomaly under the Neidre-MacNab criteria, two existing roots with separate dural sleeves, distinguishing it morphologically from conjoined variants in which adjacent roots share a single dural origin (Figure 4). Third, we describe a structured, named intraoperative sequence, the rotating neural blockade, which formalizes bevel rotation as a reproducible step in endoscopic decompression of anomalous exiting roots. Taken with prior reports, this case supports the feasibility of endoscopic management across a range of NRAs.

The technique described here is intended to be reproducible across cases and with consistent use may help prevent neurologic injury to unrecognized NRAs. While this specific technique may be used by endoscopic surgeons already, the formalization of this technique as described in this manuscript is intended to help ease of reference and open discussion amongst endoscopic spine surgeons.

Standard docking: through a series of lumbar anteroposterior (AP) view and lateral X-rays, the surgeon advances a long spinal needle with inner stylet in a transforaminal approach to the foramen of the operative level. The surgeon then removed the stylet and injected a contrast dye and methylene blue into the intervertebral disc at the operative level. The surgeon then removes the inner stylet and passes a K-wire through the spinal needle to hold their position in the foramen. Then the spinal needle is removed. At this point, the surgeon began the dilation process by passing a series of dilators over the K-wire, until they were able to pass the endoscopic cannula over the dilators to access the far-lateral foraminal disc space at the operative level.

Once docked, the key to safe decompression is visualization. The next step, in a surgically naive patient, is to directly attempt to visualize the exiting nerve and thecal sac. In situations where the patient may not be surgically naive and thus have scar tissue, or in instances where this anatomy may not initially be appreciable, very delicate removal of scar tissue, epidural fat, and disc fragment(s) should ensue with a focus on constant visualization of structures just posterior to the tissue (Figures 8,9).

Once visualized, depending on the type of aberrant anatomy, the nerves may be directly appreciated. Due to the location of the endoscope within Kambin’s prism in a far-lateral approach, the likelihood of overlying tissue aside from disc material is quite low.

If there is overlying tissue, extreme caution, in all cases should be taken to only secure non-neural tissue within the grasping instruments (Figures 8,9). Ablation and rotating burrs may not be ideal in these situations prior to neural visualization unless the degree of facet hypertrophy or obscurity is at a severe level.

In this case, there was disc material visualized after docking with an NRA visualized at the right side of the frame (Figures 8,9). The endoscope is activated prior to docking so that visualization is maintained continuously as the cannula is advanced into the foramen. Upon docking, pathological material (e.g., extruded disc, epidural fat, or ligamentum flavum) is often appreciable in the working field before the underlying neural elements can be directly visualized. A surgeon should approximate the distance from the visible pathological surface to the expected location of the traversing nerve root. This estimated distance is designated as x. Instruments are advanced only to a maximum depth of approximately x/3 when grasping pathological tissue, preserving a safety buffer of roughly 2x/3 between the instrument tip and the presumed neural plane. This guide is intended to prevent inadvertent injury of a nerve root concealed behind the target tissue. As material is progressively cleared and the neural elements become directly visualized, x is re-approximated and instrument advancement adjusted accordingly (Figure 9). While this adds a few minutes to case duration, it has, in our experience, prevented iatrogenic nerve injury, particularly in cases of aberrant anatomy.

Once the nerves are visualized, we describe a structured maneuver we term the “rotating neural blockade”. During initial docking, the bevel of the scope is oriented away from the neural elements to permit safe cannula advancement under continuous visualization. Once the neural anatomy is directly appreciated within the working field, the bevel is rotated toward the roots such that it is interposed between the neural elements and the target disc material, walling off the roots from the working corridor. The bevel functions here as a physical barrier rather than as a retractor, it does not apply active displacing force against the nerve, and the maneuver is intended to avoid the compression, traction, and rotational stresses associated with conventional neural retraction in the constrained foraminal working space. In the case of this Type IIB anomaly, this technique sufficiently allowed the surgeon to proceed with an adequate discectomy (Figures 7,10). A bifid or duplicate nerve root may be directly visualized within the transforaminal window if readily appreciable. Where it is not immediately apparent, the blockade remains a replicable injury-avoidance sequence for both exiting and traversing roots across normal and aberrant anatomic conditions, as the bevel is interposed against neural elements which are encountered within the working corridor.

Type IIB anatomy presents two specific hazards relevant to this technique. Because the two exiting roots share a common foramen and may emerge from the thecal sac at variable bifurcation points. Retraction or displacement of one limb can produce tension on the other. Additionally, with two separate dural sleeves occupying a small working zone, the surgeon cannot assume that the nerve is in a single expected location. For instance, despite visualizing one root, the surgeon may inadvertently injure an unobserved second limb concealed behind pathology or at the edge of the working corridor. The rotating neural blockade concept, when used in standard practice, at least partially hedges some risk. With the bevel acting as a physical barrier rather than a retractor, the bevel walls off neural elements from the working corridor without applying active displacement force to either limb. This limits the tension that propagates between roots. The x/3 depth concept reduces the likelihood of an out-of-view limb being caught by an advancing instrument when only one root is directly visualized. Neither maneuver eliminates the risk entirely, particularly in cases of highly variable bifurcation anatomy. Therefore, in the experience of the authors, direct visualization of both limbs prior to any instrument pass remains the principal safeguard but understandably may not be possible in each case depending on the observed pathology in the working corridor.

A limitation of this case report is the relatively short 3-month follow-up interval. Longer-term outcomes including durability of symptom resolution and absence of recurrence may instead of will be reported in subsequent follow-up.

Conclusions

The primary step to ensure sufficient decompression is visualization. Once visualized, the “rotating neural blockade” should ensue in instances of far-lateral decompression to safeguard risks of neural injury. This step can be replicated in cases of normal anatomy as well in a surgeon’s standard technique to protect against iatrogenic nerve injury. Despite its smaller profile the endoscope allows for superior visualization compared to tubular microdiscectomy techniques. This case adds to the small but growing endoscopic literature on anomalous lumbar nerve root anatomy and contributes the rotating neural blockade as a structured, named intraoperative sequence that can be incorporated into routine endoscopic practice.
