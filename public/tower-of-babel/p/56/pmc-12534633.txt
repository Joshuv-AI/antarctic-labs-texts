Economic Benefit of Aflibercept 8 mg Versus Faricimab for Neovascular Age-Related Macular Degeneration or Diabetic Macular Edema in the US
Authors: Andreas Kuznik, Anna Coughlan, Amy Pinsent, Hector Toro-Diaz, Steven Sherman, Nimesh Patel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe aim of this study was to compare the estimated direct and indirect costs with aflibercept 8 mg and faricimab in patients with neovascular age-related macular degeneration (nAMD) or diabetic macular edema (DME) over 5 years.MethodsAn economic model was developed to estimate direct and indirect costs based on the mean number of injections administered in clinical trials of aflibercept 8 mg (PULSAR and PHOTON) and faricimab (TENAYA/LUCERNE and YOSEMITE/RHINE). Injection numbers for years 1 and 2 were sourced from clinical trials, injection numbers in years 3–5 were imputed from year 2, and the base case for the analysis was a 3-year time horizon. Direct costs were estimated based on modeled mean injection numbers and 2025 wholesale acquisition costs per injection (aflibercept 8 mg: 2677.50 USD; faricimab: 2289.65 USD) plus medical monitoring costs. Indirect costs included caregiver and patient time and travel costs.ResultsOver a 3-year time horizon, the mean number of injections was lower with aflibercept 8 mg versus faricimab for nAMD (12.25 vs. 14.80 injections) and DME (11.80 vs. 15.65 injections). Total costs (direct and indirect) associated with aflibercept 8 mg were 1978.74 USD lower than faricimab for nAMD and 6032.90 USD lower than faricimab for DME. After year 1, similar differences were seen through 5 years.ConclusionsFor both nAMD and DME, less frequent dosing with aflibercept 8 mg versus faricimab is expected to reduce the direct and indirect cost burden to payers and patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40123-025-01236-6.

Introduction

Neovascular age-related macular degeneration (nAMD) and diabetic macular edema (DME) are major causes of vision loss worldwide [1–3]. The prevalence of nAMD is projected to increase as populations age, while the prevalence of DME is expected to rise as the number of diabetes cases continues to increase, which in turn will likely lead to higher health care-related costs [4, 5]. Intravitreal anti-VEGF therapy was established as the standard of care for patients with nAMD and DME following evidence of improved outcomes from randomized clinical trials [6–12].

To address the treatment burden associated with intravitreal anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) injections, new treatment options enabling patients to achieve similar outcomes to those of established agents with longer intervals between injections (i.e., requiring fewer doses) have been introduced. Aflibercept 8 mg was approved in the United States with dosing intervals of 8–16 weeks following three initial monthly doses for both nAMD and DME [13, 14] based on results from the pivotal PULSAR [15] and PHOTON [16] trials, respectively. Aflibercept 8 mg delivers a four-fold higher molar dose compared with the original 2-mg dose, which is hypothesized to increase the duration of VEGF suppression while providing similar visual gains, anatomic improvements, and safety [15–17]. In the PHOTON and PULSAR trials, patients receiving aflibercept 8 mg achieved similar best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) gains to those receiving aflibercept 2 mg at 48 weeks, with fewer injections and a comparable safety profile [15, 16]. Similar results were seen at 96 weeks [18, 19].

Faricimab, a bispecific monoclonal antibody targeting VEGF-A and angiopoietin-2, is also approved for the treatment of nAMD and DME in the United States [20]. In the pivotal TENAYA/LUCERNE and YOSEMITE/RHINE trials, patients receiving faricimab 6 mg with dosing intervals that were extendable to 16 weeks achieved similar BCVA gains to those receiving aflibercept 2 mg, with comparable safety over 2 years of treatment [21–24]. Faricimab was approved with dosing intervals of 4–16 weeks following four initial monthly doses for nAMD, and dosing intervals of either ≤ 8 weeks following six initial monthly doses or with incremental dosing interval increases following four initial monthly doses for DME [20].

Adoption of intravitreal anti-VEGF therapies with extended dosing intervals is anticipated to reduce treatment burden and optimize patient management [15, 16, 21, 23]. However, evaluating the comparative direct and indirect costs of these therapies is essential when considering their positioning in clinical practice [25]. Here, we used an economic model to compare direct and indirect costs with aflibercept 8 mg and faricimab in patients with nAMD or DME over a 5-year period.

Methods

Model Development

In the absence of head-to-head clinical trials comparing aflibercept 8 mg and faricimab, data for injection frequency from the available trials were compared indirectly. An economic model that was previously developed [26] was used to estimate direct and indirect costs of aflibercept 8 mg versus faricimab in patients with nAMD or DME over a period of 1–5 years from payer and societal perspectives in the United States. A 5-year period was chosen, as it is standard for this type of analysis. The economic model used a Markov cohort approach to model the costs of adult patients with nAMD or DME in the United States. The model has been published previously in the context of a full cost-utility analysis [26]. For the purposes of this analysis, only treated eyes were considered and fellow eye involvement was excluded. Treatment efficacy was assumed to be noninferior across trials. Further information on the model functionality is provided in the Supplementary Methods in the electronic supplementary material.

Estimates of direct and indirect costs for years 1 and 2 in the model were based on the mean number of injections administered in aflibercept 8-mg clinical trials in nAMD (PULSAR, ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT04423718) [15, 19] and DME (PHOTON, ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT04429503) [16, 18], and in faricimab clinical trials in nAMD (TENAYA, ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT03823287; LUCERNE, ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT03823300) [21, 22] and DME (YOSEMITE, ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT03622580; RHINE, ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT03622593) [23, 24] (Table 1). The trial designs have previously been reported [15, 16, 18, 19, 21–24] and are briefly described in the Supplementary Methods in the electronic supplementary material. For both PHOTON and PULSAR, mean numbers of aflibercept 8-mg injections were averaged for the aflibercept 8-mg treatment groups (aflibercept 8 mg every 12 weeks [8q12] and every 16 weeks [8q16], both after three initial monthly doses) at week 48 [15, 16] and week 96 [18, 19] to derive the number of aflibercept 8-mg injections for years 1 and 2 in the model (Table 1).

For TENAYA and LUCERNE, the mean number of faricimab injections at week 48 for both trials combined [27] was used for the number of faricimab injections for year 1 in the model (note these studies only included faricimab treat-and-extend arms [faricimab 6 mg up to every 16 weeks], with no faricimab fixed dosing arms). The mean number of faricimab injections up to weeks 104–112 in TENAYA and LUCERNE [22] was combined and averaged, and the mean number of faricimab injections from year 1 was subtracted, to derive the number of faricimab injections for year 2 in the model.

For YOSEMITE and RHINE, the mean number of faricimab injections from the treat-and-extend arms through week 56 (both trials combined and averaged) was used for the number of faricimab injections for year 1 in the model, and the mean number of faricimab injections from the treat-and-extend arms (faricimab 6 mg up to every 16 weeks) through week 96 (both trials combined and averaged) was used for the number of faricimab injections for year 2 in the model (Table 1) [24]. The number of injections from the faricimab fixed dosing arms (faricimab 6 mg every 8 weeks) were not included, as they were not relevant to this analysis.

The mean number of aflibercept 8-mg or faricimab injections beyond year 2 was not available from the aflibercept 8-mg and faricimab trials. In scenarios with time horizons longer than 2 years, the mean number of injections per year was assumed to be the same as in year 2.

Although all trials mentioned above included aflibercept 2-mg comparator arms, these were not included as they were not considered relevant for this analysis.

Calculation of Costs and Savings

Direct costs for each treatment were modeled as the product of the mean number of injections over 1–5 years and 2025 wholesale acquisition costs (WACs; aflibercept 8 mg: 2677.50 USD; faricimab: 2289.65 USD) (Table 1) [28], accounting for patient treatment discontinuation and mortality.

The direct medical monitoring costs (2025, in USD) [29, 30] included office visits, fluorescein angiography, and optical coherence tomography (OCT) (Table S1 in the electronic supplementary material). The cost of office visits (“initial” cost for the first visit, then “subsequent” cost thereafter) and OCT were assumed to be incurred at the time of each injection. All patients were assumed to incur the cost of fluorescein angiography in year 1 of treatment, independent of the number of injections.

This was calculated as follows:

Monitoring cost for initial visit in year 1 = (initial office visit + OCT + fluorescein angiography) = 534.15 USD.

Monitoring cost for subsequent visit(s) in year 1 = ([number of injections in year 1 – 1] × [subsequent office visit + OCT]) = ([number of injections in year 1 − 1] × 184.58 USD).

Direct medical monitoring costs in year 2 and thereafter (year ≥ 2) were equal to the adjusted number of injections (taking discontinuation and mortality into account) in year ≥ 2 multiplied by 184.58 USD (i.e., subsequent office visit + OCT). This was calculated as follows:

year ≥ 2 monitoring cost = number of injections in year ≥ 2 × 184.58 USD.

Indirect costs per injection for patients and carers were calculated using multiple parameters, including caregiver time taken for an injection appointment [31], the average US wage per working hour [32], reduction in hourly wage for nonworking people’s time [33], hours of lost productivity for a patient due to the injection and appointment [34], percentage of carers’ time taken from activities [34], percentage of patients who require a caregiver [34], the average distance, in miles, traveled to get to an appointment [34, 35], and cost per mile traveled [36], for an indirect total cost of 289.98 USD per injection for patient and carer (Table S2 in the electronic supplementary material).

The base case analysis assumed a treatment duration of 3 years for all drugs and included direct costs (including 2025 WAC and monitoring costs) and indirect costs. Alternative scenarios were assessed over 1, 2, 4, and 5 years of treatment. Furthermore, a sensitivity analysis of the base case analysis (3 years) was conducted and included direct costs only (WAC and monitoring costs).

A scenario analysis assessed the impact of varying the mean number of injections received in year 1 and thereafter by ± 1 standard deviation (SD). Costs associated with aflibercept 8 mg and faricimab were compared by using the higher and lower injection number estimates for both treatments in nAMD and DME. SDs were sourced from the relevant clinical trials.

Ethics

This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

Results

For patients with DME, there were approximately three fewer injections of aflibercept 8 mg versus faricimab in year 1 (mean number of injections: 5.50 vs. 8.55). This resulted in substantially lower total costs associated with aflibercept 8 mg (6246.33 USD) versus faricimab in year 1. In subsequent years, the incremental difference in the number of injections between aflibercept 8 mg and faricimab was much smaller, yielding similar estimated total costs between 2 and 5 years (Figs. 1 and 2). However, due to the large difference in total costs between aflibercept 8 mg and faricimab in year 1, substantially reduced total costs with aflibercept 8 mg versus faricimab were still apparent up to year 5.

In the sensitivity analysis that considered direct costs only over 3 years, aflibercept 8 mg was consistently associated with lower total direct costs than faricimab for both nAMD (1313.50 USD lower total direct costs) and DME (4942.31 USD lower total direct costs) (Fig. S1 in the electronic supplementary material). In the sensitivity analysis, which used a reduced estimate for the mean number of injections for aflibercept 8 mg and faricimab (i.e., 1 SD lower than the base case over 3 years), aflibercept 8 mg was associated with 2093.81 USD lower total costs than faricimab for nAMD (Fig. S2A in the electronic supplementary material). However, the use of a reduced estimate for the mean number of injections for DME resulted in 1827.57 USD higher total costs with aflibercept 8 mg than faricimab (Fig. S2A in the electronic supplementary material). This finding was due to the substantially higher uncertainty around the mean number of injections that were observed in the DME faricimab trial (SD in year 1: 2.5; year ≥ 2: 1.9) relative to the DME aflibercept trial (SD in year 1: 0.9; year ≥ 2: 1.0), resulting in more favorable cost estimates for faricimab in this scenario. The sensitivity analysis that used a higher estimate of the mean number of injections produced similar results to the overall base case findings, with aflibercept 8 mg associated with reduced total costs versus faricimab for patients with nAMD or DME (Fig. S2B in the electronic supplementary material).

Discussion

Intravitreal anti-VEGF therapy was established as the standard of care in nAMD and DME and has markedly improved outcomes for millions of patients globally [6, 12]. Anti-VEGF injections reduced the incidence of vision loss with consequent direct economic benefits, indirect benefits, and increased quality of life [37–39]. Recent treatment options (aflibercept 8 mg and faricimab) require substantially fewer injections than the first available intravitreal anti-VEGF agents, as demonstrated in the clinical trial data applied in this analysis [15, 16, 18, 19, 21–24].

This analysis compared direct and indirect costs associated with aflibercept 8 mg and faricimab injections in patients with nAMD or DME across a 5-year period, using the mean number of injections observed in pivotal clinical trials to account for dosing interval modifications that were possible in the trials based on individual patient responses to treatment. Indirect costs represent a relatively small proportion of the overall costs compared with the drug costs. In the clinical trials, substantially fewer aflibercept 8-mg injections were administered relative to faricimab during the follow-up periods [18, 19, 21, 23]. In the present analysis, this translated to lower estimates of total costs with aflibercept 8 mg versus faricimab, with greater savings for patients with DME. This was seen over years 1–5 for DME, and for years 2–5 for nAMD. The exception was year 1 for nAMD, when total costs were marginally higher with aflibercept 8 mg versus faricimab despite similar injection numbers; this reflects the higher drug acquisition cost for aflibercept 8 mg.

The differences in total costs observed between patients with DME versus those with nAMD are due, in part, to a higher number of initial monthly injections in the faricimab trials (YOSEMITE/RHINE, with four to six initial monthly injections [23]) compared with the aflibercept 8-mg trial (PHOTON; three initial monthly injections for the aflibercept 8-mg groups [16]), with less of a difference in terms of number of injections between the nAMD trials for faricimab (TENAYA/LUCERNE; four initial monthly injections [21]) and aflibercept 8 mg (PULSAR; three initial monthly injections [15]). However, the lower mean number of injections seen with aflibercept 8 mg compared with faricimab also reflected the dosing interval extension that was permitted in the second year of PHOTON [18] and PULSAR [19], with some patients assigned to dosing intervals of up to every 24 weeks, whereas the maximum dosing interval that was possible in the faricimab trials was every 16 weeks [22, 24]. Criteria for dose adjustments also differed between the aflibercept 8-mg and faricimab trials, which may have impacted the mean number of injections.

Findings from a scenario analysis over 3 years that excluded indirect costs supported the 3-year base case findings, as did the analysis using a higher estimate for mean number of injections over 3 years. However, faricimab was associated with lower total costs than aflibercept 8 mg for DME in the scenario that used a lower estimate for mean number of injections over 3 years. The differences between the results of these analyses can be attributed to the greater variability (i.e., greater SD) in the number of injections in the DME trials of faricimab compared with aflibercept 8 mg.

The overall results were sensitive to the number of injections that were assumed in the model. The costs associated with the number of injections were influenced by the elevated risk of mortality associated with DME and nAMD [40, 41], and the risk of treatment discontinuation [11, 42, 43], although the impact of both was minor. BCVA in patients with nAMD and DME also informed the risk of mortality [44], such that, each year, an elevated risk of death was applied to patients in the cohort who were considered blind (BCVA < 35 letters).

The findings of this analysis are consistent with those of a prior analysis, which showed that intravitreal anti-VEGF therapies that are associated with fewer versus greater number of injections provide greater indirect cost savings, as the number of injection visits, and consequently travel time, decrease [25]. Although associated with improved visual outcomes [6, 12], the need for frequent intravitreal anti-VEGF injections results in a substantial burden on patients [34, 45]. This burden includes the associated costs and travel time needed to receive treatment, time off work, and patient prioritization of treatment over family or other commitments [34, 45]. Furthermore, there is a consequent burden on the caregivers who support many patients with nAMD or DME [31, 34, 45].

The treatment landscape for nAMD and DME is evolving with the availability of therapies that allow for extended dosing and fewer injections [13, 20], which consequently reduce direct and indirect costs. The lower indirect costs with aflibercept 8 mg versus faricimab suggested by this analysis, which may include reduced transportation costs and reductions in lost earnings due to less frequent trips to healthcare providers, may have considerable benefits. Limited access to transport and the need to travel long distances have been highlighted as barriers for access to health care [46] and can decrease treatment adherence [47], which in turn can lead to poor visual outcomes [48]. Therefore, reducing injection frequency may improve treatment adherence and thereby help to improve outcomes. Patients qualifying for extended dosing intervals may have more prominent benefits in rural versus urban and suburban settings, as they are more likely to experience barriers to accessing treatment, such as fewer ophthalmologists available in their region and the consequent need to travel long distances to receive care [46, 49]. Furthermore, a reduced injection frequency may also be valuable in terms of time saved, fewer working days missed by patients and caregivers, and fewer office visits. Studies of intravitreal anti-VEGF agents with monthly dosing regimens reported substantial Medicare costs for patients with nAMD and diabetic retinopathy [3–5], which would be anticipated to decrease with less frequent dosing. Reduced injection frequency could also decrease the burden on treating physicians and healthcare workers [34].

Limitations of the present analysis include an indirect comparison of injection frequencies across the pivotal trials, as randomized head-to-head data are not available. Therefore, the analysis does not account for any differences in the included trials, such as inclusion and exclusion criteria, patient characteristics, and protocols. For example, the YOSEMITE and RHINE trials limited enrollment of previously treated patients with DME to 25% of the trial population [23], whereas PHOTON did not include such a limit (44% of the PHOTON population had previously been treated for DME) [16]. The aflibercept 8-mg trials (PHOTON and PULSAR) and faricimab trials (TENAYA/LUCERNE and YOSEMITE/RHINE) differed regarding the number of initial monthly injections and dose adjustment criteria [15, 16, 21, 23]. In PHOTON and PULSAR, participants received three initial monthly doses of aflibercept 8 mg, whilst in TENAYA/LUCERNE and YOSEMITE/RHINE, participants received four initial monthly doses of faricimab. This will have contributed to the higher year 1 injection number in the faricimab trials compared with the aflibercept 8 mg trials. With regards to dose adjustment criteria, a key difference between the trials that likely influenced the number of injections observed was the maximum dose interval extension possible in the aflibercept 8-mg trials (up to 24 weeks in year 2) compared with the faricimab T&E trials (up to 16 weeks). The faricimab trials also had a longer duration than the aflibercept trials (100–112 weeks vs. 96 weeks), which may have increased the number of injections observed. There is also limited generalizability to real-world settings, as cost estimates were based on dosing regimens and mean numbers of injections applied in randomized controlled trials, which may differ from those applied to broader and more diverse patient populations in the real world. This analysis assumes these services are charged concurrently and thus there is potential variability for the actual exam costs. Furthermore, this analysis was limited by the available clinical trial data (up to 2 years of treatment) and the assumption that the mean number of injections observed in the second year of the aflibercept 8-mg and faricimab trials does not account for possible loss of patient follow-up or treatment adherence in the real world. Potential changes in drug and transportation costs, as well as average wages, were not accounted for in any of the scenario analyses, which may disproportionally impact analyses with longer time periods. Finally, the applied data from clinical trials involved only one treated eye, which may not necessarily be aligned with clinical practice, where patients with nAMD or DME are often treated bilaterally. Further studies are needed to validate these findings and assess their applicability to clinical practice.

Conclusions

Economic modeling suggests that the reduced frequency of dosing with aflibercept 8 mg versus faricimab achieved in clinical trials would translate to lower direct and indirect economic costs, with the greatest savings for patients with DME in comparison to those with nAMD. Total cost benefits with aflibercept 8 mg were seen from year 1 for DME and from year 2 for nAMD, and were maintained over 5 years. The reduced cost burden with aflibercept 8 mg may benefit patients, healthcare providers, and payers.
