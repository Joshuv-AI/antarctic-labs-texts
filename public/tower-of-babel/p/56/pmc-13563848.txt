Heterogeneity in symptom burden and supportive care needs among older adults with ischemic stroke: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Hao Bai, Yating Zhan, Lingqiao Song, Zeyuan Long, Longhan Zhang, Yiting Pan, An Luo, Qian He, Jiaxin Duan, Haiyan Yang, Li Liao, Rong Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOlder adults with ischemic stroke often experience multiple coexisting symptoms during recovery, which may lead to substantial supportive care needs. However, symptom burden may vary across individuals, and its heterogeneity and relationship with supportive care needs remain unclear. This study aimed to identify latent profiles of symptom burden among older adults with ischemic stroke and to examine differences in supportive care needs across these profiles.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 388 older adults with ischemic stroke recruited from the neurology departments of three tertiary hospitals in Hengyang, Hunan Province, China, between November 2025 and March 2026. Symptom burden was assessed using the Symptom Burden Scale for Stroke Survivors, and supportive care needs were measured using the Stroke Supportive Care Needs Scale. Latent profile analysis was used to identify symptom-burden subgroups. The Bolck-Croon-Hagenaars method was applied to compare supportive care needs across profiles, and multinomial logistic regression was performed to examine factors associated with profile membership.ResultsThree latent profiles of symptom burden were identified: low symptom burden (31.7%), moderate symptom burden (37.9%), and high symptom burden-physical dysfunction (30.4%). Supportive care needs differed significantly across the three profiles and generally increased from the low symptom burden group to the high symptom burden-physical dysfunction group. Patients in the high symptom burden-physical dysfunction group had greater needs in daily life, symptom management and care, rehabilitation nursing, and psychological support. Older age was associated with a higher likelihood of belonging to the more symptomatic profiles. In contrast, female sex and lower monthly income were associated with a higher likelihood of belonging to the high symptom burden–physical dysfunction group.ConclusionsOlder adults with ischemic stroke in the convalescent phase exhibited heterogeneous symptom burden that could be classified into three distinct latent profiles. These profiles were cross-sectionally associated with different patterns of supportive care needs. These findings provide preliminary profile-based evidence for understanding symptom burden heterogeneity and may help inform the prioritization of supportive care needs among older stroke survivors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12877-026-07965-y.Keywords: Ischemic stroke, Older adults, Symptom burden, Latent profile analysis, Supportive care needs

Introduction

Stroke remains a major public health concern and is among the leading causes of disability and dependency globally [1]. Ischemic stroke constitutes the majority of all stroke cases, with older adults being disproportionately affected [2, 3]. Age-related physiological decline, the presence of multiple chronic conditions, and limited functional reserve contribute to greater challenges in recovery and rehabilitation among this population [4]. Although advances in acute stroke management have improved survival rates [5, 6], many older survivors continue to experience persistent physical symptoms, emotional distress, and functional impairments in the post-acute phase [7]. These ongoing issues not only compromise quality of life and rehabilitation outcomes but also impose substantial burdens on caregivers and healthcare systems [8]. Consequently, optimizing symptom management and long-term supportive care for older adults with ischemic stroke remains a critical priority in nursing practice [9].

Symptom burden refers to the overall impact of multiple symptoms, including their frequency, severity, and associated distress [10]. Older patients with ischemic stroke commonly experience a range of coexisting symptoms, such as pain, fatigue, sleep disturbance, anxiety, depression, and impaired physical functioning [11]. These symptoms often interact with one another and may collectively hinder participation in rehabilitation, self-management, and daily functioning [12]. From a nursing perspective, a high symptom burden also increases care complexity and may require more comprehensive assessment and intervention [13]. However, symptom burdens are not uniform among patients [14]. Traditional variable-centered approaches often focus on individual symptoms or average scores across the entire sample and may fail to identify meaningful subgroups of patients with distinct symptom patterns. As a person-centered statistical approach, latent profile analysis (LPA) can help identify hidden subgroups based on similarities in symptom burden, thereby providing a more precise basis for individualized nursing care [15].

In addition to symptom burden, supportive care needs are another major concern during stroke recovery. Supportive care needs refer to patients’ perceived needs for assistance and support across various domains, such as daily life, symptom management, information, emotional adjustment, rehabilitation, and social support [16]. Older patients with ischemic stroke often have complex and ongoing care needs because of prolonged recovery, functional impairment, and reduced self-care ability [17]. Unmet supportive care needs may hinder adaptation to illness, participation in rehabilitation, and optimal recovery outcomes. Nurses play a central role in assessing and addressing patients’ multidimensional needs across the continuum of care, making a clear understanding of supportive care needs essential to practice [18]. Evidence indicates that patients with differing symptom burdens may also vary in their supportive care needs [19–21]. However, little is known about the heterogeneity of symptom burden among older adults with ischemic stroke, and evidence regarding how different symptom burden profiles relate to supportive care needs remains limited.

Therefore, it is necessary to explore symptom burden from a person-centered perspective and further examine differences in supportive care needs among patients with different symptom burden profiles. This study aimed to: (1) identify latent profiles of symptom burden among older patients with ischemic stroke; (2) explore factors associated with profile membership; and (3) examine differences in supportive care needs among these profiles. The findings may provide descriptive evidence for identifying symptom burden subgroups and understanding differences in supportive care needs among older adults with ischemic stroke.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study recruited participants using convenience sampling from the neurology departments of three tertiary Grade A hospitals affiliated with the University of South China in Hengyang, Hunan Province, China, between November 2025 and March 2026. This study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement for cross-sectional studies (Supplementary Table 1).

Eligible participants met the following criteria: (1) age ≥ 60 years; (2) first-onset ischemic stroke confirmed by CT or MRI in accordance with the Chinese Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Acute Ischemic Stroke [22]; (3) being in the recovery phase (15 days to 6 months) with stable vital signs [23]; (4) able to understand and respond to the questionnaire, with assistance if needed; and (5) provision of written informed consent.

Exclusion criteria were: (1) severe hepatic or renal insufficiency, malignant tumors, or other serious comorbidities; and (2) severe cognitive impairment or a documented history of major psychiatric disorders that would prevent participants from understanding the questionnaire or providing reliable responses.

The sample size was estimated according to the requirements of LPA and multivariable analysis. Previous studies have suggested that at least 300 participants are generally recommended for stable classification in LPA [24]. In addition, multivariable analysis commonly requires a sample size of 5–10 times the number of candidate independent variables [25]. Given that 12 candidate independent variables were included, the minimum sample size required for multivariable analysis was 60–120 participants. As the LPA required a larger sample, the final sample size was determined primarily by that requirement. After considering a 10%–20% rate of incomplete or invalid questionnaires, at least 330–375 participants were required. A total of 388 participants were ultimately included.

Measures

General demographic and clinical characteristics

Participants’ demographic and clinical information were collected using a self-designed questionnaire (Supplementary Fig. 1). Demographic variables included age, sex, educational level, marital status, monthly income, place of residence, and living arrangement. Clinical and health-related variables included body mass index (BMI), regular exercise, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and time after stroke. BMI was calculated from height and weight. These 12 variables were selected as prespecified covariates for the primary analyses based on clinical relevance, prior evidence, and avoidance of redundant or outcome-proximal variables.

Age, BMI, and time after stroke were treated as continuous variables. Time after stroke was defined as the number of days between stroke onset and questionnaire completion. Sex was classified as male or female. Educational level was classified as primary school and below, junior/high school, and above high school. Marital status was classified as married or unmarried/divorced/widowed. Monthly income was classified as < 4000 RMB, 4000–5999 RMB, and ≥ 6000 RMB. The place of residence was classified as rural or urban. Living arrangement was classified as living with family or other household members, living in a nursing home, or living alone. Physical exercise was assessed by asking participants whether they engaged in physical activity for at least 30 min at least once per week, such as walking, rehabilitation exercise, or other exercise activities. Diabetes mellitus and hypertension were recorded based on participants’ self-reported previous diagnoses and diagnoses documented in the medical records.

Symptom burden scale for stroke survivors

Symptom burden was assessed using the Symptom Burden Scale for Stroke Survivors, developed by Shi and colleagues [26] for use in stroke populations. The scale contains 19 items across six dimensions: physical symptoms and decline in daily living ability, cognitive decline, psychological symptoms, pain, gait abnormality, and slurred speech/fatigue. Each symptom is evaluated in terms of frequency, severity, and distress. For symptoms that were present, frequency and severity were rated on a 4-point Likert scale (1–4), and distress was rated on a 5-point scale (0–4). If a symptom was absent, the item was scored as 0. For each symptom, the frequency, severity, and distress scores were averaged to generate a single-item score, and total symptom burden was calculated by summing the scores across all 19 items, with higher scores indicating greater burden. The scale has demonstrated good reliability and validity in stroke populations, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.810 in the original study and 0.97 in the current sample.

Supportive care needs scale

Supportive care needs were measured using the Stroke Supportive Care Needs Scale, developed by Yu and colleagues [27]. The questionnaire includes 34 items across six domains. Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale from 0 (“not at all”) to 4 (“extremely needy”). Total and domain scores are obtained by summing item responses, with higher scores indicating greater supportive care needs. Previous studies have reported good psychometric properties, with a total Cronbach’s alpha of 0.937; in the current sample, Cronbach’s alpha was 0.881.

Pilot study and data collection

Data were collected in the neurology departments of three tertiary Grade A hospitals affiliated with the University of South China in Hengyang, Hunan Province, China. Before the formal survey, a pilot study was conducted in November 2025. Using convenience sampling and the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, 20 older adults with ischemic stroke were recruited to assess the comprehensibility and feasibility of the study instruments. Pilot participants were not included in the final sample. Based on their feedback, the wording, order, and structure of the questionnaires were revised and optimized.

During both the pilot study and the formal survey, researchers explained the study’s purpose, procedures, and confidentiality measures and obtained written informed consent. Participants were asked to complete the questionnaires independently whenever possible. For participants who had difficulty completing the questionnaires independently due to poor vision, limited literacy, writing difficulties, limb weakness, or fatigue, trained investigators explained the items in clear, plain language. They recorded participants’ responses without suggesting or leading them. The questionnaires were completed based on participants’ own responses. Completed questionnaires were checked on site, and any missing responses were clarified immediately with participants before the questionnaires were finalized. All data were entered into a secure database and double-checked for accuracy. After on-site checks and clarifications, no item-level data were missing in the final dataset. All 388 participants had complete data for the variables used in the main analyses. Therefore, no imputation was performed, and no participants were excluded from the analyses because of missing data.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were conducted using Mplus version 8.3 and R version 4.5.1. Continuous variables were described as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Because the main study variables were collected via self-report questionnaires, common method bias was assessed using Harman’s single-factor test [28]. This test examines whether a single factor accounts for a substantial proportion of the covariance among measured variables, which may indicate potential bias attributable to the measurement method rather than the constructs of interest.

LPA was performed to identify symptom burden profiles among older adults with ischemic stroke. LPA is a person-centered, model-based approach used to identify unobserved subgroups of individuals with similar response patterns across continuous indicators [15]. In this study, participants were classified according to their symptom burden dimension scores. Models with one to five latent profiles were estimated. Model selection followed published recommendations for mixture modeling and considered model fit, classification quality, class size, parsimony, and clinical interpretability [29, 30]. Lower Akaike information criterion (AIC), Bayesian information criterion (BIC), and adjusted Bayesian information criterion (aBIC) values indicated better relative fit, while significant Lo-Mendell–Rubin likelihood ratio test (LMR-LRT) and bootstrap likelihood ratio test (BLRT) results indicated that a k-profile model fitted better than a k − 1 profile model. Entropy and average posterior probabilities assessed classification quality. Adequate class size, parsimony, and clinical interpretability helped avoid over-extracted or clinically nonmeaningful solutions. The final model was selected by balancing these criteria.

Differences in total supportive care needs and domain-specific scores across latent profiles were examined using the Bolck-Croon-Hagenaars (BCH) method, with supportive care needs treated as distal outcomes. The BCH method is a three-step approach for comparing distal outcomes across latent profiles while accounting for classification error [31]. To assess the potential influence of conceptual overlap between functional symptom indicators and function-related supportive care needs domains, a sensitivity analysis was conducted by calculating a modified supportive care needs score after excluding the daily life and rehabilitation nursing domains.

After the optimal latent profile model was selected, the most likely profile membership was used for descriptive comparisons and covariate analysis. Group differences in participant characteristics across latent profiles were examined using one-way analysis of variance for continuous variables and the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate. Multinomial logistic regression was then used to examine factors associated with membership in latent profiles, with Class 1 as the reference group. All candidate variables were entered into the model simultaneously. Multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs). Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. All tests were two-sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant guidelines and regulations. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the University of South China (approval number: 2025NHHL140). All participants were informed of the study purpose and procedures, and participated voluntarily. Written informed consent was obtained before data collection. Participants’ privacy and confidentiality were strictly maintained, and all data were anonymized and used solely for research purposes.

Assessment of common method bias

Harman’s single-factor test indicated that the first principal factor explained 21.24% of the total variance, suggesting that common method bias was not substantial.

Latent profile analysis

Latent profile models with one to five classes were fitted to determine the optimal classification of symptom burden (Table 2). Although the AIC, BIC, and adjusted BIC values decreased with the addition of classes, the four-class and five-class solutions were not supported by the LMR-LRT (p = 0.1915 and p = 0.4085, respectively), indicating that these models did not significantly improve fit over the three-class solution. In the four-class model, class proportions ranged from 19.8% to 31.7%, but no clearly distinct or clinically more meaningful subgroup emerged. In the five-class model, one subgroup was relatively small (n = 30, 7.7%), suggesting possible overextraction and limited interpretability. Entropy remained high across the two- to five-class solutions, with no substantial improvement beyond the three-class model. Although the BLRT remained significant for all models, consideration of the non-significant LMR-LRT results for the four- and five-class solutions, the small class size in the five-class model, and the principles of parsimony and clinical interpretability supported retaining the three-class solution.

This finding provided additional support for the robustness of the main results.

For the multinomial logistic regression analysis, Class 1 was used as the reference category. Living arrangement was dichotomized as living with family or other household members versus living alone or in a nursing home, because the nursing-home subgroup was small and retaining three categories could have resulted in sparse cells and unstable estimates. Educational level and monthly income were treated as ordinal variables, and age, BMI, and time after stroke were entered as continuous variables. The remaining covariates were entered according to the categories described in the Methods section.

Discussion

This study identified three distinct latent profiles of symptom burden among older adults with ischemic stroke during the recovery phase: a low symptom burden group, a moderate symptom burden group, and a high symptom burden-physical dysfunction group. The high symptom burden-physical dysfunction group was marked by prominent physical dysfunction, especially limited limb mobility, limb weakness, poor coordination, impaired balance, fatigue, and reduced self-care ability. Supportive care needs also differed significantly across the three profiles. Total supportive care needs, as well as needs related to daily life, symptom management and care, and rehabilitation nursing, increased progressively from the low burden group to the high symptom burden-physical dysfunction group. By contrast, information needs were highest in the moderate burden group, psychological needs were greater in the high symptom burden-physical dysfunction group, and social needs did not differ significantly across profiles. In addition, older age was associated with a higher likelihood of belonging to both the moderate burden group and the high symptom burden-physical dysfunction group. In contrast, female sex and lower monthly income were associated with membership in the high symptom burden-physical dysfunction group. Taken together, these findings suggest that symptom burden among older adults with ischemic stroke is heterogeneous and cross-sectionally associated with differences in supportive care needs and patient characteristics.

The three-profile solution highlights the substantial heterogeneity of symptom burden among older adults with ischemic stroke during the recovery phase. Rather than following a single recovery pattern, patients were classified into clinically meaningful subgroups with distinct levels and patterns of symptom burden [32]. Notably, the high symptom burden-physical dysfunction profile suggests that symptom burden in this population is driven mainly by persistent motor impairment and loss of functional independence [33]. This pattern is clinically plausible, as older adults often have reduced physiological reserve [34], multimorbidity [35], and slower rehabilitation progress [36], all of which may increase the overall impact of stroke-related symptoms. It is also noteworthy that depressed mood was among the most prevalent symptoms in the overall sample, suggesting that emotional symptoms remain common even when physical dysfunction is the main feature distinguishing the high symptom burden-physical dysfunction group.

The stepwise increase in total supportive care needs, as well as in daily life, symptom management and care, and rehabilitation nursing needs, further suggests that a heavier symptom burden is accompanied by broader and more intensive care demands [37, 38]. At the same time, the domain-specific findings indicate that different aspects of supportive care may reflect distinct underlying processes [39]. The higher information needs in the moderate burden group may reflect a stage at which patients are stable enough to seek information but still uncertain about recovery and self-management [40, 41]. This finding also suggests that the moderate burden group may represent a distinct subgroup rather than simply an intermediate category between the low burden group and the high burden-physical dysfunction group. By contrast, the greater psychological needs in the high symptom burden-physical dysfunction group suggest that emotional distress may become more prominent when physical dysfunction and dependence are more severe [42]. The lack of significant between-group differences in social needs may indicate that social support is a common concern among older stroke survivors and may depend more on caregiving arrangements and service context than on symptom burden alone [43, 44].

The multinomial regression findings further support the interpretation of these results by showing that symptom burden profiles were associated with demographic and socioeconomic characteristics. Although several variables differed across profiles in the univariate analyses, only age, sex, and monthly income remained significant in the multinomial regression model, suggesting that some between-group differences may be explained by shared variance among demographic and socioeconomic factors. Older age was linked to a greater likelihood of belonging to both the moderate burden group and the high symptom burden-physical dysfunction group, which may reflect reduced functional reserve, greater vulnerability to functional decline, and more barriers to rehabilitation participation after stroke [45, 46]. Female sex was associated with membership in the high symptom burden-physical dysfunction group, suggesting possible sex-related differences in symptom perception, recovery trajectory, psychosocial burden, caregiving context, or healthcare and rehabilitation use [47, 48]. In contrast, higher monthly income was associated with a lower likelihood of membership in the high symptom burden-physical dysfunction group. This may reflect broader socioeconomic resources that support access to rehabilitation services, caregiving assistance, and compensation for functional limitations [49, 50]. However, these potential pathways were not directly assessed in this study, and the associations between age, sex, and income and symptom burden profiles should therefore be interpreted with caution.

These findings suggest that symptom burden in older adults with ischemic stroke may be shaped not only by clinical factors but also by demographic and socioeconomic context. From a practice perspective, the present study does not imply that all older stroke survivors should be routinely classified using a new mandatory assessment tool. Rather, it highlights the potential value of structured symptom burden assessment in helping nurses identify patients with different supportive care priorities. In particular, patients in the high symptom burden–physical dysfunction group may require closer attention to daily life support, symptom management, rehabilitation nursing, and psychological support, whereas those in the moderate burden group may have relatively greater needs for information and self-management guidance. Older patients, women, and those with lower incomes may also warrant closer attention during supportive care assessment. Such profile-based information may serve as a reference for nurses when prioritizing supportive care needs and may facilitate communication with rehabilitation professionals and physicians. However, given the cross-sectional design, these findings should be used to inform assessment and care planning rather than to prescribe specific intervention pathways.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and does not allow determination of the temporal or directional relationship between symptom burden and supportive care needs. Second, participants were recruited via convenience sampling from three tertiary hospitals in China, which may limit the sample’s representativeness and the generalizability of the findings. Third, all data were collected using self-reported questionnaires and may therefore be subject to recall and response bias. Because participants were required to understand and respond to the questionnaires, the findings may not be directly generalizable to older stroke survivors with severe cognitive impairment or major psychiatric disorders. In addition, some stroke-related problems, such as continence difficulties, clinically diagnosed dementia, and aphasia, were not directly assessed by the symptom burden scale and may not have been fully captured in the present profiles. Potential conceptual overlap between functional symptom indicators and function-related supportive care needs domains should also be considered, although the sensitivity analysis supported the robustness of the main findings. Fourth, although the BCH approach accounted for classification error when comparing supportive care needs across profiles, the multinomial logistic regression analysis used most likely class assignment and did not apply a classification-error-adjusted three-step covariate approach. Therefore, classification uncertainty may not have been fully accounted for in the analysis of factors associated with profile membership. In addition, the events-per-variable ratio in the multinomial logistic regression model was borderline, particularly for the smallest latent profile, and the regression findings should therefore be interpreted with caution. Future longitudinal and multicentre studies are needed to more precisely record time since stroke, establish temporal precedence, examine the stability and transitions of symptom burden profiles over time, and further clarify their associations with supportive care needs.

Conclusion

Older adults with ischemic stroke in the convalescent phase exhibited heterogeneous symptom burden, which could be classified into distinct latent profiles: low symptom burden, moderate symptom burden, and high symptom burden-physical dysfunction. These profiles were cross-sectionally associated with different patterns of supportive care needs, with patients in the high symptom burden-physical dysfunction group reporting greater needs in daily life, symptom management and care, rehabilitation nursing, and psychological support. Older age, female sex, and lower monthly income were associated with a greater likelihood of belonging to more symptomatic profiles. These findings provide preliminary profile-based evidence for understanding heterogeneity in symptom burden and may help inform the prioritization of supportive care needs among older stroke survivors. Future longitudinal studies are needed to clarify the temporal and directional relationships between symptom burden and supportive care needs.
