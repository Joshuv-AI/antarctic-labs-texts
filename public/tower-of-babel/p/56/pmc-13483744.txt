Comparative effects of air-polishing and conventional prophylaxis methods on surface roughness and gloss of composite restorations
Authors: Savas Sagmak, Metin Bakir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo compare the effects of air-polishing and conventional prophylaxis methods on the surface roughness and gloss of composite restorations.MethodsSeventy composite resin specimens were prepared and randomly assigned to seven groups: control, rubber cup + pumice, brush + pumice, rubber cup + polishing paste, brush + polishing paste, Sylc bioactive glass powder, and sodium bicarbonate powder (n = 10). Surface roughness was measured using atomic force microscopy, surface morphology was evaluated by scanning electron microscopy, and gloss was measured using a glossmeter.ResultsThe highest surface roughness values were observed in the Sylc and sodium bicarbonate groups, whereas the lowest values were recorded in the control group. Conventional prophylaxis methods showed no significant differences compared with the control group, while air-polishing powders significantly increased surface roughness. Gloss values were highest in the control and pumice groups and lowest in the Sylc and sodium bicarbonate groups. A significant inverse relationship was observed between surface roughness and gloss. Air-polishing procedures significantly increased surface roughness and reduced gloss compared with conventional prophylaxis methods.ConclusionThese findings suggest that air-polishing may adversely affect the surface integrity and esthetic properties of composite restorations.

Introduction

Dental prophylaxis refers to the elimination of plaque, calculus, and stains from both visible and non-visible tooth surfaces through scaling and polishing procedures. Oral hygiene therapy is an indispensable component of periodontal treatment [1]. Even in individuals with a low susceptibility to periodontal disease and dental caries, biofilm and other dental deposits are periodically removed during routine professional care. Various methods are available for the removal of plaque and extrinsic stains. Dentists and dental hygienists primarily use two methods to eliminate plaque and stains from tooth surfaces. Traditional methods involve the application of polishing paste or pumice to tooth surfaces using rotating rubber cups or rotary bristle brushes, thereby removing plaque and stains. The performance of rubber cup and polishing paste systems is influenced by several factors, including the abrasiveness of the paste, the rotational speed of the instrument, the pressure applied during the procedure, and the duration of polishing [2, 3].

Air polishing is a practical and efficient technique for removing dental plaque biofilm, stains, and soft deposits compared with conventional methods such as rubber cups, hand instruments, and ultrasonic scalers. During the procedure, specially formulated powders, such as sodium bicarbonate or erythritol, are delivered to the tooth surface through a stream of compressed air and water, enabling effective cleaning of supragingival and subgingival biofilm, deep fissures, interproximal areas, and tooth surfaces before preventive or restorative procedures [4]. In addition to reducing operator fatigue and improving access to difficult-to-reach areas, air polishing has become widely used in clinical practice. However, conventional sodium bicarbonate particles, which may reach sizes of up to 250 μm, are relatively abrasive and may damage both dental hard tissues and restorative materials [5]. On composite resin surfaces, sodium bicarbonate air polishing has been shown to increase surface abrasion and roughness, potentially promoting bacterial adhesion, biofilm accumulation, gingival inflammation, and secondary caries [6, 7].

Concerns about the potential adverse effects of sodium bicarbonate have prompted the development of powders with lower abrasiveness [8]. In this regard, glycine-based powders were introduced in 2003, and roughly a decade later erythritol-based powders were subsequently developed [9]. In addition, sodium bicarbonate–based powders with reduced particle sizes of up to 40 μm have also been introduced. However, information regarding the effects of these newer materials on surfaces remains limited. Current studies indicate that glycine-based powders exhibit significantly lower abrasive effects on composite resins compared with sodium bicarbonate–based powders [10].

The surface characteristics of composite resins are influenced by the composition of the organic matrix and inorganic fillers, as well as the size, quantity, and distribution of filler particles and the clinical procedures applied to the restoration. Because the inorganic fillers are harder and more wear-resistant than the resin matrix, differential wear during finishing, polishing, or prophylaxis procedures may result in filler particle protrusion or dislodgement, creating surface irregularities [11]. Increased surface roughness promotes plaque accumulation, surface staining, and inflammation of the surrounding soft tissues, with a roughness threshold of approximately 0.2 μm considered critical for bacterial plaque retention [12, 13]. Moreover, surface roughness is closely associated with the optical properties of composite restorations. Smooth and glossy surfaces exhibit greater color stability, whereas increased roughness causes diffuse light reflection, resulting in reduced gloss and increased discoloration [14–16].

Although previous studies have investigated the effects of prophylaxis procedures on composite restorations, most have focused primarily on surface roughness or evaluated a limited number of prophylaxis techniques. Comprehensive studies simultaneously assessing both surface roughness and gloss following conventional and contemporary prophylaxis procedures remain limited. Therefore, further investigation is needed to provide a more comprehensive understanding of how different prophylaxis methods influence the surface properties of composite restorations.

Accordingly, the aim of this study was to evaluate and compare the effects of air-polishing and conventional prophylaxis procedures on the surface roughness and gloss of composite restorations. The null hypotheses of this study were as follows:

H01: Dental prophylaxis procedures do not affect the surface roughness of composite restorations.H02: Dental prophylaxis procedures do not affect the surface gloss of composite restorations.

Materials and methods

Study design

This was an in vitro study; therefore, ethical approval was not required because no human participants or animal specimens were involved. The sample size was determined based on an a priori power analysis performed using G*Power software. Considering a one-way ANOVA design with seven groups, a significance level of 0.05, and a statistical power of 80%, the minimum required sample size per group was calculated to detect a moderate effect size. Based on this analysis, a sample size of n = 10 per group was deemed sufficient. Specimens of a suprananohybrid light-cured composite resin (Asteria, Tokuyama, Japan) were fabricated using stainless steel molds measuring 10 mm in diameter and 2 mm in depth. The composite material was placed into the molds and light-cured for 10 s through polyester strips (Direkta Dental, Upplands Väsby, Sweden) using an LED curing unit (1200 mW/cm2 - Elipar, 3 M, USA). To ensure standardized polymerization conditions, the output light intensity of the LED curing device was monitored using a digital radiometer after every ten specimens (Bluephase Meter II, Ivoclar Vivadent, Liechtenstein). The composite specimens were polished using the Sof-Lex™ Pop-On polishing disc system (3 M Oral Care, St. Paul, MN, USA). The discs were applied sequentially using a low-speed contra-angle handpiece at 10,000 rpm for 5 s per disc under light intermittent pressure, in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations. After specimen preparation, the specimens were thoroughly mixed and randomly allocated into seven groups (n = 10 per group). Subsequently, six different dental prophylaxis procedures were applied (Table 1). The investigator performing the AFM, SEM, and gloss measurements was blinded to the group allocation throughout the measurement procedures.

Sylc bioactive glass / sodium bicarbonate

Air polishing procedures were performed by the same investigator using a standard device (AquaCare Single, Velopex, London, UK) at a pressure of 2.5 bar, according to the manufacturer’s recommended settings, with a separate powder chamber used for each powder. Care was taken to simulate clinical conditions as closely as possible. A standard air-polishing nozzle use was employed; the nozzle-to-surface distance was set at 3 mm, the angle between the nozzle and the specimen surface was adjusted to 45°, and the spraying time was electronically controlled and standardized to 5 s. During application, the nozzle was moved in a continuous sweeping motion with water spray, similar to clinical practice [17].

Rubber cup+ pumice / brush + pumice

Prophylaxis was performed on ten specimens using a rubber cup (Pro-Cup Soft, KerrHawe SA, Bioggio, Switzerland) and on another ten specimens using a brush in combination with pumice (Imicryl, Konya, Turkey). The pumice mixture was prepared by combining 30 g of pumice with 25 ml of water. A low-speed micromotor (Kavo Ltda., Joinville, Brazil) was used for the prophylaxis procedure. The application was carried out with circular motion throughout the entire surface with slight pressure limited to the weight of the micromotor (150 g), at a speed of 5000 rpm, and continuously for 5 seconds [18].

Rubber cup+ polishing paste / brush + polishing paste

Prophylaxis was performed on ten specimens using a rubber cup (Pro-Cup Soft, KerrHawe SA, Bioggio, Switzerland) and on another ten specimens using a brush in combination with a Plurapolish (Pluradent, Offenbach, Germany). A low-speed micromotor (Kavo Ltda., Joinville, Brazil) was used for the prophylaxis procedure. The application was carried out with circular motion throughout the entire surface with slight pressure limited to the weight of the micromotor (150 g), at a speed of 5000 rpm, and continuously for 5 seconds [18].

Surface roughness measurement

Relative height maps of the specimen surfaces, including both control and air-polished (AP) samples, were obtained in air using a commercial atomic force microscope (MFP-3D, Asylum Research, USA) operated in contact mode. AFM measurements were performed on all specimens in each experimental group (n = 10 per group). Surface images were acquired by scanning a 20 × 20 μm area at a scan rate of 1 Hz. The measurements were performed using probes with nominal spring constant and resonance frequency values of approximately 10 N/m and 250 kHz, respectively. Instrument calibration was performed prior to each measurement session.

SEM observation

From each experimental group, one specimen was randomly selected and evaluated using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (Hitachi SU-1500, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan) to assess surface morphological alterations, including microvoids, cracks, melting, and possible thermal damage. The dried specimens were then mounted on specimen stubs using double-sided carbon adhesive tape. Surface morphology was subsequently examined at magnifications of ×500, ×1,000, ×2,000, and ×5,000.

Gloss measurement

Surface gloss measurements were performed using a glossmeter (Micro-TRI-gloss, BYK-Gardner, Geretsried, Germany) with an effective measurement area of 9 × 15 mm and a 60° measurement geometry. Gloss values were recorded in gloss units (GU). Prior to the measurements, the device was calibrated before each testing session using the calibration standard provided by the manufacturer. Three measurements were taken from each specimen, after which the specimen was rotated by 90°, and three additional measurements were performed.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis of the obtained data was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics software (Version 26, IBM, NY, USA). Statistical significance was determined using a p-value threshold of 0.05 for all analyses. The normality of gloss and surface roughness data were evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test.

Surface roughness values were found to follow a normal distribution (p > 0.05). Intergroup differences were assessed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Homogeneity of variances was evaluated using the Levene test, which indicated that the variances were not homogeneous (p < 0.05). Therefore, pairwise group comparisons were carried out using the Games–Howell post-hoc test.

The gloss values were found not to follow a normal distribution (p < 0.05). Consequently, group comparisons were performed using the non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis test. When significant differences were detected, pairwise group comparisons were performed using the Dunn–Bonferroni post-hoc test.

Discussion

Dental prophylaxis procedures are routinely performed to maintain oral health; however, their effects on the surface characteristics of composite restorations remain a matter of concern. Therefore, this in vitro study evaluated the effects of four conventional and two contemporary prophylaxis techniques on the surface roughness and gloss of a resin composite. The results demonstrated that air-polishing procedures, particularly those using sodium bicarbonate and Sylc powders, produced significantly greater surface roughness and lower gloss values than conventional prophylaxis methods. Accordingly, both null hypotheses were rejected.

It is generally accepted that the smoothest surface of a composite material is obtained when it is polymerized against a matrix strip. However, in clinical practice, composite restorations are subjected to finishing and polishing procedures [19, 20]. Accordingly, in the present study, composite specimens that had undergone polishing with composite polishing discs were used as the control group. This approach increases the clinical relevance of the study, as it better simulates the surface condition of restorations encountered in daily practice rather than idealized laboratory surfaces.

Surface roughness is a clinically important parameter because it influences bacterial adhesion, plaque accumulation, patient comfort, and the esthetic longevity of composite restorations [21, 22]. An increase in surface roughness is generally associated with surface defects, and the abrasive potential of prophylaxis procedures is influenced by the size, hardness, and angularity of the abrasive particles [23].

The present study demonstrated that air-polishing procedures resulted in significantly higher surface roughness than conventional prophylaxis methods. In particular, sodium bicarbonate and Sylc powders produced significantly rougher surfaces than rubber cup and brush applications, whereas no significant difference was observed between the two air-polishing powders.

These findings are consistent with several previous studies demonstrating that air-polishing procedures increase the surface roughness of restorative materials compared with conventional polishing methods [24–28]. Kruse et al. [24] reported that polishing with a rubber cup and paste produced smoother surfaces than other prophylaxis procedures and suggested that an additional polishing step after air polishing may not further reduce surface roughness. Similarly, Olszowska et al. [25] observed increased surface roughness after air polishing with sodium bicarbonate, glycine, and erythritol powders, with sodium bicarbonate producing the greatest surface damage, although the differences among the powders were not statistically significant. Goes et al. [26] also demonstrated that sodium bicarbonate resulted in significantly greater surface roughness than glycine on both nanohybrid and nanofilled composites. Furthermore, Salerno et al. [27] showed that surface roughness was influenced not only by the type of abrasive powder but also by application parameters such as exposure time and spraying distance, with glycine producing smoother surfaces under specific conditions. Likewise, Biazussi et al. [28] reported that glycine powder caused significantly lower surface roughness than sodium bicarbonate during air-polishing procedures, supporting the concept that the abrasive characteristics of the powder play a critical role in determining the extent of surface alterations.

The greater surface roughness observed after air-polishing may be explained by the abrasive characteristics of the powders used, including their particle size, hardness, shape, and the kinetic energy generated during application. These factors may promote disruption of the resin matrix and dislodgement of filler particles, resulting in increased surface irregularities. Consistent with this mechanism, the present study demonstrated that air-polishing procedures produced significantly greater surface roughness than conventional prophylaxis methods. Although no significant difference was observed between the sodium bicarbonate and Sylc powders, both air-polishing powders resulted in significantly rougher surfaces than the conventional techniques, leading to rejection of the first null hypothesis. These findings suggest that even low-abrasive air-polishing powders may adversely affect the surface integrity of composite restorations and should therefore be used with caution, particularly in patients with existing composite restorations.

Surface gloss is closely related to the optical quality and esthetic appearance of composite restorations. According to the American Dental Association (ADA) professional product review, gloss values between 40 and 60 GU are generally considered clinically acceptable [29, 30]. Previous studies have consistently demonstrated an inverse relationship between surface roughness and gloss, indicating that increased surface irregularities promote diffuse light scattering and consequently reduce surface gloss [31–34]. Dennis et al. [32] reported a significant negative correlation between surface roughness and gloss across different composite materials, whereas Carvalho et al. [33] and Lai et al. [34] similarly observed that mechanical and chemical challenges increased surface roughness while significantly reducing gloss values. These findings suggest that disruption of the resin matrix and exposure or loss of filler particles alter the surface topography, thereby impairing the regular reflection of incident light.

Consistent with these reports, the highest gloss values in the present study were observed in the Control, RC-PU, and BR-PU groups, whereas the sodium bicarbonate and Sylc groups exhibited the lowest gloss values. The significant reduction in gloss observed in the air-polishing groups is likely attributable to the increased surface roughness and the resulting diffuse reflection of light. In addition, the gloss values obtained in the present study remained below the clinically acceptable range (40–60 GU) reported by the ADA. This may be related to the intrinsic characteristics of the tested composite resin, including its filler composition, filler size, and resin matrix, as well as the finishing, polishing, and prophylaxis procedures applied before gloss evaluation. Furthermore, gloss values are material-dependent and may vary considerably according to the restorative material and polishing protocol used. Therefore, direct comparison with the ADA reference values should be interpreted with caution. Accordingly, the second null hypothesis was rejected. These findings further support the concept that preservation of surface integrity is essential for maintaining the optical properties and long-term esthetic performance of composite restorations. Consequently, the selection of prophylaxis techniques should take into account not only plaque removal efficacy but also their potential effects on the surface quality of restorative materials.

These findings suggest that the choice of prophylaxis technique may influence the surface integrity and esthetic longevity of composite restorations. Therefore, clinicians should carefully consider the potential surface-altering effects of air-polishing procedures, particularly in patients with existing composite restorations.
