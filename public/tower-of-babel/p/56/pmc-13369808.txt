Development of HAUP-based method for measuring simultaneously linear birefringence and dichroism near diagonal position in anisotropic media
Authors: Kun Zhang, Komei Okano, Toru Asahi, Kenta Nakagawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOptical anisotropy is a fundamental spectroscopic property of condensed matters and is crucial for understanding their electronic structures, molecular orientations, and symmetry. Among its aspects, linear birefringence (LB) and linear dichroism (LD) are important because they reflect differences in refractive indices and absorption coefficients for orthogonally polarized light components. The high-accuracy universal polarimeter (HAUP) method is widely used for measuring LB and LD because of its high sensitivity. The conventional HAUP measurement principle is based on the extinction position, where the transmitted light intensity is minimized. However, such measurement becomes challenging for strongly absorbing samples or in short-wavelength regions with weak light intensity. To overcome these limitations, we have developed a HAUP measurement principle based on the diagonal position, where the transmitted light intensity is maximized. The measurement equations are expanded around the diagonal position, giving a different equation structure from the extinction-position method. This configuration simplifies the measurement procedure, eliminates two-dimensional measurements and eliminates systematic errors such as parasitic ellipticities of the polarizer and analyzer. We have applied this measurement principle to MgF2, and a strongly absorbing high-temperature cuprate superconductor, a Bi2Sr2CaCu2O8+δ crystals. Introduction

Optical anisotropy is a fundamental spectroscopic property of condensed matter1 and plays a key role in the development of optical materials,2 thin films with preferred orientations,3 anisotropy-based bioimaging,4 and analyses of molecular arrangements.5 The measurement of optical anisotropy in condensed matters is crucial to understand their electronic structures,6 molecular orientations,7 and symmetrical properties.8 Among the various manifestations of optical anisotropy,9 linear birefringence (LB) and linear dichroism (LD) are important,10 as they reflect differences in refractive indices and absorption coefficients between two orthogonally linearly polarized light transmitted through them as eigen polarizations.

The LB and LD of condensed matter are determined using several experimental methods, including the interferometric,11 compensatory,12 channeled spectrum, polarizing ellipse13 and refractometric methods14, ellisometers,15,16 and polarized light microscopy.17 These methods have distinct characteristics and advantages, but accurate measurements might become difficult when anisotropic matters have lower symmetry as monoclinic or triclinic crystals because of their optical indicatrix rotation. The high-accuracy universal polarimeter (HAUP) method18–20 widely used to measure LB and LD accurately in various anisotropic condensed matters including the lower symmetry crystals, which have attracted attention in chemistry,21–23 physics,24–26 and life sciences.27 The characteristic feature in the HAUP method is to offers highly precise and accurate measurements for chiroptical properties such as circular birefringence (CB, usually called optical activity) and circular dichroism (CD) in anisotropic condensed matters by evaluating and eliminating systematic errors in optical elements.

In HAUP measurements, the intensity of light transmitted through a linear polarizer (P), a sample (S), and a linear analyzer (A) is measured as a function of the polarizer and analyzer rotation angles. The HAUP measurement principle has been constructed based on the extinction position, in which the optical configuration of P, S, and A is set near conditions that minimizes the transmitted light intensity when the crossed polars P and A rotate simultaneously for the anisotropic sample in between P and A (Fig. 1). Here, the important point is that the HAUP method is different from ceonventional rotating polarimetric or ellisometric methods, in which a rotational intensity curve over a wide angular region is measured and analyzed by fitting procedures. In typical rotating-element polarimetric or ellipsometric methods, the polarizer and/or analyzer are rotated over a wide angular region to obtain the transmitted intensity as a function of the azimuth angles, i.e., the rotational intensity curve. The optical parameters are then determined by fitting the measured rotational intensity curve to the measurement principle equations.15 In ellipsometry, the ellipsometric parameters Ψ(λ) and Δ(λ), which are defined by the ratio of the Fresnel reflection coefficients for the incident and reflected light, are measured. The optical parameters such as the refractive index and extinction coefficient are obtained by fitting the wavelength dependences of Ψ(λ) and Δ(λ) to appropriate optical dispersion models such as the Sellmeier, Lorentz, or Drude models.28 Because these analysis require assumptions about both the dispersion model and the structural model such as multilayer models, the obtained optical parameters may depend on the choice of the models adopted in the fitting procedures.

Schematic representation of extinction and diagonal positions of optical elements; a linear polarizer, a sample, and a linear analyzer, of HAUP method and the transmitted light intensity from simple HAUP optical configuration. The origin of azimuth angle of the sample (or the rotation angle of the sample between the polarizer and the analyzer, which are set in a crossed-Nicols configuration) is rotated by ± 45° from the extinction position.

In contrast, the HAUP method does not analyze the entire rotational intensity curve. Instead, transmitted light intensity is measured only within a limited local angular region of a few degrees around the extinction position. Owing to this local expansion, higher-order terms can be neglected, which simplifies the expressions of the measurement principle equations and reduces the number of fitting parameters. As a result, the optical parameters at the wavelength of interest can be obtained directly without any model fitting. In addition, the total phase difference Δ and total LD E (corresponding to LB and LD, respectively) can be determined directly and simultaneously.

However, the previous HAUP measurement is much difficult in wavelength regions where the sample exhibits strong absorption or in short-wavelength regions where the incident light intensity from an Xe lamp is weak25,26.

To address this issue, we have developed a HAUP measurement principle based on the diagonal position, where the P-S-A optical configuration is set near the condition that maximizes the transmitted light intensity (Fig. 1). This enables HAUP measurement at wavelengths that are challenging for the conventional principle (based on the extinction position). The azimuth angle of the sample is rotated by ± 45° from the extinction position. It should be noted that this ± 45° rotation is not merely a coordinate offset from the extinction position, but rather changes the expansion point of the measurement principle equations, resulting in a fundamentally different equation structure. As pointed out by Dijkstra et al.29, it is not necessary to align the fast or slow axis of the sample with the laboratory coordinate system in advance, and this premise holds equally in the diagonal-position method proposed here. Using Jones vectors and the Jones matrix,30 we derive the LB and LD measurement principle near the diagonal position.

The objective of this study is to establish the theoretical framework of the diagonal-position method and to experimentally verify its validity. We demonstrate a new optical measurement principle of LB and LD using the HAUP and show experimental results for LB and LD in a well-known uniaxial crystal, MgF2, and a strongly absorbing high-temperature cuprate superconductor, a Bi2Sr2CaCu2O8+δ (Bi2212) crystal,25 with the new principle.

HAUP measurement principle based on diagonal position

We briefly outline the principles of the HAUP method and then derive a new HAUP measurement principle based on the diagonal position.18–20 The HAUP method involves a simple optical setup consisting of only two optical elements, P and A. The P and A axes are set in the crossed-Nicols configuration, and a light beam passes sequentially through P, S, and A. This simple configuration means systematic errors can be quantitatively evaluated and eliminated, such as the parasitic ellipticities of P and A (denoted by p and q, respectively) and the small angular error due to the displacement in the crossed-Nicols configuration (δ). 31

LB measurement of MgF2 crystal

We selected single-crystal MgF2 as a sample to demonstrate LB measurement using the diagonal position method. MgF2 is a uniaxial and achiral (optically inactive) crystal that belongs to the space group P42/mnm and is transparent in the ultraviolet–visible (UV–vis) region. We prepared a 58-µm-thick (100) plate of single-crystal MgF2 through polishing.

The first experimental procedure in the diagonal position method is determining the diagonal position θM at a specific temperature, sample thickness, and light wavelength. With the P and A axes set in the crossed-Nicols configuration, the transmitted light intensity I is measured at various azimuth angles. Based on these measurements, the diagonal position θM is precisely determined. Here, the new reading system of θ, whose origin is replaced by θM, is established—i.e., θ = 0 is defined at this θM.

Compared with the extinction position method, the proposed method offers the following advantages in Δ and E measurement. (1) Determining only the values of J″13, J″21, and J″31 is sufficient. Two-dimensional measurements, in which both θ and are varied, are unnecessary. In the diagonal position method, measuring I as a function of θ with fixed at 0 (crossed-Nicols configuration) and as a function of with θ fixed at 0 (rotating only the azimuth of A) is adequate. (2) In the extinction position method, without two-dimensional measurements, E = 0 should be assumed to determine Δ. To be emphasized, the diagonal position method enables the determination of both Δ and E without requiring this assumption.

LB and LD measurement of high-temperature cuprate superconductor: Bi2Sr2CaCu2O8+δ crystal

We utilized a high-temperature cuprate superconductor, the single-crystal Bi2212, as a sample to demonstrate the effectiveness of the diagonal position method to measure LB and LD. Bi2212 is a biaxial, achiral (optically inactive) crystal that belongs to the point group D2h and exhibits strong absorption throughout the UV–vis region. The importance of transmission measurements for single-crystal Bi2212 has been studied.25,26 Despite the nearly identical lattice constants along the a and b axes of Bi2212, which suggest that the optical anisotropy should be small, large LB and LD peaks along the c axis have been observed.25 Furthermore, incommensurate modulation has been observed along the b axis, which is considered to cause the large LB and LD.26 Thus, transmission measurements using ultrathin plate specimens, which allow UV–vis light to pass through the crystal, can effectively elucidate the origin of such strong optical anisotropy. However, ultrathin plate specimens that allow UV–vis light to pass through the crystal are generally difficult to obtain through polishing or exfoliation. In particular, measuring intensity signals precisely near the extinction position, where the transmitted light intensity is minimized, in the strong absorption region, would become almost impossible. Therefore, for such cases, even thinner specimens are required, whose preparation is ultimately difficult and time consuming. The diagonal position method offers a significant advantage in this respect, as it enables higher transmission intensity signals to be detected even without ultrathin specimens.

Single crystals of Bi2212 were grown using the traveling-solvent floating-zone method. We prepared a 190-nm-thick (001) plate of Bi2212 through exfoliation. Following the procedure used for MgF2, we measured LB and LD via the diagonal position method. Conclusion

In this study, we have developed a new LB and LD measurement principle for the HAUP method based on the diagonal position, in which the optical configuration is set near the condition that maximizes the transmitted light intensity. This method is advantageous for measurements at wavelengths where the sample exhibits strong absorption or in short-wavelength regions where the light intensity from the lamp is weak. We have applied this measurement principle to a well-known uniaxial crystal, MgF2, and a strongly absorbing high-temperature cuprate superconductor, Bi2212. The LB and LD values measured using the proposed diagonal position method were almost identical to those obtained using the conventional extinction position method. Considering that the accuracy of LB measurements using compensator or polarimetric or ellipsometric methods are generally on the order of about several percent33–35, the results obtained in this study confirm that the measurements were performed with sufficiently high precision. This confirmation of the agreement between the results obtained using the proposed method and those obtained using the conventional extinction-position method was an important validation step in this study. Furthermore, in the diagonal-position method proposed in this study, the measurement principle equations are expanded around the diagonal position where the transmitted intensity is close to its maximum, resulting in a different structure from that of the extinction-position method. This confirms that the diagonal-position method is not a mere coordinate offset of the extinction-position method, but represents a distinct optical configuration with a different expansion point. This difference leads to changes in the angular dependence of the transmitted intensity and in the sensitivity distribution with respect to the optical parameters, while also enabling the elimination of systematic errors. Moreover, the measurement principle equations of the diagonal-position method show that LB and LD, which required two-dimensional measurements in the extinction-position method, can be determined using a simpler measurement procedure. This provides a practical advantage in reducing the measurement time.

The HAUP method enables the simultaneous measurement of LB, LD, optical activity, and circular dichroism in various anisotropic condensed materials. Remarkably, even when systematic errors, such as the parasitic ellipticities of the polarizer and the analyzer, were assumed to coexist, these irrelevant parameters became negligible under the approximation in the diagonal position method. Thus, the proposed method is advantageous for the measurement of chiral optical properties by reducing the systematic errors that must be considered. We plan to apply this new method to chiral (optically active) and strongly absorbing condensed materials in the near future.
