Assessment of heavy metal(oid) pollution and related health risks in agricultural soils surrounding a coal gangue dump from an abandoned coal mine in Chongqing, Southwest China
Authors: Maochao Zhang, Liusan Cheng, Zhihui Yue, Lihua Peng, Lin Xiao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe coal gangue dump may introduce heavy metal(oid)s (HMs) into surrounding agricultural soils, posing potential health risks to nearby communities. This study evaluated heavy metal(oid) pollution in agricultural soils adjacent to a gangue dump at an abandoned coal mine in Chongqing, Southwest China. The concentrations of HMs (As, Cd, Cr, Cu, Ni, Pb, and Zn) were quantified using ICP-MS, and the contamination status was assessed using the Geoaccumulation Index (Igeo), Contamination Factor (CF), Pollution Load Index (PLI), and Potential Ecological Risk Index (RI). Heavy metal(oid) contamination was detected in soils across a depth of 0–30 cm, particularly pronounced in the topsoil layer (0–10 cm and 10–20 cm depths). Cu emerged as the predominant contaminant across all examined depths, with average Igeo values of 1.20, 1.21, and 1.16 for the 0–10 cm, 10–20 cm, and 20–30 cm depths, respectively, indicating moderate contamination. The CF for Cu was 3.55, 3.55, and 3.50 for these respective depths, classifying it as considerable contamination. The PLI values ranged from 1.61 to 2.50, with a mean value of 2.12, indicating overall contamination. The ecological risk assessment indicated that the soil's ecological risk was low at all depths. Cd was the major contributor to the RI, accounting for 48%, 47%, and 42% at 0–10 cm, 10–20 cm, and 20–30 cm depths, respectively. Health risk assessments revealed significant non-carcinogenic risks to children (mean HI = 1.30) and unacceptable carcinogenic risks to both adults and children (mean TCR = 3.26 × 10–4 and 1.53 × 10–3, respectively). This study underscores the critical need for comprehensive risk assessments using multiple indicators to prioritize remediation efforts for HMs, providing a scientific basis for effective environmental management and public health protection in the Three Gorges Reservoir Area.Keywords: Heavy metal, Agricultural soil, Coal gangue, Health riskSubject terms: Environmental chemistry, Environmental impact, Environmental sciences, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

Coal mining, a major industry, inadvertently contributes to environmental pollution, with coal gangue identified as a significant source of contamination1,2. Recent studies have indicated that coal gangue dumps are primary sources of trace element pollution, including Hg, Pb, Cd, Cr, As, Cu, Ni, Zn, Fe, Mn, Sn, Ge, and Se3–6. with Hg, Pb, Cd, Cr, As, Cu, Ni, Zn, and Se classified as priority control pollutants by the United States Environmental Protection Agency (USEPA) due to their toxicity and persistence7.This pollution occurs primarily through the oxidation of gangue under atmospheric conditions, which releases heavy metal(oid)s (HMs)-rich effluents into the surrounding environment3,8. Bioaccumulation of these heavy metal(oid)s leads to a diversity of toxic effects on various body tissues and organs by disrupting cellular events, including growth, proliferation, differentiation, damage-repair processes, and apoptosis9. Additionally, heavy metal(oid)s adversely affect plant growth and development, leading to reduced photosynthesis, nutrient uptake, and overall plant productivity10,11. Understanding and mitigating the environmental impact of coal gangue dumps is crucial for sustainable agricultural practices and public health protection in coal mining regions. As one of the largest producers of coal resources globally, China faces significant challenges with large quantities of coal gangue accumulating in mining areas, raising concerns about heavy metal(oid) pollution in the surrounding soils2,12. Therefore, it is imperative to evaluate the pollution level and identify the sources of contamination to implement effective control measures13.

Multivariate statistical analyses, such as principal component analysis (PCA) and cluster analysis, alongside correlation analysis, are commonly employed to distinguish between natural and anthropogenic sources of HMs in soils4,13,14. Pollution indicators like the geoaccumulation index (Igeo), contamination factor (CF) and pollution load index (PLI) are commonly applied to assess HM pollution levels in soils. However, each index has its limitations. The Igeo can be influenced by natural background variations and may overestimate pollution levels in areas with naturally high metal concentrations15. The CF does not account for synergistic effects between metals and assumes that each metal contributes equally to pollution, which might not reflect the true ecological risk16. The PLI provides a general overview of contamination but lacks specificity regarding individual metal contributions and their potential health impacts17. However, a combination of these approaches can provide a more comprehensive and accurate assessment of trace element contamination5. The potential ecological risk index (RI), which takes into account the toxicity of HMs, is a method developed to evaluate the ecological risk of HMs in sediments16 and has been utilized to assess ecological risks caused by toxic metals comprehensively5,18. HMs can pose significant risks to human health due to their toxicity, persistence, and potential for bioaccumulation. The USEPA health risk model, widely adopted internationally, is used to evaluate risks posed by HMs through ingestion, inhalation, and dermal contact in coal mining areas13,19–21.

The Three Gorges Reservoir Area in Chongqing, Southwest China, is an ecologically sensitive region crucial for the Yangtze River basin's balance. Although the Chongqing municipal government closed all coal mines in December 2020, residual pollution from coal gangue dumps continues to threaten the soil environment. Given that HMs cannot be biodegraded or destroyed, their entry into the ecosystem signifies a persistent presence, making remediation challenging22. Soil HM pollution in the vicinity of the mining area persists post-mining operations, and ongoing leaching of HMs from gangue dumps could exacerbate this issue. Consequently, residual pollution from coal gangue dumps continues to threaten the surrounding soil environment. To date, research has primarily focused on the HM content within soils proximate to the mining area23. Therefore, assessing potential HM pollution in soils surrounding coal gangue dumps is imperative for the restoration and sustainable management of soil ecosystems.

The objectives of the present study were to: (1) measure the concentrations of HMs (As, Cd, Cr, Cu, Ni, Pb, and Zn) in the soil surrounding a coal gangue dump, (2) assess the level of HM pollution in the soil based on pollution indicators, (3) evaluate the potential ecological and human health risks associated with HMs in agricultural soils surrounding coal gangue dumps, and (4) identify the sources of HMs.

Materials and Methods

Study area and sample collection

This study was conducted in a closed coal mining area in western Chongqing, SW China (Fig. 1). The area is a significant coal-bearing basin in southwest China. The Kangdian Upland (dominated by the Emeishan basalts) is the major provenance in the western part of the coal basin. The coal-bearing measures occur in the Upper Permian Longtan formation, which unconformably overlies the Early Permian Maokou formation and is conformably underlain by the Late Permian Changxing formation24.

Study area and sampling site locations.

Soil samples were collected in mid-May 2021. Figure 1 shows the locations of the sampling sites. A total of 21 composite soil samples were collected from depths of 0–10, 10–20, and 20–30 cm at each sampling point. Each composite sample consisted of a mixture of five subsamples, each weighing approximately 1.0 kg, taken from the center and four corners of a 1-m-wide square area. All samples were packed in sealed polypropylene bags and numbered. In addition, two bedrock samples were collected for geochemical compositional comparison.

Chemical analysis

The soil samples were subjected to air-drying at ambient temperature, followed by gentle crushing using a mortar and pestle, and subsequently passed through a nylon mesh sieve with a pore size of 2 mm to eliminate any gravel and plant remnants. The processed samples were then stored in plastic bags for subsequent analysis. The samples were analyzed using a Quadrupole Inductively Coupled Plasma Mass Spectrometer (Q-ICP-MS), model ELAN DRC-e, manufactured by PerkinElmer, Canada, at the State Key Laboratory of Ore Deposit Geochemistry, Institute of Geochemistry, Chinese Academy of Sciences. The elements measured were As, Cd, Cr, Cu, Ni, Pb, and Zn. The samples were digested using a mixture of HNO3 and HF, following the method described in Qi, et al. (2000)25. The precision of the data was ensured with relative standard deviations (RSD) better than 10% for most trace elements.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive Statistics and multivariate analysis (correlation analysis and hierarchical cluster analysis) were performed using SPSS v26.0 (IBM, USA) for Windows. Pearson correlation analysis was employed to identify the linear dependence between different variables. Hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA) was used to identify different geochemical groups, clustering the HMs that exhibit similar distribution in soil, and thus inferring their origin and behavior during their sojourn in soil42,43.

Results and discussion

Concentrations of HMs

Statistical descriptions and background concentrations of the seven HMs in soil, along with their concentrations in coal and coal-associated rocks, are detailed in Tables 2 and 3. The mean concentrations of As, Cd, Cr, Cu, Ni, Pb, and Zn in the study area soils were 17.35, 0.55, 142.81, 87.00, 70.30, 44.60, and 146.21 mg/kg, respectively. Compared to bedrock (limestone), these concentrations were about 5.0, 55.0, 6.3, 24.4, 3.0, 33.8, and 18.7 times higher, respectively. Relative to the background values of soils in Chongqing Municipality, these concentrations were about 2.6, 2.0, 1.9, 3.5, 2.2, 1.6, and 1.8 times higher, respectively. Compared to coal, the study area soils showed elevated levels of HMs, with As, Cd, Cr, Cu, Ni, Pb, and Zn being 2.6, 1.5, 2.0, 3.0, 2.4, 1.5, and 3.3 times higher, respectively. However, the concentrations in coal-associated rocks were significantly higher, with levels of As, Cd, Cr, Cu, Ni, Pb, and Zn being 1.2, 4.6, 2.7, 1.7, 2.0, 0.7, and 0.9 times higher, respectively, compared to the study area soils. However, the average concentrations of Pb (44.60 mg/kg) and Zn (146.21 mg/kg) in the soil were higher than all contrasting values (Table 3).

Given these comparisons, it is apparent that coal-associated rocks are unlikely sources of Pb and Zn in the study area soils, as their concentrations are lower in these rocks than in the soils. Conversely, the higher concentrations of As, Cd, Cr, Cu, and Ni in coal-associated rocks suggest that these materials could be potential sources of these HMs in the soils. The elevated levels of Cu in the study area soils, particularly in comparison to bedrock and background values, suggest significant anthropogenic contributions, likely from the coal gangue dumps, agricultural activities, transportation, and industrial emissions.

The mean values of heavy metal(oid)s concentrations in soils at depths of 0–10 cm and 10–20 cm were more similar compared to those at 20–30 cm. This discrepancy is likely due to the impact of tilling, which commonly affects depths ranging from 0 to 20 cm44. The concentrations of As, Cd, Cu, Pb and Zn in the soil were generally higher in the upper layer (0–10 cm, 10–20 cm) than in the lower layer (20–30 cm) (Table 2), whereas the concentrations of Cr and Ni were found to be higher in the lower soil profile. These distributional characteristics indicate that As, Cd, Cu, Pb and Zn tend to be enriched in the upper soil layer, while Cr and Ni are more prevalent in the deeper part of the soil profile.

The coefficient of variation (CV) elucidates the average variation level of HM concentrations across all sampling sites, with high variability indicating significant influence by external factors45. A data set is considered to exhibit significant variation when the CV exceeds 15%. In almost all samples (Table 2), the CV values of HMs exceeded 15%, and the concentrations of HMs in these samples significantly exceeded background values, suggesting external inputs into the soils at various depths (0 – 30 cm).

Source analysis

Correlation analysis of HMs in soil

The relative contents of HMs in soil are controlled by various factors, including the original content of HMs in the parent material, soil formation processes, atmospheric deposition, and anthropogenic activities49,50. Correlation analysis serves as an effective tool for elucidating the complex relationships between HMs, thereby aiding in understanding the factors affecting HM concentrations and identifying pollution sources51. The relationships between HM concentrations were examined using Pearson correlation coefficients (Table 4). High correlations among HMs in soil may suggest common sources of pollution or similar migration patterns and pathways in the study area51,52. A significant positive correlation was found between Cr and Cu (r = 0.74), Cr and Ni (r = 0.81), and Cu and Ni (r = 0.90) at the 0.01 level. As and Pb exhibited a significant positive correlation (r = 0.64), implying a common source, whereas these elements were negatively correlated with Cr, Cu, and Ni, suggesting distinct sources for As and Pb compared to Cr, Cu, and Ni. Zn did not show notable correlations with other metals, indicating a potential unique source for Zn53.

Pearson correlation analysis provides valuable insights into the sources of heavy metals, but other factors, such as the chemical properties and mobility of the elements, should also be considered when analyzing their sources in soil. These factors significantly influence their distribution and behavior. For example, elements like cadmium (Cd) and zinc (Zn), which are more mobile, can migrate through soil profiles more easily than less mobile elements like lead (Pb) and chromium (Cr). This mobility affects their spatial distribution and potential sources. Therefore, identifying the sources of heavy metals should not rely solely on statistical correlations but should be complemented by an understanding of the elements' geochemical behaviors. This comprehensive approach helps in accurately determining the sources of heavy metals in soils and assessing their environmental impact.

Hierarchical cluster analysis of HMs

HCA was employed to identify the HMs that exhibit similar distribution in soil, thereby inferring their origin and behavior during their presence in the soil43. The HCA results for the studied HMs are presented in Fig. 2 as a dendrogram. As depicted in Fig. 2, HMs in soil can be categorized into three groups: (1) Cr, Cu and Ni; (2) Zn; (3) As, Pb and Cd.

Dendrogram obtained by hierarchical cluster analysis for HMs.

HMs in soils often originate from multiple sources. The various sources of toxic metals in the soil of coal mine environments include: (1) air containing mine dust, (2) transport activities, and (3) discharge and dispersion of coal mine wastes14. However, to develop targeted soil heavy metal(oid) contamination control strategies, it is crucial to determine which source is dominant when analyzing each source of HM in the soil.

Pearson's correlation analysis and hierarchical cluster analysis suggested that Cr, Cu and Ni may have a similar origin. Ni, Cr, and Cu are commonly regarded as indicators of natural origin, a fact that has been substantiated by numerous studies across different regions54. The concentrations of Ni, Cr, and Cu in soil samples were significantly higher than in the bedrock, the background values of Chongqing soils, and the coal and coal gangue of the Longtan Formation in the study area24,46. However, they were lower than those in coal-associated rocks (Table 3), which were discarded as coal gangue near mining areas during coal extraction. These rocks may be the primary source of Cr, Ni and Cu in the soil. Generally, HMs in coal gangue are released into the soil environment in two ways: (1) gangue dust eroded by wind and suspended in the atmosphere, settling in the soil around the gangue dump, and (2) HMs leached from the gangue dump by atmospheric precipitation, entering the soil with runoff18. In general, higher precipitation and temperature can promote the accumulation of HMs55. The study area, located in the subtropics, with an average annual temperature of 18.7 °C and an average annual rainfall of 1031.3 mm, provides conditions conducive to the leaching and migration of HMs from the coal gangue dump.

Pb showed a positive correlation with As and Cd (Table 3), and they were grouped together in cluster analysis, indicating similar sources. The concentrations of Pb in soil were lower than all reference values (Table 3), suggesting that natural causes may not be the primary source of Pb contamination. The study area is located near a coal mine goods yard, where truck transportation activities were prevalent prior to the coal mine's closure, suggesting that transportation activities could be a significant source of Pb in the soil. Previous research on Cd-enriched soils in the Three Gorges region identified geological sources as the primary contributors to Cd in soils, with weathered rocks from Permian sedimentary rocks being a potential natural source of Cd56. Another study on the distribution of HMs in agricultural soils in the Sichuan Basin indicated that Pb and Cd contamination resulted from agricultural activities, while As originated from the soil parent material57. In summary, the presence of Pb, As, and Cd in the soil can be attributed to a mix of sources, including transportation and agricultural activities, as well as natural origins.

The concentration of Zn in the soil did not correlate with any other HMs (Table 3). Additionally, Zn was categorized separately in the cluster analysis (Fig. 2), indicating that it originated from a distinct source compared to other HMs. The concentrations of Zn in soil were significantly higher than the background values for soils, coal, and coal-associated rocks in Chongqing, suggesting these were unlikely the primary sources of Zn contamination. Previous studies have identified that heavy metal(oid) pollution of Zn often occurs during mechanical manufacturing, as well as in chemical and textile activities55. The study area is proximal to significant industrial bases in Chongqing, where mechanical and chemical industrial activities likely contribute to the Zn contamination in the soil.

Soil contamination indices

To assess the extent of heavy metal(oid) contamination in the soils, the Igeo, CF, and PLI indices were evaluated (Fig. 3). The mean Igeo value of Cu (1 < Igeo ≤ 2) was the highest at all soil depths compared to the mean Igeo values of the other HMs, indicating a moderately contaminated level. The Igeo values for the remaining HMs, except Pb, ranged from 0 to 1, presenting an uncontaminated to moderately contaminated level. The mean Igeo values of Pb in soil at a depth of 0–20 cm indicated soil contamination levels as uncontaminated to moderately contaminated (0 < Igeo < 1). However, the mean Igeo value of Pb in soil at a depth of 20–30 cm indicated soil contamination levels as uncontaminated (Igeo < 0). This suggested that external inputs of Pb have a relatively minor impact on the lower soil profile. In the upper soil profiles (0–10 cm and 10–20 cm), the mean values of Igeo of these HMs were ranked in descending order of Cu > As > Cd > Ni > Cr > Zn > Pb, and in the lower soil profile (20–30 cm) the order was Cu > As > Ni > Cr > Zn > Cd > Pb. In general, the mean heavy metal(oid)s Igeo values were higher in the upper soil profile than in the lower soil profile, except for Cr and Ni, which had higher values in the lower soil profile.

Boxplot of Igeo (a–c) and CF (d–f) of each HM in soil.

The mean values of CF for HMs indicated that all three soil depths were considered contaminated with Cu (3 ≤ CF < 6) and moderately contaminated with other HMs (1 ≤ CF < 3). The mean CF values for each HM in the soil at each depth were ordered consistently with the results obtained from the Igeo values. Additionally, according to the CF index, like the Igeo index, it also showed that all the HMs exhibited higher contamination levels in the upper soil profile, except Cr and Ni, which showed higher contamination levels in the lower soil profile. These characteristics indicated that the Igeo and CF indices were highly consistent in indicating the relative contamination levels of HMs.

While the Igeo index estimates the environmental impact by removing background contributions, the CF index provides a direct measure of contamination by comparing current concentrations to a baseline. Despite these methodological differences, the consistent results observed in our study may be attributed to the similar environmental behavior and mobility of HMs in the study area. This consistency might be due to the dominance of anthropogenic sources in our study area, leading to similar contamination patterns. This alignment suggests that both indices, although distinct in their calculations, effectively capture the contamination profile of the soils.

The PLI index offered a comprehensive assessment of the soil contamination level by accounting for multiple HMs present in the soil. The PLI values ranged from 1.61 to 2.50, with a mean value of 2.12, indicating that the entire site was contaminated with HMs (Fig. 4). The distribution range of PLI values was similar for the 0–10 cm and 10–20 cm soil depths, likely due to soil mixing from tillage activities. The PLI values showed greater variability in the lower soil profile compared to the upper soil profile. However, the average PLI values were lower in the lower soil profile than in the upper soil profile (Fig. 4). This implied that the upper soil profile had a higher heavy metal(oid) contamination load. The largest and smallest PLI values were both observed in the lower layer of the soil profile, suggesting possible vertical migration of HMs at certain sites.

Ecological risk indices

Potential ecological risk factors for single HM (Eri) and RI of three soil depths (0–10, 10–20 and 20–30 cm) are presented in Figs. 4 and 5, respectively. The Eri index values for all HMs, except for Cd, in the soil samples were less than 40, indicating a low level of contamination. The mean values of Cd ecological risk in all three soil depths ranged from 40 to 80, indicating a moderate ecological risk in all soils. However, the risk posed by Cd in soils at the 20–30 cm depth was lower than in soils at 0–10 cm and 10–20 cm depths. This could be because in soils contaminated with HMs, HMs tend to accumulate in the surface soil profile58. The highest (81.43) and lowest (33.96) ErCd values were both observed in the lower profile, which was consistent with the variation characteristics of Cd concentration in the lower soil profile.

Potential ecological risk factors for single HM (Eri) at different soil depths in the study area.

Ninety-five percent of the soil samples had RI values below 150 (Fig. 5), indicating that most areas were at low ecological risk from the studied HMs. The mean RI value for the soil at 20–30 cm depth (115.6) was significantly lower than that for the soil at 0–10 cm and 10–20 cm depths (134.2 and 132.4, respectively) (Fig. 6). This suggests that the surface soil (0–20 cm) in the study area bore a relatively high ecological risk. The contribution ranking of each HM to the RI values of the soils at all three depths was Cd > As > Cu > Ni > Pb > Cr > Zn (Fig. 7). Cadmium was identified as the primary ecological risk factor in the soil, contributing 48%, 47%, and 42% to the average RI value at depths of 0–10 cm, 10–20 cm, and 20–30 cm, respectively.

RI indices at different soil depths in the study area.

Contribution of each HM to the ecological risk index.

Overall, Igeo and CF analyses jointly indicated that Cu exhibits the highest contamination level among all HMs. All HMs showed higher contamination levels in the upper soil profile, except Cr and Ni, which displayed higher contamination levels in the lower soil profile. The PLI index suggested that soils at various depths exhibited heavy metal(oid) contamination, with the upper soil profile experiencing a higher level of contamination compared to the lower soil profile. The RI index revealed a low potential ecological risk across all three soil depths. Unlike the Igeo and CF indices, which identified Cu as the priority control pollutant, the RI index pinpointed Cd as the principal contributor to the RI index value. This discrepancy may be attributable to the RI index considering the toxic-response factors of each HM.

Human health risk assessment

Calculations of non-carcinogenic and carcinogenic health risks from HMs in topsoil (0–10 cm) via various pathways (ingestion, inhalation and dermal exposure) were shown in Figs. 8 and 10, and detailed results were given in Table S3.

(a) and (c) are histograms of the non-carcinogenic risk for the three exposure pathways and the hazard index; (b) and (d) are histograms of the non-carcinogenic risk for each HM.

(a) and (c) are histograms of the carcinogenic risk for the three exposure pathways and the total carcinogenic risk; (b) and (d) are histograms of the carcinogenic risk for each HM.

The range of HI values for adults (HI(a)) was 0.21 – 0.26, with an average of 0.24 (Fig. 8a, Table S3), suggesting that the non-carcinogenic health risk from HM exposure was at a safe level for adults. However, HI values for children (HI(c)) were all greater than the safe level (range 1.10–1.43, mean 1.30) (Fig. 8c, Table S3), indicating potential non-carcinogenic effects on children.

The mean HQ values for adults and children, exposed to mixed HMs through three exposure routes, followed the order of HQing > HQderm > HQinh (Fig. 8a,c). The average contribution of HQing to HI was 81.37% for adults and 97.85% for children. These results suggest that the ingestion pathway poses the most significant risk for heavy metal exposure. However, further studies are required to confirm that ingestion is the primary exposure pathway and to explore other potential routes of exposure, such as inhalation and dermal contact. The HQ values for HMs via ingestion exposure in children (HQing(c)) consistently exceeded 1 (Fig. 8c), underscoring that inhalation poses significant non-carcinogenic risks to children, warranting particular attention.

The HQ values for each HM due to multiple pathway exposure for adults followed the order of Cr > As > Pb > Ni > Cu > Cd > Zn (Figs. 8b and 9), while for children, the order was As > Cr > Pb > Ni > Cu > Cd > Zn (Figs. 8d and 9). In both scenarios, As and Cr emerged as the primary contributors to non-carcinogenic risk, together accounting for over 80% of the risk (Fig. 9). As and Cr are prevalent pollutants in the environment and are classified as Group 'A' carcinogens by the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA)59,60. Specifically, chronic exposure to As can cause skin lesions, cardiovascular diseases, neurotoxicity, and an increased risk of skin, bladder, and lung cancers61,62, while long-term exposure to Cr can lead to respiratory problems, weakened immune systems, and damage to liver and kidneys63. Although the HQ values remained within safe levels (Fig. 8b and c), chronic exposure to As and Cr in surface soil could still pose a health risk, especially for children, due to their relatively lower resistance.

Contribution of individual HMs to the HI index.

For carcinogenic risk, As, Cd, Cr, Ni, and Pb were assessed through the ingestion, inhalation, and dermal exposure pathway (Fig. 10a–d). The mean TCR values for adults and children were 3.26 × 10–4 and 1.53 × 10–3 (Table S3) respectively, indicating that HMs posed an unacceptable carcinogenic risk to both adults and children, with a significantly higher risk for children. The CR from various exposures to combined HMs for both adults and children decreased in the order of CRing > CRderm > CRinh (Fig. 10a, c). The contribution of the ingestion carcinogenic risk values (CRing) to the TCR for adults and children was 70.06% and 96.12%, respectively, indicating that ingestion was the primary pathway for carcinogenic risk from HMs. The CRing exceeded acceptable levels (CRing > 1 × 10–4) for both adults and children, suggesting the significance of ingestion in health risk management.

The CR of each HM for multiple pathway exposure to adults was ranked in the order of Ni > Cr > As > Cd > Pb (Figs. 10c and 11), while for children, the order was Ni > As > Cr > Cd > Pb (Figs. 10d and 11). For adults, the carcinogenic risk for each HM was categorized as follows: Ni posed an unacceptable risk (mean CRNi(a) value 2.28 × 10–4); As, Cd and Cr were considered acceptable, with mean values of 4.16 × 10–5, 5.74 × 10–6 and 5.03 × 10–5 for CRAs(a), CRCd(a) and CRCr(a) respectively; and Pb's risk was negligible (mean CRPb(a) value 6.46 × 10–7). Ni accounted for an average of 69.27% of the TCR values for adults (Figs. 10b, and 11). For children, both Ni and As presented unacceptable risks, with mean value of 1.18 × 10–3, 2.61 × 10–4 for CRNi(c) and CRAs(c); Cd, Cr and Pb were considered acceptable, with mean value of 3.70 × 10–5, 4.20 × 10–5 and 4.16 × 10–6 for CRCd(c), CRCr(c) and CRPb(c) respectively. Ni and As contributed 76.82% and 17.69%, respectively, to the average TCR values for children (Figs. 10d and 11). Prolonged exposure to Ni can cause lung and nasal cancers, respiratory issues, and allergic reactions64,65. In summary, with the exception of Pb, which does not pose a carcinogenic risk to adults, all other HMs pose a carcinogenic risk to both adults and children. The relatively high carcinogenic risks of Ni and As indicate that proactive measures must be taken to control health risks from these elements in the soil to protect public health in the affected areas.

Contribution of individual HMs to the TCR index.

According to the analysis above, ingestion was identified as the primary exposure pathway for both non-carcinogenic and carcinogenic risks among adults and children. However, children in the study area exhibited higher non-carcinogenic and carcinogenic risks compared with adults, warranting increased attention. Comparatively speaking, children and adult females were the relatively vulnerable populations for the non-carcinogenic and carcinogenic risks, respectively20. This study did not consider the health risk differences between male and female adults, but future research should strengthen this aspect to comprehensively understand the health risks posed by soil heavy metal(oid)s.

Conclusions

This study investigated HM contamination in agricultural soil adjacent to a gangue dump of a closed coal mine. Analyses using both the Igeo and CF indices identified Cu as the primary HM contaminant. The Igeo, CF, and PLI revealed that HM contamination risks were higher in the 0–10 cm and 10–20 cm soil depths compared to the 20–30 cm depth. The evaluation of potential ecological risk showed a low risk across all soil depths, with Cd identified as the principal ecological risk source.

The analysis demonstrated that HMs in surface soils posed no non-carcinogenic health risk to adults; however, they presented a considerable risk to children, primarily due to Cr and As being the main non-carcinogenic risk contributors. Additionally, HMs in the topsoil were identified to pose a carcinogenic risk to both adults and children, with Ni emerging as a significant risk factor. In response to these findings, it is imperative that the government develops effective ecological treatment strategies and undertakes regular monitoring and soil testing in the vicinity of these coal mines to mitigate health risks among the residents.
