Late Diagnosis of X-Linked Arginine Vasopressin Resistance Presenting With Flow Uropathy in Adulthood
Authors: Vivek Jha, Rahul Gupta, Sanja Medenica, Mirjam Christ-Crain, Pinaki Dutta
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCongenital arginine vasopressin resistance (AVP-R) is a rare inherited disorder caused by renal unresponsiveness to arginine vasopressin (AVP), leading to persistent polyuria and polydipsia. We report a 19-year-old male individual with a long history of excessive thirst and urination who remained undiagnosed until adulthood. Despite normal growth and development, he was found to have bilateral hydronephrosis and underwent pyeloplasty at age 19. Postoperative fluid restriction resulted in life-threatening hypernatremia and seizures. Further evaluation revealed persistently low urine osmolality despite hypernatremia, elevated plasma copeptin, and no response to desmopressin or vasopressin, confirming AVP-R. Genetic testing identified a hemizygous arginine vasopressin receptor 2 (AVPR2) missense variant (c.357G>C; p.Gln119His), confirming X-linked congenital AVP-R. Treatment with thiazide diuretic, sodium restriction, and liberal fluid intake led to symptom relief and biochemical stability. This case highlights the diagnostic challenges of congenital AVP-R in patients without growth impairment and underscores the importance of molecular confirmation for appropriate management and family screening.

Introduction

Congenital arginine vasopressin resistance (AVP-R), formerly known as congenital nephrogenic diabetes insipidus [1], is a rare disorder of water balance characterized by renal insensitivity to arginine vasopressin (AVP), leading to impaired urinary concentration, polyuria, and compensatory polydipsia. Without adequate water intake, patients are at risk of severe hypernatremia. The congenital form, caused predominantly by X-linked mutations in the AVPR2 gene (∼90%), and less commonly by autosomal mutations in aquaporin 2 (AQP2) (∼10%), is often under-recognized, due to underreporting and delayed diagnosis [2].

X-linked AVP-R affects approximately 1 in 100 000 live births, with ∼60% of cases diagnosed in infancy. However, delays are frequent, especially in resource-limited settings, and up to 6% are only diagnosed in adulthood [3]. Mild or partial phenotypes may go unnoticed due to patient adaptation.

Secondary AVP-R, more common than the congenital form, results from chronic kidney disease, electrolyte disturbances (eg, hypercalcemia, hypokalemia), or drugs such as lithium and amphotericin B [4]. Unlike central diabetes insipidus, AVP levels are normal or elevated in AVP-R, necessitating careful differentiation for appropriate management.

Despite advances in molecular diagnostics, congenital AVP-R remains frequently overlooked, especially by physicians taking care of adults. No standardized treatment exists, and management is based on expert consensus. Early genetic diagnosis is useful for establishing a definitive diagnosis, guiding the choice of therapy, and providing appropriate family counseling.

We report a 19-year-old male individual with a long history of polyuria and polydipsia, in whom a previously reported hemizygous AVPR2 missense variant confirmed X-linked congenital AVP-R. This case highlights the importance of considering AVP-R in patients with desmopressin-resistant polyuria, recurrent hypernatremia, or unexplained urinary tract dilatation.

Case Presentation

A 19-year-old male individual presented with long history of polyuria and polydipsia. His mother reported excessive irritability from the third day of life, relieved only by water intake, leading to early supplementation of water alongside breastfeeding. There were no perinatal complications or neonatal symptoms such as vomiting, seizures, or failure to thrive.

He was born at term via normal vaginal delivery with an uneventful antenatal period. No polyhydramnios or perinatal hypoglycemia/asphyxia was reported. At 3 days of life, he developed fever, treated empirically as sepsis. Persistent post-discharge crying was alleviated with water.

Throughout childhood, he had nocturnal polyuria and polydipsia but achieved normal growth, development, and puberty. There was no history of neurodevelopmental delay, cranial surgery, trauma, visual/hearing deficits, or salt craving. Family history was noncontributory.

At age 12, he was hospitalized for fever and abdominal pain; ultrasound showed bilateral hydronephrosis. He was treated symptomatically and discharged. At 19, he re-presented with similar symptoms. Imaging revealed bilateral hydronephrosis, a distended bladder, and left pelviureteric junction (PUJ) obstruction. He underwent left-sided Anderson–Hynes pyeloplasty.

Postoperative fluid restriction led to severe hypernatremia (181 mmol/L) and seizures. Suspecting diabetes insipidus, intravenous fluids and desmopressin were administered, but without response. Initiation of thiazide therapy resulted in clinical and biochemical improvement, prompting referral for specialized evaluation.

Diagnostic Assessment

On examination, the patient was alert and well hydrated. Height was 173 cm, weight 74 kg, and body mass index (BMI) 24.7 kg/m². General and systemic examinations were normal.

Initial laboratory evaluation showed hypernatremia with persistently dilute urine: the serum sodium concentration was 141.6 mmol/L (SI: 141.6 mmol/L) (reference range, 135-145 mmol/L [SI: 135-145 mmol/L]) and the serum potassium concentration was 4.6 mmol/L (SI: 4.6 mmol/L) (reference range, 3.5-5.0 mmol/L [SI: 3.5-5.0 mmol/L]) (Table 1). The patient's daily fluid intake ranged from 5.0 to 5.5 L/day, with urine output of 5.0 to 5.4 L/day. Urine osmolality remained low at 52 to 80 mOsm/kg (SI: 52-80 mmol/kg) (reference >800 mOsm/kg [SI: > 800 mmol/kg]). Spot urine sodium was 18 mmol/L (SI: 18 mmol/L), consistent with dilute urine. Plasma copeptin was elevated at 44.1 pmol/L (reference <10-15 pmol/L; ≥21.4 pmol/L supports AVP-R) (Table 1), indicating intact AVP secretion. A water deprivation test was terminated after 4 hours due to >3% weight loss, with an hourly urine output of approximately 8 to 10 mL/kg/h. During this test, serum sodium rose to 146 mmol/L (SI: 146 mmol/L) and serum osmolality to 320 mOsm/kg (SI: 320 mmol/kg), while urine osmolality remained < 70 mOsm/kg (SI: < 70 mmol/kg). Subcutaneous vasopressin increased urine osmolality by only 12%, confirming complete AVP-R. Targeted next-generation sequencing identified a hemizygous AVPR2 missense variant: c.357G>C (p.Gln119His). This variant has been previously reported by Mori et al [5] and is deposited in the human gene mutation database. Both maternal and paternal testing using Sanger sequencing were negative, consistent with the variant occurring as a de novo mutation in the patient. The absence of similar clinical features in family members further supports this finding, although the possibility of low-level parental mosaicism cannot be fully excluded due to the detection limits of Sanger sequencing.

Treatment

The patient was started on oral chlorthalidone at a daily dose of 12.5 mg, divided into half a tablet twice daily. To augment the effect of thiazide therapy and minimize urine output, dietary sodium restriction was recommended. He was also advised to maintain adequate fluid intake, with particular emphasis during episodes of fever, illness, or exposure to high temperatures. Desmopressin was not reintroduced in view of confirmed resistance to vasopressin.

Outcome and Follow-Up

After starting thiazide therapy, the patient experienced significant symptomatic improvement, including reduced urinary frequency and normalization of serum sodium levels. He remained clinically stable and was discharged with detailed guidance on maintaining adequate hydration, adhering to dietary sodium restriction, and ensuring regular medication use. Long-term follow-up was arranged with nephrology and urology services to monitor renal function, assess bladder dynamics, and mitigate risks associated with chronic urinary tract dilatation.

Discussion

This case illustrates the clinical spectrum of X-linked congenital AVP-R in a 19-year-old male patient who remained undiagnosed despite classic symptoms from infancy. His presentation—chronic hypotonic polyuria, progressive urinary tract dilatation, and hypernatremic seizure following fluid restriction—is typical of untreated congenital AVP-R. The absence of growth or neurodevelopmental delay likely contributed to diagnostic delay, a common scenario in milder or partially compensated phenotypes. With unrestricted access to water, such patients can achieve normal growth and development, although polyuria and polydipsia persist, especially in settings with limited access to pediatric endocrinology or genetic testing [6].

Diagnosis of AVP-R relies on recognizing key clinical features: excessive urine output, dilute urine (hyposthenuria), and compensatory polydipsia. Polyuria is defined as >3 L/day in adults or >2 L/m²/day in children [7]. A structured approach includes the water deprivation test to differentiate AVP-R from primary polydipsia. Normally, rising serum osmolality (295-300 mOsm/kg) or sodium (>145 mmol/L) triggers AVP release, raising urine osmolality >600 mOsm/kg. Failure of this response indicates either AVP-R or AVP deficiency (AVP-D).

Desmopressin helps differentiate AVP-D from AVP-R. In AVP-D, urine concentration improves with desmopressin, whereas in AVP-R, the kidneys remain unresponsive, urine stays dilute, and desmopressin-mediated stimulation of factor VIII and von Willebrand factor is absent, reflecting systemic V2 receptor dysfunction beyond renal water handling. In children, desmopressin may be used without fluid restriction to avoid dehydration. A persistently low urine osmolality despite hypernatremia and elevated AVP or copeptin confirms AVP-R [8].

Copeptin, a stable 39–amino acid glycopeptide co-released with AVP and neurophysin II from pre-provasopressin, has emerged as a robust surrogate marker of AVP secretion, overcoming the limitations of direct AVP measurement. Its diagnostic utility lies in differentiating AVP-D from AVP-R: in AVP-D, copeptin levels remain inappropriately low despite hyperosmolality, whereas in AVP-R they are elevated due to intact AVP release but impaired renal action. Stimulated copeptin ≥21.4 pmol/L during hypernatremia reliably identifies AVP-R. In our patient, a level of 44.1 pmol/L confirmed preserved AVP secretion and supported the diagnosis of AVP-R. Nevertheless, the applicability of these thresholds in infants is still under investigation.

Delayed recognition of congenital AVP-R may result in avoidable complications. Chronic polyuria exposes the urinary tract to large volumes of dilute urine, causing bladder distension, hydronephrosis, and ureteral dilation, eventually leading to chronic kidney disease. Our patient's imaging showed these classic sequelae, reinforcing the need for early diagnosis and intervention.

At the molecular level, congenital AVP-R is most commonly caused by AVPR2 mutations on chromosome Xq28, encoding the vasopressin V2 receptor (V2R), accounting for ∼90% of inherited cases [2]. V2R activation by AVP initiates intracellular signaling that promotes AQP2 insertion into the apical membrane of collecting duct cells, enabling water reabsorption. Over 270 AVPR2 mutations have been reported, with missense variants being the most frequent in familial forms [9].

These mutations are categorized by their pathogenic mechanism: Class II variants cause endoplasmic reticulum retention of misfolded receptors; Class III mutations impair signaling despite proper trafficking; and Class IV variants undergo premature internalization. All disrupt the AVP–V2R–AQP2 axis, leading to renal resistance to vasopressin and resulting in polyuria [10-12].

The patient reported here harbored a hemizygous AVPR2 variant: c.357G>C (p.Gln119His), previously reported in association with congenital AVP-R [5] and is deposited in the human gene mutation database(https://www.hgmd.cf.ac.uk/ac/index.php). The variant lies within a conserved, functionally important domain of the V2 receptor and is consistent with the classical phenotype (early-onset polyuria, low urine osmolality, stable sodium). Based on phenotype–genotype correlation and prior literature, it was classified as “pathogenic.”

Management of adult congenital AVP-R includes ensuring free fluid access, lowering renal solute load, and reducing urine output. Sodium (<6 g/day) and protein (<1 g/kg/day) restriction decrease osmolar excretion. Thiazides (eg, chlorthalidone 12.5 mg/day) enhance proximal reabsorption to reduce polyuria; potassium-sparing agents like amiloride can be added if hypokalemia occurs [13]. Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, though effective, are generally avoided in adults due to nephrotoxicity.

In our case, management and diagnostic decisions were aligned with the International Expert Consensus Statement on congenital nephrogenic diabetes insipidus [14].

Regular monitoring is essential, particularly in individuals with structural urinary tract abnormalities or underlying chronic kidney disease. Periodic renal imaging every 2 to 5 years is recommended to assess for hydronephrosis and bladder function [3]. With increasing age, polyuria may lessen due to natural decline in renal concentrating capacity, necessitating individualized adjustments in therapy. Referral to specialized centers is beneficial for multidisciplinary care, encompassing endocrinology, nephrology, urology, and genetic counseling.
