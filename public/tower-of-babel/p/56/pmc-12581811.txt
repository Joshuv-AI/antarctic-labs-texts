Peri-procedure and Mid-Long Term Effects of Pulsed Field Ablation vs. Thermal Ablation (Cryo- or Radiofrequency) on Autonomic Nervous System Function in Atrial Fibrillation: A Systematic and Quantitative Pooled-Analysis with Potential Implications for Patient Selection (The PULSE-COLD-HEAT-ANS Collaboration)
Authors: Sebastian Graeger, Sanjiv M Narayan, Christian Meyer, Dominik Linz, Andreas Rillig, Maura M Zylla, Ramin Ebrahimi, Firat Duru, Laura Perrotta, Kars Neven, Christian-Hendrik Heeger, Martin H Ruwald, Piotr Futyma, Bart A Mulder, Gozal Mirzayeva, Márcio Galindo Kiuchi, Martin Martinek, Helmut Pürerfellner, Serge Boveda, Yuehui Yin, Gang Yang, Hailei Liu, Minglong Chen, Boris Schmidt, Julian K R Chun, Mu Qin, Xumin Hou, Xu Liu, Jingquan Zhong, Shaojie Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsAblation modalities differ in their mechanisms of action, tissue specificity, and collateral effects—particularly on the cardiac autonomic nervous system. This study aimed to compare the autonomic effects of pulsed field ablation (PFA) vs. thermal ablation (TA) in patients with atrial fibrillation (AF) through a pooled analysis.Methods and resultsA systematic search of PubMed and Embase was conducted through 5 April 2025, to identify comparative studies. The primary outcome was increase in heart rate (IHR) after ablation, and the secondary outcome was increase in serum S100B (IS100B), a marker of neural injury. Eight studies involving 1007 AF patients were included (mean age: 63.39 ± 10.75 years; 36.3% female; maximum follow-up: 12 months). Baseline characteristics, including the use of antiarrhythmic drugs, were similar between the PFA and TA groups. Pooled analysis showed that PFA was associated with a significantly lower IHR compared to TA (PFA: 4.41 ± 8.86 bpm vs. TA: 10.81 ± 10.46 bpm; P < 0.00001). This difference persisted at midterm (3–6 months) and long-term (12 months) follow-up and remained consistent across age, sex, and different TA modalities (cryoballoon vs. radiofrequency). Correspondingly, the IS100B was significantly less pronounced after PFA (PFA: 33.27 ± 9.46 pg/mL vs. TA: 97.53 ± 31.88 pg/mL; P < 0.00001).ConclusionPFA-based pulmonary vein isolation in patients with AF results in a smaller post-procedural IHR and less S100B release, suggesting reduced neural damage and less disruption of the autonomic nervous system compared to TA. These effects are sustained through mid- to long-term follow-up and may have potential implications for patient selection and individualized ablation strategies.

Introduction

Pulmonary vein isolation (PVI) is the most established catheter ablation strategy for rhythm control in treating atrial fibrillation (AF). Currently the main ablation strategies include thermal energies (e.g. cryo- or radiofrequency) or pulsed field energy.1–4

Atrial fibrillation is associated with altered autonomic tone, which has been found to contribute to arrhythmogenesis.5,6 Different ablation modalities vary in their mechanisms of action, tissue selectivity, and collateral effects, particularly on the cardiac autonomic nervous system. Emerging evidence suggests that thermal ablation may induce significant autonomic modulation, often leading to denervation effect and changes in heart rate variability and other effects, which could contribute to its salutary benefits. Pulsed field ablation (PFA), as a novel, predominantly non-thermal technique, is developed mainly to spare the damage to non-myocardial tissues, raising questions about its relative impact on autonomic function.7–9

Given the growing number of studies evaluating autonomic changes post-ablation, often using different measures such as heart rate increase, heart rate variability, baroreflex sensitivity, and biomarkers for neural injury, a systematic and quantitative analysis is needed to: (i) compare autonomic effects of PFA vs. thermal ablation across studies, (ii) assess consistency and magnitude of these effects, (iii) help clinical decision-making with respect to potential patient-specific ablation strategies, and (iv) identify knowledge gaps and heterogeneity based on current evidence.

Methods

Data inclusion

The present study was performed following the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analysis) guideline.10 Literature research was performed using PubMed and Embase for published data until 5 April 2025. Study selection involved screening titles and abstracts followed by a full-text evaluation for potentially eligible studies. The literature search was performed by using the keywords: (((autonomic nervous system) OR (sympathetic activity) OR (parasympathetic activity) OR (neurocardiac damage) OR (cardiac autonomic nervous system)) AND ((pulsed-field ablation) OR (atrial fibrillation))). In Embase: (‘autonomic nervous system’ OR ‘sympathetic tone’ OR ‘parasympathetic tone’ OR ‘neurocardiac damage’ OR ‘cardiac autonomic nervous system’) AND ‘pulsed field ablation’ AND ‘atrial fibrillation’.

The inclusion criteria for the studies were as follows: (i) prospective and retrospective comparative studies, (ii) studies that compared PFA with thermal ablation in treating AF (e.g. cryoballoon ablation and/or radiofrequency ablation, PVI alone), and (iii) studies that reported outcome data related to autonomic nervous system function, e.g. increase of heart rate and serum S100B levels before and after the procedure.

Study outcomes

Based on the availability of the outcomes reported in the majority of the studies, the primary and secondary outcomes were defined as calculated (i) increase in heart rate (post-procedure minuses pre-procedure) and (ii) increase in serum S100B (IS100B) level (post-procedure minuses pre-procedure), respectively, assessed pre- and post-ablation. The definitions regarding the increase in heart rate and the increase in serum S100B level are consistent with those reported in the included studies. S100B levels were standardized to the same units (pg/mL) to ensure consistency and comparability across studies.

Data collection

The authors (S.G. and S.C.) independently extracted relevant data from the eligible studies. The extracted data include the following: (i) baseline characteristics of included studies (design, sample size), (ii) baseline characteristics of included patients (age, gender, BMI, comorbidities such as, hypertension, diabetes, coronary artery disease, and stroke), and (iii) the procedure data of included studies (procedural time, fluoroscopic time and rate of complications).

Statistical analysis

The analysis was first performed on the total population from the included studies, with subgroup analysis performed based on available baseline characteristics to examine differences in patients undergoing different ablation technologies. For studies reporting the median, range, and/or interquartile range, the sample mean and standard deviation were estimated, as previously described.11 To account for intrinsic between-study variation, a random effects model was used for all comparisons to minimize bias. Statistic value I2 assessed by Q test was used to quantify the degree of heterogeneity. The reliability of the statistical results for the primary efficacy outcome was tested by sensitivity analysis (repeating analyses by excluding one study at a time). Publication bias was assessed by visual inspection of the funnel plot. Descriptive statistics were calculated for all variables. Continuous variables, such as age, body mass index, ejection fraction, or procedure time were reported as mean and standard deviation and compared by weighted mean difference. Differences are presented as mean ± standard deviation and an unpaired t-test with Welch correction was used. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages and a χ2 test with Fisher’s exact test was performed for statistical analysis. Statistical analyses were performed using Revman [Review Manager (RevMan). Version 5.4. The Cochrane Collaboration, 2020.], R-Studio (R version 4.4.2.), and GraphPad Prism 8.3.0. Microsoft Excel was used for data management and organization.

Primary outcome (increase in heart rate)

The overall pooled effect is shown in Figure 2. PFA was associated with significantly lower increase in heart rate after ablation compared to thermal ablation [cryoballoon (CBA) or radiofrequency ablation (RFA)] (PFA: 4.41 ± 8.86 bpm; vs. thermal ablation group: 10.81 ± 10.46 bpm; P < 0.00001). Notably, the heterogeneity was high (I2 = 84%) indicating a variability between the studies.

Increase of heart rate (pre- and post-procedure) comparing pulsed field ablation and thermal ablation (cryoballoon or radiofrequency ablation) independent of time-point.

We further performed subgroup analyses based on the timing of measurement: (i) immediately post-ablation, (ii) midterm (3–6 months), and (iii) long-term (12 months). Immediately after ablation, the PFA group demonstrated a significantly smaller increase in heart rate compared to the thermal ablation group (Figure 3A, PFA: 1.63 ± 9.00 bpm; thermal ablation: 10.68 ± 10.63 bpm; P < 0.00001). Heterogeneity was high (I2 = 68%) but decreased to 44% upon exclusion of the study by Del Monte et al.12 (Figure 3B, PFA: 1.44 ± 9.48 bpm; thermal ablation group: 9.83 ± 10.60 bpm; P < 0.00001).

Increase of heart rate post-ablation (A), post-ablation without Del Monte et al.12 (B), mid-term (3–6 months, C), mid-term without Schipper et al.18 (D), and long-term (12 months, E).

Three studies reported midterm outcomes (3–6 months post-ablation). In this subgroup, the PFA group again showed a smaller increase in heart rate than the thermal ablation group (Figure 3C, PFA: 6.36 ± 8.22 bpm; thermal ablation: 11.61 ± 9.83 bpm; P = 0.05). Heterogeneity was reduced to 0% when the study by Schipper et al.18 was excluded (Figure 3D, PFA: 6,91 ± 8,19 bpm; thermal ablation group: 10.91 ± 9.42 bpm; P < 0.00001).

Only one study assessed long-term outcomes (12 months post-ablation), which also indicated a smaller increase in heart rate following PFA compared to thermal ablation (Figure 3E, PFA: 7 ± 7.24 bpm; thermal ablation group: 12 ± 8.17 bpm; P < 0.00001).

Study-level subgroup analyses based on age, female gender, and types of thermal ablation modality were performed to further analyse heterogeneity.

Subgroup analysis regarding age

Subgroup analysis revealed that patients younger than 65 years had a significantly lower increase in heart rate in the PFA group compared to the thermal ablation group (PFA: 5.8 ± 8.91 bpm; vs. thermal ablation: 11.91 ± 10.20 bpm; P < 0.00001, I2 = 79%, Figure 4A). In patients over 65 years of age, no significant difference was observed; however, a trend of increase in heart rate in the thermal ablation group could be seen, although the heterogeneity was very high (PFA: 1.60 ± 8.08 bpm; vs. thermal ablation: 7.73 ± 10.60 bpm, P = 0.07, I2 = 92%). Sequential study exclusion identified Schipper et al.18 and Del Monte et al.12 as the main contributors of the heterogeneity. Excluding these studies reduced the heterogeneity and resulted in a significant difference in increase in heart rate between the two ablation groups (PFA: 0.40 ± 7.88 bpm; vs. thermal ablation: 10.16 ± 9.64 bpm; P < 0.00001; I2 = 53%; Figure 4B).

Subgroup analysis regarding gender

We further investigated whether gender influenced the primary outcome (Figure 5). In studies with a female proportion greater than 35%, the PFA group showed a significantly lower increase in heart rate before and after the procedure (PFA: 5.70 ± 8.38 bpm; vs. thermal group: 12.15 ± 9.91 bpm, P < 0.0001, I2 = 84%, Figure 5A). In contrast, studies with a female percentage below 35% showed no significant difference but demonstrated a high heterogeneity (PFA: 1.43 ± 9.24 bpm; vs. thermal group: 8.14 ± 11.04 bpm, I2 = 89%, P = 0.06). Notably again, excluding the study by Schipper et al.18 eliminated heterogeneity and showed a significant difference in the increase in heart rate between groups (PFA: −0.04 ± 9.62 bpm; vs. thermal group: 10.00 ± 10.58 bpm; P < 0.00001, I2 = 0%, Figure 5B).

Discussion

The main findings of our pooled analysis are as follows:

Heart rate increase, measured peri-procedurally and during mid- to long-term follow-up, was significantly lower in the PFA group compared to the thermal ablation group (including cryoballoon and radiofrequency ablation). Notably, this difference remained consistent after adjusting for age, sex, and the type of thermal ablation modality used.

Heart rate increase, measured peri-procedurally and during mid- to long-term follow-up, was significantly lower in the PFA group compared to the thermal ablation group (including cryoballoon and radiofrequency ablation). Notably, this difference remained consistent after adjusting for age, sex, and the type of thermal ablation modality used.

Serum S100B levels, a marker of neural injury, showed a significantly smaller increase in the PFA group compared to the thermal ablation group.

Serum S100B levels, a marker of neural injury, showed a significantly smaller increase in the PFA group compared to the thermal ablation group.

Collectively, these findings suggest that PFA-based PVI in patients with AF results in less vagal denervation and reduced disruption to the autonomic nervous system compared with thermal ablation techniques during mid- to long-term.

Study relevance

Catheter ablation is a cornerstone in the management of AF, aiming to isolate the known most common arrhythmogenic foci and restore sinus rhythm. Nowadays two major modalities exist: thermal ablation (e.g. RFA or CBA) and the most recently developed PFA, a largely non-thermal electroporation-based technology as a result of evolution of AF ablation techniques.20,21 While all these modalities are effective in PVI, their impact on adjacent non-myocardial tissues and autonomic nervous system is increasingly recognized.

Heart rate increase post-ablation is thought to reflect autonomic denervation, particularly parasympathetic withdrawal due to collateral damage to the ganglionated plexuses around the pulmonary veins. S100B, a glial protein released during neuroglial injury, is a biomarker of blood-brain barrier disruption and peripheral nervous system injury.22 Its elevation post-ablation suggests involvement of neural structures damage.23,24

Given the anatomical proximity of autonomic ganglia plexus and nervous tissue to multiple ablation sites including PVI as well as sites that may be important to AF maintenance,8,25 understanding how different ablation modalities affect autonomic nervous system is essential for preserving neural integrity and optimizing safety or denervating cardiac ganglia for targeted patient group and potentially improving outcomes.

Findings and interpretation

The cardiac ganglia, mainly located around the pulmonary vein antra on the epicardial surface, form a complex network of autonomic nerves that modulate the cardiac function. It comprises both sympathetic and parasympathetic fibres, and, the ganglia are predominantly parasympathetic.26

In this pooled analysis, we observed that the increase in heart rate post-ablation was significantly lower in the PFA group, both peri-procedurally and at mid-long-term follow-up, and this effect was consistent across age groups, genders, and types of thermal ablation (RFA and CBA). Correspondingly, serum S100B levels increased significantly less in the PFA group, supporting that PFA results to a substantially reduced degree of neural injury compared with thermal ablation.

The sustained increase in heart rate after thermal ablation based PVI supports the fact that thermal ablation causes parasympathetic denervation, whereas PFA preserves long-term integrity of autonomic function. These findings have been well demonstrated in sub-analyses of the ADVENT randomized trial19 and the CIRCA-DOSE randomized trial.7

Although endocardial PFA has limited impact on epicardially located ganglionated plexuses, epicardial PFA delivery directly to ganglionated plexi sites have been shown to resulted in neural damage and displayed antiarrhythmic effects by effective ablation of ganglionated plexuses. This may suggest that the significant distance between the ganglionated plexuses and the endocardium, combined with a field drop at the myocardium–fat interface and the isolating effects of the fat, rather than a real tissue selectivity of PFA may explain the reduced impact of endocardial PFA on autonomic nerved.27,28

Clinical translation of these findings

Assuming comparable durability of PVI lesions across PFA and thermal ablation, the differing autonomic effects may influence clinical outcomes. Thermal ablation can cause parasympathetic denervation, which might benefit patients with predominantly vagally mediated AF, but it also carries a higher risk of collateral injury and potential autonomic imbalance in other patients. In contrast, PFA causes less parasympathetic disruption, preserving cardiac autonomic function while sparing surrounding structures. These observations are hypothesis‐generating and require confirmation in prospective studies to clarify whether tailored strategies are needed for vagally mediated AF.

Potential implications for targeted patient selection

According to this pooled analysis, PFA based AF ablation seems to be suited for general AF patient population, in particular for those who carry potential risk of collateral damage, or require short procedure times, whereas thermal ablation (RFA or CBA) seems to be a reasonable option for patients with vagally mediated AF (with or without vagally mediated bradycardia). Beyond AF ablation, our findings may also hold potential relevance for emerging applications such as cardioneuroablation in the treatment of reflex syncope and functional bradyarrhythmias. Cardioneuroablation aims to selectively target cardiac autonomic ganglia to modulate vagal tone, and procedural efficacy is closely linked to the extent and durability of autonomic denervation. Given that PFA applied from endocardially appears to produce less vagal denervation compared with thermal ablation, this differential effect on the autonomic nervous system could influence future strategies and energy-source selection in this evolving field. These observations, however, are hypothesis-generating and require validation in dedicated prospective studies. Study strengths and limitations

As this pooled analysis includes a relatively small number of studies (change of heart rate n = 8; S100B, n = 2) investigating changes of heart rate between PFA and thermal ablation, a critical discussion of potential bias and limitations are mandatory. To further minimize bias, all comparisons were performed using random-effects model; sensitivity analysis and relevant subgroup analysis were also conducted. Firstly, one study was a randomized trial, five studies were analysed prospectively, and three studies were analysed retrospectively, though with small sample sizes. Only two studies included S100B measurements. Secondly, the assessment of the change of heart rate varied across studies, with some studies using ECGs, Holter monitoring, or standard heart rate measurements. To ensure consistency, we included only studies that calculated the change in heart rate themselves. Thirdly, publication bias could be possible for funnel plots do not provide definitive evidence, although our analysis revealed no signs of publication bias of the outcomes (as demonstrated in Supplementary material online, file: funnel plots). It should be noted that not all PFA systems are equivalent; variations in PFA waveform characteristics, catheter design, and the site of application (including endocardial vs. epicardial approaches) may significantly influence the extent and pattern of neural injury following ablation. The majority of the included studies used the pentaspline bipolar PFA system, and therefore the autonomic effects of other bipolar PFA platforms, of monopolar PFA platforms and of different catheter designs, remain uncertain. Based on the available reported data, baseline and follow-up use of beta-blockers and antiarrhythmic drugs appeared similar between groups. However, we acknowledge that changes or variations in their administration during follow-up could occur and might have influenced the observed heart rate and autonomic outcomes. Nonetheless, studies with longer follow-up are needed to evaluate the persistence of the autonomic denervation effect over time and its impact on freedom from arrhythmias.

Heart rate increase, measured peri-procedurally and during mid- to long-term follow-up, was significantly lower in the PFA group compared to the thermal ablation group (including cryoballoon and radiofrequency ablation). Notably, this difference remained consistent after adjusting for age, sex, and the type of thermal ablation modality used.

Translational perspective

Based on current evidence, PFA-based AF ablation appears well-suited for the general AF population—particularly for patients at increased risk of collateral damage or those who would benefit from a shorter procedure time. In contrast, thermal ablation modalities (radiofrequency or cryoballoon) may be more appropriate for patients with vagally mediated AF, with or without associated vagally mediated bradycardia.

However, further studies with extended follow-up are needed to assess the durability of autonomic denervation and its impact on arrhythmia recurrence (summarized in Graphical abstract).
