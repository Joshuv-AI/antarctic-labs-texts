Introduction

Catheter ablation is a cornerstone treatment for atrial fibrillation, with radiofrequency ablation (RFA) being widely used to achieve pulmonary vein isolation (PVI). Although the procedure is generally safe and effective, it is not without risks, including rare but serious vascular and pulmonary complications. Among these, alveolar hemorrhage stands out as a potentially life-threatening event, although its pathophysiological mechanisms remain poorly understood.

Alveolar hemorrhage typically presents with hemoptysis and respiratory distress during or after the procedure. Computed tomography (CT) is the diagnostic gold standard, often revealing ground-glass opacities in the acute phase. To date, only a few cases of pulmonary hemorrhage unrelated to pulmonary vein stenosis have been reported, after RFA. Housley et al1 and Rao et al2 documented immediate postprocedural hemoptysis confirmed by bronchoscopy, with CT findings suggestive of alveolar hemorrhage, and Leon et al3 described a delayed case occurring 6 days after the procedure. In all cases, no clear etiology was identified, whereas Fujii et al4 reported a pseudoaneurysm in the right superior pulmonary vein.

Given its severe and potentially fatal prognosis, early recognition and prompt management of alveolar hemorrhage are critical.

Case report

A 70-year-old patient with a history of breast cancer (treated with mastectomy, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy), overweight, and dyslipidemia was referred for persistent, symptomatic, drug-refractory atrial fibrillation (currently on amiodarone). Preprocedure evaluations, including transthoracic echocardiography and CT angiography, revealed mild left atrial dilation, preserved biventricular function, normal drainage of the pulmonary veins in the left atrium, and discrete fibrosing lung changes likely related to previous radiotherapy.

The patient underwent PVI using RFA under general anesthesia. Induction was achieved with fentanyl, lidocaine, and propofol, followed by neuromuscular blockade with rocuronium. Invasive ventilation was established via orotracheal intubation, which was successfully achieved on the first attempt. No evidence of trauma or complications was documented. General anesthesia was maintained with inhaled sevoflurane and nitrous oxide. Unfractionated heparin was administered for anticoagulation, with a total dose of 11,000 IU, achieving a maximum activated clotting time of 351 seconds. The procedure was performed using a multipolar mapping catheter, a 3.5 mm irrigated ablation catheter, and a steerable sheath. Catheter advancement into the pulmonary veins was smooth and unimpeded. Antral ablation was delivered at 35 W on the anterior wall (target ablation index of 500) and 30 W on the posterior wall (target ablation index of 380), followed by an additional anterior mitral line ablation (Figure 1).

The procedure, extubation, and postanesthetic period were uneventful, with no evidence of mucous plugging, laryngeal spasm, or other complications. Dabigatran was resumed 6 hours after the procedure after ruling out pericardial effusion. During the routine evening assessment by the nursing staff, the patient was clinically stable. During the night, she developed complaints of cough, although without respiratory distress, chest pain, or other associated respiratory symptoms. Peripheral oxygen saturation remained within the normal range.

The following morning, the patient developed hemoptysis, progressive dyspnea, and respiratory failure. CT angiography showed diffuse ground-glass opacities and a “crazy paving” pattern, consistent with diffuse alveolar hemorrhage (Figure 2). Dabigatran was reversed with idarucizumab, and she was transferred to the intensive care unit.

In the first few days after the procedure, the patient required high-flow oxygen therapy for hypoxemia. She remained clinically stable, with slight improvement in hemoptysis. However, subsequently, the respiratory status deteriorated, necessitating increased ventilatory support, including periods of noninvasive ventilation (continuous positive airway pressure of 6 mm Hg) by the third day after PVI. A nosocomial infection developed, requiring empiric broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy with intravenous vancomycin and piperacillin/tazobactam. No microbiologic agent was identified.

On the sixth day after PVI, the patient’s condition worsened further, with hypoxemia refractory to all interventions. Venovenous extracorporeal membrane oxygenation was initiated. Serial chest radiographs demonstrated no improvement, showing a persistent bilateral “white lung” appearance. Neither repeated chest CT nor bronchoscopy was performed during hospitalization.

After completion of antibiotic therapy, methylprednisolone was introduced in an attempt to improve respiratory function. Despite this, her condition continued to decline, progressing to refractory hypoxemia and multiorgan failure, culminating in death 18 days after PVI. No clinical autopsy was performed.

Discussion

This case illustrates a rare but devastating complication after RFA for atrial fibrillation. Multiple factors may have contributed to the development of diffuse alveolar hemorrhage in this patient, highlighting the possible multifactorial nature of this complication.

Preexisting pulmonary fibrosis and previous thoracic radiotherapy likely played a significant role. Fibrotic lung tissue is more fragile, with compromised alveolar-capillary integrity, which may predispose to hemorrhage under even minor hemodynamic or procedural stress. Previous chest radiotherapy further increases the susceptibility to injury.

Procedural factors must also be considered. Although the ablation was performed uneventfully, RFA involves localized thermal injury and manipulation of catheters within the left atrium and pulmonary veins, which can theoretically result in mechanical trauma or endothelial injury to adjacent pulmonary vessels.

Anesthetic management, although uneventful in this case, may also have been a contributing factor. Positive-pressure ventilation can transiently increase alveolar-capillary pressures, which may precipitate hemorrhage in already vulnerable lungs.

In summary, postablation alveolar hemorrhage in this patient may have resulted from the combined effects of preexisting pulmonary fibrosis and previous thoracic radiotherapy, along with procedural factors inherent to RFA and the potential hemodynamic impact of positive-pressure ventilation during anesthesia.

Conclusion

Alveolar hemorrhage is a rare but severe complication of atrial fibrillation ablation, so far only reported with thermal energy-based techniques such as cryoablation or RFA. Early diagnosis and intensive supportive care are essential, although outcomes can be poor. The potential influence of preexisting lung disease, previous chest radiotherapy, or anticoagulant type on its development has yet to be determined. Further research is needed to elucidate the underlying mechanisms, identify risk factors, and develop preventive strategies to mitigate this life-threatening complication.
