ATF6 ameliorates renal warm ischemia-reperfusion injury through FHL2-mediated NF-κB signaling pathway
Authors: Xuan Wu, Ji-Hua Shi, Yang Bai, Dong-Jing Yang, Meng-Yao Jia, Dan-Feng Guo, Jia-Kai Zhang, Xiao-Yi Shi, Hua-Peng Zhang, Wen-Zhi Guo, Shui-Jun Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryRenal ischemia-reperfusion (I/R) injury is a major cause of acute kidney injury and transplant dysfunction, involving endoplasmic reticulum stress. Although activating transcription factor 6 (ATF6) regulates ER stress resolution through the unfolded protein response, its specific role in renal I/R injury remains undefined. Here, we employed murine models of renal I/R and cellular hypoxia/reoxygenation (H/R) models to systematically investigate ATF6’s function. Our results show that I/R injury significantly upregulates ATF6 expression, particularly in proximal tubular epithelial cells. Functionally, ATF6 activation improved renal function and attenuated inflammation, whereas its inhibition exacerbated tubular damage. Mechanistically, we demonstrated that ATF6 transcriptionally represses four and a half LIM domain protein 2 (FHL2) through direct promoter binding. FHL2, in turn, interacts with TRAF6 to activate the nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB) pathway. ATF6 overexpression effectively counteracted FHL2-mediated NF-κB hyperactivation, establishing a protective ATF6/FHL2/NF-κB axis. These findings identify ATF6 as a key renoprotective factor and reveal mechanistic avenues for potential therapies targeting renal I/R injury and transplant complications.Subject areas: physiology, molecular biology

Introduction

Warm renal ischemia-reperfusion (I/R) injury constitutes a major cause of acute kidney injury (AKI), frequently occurring in clinical settings such as kidney transplantation and post-resuscitation care.1,2 During transplantation, warm I/R injury arises from the temporary interruption of renal blood flow and subsequent reperfusion, a process that can lead to tubular cell necrosis and apoptosis.2,3 The underlying pathophysiology involves a complex interplay of cellular and molecular events, with the inflammation of proximal tubular epithelial cells (PTECs) playing a central role.4

PTECs are central to renal pathophysiology, orchestrating vascular homeostasis and modulating inflammatory responses during I/R injury.5,6 The inflammatory cascade in renal warm I/R is driven by cytokines and chemokines released from both tubular cells and infiltrating immune cells. Notably, PTECs can dynamically regulate their injury response through alterations in chromatin accessibility.7,8 Key inflammatory mediators such as interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) are significantly elevated during I/R injury, contributing directly to renal dysfunction and tubular cell death.9,10

A pivotal feature in the pathophysiology of renal IRI is endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, which arises from an accumulation of misfolded or unfolded proteins within the ER lumen.11,12 This condition triggers the unfolded protein response (UPR)—an adaptive signaling network that can promote cellular survival under transient stress but may induce apoptosis if the stress persists. Among key UPR sensors, activating transcription factor 6 (ATF6) becomes activated during ER stress and plays a critical role in regulating its outcomes.13,14 The functional impact of ATF6, however, is highly context-dependent. For instance, in vascular endothelial cells under prolonged ER stress, ATF6 has been shown to alleviate oxidative stress and inhibit cell death. Recent studies further underscore the ability of ATF6 to interface with broader cellular processes, including autophagy and inflammation,15 highlighting its central role in determining cell fate under stress conditions. Our previous work demonstrated that ATF6 expressed in liver endothelial cells alleviates hepatic IRI via the CTGF-ERK1/2 pathway.16 Building on these findings, the present study seeks to define the renoprotective role of ATF6 in renal warm I/R injury and to decipher the underlying molecular mechanism, with a particular focus on the FHL2-mediated regulation of NF-κB signaling.

Results

Renal warm ischemia-reperfusion injury induced the expression of activating transcription factor 6

We performed high-throughput RNA sequencing to compare gene expression profiles between sham and I/R-treated kidney tissues. Transcriptome analysis identified 886 upregulated and 812 downregulated genes in the I/R group relative to sham controls (Figures S1A and S1B). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of these differentially expressed genes is presented in the bubble chart (Figure S1C). Notably, ATF6 expression was significantly elevated in the I/R group according to RNA-seq data.

To validate these findings, we examined ATF6 expression using RT-qPCR, IHC, and Western blot. RT-qPCR confirmed that ATF6 mRNA levels were significantly higher in I/R tissues than in sham controls (Figure 1A). This upregulation was even more pronounced at the protein level. Western blot analysis showed that both full-length and cleaved ATF6 were markedly increased in the I/R group (Figures 1B and 1C). IHC further revealed that ATF6 expression was predominantly localized to PTECs and strongly enhanced following I/R injury (Figures 1D and 1E).

Immunofluorescence confirmed that ATF6 was primarily expressed in PTECs, as indicated by colocalization with the marker CD10 (Figures S2A and S2B). To model I/R injury in vitro, we subjected HK-2 cell lines to H/R. Both RT-qPCR and Western blot analyses demonstrated that H/R treatment significantly induced the expression of activated ATF6 in these cells (Figures 1F–1H).

Together, these results indicate that I/R injury robustly induces ATF6 expression, particularly in renal PTECs, suggesting a potential role for ATF6 in the pathophysiology of renal I/R injury.

Activating transcription factor 6 in PTECs plays a protective role in renal warm ischemia-reperfusion injury

To explore the effect of ATF6 on kidney injury, we established a renal warm I/R model in mice using an ATF6 agonist (AA147) and an ATF6 antagonist (Ceapin-A7). Compared to the IR group, serum Cr and BUN levels were higher in the ATF6 antagonist group and lower in the ATF6 agonist group (Figures 2A and 2B). The serum levels of IL-6 and TNF-α were higher in the ATF6 antagonist group and lower in the ATF6 agonist group compared to the IR group (Figures 2C and 2D). Inhibition of ATF6 exacerbated IR injury in mouse kidneys following IR, as evidenced by increased damage to PTECs and tubular edema after using the ATF6 antagonist. In contrast, H&E staining showed that renal tissue damage was alleviated by using the ATF6 agonist (Figures 2E and 2F). We also used ELISA to detect the expression of IL-6 and TNF-α in mouse serum.

To further investigate the role of ATF6 in renal warm I/R injury, ATF6 in PTECs was knocked down through AAV-shRNA. Western blot and IHC demonstrated that the protein expression of ATF6 in PTECs was significantly decreased (Figure S4). Consistent with the histological results, ATF6 knockdown significantly increased the levels of common serum renal injury markers (Cr and BUN) (Figures 3A and 3B). Inflammatory markers (IL-6 and TNFα) in mouse serum also significantly increased (Figures 3C and 3D). Notably, the area of tubular damage in renal sections of AAV-shATF6 mice after I/R was significantly increased compared with AAV-shNC mice (Figures 3E and 3F). These results indicate that kidney PTECs-specific ATF6 deficiency exacerbates warm I/R-mediated renal injury.

Furthermore, we used the HK-2 cell line to explore the effect of ATF6 on renal injury in vitro. We treated the HK-2 cells with AA147 and Ceapin-A7 under normoxic or H/R conditions. The expression of proinflammatory factors, including IL-6 and TNF-α, was significantly decreased following AA147 and significantly increased after Ceapin-A7 (Figures 3G and 3H). The mRNA levels of TNFα and IL-6 did not change significantly after ATF6 knockdown using siRNA. When the cells were in a state of H/R, the secretion of TNFα and IL-6 increased in cells transfected with siATF6 after H/R stimulation (Figures 3I and 3J). These findings suggest that ATF6 in PTECs plays a protective role in renal warm I/R injury and reduces inflammation both in vivo and in vitro.

Activating transcription factor 6 inhibits kidney inflammatory injury through the nuclear factor kappa-B pathway

Our previous results demonstrated the protective role of ATF6 in renal warm I/R injury, yet the underlying mechanism remained to be elucidated. GO, and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) from RNA-seq data indicated significant enrichment in TNF and NF-κB signaling pathways among others (Figures S1C and S1D), suggesting a potential link between ATF6 and NF-κB-mediated inflammation during I/R injury.

To test this hypothesis, we first examined the activation status of the NF-κB pathway in I/R-injured kidney tissues and in HK-2 cells subjected to H/R. Consistent with the inflammation injury markers (IL-6, TNF-α, Cr, and BUN, Figures 3A–3D), Western blot analysis revealed a marked increase in the phosphorylation of NF-κB-p65 and IκBα following I/R or H/R treatment (Figure 4A), confirming NF-κB pathway activation under these conditions. We next assessed whether ATF6 modulates NF-κB signaling. Knockdown of ATF6 in HK-2 cells under H/R conditions led to a significant increase in the phosphorylation levels of p65 and IκBα (Figure 4C). Conversely, ATF6 overexpression suppressed the H/R-induced phosphorylation of these NF-κB pathway components (Figure 4E).

Together, these results indicate that ATF6 acts as a negative regulator of the NF-κB signaling pathway in the context of renal warm I/R injury.

Four and a half LIM domain protein 2 is transcriptionally regulated by activating transcription factor 6

Our previous RNA-seq data indicated that FHL2, as the downstream target of ATF6, was significantly upregulated after knocking down ATF6 (Figure 5A). At the same time, we used the JASPAR website to predict the possible downstream targets of ATF6. FHL2 is a scaffold protein that plays an important role in vivo and can regulate the NF-κB pathway in tissue-and cell-specific ways.17,18,19 This analysis identified that ATF6 potentially regulates the NF-κB through FHL2.

We first assessed FHL2 expression in I/R-injured mouse kidney tissues and observed a significant decrease in the I/R group compared with sham controls, as confirmed by Western blotting, RT-qPCR, and IHC (Figure S3). Conversely, ATF6 knockdown in PTECs via AAV-shATF6 in mice significantly upregulated FHL2 expression after I/R (Figures 5B–5E). The pharmacological inhibition of ATF6 also restored FHL2 expression in I/R kidneys, particularly in PTECs, whereas ATF6 activation did not cause further significant changes (Figures S3A–S3E).

Similarly, in HK-2 cells under H/R conditions, FHL2 expression patterns mirrored those observed in vivo (Figure 5F). ATF6 knockdown via siRNA increased FHL2 protein levels, whereas ATF6 overexpression via adenovirus (Ad-ATF6) reduced them (Figures 5H and 5J), supporting the notion that ATF6 transcriptionally suppresses FHL2.

To determine whether ATF6 directly binds the FHL2 promoter, we performed ChIP-qPCR in HK-2 cells, which confirmed ATF6 enrichment at the FHL2 promoter (Figure 5L). JASPAR database analysis predicted a high-confidence ATF6 binding site at positions −140/−127 of the human FHL2 promoter. Luciferase reporter assays showed that H/R significantly reduced activity in cells transfected with wild-type FHL2 promoter constructs, but not in those with mutated ATF6 binding sites (Figure 5N). As shown in Figure 5M, under H/R conditions, the relative luciferase activity of the WT-FHL2 promoter was significantly suppressed compared to the Normoxia group. However, this H/R-induced suppression was notably reversed in the MUT-FHL2 group, suggesting that the repression of FHL2 during H/R is dependent on this specific promoter region. To confirm the direct regulatory role of ATF6, we manipulated ATF6 levels under H/R conditions. Overexpression of ATF6 resulted in a marked reduction in the luciferase activity of the WT-FHL2 reporter, whereas the activity of the MUT-FHL2 reporter remained unaffected (Figure S5A). Conversely, knockdown of endogenous ATF6 using siATF6 significantly alleviated the repression of WT-FHL2 promoter activity compared to the siNC, while no significant difference was observed in the MUT group (Figure S5B).

To further verify the regulatory effect of ATF6/FHL2 on the NF-κB signaling pathway, a Western blot was used to detect the activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway in vitro. FHL2 overexpression promoted the phosphorylation levels of P65 and IκBa after H/R (Figures 6B and 6C). This indicates that FHL2 promotes the signal transduction of the NF-κB signaling pathway during H/R.

To study how ATF6/FHL2 regulates the NF-κB signaling pathway, we predicted through AlphaFold Server that FHL2 may interact with TRAF6, a key protein of NF-κB.20,21 Binding affinity analysis of the minimized complex was performed using the Prodigy web server, yielding a strong binding energy of −15.0 kcal/mol for the FHL2-TRAF6 interaction. We used immunofluorescence to detect the expression of TRAF6 and FHL2 in tissues.22 The results of immunofluorescence showed that in the I/R group, TRAF6 and FHL2 co-localized in the cytoplasm and other areas (Figure 6A), suggesting that the two proteins may be involved in the same biological process or interact with each other in cells. To verify whether there is protein interaction between FHL2 and TRAF6, we used the classic co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) experiment (Figure 6D). The results of the Co-IP experiment strongly confirmed that there is a direct interaction between the FHL2 protein and the TRAF6 protein.

To determine whether ATF6/FHL2 suppresses the NF-κB pathway via TRAF6, we performed a rescue experiment in HK-2 cells. Overexpression of FHL2 alone markedly increased the levels of TRAF6, phospho-IκBα, and phospho-p65, indicating enhanced NF-κB pathway activation. However, when ATF6 was co-overexpressed with FHL2, the expression of these NF-κB components—particularly phospho-p65—was significantly reduced compared to FHL2 overexpression alone (Figures S6A and S6B). These results suggest that ATF6 can counteract FHL2-induced NF-κB activation.

To further validate this regulatory relationship, we inhibited ATF6 using siRNA while overexpressing FHL2. Under these conditions, we observed a pronounced increase in NF-κB pathway activation (Figures S6C and S6D). This supports the conclusion that ATF6 normally restrains NF-κB signaling through the suppression of FHL2, and that the loss of ATF6 unleashes FHL2-mediated enhancement of the NF-κB pathway.

Discussion

Kidney transplantation serves as a cornerstone treatment for end-stage renal disease, significantly improving patient survival and quality of life.23 Nevertheless, warm ischemia-reperfusion injury (IRI) poses a major obstacle to transplant success, often leading to delayed graft function, increased rejection rates, and ultimately compromising long-term graft survival.3,9,24 A deeper understanding of its molecular mechanisms is therefore essential for developing targeted therapeutic strategies.

I/R injury induces substantial cellular damage and inflammation, largely driven by the activation of key signaling pathways such as NF-κB.25,26,27 As a central transcription factor, NF-κB regulates immune and inflammatory responses, as well as cell survival processes during I/R injury.18,28,29 In PTECs, its activation aggravates tubular damage and promotes a maladaptive inflammatory cascade. Studies have shown that the selective inhibition of NF-κB in T cells confers protection against renal I/R injury, underscoring the pivotal role of this pathway in disease progression.17,19,29 Furthermore, NF-κB activation enhances the expression of inflammatory mediators, including TNF-α and IL-6, which are markedly elevated during renal I/R and contribute significantly to the pro-inflammatory milieu.30,31

This study elucidates a regulatory axis involving ATF6/FHL2-TRAF6/NF-κB pathway in renal warm I/R injury, highlighting ATF6 as a critical protective mediator through its transcriptional suppression of FHL2 and subsequent modulation of inflammatory signaling. Our findings demonstrate that ATF6 activation in PTECs mitigates renal injury by attenuating NF-κB pathway activation, while its inhibition exacerbates inflammation and tubular damage. These findings were further validated in vitro using I/R injury cell models with HK-2 cell lines.

As a key transcription factor in the UPR, ATF6 alleviates ER stress by enhancing protein-folding capacity and modulating oxidative stress, autophagy, and inflammation, thereby promoting cell survival under stress conditions.16,32 Our RNA-seq analysis revealed significant upregulation of ATF6 and marked enrichment of the NF-κB pathway in mouse kidneys following renal IRI, suggesting that both play important roles in warm I/R injury. Consistent with this, ATF6 expression was significantly increased in both in vivo and in vitro IRI models. Although we initially attempted to generate ATF6-knockout cells using CRISPR-Cas9, observed effects on cell death after ATF6 knockout led us to employ siRNA-mediated knockdown and pharmacological antagonist for subsequent functional studies. Further supporting a protective role, administration of an ATF6 agonist significantly attenuated renal injury, as reflected by reduced serum Cr and BUN levels, whereas ATF6 inhibition exacerbated renal damage. These findings underscore the therapeutic potential of ATF6 agonists such as AA147, warranting further investigation in preclinical renal transplantation models. Future validation using human kidney organoids or transplant biopsy specimens will be essential for clinical translation.

A key finding of this study is the identification of FHL2 as a downstream transcriptional target of ATF6, uncovering a previously unknown pathway that connects ER stress to inflammatory signaling under ischemic conditions. Although FHL2 has been associated with renal tubular cell survival, its direct regulation by ATF6 in the context of I/R injury represents a mechanistic insight.33,34 Using ChIP and dual-luciferase reporter assays,35,36 we demonstrated that ATF6 transcriptionally represses FHL2 through direct binding to its promoter region at the −140/−127 site. This regulatory relationship was further confirmed in vitro, where ATF6 knockdown increased FHL2 expression, whereas ATF6 overexpression suppressed it. Notably, the downregulation of FHL2 in IRI and its recovery upon ATF6 inhibition suggest that the ATF6-mediated repression of FHL2 acts as an adaptive mechanism to restrain NF-κB-dependent inflammation.

Our study demonstrates that FHL2 interacts with TRAF6 with high binding affinity and hydrogen bond-mediated stability, suggesting that FHL2 acts as a scaffold or co-activator to enhance TRAF6-dependent NF-κB activation during renal IRI. Although the FHL2–TRAF6 interaction has been reported in other contexts,37,38,39 our work defines its functional significance in renal I/R injury and its direct regulation by ATF6. This kidney-specific mechanism distinguishes our findings from earlier studies in osteoclasts or hepatocytes. Furthermore, we identify a protective role for ATF6 in renal IRI that extends beyond its canonical function in resolving ER stress. ATF6 also exerts anti-inflammatory effects in endothelial and tubular cells, indicating its dual role in modulating both proteostatic and inflammatory pathways. These findings position ATF6 activation as a multifaceted therapeutic strategy in renal IRI.

In summary, this study establishes ATF6 as a central regulator in renal IRI by transcriptionally repressing FHL2 and thereby inhibiting TRAF6/NF-κB-driven inflammation. These insights advance our understanding of ER stress-immune interactions in AKI and open avenues for targeting the ATF6–FHL2 axis as a therapeutic strategy. Future work should focus on the temporal regulation of this pathway during injury and its role in other inflammatory kidney diseases.

Limitations of the study

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, our findings are primarily derived from murine models and immortalized cell lines, which may not fully capture the complexity of human renal pathophysiology. Validation in human kidney tissues or patient-derived organoids would improve the clinical translatability of our results. Second, although we have established the functional importance of the ATF6–FHL2–NF-κB axis, other upstream modulators or parallel signaling pathways that may influence this network remain uncharacterized. For example, potential crosstalk between ATF6 and other UPR branches, such as PERK or IRE1α, could contribute to inflammatory regulation in IRI.40 Although using only male mice minimized inter-individual variability, it limits our conclusions to males. The potential for sex-specific differences, therefore, requires investigation in future studies. Finally, the therapeutic relevance of targeting ATF6 or FHL2 in renal IRI warrants further investigation through well-designed in vivo studies employing pharmacological or genetic approaches in clinically relevant animal models.

Experimental model and study participant details

Renal warm I/R injury model of mice

Male C57BL6/N mice (6–8 weeks old) were purchased from Vital River (Batch number: 422023600013109, Beijing, China). The mice were maintained in specific-pathogen-free grade cages with constant temperature (22 ± 1°C) in a 12/12h dark/light cycle. We randomly assigned C57BL/6N mice to sham-operated (Sham, n = 6) or I/R (n = 6) groups. The I/R group received pentobarbital sodium anesthesia followed by renal pedicle occlusion. Mouse renal warm I/R injury model was used to mimic the I/R injury. After 60 min of ischemia, we restored blood flow for 6h or 24h reperfusion before euthanasia and sample collection. Sham controls underwent identical procedures without vascular clamping. We assessed protein expression via Western blot and mRNA levels by RNA-seq, RT-qPCR in renal tissues. Kidney injury was assessed by measuring Cr and BUN levels, as well as histological examination through hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining.

For PTECs-specific ATF6 knockdown, adeno-associated viruses (AAV)-based delivery system with pAAV9-MCS-miR30shRNA (Atf6)-WPRE or pAAV9-MCS-miR30shRNA (NC)-WPRE (0.5 × 1012 vg, OBiO Technology, Shanghai, China) were injected by tail vein injection to C57BL/6 mice. Three weeks after viral injection, gene detection and I/R model were employed (N = 6 per group at each time point). ATF6 knockdown in mice was verified by western-blot and IHC.

Cell culture and treatment

Human kidney proximal tubular cell (HK-2; Cell Bank of Chinese Academy of Sciences, Shanghai) was maintained in Endothelial Cell Medium (ECM; Gibco 11330-032) and DMEM/F12 (Gibco 11330-032) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum. 293T cell (Cell Bank of Chinese Academy of Sciences, Shanghai) was maintained under standardized culture protocols along with 293T embryonic kidney cells (DMEM). To simulate warm I/R injury, cells underwent 2h hypoxia (1% O₂, 5% CO₂, 94% N₂) in a Galaxy 48 R chamber (Eppendorf), followed by 6h normoxic reoxygenation (21% O₂, 5% CO₂). All cell lines were identified by short tandem repeat and routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination, with all results negative.

Construction of overexpression and knockdown cell lines

To generate HK-2 cells overexpressing ATF6 (designated as ATF6-OE), we employed the jetPRIME transfection system from PolyPlus (Shanghai, China) along with the following plasmids: pLVX-mNeonGreen-nATF6, pCMV-dR8.2 dvpr, and pCMV-VSV-G. In parallel, ATF6-targeting siRNAs were produced by GenePharma (Shanghai, China) and introduced into cells using the jetPRIME transfection reagent (PolyPlus, Shanghai, China), as detailed in Table S1. Total RNA from cells overexpressing ATF6 was isolated using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and further subjected to high-throughput sequencing. The sequencing results identified FHL2 as a possible downstream target of ATF6.

Method details

RNA-seq

Mice kidney samples (n = 5) was used for total mRNA extraction, following the protocol provided with the mirVana mRNA Isolation Reagent (Ambion, USA). The Illumina HiSeq X Ten platform (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) was employed for transcriptome sequencing, and bioinformatics analysis was carried out in partnership with OE Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Enzyme linked Immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Inflammatory cytokine levels in the liver were measured according to the manufacturer’s protocols (KE20001, Proteintech, Wuhan, China; EK0412, Boster Biological Technology, Wuhan, China).

Western blot analysis

Western blotting was performed as described. Primary antibodies included: anti-ATF6 (1:1000, 24169-1-AP, Proteintech), anti-FHL2 (1:1000, 21619-1-AP, Proteintech), anti-NF-κB p65 (1:1000, 10745-1-AP, Proteintech), anti-phospho-NF-κB p65 (1:1000, 82335-1-RR, Proteintech), anti-TRAF6 (1:1000, AF5376, Affinity Biosciences), anti-IκBα (1:1000, 10268-1-AP, Proteintech), anti-phospho-IκBα (1:1000, 82349-1-RR, Proteintech), anti-HA (1:500, GB12939, Servicebio), and anti-Flag (1:500, GB15938, Servicebio). Anti-GAPDH (1:5000, 60004-1-Ig, Proteintech) served as loading control. Horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies (anti-rabbit: 1:2000, SA00001-2; anti-mouse: 1:2000, SA00001-1; Proteintech) were used for detection.

RT-qPCR

RT-qPCR analyses were performed with ChamQ TM SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Q711-02, Vazyme, Nanjing, China) for quantification of messenger RNA expression. The primer sequences for the target genes analyzed in this study are provided in Table S1.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Formalin-fixed kidney sections were processed using standard protocols. Sections were incubated with anti-ATF6 1:200, 24169-1-AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), anti-FHL2 (1:200, 21619-1-AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), or anti-TRAF6 (1:200, AF5376, Affinity Biosciences, Zhenjiang, China). Detection used IHC reagents (1:100, SPN-9001/SPN-9002, Beijing Zhongshan Golden Bridge Biotechnology Co., Ltd.).

Immunofluorescence (IF)

Immunofluorescence was conducted with primary antibodies against ATF6 1:50, 24169-1-AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), CD10 (1:50, 18008-1-AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), CD31 (1:50, 11265-1-AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), FHL2 (1:50, 21619-1-AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), or TRAF6 (1:50, AF5376, Affinity Biosciences, Zhenjiang, China).

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)

H/R-treated HK-2 cell was analyzed using a ChIP Kit (KT101-01, GZSCBIO, Guangzhou, China). Chromatin was immunoprecipitated with anti-ATF6 (1:500, sc-166659, Santa Cruz Technology, Texas, USA) or rabbit IgG control (KT101-01, GZSCBIO, Guangzhou, China). Precipitated DNA was quantified by RT-qPCR with primers from Table S1.

Luciferase assay

A dual-luciferase reporter assay was performed as previously to confirm the regulation of ATF6 on FHL2. The promoter region DNA sequences of the human FHL2 gene were cloned into the psiCHECK-2 plasmid vector (C8021, Promega, Beijing, China). The constructed plasmids were then transfected into 293T cells. Luciferase activity was measured using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (E1910, Promega, Beijing, China) and normalized to Renilla luciferase activity.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

For co-immunoprecipitation experiments, cells were lysed using IP lysis buffer (A10023, abmart, Shanghai, China), and the lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 x g for 10 min at 4°C to pellet cellular debris. The clarified protein lysates were incubated overnight at 4°C with Protein A + G agarose beads (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) and the corresponding primary antibodies. Subsequently, the beads were washed three times with IP lysis buffer to remove unbound proteins. The immunoprecipitated protein complexes were eluted from the beads by boiling in 2X loading buffer at 95°C for 5 min. Finally, the eluted proteins were subjected to immunoblot analysis.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Normally distributed continuous variables are presented as the means ± standard deviations (SDs). Categorical variables are tested using the chi-square test. Data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro‒Wilk test. Intergroup comparisons were performed with two-tailed Student’s t tests for parametric data, whereas one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed for multigroup comparisons. All analyses were conducted using SPSS software version 21.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA), with statistical significance thresholds set at ∗p < 0.05 after the false discovery rate adjustment for multiple comparisons.
