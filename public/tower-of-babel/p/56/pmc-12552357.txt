Dermatofibrosarcoma Protuberans (DFSP): Current Treatments and Clinical Trials
Authors: Piotr Remiszewski, Agata Pisklak, Kinga Filipek, Mateusz J Spałek, Anna Szumera -Ciećkiewicz, Bartłomiej Szostakowski, Maria Krotewicz, Anna M Czarnecka
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Opinion StatementDermatofibrosarcoma protuberans (DFSP) is a rare soft tissue sarcoma with an incidence of 0.008 to 0.045 cases per 100,000 per year, accounting for less than 1% of all soft tissue sarcomas. It mainly affects adults aged 30–50 years, usually on the trunk and proximal extremities. DFSP is Locally aggressive, with metastases occurring in up to 5% of cases, typically < 1%. Diagnosis is based on histology, including CD34 antigen expression and COL1A1-PDGFB fusion detection by FISH or RT-PCR. Mohs micrographic surgery is the mainstay of treatment, ensuring clear margins to minimise recurrence. Radiotherapy is used as adjuvant or preoperative therapy to improve Local control. Imatinib, a tyrosine kinase inhibitor, is highly effective in unresectable or metastatic DFSP, especially in cases with PDGFB mutations, achieving disease control in over 70% of patients and partial responses in 36%. The 10-year overall survival rate is 90.7%, although the fibrosarcomatous variant (DFSP-FS) has worse outcomes, with a 5-year Local progression-free survival rate of 33%. Rare metastases to the lungs, lymph nodes or brain are treated surgically; chemotherapy remains ineffective. Introduction

Epidemiology

Dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans (DFSP) is one of the rarest tumours, with an incidence rate between 0.008 and 0.045 cases per one hundred thousand persons per year [1]. It accounts for less about 0.1% of all malignancies and 1.8%−6% of all soft tissue sarcomas [2–4]. There have been two extensive epidemiological studies in the United States [3, 5]. The first one [3] analysed 2885 cases that occurred in the years 1973 to 2002, whereas the second one analysed 7250 cases in the years 2000 to 2010. There are more cases of DFSP occurring in women than in men, although there is a slight predominance [6]. Data indicates a significant disparity in case prevalence between Black and White populations, with higher amongst Black individuals [3]. DFSP occurs with an increased incidence between the ages of 30 and 50 [3]. However, congenital and paediatric cases have also been reported [7, 8].

Prognosis

Studies suggest that the prognosis for DFSP is generally good, especially if complete surgical resection is achieved (Table 1). For example, one study found that the 10- and 15-year OS rates were 90.7% and 85.7%, respectively. The 10- and 15-year DFSP-specific survival rates were 98.5% and 98.0%, respectively [27]. However, factors such as fibrosarcomatous transformation (FS), advanced age, male gender, larger tumours (≥ 10 cm), positive surgical margins and multiple recurrences are associated with an unfavourable prognosis [28]. Although rare (less than 1% of cases), metastasis can occur and is associated with a much worse prognosis. Follow-up recommendations include clinical assessment every 6 months for the first 5 years, then annually, with imaging reserved for recurrent DFSP or DFSP with fibrosarcomatous changes [29]. Some guidelines recommend patient education about regular self-examination. The systematic review [13] identifies significant prognostic factors for DFSP with and without fibrosarcomatous (FS) change. DFSP-FS showed a significantly higher risk of adverse outcomes compared to classic DFSP. Specifically, the risk of Local recurrence was 29.8% in DFSP-FS, significantly higher than the 13.7% observed in DFSP, with a risk ratio (RR) of 2.2 and a 95% confidence interval (CI) of 1.7–2.9. The metastasis rate in DFSP-FS was significantly higher at 14.4% compared to 1.1% in DFSP, with an RR of 5.5 and a CI of 4.3-7.0. Similarly, the disease-specific death rate was 14.7% in DFSP-FS compared with 0.8% in DFSP, giving an RR of 6.2 with a CI of 5.0-7.8. These results highlight the aggressive nature of DFSP-FS. Notably, the extent of FS change, which ranged from 5 to 100%, did not significantly influence these results, as even cases with minimal FS change experienced metastasis and disease-specific death.

Locally Advanced DFSP

The primary goal in the treatment of DFSP is complete surgical removal of the tumour (Fig. 1) [8, 30]. DFSP tumours are generally considered resectable if confined to the skin and subcutaneous tissue without distant metastases [8, 31]. However, large tumours or those with fibrosarcomatous transformation can be challenging [32, 33]. A multidisciplinary approach involving surgical oncologists and plastic surgeons is often used in these cases [34]. In specific cases, such as vulvar DFSP, radical vulvectomy may be considered [35]. Narrow surgical margins (< 3 cm) are associated with a higher risk of local recurrence [10]. A meta-analysis of eight trials showed that margins ≥ 3 cm were significantly better at reducing local recurrence than margins < 3 cm margin with the recurrence rate (RR = 0.17, 95% CI 0.09–0.31) and the positive surgical margin rate (RR = 0.09, 95% CI 0.02–0.46) [10]. This highlights the advantage of Mohs Micrographic Surgery (MMS) in achieving clear margins with minimal tissue removal. The median maximum lateral margin of 1–2 cm used in MMS trials has been shown to result in complete excision of most tumours [36–38]. In a study of 103 DFSP cases treated with MMS, 41 of which were in the head and neck, a median first layer margin of 1.6 cm was used [30].In addition, larger tumours (≥ 5 cm) are more prone to local recurrence [29]. The likelihood that a given width of excision would remove the entire microscopic extent of a DFSP tumour was determined as follows: a width of 1 cm would leave residual tumour in 70.7% of cases, 2 cm in 39.7%, 3 cm in 15.5% and 5 cm in 5.2% [39]. Local spread and recurrence of DFSPs are primarily attributed to incomplete surgical excision, where the tumour is not completely removed [36, 38, 40].

Diagnostic and Treatment Algorithm for DFSP

MMS involves the step-by-step removal of thin layers of tissue (Fig. 2), with each layer examined microscopically to map the extent of the tumour [12, 41]. This method is particularly effective for DFSP, which often grows asymmetrically with subclinical extensions that are not visible on the skin surface. The key advantage of MMS is its ability to assess nearly 100% of the tumour margins, ensuring complete removal of cancer cells and minimising damage to surrounding healthy tissue [41]. However, historically, complete resection of DFSP has relied on wide local excision (WLE), in which the tumour is removed with 2–3 cm margins of healthy tissue surrounding the lesion (Fig. 3) [41]. This technique has been the standard approach, as described in the literature, to ensure negative margins and reduce the risk of recurrence (Fig. 4). However, recurrence rates following WLE remain high, ranging from 13 to 73% [42], prompting a re-evaluation of surgical strategies. Mohs micrographic surgery (MMS) has become the preferred method for DFSP Due to its ability to provide intraoperative microscopic control of surgical margins, significantly reducing recurrence rates to less than 1% (ranging from 0.0 to 8.3%) [39, 41, 43–46]. This technique has shown particular advantages in anatomically sensitive areas such as the cervicofacial region, where preserving healthy tissue while maintaining oncological control is critical. While a pathologist may also be involved due to the surgeon’s background and regulatory constraints, the surgeon’s understanding of the tissue margins informs the re-excision process. The tissue is divided, stained and mapped by the surgeon, who works with a pathologist to define areas that require additional excision. The standard fresh tissue technique is sometimes used, but the permanent tissue slow MMS technique is often preferred, where the tissue is paraffin-embedded and sectioned horizontally after 48 h, allowing more detailed examination. This slow MMS technique, sometimes called modified MMS, uses formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue for horizontal sectioning, allowing detailed pathological analysis and CD34 immunostaining. This method is similar to the Mohs Tubingen Technique (MTT), which uses initial margins of 0.5 cm and a thin circular strip of tissue for analysis. Immunohistochemical staining with CD34 is useful in assessing tumour margins, particularly in recurrent cases and in differentiating DFSP from other conditions. Although some surgeons use intraoperative immunostaining with frozen sections, it’s often considered less reliable than permanent sections. The average number of MMS layers required is around 1.6, but only 34.4% of lesions are completely removed after the first layer. Once clear margins are confirmed, the wound is repaired depending on the location, size, depth and patient preference, sometimes requiring general anaesthesia.

The complex growth patterns, combined with the surgeon’s goal of preserving healthy tissue, present significant surgical challenges. In addition, the infiltrative growth and poor cytological characteristics of DFSP make accurate microscopic margin assessment difficult for pathologists. Therefore, three-dimensional histology surgery (3D histology) was developed [47]. This retrospective case series analysed 29 patients treated between 2000 and 2011. The mean age of the participants was 40.7 years, with a roughly even gender distribution. Tumours were predominantly located on the trunk (52%), followed by the extremities (38%) and the head and neck (10%). Seventeen patients presented with previously incompletely excised tumours, while 12 had primary lesions. The surgical approach was to excise the tumour with 1–1.5 cm lateral margins and fascia. The excised tissue was meticulously processed for histological examination using paraffin embedding, which provides greater diagnostic accuracy than traditional frozen sectioning. Any positive margins required further excision in a systematic staged manner until tumour clearance was confirmed. The results showed that an average of 1.4 surgical stages were required to achieve tumour-free margins. The mean post-operative defect size was 26 cm², with closure achieved by direct sutures in most cases (66%), Local flaps in 31% and skin grafting in one patient. Notably, there were no reported surgical complications. Over a mean follow-up of 68 months, no recurrence was observed (0% recurrence rate), underlining the oncological efficacy of this technique. In addition, histological examination of the excised margins confirmed the absence of residual tumour invasion. One patient with an initially unresectable tumour underwent neoadjuvant therapy with imatinib mesylate, which facilitated surgical resection. The study highlights the effectiveness of 3D histology surgery with paraffin sections for DFSP. By minimising excision margins while ensuring tumour clearance, the approach achieved favourable aesthetic and functional outcomes without compromising oncological integrity.

Regardless of the surgical technique used, it is important to remove the deep fascia to eliminate any potentially infiltrating tumour cells [6]. This is because DFSP can invade surrounding tissue through fat Lobules and fascial planes into the muscle. In cases where the tumour extends deeper, such as in stage IIB, excision of the muscle fascia or periosteum is recommended. A retrospective analysis of 48 patients [37] with DFSP treated with micrographic surgery evaluated the feasibility and safety of fascial preservation During tumour excision. Histological evaluation revealed that 93% of tumours on the trunk and extremities were confined above the fascia, compared with only 14% of tumours in the head and neck region. Head and neck tumour location was identified as a significant risk factor for subcutaneous extension (p < 0.001). Overall, fascial preservation was achieved in 44% of cases, while deeper excision was required in 56% of cases. Among the cases, two deeply infiltrating head tumours recurred (4%), but fascial preservation was not attempted in these lesions. These results suggest that micrographic surgery can safely preserve fascia in superficial DFSP outside the head and neck region.

Reconstruction of the tissue defect should be delayed until thorough examination confirms that all peripheral and deep margins are negative. Large skin grafts should be delayed until histopathological results confirm the microscopic radicality of the procedure. Plastic surgery techniques may make it difficult to detect early local recurrence of the disease (Table 2). Postponement of reconstruction is recommended in cases requiring extensive resection or tissue manipulation, to allow confirmation of negative margins before proceeding. Options for reconstruction include rotational flaps or full or split thickness skin grafts, depending on the size, depth and location of the defect [48]. Local flaps are often the first choice for smaller or less complex defects, especially in areas such as the abdomen, trunk or proximal extremities. These include rotational or propeller flaps, which are designed according to the specific tissue Loss and anatomical location. Local flaps offer the advantage of preserving the functionality of nearby tissues and providing a good aesthetic match. For larger or more complex defects, free flaps such as the anterolateral femoral flap or the latissimus dorsi flap are often used. These free flaps are particularly valuable for covering large areas that local flaps cannot reach. For example, latissimus dorsi free flaps have been successfully used for abdominal reconstruction, combining coverage of musculoaponeurotic and superficial defects. In addition, free flaps allow tailored reconstruction in areas with composite tissue requirements, such as the forearm and shoulder. In the chest wall, pectoralis major or myocutaneous flaps are commonly used for defects up to 15× 23 cm. However, the use of these flaps may be limited by previous interventions, such as coronary artery revascularisation using the internal mammary artery. For the periumbilical region, free tissue transfer is preferred due to the lack of viable local flap options, whereas pedicled femoral flaps can effectively address hypogastric defects. Innovative techniques, such as the use of thoracoabdominal flaps for chest wall sarcomas and supraclavicular artery island flaps for the parotid region, highlight the adaptability of reconstruction strategies to achieve both functional and aesthetic results. In the head and neck, rotational scalp flaps based on major vascular territories have been successfully used to reconstruct significant defects [48]. Reconstruction efficacy is closely related to achieving tumour-free surgical margins, as recurrence often requires additional surgery and affects long-term outcomes. Studies have shown that wider resection margins correlate with increased use of reconstructive flaps and improved recurrence-free survival rates. For example, free anterolateral femoral flaps have shown favourable results with minimal recurrence and good functional outcomes.

The ideal width of surgical margins is still debated. While some guidelines recommend a margin of 2 cm, others suggest 3–5 cm for wide Local excision. Data suggest that a 1 cm margin is often insufficient, with studies showing high rates of positive margins at this width. For example, a 1997 study found that 70% of patients had positive margins with a 1 cm margin. A 2 cm margin around the tumour would leave microscopic residual mass in 39.7% of cases, 3 cm in 15.5% and 5 cm in 5.2%. Therefore, a 3 cm margin seems to be more effective in preventing recurrence [60]. Goldblum et al. [61]. suggested an intriguing hypothesis that DFSPs with clear surgical margins might represent a less aggressive subset of these tumours. The National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines recommend margins of 2–4 cm for standard excision of DFSP. A study [62] conducted at three tertiary centres evaluated the adequacy of surgical margins for DFSP, focusing on their impact on recurrence rates and associated surgical morbidity. A total of 279 patients with a median age of 39 years and a median follow-up of 50 months were analysed. Clear pathological margins were achieved in 86% of cases overall, although results varied depending on the surgical approach and initial treatment setting. The study revealed a significant discrepancy between the macroscopic surgical margins observed at surgery and the final pathological margins. The median difference was 22 mm, ranging from − 10 to 46 mm. Patients with fibrosarcomatous (FS) changes had higher recurrence rates (21.9%) than those without (6.6%), highlighting the need for tailored surgical strategies in these cases. Wide resection margins correlated with a higher Likelihood of achieving clear pathological margins, but required more reconstructive procedures. For example, 69% of patients who underwent reconstruction did so before margin analysis, some of whom were later found to have positive margins. The median resection margin in this group was 3 cm. In patients with smaller surgical margins (< 3 cm), clear pathological margins were achieved in 87%, while all patients with 3 cm margins achieved clear results. However, the width of the pathological margin beyond 1 mm had no significant effect on recurrence rates. Patients initially treated at tertiary centres had better outcomes, with clear margins achieved in 93% of primary resections. In contrast, only 9% of patients referred after unplanned excisions had clear margins at the first operation. This group required an average of two operations to achieve adequate resection, highlighting the benefits of early, expert surgical intervention. Adjuvant treatments included radiotherapy in 6% of cases and neoadjuvant imatinib for selected patients with large or anatomically challenging tumours. Both modalities were shown to be effective, with imatinib contributing to tumour shrinkage and radiotherapy aiding local control in patients with close or positive margins. The study concludes that a balance must be struck between achieving clear margins and minimising surgical morbidity. While wider margins improve the likelihood of clear resection, the associated need for complex reconstructions must be carefully considered. The results support individualised surgical planning based on tumour characteristics and patient factors, supported by expert multidisciplinary care [62].

Radiotherapy

DFSP has a high local recurrence rate, and the treatment aims to reduce the recurrence probability. Radical treatment includes surgery to obtain clear margins, as sufficient resection significantly reduces the risk of recurrence. However, anatomical limitations (e.g., perineal area, face) may prevent wide excision due to potential disfigurement or functional impairment. Adjuvant radiotherapy may improve local control in patients with close or positive margins [63].

National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) Guidelines Version 1.2024 for Dermatofibrosarcoma Protuberans defines radiotherapy’s role in treating DFSP in different circumstances. For adjuvant radiotherapy in cases with positive margins or gross disease, doses of 50–60 Gy are recommended for indeterminate or positive margins, with up to 66 Gy for positive margins or gross tumour, administered in 2-Gy fractions per day. The radiation Fields should extend widely beyond surgical margins e.g., 3–5 cm when clinically feasible. In cases with negative margins, radiotherapy is not recommended if Mohs surgery or other forms of peripheral and deep en face margin assessment (PDEMA) are utilised. However, if these techniques are not used, radiotherapy should be considered if the margins are less than 1 cm. For recurrence or metastasis where further resection is not feasible and RT has not been previously administered, similar RT doses and field extensions are recommended for adjuvant radiotherapy in cases with positive margins or gross disease [64].

The role of radiotherapy in treating DFSP has been explored in multiple retrospective studies. Most of the patients in these studies underwent surgical tumour removal before radiotherapy. A recent systematic review [65] analysed twenty-two retrospective studies published between 1989 and 2023, with 994 patients included. The review highlighted critical findings regarding the role of adjuvant radiotherapy after primary surgical management, indicating it should be considered as an adjuvant treatment in the presence of risk factors such as close margins, recurrent tumours, or fibrosarcomatous transformation of dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans (FS-DFSP), and as a salvage treatment for positive margins. Total doses ranged from 50 to 70 Gy in conventional fractions, depending on the tumour size and location, with adverse events infrequently reported. The analysis also outlined fractionation schemes, highlighting that standard fractionation (1.8–2.0 Gy per fraction) was the most commonly used approach, while hypofractionated regimens were rarely used. Dose escalation beyond 66 Gy was primarily reserved for cases with gross residual disease, whereas lower doses (50–60 Gy) were sufficient for microscopic residual disease or in the adjuvant setting (Fig. 5). Overall, radiotherapy remains an effective modality in multimodal treatment with an acceptable toxicity profile.

Adjuvant radiotherapy for vulvar DFSP after non-radical surgery

Only a few studies evaluated preoperative radiotherapy’s role in managing DFSP. Marks et al. [66] reported one female patient treated with preoperative radiotherapy, who had a history of 13 years of recurrent DFSP. The patient received 46 Gy preoperative radiotherapy in 23 fractions over 34 days for a new lesion in the previous scar, which was surgically removed three weeks later with 18 Gy of postoperative boost. The patient showed no evidence of the disease at 38 months of follow-up. Suit et al. [67] reported 3 patients who were administered preoperative radiotherapy. No detailed data about the patients, indications, or dosage were provided. The patients had no evidence of disease at follow-up (length not specified for those patients). Ballo et al. [68] reported two cases of patients treated with preoperative radiotherapy who received 50 Gy, and in both cases, the final margins after resection were negative. Castle et al. (2013) [69], in a retrospective study with 53 cases of DFSP, included seven patients who received radiotherapy before re-excision, with a total dose of 50–60 Gy. In this study, 98% of all patients achieved local control at five years of follow-up. However, due to the limited number of studies and cases, further research is needed to fully understand the efficacy and optimal protocols for preoperative radiotherapy in DFSP.

Radical radiotherapy might serve as an option for patients with unresectable DFSP or for those deemed unsuitable for surgery due to comorbidities. Additionally, as an option when surgical resection would result in the removal of extensive tissue, leading to significant disfigurement or loss of function. The limited number of cases treated with radiotherapy as the sole local therapy for control of DFSP have been described in the literature (Table 3). Marks et al. [66] reported three patients managed with radical radiotherapy. Two of those patients showed no evidence of the disease at 24 and 26 months of follow-up, and one patient with extensive nodularity at the right side of the chin at 33 months of follow-up showed slowly resolving nodules. Further follow-up of those patients was reported by Suit et al. [67]. Each of the three patients was free of Local and distant relapse and in overall good condition at 85–108 months of observation. Cosmetic results of the procedure in two of the three patients were good, mild atrophy and telangiectasia were observed in one patient. Mark et al. [70] reported a case of an 83-year-old patient who received radical radiotherapy and was qualified as disease-free, the patient died 3 years after treatment with no evidence of recurrence (cause of death not given). Ballo et al. [68] reported a case of a female patient with a tumour in the right labia, histologically typical DFSP, who received radiotherapy after double valvulectomy for recurrent disease. The patient despite radiotherapy followed by chemotherapy died from metastatic disease. Hamid et al. [72] reported another case of a patient who developed metastatic disease despite undergoing radiotherapy and died three years later (details of the histopathological examination were not provided for this patient). Additionally, five other patients included in this study, receiving only radiotherapy, were still alive at the time of publication. However, the study did not provide details about the length of follow-up for these patients or whether any of them experienced a recurrence. Williams et al. [73] reported another case of a 53-year-old male patient who was qualified as non-operative due to multiple comorbidities and received radical radiotherapy, achieving Local control at an 11-month follow-up. Llombart et al. [74] emphasize an approach that seeks to balance the therapeutic benefits with the potential risks, highlighting the potential risk of radiotherapy inducing another sarcoma in the treated area.

Treatment of Metastatic/unresectable Disease

Palliative Radiotherapy

Palliative radiotherapy is a treatment modality employed for DFSP when complete surgical resection is not feasible or when the disease is metastatic. This treatment focuses on symptom management and improving quality of life [10, 63, 75].

Despite the challenge of local control with radiation alone, the role of radiation in palliating symptoms arising from local or distant metastatic disease is significant. The primary objective of palliative radiotherapy in DFSP is to alleviate symptoms such as pain or dyspnoea, which often requires achieving some degree of tumour shrinkage. However, in cases where symptoms are minimal, the primary objective may be disease stabilisation, to prolong patients’ quality of life [63, 76].

The approach to palliative radiotherapy is informed by the presence and severity of symptoms, as well as the potential toxicities of the treatment. A range of dose-fractionation regimens can be employed, with the selection being made on an individual patient basis. These regimens may include a single fraction of 8 Gy, or multiple fractions such as 20 Gy in 5 fractions over 1 week, 30 Gy in 10 fractions over 2 weeks, 36 Gy in 12 fractions over 2.5 weeks, or 40 Gy in 15 fractions over 3 weeks. In cases where the primary objective is palliation, a total dose of 50 Gy may be adequate, contingent on the location of the tumour and the proximity to vital structures [10, 76].

A number of studies have addressed the use of radiotherapy in DFSP, albeit with a focus on adjuvant rather than palliative settings. For instance, one study., which included 35 cases of DFSP, showed that doses of adjuvant radiotherapy ranging from 46 to 68 Gy resulted in some patients experiencing grade 2 fibrosis or telangiectasia [77]. Furthermore, Castle et al. [69] reported that 13% of patients receiving radiation therapy experienced radiation-related complications at 5 and 10 years. Furthermore, it was reported that some patients developed radiation-induced sarcomas at their primary irradiated site after a median of 7.35 years of radiotherapy, highlighting a long-term risk associated with this treatment [78]. While these studies did not specifically address palliative radiotherapy, they underscore the necessity for meticulous consideration of potential long-term toxicities associated with radiation.

A recent study suggests that patients with DFSP may benefit from postoperative radiotherapy in terms of disease-free survival (DFS), with a pooled estimate of the recurrence rate for the surgery and adjuvant RT group being 11.74% [79]. One study utilised a propensity score-matched analysis to minimise selection bias and found that DFS was significantly higher in the surgery and RT group than in the surgery group (5-year DFS, 88.1% vs. 56.2%, P = 0.044) [76]. While the primary focus of this study was not on palliation, it does support the hypothesis that radiotherapy can have an effect on disease control.

It is important to note that, according to the studies, adjuvant radiation of the tumour bed after complete excision of DFSP is not recommended. A systematic review of twelve retrospective clinical trials demonstrated a tendency towards reduced local recurrence rates following adjuvant postoperative radiotherapy in comparison to surgery alone, although the difference was not significant. Radiotherapy is typically reserved for situations where surgery is not feasible, when microscopic or macroscopic evidence of residual tumour is present, or when there is a history of multiple recurrences. It is imperative to acknowledge that radiation therapy does not substitute for adequate surgical excision and is not indicated for post-operative treatment of patients with tumour-free margins [63, 75, 76].

In instances of imatinib resistance, alternative tyrosine kinase inhibitors, such as sunitinib or pazopanib, may be employed (Table 4). Pazopanib inhibits tyrosine kinases associated with angiogenesis and tumour growth, including VEGFR, PDGFR, and KIT. A retrospective study of 27 patients demonstrated the efficacy of neoadjuvant therapy with imatinib or pazopanib. Of these patients, 9 had fibrosarcoma transformation. These patients achieved durable relapse-free survival. Palliative Systemic Treatment

The efficacy of chemotherapy in the treatment of DFSP is widely considered to be limited, with the disease often regarded as refractory to standard chemotherapeutic regimens [81]. Nevertheless, in select circumstances, particularly for advanced, metastatic, or fibrosarcomatous DFSP (FS-DFSP), the administration of chemotherapy may be contemplated, predominantly within specialist centres that possess expertise in the management of sarcomas [82].

Conventional chemotherapy is not known to offer significant benefits in treating DFSP that is locally advanced or metastatic [74]. This is partly because DFSP is characterised by a specific genetic translocation, t(17;22)(q22;q13), which results in the COL1A1-PDGFB fusion gene [83]. This fusion gene leads to continuous activation of the PDGF-β signalling pathway, which is more effectively targeted by tyrosine kinase inhibitors such as imatinib [84, 85]. Consequently, chemotherapy is generally reserved for situations where other treatments, such as surgery, radiation, and targeted therapies, are not viable options [8].

Despite the limited efficacy of this approach, there is a body of research, including case reports, that explore the potential of various chemotherapeutic agents. These are primarily in the context of treating FS-DFSP or cases where targeted therapies have failed. For instance, a patient with metastatic FS-DFSP who had not responded to previous treatments, including mesna, doxorubicin, ifosfamide [6, 86], dacarbazine, and gemcitabine-docetaxel, was reported to have a partial response to pazopanib. A separate case report details a patient with DFSP who was successfully treated with sorafenib [87]. However, it is important to note that these findings are limited to isolated cases and should not be interpreted as indicating a general effectiveness of these agents in DFSP.

In cases of rapidly progressing, metastatic FS-DFSP, chemotherapeutic regimens typically used for soft tissue sarcomas may be attempted [88, 89]. However, the studies do not provide a comprehensive description of these regimens, instead noting that patients should be referred to a specialist sarcoma centre for such treatment [8]. One study mentions the use of a combination chemotherapy regimen consisting of ifosfamide (1,600 mg/m2 intravenously on days 1 to 5), cisplatin (25 mg/m2 intravenously on days 1 to 3), and apatinib (500 mg daily oral administration on days 1 to 14) in a patient who had refused imatinib, due to its cost, with some success [33].However, a subsequent study has highlighted that, in general, DFSP is considered refractory to standard chemotherapy.

It is important to note that the treatment of sarcomas in general may include agents such as doxorubicin, ifosfamide, dacarbazine, and gemcitabine, depending on the histological subtype of the sarcoma [90, 91]. However, for DFSP, these agents are typically reserved for use as a last resort, when targeted therapies like imatinib have proven ineffective or impractical [92]. he precise dosages and combinations of these medications remain unspecified for DFSP in the referenced studies.

A study focusing on advanced DFSP treated with imatinib also discussed subsequent treatments after disease progression on imatinib [92]. Eight cases exhibited disease progression on imatinib, and following two cases being lost to follow-up, six cases were treated with increased doses of imatinib (600 mg or 800 mg) and other agents, including dacarbazine, pazopanib, sorafenib, cabozantinib and doxorubicin [24].

Subtype Differences

DFSP comprises a number of subtypes, each of which exhibits unique characteristics that impact clinical presentation, diagnosis and treatment strategies (Table 5). However, the fibrosarcomatous variant is an exception in that it exhibits more aggressive clinical behaviour [113]. The overall prognosis for most variants is similar. Classic DFSP, the most common subtype, is defined by its Low metastatic rate of approximately 0.5% and its characteristic storiform pattern of uniform spindle cells infiltrating the subcutaneous tissue [114]. Classic DFSP, the most common subtype, is defined by its Low metastatic rate of approximately 0.5% and its characteristic storiform pattern of uniform spindle cells infiltrating the subcutaneous tissue [115]. This more aggressive nature necessitates its distinction from other sarcomas. Sclerotic DFSP manifests with areas of reduced cellularity and collagen bundles, which can result in misdiagnosis as sclerotic fibroma or other benign sclerosing tumours [76, 116]. Myxoid DFSP, while histologically distinct with its abundant myxoid stroma, generally shares a similar clinical course to classic DFSP [79, 100]. Pigmented DFSP, also known as Bednar tumour, is characterised by the presence of melanin-containing dendritic cells and is observed more frequently in Black patients [77, 117]. Atrophic DFSP is a rare variant presenting as a depressed plaque with reduced dermal thickness, and is most often seen in children [118]. Myoid DFSP, frequently found in the fibrosarcomatous subtype, can pose diagnostic challenges with smooth muscle tumours [110]. However, myoid DFSP is typically less mitotically active and has less atypia than leiomyosarcoma. Granular cell DFSP is distinguished by the presence of granular cells [112].

The primary treatment modality for all DFSP subtypes is surgical resection [119]. Mohs micrographic surgery (MMS) is generally preferred over wide local excision (WLE) due to its higher rate of clearance, lower recurrence rates, and smaller surgical defects [120, 121]. MMS has been shown to result in a reduced need for postoperative radiation therapy and superior tissue preservation. Studies have shown a 1% recurrence rate with MMS compared to 6.3–8.8% with WLE [122]. In the case of subcutaneous DFSP, particularly when Located on the head, more extensive surgery and a higher number of Mohs stages may be required. However, a larger lateral safety margin of 3 cm is advisable where standard histopathological procedures are used. In addition to surgical approaches, imatinib, a tyrosine kinase inhibitor, plays a crucial role in managing advanced or metastatic DFSP, and may be used as neoadjuvant therapy to reduce tumour size prior to surgery [123, 124]. The efficacy of imatinib is enhanced when the COL1A1-PDGFB fusion is present [125]. The recommended initial dosage of imatinib is 400 mg daily, which has been shown to be as effective as higher doses but is better tolerated [126]. The response rates to imatinib range from 36 to 57% [127–129]. Moreover, studies have demonstrated that the objective response rate of imatinib was 36% in a trial of 25 patients with resectable DFSP, and 57.1% in a trial of locally advanced or recurrent DFSP [125, 130]. It is recommended that treatment be continued for a minimum of three months to achieve an objective response. Radiation therapy serves as an adjunct to surgery, particularly when surgical margins are positive or close. The radiation doses employed in these cases have ranged from 55 to 65 Gy [68, 131]. The consideration of preoperative radiotherapy is recommended for large or borderline resectable tumours. In addition to the aforementioned treatment modalities, the use of tyrosine kinase inhibitors, such as PDGFR and FGFR inhibitors, holds promise as a therapeutic option [132]. The implementation of genetic profiling in the identification of specific alterations in FGFR genes serves to inform targeted treatment strategies, thereby enhancing the precision and efficacy of the therapeutic approach.

Racial disparities are a significant factor, with DFSP occurring more frequently in Black individuals and presenting at a more advanced stage and with larger tumour sizes [133]. Black patients are more likely to undergo surgery with radiation, whereas MMS is more frequently administered to White patients [134]. These observations underscore the importance of acknowledging potential disparities in presentation, diagnosis and treatment to enhance patient outcomes. The location of the lesion, particularly in high-risk areas such as the head and neck, may influence the choice of treatment and the likelihood of success [13]. Conclusions

DFSP remains a rare and locally aggressive soft tissue sarcoma with a strong tendency for local recurrence. The optimal management of DFSP is surgical excision with negative margins, as complete resection is associated with favourable long-term survival outcomes. Mohs micrographic surgery (MMS) provides superior margin control and significantly reduces local recurrence rates compared to wide local excision (WLE) but remains underutilised in clinical practice. The reluctance to adopt MMS may be due to limited availability, procedural complexity, and the misconception that WLE with conventional margin assessment is sufficient to prevent recurrence.

The fibrosarcomatous (FS) variant of DFSP represents a major therapeutic challenge due to its aggressive nature and significantly higher rates of local recurrence, metastasis and disease-related mortality. Patients with FS-DFSP have a markedly increased risk of metastasis, particularly to the lungs, lymph nodes and brain, necessitating a more aggressive therapeutic approach. The loss of CD34 expression and increased mitotic activity in FS-DFSP further complicates its diagnosis and differentiation from conventional fibrosarcoma. The poorer prognosis associated with FS-DFSP highlights the need for increased clinical awareness and more rigorous follow-up protocols (Table 7).

Despite advances in diagnosis and treatment, DFSP remains a challenging entity due to its potential for local recurrence and, in the case of FS-DFSP, metastatic spread (Table 8). Current staging systems for DFSP remain inconsistent and there is no universally accepted classification for risk stratification. The proposal of a new staging system incorporating tumour size, depth, FS transformation and metastatic potential may improve prognostic assessment and treatment planning. Future research should focus on refining surgical strategies to maximise tumour clearance while preserving function, developing novel systemic therapies to overcome imatinib resistance, and further elucidating the molecular pathways driving DFSP progression. Increased awareness of the aggressive behaviour of FS-DFSP and the need for early, comprehensive intervention will be critical to improving patient outcomes.
