Investigation of photoinduced deterioration in shadow puppet artifacts collected from the National Shadow Pupperty Museum in Chengdu, China
Authors: Xiangjun Zhang, Chen Min, Muzi Zhang, Li Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Shadow puppet artifacts are a special type of cultural heritage made from animal skin, possessing significant cultural and artistic value. However, there is insufficient attention to the scientific preservation of these artifacts, and systematic research on their protection is lacking. In particular, light as an important factor and related conservation studies are weak. This study investigates the photoinduced deterioration of shadow puppet artifacts from the National Shadow Pupperty Museum in Chengdu collection. Based on the xenon arc lamp are a common method of light aging, the modern rawhide samples were aged by the xenon arc lamp to simulate photoinduced deterioration. After artificial light aging, the samples were stored in a stable environment (with temperature maintained at 22 ± 2 °C and relative humidity maintained at 50 ± 5%RH) for 150 days. It attempts to avoid further deterioration of the samples due to drastic cyclic changes in the environment. And continuously observe changes in characteristics during this process to verify the potential reversibility of photoinduced deterioration. Comprehensive assessments were conducted to gauge the extent of deterioration. In order to assess the deterioration, a series of analytical techniques were used. Moisture gain or loss was inferred from mass changes, and colorimetric measurements were measured using the CIELAB formula. Macroscopic and microscopic morphological damage was documented through camera, stereo microscope, and scanning electron microscope observations. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) assessed amide bond degradation and moisture loss, while thermogravimetry/differential thermogravimetry (TG/DTG) evaluated collagen denaturation and thermal stability. The results indicated that prolonged light exposure caused significant deterioration, including color fading, moisture evaporation, and structural damage such as shrinkage and cracking. The findings indicate that light exposure is a crucial factor in the preservation of shadow puppet artifacts. Therefore, it is essential to implement careful management strategies in museum environments to ensure the protection of these artifacts.

Introduction

Shadow puppetry is a traditional form of folk opera with a long history and rich cultural connotations. It is a quintessential representation of traditional plastic and performing arts in China and Southeast Asia, often using shadow puppet artifacts as the physical medium for storytelling [[1], [2], [3]]. The materials for shadow puppets vary by region and can be categorized into several primary types, including cowhide, sheepskin, donkey skin, and paper [4]. Shadow puppet artifacts, different from leather artifacts [5,6], are untanned and similar in character to parchment [[7], [8], [9]]. These artifacts are made of organic materials that are more sensitive to changes in the preservation environment and are often in poor condition. However, compared with leather and parchment, shadow puppet artifacts have received little attention. There is a serious lack of research on the aging mechanism of shadow puppet artifact materials and related conservation efforts.

The artificial simulation of aging is the main research method used to study the aging characteristics of various types of material artifacts. At present, the researchers have studied the characteristics of leather artifacts deterioration caused by heat through artificial heat aging. And initially formed a standard method of artificial heat aging. For example, artificially aged leather samples can be obtained by heating leather samples at 120 °C or 150 °C for 24 h and then stabilizing them for 12 weeks (stabilized conditions of 40 °C, 35 % RH, 20 ppm SO2 and 10 ppm NO2). These samples are subject to oxidation of the collagen and vegetable tanning agents and hydrolysis of the peptide chains between the collagen, resulting in degradation of the leather fibers [10,11]. Other scholars have done research on the deterioration characteristics of parchment. And artificial heat aging is a commonly used experimental method of artificial aging, which is primarily divided into two types: dry heat aging and humid heat aging. Experimental results indicate that in a dry heat environment, 100 °C is the critical temperature at which parchment begins to exhibit significant signs of aging [12]. Under dry heat conditions, oxidative damage to amino acid side chains [13]. Under conditions of high humidity and heat, this critical temperature is lowered. Collagen is also deconstructed under the conditions [14].

Light, temperature, humidity, and biological factors are all important factors that affect the aging of organic artifacts. However, it can be seen from the current study that the attention of the study on the deterioration characteristics of animal skin artifacts is mainly focused on heat factors, and there are fewer studies related to other factors. In the museum, light is likely to be one of the major factors causing deterioration of organic artifact materials. Photoinduced deterioration is a major concern for both textile and paper artifacts [[15], [16], [17], [18]]. Therefore, focusing on the light factor, this paper hopes to explore the deterioration characteristics of shadow puppet artifact materials in the museum environments.

Through a variety of analytical techniques to comprehensively record the deterioration phenomenon of materials and summarize the rule of deterioration has become a common strategy to study the deterioration of artifact materials in the field of heritage conservation. For leather artifacts, researchers have identified water loss through mass loss [19]. Colorimetric measurements suggest photodegradation and collagen degradation [20,21]. Macromorphological observation and optical microscopy to record surface changes of samples [22]. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) reveals deterioration of micromorphology [23]. Analysis of structural changes in collagen by fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) [[19], [20], [21], [22]]. Thermogravimetry/differential thermogravimetry (TG/DTG) to evaluate the thermal stability of samples [[24], [25], [26]]. For example, researchers have found that heat aging at 150 °C causes leather to deteriorate by loss of mass, deeper and darker color, decreased softness, weakened hydrogen bonding of collagen fibers, decreased thermal stability, decreased water absorption, smaller spacing of collagen fiber molecules, and obvious changes in morphology [19]. Some scholars have also analyzed parchment samples with different degrees of artificial heat aging using analytical methods such as FTIR and colorimetric analysis. The experimental results show that heat induces denaturation and gelatinization of collagen in parchment and browning [20]. In summary, it has been demonstrated that heat induces denaturation and gelatinization of collagen, and the denatured collagen exhibits browning by a Maillard type reaction, which makes the samples show obvious changes in color, fiber structure, and thermal stability. These can be an important reference for studying the photoinduced deterioration characteristics of shadow puppet artifacts.

In recent years, scholars have gradually focused on the problem of the potential effects of light on animal skin artifacts [27]. Light is gradually being used as a synergistic factor in experiments simulating the deterioration of animal skin artifacts [28]. In photoinduced deterioration experiments, frequently utilized light sources include carbon arc lamps, fluorescent ultraviolet lamps, xenon arc lamps, and metal halide lamps. Among these, xenon arc lamps are extensively used for their effective simulation of sunlight, which includes both ultraviolet and visible light components [29]. Some scholars have studied the low-heat aging of parchment by using light as a secondary deterioration factor and using xenon arc lamp for aging [30]. Another study of photoinduced deterioration of modern parchment, conducted directly with a xenon arc lamp, showed that light is not a single factor. It makes the environment hot and with low relative humidity, exacerbating the deterioration and allowing the collagen of the samples to deconstruct, with the appearance of pregelatinized collagen and gelatin [21]. In summary, light, as a common factor affecting the aging of artifacts in collections, deserves attention for its potential deteriorating effects on shadow puppet artifacts.

The National Shadow Pupperty Museum in Chengdu has a rich collection of shadow puppet artifacts, and some samples of typical artifacts were collected for this study. Previous research has shown that the main material of these shadow puppet artifacts is yellow cowhide [8]. Therefore, we used commercially available materials for shadow puppets made of raw cowhide as research samples, and the samples with different degrees of aging were obtained by artificial light aging. Then, various analytical techniques such as mass change, colorimetric analysis, stereo microscopy, scanning electron microscopy (SEM), fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), thermogravimetry/differential thermogravimetry (TG/DTG), etc., were used to record the deterioration characteristics of the samples, which were then compared with the two shadow puppet artifact samples to summarize the phenomena and rules of photoinduced deterioration of shadow puppet artifact materials. The purpose of this study is to explore the potential effects of light on such artifacts and to help museums better deal with photoinduced deterioration.

Methods

Preparation and preservation of artificially aged samples

Solar radiation can be classified into ultraviolet, visible, and infrared. Among them, ultraviolet light is the main factor affecting the preservation of cortical artifacts, visible light is persistent, and infrared light mainly produces heat [31]. Xenon arc weathering test chamber (B-SUN-II, Shanghai Yiheng Scientific Instrument Company Limited) was used to simulate the effects of sunlight-induced deterioration on rawhide under indoor environmental conditions. The experimental conditions were set at: wavelength of 420 nm, irradiance of 1.01 W/m2, and black panel temperature of 50 °C [30,32].

The experimental samples were divided into 5 groups, with each group comprising 4 samples. To observe and summarize deterioration problems in stages, the aging duration commenced at 48 h and incremented by 48 h for each subsequent group, up to a maximum deterioration duration of 240 h [30]. To simulate actual museum storage conditions as closely as possible, each group of samples was exposed to light for a duration ranging from 3 to 10 h per day until the predetermined cumulative deterioration duration was reached. The sample numbers were designated as A1 through A20.

Since the periodic changes in temperature and humidity are not conducive to the preservation of animal skin artifacts. After the completion of the artificial light aging, the samples were placed in a thermostatic chamber to simulate a relatively stable preservation environment. The temperature range for optimal conservation conditions for leather artifacts is 16–25 °C and relative humidity is 55–65 % [27]. Temperatures above 22 °C pose a risk of biological damage to leather, parchment and skin. Similarly, relative humidity above 60 % promotes microbial growth or structural deterioration [33,34]. Therefore, a temperature of 18–22 °C and a relative humidity of 50 % ± 5 % is recommended for leather or untanned animal skin artifacts. In addition, leather artifacts on display or in storage should preferably be maintained at an ambient temperature of approximately 20 °C and 55–60 % relative humidity, with illumination not to exceed 50 lux to mitigate light damage [35]. It has been found that animal skin artifacts in different states may require different preservation conditions. The storage temperature for damp or water-saturated leather artifacts should be controlled at 1–5 °C, while for dry animal skin artifacts, it is suggested to maintain the temperature around 18 °C with relative humidity controlled at 50–55 % [10].

As a result, the temperature of the thermostatic chamber was set at 22 ± 2 °C. A glycerol-water mixture with a mass fraction of 75 % was prepared in the chamber, and the humidity was controlled within a range of 50 ± 5 % relative humidity to maintain the proper temperature and relative humidity. After 150 days, the samples were observed for recovery to assess the reversibility of photoinduced deterioration damage on raw cowhide samples. The preservation conditions described are fundamentally in accordance with the recommended temperature and humidity standards for the conservation of dry leather artifacts.

Weight loss measurement

Before and after the deterioration and stabilization processes, the samples were individually weighed using an electronic balance (METTLER-TOLEDO, ME204/02, Switzerland) with a graduated values of 0.0001g to denote changes in mass. The formula is as follows [19].Δm1=m0−m1Δm2=m0−m2WeightLossRate(%):W1=Δm1m0×100%W2=Δm2m0×100%

m0 is the original mass of the sample. m1 is the mass of the sample after artificial aging. m2 is the mass of the sample after 150 days of stabilization. Δm1 is the change in the mass of the sample before and after the aging. Δm2 is the change in mass of the sample after stabilization compared to the original mass. W1 is the rate of weight loss of the sample after aging. W2 is the weight loss rate of the sample after stabilization compared to the original mass.

Δm1, Δm2, W1 and W2 are expressed as the mean values for each group in the following. The average of mass change (Δm) for each group is calculated by adding the remaining values after removing a maximum and minimum value, respectively, and dividing by two. The average of weight loss rate (W) is calculated in the same way.

Colorimetric measurements

To investigate the impact of photoinduced deterioration on the color of raw cowhide, before and after aritificial light aging and stabilization processes, chromatic measurements were conducted on the central regions of the samples using a colorimeter (Konica Minolta, CM-700d/600d, Japan). The calculation of color differences was based on the CIELAB formula [15]. The formula is as follows:ΔE=ΔL2+Δa2+Δb2In the formula, ‘L’ denotes brightness, while ‘a’ and ‘b’ represent the color coordinates. The color difference value, ΔE, is employed to quantify the extent of color change; a smaller ΔE indicates a less perceptible color alteration.

The average value of ΔE is calculated by removing a maximum value and a minimum value and dividing the remaining sum by two. ΔE1 represents the average of the color difference before and after aging of the samples. ΔE2 represents the average of the color difference between the stabilized and the original state of the samples.

Morphological observation

Photographic documentation and recording of shadow puppet artifacts and aged samples 3 were conducted using a digital camera (Nikon, D5600, Japan), with a particular focus on notable deterioration characteristics such as bending, wrinkling, and cracking.

Microscopic observation and documentation of samples using a stereo microscope (Nikon, SMZ1270, Japan) and scanning electron microscope (SEM) (Thermo Scientific, Quattro S, USA).

Samples were secured onto a specimen stage and sprayed with gold to enhance their electrical conductivity. Microscopic morphological observations of the samples were then carried out using SEM. In conjunction with energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS), the elemental composition and relative abundances within the samples were obtained.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

A small quantity of hide powder was scraped from the lateral surface of each sample with a disposable scalpel. Thereafter, the powder samples were subjected to fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis (ThermoFisher, iN10, USA) using the potassium bromide (KBr) pellet method. The measurements were taken between 4000 and 400 cm−1 in absorbance mode with a resolution of 4 cm−1 and 32 scans [[36], [37], [38]].

Thermogravimetry/differential thermogravimetry (TG/DTG)

Samples were subjected to thermal property analysis using the synchronous thermal analyzer (Netzsch, STA-2500, German). The samples were tested in a static air atmosphere, heated at a constant rate of 10 °C/min from room temperature to 800 °C. The thermal stability and extent of degradation were compared between the shadow puppet artifacts and the artificially aged samples [24,39].

Rawhide has a high content of moisture [40]. The water content of the sample affects the weight [41]. Therefore, the loss of samples mass after artificial aging may be due to the reduction of moisture. Based on the results of the drying experiments at 105 °C for 3h, it can be postulated that the aged 144h raw cowhide samples may have reached the limit of water loss. Perhaps it can be explained that there is little difference in the mass change of the samples after aging for 144h.

It is noteworthy that Δm1 and w1 of the 192h and 240h aged samples are instead smaller than those of the 144h aged samples. This may be due to the fact that as aging progresses, the degradation of the collagen fibers and the advancing loss of inter- and intrafibrillar bonds create a more loose and porous material, which results in an increased moisture absorption capacity [30]. Samples are more likely to absorb moisture from room temperature environments. This could lead to an increase in mass upon weighing, resulting in a decrease in Δm1 and a lower W1.

Following the period of 150 days in a stabilized environment, the mass of the samples exhibited a gradual recovery, approaching that of the original samples (Table 1). The mass of the sample is affected by its own moisture content, which is related to its ability to absorb moisture. This capacity is affected by its own fiber structure and the humidity of the environment [30]. Under the same stabilizing environment and time, the mass change and mass loss rate of the samples aged for 144h and above were still greater. It may indicate that the collagen fiber structure of these samples is more damaged than the 48h and 96h samples.

The experimental results showed that the samples were in a dry environment during the artificially aged experiments, and the mass tended to decrease, probably due to the reduction of moisture. After a certain amount of aging, the collagen fibers degraded and became loose and porous. At the end of artificial aging, samples with severe degradation of collagen fibers have an increased ability to absorb water when they are in an environment with higher relative humidity, and the change in mass becomes smaller instead. After 150 days in the relatively comfortable and stable environment, the samples gradually increased in mass, possibly due to water absorption. Although the samples did not return to their original mass, they were still somewhat self-recovering in terms of weight.

After aging, the mean color difference (ΔE) of the samples increases progressively with the extension of the aging period (Table 2). After 48 h of aging, the average ΔE of the sample has exceeded 3, indicating a significant color change in the sample after aging. After 150 days of stabilization, the color difference of the samples was measured and calculated again, and it was found that the color difference values of each group of samples were similar to those before stabilization (Table 2). Typically, the measurement error of a colorimeter on organic polymer materials remains within ΔE≤1 [30,42]. Therefore, it was concluded that modern rawhide samples exhibited fading under photoinduced deterioration, which was attributed to the bleaching effect of artificial light aging [30]. Prolonged aging exacerbates the discoloration. This damage is probably irreversible (S-1).

Scanning electron microscope (SEM) observation results

Using scanning electron microscopy (SEM), it was observed that the surface of modern, unaged raw cowhide is generally smooth, but still has pores, fine wrinkles, and a small amount of debris (Fig. 5b, c, 5d). As the aging time increases, the deterioration of the sample surface and cross section becomes progressively more severe (Fig. 5b, c, 5d). After 48 h of aging, the surface of the sample remains relatively smooth, but is now adorned with debris, with pores and textures becoming more pronounced, accompanied by the appearance of minor cracking. Samples aged for 96 h show an increase in surface debris, deepening of textures, and the onset of lifting and cracking phenomena, with cracks mainly radially distributed around the pores. In samples aged for 144 h, cracking and spalling are exacerbated, with more pronounced layering, enlarged and deepened pores, and more severe cracking problems. The radial cracks are longer and wider, with some intersections. Samples aged 192 h show increased surface fragmentation, significant debris adhesion, further pore enlargement and deepening, and widespread and prominent lifting and cracking. Radiating cracks are more extensive in length and width, with some intersections. In general, The effect of photoinduced deterioration on raw cowhide is mainly characterized by surface cracking (Fig. 5b), splitting, and debris (Fig. 5c and d). Cracking on the surface of the samples is quite common. Splitting problems are more pronounced in the cross section. And at the junction of the plane and the cross section, the problem of debris is even more evident. These deterioration problems are also found on shadow puppet artifacts. The surface of Chengdu sample-1 appears more fragmented, with noticeable cracks and wrinkles, and is adhered with a greater amount of debris or contaminants (Fig. 5a). While Chengdu sample-2, despite having numerous wrinkles and textures, is relatively smoother compared to Chengdu sample-1, and also has cracks, debris or contaminants adhered (Fig. 5a).

In order to determine whether the attachment of the samples were cortical debris or contaminants, the modern and shadow puppet artifact samples were scanned separately using energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS). The primary chemical constituents of leather are C, H, O, N, and S [44]. The main elements of modern artificially aged samples are C, O, N, and S. The surface and cross-section of Chengdu sample-1 and Chengdu sample-2 were scanned by spot scanning. The results indicated a significant increase in the variety and relative content of non-hide primary elemental constituents, such as Ca, Si, Al, and Fe, in both the surface and cross-section of Chengdu sample-1 (S-2). This phenomenon can be attributed to the fact that the surface of the sample is more fragmented and thicker, thus providing a larger niche space for contaminants to hide. Chengdu sample-2 is relatively thin, with a diverse range of elemental species, particularly high in Fe and Si elements (S-3a). However, the cross section of this sample is still predominantly composed of C, N, and O, with other elements present in very low concentrations (S-3b). This suggests that the contaminants attaching to this sample are primarily concentrated on the surface. As a consequence, the attachment of both shadow puppet artifact samples are mainly debris and contaminants. The specific composition of the contaminants and their formation processes are not discussed in detail here.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis

FTIR spectroscopy can be used to analyze changes in collagen structure in artifacts and artificially aged samples [45]. In α-amino acids, the primary functional groups include the methylene, carboxyl, and amine groups. The characteristic absorption peak for the stretching vibration of the -NH2 group is observed near 3323 cm−1, which is nearby amide A [46]. The asymmetric stretching vibration, the symmetric stretching vibration, and the scissoring vibration of the -CH2- group are represented by characteristic absorption peaks near 2925 cm−1, 2854 cm−1, and 1452 cm−1, respectively. The stretching vibration of the carbonyl group within the carboxyl group, which is the strongest absorption band in the protein infrared spectrum known as the amide I band, is observed near 1652 cm−1. Near 1552 cm−1, the characteristic absorption peak for the stretching vibrations of -CN- and the scissoring vibration of -NH2 forms a strong absorption band known as the amide II band. The characteristic absorption peak for the stretching vibration of the carbonyl group in the carboxyl group and the stretching vibration of the carbon with the amine group, known as the amide III band, is observed near 1238 cm−1 [47,48].

Overall, the peak shape of the artificially aged samples flattens with increasing aging time. The peak positions of shadow puppet artifacts are essentially similar to those of the unaged samples; however, the overall spectral curves are more subdued. Compared to the unaged samples, the primary characteristic peaks of the other samples mostly shifted to lower wavenumbers, although the change is very small (Fig. 6). Amide A, amide I, and amide II bonds are related to the strength of hydrogen bonds between collagen fibers [19]. The movement of the peak may be related to the stabilization of hydrogen bonds in collagen [49]. Therefore, it indicates that weakening or disruption of the hydrogen bonding of collagen in the modern aged samples and the shadow puppet artifact samples, which may affect the stability of the peptide chains in collagen and the stability of the collagen structure [24,45,50].Fig. 6The FTIR results of shadow puppet artifacts and modern artificially aged samples aged for different periods of time. The longer the aging time of the artificially aged samples, the more pronounced the shift. The shadow puppet artifact sample has the most pronounced shift. The characteristic peaks of amide I and amide II of the artificially aged samples become flatter with increasing aging time compared to the unaged samples. The characteristic peaks of amide I and amide II of the shadow puppet artifact samples are more flatter, and the ordered state structure may be destroyed more severely. This may be due to the gradual decrease in the proportion of helical structures, the destruction of ordered state structures, and the increase in the proportion of random nematic structures with increasing aging time [50].

Compared with the unaged sample, the characteristic peak of amide III of the artificially aged sample gradually disappeared with the longer aging time. Meanwhile, it can be found that the characteristic peak of amide III of the shadow puppet artifact sample also disappeared. This may be due to extensive cleavage of the amide bond under prolonged irradiation [24].

Besides collagen, water is also the major components of rawhide [40]. Liquid water molecules exist in hydrogen bonded H-O...H bond and appears as a broad absorption band with the main peak at 3400 cm−1 [51]. The peak shapes of the photoinduced deterioration samples at this position become flatter with the aging time. It may indicate that the loss of water within the collagen fibers is more serious with the aging time [19]. Especially the peaks at 192h and 240h are more consistent. Combined with the change in mass, it may suggest that the loss of water in the aged samples at 144h and later has reached its limit. In addition, the shapes of the absorption peaks at this location for the 192h and 240h artificially aged samples are very similar to those of the shadow puppet artifact samples. It means that the water loss in the shadow puppet artifacts may also be more serious.

Thermogravimetry/differential thermogravimetry (TG/DTG) analysis

The thermal deterioration trends of modern unagrawhide, artificially aged rawhide, and shadow puppet artifacts are essentially consistent (Fig. 7), with the process roughly divided into three stages. The first stage occurs from room temperature to about 205 °C, with a weight loss of about 4–9%, primarily due to water and other small molecular substances escaping from the samples. The second stage spans a temperature range of approximately 200–420 °C, resulting in a weight loss of approximately 40–50 %. The third stage for modern rawhide and simulated aged samples occurs at about 450–670 °C, while for Chengdu shadow puppet samples it is in the range of 470–630 °C, with a weight loss of about 30–35 %. The second and third stages are mainly characterized by thermolysis, thermoxidation and decomposition of collagen [52,53], during which peptide bonds are cleaved and collagen is gradually degraded into polypeptides and amino acids. Finally, amino acid residues are destroyed by deamination and dehydration [54]. At this stage, the peak shapes of the unaged, 48 h aged, 96 h aged and 144 h aged samples clearly show a tendency to flatten with increasing aging time. Samples aged 144 h and above show very similar curve shapes at this stage. Except for the unaged sample and the sample aged for 48 h, the artificially aged samples from 96 to 240 h showed a more flat trend on the TG/DTG curves without clear peaks. It may be attribute to the low content of adsorbed or not-structured water in the samples after artificially aging [55]. In addition, other small molecular substances in the samples may have gradually disappeared during the simulated aging process, resulting in a flatter curve. The sample photoinduced for 240 h showed a significant difference in the peak corresponding to the maximum rate of weight loss (142.96 °C) in the first stage compared to other simulated aged samples (Fig. 8), which may be related to the gelatinization and degradation of collagen fibers at temperatures around (150 ± 10)°C and (220 ± 10)°C [56].

However, it is also found that the differences in the rate of weight loss, TG/DTG curves, and peak degradation temperatures during the first stage for the shadow puppet artifact samples, which may be attributed to the fact that Chengdu sample-1 is thicker than Chengdu sample-2. Consequently, Chengdu sample-1 is likely to retain more water and small molecular substances, which begin to escape at relatively lower temperatures.

In the second stage, the DTG peak starting temperatures of the artificially aged samples were in the range of 210–230 °C, with no obvious pattern for the time being. And the ending temperatures of the DTG peaks basically tended to increase with the extension of the aging time. The DTG peak temperature range of the shadow puppet artifact samples is close to that of the artificially aged samples of 192h and 240h (Table 3). The weight loss rate for all samples reaches its maximum, which are basically similar. Among them, the weight loss of the artificially aged sample for 240h is close to that of Chengdu sample-2.

The peak corresponding to the maximum weight loss rate for modern samples (including artificially aged samples) occurs at approximately 300 °C (Fig. 8). The peak temperatures of the shadow puppet artifacts were slightly higher than those of the artificially aged samples. The peak for Chengdu sample-2 is at about 309 °C, while the peak for Chengdu sample-1 is even higher, at about 320 °C. At around 300 °C, it occurs the scission of the hydroxyl groups during the thermal decomposition of leather and parchment [55]. It is noteworthy that gelatin and collagen begin to degrade at (300 ± 5)°C, with secondary degradation of collagen occurring at (340 ± 10)°C and that of gelatin at (420 ± 10)°C [56]. It is clear that secondary degradation of the collagen in the samples is occurring at this stage.

Therefore, the combined DTG peak temperature range, weight loss rate and collagen and gelatin degradation temperatures indicate that the deterioration of the artificially aged samples is more severe as the aging time increases. In particular, the artificially aged samples of 192h and 240h may be closer to the deterioration state of the shadow puppet artifact samples, and the secondary degradation of gelatin may have occurred in these samples at this stage, indicating that the samples contain more gelatin and the deterioration is more serious.

In the third stage, the rate of weight loss is approximately the same for all samples tested (Table 3). However, the DTG peak temperature ranges of the modern artificially aged samples are inconsistent, and more broader than the shadow puppet artifact samples. The DTG peak temperature range of the shadow puppet artifact samples is more concentrated. The DTG curves of the modern artificially aged samples are flatter and the shadow puppet artifact samples are obviously sharper. Moreover, the peak DTG temperature of the modern artificially aged samples was significantly higher than that of the shadow puppet artifact samples. It is likely that the long-term use, wear, and aging processes experienced by these shadow puppet artifacts have reduced their thermal stability, thereby accelerating the rate of mass loss during the thermal decomposition process at this stage. In addition, the thermogravimetric results of the shadow puppet artifact samples may also be affected by staining and contaminants.

The percentage of residual mass of the samples ranges from 1 to 8% (Table 3). Except for the unaged modern samples, the 48h artificially aged samples, and the Chengdu sample-1, the remaining samples have similar residual masses, all around 5 %. Relevant research indicates that approximately 13 % of the carbonaceous residue in vegetable tanned leather is from tannins [57,58]. The absence of detectable residues of tannins or other vegetable tanning agents in the above samples confirms that the experimental samples have not been tanned.

Discussions

The results of artificial light aging show that the materials of aged samples suffer from a series of problems in terms of morphology, structure, and performance after photoinduced deterioration. After stabilizing the aged samples by placing them in the suitable environment, the other deterioration problems are irreversible, except for the loss of mass, which can be partially recovered. Moisture and collagen are the main components of the materials used in shadow puppet artifacts [40]. It is inferred that the photoinduced deterioration of the material of the shadow puppet artifact materials may be closely related to the changes in the moisture content and the deterioration or degradation of the collagen in the materials.

Prolonged exposure to light induces Photothermal Conversion (PTC). The samples have converted the solar radiation energy into sufficiently high temperature heat energy by reflecting, absorbing, or otherwise concentrating the solar radiation energy [59], which eventually causes a large loss of water in the material. This process is confirmed by many of the results of the experiment. The mass loss of the artificially aged samples basically reaches the extreme value after the artificial light aging time reaches 144 h. The mass loss of the samples after light aging is very close to the mass change after drying treatment of the samples. The results of the analytical techniques also confirm the loss of water at the molecular level. In the analysis of the FTIR results, the change in peak shape near 3400 cm−1 may indicate a change in the H-O …. H bond. The comparison shows that the peak shape at this position basically flattens with aging time. In particular, the shape of the curve at this position is very similar for the 192h and 240h aged samples. This is related to the change of moisture in the material. In addition, the flat curve of the first stage in TG/DTG may also indicate that moisture and small molecules have escaped during the deterioration process [55] and that the moisture content of the aged samples is obviously reduced. In particular, the samples aged for 144h and above have very similar shapes of TG/DTG curves at this stage, which are all relatively flat. This is consistent with the regularity of mass change. From the analysis of the above experimental data, it was comprehensively deduced that the artificially aged samples showed water loss due to aging, and this deterioration had a certain trend [60]. The experimental results showed that before reaching the extreme value of water loss, the water loss in the samples became more serious with increasing aging time.

The loss of water may also affect the structure, properties and morphology of materials used in shadow puppet artifacts [61,62]. Studies have shown that the elasticity modulus of rawhide materials decreases significantly with decreasing humidity, while the critical strain increases. This leads to rawhide materials being more prone to permanent deformation, thereby affecting their durability [63]. And with the loss of moisture, the tensile properties and elasticity of the samples also decrease significantly, which may cause damage to the structure of the material [64]. It suggests that structural deterioration of materials due to water loss may exhibit a range of disease problems, including shrinkage, hardening, and bending.

Moisture changes in the material of shadow puppet artifacts may also affect the stability of the molecular structure of collagen. In rawhide materials, sufficient moisture ensures the formation of hydrogen bonds in the collagen and contributes to structural stability [41]. It has been shown that as the moisture content decreases, the denaturation temperature and enthalpy of collagen in skins decreases, increasing its structural heterogeneity and disorder, which may lead to the denaturation and degradation of collagen fibers [65]. In addition, as the sample aged, there was more and more water loss and the binding within the proteins decreased, which in turn caused the collagen triple helix structure to break down. It causes the internal structure of the samples to become porous and loose. When the relative humidity of the sample environment increases, the samples absorb water more readily from the external environment [30]. It causes aged samples to be more sensitive to environmental changes.

Deterioration and degradation of the collagen in the materials may be another important cause of disease formation in shadow puppet artifacts. Light exposure may induce a series of photochemical effects, such as photodegradation, photooxidation, and photocrosslinking, which will change the molecular structure of collagen [66]. Photoinduced deterioration also directly affects the structural stability of the collagen in the material of shadow puppet artifacts [67]. The main ingredient in shadow puppet artifacts is collagen, which is basically type I collagen, which consists of a sequence of amino acids (mainly glycine-proline-X or glycine-X-hydroxyproline (X: another amino acid)) twisted by three peptide chains to form a helical structure [68]. Photodegradation causes covalent bonds between collagen molecules to break, generating free radicals, which in turn undergo reactions such as oxidation [69]. Furthermore, the two main complex processes occur during the natural aging of collagen-based materials: the breaking of the cross-linking bonds and the splitting off of collagen macromolecules by hydrolysis with water vapors, followed by the oxidation of the lower macromolecules [70]. These changes can easily lead to changes in the molecular structure of collagen, specifically the deconstruction and disruption of its triple helix structure. Comparison of the FTIR data of the aged samples shows that the main characteristic peaks of the samples are increasingly shifted to lower wavelengths as the degree of light aging increases. Amide A, amide I, and amide II are related to the strength of hydrogen bonding in collagen, and the shift of their peak positions may indicate that the hydrogen bonding in the samples was weakened or destroyed. In addition, the peak shapes of amide I and amide II gradually flattened with increasing aging time, and the characteristic peak of amide III even gradually disappeared, suggesting that the ordered structure of collagen was damaged and the proportion of disordered structure increased. These results confirm that the collagen in the material of the aged samples has suffered from molecular structure damage and degradation. Partially degraded collagen may remain in a pre-gelatinized state or form gelatin when combined with water in the environment [61,71,72], making the sample sticky. In addition, the temperature at which gelatin occurs secondary degradation is (420 ± 10)°C. According to the temperature range of the second stage curve of TG/DTG, the temperature range of the second stage of the artificially aged samples of 192h and 240h and the shadow puppet artifact samples are similar to this temperature, so it is assumed that gelatin may be formed in the above samples. Moreover, the appearance and aggregation of the white gelatinous substance at the edges of the sample surface was recorded in the micro-morphological observations of the aged samples. The combination of analytical techniques suggests that these newly produced white gelatinous substances are most likely gelatin. The formation of gelatin is also an important feature of collagen degradation and damage in the material. Therefore, it is not difficult to confirm that photoinduced deterioration has caused degradation and structural damage to collagen in the material. These damages can also further cause or show up as macroscopic diseases of the samples, including the surface with stickiness and cracking.

Furthermore, structural changes in collagen in aged samples cause macroscopic color differences. The fading or color difference in this material is a photochemical deterioration process [73]. When exposed to simulated sunlight, the rawhide material appears to whiten or lighten significantly. This change may be a complex process, most likely a combination of photodegradation and photooxidation of collagen, causing the bleaching phenomenon. Free radicals from photodegradation cause oxidation of collagen molecules, producing peroxides and other oxidation products that result in fading. Other studies have shown that prolonged exposure of collagen to solar UV light results in collagen crosslinking, destruction of tyrosine, and phenylalanine residues, conformational change with concomitant chain degradation, and suppression of fibril formation, which in turn affects the chromophores [43]. Consequently, the fading phenomenon may be a typical feature of photoinduced deterioration of the structural stability of collagen, or even degradation. It was also confirmed by colorimetric analysis in the experimental results. The samples showed a decreasing trend in L value (brightness) with increasing aging, a general decrease in b value, and a gradual increase in both ΔL and ΔE, indicating that the effect of light on the samples at the macroscopic color caused by the aging time is obvious with increasing aging time.

In summary, prolonged light exposure can lead to a series of deterioration problems in the materials of shadow puppet artifacts. The comparative analysis shows that the shadow puppet artifact samples collected in this study also suffer from many similar deterioration problems in artificially aged samples, such as bending, shrinkage, cracking and other morphological changes. The results of FTIR and TG/DTG analysis showed that the shadow puppet artifact samples also suffered from moisture loss, damage to the collagen structure, and decreased thermal stability. It suggests that the shadow puppet artifacts have already appeared obvious deterioration problems, and photoinduced deterioration is likely to be one of the important factors contributing to these deterioration problems. The samples of shadow puppet artifacts collected are all museum collections. It is inevitable that they will be exposed to light conditions during display. Therefore, attention needs to be paid to the effects of light on the materials of the shadow puppet artifacts to minimize the negative effects of light.

Conclusions

In this study, a series of samples of the same material as the shadow puppet artifacts with different degrees of aging were prepared by artificial light aging. Subsequently, we recorded the various types of diseases caused by artificial light aging of the samples through various analytical techniques, including mass loss, color differences, shrinkage, hardening, and enrichment of gelatinous substances on the surface. Finally, we analyzed the deterioration characteristics of the artificially aged samples and the shadow puppet artifact samples by microscopic observation, FTIR, and TG/DTG. Analysis of the experimental results shows that artificial light aging likely caused water loss and collagen degradation and structural damage in the collagen-based materials. The characteristic water-related peaks in the FTIR results of the samples changed significantly as the degree of light aging increased. Meanwhile, the peak shape of the first stage in the TG/DTG of the samples flattened with increasing light aging. All these changes may be related to water loss from the collagen-based material, which in turn caused a variety of diseases such as shrinkage, hardening, and bending. Obvious changes in the characteristic peaks of collagen such as amide A, amide I and amide II can also be observed in the FTIR results. These changes are closely related to the degradation of collagen. Furthermore, based on the temperature range of the second stage curve of TG/DTG, it is assumed that gelatin is likely to be formed in some of the artificially aged samples and shadow puppet artifact samples, which confirms the phenomenon of accumulation of gelatinous material on the surface of the aged samples in microscopic observation. Gelatin formation also confirms that collagen degradation and damage occurred in the samples during the light aging process. This change likely caused the artificially aged samples and the shadow puppet artifact samples to appear diseased such as cracking and fading.

It is essential to manage light exposure during the collection and exhibition of shadow puppet artifacts in museums to mitigate photoinduced material deterioration. For example, necessary light-blocking measures can be employed during storage, and fluorescent or LED lamps specifically designed for artifacts can be used during exhibitions to reduce photoinduced deterioration caused by ultraviolet and infrared radiation [31].

It is noteworthy that the stability conditions set during the experiments were 22 (±2)°C and 50 (±5)% RH. Under these stable conditions, the samples did not exhibit significant deterioration over a short period. And some of the photoinduced deterioration issues were alleviated to a certain extent, although full restoration to the unaged state was challenging. Therefore, we recommend that shadow puppet artifacts in museum collections be stored in environments with appropriate and stable temperature and humidity levels. This is likely to be crucial for the long-term preservation of these valuable cultural artifacts.
