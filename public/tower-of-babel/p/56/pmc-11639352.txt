A tracking control method for electricity-carbon emission forecasting☆
Authors: Hongyin Chen, Songcen Wang, Jianfeng Li, Yaoxian Yu, Dezhi Li, Lu Jin, Yi Guo, Xiaorui Cui
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis paper introduces a novel carbon emission prediction method based on tracking control, leveraging historical CO2 emission prediction errors and feed-forward integration of electricity consumption data to enhance forecasting accuracy and minimize lag. Comparative analysis with pre-trained models such as LSTM and ARDL using Python showcases the proposed method's substantial reduction in prediction errors compared to singular reliance on electricity data, while also significantly reducing computational time in contrast to LSTM models. The findings establish a valuable reference for policymakers and researchers in refining carbon emission prediction methodologies and formulating effective carbon reduction policies.Keywords: Carbon emission, Forcast, Tracking control, LSTM, ARDL

Introduction

In recent years, the accurate calculation of CO2 emissions has become more and more import. As the global focus on carbon emissions and their reduction has escalated significantly, garnering widespread attention from countries worldwide. However, the prospect of effectively reducing carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions remains daunting. As per the IEA report [1], global CO2 emissions exhibited a 0.9% increase, reaching a total of 36.8 gigatonnes in 2022. Reducing carbon dioxide emissions has become a global consensus [2]. In this context, the accurate calculation of CO2 emissions for individual buildings, companies, regions, cities, and countries assumes a pivotal role in the endeavor to curtail CO2 emissions. This pursuit has emerged as a burgeoning focal point in contemporary research.

The fundamental concept of CO2 prediction is based on the time series method. Given that carbon emission data constitutes a time-series dataset, both conventional time series models and contemporary deep learning models have found widespread application in analyzing such emissions. Many new models have achieved significant success in this area of research [3], [4], [5], [6].

The conventional time series models, such as the AutoRegression Distributed Lag (ARDL) method, have gained widespread utilization in various macro-level research endeavors. For instance, Mirziyoyeva et al. [7] employed panel data techniques to scrutinize the relationship between CO2 emission intensity and the proportion of renewable energy within the top 50 carbon-intensive economies from 2000 to 2015. Gao et al. [8] introduced a forecasting model for carbon emission growth utilizing Gompertz's law. Additionally, Xu et al. [9] delved into the correlations among green and non-green energies, GDP, and CO2 emissions across ten Asian countries spanning from 1995 to 2018. Hu Zikun et al. [10] utilized the ARDL using China's statistical energy and industrial data from 1999 to 2021 to empirically ascertain the influencers of China's carbon emissions. Similarly, Liu Yunlong et al., based on China's historical data from 1990 to 2019, employed the ARDL method to investigate the impact of economic growth, industrial structure, and dependency ratio on per capita carbon emissions, revealing a long-term cointegration relationship. Abbasi et al. [11] proposed a novel dynamic ARDL method to study Thailand's CO2 emissions, energy consumption rates, and GDP from 1980 to 2018. Dang Yuting [12], utilizing China's provincial panel data from 1990 to 2016, estimated the long-term and short-term impacts of trade, foreign direct investment, and economic growth on China's carbon emissions through panel ARDL, revealing a significantly positive correlation between per capita real GDP and carbon emissions in both short and long runs.

Moreover, deep learning models, particularly Long Short-Term Memory Network (LSTM)-based prediction methods, have emerged as a prominent choice in time series forecasting, garnering significant attention. Researchers have successfully applied LSTM in fields like wind power generation (Liu Yongqian [13], Li Chunping [14]). Regarding carbon emission prediction, scholars have made strides; for example, Zhang Xueqing et al. [15] developed a model combining convolutional neural networks and LSTM to forecast China's carbon emissions from 2019 to 2020 using Carbon Monitor website data, enhancing the model's generalizability and timeliness. Furthermore, LSTM has been applied to predict pollutant gas emissions (Ahmed et al. [16]) and other time series data (Ouyang et al. [17]). AlKheder et al. [18] utilized Support Vector Machine (SVM), Artificial Neural Network (ANN), and Deep Learning (DL) methods to forecast Kuwait's power sector CO2 emissions for 2018-2022 based on US EPA and IPCC data, revealing DL as the optimal method for fitting historical data.

Despite its proficiency in capturing complex nonlinear relationships within data, LSTM models might overfit with limited datasets and demand significantly higher computational resources compared to traditional statistical methods like ARDL, extending computational times for problems of similar scale.

Despite the notable advancements made by current methods in carbon dioxide forecasting, several challenges and limitations still persist:

•Data Quality and Quantity: Forecasting models rely heavily on the quality and volume of historical data. In practical applications, challenges like missing data and noise are prevalent, highlighting the need for more robust data preprocessing and management techniques [19].

Taking these factors into account, this paper introduces a carbon emission prediction method rooted in the principles of tracking control. This method predicts future carbon emissions by utilizing historical carbon emission data and past forecasts. By capitalizing on the convenience and real-time availability of electricity consumption data—without the need for additional sensors or equipment—this approach employs feed-forward correction to refine carbon emission predictions. It adjusts for variations in electricity consumption data to reduce carbon emission errors. The proposed method enhances model interpretability and generalizability, while also requiring less stringent conditions regarding data quantity and quality.

The subsequent sections of this article are structured as follows: Section 2 introduces the fundamental principles underlying the tracking control forecast. Section 3 delves into the analysis of stability and steady-state errors associated with this method. Section 4 offers a simulation example, while Section 5 summarizes the entirety of this study.

Carbon emission forecast method based on tracking control

An alternative perspective on the carbon emission prediction problem involves conceptualizing it as a tracking problem. In this analogy, the historical carbon emission data serves as the input reference signal, while the predicted value acts as the output tracking signal. The underlying principle is elucidated below: for the value YK awaiting prediction, the error eK−1 between the historical value YK−1 and the previously predicted value YK−1⁎ (which obtained at the former timestep using the same method) is utilized as the input. Subsequently, the PID method is utilized to calculate the adjusted value ΔYK−1⁎, subsequently combining it with the previously predicted value YK−1⁎ to yield the predicted value YK⁎. This process results in the formulation of the updated prediction. The conceptualization is visually represented in Fig. 1.

Empirical analysis

Data and models

To substantiate the proposed method, a practical example is employed for verification.

Data sources

The example utilizes data encompassing carbon emissions and energy consumption within the cement industry across 31 provinces in Mainland China with 21 years (from 1996 to 2016). These datasets are sourced from the China Carbon Accounting Database [23], comprising a total of 651 data records. The dataset encompasses CO2 emissions, electricity consumption, coal consumption, and cement/clinker production.

Data from each of the 31 provinces were individually input into each of the six models, with each model being tested 31 times. Evaluation was based on Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) and Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE) average of the prediction data across the 31 provinces. Additionally, prediction time was taken into consideration.

It is important to note that the first four models are based on pre-trained models, requiring the division of each province's 21-year data into a 16-year training set and a 5-year test set. In contrast, model 5 and 6, proposed in this paper, are not based on pre-trained models and therefore do not require the division into training and test sets. Therefore, all the 21-year data can be used to calculate the evaluation metrics for model 5 and 6.

Test environment

The aforementioned four models are programmed and implemented in Python 3.10. The LSTM method is implemented using tensorflow 2.6, while the ARDL method utilizes statsmodels 0.13.5. The hardware environment comprises an Intel Core i5-11400 processor coupled with 16GB of RAM.

Result analysis

The observed outcomes arise from several key factors. Firstly, carbon emissions are influenced by various forms of energy consumption. Models like LSTM and ARDL, relying solely on electricity consumption data, overlook the impact of other significant carbon sources, such as coal consumption. This omission results in considerable prediction errors across methodologies, emphasizing the necessity of incorporating historical coal consumption data to improve forecasting accuracy.

Secondly, the proposed tracking prediction model showcased notable advantages in requiring less data and computational time, while still yielding more accurate forecasts.

However, the tracking prediction model in this paper has its inherent limitations. Firstly, the prediction process operates as a one-step forecast, where increasing data doesn't necessarily correspond to higher accuracy.

Conclusion

This paper introduces a novel carbon emission prediction method based on tracking control, utilizing historical CO2 emission prediction errors to forecast future carbon emissions. Additionally, a feed-forward approach incorporating electricity consumption data minimizes forecasting lag. The method is implemented and compared with pre-trained models like LSTM and ARDL using Python. Results demonstrate a notable reduction in error compared to predictions solely based on electricity data. Compared to the LSTM and ARDL forecasting methods, the approach proposed in this paper demonstrates lower error rates and faster processing speed. However, determining the parameters of the forecasting model, especially how to adaptively adjust these parameters during the forecasting process, remains an area for further research in this method. The findings presented herein serve as a valuable reference for carbon emission prediction and the formulation of effective carbon reduction policies.
