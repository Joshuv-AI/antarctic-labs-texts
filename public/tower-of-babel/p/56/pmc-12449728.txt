Dual-action nanotherapy: Temozolomide-loaded, anti-PD-L1 scFv-functionalized lipid nanocarriers for targeted glioblastoma therapy
Authors: Eren Aytekin, Semer Maksoud, Bakhos A Tannous, Sibel Bozdağ Pehlivan, Christian E Badr
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlioblastoma (GBM) is a highly malignant brain tumor with limited treatment options and poor prognosis.GBM exhibits resistance to conventional therapies, including temozolomide (TMZ), radiotherapy, and immunotherapy, partly due to immunosuppressive mechanisms such as programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) overexpression. To address these challenges, we developed TMZ-loaded nanostructured lipid carriers (NLCs) conjugated with anti-PD-L1 single-chain variable fragments (scFv) for dual chemo-immunotherapy.The anti-PD-L1 scFv enabled active targeting of PD-L1-expressing tumor cells while mitigating immune evasion, and the NLCs efficiently crossed the blood-brain barrier (BBB), delivering TMZ to the tumor site. In vitro studies confirmed nanoparticle internalization by GL261 glioma cells and specific binding of the conjugated scFv to PD-L1. In vivo studies using the GL261/C57BL/6 mouse model demonstrated that TMZ-NLCs conjugated with anti-PD-L1 scFv (TMZ-NP-scFv) stabilized tumor growth by the third week, unlike other treatment groups. While all therapeutic groups (TMZ solution, TMZ-NP, and anti-PD-L1 scFv alone) significantly improved survival compared to controls, the TMZ-NP-scFv group exhibited the most pronounced survival benefit.These results highlight the potential of TMZ-loaded, PD-L1-targeted NLCs as a synergistic strategy to enhance GBM treatment by combining chemotherapy and immune checkpoint blockade.Keywords: Glioblastoma, Pd-l1, Single chain variable fragment (scfv), Nanostructured lipid carriers, active targeting

Introduction

Glioblastoma (GBM), the most aggressive primary brain tumor, represents nearly half of all malignant central nervous system tumors and remains a therapeutic challenge with median survival rates of only 14.6 to 21.1 months despite current standard treatments involving maximal surgical resection followed by radiotherapy and temozolomide (TMZ) chemotherapy approaches (Brown et al., 2022; Weidemann et al., 2023). The poor prognosis reflects both the intrinsic resistance of GBM to conventional therapies and its remarkable capacity for immune evasion.

The PD-1/PD-L1 pathway is fundamentally an immune checkpoint that regulates the immune system, protecting the body against autoimmune responses (Salmaninejad et al., 2019). While PD-1 is typically expressed on the surface of activated T cells, its ligand, PD-L1, can be expressed not only on immune cells such as macrophages and dendritic cells but also on the surfaces of cancer cells (Han et al., 2020). Through this mechanism, cancer cells can evade the immune response and exhibit immunosuppressive effects. The central nervous system (CNS) was long regarded as an immune-privileged site due to the blood-brain barrier (BBB) and the apparent absence of conventional lymphatic drainage. However, recent studies have revealed a functional meningeal lymphatic system and demonstrated that immune cells (e.g., T cells) and antigens can access the CNS (Litak et al., 2019). Despite this immune surveillance, GBM exploits multiple mechanisms to evade immune destruction, including overexpression of PD-L1 on tumor cells (Berghoff et al., 2015) and secretion of immunosuppressive extracellular vesicles (EVs) that shape the tumor microenvironment (Schweiger et al., 2024). While immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) has emerged as a potential therapeutic strategy, supported by evidence that PD-1-targeting scFvs delivered via AAV9 vectors exert antitumor effects (Maksoud et al., 2024), monotherapy with ICBs has shown limited clinical efficacy in GBM (Chan et al., 2021). Combination approaches, particularly with temozolomide (TMZ), have thus gained attention (Dai et al., 2018). However, TMZ dosing is critical: standard doses induce T-cell exhaustion and undermine ICB efficacy, whereas lower doses avoid this suppression (Karachi et al., 2019). Previous studies have also demonstrated that TMZ induces PD-L1 upregulation in GBM cells (Wang et al., 2019; Zhao et al., 2023; Lee et al., 2021), thereby facilitating immune evasion.

To address these challenges, we developed a novel nanotherapeutic approach using nanostructured lipid carriers (NLCs) to deliver a low dose of TMZ (5 mg/kg), aiming to reduce T-cell dysfunction while leveraging the dual role of TMZ as a chemotherapeutic agent and a modulator of PD-L1 expression. NLCs represent an advanced drug delivery platform particularly suited for brain tumor therapy due to their ability to cross the blood-brain barrier, a property derived from their lipid composition, small particle size, and physiological stability (Garg et al., 2022; Eleraky et al., 2020; Correia et al., 2022). These nanoparticles not only enhance drug delivery to tumor sites but also allow for surface modification with targeting ligands (Jnaidi et al., 2020; Di Filippo et al., 2022). In this study, we functionalized TMZ-loaded NLCs with PD-L1-specific single-chain variable fragments (scFvs) to create a dual-function therapeutic agent. This design capitalizes on the advantages of NLC-mediated drug delivery while adding active targeting capability to PD-L1-overexpressing tumor cells. The conjugated scFv fragments fulfill two complementary functions: they guide TMZ-loaded nanoparticles to tumor cells and concurrently inhibit the PD-1/PD-L1 immune checkpoint to counteract tumor-driven immunosuppression. Integrating chemotherapy and targeted immunotherapy within a single nanoparticle platform, this strategy seeks to address the limitations of current GBM therapies through synergistic mechanisms, while reducing the systemic toxicity commonly associated with conventional treatment regimens.

Materials and methods

Materials and reagents

The Compritol ATO 888, Gellucire 44/14, and Labrafac WL 1349 used in producing the nanostructured lipid carriers were kindly gifted by Gattefossé. Stearylamine (305,391) and Cremophor EL (C5135) were purchased from Sigma. The PD-L1 single-chain antibody fragment (scFv - HPAB-0646-WJ-S(P)) was purchased from Creative Biolabs. The Sulfo-SMCC (22,322), Traut’s Reagent (26,101), Ellman’s Reagent (22,582), and Zeba Spin Columns (89,892) used in the conjugation processes were obtained from Thermo Scientific. Temozolomide (AC466760050) was purchased from Fisher Scientific. All remaining materials and solvents were used at Pharma grade and HPLC Grade.

Design of nanostructured lipid nanoparticles

TMZ-loaded NLCs were prepared using the melt emulsification technique. Initially, lipid components including 50 mg of Compritol ATO 888, 50 mg of Gelucire 44/14, 25 mg of Labrafac, and 2 mg of Stearylamine were mixed and heated to 80 °C. Similarly, 10 mL of distilled water was mixed with 200 mg of Cremophor EL and heated to the same temperature. TMZ (10 mg) was added to the aqueous phase just prior to combining it with the lipid phase to avoid any potential decomposition. The aqueous phase containing TMZ was incorporated into the hot lipid mixture. This combined mixture was then homogenized using an Ultra-Turrax at a speed of 10,000 rpm and a temperature of 80 °C for 10 min. The optimum formulation was determined using factorial design studies with the Minitab program.

The functionalization of nanoparticles with anti-PD-L1 single-chain fragment variable (scFv) was achieved using the thiol-maleimide reaction (Martínez-Jothar et al., 2018). The nanoparticles were initially dispersed in a phosphate buffer (pH 7.2). Traut’s reagent, which introduces thiol groups onto the nanoparticle surface, was added to the dispersion. This mixture was stirred at room temperature (20–25 °C) for 1 h to ensure adequate surface modification. Following the reaction, the mixture was filtered using an Amicon Centrifuge Filter (4 mL-10 KDa cut-off). Concurrently, the PD-L1 scFv was prepared for conjugation by mixing with sulfo-SMCC in pH 7.2 phosphate buffer. This mixture was allowed to react for 30 min. Excess sulfo-SMCC in the solution was removed using a Zeba spin column. The thiolated nanoparticles were then combined with the prepared scFv. The mixture was left to incubate at room temperature for 30 min, allowing the maleimide groups on the scFv to form stable thioether bonds with the thiol groups on the nanoparticles (Fig. 1).

Characterization studies

Particle size and zeta potential

The particle size, polydispersity index (PDI), and surface charge (zeta potential) of the NLC formulations were assessed using the Horiba nanoPartica SZ-100V2 Series instrument. Results from the particle size and zeta potential measurements were reported as the mean ± standard deviation, based on three individual replicates.

For SDS-PAGE analyses, Invitrogen 10-well mini NuPAGE Bis-Tris precast gels (NP0321BOX) were used. GelCode Blue Stain Reagent (24,590) was employed for staining. The Prime-Step™ Prestained Broad Range Protein Ladder (773,302) was used as the protein ladder.

Drug loading efficiency

In drug loading studies, a 3 mL suspension of nanoparticles was centrifuged at 3700 g for 30 min using a 100 kDa Amicon filter. Subsequently, 100 μL of the remaining 500 μL suspension retained above the filter was taken and diluted to 5 mL with the HPLC mobile phase. The sample underwent ultrasonication for 30 s to ensure proper disruption of nanoparticles and to dissolve the released TMZ. Following this, the solution was filtered through a 0.45 μm injector filter and further diluted ten-fold with the HPLC mobile phase for analysis using HPLC.

The TMZ content was determined using High Performance Liquid Chromatography (ultra HPLC, Agilent Technologies 1290 series, USA). The HPLC settings were as follows: mobile phase, Methanol / 0.5 % Acetic Acid (20:80); injection volume, 20 μL; flow rate, 1.1 mL/min; detector, DAD at 330 nm; column, C18 (150 × 4.6 mm, 5.0 μm); temperature, 35 °C. The following parameters were considered in evaluating the HPLC method and the results obtained: linearity, accuracy, precision, repeatability, and sensitivity.

Drug release study

In vitro release studies for NLC formulations were carried out using the Dialysis Bag Method. 1 mL of NLC formulation was placed inside a dialysis membrane (Sigma, D9277 - MW 14,000 cut-off) and fully immersed in 10 mL appropriate release medium (PBS pH 6.8 and pH 4.5 acetate buffer) at 37 °C while agitated at 200 rpm. Samples were collected at predetermined time intervals (0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, and 24 h), and the amount of TMZ was analyzed using HPLC.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

For TEM imaging, 400 mesh carbon-coated copper grids were used. Each grid was loaded with 3.5 μL of the sample and left to settle for 20–30 s before being blotted to remove any excess. Following this, the grids underwent negative staining three times using 2 % Uranyl acetate, filtered to ensure clarity. Each staining session lasted 30 s. After each staining, the grids were blotted again and left to dry completely. Once dry, the grids were examined using a JEOL JEM-1200 EX II (Japan) transmission electron microscope equipped with a 1k CCD camera.

NLC stability

The NLCs were subjected to stability analysis to determine particle size and zeta potential at two different temperatures, room temperature and 4 °C. These measurements were conducted over specific time intervals, namely 3 and 7 days, to assess the stability and consistency of the NLC formulations under varying storage conditions.

Cytotoxicity studies

The GL261 murine glioma cell line was cultured using RPMI cell culture medium enriched with 10 % fetal bovine serum and 1 % penicillin-streptomycin. These cells were kept in a humidified atmosphere at 37 °C with 5 % CO2, ensuring optimal conditions for their growth. Regular passaging was performed to maintain the cells in their exponential growth phase.

To assess cell viability under various experimental conditions, GL261 cells were seeded at a density of 5000 cells per well into 96-well plates. The CellTiter-Glo® 2.0 assay (Promega Corporation, USA) was utilized to measure the cell viability. TMZ solutions, empty nanoparticles, and TMZ-loaded NLCs (TMZ-NP) were evaluated across a concentration range spanning from 0 to 1000 μM, with specific concentrations tested at 0, 2.5, 5, 10, 25, 50, 100, 250, 500, and 1000 μM.

Cellular uptake studies

Cellular uptake studies of nanoparticles were conducted using two approaches, both of which involved fluorescent labeling to facilitate visualization and measurement. In the first approach, GL261 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 with l-glutamine (Corning, USA) supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum and 1 % penicillin-streptomycin and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5 % CO2. GL261 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 3 × 105 cells per well and allowed to attach overnight. GL261 cells were seeded in a 6-well plate at a density of 3 × 105 cells per well. The nanoparticles were labeled with the fluorescent DiL dye (30,423 - Cayman Chemical, USA) (10 μL from 1 ppm DiL solution in ethanol) for tracking purposes. The following day, these labeled nanoparticles were administered to the cells at 5 μM and 10 μM. After 24 h, the cells were washed twice with PBS to remove unbound nanoparticles and then detached from the plates using then detached using 0.25 % trypsin-EDTA solution (Gibco, USA). The cells were centrifuged at 1250 rpm for 3 min, resuspended in PBS, transferred to a 96-well plate, and analyzed for fluorescence. The fluorescence signals were read at an excitation wavelength of 535 nm and an emission wavelength of 580 nm.

In the second approach, GFP-expressing CT2A cells (CT2A-GFP) were used to further assess nanoparticle uptake. CT2A-GFP cells were cultured in DMEM 1x with 4.5 g/L glucose, l-glutamine and sodium pyruvate (Corning, USA) supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum and 1 % penicillin-streptomycin and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5 % CO2. Sterilized cover slides coated with poly-d-lysine (0.1 mg/mL, Gibco,USA) were placed in a 24-well plate, and CT2A-GFP cells were seeded at 5 × 104 cells per well. The following day, nanoparticles labeled with fluorescent DiL dye (30,423 – Cayman Chemical, USA) (10 μL from 1 ppm DiL solution in ethanol) were applied to these cells. After an incubation period of 2 h at 37 °C, during which uptake occurred, the cells were washed twice with PBS, fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature and washed again with PBS. Then cells were stained with DAPI to label the nuclei. The cells were then visualized under a fluorescence microscope to observe the distribution and localization of the nanoparticles within the cells.

In vitro ligand expression assessment

The targeting efficiency of nanoparticles was assessed through flow cytometry, employing APC anti-PD-L1 antibody (124,311 – BioLegend, USA) as a fluorescent marker. Untreated tumor cells, which lack exposure to the APC anti-PD-L1, serve as a baseline by not emitting any fluorescent signal. In contrast, tumor cells treated with APC anti-PD-L1 exhibit a robust fluorescence, indicating successful binding of the antibody to the PD-L1 ligands on their surface.

In vivo blood-brain barrier penetration study of nanoparticles

The ability of nanoparticles to penetrate the BBB was explored with this study design. For tracking purposes, the nanoparticles were labeled with fluorescent DiR dye (34,954 – Cayman Chemical, USA) dye. Two doses of these labeled nanoparticles, 100 μL (8 mg NLCs) and 250 μL (20 mg NLCs), were administered intraperitoneally to 12-week-old female athymic nude mice.

Using the IVIS imaging system (Xenogen IVIS 200 Imaging System, Perkin Elmer, USA), live imaging was first conducted to monitor the real-time distribution and migration of the nanoparticles throughout the mice’s brains. Following this, the mice were euthanized, and their brains were removed. Then, ex vivo imaging of the brains was performed using the IVIS system. The binning factor was 4, the f-number was 4, the emission filter was 800 nm, the excitation filter was 745 nm, and the exposure time was 25-sec for brain tissue imaging, and 50 s for whole mice head imaging. This step was carried out to confirm that the nanoparticles had indeed penetrated the brain tissue.

In vivo tumor response and survival study

Animal experiments using C57BL/6 were approved by the Massachusetts General Hospital Subcommittee on Research Animal Care (IACUC Protocol #2016N000267). The study involved GL261 cells genetically modified to express FLUC (firefly luciferase) and mCherry. FLUC provides bioluminescence for imaging in the IVIS system, and mCherry is a fluorescent protein that enables visualization under a fluorescence microscope.

To establish tumors, each mouse was injected with 105 FLUC-expressing GL261 cells directly into the left frontal lobe. The injection site was located 2 mm lateral and 0.5 mm anterior to the bregma, at a depth of 2.5 mm from the skull surface. Post-implantation, tumor growth was monitored using the IVIS system. Seven days post-injection, when the Flux signal in the animals reached a level of 105, treatments were initiated.

The dosage of TMZ for the groups receiving TMZ solution, TMZ loaded NLCs (TMZ-NP), and scFv-conjugated NLCs (TMZ-NP-scFv) was 5 mg/kg. Similarly, the dosage of scFv for the group receiving only scFv and the group receiving TMZ-NP-scFv was 2 mg/kg. These formulations were administered intraperitoneally on the 7th, 9th, and 11th days following tumor cell implantation.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis of the data was conducted using GraphPad Prism 9. Depending on the experimental design and data distribution, an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test or one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s Multiple Comparison Test was employed to evaluate statistical differences between groups. Significance levels were denoted as ***P < 0.001, **P < 0.01, *P < 0.05, and ns for non-significant.

When analyzing the Pareto charts (Fig. 2A), which visually represent the influence of independent variables on specified outcomes, it is noted that all independent variables significantly affect the particle size with a similar degree of impact. Furthermore, the analysis reveals that the amount of surfactant predominantly influences PDI and Zeta potential. This indicates that variations in surfactant concentration are critical for adjusting the dispersity and stability of the nanoparticles. On the other hand, the homogenization speed exhibits a moderate influence on both PDI and Zeta potential, pointing to its role in affecting the physical properties of the dispersion, albeit less critically than the surfactant concentration. Lastly, filtration has no significant impact on either PDI or Zeta potential.

The main effect charts (Fig. 2B) highlight several key trends related to the formulation adjustments and their impact on NLCs. Increasing the surfactant amount reduces particle size and PDI, indicating a trend toward smaller and more uniform nanoparticles. Increasing the homogenizer RPM effectively reduced the particle size and PDI. Nonetheless, these mechanical adjustments did not significantly alter the zeta potential. Filtering the nanoparticle formulations appeared to refine the particle size and PDI further, making the particles more uniform, yet it did not statistically affect the zeta potential.

Factorial design results were utilized to identify the optimum formulation based on the gathered data (Fig. 2C). The parameters set for optimization included maximizing the zeta potential value while minimizing particle size and PDI. Among these parameters, minimizing particle size was prioritized as more significant and critical. Based on these parameters, the optimum formulation was determined to be one where the surfactant concentration is 2 %, the homogenization speed is 10,000 rpm, and the formulation has undergone filtration. This specific formulation corresponds to the NLC coded as NLC 15. Subsequent studies and experiments continued with this optimized formulation, leveraging its properties for further development and application.

Next, the NLCs were conjugated with anti-PD-L1 scFv using the maleimide-thiol conjugation (Martínez-Jothar et al., 2018). Following the conjugation process, the samples were centrifuged using 100 kDa filter tubes to separate the unconjugated anti-PD-L1 scFvs (Fig. 3A). The retentate (material retained on the filter) and the filtrate (material passed through the filter) were analyzed. The Bicinchoninic Acid (BCA) assay was performed to determine the anti-PD-L1 scFv concentrations. The analysis showed that anti-PD-L1 scFv concentration in the retentate was 799.24 μg/mL, while the filtrate had a 19.18 μg/mL concentration. These results indicate that nearly all anti-PD-L1 scFvs at an initial 1 mg/mL were conjugated with the NLCs and restraint in the retentate. These findings were consistent with the results of the SDS-PAGE analysis conducted. Initially, anti-PD-L1 scFvs used in the study, demonstrated a characteristic band around 30 kDa in SDS-PAGE characterization studies, similar to those documented by the supplier. When the same samples analyzed by BCA were assessed using SDS-PAGE, no anti-PD-L1 scFv band was observed in the retentate. This absence indicates that anti-PD-L1 scFvs conjugated with the NLCs did not migrate in the gel, implying a successful conjugation as they form larger complexes with the NLCs that are too large to enter the gel matrix (Fig. 3B). Had there been only a physical interaction (non-covalent binding) between the anti-PD-L1 scFvs and the NLCs, anti-PD-L1scFvs would likely still appear in the SDS-PAGE analysis despite the positive results in the BCA assay. No bands were detected in the filtrate as expected, nor were there any bands indicative of NLCs, which also do not migrate due to their size and composition. Taken together, these results affirm that the conjugation was successfully executed, effectively chemically attaching the anti-PD-L1 scFvs to the NLCs.

Characterization studies

Drug loading studies

To ascertain the loading percentage of TMZ in the NLCs, a validated HPLC method was employed, as detailed in the Supplementary File Section 1. The drug loading efficiency was found to be 6.85 %.

Drug release study

Release studies were conducted in two different environments: pH 6.8 phosphate buffer and pH 4.5 acetate buffer. The results show that, as expected due to the very small nanoparticle sizes, nearly 100 % release occurred within the first hour in both environments (Fig. 4).

NLC stability

The stability studies of the formulations, conducted at room temperature and at 4 °C over 3 and 7 days, indicate that increasing the amount of surfactant significantly enhances stability. The highest concentration of surfactant (2 %) tested was effective in maintaining nanoparticle stability, as evidenced by the samples highlighted in green (Supplementary File, Section 2).

Cytotoxicity studies

The results of the cytotoxicity study performed in GL261 cells are presented in Fig. 6. Since temozolomide (TMZ) is an alkylating agent that causes DNA modifications, the resulting cytotoxic effect takes time to manifest. In this study, similar to examples in the literature (Villamañan et al., 2021; Soni et al., 2021), the cytotoxic effect becomes more apparent as time progresses after TMZ administration. While no cytotoxic effect is observed 24 h after TMZ application, a noticeable cytotoxic effect emerges after 72 h. An increase in the efficacy of TMZ was also observed in TMZ loaded NLCs.

Cellular uptake studies

The cellular uptake of NLCs was assessed using two different methodologies outlined in the methods section, with the findings given in Fig. 7. Upon examining Fig. 7A, it was observed that neither the PBS control nor the untreated cells exhibited any fluorescent signal. This indicates that no autofluorescence or background noise interfered, providing a clean baseline for assessing the uptake of fluorescently labeled NLCs in this experiment. In contrast, cells treated with the NLC formulation displayed visible fluorescent signals, indicating successful uptake of the fluorescently labeled NLCs. Yet, the analysis revealed no statistically significant differences in fluorescence intensity among the different cell groups treated with the formulation. This uniformity in fluorescence among treated groups suggests that the cells have a certain capacity to internalize nanoparticles, reaching a saturation point beyond which no additional uptake is observed.

The nanoparticles, which are labeled with DiL dye, exhibit red fluorescence. Additionally, the cell nuclei have been stained with DAPI, causing them to emit blue fluorescence. When these images are merged, the combination of colors within the cells confirms that cells have successfully internalized the nanoparticles. This internalization is indicated by the overlapping of green, red, and blue fluorescence, highlighting the effective delivery of nanoparticles to the target cells.

In vitro ligand expression assessment

The targeting efficiency and capability of nanoparticles was conducted using flow cytometry. The potential outcomes of this experiment are schematized in Fig. 8A. The flow cytometry results obtained following the application of pure anti-PD-L1 scFvs (Fig. 8B) and anti-PD-L1 scFv-conjugated NLCs (Fig. 8C) demonstrate the effective binding of both forms to their target, indicating successful surface functionalization and preserved bioactivity of the scFvs upon conjugation.

PD-L1 ligand expression analysis in GL261. A. Schematic diagram illustrating the experimental design for flow cytometry-based assessment of PD-L1 surface expression. B. Quantitative analysis of PD-L1 expression (measured as mean fluorescence intensity, MFI) following treatment with anti-PD-L1 scFv at 2.5 and 10 μg/mL. C. PD-L1 expression levels after treatment with NLC at increasing concentrations of conjugated scFvs (2.5–10 μg/mL).

In this setup, the fluorescent substance is APC-anti-PD-L1, which will produce a signal depending on its binding affinity and amount to PD-L1 ligands on GL261 cells. No fluorescence signal is expected without APC-anti-PD-L1 since there is no intrinsic fluorescence emission from the tumor cells. When tumor cells are treated with APC anti-PD-L1 antibodies, these antibodies are expected to bind to ligands on the cell surface, producing a high fluorescence signal level due to this interaction. When tumor cells treated with anti-PD-L1 scFv-conjugated NLCs are subsequently incubated with APC anti PD-L1, it is expected that the anti-PD-L1 scFvs on the NLCs will bind to the ligands on the cell surface, thereby reducing the level of binding of the APC anti-PD-L1 antibodies and resulting in a lower fluorescence emission. Similarly, if tumor cells are treated directly with only scFvs (unconjugated, pure) and then with APC anti-PD-L1, the scFvs are expected to saturate the ligands on the tumor surface, thereby reducing the level of APC anti PD-L1 antibody binding and resulting in a low fluorescence signal.

When analyzing the flow cytometry results after applying pure anti-PD-L1 scFvs (Fig. 8B), as expected, no signal was obtained in cells that were not treated with APC anti PD-L1. Observations from the control group, which received only APC anti-PD-L1, showed that 70 % of PD-L1 ligands were expressed on GL261 cells. This confirms that the GL261 cell line is suitable for studies involving PD-L1. When a concentration of 2.5 μg/mL of pure anti-PD-L1 scFvs was applied, the level of PD-L1 expression decreased from 70 % to around 15 %. This significant reduction indicates that anti-PD-L1 scFvs effectively bind to PD-L1 ligands on the tumor cell surface, blocking their availability. Increasing the concentration of anti-PD-L1 scFvs to 10 μg/mL did not significantly decrease the amount of available ligands on the cell surface. This suggests that a concentration of 2.5 μg/mL of anti-PD-L1 scFvs is sufficient to effectively block the ligands on the cell surfaces, indicating that higher concentrations do not enhance this blocking effect.

Similar results have been observed in groups treated with anti-PD-L1 scFv-conjugated NLCs (Fig. 8C), mirroring the outcomes obtained with groups treated with the same concentration of pure anti-PD-L1 scFvs. This demonstrates that the conjugation of anti-PD-L1 scFvs to NLCs does not impair their ability to bind to PD-L1 ligands. As the concentration of anti-PD-L1 scFv used increased, there was a slight decrease in the amount of available PD-L1 ligands on the tumor cell surfaces. However, this decrease was not significant, indicating that increasing the anti-PD-L1 scFv concentration beyond a certain level does not substantially enhance the blocking of PD-L1 ligands on tumor cells.

In vivo blood brain barrier penetration study of nanoparticles

The ability of NLCs to cross the BBB was evaluated 2 h after 100 μL (8 mg NLCs) and 250 μL (20 mg NLCs), were administered intraperitoneally to 12-week-old female athymic nude mice. As shown in Fig. 9A, an increase in the injected dose of NLCs, results in a corresponding increase in the fluorescence signal detected, indicating higher concentrations of NLCs reaching the brain. Subsequently, the animals were euthanized, and their brains were extracted for further analysis.

BBB penetration assessment of NLCs. A. In vivo fluorescence imaging at 2 h post-intraperitoneal administration of fluorescent DiR labeled NLCs (dose: 8–20 mg) in naive mice. B. Ex vivo fluorescence imaging of excised brains demonstrating successful NLC accumulation. Color scale indicates fluorescence intensity.

Ex vivo imaging results, presented in Fig. 9B, corroborate the in vivo imaging findings, showing a similar trend of increased fluorescence with higher doses of NLCs. These consistent results across both in vivo and ex vivo analyses underscore the capability of the NLCs, when administered intraperitoneally, to cross the BBB and accumulate within brain tissue successfully.

In vivo tumor response and survival study

To facilitate in vivo studies and enable the monitoring of tumor growth, GL261 cells were initially transduced with a lentivirus expressing firefly luciferase (Fluc) and mCherry fluorescent protein. Fluc enables bioluminescence imaging to track tumor progression over time, while the mCherry protein provides a fluorescence imaging option (detailed in the Supplementary File Section 3).

The summary of the animal experiments is presented schematically in Fig. 10A. Treatments started seven days post-tumor implantation when the Fluc bioluminescence signal reached 105, with formulations administered three times at a 2-day interval. Tumor growth was monitored using the IVIS system (Fig. 10A). The results of the tumor response studies are presented in Figs. 10B and 10C. Initially, for the first two weeks, tumor growth progressed similarly across all experimental groups, showing no statistical differences. However, from the third week onwards, a sharp increase in tumor volume was noted in all groups except for the one treated with TMZ-loaded anti-PD-L1 scFv NLCs (TMZ-NP-scFv), which remained stable when compared to the rest of the groups.

In vivo therapeutic efficacy evaluation of TMZ-loaded NLCs in orthotopic GL261 glioma model. A. Schematic overview of the experimental timeline and treatment groups. B. Representative in vivo bioluminescence imaging (IVIS) and corresponding flux measurements (photons/sec) of tumor-bearing mice at day 21 post-implantation. C. Quantitative tumor growth kinetics monitored by weekly bioluminescence flux measurements (mean ± SEM, n = 5 mice/group). Statistical significance was determined by one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post-hoc test (*P < 0.05 vs control; ns = not significant). D. Kaplan-Meier survival curves showing significant prolongation of median survival in treatment groups compared to controls (P < 0.05 by log-rank Mantel-Cox test). Treatment groups included: (i) saline control, (ii) free TMZ (5 mg/kg), (iii) TMZ loaded NLCs (5 mg/kg), (iv) free scFv (2 mg/kg), (v) TMZ-loaded (5 mg/kg) anti-PD-L1 scFv conjugated (2 mg/kg) NLCs.

We also assessed overall survival, depicted in Fig. 10D. All animals in the control group had died, with no deaths occurring in the other groups during the same period. There was no significant difference in overall survival in cohorts treated with TMZ solution, TMZ-loaded NLCs (TMZ-NP), and pure anti-PD-L1 scFv (P > 0.05). However, the median survival in these three groups was significantly prolonged compared to the control group (**, P < 0.01). Mice treated with TMZ-NP-scFv had the longest overall survival (47 in days; compared to the other three treatment groups **P < 0.01), with one animal still alive at day 45 post-implantation when this experiment was terminated.

Overall, these results demonstrate that while all treatment groups were somewhat effective in extending overall survival compared to the control group, mice treated with TMZ-NP-scFv had the best survival outcome, highlighting the potential of our therapeutic approach in this experimental model.

Discussion

In this study, we developed a dual-action nanotherapeutic strategy for GBM that combines TMZ chemotherapy with PD-L1-targeted immunotherapy using scFv-functionalized NLCs. Our approach leverages the benefits of both chemotherapeutic and immunomodulatory agents, aiming to improve tumor targeting, overcome immune suppression, and enhance therapeutic efficacy while maintaining a favorable safety profile.

A central innovation of our system is the conjugation of anti-PD-L1 scFv fragments to TMZ-loaded NLCs, which enables simultaneous chemotherapy and immune checkpoint blockade. Importantly, post-conjugation, the scFv retained its binding affinity for PD-L1, as confirmed by flow cytometry. This is particularly relevant given that TMZ itself can induce PD-L1 expression in GBM cells, potentially exacerbating immune evasion (Wang et al., 2019; Zhao et al., 2023; Lee et al., 2021). By integrating an anti-PD-L1 component directly into the TMZ delivery vehicle, our design directly counters this mechanism of resistance.

Systematic analysis of formulation parameters revealed that all independent variables: surfactant concentration, homogenization speed, and filtration, significantly influenced particle size, though to a similar degree. Surfactant concentration was the primary factor affecting both PDI and zeta potential, indicating its critical role in determining nanoparticle dispersity and stability. In contrast, homogenization speed exerted a moderate influence on PDI and zeta potential, suggesting that while it affects physical dispersion properties, its impact is less pronounced than that of surfactant concentration. Filtration, however, did not significantly influence either PDI or zeta potential. Main effect analysis further demonstrated that increasing surfactant concentration reduced both particle size and PDI, resulting in smaller, more uniform nanoparticles. This is consistent with previous findings showing that higher surfactant concentrations enhance surface stabilization and prevent particle coalescence during formation. For example, Matarazzo et al. reported that increasing Synperonic™ PE/F68 surfactant from 5 % to 10 % reduced particle size from 260 nm to 160 nm (Matarazzo et al., 2024). Similarly, increasing homogenization speed also led to reductions in particle size and PDI, as supported by Mai et al. (Mai et al., 2018), who observed that higher homogenization speeds yielded smaller nanoparticles. These findings underscore the importance of optimizing both surfactant concentration and homogenization speed to achieve desired nanoparticle characteristics. Using standard hot-emulsification (González-Mira et al., 2011; Vieira et al., 2020), we achieved sub-100 nm NLCs by optimizing key parameters: reduced lipid content, increased surfactant (2 %), and high homogenization speed (10,000 rpm). While this approach typically yields larger particles (Li et al., 2020; Neha et al., 2023), careful parameter balancing enabled smaller sizes without significantly compromising drug loading.

The anti-PD-L1 scFv was successfully conjugated to NLCs via maleimide-thiol chemistry, achieving >80 % conjugation efficiency. Comprehensive evaluation revealed a TMZ loading capacity of 7 %, consistent with literature values for similarly sized NLCs (Song et al., 2016; Qu et al., 2016) and attributable to the high aqueous solubility of TMZ. The formulation showed rapid drug release kinetics, likely due to the small particle size (increased surface area) and hydrophilicity of TMZ. As expected from the pH-sensitive stability of this alkylating agent (Waghule et al., 2021), degradation was more pronounced at pH 6.8 than pH 4.5.

In cytotoxicity studies, higher toxicity was observed in the blank NLC group, likely due to stearylamine. The other lipids used—Compritol ATO 888, Gelucire 44/14, and Labrafac WL 1349—are GRAS-listed and not associated with significant cytotoxicity (Yuan et al., 2008; Mura et al., 2021; Bondì et al., 2007). Although stearylamine is known to be cytotoxic in vitro (Dukovski et al., 2019; Tahara et al., 2018), in vivo data suggest a more favorable safety profile. According to the GHS classification (Gadaleta et al., 2019), it falls into the lowest toxicity category for oral administration (Group 5, LD50 > 2000 mg/kg) and moderate for parenteral use (Group III, LD50 = 250 mg/kg). Previous in vivo studies using higher stearylamine doses also reported no systemic toxicity (Jeengar et al., 2022; Mallén et al., 2024; Dhaundiyal et al., 2016). In line with these findings, no adverse effects were observed following NLC administration in our in vivo experiments.

In vitro cellular uptake and ligand blocking assays in GL261 and CT2A cells confirmed robust nanoparticle internalization and PD-L1 targeting efficiency. These results validate the dual functionality of the construct, tumor-specific delivery and immune checkpoint blockade. The in vivo results further underscore the translational promise of the platform. The TMZ-NP-scFv group showed delayed tumor progression and significantly extended survival compared to monotherapy groups and controls. While survival improvement was moderate compared to some studies reporting complete remission (Wu et al., 2020; Simonds et al., 2021; Park et al., 2019), this can be attributed to the conservative dosing regimen used here (TMZ: 5 mg/kg; scFv: 2 mg/kg × 3 doses). In contrast, prior studies often employed higher or more frequent doses of TMZ (30–60 mg/kg) and antibodies (100–500 μg/dose) over longer periods. Our goal was to minimize systemic toxicity and focus on demonstrating the feasibility and additive benefit of dual therapy at clinically relevant, lower doses. It is worth noting that while the NLCs provided clear benefit, future work should address further pharmacokinetic profiling, organ-level biodistribution, and long-term immunogenicity. More comprehensive immune profiling and combinatorial regimens (e.g., with radiotherapy or checkpoint inhibitors like anti-CTLA-4) could provide additional insights and therapeutic gains.

Limitations of the study

Despite the promising results, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, while PD-L1 binding was demonstrated, the absence of isotype or PD-L1 knockout controls limits definitive conclusions regarding binding specificity. Second, although the nanoparticles were designed to modulate the immune response, we did not directly evaluate changes in immune cell infiltration or activation within the tumor microenvironment. An important consideration in our study is the use of FLUC-expressing GL261 cells, which may introduce mild immunogenicity (Sanchez et al., 2020). However, as FLUC expression was consistent across all groups and supported by survival data, its confounding effect is likely negligible. Additionally, the TMZ loading efficiency was modest, and rapid drug release could affect sustained efficacy, though this was partially offset by improved BBB penetration. The use of a single syngeneic GBM model (GL261) limits generalizability, and the therapeutic regimen was restricted to three low-dose administrations, potentially underestimating full therapeutic potential. Finally, long-term safety and systemic biodistribution were not addressed but will be explored in future work.

In summary, our dual-action nanotherapeutic strategy integrates TMZ chemotherapy with PD-L1-targeted immunotherapy in a single delivery platform, addressing key challenges in GBM treatment. The optimization of formulation parameters, particularly surfactant concentration and homogenization speed, is critical for generating nanoparticles with optimal size, stability, and therapeutic potential. This approach holds promise for improving outcomes in GBM by simultaneously targeting tumor cells and the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment.
