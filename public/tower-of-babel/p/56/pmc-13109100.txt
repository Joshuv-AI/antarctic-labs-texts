Circadian misalignment and adolescent obesity risk behaviors: physical & sedentary activity, dietary intake, and food reward
Authors: Kara M Duraccio, Afton B Craig, Sarah Kamhout, Isabella D Wright, Gracie T Crandall, Nicholas York, Chad D Jensen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesSleep duration and quality frequently predict obesity-related behaviors, though less is known about how circadian misalignment relates to obesogenic behaviors. The current study evaluated the impact of experimentally induced circadian misalignment on dietary intake, food reward, and physical/sedentary activity in adolescents.MethodsThirty-one adolescents (ages 14–18 (M = 15.5, SD = 0.89); 37.5 percent female) with night owl tendencies underwent a randomized cross-over experimental sleep timing manipulation. Specifically, adolescents spent both 5 nights in an aligned sleep pattern (spending from 1:00 to 10:00 am in bed) and 5 nights in a misaligned sleep pattern (spending from 9:30 pm to 6:30 am in bed), with the order of the condition randomized. All adolescents wore an Actiwatch 2 to determine adherence to study protocols and an ActiGraph GT3x + to measure physical and sedentary activity; adolescents completed dietary recalls throughout the study protocol and the Power of Food Scale after each manipulation. We ran a series of repeated-measure general linear models to test for differences in adolescent dietary intake, food reward, and physical/sedentary activity.ResultsParticipants consumed a moderate amount of more carbohydrates in the misaligned condition, compared to the aligned condition (t(15) = -2.14; p = .049; g = -.522). There were no other differences in dietary intake noted across conditions, nor any differences in food reward or physical activity outcomes across conditions.ConclusionsCircadian misalignment, independent of sleep loss, may play a limited role in shaping short-term obesogenic behaviors in adolescents, highlighting the need for future interventions and research to continue prioritizing sleep duration and quality while clarifying the contexts in which sleep timing may be most relevant.Keywords: sleep, obesity, adolescents, circadian misalignment, physical activity, dietary intake

Adolescents are prone to circadian misalignment (CM), yet its impact on obesity risk behaviors is poorly understood. In this experimental study, we found that adolescents consumed a greater proportion of carbohydrates when misaligned, even without sleep debt. However, CM did not independently influence overall caloric intake, other macronutrients, food reward, or physical activity. These mixed findings suggest that CM may selectively affect dietary patterns rather than broadly altering weight-related behaviors. Because adolescents frequently experience CM in combination with short sleep, future research should examine their joint and prolonged effects. Understanding how multiple facets of sleep health interact will be critical for informing obesity prevention strategies during this vulnerable developmental period.

Introduction

Obesity prevalence in adolescents remains an urgent public health concern [1]. Obesity increases risk for health conditions such as diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and cancer [2] and persists into adulthood [3, 4]. Sleep has been identified as a modifiable health behavior with considerable implications for obesity development and treatment in adolescents [5, 6]. Unfortunately, adolescents have experienced the greatest decline in total sleep duration over recent decades compared to any other developmental group [7], likely due to increases in circadian misalignment (CM; the magnitude of the discrepancy between the body’s intrinsic sleep schedule (circadian phase) and a person’s actual sleep timing). Adolescents experience developmental changes in the bioregulatory processes that influence their circadian phase (i.e. circadian phase delay of up to 2 hours, sleep pressure building more slowly across the day), alongside reduced sleep opportunity due to significant social pressures, including screen time, evening social activities, and early school start times [2, 8–10], leading to increased rates of CM. Therefore, research examining how CM may serve as a potential contributor to obesity risk has become imperative in this population.

The majority of adolescent research has focused on how sleep duration is related to obesity risk. Specifically, short sleep duration is associated with numerous eating-related behaviors that can increase risk for obesity in adolescents [11–13]. Specifically, sleep deprivation is associated with greater loss of control while eating [14] as well as greater intake of foods with higher glycemic load, carbohydrates, and sugars [15–17]. Moreover, experimental studies suggest sleep deprivation and poor sleep quality in adolescents increase food reward, value, and appeal [18–20]. While it has become accepted that shortened sleep duration influences dietary quality and the rewarding properties of food, the literature is still mixed as to how sleep duration influences the quantity of foods consumed [21–23]. Similarly, research findings are mixed regarding sleep duration’s impact on physical activity [24, 25] though there is greater evidence for sleep duration to increase next day sedentary activity [21, 26–29]. Taken together, extant research indicates that insufficient sleep increases in consumption of high-calorie foods, loss of control eating, food reward, and time spent in sedentary activity, which may serve as potential mechanisms by which short sleep confers risk for poorer dietary quality.

Although considerable attention has focused on the effects of sleep duration and quality on obesity risk behaviors, minimal research has examined effects of circadian factors on weight-related health behaviors. CM in adolescents has been associated with many negative outcomes in areas such as mental health, risk taking, executive functioning, and metabolic functioning [30–34], and recent cross-sectional research has shown promising results that CM might result in less healthy eating behaviors in adolescents [34]. For example, one correlational study found that a circadian pattern shift on weekends was associated with increased intake of sugary drinks and food preoccupation [35]. Shifts in weekday to weekend sleep patterns are common among adolescents with CM [8] and have been linked to worsened cardiometabolic health outcomes [36] and less healthy eating behaviors in adolescents [37]. Furthermore, adolescents who slept according to a “morning-lark” pattern had decreased caloric intake when undergoing an experimental early bedtime sleep extension condition, but adolescents with more “night-owl” tendencies trended toward eating more when undergoing the same early bedtime sleep extension condition [38]. Together, these findings point to CM as a relevant factor in adolescent eating behaviors and obesity risk, extending sleep and obesity research beyond sleep duration alone.

Despite these findings, existing research has been largely cross-sectional, confounded by sleep duration, or has examined circadian timing only indirectly, leaving it unclear whether CM itself causally influences dietary intake, food reward, or physical and sedentary activity. Critically, no study to date has experimentally manipulated circadian alignment while holding sleep opportunity constant to isolate the behavioral effects of CM in adolescents. As a result, the role of CM as a potential mechanism linking poor sleep to obesogenic health behaviors remains poorly understood. The current study addresses this gap and extends upon previous findings by experimentally inducing CM in adolescents with night-owl tendencies while controlling for sleep duration to evaluate how experimentally-induced CM impacts sedentary and physical activity, food reward, and eating behavior. First, we hypothesized that adolescents experiencing CM would demonstrate increased sedentary activity, greater reported food reward, and increased consumption of added sugars, total sugars, and carbohydrates, compared to when they experienced circadian alignment. We also explored the differences across experimental groups in light physical activity, moderate-to-vigorous physical activity, kilocalorie consumption, and fat and protein consumption.

Materials and Methods

Participants

Research staff recruited participants via personal contact, word of mouth, university mail, community flyers, and classroom announcements. Interested adolescents completed an eligibility survey online through Qualtrics. Participants were eligible if they were between the ages of 14 and 18 and reported a habitual bedtime of 12:00 am or later. Exclusion criteria included inability to commit adequate time to follow the study protocol as well as the following: medications that may alter sleep outcomes (e.g. stimulants); self- or parent-report of organic sleep disorder diagnosis; highly atypical sleeping patterns that might indicate the presence of an undiagnosed sleep disorder (e.g. regularly sleeping <5 hours or >10 hours on school nights); presence of ongoing psychiatric disorders that could be negatively impacted by sleep restriction (e.g. bipolar disorder); history of neurological illness or injury (e.g. epilepsy, traumatic brain injury); intellectual disability or autism spectrum disorder; recurrent use of illegal substances; and daily caffeine consumption that exceeded more than a single cup of coffee/energy drink or more than two caffeinated sodas per day. As research findings aim to help inform obesity-prevention models, we also excluded adolescents with a body mass index (BMI) percentile at least 95 percent.

Procedure

All study procedures (Figure 1) were approved by the principal investigator’s Institutional Review Board. To avoid potential negative interference with school performance, this study took place over the summer months. All participants and parent/guardians were informed about the study protocol, potential risks, benefits, and compensation before offering assent/consent. Participants then completed baseline sleep and voluntary demographic questionnaires. Following the baseline appointment, participants began a 5-night stabilization period, in which they self-selected a consistent preferred bedtime and waketime. Specifically, they were asked, “Imagining a night where you had no obligations, responsibilities, activities to distract yourself, or any obligations the following morning that might influence what time you fall asleep; what time would you naturally fall asleep under these conditions?” Waketime was then determined by adding 9 hours to the bedtime of their choosing. During this period, adolescents were also asked to wear a wrist-worn and waist-worn actigraphy device and complete a sleep diary via Qualtrics each morning.

Study design overview. DLMO = dim-light melatonin onset.

Following the stabilization period, adolescents underwent a dim-light melatonin onset (DLMO) appointment, where melatonin concentrations were measured over 6 hours via saliva samples. Following the DLMO appointment, participants once again adhered to their self-selected bedtime and waketime over a 2-night wash-out period to avoid confounding effects of fatigue after late-night appointment on experimental condition.

Following this wash-out period, the experimental phase consisted of two 5-day arms and a 2-night wash-out period separating the two conditions for a total of 12 nights. One arm consisted of an aligned phase where the participant slept from 1:00 to 10:00 am (similar to night-owl tendencies) and the other arm consisted of the misaligned phase where the participant slept from 9:30 pm to 6:30 am (similar to morning-lark tendencies). The order of the conditions was randomized for each participant. While in each experimental arm, participants wore a wrist-worn and waist-worn accelerometer, completed a daily sleep diary via Qualtrics, and completed 2 24-hour dietary recalls [39] on two randomly selected nights. Following each experimental manipulation phase, adolescents were also asked to complete the Power of Food Scale [40]. A total of $350 in compensation was provided for participants’ time.

Measures

Demographics

Demographic information was collected through a voluntary 12-question online self-report survey and included biological sex (male/female), age, ethnicity, race, and current medications.

Body mass index (BMI) percentile

During the baseline appointment, height and weight were assessed via a standardized imperial system scale and stadiometer. Participant sex, age, height, and weight were inputed into the CDC BMI Calculator for children and teens (2023). This calculator uses the formula BMI = weight (lbs)/height (in2) × 703, according to Keys et al., but adjusts for adolescent age and sex [41] to create a percentile score. We included BMI percentile as a demographic factor within our sample.

Dietary intake

To evaluate dietary intake, participants completed the Automated Self-Administered 24-hour Dietary Assessment Tool (ASA24) version 202239 for a total of four times during the study (twice during each of the experimental periods). Data from these assessments served as final outcomes in our models. These self-report assessments were assigned on randomized days within the protocol so as to avoid observer effects on participant’s food choices. Participants were notified via text message the morning of an assessment day to recall their food choices from the 24 hours before. If participants did not fill out the report that morning, they were reminded in the evening via text. ASA24 outcomes of interest included daily caloric intake, grams of fat, protein, carbohydrate, sugar, added sugar. Daily macronutrients were divided by daily calories consumed to reflect proportion of daily calories; kilocalories and the daily percentages of macronutrient consumed (%fat, %carbohydrates, %fat, %protein, %sugar, and %added sugar) were averaged across the two measurements to create an averaged value for each eating behavior outcome for each condition.

Baseline sleep characteristics

sleep duration

To measure adherence to the protocol, participants wore Philips Respironics Actiwatch 2 devices on their non-dominant wrists, a reliable measure of sleep [42]. Participants were instructed to put on the device every night before entering bed with the intention to sleep and to take it off in the morning when they got out of bed with the intention to start their day. Wrist-worn accelerometers were initialized for an epoch length of 30 seconds. Data were downloaded in 60-second epoch intervals utilizing the Actiware software. We used daily sleep diaries (completed through a self-administered Qualtrics survey) in collaboration with the donning and doffing of the Actiwatch to guide participant scoring. In these diaries, participants answered questions about what time they entered bed, what time they fell asleep, what time they woke up, what time they got out of bed, and any waking (frequency and duration) in the night. We generated a sleep report for each participant using Sadeh et al.’s sleep algorithm [43] to generate bedtime, sleep onset time, sleep offset time, sleep efficiency, and out-of-bed time to determine adherence.

physical activity

To record physical activity throughout the experimental manipulation protocol, each participant wore a waist-worn Actigraph (GT3X+) over their dominant hip at all times unless there was risk of physical or water damage. These devices were calibrated based on the participants’ height and weight. We used a sample rate of 30 seconds and recorded the data using an epoch length of 15 seconds. The Actigraph GT3X is a reliable method of measuring activity within adolescents [44]. Using Actiware 6.3 software, we analyzed the data using the Freedson et al., cutoffs (sedentary activity (0–25 counts, 15-second epochs), light physical activity (26–555 counts/15-second epochs), and moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) estimates (556+ counts/15-second epochs) across each day [45, 46]. We then averaged daily estimates across both the aligned and misaligned conditions in minutes spent in sedentary, light, and MVPA activity as outcomes in this study.

power of food scale

We measured food reward using the Power of Food Scale (PFS) [40], with the PFS total score serving as the primary outcome in our final models. Participants completed the questionnaire at their baseline appointment and again following each experimental phase. The PFS measures an individual’s appetite-related thoughts, feelings, and motivations toward palatable foods [40], with higher scores reflecting greater food reward. The PFS has a reported Cronbach’s α of .91 [40]; internal consistency for the PFS within our sample was excellent at .945.

circadian timing

We used a DLMO protocol to measure intrinsic circadian timing. These estimates were used to assess whether we adequately recruited a night-owl population and to inform associated subject exclusion. All participants with a DLMO time of 10:00 pm or later were considered to be “night owls,” as this DLMO time would suggest, based on averaged phase angles for adolescents [47], a habitual bedtime of around midnight or later). Conversely, all participants with a DLMO time earlier than 10:00 p.m. were considered to be “morning larks” and were excluded from final analyses.

DLMO is an accurate tool for determining circadian rhythm by measuring melatonin in saliva or blood over time [48]. Specifically, salivary samples of melatonin have been shown be a reliable way of measuring melatonin, having a 0.93 correlation coefficient with plasma DLMO [49]. DLMO appointments within this study lasted a total of 6 hours, beginning 5 hours before and ending 1 hour after a participant’s habitual bedtime. The room was dimly lit (no brighter than 5 lux at eye level, with light levels being confirmed every 30 minutes). Participants provided a saliva sample every 30 minutes for a total of 12 samples across the six-hour period. Ten minutes prior to each sample collection, participants were instructed to refrain from eating/drinking food or beverages and ensure that their mouth was clean by brushing their teeth with a provided soft-bristled disposable toothbrush. Participants were then instructed to place a cotton swab in their mouth for 2–5 minutes, or until the cotton swab was saturated with saliva. Research staff weighed the samples immediately following collection to ensure that the samples had >2 mL of saliva. Samples were then time-stamped and immediately refrigerated. The following day, the samples were centrifuged in a refrigerated centrifuge (at 8°C) 5000 g for 5 minutes to extract the saliva from the swab, and immediately frozen. The frozen samples were then shipped to SolidPhase for DLMO analysis, an organization that uses Novolytix RIA kits to extract salivary melatonin concentrations. DLMO was determined as the time in which saliva melatonin concentration values exceeded 4 pg/ml, a previously established threshold in pediatric populations [50].

Statistical analysis

We conducted a series of repeated-measures general linear models in SPSS (version 28) to examine the impact of CM on eating behavior outcomes, physical activity outcomes, and food reward. One participant dropped out of the study prematurely, and we excluded ten participants for not adhering to the study protocol. Participants were considered non-adherent if they had an averaged sleep midpoint that deviated at least 2 hours from their assigned condition’s midpoint or instances where difference between the participant’s averaged sleep midpoint in each condition was <2 hours. We also excluded four participants who had a DLMO before 10:00 pm, as this would significantly alter whether the conditions were truly “aligned” or “misaligned” for the individual. Given our reduction in sample from 31 to 16 participants, we ran a post-hoc power analysis in G*Power 3.1.9.7 and determined that we would still have 0.84 power to detect a medium effect group difference using within-subjects repeated measures design, with alpha set to 0.05, correlation among repeated measures set to 0.80. A two-tailed p-value of .05 was selected to determine significance for primary outcomes, and a two-tailed p-value of .01 was selected for all secondary outcomes to reduce the risk of Type 1 error.

Discussion

We conducted a study where we experimentally induced CM in adolescents to determine whether CM altered dietary intake, food reward, or sedentary and physical activity. We observed that adolescents ate a greater proportion of carbohydrates when misaligned, compared to sleeping in an aligned fashion. Similar findings have been demonstrated in experimental sleep restriction studies, where adolescents experiencing short sleep consumed more grams of carbohydrates and foods higher in glycemic index, as compared with intake under adequate sleep conditions [15–17]. Some theorize that adolescents gravitate toward foods higher in carbohydrates when sleep deprived to help obtain fast-acting energy that might combat fatigue; such a theory may hold for this study as well, as those experiencing CM often report greater levels of fatigue and sleepiness [51–53]. Increased carbohydrate consumption rates have been associated with increased risk for obesity and cardiometabolic disease [54, 55], suggesting that advocating for sleep patterns that allow for adolescents to sleep in an aligned fashion may serve as an intervention target for health promotion in adolescents [56].

Outside of differences in carbohydrate consumption, we did not observe differences in consumption of macronutrients or total calories across aligned vs. misaligned groups. Our hypotheses regarding eating behavior were informed by Beebe et al. [38], who found that sleep restriction affected adolescents’ dietary intake differently based on chronotype. We speculate that the absence of observed differences in dietary intake within this study is because only CM was manipulated; based on Beebe et al.’s38 findings, it possible that CM combined with insufficient sleep duration may be more influential in eating behavior than either factor in isolation. Additional studies parsing the effects of short sleep vs. CM are warranted.

Similarly, we found no difference in sedentary and physical activity between the misaligned and aligned conditions. Previous research on sleep duration and physical activity is mixed, with some evidence suggesting that sleep duration does not influence physical activity levels, which would also be in line with our findings for CM [28, 29]. However, we were surprised to find no difference in sedentary activity between the circadian conditions, given that higher levels of CM are often associated with decreased sleep duration, which has been linked to an increase in sedentary activity in adolescents [19, 26, 27]. Similarly, we did not observe significant differences in food reward across CM conditions, despite previous research indicating that insufficient sleep in adolescents is associated with increased food reward [18, 21]. As speculated previously, it is possible that because our experimental paradigm attempted to have sleep duration remain similar across conditions, only changing CM did not influence activity counts and food reward. Additional follow-up studies are likely needed in order to fully understand the relationship between multimodal facets of sleep health, activity, and food reward.

This study has several salient strengths. As one of the first of its kind, this study provides a new perspective on the interaction between CM and food reward, dietary intake, and physical activity. Further, outcomes were measured through multiple modalities to provide a comprehensive characterization of changes on alignment and obesity risk by including measurements of food reward, dietary patterns, and physical activity. This study also recruited adolescents, a population that is historically understudied despite being at great risk for CM and poor sleep. To ensure that this sample included only adolescents who were truly experiencing CM, we used a DLMO protocol to distinguish true “night-owls” from adolescents who have a later bedtime due to environmental factors. Future studies can use these results and methodology with larger sample sizes to continue to explore the nuanced relationship between sleep duration, CM, and obesity-related behaviors.

Despite its strengths, our study is not without limitations. As a pilot study, we recruited a relatively small sample size, with only 16 participants included in the final analysis—half of the original sample goal—largely due to nonadherence to the protocol and misclassification of chronotype after the DLMO protocol. Nonadherence was likely driven by our recruitment of adolescents with habitual bedtimes of midnight or later; nearly all participants excluded were due to an inability to follow the early sleep timing assignments within the misaligned sleep condition. This suggests that our protocol may have been too extreme to support adherence, even though the misaligned schedule mirrored bed and wake times commonly recommended for teens. While our statistical power was still sufficient to detect effects due to the within-subject nature of this protocol, a larger sample size would allow for the detection of smaller effects, if present. Additionally, the participants in our study were predominantly white and from higher socioeconomic backgrounds, limiting the generalizability of our findings. Future research should prioritize recruiting a larger and more diverse sample to enhance representativeness and ensure findings are broadly applicable.

Another limitation of our study was the short duration of the experimental conditions, which were limited to five nights. This is much shorter than the length of time adolescents typically experience CM throughout the school year [57]. Furthermore, we acknowledge that obesity status is not a static condition, suggesting that longer study designs or longitudinal research are needed to better capture the potential compounding effects of CM on obesity risk over extended periods, which may mirror real-world adolescent experiences (particularly during the academic year). Moreover, while this study controlled for sleep duration across conditions to isolate the effects of CM, this approach may have limited the ecological validity of our findings. Many adolescents experiencing CM also experience chronic sleep deprivation, a factor that may independently or interactively influence health outcomes. Future studies should examine the combined effects of CM and sleep deprivation on sedentary behavior, physical activity, food reward, and dietary intake to better reflect the lived experiences of adolescents.

Conclusions

The purpose of this study was to investigate the role of CM in obesity-related behaviors among adolescents. Although adolescents consumed a greater proportion of their daily calories from carbohydrates during CM compared to circadian alignment, no significant changes were observed in sedentary or physical activity, food reward, or dietary intake. Taken together, these findings suggest that CM, when experimentally manipulated in the absence of sleep loss, may only modestly influence adolescents’ obesity-related behaviors. Importantly, this study contributes to the growing literature by experimentally isolating CM from sleep duration, helping to clarify the specific pathways through which CM may or may not influence adolescent health.

Future research should build on these findings by exploring how CM interacts with other aspects of sleep health, such as sleep duration, over longer periods of time and in larger, more diverse populations. Such investigations will help unravel the complex interplay between circadian rhythms, sleep behaviors, and obesity risk, while increasing external validity. As obesity continues to be a pressing public health issue, understanding how alignment between biological and social sleep schedules in adolescents holds potential to inform obesity prevention and intervention efforts. Addressing CM may serve as a crucial, modifiable target for improving dietary habits and overall health in this vulnerable population.
