Hematologic immune-related adverse effects of immune checkpoint inhibitors: a review
Authors: Maria T. Cruz Carreras, Francisco González Landrón, Natalie López, Leticia Padrón Alcántara, Dana M. Markides, Kathryn J. Burk, Patrick Chaftari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) targeting CTLA-4, PD-1, and PD-L1 have transformed cancer therapy but may trigger rare, sometimes fatal hematologic immune-related adverse events (irAEs). The most frequent hematologic irAEs are anemia, thrombocytopenia, and pancytopenia, which occur more often with CTLA-4 inhibitors and in patients receiving combination therapy. Risk is heightened when ICIs are combined with chemotherapy or radiation. Common symptoms include fatigue, abnormal bleeding, easy bruising, and recurrent infections. Diagnosis relies on laboratory studies, histologic evaluation, and review of medication history. Management typically involves ICI discontinuation and immunosuppression with corticosteroids or rituximab; adjunctive options include IVIG, transfusions, or plasmapheresis depending on severity. Research is also exploring cytokines such as IL-2, IL-7, and IL-15 for ICI-related lymphopenia. Current evidence is drawn mainly from case reports and small series, highlighting the need for larger studies to clarify incidence, mechanisms, and optimal management. Recent pharmacovigilance and outcomes data confirm these events remain rare and can present late, underscoring the need for long-term surveillance and robust monitoring systems [Fletcher K and Johnson DB 2024; Durbin SM et al. 2025; Yang Y. et al. 2024; Han X. et al. 2025].

Introduction

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) are used as standalone treatments and in combination with other therapies to manage multiple cancer types. The immune system uses checkpoint proteins to establish self-tolerance, which enables proper immune system regulation of cell destruction. The activation of these proteins and their receptors by cancer cells leads to the suppression of immune cell attacks against tumors [1]. The development of ICIs focused on blocking immune checkpoint proteins and their receptors to enhance T-cell antitumor responses [2]. The most common targets for monoclonal antibody treatment among immune checkpoint proteins and receptors include cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (CTLA-4), programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1), and programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-L1). The cancer treatment benefits of ICIs come with various side effects, which healthcare providers need to evaluate. The biological processes of cancer cells and healthy cells throughout the body experience disruption as the main cause of ICIs’ toxic effects. The adverse effects from immune system activation, known as immune-related adverse events (irAEs), impact various body systems and organs. The skin, liver, gastrointestinal tract, endocrine organs, and lungs represent the primary organs affected by irAEs [3]. The incidence of hematologic irAEs stands lower than other irAEs, with reported frequencies between 0.04 and 3.6%4, and mortality can be substantial in severe cases [4]. The main objective of this review examines hematologic irAEs from ICIs by analyzing their occurrence rates, clinical manifestations, and treatment approaches.

Epidemiology

The most commonly seen hematologic irAEs include anemia, which represents 5% of all irAEs, and leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, and neutropenia, each representing 2% (Table 1). The incidence of these cytopenias is noted to increase with combination therapy. Hematologic irAEs associated with CTLA-4 inhibitors usually develop earlier (within 40 days) than those associated with PD-1 and PD-L1 inhibitors [5]. Studies indicate that ICI-related hematologic adverse events most frequently develop in patients aged 59 to 66 years and are more commonly seen in patients presenting with lung cancer or melanoma [5]. Anemias

Autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA)

In autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA), autoantibodies (IgG, IgM, or both) against red blood cells (RBCs) are formed, substantially decreasing the RBCs’ lifespan (Table 2). Although AIHA is an unusual event, it is the most common hematologic ICI-related adverse event. It occurs in 0.15 to 0.25% of patients treated with PD-1 and PD-L1 inhibitors and is less common in patients treated with CTLA-4 inhibitors, occurring in only 0.06%⁶. AIHA most often occurs in patients who have malignant melanoma, followed by those with lung cancer, Hodgkin lymphoma, and renal cell carcinoma. It can develop up to 78 weeks after the last exposure to ICI, with a 10-week median time to occurrence [6]. Approximately 15% of ICI-related AIHA cases are fatal, although definitive causality could not always be established⁷. Autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA) associated with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs)

The two types of drug-induced AIHA are warm AIHA and cold AIHA. Warm AIHA is modulated by IgG, with autoantibodies active at > 37 °C. Cold AIHA is modulated by IgM, with autoantibodies active at 0–4 °C. The warm type is the most common ICI-related AIHA. Patients who develop ICI-related AIHA may have an existing lymphoproliferative disorder or present with other conditions such as babesiosis, viral infections, or prior allogeneic stem cell transplant [6].

Diagnosis is based on clinical findings—fatigue, dyspnea, lightheadedness, jaundice, tachycardia, pallor, and splenomegaly [8]. Severity correlates with the degree of hemolysis. Cold AIHA has additional features, including cold-induced acrocyanosis, Raynaud phenomenon, and livedo reticularis [11].

Laboratory findings usually show decreased haptoglobin and hemoglobin (> 2 g/dL below baseline), elevated indirect bilirubin, LDH, and reticulocytes [2]. Peripheral smear helps differentiate warm AIHA (spherocytes) from cold AIHA (RBC agglutination due to IgM autoantibodies) [7]. Diagnosis of ICI-related AIHA also requires a temporal relationship to ICI therapy and exclusion of other etiologies [7].

First-line therapy is glucocorticoids, typically oral prednisone 0.5–2 mg/kg, or IV methylprednisolone for acute severe cases or if the patient cannot tolerate enteral therapy [7]. Barcellini et al. reported a 75% overall response among 308 steroid-treated patients [13]. Alternative/adjunctive options include IVIG, transfusions, and hematopoietic growth factors [9]. Splenectomy may be considered second-line, with infection precautions against encapsulated bacteria. Rituximab (anti-CD20) is another effective second-line therapy with ~ 70–80% response, sometimes studied with steroids [7]. Immunosuppressants (cyclosporine, cyclophosphamide, azathioprine, mycophenolate mofetil) may be used for refractory cases. Warm AIHA responds best to these strategies, while cold AIHA shows limited steroid/splenectomy benefit; rituximab is first-line in cold AIHA [7].

Cold agglutinin syndrome (CAS)

Cold agglutinin syndrome (CAS) results from cold agglutinins binding to RBCs at low temperatures, causing agglutination and complement-mediated hemolysis. CAS and cold agglutinin disease differ: CAS is secondary (e.g., infections, malignancies, ICI therapy), while CAD is primary clonal B-cell lymphoproliferation [10]. CAS occurrence does not depend on ICI dose or treatment duration [10].

Diagnosis requires bone marrow biopsy, direct antiglobulin testing, smear evaluation, and flow cytometry. Most ICI-related CAS cases recover spontaneously. Persistent cases may require transfusion, plasmapheresis, steroids, or rituximab [12].

Pure red cell aplasia (PRCA)

PRCA is a rare disorder characterized by severe normocytic normochromic anemia with decreased reticulocytes. Bone marrow shows absent erythroid precursors with otherwise normal hematopoiesis [14]. Acquired PRCA occurs with ICI therapy, neoplasms, autoimmune diseases, or infections, due to autoantibodies against erythroid progenitors or erythropoietin.

PRCA presents with reticulocyte counts < 10,000/μL, normal WBCs and platelets, and is confirmed by marrow biopsy [15]. Differential diagnosis is essential [15]. Treatment is corticosteroids, IVIG, and T-cell–directed therapies, sometimes requiring prolonged courses due to relapse risk [16].

Guo et al. studied 13 PRCA patients and reviewed 38 pharmacovigilance reports, finding nivolumab most frequently associated, followed by nivolumab/ipilimumab and pembrolizumab. PRCA typically developed after four cycles, with a mortality rate of 51.7% [17].

Aplastic anemia (AA)

AA is bone marrow failure with pancytopenia and hypocellular marrow. Severe AA is defined by cellularity < 25% or 25–50% with < 30% hematopoietic cells, neutrophils < 0.5 × 10⁹/L, platelets < 20 × 10⁹/L, or reticulocytes < 20 × 10⁹/L [18, 19].

Patients present with pancytopenia and symptoms including fatigue, infections, and easy bruising [5]. Diagnosis requires history, exam, smear, reticulocytes, CBC with differential, and marrow aspirate. Cytogenetics and FISH aid in exclusion of other causes.

Treatment depends on severity and age. Supportive care includes transfusions, antifungal prophylaxis, and antibiotics for severe neutropenia. Definitive therapy is immunosuppressive therapy (horse ATG + cyclosporine) in patients > 40, or bone marrow transplant in younger adults/children [19]. Eltrombopag, a thrombopoietin receptor agonist, shows promise in sustaining hematopoiesis [20], [21]. Cyclosporine A maintenance delays relapse, as shown in an 11-year follow-up [22].

Thrombocytopenia and purpura

Immune thrombocytopenia (ITP)

Immune thrombocytopenia (ITP) occurs when autoantibodies target and destroy platelets. It presents as isolated thrombocytopenia without anemia, leukopenia, or another apparent cause (Table 3). Patients may have purpura, petechiae, hemorrhage, epistaxis, and fatigue. Diagnosis is based on exam, complete blood count (CBC), reticulocyte count, and peripheral smear [23]. Bone marrow biopsy is not typically required but, when performed, shows increased megakaryocytes from destruction exceeding production. Acquired thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP)

Acquired TTP is a thrombotic microangiopathy caused by severe ADAMTS13 protease deficiency, leading to unchecked von Willebrand factor activity and platelet aggregation. Thrombi form in small vessels, damaging organs. The classic pentad includes hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia, fever, neurologic abnormalities, and renal dysfunction [25]. Additional symptoms may include nausea, abdominal pain, diarrhea, dyspnea, chest pain, bleeding, or easy bruising.

TTP is a medical emergency. Standard treatment includes plasma exchange and methylprednisolone (1 g daily × 3 days after plasma exchange). Rituximab is frontline in patients receiving blood/plasma transfusions [23]. ICI-associated hemostatic complications—including TTP and acquired hemophilia—are increasingly cataloged and should trigger rapid multidisciplinary management [24].

Post-transfusion purpura (PTP)

PTP is a rare delayed complication of transfusion, usually 5 to 10 days post-transfusion, often triggered by alloimmunization to platelet antigens (e.g., HPA-1a). Platelet destruction results from complement fixation. Diagnosis relies on clinical suspicion and serologic testing, though treatment should begin empirically if PTP is suspected, as serology may not be readily available. Standard treatment includes IVIG, plasmapheresis, and corticosteroids. Discontinuation of ICI therapy may be required in severe cases5.

Cytopenias

Neutropenia/agranulocytosis

ICI-induced neutropenia is far less common than chemotherapy-induced neutropenia (Table 4). Infection risk rises with the grade and duration of neutropenia; bacterial or fungal infection markedly increases mortality. Bone marrow biopsy may show defects in neutrophil precursors, sometimes lipid-laden macrophages; the granulocytic line may be hypoplastic, absent, or normal, and antineutrophil antibodies can be detected [23]. Treatment: Supportive management includes prompt empiric antibiotics and granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF). Other reported options include cyclosporine, IVIG, and rabbit anti-thymocyte globulin25.

Bicytopenia/pancytopenia

While most ICI-related hematologic events affect a single cell line, some patients develop bicytopenia (two lines) or pancytopenia (all three). Bicytopenia is often secondary to conditions such as TTP or DIC. Pancytopenia has been reported with PD-1 inhibitors (e.g., nivolumab) and CTLA-4 inhibitors (e.g., ipilimumab) [27].

Lymphopenia

Lymphopenia (absolute lymphocyte count < 1000 cells/mm3) produces clinically relevant immunosuppression with susceptibility to viral and bacterial infections [28]. Presentations range from mild (upper respiratory symptoms, rash, night sweats) to severe (pneumonia, skin/UTI infections, endocarditis, sepsis) [8]. A large population study associated lymphopenia with higher infection-related mortality (HR 1.70; 95% CI 1.37–2.10) [28]. Retrospective data in melanoma and NSCLC link treatment-emergent lymphopenia with shorter progression-free and overall survival; risk is higher when radiation is combined with immunotherapy [30, 31]. Delayed irAEs after ICI—including cytopenias—can appear many months after exposure, supporting extended surveillance³⁹, and are detected in pharmacovigilance datasets as late-onset safety signals [33].

Management: Intervene when lymphocytes fall below ~ 0.25 × 10⁹/L: provide Mycobacterium avium complex and Pneumocystis jirovecii prophylaxis (avoid TMP-SMX if myelosuppression is a concern), screen for cytomegalovirus, and consider EBV testing if lymphadenopathy or hepatitis is present [8]. Hemolytic uremic syndrome (HUS)

ICI-related HUS involves endothelial injury and inflammation in small blood vessels, triggering complement activation and formation of microthrombi with downstream organ damage. Clinical manifestations include decreased urine output, hematuria, bloody diarrhea, abdominal pain, pallor, fever, bruising/epistaxis, hypertension, fatigue, confusion, and generalized edema [8]. Conclusion

Timely recognition of hematologic irAEs is essential because these rare events can be fatal if missed. The most frequent hematologic irAEs include anemia, thrombocytopenia, and pancytopenia [5]. Risk appears higher with CTLA-4 inhibitors versus PD-1/PD-L1 agents and with combination ICI therapy [4, 5]. Concomitant chemotherapy and/or radiation further increases the likelihood of cytopenias, including clinically significant lymphopenia and its downstream effects [31]. Hematologic irAEs are reported more often in patients aged 59–66 years and in those with lung cancer or melanoma [5]. Symptoms span fatigue, weakness, dyspnea, abnormal bleeding/bruising, and recurrent infections. Diagnosis relies on labs, blood smear/histology, and medication history review [5]. Management depends on severity but commonly includes ICI interruption and immunosuppression (e.g., corticosteroids, rituximab), with IVIG, transfusion support, and plasmapheresis in selected situations [8], [7]; cytokines (IL-2, IL-7, IL-15) remain investigational for ICI-associated lymphopenia [29]. More recent evidence emphasizes evolving safety signals and care models, including network meta-analyses of hematologic toxicities [39], comprehensive reviews of hemostatic complications [24], characterization of chronic irAEs necessitating longer-term survivorship planning [40], and late-onset presentations that support extended surveillance [32] as well as real-world pharmacovigilance of delayed irAEs [33].
