Nanoparticle induction of antigen-presenting monocyte-derived dendritic cells relieves immunosuppression and inhibits metastasis
Authors: Jeffrey A. Ma, Kate V. Griffin, Kathryn Kang, Agustina Diener, Ian A. Schrack, Elizabeth J. Bealer, Laila M. Rad, Rebecca S. Pereles, Jacqueline S. Jeruss, Lonnie D. Shea
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYMyeloid immune cells play a major role in establishing a suitable microenvironment for metastasis by suppressing antigen-presentation pathways and effector T cell responses at metastatic sites, and reprogramming these cells may enhance anti-tumor cytotoxicity. Herein, we target myeloid cells with intravenously delivered nanoparticles to reduce the accumulation of neutrophils at the metastatic niche and induce the differentiation of monocytes into monocyte-derived dendritic cells (moDCs). Internalization of nanoparticles was linked to increased antigen presentation, T cell stimulation, and activation of Th1 cells. Nanoparticle administration increased the proportion of Th1 and Th17 CD4+ cells and did not inhibit metastasis in mice where monocytes or T cells were depleted, indicating that interactions between moDCs and T cells are essential to the mechanism of action. Our findings demonstrate that nanoparticles reprogram circulating monocytes into antigen-presenting moDCs, which are necessary to modulate the immunosuppressive microenvironment and upregulate anti-tumor helper T cell populations within the metastatic niche.

INTRODUCTION

Cancer metastasis, the colonization of tumor cells at distal organs, is the leading cause of breast cancer-associated deaths.1 While prognoses for localized tumors have greatly improved, treatments for metastatic disease remain largely ineffective.2 Metastasis is supported by the aberrant accumulation of dysregulated myeloid cells at distal sites, which establishes a tumor-permissive metastatic niche.3 Immature myeloid cells are released from bone marrow into the bloodstream to circulate and home to distal organs, where they induce a tumor-supportive and immunosuppressive microenvironment.4,5 These tumor-supportive myeloid cells, which are referred to as myeloid-derived suppressor cells, have been directly implicated in promoting tumor cell colonization and are linked to poor clinical outcomes.6 Dysregulated myeloid cells are an influential population at the metastatic niche and signal to tumor cells, stromal cells, and surveilling immune cells to create a tumor-supportive niche.

Myeloid cells play a major role in the suppression of anti-tumor immunity in the metastatic niche. Myeloid-derived suppressor cells inhibit T cell function both by directly impairing T cell cytotoxicity and by inhibiting the function of antigen-presenting cells to reduce T cell priming and activation.7–10 Inhibition of immunosuppressive myeloid cells in experimental models has been shown to enhance T cell cytotoxicity.11 However, myeloid cells are a highly heterogenous population and can also contribute to the induction of T cell responses. Monocytes can differentiate into dendritic cells (DCs) to promote the priming and infiltration of cytotoxic T cells, but the metastatic niche is dominated by immunosuppressive myeloid subsets that impair the function of monocyte-derived DCs (moDCs).12–14 Immunotherapies that reprogram myeloid cells to reduce immunosuppression while simultaneously promoting antigen presentation may more efficiently disrupt the metastatic niche.

Nanoparticles (NPs) can manipulate myeloid cell behavior with the potential to alleviate cancer-induced immune dysregulation.15 NPs are a highly tunable platform that has historically been developed for drug delivery and are frequently used to deliver antigen and adjuvant to antigen-presenting cells as a cancer vaccine.16 While NP vaccines are effective at inducing antigen-specific T cell responses, these vaccines are limited by inherent challenges with tumor-specific antigen identification and mutation. Further, T cell cytotoxicity induced by the vaccine is still limited by the immunosuppressive metastatic niche.17 Recent investigations leveraged the intrinsic, immunomodulatory properties of NPs to reprogram the phenotypes of tissue-infiltrating myeloid cells and ameliorate pathological inflammation.18,19 In particular, poly(lactide-co-glycolide) NPs (PLG NPs) have reduced myeloid inflammation in models of traumatic injury, autoimmune disease, and viral infection, without negative side effects commonly associated with immunotherapies.20–23 Rather than delivering immunological agents, drug-free NPs are hypothesized to associate with myeloid cells in circulation and alter cell phenotype and trafficking.21 While PLG NPs have primarily been used to reduce inflammation or induce immune tolerance, distinct formulations of PLG NPs have also reduced immunosuppression and enhanced adaptive immune responses in models of cancer.24–26 Surfactant selection can impact the immunomodulatory properties of PLG NPs.24,27 Here, we investigate polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) and poly(ethylene-alt-maleic anhydride) (PEMA), which impart negative charge on the NP, which can target scavenger receptors for cell internalization.21 The distinct immunological effects of NPs across multiple disease models motivate mechanistic studies into the relationship between myeloid cell responses and NP properties.

In this study, we report that PLG NPs promote anti-tumor immunity in the metastatic niche by inducing moDC maturation in a formulation-dependent manner. NPs made with PVA (NP-PVA) as a surfactant reduced immune dysregulation and inhibited metastasis in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), while NPs made with PEMA (NP-PEMA) did not influence disease progression. Although the two NP formulations were similar in size and surface charge, NP-PVA was more effective at associating with circulating myeloid cells and reducing the trafficking of neutrophils to metastatic sites. NP-PVA also preferentially associates with monocytes at metastatic sites and induces their differentiation into moDCs. Even though the NPs are not loaded with tumor antigens or adjuvant, we find that NP-PVA induces DC maturation and upregulates gene expression programs associated with antigen presentation. NP treatment enhanced Th1 and Th17 T cell responses and failed to inhibit lung metastasis with the depletion of monocytes or T cells, indicating that moDCs are necessary for T cell induction and driving NP efficacy. Collectively, our findings demonstrate that polymeric NPs can be designed to reduce immunosuppression and enhance T cell priming as an immunotherapy for metastatic disease.

RESULTS

Cargo-free NPs inhibit metastasis and modulate the metastatic niche in a formulation-dependent manner

We investigated whether immunomodulation by cargo-free PLG NPs inhibits cancer metastasis. NPs were fabricated from a single emulsion using the surfactants PVA or PEMA. Both NP formulations are approximately 500 nm in diameter, have a negative surface charge, are stable under physiologic conditions in circulation, and can be readily phagocytosed by circulating immune cells (Figures S1A, S1B, and S1C). Based on previous investigations of PLG NPs in in vitro models, we hypothesized that NP-PVA would have an immunostimulatory effect and promote anti-tumor immune responses.27 Immunocompetent BALB/c mice bearing orthotopic 4T1 tumors were intravenously administered NP-PVA, NP-PEMA, or saline (Figure 1A). Both NP-PVA and NP-PEMA significantly reduced primary tumor (PT) growth, resulting in mammary tumors that were 1.64-fold (p < 0.000001) and 1.48-fold (p = 0.000029) smaller than control mice 21 days after tumor inoculation, respectively (Figure 1B). Interestingly, only NP-PVA was able to inhibit lung metastasis. NP-PVA reduced the incidence of metastasis to 30% at 21 days post-inoculation, while untreated mice and mice treated with NP-PEMA had incidence rates of 90% and 70%, respectively (Figure 1C). Metastatic burden, as quantified by luminescence, was reduced in NP-PVA-treated mice 20-fold relative to untreated mice (p = 0.012) (Figure 1D). We thus concluded that the inhibition of lung metastasis, but not primary tumor growth, is dependent on NP formulation.

This reduction of metastasis in the 4T1 TNBC model was subsequently investigated in the metastatic EO771 breast cancer model to characterize the robustness of NP-PVA efficacy. Female C57/BL6 mice were inoculated orthotopically with EO771 and administered either NP-PVA or NP-PEMA following the same dosing schedule as the 4T1 model. Neither NP treatment significantly reduced primary tumor growth (Figure S1D). NP-PVA significantly reduced the incidence of metastasis to 40% from 80% in the control group (Figure S1E) at 21 days. Additionally, the NP-PVA treatment significantly reduced the severity of metastasis by 2.5-fold (p = 0.0081) as quantified by radiant flux of fluorescent tumor burden (Figure S1F). Neither the reduction in incidence nor radiant flux with NP-PEMA treatment was statistically significant compared with the untreated control.

Since NPs are delivered intravenously, we hypothesize that NPs associate with immune cells in circulation and indirectly modulate cell accumulation in peripheral tissues. We subsequently characterized the immune composition at the lung following NP delivery. We found that NP-PVA, but not NP-PEMA, significantly reduced the accumulation of neutrophils and shifted the immune milieu toward monocytic populations. The lungs of mice treated with NP-PVA displayed a 1.3-fold reduction in the proportion of Ly6G+ neutrophils (p = 0.002) with corresponding 1.80- and 3.19-fold increases in the proportion of Ly6C+ monocytes (p = 0.0002) and CD11c+ DCs (p = 0.0004), respectively (Figure 1E). While mice treated with NP-PEMA trended toward a decrease in the proportion of neutrophils and an increase in the proportion of DCs, neither was statistically significant. The proportion of F4/80+ macrophages did not significantly differ with either NP formulation. In the spleen, both NP formulations reduced the proportion of neutrophils (PEMA 1.45-fold and PVA 1.72-fold reductions, p < 0.0001 for both) but did not significantly influence any other myeloid cell type (Figure S1G), indicating that immune modulation varies by tissue. Collectively, that data indicate that NP-PVA uniquely inhibits lung metastasis and influences immune cell accumulation to increase the proportion of monocytic cells in the lung.

To verify that these NP-driven changes were robust across models, we examined the immune composition of the lung at D14 in EO771 tumor-bearing mice. The relative composition of immune cells in control, NP-PVA, and NP-PEMA treated lungs was examined. NP-PVA significantly increased the population of CD11c+ DCs (1.88-fold relative to control, p = 0.0028) (Figure S1H) While NP-PVA in EO771 did not significantly reduce the neutrophil population in the lung, the pathology of EO771 is less neutrophilic compared with 4T1 TNBC.28 NP-PEMA did not significantly impact any of the major populations (CD11c+ DCs, F4/80+ macrophages, Ly6C+ monocytes, or Ly6G+ neutrophils) compared with the control group (Figure S1H).

NPs alter immune cell trafficking and reduce neutrophil accumulation at the metastatic niche

We next investigated the mechanisms through which NPs influenced myeloid cell accumulation at the lung. Bone marrow-derived cells from time-matched, tumor-bearing mice were adoptively transferred at D13 into mice concurrently treated with NP-PEMA or NP-PVA and recovered at D14 to characterize the accumulation of transferred cells at the lung. NP-PVA significantly reduced cell trafficking to the lung compared with untreated mice (2.06-fold reduction) (p < 0.0001) or mice treated with NP-PEMA (1.83-fold reduction) (p < 0.0001) (Figure 2A). Treatment with NP-PVA significantly increased the trafficking of monocytes (3.04-fold increase) (p < 0.0001) and reduced the trafficking of neutrophils (1.14-fold reduction) (p < 0.0001) relative to untreated mice (Figures 2B and 2C). While most cells trafficking to the lung were neutrophils (Ly6G+), neutrophil trafficking in mice treated with NP-PVA was comparable to immune cell trafficking in tumor-free mice and reflects the high proportion of granulocytes in the donor cell population (Figure S2A). NP-PEMA did not significantly influence immune cell trafficking to the lung relative to untreated mice.

Adoptive transfer of cells without NPs into NP-treated mice was used to determine whether changes in immune cell trafficking were directly driven by the association of immune cells with NPs in circulation or indirectly driven by changes in chemotactic signaling from the lung due to NP treatment. The indirect effects of NP delivery were investigated by adoptively transferring cells into mice that were treated with NPs until the time of adoptive transfer but did not receive NPs after the injection of donor cells on D13 (Figure S2B). As a result, adoptively transferred cells are subject to changes in the lung microenvironment resulting from previous NP injections, referred to as “conditioning,” but cells do not directly interact with NPs in circulating blood. NP-PVA-conditioned mice exhibited a 1.31-fold reduction in the trafficking of immune cells to the lung relative to untreated mice (p = 0.0001), which was less than the reduction observed in mice that received NPs with adoptive transfer (Figure 2D). Furthermore, conditioning by NP-PVA did not influence the proportions of recruited Ly6C+ monocytes or Ly6G+ neutrophils (Figures 2E and 2F). We thus concluded that while indirect conditioning by NP-PVA reduces overall immune cell trafficking to the lung, direct interactions between NP-PVA and circulating immune cells influence the composition of the trafficking cells.

We next investigated the influence of NP-PVA on immune cell accumulation in the lungs. Interestingly, NP-PVA reduced the absolute number of neutrophils (2.02-fold) (p = 0.0012) but did not influence the absolute number of monocytes relative to lungs in untreated, tumor-bearing mice (Figure 2G). This result indicates that the increased proportion of monocytes with NP-PVA is not due to increased infiltration but instead reflects a reduction in the number of recruited neutrophils. To determine whether changes in myeloid cell recruitment were driven by changes in the number of circulating myeloid cells, we quantified the LyC+ and Ly6G+ cells present in blood. NP-PVA significantly reduced the absolute number of both Ly6G+ neutrophils (2.78-fold) (p = 0.0002) and Ly6C+ monocytes (1.65-fold) (p = 0.044) per volume of circulating blood (Figure 2H). Taken together, these data indicate that NP-PVA systemically reduces the number of circulating myeloid cells and the accumulation of neutrophils, but not monocytes, in the lung.

PVA NPs associate with monocytic cells and induce antigen-presenting phenotypes

The direct effect of NPs on cell trafficking suggested that NP internalization could influence myeloid cell phenotype. We investigated NP association with myeloid cells using fluorescently labeled NPs. NP-PVA associated with cells at significantly higher rates than NP-PEMA in both the lung and spleen (1.76-fold, p < 0.0001 and 7.92-fold, p < 0.0001 increases, respectively) (Figures 3A and S3A). NP-PEMA predominantly associated with neutrophils, the most common myeloid cell in the lung (Figure 3B). By contrast, NP-PVA associated with CD11c+ DCs and Ly6C+ monocytes at approximately equal rates as it was with Ly6G+ neutrophils, although monocytes and DCs compose a smaller proportion of myeloid cells in the lung relative to neutrophils (Figures 1E and 3B). The association of NP-PVA and NP-PEMA did not significantly differ in the spleen, suggesting that the preferential association of NP-PVA with monocytes and DCs is tissue-specific (Figure S3B). We hypothesized that NP-PVA was inducing the preferential accumulation of monocytes and DCs at the lung. Interestingly, while NP-PVA did not influence the absolute number of monocytes in the lung, this treatment increased the number of CD11c+ DCs 1.44-fold relative to untreated mice (p = 0.0065) (Figures 2G and 3C). This result indicates that NP-PVA preferentially associate with monocytes and DCs at the lung, yet that this change is not driven by increased monocyte accumulation.

CD11c+ cells in the lung were further examined to identify specific macrophage and DC subtypes that associate with NPs.29 NP-PVA primarily associated with monocyte-derived populations such as interstitial macrophages (IMs) (CD11b+MHCII+CD11c+CD64+CD24−) and CD11b+ DCs (CD11b+MHCII+CD11c+CD24−CD64−) in the lung (Figure 3D). CD11b+ DCs may be either moDCs or immature conventional type 2 DC (cDC2) phenotypes. Examining the overall myeloid composition of the lung, NP-PVA increased the relative populations of CD11b+ DCs and IMs compared with control (2.42, p < 0.0001 and 2.71-fold, p = 0.0017 increases, respectively) (Figure 3E). Other CD11c−expressing cells, such as conventional type 1 DCs (cDC1) (CD11b−CD11c+CD103+CD24+CD64−), Ly6C+ monocytes/undifferentiated macrophages (MΦ) (CD11b+MHCII−CD64+/−Ly6Clo), and resident alveolar macrophage populations (SiglecF+CD11b−CD11c+CD64+), collectively made up less than 3% of NP+ cells, and the abundance of these populations was not significantly affected by NP treatment (Figure S3C). Collectively, these data indicate that the NP+ cells expressing CD11c are not resident alveolar macrophages or cDC1. Rather, the CD11c+ populations induced by NP-PVA are monocyte-derived populations of moDCs/cDC2 (CD11b+ DCs) and IMs.

The origin of NP-induced CD11b+ DC accumulation in the lung was investigated. Since NPs did not increase the number of circulating CD11c+ DCs (p = 0.0006), we hypothesized that the increased CD11c expression in the lung is driven by monocyte differentiation to moDCs (Figure S3D). Approximately 42.2% of CD11c+ DCs in the lungs of NP-PVA-treated mice expressed Ly6C compared with 27.5% of DCs in untreated mice (p < 0.0001) (Figure 3F). Furthermore, treatment with NP-PVA increased the absolute count of CD11c+Ly6C+ DCs in the lung 2.69-fold without affecting the number of Ly6C+ monocytes (p < 0.0001) (Figures 2G and 3G). Similarly, in the EO771 model, NP-PVA increased both the proportion (p < 0.0001) and absolute population (p = 0.0013) of Ly6C+CD11c+ moDCs (1.34- and 3.89-fold, respectively) (Figure S3E). Collectively, these data suggest that NP-PVA are inducing the differentiation of monocytes into moDCs.

The direct influence of NP treatment on monocyte differentiation was investigated by adoptive transfer of bone-marrow-derived monocytes that were or were not treated with NP-PVA in vitro. Recipient mice received both treated and untreated monocytes from the same pool of donor cells (Figure S3F). Adoptive transfer recipient mice had an increased overall population (2.22-fold increase) of CD11c+Ly6C+ cells compared with those that did not receive adoptive transfer (p = 0.021) (Figure S3G). Within individual recipients, adoptively transferred monocytes that were treated with NPs expressed higher levels of CD11c+ compared with monocytes that were not treated (p < 0.0001), indicating that NP uptake directly drives CD11c+ expression (Figure 3H). Further, NP+ CD11c+ cells expressed higher levels of MHCII (p = 0.0001), CD80 (p < 0.0001), and CD86 (p = 0.042) (1.44-, 21.5-, and 1.87-fold increases), indicating that mature, antigen-presenting phenotypes are associated with NPs in vivo (Figure 3I). These data suggest that NP-PVA associates with monocytes to induce differentiation into functional moDCs.

NP association upregulates anti-tumor inflammatory programs in monocytic populations

NP-induced transcriptomic changes within the lung were investigated through single-cell RNA sequencing on cells of tumor-bearing NP-PVA-treated and untreated mice. Treated lungs were sorted into stromal (CD45−), NP+ (CD45+Cy5.5+), and NP− (CD45+ Cy5.5−) groups prior to sequencing (Figure S4A). A total of 57,630 high-quality cells were sequenced from the four groups (untreated, treated: NP+, NP−, and stromal), with 10,000 to 20,000 cells per group (Figure 4A). NP+ immune cells were primarily identified as myeloid cells (Figures 4B and S4B). Approximately half of NP+ cells were identified as neutrophils, which was similar to the proportion of neutrophils in untreated lungs or among NP− cells. However, NP+ cells were enriched for monocyte-derived populations compared with both NP− cells and untreated lung cells. Monocyte-derived populations were predominantly composed of monocytes, recruited macrophages, and moDCs.

NP+, NP−, and stromal cells were reconstructed into the treated group for comparison with the untreated tumor-bearing group. CellChat was used to map the signaling pathways from each major cell group in the lung.30 Investigating the major inflammation-related pathways present in the network, NP-treated moDCs increased transforming growth factor β (TGF-β), CCL (Chemokine Ligand), interleukin (IL)-1, IL-12, and tumor necrosis factor (TNF) signaling, suggesting moDCs in the treated condition are more inflammatory and send multiple Th1 and Th17 induction signals (Figure 4C).31,32 moDCs also received more TGF-β and IL-6 signaling in the NP-treated condition, while central memory T cells switched from IL-2 to an IL-12 program, which can induce T cells from central to effector memory subsets (Figure S4C).33,34 Further examination of signaling from moDCs to T cell subsets showed increased VEGF (vascular endothelial growth factor), MIF (macrophage migration inhibitory factor), IL-12, IL-1, GALECTIN (β-galactoside-binding lectins), and TGF-β pathways upregulated in NP that were not present in the untreated condition (Figure 4D).

Mapping the specific receptor-ligand pairs upregulated in treated relative to untreated present in moDC to T cell signaling showed most upregulated receptor-ligand pairs are involved in Th1 and Th17 T cell phenotype induction (Figure 4E). All T cell subsets upregulate IL-27, Tnfrsf, and Ighm signaling, which are involved in driving Th1 differentiation, T cell accumulation, and T cell function.35,36 Several of the signals have been shown to stimulate Th1 activation, induction, and recruitment, such as IL-27RA, IL-16, Ccr2, Ccr5, IL18-R1, and Cxcr6.35,37,38 Computing the upregulated differentially expressed genes in untreated and treated conditions for the Th1 cluster and using those genes as an input for Gene Ontology (GO) gene set enrichment showed an increase in immune receptor activity and major histocompatibility complex (MHC) binding in Th1 cells from the treated condition (Figure S4D). Untreated Th1 cells did not have any enriched GO terms. Similar examination of the moDCs showed increased pattern recognition receptor (PRR) activity and immune cell receptor binding in the NP+ moDCs (Figure 4F). IL-16-Cd4 signaling was downregulated across all T cell subsets, suggesting the recruitment of all CD4+ T cell subsets via IL-16 is reduced with NP treatment (Figure S4E).32,39 Collectively, these analyses suggest that NP treatment induces a more inflammatory and active moDC population, which secretes Th1 and Th17 inducing signals IL-12, IL-1, IL-16, TNF, and TGF-β.32 This signaling to T cells induces Th1 phenotypes and increased markers of cytotoxicity that are associated with increased anti-tumor immunity programs.

NP-induced moDCs stimulate Th1 and Th17 responses to inhibit metastasis

CD11c+ lung DCs have been reported to induce Th17 anti-cancer phenotypes when immature and retaining Ly6C+ expression.40 The influence of NP-PVA treatment on T cell phenotypes was investigated by looking at T cell subtypes in the lung following NP treatment. CD4+ T cells in NP-treated mice trended toward higher expression of T-bet and expressed significantly higher levels of RORγt (p = 0.001) (1.37- and 6.71-fold increases), which are associated with Th1 and Th17 phenotypes (Figure 5A). NP-PVA did not increase the proportion of CD8a+ T cells or influence the expression of granzyme B, suggesting that NPs do not directly stimulate anti-tumor cytotoxicity (Figure S5A). However, T cells were essential for NP efficacy, as NPs did not inhibit tumor growth or lung metastasis with the depletion of both CD4+ and CD8a+ (p < 0.0001) or CD8a+ T cells alone (p = 0.0006) (Figures 5B, S5B, and S5C). Depletion of CD4+ T cells alone and with NPs was effective at inhibiting PT growth and reducing metastases (Figures 5B, S5B, and S5C), which may result from depleting immunosuppressive CD4+ T cells, such as regulatory T cells, in addition to anti-tumor subsets.41 We next investigated the role of moDCs in upregulating T cell populations by depleting monocytes 24 h prior to each NP injection. The administration of anti-Ly6C reduced the accumulation of DCs (p = 0.022) and moDCs (p = 0.0014) without significantly influencing the number of neutrophils in the lung (Figures 5C and S5D). Monocyte depletion inhibited the induction of Th1 (p = 0.042) and Th17 (p = 0.0073) phenotypes in CD4+ T cells, suggesting that the upregulation of anti-tumor T cell phenotypes is associated with increased stimulation by monocytic cells rather than the reduced accumulation of neutrophils (Figure 5D). Importantly, NPs failed to inhibit lung metastasis in monocyte-depleted mice, demonstrating that monocytes and moDCs are critical in the NP mechanism of action (Figures 5E and S5E). Collectively, these data indicate that NP-PVA induces moDCs that stimulate CD4+ T cell responses to inhibit lung metastasis.

DISCUSSION

The immunosuppressive metastatic niche is a major barrier to the elimination of metastatic tumor cells. While tumor-specific T cells and natural killer (NK) cells are present in cancer-dysregulated systems, the infiltration and cytotoxicity of effector immune cells to metastatic sites are limited by microenvironmental cells such as stromal and myeloid cells.42,43 Immunotherapies that seek to stimulate or supplement T cell responses, such as immune checkpoint inhibitors or adoptive T cell therapies, are subject to the same microenvironmental barriers, which may limit their efficacy.44 Alternative strategies that instead seek to disrupt the metastatic niche and alter myeloid cell phenotypes may enhance intrinsic anti-tumor cytotoxicity and the efficacy of T cell immunotherapies.45 In this report, we demonstrate that cargo-free PLG NPs reduce the accumulation of neutrophils and induce the differentiation of monocytes into antigen-presenting DCs within the metastatic niche, which are necessary to promote anti-tumor T cell responses and inhibit metastasis.

We report that the intravenous delivery of cargo-free PLG NPs disrupts the recruitment of myeloid cells to the lung and inhibits tumor cell colonization in triple-negative breast cancer and demonstrate the robustness of this mechanism in another metastatic breast cancer model. Cancer metastasis is linked to the increasing accumulation of dysregulated myeloid cells at metastatic sites, and depleting these cells has been found to inhibit tumor cell colonization.46 Treatment with NP-PVA significantly reduced the trafficking of Ly6G+ neutrophils to the lung and increased proportions of CD11c+ DCs and Ly6C+ monocytes. Notably, NP treatment reduced the absolute number of monocytes in circulation, suggesting that NPs induce preferential homing of monocytes to the lung while reducing the overall number of myeloid cells in circulation. Changes in the composition of cells recruited from circulation to the lung were only observed with the direct injection of NPs, suggesting that NPs alter myeloid cell trafficking through a direct association-dependent mechanism, rather than by conditioning chemokine signals in the metastatic niche. The negative charge can facilitate interactions with scavenger receptors, leading to internalization and limiting circulation time, though the phenotypic shifts induced by the particles may impact responses on longer time scales.23

PLG NPs have previously been found to interact with inflammatory monocytes through a macrophage receptor with a collagenous structure (MARCO)-dependent mechanism.21 MARCO, a class-A scavenger receptor protein, is hypothesized to bind to plasma proteins that adsorb to the surface of the NP. However, the expression of MARCO and other scavenger receptors is limited to monocytic cells or B cells, suggesting that NP-PVA interacts with neutrophils through a different mechanism.47 Differences in surfactant may impact the protein corona that adsorbs to the NPs and result in differences in immune uptake and activation.48 We found that NP-PEMA, which has a modestly more negative surface charge than NP-PVA due to the difference in surfactant, associated with monocytes at significantly lower rates. Together, these differences highlight the impact of surfactant selection on the bioactivity of NPs and their mechanism of interaction with immune cells.

NP interactions with immune cells are also influenced by polymer composition. PLG NPs made with a higher molecular weight polymer tended to associate with neutrophils, while NPs made with a lower molecular weight polymer tended to associate with monocytes.19 Interestingly, PLG NPs with a higher molecular weight had a less negative surface charge and were more effective at reducing neutrophil recruitment to tissues, reinforcing the idea that slight differences in surface charge may influence NP association with cells and modulate trafficking to tissues.19 NP-PVA has been reported to accumulate in the primary tumor, spleen, liver, and lungs and is associated with minimal adverse events.24 Collectively, the data herein demonstrate that the NP design can redirect specific myeloid cell populations in a tissue-specific manner.

NP-PVA associated with Ly6C+ monocytes and induced differentiation into antigen-presenting moDCs. Treatment with NP-PVA increased the accumulation of moDCs at the lung, and in vitro treatment with NPs prior to adoptive transfer promoted monocyte differentiation in vivo. moDCs are induced in response to inflammatory states, such as infections or cancer, and have a distinct phenotype from conventional DCs, such as cDC1, but can be hard to distinguish from immature cDC2 phenotypes.31,49,50 In the context of cancer, moDCs complement conventional DCs by supporting cytotoxic responses and directly mediating anti-tumor cytotoxicity.12 moDCs and cDC2s, in particular, when immature and expressing CD11b, have been shown to stimulate Th17 anti-cancer phenotypes.40 In our studies, NP-induced DCs expressed higher levels of costimulatory and antigen-presentation markers, indicative of an inflammatory, T cell-stimulatory phenotype. Furthermore, sequencing of NP+ myeloid cells indicated the upregulation of gene expression programs related to T cell stimulation and anti-tumor immunity programs.51 The upregulation of these transcriptomic programs in DCs has been shown to robustly correlate with the increased expression of antigen-presentation molecules and inflammatory cytokines promoting T cell cytotoxicity.52,53 Modeling the signaling in the metastatic niche suggested moDCs highly upregulated the induction of Th1 and Th17 subsets compared with control. Additionally, there were significant differences in the gene programs of NP+ and NP− -treated moDCs, suggesting that moDC phenotypes are directly driven by NP treatment. Collectively, these findings suggest the effects of NPs are both directly inducing changes in myeloid cells and indirectly promoting inflammatory T cell subsets, which may improve anti-tumor cytotoxicity.

While PLG NP-PVA was employed in the present studies to induce mature DC phenotypes, PLG NPs with a range of surfactants have previously been found to induce tolerogenic phenotypes and reduce inflammatory responses, primarily in models of autoimmune disease.18 In models of rheumatoid arthritis and autoimmune diabetes, PLG NPs have reduced the expression of costimulatory molecules and induced regulatory T cell phenotypes, resulting in reduced autoimmune T cell cytotoxicity and a reduction in pathological inflammation.19,22,54 PLG NPs reduced interferon (IFN) expression and induced regulatory T cell phenotypes in models of multiple sclerosis or graft transplant.55,56 Conversely, other studies have found that cargo-free PLG NPs activated the STING (stimulator of interferon genes) pathway and induced IFN signaling to stimulate T and NK cell responses in models of melanoma and breast cancer.25,57 The induction of immunogenic or tolerogenic responses by PLG NPs may vary by inflammatory state and NP formulation.15,18,19 In particular, the surfactant used to create NPs can alter its bioactivity and properties, including what cells the NPs associate with and what tissues it can target.58–61 Both PEMA and PVA were tested, as they impart negative charge to PLG NPs, which can target scavenger receptors for cell internalization.21,27 PLG NPs made with PEMA suppress inflammation and reduce cytokine secretion, while NPs made with PVA induce inflammatory phenotypes in macrophages.27 This suggests the differing bioactivity of NP-PEMA and NP-PVA is likely due to the combination of different surfactants and surface charges altering how each NP interacts with the immune system.

The induction of moDCs by NPs was linked to the upregulation of Th1 and Th17 T cell responses and the T cell-dependent inhibition of metastasis. moDCs play critical roles in T cell priming and anti-tumor cytotoxicity, but the generation of moDCs is impaired in a cancer-dysregulated system.12,62 We found that treatment with NP-PVA induced Th1 and Th17 phenotypes in CD4+ T cells, which are linked to anti-tumor responses.63 The induction of anti-tumor CD4+ phenotypes and inhibition of metastasis was driven by moDCs, as depletion via anti-Ly6C abrogated Th1 and Th17 phenotypes and NP efficacy. These findings are consistent with previous reports that moDCs support conventional DCs by secreting Th1- and Th17-inducing cytokines.64 Surprisingly, while Th1 and Th17 CD4+ T cells are reported to promote the expansion and cytotoxicity of CD8+ T cells, we did not find significant changes in the proportion of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells or the expression of granzyme B.65–67 The lack of CD8+ T cell stimulation in our studies may be due to differences in DC biology between mice and humans, as cross-presentation is reported to be restricted to the cDC1 phenotype in mice.49,68,69 The anti-tumor effects of NPs may be driven directly by Th1 and Th17 CD4+ T cells, which are reported to possess some cytotoxic capacity through the secretion of cytokines such as TNF-α or IFNγ.70,71 Alternatively, anti-tumor cytotoxicity could also be driven directly by monocyte-derived cells. A dectin-1 and CD40 immunotherapy was similarly reported to stimulate anti-tumor cytotoxicity in macrophages through T cell-derived IFNγ.53 NP-PVA may also synergize effectively with existing T cell-targeting immunotherapies by alleviating immunosuppression in the metastatic niche and potentially increasing anti-tumor T cell signals. Collectively, our studies demonstrate that NP-PVA stimulates anti-tumor T cell phenotypes by inducing mature moDCs and motivate further investigation into the mechanisms by which the NPs promote anti-tumor responses.

In conclusion, we report that PLG NP-PVA reprograms the metastatic niche and induces moDCs to inhibit metastasis in a T cell-dependent manner. NPs made with PVA as a surfactant were more effective at associating with myeloid cells and inducing the differentiation of monocytes into moDCs than NPs made with PEMA. NP-PVA-induced moDCs expressed costimulatory markers and gene programs associated with antigen presentation in order to stimulate Th1 and Th17 phenotypes in CD4+ T cells. The reduction of metastasis by NPs is controlled by NP formulation, which motivates further investigation into the mechanisms by which NPs influence myeloid cell phenotype. Our work indicates that NPs can be tuned to modulate myeloid cells to achieve desired therapeutic outcomes in cancer metastasis.

METHODS

Ethics statement

All animal work was performed under IACUC PRO00011621 and PRO00009777 approved by the University of Michigan Institutional Animal Care & Use Committee.

Animal studies and tumor models

4T1-tdTomato-luc2 purchased from Perkin-Elmer were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Avantor) at 37°C and 5% CO2. 4T1 cells were authenticated by short tandem repeat profiling (ATCC). 4T1 cells were enzymatically detached using 0.25% trypsin-ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) (Gibco) and resuspended in Dulbecco’s phosphate buffer saline (DPBS, Gibco) at a density of 2e6 cells/mL for inoculation. 8-week-old, female BALB/c mice (Jackson strain #000651) were orthotopically inoculated with 50 μL of cell suspension in their fourth right mammary fat pad. EO771-luc2 was donated by the Wicha lab at the University of Michigan and cultured in DMEM (Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS and 20 mM 4-(2-hydroxyethyl)-1-piperazineethanesulfonic acid (HEPES) (Gibco) at 37°C and 5% CO2. EO771 cells were enzymatically detached with 0.25% trypsin-EDTA and resuspended DPBS at a density of 20e6 cells/mL for inoculation. EO771 cells were mixed 1:1 with Matrigel (Corning) (25 μL each), then inoculated orthotopically into 8-week-old female C57/BL6 (Jackson strain #000664) following the same method as the 4T1 model.

Mice were intravenously injected with 1 mg of NPs or saline every 3 days starting 1 day after inoculation for the duration of the study. Mice were euthanized at 14 days after inoculation for immune analysis or at 21 days after inoculation for quantification of lung metastases. Primary tumor sizes were measured using calipers following the formula V = 0.5 × L × W2, where L denotes the length of the longest dimension of the tumor and W denotes the width perpendicular to the longest dimension. For depletion studies, 100 μg of anti-CD4 (clone GK1.5), anti-CD8α (clone 53–6.7), or anti-Ly6C (clone Monts1) purchased from Bio X Cell were intraperitoneally injected at a concentration of 1 mg/mL. Anti-CD4 and anti-CD8α were administered on days 7 and 14 after inoculation, while anti-Ly6C was administered 1 day prior to each NP injection. All animal work was performed following protocols approved by the University of Michigan Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee under protocols #00009715 or #00011457.

Tissue processing and cell isolation

Lungs, blood, and spleens were extracted from tumor-bearing mice 14 days after inoculation and processed for flow cytometry. Lungs were mechanically minced with scalpels and enzymatically digested with TM liberase (Roche) and 150 U/mL DNase I (Sigma) for 30 min at 37°C. Spleens and digested lungs were then passed through 70 μm cell strainers (Corning) and washed with DPBS supplemented with 0.5% bovine serum albumin and 2 mM EDTA (MACS buffer). Lungs, spleens, and blood were then processed with ammonium-chloride-potassium (ACK) lysing buffer (Gibco) for 5 min at room temperature to lyse erythrocytes and quenched with magnetic-activated cell sorting (MACS) buffer. The resulting single-cell suspensions were resuspended at a concentration of 1e6 cells/100 μL for flow cytometry.

Adoptive transfer

Bone marrow was isolated from tumor-free or time-matched (D13) tumor-bearing mice and collected in culture media. Cells were passed through a 70 μm cell strainer and centrifuged at 500 × g for 5 min at 4°C. Subsequently, cells were cultured in ACK buffer for 2 min to lyse erythrocytes. Cells were washed with MACS buffer and resuspended in DPBS and stained with CellTrace Far Red or CellTrace carboxyfluorescein succinimidyl ester (CFSE) (Thermofisher) for 5 min and washed with culture media. For studies in which cells were treated with NPs in vitro, cells were incubated with 100 μg/mL NPs in culture media for 1 h at 37°C and 5% CO2 and washed with DPBS. Cells were resuspended in DPBS at a concentration of 50e6 cells/mL, and a volume of 100 μL (5e6 cells) was intravenously administered to mice on D13. Tissues were harvested approximately 16 h later on D14.

NP fabrication and preparation

NPs were fabricated from an oil-water emulsion as previously described.19,27 Acid-terminated PLG with an inherent viscosity of 0.55~0.75 dl/g (Evonik) was dissolved in dichloromethane (Sigma) at a concentration of 200 mg/mL. The dissolved polymer was then added to 2% w/v PVA (Sigma) or 2% PEMA (Polysciences) in MilliQ water at a 1:5 ratio and sonicated at 100% amplitude for 30 s with a Cole-Parmer Ultrasonic processor (Model XPS130). The resulting emulsion was then added to a 200 mL bath of 0.5% w/v PVA or 0.5% w/v PEMA in MilliQ water, and the dichloromethane was allowed to evaporate overnight. NPs were centrifuged at 5,000 × g for 15 min at 4°C and washed with 0.1 M sodium bicarbonate (Polysciences) or MilliQ water for a total of 5 washes to remove remaining dichloromethane. NPs were then passed through a 40 μm strainer and mixed with a cryoprotectant solution of 4% w/v sucrose and 3% w/v mannitol (Sigma). The solution was then frozen at − 80°C and lyophilized for 3 days prior to use. To resuspend NPs for injection, lyophilized particles were washed with 1 mL of distilled water to remove cryoprotectant, then resuspended in DPBS at a concentration of 10 mg/mL and passed through a 40 μm strainer. NPs were characterized with a Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZSP to analyze their size, zeta potential, and polydispersity index.

For studies using fluorescently labeled NPs, PLG was conjugated to Cyanine-5.5 via 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl) carbodiimide hydrochloride (EDC)- N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS) chemistry. Acid-terminated PLG was incubated with EDC (Thermofisher) at a 1:5 molar ratio for 5 min, followed by the addition of the same molar ratio of NHS (Thermofisher) for 10 min. The polymer was then incubated with Cyanine-5.5 (Lumiprobe) overnight. The conjugated polymer was dialyzed for 4 days in distilled water with SnakeSkin Dialysis Tubing (Thermofisher). The polymer solution was frozen at −80°C and lyophilized. For NP manufacture, Cy5.5-conjugated PLG was mixed with unconjugated PLG at a 1:20 w/w ratio.

Scanning electron microscopy

NP morphology and topography were assessed via scanning electron microscopy. NPs were washed twice with 1 mL distilled water to remove cryoprotectant, then resuspended in 1 mL of ethanol, dried, and coated in gold prior to imaging. Images were taken at 10,000X, 20 kV, and scan speed 6.

Colloidal stability

Colloidal stability of NP-PVA and NP-PEMA was assessed. Blood was collected via cardiac puncture with 50 mM EDTA and centrifuged at 2,000 g × 5 min at 4°C to collect plasma supernatant. To create tissue homogenates, the spleen was homogenized at 10,000 rpm (IKA, Wilmington, NC) in 800 μL PBS and then centrifuged at 9,000 g × 10 min at 4°C.72,73 Supernatant was collected and stored at −20°C. Tissues were then diluted 1:10 (v/v) with deionized water and centrifuged at 1,100 g × 10 min, and the supernatant was recovered to acquire transparent solutions. Homogenates were incubated with 200 μg/mL of NP-PVA and NP-PEMA and incubated at 37°C on a shaker for 48 h to mimic biological conditions. NP size was assessed every 24 h on a Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZSP.

Flow cytometry

Single-cell suspensions at a concentration of 1e6 cells/100 μL in DPBS were stained with Zombie Violet or Zombie Red Fixable Viability Kit (Biolegend) for 15 min at room temperature. Cells were washed with MACS buffer and incubated with anti-CD16/32 (Biolegend) for 15 min at 4°C to prevent nonspecific binding. Cells were then stained with the following antibodies for 30 min at 4°C: BV510-CD11b (clone M1/70), BV711-Ly6G (clone 1A8), fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-Ly6C (clone HK-1.4), PE-CD11c (clone N418), PE/Cy7-F4/80 (clone BM8), AF700-CD45 (clone 30-F11), BV421-CD45 (clone 17A2), BV510-CD11c (clone N418), BV711-CD80 (16–10A1), FITC-I-A/I-E (clone M5/114.15.2), PE-CD86 (clone GL-1), BV510-CD4 (clone RM4–5), BV711-SiglecF (clone S17007L), PE/Cy5-CD64 (clone X54–5/7.1), Pacific Blue-MHCII (clone M5/114.15.2), AF594-CD45 (clone 30-F11), APC/Cy7-CD11c (clone N418), AF594-CD8a (clone 53–6.7), and AF700-CD3 (clone 17A2). For intracellular staining, samples were permeabilized with the Biolegend True-Nuclear Transcription Factor Buffer set following manufacturer instructions. Cells were stained with AF488-RORγt (Thermofisher, clone B2D) and PE-Tbet (clone 4B10) for 30 min. All antibodies are from Biolegend unless specified otherwise. Samples were then washed and resuspended in MACS buffer and passed through 40 μm cell strainers. Prior to analysis, 50 μL of CountBright Plus Absolute Counting Beads were added to each sample. Samples were then analyzed on the Bio-Rad ZE5 Cell Analyzer, and the resulting data were analyzed using FlowJo (BD).

Single-cell RNA sequencing

Lung samples were prepared for single-cell RNA sequencing by generating a single-cell suspension as described above. Cells were then prepared for flow-assisted cell sorting by staining with AF488-CD45 (Biolegend) and sorted into CD45+NP+, CD45+NP−, and CD45− populations on the Bigfoot Cell Sorter (Invitrogen). Sorted samples were fixed with the 10× Chromium Next GEM Single Cell Fixed RNA Sample Preparation Kit following manufacturer instructions. Fixed samples were submitted to the University of Michigan Advanced Genomics Core for library preparation with the 10× Single Cell Fixed RNA Hybridization & Library Kit and Single Cell Fixed RNA Mouse Transcriptome Probe Kit following manufacturer guidelines for the 10× Single Cell Gene Expression Flex platform. Samples were sequenced on the NovaSeq X 10B flow cells (300 cycle) at a depth of approximately 25,000 reads per cell.

Filtered and unfiltered objects for each sample output from CellRanger (v8.0.0) were pre-processed on a per-sample basis with SoupX to remove background mRNA contamination, ddqcR for quality control, and DoubletFinder to remove doublets.74–77 Data were then merged, normalized with SCTransform, and integrated following the standard Seurat v5 pipeline.78,79 The full pipeline, including code, for preprocessing and analysis is available on GitHub. The R packages used for analysis of transcriptomic data are listed in Table 1.

Dimensional reduction via principal-component analysis (PCA) was applied to integrated lung samples with 30 dimensions and clustered using uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) with 30 dimensions and a resolution of 0.6. Clusters were identified by canonical marker genes and top conserved differential expression between clusters, provided in Table S1. Once identified, clusters were further assessed to analyze differential gene expression between treatments within the same cell type. CellChat was used to model receptor-ligand signaling pathways between treated (combined NP-treated groups) and untreated control groups.30 Differentially expressed genes were calculated with Seurat’s FindMarkers function with a log-fold-change threshold of 0.5 and an adjusted p value cutoff of 0.05. ClusterProfiler was used to calculate GO term enrichment between groups.82

Bioluminescence imaging

Metastases were quantified using an in vivo imaging system (IVIS) Lumina LTE imaging system (Caliper Life Sciences). Lungs were incubated in serum-free RPMI 1640 supplemented with 630 μM d-luciferin (Perkin-Elmer) for 8 min and imaged using automatic exposure settings. Images were analyzed and quantified using the Living Image software (Caliper Life Sciences).

Statistics

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 software. Single comparisons between two conditions were analyzed with unpaired Student’s t tests, and multiple comparisons were made using one- or two-way ANOVA for one or two independent variables, respectively. Comparisons made from the same sample (NP+ and NP− conditions in Figure 3I) were analyzed using a paired t-test. Transcriptomic analyses were analyzed using R as described above. p values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant, with stars indicating level of significance (**** p ≤ 0.0001, *** p ≤ 0.001, ** p ≤ 0.01, * p ≤ 0.05). All error bars indicate mean ± standard deviation.
