Enhancing Extraction and Suppressing Cooling of Hot Electrons in Lead Halide Perovskites by Dipolar Surface Passivation
Authors: Zhaobo Zhou, Yang Wu, Junjie He, Thomas Frauenheim, Oleg V. Prezhdo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Slowing hot carrier (HC) cooling and improving HC extraction are considered two pivotal factors for enhancing power conversion efficiency in emerging HC photovoltaic applications of perovskites and other materials. Employing ab initio quantum dynamics simulations, we demonstrate the simultaneous slow cooling and efficient extraction of hot electrons at the C60/CsPbI3 interface through dipolar surface passivation with phenethylammonium and 4-fluorophenethylammonium ligands. The passivation effectively suppresses I–Pb lattice vibrations, weakens the hot electron–phonon interaction in CsPbI3, and thus slows down the HC cooling. At the same time, the dipolar surface passivation elevates the LUMO + 1 state in C60 and reduces the energy gap for HC extraction. Concurrently, higher-frequency vibrations of the dipolar layer enhance the coupling between C60 and CsPbI3, promoting efficient HC extraction further. These phenomena are intensified with increased polarity of the dipolar layer. Furthermore, we find that dipolar passivation has the opposite influence on cold electron collection at the band edge, underscoring the fact that the observed improvement in photovoltaic performance stems preferentially from the effective utilization of HCs rather than cold electrons. The work provides a new strategy for achieving high-performance HC perovskite solar cells.

Introduction

Hot carriers (HCs) refer to charge carriers (electrons and holes) that acquire excess kinetic energy from external excitation, with hot electrons (HE) having energies above the conduction band minimum (CBM) and hot holes having energies below the valence band maximum (VBM). Efficiently exploiting HCs to improve power conversion efficiency (PCE) has become the focus of lead halide perovskite (LHP) solar cells, benefiting from the excess energy carried by HCs. In principle, the PCE of solar cells can break the Shockley-Queisser thermodynamic limit of around 33% and even approach 66% under 1 sun illumination, if this excess energy can be fully utilized before HCs cool down to the band edges.1−5 To achieve this goal, two primary issues should be addressed: slow HC cooling and efficient HC extraction. Slowing HC cooling in LHPs can minimize the loss of energy as heat into the inorganic lattice.6,7 More importantly, efficient HC extraction boosts concentration of mobile carriers, validating their potential for enhancing device efficiency and realizing viable HC solar cells.6,8−11 Establishing well-designed strategies for either slowing HC cooling, or fast and efficient HC extraction is thus crucial for novel LHP HC solar cells.

Slowing HC cooling in LHPs has shown promising progress over the past decade. A series of impressive works in exploring size-,12 component-,13−16 excitation intensity-,17,18 and defect-dependent7,19 slowing HCs cooling of various organic or inorganic LHPs have been achieved. Besides, several well-known origins and mechanisms under different HC densities have been proposed, including polaron formation,14,20,21 hot phonon bottleneck,22−24 Auger heating,25,26 band-filling effects27 and Rashba splitting.28 Despite numerous experimental and theoretical advances in slowing HC cooling, realizing practical HC photovoltaic devices remains challenging. The primary obstacle lies in efficiently extracting HCs from LHPs to a charge transport layer (CTL) while HCs are still hot. The extraction process requires rapid charge transfer through the LHP/CTL interface to minimize energy loss.29 Given this, assessing how properties of the LHPs/CTL interface influence the HC extraction dynamics is imperative.

Photoactive LHP layers are typically synthesized through rapid crystallization at a relatively low temperature, which inevitably results in high-density of defects at LHP surface due to their low formation energy.30−33 The defects serve as undesirable nonradiative recombination centers, leading to notable energy loss and imposing severe limitations on both the PCE and stability of the device during operation.34−37 Effective defect passivation strategies are thus essential for achieving highly efficient and stable perovskite solar cells. Surface passivation of LHPs using organic ligands, e.g. organohalides,38−41 Lewis bases42,43 and dipolar compounds,44,45 has been widely explored experimentally. The approach eliminates various defect states and enhances the stability of LHPs, thereby boosting their optoelectronic performance. For instance, using diammonium ligands, in which one ammonium ion (−NH3+) group anchors to the surface of LHPs and the other side extends away from it, induces a surface dipole and n-type doping in C60/LHPs inverted perovskite solar cells, yielding a certified PCE of 25.1% with stable operation (>2000) at 65 °C in ambient air.46 However, improvements in the PCE are typically achieved from effective harnessing of band-edge cold carriers and mitigation of electron–hole recombination. In contrast, exploration of surface passivation-dependent HC extraction and cooling dynamics is still rare. That is because most focus is placed on surface defect-induced trap states that appear in the forbidden band gap zone rather than the conduction/valence band (CB/VB) zone, and such band gap states have little influence on the ultrafast HC-related dynamics processes. Nonetheless, surface passivation can modulate the electronic and vibrational properties of the LHP surface, thereby affecting HC-phonon scattering, polaron formation and phonon–phonon interactions.47−49 Therefore, exploring the impact of surface passivation on HC extraction and cooling dynamics in LHP/CTL systems, and elucidating the underlying mechanisms, is critical for advancing further the practical applications of HC photovoltaic devices.

In this work, focusing on the commonly used C60/CsPbI3 system, we systematically investigate the dynamics of HE cooling and extraction without (control) and with phenethylammonium (PEA) and 4-Fluorophenethylammonium (F-PEA) dipolar surface passivation, using time-dependent density functional theory (TDDFT) calculations and ab initio nonadiabatic molecular dynamics (NAMD) simulations. Our study highlights dipole-dependent HE cooling and extraction dynamics processes. As the polarity of the ligand increases, the HE cooling can be effectively slowed down by a factor of 2.5–4, compared to the control system. This is ascribed to the diminished frequency and amplitude of I–Pb lattice vibrations following the dipolar passivation, leading to weakened electron–phonon (e-ph) interaction and corresponding reduction of the nonadiabatic coupling (NAC). Importantly, utilizing dipolar passivation both reduces the energy gap for HE extraction and introduces high-frequency vibrations of the passivation layer participating in the HE extraction, both factors enhancing the HE extraction rate. We infer that the observed dipole-dependent improvement in the photovoltaic performance of the C60/LHPs system preferentially arises from the utilization of HEs prior to their relaxation to the band edge.

Results and Discussion

To elucidate the dipole control on HE relaxation and extraction in C60/CsPbI3, an initial investigation into the geometric and electronic structures of C60/CsPbI3 passivated by the dipolar ligands is essential. We employ the well-accepted passivator PEA, which contains a -NH3+ group on one side. To increase the ligand dipole moment, we incorporate a fluorine atom on the opposite side, forming F-PEA. Both ligands tend to adsorb at surface Cs cation (Cs+) vacancy defects and form a passivation layer,50,51 making the C60/dipole layer/CsPbI3 sandwich structure (Figure 1a). Thus, we construct the pristine C60/CsPbI3 system featuring nondipolar Cs+, and the systems passivated with dipolar PEA and F-PEA, which occupy surface Cs+ vacancy sites. They are named as control, PEA and F-PEA systems, respectively. (More details are shown in Figure S1 and Table S1 of the Supporting Information). To estimate the stability of the dipolar passivation layer, the binding energy (Eb) of the passivator is calculated using the formula: Eb = Esur – Esub – Emol, where Esur, Esub, and Emol are the total energies of the perovskite surface after and before passivation, and that of the isolated passivator, respectively. Figure 1b illustrates that both dipolar passivators exhibit stronger binding to the CsPbI3 perovskite compared to Cs+. Comparing the two organic ligands, the stronger binding of F-PEA can be attributed to its higher polarity, potentially augmenting the binding between C60 and the passivated perovskite layer.38 The above results suggest that an enhanced surface passivation can be achieved with dipolar molecular layers, surpassing the effects of pristine Cs+. The thermal stability of the control, PEA, and F-PEA systems is also evaluated by analyzing the Fourier transform and the standard deviation of energy evolution from ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) simulations, as shown in Figure S2. The energy evolution decreases from ∼ −5.5 eV in the control system to ∼ −5.7 eV in the PEA and F-PEA systems. Additionally, dipolar passivation reduces the peak value in the Fourier transform spectral density and decreases the standard deviation of the energy curves. These further illustrate that dipole passivation can suppress surface structural vibrations, leading to enhanced structural stability. This capability demonstrates significant potential for improving the quality of LHP films.

To establish how the dipolar ligands influence the electronic structure of the interface, we present densities of states for the three systems, Figure 1c. We observe that the second lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO + 1) state of C60 closely aligns with the CBM of the control system. However, introducing dipolar PEA and F-PEA at the surface notably elevates the molecular orbitals of C60. In this case, HEs possessing equivalent excess energy (ΔE) in the dipolar passivated systems can be extracted more easily by C60 compared to the control system, due to the reduced donor–acceptor energy gap. Meanwhile, the LUMO of C60 is closer to the CsPbI3 CBM in the dipolar passivated system as well. These findings suggest that the dipolar layer passivation should enhance the extraction of HEs to C60, motivating a systematic exploration of the HE dynamics.

After the photoexcitation, HEs with excess energy ΔE relax to the conduction band edge and release energy into the surrounding medium, e.g. lattice. Taking the sunlight energy window (1.6–3.1 eV) into account, we study the HE relaxation dynamics with a series of ΔE (∼0.2 to ∼0.8 eV) in the control, PEA, and F-PEA systems. The evolution of HE populations for three perovskite systems is shown in Figures S3–S5. The corresponding relaxation times are derived by fitting with f(t) = exp(−0.5(−t/τ)2) and are shown in Figure 2b. Figure 2c depicts HE relaxation in the three systems starting with different excess energies: ① 0.24 eV, ② 0.33 eV, ③ 0.50 eV, ④ 0.61 eV, ⑤ 0.70 eV, and ⑥ 0.77 eV. We observe a gradual decrease in the HE relaxation time of the systems with the increase of ΔE, illustrating the dependence of HE relaxation on the excess energy. Similar observations have been reported in the previous works.7,16,52,53 Here, the HE relaxation is considerably faster for ΔE > ∼0.6 eV compared to ΔE < ∼0.6 eV. Importantly, passivation by both PEA and F-PEA results in a robust suppression of the HE cooling. In particular, the F-PEA passivation slows down the HE cooling substantially, by a factor of 2.5–4 compared to the control system. These results provide important insights into the novel dipole-dependent strategy to decrease the HE cooling.

In addition to the slow HE cooling, efficient HE extraction plays a key role in improving the PCE. Rapid HE extraction allows utilization of HEs before they cool. Thus, we analyze the HE extraction process starting at the six different HE excess energies in the control, PEA and F-PEA systems, as illustrated in Figure 2d. We observe that the HE extraction by C60 occurs at ΔE > 0.24 eV in the control system, increasing to ΔE > 0.33 eV and ΔE > 0.50 eV in the PEA and F-PEA systems, respectively. The difference stems from the alignment of the LHP and C60 LUMO + 1 energies: the favorable alignment occurs already in region ① for the control system, but in region ② for the PEA system and region ③ for the F-PEA system. Notably, HEs exhibit dipole-dependent extraction. Particularly for region ③, C60 extracts 15 and 40% of HEs in the PEA and F-PEA systems, respectively, within 800 fs, which is 3 times and 8 times more than that in the control system (5%), respectively. These results allow us to provide a clear physical picture of dipole-dependent HE cooling and extraction dynamics at the C60/LHP interface, as illustrated in Figure 2a. For pristine C60/LHP, the photoexcited HE undergoes either fast cooling or slow extraction to C60, which will reduce the HE yields and lead to poor PCE. However, inserting PEA ligands into the C60/LHP interface to form a dipolar passivation layer elevates the acceptor state, LUMO + 1 of C60, slows the HE cooling, and enhances the HE extraction to C60. Increasing the polarity of the molecular passivation layer further (F-PEA) amplifies these effects, thereby significantly improving the PCE of perovskite hot carrier solar cells.

In general, hot carrier dynamics, whether cooling or extraction, are governed by a combination of factors. The NAC, dij = ⟨φi|∇RH|φj⟩·Ṙ/(εi – εj), can be used to qualitatively describe the major factors contributing to the dynamics. According to this expression, the NAC is proportional to the matrix element representing changes in the electronic Hamiltonian due to atomic motion, the atomic velocity, and the inverse of the energy gap between initial and final states. Table 1 lists the calculated average absolute NAC of HE cooling and extraction, corresponding to different ΔE in the control, PEA and F-PEA systems. The reduced NAC in the PEA and F-PEA systems lead to the slower HE cooling. For example, at ΔE = 0.24 eV, the HE cooling time in the F-PEA system is significantly slower than that in the control system, because the NAC is reduced from 2.56 to 0.12 meV. Conversely, an increased NAC facilitates accelerated HE extraction (Figure 2d). In this case, the enhanced NAC can be rationalized by narrowing of the energy gap between the donor state in CsPbI3 and the acceptor state in C60 after the dipole passivation, due to the elevation of the LUMO + 1 level of C60, Figure 2a. Concerning the HE cooling, dipole passivation-induced changes in the CsPbI3 conduction band are insignificant, Figure S6, for instance, in comparison to the changes arising from iodine interstitial defects.7 Hence, further exploration of the factors influencing the NAC and the HE dynamics is warranted. In particular, the NAC is proportional to the atomic velocity Ṙ, which depends on lattice temperature and frequencies of atomic motions that couple to the electronic subsystem. Since the temperature is the same in all simulations (300 K), we focus on the frequencies of active vibrations.

To characterize the interaction of HEs with lattice vibrations, the evolution of electronic levels and corresponding Fourier transforms of the initial HE states at 0.24, 0.33, and 0.5 eV and the C60 acceptor state in the control, PEA and F-PEA systems are depicted in Figure 3. The Fourier transforms are known as influence spectra, or spectral densities. Compared to the control system, the vibrational peaks display a noticeable redshift in the PEA and F-PEA systems (Figure 3d–f). Especially for the peaks below 100 cm–1 attributed to the I–Pb stretching and bending motions,54,55 the dipole passivation effectively changes the I–Pb octahedron vibration mode, reducing the e-ph coupling and thereby slowing the HE cooling. Conversely, for the HE extraction process, the activation of vibrational peaks with higher frequencies at 130–170 cm–1 in the PEA and F-PEA systems is observed (Figure 3h,i). These peaks can be attributed to the organic cation librations and torsion,54,55 Participation of the higher-frequency vibrations rationalizes the increased NAC observed for the HE extraction in the PEA and F-PEA systems, demonstrating involvement of the PEA and F-PEA dipolar ligands in the HE extraction process. Visualization of the corresponding vibrational vectors is given in Figure S7. In the PEA and F-PEA systems, the modes originating from the thermal fluctuations of I–Pb lattice exhibit smaller vibration vectors compared to those in the control system. Meanwhile, the strong vibration of dipolar surface passivators in the PEA and F-PEA systems notably contributes to the HE extraction from CsPbI3 to C60. These further underscore the positive impact of the dipolar passivation on reducing the HE cooling and accelerating the HE extraction.

So far, we have established that dipole passivation effectively slows the HE cooling and enhances the HE extraction in C60/LHP. Previous work reported that the electron extraction in the PEA and F-PEA systems is more efficient than that in the control system due to the narrower energy gap between the CBM of LHP and the LUMO of C60.38 This dynamic process involves electron transfer from the CBM of LHP to the LUMO of C60 after complete hot electron cooling (Figure 4a). We model the cold electron extraction by NAMD, and the NAMD results demonstrate the opposite trend, Figure 4. The population of the cold electron in the LUMO of C60 in the control, PEA and F-PEA systems reaches 0.43, 0.35, and 0.24, respectively, within 1600 fs (Figure 4b). Thus, the fastest electron transfer (1.46 ps) occurs in the control system, while the slowest electron transfer (2.08 ps) is in the F-PEA system (Figure 4c). We attribute the above phenomena to the strong overlap between the LUMO + 1 of C60 and the CBM of CsPbI3 in the control system during the dynamics process, as shown in Figure 3a–c. Consequently, the LUMO + 1 of C60 is capable of extracting the electron from the CBM of CsPbI3 at some point. However, following the dipolar passivation, the upshift of the LUMO + 1 of C60 and the reduction in the donor–acceptor orbital overlap are not conducive to the cold electron extraction. Additionally, the built-in electric field (Ein) at the interface can influence electron transfer. To evaluate its impact, we calculate the electrostatic potential for the control, PEA and F-PEA systems, as shown in Figure S8. It shows that dipolar passivation significantly reduces the electrostatic potential difference, from 1.55 eV in the control system to 1.02 eV in the PEA system and 0.39 eV in the F-PEA system, resulting in a weaker Ein. The weakened Ein is also less favorable for cold electron extraction after dipolar passivation. These findings collectively indicate that electron extraction from dipole-passivated perovskites to C60 is impeded when the electron cools to the CBM. Therefore, we infer that the enhancement of the electron transfer primarily arises from HE extraction following dipole passivation.

Conclusions

In summary, using state-of-the-art TDDFT and NAMD simulations, we have demonstrated that dipolar surface passivation of LHPs efficiently slows down HE cooling and enhances HE extraction. Focusing on the C60/CsPbI3 systems with and without the dipolar passivation, our systematic investigation has provided key mechanistic insights into the HE relaxation and transfer dynamics. Both processes exhibit strong polarity dependence. As the molecular polarity of the dipolar passivation layer increases, the HE relaxation in CsPbI3 becomes slower and the HE extraction by C60 becomes faster, compared to the control system without the passivation. We attribute the slowed HE cooling to suppression of the amplitude and frequency of I–Pb octahedral thermal vibrations by the passivating molecules, thereby weakening the e-ph interaction and reducing the NAC. Concerning the HE extraction, on one hand, the dipolar passivation raises the energy of the LUMO + 1 level of C60, thereby reducing the energy gap for the HE extraction. On the other hand, fast, high-frequency vibrations of the ligand layer actively participate in the extraction process, accelerating the extraction. Furthermore, our study reveals the negative impact of the dipolar passivation on cold electron transfer from the CBM of CsPbI3 to the LUMO of C60, further underscoring that the HE extraction dominates over the cool electron extraction and rationalizing the experimental data. Our simulations suggest a new strategy to simultaneously slow down HC cooling and improve HC extraction in LHPs, providing means to design novel HC photovoltaic devices.

Computational Details

All the ground state calculations were carried out with the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP).56,57 The Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) functional within the generalized gradient approximation was employed for exchange-correlation interactions.58 Electron–ion interaction was described using the projector-augmented wave method.59,60 An energy cutoff of 500 eV and a 7 × 7 × 1 Monkhorst–Pack k-mesh grid were adopted for geometry optimization and electronic structure calculations. Full relaxation of lattice constants and atomic positions was carried out until atomic forces were below 0.02 eV Å–1, with an electron relaxation convergence criterion of 10–5 eV. The van der Waals interaction was treated using the Grimme DFT-D3 method.61 To prevent the interaction between two neighboring periodic units, a vacuum region of 20 Å along the out-of-plane direction was used.

The photoexcited hot electron cooling and extraction dynamics were stimulated based on nonadiabatic molecular dynamics (NAMD) implemented with the Hefei-NAMD code.62 Such a computational approach is based on the combination of the fewest-switches surface hopping (FSSH) approach and Kohn–Sham (KS) single-particle description within the so-called classical path approximation (CPA). All the AIMD and NAMD calculations were performed at the Γ point. Following geometry optimization with VASP, the relaxed structures were heated to 300 K during 2 ps in the canonical ensemble (NVT) via repeated velocity rescaling. Subsequently, 3 ps AIMD trajectories were generated in the microcanonical ensemble (NVE) with a time step of 1 fs. The average absolute NAC value is obtained by averaging the NAC element obtained under the entire trajectory. 200 initial configurations were selected randomly in the first 1 ps of the AIMD trajectories, and 20,000 NAMD trajectories were sampled for each chosen initial structure. In this case, HE cooling and extraction dynamic process simulations were carried out over 2 ps. More theory and method details are given in the Supporting Information.
