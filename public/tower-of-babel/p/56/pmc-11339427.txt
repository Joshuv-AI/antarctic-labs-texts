A novel infrared spectroscopy marker for assessing the postoperative infection risk in patients with upper urinary tract calculus
Authors: Jiexiang Lin, Zesong Yang, Yun Hong, Wanghai Cai, Honghong Pan, Jie Lin, Liefu Ye
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The aim of this study is to evaluate the ability of infrared wavenumber of calculus to predict postoperative infection in patients with upper urinary tract calculus (UUTC), and to establish a predictive model based on this. From March 2018 to March 2023, 480 UUTC patients from Fujian Provincial Hospital were included in this study. The infrared-wavenumbers related infection score (IR-infection score) was constructed by univariate analysis, multicollinearity screening, and Lasso analysis to predict postoperative infection. Continually, the Delong test was used to compare the predictive power between the IR-infection score and traditional indicators. Afterward, we performed urine metagene sequencing and stone culture to prove the correlation between calculus toxicity and IR-infection score. Finally, logistic regression was used to build a nomogram. IR-infection score composed of four independent wavenumbers could precisely predict postoperative infection (AUCvalidation cohort = 0.707) and sepsis (AUCvalidation cohort = 0.824). IR-infection score had better predictive ability than commonly used clinical indicators. Moreover, metagenomics sequencing and calculus culture confirmed the correlation between IR-infection score and calculus toxicity (all P < 0.05). The nomogram based on the IR-infection score had high predictive power (all AUCs > 0.803). Our study first developed a novel infrared spectroscopy marker and nomogram, which can help urologists better predict postoperative infection in UUTC patients.

Introduction

Upper urinary tract calculus (UUTC) is a common urologic disease worldwide with a rising incidence1. Fever, renal pain, and hematuria are the main clinical symptoms that greatly impair patients’ physical and mental health. UUTC patients are mainly treated by conservative therapy, extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy, percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL), retrograde intrarenal surgery (RIRS), laparoscopic surgery, or open surgery depending on stone size and position2. PCNL and RIRS are widely applied for stones larger than 1.0 cm3. With the tremendous development of surgical techniques, postoperative complications have significantly decreased. However, urinary tract infection still remains most common and often occurs in the first 48 h after PCNL or RIRS4. The incidence of postoperative infection ranged from 7.6 to 18.9%, with 0.3–7.7% progressing to sepsis5,6. Urinary septic shock often arises in the first 6 h after surgery and develops very quickly, with a mortality rate of 80%4,6. Hence, prevention in advance is more important than treatment. Clinical attention should be given to finding a precise and effective marker in predicting postoperative infection and taking adequate preventive measures.

Many preoperative laboratory markers could be used as influential risk factors for postoperative infection prediction, such as blood white blood cells (BWBC), procalcitonin (PCT), urine white blood cells (UWBC), urine bacterial count (UBACT), urine nitrite (UNIT), and urine culture. So are some postoperative factors, including renal pelvis urine culture, calculus culture, and calculus composition6–8. However, they are not excellent enough. For example, the high false-negative rate of urine culture impairs patients’ benefits9. Although renal pelvis urine culture and calculus culture have more accurate rates, the values of early prediction are minimal10. Calculus mineral composition analysis is recommended by numerous guidelines to aid UUTC patient management, but their role in predicting postoperative infections remains understudied11–14. Therefore, it is necessary to discover a novel and effective marker that can timely and accurately predict postoperative infections in UUTC patients to provide clinical treatment guidance.

Infrared spectroscopy is an extensively utilized method for structural analysis and chemical characterization. Chemists can identify a sample’s functional parts and chemical constituents simply by analyzing the peaks of transmittance15. Calculus is a mixture of minerals and organic matter. In clinical practice, infrared spectroscopy is the gold standard for analyzing the mineral composition of calculus16. It is worth noting that the transmittance of organic composition, which clinicians always overlook, is also recorded. Although organic matter accounts for only approximately 5% of the stones, it contains bacteria and their metabolites, which are proven to be strongly associated with postoperative infection17–19. Identifying what organic components by transmittance peaks are is very difficult. However, we could change our focus to the correlation between the transmittance of organic composition and postoperative infection. It is our first attempt to construct and validate a novel marker based on the transmittance of calculus infrared wavenumbers to predict postoperative infection in UUTC patients.

Results

Clinical data and calculus composition

The workflow is shown in Fig. S1. 480 patients were eventually included and analyzed, of which 328 patients from Fujian Provincial Hospital South Branch were used as the construction cohort for the infrared-wavenumbers related infection score (IR-infection score) and nomogram, and 152 patients from Fujian Provincial Hospital were used as the validation cohort. In the construction cohort, the medium age was 54 (44,62) years. 60.37% were men. The most common stone type was calcium oxalate monohydrate (COM) (58.54%), followed by carbonate apatite (CaPa) (23.17%) and calcium oxalate dihydrate (COD) (16.16%). The incidence rates of postoperative infection and sepsis were 9.45% and 3.05%, respectively. In the validation cohort, the median age was 51 (42,59) years. 63.82% were men. The most common type was the same as the construction cohort: COM stone (66.45%), CaPa (17.11%), and COD (13.16%). The incidence rates of postoperative infection and sepsis were 9.87% and 3.29%. Details are described in Table S1 and Fig. S2.

Infection-related infrared spectrum features extraction and IR-infection score construction

In the construction cohort, 3736 transmittance values of infrared wavenumbers were measured by Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR). By univariate analysis, 1306 wavenumbers were identified to be related to postoperative infection in UCTC patients (P < 0.05) (Figure S3A,B). By multicollinearity screening, 1290 infection-related wavenumbers were excluded (intraclass correlation coefficient ≥ 0.9). Then, the remaining 16 relatively independent infection-related wavenumbers were anchored (intraclass correlation coefficient < 0.9) (Fig. 1A). During the process of Lasso analysis, we utilized ten-fold cross-validation to select the penalty term, lambda (λ). Four infrared wavenumbers were chosen to build the IR-infection score system when λ equaled 0.015653, which made the minimum of the model error. Those infrared wavenumbers were WN_1261.22, WN_1320.03, WN_1479.13, and WN_1966.07 (Fig. 1B,C and Table 1). We calculated each patient's IR-infection score based on the formula as follows: IR-infection score = − 2.23639 + (− 1.05822) × (WN_1261.22 transmittance) + (2.46380) × (WN_1320.03 transmittance) + (− 1.17410) × (WN_1479.13 transmittance) + (0.98789) × (WN_1966.07 transmittance). As for the validation cohort, 694 wavenumbers were identified to be related to postoperative infection (Figure S3A,B). Assessment of IR-infection score for postoperative infection

In the construction cohort, the IR-infection score had a favorable performance in predicting postoperative infection of UUTC patients (AUC = 0.708). Then, calibration curves showed the reliability and stability of our IR-infection score (Hosmer–lemeshow test: P = 0.756). Moreover, the decision curves showed the superiority of the IR-infection score based on the net benefit and threshold probabilities (6.3%-45.4%) (Fig. 2A–C). Our IR-infection score also functioned well in the validation cohort (AUC = 0.707). The correlation between IR-infection score and stone toxicity

The bacteria relative abundance in patients with high IR-infection scores was higher than that of patients with low IR-infection scores (P = 0.042) (Figure S4A). However, the relative abundance of fungi and microorganisms showed no difference in the two groups (P > 0.05) (Figure S4B,C). In addition, the positive stone culture of patients with high IR-infection scores was higher than that of patients with low IR-infection score group (P = 0.027) (Table S4).

Predictive model construction

In the construction cohort, we first targeted 5 risk factors by univariable analysis (all P < 0.001) to build the nomogram, including gender, PCT, UWBC, urine culture and IR-infection score. Each factor's coefficient is shown in Fig. 3A. We calculated each patient’s risk score based on the formula below: risk score = − 1.04317 + (− 0.60909) × (gender) + (1.12194) × (PCT) + (0.92052) × (UWBC) + (0.95064) × (urine culture) + (0.87541) × (IR-infection score). The nomogram well exhibited its prediction power (Fig. 3B). The AUC of the nomogram was 0.803 in the construction cohort and 0.873 in the validation cohort. Then, calibration curves showed our nomogram was reliable (Hosmer–lemeshow test: Pconstruction cohort = 0.385, Pvalidation cohort = 0.920). Also, as shown in Fig. 3C,D, the decision curves showed the superiority of the nomogram based on the net benefit and threshold probabilities (4.0–80.6% in the construction cohort and 2.2–30.4% in the validation cohort).Figure 3Assessment of nomogram’s predictive power. Assessment of IR-infection score and nomogram for postoperative sepsis

In the construction cohort, univariate analysis showed that sepsis was related to IR-infection score, gender, BMI, calculus volume, surgical procedure, operation time, infection stone 1, WBC, UWBC, UPRO, UBACT, UNIT, and urine culture (all P < 0.05). Subsequently, multivariate analysis anchored gender and IR-infection score as independent risk factors in the construction cohort (P < 0.05). Details are shown in Table S6. Compared with 12 traditional indicators, the IR-infection score had more outstanding predictive performance in the two-combined cohort. The same trends were also found in construction and validation cohorts (Table S7–S9). In the construction cohort, the IR-infection score and nomogram were all favorable in predicting sepsis (AUC IR-infection score = 0.690, AUC nomogram = 0.896) (Figure S5A,B). They also functioned well in the validation cohort (AUCIR-infection score = 0.824, AUC nomogram = 0.879) (Figure S5C,D).

Discussion

Unlike other sterile surgical procedures, PCNL and RIRS are performed in the bacteria environment due to the unique characteristics of the stone. The human immune cells and antibiotics can not penetrate the “stonewall,” composed of minerals and bacteria biofilms, leading to pathogenic bacteria protection. Therefore, urinary tract calculus are an excellent resort for bacteria planning and growing.20. On the other hand, the large amount of endotoxin and bacteria metabolites protected by the stones is a crucial trigger for postoperative infection and sepsis18,19,21. During PCNL and RIRS, many bacteria and endotoxins will be released in urine when the “stonewall” collapses. So, even if the preoperative urine culture indicates that the urine is clean, postoperative infection still could happen. In addition, knowledge of the mechanism of postoperative fever and sepsis has not been well established. Systemic absorption of bacteria and endotoxin-containing irrigation fluid is currently the most widely accepted theory (Figure S6). During the surgery, surgeons keep pressurizing and infusing fluid into the urinary system to maintain clear vision, resulting in higher pressure in the renal pelvis than in the blood. In this opportunity, bacteria, endotoxins, and bacteria metabolites in the irrigation flow into the blood and spread all over, triggering a system inflammatory response5,6,22. Therefore, evaluating the “toxicity” of stones preoperative is particularly important for preventing postoperative infections.

Preoperative urine culture, pelvic urine culture, and stone culture could be direct bacteriological evidence to guide the prevention of postoperative infection10. However, they have certain limitations in application. Patients with positive preoperative urine culture will receive systematic anti-infection treatment until urine culture turns negative, which could change microbiota homeostasis in the urinary system. Pelvic urine culture and stone culture reflect the bacteriological composition during surgery. But, it is time-consuming and cannot be guaranteed to obtain the results before infection symptoms appear4. Infectious minerals are well recognized as indirect evidence of the presence of bacteria17. While non-mineral parts of the stone, including bacteria, endotoxins, and other metabolites, are indeed primary triggers for postoperative infection17–19. In clinical practice, clinicians usually analyze the infectious minerals to predict postoperative infection and overlook non-minerals that contain all the vital information about the infection, including bacteria, endotoxins, and their metabolites. FTIR is widely used for analyzing the biochemical properties of biological samples23. However, the organic components of the stones, the primary triggers of post-operative infections, are complicated and inconvenient to detect by infrared spectroscopy. It is a novel and feasible perspective that we use infrared spectral data to predict post-operative infection without knowing the authentic materials.

Our retrospective study demonstrated the successful attempt to stratify infrared wavenumber transmittance of toxicity calculus to predict postoperative infection and sepsis in UUTC patients. We identified a valuable and unreported IR-infection score system based on four infection-related infrared wavenumbers, including WN_1261.22, WN_1320.03, WN_1479.13, and WN_1966.07. Although the AUCvalidation cohort value of the IR-infection score was only 0.707 in predicting postoperative infection, it is higher than commonly used indicators in the literature, such as urine culture, UNIT, and UWBC24,25.

The transmittance of WN_1261.22 and WN_1479.13 exhibited a negative correlation with the IR-infection score, whereas WN_1320.03 and WN_1966.07 showed a positive correlation. Patients with lower WN_1261.22 and WN_1479.13 or with higher WN_1320.03 and WN_1966.07 were more susceptible to postoperative infections. This suggests that substances with higher absorption in WN_1261.22 and WN_1479.13 and lower absorption in WN_1320.03 and WN_1966.07 might have some associations with postoperative infections, which need further investigation. Analysis of FRIT data from previous studies26,27 revealed that WN_1320.03 might be related to calcium oxalate. However, WN_1261.22, WN_1479.13, and WN_1966.07 showed no significant associations with specific minerals (Table S10). The same results were also confirmed in our experiment by analyzing the infrared spectra of chemically synthesized samples including calcium oxalate, uric acid, carbonate apatite, hydroxyapatite, and magnesium ammonium phosphate hexahydrate (Figure S7). Therefore, WN_1320.03 might be associated with mineral composition. Moreover, based on the result from metagenomics third-generation sequencing of urine and stone culture, we deduced that WN_1261.22, WN_1479.13, and WN_1966.07 might be correlated with bacteria and their metabolites (Figure S4 and Table S4). Hence, we speculate that the infrared wavenumbers associated with infectious organic substances might contribute to the infrared infection scoring system. In the next step, we will focus on these wavenumbers and their corresponding substances in the etiology of infection for UUTC patients.

IR-infection score is a favorable indicator that deserves to be recommended in the clinic. Besides excellent predictive power, it is time-saving and low-cost, with clinical feasibility and broad prospects for clinical application. First, the IR-infection score is calculated by infrared spectroscopic analysis which is easy to operate and time-saving (2 h), ensuring timely preventive measures for infection. It is a practical reference for ahead antibiotic application, reducing the risk of death from sepsis. Second, infrared spectral analysis is economical and can reduce patients' financial burden. Third, as we all know, infrared spectroscopy has been widely applied for postoperative mineralogical analysis of stones by numerous guidelines13,14. Hence, we presumed the IR-infection score could also be quickly popularized in large hospitals. In addition, our score system specifies and scores each individual, maintaining individual heterogeneity. The calculus toxicity assessed by infrared spectroscopy can benefit UUTC patient management. Clinicians can predict or even prevent postoperative infection by just knowing specific IR-infection scores in advance.

We also built a nomogram based on IR-infection score with valuable performance in predicting postoperative infection and sepsis. The IR-infection score and nomogram were all validated to perform well. Our IR-infection score and nomogram could provide clinical treatment guidance.

The strength of our study is to offer new insights into extending the application of the infrared spectrum in predicting postoperative infection in UUTC patients. Moreover, our study innovatively used multicollinearity screening and Lasso regression to process infrared spectral data and successfully constructed an IR-infection score system to predict postoperative infection in UUTC patients. Compared to traditional mineral analysis, our scoring system, which includes organic information about the stones, could better reflect the "toxicity" of the stone.

The major limitation of our work is listed as below. First, this study was retrospective and had selection biases. Second, the relationship between the four wavenumbers and stone composition still needs to be clarified. In the next step, we will conduct basic experiments to deeply investigate potential correlations. Third, patients with the same IR-infection score might have different organic components due to individual heterogeneity, which is worth an in-depth study. Fourthly, the clinical application of IR-infection scores might be hampered because small and medium-sized hospitals rarely have machines for infrared spectral analysis. Finally, multi-institutional and randomized studies are needed to prove the effectiveness and stability of the IR-infection score.

Conclusion

Our study first identified 653 infrared wavenumbers related to infection, including WN_1261.22, WN_1320.03, WN_1479.13, and WN_1966.07. We were also the first to use infrared wavenumbers instead of mineral composition to evaluate the tendency of postoperation infection. IR-infection score and nomogram could help clinicians predict which UUTC patients have a high risk for postoperation infections and make appropriate therapeutic strategies.

Materials and methods

Study population

We retrospectively reviewed UUTC patients who visited the Fujian Provincial Hospital South Branch and Fujian Provincial Hospital from March 2018 to March 2023. All patients received preoperative and postoperative antibiotic treatment. The inclusion criteria were: (1) Patients diagnosed with UUTC. (2) Patients underwent PCNL or RIRS. (3) Patients had done postoperative stone composition analysis by infrared spectroscopy. (4) Patients' age range from 18 to 80. We excluded (1) patients with incomplete or duplicate clinical data. (2) Patients with tumors, blood system diseases, and immune system diseases. (3) Patients with preoperative infections in other systems. (4) Patients undergoing other operations during the same period. This study was approved by the institutional review board of the Ethics Committee of Fujian Provincial Hospital (K2023-01-012, K2023-01-013) and adhered to the Helsinki Declaration.

Data collection

Patient medical records were retrospectively reviewed, including age, gender, BMI, diabetes, hypertension, urolithiasis history, hydronephrosis, surgical procedure, operation time, calculus volume and position, BWBC, PCT, urine red blood cells, UWBC, urine protein, UBACT, UNIT, urine PH, urine glucose, urine culture, and the occurrence of postoperative infection and sepsis.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy analysis

Calculus mineral composition and infrared wavenumber transmittance values were measured by FTIR (LIIR-20, Lamoride Company, Tianjin, China). The calculus were stored in sterile containers and measured within 24 h after surgery. The whole stones were crushed into fine powder and mixed evenly. The stone powder was then air-dried using a air dry oven at 25 °C. Then, we mixed stone powder (1.5 mg) with dry potassium bromide (150 mg) and ground it to the 100 microns level. Finally, we compressed the mix into a translucent sheet using a standard pressure of 8 tons. We placed the sheet in the infrared beam of the spectrometer. The spectral region investigated was 4000–400 cm−1; 32 scans were averaged with a 4 cm−1 resolution for each spectrum. Spectra were recorded and compared to a computerized library of single and mixed constituents reference spectra. Each assessment is double-checked by trained and qualified personnel to ensure the accuracy of the analysis. Infrared spectrum of common minerals in urinary calculus (chemical synthesis) were show in Figure S7.

Infection-related infrared spectrum features extracting and construction of IR-infection score

Infrared wavenumber transmittance values of UUTC patients’ calculus were extracted by FTIR. Pre-processing infrared spectral data using min–max normalization. Formula: X′ = (X − min)/(max–min). U-test was used to analyze the correlation between wavenumbers and postoperative infections. Infection-related infrared wavenumbers were screened by using multicollinearity and Lasso analysis. The multicollinearity screening was stopped when the intraclass correlation coefficients of all features were < 0.9. Features with intraclass correlation coefficients less than 0.9 were included for Lasso analysis. Ten-fold cross-validation was applied to select the penalty term λ and identify features that minimized the model's error. Finally, the targeted features filtered by Lasso analyses were used to construct the IR-infection score.

Metagenomics third generation sequencing, and calculus culture

The metagenomics third-generation sequencing was performed on the 22 patients’ morning urine before antibiotic usage to demonstrate the correlation between IR-infection score and stone toxicity. The samples were sent to the Hangzhou Shengting Medical Laboratory for bacteria relative abundance analysis. We also conducted a stone culture of 70 patients after surgery. Cleaned and ground calculus cores in a sterile environment were used for microbial cultivation as previously described28.

Criteria of infection stone

The infection stone criteria vary widely across the literature29–31. Two recognized standards are listed as follows: infection stone 1: magnesium ammonium phosphate hexahydrate, apatite carbonate, or ammonium urate, presented in the calculus. Infection stone 2: the combined proportion of magnesium ammonium phosphate hexahydrate or apatite carbonate in calculus is more than 50%.

Criteria of postoperative infection

Postoperative infection meets one of the following indices: (1) positive blood culture. (2) Body temperature above 38.5 °C without concurrent infection in other systems or unknown fevers22.

Sepsis criteria

Sepsis should be defined as life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection. Organ dysfunction can be represented by an increase in the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score of two points or more32.

Statistical analysis

SPSS (28.0 version) was used to analyze data. The independent sample t-test was performed to compare standard distribution measurement data, and a rank-sum test was used to compare non-normal distribution measurement data. The chi-square test was used to compare the enumeration data. Variables that showed significant differences (P < 0.05) were included in a multivariate logistic regression analysis. A nomogram was set up to predict infection by multivariate logistic regression. The “Excel 2021” software was used for maximum-minimum normalization of the infrared spectral data. Python (version 3.8.5) software was used for the multicollinearity screening33–35. R software (version 4.2.1) was used for the Lasso analysis36, the DeLong test37, visualization of the intraclass correlation coefficient, and the assessment of IR-infection score and nomogram (receiver operating characteristic curve, Hosmer–Lemeshow test38, calibration curve, and the decision curves39. The P value > 0.05 in the Hosmer–Lemeshow test indicated a good calibration performance. The statistical methods used in this study and the tools needed to reproduce the results are detailed in the Table S11.
