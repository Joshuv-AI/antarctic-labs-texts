Prophylactic Cranial Irradiation and Survival in Limited-Stage SCLC: A Multicenter Real-World Analysis From the Canadian SCLC Database (CASCADE)
Authors: Abdul Hadee Lone, Xiang Y Ye, Luna Jia Zhan, Haechan Yang, Victoria Smyth, Riham H Abdelaziz, Stephanie Snow, Sofia Genta, Sara Kuruvilla, Jason Agulnik, Vishal Navani, Cheryl Ho, David Dawe, Paul Wheatley-Price, Sara Moore, Geoffrey Liu, Benjamin H Lok
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionProphylactic cranial irradiation (PCI) is frequently used in limited-stage SCLC (LS-SCLC) to reduce the risk of brain failure after a response to chemoradiation. However, its benefit remains debated in the modern era, with improved imaging and surveillance techniques and evolving systemic therapy options. We conducted a nationwide real-world study using the CASCADE database to evaluate the impact of PCI on survival outcomes in patients with LS-SCLC.MethodsWe analyzed patients from CASCADE, a Canadian multi-institutional database that includes patients with SCLC from nine institutions. Eligible patients with pathologically confirmed LS-SCLC diagnosed between 2004 and 2022 were included. We compared outcomes between patients receiving PCI and those omitting this treatment. Overall survival and progression-free survival were assessed using Kaplan–Meier methods and Cox proportional hazards models. Brain metastasis incidence was evaluated in a predefined subgroup of patients with data on brain failure outcomes using competing-risk methods, and a predefined subgroup analysis was conducted in patients with baseline brain magnetic resonance imaging.ResultsWe identified 607 patients eligible for analysis, with 388 receiving PCI and 219 without. Median overall survival was significantly increased in patients treated with PCI (29.8 versus 19.0 mo; p < 0.0001). With multivariable analysis, PCI remained independently associated with improved overall survival (hazard ratio = 0.70, 95% confidence interval: 0.57–0.86) and progression-free survival (hazard ratio = 0.61, 95% confidence interval: 0.50–0.74). Patients treated with PCI had a reduced incidence of brain metastasis. Notably, this improvement in survival was observed in patients who received baseline brain magnetic resonance imaging.ConclusionsIn this large, multi-institutional, real-world cohort, PCI for the treatment of LS-SCLC was associated with improved overall survival, likely driven in part by a reduced brain metastasis risk. These findings support the continued role of PCI and highlight the need for further study in the modern imaging era.Keywords: Small cell lung cancer, Limited-stage SCLC, Prophylactic cranial irradiation, Brain metastases, Real-world outcomes

Introduction

SCLC is a highly aggressive neuroendocrine malignancy, accounting for approximately 12% of all lung cancer cases.1 SCLC has a high propensity for brain metastases (BM), occurring in approximately 40% to 60% of patients.2,3 In the limited-stage setting, the standard of care is systemic therapy combined with thoracic radiotherapy (RT), with the recent addition of adjuvant immunotherapy.4 To mitigate the risk of intracranial failure, prophylactic cranial irradiation (PCI) is often administered to patients who achieve a complete or partial response to initial therapy.

Historically, PCI has become part of the standard of care in patients with LS-SCLC responding to initial therapy. Pooled analyses in the pre–magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) era revealed a reduction in the incidence of BM and an improvement in overall survival (OS) in these patients receiving PCI.5 More recently, a randomized phase III trial by Takahashi et al. failed to demonstrate prolonged OS in patients with extensive-stage SCLC (ES-SCLC) receiving PCI compared with MRI observation, raising questions about whether the findings apply to LS-SCLC.6 Since then, numerous retrospective studies and meta-analyses have investigated the role of PCI in the context of brain MRI surveillance for LS-SCLC, with mixed results.7, 8, 9, 10

Current guidelines for LS-SCLC recommend PCI in patients who achieve a response to systemic therapy and have a good performance status.11,12 However, active MRI surveillance with salvage radiation has been proposed as an alternative in select patients with a low disease burden who have undergone definitive therapy, as found in recent National Comprehensive Cancer Network guidelines.12 Given the risk of neurocognitive impairment and the lack of definitive guidance regarding its benefit in the MRI and positron emission tomography (PET) imaging era, the role of PCI has become increasingly debated. The improved sensitivity of MRI for the early detection of asymptomatic BM may spare the need for upfront radiation, potentially delaying toxicity. Alternatively, as consolidative radiation therapy strategies improve local control and with the addition of adjuvant immunotherapy, the ability of PCI to reduce central nervous system (CNS) failure may become increasingly important.

Overall, these mixed findings and ongoing concerns continue the debate on the utility of PCI. In contrast to randomized clinical trials, real-world patients often have varied access to high-quality MRI surveillance, a higher burden of comorbidities, and greater heterogeneity in treatment delivery, all of which may influence the risk of intracranial progression. This discrepancy highlights an evidence-efficacy gap, where efficacy from randomized controlled trial conditions may differ from the broader real-world context. Therefore, we conducted a nationwide multicenter real-world study to evaluate the impact of PCI on OS and progression-free survival (PFS) in patients with LS-SCLC.

Materials and Methods

Study Population

We evaluated outcomes of patients with LS-SCLC in Canada from CASCADE, a real-world Canadian database of patients with SCLC across nine institutions. Eligible patients were 18 years or older with biopsy-confirmed LS-SCLC according to the Veterans Administration Lung Study Group staging system, generally defined as disease confined to one hemithorax without extrathoracic metastases, which could be treated adequately in a safe thoracic radiation field.13 Eligibility included patients who had a minimum of 4 cycles of chemotherapy with concurrent or sequential thoracic RT and who achieved physician-assessed complete response (CR) or partial response (PR), stable disease (SD), or clinical benefit after first-line chemoradiotherapy, defined as disease control without evidence of progression. Patients with progressive disease after chemoradiotherapy were excluded because they would not have been able to receive PCI. A few included patients had low-burden metastatic disease that could be encompassed in a single thoracic radiation field and were therefore classified as having LS-SCLC according to the Veterans Administration Lung Study Group criteria, such as those with nonregional thoracic lymph nodes or contralateral pulmonary nodules.13 In addition, patients with thoracic radiation less than 40 Gy or with incomplete information were excluded from the analysis.

This trial and the CASCADE database have central approval from the University Health Network Research Ethics Board, with each additional site receiving approval from its respective ethics board. A waiver of informed consent was granted by the institutional review board because all data were deidentified before analysis.

Clinical and Treatment Characteristics

We abstracted demographic and clinical characteristics from the CASCADE data set, including age, sex, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status, smoking history, and clinical stage at diagnosis. Evaluation and staging were conducted using the eighth edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer staging system. Data on the use of staging computed tomography (CT) and PET-CT imaging, including brain CT or MRI, were abstracted. Patients were assessed with clinical and radiographic surveillance as per institutional protocols and routine clinical practice.

First-line systemic treatment for patients, including types of chemotherapy, duration of treatment, and number of cycles, was abstracted. Thoracic RT dose and fractionation, including whether RT was delivered concurrently or sequentially, were gathered. We assessed whether patients received PCI, including the use of stereotactic radiosurgery or whole-brain RT.

Study End Points

The primary end point was OS. OS was assessed from the time of pathologic diagnosis of SCLC to death from any cause or last follow-up. Patients alive without an event at the time of last follow-up were censored on that date. Secondary end points included PFS, which was defined as the time from pathologic diagnosis of SCLC to radiographic or clinical progression, or death, or last follow-up, with disease assessments conducted as per institutional practice. We also conducted predefined subgroup analyses on patients with data on BM incidence and in patients with baseline brain MRI staging. The time to BM was calculated as the time from LS-SCLC diagnosis to the first BM, or death, or last follow-up, where death without BM was treated as a competing event and the case of being alive without BM was treated as a censoring event.

Statistical Analysis

The study population was summarized descriptively. Patient characteristics and clinical outcomes were compared between two groups, PCI and non-PCI, using chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables and Student’s t test or Wilcoxon ranked sum test, as appropriate, for continuous variables. OS and PFS were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared between the PCI and non-PCI groups using the log-rank test. The association between PCI, clinical covariates, and survival outcomes was evaluated with univariate and multivariable Cox proportional hazards models. Patient and clinical characteristics associated with the outcomes (p < 0.1) identified in the univariate analysis (UVA) or deemed clinically relevant were included in the multivariable analysis (MVA). As a sensitivity analysis, we also conducted an inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) analysis to evaluate the association of OS with PCI. The probability of treatment (propensity scores) was first estimated with logistic regression using covariates including age, sex, ECOG scores, T stage, staging imaging, thoracic radiation timing, and chemotherapy characteristics. The weight for each patient was then calculated as the inverse probability of treatment (PCI). The balance of the covariates between the PCI groups in the original and weighted cohorts was evaluated and compared descriptively using the standardized mean difference (SMD), with an absolute SMD less than 0.1 indicating adequate balance. The impact of PCI on OS was subsequently assessed using weighted Cox proportional hazard models. As an additional sensitivity analysis, we evaluated the impact of PCI on OS by treating PCI as a time-dependent treatment in the MVA to account for potential immortal-time bias. The cumulative incidence of BM for the subpopulation with BM data was also estimated using the Aalen-Johansen method and compared using Gray’s test. To determine the association of BM with PCI and factors of interest, similar methods were applied using UVA or MVA Fine-Gray’s competing risk models for the subpopulation. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC). A two-sided p value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline and Treatment Characteristics

Between January 2004 and December 2022, we identified 607 patients with LS-SCLC who received first-line systemic therapy and met the inclusion criteria (Fig. 1). Of these, 388 patients received PCI and 219 did not.

Several clinical factors were significantly associated with the use of PCI (Table 2). Generally, patients received CT staging, with most institutions using positron emission tomography (PET-CT), though the use of PET-CT varied over time. Most patients also received staging brain imaging with either MRI or CT. Patients in the PCI group were less likely to undergo PET-CT scan staging compared with the non-PCI group (67.9% versus 79.1%, p = 0.018). In patients with data on staging brain imaging, those receiving PCI were less frequently staged with brain MRI (60.9% versus 73.5%, p = 0.014); otherwise, there were no significant differences between the groups in the overall use of brain imaging modalities. Since 2009, a greater proportion of patients received PET-CT scans, whereas the proportion of patients receiving PCI declined, which could explain some of the differences found between the groups (Supplementary Fig. 1). Between 2010 and 2022, the rate of PCI utilization decreased by 4% per year, whereas the rate of PET-CT staging increased by 7% per year (p = 0.0003).

Regarding first-line treatment, most patients received between 4 and 6 cycles of cisplatin or carboplatin with etoposide, with a few patients receiving other regimens. Those who received PCI were more likely to complete six or more chemotherapy cycles, whereas the non-PCI group had a higher proportion of patients receiving four cycles. However, we found no difference in OS in patients receiving four versus more than four chemotherapy cycles. Patients generally did not receive immunotherapy because immunotherapy was not part of the standard of care in most of the study period. Patients received concurrent or sequential thoracic RT, with doses varying from 40 Gy/15 fractions to 60 Gy/30 fractions. Concurrent thoracic RT was more often delivered in the PCI group (86.2% versus 80.2%, p = 0.021).

Survival and Outcomes

Patients receiving PCI demonstrated a significantly longer median OS compared with those who did not receive PCI (29.8 mo, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 26.7–34.5 versus 19.0 mo, 95% CI: 16.6–23.5; p < 0.0001) (Fig. 2). The 1-year and 3-year OS rates for the PCI versus non-PCI groups were 87.8% versus 75.0% and 43.0% versus 33.9%, respectively. On MVA, the use of PCI remained an independent predictor of improved OS (hazard ratio [HR] = 0.70, 95% CI: 0.57–0.86; p < 0.001) (Table 3). In contrast, increasing age at diagnosis was associated with worse survival, with each additional year associated with a 2% higher risk of death (HR = 1.02, 95% CI: 1.00–1.03; p = 0.01). Differing responses to first-line chemotherapy, including CR/PR, clinical benefit, or stable disease, were not significantly associated with OS. When treated as a time-dependent covariate in a sensitivity analysis, PCI remained significantly associated with improved OS (HR = 0.77, 95% CI: 0.62–0.94; p = 0.013), consistent with the primary analysis (Supplementary Table 2).

In the IPTW analysis (Table 3), after weighting, all measured baseline covariates achieved an absolute SMD less than 0.1. In the weighted Cox proportional hazards model (Supplementary Table 3), PCI remained significantly associated with improved OS (HR = 0.70, 95% CI: 0.58–0.85; p = 0.0003), consistent with the results of the previous conventional MVA.

PCI was also associated with improved PFS, with a median PFS of 17.5 months in the PCI group compared with 10.9 months in the non-PCI group (HR = 0.60, 95% CI: 0.50–0.72; p < 0.0001) (Fig. 3). PCI was associated with increased PFS on MVA (HR = 0.61, 95% CI: 0.50–0.74; p < 0.001), whereas stage T3/T4 disease was associated with reduced PFS compared with T1/T2. There was no association between PFS and patients staged with PET-CT, use of brain staging, age, number of cycles, or timing of chemotherapy.

We conducted a subgroup analysis for 382 patients with surveillance data suitable for analysis of BM outcomes (Fig. 4). In this subset, treatment with PCI significantly reduced the cumulative incidence of BM (HR = 0.41, 95% CI: 0.28–0.60; p < 0.001). On MVA, the risk of BM for patients receiving PCI was 63% lower compared with non-PCI patients. In addition, patients who did not receive PET-CT scan staging were at a significantly increased risk for developing BM (HR = 1.58, 95% CI: 1.06–2.36; p = 0.026). An absence of PET-CT staging may lead to underdetection of extracranial disease and understaging, potentially increasing the risk of future BM. Furthermore, as the use of PET-CT staging increased in the study period (Supplementary Fig. 1), this finding may partially reflect more accurate staging and improved detection of subclinical BM in more recent patients.

We conducted another subgroup analysis of 217 patients who underwent baseline MRI brain staging to account for the potential of subclinical BM (Fig. 5). In this subgroup, PCI treatment again significantly improved median OS: 32.8 months with PCI compared with 19.4 months without PCI (p = 0.0091). After adjusting for confounders on MVA, treatment with PCI was significantly associated with improved OS compared with patients receiving brain imaging surveillance alone (HR = 0.68, 95% CI: 0.47–0.98; p = 0.037) (Supplementary Table 1). There were no significant differences in OS, PFS, or BM outcomes associated with the use of PET-CT staging in this cohort.

Discussion

Patients with SCLC are at high risk for metastasis, which confers a poor prognosis and limited treatment options. SCLC has a high propensity for BM in up to 60% of patients during the course of their disease.14 Regardless of treatment, OS in patients with BM remains only approximately 5 to 8.5 months, with the burden of disease greatly affecting quality of life.15,16 Meta-analyses of prior clinical trials have revealed that PCI is an effective strategy to decrease the risk of BM and improve OS in patients with limited-stage disease.5,17

In our multi-institutional cohort of patients with LS-SCLC, PCI was associated with a reduction in the incidence of BM and improved OS and PFS among patients who responded to chemoradiotherapy. This improvement in OS remained apparent in patients who underwent MRI brain staging, suggesting that the observed survival benefit persists despite more contemporary staging practices. This benefit is likely driven in part by the lower incidence of BM development among patients who received PCI. A reduction in the incidence of BM with PCI is well established in prior studies and was similarly demonstrated in this cohort. These findings highlight the benefit of PCI in LS-SCLC across evolving eras of staging and treatment, demonstrating its continued relevance in real-world practice across institutions.

Patients who did not receive PCI were, on average, 3 years older than those receiving PCI, with increasing age associated with inferior OS in our study. Given the higher risk of neurocognitive decline with advancing age, this difference could reflect a selection for younger patients to receive PCI to mitigate potential neurotoxicity concerns in older patients. However, given the relatively small age difference, this is unlikely to fully explain the observed survival benefit. Strategies including hippocampal-avoidance PCI and the use of neuroprotective agents may help mitigate cognitive toxicity while preserving intracranial control.18,19

Notably, patients who did not receive PCI were more likely to have received PET-CT staging than those who did. In our analysis, the absence of PET-CT staging was associated with an increased risk of developing BM. In addition, there was a trend toward worse OS in patients without PET-CT staging (HR = 1.34, 95% CI: 0.99–1.81; p = 0.062). Current guidelines recommend the use of PET-CT for initial staging in SCLC because of its superior sensitivity in detecting extrathoracic nodal disease and distant metastasis. However, PET staging was not routinely funded in Ontario before 2009, a fact reflected in our findings as an increase in the proportion of patients receiving PET-CT after this year.20 Though it is possible that some of these patients received alternative nuclear medicine imaging, such as bone scans, these data were not available.

A recent meta-analysis of FDG PET-CT staging found that the use of PET-CT may alter the binary SCLC stage in up to 15% of patients (95% CI: 9%–21%).21 In addition, a comparison of the HAST and THORA trials, both of which used CT and MRI brain staging, with PET-CT staging added in THORA, revealed a reduction in acute toxicity with PET-CT staging, although no OS difference was noted between the two trials.22 In our cohort, the use of PET-CT scans increased over the time frame of the study, coinciding with a decline in PCI use. With the omission of PCI becoming more common, accurate initial staging becomes increasingly critical. The absence of PET-CT staging may increase the likelihood of misclassifying patients with occult metastatic disease as having limited-stage disease and subsequently increasing the risk of developing BM. Our findings support the routine use of PET-CT staging in patients with LS-SCLC, which may be of additional importance in patients who are not candidates for PCI or who choose to omit PCI.

These findings contrast with the randomized trial by Takahashi et al. in ES-SCLC, which demonstrated no survival benefit from PCI compared with patients receiving regular MRI surveillance.6 However, patients with LS-SCLC generally experience improved intrathoracic disease control and longer survival after chemoradiation, potentially increasing the clinical importance of durable CNS control. The ongoing MAVERICK trial is evaluating MRI surveillance versus PCI in both LS-SCLC and ES-SCLC; although the trial protocol allows for concurrent or adjuvant immunotherapy, it does not mandate this treatment. Recent results from the ADRIATIC trial in LS-SCLC, including CASPIAN in ES-SCLC, highlight the role of immunotherapy as part of a new standard of care.4,23 However, the role of PCI given the changing treatment landscape remains uncertain. In an updated analysis of the ADRIATIC trial presented at ELCC 2025, patients treated with adjuvant durvalumab had a reduced incidence of CNS metastasis at first progression compared with patients receiving placebo (6.8% versus 12.4%).24 The CNS was the most common site of extrathoracic metastasis in all patients, regardless of immunotherapy use. Notably, there was a reduction in CNS metastases in patients who received PCI, with or without adjuvant immunotherapy.

With improved control of thoracic and extracranial disease through chemoradiation and adjuvant immunotherapy, the potential value of PCI in reducing CNS relapse may become increasingly important. However, as patients experience longer survival with immunotherapy and emerging treatment options, such as bispecific T-cell engager treatment, the potential neurocognitive toxicity and quality-of-life impacts from PCI require careful consideration. Further investigation of the role of PCI, through the MAVERICK, PRIMALung, and other trials incorporating modern MRI surveillance and immunotherapy, is essential to determine the role of PCI in the current SCLC treatment landscape.

This multi-institutional analysis provides one of the largest real-world evaluations of PCI in patients with LS-SCLC. Our findings reaffirm the association between PCI and improved survival outcomes, supporting its ongoing role in disease control, even as staging and systemic therapies evolve. However, several limitations must be acknowledged given the retrospective nature of this study introducing inherent risks of bias, particularly selection bias. These include nonrandomized treatment allocation and unmeasured confounders. The lack of standardized imaging schedules across institutions may have influenced detection rates of BM and progression events. Albeit, this reflects real-world practicalities and patient adherence that are different from the rigors of clinical trials and patients who are both eligible and choose to participate in studies. Selection bias is a confounder, as patients chosen to receive PCI were younger, and although we did not detect a difference in ECOG scores or chemoradiation response, they may have been fitter or had fewer comorbidities compared with those who did not receive PCI. In addition, institutional variations in treatment protocols, imaging practices, and eligibility criteria for PCI could contribute to heterogeneity in outcomes. Although we conducted MVA to adjust for several potential confounding factors, unmeasured confounders likely remain and may have influenced our results. This analysis also did not include data on long-term neurocognitive outcomes from PCI, which is one of the primary deterrents limiting PCI utilization. Finally, most patients in this cohort were treated before the widespread adoption of immunotherapy for LS-SCLC. Despite these limitations, the study’s large sample size, national multi-institutional representation, and extended time frame strengthen the generalizability of the results and provide valuable insight into the evolving role of PCI in LS-SCLC.

This nationwide real-world analysis supports the continued use of PCI in patients with LS-SCLC who achieve disease control after chemoradiation. Although modern MRI surveillance is increasingly used, our findings suggest that PCI may continue to provide a meaningful survival benefit, including among patients who undergo baseline MRI staging. These findings suggest that PCI and active surveillance strategies may be complementary rather than mutually exclusive. Future prospective studies incorporating MRI surveillance, immunotherapy, and cognitive-sparing strategies are essential to determine the optimal role of PCI in the modern management of LS-SCLC.
