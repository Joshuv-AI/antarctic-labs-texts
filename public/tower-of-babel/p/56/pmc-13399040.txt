Heparan sulfate and glycomimetics: Advances in synthesis and biological applications for post‐stroke neurorepair
Authors: Mozammel H Bhuiyan, Madeleine J Smith, Simon F R Hinkley, Andrew N Clarkson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHeparan sulfate, a structurally diverse glycosaminoglycan that is abundant in the central nervous system (CNS), orchestrates essential processes fundamental to neural plasticity, neurorepair, and neuroprotection. The ability of heparan sulfate to promote regeneration, in stark contrast to the inhibitory effects of chondroitin sulfate and related glycosaminoglycans, has sparked a growing interest in harnessing heparan sulfate and synthetic glycomimetics for post‐stroke neurorepair, as well as numerous other neurodegenerative diseases. However, significant gaps persist in our understanding of how sulfation patterns of heparan sulfate can govern functional outcomes, and major barriers remain for clinical translation. Here, we critically review advances in the synthesis and application of heparan sulfate‐based glycomimetics, delineate the mechanistic duality of glycosaminoglycans in CNS diseases, and highlight how new preclinical and emerging clinical data are reshaping prospects for bioengineered extracellular matrix therapies. We identify unresolved challenges in delivery, specificity and efficacy, and propose future research directions to bridge these translational divides.Keywords: chondroitin sulfate proteoglycans, extracellular matrix, glycoasaminoglycans, heparan sulfate proteoglycans, inflammation, intercellular signalling peptides and proteins, neurodegenerative diseases

INTRODUCTION

Stroke remains one of the leading causes of death and long‐term disability worldwide, 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 with limited therapeutic options beyond thrombolysis and mechanical thrombectomy. 5 Despite rapid advances in acute reperfusion strategies, most stroke survivors are left with persistent neurological deficits, highlighting the urgent need for therapies that enhance neuroprotection when administered early (within hours), or promote plasticity and regeneration when administered at a delay (days to weeks) after stroke. 6

A defining feature of stroke pathology is the extensive remodeling of the extracellular matrix (ECM) in the central nervous (CNS). The ECM is a structurally and functionally dynamic network of glycoproteins, proteoglycans, and polysaccharides that surrounds neurons and glial cells. 7 Far from being an inert scaffold, the ECM regulates intracellular signaling, neurotransmission and ion homeostasis, while also providing structural stability and compartmentalization. 8 , 9 Importantly, the ECM modulates neuronal plasticity and repair following injury, which makes the ECM an important regulator of both CNS health and disease. Alterations in ECM composition, whether through ageing, trauma, or neurodegenerative disease, profoundly influence neuronal survival and regeneration. 7 , 10 , 11

Among ECM components, glycosaminoglycans (GAGs) are particularly influential because their structural heterogeneity enables precise regulation of receptor–ligand interactions, chemokine gradients and growth factor activity. GAGs are important regulators for CNS development, guiding axon pathfinding, dendritic branching, and synapse formation. In the mature CNS, they maintain extracellular ionic balance, regulate neurotransmission, and consolidate plasticity via perineuronal nets. Importantly, GAGs also orchestrate CNS responses to injury, where changes in sulfation motifs and proteoglycan expression can either promote repair or establish inhibitory environments that restrict regeneration.

Heparan sulfate is a prominent GAG, and a key component of the glycocalyx and extracellular space. Heparan sulfate regulates diverse biological processes, including cell proliferation, differentiation, migration, and intercellular communication. 12 Structurally, heparan sulfate consists of repeating uronic acid‐(1,4)‐d‐glucosamine disaccharides, variably N‐sulfated, O‐sulfated, or N‐acetylated. This structural heterogeneity enables heparan sulfate to bind numerous protein partners with high specificity, regulating signaling pathways ranging from fibroblast growth factor to Wnt signaling. 13 , 14 Heparin, a structural analogue of heparan sulfate, also consists of repeating units of the same monomers; however, heparin has a higher degree of sulfation than heparan sulfate. 15 While heparan sulfate is ubiquitously found on the cell surface of most cells, heparin is mainly expressed in mast cells present in lungs, liver, and skin; therefore, it is not considered an ECM GAG constituent.

The capacity of heparan sulfate to promote regeneration contrasts sharply with the inhibitory effects of other GAGs, such as chondroitin sulfate. This dichotomy has generated interest in glycomimetics, which are synthetic molecules that mimic heparan sulfate binding motifs to promote repair, overcome inhibitory signals, and modulate neuroinflammation. Advances in glycomimetic synthesis now enable structural control at the level of sulfation motifs, chain length, and stability, making glycomimetics promising treatment candidates for CNS injury and other diseases.

Understanding the role of GAGs in stroke and other neurological conditions requires integrating two dimensions: their structural diversity, which encodes complex molecular information and their functional duality, whereby one GAG molecule can be protective, and another GAG molecule can be detrimental. For example, heparan sulfate stabilizes growth factor signaling and supports recovery, whereas chondroitin sulfate proteoglycans (CSPGs) accumulate in the glial scar, inhibiting regeneration. 16 This review examines the diverse roles of GAGs in the CNS with a particular focus on stroke and post‐stroke recovery, while also drawing parallels to neurodegenerative disorders such as Alzheimer's disease and Parkinson's disease, where ECM dysregulation contributes to pathology development. 17 , 18 We conclude by exploring heparan sulfate glycomimetics as a promising class of therapeutics for stroke repair and neurodegeneration.

GLYCOSAMINOGLYCANS: STRUCTURE AND BIOSYNTHESIS

GAGs are linear, negatively charged polysaccharides composed of repeating disaccharide units. GAGs can be classified into four major families: chondroitin sulfate/dermatan sulfate, heparin/heparan sulfate, keratan sulfate, and hyaluronan 19 , 20 (Table 1 and Figure 1). These GAGs share a common architecture of alternating uronic acid and amino sugars, but they differ in their repeating units, modifications, and modes of attachment to proteoglycans. This structural variation confers a wide functional repertoire, enabling GAGs to interact with growth factors, guidance cues, and immune mediators. 10 , 11 , 32

Overview of major glycosaminoglycans (GAGs) classes and associated proteoglycans in the brain. Cell membrane‐associated proteoglycans, for example, syndecans (II and IV) and glypicans, predominantly carry heparan sulfate chains and are therefore, classified as heparan sulfate proteoglycans (HSPGs). In contrast, other proteoglycans, for example, syndecans (I and III), perlecan, and agrin can carry both HSPGs and chondroitin sulfate chains, reflecting structural heterogeneity. The lectican family members brevican, neurocan, versican, and aggrecan, as well as phosphacan are primarily chondroitin sulfate proteoglycans (CSPGs). Aggrecan and phosphacan additionally contain keratan sulfate chains. Lumican is a keratan sulfate‐containing proteoglycan. Decorin and biglycan belong to the small leucine‐rich proteoglycan family and carry either chondroitin sulfate or dermatan sulfate chains.

Core disaccharide structures

Most GAGs comprise an amino sugar (N‐acetylglucosamine or N‐acetylgalactosamine) and a uronic acid (iduronic or glucuronic acid). Keratan sulfate is the exception, consisting of the neutral sugar galactose and N‐acetylglucosamine. 19 The repeating units can undergo variable sulfation (O‐ and N‐linked) and acetylation (N‐linked), giving rise to structural heterogeneity.

Linkage to core proteins

GAGs are typically covalently attached to proteoglycan core proteins via serine residues, connected through a tetrasaccharide linker composed of xylose–galactose–galactose–glucuronic acid. 33 Hyaluronan is unique among GAGs in that it is synthesized directly at the plasma membrane by hyaluronan synthases and not covalently attached to proteins. 34 Keratan sulfate chains are attached either through N‐linked glycans (complex‐type oligosaccharides) or O‐mannose linkages. 19

Biosynthesis and post‐translational modifications

GAG biosynthesis begins in the cytoplasm with the synthesis of nucleotide sugars such as UDP‐glucuronic acid and UDP‐N‐acetylglucosamine. 35 These precursors are transported into the Golgi apparatus, where GAG chains are polymerized and undergo extensive post‐translational modification. 36 Enzymatic modifications include sulfation at various positions (N‐, 2‐O, 4‐O, 6‐O, and rarely in heparin 3‐O), acetylation (N‐linked), and epimerisation (C‐5 of the uronic acid). The degree and position of sulfation create immense heterogeneity, often referred to as the “sulfation code,” which determines binding affinity for specific ligands and receptors. 37 , 38

Structural heterogeneity and functional implications

The ability of GAGs to modulate CNS processes arises directly from their structural heterogeneity, particularly in their sulfation patterns. The assembly of the core disaccharide units in the GAGs are not template‐driven, rather a stepwise chain elongation and position‐specific sulfation and acetylation reactions catalyzed by enzymes, such as glycosyltransferases, epimerases, and sulfotransferases, etc. These position‐specific structural modifications enable GAGs to be recognized by distinct protein ligands such as growth factors, chemokines and adhesion molecules. 39 For instance, distinct sulfation motifs in heparan sulfate are required for fibroblast growth factor receptor binding, 14 while unique chondroitin sulfate sulfation patterns determine whether axonal growth is promoted or inhibited. 40 This molecular diversity provides a mean by which the ECM can encode complex biological processes to maintain growth and homeostasis, even though the primary disaccharide building blocks are relatively simple. 41 The structural modifications of GAGs can vary across cell and tissue types, and developmental stages due to changes in the expression and activity of relevant biosynthetic enzymes, and can further vary due to post‐synthetic modifications by enzymes, such as extracellular sulfatases. Changes in sulfation and acetylation patterns are often linked to aberrant signaling and extracellular matrix remodeling, resulting in pathological conditions including chronic inflammation, cardiovascular disease and neurodegeneration. 42 , 43 For example, upregulation of CSPG can reduce the process outgrowth of oligodendrocyte progenitor cells, worsening the generation of new myelin sheath in multiple sclerosis patients. 44 In a recent review, Wang et al. (2022) 45 summarized how modifications to the GAG structure can affect various disease conditions. On the other hand, chronic disease conditions, lifestyle changes, and medical interventions can also initiate the structural modifications in GAGs, worsening tissue damage and functional outcome. For example, the level of syndecan‐1 and heparan sulfate is positively correlated with the shedding of endothelial glycocalyx in the patients undergoing vascular surgery following regional and global ischemia in cardiac, pulmonary, and renal tissues. 46 , 47 Stroke also promotes breakdown of glycocalyx, resulting in increased plasma concentration of GAGs and their breakdown products, which can be used as biomarkers of vascular health. 48 Due to their role in maintaining tissue homeostasis and relevance to numerous disease conditions, structural modifications of GAGs can be targeted to develop novel therapeutics to prevent disease progression.

BIOLOGICAL ROLES OF GAGS IN THE CNS

Organization of the extracellular matrix, perineuronal nets and implications for synaptic plasticity

Synaptic plasticity is critical for post‐stroke recovery, as surviving neural circuits must reorganize to compensate for lost tissue functions. GAGs are central regulators of this process. In the CNS, GAGs are distributed throughout the ECM either diffused or condensed into highly organized structures known as perineuronal nets. 7 , 49 Perineuronal nets are lattice‐like ECM condensations that enwrap subsets of neurons, most prominently fast‐spiking parvalbumin‐positive interneurons. 50 They are anchored to the neuronal surface by hyaluronan, stabilized by CSPGs such as aggrecan and brevican, and crosslinked by tenascin‐R and link proteins. 33 While beneficial for maintaining mature circuits, this stability restricts plasticity after injury. Stroke induces perineuronal net remodeling, with upregulated aggrecan and brevican deposition leading to premature re‐formation of inhibitory ECM around peri‐infarct neurons. 51

The emergence of perineuronal nets coincides with the closure of critical periods of developmental plasticity, suggesting they function to stabilize synaptic connections once neural circuits have matured. 49 By physically restricting receptor mobility, buffering ions, and presenting inhibitory chondroitin sulfate motifs, perineuronal nets act as molecular brakes on plasticity. 52 , 53 In contrast, heparan sulfate promotes plasticity by facilitating growth factor signaling. Heparan sulfate‐proteoglycans (HSPGs) stabilize brain‐derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and fibroblast growth factor 2, enhancing synaptic potentiation and dendritic growth. 28 Experimental degradation of heparan sulfate impairs long‐term potentiation (LTP) and learning, while supplementation with heparan sulfate mimetics restores synaptic plasticity in models of both stroke and Alzheimer's disease. 51 , 54

Experimental disruption of perineuronal nets with enzymes such as chondroitin ABC lyase (ChABC) reinstates a juvenile‐like state of plasticity and enhances axonal sprouting and regeneration. 54 , 55 These observations implicate perineuronal nets not only as stabilizers of mature circuits but also as potential therapeutic targets for promoting repair.

HSPGs: Permissive signals

HSPGs are expressed at the cell surface and within the ECM, acting as co‐receptors and signaling modulators. They regulate multiple developmental processes, including axon guidance, synapse formation, and dendritic branching. 56 , 57 For example, HSPGs modulate fibroblast growth factor signaling, which is critical for hippocampal LTP and memory formation. 58 Heparan sulfate binding to growth factors such as BDNF and midkine further enhances neuronal survival and outgrowth. 28 , 59

CSPGs: Inhibitory signals

In contrast, CSPGs generally function as inhibitors of axonal growth and regeneration. Upregulated after CNS injury, CSPGs such as aggrecan, brevican, versican, and phosphacan engage receptors including RPTPσ, LAR, and Nogo receptors, activating intracellular pathways that collapse growth cones and inhibit neurite extension. 16 , 60 , 61 CSPGs also limit synaptic plasticity in the uninjured adult CNS, stabilizing established connections but restricting circuit rewiring.

GAGS AND NEUROVASCULAR INTERACTIONS IN DISEASE

Stroke pathophysiology is tightly linked to the neurovascular unit (NVU), where GAGs exert a critical influence. Heparan sulfate at the endothelial surface regulates blood–brain barrier (BBB) integrity by binding to vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and angiopoietins. 65 Ischemia triggers heparan sulfate shedding, weakening the glycocalyx and exacerbating vascular leakage. 66 Hyaluronan fragments released into the extracellular space act as DAMPs, activating microglia and promoting leukocyte infiltration. 63

CSPGs further hinder vascular repair, as peri‐infarct deposition of neurocan and phosphacan blocks endothelial sprouting and angiogenesis. 67 These processes mirror neurodegenerative pathology: in Alzheimer's Disease, vascular heparan sulfate and hyaluronan are dysregulated, impairing clearance of amyloid‐beta and worsening neuroinflammation. 24 The parallels between stroke and neurodegeneration are striking. In both conditions, perineuronal nets restrict plasticity, heparan sulfate deficiency impairs growth factor signaling, and hyaluronan fragments exacerbate inflammation. However, the acute and dynamic ECM changes in stroke (within hours to days) contrast with the slow, progressive remodeling seen in Alzheimer's disease and Parkinson's disease. These shared mechanisms underscore the potential for common GAG‐targeted interventions across stroke and neurodegeneration.

GAGS and excitotoxicity

Excitotoxicity is a major driver of neuronal death after ischemia. Heparan sulfate contributes to neuroprotection by sequestering glutamate, limiting excitotoxic damage. 68 Conversely, CSPGs exacerbate excitotoxic stress by restricting glutamate clearance and glial glutamate transporter expression. 55 Hyaluronan fragments have also been implicated in excitotoxic cascades, amplifying microglial activation and cytokine release. 63

Effects of GAGs on inflammation

Neuroinflammation is a hallmark of ischemic stroke, characterized by rapid microglial activation, leukocyte infiltration, and cytokine release. GAGs are potent modulators of immune and inflammatory processes in the CNS. Following injury or disease, ECM components are rapidly remodeled, with the release of soluble GAG fragments and altered sulfation patterns shaping immune cell recruitment and activation 69 (Table 2).

Heparan sulfate in immunomodulation

During ischemia and reperfusion, heparan sulfate is shed from the endothelial glycocalyx and ECM, releasing soluble fragments into the extracellular space. Heparan sulfate is particularly active in immunity, acting both as a danger‐associated molecular pattern (DAMP) and a regulator of cytokine signaling. Tissue damage leads to the rapid release of heparan sulfate fragments from cell‐surface and ECM stores, which stimulate dendritic cells and microglia via TLR4, amplifying the inflammatory cascade and cytokine secretion. 22 , 70 Heparan sulfate also binds chemokines such as CCL21, stabilizing gradients that guide leukocyte migration into the CNS. 71 Mice deficient in heparan sulfate biosynthesis display impaired dendritic cell recruitment and defective adaptive immune responses. 71 Administration of exogenous syndecan‐4, a heparan sulfate‐bearing proteoglycan, inhibited infiltration of leukocytes and macrophages into liver tissues by binding with the heparin‐binding domain of osteopontin. 76

Hyaluronan in inflammation

Hyaluronan undergoes extensive degradation after stroke, with the immune function of hyaluronan being size‐dependent. High‐molecular‐weight hyaluronan (HMW‐HA) maintains tissue homeostasis and dampens inflammatory responses. In contrast, fragmented low‐molecular‐weight hyaluronan (LMW‐HA) acts as a pro‐inflammatory signal, activating Toll‐like receptor 2 and 4 (TLR2/4) on macrophages and dendritic cells, leading to NF‐κB activation and cytokine release. 63 , 64 These fragments persist in peri‐infarct tissue, prolonging inflammatory responses that hinder recovery. Similar mechanisms occur in multiple sclerosis and Alzheimer's Disease, where hyaluronan fragmentation perpetuates chronic gliosis. 73

Chondroitin sulfate and other GAGs on inflammation

CSPGs are upregulated in the post‐stroke glial scar and influence immune cell behavior. Chondroitin sulfate and dermatan sulfate also participate in immune regulation. Specific sulfation patterns in chondroitin sulfate have been shown to alter complement activation and leukocyte adhesion. Sulfated motifs serve as binding sites for chemokines, influencing gradient formation and immune cell trafficking. The context‐dependent effects of chondroitin sulfate, inhibitory in neuronal regeneration but modulatory in immunity, underscore its functional diversity. 74 This dual action mirrors findings in traumatic brain injury and Parkinson's disease, suggesting a conserved inhibitory role for chondroitin sulfate‐rich matrices in CNS inflammation. 75

AGEING, PERINEURONAL NETS, AND ECM REMODELING

The composition of CNS ECM undergoes profound changes with ageing, resulting in diminished synaptic plasticity, impaired memory, and reduced regenerative potential. Perineuronal nets, which are already inhibitory in the adult brain, become increasingly restrictive in aged tissue due to shifts in GAG sulfation patterns. 77 One of the most consistent age‐related changes is the decline in 6‐sulfated chondroitin sulfate (C6S), accompanied by a relative increase in 4‐sulfated chondroitin sulfate (C4S). 25 , 26 , 51 This age‐related shift increases the inhibitory properties of the ECM, leading to reduced axonal sprouting and impaired plasticity (Table 3).

Experimental manipulation of chondroitin sulfate sulfation highlights the importance of this balance. Loss of C6S in animal models leads to impaired hippocampal plasticity and memory deficits, whereas restoration of C6S through genetic or enzymatic approaches reinstates plasticity and improves memory performance. 54 Following a stroke, this imbalance further restricts plasticity in peri‐infarct cortex, contributing to poorer recovery in the aged brain compared with the young brain. These mechanisms overlap with Alzheimer's Disease, where increased inhibitory sulfation within perineuronal nets is linked to cognitive decline. 51 Thus, ECM ageing constitutes a shared barrier to functional recovery in both stroke and neurodegeneration.

Interventions targeting the composition of perineuronal nets are emerging as strategies to rejuvenate the ageing brain. Enzymatic digestion with ChABC restores plasticity in aged animals, though clinical translation remains limited due to enzyme instability. Alternative strategies include manipulation of sulfotransferase expression to rebalance C6S/C4S ratios and the use of biomaterials that sequester or release GAG fragments in a controlled manner.

GAGS IN NEUROREGENERATION

GAGs play a multifaceted role after injury, including stroke. CNS injury triggers a robust ECM response, which regulates growth factor signaling, chemokine gradients, infiltration of peripheral immune cells, and ECM remodeling and plasticity. 80 This regulation is maintained by specific sulfation patterns and structural motifs of different GAGs. 81

CSPGs as a growth inhibitory signal

Within days after stroke, reactive astrocytes and glial progenitors form a dense glial scar rich in CSPGs, including aggrecan, brevican, neurocan, and phosphacan. 24 While the scar serves to contain inflammation and preserve tissue integrity, it also generates a biochemical barrier that suppresses axonal sprouting. CSPGs engage receptors such as RPTPσ and NgR1 to activate RhoA/ROCK signaling, collapsing growth cones and halting regeneration. 16 , 61

ChABC digestion of CSPGs restores axonal growth and improves recovery in preclinical stroke models, but translational application is limited by enzyme instability and immune recognition. 10 Newer strategies include stabilized ChABC variants, nanoparticle delivery, and pharmacological inhibitors of CSPG signaling. 82 Hettiaratchi and colleagues redesigned the native ChABC structure by point mutations to increase the stability of this protein. 83 , 84 The redesigned ChABC with a point mutation at 37 amino acids has 6.5 times greater half‐life than the native ChABC and promotes axonal regrowth after hydrogel‐mediated delivery following stroke. 83 , 84 Among pharmacological modulators of CSPG, pleiotrophin, a heparin‐binding growth factor, is particularly notable. Recently, Gupta and colleagues showed that pleiotrophin can overcome the growth‐inhibitory effect of CSPG and promote neurite outgrowth and oligodendrocyte maturation in vitro in the presence of CSPG. 85 , 86

Heparan sulfate as a pro‐regenerative signal

In contrast to CSPGs, heparan sulfate plays largely growth‐permissive and supportive roles in regeneration. Genetic studies in C. elegans and mice reveal that heparan sulfate is essential for axon guidance, branching, and regeneration, acting through modulation of guidance cues such as netrins and slits. 31 , 87 Heparan sulfate also interacts with and enhances growth factors, including FGF, BDNF, and VEGF, as well as adhesion molecules, such as neural cell adhesion molecule, to promote neurite extension, synaptic reorganization, oligodendrocyte maturation, and angiogenesis in the peri‐infarct region. 28 , 59

The specific sulfation patterns of heparan sulfate play a major role in determining functional outcomes. For instance, N‐sulfation supports oligodendrocyte differentiation and myelination, whereas 6‐O and 2‐O sulfation enhance axon growth and synaptogenesis. 23 These findings suggest that precise modulation of heparan sulfate sulfation could bias post‐stroke responses toward regeneration rather than inhibition, warranting further investigation.

Summary of mechanistic contrast between heparan sulfate and chondroitin sulfate

Although both heparan sulfate and chondroitin sulfate bind to receptors such as RPTPσ, their functional outcomes differ (Figure 2). Heparan sulfate engages receptors in a multivalent manner that stabilizes permissive signaling complexes, whereas chondroitin sulfate motifs induce inhibitory signaling cascades. 88 This mechanistic divergence explains the dichotomy between heparan sulfate‐mediated promotion of repair and chondroitin sulfate‐mediated inhibition of regeneration.

Contrasting roles of chondroitin sulfate proteoglycans (CSPGs) and heparan sulfate proteoglycans (HSPGs) in recovery after stroke or brain injury. CSPGs contribute to adverse outcomes by promoting (I) glial scar formation, (II) inflammation, (III) neurodegeneration, and (IV) inhibition of neural plasticity by forming peri‐neuronal nets (PNN). In contrast, HSPGs promote repair and regeneration by enhancing (I) axonal sprouting, (II) neurogenesis, (III) angiogenesis, and (IV) extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling.

HEPARAN SULFATE GLYCOMIMETICS: THERAPEUTIC POTENTIAL

The contrasting roles of heparan sulfate and chondroitin sulfate in regeneration have inspired the design of heparan sulfate glycomimetics as therapeutic tools. These synthetic molecules aim to mimic the structure and function of heparan sulfate, harnessing its pro‐regenerative properties while counteracting the inhibitory effects of CSPGs. Further, these engineered molecules are being investigated as interventions to promote recovery after stroke and in neurodegenerative disorders characterized by ECM dysfunction (Table 4 and Figure 3).

Advances in glycomimetic design

Recent synthetic strategies allow precise control of sulfation motifs, chain length, and backbone structure. 93 , 94 This enables the generation of heparan sulfate mimetics with defined biological activities, such as enhancing growth factor signaling or modulating immune responses. 95 , 96 Chemical modifications improve stability against enzymatic degradation, while conjugation to biomaterials allows targeted and sustained delivery. 97 , 98 It is also possible to purify a specific population of heparan sulfate with high bioactivity using a native population of heparan sulfate to tune the bioactivity. For example, HS7 was purified from native heparan sulfate by affinity chromatography using a selected portion of resin‐bound VEGF165. 92

Preclinical applications for stroke and neurodegeneration

In rodent stroke models, heparan sulfate glycomimetics enhance axonal sprouting, stabilize growth factor signaling, and reduce inflammation. For example, treatment with intravenous HSm4131, a synthetic heparan sulfate, demonstrated lasting neuroprotection and functional benefits in a rat model of stroke. 99 Treatment improves behavioral outcomes, including motor function and cognitive recovery. 28 , 54 , 100 Treatment with HS7, a heparan sulfate fraction with high affinity for VEGF165, also improved neurological outcome in a rat model of stroke. 95 Glycomimetics also restore glycocalyx integrity and support angiogenesis, accelerating vascular recovery. In Alzheimer's Disease and Parkinson's Disease models, heparan sulfate mimetics limit protein aggregation (amyloid‐β, α‐synuclein), protect neurons from excitotoxic stress, and enhance synaptic resilience. 24 , 29 Thus, heparan sulfate mimetics represent a unifying therapeutic approach across both acute (stroke) and chronic (neurodegeneration) contexts.

Translational challenges

Despite these advances, several hurdles remain. The structural complexity of native heparan sulfate is difficult to replicate, 101 raising questions of how much mimicry is required for therapeutic efficacy. Delivery to the CNS remains challenging, particularly across the BBB. Furthermore, heparan sulfate mimetics must balance efficacy with specificity, avoiding off‐target effects in peripheral tissues. Nonetheless, the ability of heparan sulfate mimetics to both counteract CSPG‐mediated inhibition and promote endogenous repair positions them as leading candidates in the next generation of stroke and dementia therapeutics.

CLINICAL TRIALS OF GAGS AND HEPARAN SULFATE‐MIMETICS IN STROKE AND NEURODEGENERATIVE CONDITIONS

Interest in heparan sulfate‐based “matrix therapy” and synthetic glycomimetics has also started to move beyond preclinical models into first‐in‐human studies (Table 5). Clinical studies are emerging both in acute ischemic stroke, as well as into other disease areas (such as oncology, vascular medicine, tissue regeneration), which provide valuable safety and mechanistic data to inform the development of new neurotherapeutics. 104 Work in rodent models of stroke demonstrated that heparan sulfate‐mimetic polymers of the ReGeneraTing Agents (RGTA®s) family (OTR413x compounds) preserve the perivascular glycocalyx, stabilize growth‐factor signaling, reduce infarct volume and improve functional recovery, and positive results from these studies have supported the translation into early human studies. In addition to this, some of the polymers used to synthesize these mimetics are currently used in the clinic as regenerative agents. 105 , 106 , 107 , 108 By protecting HBGF from proteolytic degradation, RGTA® fostered substantial tissue repair in various in vivo injury models. 105 , 106

The most advanced program to date is OTR4132 (RGTA® family). It progressed from a first‐in‐human, ascending‐dose safety study (MATRISS, NCT04083001) to a randomized Phase‐2 trial (MATRISS‐II, NCT06700824). OTR4132 is delivered intra‐arterially after mechanical thrombectomy, aiming to act locally on reperfused peri‐infarct tissue and support microvascular and parenchymal repair. Early reports indicate acceptable safety in the first‐in‐human cohort, and MATRISS‐II is now enrolling participants to assess efficacy outcomes, including National Institute of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS), MRI lesion volume, and modified Rankin Scale (mRS).

Beyond stroke, several heparan sulfate‐mimetic and heparanase‐targeting agents have undergone human testing, primarily for oncological and vascular diseases. Examples include muparfostat (PI‐88), roneparstat (SST0001), necuparanib (M402), and sulodexide. These trials inform tolerability, systemic pharmacology, off‐target effects, and large‐scale manufacturing feasibility, even though they are not CNS‐directed. Importantly, some trials revealed safety or metabolic signals (e.g., muparfostat/PI‐88 toxicity) that underscore the need for CNS‐specific delivery strategies and careful dose selection (NCT01402908). 109 Further, in Alzheimer's disease and Parkinson's disease, no robust randomized trials of heparan sulfate mimetics are yet registered. Observational studies and mechanistic human data suggest heparan sulfate and heparin/heparinoids may influence dementia progression. For instance, retrospective analyses have linked systemic anticoagulant use to delayed Alzheimer's disease onset, and mechanistic studies implicate heparan sulfate in Aβ aggregation and clearance. 110 However, interventional heparan sulfate‐mimetic RCTs for Alzheimer's disease/Parkinson's disease are not yet available, meaning the translational case relies on preclinical data as well as clinical safety information from non‐CNS indications.

The clinical landscape currently shows: one clear stroke‐directed program (OTR4132) advancing through early human testing; several systemic oncology and vascular programs that provide relevant safety and mechanistic insights; and a gap in randomized neurodegeneration trials, although observational and mechanistic evidence support plausibility. The next 2–3 years, particularly the MATRISS‐II readouts, will be critical for establishing whether heparan sulfate‐mimetic “matrix therapy” can become a viable adjuvant to reperfusion in acute ischemic stroke.

CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS

GAGs act as both barriers and facilitators of recovery after stroke. Heparan sulfate and chondroitin sulfate epitomize this duality, with heparan sulfate driving permissive plasticity and chondroitin sulfate imposing inhibitory constraints. Stroke amplifies these dynamics by releasing heparan sulfate and hyaluronan fragments that activate immune pathways, while simultaneously promoting CSPG‐rich scar formation that locks tissue into a non‐regenerative state.

Emerging glycomimetic technologies provide an opportunity to tip this balance in favor of recovery. By mimicking heparan sulfate functions, stabilizing growth factors, and disarming CSPG inhibition, heparan sulfate glycomimetics show promise not only for stroke but also for neurodegenerative diseases, where ECM rigidity and impaired signaling can limit plasticity.

Future work should focus on integrating glycobiology with materials science and regenerative neuroscience to design the next generation of glycomimetics. Approaches such as biomaterial scaffolds delivering heparan sulfate mimetics, CRISPR‐based modulation of sulfotransferases, and combination therapies with rehabilitation hold potential to unlock the CNS's intrinsic regenerative capacity.
