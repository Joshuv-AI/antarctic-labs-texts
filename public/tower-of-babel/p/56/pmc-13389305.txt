metaWEPP: leveraging biobank-scale intra-species phylogenies for near-haplotype resolution in metagenomic analysis
Authors: Pranav Gangwar, Qiwen Xu, Jaden Seangmany, Pratik Katte, Yatish Turakhia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMetagenomic sequencing is transforming diverse areas of health and biological sciences, including pathogen surveillance, clinical diagnostics, and microbiome research. However, the inherent complexity of metagenomic data limits most computational tools to species-level classification and abundance estimation, overlooking within-species genetic diversity that drives key phenotypes. We present metaWEPP, a novel computational pipeline that achieves near-haplotype resolution in metagenomic analysis for species with adequate representation in reference genome biobanks and having sufficient sequencing depth and genome coverage. Specifically, metaWEPP assigns sequencing reads to species using standard taxonomic classifiers, phylogenetically places them onto species-specific mutation-annotated trees of publicly available sequences, and selects the haplotypes that best explain the sample. It also reports unaccounted alleles indicative of novel variants and provides an interactive dashboard for read-level visualization. Applied to diverse metagenomic and mixed-genome samples from prior studies, metaWEPP produced concordant species-level results, while revealing finer lineage- and haplotype-level insights not captured by existing tools. On various clinical samples, metaWEPP identified infecting pathogens and additionally provided credible lineage- and haplotype-level information that can support clinical decision-making. On wastewater samples, metaWEPP uncovered previously undetected haplotype clusters of epidemiological relevance. These findings demonstrate metaWEPP’s ability to advance various clinical, epidemiological, and research applications with deeper, actionable insights.

Introduction

High-throughput sequencing has transformed metagenomics as it enables direct, unbiased profiling of microbial communities from environmental and clinical samples—something traditional culture methods cannot match. Specifically, by sequencing all genomic content in a sample, metagenomic approaches are providing unprecedented insights into the structure, function, and evolutionary dynamics of complex ecosystems [1–4]. Consequently, metagenomics has been widely adopted across a range of disciplines, such as clinical diagnostics [5–7], environmental surveillance [8–11], food microbiology [12–14], pharmacology [15, 16], and microbiome research [17, 18].

Despite these advances, most metagenomic pipelines stop at species-level classification and abundance estimation. While these approaches excel at identifying the species present in a sample using standard tools such as Kraken [19] and Centrifuge [20], they do not reconstruct the full haplotypes. Yet, subspecies variation often determines key phenotypes, such as improved pathogenicity, antimicrobial resistance, or immune evasion [21–24]. While lineage or strain-level subclassification can capture some phenotypes, haplotype-level resolution remains the “holy grail” in metagenomics, as previously unseen lineages, strains, and even species are often found in these samples [25–28].

Previous work has sought to achieve haplotype-level resolution in complex metagenomic samples using de novo assembly of genomes, yielding what are called metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) [29–32]. However, this approach frequently produces highly fragmented and incomplete MAGs for short-read sequencing [33, 34]—the dominant technology in metagenomic studies—and often collapses closely related variants (e.g. from low-divergence lineages) [35–37]. These issues are amplified in certain environmental samples, such as wastewater, where sequencing read lengths are inherently limited due to genome degradation [38, 39].

An alternative strategy to assembly leverages the rapidly expanding databases of microbial genomes [40]. This is based on the idea that as sequencing density increases, the odds of finding closely matching haplotypes or strains in existing databases increase. Recent methods, including Strainify [41] and StrainScan [42], capitalize on this principle to reach strain-level resolution for single-bacteria-targeted samples, but their performance on low-divergence viral populations, multi-pathogen samples, and pathogens lacking lineage designations remains unclear. A recent method, WEPP [11], also leverages existing databases, but has been shown to achieve near-haplotype resolution for single-pathogen-targeted wastewater sequencing. It can also flag novel variants by reporting “unaccounted alleles”—those that are found in sequencing reads but not in selected haplotypes.

In this work, we present metaWEPP, a novel pipeline that combines existing taxonomic classifiers with WEPP to deliver haplotype-level resolution in metagenomic samples for species that have been sequenced at sufficient depth and genome coverage in the sample with adequate representation in reference genome biobanks (e.g. NCBI GenBank [43]). The pipeline integrates species-level read assignments based on a taxonomic classifier, with WEPP-based haplotype-level characterization using existing species-specific databases. metaWEPP reports species-level abundances in a sample and, for each species, provides haplotype and lineage abundances, along with unaccounted alleles. Additionally, an interactive dashboard allows users to explore the phylogenetic context of each haplotype and perform read-level analyses, such as a detailed examination of unaccounted alleles.

Using simulated and real-world clinical and wastewater samples, we demonstrate that metaWEPP accurately identifies haplotypes, reveals intra-host diversity, and recovers co-circulating clusters, including those from previously undesignated lineages. Compared to de novo metagenomic assemblers [29, 44], metaWEPP results in concordant but less fragmented results. Collectively, these advances open new frontiers for performing pathogen surveillance, targeted drug therapies, co-infection analysis, and epidemiological research using metagenomic samples.

Methods

MetaWEPP: algorithmic and implementation details

metaWEPP is an automated, Snakemake-based [45] pipeline (Fig. 1, Methods) designed to analyze both untargeted metagenomic and targeted (i.e. primers-based amplification of specific pathogens) mixed-genome datasets from environmental (e.g. wastewater) and clinical [e.g. from cell-free DNA (cfDNA) or nasal swabs] samples. It takes metagenomic sequencing reads and a Kraken2 [19] database as input, with optional UShER-based mutation-annotated trees (MATs) [46] for detecting the haplotypes of species of interest. In metaWEPP, a haplotype refers to the set of alleles (variants of the nucleotide sequence at a particular genomic locus) present in a genome represented by the tips or the inferred ancestral nodes in the UShER-MAT, while a species could refer to any taxonomic unit for which a corresponding UShER-MAT is maintained, which, for viruses, typically follows the virus Operational Taxonomic Unit (vOTU) criteria [47].

Overview of the metaWEPP pipeline. Metagenomic or mixed-genome samples from environmental sources and clinical specimens (such as cfDNA and nasopharyngeal swabs) can be sequenced using either targeted (using primers) or untargeted sequencing approaches and analyzed with metaWEPP to obtain haplotype-level insights. Sequencing reads are first taxonomically classified using Kraken2, followed by haplotype-level analysis of each detected species with WEPP. The pipeline reports haplotype and lineage proportions, unaccounted alleles indicative of potential novel variants, and read-to-haplotype mappings for each species.

metaWEPP then uses haplotype and lineage annotations available in the MATs to report species-, lineage-, and haplotype-level abundances, read-to-haplotype assignments, and unaccounted alleles, which can all be visualized through an interactive dashboard (Figs. 1 and 3). Internally, the workflow begins with taxonomic classification of reads using Kraken2 to identify species present in the sample. Users are then prompted to select species, beyond those pre-specified, that exceed a specified proportion (default: 1%) for haplotype-level analysis. For each selected species, metaWEPP either uses a user-provided MAT or builds one from scratch using viral_usher (https://github.com/AngieHinrichs/viral_usher, manuscript under preparation) by downloading NCBI GenBank sequences [43] and Nextclade-based lineage annotations [48] if available. metaWEPP also retrieves the reference genome from NCBI RefSeq [49] corresponding to each MAT and runs WEPP for each species individually to place reads onto MATs, select haplotypes that best explain the reads, and generate results for dashboard visualization.

The metaWEPP pipeline comprises four main components: (i) reference genome preparation, (ii) MAT construction, (iii) taxonomic classification, and (iv) WEPP analysis. The first two components are executed once for database construction and can be skipped for subsequent metagenomic samples by setting “ADD_SPECIES_RUNTIME” to false, allowing only the per-sample analysis to be run. The workflow requires two primary inputs: raw sequencing reads and a pre-built Kraken2 database, specified either as a URL or a local path. Users can supply non-default parameters either through the command line or using a configuration file in YAML format [50].

WEPP Analysis: In the final step, metaWEPP leverages the species-level read segregation from the previous stage to run WEPP independently for each user-selected species. By combining species-specific reads with the corresponding reference genome and the MAT, WEPP selects the haplotypes from the MAT that best explain the observed sequencing reads. This is achieved through parsimonious placement of reads on the MAT, followed by selection of an initial most-probable haplotype set based on the resulting placement distribution, along with some of their nearest neighbor haplotypes. This candidate set is then iteratively refined using a deconvolution algorithm that estimates the relative abundance of each haplotype, discards those below a specified proportion threshold, and updates the set by adding some of the most probable neighbors of the remaining haplotypes. The algorithm terminates when the selected haplotype set remains unchanged across consecutive iterations or when a maximum number of iterations is reached. We note that previous phylogenetic placement methods, such as EPA [53], EPA-ng [54], pplacer [55], APPLES [56], and App-SpaM [57], could not be used instead of WEPP in this stage as they: (i) are designed primarily for inter-species phylogenies, (ii) do not scale well to high read and haplotype counts typical to the metagenomic analyses, and (iii) do not support haplotype abundance estimation.The Snakemake workflow parallelizes WEPP across multiple species and additionally supports batch processing of multiple samples, allowing users to specify a list of sample names via the configuration file or command-line options for concurrent analysis and efficient resource utilization. Additionally, WEPP’s scalable, multi-threaded algorithm further accelerates computation, making the software well-suited for complex metagenomic samples containing diverse species. After the haplotype inferences, metaWEPP outputs commands for interactive visualization for each species’ haplotypes within their phylogenetic context and supports read-level analysis to examine unaccounted alleles, which may indicate the presence of previously unobserved haplotypes.

Kraken2 database and mutation-annotated trees

We used the pre-built Kraken2 Standard database (139 GB) downloaded from https://benlangmead.github.io/aws-indexes/k2, which includes NCBI RefSeq sequences for archaea, bacteria, viruses, plasmids, and humans, along with the UniVec_Core database.

For SARS-CoV-2 analysis across all simulated datasets, wastewater samples, and clinical specimens, we employed public MATs maintained by UCSC (https://hgdownload.gi.ucsc.edu/goldenPath/wuhCor1/UShER_SARS-CoV-2) [58] that incorporate sequences from GenBank [43], COG-UK [59], and the China National Center for Bioinformation [60]. The MAT used for analyzing the co-infection sample from Li et al. [61] was dated 1 December 2024, while all other SARS-CoV-2 samples were analyzed using the MAT dated 1 April 2023. MATs for RSV-A, RSV-B, and monkeypox (Mpox) were dated 25 April 2025, and downloaded from https://usher.bio. SARS-CoV-2 MATs were preannotated with both Nextclade and PANGO lineage assignments [62, 63], whereas Mpox, RSV-A, and RSV-B contained Nextclade assignments. MATs for other species, including Tomato brown rugose fruit virus (ToBRFV), Human metapneumovirus (HMPV), Rhinovirus A, Adenovirus A, Parvovirus B19, Zika virus, Measles, Gammaherpesvirus-4, and Alphaherpesvirus-1, were automatically constructed using viral_usher (https://github.com/AngieHinrichs/viral_usher) with Nextclade lineage assignments wherever available, as part of the metaWEPP pipeline, with quality control parameters “min_length_proportion = 0.1” and “max_N_proportion = 0.5.”

De novo assemblers

We used SAVAGE [29] and metaSPAdes [44] assemblers to validate and compare against metaWEPP’s selected haplotypes. SAVAGE (v0.4.2) was run with parameters “--split 60 --min_overlap_len 100 --merge_contigs 0.01,” while metaSPAdes was executed as part of SPAdes (v4.2.0) using the “--metaviral” option. We used the fasta files of contigs generated by “Stage C” of SAVAGE, and the scaffolds generated by metaSPAdes for evaluation.

Evaluation metrics

We benchmarked metaWEPP’s performance on simulated data using three key metrics: accuracy of species-level abundance estimates (Species Abundance RMSE and Aitchison distance [68]), sensitivity of detected haplotypes (Weighted Haplotype Branch Distance), and their precision (Weighted Peak Branch Distance) for each species.

Species abundance RMSE and Aitchison distance: Measures the root mean squared error as well as Aitchison distance between the expected and estimated species abundance reported by Kraken2 using default parameters.

Species abundance RMSE and Aitchison distance: Measures the root mean squared error as well as Aitchison distance between the expected and estimated species abundance reported by Kraken2 using default parameters.

Weighted haplotype branch distance: Evaluates how well the most abundant haplotypes in a metagenomic sample are recovered for each species, giving greater weight to higher proportion true haplotypes. Each true haplotype is matched to the closest estimated haplotype based on the minimum phylogenetic branch length difference, which serves as a measure of sensitivity; lower values indicate more accurate recovery of true haplotypes.

Weighted haplotype branch distance: Evaluates how well the most abundant haplotypes in a metagenomic sample are recovered for each species, giving greater weight to higher proportion true haplotypes. Each true haplotype is matched to the closest estimated haplotype based on the minimum phylogenetic branch length difference, which serves as a measure of sensitivity; lower values indicate more accurate recovery of true haplotypes.

Weighted peak branch distance: Captures precision by assessing how closely the most abundant estimated haplotypes of a species correspond to the true haplotypes present in the sample, with lower values indicating fewer spurious inferences.

The weighted branch distance metrics are defined more formally as follows. Let H = {h1, h2, …., hn} denote the set of true haplotypes in the metagenomic sample with corresponding proportions given by wh, and P = {p1, p2, …., pm} denote the set of haplotypes inferred by metaWEPP with corresponding proportions given by wp. The branch distance, denoted by Dist(hi,pj), represents the number of phylogenetic branches separating a true haplotype hi from an inferred haplotype (pj).

To evaluate reference-free assemblers, we analyzed contigs generated by SAVAGE and metaSPAdes. These assemblies were then aligned to the reference haplotypes present in each sample using minimap2 [52]. A contig was mapped to one or more haplotypes if it differed from them by at most one single-nucleotide site. Haplotype coverage was then calculated as the fraction of the haplotype genome covered by the contigs mapping to it.

Execution environment and runtime analyses

All experiments were performed on an Intel Xeon Silver 4216 processor with 768 GB DDR4 memory and 4.0 TB SATA storage. Metagenomic samples were analyzed using 48 CPU threads, with runtimes ranging from a few minutes to ~3 h, depending on the size of the pathogen-specific MAT and the number of reads assigned to these pathogens by Kraken2. As shown in Supplementary Table S3, samples containing SARS-CoV-2 reads exhibit runtimes approximately an order of magnitude higher than those for other pathogens with comparable read counts. This is primarily due to the substantially larger SARS-CoV-2 MAT, which contains 8 ,260, 461 sequences—around three orders of magnitude higher than the MATs of other pathogens analyzed in this study, containing only a few thousands or fewer sequences. We further observe that runtime scales sub-linearly with increasing read counts for the same pathogen, as illustrated by samples SRR17735512 and SRR17735514, due to several optimizations incorporated in WEPP.

Analysis of peak memory usage shows that it is dominated by the Kraken2 standard database, which requires approximately 93 GB of memory for every run (Supplementary Table S3). In contrast, the peak memory required for WEPP’s haplotype analysis is substantially lower, ranging from ∼0.2 GB for the Rhinovirus-A MAT with 195 sequences to ∼51 GB for the SARS-CoV-2 MAT that contains over 8 million sequences.

We accounted for differences across datasets by adjusting a few metaWEPP parameters. Specifically, the “MIN_PROP” parameter, which controls the minimum proportion required to retain a haplotype, was set to “0.005” for wastewater samples and “0.05” for clinical samples to reduce false positives in the latter. Additionally, the “MIN_LEN” parameter, which filters out reads below a specified length after primer trimming, was reduced from “80” to “40” for datasets with average read lengths below 80 bp.

Results

metaWEPP achieves near-haplotype resolution on simulated metagenomic samples

We evaluated metaWEPP’s performance using 24 simulated metagenomic samples, each containing two to ten species with up to four distinct haplotypes per species, with the most complex sample comprising 40 haplotypes across 10 species (the “Methods” section). The simulated species included SARS-CoV-2, Mpox, RSV-A, RSV-B, HMPV, Rhinovirus A, Adenovirus A, Parvovirus B19, Zika virus, Measles, and Gammaherpesvirus-4, with their relative abundances varying between 2.5% and 90%. Performance was assessed using the species-level root mean square error (RMSE) and Aitchison distance [68] between expected and estimated abundances, and two haplotype-level metrics within each species: (i) the weighted haplotype branch distance and (ii) the weighted peak branch distance, which reflect sensitivity and precision of the haplotype selections, respectively, with smaller values implying better performance on both metrics (the “Methods” section).

The species-level abundance estimates produced by metaWEPP were largely accurate across both high and low abundance ranges, with an RMSE of only 0.029 and an Aitchison distance of 1.523 (Fig. 2A). Since read classifications are based on Kraken2, which uses k-mers to assign taxonomic labels to each read, we sometimes observed reads misclassified to nearby species within the same genus or even only to the genus level. To mitigate its impact on haplotype detection within each species, metaWEPP aggregates all reads assigned to a genus and its constituent species and reclassifies them to the appropriate species by performing full alignments using minimap2 [52] against the reference genome of the species selected for haplotype-level analysis (the “Methods” section). Overall, metaWEPP consistently demonstrates both high sensitivity and precision in identifying haplotypes from metagenomic samples, which is evident from the low values of both weighted haplotype and peak branch distances (Fig. 2B and C). We additionally evaluated two de novo assemblers, SAVAGE [29] and metaSPAdes [44], on the same datasets (the “Methods” section). Both assemblers produced fragmented assemblies (which is why weighted peak and haplotype branch distances could not be calculated directly for them), with SAVAGE generating shorter contigs than metaSPAdes, while achieving higher genome coverage across the haplotypes present in the simulated metagenomic samples (Fig. 2D and E). Both assemblers also missed several haplotypes, suggesting that several low-diversity variants were collapsed into one. While even this level of recovery is impressive for these assemblers, given that the assembly was performed in a reference-free manner, our results also underscore the advantage of metaWEPP’s approach, of leveraging prior information from large-scale sequencing datasets to enable a more complete and precise recovery of full haplotypes. This improves the resolution of detection and is particularly useful for clinical and public health surveillance applications where such reference databases exist.

To assess the limits of haplotype detection, we simulated metagenomic samples containing four haplotypes of each species, namely SARS-CoV-2, Mpox, RSV-A, and RSV-B, across a range of sequencing depths (6.25–75× per haplotype, Fig. 2F and G). We observe a gradual decline in metaWEPP’s precision (increase in weighted peak branch distance, Fig. 2G) as read depth is reduced from 50× to 6.25×, with sensitivity degrading when the depth is below 25× per haplotype. This highlights that accurate haplotype detection from metagenomic samples with metaWEPP requires sufficient sequencing depths, ideally >25× per haplotype. A much higher level of coverage is typical for wastewater and clinical samples, where high viral load or targeted amplification generates much higher sequencing depths.

metaWEPP identifies near-haplotypes of pathogens in clinical samples from targeted and cell-free DNA sequencing

We used metaWEPP to analyze clinical metagenomic specimens from previous studies, which included targeted sequencing of specific pathogens obtained from bronchoalveolar lavage fluid [61] and nasopharyngeal swabs [65], as well as untargeted sequencing of cfDNA from plasma [5] (the “Methods” section). Although the infecting pathogen species were reported in previous studies, none performed haplotype-level analysis, which metaWEPP enables.

One patient sample that was collected in September 2023 by Li et al. in Shanghai [61] was reported to be a case of co-infection involving SARS-CoV-2 and HMPV. Using metaWEPP, we reanalyzed the sample and identified a SARS-CoV-2 haplotype belonging to lineage EG.5.1.1, which was circulating locally at the time of sample collection as per the GISAID data [69] [Fig. 3A(i)]. Nearly all alleles of the selected haplotype showed 100% allele frequency at the sites covered by sequencing reads, supporting the reliability of the haplotype detection (Supplementary Fig. S1A). Additionally, metaWEPP detected two closely related HMPV haplotypes belonging to the A2b2 lineage—one dominant (85%) and one minor (15%) [Fig. 3A(ii)]. We observed in the phylogenetic tree that the dominant haplotype and several of its close neighbors originated in China around a similar timeframe, supporting its presence in the patient, whereas the minor haplotype was traced to the United States. metaWEPP reported several terminal branch mutations of the dominant haplotype as reversal mutations (reverting to the reference) in the unaccounted alleles, while a few terminal branch mutations of the minor haplotype were completely absent from the reads (Fig. 3B and C, Supplementary Fig. S1B, and Supplementary Table S1). Together, these metaWEPP findings suggest that the infection might be caused by a potentially novel HPMV haplotype that, although not present in the MAT, could be phylogenetically placed between the two detected haplotypes [indicated by the highlighted branch in Fig. 3A(ii)].

Next, we analyzed a sample collected during the 2017–2018 RSV season from a two-month-old infant patient in the United Kingdom [65], who was reportedly co-infected with RSV-A and Rhinovirus-A. Re-analysis with metaWEPP identified these pathogens and selected a single haplotype from each corresponding MAT [Supplementary Fig. S5A(i,ii)]. The RSV-A haplotype identified by metaWEPP clustered with other haplotypes collected in the United Kingdom during 2018 [Supplementary Fig. S5A(i)], which suggests local transmission and adds confidence to the accuracy of haplotype detection. The metaWEPP-selected haplotype belongs to lineage A.D.1, which also concurs with the ON1 genotype [70] reported in the original study, further validating the results. However, geographical validation of the accuracy of the Rhinovirus-A haplotype identified by metaWEPP was challenging [Supplementary Fig. S5A(ii)], as only 85 out of 11 652 (0.7%) high-quality publicly available Rhinovirus-A sequences in the MAT were contributed by the United Kingdom. Moreover, sequencing coverage for Rhinovirus-A in the sample was also poor (fewer than 9% of the genome was covered by greater than 10 reads in the sample).

Lastly, we also analyzed an untargeted cfDNA sample, collected from a sepsis patient’s plasma in the United States during a study by Karius Inc. in 2016–2017 [5]. The patient was reported to contain Alphaherpesvirus-1, which metaWEPP also confirmed (Supplementary Fig. S5B). Here, too, the haplotype identified by metaWEPP has geographical support, as it clustered with other Alphaherpesvirus-1 haplotypes in the MAT that were collected in the United States in 2017 (Supplementary Fig. S5B).

In summary, across both targeted and untargeted clinical samples, metaWEPP helped identify haplotypes of diverse viruses that matched locally circulating haplotypes at the time of collection. These results demonstrate that in addition to accurately identifying the infecting pathogens, metaWEPP could contribute new haplotype- and lineage-level insights to clinical diagnosis and management.

metaWEPP enables fine-grained analysis of patients co-infected with distinct SARS-CoV-2 variants

The COVID-19 pandemic spurred heightened scientific interest in analyzing patients co-infected with multiple SARS-CoV-2 variants (e.g. Delta and Omicron), as they could lead to fitter recombinant strains [71–73]. However, most studies were performed at the lineage or variant levels. We evaluated metaWEPP’s ability to uncover new insights by reanalyzing the sequencing data from two such cases: (i) an Alpha-Epsilon co-infection reported by Wertheim et al. [66] and (ii) a Delta–Omicron co-infection reported by Realingo et al. [67].

The Wertheim et al. study involved two SARS-CoV-2 patients: one co-infected with the Alpha (B.1.1.7) and Epsilon (B.1.429) variants, and their partner, infected solely with Alpha. In the co-infected patient’s sample, metaWEPP detected an Epsilon (B.1.429) haplotype at 21.4% proportion and two Alpha haplotypes at 48.7% and 29.9% proportions (Fig. 4A and B), while in their partner’s sample, metaWEPP identified the same Alpha haplotypes at 79.7% and 20.3% proportions, respectively (Fig. 4B). The original study reported the Epsilon variant at 20% in the co-infected individual, concordant with metaWEPP, and identified a polymorphism at site 23,099 in both individuals, which is also reflected by the two Alpha haplotypes selected by metaWEPP (last allele of “node_1 367 487” in Fig. 4B and C). To further validate these results, we assembled the reads using two reference-free metagenomic assemblers, SAVAGE [29] and metaSPAdes [44]. SAVAGE produced 86 contigs (Supplementary Table S2), where 64 contigs lacked informative alleles and aligned perfectly to all 3 metaWEPP-selected haplotypes, while 20 of them aligned to both Alpha haplotypes (19 of them with an exact match), and 2 aligned perfectly to the Epsilon haplotype selected by metaWEPP. The assembly with metaSPAdes was less fragmented, yielding 17 contigs. This is possibly because metaSPAdes collapses low-diversity variants more aggressively, as all of its contigs perfectly aligned with only one of metaWEPP’s selected Alpha haplotype. Nevertheless, our results suggest that independent assessment through de novo genome assembly, though resulting in fragmented assemblies, helped in corroborating the haplotypes identified by metaWEPP and further reinforcing confidence in its results.

We also analyzed a Delta–Omicron co-infection sample from Realingo et al. [67] with metaWEPP and identified two Delta haplotypes, one from lineage B.1.617.2 and one from AY.14, and one Omicron haplotype from lineage BA.2.3 (Supplementary Fig. S2). Allele-frequency profiles showed strong read support for each of these calls. The two Delta haplotypes differed by only a few mutations, suggesting the sample might contain a novel Delta haplotype not represented in the MAT but closely related to the selected ones. metaWEPP also flagged many unaccounted alleles, likely arising due to systematic errors of the Oxford Nanopore Technologie platform, on which this sample was sequenced.

Together, these results demonstrate metaWEPP’s ability to accurately identify haplotypes from MATs that best explain each sample, even in cases of co-infection by different variants of the same pathogen. It surpasses assembly-based methods, which often produce highly fragmented assemblies and fail to recover complete haplotypes. This makes metaWEPP particularly valuable for pathogen surveillance and for investigating co-infections that may give rise to recombinant lineages.

metaWEPP reveals haplotype clusters of pathogens from metagenomic wastewater samples

We further evaluated metaWEPP’s ability to improve resolution from species-level classification to haplotype- and lineage-level analysis on untargeted metagenomic wastewater samples. Specifically, we analyzed a wastewater sample from Los Angeles collected on 22 December 2020, by Rothman et al. [10], who only reported species-level compositions in their study.

The species-level abundances of metaWEPP closely matched those reported in the original study, with SARS-CoV-2 and Tomato brown rugose fruit virus (ToBRFV) being the two most abundant viruses in the sample (Fig. 5A). The SARS-CoV-2 lineages identified by metaWEPP have strong evidence of local circulation, as they were all found among GISAID submissions from Los Angeles during the same time period (15 December 2020 to 15 January 2021) (Fig. 5B). For ToBRFV, a highly virulent plant virus that is included on the EPPO Alert List [74] for causing major outbreaks in Jordan (2015) and Israel (2014), metaWEPP identified three distinct haplotype clusters (Fig. 5C). Two of these clusters correspond to clades previously reported in U.S. isolates [75], namely Clade_4 (58.9%; Middle Eastern origin but also found in North America) and Clade_7 (33.4%; predominantly North America). The third cluster (7.7%) was placed in an undesignated clade (Fig. 5C). To our knowledge, this is the first time a clade- and haplotype-level analysis has been performed for ToBRFV on a wastewater sample.

Together, these results demonstrate metaWEPP’s capability to achieve high-resolution tracking of viral variants from untargeted metagenomic wastewater data, enhancing real-time surveillance of both human and plant pathogens.

Discussion

Metagenomics is becoming central to clinical diagnostics and public-health surveillance. In this paper, we presented metaWEPP, a novel pipeline that, by enabling haplotype-level characterization, addresses a long-standing gap in the analysis of metagenomic and targeted mixed-genome samples, leading to new applications and insights. For example, cfDNA-based point-of-care diagnostic tests for infections such as sepsis and urinary tract infections are rapidly emerging in clinical settings [5, 76]. In this context, metaWEPP’s increased resolution could support more targeted therapy by identifying pathogen mutations that are known to be linked to antiviral or antibiotic resistance [77, 78]. metaWEPP has also been demonstrated to uncover precise haplotypes of the same pathogen co-infecting a patient, even in cases where metagenomic assemblers produce highly fragmented assemblies [35, 79], making it a powerful tool for studying intra-host diversity and epidemiological research. Metagenomic wastewater sequencing is another fast-growing application [8, 10], where we demonstrated that metaWEPP’s increased resolution supports fine-grained tracking not only of human pathogens but also of plant pathogens such as ToBRFV, which lack the clinical surveillance infrastructure available for human pathogens. Similarly, metaWEPP can be applied to other applications where sufficiently comprehensive reference genomes are available, such as analyzing environmental DNA to detect plant viruses in agricultural environments [80, 81, 82], analyzing air and surface samples for pathogens [83, 84, 85], and analyzing stool metagenomic samples to identify pathogens and their strains associated with infections [86, 87]. Like WEPP, metaWEPP remains sensitive even at low abundances as long as the sequencing depth is sufficient, identifying the correct haplotypes for pathogens even at 0.16% abundance in our simulated datasets (Supplementary Fig. S4).

In terms of limitations, while metaWEPP can be used to identify novel variants of known species, it is not designed to detect entirely new species that could be present in metagenomic samples or species whose reference genomes are not included in the underlying database. This capability could be added by performing metagenomic assembly of unmapped and unclassified reads—an extension we plan to explore in future versions. Incorporating such functionality would align metaWEPP with emerging priorities in metagenomic biosurveillance, including early detection of naturally occurring and engineered biological threats [88, 89]. Additionally, our validations of metaWEPP are primarily based on samples containing viruses. This is a limitation of the MAT-building tool, viral_usher, which only supports viral genomes currently, and not an inherent limitation of metaWEPP per se. To substantiate this, we simulated a metagenomic sample containing Mycobacterium tuberculosis and successfully analyzed it using its publicly available MAT [90] and achieved accurate results similar to those with viral samples (Supplementary Fig. S4). Ongoing work aims to extend viral_usher to support automatic MAT generation for bacterial species as well. Another limitation is that accurate haplotype detection from metagenomic samples using metaWEPP requires sufficient sequencing depth and coverage of the target species; however, this constraint is also shared by existing MAG-based methods [29–32, 91]. Currently, metaWEPP is optimized for metagenomic applications involving tens of haplotypes belonging to species sequenced at high depths and genome coverage, and may not be practical for more complex datasets, such as gut or soil microbiomes, which could comprise hundreds to thousands of known and unknown species with low per-species read count [92, 93]. However, metaWEPP’s haplotype-level resolution remains useful for analysis of specific species following targeted resequencing of these species from such complex datasets (e.g. focusing on a particular pathogen found in the gut). Extending metaWEPP to untargeted complex microbiome data would necessitate further algorithmic advances, particularly for the discovery of novel species and robust haplotype resolution under lower sequencing depths and coverage. Lastly, we observe that the sensitivity of taxonomic classifiers is reduced when sequencing reads originate from haplotypes that are divergent from the reference sequences present in the classifier’s database. In such cases, a substantial fraction of reads may remain unclassified. This limitation can be mitigated by incorporating multiple representative genomes per species into the database to improve sensitivity. In future work, we plan to incorporate this approach to automatically include all divergent sequences for user-specified pathogen species, thereby enhancing taxonomic classification sensitivity.

metaWEPP is parallelized for multi-core environments and scales well, capable of processing metagenomic samples in minutes to hours (Supplementary Fig. S3 and Supplementary Table S3). Future extensions could involve accelerating WEPP using GPUs, which is the most computationally intensive step in metaWEPP. metaWEPP could also be expanded to support PanMATs [94]—a pangenomic extension of MATs that captures a more complete view of species-level genetic variation. Additionally, the metaWEPP pipeline is modular and can be easily configured to use alternative taxonomic classifiers beyond Kraken2 [20, 95], which could further enhance its capabilities. Nonetheless, for the cfDNA-based point-of-care diagnostic and metagenomic wastewater applications analyzed in this study, the current implementation performs robustly. Even in its present form, metaWEPP delivers substantial benefits over existing approaches, providing fast, actionable, and deeper insights for a wide range of clinical, epidemiological, and research applications.
