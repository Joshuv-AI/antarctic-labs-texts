Association between blood urea nitrogen-to-albumin ratio and mortality among atrial fibrillation patients admitted to the intensive care unit
Authors: Chuannan Wu, Yuejiao Lan, Jingsong Liu, Mingda Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate the association between the blood urea nitrogen-to-albumin ratio (BAR) and both short- and long-term all-cause mortality in critically ill patients with atrial fibrillation (AF).MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted using the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV) database, including 4,697 critically ill AF patients. Participants were stratified into tertiles based on their BAR levels (Q1: <6.36, Q2: 6.36–12.43, Q3:>12.43). The primary outcomes were 30-day and 365-day all-cause mortality. Kaplan-Meier survival curves, multivariable Cox proportional hazards models, and restricted cubic spline (RCS) analyses were employed to assess the association. A two-piecewise linear model was used to identify the threshold effect. Subgroup analyses were performed to evaluate the consistency of the association.ResultsPatients in higher BAR tertiles had significantly higher mortality rates at all time points (P < 0.001). After full adjustment for demographics, comorbidities, laboratory parameters, and treatment measures, the highest BAR tertile (Q3) was independently associated with increased risks of 30-day (hazard ratio [HR] = 2.34, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.94–2.82) and 365-day mortality (HR = 2.04, 95% CI: 1.78–2.35), compared to the lowest tertile (Q1). RCS analysis revealed a nonlinear, L-shaped relationship between BAR and mortality. A threshold effect was identified, with inflection points at BAR levels of 11.52 for 30-day mortality and 14.41 for 365-day mortality. The association remained robust across all predefined subgroups.ConclusionIn intensive care unit (ICU) patients with AF, elevated BAR levels independently predicted increased 30-day and 365-day all-cause mortality risks. Introduction

Atrial fibrillation (AF), one of the most prevalent cardiac arrhythmias globally, poses a growing public health burden and is associated with substantial mortality risk [1]. The prevalence of this disease is growing amid worldwide population aging [2, 3]. Global epidemiological data show that approximately 330 million people had AF in 2021. Prevalence increases markedly with age, affecting 10–12% of octogenarians [4, 5]. AF increases all-cause mortality 1.5- to 3.5-fold and elevates risks of stroke and heart failure, consequently generating substantial healthcare costs and socioeconomic burdens [4, 6]. Notably, approximately 14% of critically ill patients in intensive care units (ICUs) present with comorbid AF, a population characterized by clinical complexity and poor prognoses [7]. The pathophysiology features a cascade of systemic inflammation, metabolic dysregulation, and multi-organ dysfunction.

Blood urea nitrogen (BUN) indicates renal function and protein metabolism. Its pathophysiological significance extends beyond renal impairment. Elevated BUN promotes myocardial fibrosis and inflammation via uremic toxin accumulation (e.g., indoxyl sulfate) [8]. Albumin, the predominant plasma protein responsible for maintaining colloid osmotic pressure and exerting antioxidant/anti-inflammatory effects, plays a critical role in cardiac electrophysiological stability [9]. Reduced albumin levels have been demonstrated to correlate significantly with impaired atrial electrical activity [10, 11]. A meta-analysis further revealed that every 10 g/L increment in serum albumin corresponds to a 36% reduction in AF risk [12]. Building upon these pathophysiological linkages, the urea nitrogen-to-albumin ratio (BAR), a novel composite biomarker, demonstrates unique clinical potential. By integrating multidimensional pathophysiological information encompassing renal function, nutritional status, and inflammatory activity, BAR has demonstrated robust mortality predictive capacity across diverse conditions including sepsis, pneumonia, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, acute kidney injury and chronic heart failure [13–17]. However, BAR’s prognostic value for AF-related mortality remains unestablished. Accurate risk stratification to identify high-risk individuals remains a critical challenge in the clinical management of AF [18]. This study aims to investigate the association between BAR and all-cause mortality in critically ill AF patients, thereby addressing this knowledge gap and providing evidence-based support for risk stratification and personalized therapeutic strategies.

Methods

Source of data

We conducted this retrospective analysis using data from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV, v3.1), a publicly accessible critical care database. As a validated repository, MIMIC-IV contains de-identified clinical records of over 250,000 ICU admissions from 2008 to 2019 at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (Boston, USA). The database integrates comprehensive electronic health record (EHR) data encompassing demographics, vital signs, laboratory tests, medication administration, and clinical outcomes, with stringent validation protocols to ensure data accuracy and consistency. To access the database, all authors completed the National Institutes of Health (NIH) training course on the protection of human research participants and passed the Collaborative Institutional Training Initiative (CITI) program assessment. The study protocol received exemption from informed consent approval by the institutional review board (IRB) of the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, as the database exclusively contains anonymized data without protected health information.

Study design and population

From an initial cohort of 40,284 adult patients (aged ≥ 18 years) undergoing first intensive care unit (ICU) admission with ICU stays longer than 24 h, 12,137 cases of AF were identified based on diagnostic codes from the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision (ICD-9: 427.31) and Tenth Revision (ICD-10: I48, I48.0, I48.1, I48.2, I48.9, I48.11, I48.19, I48.20, I48.21, I48.91) [19]. We could not differentiate between new-onset and pre-existing AF due to the constraints of the ICD coding system in the MIMIC-IV database. Patients without recorded BUN or serum albumin measurements were excluded from subsequent analyses. The final analytical cohort comprised 4697 patients stratified into tertiles (Q1-Q3) based on BAR values obtained during ICU admission (Fig. 1).

Data extraction, definitions and study endpoint

We conducted a retrospective cohort study using the MIMIC-IV database, which comprises de-identified EHR from ICU patients at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center. Data extraction was conducted using Navicat Premium (version 16.1.12) with SQL integration to retrieve demographic details, comorbidities, vital signs, laboratory parameters, severity scores, treatment measures, and clinical outcomes. The extracted variables were meticulously selected to align with the subsequent analyses and are comprehensively listed in Table 1. Specifically, these included: Demographics: age, sex, race, and weight; Comorbidities: hypertension, diabetes, myocardial infarction, congestive heart failure, chronic pulmonary disease, dementia, stroke, severe liver disease, renal disease, sepsis, and rheumatic disease; Vital Signs: systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), mean arterial pressure (MAP), heart rate (HR), respiratory rate, and peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO₂); Laboratory Parameters: blood urea nitrogen (BUN), albumin, red blood cell (RBC) count, white blood cell (WBC) count, platelet count, calcium, sodium, chloride, potassium, creatinine, glucose, prothrombin time (PT), gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), hemoglobin, high density lipoprotein (HDL), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), triglycerides, total cholesterol, procalcitonin (PCT) and C-eactive Protein (CRP); Severity Scores & Treatments: Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score, Simplified Acute Physiology Score II (SAPS II), continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT), mechanical ventilation (MV), and medications (beta-blockers, digoxin, calcium channel blockers (CCBs), amiodarone, anticoagulants, statins, clopidogrel, and aspirin); Outcomes: hospital and ICU length of stay (LOS), and all-cause mortality at 30, 90, 180, and 365 days. All laboratory parameters and vital signs were defined as the first value recorded within 24 h of ICU admission. Mortality data were obtained from linked Social Security Administration death records. Variables with a missing rate exceeding 20% including hemoglobin, HDL, LDL, triglycerides, total cholesterol, GGT, ALT, ALP, AST, PCT and CRP, were excluded from the multivariable analysis. For the remaining variables with less than 20% missing data, multiple imputations by chained equations (MICE) were performed, assuming data were missing at random. The urea nitrogen-to-albumin ratio (BAR, mg/g) was calculated as the ratio of initial BUN (mg/dL) to serum albumin level (g/dL). The primary endpoints of this study were 30-day and 365-day all-cause mortality.

Statistical analysis

Patients were categorized into tertiles according to their BAR levels to facilitate baseline comparisons. The distribution of continuous variables was evaluated for normality using appropriate statistical tests. Non-normally distributed variables were analyzed using the Kruskal-Wallis H test and presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs). Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, and expressed as counts (percentages). The association between BAR tertiles and all-cause mortality was assessed using Kaplan-Meier curves and multivariable Cox proportional hazards models. We constructed three hierarchical models: an unadjusted model; Model 1, adjusted for sex, age, race, and body weight; and Model 2, which further incorporated adjustments for comorbidities (stroke, congestive heart failure, severe liver disease, renal disease, sepsis, myocardial infarct, dementia, chronic pulmonary disease, rheumatic disease, hypertension, diabetes), laboratory parameters (PT, platelets, chloride, potassium, RBC, WBC, creatinine), treatment measures (CRRT, MV, beta-blockers, statin, amiodarone, digoxin, CCB, clopidogrel, anticoagulant drugs), and the SOFA score. The lowest BAR tertile served as the reference group. The proportional hazards assumption for the Cox models was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals (Supplementary Table 4). Variables with significant time-dependent effects were further analyzed using time-dependent Cox regression models with time stratification to account for non-proportional hazards (Supplementary Table 5). Multicollinearity among covariates in the final Cox model was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), with all values below 2.0, indicating the absence of significant collinearity (Supplementary Table 1). To evaluate the potential impact of competing risks, we performed a sensitivity analysis using competing risk regression models, considering non-mortality discharge and non-cardiovascular death as competing events (Supplementary Table 2). Additionally, the independent associations of the individual BAR components (BUN and albumin) with mortality were examined using Cox models, with these variables analyzed in tertiles (Supplementary Table 3). Restricted cubic splines (RCS) were applied to model BAR as a continuous variable, enabling the assessment of potential nonlinear dose-response relationships. Threshold effect analyses were conducted to identify inflection points in cases of significant non-linearity. Subgroup analyses were conducted to evaluate the consistency of the association between BAR (as a continuous variable) and mortality across various pre-specified strata, including gender, age (< 65 vs. ≥65 years), race (White vs. Non-White), SOFA score (≤ 4 vs. >4), and multiple comorbidities and treatment measures. Interaction P-values were calculated for each subgroup. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 27.0 (IBM Corp., USA) and R version 4.4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Austria). A two-tailed P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

From the MIMIC-IV database, a total of 4,697 adult patients with AF were ultimately included in the study. (Fig. 1). The study population had a median age of 75.62 years, and 57.59% were male. Baseline characteristics were analyzed by BAR tertiles at ICU admission (Q1: <6.36, Q2: 6.36–12.43, Q3: >12.43) and are summarized in Table 1. Compared to the Q1 group, patients in the Q3 group were older, had a higher proportion of males, and had a higher prevalence of comorbidities such as hypertension, myocardial infarction, congestive heart failure, chronic pulmonary disease, diabetes, severe liver disease, renal disease, and sepsis. Conversely, the prevalence of stroke was lower in the Q3 group. Regarding laboratory parameters, compared with Q1, Q3 patients showed significantly elevated WBC count, potassium, creatinine, glucose, PT, GGT, ALT, ALP, AST, and SOFA and SAPS II scores, as well as prolonged hospital and ICU LOS. Conversely, the Q3 group had lower levels of RBC count, platelet count, calcium, sodium, chloride, systolic and diastolic blood pressure, and MAP. In terms of treatment measures, the utilization rates of continuous CRRT and MV were significantly higher in the Q3 group. Conversely, the usage rates of beta-blockers, CCB, statins, and aspirin were significantly higher in the lower BAR tertiles (Q1). Statistically significant differences in both BUN and albumin levels were observed across the BAR tertiles (P < 0.001). Median BUN concentrations progressively increased across tertiles: 15.00 mg/dL (12.00–18.00) in Q1, 26.00 mg/dL (22.00–31.00) in Q2, and 54.00 mg/dL (43.00–72.00) in Q3. Conversely, median albumin levels decreased across tertiles from 3.30 g/dL (3.00–3.70) in Q1 to 2.80 g/dL (2.40–3.20) in Q3（Table 1）.

All-cause mortality in different groups

Among the 4,697 included patients, 1,180 (25.12%) died within 30 days and 2,126 (45.26%) within 365 days (Table 1). Kaplan-Meier curves revealed significant differences in 30-day and 365-day mortality rates across BAR tertiles (P < 0.001). Patients in the highest BAR tertile showed significantly lower survival rates at both 30-day and 365-day follow-ups compared to the lowest tertile (Fig. 2A, B).

Association between BAR Tertiles and Mortality

Multivariate Cox proportional hazards models were utilized to evaluate the independent prognostic value of the BAR index for predicting 30-day and 365-day all-cause mortality in critically ill AF patients (Table 2). A statistically significant, dose-dependent increase in mortality risk was observed across ascending BAR tertiles. In the unadjusted model, progressively higher hazard ratios (HRs) for 30-day mortality were observed: Q2 (HR = 1.71, 95% CI: 1.45–2.01, P < 0.001) and Q3 (HR = 2.76, 95% CI: 2.37–3.21, P < 0.001). Correspondingly, elevated risks for 365-day all-cause mortality were observed in Q2 (HR = 1.57, 95% CI: 1.40–1.77, P < 0.001) and Q3 (HR = 2.54, 95% CI: 2.27–2.83, P < 0.001).

After adjustment for demographics (Model 1) and further comprehensive adjustment for comorbidities, laboratory parameters, treatment measures, and SOFA score (Model 2), the effect sizes were attenuated but retained strong statistical significance. In the fully adjusted model (Model 2), the highest BAR tertile (Q3) remained independently associated with significantly increased risks of 30-day (HR = 2.34, 95% CI: 1.94–2.82, P < 0.001) and 365-day all-cause mortality (HR = 2.04, 95% CI: 1.78–2.35, P < 0.001). Consistently, the competing risk analysis, accounting for the potential influence of competing events, confirmed the significant association between higher BAR levels and increased mortality risks at both 30 days and 365 days (Supplementary Table 2).

To evaluate the individual contributions of the components constituting the BAR, separate Cox proportional hazards models were constructed for blood urea nitrogen (BUN) and serum albumin (Supplementary Table 3). When analyzed as tertiles, both higher BUN and lower albumin levels were independently and significantly associated with increased risks of 30-day and 365-day all-cause mortality across unadjusted, minimally adjusted (Model 1), and fully adjusted (Model 2) models. For 365-day mortality in the fully adjusted model, the highest BUN tertile (Q3) was associated with a HR of 1.86 (95% CI: 1.62–2.13, P < 0.001), while the lowest albumin tertile (Q1) was associated with an HR of 1.56 (95% CI: 1.32–1.85, P < 0.001), using the highest albumin tertile as the reference. The effect sizes for both components, while statistically significant, were notably lower than those observed for the combined BAR index (Q3 HR = 2.04) in the corresponding model (Table 2). This comparative analysis underscores that the integration of both renal function (via BUN) and nutritional status/inflammation (via albumin) into the single BAR index provides a more powerful prognostic tool than either marker alone in predicting mortality among critically ill AF patients. To further validate the robustness of our findings, we assessed the proportional hazards assumption using Schoenfeld residuals. The BAR index itself satisfied the proportional hazards assumption for both 30-day and 365-day mortality (P > 0.05), although some covariates (e.g., race, anticoagulant drugs, beta-blockers) showed evidence of time-varying effects (Supplementary Table 4). We therefore conducted a time-stratified Cox regression analysis, which confirmed that the association between BAR tertiles and mortality remained significant and consistent across time strata, with no significant interaction between BAR categories and time periods (Supplementary Table 5). These results reinforce the stability of BAR as a prognostic indicator over time.

The detection of nonlinear relationships

RCS analysis revealed a nonlinear L-shaped association between the BAR index and both 30-day and 365-day all-cause mortality (P for non-linearity < 0.001; Fig. 3A, B). The threshold effect analysis revealed a nonlinear relationship between the BAR and all-cause mortality (Table 3). When modeled as a continuous variable using a standard linear Cox model, each 1-unit increase in BAR was associated with a 4% and 5% higher risk of 30-day (HR = 1.04, 95% CI: 1.03–1.05) and 365-day mortality (HR = 1.05, 95% CI: 1.04–1.06), respectively (both P < 0.001). However, a two-piecewise linear model provided a significantly better fit (Log-likelihood ratio P < 0.001), identifying inflection points at BAR values of 11.52 for 30-day mortality and 14.41 for 365-day mortality. Below these inflection points, the mortality risk increased sharply with each unit increase in BAR: for 30-day mortality below BAR 11.52, the HR was 1.16 (95% CI: 1.11–1.20, P < 0.001), and for 365-day mortality below BAR 14.41, the HR was 1.12 (95% CI: 1.09–1.14, P < 0.001). In contrast, above these inflection points, the association was markedly attenuated, with HRs of 1.01 (95% CI: 1.00-1.02, P = 0.006) for 30-day mortality and 1.02 (95% CI: 1.00-1.03, P = 0.009) for 365-day mortality.

For 365-day mortality, the strength of the association varied significantly by the presence of congestive heart failure (P for interaction = 0.025), hypertension (P for interaction = 0.037), renal disease (P for interaction = 0.003), myocardial infarct (P for interaction < 0.001), dementia (P for interaction < 0.001), and specific treatments including statin use (P for interaction = 0.002) and amiodarone use (P for interaction = 0.027). Discussion

Our findings establish a significant independent association between elevated BAR and increased all-cause mortality in AF patients at both 30-day and 365-day intervals. This robust association persisted after comprehensive adjustment for potential confounding variables. The observed L-shaped relationship between BAR levels and mortality risk indicates that critically ill AF patients with elevated BAR values face significantly elevated mortality. These findings highlight a potential association between BAR and mortality risk, which may support further investigation into its utility for risk stratification in critically ill AF patients.

Accumulating evidence suggests that dysregulated BUN metabolism is involved in the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying AF. Lan et al. reported a significant association between elevated BUN levels and the incidence of AF in elderly female populations [20]. Similarly, Akgul et al. observed significantly elevated BUN concentrations in patients who developed AF following coronary artery bypass grafting, compared with those who did not, suggesting that renal impairment (RI) may predispose individuals to AF through disruptions in BUN metabolism [21]. Based on existing evidence, it is hypothesized that elevated BUN is associated with several pathophysiological processes that may influence AF development. First, neuroendocrine activation and cardiorenal crosstalk may play crucial roles in mediating this relationship. Elevated BUN concentrations may indicate systemic overactivation of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) and the sympathetic nervous system (SNS) [22, 23]. Activation of the RAAS promotes atrial electrical instability by inducing myocardial fibrosis, remodeling of ion channels, and initiating pro-inflammatory responses. Concurrently, elevated neurohormonal activity exacerbates oxidative stress and endothelial dysfunction, thereby further compromising cardiac electrophysiological homeostasis [24, 25]. Notably, AF may exacerbate renal dysfunction by reducing cardiac output and activating the RAAS, thereby contributing to a detrimental cardiorenal feedback loop [26, 27]. Second, RI-associated elevations in BUN are frequently accompanied by electrolyte imbalances (e.g., hyperkalemia, hypocalcemia), which may directly alter the duration of cardiomyocyte action potentials and increase the propensity for reentrant arrhythmias. Additionally, the accumulation of uremic toxins (e.g., indoxyl sulfate, p-cresyl sulfate) has been shown to induce cardiomyocyte apoptosis, promote interstitial fibrosis, and accelerate atrial structural remodeling through the activation of inflammatory mediators [21]. Third, elevated BUN levels are frequently associated with dehydration, which may result in hemoconcentration, prerenal azotemia, and impaired perfusion of vital organs. Previous studies have indicated that cerebral hypoperfusion may initiate AF by disrupting autonomic regulation, whereas myocardial hypoperfusion contributes to increased oxidative stress and electrical instability [28, 29]. Finally, clinical investigations have established a positive association between elevated BUN levels and systemic immune-inflammation index (SII) [30]. Inflammatory processes have been shown to increase the risk of AF onset by promoting atrial structural remodeling (e.g., fibrosis) and inducing electrophysiological abnormalities, such as impaired calcium homeostasis and connexin dysfunction [31].

Serum albumin, an essential plasma protein, plays a pivotal role in maintaining colloid osmotic pressure, facilitating the transport of various endogenous and exogenous substances, and contributing to nutritional metabolism. Accumulating evidence indicates that serum albumin levels are significantly reduced in patients with AF, and epidemiological studies have identified an independent inverse association between serum albumin concentrations and AF incidence, suggesting a potential protective role of higher albumin levels in preventing AF development [32, 33]. The antiarrhythmic effects of albumin may be attributed to its regulatory influence on inflammatory signaling pathways, oxidative stress balance, metabolic homeostasis, and endothelial function. First, albumin exhibits anti-inflammatory effects by inhibiting tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α)-mediated inflammatory cascades and reducing monocyte adhesion. Hypoalbuminemia may exacerbate systemic inflammation, thereby contributing to atrial fibrotic remodeling [34, 35]. Second, albumin mitigates myocardial oxidative injury through its free radical scavenging capacity mediated by sulfhydryl groups and its role in nitric oxide (NO) transport. Albumin deficiency may predispose individuals to abnormalities in calcium handling and the development of proarrhythmic electrical substrates [36]. Third, the positive correlations between serum albumin and lipid parameters such as total cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol underscore its role in maintaining lipid homeostasis. Dyslipidemia secondary to hypoalbuminemia may indirectly increase the risk of AF through mechanisms involving metabolic dysregulation [37, 38]. Finally, decreased serum albumin levels have been associated with exacerbated endothelial dysfunction, which may promote atrial electrical remodeling by impairing NO bioavailability [39]. Accumulating evidence supports the prognostic role of serum albumin in cardiovascular diseases, including stroke and atrial fibrillation. A recent large-scale propensity-matched analysis by Bucci et al. demonstrated that hypoalbuminemia (albumin < 3.4 g/dL) measured within 24 h after ischemic stroke was independently associated with a significantly higher risk of early cardiovascular complications and all-cause mortality at 30 days (HR 1.48 for composite outcome; HR 2.77 for all-cause death) [40]. This study, involving over 500,000 stroke patients, underscores that albumin levels reflect systemic vulnerability to adverse cardiovascular events, likely mediated by its anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and antithrombotic properties. Our findings are consistent with this external evidence and further extend the prognostic relevance of albumin to critically ill AF patients in the ICU setting. The integration of albumin with blood urea nitrogen in the BAR index thus captures both nutritional-inflammatory status and renal metabolic stress, providing a composite biomarker that enhances risk stratification across different acute cardiovascular states.

The BAR, a novel composite index that integrates two key biomarkers—blood urea nitrogen, indicative of renal function, and albumin, reflecting nutritional and inflammatory status—has emerged as a potentially valuable surrogate marker for risk stratification in critically ill patients with AF. It is important to note that the observed association between BAR and mortality does not imply a direct causal relationship. In the context of critically ill AF patients, BAR likely serves as a composite surrogate marker reflecting the interplay of multiple pathophysiological processes, including renal dysfunction, systemic inflammation, nutritional depletion, and fluid imbalance. These factors collectively contribute to the overall illness severity and comorbidity burden, which are well-established drivers of adverse outcomes in AF and other critical care settings. For instance, Rigutini et al. highlighted the prognostic importance of comorbidity complexity in AF patients [18], while Bucci et al. demonstrated the independent prognostic value of hypoalbuminemia in acute stroke [40]. Thus, while BAR provides a readily accessible and integrative prognostic tool, its elevation should be interpreted as an indicator of underlying physiological dysregulation rather than a direct mediator of mortality.

The effective management of critically ill patients in ICUs remains a significant clinical challenge, highlighting the need for easily obtainable admission parameters, such as BAR, to facilitate early identification of high-risk individuals, reduce mortality, and improve prognostic outcomes. Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design based on the MIMIC-IV database precludes the ability to infer a causal relationship between the BUN/albumin ratio and mortality, despite comprehensive multivariable adjustments and subgroup analyses to account for potential confounding factors. Second, the lack of detailed AF classification (e.g., paroxysmal or persistent) and disease trajectory information in the MIMIC-IV database may limit the precision of patient stratification. Third, although the sample size in our cohort was moderate, validation of these findings necessitates larger-scale prospective investigations. Fourth, our study is limited by the lack of detailed information on AF classification. Specifically, we could not differentiate between new-onset and pre-existing AF due to the constraints of the ICD coding system in the MIMIC-IV database. These two entities may have different underlying pathophysiologies and prognostic implications. Our findings should therefore be interpreted as applicable to a general ICU population with AF, and future studies are warranted to investigate whether the prognostic value of BAR differs between new-onset and pre-existing AF. Importantly, the underlying biological mechanisms that explain the association between BAR and mortality in critically ill AF patients remain insufficiently understood. Furthermore, the absence of precise data on AF onset timing and cause-specific mortality constrains the clinical interpretability of our findings. To address these limitations, future research should prioritize well-designed prospective multicenter studies and randomized controlled trials to confirm and expand upon the present findings.

Conclusions

This study identified the BAR as a potential predictor of 30-day and 365-day all-cause mortality in critically ill patients with AF. Furthermore, a non-linear L-shaped association was observed between BAR levels and the risk of all-cause mortality, indicating a threshold effect.

These findings suggest that BAR may serve as a potential prognostic indicator for risk stratification in critically ill AF patients, though further validation is needed before clinical application.
