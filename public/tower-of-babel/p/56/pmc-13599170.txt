Thermoforming time variability of PET-G and TPU aligner sheets across consecutive thermoforming cycles: an in vitro study

Background

Clear aligner (CA) therapy has become an established modality in contemporary orthodontic treatment due to its aesthetic advantages and patient acceptance [1, 2]. Most thermoformed aligners are fabricated by heating thermoplastic sheets and pressure-forming them over three-dimensional printed dental models [3, 4]. During this process, heating duration directly influences polymer softening, material flow, thickness distribution, and adaptation accuracy [5–7].

Thermoforming behavior depends on multiple interacting factors, including polymer composition, sheet thickness, heating temperature, environmental conditions, and thermoforming-unit characteristics. Manufacturers provide recommended heating durations; however, these values are typically derived under standardized or idealized testing conditions. In routine laboratory workflows, thermoforming units are frequently operated in consecutive cycles, potentially resulting in cumulative heat retention within the heating chamber. Such heat accumulation may alter the actual heating duration required to achieve consistent material softening.

Most commercially available thermoformed aligner sheets are primarily based on either polyethylene terephthalate glycol (PET-G)–based multilayer composites or single-layer thermoplastic polyurethane (TPU) [8]. Multilayer PET-G/TPU systems typically consist of rigid outer layers and a more elastic core, whereas single-layer TPU sheets exhibit more homogeneous viscoelastic properties [9]. Differences in polymer architecture and thermal conductivity may influence heat diffusion dynamics and stabilization behavior during repeated thermoforming cycles.

Previous investigations of aligner materials have predominantly focused on thickness reduction, force transmission, or thermomechanical aging [5, 10–12]. However, the dynamic variation of heating duration across consecutive thermoforming cycles has not been systematically quantified. Specifically, limited data exist regarding whether heating time decreases predictably during early cycles, when thermal stabilization occurs, and whether stabilization patterns differ between distinct polymer systems.

Therefore, the aim of this in vitro study was to quantify heating-time variation across consecutive thermoforming cycles and to compare stabilization behavior between a multilayer PET-G/TPU composite material and a single-layer TPU material under controlled laboratory conditions. This study was designed to characterize thermoforming-time variability rather than to evaluate mechanical performance or clinical superiority. Consequently, the experimental design was not intended to replicate ISO or YY/T 1819 mechanical testing standards for orthodontic thermoplastic sheets, as the objective was limited to evaluating heating-time dynamics during thermoforming.

Materials and methods

Study design and experimental setting

This in vitro experimental study was conducted to evaluate variation in heating duration across consecutive thermoforming cycles in two thermoplastic aligner materials under standardized laboratory conditions. The primary outcome variable was heating time (seconds) required to reach a predefined sag depth during thermoforming.

All experiments were performed in a temperature-controlled laboratory environment. Ambient temperature was maintained at 23 ± 1 °C and relative humidity at 50 ± 5%, continuously monitored throughout the experimental period.

A “cold start” condition was operationally defined as a heating-block surface temperature of ≤ 26 ± 1 °C, measured using a calibrated non-contact infrared thermometer prior to initiation of each session.

This study focused on thermoforming-time variability rather than standardized mechanical testing of orthodontic thermoplastic materials.

Thermoforming equipment and setup

All thermoforming procedures were performed using a Drufomat Scan pressure-forming unit (Dreve, Germany) under the standard “F1” program.

The preset program specifies a maximum heating time of 4 min as a safety limit. In this study, heating was manually terminated upon reaching the predefined sag depth; therefore, the recorded heating time represents the actual duration required to achieve the standardized endpoint rather than the preset program limit.

Standardized three-dimensional printed dental models were used as molds for thermoforming. Models were centrally positioned on the platform with the occlusal plane parallel to the heating chamber to ensure consistent deformation geometry.

Internal pilot assessment and justification of the 16-mm sag depth

The selection of the 16-mm sag depth was based on both manufacturer recommendations and internal pilot observations conducted prior to formal data collection.

Manufacturer instructions indicate a recommended sag range of 12 mm to 20 mm for thermoforming [14]. To validate this range under the present experimental conditions, preliminary pilot testing was performed using both materials across varying sag depths (12 mm, 14 mm, 16 mm, 18 mm, and 20 mm). Preliminary pilot assessment included ten repeated thermoforming trials at each evaluated sag-depth condition (12 mm, 14 mm, 16 mm, 18 mm, and 20 mm) for both materials. Adaptation quality and visible material overextension were qualitatively assessed by the same trained operator immediately after thermoforming.

Pilot observations revealed:

Sag depths < 14 mm were associated with incomplete adaptation of the thermoplastic sheet to the cervical margins of the 3D model.

Sag depths > 18 mm produced visible thinning and overextension of the material during pressure forming.

Sag depths within 14–18 mm demonstrated consistent adaptation without observable over-thinning.

Based on these observations, 16 mm was selected as the midpoint within the recommended range to standardize material softening while minimizing the risks of under- or over-heating.

All sag measurements were performed by a single trained operator to reduce procedural variability.

Experimental procedure

For each material, ten independent experimental sessions were conducted. Each session consisted of 20 consecutive thermoforming cycles performed without interruption, beginning from a defined cold-start condition.

For each cycle:

A new thermoplastic sheet was placed and clamped. Heating was initiated and manually terminated when sag depth reached 16 mm. Heating duration (seconds) was recorded using a digital stopwatch (accuracy ± 1 s). Pressure forming was then performed according to the manufacturer’s standard protocol. A cooling period of 1 min was observed before initiating the next cycle.

After completion of 20 consecutive cycles, the machine was allowed to cool until the heating-block surface temperature returned to ≤ 26 ± 1 °C before beginning the subsequent session. A minimum interval of 60 min was maintained between sessions.

In total, 200 heating-time measurements were obtained per material.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using JASP software (version 0.19.3).

For each material, heating-time variation across cycles was evaluated using a linear mixed-effects model. Cycle number was treated as a fixed effect, and session was included as a random intercept to account for inter-session variability.

Bonferroni-adjusted post hoc comparisons were performed to identify pairwise differences between cycles.

Thermal stabilization was operationally defined as the first cycle after which five consecutive cycles demonstrated no statistically significant differences (Bonferroni-adjusted p > 0.05).

Simple linear regression models were applied to the pre-stabilization phase to quantify the rate of heating-time reduction per cycle. Regression equations were reported in the Results section. Model fit was evaluated using R² values. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Variation in heating time of the two thermoformed aligner materials

For the ZF material, heating time exhibited a clear downward trend over consecutive thermoforming cycles. The recorded heating duration ranged from 59.8 to 112.2 s across all sessions (Fig. 4). Dots represent mean values, and the gray-shaded area represents the 95% confidence interval. Mixed-effects model analysis revealed a significant decrease in heating time between the initial cycles (cycles 1–5), followed by a gradual stabilization phase. This point, indicated by the red dashed line in Fig. 4, was therefore defined as the steady-state phase for ZF. During this steady phase (cycles 6–20), heating time maintained a consistent mean of 64.59 ± 3.58 s, reflecting stabilized heating-time behavior under repeated operation.

Heating-time variation across consecutive thermoforming cycles for Zendura FLX

Specifically, the first thermoforming instance, performed from a cold start, required an average of 112.2 s, which progressively decreased to 73.8 s by the fifth cycle. Bonferroni-adjusted post-hoc comparisons confirmed statistically significant differences between each of the first five cycles (p < 0.001), indicating rapid heat accumulation and machine warm-up during early operation. From the sixth cycle onward, changes in heating duration were no longer statistically significant (p > 0.05), demonstrating the onset of a stable thermal equilibrium between the thermoforming unit and the material (Supplementary Table S1).

For the MF material, heating time varied between 69.4 and 112.9 s across all thermoforming sessions (Fig. 5). Dots represent mean values, and the gray-shaded area represents the 95% confidence interval. According to the mixed-effects model analysis, the heating process for MF was slower and more complex compared to ZF.

Heating-time variation across consecutive thermoforming cycles for Maxflex

An early indication of stabilization appeared between cycle 8 and cycle 9, where the difference in heating time was not statistically significant (p > 0.05). However, subsequent cycles again showed significant reductions, suggesting that thermal equilibrium had not yet been fully achieved. Based on the mixed-effects model with Bonferroni post-hoc comparisons, true stabilization of heating time was reached only from the 12th cycle onward. The red dashed line in Fig. 5 indicates the onset of thermal stabilization. From cycle 12 through cycle 20, no statistically significant differences were observed (p = 1.000), confirming that the machine–material system had achieved a steady-state thermal condition (Supplementary Table S2).

Comparison of heating time between the two thermoplastic materials

Heating time for both materials decreased rapidly during the first 5–7 thermoforming cycles, after which material-specific stabilization patterns became evident. Across all 20 cycles, ZF consistently exhibited shorter heating times than MF, and its steady-state phase occurred at a lower range (62–67 s for ZF vs. 68–72 s for MF).

Mixed-effects analysis with Bonferroni-adjusted post hoc comparisons identified that thermal stabilization was achieved at the 6th cycle for ZF and at the 12th cycle for MF (Fig. 6). This indicates that ZF reached a steady-state condition earlier than MF.

Discussion

This study investigated variation in thermoforming time across consecutive fabrication cycles for two thermoplastic aligner materials under standardized laboratory conditions. A progressive reduction in heating time was observed for both materials, followed by material-specific stabilization thresholds. Thermal stabilization occurred at cycle 6 for ZF and at cycle 12 for MF. Even after stabilization, heating durations remained substantially greater than the Supplier-Recommended Thermoforming Time (SRTT) for both materials. These findings indicate that thermoforming during repeated aligner fabrication exhibits material-dependent changes in heating-time behavior across consecutive cycles, consistent with progressive thermal accumulation within the thermoforming system. To the authors’ knowledge, no previous orthodontic study has quantitatively investigated heating-time variation across consecutive thermoforming cycles of aligner materials. Most previous investigations have focused on thickness reduction, adaptation accuracy, force transmission, thermomechanical aging, or material-property changes after thermoforming rather than on the thermal behavior of the thermoforming process itself [5, 7, 10, 11, 15, 16]. Consequently, the present study provides novel information regarding the dynamic changes in heating requirements that occur during repeated aligner fabrication and highlights a previously underexplored source of variability within the manufacturing workflow.

Thermoforming has been shown to alter aligner thickness distribution, adaptation accuracy, and force-delivery characteristics, potentially affecting biomechanical consistency during orthodontic treatment [6, 10, 11, 15–17]. The present study extends these previous findings by demonstrating that thermoforming conditions themselves are not thermally static during repeated fabrication cycles, even when the same thermoforming unit, material, and protocol are used under controlled environmental conditions.

Both materials demonstrated a pronounced reduction in heating time during the early thermoforming cycles. This pattern is likely explained by progressive heat accumulation within the thermoforming system as the unit transitions from a cold-start condition to a thermally stabilized operating state. During the first thermoforming cycle, greater energy input is required for the thermoplastic sheet to reach the predefined sag endpoint. As additional cycles are performed, residual heat retained within the heating elements and surrounding chamber components reduces the energy required to achieve equivalent material softening. The stabilization phase observed in the present study therefore likely reflects the establishment of a dynamic thermal equilibrium between the thermoforming unit and the thermoplastic material during continuous operation. Thermoforming engineering literature has emphasized that heating conditions, thermal history, and temperature control are important determinants of material-softening behavior and process consistency during thermoplastic forming operations [18].

Despite identical sheet thickness and standardized thermoforming conditions, the two materials demonstrated different stabilization patterns. ZF reached steady-state heating conditions after six cycles, whereas MF required approximately twelve cycles to achieve thermal equilibrium. These differences may be associated with structural and compositional characteristics of the thermoplastic materials. ZF consists of a multilayer configuration incorporating polyethylene terephthalate glycol (PET-G) outer layers and a thermoplastic polyurethane (TPU) core, whereas MF is a monolayer TPU material. Variations in polymer architecture, heat-transfer behavior, and viscoelastic softening characteristics may therefore influence the rate at which thermal equilibrium is achieved during repeated thermoforming cycles. Previous investigations have reported that different thermoplastic aligner materials exhibit distinct thermal and mechanical behavior because of differences in polymer composition and structural organization [8–10, 19]. These findings suggest that thermoforming protocols should not necessarily be generalized across different thermoplastic systems. Although the present study did not evaluate the properties of the final thermoformed aligners, previous investigations have demonstrated that thermoforming can influence aligner thickness distribution, adaptation accuracy, gap formation, force transmission, and mechanical behavior [5–7, 10, 12, 15, 16]. Because thermal exposure is a fundamental component of the thermoforming process, variability in heating duration during repeated fabrication cycles may represent a previously underrecognized source of manufacturing variability. Nevertheless, the present findings do not establish a direct relationship between heating-time variation and clinical aligner performance, and further studies are required to determine whether the observed thermal differences result in measurable changes in aligner properties.

Another important finding of the present study is the substantial discrepancy between experimentally observed heating durations and manufacturer-recommended thermoforming times. For both materials, heating durations during both transient and steady-state phases were consistently longer than the SRTT values provided by the manufacturers. ZF demonstrated a steady-state heating duration of approximately 64.6 s compared with the recommended 50-second heating time, whereas MF stabilized at approximately 69.4 s, substantially exceeding the recommended range of 20–30 s. These discrepancies may reflect differences in thermoforming equipment, environmental conditions, endpoint definitions, and operational procedures between manufacturer testing environments and the present experimental setting. The discrepancy was particularly evident during the initial thermoforming cycles when the thermoforming unit operated from a cold-start condition and required additional heating time to achieve sufficient material softening. Therefore, fixed heating durations may not always correspond to equivalent material-softening conditions when thermoforming units are operated repeatedly.

The progressive reduction in heating time across consecutive thermoforming cycles may also have clinically relevant implications for CA reproducibility. Although the clinical significance of the observed differences remains uncertain, previous studies have shown that thermoforming can influence aligner thickness, adaptation, and mechanical behavior [5, 7, 15, 16]. Therefore, heating-time variability during repeated fabrication cycles may represent a potential source of manufacturing variability that warrants further investigation.

The regression models developed in this study further quantified the reduction in heating time during the transient phase prior to stabilization. Heating duration decreased by approximately 8.66 s per cycle for ZF during the first five cycles, whereas MF demonstrated a smaller but more prolonged reduction of approximately 4.17 s per cycle during the first eleven cycles. Although heat accumulation within thermoforming systems represents a complex thermodynamic phenomenon, the early-cycle reduction in heating time followed an approximately linear trend within the evaluated cycle range. These regression relationships therefore provide a quantitative characterization of machine warm-up behavior before thermal equilibrium was established. Once steady-state conditions were reached, heating durations remained relatively stable and the predictive models were no longer applicable.

From a practical perspective, the present findings highlight the importance of considering machine warm-up behavior during repeated thermoforming procedures. Continuous batch thermoforming without adequate cooling intervals may progressively alter thermoforming conditions over time. Material-specific thermoforming protocols and monitoring of sheet deflection during heating, rather than relying exclusively on preset heating durations, may therefore improve fabrication consistency. Periodic verification of thermoforming-unit performance may also be beneficial because cumulative thermal accumulation could alter operational heating behavior during repeated use. Increased variability in thermoforming conditions may additionally contribute to CA remakes, additional chairside adjustments, and reduced workflow efficiency during in-office aligner production. These findings further emphasize that thermoforming conditions may progressively change during repeated clinical or laboratory workflows, potentially contributing to variability in thermoformed CA fabrication.

Overall, this study characterizes the temporal pattern of heating-time reduction and stabilization during consecutive thermoforming cycles and demonstrates clear material-dependent differences in thermoforming dynamics. The findings provide quantitative evidence that thermoforming behavior varies across consecutive fabrication cycles and is consistent with progressive thermal accumulation during repeated thermoforming operations.

Conclusion

Thermoforming time decreased progressively across consecutive fabrication cycles for both materials, with stabilization occurring at cycle 6 for ZF and cycle 12 for MF. In both transient and steady-state phases, heating durations were significantly greater than the Supplier-Recommended Thermoforming Time. These findings quantify material-dependent differences in heating-time dynamics that are consistent with progressive thermal accumulation during repeated thermoforming cycles.

Limitation

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the present findings. First, only two thermoplastic materials with a single thickness (0.76 mm) were evaluated. Second, all experiments were performed using a single thermoforming unit under controlled laboratory conditions, which may limit generalizability to other devices and clinical environments. Third, internal chamber and heating-element temperatures were not continuously monitored; therefore, thermal accumulation was inferred indirectly from changes in heating duration. Finally, post-thermoforming thickness distribution, adaptation accuracy, dimensional stability, and mechanical properties were not directly assessed. Future investigations should combine direct thermal monitoring with evaluation of thermoformed aligner properties to clarify the practical consequences of heating-time variability and determine its potential impact on clinical performance.
