Integrated spatio-temporal modeling with hybrid graph convolutions and the graph fourier neural operator for traffic prediction
Authors: Seyed-Majid Hosseini, S. Mozhgan Rahmatinia, Seyed-Amin Hosseini-Seno
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accurate long-term traffic forecasting is pivotal for resilient intelligent transportation systems (ITS), enabling proactive congestion mitigation, energy optimization, and enhanced urban mobility. However, existing methods struggle to capture the intricate interplay of spatial and temporal dependencies in non-Euclidean road networks. Classical autoregressive approaches fail to model nonlinear dynamics, while deep learning techniques—such as RNN-based graph models, attention-driven Transformers, and state-space architectures—often decouple spatial and temporal learning, rely on computationally expensive mechanisms, and exhibit limited scalability and training instability in long-horizon settings. Although recent advances in spatio-temporal fusion and adaptive graph learning partially address multi-scale interactions, they remain constrained by efficiency and the lack of unified global temporal modeling. To overcome these limitations, we propose HG-GFNO (Hybrid Static–Adaptive Graph Convolutions and Graph Fourier Neural Operator), a unified and parameter-efficient framework that combines hybrid graph convolutions for localized and dynamic spatial encoding with a novel GFNO that extends spectral operators to graph domains for linear-complexity long-range temporal modeling. Extensive experiments on four benchmark datasets (PEMS03, PEMS04, PEMS07, and PEMS08) demonstrate that HG-GFNO consistently outperforms state-of-the-art baselines by up to 10.9% in RMSE and 11.9% in MAE across forecasting horizons, while using fewer parameters and exhibiting superior stability. These results position HG-GFNO as a scalable and practical solution for real-world intelligent transportation systems in smart city environments.

Introduction

The rapid growth of smart cities and intelligent transportation systems (ITS) has intensified the demand for accurate and scalable traffic forecasting models, particularly for long-term prediction horizons. Reliable long-horizon traffic forecasting is essential for proactive traffic control, congestion mitigation, energy-efficient urban planning, and enhancing the resilience of transportation networks under dynamic conditions such as accidents, weather disruptions, and special events. However, long-term traffic forecasting remains fundamentally challenging due to the complex interplay of spatial dependencies across road networks and multi-scale temporal dynamics evolving over extended time horizons.

From a spatial perspective, traffic systems naturally form non-Euclidean graph structures, where traffic conditions at each sensor are influenced not only by physical proximity but also by latent, time-varying relationships shaped by functional regions, travel demand patterns, and external factors. Fixed adjacency matrices, commonly constructed from geographical distances, fail to capture these evolving correlations, motivating the use of adaptive graph learning mechanisms1–3. From a temporal perspective, traffic data exhibits strong periodicity (e.g., daily and weekly cycles) alongside irregular fluctuations caused by non-recurring events. Effectively capturing both short-term variations and long-range temporal dependencies is therefore critical for robust forecasting.

Early statistical models such as ARIMA4,5 and VAR6,7 are inherently limited by linear assumptions and stationarity constraints, making them unsuitable for modeling complex spatio-temporal traffic patterns. Deep learning approaches based on recurrent architectures (e.g., LSTM and GRU) partially addressed nonlinearity8–10. but their sequential nature leads to vanishing gradients and limited scalability for long-horizon forecasting. Transformer-based models were later introduced to overcome these limitations by leveraging self-attention mechanisms to model long-range dependencies11–13. Despite their success, Transformers suffer from quadratic computational complexity with respect to sequence length and rely heavily on positional embeddings, which are often suboptimal for time-series forecasting tasks14,15. More recently, State Space Models (SSMs), such as Mamba, have been proposed to reduce computational complexity to linear time16. However, these models are primarily designed for sequential processing and lack an explicit mechanism to incorporate the structural information of road networks.

In parallel, Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) have emerged as a powerful paradigm for modeling spatial dependencies in traffic networks17–19.While early spatio-temporal GNNs achieved promising results, most rely on static graphs and treat spatial and temporal modeling as separate stages. Even recent hybrid architectures that introduce adaptive graphs or combine GNNs with recurrent or Transformer modules often suffer from high computational overhead, limited parallelism, or insufficient interaction between spatial and temporal representations1–3. As summarized in recent studies (Table 1), many state-of-the-art models still depend on recurrent processing or attention-heavy temporal modules, which hinders scalability and efficiency for long-range forecasting scenarios.

To address these limitations, we propose HG-GFNO, a novel spatio-temporal forecasting framework that unifies adaptive graph modeling with spectral-temporal learning. The core of our approach is the Graph Fourier Neural Operator (GFNO), which extends the Fourier Neural Operator to graph-structured data and enables efficient modeling of global spatio-temporal dependencies directly in the graph spectral domain. By introducing a Sequence-as-Token embedding strategy, HG-GFNO transforms long temporal sequences into latent tokens, allowing temporal dependencies to be learned with linear complexity and without relying on recurrent or attention-based mechanisms.

Furthermore, we design a hybrid static–adaptive graph convolution module (A-FGCN) that jointly leverages physical road topology and data-driven adaptive connectivity. This hybrid design enables the model to dynamically adjust spatial relationships while preserving structural priors, leading to more robust and expressive spatial representations. Unlike existing architectures that process spatial and temporal components independently, HG-GFNO integrates the output of the spatial module directly into the GFNO block, enabling synergistic spatio-temporal learning in a unified framework.

Related works

The evolution of research in traffic forecasting represents a continuous effort to address a central challenge: the effective and simultaneous modeling of complex spatial and temporal dependencies. The inherent limitations of classical approaches, including statistical models and traditional machine learning methods, in fully capturing these nonlinear relationships have led to a paradigm shift toward deep learning architectures. These architectures can be broadly categorized into two main streams: the early approaches, which primarily focused on temporal dependency modeling, and the more advanced models that integrate the structural characteristics of road networks to develop spatio-temporal frameworks. This section reviews this evolutionary path, critically analyzing each category of methods to illuminate the research gaps that motivate our proposed model.

Classical approaches

Before deep learning techniques emerged, efforts in traffic forecasting were primarily based on two paradigms: parametric statistical models and classical machine learning algorithms. Although these approaches laid the early foundations of this research field, their fundamental assumptions and limited capacity to capture the complexity of traffic data ultimately led to performance bottlenecks.

The first wave of studies was dominated by statistical time series models such as ARIMA20–22, VAR23, and Kalman filtering techniques24–26. These models attempted to extract temporal patterns by relying on autocorrelation analysis of historical data. However, their performance was highly constrained by simplistic assumptions, particularly stationarity and linearity of dependencies. Given that traffic data is inherently nonlinear and dynamic, these models were practically incapable of capturing complex patterns such as peak hours, sudden events, and nonlinear dependencies. As a result, their predictive accuracy tended to degrade substantially in practical applications27.

To overcome the limitation of linearity, the subsequent generation of research turned to classical machine learning algorithms such as Support Vector Machines (SVM)28,29, Support Vector Regression (SVR), Random Forest30,31, and K-Nearest Neighbors (K-NN)32–34. These methods, with their ability to capture nonlinear relationships, represented a step forward. However, they also introduced challenges in handling large-scale, high-dimensional data with complex features, leading to difficulties in accurately modeling the dynamic temporal correlations in traffic data35. A more fundamental limitation of these algorithms was their lack of structural awareness: they treated traffic data merely as points in a high-dimensional feature space. This perspective not only created computational challenges at scale but also left the models inherently agnostic to the underlying network topology. Consequently, essential spatial correlations embedded in the non-Euclidean structure of road networks were disregarded. This inability to jointly model spatial information clearly demonstrated the need for a new paradigm capable of simultaneously and integrally capturing both spatial and temporal dimensions.

Deep learning approaches

The shift toward deep learning emerged as a direct response to the limitations of classical methods in traffic forecasting. With their ability to learn nonlinear and hierarchical representations, deep learning relieved researchers from the need for manual feature engineering. However, this shift also moved the central challenge from “feature selection” to “architecture design”—that is, how to jointly and effectively capture both temporal and spatial dependencies. Along this trajectory, two major research streams evolved: non-graph-based methods, which often ignored or only implicitly represented network structure, and graph-based methods, which explicitly incorporated the topology of road networks. Non-Graph-Based Approaches: Focus on Sequence and Implicit Spatial Modeling.

The first generation of deep learning architectures treated traffic data as multivariate time series. Models based on RNNs and their advanced variants, such as LSTM and GRU, were initially successful in capturing short-term temporal patterns. However, they were inherently weak in modeling spatial dependencies. To address this limitation, hybrid architectures were introduced. For example, Zheng et al.36 proposed a Conv-LSTM model that integrates an attention mechanism and Bi-LSTM to jointly capture local spatial features and long-term periodicity. Similarly, other works utilized Attention-LSTM to learn temporal dependencies while highlighting the most influential variables through weighting schemes37. This line of research was further extended by incorporating context-aware modules, such as the CNN-GRU structure proposed by Rahmatinia et al.38 for long-term prediction on non-graph data.

Efforts to enhance these models also included integrating meta-heuristic algorithms, such as the Grey Wolf Optimizer in GWO-attention-LSTM, to refine attention weights and improve short-term prediction accuracy39. More complex hybrid models also emerged. For instance, the SAACL model combines ARIMA, Conv-LSTM, and a shuffle attention mechanism to extract linear, spatio-temporal, and periodic features40. Other research focused on advanced signal processing techniques, such as using Optimized Variational Mode Decomposition (OVMD) with L-BiLSTM to handle non-stationary data41. In a similar vein, Hu et al.42 combined Conv-LSTM with shuffle attention and dual BiLSTM units to create a comprehensive feature representation.

Despite these innovations, the fundamental weakness of this entire class of models remained their inability to explicitly model the network’s graph structure. This limitation naturally set the stage for the development of more powerful, graph-aware architectures.

Transformers and Mamba models: a step toward long-range dependencies

To overcome the limitations of RNNs in capturing long-term dependencies, Transformer architectures were introduced. Wen et al.43 proposed RPConvformer, which combines a Transformer with causal convolutional embedding to model local and global dependencies simultaneously. Yu et al.44 demonstrated with the Spatial-Temporal Autoformer (STAF) that the Autoformer architecture is capable of spatio-temporal clustering while effectively capturing long-range dependencies. In a complex hybrid approach, Ren et al.45 introduced TE-TCN, a model that integrates GCN, GRU, and a multi-head Transformer to cover both short- and long-term dependencies concurrently.

More recently, Mamba architectures have been proposed with linear computational complexity. Zhiiqi et al.46 developed ST-Mamba, which utilizes a Selective State Space and a Spatial-Temporal Mixer module to cohesively integrate spatio-temporal data, improving both speed and accuracy over Transformers. The MCST-Mamba model47 demonstrated higher scalability by separating spatial and temporal dependencies using independent Mamba blocks. Furthermore, ST-MambaSync48 combined Mamba and Transformer models to enable long-term dependency modeling without the need for a graph structure. While this line of research shows promise in developing more efficient architectures, its inherent limitation remains the lack of explicit graph representation, which constrains performance in complex traffic scenarios.

Graph-based approaches: explicit modeling of network topology

Given the inherent non-Euclidean nature of road networks, GNNs have become the primary tool for capturing spatial dependencies. Pioneering models such as STGCN49 and T-GCN17, which combined GCNs with temporal modules, established the first explicit spatio-temporal frameworks. However, their reliance on a static adjacency matrix limited their ability to adapt to the dynamic nature of traffic. To address this limitation, a subsequent wave of research introduced adaptive adjacency matrices learned directly from data. GraphWaveNet1 and AGCRN2 were among the first to successfully implement this concept. More advanced models like DDGCRN3 and AST-DGCN50 took this further by generating dynamic graphs at each time step. Innovations such as integrating the graph generation process directly into the GRU cell computations, as seen in AST-DGCN, allowed for a more accurate capture of the network’s instantaneous dynamics. Similarly, the STFDSGCN51 model introduces a spatio-temporal fusion framework by integrating dynamic sparse graph convolution directly within its GRU units. The model also incorporates a spatio-temporal attention fusion scheme, complemented by a gating mechanism, to better capture long-range dependencies and improve responsiveness to sudden events. “The versatility and effectiveness of adaptive graph convolutions have also been verified in broader spatio-temporal forecasting domains beyond traffic. For instance, recent research52 successfully applied an Adaptive-GCN coupled with attention mechanisms for multi-band spectrum prediction, demonstrating the robustness of learnable adjacency matrices in capturing dynamic dependencies across diverse data types. In line with these adaptive approaches, our preliminary study, LeaGCN53, proposed a learnable graph convolutional network enhanced with temporal attention. While LeaGCN effectively captured hidden spatial dependencies through a data-driven adjacency matrix, it relied on LSTM units for temporal modeling, which can be computationally demanding for extended sequences. In this work, HG-GFNO advances this trajectory by substituting recurrent components with the Graph Fourier Neural Operator (GFNO), thereby achieving linear complexity and superior scalability for long-term forecasting.

More recent research has explored increasingly complex architectures. For instance, TPGraph27 combined a spatio-temporal Transformer with multi-graph fusion, while DGCRN54 utilized adaptive graphs and dynamic filters to outperform earlier models like STGCN and DCRNN. Other research has focused on incorporating novel structural and causal insights into GNNs. For instance, Khaled et al.55 proposed the TPSC model, which employs K-Prototype clustering to identify and model similarity and causal relations within the GCN framework. Liu et al.56 addressed the limitations of T-GCN by introducing a dual adaptive adjacency matrix within a GRU-based architecture. Pushing the boundaries of graph-based Transformers, Moon and Cho57 developed a model that utilizes subgraph partitioning, achieving an accuracy improvement of up to 12.33% over standard GNNs. Further refining attention mechanisms, Fan et al.58 introduced RGDAN, which uses random graph diffusion attention to enhance prediction accuracy by 2–5%. Furthermore, recent research has expanded beyond core architectural innovations to address other critical challenges. For example, STFGCN35 introduced dedicated modules (MSFC and SAFC) to model multi-scale temporal and dynamic spatial dependencies. Concurrently, efforts have been made to enhance model adaptability and automation, such as the reinforcement learning-based framework for hyperparameter optimization proposed by Khairy et al.59, which achieved a 9.8% reduction in MAE. Addressing practical deployment constraints, Yang et al.60 developed FedGTP, a federated learning framework that manages data privacy and models inter-client spatial dependencies.

Comprehensive surveys on urban computing61 highlight that while STGNNs have become dominant, capturing complex dependencies remains challenging. To overcome the limitations of predefined static graphs, recent research has pivoted towards automated neural architecture search (NAS). For instance, Auto-DSTSGN62 introduces a gradient-based search mechanism to discover optimal graph structures dynamically, coupled with dilated convolutions for long-term modeling. Similarly, AutoSTS63 employs a fine-grained NAS framework to automatically select the optimal combination of multiple candidate graphs for synchronous modeling.

Despite the effectiveness of these automated approaches, their search phases often incur high computational costs. Conversely, to prioritize efficiency, some studies have proposed graph-free architectures like M3-Net64, which utilizes a pure MLP-based structure with a Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) mechanism to capture dependencies without explicit graph construction. While scalable, such methods may overlook subtle topological signals essential for accurate spatial reasoning.

Recent advancements in Graph Representation Learning (GRL) have increasingly focused on integrating rich node attributes with complex topological structures while maintaining computational efficiency. For instance, in biological network analysis, the TREE framework65 employs a transformer-based architecture with global structural encodings to capture long-range dependencies in heterogeneous graphs interpretably. Similarly, in the domain of graph clustering, the LCAAG model66 introduces an approximate generative Bayesian approach that operates on graph skeletons, significantly improving scalability by inferring node behaviors through link structures rather than direct node modeling. These studies collectively highlight the critical need for architectures that can efficiently process high-dimensional attributed graphs and capture higher-order structural patterns, a principle that also underpins our proposed HG-GFNO framework for spatio-temporal traffic forecasting.

Finally, the most recent hybrid models have begun to integrate the strengths of graph-based approaches with the latest generation of sequence models. As a prime example, DST-Mamba67 combines a graph module with the Mamba architecture to improve both prediction accuracy and scalability. Similarly, the MGCN model68 integrates a GCN layer, enhanced with Chebyshev convolution, and a bidirectional Mamba architecture to provide a more comprehensive model of spatio-temporal dependencies.

Summary and research gap

A review of existing studies reveals a fundamental dichotomy in traffic forecasting. Non-graph-based methods (e.g., LSTM, Attention, and Mamba) successfully learn temporal sequences but lack an explicit understanding of network topology. Conversely, graph-based models effectively capture spatial relationships but are often constrained by their reliance on static graphs or require complete data availability. Even advanced hybrid models, such as MGCN68, have not fully bridged the gap between scalability and dynamic structural awareness.

While recent advancements in 2024 and 2025 (summarized in Table 1) have addressed dynamic correlations and signal heterogeneity, a critical limitation persists across most state-of-the-art (SOTA) models. As highlighted in Table 1, architectures like SSGCRTN, MTEGCRN, and DMFGCRN predominantly rely on recurrent structures (RNN/GRU) or complex Transformer layers for temporal modeling. These approaches often suffer from high computational complexity, limited parallelization capabilities, and scalability issues when applied to large-scale networks with sensor-specific parameters.

Therefore, the key research gap lies in designing a scalable, inherently graph-aware architecture capable of learning global spatio-temporal dependencies without the computational bottleneck of heavy recurrent or attention mechanisms. To address this need, we propose the HG-GFNO framework. Unlike previous methods that rely on recurrent decoupling or heavy interaction modules, our approach leverages the Graph Fourier Neural Operator (GFNO) combined with a “Sequence-as-Token” embedding strategy. This allows the model to capture global spatio-temporal correlations in the frequency domain with linear complexity, offering a more efficient and stable solution for long-range forecasting while maintaining high accuracy.

Methodology

In this section, we provide a detailed exposition of our novel framework, termed HG-GFNO. This architecture is specifically designed to overcome the key challenges in long-range traffic forecasting, particularly the difficulty of disentangling spatial and temporal information. As illustrated in Fig. 1, our model features an end-to-end architecture that facilitates a seamless information processing flow, from spatial feature extraction to temporal modeling and final prediction.

Fig. 1Overall architecture of the proposed HG-GFNO framework, integrating spatial feature extraction and spectral-temporal modeling in an end-to-end design for long-range traffic forecasting.

Overall architecture of the proposed HG-GFNO framework, integrating spatial feature extraction and spectral-temporal modeling in an end-to-end design for long-range traffic forecasting.

The processing pipeline commences by simultaneously taking the traffic time series and the network topology graph as input. In the initial step, the multi A-FGCN block is responsible for extracting rich spatial representations. By intelligently leveraging both static and adaptive adjacency matrices, as well as learning node-specific parameters, this block is capable of accurately encoding both static and dynamic spatial correlations.

The output of this block is then passed through an embedding layer, which maps the features into a higher-dimensional space, before being fed into the GFNO block. The GFNO block, which constitutes the core temporal innovation of our model, operates in the spatio-spectral domain to efficiently capture long-term dependencies and global patterns. To ensure training stability and preserve information flow, Residual Connections and Layer Normalization are employed throughout the architecture. Finally, a Projection Layer maps the final representation to the desired output dimension for the prediction horizon, generating the forecast results.

Spatial feature extraction block: multi A-FGCN

The first step in the HG-GFNO architecture is to extract rich and comprehensive representations of spatial dependencies. This responsibility is handled by the multi A-FGCN block, which is designed to simultaneously encode both static correlations, derived from the network structure, and dynamic correlations, learned from the data itself. The prefix “multi” in the block’s name refers to its multi-layer structure.

The prefix multi in the block’s name highlights its multi-layer structure. As illustrated in Fig. 2, the block consists of two stacked A-FGCN layers (short for Adaptive-Fixed Graph Convolutional Network), combined with a residual connection. This design enhances the depth of representation while facilitating gradient flow during training. The detailed architecture of the base A-FGCN layer is described in the following subsection.

The ideal solution to overcome this limitation would be to define learnable parameters individually for each node. However, this approach directly leads to parameter explosion and severe overfitting, particularly in networks with a large number of nodes and limited training data.

To address this challenge, we adopt a matrix factorization technique, inspired by the AGCRN model18. Instead of learning large weight tensors directly, we decompose them into the product of two smaller matrices: a node-specific representation matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{{E}_{G}}_{1},\:{E}_{{G}_{2}}\in\:{R}^{N\times\:d}$$\end{document} and a weight matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{{W}_{G}}_{1},\:{W}_{{G}_{2}}\in\:{R}^{d\times\:D\times\:D}$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:D$$\end{document} is the hidden dimension and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:d\ll\:D$$\end{document} is the factorization rank. This allows the model to map the input tensor \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:X\in\:{R}^{B\times\:T\times\:N\times\:D}$$\end{document} to the output Z of the same dimension while learning unique spatial patterns for each node. This approach enables the model to learn node-specific spatial patterns with a manageable number of parameters. Consequently, the updated formulation for each layer is revised as Eq. 2:2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:Z=\left({I}_{N}+{D}^{-\frac{1}{2}}A{D}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)X{{E}_{G}}_{1}{{W}_{G}}_{1}+softmax\left(ReLU\left({E}_{A}\cdot\:{E}_{A}^{\top\:}\right)\right)X\:{E}_{{G}_{2}}{W}_{{G}_{2}}$$\end{document}

This formulation forms the core of the A-FGCN block, effectively leveraging both the structural knowledge of the network and the dynamic, node-specific spatial dependencies in an efficient manner.

Adaptive gating mechanism and normalization

To dynamically balance the information from the static and adaptive components, we employ a simple yet effective gating mechanism. A learnable parameter, g, is passed through a sigmoid function to produce a coefficient between 0 and 1. This allows the model to adaptively assign weight to each of the two informational components.

The final output of the block is obtained by combining the weighted contributions of the two components with the initial input (via a residual connection), followed by layer normalization to stabilize the training process:3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:gate=\sigma\:\left(g\right)=\:\frac{1}{1+\mathrm{e}\mathrm{x}\mathrm{p}(-g)}$$\end{document}4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:Z=\:Z+gate\times\:X$$\end{document}5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:Z=layerNorm\left(Z\right)$$\end{document}

Embedding layer: sequence-as-token representation

After the multi A-FGCN block extracts rich spatial representations, these features must be prepared for temporal analysis. Traditional approaches to time-series modeling typically map each time step independently into a latent space. In this work, we propose a novel and more efficient paradigm, drawing direct inspiration from the advanced iTransformer architecture77. The central idea of this approach is to treat the entire temporal sequence of each node as a unified information unit, or token. Rather than focusing on individual time points, this strategy enables the model to learn representations that encapsulate the global patterns and holistic characteristics of each time series. Such representations serve as a highly suitable input to the GFNO block, which is designed to capture long-range and global dependencies.

To implement this idea, a learnable linear transformation is applied to the entire input sequence of length \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:S$$\end{document} for each node independently. As shown in Fig. 3, the linear transformation flattens the temporal and feature dimensions of the input \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:Z\in\:{R}^{B\times\:T\times\:N\times\:D}$$\end{document} and projects them into the latent token space. Graph fourier operator layer (GFNO): the core of spectral processing

The GFNO layer represents the central innovation of our model and serves as a direct alternative to the multi-head attention mechanism. While multi-head attention learns dependencies by computing relationships between all pairs of time points, it faces two significant limitations: a quadratic computational complexity of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:O\left({L}^{2}\right)$$\end{document}, which hinders scalability, and a strong tendency to focus on local relations unless numerous layers are stacked.

In contrast, GFNO provides a global and efficient strategy for modeling long-range dependencies by transforming the signal into the spectral domain. By filtering graph frequencies, this layer directly extracts global patterns with substantially lower computational complexity. This property is particularly advantageous for large-scale graph problems, as it accelerates training, enhances generalization, and reduces the risk of overfitting.

After the spatio-temporal representations have been extracted by the stacked GFNO-former blocks, a final output block is employed to transform these features into the ultimate prediction. Computational complexity analysis

We analyze the computational complexity of the proposed HG-GFNO model and contrast it with representative long-range forecasting approaches based on Transformer architectures. A key distinction lies in the absence of temporal self-attention in HG-GFNO, which fundamentally alters the scaling behavior with respect to the input sequence length \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:T$$\end{document}.

In the initial spatial–temporal modeling stage, the A-FGCN module preserves the temporal dimension while operating on graph-structured data. Under typical sparse graph assumptions, the corresponding computational cost scales linearly with the input sequence length \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:T$$\end{document}, resulting in a complexity of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathcal{O}(T\cdot\:N\cdot\:D)$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:N$$\end{document}denotes the number of nodes and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:D$$\end{document}the hidden dimension. Crucially, no pairwise temporal interactions are computed at this stage.

Subsequently, the temporal sequence associated with each node is encoded into a latent token representation via the sequence-as-token embedding mechanism. This transformation aggregates temporal information into a compact latent representation, after which the temporal dimension is no longer explicitly involved in subsequent computations. The GFNO block then performs spectral filtering in the graph Fourier domain with a dominant complexity of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathcal{O}(N\cdot\:K\cdot\:{d}_{model})$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:K$$\end{document}(\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:K\ll\:N$$\end{document}) denotes the number of retained frequency modes. As a result, the spectral–temporal modeling stage becomes independent of the input sequence length.

In contrast, Transformer-based long-range forecasting models, including variants such as Informer, Autoformer, and iTransformer, rely on attention or attention-inspired mechanisms to model temporal dependencies. While these designs reduce the quadratic complexity of standard self-attention and often achieve sub-quadratic scaling (e.g., \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathcal{O}(T\mathrm{l}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{g}T)$$\end{document}), they remain sensitive to increasing sequence lengths.

Overall, HG-GFNO exhibits linear complexity with respect to the input sequence length in its early stages and becomes independent of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:T$$\end{document}after tokenization, making it particularly suitable for long-range forecasting scenarios where computational scalability is critical.

Experiments

In this section, we design and conduct a set of experiments to validate the proposed model, HG-GFNO, and to comprehensively evaluate its performance in long-term traffic forecasting. The primary objective of this chapter is not only to demonstrate the quantitative superiority of our model in comparison with state-of-the-art methods but also to dissect its architecture through ablation studies and sensitivity analysis to clearly elucidate the fundamental reasons for its distinguished performance.

Experimental setup

To ensure reproducibility and establish a fair basis for comparison, all experiments were carried out under a consistent and carefully defined protocol. The details of the datasets, baseline models, evaluation metrics, and implementation parameters are elaborated in the following sections.

Datasets

To conduct a comprehensive and accurate evaluation of the proposed model, its performance was assessed on four widely used standard traffic datasets, namely PEMS03, PEMS04, PEMS07, and PEMS08. The raw data from this system is recorded at a 30-second frequency. In this study, following a standard preprocessing step, the data was aggregated into 5-minute intervals to align with the common prediction horizons in this research area. Architectural analysis and performance discussion

This remarkable advancement stems from an intelligent and synergistic architectural design that simultaneously overcomes the key limitations of dominant paradigms, namely Transformers and State Space Models (such as Mamba):

Spatial dimension. The multi A-FGCN block learns an adaptive graph with node-specific parameters, enabling a more precise representation of dynamic spatial correlations. This makes it more effective than simpler GCN architectures (such as MGCN). Such an approach frees the spatial modeling from the constraints of the physical network structure, allowing the model to discover latent relationships and data-driven patterns.

Temporal dimension. The GFNO block takes a completely different approach from its competitors. Unlike Transformers, which rely on attention mechanisms, or Mamba-based models, which use sequential processing to capture dependencies, the GFNO block transforms the data into the frequency (spatio-spectral) domain. This allows it to recognize global patterns and long-term dependencies instantly and in parallel. Its ability to analyze signal frequencies provides an inherent advantage for identifying recurring and long-range patterns—ones that competing models typically require much deeper and more complex architectures to learn.

In-depth model analysis

After establishing the superiority of HG-GFNO over state-of-the-art methods, this section provides a deeper analysis of its architecture to uncover the underlying reasons behind its strong performance. The analysis includes a comprehensive ablation study to evaluate the contribution of each key component, as well as a sensitivity analysis to examine the model’s behavior with respect to the main hyperparameters.

Ablation study

To rigorously validate the architectural design and assess the contribution of each core component, we conducted a comprehensive ablation study on two representative datasets, PEMS04 and PEMS08, for a 96-step forecasting horizon. In this study, we systematically evaluated the impact of three key components: the spatial module (S), the spectral module (F), and the adaptive graph mechanism (A). For clarity, in each scenario the disabled component was replaced with a simple baseline operation:

Parameter sensitivity analysis

To gain a deeper understanding of the model’s behavior and determine optimal hyperparameter configurations, we conducted a comprehensive parameter sensitivity analysis. A critical factor in these experiments was the significant variation in graph scale and complexity across different datasets. Consequently, we performed hyperparameter tuning separately on two representative datasets: PEMS04 (as a representative of a large and dense graph) and PEMS08 (as a representative of a smaller graph). Both datasets were evaluated on the challenging task of predicting 96 time steps ahead.

The results of this analysis indicate that the model’s hyperparameters can be broadly categorized into two groups: stable parameters and scale-sensitive parameters.

Stable Parameters:

These parameters exhibited consistent behavior across both graphs, reflecting fundamental principles in traffic modeling.

Input sequence length. The results clearly demonstrated that increasing the input sequence length up to 192 steps (Fig. 10a and b) consistently reduced prediction error for both PEMS04 and PEMS08. This finding confirms the strong capability of the HG-GFNO model to effectively leverage long-term temporal histories.

Conclusion

In this study, we successfully addressed a major architectural challenge in high-fidelity long-term traffic forecasting: the inefficient decoupling of spatial and temporal modeling prevalent in state-of-the-art architectures. We proposed HG-GFNO, an integrated framework designed to eliminate this flaw through synergetic processing. Our model features two fundamental innovations: a hybrid spatial block (multi A-FGCN) that captures highly expressive, adaptive and static graph representations, and most notably, the Graph Fourier Neural Operator (GFNO).

The introduction of GFNO, achieved by generalizing the Fourier Neural Operator to the non-Euclidean graph domain, represents our key contribution to the field of intelligent systems. By operating directly in the graph’s spectral domain, GFNO models temporal dependencies in a topologically unified manner, offering an architecturally superior method for capturing global and long-range patterns. Comprehensive evaluation across four benchmark traffic datasets validated this superiority, with HG-GFNO decisively establishing a new state-of-the-art in long-term forecasting accuracy.

The broader significance of HG-GFNO is two-fold. First, its success validates a new architectural principle: that inherently graph-aware temporal modeling leads to more accurate and robust spatio-temporal representations. Second, its parameter-efficient design demonstrates that Fourier-based intelligent operators are a computationally compelling and scalable alternative to dominant, yet demanding, paradigms like Transformer and Mamba models. These findings position HG-GFNO as a critical enabler for the next generation of predictive and resilient Advanced Intelligent Transportation Systems.
