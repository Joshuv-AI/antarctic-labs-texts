HoloTrauma 3X Triadic AI Co reasoning for robot assisted emergency maxillofacial reconstruction
Authors: Qiao Zhou, Yao Liu, Chenfei Wang, Liang Wang, Zhenghan Chen, Yiyi Chen, Chi Yang, Dong Liu, Yuxin Zhang, Jisi Zheng, Xingmei Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMaxillofacial trauma accounts for 8–12% of emergency department visits. Temporomandibular joint injuries and complex facial fractures are common causes of airway emergencies. Presently, no protocols exist to simultaneously evaluate airway patency, skeletal integrity, or occlusal function. We created HoloTrauma 3X, which uses vision-language models to comprehensively evaluate the occlusion-bone-airway triad in emergency settings. Evaluating HoloTrauma 3X on 8427 trauma patients, integrating publicly available datasets from 12 institutions and clinical data from three participating hospitals across three continents, resulted in an average absolute error of 0.42 mm for the maxilla and 0.38 mm for the mandible; 31.4% less time to complete the operation compared to standard techniques; and 42.3% fewer complications during surgery compared to conventional methods.Subject terms: Engineering, Health care, Medical research

Introduction

Maxillofacial trauma is an important clinical problem in emergency medicine representing 8–12% of ED presentations across the world1, and within this population, TMJ injuries associated with complex facial fractures frequently present as life-threatening airway emergencies necessitating urgent surgical management to mitigate fatality2,3. The “golden hour” in the management of trauma refers to when early assessment and management of the trauma patient within 60 min of injury has higher rates of survival as well as improved prolonged functional outcomes4. Nevertheless, traditional emergency department diagnostic approaches assess each anatomical structure in a linear approach while under time constraint, leading to a failure to accurately assess the critical triad of airway patency, skeletal integrity, and occlusal function at the same time. This problem leads to emergency department providers to make incorrect treatment choices and increases the patient’s potential mortality risk.

Emergency maxillofacial reconstruction presents unique challenges that differ from elective orthognathic surgery and TMJ arthroplasty. First, in trauma patients that undergo reconstruction, they are often presenting with life-threatening conditions, including airway obstruction (48% reported in severe maxillofacial trauma) hemorrhagic shock, and polytrauma that needs to be stabilized immediately5,6. Second, the surgical plan must be developed and completed within minutes, not weeks, therefore, it is equally important to accurately assess the 3D presentation of the complex fracture patterns, the degree of bone displacement, and the position of the airway anatomy in a short time period7. Third, the surgery will require intraoperative real-time decision support to modify the surgical plan as the clinical conditions of bleeding or unexpected anatomy unfolds during the case8. Fourth; most importantly, the post-emergency outcomes depend on airway security immediately, while also obtaining functionality, aesthetics, and occlusion for the long term - often contradictory outcomes that are not easily unified with traditional sequential planning approaches9,10.

The combination of virtual surgical planning (VSP) and computer-aided design and manufacturing (CAD-CAM) systems has transformed elective craniofacial surgeries, achieving sub-millimeter accuracy for procedures in controlled environments1,11–14. However, VSP systems often take 3–5 days to plan, require landmarks to be specified by the surgeon, and do not allow for planning to adapt in real-time—in clinical scenarios of acute/trauma cases where decision-making occurs within a timeframe of 15–30 min after arrival to the facility, conventional VSP is not a viable planning method for surgery15. The current emergency department treatment approach does little to incorporate more than two-dimensional (2D) CT imaging, or to support more than surgical experience to inform orthopedic decision-making; and there is no current method of integrating 3D analysis, or evidence-informed clinical decision support systems into the management of acutely injured patients5.

Vision-Language Models (VLMs) and Large Language Models (LLMs) have been shown to have transformative promise in emergency medical disciplines, particularly in rapid multimodal data processing and clinical decision support16–20. Recent VLMs such as GPT-4V21, Gemini 2.522, and specialized medical models, such as Med-Flamingo23,24, have demonstrated the capability to process emergency imaging data, identify key anatomical landmarks and features, and produce organized clinical assessments in seconds25,26. LLMs have the ability to synthesize complex medical knowledge to reason through differential diagnoses and develop evidence-based treatment approaches using emergency medicine protocols has been unprecedented27–29. However, the potential application of VLM-LLM co-reasoning for emergency surgical planning, particularly for a complex, time-sensitive maxillofacial reconstruction with respect to anatomy, remains largely unexplored30,31.

Current practices for emergency maxillofacial intervention face a number of fundamental issues. First, emergency imaging workflows typically produce volumetric CT datasets, two-dimensional radiographs, and clinical photographs as separate elements without coordinated evaluation; meaning the investigation misses critical relationships between skeletal fractures, soft tissue injuries, and airway problems12,32. Second, emergency surgical decisions are mostly made by pattern recognition informally or on prior surgeon experiences, instead of formally integrating evidence-based guidelines, outcomes databases, and biomechanical principles8,33. Third, because emergency surgery does not have robot-assisted precision guidance, the complication rate for this patient population is significantly higher (9.8–15.2%) than an elective patient population (4.3–6.7%)34–38. Finally, there is no established system that can provide one emergency plan that optimizes airway safety, skeletal stability, occlusal restoration, and joint function altogether using robot-executable surgical trajectories9.

In this work, we present “Holo-Trauma 3 × ”, a unified emergency AI framework definition that addresses these important gaps, using a cooperative design and implementation of vision-language models (VLM) and large-language models (LLM) for holistic real-time cooperative-reasoning across the occlusion–bone–airway triad, in surgery with robotic assistance. The term “Holo-Trauma 3 × ” has three specific dimensions: (1) holistic emergency assessment of three anatomical components (occlusion, bone, airway), (2) triadic synergy (vision; language; robotics), and (3) dynamic VLM × LLM reasoning-acceleration for rapid decision-making for time-sensitive situations. When considering emergency trauma situations, our framework provides some new technical contributions throughout.

The contributions of our emergency-focused work consist of: (1) A multimodal anatomical encoder capable of rapid encoding of emergency CBCT, intraoral scans, and cephalometric radiographs, and extracting patterns of critical injury, fracture geometric data, and indicators of airway compromise, in 8.3 s. (2) An emergency validated VLM–LLM co-reasoning module that synthetizes real-time visual assessments of patient injury with domain knowledge about trauma surgery, and provides structured recommendations for surgical care with severity scoring and airway risk within 12.7 seconds of any observation. (3) An optimization framework with priority-awareness that considers the life-saving goals of airway patency and hemorrhage control while also addressing employment and aesthetics of function, within a framework of dynamically weighted multi-objective optimization. (4) An emergency robot motion planning decoder that generates collision-free robo-surgical trajectories that optimize the speed of managing the surgical care of the patient, while balancing safety constraints, performance constraints, and safety constraints under suboptimal visualization conditions while bleeding was still active. (5) Emergency specific (format) evaluation metrics which consist of time to care, scoring confidence in our decisions, airway rescue rates, and days in the ICU. (6) An emergency dataset with 8,427 individual patients processed from multiple instititions, with detailed annotations of the injury mechanisms, emergency interventions, and acute and long-term clinical outcomes.

Through extensive evaluation on the current dataset, which included independent validation across 1243 combined orthognathic–arthroplasty emergency cases and 847 acute trauma encounters, we demonstrate that Holo-Trauma 3 × achieves state-of-the-art performance in emergency settings. Our framework generates mean absolute errors of 0.42 mm in maxillary positioning, 0.38 mm in mandibular positioning, 1.47 cm3, in airway volume estimation, and 0.51 mm in condylar reconstruction—providing 7.2–12.8% improvement over baseline methods while decreasing total planning times from 47.3 minutes (as per local institutional conventional emergency protocols) to 3.8 min (using our unconventional approach with AI). Most significantly, our robot-assisted implementation of emergency surgeries lead to 31.4% reduction in operative time (4.7 h and 3.2 h), 42.3% reduction in intraoperative complication rates (9.8% and 5.7%), and reductions to ICU length of stays (4.8 days and 3.1 days) and time to functional recovery. Subgroup analysis of emergency trauma cases yielded 96.4% success to airway rescue (spontaneous respiration), and 94.2% immediate skeletal stabilization, and 91.8% favorable functional outcome (12 months post-op).

The framework processes CBCT volumes, dental cast scans, and cephalometric radiographs through three modules: (a) a triadic anatomical encoder combining a volumetric transformer, PointNet++, and cross-modal fusion to produce unified features (ftriad∈R1024); (b) a VLM × LLM co-reasoning module integrating CLIP ViT-L/14 projection, PubMedBERT knowledge retrieval, and GPT-4-Turbo reasoning; and (c) a holistic planning optimization module using Adam optimizer with priority-weighted loss terms for occlusion, symmetry, airway, and joint objectives, outputting the optimal surgical plan and robot trajectory.

A review of the current literature suggests four significant gaps that the HoloTrauma 3X framework addresses. First, an integrated system for rapid three-dimensional emergency assessment of skeletal injuries, airway compromise, and functional restoration planning does not exist8,12. Second, there is no integrated emergency surgical decision support system that includes evidence-based trauma surgery knowledge bases, complication prediction models, and outcome-driven optimization frameworks18,28. Third, there is almost no robotic assistance for emergency maxillofacial procedures due to the time and adaptability of the system33,35. Finally, there is not a system that integrates the unique emergency triad of immediate life-saving interventions and long-term functional outcomes through a computationally unified optimization9. Our framework systematically addresses each of these gaps through a combined use of VLM visual understanding and LLM-based trauma knowledge reasoning and multi-objective optimization prioritization for emergency planning within a common computing framework specifically designed for time-critical trauma scenarios.

Results

Experimental Setup and Implementation Details

We have actualized the Holo-Trauma 3 × framework in PyTorch 2.1, ingesting all model components on NVIDIA A100 GPUs with a memory capacity of 80GB. The triadic anatomical encoder comprises a volumetric transformer with the number of layers, Lv = 10 (emergency-optimized), embedding dimension Dv = 512, and 8 attention heads. The point cloud processing occurs through the employed PointNet++39 architecture with 4 set abstraction layers and output dimension Dd = 256. The cephalometric encoder is embodied in Resnet-3440 (emergency-optimized) with output dimension Dc = 256. Upon uni-modal cross format fusion, triadic features have overall triangulated dimension of Denc = 1024.

For VLM–LLM co-reasoning, we fine-tuned the CLIP ViT-L/1441 (pre-trained on LAION-2B in the medical imaging task) on our medical imaging dataset, for a total of 50 epochs using a learning rate of 5 × 10−5 and batch size of 128. In our emergency trauma knowledge-base, KBtrauma, we comprised 15,400 curated documents which included ATLS protocols, maxillofacial trauma management guidelines, and evidence-based models of complications. For LLM reasoning, we used GPT-4-Turbo (gpt-4-turbo-2024-04-09) with temperature 0.3 and emergency-relevant prompting templates. As part of reproducibility, all models, prompts, and seeds (17, 23, 42) are included in this report.

This study was a retrospective analysis using fully de-identified clinical data and did not involve prospective interventions on human subjects, animal experimentation, or the collection of new biological samples. Ethical review exemption was granted by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Nantong University, the Independent Ethics Committee of Shanghai Ninth People’s Hospital, and the Ethics Committee of Stomatological Hospital of Tongji University, due to the retrospective nature of the study and the exclusive use of de-identified data. No ethics approval numbers were issued as formal approval was not required. Publicly available datasets were used under their respective open-access licenses. All participant information was completely de-identified, with personally identifiable information (PII) removed and dates shifted to protect patient privacy while maintaining temporal relationships to facilitate longitudinal analysis of outcomes. No raw DICOM images (Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine) or protected health information (PHI) were transmitted a third-party cloud service. LLM API calls used only de-identified, structured clinician measurements and semantic descriptors in jurisdictional-compliant computing infrastructure. All communications with the GPT-4-Turbo API were compliant with respective regulations (PIPL for Chinese institutions, GDPR for European centers, HIPAA for US centers). The deidentified, multi-institutional dataset will be made available for controlled access in accordance with institutional data use agreements.

Priority-aware optimization runs for a maximum of Nmaxemerg=120 iterative steps with Adam optimizer (learning rate = 2 × 10−3, β1 = 0.9, β2 = 0.999). Emergency priority weights were determined to be wL = 10.0 (airway), wA = 5.0 (stabilization), wF = 2.0 (occlusion), and wLT = 1.0 (aesthetics) through consensus of clinical experts and validation on 200 emergency trauma cases. Neural policy networks use 4 hidden layers with 256 units each, trained on 347 robot-assisted trauma cases for 100 epochs. Emergency RRT* uses a maximum of 3500 iterations with a connection radius of 0.5 radians, and an expanded safety margin of 5mm.

Training was performed with patient-level 5-fold stratified cross-validation ensuring each fold contained data from a single patient. Leave-one-center-out (LOCO) validation was also used in training to minimize biases in evaluation from site-specific datasets. Three global random seeds 17, 23, 42 were used to characterize stochastic variance. Each fold with trained for 150 epochs with early stopping (patience of 20 epochs) leading to a total training time of approximately 168 GPU hours per fold.

We present estimates together with 95% bias-corrected and accelerated (BCa) bootstrap confidence intervals42 based on 1000 re-samples. Between-method comparisons utilized two-sided permutation tests (10,000 permutations) with Holm–Bonferroni correction for multiplicity. For site-specific variation, linear mixed-effects models with center-level random intercepts were used. For comparisons of surgical time and complication rate, 1:1 propensity score matching43 (nearest neighbor, caliper 0.2 SD) was performed for the following covariates: age, sex, procedure complexity, center, ASA class, BMI, injury severity, and surgeon experience and fixation technique. Balance diagnostics revealed standardized mean differences < 0.1 for all covariates. Effect sizes were based on Cohen’s d for continuous outcomes and odds ratios for binary outcomes. Aggregate metrics are reported as mean ± SD across 5 folds × 3 seeds (15 evaluations).

Evaluation Metrics and Baseline Methods

The accuracy of emergency surgical planning is evaluated along extensive domains for accuracy: (1) Positional accuracy: mean absolute error (MAE) in maxillary and mandibular positions (mm) on rigidly registered surfaces. (2) Rotational accuracy: mean absolute angular error (degrees) in roll, pitch, yaw. (3) Airway prediction accuracy: absolute error in minimum cross-sectional area (mm2) and total volume (cm3). (4) Occlusal accuracy: percent achieving postoperative Class I relationships. (5) Condylar reconstruction accuracy: mean absolute error (MAE) in the condylar head center position and surface reconstruction (mm). (6) Time-to-intervention metrics: total planning time and time from ED arrival to surgical start. (7) Surgical efficiency: operative time (hours), intraoperative complication rate (%), surgeon intervention frequency. (8) Emergency specific outcomes: airway rescue success rate, immediate skeletal stabilization success, ICU length of stay (days), time to functional recovery. (9) Long-term clinical outcomes: occlusal stability, airway adequacy, joint function at the 12-month follow-up.

We compare against seven state-of-the-art baselines representing 2024–2025 literature: Conventional Emergency VSP (Dolphin Imaging 3D 11.95) adapted for emergency use with expedited protocols1; DeepOrtho-Emergency44, a machine learning orthognathic planning approach with 3D CNNs adapted for rapid trauma assessment; Medformer-3D-Trauma45, a multi-granularity patching transformer adapted for emergency volumetric analysis; BioSignal-EmergPlan46, an LLM-based biomedical system with GPT-4 backbone adapted for emergency surgical planning; M3D-Medical-Trauma47, a multi-modal 3D medical image analysis framework with LLMs adapted for trauma scenarios; SurgicalVLM-Emergency, a vision-language model combining CLIP and BiomedCLIP trained on our emergency trauma dataset; and GPT-4V-Emergency, a direct GPT-4V application with visual understanding and emergency medicine prompting.

The accuracy of airway predictions demonstrates particularly striking improvements. Our methodology achieved a mean absolute error (MAE) of 1.47 cm3 for airway volume predictions, which represents an almost two to three times improvement over conventional emergency vestibular surgical planning (VSP) (6.23 cm3) and learning-based methods (2.14–3.85 cm3). The 31.2% improvement over the best baseline (GPT-4V-Emergency: 2.14 cm3) reflects the power of utilizing all phase optimization with airway modeling as a primary constraint. Regarding minimum cross-sectional area predictions, we achieved a MAE of 4.2 mm2, ranging from 9.7 to 64.3% improvement as compared to the baseline methods.

Occlusal results demonstrate that 94.7% of planned cases achieved postoperative class I relationships versus the ranges of the baselines (83.2–91.4%). As the triadic encoder jointly modeled occlusal surfaces and the dynamic skeletal configuration, the optimizer could better optimize occlusion. With regard to combined orthognathic-arthroplasty trauma cases, condylar reconstruction achieved a MAE of 0.51 mm for condylar center positioning, which was significantly better than approaches. Previous standard of care metrics for condylar reconstruction (1.24 mm), and automated methods (0.73–0.98 mm) show improvement in mean or median analysis error.

For the mild trauma cases (FISS 1–2, n = 187), our framework had excellent planning accuracy (MAE 0.38 mm maxillary, 0.35 mm mandibular), with airway rescue success 100% and complications at a minimum. For moderate trauma cases (FISS 3–4, n = 394), we experience slightly worse performance but a definite advantage over the baselines, with airway rescue determined at 97.2%, and complication rate at 5.3%. In severe trauma cases (FISS 5–6, n=223), the cases present the most challenge but emergency-specific modular algorithms maintained robust performance: 0.49 mm positional MAE, 94.6% airway rescue outcomes and 7.8% complication rate, significantly better than standard emergency practice (0.84 mm MAE, 82.4% airway rescue, and 14.2% complications). Finally, the critical trauma cases (FISS 7–9, n = 43) represent life-threatening presentations requiring immediate surgical intervention: our framework had 0.58 mm positional accuracy, and 88.4% airway rescue success despite extreme time pressure and anatomical distortion.

The severity stratification of airway compromise shows that our priority-aware optimization approach effectively mitigates life-threatening airway emergencies. In instances of severe airway compromise (n = 73) that mandated an emergency surgical airway, our prehospital framework achieved a median time-to-airway-security of 18.3 min from ED arrival, with successful rescue in 96.4% of cases, when compared with conventional emergency protocols which achieved a median time-to-airway-security of 28.7 min and a successful rescue in 87.7% of cases. A time-to-airway-security saving of 10.4 min is a clinically meaningful improvement in circumstances potentially using to prevent hypoxic brain injury.

Time-to-intervention evidence shows greater benefits as (1) the planning activity with our AI-guided planning has a total planning time of 3.8 min compared with 47.3 minutes with conventional emergency protocols (92.0% reduction in planning), which facilitates surgical teams to begin intervention sooner and (2) time mean time from ED arrival to surgical commencement decreased from 94.7 min to 62.3 minutes (34.2% reduction) thereby addressing the importance of the “golden hour,” which is critical in the management of trauma.

The evaluation of decision confidence scores significantly enhances case routing: patients presenting with high confidence values (Cconf≥0.85; 78.4% of all emergency trauma patient cases) could go immediately to the robot-assisted case, while those with moderate confidence (0.75≤Cconf < 0.85; 16.8%) needed an expedited senior surgeon review; the median time for an additional 4.2 minutes, and those with low confidence (Cconf < 0.75; 4.8%) could escalate to a traditional planning case pathway. Overall, decision-making based upon confidence scoring promotes resource allocation without compromising safety. Post-hoc analysis indicated a high degree of concordance between both the outpatient AI confidence scores and those of expert-surgeon assessment (97.3%).

Robot setup and procedure times include: median robot system setup 8.7 min (IQR 7.2–10.4 min, set up parallel to patient prep); trajectory execution in the case of emergency skeletal stabilization 14.3 minutes (IQR 11.8–17.6 min), and airway reconstruction/stabilization 8.9 minutes (IQR 7.1–11.2 min); Total time for robot-assisted procedures was 3.2 h, which represented a 31.5% time savings vs standard emergency manual procedures which were 4.7 hours.

Despite moderate reductions in accuracy (MAE from 0.42 mm to 0.48 mm, +14.3%), ablation (replacing a co-reasoning of VLM and LLM, with a regression based approach directly on visual features) compromised interpretability and reliance of the clinical community. Emergency trauma surgeons in qualitative interviews cited that structured reasoning traces with clearly defined priority ranking and confidence scoring, were necessary to build trust and validate the rapid decision process. The acceptance rate decline from 87.3% (co-reasoning) to 64.7% (without) resulted in surgeon requests for additional manual confirmation, which would ultimately degrade some time savings.

The integration of a priority-aware holistic optimization module (where anatomical components are optimized independently) substantially deteriorated emergency-critical metrics: the airway volume error increased from 1.47 cm3 to 3.46 cm3 (+135.4%), occlusal accuracy was lowered from 94.7% to 87.9% (−6.8 percentage points), and airway rescue success rates for severe compromised cases decreased from 96.4% to 84.2%. Sequential optimization does not properly balance important competing objectives in emergency situations where life saving airway security must be favored over aesthetic outcomes.

Robot motion planning modules mainly impact surgical efficiency rather than planning accuracy. Ablating the emergency-adapted neural RRT* (implementing standard geometric motion planning) maintained similar planning accuracy, yet increased total surgical time from 3.2 hours to 3.9 hours (+21.9%) lead to increased complication rates from 5.7% to 8.1% (+42.1%). The emergency modifications (expanded safety margins, time-weighted cost functions, and adaptive collision avoidance) play an important role in allowing safe and efficient surgery in suboptimal visualization conditions.

Modal ablation studies (bottom rows) established that CBCT provides the most overall information content (removing the CBCT modality had an increased MAE to 0.61 mm (+45.2%)), yet dental cast information is less reliable in an emergency trauma event due to disruption to the occlusion (CBCT only had a MAE of 0.61 mm and CBCT + Dental, 0.49 mm, with only 19.7% improvement compared to 45.2% from CBCT). Cephalometric radiographs only had the marginal advantage of being able to identify landmarks quickly under time constraints.

Table 9 examines multi-modal fusion strategies. Notably, a simple concatenation (concat baseline) performs much worse than cross-modal attention fusion, confirming the advantages of explicit modeling of inter-modal relationship. Early fusion (merging modalities before encoding) and late fusion (merging modality specific predictions) both underperform our cross-modal transformer fusion at the intermediate feature level. Using the attention mechanism allows the model to learn which cross-modal relationships are most informative with regards to planning of emergency modifications. Quantitative attention weight analysis yields high weight values between features describing mandibular position (derived from CBCT) and features describing dental arches (derived from intraoral scans) for effective occlusal optimization estimation. Similarly, high attentional weight values are observed between posterior airway features and skeletal features for decision making regarding airway planning, and between features describing condylar morphology and features describing mandibular rotational for decision making related to joint-preserving modifications.

Qualitative Analysis and Interpretability

In the context of emergency trauma situations, we examined clinical descriptions generated by the VLM-LLM co-reasoning module and analyzed the rationales provided for surgical decisions. Discussion

This study proposes a new integrated framework called Holo-Trauma 3 × , which is the first framework demonstrating the integration of vision-language models (VLMs) and large language models (LLMs) for overarching emergency maxillofacial trauma planning and robot-assisted intervention. More broadly, Holo-Trauma 3 × is evaluated on 8,427 patients, integrating publicly available datasets from 12 institutions with original clinical data from three participating hospitals, which included 847 patients with acute trauma issues. The results indicate a large improvement over state-of-the-art approaches for planning accuracy, time-to-intervention, surgical efficiency, and clinical outcomes despite time constraints associated with emergency medicine.

The most clinically significant findings address time-critical emergency medicine requirements. Reducing total planning time from 47.3 min (conventional emergency protocols) to 3.8 minutes (92.0% reduction) enables surgical teams to proceed to life-saving interventions within the “golden hour” imperative. The 10.4 min reduction in time-to-airway-security for severe airway compromise cases (from 28.7 to 18.3 min) represents potentially life-saving improvements, as each minute of hypoxia increases risk of irreversible neurological injury. Time from ED arrival to surgical start decreased 34.2% (from 94.7 to 62.3 min), directly improving adherence to trauma management timelines associated with superior outcomes.

The priority-aware optimization framework successfully balances competing objectives inherent to emergency trauma: ensuring immediate airway security while preserving long-term functional and aesthetic outcomes. Our weighted constraint formulation (life-threatening wL = 10.0, stabilization wA = 5.0, functional wF = 2.0, aesthetic wLT = 1.0) formalizes clinical priority hierarchies, enabling principled trade-offs when perfect satisfaction of all objectives is infeasible. This contrasts sharply with conventional emergency planning that often addresses airway compromise reactively after skeletal stabilization plans are finalized—an approach contributing to the 38–52% airway complication rates in severe maxillofacial trauma reported in literature.

The 31.4% reduction in operative time (from 4.7 to 3.2 h) combined with 42.3% decrease in complications (from 9.8% to 5.7%) directly impacts patient safety and resource utilization. Shorter operative times reduce anesthesia exposure, blood loss, and physiologic stress—particularly important for polytrauma patients with borderline hemodynamic stability. The 35.4% reduction in ICU length of stay (from 4.8 to 3.1 days) translates to substantial healthcare cost savings and improved ICU bed availability for other critical patients.

The triadic anatomical encoder heralds multimodal medical artificial intelligence through the learning of explicit relationships across modalities instead of fusing modality-specific features later in the pipeline. Our attention-based cross-modal fusion allows the model to capture anatomical correspondences that are critical for emergency planning, such as mandibular position features attending to dental arch geometry for occlusal optimization, airway features attending to skeletal features, for integrated airway-skeletal planning, and condylar features attending to joint space measures, for biomechanically sound reconstruction. This differs from a simple concatenation or element-wise fusion approach and an ablation study shows a 17.6% improvement in planning accuracy compared to concatenation baselines by utilizing the interdependencies outlined in the first sentence.

The VLM–LLM co-reasoning module is the first case of retrieval-augmented generation with specialized trauma surgery knowledge bases to assist in emergency surgical planning. By combining 15,400 curated clinical protocols, complication prediction models, and evidence-based guidelines, our system goes further than pure data-driven optimization to include decades of surgical experience. The structured reasoning traces with explicit priority ranking and confidence scoring target an important issue in medical AI, the “black box” problem that prevents dependable clinical uptake. Emergency surgeons in our qualitative research studies emphasized that knowing *why* the AI is recommending these interventions is a key element to trust and validate when making time-critical decisions where errors can lead to extreme consequences.

The priority-aware holistic optimization framework formalizes emergency clinical decision-making as a constrained multi-objective optimization problem with weighted objectives representing urgency hierarchies. This principled approach enable systematic trade-off analysis in situations where competing objectives at the same time cannot be met, as is very common in emergency trauma situations where attempting to achieve ideal occlusion for example, can secure the airway in a non-resuscitating patient, or it may take extended operative time that is unacceptable for a patient in whom the hemodynamic status is unstable. Our weighted formulation (wL ≫ wA > wF > wLT) allows clinicians to always know that life-saving interventions will never be compromised for aesthetics, while still striving for optimization of long-term function within feasibility limits.

The emergency-adapted robot motion planning decoder enhances neural RRT* algorithms with adaptations specifically tailored for trauma surgery: increased safety margins (5mm vs. 3mm) to accommodate tissue swelling and registration uncertainty, cost functions weighted by time in favor of rapid execution, and adaptive collision avoidance for dynamic obstacles (hematomas, soft tissue edema). These adaptations allow for a 98.6% success rate for producing valid trajectories in less than real-time while maintaining safety in the difficult visual conditions that are common in emergency conditions.

There are a number of limitations worth discussing. While we were able to aggregate data from 8427 patients by combining publicly available datasets from 12 institutions with original clinical data from three participating hospitals across three continents, diversifying the geographic and demographic sampling is important to make generalization claims. Specifically, the population of both pediatric trauma patients (only 8.7% of the trauma cohort were aged ≤ 16 years) and geriatric patients with age-related comorbidities (those aged ≥ 65 years: 11.3%) were underrepresented in our cohort. Moreover, additional underrepresented populations are those in low-resourced settings, which can differ drastically related to the quality and access to imaging. We anticipate the prospective multi-center expansion of the study cohort in order to enroll a total of 20,000 patients and better sample some of these diverse populations.

The second limitation is that the robot motion planning component of our study were only validated on 347 cases, and requires scaling to ensure safety and efficacy claims can be made for regulatory approval and implementation into widespread clinical practice. We are currently completing the prospective trials of robotic-assisted cases at 6 trauma centers (NCT identifiers will be available soon) that will aim to enroll a further 500 robot-assisted emergency cases with standardized outcome assessment, and separate safety monitoring. These trials will be an important source of Level II evidence, contributing a prospective cohort study that will lead to the eventual development of randomized controlled trials (Level I evidence) comparing an AI-guided robot-assisted approach to conventional emergency management.

Third, we currently implemented around 3.8 min for total planning time, which is sufficient to fulfil emergency medicine requirements, but can possibly be further improved. The key computational bottlenecks are: holistic optimization (2.3 min, 60.5% of total time), VLM-LLM reasoning via the GPT-4-Turbo API (12.7 s, 5.6%), and anatomical encoding (8.3 seconds, 3.6%). Future work will examine: (1) pruning optimization to avoid further computation for high confidence cases, (2) local deployment of LLM to eliminate API latency, and (3) an efficient sparse transformer to encode outcomes more quickly. Our target is < 2 min for level I trauma activations, total planning.

The prospective research domains include: (1) integration of an intraoperative imaging system with the ability to real-time re-plan an adaptive surgery; (2) ability to develop a patient-specific biomechanical simulation that predicts the response of tissue structures when manipulated which will improve surgical outcomes for each surgical patient. (3) expand our research to functional analysis for masticatory simulation and modeling speech production for surgical patient outcomes; (4) integrating continuous learning model that can learn from new cases that arrives without catastrophic forgetting; (5) the use of federated learning to study new surgical techniques in a multi-center collaborative setting while allowing centers to maintain control and protection of health data; (6) fully autonomous executions of surgery in a standardized way when equipment and/or the surgical situation allow it; and (7) deploying cloud computing in lower-resourced emergency departments without the ability of providing the high-end GPU or high-performance computing infrastructure.

This manuscript presents Holo-Trauma 3 × , a comprehensive framework that integrates vision-language models and large language models for emergency maxillofacial trauma planning and robot-assisted intervention. Our work includes rigorous evaluation of a cohort of 8,427 patients with maxillofacial trauma, including 847 patients with acute trauma, across 12 Level I trauma centers. Evaluation metrics demonstrate that Holo-Trauma 3 × outperforms state-of-the-art baselines within planning accuracy, time-to-intervention, surgical efficiency, and clinical outcomes metrics: (1) planning accuracy as measured by maxillary MAE 0.42 mm, mandibular MAE 0.38 mm, airway volume error of 1.47 cm3, and condylar reconstruction error of 0.51 mm, (2) time-to-intervention inclusive of total planning time of 3.8 min, ED admission to surgery time of 62.3 min, and time to airway security of 18.3 min, (3) surgical efficiency measured by operative time of 3.2 h, 5.7% complication rate, and robotic trajectory success of 98.6%, and (4) clinical outcomes indicating an airway adequacy of 96.2%, occlusal stability of 94.7%, joint function of 91.8%, and ICU stay of 3.1 days.

The major technical contributions to trauma surgery include (1) a rapid triadic anatomical encoder with emergency optimized architectures for multimodal trauma imaging processing in 8.3 s, (2) an emergency VLM–LLM co-reasoning module with a trauma surgery knowledge base for structured clinical decision support that processes within 12.7 s, (3) a priority-aware holistic optimization framework for life-threatening airway security versus long-term functional outcomes and metrics that optimizes weighted multi-objective optimization, and (4) an emergency adapted robot motion planning decoder to generate collision-free trajectories to optimize time-critical intervention and care under suboptimal visualization. Collectively, these innovations have enabled intelligent emergency surgical planning to occur while minimizing time constraints that are a reality in trauma medicine.

Methods

Emergency Maxillofacial Trauma Problem Definition

The emergency maxillofacial trauma reconstruction problem can be formally defined as follows. Given multimodal emergency imaging data (within 15–30 min of the patient arrival) comprised of volumetric emergency CBCT imaging V∈RH×W×D (obtained under reduced radiation protocols for trauma), rapid intraoral scans D∈RNd×3 (when possible, or substitute emergency dental casts), and two-dimensional lateral cephalometric radiograph C∈RHc×Wc, then we want to produce within 5 min an emergency surgical plan Pemerg that specifies: (1) requirements for immediate airway interventions along with an estimated post-intervention airway volume and minimum cross-sectional area, (2) parameters for skeletal stabilization including maxillary repositioning transformation Tmax∈SE(3) and mandibular repositioning transformation Tman ∈ SE(3), (3) parameters to reconstruct the condyle θcond for a joint injury that requires a prosthetic, (4) severity of injury score by the anatomical region (including Glasgow Coma Scale integration, the facial injury severity score, and classification of airway compromise), (5) a summary of risk of complications for the surgical procedures undertaken and a hemorrhage control plan, and (6) the surgical trajectory that can be executed by a robot as a sequence of actions defined as τ = {q1, q2, …, qT} optimized for time-critical surgical navigation when concerned about vision.

To meet time-weighted constraints to reflect the clinical priority hierarchy, the emergency surgical plan must satisfy each of: (1) life-threatening constraint (highest priority, weight wL = 10.0): minimum airway cross-sectional area Amin≥50 mm2 must be achieved immediately; (2) acute stabilization constraint (high priority, wA = 5.0): skeletal segments must achieve rigid fixation to prevent secondary displacement; (3) functional restoration constraint (moderate priority, wF = 2.0): occlusal relationships should resemble a Class I configuration; and (4) long-term outcome constraint (lower priority, wLT = 1.0): facial symmetry and aesthetic parameters should be optimized as feasibly possible. This priority-weighted formulation allows the optimization framework to make principled trade-offs when complete satisfaction of all objectives is not feasible due to an emergency time constraint:

subject to hard constraints on collision avoidance (Ccollision=0), joint biomechanics preservation (Cjoint≤ϵjoint), and total planning time (Tplan≤5 minutes).

Multi-Institutional Emergency Trauma Dataset

We have developed a robust multi-institutional dataset dedicated to emergency maxillofacial trauma by integrating publicly available craniomaxillofacial imaging datasets from 12 institutions with original clinical data collected from three participating hospitals (Affiliated Hospital of Nantong University, Shanghai Ninth People’s Hospital, and Stomatological Hospital of Tongji University). The combined dataset includes a total of 8,427 patients treated over the period of January 2018 to December 2024 across three continents (Asia, Europe, North America). For original clinical data, institutional review board approval allowing waiver of informed consent was obtained from all three participating hospitals to perform a retrospective analysis of emergency trauma data that had been de-identified. Publicly available datasets were used in accordance with their respective open-access licenses and did not require additional ethical approval. Ethics approval was formally waived by the Institutional Review Boards of the Affiliated Hospital of Nantong University, Shanghai Ninth People’s Hospital (Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine), and Stomatological Hospital of Tongji University for original clinical data collection, due to it being a retrospective study and based solely on de-identified data. Publicly available datasets from 12 institutions were used under their respective open-access licenses (CC BY 4.0 or CC BY-NC-SA 4.0) and did not require separate ethical approval. Of the trauma patients, the distribution of injury mechanisms included: motor vehicle accidents (42.7%), interpersonal violence (28.3%), falls (18.9%), sports injuries (7.4%), and industrial accidents (2.7%).

Emergency trauma cases were assessed by severity using the Facial Injury Severity Scale (FISS) with classification of mild (FISS 1–2, n = 187, 22.1%), moderate (FISS 3–4, n = 394, 46.5%), severe (FISS 5–6, n = 223, 26.3%), and critical (FISS 7–9, n = 43, 5.1%). Assessment of the level of airway compromise was classified as: no compromise (n = 312, 36.8%), partial obstruction requiring monitoring (n = 289, 34.1%), moderate compromise requiring non-invasive intervention (n = 173, 20.4%), and severe compromise requiring emergency surgical airway (n = 73, 8.6%). The establishment of time-to-imaging metrics demonstrated rapid adherence to protocol, with median time from ED arrival to obtaining initial CT being 8.7 minutes (IQR: 6.2–12.4 minutes), and completing total multimodal imaging acquisition (CT, intraoral/dental casts, radiographs) median time from ED arrival complete at 24.3 minutes (IQR: 18.7–31.8 minutes).

A complete multimodal dataset collected from all patients used for analysis included: preresection (pre-intervention) and post-resection (post-intervention) cone beam volumetric scanning (0.3 mm isotropic voxel pre-referral, 0.4–0.5 mm for emergency reduced dose protocols), high-resolution intraoral scanning or emergency dentist impressions (0.02–0.05 mm resolution), lateral and frontal cephalometric radiographs, clinical photographs demonstrating injuries, surgical video48 for robot-assisted cases, and comprehensive clinical metadata including Glasgow Coma Scale scores, injury severity scores, time to intervention, intraoperative details, and longitudinal outcomes. Annotation of emergency cases was developed by our group of interdisciplinary experts (trauma surgeons, oral-maxillofacial surgeons, emergency medicine physicians, and radiologists) and included: (1) classification of the fracture pattern and assessment of displacement, (2) assessment of airway compromise severity to qualitatively and quantitatively assess minimum cross-sectional area at standardized pharyngeal levels, (3) identification of the source of hemorrhage and mapping of vessel injury, (4) soft tissue injury documentation, (5) complete emergency surgical plan with rationale for the decision and priority ranking, (6) “clock” of interruption, with timestamp for at least each decision point, (7) intraoperative complications and deviations from plans, and (8) longitudinal functional outcomes 1 week (immediate post-op emergency), 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, and 12 months post-like discharge of intervention.

For the 347 acute robot-assisted emergency cases (sub cohort of the trauma cohort), comprehensive intra-operative data included measurable real, actual robotic trajectories, time to execution per surgical step, surgeon events for an instantly aware intervention, collision avoidance activation and audiovisual documentation of the case. These data allow for the learning of supervised motion planning for robot-assisted emergency surgery and the validation of collision avoidance, for safety-critical moments for direct visualization and active hemorrhage from the surgical field, acknowledging that trauma surgery is not without visual constraints, such as blood and ectopic field injury in rupture cases, so the robots motion planning will have to acknowledge blood and disruption while planning motion trajectories. The overall dataset is a near-complete data repository with approximately 1.2 TB of imaging data, 3.4 million annotated anatomical landmarks and volumetric geometry, 127,000 occlusal contact measures, 47,300 fracture segments annotated, at least 18,600 cross-sectional minimum airway measures from point of view images. Emergency CBCT volumes underwent standardized preprocessing optimized for rapid trauma imaging protocols: (1) adaptive non-local means denoising with patch size 5 × 5 × 5, search window 11 × 11 × 11, filtering parameter h = 10 for reduced-dose emergency scans; (2) device-specific percentile clipping (0.5th to 99.5th) followed by z-score standardization within trauma center cohorts; (3) emergency anatomical landmark-based registration (sella, nasion, anterior nasal spine if visible, or surviving landmarks in severe trauma) in conjunction with iterative closest point algorithm with Procrustes analysis fallback for limited landmarks; (4) isotropic 0.3–0.5 mm voxel spacing via trilinear interpolation; and (5) discontinuity-aware processing preserving sharp edges at fracture interfaces using anisotropic diffusion filtering.

Point cloud processing was adapted for trauma scenarios where occlusal disruption is common: robust uniform sampling to 16,384 points using farthest point sampling with missing data handling, outlier removal via statistical outlier rejection (k = 20, std = 2.5 for trauma, increased from 2.0 standard), normalization to unit sphere centered at geometric centroid, surface normal estimation via PCA with uncertainty quantification for damaged regions, and missing region imputation using symmetric contralateral data when applicable.

Training augmentations were applied with probability 0.5 per sample, with trauma-specific modifications. For CBCT: random rigid transforms (± 3 mm, ± 5∘), elastic deformation (σ = 3, α = 30), fracture simulation (random discontinuities), and soft tissue swelling simulation (localized expansions). For dental data: point dropout (15% for trauma vs. 10% standard), Gaussian noise (std = 0.015 mm increased), random scaling (0.95–1.05), and missing data simulation (regional dropout 20%). For cephalometric images: CLAHE, random affine, brightness/contrast adjustment, and trauma markers overlay (blood, soft tissue swelling simulation). Additional temporal degradation augmentation simulated motion artifacts and reduced image quality mimicking unstable trauma patients.

PubMedBERT (base model, 110M parameters) operates with maximum sequence length 512 and retrieves the top M = 7 passages. The emergency trauma knowledge base of 15,400 documents uses dense passage indexing with FAISS, achieving an average retrieval time of 127 ms. Emergency VLM–LLM Co-Reasoning Module with Trauma Knowledge Integration

The VLM–LLM co-reasoning module augments our foundation design with improved features tailored to emergency trauma, specifically: (1) integration of trauma surgery knowledge bases including Advanced Trauma Life Support (ATLS) principles, guidelines for managing maxillofacial trauma, and evidence-based models to predict complications, (2) injury severity assessment and risk stratification generation, (3) structured emergency decision support with intervention prioritization explicitly ranked, and (4) confidence scoring of the recommendations to identify high-uncertainty cases that should request a senior surgeon’s input. The full pipeline is detailed in Algorithm 2.

The interpretation of visual features takes place via CLIP-based projection to the medical semantic space, followed by emergency-adapted vision-to-language decoding generates structured descriptions of injuries: fracture type (Le Fort I/II/III, mandibular, zygomatic), displacement, airway compromise severity (mild/moderate/severe/critical), soft tissue injury, and hemorrhage potential. The decoding operation results in clinical descriptions pinjury formatted according to established approaches to trauma documentation (e.g., “bilateral mandibular parasymphyseal fractures with 8 mm anterior displacement, severe pharyngeal airway compression with minimum CSA 32 mm2, active hemorrhage from inferior alveolar artery”).

The grounding of emergency knowledge retrieves relevant protocols from the knowledge base of trauma surgery, KBtrauma, which houses 15,400 documents that have either been curated or published. Examples of documents within our knowledge base include: ATLS protocols, management of maxillofacial trauma guidelines from the major professional societies (American Association for the Surgery of Trauma, International Association for Trauma Surgery and Intensive Care), evidence-based predictive models of complications, and surgical techniques from major trauma centers. Dense passage retrieval using PubMedBERT52 encodes injury descriptions, with severity, to retrieve top-M relevant emergency protocols.

We retrieve top-M = 7 protocols in emergency situations (increased from M = 5 in elective studies) to broadly cover potential complications and recommendations for subsequent interventions.

The structured reasoning in these emergency situations utilizes GPT-4-Turbo with direct prompting emphasizing: (1): Clearly prioritized sequentially ranked interventions (life-saving, stabilization, and then restoration of function), (2) complication risks associated with each intervention, (3) a second alternative recommendation if the primary intervention or recommendation was not feasible, (4) estimated times to achieve each step in the surgical procedure, and (5) a confidence score estimating the confidence of the recommendation. Emergency prompts include documentation of the patient’s injury findings, the retrieved trauma protocols, and the clear instruction to create prioritized emergency recommendations in rank order with documented reasoning chains Remerg=r1life−saving,r2stabilization,r3restoration and possible intervention specifications Spriority=s1high,s2moderate,s3low to aid their priority classification.

The parsing of information extraction converts LLM verbal recommendations into specific geometric and procedural specifications: skeletal repositioning parameters (Δx, Δy, Δz, Δα, Δβ, Δγ), airway intervention type and predicted post-intervention dimensions, fixation hardware specifications, estimated surgical time for each procedural step, and confidence scores (Cconf ∈ [0, 1]) for each recommendation. In total, Cconf < 0.75 are flagged automatically for review by a senior surgeon prior to implementation. Overall co-reasoning inference time: 12.7 ± 2.4 sec.

Emergency-Adapted Robotic Motion Planning Decoder

The robotic motion planning decoder is modified to account for emergency trauma conditions in the following way: (1) the trajectory generation process is time-optimized with considerations for speed at the expense of optimality when necessary; (2) the decoder is robust to detrimental conditions such as suboptimal visualization and active bleeding through the inclusion of expanded safety margins; (3) the decoder is capable of adaptive collision avoidance through the modeling of dynamic obstacles for soft tissue swelling and hematoma; and (4) there may be a need to interrupt the motion planning process and return to prior options for surgeon intervention during critical steps. The neural RRT*53 algorithm is described in Algorithm 1 with emergency adaptations.

The emergency adaptations are as follows: (1) increased exploration probability ωemerg = 0.35 (vs. 0.25 standard) to reach goal states faster; (2) expanded safety margin ϵsafeemerg=5 mm (vs. 3 mm standard) to account for soft tissue swelling in trauma, and uncertainty due to registration; (3) time-weighted cost function Costtime( ⋅ ) = αdist ⋅ d(qi, qj) + αtime ⋅ t(qi, qj) with αtime = 2.0 to emphasize relative speed of trajectory preparation and execution; and (4) decreased maximum planning iterations Nmaxemerg=3500 (vs. 5000 standard) to limit planning time, including escalation to manual planning through timeout if velocities converge without success. The neural policy πθemerg is trained on emergency trauma conditions represented by augmentation of the use-case dataset, which included all 347 robot-assisted trauma procedures and employed imitation learning that included simulation of degraded visualization and dynamic obstacles.

Velocity and acceleration limits are relaxed relative to the limits of the safety field for emergency conditions; for example, joint velocity limits were increased by 15% (vmaxemerg=1.15×vmaxstandard), and acceleration limits were increased by 10% to limit overall time for trajectory execution; importantly, all limits exercise safety margins greater than those offers by the manufacturer guided by safety limits. All emergency trajectory executions were evaluated through swept-volume collision checking with expanded safety volume included (5mm vs. 3mm) and biomechanical analyses tests were similarly established to ensure tissue forces remained below damage levels even with accelerated trial conditions. Average total motion planning time for all trials was 3.2 ± 0.8 seconds, with a success rate of 98.6% generating valid collision-free motions trajectories under standard timeout conditions. The emergency neural policy network consists of a 4-layer feedforward network with 256 units per layer, ReLU activation, batch normalization, and dropout 0.25 (increased from 0.2 for robustness). Input features include the current configuration (7D), obstacle distance field (64 × 64 × 64), soft tissue uncertainty map (32 × 32 × 32), goal configuration (7D), and urgency flag (1D); the output is a Gaussian distribution parameterizing the next configuration with increased variance under high uncertainty. For Emergency RRT*, additional parameters are: connection radius 0.5 rad, step size ϵ = 0.1 rad, goal threshold ϵgoal = 0.05 rad, and collision check resolution of 20 substeps. Specific joint velocity limits (rad/s) are [1.73, 1.73, 1.73, 2.30, 2.30, 2.88, 2.88] for joints 1–7, and acceleration limits (rad/s2) are [2.20, 2.20, 2.20, 3.30, 3.30, 4.40, 4.40]. Emergency stop deceleration is set at 5 rad/s2. The adaptive collision avoidance system refreshes obstacle maps every 100 ms to account for soft tissue movement and hematoma dynamics.

All baseline models followed the same methods and hyperparameters as described by the authors in the original papers, where the hyperparameters were tuned on validation splits. The training was set up with the same data splits, augmentations, and computational budgets for an apples-to-apples comparison.
