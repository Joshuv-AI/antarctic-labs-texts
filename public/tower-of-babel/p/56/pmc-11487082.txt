Using “Wordle” to assess the effects of goal gradients and near-misses
Authors: Mike J Dixon, Brittany S Gunpat, Isabelle A Boucher, Monica Tsang, Sara Ahmed, Greg Shaikevich, Isha Dhode, Joshua Leung, Tyler B Kruger
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs individuals near a desired goal, they become more motivated, and happier. When thwarted in their goal pursuit, frustration and negative affect ensue. Slot-machine research indicates that near-misses (being one symbol away from a jackpot) are highly arousing, frustrating, yet motivating outcomes. We used the popular game Wordle to illustrate goal gradient and near-miss effects. In Wordle, an individual tries to guess a five-letter word in six attempts. Wordle gives feedback — letters turn green, orange, or grey depending on how closely guesses match the target word. By analyzing feedback across guesses, players can gauge whether they are approaching or thwarted in their goal pursuit. Wordle also contains near-misses (guesses only one letter away from the target). When feedback indicated players were approaching their goal, positive affect and motivation significantly increased compared to thwarted outcomes which increased frustration. Near-misses were significantly more subjectively arousing than other outcomes. The first appearance of a near-miss (an “approaching” outcome) led to higher motivation and positive affect and less frustration than if the next guess revealed the same four green letters (a “thwarted” near-miss). These findings shed new light on erroneous cognitions among slot-machine players who misinterpret slots games as having goal gradient properties.Subject terms: Psychology, Human behaviour

Introduction

Goal gradients have been studied frequently across many fields in psychology. They reveal that both positive affect and motivation increase as goals are perceived to be near completion1–3. Frustration and its accompanying negative affect can also increase motivation. Adelman and Rosenbaum showed that when frustrating outcomes occur close to the goal, participants persisted longer in the task4.

Another example of motivating effects of frustration involve the so-called near-miss outcome in slot machines (e.g., having two “jackpot” symbols fall on the payline, while the third symbol needed to win the jackpot settles “just off” the payline). Dixon et al. showed that such outcomes trigger high arousal (measured by skin conductance changes)5. They also showed that players pressed the spin button harder following near-misses compared to regular losses—a direct measure of their frustration6. Players also spun more quickly following near-misses (i.e., displayed shorter post-reinforcement pauses) compared to any kind of win—as though they were anxious to quickly move on from their frustrated state5,6.

Although near-miss outcomes are frustrating, they are also highly motiving. For example, Côté et al. showed that players persisted longer on slot-machines with near-misses than machines without these outcomes7. Likewise, Clark et al. showed that following near-misses players experienced increases in the urge to keep playing8.

Here we sought to examine the reactions of participants playing the popular game Wordle to show how goal gradients and affect, influence motivation. Wordle is an interesting goal gradient scenario, because it not only has both a clear obtainable “goal”, but also instances of near-misses.

In Wordle, the goal is to guess a five-letter word within six guesses. Players make guesses by entering five-letter words. For each guess they receive feedback. Figure 1 shows a player attempting to guess “SPARE”. Her first guess is “TRACE”. Since the A and E are both in the target word, and in the correct location, they both turn green. The R turns orange, since it is in “SPARE”, but in the wrong position. The T and C turn grey as neither letter is in the target. Every guess in Wordle can be seen as a progression along a goal gradient. Based on the feedback of green and orange letters, players may be seen as approaching the goal when coloured letters increase or getting thwarted in their goal pursuit when no new coloured letters are uncovered. In Fig. 1, the guess “FLARE” would be seen as “approaching” the goal since the R is now in the correct position and hence turns green. For “AWARE”, since no new coloured letters emerge, their progress would be considered “thwarted” - they are no closer to the goal than on their previous guess. In goal gradient terms, “STARE” would be an “approaching” near-miss - the newly uncovered green letter means they are now only 1 letter away from the target. “SNARE,” would be considered a “thwarted” near-miss since no new coloured letters are revealed. The final guess “SPARE” reveals 5 green letters, and solves the puzzle (a win). Had they guessed “SHARE” an “out of turns” message would indicate they lost. Thus, there are seven possible outcomes: baseline, approach, thwarted, approach-near-miss, thwarted near-miss, win, and loss.

Hypotheses

Players’ reactions to the outcomes shown in Fig. 1 allow us to test seven preregistered hypotheses found here. The first three concerned contrasting regular approach outcomes versus regular thwarted outcomes. H1 predicted that approach outcomes should elicit higher positive affect than thwarted outcomes. H2 predicted that approach outcomes should elicit higher motivation than thwarted outcomes. H3 predicted that thwarted outcomes in Wordle should elicit higher frustration than approach outcomes. The next hypotheses concerned near-miss outcomes. Slot-machine research shows that near misses are highly arousing (e.g., the large increases in skin conductance compared to regular losses shown by Dixon et al.)5,6. H4 is that near-misses should be more arousing than regular approach and thwarted outcomes. Here we predict near-misses should elevate subjective arousal, as well as skin conductance responses. Since nearing the goal increases positive affect, H5 is that approach near-misses will cause an increase in positive affect over and above previous approach guesses (as well as thwarted guesses). Importantly, this hypothesis runs counter to research from slot machines which suggest that near-misses should be frustrating and hence low in positive affect. In this conflict, we predict that goal gradient effects will prevail.

For frustration, near-miss outcomes are again of particular interest. Slot machine research suggests that near-misses are frustrating outcomes, but if goal-gradient status (approach vs. thwarted) biases the interpretation of near-misses then only thwarted near-misses should be frustrating. As such, H6 predicts that thwarted near-miss outcomes will be more frustrating than their approach near-miss counterparts.

We know from slot-machine research that near-misses are highly motivating. Thus, even thwarted near-misses should still be motivating. Hence, for H7, we predicted that despite being blocked during goal pursuit, thwarted near-misses will be associated with higher motivation to continue the game than regular thwarted outcomes.

Theoretical importance

Wordle gives us a forum to adjudicate between predictions from discrepant types of research. Pitting slots research against goal-gradient effects can, at minimum, illustrate important caveats to existing theories by potentially showing for example, that not all near-misses are frustrating (e.g., in H6 we expect only thwarted near-misses will be frustrating, not approach near-misses, and in hypothesis H5, approach near-misses will actually elicit positive affect rather than frustration). In addition, consistent with slot-machine and goal-gradient research, we will show that not all thwarted outcomes result in a reduction in motivation (e.g., in hypothesis H7 we expect that thwarted near-misses will trigger an increase in motivation compared to other thwarted outcomes that were less proximal to the desired goal). Finally, in Wordle, since thwarted near-misses always follow approach near misses they must be considered more proximate to the goal. Consider the outcomes in Fig. 1. After guessing, “STARE” (the approach near-miss), they have ruled out the T as the letter that completes the puzzle. Three other candidate letters N, H, and P, all form viable words (SNARE, SHARE, SPARE) that could be the solution. On their next guess they unsuccessfully try SNARE. On this thwarted near-miss, they are closer to the goal than on the approach near-miss, by virtue of having ruled out an additional candidate-letter. As such, comparing approach and thwarted near-misses allow us to contrast the effects of goal proximity (being closer to the goal should elevate motivation and positive affect on thwarted near-misses) against the role that affect plays on motivation (thwarted near-misses should reduce positive affect, and hence reduce motivation compared to approach near-misses). If thwarted near-misses are less motivating than approach near-misses it will demonstrate that being closer to the goal does not always increase motivation – an important caveat for goal-gradient research.

Results

Results are presented in a way that deviates from our preregistration. In the review process a reviewer requested that losing outcomes (where players run out of turns) be analyzed. Instead of analysing subsets of outcomes as per the preregistration, all seven outcomes (baseline, approach, thwarted, approach near-misses, thwarted near-misses, wins and losses) were analyzed together and the preregistered hypotheses (presented verbatim) were tested using adirectional Tukey posthoc comparisons as a conservative measure. The preregistered hypotheses were based on a pilot study of 53 participants which indicated sufficient power to detect significant results for the preregistered hypotheses.

Data is available on OSF and was analyzed using Jamovi (2.3.21.0). Separate repeated-measures analyses of variance (ANOVA) were conducted for motivation, positive affect, frustration, and arousal. In assessing the significance of main effects, in cases where sphericity was violated, Greenhouse-Geisser corrections were applied to the degrees of freedom. Analyses were conducted on the 66 participants who had complete data for all seven possible outcomes (those shown in Fig. 1, plus losses).

Discussion

Based on goal gradients, we predicted and found that approach outcomes were more positively affecting than thwarted outcomes (H1) and were also more motivating (H2), while thwarted items were more frustrating than approach items (H3). This pattern of data supports literature showing that affect has profound effects on motivation (increasing motivation when affect is positive and decreasing motivation when affect is negative)10.

Consistent with H4, and findings from slot machine research, both types of near misses were significantly more subjectively arousing than regular approach and thwarted outcomes. Although these near-misses led to nominally higher SCRs (See panel D of Fig. 2), than outcomes earlier in the goal-gradient-sequence, they were only significantly more arousing than baseline outcomes. As can be seen by comparing the Loftus and Masson error bars9 for repeated measures in Panel D Fig. 2, SCRs were substantially more variable than subjective responses. Physiological measures have more sources of variability than subjective measures of arousal (e.g., amount of pigment in the skin, micromovements affecting measures etc.). As such we put greater interpretational weight on the subjective arousal data.

Supporting H5, approach near-misses triggered higher positive affect than regular approach and thwarted outcomes. They also triggered higher positive affect than thwarted near-misses which triggered higher frustration as opposed to positive affect (H6). Finally, although thwarted near-misses were associated with higher motivation to continue the game than regular thwarted outcomes (H7), their motivation-inducing properties were significantly less than approach near-misses which were the most motivating of all outcomes. This finding is important since the outcome with the highest motivation was not the outcome closest to the goal. Despite being the closest of all outcomes to the goal, thwarted near-misses fostered significantly less motivation than approach near-misses – an outcome further away from the goal. As such, whether one sees classic goal-gradient effects on motivation seems to depend on emotion. Succinctly, in a goal-gradient scenario like Wordle, how players react to near-misses appears to depend more on whether players view themselves as approaching their goal or are thwarted in their goal pursuit than how objectively close they are to achieving their goal.

Our results also have profound implications for near-misses in other scenarios. In a slot-machine context, for most players, research indicates that near-misses do not elicit positive affect, but rather frustration5,6. Consistent with their frustrating properties, they are associated with large skin conductance increases but short post-reinforcement pauses as players tend to spin again quickly as though to remove themselves from this frustrated state5. In an investigation of slot machine near-misses we found that the vast majority of players realize that near-misses are simply randomly selected events – one among many losing outcomes6. In that study, most players (), correctly refuted the statement “near-misses indicate that a win is imminent”. Of particular interest, however, are the 14 of players who agreed or strongly agreed with this statement belying an erroneous cognition. Players holding these erroneous cognitions scored higher on measures of problem gambling severity6. The Wordle findings presented here may shed interesting light on such players. In a goal-gradient context such as Wordle, near-misses actually do predict “that a win is imminent”. As such, it may be that players who hold erroneous cognitions about near-misses may mistakenly apply their experiences from goal gradient contexts (like Wordle) to gambling contexts that do not have goal gradient properties. The finding that approach near-misses in Wordle were associated with positive affect rather than the frustration typically displayed by most slots players may also suggest that those who hold erroneous cognitions about near-misses in slots, may actually experience near-misses as rewarding rather than frustrating — a finding that could encourage even further play. Our Wordle findings prompted us to reanalyze data from our previous study on near-misses in slot-machines6. We compared slots players who mistakenly thought near-misses were harbingers of wins to those who correctly assumed near-misses were just another type of loss. Although we did not measure positive affect in that study, we did record post-reinforcement pauses (a reasonable proxy for positive affect given that in that study all players paused longer between spins for winning outcomes compared to losses6). Figure 3 shows the results of a new analysis comparing loss PRPs and near-miss PRPs. Players who correctly realized that near-misses are just another type of loss showed no differences between loss and near-miss PRPs. Players who thought that near misses were harbingers of wins showed significantly longer PRPs for near-misses than losses. They appeared to be reacting to these outcomes as positively rewarding – akin to Wordle players’ reactions to approach near-misses. Our finding that approach near-misses elicited the greatest motivation to keep playing is important, as it suggests that those who think that near-misses in slot machines are harbingers of wins may be prone to play longer and compound their losses, contributing to the severity of their gambling problems.

In sum, our Wordle findings not only document how goal gradient and near-misses affect the more than two million Wordle players each day, but also shed light on erroneous cognitions among slot-machine players and suggest new avenues of exploration.

Methods

Undergraduate students () who had played Wordle were recruited via a paid participant pool. Of these, had complete data for all baseline, approach, thwarted, approach near-misses, thwarted near-misses, wins and losses. A stand-alone version of Wordle was programmed using PsychoPy (2022.2.5); the program code is available upon request11. It presented a practice game (not analyzed) followed by five test games on an iMac G5 computer. Players were told they must use all coloured letters in subsequent guesses (equivalent to “hard mode” in regular Wordle). The to-be-guessed targets in the five experimental games were: “HOUND”, “JUDGE”, “BLAND”, “LAYER”, “SKUNK”. After obtaining informed consent, participants were connected to an ADInstruments PowerLab unit via electrodes to record their physiological responses during a 10 s period following each guess. After this delay the program then polled their subjective reactions. Subjective frustration was assessed using a 9-point scale with “no frustration” and “extreme frustration” as anchors. For motivation a 9-point scale was used with “not motivated”, “somewhat motivated” and “extremely motivated” as anchors. Subjective arousal and affect were measured using the Self-Assessment Manikins12. Players clicked on the mannikin that best captured their affective, and arousal state following feedback from their guesses. Skin conductance responses (SCRs) were measured by recording a 6-second window starting 1 s after the players entered their guess. The value (in microsiemens) at the beginning of the window was subtracted from the peak microsiemen value within the window, and square root transformations were applied to these SCRs to minimize skew. For each participant, baseline, approach, thwarted, approach-near misses, thwarted near-misses and winning guesses for the five experimental games were binned, then averaged. Thus, each of the 66 participants with complete data had seven means, one for each outcome type.

For each game, participants continued guessing until they either won the game or used up their six attempts (i.e. lost the game). The cycle of guesses and subjective and physiological assessments was repeated for the five games. After debriefing participants were paid Can$15.00.
