pHyph, a novel antibiotic-free treatment for bacterial vaginosis, promotes Lactobacillus growth and reduces the abundance of pathogens in the vagina
Authors: Emilia Lahtinen, Luisa W Hugerth, Gabriella Edfeldt, Annette Säfholm, Sten Kornfält, Lars Engstrand, Helena Strevens, Ina Schuppe-Koistinen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBacterial vaginosis (BV) is the most common vaginal infection among women of reproductive age worldwide, caused by dysbiosis of the vaginal microbiome. Despite its prevalence, the underlying molecular mechanisms remain poorly understood, making diagnosis and treatment challenging. Although antibiotics are the standard therapy, recurrence rates are high, up to 50% within 1 year, and repeated treatments contribute to the rise of antimicrobial resistance. There is an urgent need for alternative, sustainable, and microbiome-friendly treatment options.ObjectiveTo evaluate whether pHyph, a novel antibiotic-free treatment, promotes a shift in the vaginal microbiome toward a health-associated composition in women with clinically diagnosed BV.Study designIn a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial, vaginal swabs were collected from 152 women diagnosed with BV. Microbiome composition was assessed before and after a 6-day treatment with pHyph or a placebo-like comparator, using qPCR targeting 25 bacterial, 2 viral, and 8 eukaryotic species.ResultspHyph significantly increased the abundance of Lactobacillus iners, Lactobacillus gasseri and Lactobacillus jensenii after treatment, while decreasing the abundance of BV-associated species, such as Gardnerella vaginalis, BVAB2, and Fannyhessea vaginae (Wilcoxon signed rank test, P<.05). The growth-promoting effect on Lactobacillus was more pronounced in women who had detectable levels at baseline. Notably, a substantial number of participants in the pHyph group (n=33) transitioned to a Lactobacillus-dominated community state type (CST), compared to none in the comparator group.ConclusionspHyph supports BV resolution by promoting a Lactobacillus-dominated vaginal microbiome and reducing BV-associated species, offering a promising antibiotic-free alternative.Keywords: antibiotic-free treatment, bacterial vaginosis, dysbiosis, lactobacilli, qPCR, vaginal microbiome

AJOG Global Reports at a Glance

Why was the study conducted?

Bacterial vaginosis (BV) is currently treated with antibiotics, which contribute to the high recurrence rate. There is an urgent need to find less disruptive and sustainable alternatives. We conducted a secondary analysis of a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial to assess the tolerability and safety of pHyph in treating BV and elucidate its impact on key species in the vaginal microbiome.

Key findings

pHyph promoted the growth of lactobacilli while decreasing the abundance of BV-associated species. Overall, it promoted a shift towards a more favorable vaginal microbiome composition.

What does this study add to what is known?

pHyph provides an alternative to antibiotics for the treatment of BV while promoting a favorable vaginal microbiome composition.

Introduction

Bacterial vaginosis (BV) is the most common vaginal infection among reproductive age women globally.1 It is typically characterized by a shift in the vaginal microbiome (VMB) from dominance by 1 of 4 Lactobacillus species (L. crispatus, L. jensenii, L. iners, and L. gasseri) towards more diverse microbial communities depleted of these beneficial lactobacilli, a state referred to as dysbiosis. BV is associated with increased risk of adverse outcomes, such as gynecological infections, HIV- and HPV-infections, possibly leading to cervical dysplasia and cervical cancer, as well as pelvic inflammatory disease, and preterm birth.2, 3, 4, 5 Common signs and symptoms associated with BV include increased pH (>4.5), irritation, odorous smell, and a thin, grey discharge.6 Diagnosis is typically based on Amsel’s criteria, which combine patient symptoms with the microscopic evaluation of a vaginal smear.

The VMB is commonly divided into community state types (CST) based on the dominant species.7 Generally, healthy VMBs are dominated by different Lactobacillus species: CST I (L. crispatus), CST II (L. gasseri), CST III (L. iners), and CST V (L. jensenii). In contrast, CST IV is characterized by dominance of other anaerobic bacteria, such as Fannyhessea vaginae (formerly Atopobium vaginae), Gardnerella vaginalis, and Prevotella bivia,8,9 and is associated with adverse gynecological conditions such as BV. CST IV is often subdivided into IV-A (high BVAB1 and G. vaginalis), IV-B (moderate G. vaginalis and F. vaginae), and IV-C (low G. vaginalis, BVAB1 and lactobacilli, with more diverse anaerobes). L. iners is also frequently detected in a dysbiotic VMB and in BV, likely due to its limited protective capacity, such as in producing only L-lactic acid.10

Biofilms, which aid microbial adhesion, interactions, and resistance to the immune system and medication, are a major contributor to recurrent BV.11,12 The development of biofilm in BV is not fully understood, but G. vaginalis, considered as the primary pathogen in BV, is capable of biofilm formation.13,14

Primary treatment for BV involves antibiotics clindamycin or metronidazole, which can achieve cure rates of up to 80-90%.15,16 However, antibiotics simultaneously disturb microbial balance and fail to remove BV-associated biofilms,17, 18, 19 with over half of treated women experiencing recurrence within a year.20 Antibiotics can also increase the abundance of antibiotic resistance genes in the VMB.21 Therefore, it is crucial to identify alternative therapeutic approaches that can provide effective, sustainable, and minimally disruptive solutions for treating BV.

pHyph [slowly releasing glucono-delta-lactone and natrium-gluconate] is an antibiotic-free, intra-vaginal tablet designed to treat BV and vulvovaginal candidiasis (VVC). pHyph aims to disrupt the pathogenic polymicrobial biofilm and inhibit growth of harmful bacteria by restoring a favorable vaginal pH and promoting growth of lactobacilli by its active ingredients. A previous uncontrolled clinical study reported that pHyph was able to achieve a cure rate of 82% by day 7 among BV patients.22 Here, we present a secondary analysis of data from a larger clinical study, NEFERTITI23 (ClinicalTrial.gov ID: NCT04640922, completed on June 1st, 2022, and designed as phase II clinical trial), investigating the effect of pHyph on the vaginal microbiome among patients with clinically diagnosed BV. Evaluating the difference in vaginal dysbiosis between Days 0 and 7 was a secondary endpoint in the study, while further evaluation of the microbiome was a protocol specified exploratory endpoint.

Materials and methods

Ethical statement

The clinical trial included 3 private gynecological clinics as well as a research center in Sweden and 6 sexual health clinics in the UK. All procedures were performed in compliance with relevant laws and institutional guidelines. Approvals were granted by the Swedish Ethical Review Board (DNR2020-04556, approved September 15th, 2020) and North Scotland Research Ethics Committee (EC reference: 20/NS/0087, approved November 12th, 2020). The privacy rights of the participants have been observed, and all participants provided written informed consent on the first visit.

Study design

A randomized, double-blind placebo-controlled multi-center clinical trial (NEFERTITI) was carried out to assess the clinical performance, tolerability, and safety of pHyph in the treatment of BV (ClinicalTrial.gov ID: NCT04640922). 266 adult post-menarchal, pre-menopausal and non-pregnant women aged 18 years or older, visiting the study centers with BV symptoms, were screened for inclusion. Recruitment also utilized social media advertisements. In total, 152 women enrolled. Patients refrained from using any intravaginal products during the study period and from sexual intercourse until Day 7. BV was diagnosed according to Amsel’s criteria, defined as having at least 3 of the 4 following criteria: 1) thin, white, yellow, homogenous discharge, 2) clue cells on microscopy (more than 20% of epithelial cells), 3) pH of the vaginal fluid above 4.5, and 4) release of fishy odor, i.e. a “positive whiff test” when adding an alkali (10% potassium hydroxide [KOH]) solution.

Treatment

pHyph is a vaginal pessary slowly releasing glucono-delta-lactone (GDL) and sodium gluconate (NaG) that disrupt biofilm formation and restore normal vaginal pH. The intended placebo treatment was a vaginal tablet of identical size containing the same excipients as pHyph, but with sugar alcohol isomalt replacing GDL and NaG. However, isomalt turned out to increase the abundance of the vaginal lactobacilli, which caused a pH reduction later confirmed in vitro (unpublished results). The study subsequently showed isomalt to be an active ingredient, whereby the placebo group cannot be regarded as such; this group will be referred to as the isomalt group.

Sample collection

Enrolled patients were randomized in a 4:1 relation to receive self-administered treatment, with 6 doses once daily (Days 0-5), of either pHyph or isomalt. Detailed description of randomization, allocation ratio, power calculations, placebo selection (isomalt) and dosing rationale can be found in Supplementary Methods. Patients were re-examined by the gynecologist on Day 7 for clinical cure. Patients not clinically cured were offered rescue treatment (metronidazole) for 7 days. Vaginal swabs were collected before and after treatment at Days 0 and 7 (Omnigene Vaginal OMR-130, DNA Genotek Inc, Canada). After collection, samples were stored for up to 30 days at room temperature prior to transfer to -80°C for long-term storage. The patients also completed questionnaires via a mobile app to report symptoms, any adverse effects, and adherence to the study protocol.

DNA extraction

DNA extraction was performed at the Centre for Translational Microbiome Research (CTMR), Karolinska Institutet, Sweden as previously described.24 In short, the samples underwent bead-beating at 1600 rpm for 1 min in ZR Bashing Bead lysis matrix prior to the DNA extraction (S6012; Zymo Research, Irvine, CA, USA). DNA was extracted using the Quick-DNA Magbead Plus kit (D4082; Zymo Research, Irvine, CA, USA), based on the manufacturer’s instructions with minor modifications. Following bead-beating, the samples were incubated in a lysosome solution at 37°C for 60min (lysozyme recipe: 20 mM Tris-Cl, pH 8; 2 mM sodium EDTA [Tris-EDTA; Sigma-Aldrich, catalog no. T9285]; lysozyme [Sigma-Aldrich, catalog no. L6876-100G] to 100 mg/ml). This was followed by 30 min incubation in proteinase K at 55°C (20 mg/ml, part of the extraction kit) prior to the DNA cleanup with Freedom EVO robot (Tecan, Männendorf, Switzerland).

Profiling of key vaginal taxa

The abundance of key vaginal taxa was analyzed with the Precision Microbiome Platform (PMP™) (Bio-Me, Oslo, Norway). PMP™ is a validated qPCR method based on TaqMan™ qPCR assay. The panel used targeted 25 bacteria, 2 viruses, and 8 eukaryotic species relevant to the VMB’s health status (Supplementary Table 1). Samples were normalized against total dsDNA input.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were carried out in R (v. 4.3.1). Differential abundance between independent samples was assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, while the Wilcoxon paired signed-rank test was used for dependent comparisons. Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) adjustment was used to control false discovery rates. Fisher’s exact test was used for categorical comparisons. Relative abundance of taxa was calculated by dividing each taxon’s count by the total counts per sample for plotting purposes. The CST classification was based on normalized absolute abundance counts with VALENCIA,25 a centroid-based method that offers a standardized option for CST classification.

Results

Cohort description

In total 145 patients were included in the full analysis set (FAS) for the trial. Within the FAS, the clinical cure was defined as lack of Amsel’s criteria on Day 7, with cure rates of 50.4% in the pHyph group and 46.7% in the isomalt group. Of the patients included in the FAS, 128 patients provided a sample on both Day 0 and Day 7, with 103 receiving pHyph and 25 receiving isomalt. All these paired samples were included in the current analysis. Figure 1 depicts the subject and sample flow during the study.

On Day 7, BV resolution was assessed based on the absence of Amsel’s criteria 1, 2, and 4 as described above. Patients who met none of the criteria were considered as having resolved BV (54.3% in pHyph group and 56.0% in isomalt group) while those who still met any were considered having unresolved BV (45.7% in pHyph group and 44.0% in isomalt group).

Comparison between treatment groups

There were no significant differences in the species counts between the pHyph and isomalt groups at baseline (Day 0) or after treatment (Day 7) (Wilcoxon rank sum test, p > 0.05). In both groups, Megasphaera 1 was the most common species detected. Notably, both treatments were able to increase L. iners abundance and decrease the abundance of BVAB2 and F. vaginae (Supplementary Table 3). Changes in microbial abundance after pHyph treatment

Patients receiving pHyph showed a significant increase in the abundance of L. iners, L. gasseri, and L. jensenii and a decrease in BV-associated species F. vaginae, BVAB2, G. vaginalis, and Mobiluncus curtisii (Wilcoxon paired signed-rank test, P<.05) (Figure 3, Supplementary Table 3). To assess the effect of pHyph on the growth of lactobacilli, we compared the number of samples with detectable counts of each Lactobacillus species at each timepoint. For all species, the number of patients with detectable counts increased from Day 0 to Day 7 (Figure 4). Comparison between patients with resolved and unresolved BV following pHyph treatment

To further understand pHyph’s impact on the VMB and identify species associated with BV resolution, we compared patients with resolved and unresolved BV following treatment. Overall, pHyph was able to significantly decrease BV-associated species abundance. The abundance of F. vaginae, BVAB2, G. vaginalis, Megasphaeara 1 and P. bivia were lower among patients with resolved BV (Wilcoxon rank sum test, P<.05) (Figure 5).

Changes in microbial abundance after isomalt treatment

Isomalt was used as the intended placebo treatment in the study. Notably, isomalt treatment was associated with a significant increase in Megasphaera 1 abundance (Supplementary Table 3). Additionally, L. iners abundance increased while F. vaginae and BVAB2 decreased, although these changes were not statistically significant (Wilcoxon paired signed-rank test, p > 0.05) (Supplementary Table 3). Thus, the intended “placebo” isomalt could not be considered an entirely inactive agent, but rather a comparator compound.

Comment

Principal finding

This study investigated how pHyph, a novel, intra-vaginal, antibiotic-free treatment designed for the management of BV and VVC, modulates key taxa of the VMB. The NEFERTITI study, a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, multi-center clinical trial, represents the first of its kind on pHyph and builds upon findings from a previous open-label study. The current secondary analysis focuses on comparing the abundance of VMB-associated taxa before and after a 6-day treatment with pHyph or the intended placebo (isomalt), using a targeted qPCR panel.

There were no significant differences in the species abundance between the treatment groups. However, both pHyph and isomalt increased L. iners abundance. Since isomalt is a disaccharide alcohol derived from sucrose and disaccharides have been shown to promote growth of commensal lactobacilli in the vagina,26 we repeated the assay from Collins et al. with the addition of isomalt. These in vitro investigations demonstrated isomalt’s ability to stimulate L. iners growth (data not shown), which may have contributed to lowering vaginal pH, thereby leading to BV resolution.

pHyph also increased the abundance of the beneficial Lactobacillus species L. gasseri and L. jensenii and promoted a shift to a more Lactobacillus-dominated VMB after 6 days of treatment. Post-treatment, more patients had detectable levels of each Lactobacillus species, with the most notable increase regarding L. gasseri, for which 15 additional patients (14.6%) had detectable counts.

Furthermore, several BV-associated species decreased in abundance following pHyph treatment. After treatment, the abundance of F. vaginae, BVAB2, G. vaginalis, and M. curtisii significantly decreased. When comparing patients with resolved and unresolved BV, F. vaginae, BVAB2, G. vaginalis, M. mulieris and P. bivia were less abundant among those with resolved BV. These findings suggest that reducing these taxa may be associated with treatment success.

Results in the context of what is known

A VMB dominated by specific Lactobacillus species (L. crispatus, L. jensenii, L. gasseri) is associated with positive health outcomes, while a dysbiotic, Lactobacillus-depleted VMB is associated with adverse outcomes.27 However, the role of L. iners is less clear, as it appears in both healthy and BV-affected individuals.28 L. iners can be considered a transitional species, associated with a VMB that can shift toward either a beneficial Lactobacillus-dominated or a dysbiotic composition.29,30 In our study, pHyph treatment significantly increased L. iners abundance, suggesting that pHyph may help shift the VMB towards a more favorable composition that facilitates growth of other beneficial Lactobacillus species.

Moreover, pHyph treatment significantly decreased the abundance of several species associated with acute and recurrent BV, including BVAB2, F. vaginae, and G. vaginalis.7,8,31 Although this reduction was observed in both resolved and unresolved BV, it was significantly greater in patients with resolved BV, indicating that these taxa play an important role in the treatment response. Disrupting these key BV-associated species could be essential for successful treatment.

pHyph’s ability to promote lactobacilli growth while decreasing the abundance of BV-associated species indicates its potential to create a healthy environment. Both GDL and NaG are used as food additives and are recognized as safe by the FDA (General Recognized as Safe, GRAS). Upon contact with vaginal fluid, both GDL and NaG convert to gluconic acid, forming a pH-dependent equilibrium, which lowers the vaginal pH, creating a more favorable environment for lactobacilli growth over BV-associated species. Additionally, GDL can disrupt Candida biofilm formation in vitro, likely by providing an alternative carbohydrate source to glycogen from vaginal epithelial cells and thereby promoting the yeast form of C. albicans, while also lowering the vaginal pH.32 Although BV and fungal biofilms differ, biofilm disruption may also contribute to pHyph’s effectiveness.

Interestingly, C. albicans abundance increased following pHyph treatment. This may reflect a shift in the VMB composition, creating a more favorable environment for C. albicans to grow. Notably, L. iners dominated communities are more likely to harbor Candida species compared to L. crispatus dominated ones.33 C. albicans is a common vaginal commensal and dimorphic fungus capable of existing in both pathogenic hyphal and non-pathogenic yeast forms.34 In carbohydrate-rich environments, it tends to remain in its yeast form and is less likely to invade epithelial cells. An increase in C. albicans abundance might therefore not indicate infection, but the opposite. VVC is a known complication following BV; yet in this study, the rate of VVC infection was a mere 4.3% in the pHyph group and 3.3% in the isomalt group, which is lower than previously reported incidences following antibiotic treatment.35

Furthermore, the abundance of U. urealyticum, a species often associated with BV, increased following pHyph treatment, although its exact role in BV development remains unknown.36 However, the increase in counts was relatively small, and its potential biological relevance warrants further investigation.

Both treatments increased L. iners abundance, while decreasing BVAB2 and F. vaginae. However, isomalt also significantly increased the abundance of Megasphaera 1, a species strongly associated with BV, during treatment period, while pHyph decreased it.37 Overall, pHyph appeared more effective, as it reduced a broader range of BV-associated species while increasing the abundance of other beneficial Lactobacillus species, increases which were lower in the isomalt group.

Clinical implications

The findings suggest that pHyph may serve as an alternative to antibiotics such as metronidazole for BV treatment, with a potential role in promoting a more favorable VMB composition, thereby decreasing the risk of recurrence. Before clinical implementation as a preventive application, further studies are required to evaluate its long-term efficacy and safety.

Research implications

Further research should validate pHyph’s efficacy in treating BV across various patient populations, such as pregnant women. More double-blind, randomized, and placebo-controlled trials studying alternative treatments for BV are also warranted to find effective treatments providing long-term cure. Future studies should evaluate treatment frequency with pHyph, its use as a preventative treatment following BV resolution, and its potential in combination with antibiotics or vaginal probiotics for BV treatment. Furthermore, studying isomalt as an alternative BV treatment presents an interesting avenue for future research.

Strengths and limitations

We analyzed vaginal swab samples obtained from clinically diagnosed BV patients as part of a large double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled clinical trial conducted across 2 countries. However, the placebo used in the study turned out to be active, complicating conclusions on pHyph’s effectiveness. The isomalt should be considered as a comparator compound instead of a true, non-active placebo. While qPCR enabled absolute quantification of key microbial species, it was limited to a predefined subset of the microbiome and did not capture microbial activity, which may influence treatment outcomes. Metagenomics could provide more information on compositional shifts and mechanisms underlying pHyph’s effects. In addition, the 4:1 randomization ratio may limit the study by unbalancing the sizes and increasing the risk of type II error.

Conclusions

Our results demonstrate that pHyph promotes the growth of lactobacilli and induces a shift in the vaginal microbiome toward a Lactobacillus-dominated composition. Simultaneously, pHyph reduces the abundance of BV-associated pathogens, supporting its association with clinical resolution of BV.
