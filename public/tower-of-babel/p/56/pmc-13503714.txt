Detection of early-stage Parkinson’s disease using wearable sensors at multiple body locations and convolutional neural networks
Authors: Hyejin Choi, Changhong Youm, Hwayoung Park, Bohyun Kim, Juseon Hwang, Sang-Myung Cheon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Early-stage Parkinson’s disease (PD) presents with subtle motor symptoms that complicate timely diagnosis. We developed a non-invasive detection framework using wearable sensors and a convolutional neural network (CNN) during a 6-min walk test. Time-series data were collected from 78 patients with early-stage PD and 50 controls across six body locations. Straight walking segments were analyzed, and 34 non-linear gait features were additionally extracted to complement deep learning with interpretable machine-learning models. The CNN achieved 95.6% accuracy using left-arm gyroscope data during the first minute of straight walking. Temporal analyses suggested that classification performance remained relatively stable across 1–2-min measurement windows, indicating the potential utility of short-duration gait assessments for early-stage PD detection. Feature-based machine-learning models using a reduced set of selected non-linear features demonstrated performance comparable to models using the full feature set, with peak discrimination observed during early and late test intervals. Although the highest accuracy was obtained from first-minute CNN analysis, multi-segment evaluation revealed complementary time-dependent motor signatures captured by interpretable features. These findings suggest that short-duration straight-walking data enable accurate and efficient early PD screening, while multi-segment analysis provides a more comprehensive and physiologically meaningful characterization of early motor dysfunction.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-61801-2.

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by motor symptoms, including bradykinesia, rigidity, resting tremor, and postural instability1. Gait disturbances are particularly disabling and often emerge early, manifesting as reduced step length, decreased walking speed, festination, and impaired turning2,3. Subtle asymmetries, such as unilateral shuffling and diminished arm swing, frequently appear in early-stage PD but may go undetected without overt gait complaints4,5, especially since some features overlap with normal aging6.

Current clinical assessments, including the Hoehn and Yahr (H&Y) Scale and Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale, rely on clinician observations and are subject to inter-rater variability and subjectivity7,8. Although laboratory-based systems, such as three-dimensional motion capture and instrumented walkways, provide precise gait measurements, their high cost and operational complexity limit their routine clinical use9. In contrast, wearable sensors offer a portable, low-cost, and scalable solution for continuous, real-world gait monitoring10. Consequently, there is growing interest in leveraging wearable sensor data to develop objective tools for PD detection and severity assessment11.

The 6-min walk test (SMWT) is widely used to assess functional endurance through continuous walking and may better reflect sustained walking performance than shorter laboratory-based gait assessments12. While previous studies have primarily focused on total walking distance as the main outcome13,14, qualitative gait dynamics over time during the test may better reveal early motor impairments15. Despite recommendations to use segment-based analysis, few studies have examined temporal variations in gait quality during the SMWT. Segment-based analysis involves dividing the walking test into smaller, distinct time intervals to examine temporal variations in gait quality. For example, Bohannon et al. and Valet et al. reported that performance during the first 2 min approximates full-test outcomes, although their analyses focused exclusively on walking distance13,14. Hadouiri et al. extended this approach in patients with multiple sclerosis by analyzing gait data minute by minute, revealing time-dependent changes in spatiotemporal metrics16. However, time-segmented (minute-by-minute) analyses in PD using deep-learning techniques remain limited. Moreover, existing studies have primarily focused on lower-limb data, often overlooking upper-limb and trunk movements.

Deep-learning methods, particularly convolutional neural networks (CNN), have shown promise in detecting PD from wearable sensor data by capturing complex non-linear gait patterns17–19. Our previous work demonstrated that CNN trained on full-duration SMWT data could effectively distinguish patients with early-stage PD from healthy controls20. However, CNN performance across short time intervals has not been systematically investigated. Determining whether early-stage PD can be accurately classified within a shorter timeframe, such as the first or last minute, could streamline assessments and reduce participant burden21.

Although CNN offer high classification performance, they are often criticized for limited interpretability. To complement the black-box nature of deep models, feature-based machine learning provides an interpretable benchmark by linking explicit gait descriptors to classification outcomes. In this context, non-linear gait metrics, including asymmetry, variability, coordination, and trunk stability, may serve as clinically meaningful biomarkers that enhance both diagnostic sensitivity and model transparency22,23. These features can also elucidate which sensor locations and movement characteristics contribute most to classification, particularly within specific time windows during the SMWT.

Therefore, in this study, we aimed to: (1) evaluate CNN-based classification of individuals with early-stage PD using multi-location wearable sensor data collected during the straight-walking phase of the SMWT; (2) compare classification performance across different time intervals and sensor locations to determine the minimal reliable measurement duration and optimal sensor placement, with a particular focus on the sensor previously shown to yield the highest classification accuracy; and, (3) extract non-linear gait features and compare multiple machine-learning models using full and Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO)-selected feature sets to identify key biomarkers across 1–6 min of the SMWT, thereby improving model interpretability and clinical relevance.

Methods

Participants

Overall, 78 individuals with early-stage PD and 50 age-matched controls were enrolled. PD participants were diagnosed with idiopathic PD according to the Movement Disorder Society Clinical Diagnostic Criteria for Parkinson’s Disease24, as confirmed by a neurologist. Inclusion criteria were H&Y stages 1–2 and scores ≥ 24 on the validated Korean version of the Mini-Mental State Examination (K-MMSE)25–27. Individuals with comorbid musculoskeletal, cardiovascular, or cognitive conditions, or those with recent orthopedic surgery, were excluded. Only participants who completed the full SMWT were included in the final analysis. The recruitment flow diagram is shown in Fig. 1, and demographic data are presented in Table 1. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Dong-A University Medical Center (IRB number: DAUHIRB-22-089) and conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. All participants provided written informed consent. The study was registered with the Clinical Research Information Service of Korea (KCT0009353; April 19, 2024; http://cris.nih.go.kr).

Experimental procedures

All participants completed the SMWT along a 20-m hallway delineated by cones placed at each end (Fig. 2a). The task was performed under standardized instructions: walk as fast as possible without running, with safety monitoring by study personnel. All tests were conducted approximately 2 h after medication intake during the “ON” medication state.

Six Xsens DOT sensors (Movella Technologies, Enschede, Netherlands) were affixed using a stretchable belt at the following locations: the left and right upper arms (5 cm above the lateral humeral epicondyle), left and right thighs (10 cm above the lateral femoral epicondyle), thoracic spine (T10), and lumbar spine (aligned with the midpoint of the posterior superior iliac spine) (Fig. 2a). Sensor dimensions were 36.3 × 30.35 × 10.8 mm, and weight was 11.2 g. All sensors sampled tri-axial acceleration and gyroscopic data at 60 Hz, using the East-North-Up coordinate system. Signal-based time-series (TS) gait characteristics were extracted from accelerometer and gyroscope signals along the three sensor axes. Accelerometer signals represent linear acceleration along each sensor axis, whereas gyroscope signals represent angular velocity about each sensor axis. To facilitate biomechanical interpretation, the sensor axes were defined relative to the anatomical orientation of each body segment (Fig. 2a). For the upper-arm and thigh sensors, the X-axis was aligned with the longitudinal axis of the humerus and femur, respectively. These sensors were attached such that the Y- and Z-axes corresponded to the anteroposterior and mediolateral directions, respectively. Therefore, the gyroscope X-axis component represents the angular velocity about the longitudinal axis of the upper arm and thigh. The gyroscope Y-axis component represents angular velocity about the anteroposterior axis and may be related to frontal-plane movement, whereas the gyroscope Z-axis component represents angular velocity about the mediolateral axis and is more directly related to sagittal-plane movement. For the thoracic and lumbar sensors, the X-axis corresponded to the vertical axis of the trunk, whereas the Y- and Z-axes represented mediolateral and anteroposterior trunk orientations, respectively. The gyroscope X-axis component reflected axial trunk rotation, the Y-axis component reflected flexion–extension-related trunk rotation about the mediolateral axis, and the Z-axis component reflected lateral-bending-related trunk rotation about the anteroposterior axis.

Raw data were transmitted via Bluetooth 5.0 to an iPad (iOS 15.6.1; Apple, Cupertino, CA) using the MovellaDOT application. All subsequent analyses were performed using MATLAB R2023a (MathWorks, Natick, MA). Figure 2b illustrates the overall framework adopted in this study for classifying people with early-stage PD and controls using wearable sensor data. The process encompasses four primary stages: collecting raw TS data via wearable sensors, applying preprocessing techniques to prepare the data, splitting the dataset into training and testing subsets and, finally, training a CNN model to assess classification performance. In addition to the CNN-based classification pipeline, a complementary feature-based machine learning framework was implemented. Non-linear gait features were extracted from the preprocessed straight walking data, followed by feature selection using LASSO and classification using multiple machine-learning algorithms. This parallel analysis was designed to enhance interpretability and identify clinically meaningful gait biomarkers that support and contextualize the deep learning findings.

Data preprocessing and gait phase detection

Raw TS data from all six sensors were divided into six 1-min intervals to facilitate time-dependent analysis. Within each 1-min interval, turning gait events were detected using lumbar gyroscope data, following the approach of El-Gohary et al.28. Turning gait was identified when angular velocity exceeded 15°/s, and the end of turning was defined as an angular velocity < 5°/s. To exclude transient movements or artifacts, a minimum turning duration of 0.5 s (30 frames) was applied. Straight-walking bouts were then defined as the intervals between consecutive turning events. Straight-walking segments were identified between detected turning events and were retained only when their duration exceeded 5 s. This procedure was designed to capture continuous and natural straight-walking periods occurring during the SMWT while excluding short transient movements and turn-related events. A straight-walking bout was retained only when its duration was ≥ 5 s (300 frames), which was used as a minimum-duration criterion to ensure that the extracted segment contained sufficient straight-walking information. Importantly, the retained bouts were not cropped to a fixed 5-s duration; therefore, the lengths of valid straight-walking bouts varied according to the naturally occurring interval between turns. Up to three valid straight-walking bouts were extracted from each 1-min interval. The identified frame indices were synchronized across all sensors to preserve temporal alignment. Data pre-processing was implemented using MATLAB and Python (Python 3.10; Python Software Foundation).

Normalization, filtering, and resampling

To mitigate sensor placement variability and anthropometric differences, all signals were normalized by subtracting the median value for each axis (X, Y, and Z). A second-order low-pass Butterworth filter with a 6-Hz cutoff frequency was applied to reduce high-frequency noise.

For the CNN analysis, the retained straight-walking segments were standardized to 896 frames using uniform linear interpolation-based temporal resampling to satisfy the subsequent TS image-generation procedure. Specifically, equally spaced indices were generated across the original segment length, and linear interpolation was applied to resample each segment to the target length of 896 frames. Segments longer than 896 frames were linearly compressed, whereas segments shorter than 896 frames were linearly expanded. The standardized 896-frame TS signals were then converted into recurrence plot images. Recurrence plots were generated for both individual axes and resultant values derived from the accelerometer and gyroscope data. In the image-generation pipeline, NaN values, if present, were replaced with zero before image transformation; this was used only for missing-value handling and did not alter the segment length. The generated recurrence plot images were saved as grayscale PNG files and subsequently resized to 224 × 224 pixels during PyTorch data loading for CNN input29. This process yielded eight TS image types per segment based on acceleration and angular velocity data during straight walking.

Data generation

To train and evaluate the CNN models, the accumulated imaging dataset was analyzed incrementally per minute. Specifically, datasets from 1 min were analyzed first, followed by cumulative datasets from 1 to 2 min, 1–3 min, and so on, up to 6 min. For each dataset, training, validation, and test sets were randomly split at the participant level (subject-wise split) in ratios of 50%, 20%, and 30%, respectively, to prevent subject-level data leakage. Therefore, image segments originating from the same participant were not simultaneously included across different dataset partitions.

Five-fold cross-validation was subsequently performed based on these subject-wise partitions. After subject-level partitioning, sample-level indices were independently generated within each fold, and class imbalance handling was performed only within the training fold using the imbalanced-learn Python package (version 0.10.1)30. Oversampling procedures were applied exclusively to the training data after fold partitioning, whereas the validation and test folds remained unchanged and were used solely for independent model evaluation.

As sample counts varied across minutes (Supplementary Table S1), a representative 1-min example illustrates the oversampling procedure. That dataset initially comprised 234 early-stage PD samples and 150 control samples per segment across six sensors (left arm, right arm, left thigh, right thigh, thoracic spine, and lumbar spine). To reduce class imbalance during model training, minority-class samples within the training fold were randomly oversampled to achieve class parity30. Consequently, 234 samples per class (468 total) were generated per sensor segment for the 1-min dataset. The same balancing strategy was applied to all other minute-level datasets.

CNN model training

Model training and classification were conducted for each accumulated minute using the processed TS image dataset. For variance analysis, a five-fold cross-validation was applied. The batch size used during training was 128. Adam optimizers were employed, with a learning rate of 1e-05. After 150 epochs, the proposed model converged, and training was halted using early stopping. To reduce the risk of overfitting, only the best model was retained. This implies that during the training phase, the model was saved if the validation accuracy of a given epoch surpassed the highest accuracy achieved thus far.

To classify people with early-stage PD and controls based on the processed TS images, the following three CNN architectures were employed: ResNet31, DenseNet32, and SqueezeNet33. The input image size for all CNN models was set to 224 × 224 pixels. We applied the ResNet model comprising 18 layers. The DenseNet-121 model, composed of four dense blocks with 6, 12, 24, and 16 channels, was also adopted. Similarly, SqueezeNet v1.0 was employed. These architectures were selected because of their proven performance and efficiency in image-based classification. All CNN models were initialized using ImageNet-pretrained weights and fine-tuned using the study-specific dataset. Participant-level five-fold cross-validation was performed to prevent subject-level data leakage, and random oversampling was applied exclusively within the training data of each fold to address class imbalance. Detailed information regarding the CNN architectures, training parameters, validation framework, class-balancing procedures, and computational environment is summarized in Supplementary Table S2.

The performance of the binary classification models was evaluated using standard metrics, including accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score. Accuracy was used as the primary evaluation metric for classification problems that are well-balanced and not skewed or affected by class imbalance. In addition, confusion matrix components—true positive, true negative, false positive, and false negative—were computed to derive the precision and recall values. Precision indicates the proportion of correct positive predictions, whereas recall quantifies the number of actual positive cases accurately identified by the classifier. This metric is also known as sensitivity. The F1-score integrates both precision and recall and serves as a comprehensive metric, commonly defined as the harmonic mean of these two components. The harmonic mean is considered more appropriate than the arithmetic mean for averaging ratios such as precision recall34,35.

Non-linear feature extraction

From the preprocessed straight walking data from six sensor locations, 34 non-linear features were extracted using MATLAB. The non-linear features were calculated from the segmented and filtered gait signals prior to the CNN image-generation process to preserve the original temporal and physiological characteristics of the walking signals. Maximal values of acceleration and angular velocity were extracted to represent the peak magnitude of body segment motion, which may reflect involuntary or exaggerated movements characteristic of early-stage PD. RMS values were calculated to quantify signal variability over time, providing insights into the consistency and stability of the motor patterns. In addition, maximal jerk was computed for both acceleration and angular velocity signals. Jerk, defined as the rate of change in acceleration, has been hypothesized to capture abrupt, jerky movements and may serve as an indicator of neuromotor irregularities associated with PD36. To assess the complexity of gait dynamics, we calculated the sample entropy of the acceleration and angular velocity signals. Sample entropy is a non-linear measure that quantifies the predictability of time series data by evaluating the extent to which patterns are repeated over time. Lower entropy values indicate more regular and less complex motor control, whereas higher entropy values indicate greater variability and adaptability within the motor system. All features were computed using MATLAB.

All features were computed for each axis (X, Y, and Z) and the resultant vector (XYZ). The average feature values across the three straight gait segments per minute were used in the subsequent analyses. These averaged values were used for the non-linear analysis at each 1-min interval. This process was repeated for 6 min, and the resulting feature values from each minute were further averaged to generate the cumulative feature set used for the 6-min analysis. Angular velocity was converted from degrees/s to radians/s to ensure consistency. The definitions of the domain-specific gait features and their physiological interpretations are summarized in Supplementary Table S3.

Statistical analysis

The Shapiro–Wilk test assessed multivariate normality. Fisher’s exact test, the Mann–Whitney U test, or the independent t-test evaluated differences in physical and clinical characteristics between people with early-stage PD and controls. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 21.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY); MATLAB; and, Python. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Repeated-measures ANOVA assessed the effect of measurement duration (1–6 min) on classification accuracy across top-performing sensor-variable combinations. Bonferroni-adjusted post hoc t-tests compared specific time intervals.

For non-linear gait features, LASSO logistic regression was performed to evaluate the stability of the feature-selection procedure. LASSO applies L1 regularization to reduce model complexity and mitigate overfitting by shrinking less informative coefficients toward zero. Variables with non-zero coefficients were retained and used to identify the most discriminative gait features for distinguishing early-stage PD from controls. Classification performance was evaluated using accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, precision, F1-score, and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were additionally generated for sensor-specific LASSO-selected feature models to visualize classification performance and discriminative ability.

A machine-learning approach was applied to explore the potential of non-linear gait features to classify people with early-stage PD and controls. Several machine-learning algorithms were employed to evaluate the suitability of these gait variables for classification across different groups, with each algorithm providing unique insights into the data, thus necessitating an assessment of the variation in the classification accuracy. To address the classification tasks, seven machine-learning algorithms were applied (Fig. 2b): logistic regression (LR)37, k-nearest neighbors (KNN)38, naïve Bayes (NB)39, linear discriminant analysis (LDA)40, quadratic discriminant analysis (QDA)40, support vector machine (SVM)41, and random forest (RF)42. The model hyperparameters for each classifier were tuned using a grid search (Supplementary Table S4).

Five-fold cross-validation was used to evaluate the accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 scores for the classification performance of the seven algorithms. In this method, the dataset was divided into five parts, with four parts used as training data and one part used as test data in each iteration. The process was repeated five times, and the average of the results from the test sets was used as the final accuracy. We also calculated the Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC), which uniquely maximizes the sensitivity, specificity, precision, and negative predictive value, providing an accurate assessment of the overall performance of the algorithm on a given dataset. The MCC is often scaled to a normalized version (norm MCC) within the interval [0, 1], where 0 represents the worst performance and 1 represents the best performance43. Given the imbalanced class distribution, a random oversampling method was applied to the minority class in the training set of each cross-validation fold to mitigate class imbalance44.

Heat-map accuracies were consistently higher during straight gait across all sensor-variable combinations and timepoints (Fig. 3). The left arm gyroscope X-axis data (Larm_Gyr_X) during straight walking yielded the highest accuracy—95.6% (precision, recall, F1-score = 0.956)—at 1 min, surpassing the full 6-min accuracy of 94.6% (precision, recall, F1-score = 0.946), suggesting that short-duration straight gait effectively captures early-stage PD features (Fig. 3).

In addition to the left arm, the sensors on the right arm (Rarm) and both thighs (Lthi and Rthi) showed strong performance during straight walking, with several variables achieving > 90% accuracy at 6 min. Notable examples included Rarm_Gyr_Z (accuracy: 0.907; precision: 0.908; recall: 0.907; F1-score: 0.907), Lthi_Acc_X (accuracy: 0.905; precision: 0.906; recall: 0.905; F1-score: 0.905), Lthi_Acc_Y (accuracy: 0.919; precision: 0.919; recall: 0.919; F1-score: 0.919), Lthi_Gyr_X (accuracy: 0.903; precision: 0.903; recall: 0.903; F1-score: 0.903), Lthi_Gyr_Y (accuracy: 0.901; precision: 0.901; recall: 0.901; F1-score: 0.901), Lthi_Gyr_Z (accuracy: 0.901; precision: 0.902; recall: 0.901; F1-score: 0.901), Lthi_Acc_XYZ (accuracy: 0.909; precision: 0.910; recall: 0.909; F1-score: 0.909), and Rthi_Gyr_Y (accuracy: 0.902; precision: 0.903; recall: 0.902; F1-score: 0.902).

Determining the optimal measurement duration

Analysis of optimal sensor and variable combinations

To assess how measurement duration influenced classification, a repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed using classification accuracy (averaged across the three CNN models) as the dependent variable. The top 10 sensor–variable combinations were identified based on mean accuracy across six timepoints. Eight of the top 10 variables were from the left arm (Larm), highlighting its consistent performance; the left thigh (Lthi) and lumbar spine (Lumba) ranked 9th and 10th, respectively. The top-ranked variables were: Larm_Gyr_X: 93.7% (F = 5.24, P = 0.013); Larm_Gyr_Y: 93.0% (F = 1.56, P = 0.257); Larm_Acc_XYZ: 92.3% (F = 33.36, P < 0.001); Larm_Gyr_Z: 92.2% (F = 28.88, P < 0.001); Larm_Acc_X: 92.1% (F = 16.29, P < 0.001); Larm_Gyr_XYZ: 91.1% (F = 17.12, P < 0.001); Larm_Acc_Y: 90.6% (F = 48.57, P < 0.001); Larm_Acc_Z: 90.4% (F = 0.33, P = 0.886); Lthi_Acc_XYZ: 87.2% (F = 31.04, P < 0.001); and, Lumba_Acc_Y: 86.5% (F = 2.61, P = 0.092). These variables demonstrated robust classification performance and statistical significance over time.

Determining the optimal measurement duration based on top variables

To identify the minimum duration required for stable classification, repeated-measures ANOVA and Bonferroni-corrected paired t-tests were conducted across 1–6 min intervals for each top variable. If no significant difference emerged between 1 and 6 min, additional comparisons were made between 1 and 2 min.

For Larm_Gyr_X, Gyr_Y, Acc_XYZ, Gyr_Z, Acc_X, Gyr_XYZ, and Acc_Z, no significant differences were observed between 1 and 6 min (adjusted P ≥ 0.579), or between 1 and 2 min (adjusted P ≥ 0.075), indicating that 1-min measurements suffice. Larm_Acc_Y required 2 min (1 vs. 2 min: adjusted P = 0.034; 2 vs. 6 min: adjusted P = 1.000), and Lthi_Acc_XYZ likewise required ≥ 2 min (1 vs. 6 min: adjusted P = 0.048; 2 vs. 6 min: adjusted P = 0.490). In contrast, Lumba_Acc_Y was stable even at 1 min (adjusted P = 1.000). Results are presented in Supplementary Table S5.

Feature selection and key non-linear gait biomarkers

LASSO-based feature selection identified sensor- and time-specific key variables that best discriminated people with early-stage PD from controls during straight walking (Supplementary Table S6). Across upper-limb and axial sensors, variables reflecting signal complexity (sample entropy) and movement magnitude/smoothness (root mean square [RMS], maximum jerk of angular velocity) were repeatedly selected. Figure 6 presents ROC curves for the 1-min LASSO-selected feature models across the six sensor locations, allowing comparison of classification performance among sensor-specific models.

For the left arm, the strongest overall model performance was observed among the six sensor locations, with AUC values peaking during the early measurement intervals. Across the 2–6 min analyses, the most consistently selected predictors were max_angvel_jerk_Gyr_X, rms_Acc_XYZ, and saen_Acc_XYZ, with AUC values of 0.908 or higher. Notably, the 2 min model achieved the highest AUC (0.929) and included max_angvel_jerk_Gyr_X, rms_Gyr_Y, rms_Acc_XYZ, and saen_Acc_XYZ, suggesting that alterations in upper-limb movement characteristics are detectable within the first 2 min of walking in individuals with early-stage PD.

In the right arm, mean_Acc_XYZ was repeatedly selected across multiple time segments, with AUC values ranging from 0.756 to 0.813, reflecting reduced acceleration magnitude (i.e., diminished arm-swing amplitude) in early-stage PD. Jerk-related variables such as max_jerk_Acc_Z additionally emerged at specific minutes (e.g., 2 and 4 min), implying reduced smoothness and increased movement irregularity.

For the left thigh, the models consistently selected rms_Acc_Y, max_Gyr_Z, mean_Acc_XYZ, saen_Acc_XYZ, and rms_Gyr_XYZ at 2 min (AUC values ≥ 0.867), indicating that reduced lower-limb rotational dynamics and decreased acceleration magnitude were robust lower-limb signatures of early-stage PD across time.

For the right thigh, the models repeatedly selected mean_Gyr_Y and mean_Acc_XYZ, along with jerk-related measures (e.g., max_jerk_Acc_Z), yielding AUC values of 0.756 or higher, highlighting that impaired propulsion-related dynamics and reduced movement smoothness/asymmetry-related signatures are captured most sensitively at the thigh, particularly in the early phase of the task.

For the thoracic sensor, the selected variables emphasized trunk stability and complexity, including rms_Acc_XYZ and sample entropy measures (saen_Gyr_XYZ) as well as acceleration-derived RMS features, suggesting progressive disruption in axial control and altered signal structure during sustained walking.

For the lumbar sensor, models consistently included mean_Acc_XYZ and gyroscope-related magnitude/complexity variables, indicating that reduced overall movement magnitude and altered control patterns at the trunk are informative. AUC values remained above 0.863 across all measurement durations, peaking at 0.869 at 2 min, confirming that lumbar dynamics provide stable discriminatory information even with short measurement durations.

Machine-learning approaches

Machine-learning classification using all 34 non-linear features

Using all 34 non-linear features, five-fold cross-validation across seven classifiers yielded sensor- and time-dependent performance patterns (Table 2). For upper-limb sensors, the left arm achieved the highest accuracies with SVM (SVM; 86.9% at 1 and 6 min), whereas the left thigh showed strong performance with RF (RF; up to 85.6% at 6 min). In contrast, the right thigh achieved moderate-to-high accuracies, peaking around early time segments (e.g., RF = 79.9% at 2 min), and the thoracic and lumbar sensors demonstrated comparatively stable performance with improvements toward later minutes for thoracic (e.g., RF = 81.3% at 6 min) and high early performance for lumbar (LR ≈ 80% at 1–2 min).

Discussion

This study extends our previous work20 by analyzing gait phase- and time-dependent classification of early-stage PD using wearable sensors and CNN. While earlier studies focused on total gait metrics or walking distance13,14, our findings highlight the diagnostic value of segmenting gait—particularly straight walking—and demonstrate the feasibility of reducing measurement duration without compromising accuracy.

Classification accuracy was consistently higher during straight walking across all sensors, likely due to its repetitive, stable nature17,45, which enabled CNN to detect subtle motor deviations more effectively. The Larm_Gyr_X achieved the highest accuracy (95.6% at 1 min), highlighting the diagnostic importance of upper-limb motion46. In addition, our approach used sustained, fast-paced walking with full-body sensors, potentially capturing more consistent motor deviations in early-stage PD4,47. In our previous study, CNN analysis using the full 6-min walk test signals without phase-specific separation achieved a maximum classification accuracy of 83.5% using lumbar gyroscope data20. In contrast, the present study focused on straight-walking segments and demonstrated a peak classification accuracy of 95.6% using left-arm gyroscope data during the first minute of walking, demonstrating the value of separately analyzing straight-walking characteristics to uncover task-specific gait features48.

Sensor-wise, upper-limb sensors—particularly the left and right arms—outperformed trunk sensors during straight walking, frequently exceeding 90% accuracy. Thigh sensors were more predictive during turning, likely reflecting their role in pivoting and balance49. Notably, the lumbar spine sensor, which was previously optimal for non-segmented data, showed only moderate performance here, indicating that sensor relevance is highly phase specific50. The strong performance of the left arm sensor aligns with early-stage PD asymmetry in arm swing51 and supports context-specific sensor selection52.

The left-arm sensor showed the highest classification performance within the present cohort, which may have biomechanical relevance. In the present study, the highest classification accuracy was observed for the gyroscope X-axis signal (Larm_Gyr_X). Based on the anatomical orientation of the upper-arm sensor, the X-axis was aligned with the longitudinal axis of the humerus. Therefore, the Larm_Gyr_X signal represents the angular velocity about the long axis of the upper arm. This component may reflect axial rotational characteristics of the upper limb that occur in coordination with natural arm swing and trunk rotation during walking. In healthy individuals, reciprocal arm swing is accompanied by coordinated shoulder and trunk rotation, whereas individuals with PD may show reduced arm-swing amplitude, bradykinesia, rigidity, and impaired interlimb coordination53. These disease-related changes may be associated with altered upper-limb axial rotation and shoulder–trunk coordination during gait. Therefore, abnormalities in arm-swing-associated upper-limb axial rotation and coordination may have contributed to the strong discriminatory performance of the Larm_Gyr_X signal observed in the present study.

The superior performance of the left-arm sensor may reflect PD-related asymmetry. Although PD often presents unilaterally during the early stages3,54, symptom laterality does not necessarily correspond to limb dominance55, suggesting substantial inter-individual variability56. In the present cohort, 39 of 78 participants with PD (50.0%) exhibited left-side symptom predominance, whereas 29 participants (37.2%) exhibited right-side predominance and 10 participants (12.8%) showed bilateral involvement (Table 1). Therefore, the observed left-arm sensor performance should be interpreted with caution, as it may partially reflect the symptom-laterality distribution of the present cohort rather than indicate a universally optimal sensor placement. Because the primary objective of this study was to distinguish individuals with early-stage PD from controls, rather than to directly quantify side-specific gait asymmetry, the relative contributions of symptom laterality, arm-swing asymmetry, and limb dominance could not be fully separated.

To complement this interpretation, we performed an exploratory sensitivity analysis in which upper-arm sensor data were realigned according to the more-affected side (MAS) and less-affected side (LAS), given the potential influence of symptom laterality (Supplementary Table S8). Overall, MAS-aligned upper-arm signals showed higher classification performance than LAS-aligned signals, suggesting that symptom laterality may influence sensor-specific classification performance. However, because this analysis was limited to upper-arm sensors and was not designed to fully disentangle anatomical side, limb dominance, and symptom laterality, these findings should be interpreted as supportive rather than confirmatory. These results further support the need to consider symptom laterality while interpreting the apparent left-arm advantage observed in the present cohort57.

To further characterize gait, we analyzed frequency-domain patterns for each minute of the SMWT, focusing on straight walking in individuals with early-stage PD and healthy controls (Fig. 5). For straight walking, the highest classification accuracy was observed in the Gyr_X component of the left arm. Differences in frequency patterns between people with early-stage PD and controls likely influenced the generated images and contributed to classification accuracy20,58. Building on this, we further extended the analysis by incorporating non-linear gait features that capture signal complexity, variability, and dynamic irregularity and evaluated their discriminative value using machine-learning models59,60.

Although certain sensors showed peak performance at specific minutes, the present findings collectively indicate that early-stage PD manifests as distributed motor alterations across the upper limbs, lower limbs, and trunk61. The arm sensors primarily captured changes in swing amplitude, acceleration and angular velocity magnitude, and signal complexity (e.g., entropy-related measures), whereas the thigh sensors reflected propulsion/stride regulation-related dynamics and reduced smoothness (e.g., mean acceleration/gyroscope variables and jerk/angular velocity-jerk terms)51. Trunk sensors (thoracic and lumbar spine) contributed information on postural control and axial stability, with time-dependent increases in thoracic discriminatory capacity suggesting accumulating control demands during sustained walking62. These findings suggest that relying solely on a single sensor may be insufficient and that concurrent consideration of appendicular and axial signatures could improve the physiological interpretability of early-stage PD63.

From a practical screening perspective, our minute-by-minute analyses suggest that shortened measurement durations can still preserve meaningful discriminatory information13,14. Consistent with this finding, the LASSO-based analyses demonstrated that a relatively small set of non-linear gait features could provide strong discriminatory performance within a short walking duration. Among all sensor locations, the left thigh achieved the highest classification accuracy at 1 min (RF = 89.3%), followed by the left arm, which demonstrated strong performance (KNN = 83.1%). The repeated selection of max_angvel_jerk_Gyr_X, rms_Acc_XYZ, and saen_Acc_XYZ suggests that movement magnitude, smoothness, and signal complexity may be particularly informative sensor-derived characteristics for identifying early-stage PD. These findings support that approximately 2 min of straight-walking data may be sufficient for capturing prominent upper- and lower-limb motor signatures of early-stage PD63.

In contrast, axial sensors appeared to benefit from slightly longer durations to capture time-dependent postural-control changes. The thoracic spine models achieved strong performance with selected features at around 3 min (RF = 83.3% at 3 min), consistent with the interpretation that sustained walking imposes growing stabilization demands that may reveal subtle axial rigidity and control deficits in early-stage PD62. Moreover, the lumbar spine sensor achieved strong performance with selected features at around 4 min (RF = 88.5% at 4 min), indicating that a ~ 4-min window can adequately reflect trunk-related control signatures while remaining feasible for clinical or community-based screening settings61.

Overall, minute-by-minute non-linear and machine-learning analyses demonstrated that early-stage PD-related motor differences are not uniform across time; instead, discriminatory information emerges in sensor-specific temporal patterns. By combining appendicular and axial perspectives and adopting optimized short durations (2 min for arm and thigh sensors; approximately 3 min for lumbar and thoracic sensors), the proposed approach offers a feasible and interpretable strategy for early-stage PD screening using straight-walking data. Compared with previous machine-learning studies that classified early-stage PD using fast-paced or continuous walking tasks, which reported classification accuracies of 67.3%, the present approach achieved substantially higher performance64. This may be attributed to the combined use of non-linear gait features, sensor-specific temporal optimization, and straight-walking segments that emphasize subtle motor deficits while minimizing task-induced variability65. Some variability in classification performance was observed across time intervals across several sensor–classifier combinations. These fluctuations may reflect temporal changes in gait behavior during prolonged walking12–14. Future studies are needed to determine whether fatigue, motor adaptation, or other time-dependent factors contribute to these performance changes.

Although the SMWT is a clinical standard, 1 min of straight-walking data may be sufficient for accurate early-stage PD classification using left arm sensors66. Most top-performing features, including Larm_Gyr_X, showed no significant accuracy difference between 1 and 6 min, with peak performance at 1 min. These results support the feasibility of brief, task-specific gait assessments that reduce physical burden and simplify clinical workflows13,14. Previous studies also endorsed abbreviated testing: Bohannon et al.13 reported strong correlations between 2-min and full SMWT distances (r = 0.968), while Valet et al.14 found that the 2-min distance accounted for 98% of total SMWT performance in patients with stroke. However, distance-based metrics may overlook subtle motor deficits. In contrast, our CNN-based TS analysis captured finer spatiotemporal gait abnormalities relevant to early-stage PD67.

The stability of the classification accuracy across time intervals indicates that properly segmented, short gait recordings can effectively detect motor impairments14. This aligns with recent multiple sclerosis studies showing time-dependent gait changes even within short intervals16. Unlike prior studies focusing on stride length or gait speed, our phase-specific deep learning approach enabled the granular analysis of motor features. Most top variables stabilized within 1–2 min, supporting the feasibility of time-efficient screening protocols68,69.

Despite these contributions, this study has several limitations. First, all data were collected in the medication “ON” state to ensure participant safety and reflect commonly encountered clinical walking conditions during functional mobility assessments. However, dopaminergic medication may partially attenuate disease-related motor abnormalities and mask subtle gait characteristics associated with early-stage PD. Therefore, the observed classification performance may reflect gait patterns influenced by medication status rather than untreated disease-specific motor manifestations alone70. Future research should include “ON” and “OFF” states to evaluate generalizability70. Second, although we observed significant group differences in K-MMSE25–27 and the Montreal Cognitive Assessment71,72 scores, larger and more demographically diverse samples are needed to improve generalizability. Validation in external cohorts with different regional and population characteristics is also warranted to ensure robustness. Third, this study focused only on early-stage PD (H&Y stages 1–2). Including later stages could enhance the understanding of disease progression and improve classification performance. Another limitation is that symptom laterality and limb dominance were not explicitly incorporated into the primary classification framework. Because arm-swing characteristics may reflect both disease-related asymmetry and natural differences between dominant and non-dominant limbs, future studies should incorporate handedness information and investigate the combined effects of symptom laterality and limb dominance on sensor-specific classification performance. In addition, gait initiation, acceleration, steady-state walking, and turning phases should be examined separately to better characterize their respective contributions to early PD classification. Although the SMWT provides a standardized assessment of sustained walking performance, the controlled hallway environment, supervised testing procedures, and standardized instructions differ from real-world walking conditions. Future studies should validate these findings using home-based assessments, long-term wearable monitoring systems, and smartphone-based sensor data collected during daily activities. Fourth, the use of uniform linear interpolation to standardize segment length and image resizing to match CNN input dimensions may still affect the temporal or spatial representation of gait signals. Therefore, the possibility that input-standardization procedures influenced the CNN representations cannot be completely excluded. Future studies should compare alternative strategies, such as fixed-duration cropping, masking-based CNN, or variable-length sequence models, to further evaluate the robustness of the proposed framework. Finally, while our CNN-based supervised learning approach demonstrated high classification accuracy, its limited interpretability remains a challenge. Future studies should integrate explainable AI techniques and explore unsupervised or semi-supervised methods such as clustering or representation learning to uncover latent gait phenotypes and improve model interpretability29. Applying this analytical framework to older adults with a history of falls may also enable early classification of prodromal or early-stage PD by detecting subtle gait abnormalities predictive of fall risk, while also offering a low-cost, scalable solution for quantitative gait assessment in various settings, including home-based monitoring60.

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that patients with early-stage PD can be accurately classified using deep-learning models applied to phase-segmented gait data collected from wearable sensors. By separating straight and turning gait phases and analyzing time-dependent classification performance, we identified straight gait––particularly within the first minute––as the most diagnostically informative segment. Among the evaluated sensor–variable combinations, the highest classification accuracy was observed for left-arm gyroscope data (Larm_Gyr_X) within the present cohort, highlighting the potential value of upper-limb motion characteristics for early-stage PD detection. Complementary feature-based machine-learning analyses further showed that a small subset of non-linear gait features achieved classification performance comparable to models using the full feature set, providing physiologically interpretable biomarkers that supported the deep-learning results. These findings support the development of time-efficient, phase-specific, and interpretable digital gait assessments for early PD screening. Furthermore, longitudinal analysis of minute-by-minute non-linear feature trends could provide additional insights into disease trajectory and progression73. Future research should extend this framework to include diverse disease stages and medication states and explore unsupervised learning approaches to uncover latent motor phenotypes.
