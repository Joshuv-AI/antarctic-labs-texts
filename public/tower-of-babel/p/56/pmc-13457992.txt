Statistics in Motion: Does the Infant Motor System Predict Actions Based on Their Transitional Probability?
Authors: Tommaso Ghilardi, Marlene Meyer, Claire D. Monroy, Sarah A. Gerson, Sabine Hunnius
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMotor theories of action prediction propose that our neural motor system combines prior knowledge about action outcomes with current sensory input to predict other people's behavior. This knowledge can be acquired through observational experience, more specifically statistical learning. Recently, it has been shown that infants can detect in a stream of actions two actions that follow each other deterministically and that their motor system uses this knowledge to predict upcoming actions. However, real‐life actions are more complex: actions rarely follow one another with 100% probability, certain actions are more likely to follow one another than others. Here, we examined whether infants can learn the statistical structure of action sequences through observation and whether the activity of their motor system reflects the statistical likelihood of upcoming actions. We trained 18‐month‐old infants at home with videos of action sequences featuring different transitional probabilities. At test, motor activity was measured using EEG during identical time windows that linked actions with four probability levels. While the planned analysis did not reveal a significant effect of probability, an exploratory analysis revealed that infants' motor activity over the left‐central hemisphere showed a linear relationship with transitional probability of action pairs specifically in the beginning of the experiment, when infants were not fatigued, yet. Predictive motor activity was strongest when the probability was highest and weakest when no prediction was involved. These results suggest that infants' motor system is sensitive to the statistical likelihood of upcoming actions and underline the important role of statistical learning for infants' developing action understanding.

Introduction

As adults, we are good at making predictions about how an action we observe will likely unfold (Flanagan and Johansson 2003). When we perceive someone reaching for a coffee mug, for instance, we readily anticipate they will next bring the mug to their mouth and then drink from it. This ability allows us to navigate our social world, infer the goals and mental states of others (Becchio et al. 2012; Gallese and Goldman 1998; Ondobaka et al. 2015), and shape our social interactions accordingly (Sebanz and Knoblich 2009). However, the same action can potentially lead to various subsequent actions. For example, grasping a mug can indeed lead to drinking, but it can also be followed by adding sugar to it or passing the mug to someone else. Recent probabilistic accounts based on principles of predictive processing suggest that in order to overcome the ambiguity of the upcoming action and predict the most likely one, we combine sensory input with prior knowledge (Kahl and Kopp 2018; Kilner et al. 2007; Ondobaka et al. 2015; Wolpert et al. 2003). For instance, we might know from experience that, most of the time, when people reach for a mug, they subsequently bring it to their mouth and only sometimes do they reach for a spoon to add some sugar. According to such probabilistic accounts, we integrate our current observation (i.e., someone grasping a cup) with our prior knowledge to make an action prediction. Although predictive processing theories might provide a solution to the ambiguity problem, they do not explain how novel action knowledge could be acquired in the first place.

The acquisition of new action knowledge is crucial for interacting in a social world. Although young infants have little action experience of their own, they are able to quickly acquire novel action knowledge and interact with others. Already at a young age, infants are able to anticipate the outcome of observed actions and the trajectories of an actor's movement as they unfold (Ambrosini et al. 2013; Cannon and Woodward 2012; Kanakogi and Itakura 2011; Stapel et al. 2010). Even though they have only observed these actions, infants predictively look to the mouth when they see a person grasp a cup and to the ear when they see her pick up a phone (Hunnius and Bekkering 2010). Indeed, observational experience has been shown to play a key role in the processing and understanding of others' actions, especially during early development. For instance, it has been shown that infants have powerful statistical learning skills—the ability to detect regularities in continuous sensory input (Ruffman et al. 2012; Saffran and Kirkham 2017; Tummeltshammer et al. 2017). These skills might also allow infants to pick up information about the regularities in their social environment (Hunnius and Bekkering 2014; Ruffman et al. 2012). In the context of action observation, recent studies have demonstrated that both adults and infants are able to extract regularities from continuous streams of human action (Ahlheim et al. 2014; Baldwin et al. 2008; Monroy et al. 2017b; Stahl et al. 2014) and make predictive eye movements based on statistical regularities to the location of an upcoming action before it occurs (Monroy et al. 2017b). These findings provide evidence that adults and infants can use their statistical learning skills to acquire new action knowledge.

The prediction of an upcoming action has been traced back to cortical pre‐motor regions (Debnath et al. 2019; Elsner et al. 2013; Kilner et al. 2004), the same areas that are active during action execution are involved in generating predictions about actions. As measured using electroencephalography (EEG), motor activation is reflected by a suppression in power of the alpha frequency band over sensorimotor regions, also called mu suppression. Oscillations in this band are thought to originate from sensorimotor cortex and are identified as maximal over central electrode sites (Pineda 2005). Mu suppression has been demonstrated during action observation both in adults (Gastaut and Bert 1954; Muthukumaraswamy et al. 2004) and in infants (Fox et al. 2016; Southgate et al. 2009; Elk et al. 2008). Additionally, various EEG studies show that the motor system is activated even prior to the onset of action stimuli (Kilner et al. 2004; Southgate et al. 2009). Converging evidence suggests that this predictive activation over the motor area during action observation constitutes a prediction of the upcoming action, generated by the motor system (Elsner et al. 2013; Stapel et al. 2016). These findings lead to the question: how does new knowledge about actions translate into predictive neural motor activity?

To investigate this question, Monroy and colleagues (Monroy, Meyer, et al. 2019) tested the hypothesis that knowledge about action sequences can be acquired through visual statistical learning and fed into infants' neural motor system to predict upcoming actions. In this study, 18‐month‐old infants were first afforded visual experiences with streams of actions for 21 minutes over 3 days at home. They observed videos of action sequences in which a deterministic pair of actions occurred among a random sequence of actions. In other words, whereas one action was always followed by another specific action, making this second action 100% predictable, the remaining actions in the sequence could occur in any order and were not more predictable than chance. Following the training, infants participated in an EEG experiment in which they observed a novel sequence featuring the same statistical structure (same probabilities between actions) of actions as in the training phase, while their neural motor activity was recorded. Results demonstrated that during the time windows that preceded predictable (deterministic) actions, infants' mu rhythm was suppressed over sensorimotor cortex, reflecting an increase in motor activity in anticipation of the upcoming action. Therefore, infants were able to predict the upcoming action based on statistical regularities learned through visual observation, and this prediction was reflected in their motor system. While this study provides initial evidence on how infants might translate observational experience into action predictions in the motor system, it leaves open a crucial question. In their study, Monroy and colleagues compared action sequences that were 100% predictable (i.e., deterministic) with random actions. Yet, in everyday life, very few actions are linked in a deterministic fashion. Rather, in most cases, one action can precede a variety of different subsequent actions, as in the cup example described above.

If, as suggested by Monroy and colleagues (Monroy, Meyer, et al. 2019), statistical learning is an important skill that supports infants' action predictions, then infants should be sensitive to different transitional probabilities of upcoming actions and represent them in their predictive action models. On the contrary, if statistical information is not directly reflected in motor activity, infants may have different strategies to predict actions, for example, predicting only high probability events without representing low probability transitions. There are some first indications from adult research that the probability we allocate to different upcoming actions is reflected by the activation of the neural motor system (Ahlheim et al. 2014; Braukmann et al. 2017; Ghilardi et al. 2023; Monroy, Meyer, et al. 2019; Tzagarakis et al. 2010). These studies use different methodologies, yet report converging results: the activity of the sensorimotor areas after perceiving an action is modulated by the predictability of the following action. For instance, Ahlheim et al. (2014) investigated whether human observers spontaneously exploit the statistical structure underlying continuous action sequences. In a functional magnetic resonance imaging experiment, participants were trained in an action observation paradigm, viewing video clips showing sequences with varying numbers of action steps and transitional probabilities (e.g., from 25% to 75%). The results of the study showed that an action step's predictability activated numerous areas, including sensorimotor regions. Yet it remains unclear if infants, like adults, can extract varying transitional probabilities between actions, and if they represent this knowledge in their motor systems.

The current study tested the hypothesis that infants can learn varying transitional probabilities between actions through observation and that the activation of their neural motor system upon seeing an action is modulated by these transitional probabilities. We made use of the same paradigm and age group as Monroy and colleagues (Monroy, Meyer, et al. 2019) with the crucial manipulation of parametrically varying the transitional probabilities between actions. During a learning phase, 18‐month‐old infants watched videos at home of action sequences containing four levels of transitional probability (25%, 50%, 75%, 100%). As in Monroy, Meyer, et al. (2019), infants then participated in an EEG experiment in the laboratory in which they were presented with an action sequence featuring the same statistical structure of the sequences they observed at home. To assess whether the motor system represents the transitional probabilities within action sequences, we assessed power in the mu frequency band in the time window between the paired actions. We hypothesized that the activity over the motor area (C3, Cz, and C4) would be modulated parametrically by transitional probability, resulting in the highest activation for actions that were 100% probable and lower activation for actions that were only 25% probable. Additionally, given that motor predictions may be reflected across broader sensorimotor regions, we planned to examine extended central electrode clusters to ensure comprehensive coverage of predictive motor activity.

While suppression of alpha power over central brain regions (i.e., mu rhythm) reflects motor processes, alpha power over occipital regions reflects visual processes (Bowman et al. 2017; Fox et al. 2016; Hobson and Bishop 2016). To investigate the specificity of the observed suppression to the motor system, we also examined the activity in the mu frequency band over the visual cortex. We expected to find no difference between the probability conditions over occipital channels (O1, Oz, and O2) as infants received identical visual input.

Method

Participants

To determine sample size, we conducted a power analysis using GPower 3.1 (Erdfelder et al. 2009; Faul et al. 2007). Based on Monroy, Meyer, et al. (2019) effect size (η2 p = 0.16) and our additional probability conditions, we conservatively estimated a medium effect size (η2 p = 0.09), indicating 20 participants would provide adequate power (95%, α = 0.05). We aimed to recruit 28 valid participants to match Monroy, Meyer, et al. (2019). Given typical infant EEG attrition rates (Stets et al. 2012), we planned to test up to 60 participants to achieve 20–28 valid datasets, stopping when either criterion was met. We recruited 59 participants (mean age: 19.46 months, SD: 10.46 days; 26 males) from a database of interested families from the surrounding region of Nijmegen, a middle‐sized city in the Netherlands (Ethical approval ECSW2016‐0905‐396). Of these, 17 refused the EEG cap, leaving 42 who completed recording. After artifact rejection and applying inclusion criteria (see EEG processing), our final sample comprised 29 infants (mean age: 19.5 months, SD: 9.35 days, range: 18.8–19.9 months; 16 males).

We tested 18‐month‐old infants because, in addition to allowing for a direct comparison with the foundational work of Monroy, Meyer, et al. (2019), at this age infants have typically gained sufficient motor experience to understand the demonstrated goal‐directed actions (Cannon and Woodward 2012; McCarty et al. 2001; Monroy et al. 2017b). Moreover, 18‐month‐olds have demonstrated robust statistical learning abilities (Monroy et al. 2017a), and possess the attentional capacity necessary for our paradigm.

Stimuli and Materials

The stimulus materials included the same individual action videos as in Monroy and colleagues (Monroy, Meyer, et al. 2019) with the only modifications being the statistical structure (transitional probabilities between actions, see Figure 1). More specifically, three training videos and one test video were created, with identical duration (8.6 min) and statistical structure. Videos featured an adult performing an action sequence on a box‐shaped toy which afforded six object‐directed actions. Action sequences consisted of action pairs, a cue (first) action and a paired (second) action (see Figure 2), linked by different levels of transition probabilities as displayed in Figure 1: 100%, 75%, 50%, and 25%.

Example of a transition matrix ruling the presented action sequences. Three transition matrices were counterbalanced across participants, each maintaining the same probability structure (100%, 75%, 50%, 25%) but with different action pair assignments. Rows depict the first action (cue), columns show possible second actions with their transition probabilities. In this example, Squeeze (first row) was always followed by Push (probability = 100%), while Open could be followed by Tilt (probability = 25%) or Slide (probability = 75%). These probabilities are based on the videos presented to infants that were composed of 72 trials.

Example frames from video stimuli illustrating two trials. Trials began with a static fixation cross, followed by four successive clips played in one continuous movie (7s in total). EEG markers were time‐locked to the onset of each clip. Yellow boxes indicated two condition time windows used for comparison in the EEG analysis: conditions were perceptually identical (a 1s still frame with no action occurring), but in the Deterministic condition, infants could make a certain specific prediction about the subsequent action based on statistical likelihood, whereas in the 75% condition they could make a prediction accounting for two different outcomes with different probabilities. The fixation cross was used as a 0% condition in the analysis.

Crucially, action pairs and their transitional probabilities remained identical across all training and test videos per infant; only the temporal order of presentation varied (hence “novel sequence”). The 100% pairs always occurred in the same order (completely predictable; e.g., “squeeze‐push”). The 75% pairs had the cue action followed by the paired action 75% of the time and another action 25% of the time (e.g., “slide” was followed by “bend” in 75% of cases and by “squeeze” in 25%). The 50% pairs had the cue action followed by one action half the time and two other actions equally in the remaining instances. The 25% pairs consisted of three cue actions, each followed by four actions with equal probability. The 1‐s fixation cross served as the baseline condition.

Figure 2 illustrates the presentation of an action pair. Each trial started with 1‐s presentation of a fixation cross and 1‐s still frame of the toy. The two actions, each with a duration of 2 seconds, were next performed consecutively. The two actions were interleaved with a still frame of the toy presented for 1 second, constituting the predictive time window of interest. After the paired action a new trial was presented, separated by a fixation cross from the previous action pair. Each stimulus video featured 12 deterministic pairs (100% transitional probability), 12 pairs with a transitional probability of 75%, 12 pairs with a transitional probability of 50% and 36 pairs with a transitional probability of 25%. Thus, infants were presented with 144 individual actions (72 action pairs) for each video for a total of 432 individual actions during the learning phase. All cue and paired actions were presented an identical number of times (i.e., 12) to ensure that infants received equal exposure to all six actions. Videos were divided into four blocks (18 action pairs each). Halfway each block and between each block an attention grabber was displayed. In each block, the orientation of the toy‐box relative to the camera was rotated to one of the four possible positions perpendicularly to the observer. This ensures that infants were not simply learning the physical location of each action on the screen, as the objects' spatial locations were dissociated from their temporal position in the action sequence. This was identical to the stimulus presentation of Monroy, Meyer, et al. (2019). In addition, as in Monroy, Meyer, et al. (2019), we imposed the following constraints: no pair could occur more than two times consecutively and pairs could not consist of the same action twice. One modification of the Monroy, Meyer, et al. (2019) paradigm was that videos were accompanied by a musical background of several instrumental songs for children. The music was included in order to maintain infants’ attention throughout the session, taking into consideration the slightly longer action sequences in our study compared with Monroy, Meyer, et al. (2019). Thus, all stimulus features except for the additional transitional probabilities and the use of music soundtracks to maintain infants' attention were identical to Monroy, Meyer, et al. (2019).

Procedure

Training Phase

For the training phase, we initially planned to provide parents with an encrypted USB‐drive containing a stand‐alone program implemented in Python (https://www.python.org). However, to reduce burden on families, we instead used a web interface implemented with jsPsych (de Leeuw 2015).

Parents received three links via email and were instructed to show their infants one video per day during the 3 days preceding the test phase. Although the videos differed in their specific sequence of presented action pairs, they maintained the same statistical structure. The web interface recorded infants watching the videos, allowing offline coding of visual attention. Recordings were saved directly on Radboud University servers to ensure privacy.

Parents were instructed to present videos in a quiet setting with minimal distraction and to seat infants on their lap approximately 60 cm from the screen, recreating the laboratory conditions infants would experience during the EEG session.

Test Phase: EEG Session

Test sessions took place on the day following the last day of training and consisted of an action observation phase followed by an action execution phase, as in Monroy, Meyer, et al. (2019). After familiarization of parent and infant with the environment, the experimenter fitted an infant‐sized EEG cap on the infant (ActiCap, Brain Products, Munich, Germany) with 32 active electrodes arranged according to the standard 10–20 configuration. To optimize electrode contact and reduce impedance in the active electrode EEG set‐up, a conductive gel was applied to the electrodes. We strived to keep impedance levels below 20 kOhms. Following capping, EEG was recorded continuously while the infant was sitting on their caretaker's lap in a shielded testing room. The EEG signal was referenced online to FCz and re‐referenced offline to the average of all electrodes, after excluding noisy channels and epochs. The signal was amplified using a BrainAmp amplifier, band‐pass filtered (0.1–125 Hz) and digitized at 500 Hz.

The test video was displayed on a monitor, at approximately 60 cm distance from the infant and caretaker. The infants' behavior was monitored online and recorded on video from an adjacent room by the experimenter. When an infant looked away from the screen for longer than an entire trial (7 seconds), the experimenter displayed an additional attention‐grabber between trials to regain the infant's attention to the screen. After completing all the trials or when unable to regain infant's attention, the action execution phase was initiated. The purpose of this phase was to identify the sample‐specific mu frequency range, as defined by a decrease in power over central channels during overt movement relative to baseline (Meyer et al. 2016; Monroy, Meyer, et al. 2019). The experimenter turned off the video monitor and placed an infant‐sized version of the toy stimulus in front of the infant, allowing them to play for several minutes or until they lost interest. The entire session was video‐recorded for offline movement and behavior analysis.

Data Analysis

Coding for Visual Attention (Training Phase)

Video recordings of the training phase were coded offline frame‐by‐frame to quantify infants' visual attention to the screen. The coder documented whether the infant's gaze was directed to the screen or not. That allowed us to calculate the percentage of time infants looked at the screen for each session. We had planned to exclude infants from the analysis if they had watched less than 20% of a video for more than one session. However, none of the infants met this criterion, so no infants were excluded. The total duration of visual attention to the training videos across all three sessions was calculated and included as a covariate (“training time”) in subsequent analyses to control for potential effects of visual exposure during home training.

Coding for Movement Artifacts and Visual Attention (Test Phase)

Video recordings of the observation phase during the EEG session were coded offline. More specifically, each trial was divided in 2 epochs of interest (i.e., the fixation cross and 1‐s predictive time window, see Figure 2). Epochs were coded frame‐by‐frame for whole body (e.g., kicking) and single‐limb movements (e.g., moving one arm) as well as looking behavior to the screen. Any epoch during the fixation cross and during the predictive time window containing movements was removed from the analyses to exclude overt movement as source of neural motor activity during action observation (Marshall et al. 2011; Warreyn et al. 2013). Epochs during the cue action presentation and during the predictive time window in which the infant was not looking at the screen were discarded from further analyses.

Identification of the Mu Rhythm

To identify the sample‐specific mu rhythm frequency band, video recordings of the action execution phase were coded offline, and epochs were selected during infant reaching or manipulation of the toy‐box. Only 31 participants (73.8%) had usable action execution epochs and were included in this analysis phase (mean age: 19.46 months, SD: 10.2 days, range: 18.6–20 months; 12 males). Epochs were segmented into 1‐s trials for data processing (see EEG processing). Power was analyzed in the 2–30 Hz range over central electrodes (C3, Cz, C4) (Meyer et al. 2016; Monroy, Meyer, et al. 2019; Perry et al. 2010) to visually identify the alpha range in which action execution was lower than baseline. The fixation cross presented during the observation phase was used as the baseline for this comparison. In line with previous research (Meyer et al. 2016; Monroy, Meyer, et al. 2019), data inspection indicated a clear negative alpha peak around 8 Hz (+/−1 Hz) which power during action execution was lower compared to baseline (see Figure 3). The extracted sample‐specific frequency range was used to compare our conditions of interest in the action observation phase.

EEG Processing

Data was analyzed using Fieldtrip, an open source toolbox for Matlab (Oostenveld et al. 2011). Continuous data was segmented into 1‐s epochs as follows: baseline epochs were defined as the 1‐s fixation cross period and for the experimental conditions the 1‐s time window of the still frame preceding the second action was analyzed (i.e., the predictive time window, see Figure 2). Epochs were first de‐trended (linear trends were removed to eliminate slow signal drifts) and then visually inspected for artifacts such as noisy channels or muscle artifacts (Marshall and Meltzoff 2011; Meyer et al. 2016). Any epochs containing artifacts were removed, and noisy channels were interpolated using FieldTrip's spherical spline method, which computes weighted averages across all available channels based on spatial distance. During EEG setup, we prioritized impedance optimisation for our regions of interest (C3, Cz, C4 for sensorimotor activity; O1, Oz, O2 for occipital control analyses). Across the final sample, the mean number of interpolated channels was 5.55 (SD = 3.01), with rejection concentrated in peripheral rather than task‐relevant electrodes. Central electrodes C3 and C4 were never rejected, Cz required interpolation in 5 participants, and occipital electrodes showed minimal rejection (O1 = 3 participants; O2 = 2; Oz = 1). Independent component analysis (ICA) using the logistic infomax algorithm was run on the epochs to correct for eye movement related components. After ICA, epochs were again visually inspected for remaining artifacts and re‐referenced to the average of all electrodes. After artifact rejection, epochs were transformed using a Fast Fourier transformation using the multitaper method (Hanning taper) to estimate power values in the sample‐specific frequency range (see Identification of the mu rhythm), resulting in a single power value for each trial. Finally, power values across the central electrodes (C3, Cz, C4) were log‐transformed for statistical analyses. In addition, power values over occipital electrodes (O1, Oz, O2) were log‐transformed. As outlined in our initial analysis plan, we intended to follow the procedure of Monroy, Meyer, et al. (2019) by including in the final data set only participants who had contributed at least three artifact‐free trials per experimental condition. Given the additional conditions in our study compared to Monroy, Meyer, et al. (2019), we had to modify the inclusion criteria. As a result, participants were excluded from the analysis if, after trial rejection, they did not have at least one clean trial for each of the five conditions (baseline, 25%, 50%, 75%, 100%) and at least two trials for three of the conditions. In addition, participants were excluded if they had fewer than 19 usable channels. In the end, 29 infants were included in the analysis (mean age: 18.6 months, SD: 3.4; 15 males). Analysis

We preregistered a repeated‐measures ANCOVA with normalized mu rhythm power over central electrodes (C3, Cz, C4) as the dependent variable, Condition (baseline, 25%, 50%, 75%, 100%) as the within‐subjects factor, and training time as a covariate. However, after artifact rejection, participants had unequal trial numbers and some missing observations. We therefore used linear mixed‐effects models instead, which handle missing data and unbalanced designs while modeling hierarchical variance structure (Heise et al. 2022).

We fitted linear mixed‐effects models (function “lmer”; package “lme4” in R; Bates et al. 2015) using mu rhythm log‐power over central sites (C3, Cz, C4) as the dependent variable. Probability level (baseline, 25%, 50%, 75%, 100%) was entered as a categorical fixed effect and training time as a covariate. To account for repeated testing and the different distribution of activity over the three channels, all models included random intercepts per electrode nested within participant (Ghilardi et al. 2023; Ibrahim et al. 2013; Karas et al. 2019; Metzger et al. 2020; Verweij et al. 2014). Subsequently, we extracted F‐tests and p‐values using the ANOVA function of the LmerTest package (Kuznetsova et al. 2017). We refer to this approach as linear mixed‐effect model ANOVA. Finally, we performed contrast analyses with Holm corrections using the Modelbased package (Makowski et al. 2020). All model assumptions (linearity, normality, homoscedasticity) were verified and met.

In line with our study's predefined analysis plan, we assessed mu rhythm activity beyond the predefined electrode cluster to systematically identify the optimal spatial configuration for detecting motor‐related activity in our paradigm. We conducted linear mixed‐effects models over three additional clusters: in addition to the small cluster over central channels (C3, Cz, C4), we examined an extended cluster over central channels (FC1, C3, CP1, Cz, FC2, C4, CP2), a right‐central cluster (FC2, CP2, C4, FC6, CP6) and a left‐central cluster (FC1, CP1, C3, FC5, CP5). We used the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) to compare the resulting models. AIC determines the relative information value of the model using the maximum likelihood estimate and the number of parameters (independent variables) in the model. The smaller the AIC value, the better the model fit. The cluster with the lowest AIC value was selected as the best model and subsequently considered as the best one to explore the motor activity in relation to the probability.

To investigate whether effects of transitional probability on alpha power were specific to action processes or also reflected in visual processing, we also examined the activity in the same frequency range over the visual cortex (O1, Oz, O2). Such an approach is also suggested by Cuevas et al. (2014) as a best practice for infant EEG studies that focus on the mu rhythm. For this purpose, we conducted the same statistical comparison as described above, with probability entered as a categorical fixed effect, for power values from central electrode sites for occipital electrode sites. Based on Monroy, Meyer, et al. (2019) we expected to find less suppression of alpha power during the baseline (fixation cross) in comparison with the other predictive time window conditions (displaying the toy stimulus) regardless of the upcoming action probability.

In addition to the planned analyses, we conducted exploratory Bayesian linear mixed‐effects models over central and occipital channels. Our planned analyses assumed homogeneous activation patterns over time and across central channels irrespective of laterality. However, true effects might be specific to particular temporal or spatial domains. We therefore included two additional dimensions: channels and time. Channels were added as an interaction term to account for possible lateralization effects, as found in previous infant studies during action observation (Shen et al. 2017; Southgate et al. 2010, 2009). Time (trial number) was included to account for time‐dependent variables such as fatigue and boredom.

We chose a Bayesian framework for its ability to quantify evidence strength, allowing more nuanced interpretation beyond significance testing. Following Ghilardi et al. (2023), we treated probability as a continuous variable, assigning 0% probability to the fixation cross period (when the toy box was absent).

The model included a 3‐way interaction between probability, channels, and time point (trial) and had mu rhythm log power over central sites (C3, Cz, C4) as the dependent variable. To control for the non‐independence of data points resulting from repeated measurements on the same participants, the model incorporated a random intercept for each participant. Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling methods were utilized to estimate the model, which was fitted using the “brms” package (Bürkner 2017). Full model specifications are provided in the supplementary materials.

A topographic representation of the power‐frequency variation for every level of expected probability is included in Supporting Information S1: Figure 1.

Results

Motor Area

Planned Analyses

The linear mixed‐effects model ANOVA conducted over the sensorimotor cortex did not reveal a significant effect of probability (F(4, 4095.6) = 1.27, p = 0.279; Eta2 (partial) = 1.24e‐03, 95% CI [0.00, 1.00]) or of training time (F(1, 27.2) = 0.01, p = 0.911; Eta2 (partial) = 4.65e‐04, 95% CI [0.00, 1.00]). As previously planned, given the non‐significant effect of training time, a second model only including the factor probability was run. This model also did not yield a significant effect of probability (F(4, 4095.7) = 1.27, p = 0.279; Eta2 (partial) = 1.24e‐03, 95% CI [0.00, 1.00]). Thus, these findings do not provide evidence for an effect of probability level of the upcoming actions on mu rhythm activity over central channels.

The model comparison conducted on the different central clusters revealed that the small predefined electrode cluster (C4, Cz, C3) had the lowest AIC score (see Table 2). This result validates our selection of this particular cluster as the optimal choice for monitoring mu suppression during action prediction.

Exploratory Analyses

The Bayesian linear mixed‐effects model demonstrated a significant main effect of time point (median β = −1.7e‐03, 89%CI = [−3.1e‐03, −3e‐04]), indicating increased suppression of the mu rhythm over time. Furthermore, the model identified a significant channel‐by‐probability interaction (median β = −1.7e‐03, 89% CI = [−3.4e‐03, −1.1e‐04]), indicating that the effect of probability on mu suppression differed across channels. To gain a clearer understanding of this interaction, we utilized the “estimate_slopes” function from the “modelbased” package (Makowski et al. 2020). This function calculates the rate of change, or “slope”, in mu rhythm suppression for probability levels across channels. This analysis, however, revealed that the slope of probability was not significant for any of the three channels. This may be attributed to another significant interaction effect discovered by the model, indicating an interaction between probability, channel and time (median β = 5.3e‐05, 89% CI = [4.5e‐06, 1.0e‐04]). In order to further investigate this three‐way interaction, we again used the “estimate_slopes” function. This allowed us to estimate the slope of mu rhythm suppression across time point within each channel. The result revealed a significant effect over C3 but not C4 or Cz at the beginning of the study, which disappeared over time (trial; see Supporting Information S1: Table S1). Specifically, mu rhythm activity changed as a function of action probability on channel C3 at the beginning of the study (e.g., time point 5: coefficient: −1.00e‐03, 89% CI [−2.00e‐03, −3.00e‐04]), and this suppression effect diminished around time point 25 (coefficient = −7.00e‐04, 89% CI [−1.00e‐03, 5.00e‐06]), illustrating the time‐dependent nature of the interaction (see Figure 4).

Occipital Area

Planned Analyses

Differently to the findings over the motor areas, the linear mixed‐effects model ANOVA conducted for alpha power in channels over the occipital cortex revealed a significant effect of probability (F(4, 4103.6) = 15.70, p < 0.001; Eta2 (partial) = 0.02, 95% CI [8.86e‐03, 1.00]), but not of training time (F(1, 27.5) = 0.21, p = 0.647; Eta2 (partial) = 7.75e‐03, 95% CI [0.00, 1.00]). A second model only including probability was run and revealed a significant effect of probability (F(4, 4103.7) = 15.70, p < 0.001; Eta2 (partial) = 0.02, 95% CI [8.86e‐03, 1.00]). The pairwise contrasts analysis showed that alpha power was significantly higher in the baseline than all other levels of probability (all ps < 0.001; 25%: Difference = −0.10, 95% CI [−0.13, −0.07], t(4177) = −5.94; 50%: Difference = −0.14, 95% CI [−0.19, −0.09], t(4177) = −5.31; 75%: Difference = −0.10, 95% CI [−0.15, −0.05], t(4177) = −4.08; 100%: Difference = −0.09, 95% CI [−0.14, −0.04], t(4177) = −3.48). However, no differences were observed between the probability levels themselves (see Figure 5). The alpha rhythm in occipital brain regions is thought to reflect processing of visual stimuli. The observed reduction in occipital alpha power thus likely reflects enhanced visual processing in anticipation of a stimulus. These data suggest that infants' visual processing was enhanced when expecting a likely action but no parametric modulation in relation to the probability levels was found.

Exploratory Analyses

As part of our planned investigation, we explored whether the effects of transition probability on alpha power were specific to action processes or also reflected visual processing, we ran the same exploratory model over the occipital area. Contrary to expectations, the model revealed a significant effect of probability (median β = −1.5e‐03, 89%CI = [−2.5e‐03, −5.2e‐04]) and time (median β = −1.9e‐03, 89%CI = [−3.2e‐03, −7e‐04]). These findings indicate an increase in suppression over the occipital area, as a function of the probability of the upcoming action and as time passed. However, unlike the motor area, no significant interaction was identified.

Discussion

Recent studies have shown that infants can extract statistical regularities from action sequences and that this knowledge is then reflected in their motor system (Monroy et al. 2017b; Monroy, Meyer, et al. 2019). However, these studies exclusively compared action sequences that were 100% predictable (i.e., deterministic) to random actions, whereas action prediction in everyday life relies on far more complex statistical structures. The present study examined whether infants can learn the statistical structure of action sequences through observation and whether the activity of their motor system reflects the specific transitional probabilities.

Exploratory Evidence for Lateralized Motor Responses to Transitional Probabilities

Differently from what was hypothesized, our planned analysis did not reveal a parametrical change in suppression of the infant mu rhythm, an index of motor activity, during anticipation of upcoming actions. However, a follow‐up exploratory analysis including an interaction with channels and time revealed a linear pattern over the left‐central electrode. That is, the higher the probability of the upcoming stimulus, the more activity over infants' motor area was registered. This suppression was evident at the beginning of the study but disappeared over time.

The observed lateralization aligns with previous research on action observation (Southgate et al. 2010, 2009) and supports theories proposing that infants anticipate actions by activating their motor system in a way that mirrors the observed action (Hunnius and Bekkering 2014; Kilner et al. 2004; Köster et al. 2020; Paulus et al. 2013; Rizzolatti and Craighero 2004). It is worth noting that the videos presented in this study exclusively showed a right hand entering the screen to perform the actions. This consistent presentation of a right hand entering the frame to interact with objects may have influenced on the infants' internal representation of these actions. The repeated observation of actions carried out by the right hand may have resulted in infants representing these movements in the left‐central hemisphere, which is responsible for the right side of the body's movements.

The effect of time suggests that suppression of the mu rhythm was pronounced in the early phase of the study but diminished as the experiment progressed. The decrease can be attributed to a reduction in the number of infants who contributed to usable trials in the later stages of the study, due to several factors, including the infants' decreasing attention span and increasing restlessness over time. This hypothesis is further supported by an additional analysis, which demonstrated a significant decrease in the number of infants who contributed to usable trials over time (see Supporting Information S1).

Our results support and extend previous research proposing that infants' motor system might be involved in translating observational experience into action predictions. Previous studies showed that infants were able to learn whether an action was predictable or not and that this information was reflected in the activity of the motor system (Monroy et al. 2017b; Monroy, Meyer, et al. 2019). Our study extends these findings by showing that infants are able to extract varying probabilities from an action sequence and that this information is reflected in the neural motor system when predicting an upcoming action. As in Monroy, Gerson, et al. (2019), Monroy, Meyer, et al. (2019), infants were not simply learning how often an action occurred, as all actions were presented an equal number of times, but infants showed sensitivity to the transitional probabilities between actions. Being able to pick up how probable an action is from observation is particularly important for generating predictions about others' actions in everyday life. Returning to the example given in the introduction, grasping a cup can be followed by multiple actions: we can drink from it, add sugar, pour milk, or hand it to someone else. Our findings suggest that infants are able to learn, not only to predict an action, but also the probability associated with each specific possible following action.

However, these findings require cautious interpretation. Our pre‐registered analyses found no significant effects; the results emerged only in exploratory analyses accounting for spatio‐temporal patterns. The effects are specific to left‐hemisphere activity and diminished over time. Replication studies specifically designed to test these spatial and temporal parameters are necessary to establish whether infants reliably represent graded statistical regularities in their motor system.

Specificity: Motor and Visual Processing in the Infant Brain

Suppression in the alpha range has also been linked with visual processing when associated with occipital brain areas (Bazanova and Vernon 2014). As we expected, the planned analysis of the occipital channels showed that the visual area was more active when expecting an action than compared to the baseline but no differences between the probability levels was found. Thus, this effect might suggest that infants made a general visual prediction that an action would come up but did not distinguish the different levels of probability with which the actions were associated.

Conversely, after controlling for time and channel, the exploratory model showed a parametric increase in alpha suppression corresponding to the probability of the upcoming actions. This increase in activity over the occipital area with higher stimulus probability is similar to the observed pattern over the motor area. Nevertheless, there exist notable distinctions: in contrast to the motor area, where activity is lateralized and influenced by time progression, the occipital cortex did not display these traits. The contrasting patterns between the motor and occipital regions suggest that the processes we identified in the motor cortex is distinct from those in the occipital cortex, confirming the specificity of our findings for motor‐related anticipatory processes.

In infant EEG data, it can be difficult to reliably disentangle activity originating from visual and motor areas (Bowman et al. 2017). However, in the design of our study we included several measures to prevent that effects found in motor activation could be caused by visual activity. First, in our stimulus material, the toy‐box was rotated throughout the experiment. As a consequence, infants could not simply learn and predict the location of the next action but had to focus on the action themselves. This provides some confidence that the infants in our study employed their motor system to generate a prediction (Monroy, Meyer, et al. 2019). Second, we included an action execution phase during which infants interacted with the toy‐box to identify the sample‐specific mu frequency range. This allowed us to identify the subset of frequencies that reflected specifically activity of the motor area, avoiding relying on a more general alpha range, associated with processing of visual stimuli (Cuevas et al. 2014).

Limitations and Future Directions

Although this study examined activity in the motor system during action prediction, it did not examine whether the source of the predictions is within the motor area or if it originates from a different location and is then relayed to the motor area. Recent studies suggest that the motor system receives an integrated signal from sensory cortices and deeper brain structures, which is then processed into an action prediction by the motor area (Ahlheim et al. 2014; Harrison et al. 2006; Monroy, Meyer, et al. 2019; Theves et al. 2021). Because the current study was not designed to answer this question, more research is needed to determine the exact role of the motor area in generating action predictions.

One aspect to consider is the number of trials retained after preprocessing. This study sought to extend the boundaries of existing research by investigating a broader range of transitional probabilities than previous infant EEG studies on action prediction. This expanded scope, coupled with infants' inherent attention limitations and our rigorous artifact rejection procedure, naturally resulted in a reduced trial count. However, the number of trials obtained is comparable with that of previous infant EEG studies in the field investigating action prediction (Monroy, Gerson, et al. 2019; Monroy, Meyer, et al. 2019; Upshaw et al. 2016). To address this, we employed Linear Mixed‐Effects Models (LMMs), which are robust to missing data and unequal trial numbers across participants. LMMs can utilize individual trial data and allow for including participants with unequal numbers of trials, providing a significant advantage over traditional approaches like ANOVAs that require averaging across balanced trial counts.

Our experimental design substantially increased learning demands compared to previous studies. Unlike Monroy, Meyer, et al. (2019), where infants distinguished only between deterministic (100%) and random (25%) action pairs, our study required learning four distinct probability levels. The subtle differences between closely spaced probabilities may have contributed to the absence of effects in our planned analyses. Future studies may benefit from testing fewer probability levels (e.g., three levels: 100%, 75%, 50%) to reduce cognitive load while maintaining the goal of studying multi‐level probability learning beyond binary distinctions.

Furthermore, this study investigated the activity of the sensorimotor area during action prediction, focusing on central channels (C3, Cz, C4). Given the relatively low spatial accuracy offered by a 32‐channel EEG system, the recorded signal most likely reflects activation of the sensorimotor cortices more broadly (including motor, pre‐motor, and sensory cortices). Central channels were selected to best capture the activity of the sensorimotor cortices without activity in non‐relevant areas (Ghilardi et al. 2023; Meyer et al. 2016; Monroy, Meyer, et al. 2019; Perry et al. 2010; Pineda and Hecht 2009). However, future studies using methodologies with higher spatial resolution, such as MEG or fNIRS, are needed to examine spatially distinct activity patterns over the brain related to the prediction of actions and their statistical structure.

In addition, previous studies with adults have demonstrated that multiple forms of statistical information are extracted when observing action sequences and that the motor area integrates these different aspects when making predictions (Ahlheim et al. 2014; Ghilardi et al. 2023). In a recent study, Ghilardi et al. (2023) found that adults can track not only the likelihood of the next action step, but also all potential actions that could occur and their relative probabilities. It remains unknown whether infants have the same ability and future research is integral to better understand whether infants can extract and integrate different statistical properties in their predictions during action observation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the present EEG study shows that infants readily pick up statistical structures that they observe in sequences of actions and use this knowledge to generate action predictions in their neural motor system. This study extends previous evidence by demonstrating that the activity of the motor system reflects the specific statistical likelihood of upcoming actions. As such, it provides evidence for a functional role of statistical learning for infants' developing action understanding.
