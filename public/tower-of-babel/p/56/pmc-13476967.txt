Outcomes of suction mini-percutaneous nephrolithotomy using disposable versus reusable sheaths: Real-world observations and inferences from the Suction Technology Utility in Mini-Percutaneous nephrolithotomy Study (STUMPS) registry
Authors: Carlo Giulioni, Vineet Gauhar, Daniele Castellani, Steffi Kar-Kei Yuen, Federico Falsetti, Angelo Cafarelli, Khi Yung Fong, Jaisukh Kalathia, Nariman Gadzhiev, Alexey G Martov, Vigen Malkhasyan, Abu Baker, Arun Chawla, Abhay Mahajan, Kazumi Taguchi, Lazaros Tzelves, Leonardo Gomes Lopes, Yiloren Tanidir, Mohamed Omar, Mahmoud Laymon, Mehmet İlker Gökce, Chin Tiong Heng, Kaushikkumar Shah, Dilip Kumar Mishra, Andreas Skolarikos, Bhaskar Somani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the perioperative outcomes of suction mini-percutaneous nephrolithotomy (SM-PCNL) using disposable versus reusable sheaths, based on data from the global Suction Technology Utility in Mini-Percutaneous nephrolithotomy Study (STUMPS) registry.MethodsThis prospective, multicenter observational study analyzed adult patients undergoing SM-PCNL using either disposable or reusable suction sheaths (14–22 Fr) between March 2024 and November 2024 across 30 centers in 21 countries. Propensity score matching was employed to balance key variables, including age, sex, Guy's stone score, stone volume, and patient position. Primary outcomes included 30-day stone-free rates, complications, and operative metrics. Multivariable logistic regression was performed to identify predictors of complete (Grade A) stone clearance.ResultsAmong 1524 patients, 1024 were matched for analysis (512 in each group). Both groups had comparable distributions of 30-day postoperative residual fragment grades (p=0.90), with similar stone-free rates (Grade A clearance: 80% in the reusable group vs. 81% in the disposable group). The disposable group demonstrated a significantly lower 30-day complication rate (9.4% vs. 15%; p=0.01), including fewer renal pelvic injuries and bleeding events. However, operative time was longer in this group (median 50 min vs. 44 min; p=0.02), and the reintervention rate was higher (3.3% vs. 1.0%; p=0.02). Predictors of reduced stone clearance included supine positioning, smaller sheath size, and larger stone volume.ConclusionBoth disposable and reusable sheaths yield comparable stone clearance in SM-PCNL. However, disposable sheaths are associated with fewer complications and may offer practical advantages in settings with limited reprocessing capabilities. Further randomized trials and cost-effectiveness studies are warranted to guide sheath selection in diverse clinical environments.Keywords: Suction access sheath, Percutaneous nephrolithotomy, Minimally invasive surgery, Disposable sheath, Urolithiasis

Introduction

Kidney stone disease remains a prevalent and increasingly burdensome global health issue, affecting millions worldwide [1]. The management of large and complex renal calculi often necessitates surgical intervention, with percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL) standing as the gold standard [2]. Central to the success and safety of PCNL is the sheath, a cylindrical conduit that maintains a working channel and provides an unimpeded pathway across the percutaneous tract for nephroscopes, lithotripters, and retrieval instruments.

Traditional reusable PCNL sheaths, usually made of stainless steel, have been a staple in urology for decades. Designed for multiple uses, they undergo thorough cleaning and sterilization after each use. Their main benefits include lower initial cost, durability through many sterilization cycles, and reliable support during tract dilation and instrumentation—especially in complex cases. Surgeons' familiarity and established hospital reprocessing systems further support their continued use [3].

Reprocessing reusable medical devices is necessary for patient safety but poses challenges such as being labor-intensive, time-consuming, and reliant on trained staff, specialized equipment, and strict quality control to prevent healthcare-associated infections. Delays in the sterilization department can disrupt surgical schedules, and limited after-sales service or equipment availability in some regions may further hinder operating room efficiency [4].

There has been an evidence-based transition toward smaller disposable alternatives incorporating suction or aspiration capabilities, driven by a multifactorial interplay of considerations including the incidence of procedural complications, facilitation of tract dilation and access, cost-effectiveness, operative efficiency, and the evolving paradigm of healthcare resource allocation [5]. This trend has gained particular momentum with the advent of miniaturized and suction-aspiration PCNL techniques [6]. These disposable sheaths, designed for single use, address infection control by eliminating reprocessing and thereby reducing the risk of cross-contamination and hospital-acquired infections. Their pre-sterilized and ready-to-use nature streamlines procedures, reduces reliance on central sterile supply, and improves efficiency. Advances in materials integrated into customizable sheaths have made disposable sheaths as robust and functional as reusable ones, with features like low-friction surfaces for easier instrument passage. The integration of suction technology has further increased their adaptability, allowing customization to meet any surgeon's requirements for managing children and adults alike [7,8]. Yet, the adoption of disposable or reusable equipment, including PCNL sheaths, is multifaceted, often influenced by a complex interplay of factors unique to each healthcare institution, and not without its own set of considerations, primarily revolving around cost and environmental impact [9,10].

The impact of disposable and reusable equipment in endourology has been profound in flexible ureteroscopy ever since the Lithovue endoscope by Boston Scientific was launched [11], and since then technological evolution and its impact on flexible ureteroscopy have been profound [12]. This has been well documented from clinical, cost-effective, and training perspectives. However, to our knowledge, even though there are several disposable and reusable suction mini-percutaneous nephrolithotomy (SM-PCNL) sheaths, no multicenter dedicated study has examined whether sheath type has any impact on surgical outcomes. The Suction Technology Utility in Mini-Percutaneous nephrolithotomy Study (STUMPS) registry was the first of its kind to include patients in whom all known device types were used across different countries [13]. Hence, the primary aim of this observational study is to analyze 30-day perioperative outcomes in patients undergoing SM-PCNL, comparing patients operated on with commercially approved disposable sheaths with those operated on with reusable sheaths of all sizes.

Patients and methods

SM-PCNL is defined in this study as a PCNL procedure utilizing either disposable or reusable suction access nephrostomy sheaths no more than 22 Fr in conjunction with various energy devices for stone fragmentation. Anonymous data were collected through the STUMPS registry, administered by the Asian Institute of Nephro-Urology with ethical approval under protocol AINU #01/2024. Between March 2024 and November 2024, adult patients presenting with kidney stones were enrolled prospectively at 30 medical centers across 21 countries. The full study protocol has already been published [13]. Briefly, eligible participants met the following criteria: a minimum age of 18 years, exclusive presence of kidney stones, use of 14–22 Fr suction sheaths, normal renal anatomy, and the availability of 2-mm slice non-contrast CT performed within 6–8 weeks preoperatively and 30 days postoperatively. Patients were excluded if they had renal anomalies, were under 18 years old, presented with concurrent ureteral stones, failed to discontinue antiplatelet or anticoagulant medications, were unable to provide informed consent, or had incomplete medical records.

The following data were gathered: baseline patient demographics, stone characteristics, surgical approaches, lithotripsy methods, operative duration, complications, stone-free rates (SFRs), and ancillary procedures. In cases of multiple stones, the maximum diameter of the largest stone was measured, with stone volume calculated using the ellipsoid formula (length×width×depth×π×0.167). Guy's stone scoring system was additionally calculated. Preoperative urine cultures were performed, with infections managed using targeted antibiotic therapy based on antibiograms. Prophylactic antibiotics were administered per hospital protocols, while anticoagulant and antiplatelet agents were withheld for 3–7 days before surgery and restarted according to clinical assessment.

Surgeons performed SM-PCNL procedures according to their own practice, and tracts and the number of tracts were chosen according to each clinical case, while maintaining compliance with study criteria but without specific procedural mandates on how many tracts were needed, the type of lithotripsy device, and other parameters. Suction and irrigation parameters were not predefined.

First-day postoperative pain was evaluated using a 10-point visual analogue scale, in which a score of 0 indicated no pain and a score of 10 indicated the worst possible pain. Adverse events occurring within 30 days following surgery were graded according to the Clavien–Dindo classification system for PCNL.

Postoperative evaluation involved a 30-day low-dose non-contrast CT scan to assess residual fragments (RFs) using a bone window. SFR was classified into three categories: Grade A (complete absence of RFs [100% stone clearance; zero RFs]), Grade B (single fragment measuring ≤4 mm), and Grade C (single RF exceeding 4 mm or presence of multiple RFs regardless of size).

Statistical analysis

For this analysis, patients were divided into two groups. Group 1 included patients operated on using a reusable sheath, while Group 2 included patients operated on with a disposable sheath. Continuous variables were described using the median and interquartile range, while categorical variables were described using absolute numbers and percentages. For comparison between the two study arms, data were analyzed using the χ2 test for categorical parameters and the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables.

Propensity score matching (PSM) was used to reduce confounding in the statistical comparisons. Propensity scores were calculated using a logistic regression model, and 1:1 nearest neighbor matching was performed for age, sex, Guy’s stone score, stone volume, and patient position. To ensure optimal matching of covariates, caliper width was initially set at 0.2 [14,15]; the target of an absolute standardized mean difference <0.1 for all covariates [16] was achieved at a caliper width of 0.12. All statistical comparisons were then repeated for the PSM cohort, as for the overall cohort.

Finally, two multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed, with a priori selection of variables, in the PSM population to evaluate factors associated with 100% SFR (Grade A). Besides the comparison of sheath type, variables that have been suggested in previous literature to impact SFR (stone volume, tract dilation method, type of lithotripsy, patient position, and sheath size) were entered into the models to assess their significance as independent predictors [17,18]. These predictors were described using odds ratios (ORs), 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and p-values. All statistical analyses were performed using R statistical language, version 4.3.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), with p<0.05 indicating statistical significance.

Stone fragmentation modalities differed significantly between groups (p<0.001), with the fragmentation-only technique being more common in Group 2 (61% vs. 54%). The need for additional basket extraction was significantly higher in Group 2 (26% vs. 12%, p<0.001). Despite similar median lithotripsy times between Group 1 and Group 2 (17 min vs. 19 min, p=0.80), median total operative time was longer in Group 2 (50 min vs. 44 min, p=0.02). Sheath access to all parts of the kidney was equivalent.

Intraoperative SFR, as assessed by surgeons via fluoroscopy or visual inspection, differed significantly between the two groups (overall p<0.001), with the rate of 100% stone clearance higher in Group 2 than in Group 1 (83% vs. 76%). Exit strategies differed significantly (p<0.001), with tubeless procedures (with PCN) being more common in Group 1 (33% vs. 9.2%), while tubeless procedures with ureteral stenting were more frequent in Group 2 (52% vs. 42%). Totally tubeless procedures were more common in Group 2 (2.0% vs. 0.59%).

Intraoperative complications were summarized in Table 4. The overall complication rate was significantly higher in Group 1 compared with Group 2 (15% vs. 9.4%, p=0.01) (Table 4). Although blood transfusion rates were numerically higher in Group 1 (2.1% vs. 0.78%, p=0.12), this difference was not statistically significant in the matched cohort. Renal pelvic perforation requiring prolonged nephrostomy tube placement or postoperative nephrostomy insertion was significantly more common in Group 1 (2.5% vs. 0.59%, p=0.02). Pneumothorax rates were numerically higher in Group 1 (1.6% vs. 0.39%, p=0.11), though not statistically significant. The infectious complications rate was low and did not differ significantly between groups. Readmission rates within 72 h were comparable between Group 1 and Group 2 (1.8% vs. 1.6%, p=0.99). Hospital stay duration was similar, with a median of 3 days for Group 1 and 2 days for Group 2 (p=0.50).

SFR evaluation at 30-day CT showed no significant difference between Group 1 and Group 2 (p=0.90). Grade A clearance was achieved in 80% of patients in Group 1 compared with 81% in Group 2. Grade B clearance occurred in 15% versus 14% of the patients, while Grade C clearance was observed in 4.9% versus 4.9% of the patients.

The rate of reintervention was significantly higher in Group 2 (3.3% vs. 1.0%, p=0.02), with similar median time to reintervention between Group 1 and Group 2 (5 days vs. 4 days, p=0.55).

In the multivariable logistic regression analysis (Table 5), significant predictors of lower odds of Grade A stone-free status included supine positioning (OR 0.64, 95% CI 0.42–0.95, p=0.03), stone volume (OR 0.94 per cm3 increase, 95% CI 0.91–0.97, p=0.001), and sheath size <20 Fr (OR 0.20, 95% CI 0.10–0.38, p<0.001), while single-step dilation (OR 2.21, 95% CI 1.37–3.57, p=0.001) was associated with higher odds. For overall complications (Table 5), disposable sheath use was associated with significantly reduced odds of complications (OR 0.57, 95% CI 0.38–0.86, p=0.01) compared with reusable sheath use. Mechanical lithotripsy was associated with increased odds of complications (OR 1.96, 95% CI 1.15–3.29, p=0.01), as was greater stone volume (OR 1.04 per cm3 increase, 95% CI 1.02–1.07, p=0.01).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this analysis of the multicenter, real-world STUMPS registry presents the most comprehensive comparative study to date on the use of disposable versus reusable suction sheaths in mini-PCNL. With PSM applied across a large international cohort, this analysis offers critical insights into how sheath type affects surgical efficiency, safety, and stone clearance outcomes.

A notable finding was the longer operative time observed with disposable sheaths (median 50 min vs. 44 min, p=0.02), despite comparable lithotripsy times. This result may initially seem counterintuitive, as suction-assisted systems are designed to facilitate efficient fragment evacuation. However, the difference is likely attributable to intraoperative procedural strategies (e.g., patient positioning and time to access) rather than device inefficiency. The disposable sheath group demonstrated greater reliance on fluoroscopy-only access, a higher frequency of fragmentation-only techniques, and more frequent use of basket extraction for RFs. This contrasts with earlier reports of shorter operative and fluoroscopy times using disposable suction sheaths such as the ClearPetra system. Lievore et al. [9], for instance, showed reduced fluoroscopy exposure and fewer ancillary retrieval devices when using suction systems. Additionally, real-time aspiration of fragments and dust has been shown to reduce manual manipulation during lithotripsy, streamlining the procedure [19]. Across the literature, suction-assisted approaches are estimated to reduce operative time by approximately 19 min [20]. The prolonged operative times observed in the disposable group likely reflect a broader shift toward miniaturized, tubeless approaches with narrower tract sizes, coupled with longer lasing strategies and adherence to perioperative precautions, including thorough inspection of all calyces to achieve a zero-fragment stone-free status, rather than limitations inherent to the disposable sheaths. These findings emphasize the need to interpret operative duration within the context of overall surgical technique. Regarding postoperative outcomes, both sheath types achieved high and statistically similar 30-day SFRs (Grade A clearance: 80% vs. 81%). This is consistent with the existing literature, in which SM-PCNL—though not always statistically superior—tends to yield improved outcomes. Therefore, unlike flexible ureterorenoscopes—in which single-use flexible and navigable access sheaths demonstrated superior stone clearance compared with reusable ones (OR 1.659, p=0.018) [21]—no significant difference was observed in this study regarding the type of sheath used. Consequently, both sheath types can be considered appropriate for use in SM-PCNL procedures. Moreover, our multivariable analysis identified supine positioning, smaller sheath size (<20 Fr), and larger stone volume as independent negative predictors of complete stone clearance. These results support previous findings suggesting that while miniaturization may enhance safety and reduce morbidity, it can compromise fragment evacuation, particularly in larger stone burdens due to reduced outflow efficiency and prolonged lasing times, especially in hard, complex stones [22].

The disposable group showed a higher prevalence of single-tract procedures and more frequent use of small-caliber sheaths, consistent with current endourological trends favoring reduced invasiveness. Notably, a smaller sheath size was independently associated with decreased odds of achieving immediate zero-fragment status (although overall SFR was comparable), corroborating evidence that smaller tracts, while safer, may impair evacuation efficiency [7]. Single-tract procedures were significantly more common in the disposable group (96% vs. 90%, p<0.001), whereas multi-tract approaches were more frequent in the reusable group. This likely reflects sheath-specific ergonomics and institutional preferences. Disposable systems, often tailored for streamlined access, are suited to single-tract strategies. In contrast, reusable sheaths are generally favored in complex cases requiring multi-tract access to manage larger or multifocal stones [23]. Nevertheless, the advancement in technology with artificial intelligence and robotics for percutaneous kidney access may address the inherent limitations of traditional techniques, enabling a safe, precise, rapid, and effective puncture of the renal collecting system [24].

Predictive modeling reinforced that supine positioning, larger stone volume, and smaller sheath size negatively affected stone clearance, while single-step dilation was associated with improved outcomes. Although patient positioning remains debated, some meta-analyses favored the prone approach due to better access to lower pole calyces and greater tract stability [5]. These factors must be considered when evaluating procedural success.

A key advantage of disposable sheaths was their association with a significantly lower overall complication rate (9.4% vs. 15%, p=0.01), primarily due to fewer instances of renal pelvic perforation and significant bleeding. These findings are in line with a prior comparative study showing reduced complication rates in patients treated with suction-assisted sheaths such as the ClearPetra sheath [25]. Complication rates for suction systems in the literature vary widely—from 1.5% to 38.9%—depending on sheath design and patient characteristics [20]. In our study, bleeding complications were infrequent and generally managed conservatively. This is consistent with previous reports indicating low bleeding rates in SM-PCNL, in which bleeding incidence may be associated with a significantly lower hemoglobin decrease (mean difference, −0.68; 95% CI, −1.05 to −0.31; p=0.0003) and fewer blood transfusions (OR=0.36; 95% CI, 0.18–0.71; p=0.003) compared with standard PCNL [26]. Nevertheless, the difference observed between the two groups may also be attributable to sheath-related factors. Notably, the incidence of sheath size <20 Fr was significantly higher in the disposable group (80% vs. 71%, p=0.001). In a prospective series of 122 consecutive patients undergoing mini-PCNL with a 16 Fr ClearPetra sheath, the median hemoglobin drop was 1.5 g/dL, and the transfusion rate was only 0.8% [26]. Moreover, the disposable group exhibited a higher rate of single-tract access (96% vs. 90%, p<0.001). Although increased stone burden is generally expected to necessitate multiple tracts, a comprehensive review reported that it was not associated with postoperative bleeding in most studies, albeit without definitive conclusion [27].

Infectious complications remain a major concern in endourology due to the high prevalence of bacterial colonization in stones and the risk of bacteremia from increased intrarenal pressures. Maintaining optimal intrarenal pressure and temperature is critical for preserving epithelial integrity and preventing retrograde bacterial migration through pyelovenous and pyelolymphatic reflux [28]. In our cohort, infection-related complications were comparable between groups, suggesting that rigorous perioperative protocols, including targeted antimicrobial prophylaxis and preoperative urine sterilization, are effective across both sheath types. Nonetheless, in resource-limited environments with constrained access to high-standard reprocessing infrastructure, disposable sheaths may offer a practical advantage by mitigating the risk of healthcare-associated infections [29].

This study has several limitations. First, although robust matching was employed, the observational nature of the analysis allows for potential residual confounding due to unmeasured variables. Second, variability in surgeon experience, sheath models, and institutional protocols may have influenced outcomes. Third, key intraoperative parameters—particularly suction and irrigation pressure settings—were not collected, despite their known impact on surgical performance. Fourth, the sample size from each country may not reflect national-level practice, limiting generalizability. Additionally, the study did not assess long-term clinical outcomes, cost-effectiveness, or environmental impact—all of which are increasingly relevant considerations. Notably, factors such as sustainability, sterilization logistics, and supply chain availability were beyond the scope of this analysis. This is particularly important when evaluating single-use devices, which may offer logistical and clinical advantages in high-volume or resource-limited settings by reducing equipment turnover delays and minimizing the risks of cross-contamination and device degradation. Finally, the anonymized nature of our centralized database prevented analysis of center-specific practices regarding disposable versus reusable sheath use, which may have influenced outcomes. Future research should aim to integrate clinical outcomes with cost-benefit analyses and sustainability metrics to determine the most appropriate sheath type across diverse clinical environments. Similarly, it could assess quality of life and acute pain management in relation to different sheath sizes and types, particularly in SM-PCNL.

Conclusion

This multicenter study demonstrates that both disposable and reusable suction sheaths achieve high and comparable SFRs in SM-PCNL. However, sheath selection influences distinct intraoperative strategies with important outcome implications. Disposable sheaths were associated with lower complication rates and distinct procedural patterns such as more frequent single-tract approaches, smaller sheaths (<20 Fr), fluoroscopy-only guidance, and fragmentation techniques. Yet, reusable sheaths correlated with multi-tract procedures, combined imaging guidance, and mechanical lithotripsy. Moreover, supine positioning, smaller sheath size, and larger stone volume negatively predicted stone clearance, while single-step dilation improved outcomes. This suggests that surgical success depends on the interplay between device selection and procedural technique rather than sheath type alone. Although both sheath types demonstrate effectiveness, disposable sheaths may offer advantages in resource-limited settings with variable sterilization infrastructure. However, optimal outcomes appear to depend more on appropriate matching of surgical strategy to stone complexity than on device selection alone.
