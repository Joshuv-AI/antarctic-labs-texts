Effective charge saturation in ferritin cages
Authors: Takumi Kuwata, Yuusuke Murakami, Daisuke Sato, Kazuo Fujiwara, Masamichi Ikeguchi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ferritins are proteins consisting of 24 subunits that make a spherical cage structure. We prepared a series of net-charge mutants of Escherichia coli and Pseudo-nitzschia multiseries ferritins (EcFtn and PmFtn, respectively) in which glutamate (Glu) and aspartate (Asp) residues located at the outer surface of the cage were substituted with glutamine and asparagine or vice versa. The effective negative charges of mutant proteins were evaluated by nondenaturing polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and ζ-potential measurement. Surprisingly, the effective negative charges of mutants did not increase when the number of Glu and Asp residues increased, while the effective negative charges decreased as the number of Glu and Asp residues decreased. These results suggest that there is a physical net-charge limit in ferritins, and that EcFtn and PmFtn bear maximally possible negative charges. Amino acid sequence analysis has shown that many bacteria and plant ferritins seem to have nearly maximum negative charges, but animal ferritins have fewer negative charges.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-57639-3.

Introduction

The protein net charge is an important factor contributing to protein function, stability, and solubility1,2. Furthermore, it is frequently utilized to control larger-scale artificial structures or cargo encapsulation in protein cages3–11. Previously, we prepared a series of net-charge mutant proteins to investigate the effect of net charges on the assembly rate of Escherichia coli ferritin (EcFtn)12. Ferritins are iron-storage proteins consisting of 24 identical or similar subunits13, which are arranged in 432 octameric symmetry and form a spherical cage structure. In mammalian cytosol, ferritins are heterooligomers consisting of H- and L-chains. The former has ferroxidase activity, whereas the latter does not. Almost all species have H-chain-like ferritins, but only vertebrates have L-chains. L-chains are thought to nucleate the iron mineral14. In mitochondria, there is a ferritin that is different from cytosol ferritins but shows biochemical properties similar to H-chain (FTMT)15. In fishes, amphibians, and reptiles, furthermore, M-chain exists in addition to H- and L-chains16–19. M-chain contains both the ferroxidase site and nucleation site. In bacteria, the heme-bound ferritin (BfrB) and DNA-binding protein from starved cells (DPS) are present in addition to the H-chain-like ferritin (FtnA)20. DPS has a smaller cage structure formed by 12 identical subunits arranged in 32 tetrahedral symmetry. Thus, ferritins have diverged variously during evolution.

In a previous study12, we substituted glutamate (Glu) residues located at the outer surface of EcFtn with lysine (Lys) or glutamine (Gln). Although Lys substitutions destabilized the protein, Gln-substituted EcFtn (EQ mutants) maintained the cage structure, and its isoelectric point (pI) was increased with the increasing number of Glu/Gln substitutions, as expected12. The assembly rate from the acid-dissociated state was measured by time-resolved small-angle X-ray scattering and shown to increase as the number of substitutions increased, probably because the electrostatic repulsion between negatively charged subunits was weakened by reducing the subunit net charge12. In the present study, expecting the slower assembly, we created a series of mutant ferritins in which surface Gln residues are replaced with Glu (QE mutants). Then, we found that the effective negative charges of QE mutants do not increase, although the number of Glu residues increased. Here, we report this surprising finding with a similar result on Pseudo-nitzschia multiseries ferritin (PmFtn). These results brought to mind a hypothesis that ferritins have evolved to have the maximal negative charge physically possible. Analyses of amino acid composition of ferritins from various organisms indicate that ferritins from bacteria and plants tend to have maximum or nearly maximum net charges.

Results

Net charge mutants of EcFtn

In the present study, we substituted Glu for Gln residues located on the outer surface of EcFtn (Fig. 1). We considered that QE mutation as well as EQ mutation could minimize the influence of substitutions except for the charge effect because the sidechains of Glu and Gln have comparable size and shape. The combination of substitutions in a series of net-charge mutants is summarized in Table 1, in which EQ mutants previously constructed are included. The structures of EQ mutants had been previously investigated by circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy and small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS), and the structures were found to be similar to that of wild-type (WT) EcFtn12. In the present study, the structures of QE mutants were also investigated by CD, analytical ultracentrifugation (AUC), and SAXS. Except for QE5, all mutants showed CD spectra like that of WT EcFtn (Fig. S1), suggesting that the secondary and tertiary structures of the subunit were maintained in QE mutants. The sedimentation coefficients of QE1, QE2, QE3, and QE4 are also similar to that of WT (Table 1), indicating that the mutants maintain a 24-meric cage structure. QE5 showed two sedimentation coefficients corresponding to the subunit dimer (2.9 S) and 24-mer (16.1 S). Two peaks were also observed in a size-exclusion chromatogram of QE5 (Fig. S1). These results indicate that QE5 is in equilibrium between the dimer and the 24-mer. Previously, we reported that EcFtn dissociates into the subunit dimer, maintaining the subunit tertiary structure at acidic pH21. The dimer showed a near-ultraviolet (UV) CD band less intense than that for the 24-mer. Therefore, the less intense near-UV CD of the QE5 (Fig. S1) is probably the result of the partial dissociation of the 24-mer into the dimer. QE5 contains the Q121E mutation. Because Q121 is located on the inner surface near the threefold symmetry axis (Fig. 1), the proximity of three negatively charged E121 probably introduces the electrostatic repulsion and brings Q121E to the partial dissociation. Another mutation near the threefold symmetry axis, F117A, has been known to produce the equilibrium mixture of the dimer and the 24-mer by weakening hydrophobic interactions between subunits21. Except for QE5, which contains the Q121E mutation, QE mutants were also characterized by SAXS. The scattering profiles of the mutants were similar to the profile of WT EcFtn. The radius of gyration (Rg) at infinite dilution was obtained by linear extrapolation of Rg values obtained at various protein concentrations (Fig. S2 and Table 1). Both AUC and SAXS results show that all net-charge mutants have a size and shape similar to those of WT EcFtn.

A series of QE mutants was applied to nondenaturing polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (native PAGE) with a series of EQ mutants constructed previously12. The mobility in native PAGE depends on both the intrinsic charges (strictly speaking, electrokinetic charge, see the Discussion) and the sizes and shapes of proteins. As described above, because the sizes and shapes of the net-charge mutants are the same, we expected the mobility to change with the number of EQ and QE substitutions. Although the electrophoretic mobilities of EQ mutants were decreased with increasing number of substitutions, as expected, the mobilities of QE mutants were surprisingly unchanged (Fig. 2A). This unchanged mobility indicated that the number of negative charges does not increase even though the number of Glu residues is increased, that is, the carboxyl groups of Glu residues (including inherently present ones) or inherently present aspartate (Asp) residues do not dissociate even at pH 8 (pH during electrophoresis). Native PAGE at a higher pH of 9.5 gave the same result (Fig. S3). To confirm the charge saturation of QE mutants, we measured the ζ-potentials of net-charge mutants under conditions similar to native PAGE, 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0). The ζ-potentials of EQ1 and QE mutants were not changed significantly, although those of EQ2, EQ3, and EQ4 decreased with the number of substitutions (Fig. 2B). This feature is qualitatively similar to the results of native PAGE. The ζ-potentials of EQ4, WT EcFtn, and QE4 were also measured at a higher salt concentration, 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0) and 100 mM NaCl. Although the addition of 100 mM NaCl decreased absolute values of ζ-potentials, the potential of QE4 was almost the same as that of WT EcFtn, whereas the potential of EQ4 was much smaller than that of EcFtn (Fig. 2C). Taken together, these results show that the number of negative charges does not increase even though the number of Glu residues is increased, suggesting that the carboxyl groups of Glu residues (not always introduced residues, including inherently present Glu/Asp) do not dissociate, even at pH 9.5.

The acid dissociation constants (pKs) of ionizable groups can be varied depending on their environments22. Although the intrinsic pK values of carboxyl groups of Asp and Glu are known to be 3.9 and 4.3, respectively, in some cases, they were reported to rise to 9.2 and 8.8, respectively22. Factors increasing pK values of carboxyl groups are negative electrostatic potentials, dehydration, and hydrogen bonding22. In the present case, the possibility of dehydration (i.e., burial of the side chain) is excluded because all substituted residues (except for Q121) are exposed at the outer surface. Although we investigated the possibility that the sidechain carboxy group would make a hydrogen bond with surrounding hydrogen-bond acceptors based on the crystal structure of WT EcFtn, we could not find any possible acceptor (see Fig. S4). Therefore, a negative electrostatic potential is the most likely factor. Šiber and Podgornik24 calculated the electrostatic potential on the inner and outer surfaces of spherical virus capsids based on a simplified model. They showed that the symmetrical shape of the virus capsid can produce extremely positive or negative electrostatic potentials at the inner and outer surfaces, respectively. In the case of ferritins, there are many negatively charged residues at both inner and outer surfaces, so that the electrostatic potential at the inner and outer surfaces is extremely negative. Therefore, it is possible that the pK values of some carboxyl groups of QE mutants increased beyond 9.5, and that they are protonated at pH 9.5. To examine this, we measured the electrophoretic mobility of the subunit dimer of QE mutants. It is known that WT EcFtn and EQ mutants dissociate into the dimer at acidic pH and reassemble at neutral pH21. The assembly rate increased with the decreasing number of Glu residues12. Therefore, we disassembled net-charge mutants at pH 2.5, and the pH of the solution was then returned to pH 8 just before applying native PAGE. The results are shown in Fig. 2D. For QE mutants, two bands can be found. The upper bands correspond to the 24-mer showing the same mobility as WT. The lower bands correspond to the subunit dimers in which migration was increased with the number of Glu residues, indicating that the negative charges of dimeric QE mutants increase with the number of Glu residues. Although the dimer bands were not observed for WT and EQ mutants, this is thought to be due to the fast reassembly rates of WT and EQ mutants12. As previously shown, the assembly rate decreases with increasing subunit net charge12. For the same reason, the dimer bands of QE1 and QE2 could barely be observed (Fig. 2D). That the negative charges of QE mutants increase with the number of Glu residues in the dimeric state, but not in the 24-meric state, shows that the spherical cage-like shape is an origin of the pK shift, which is qualitatively consistent with the theory of Šiber and Podgornik24.

Isoelectric points (pIs) were also determined (Table 1). At pH near pI, the net charges of EcFtn and its mutants are close to zero, where the large pK shift due to the symmetrical sphere electrostatic potential would not occur. Therefore, pIs of QE mutants gradually decrease with the number of mutations (Table 1). The degree of pI change per one QE mutation is similar to that between EQ1 and WT. As pI becomes far from the intrinsic pK of the side chain carboxy group of Glu (4.3), the degree of change becomes remarkable (e.g., EQ4 shows one pH unit higher pI than EQ3). These observations are also consistent with our conclusion that a highly negative electrostatic potential owing to the spherical cage-like shape is the cause of the pK shift.

Net charges of other ferritins

Noteworthy is that the electrophoretic mobility, that is, the effective negative charge, does not increase beyond WT. This lack of increase beyond WT brings to mind a hypothesis that ferritins evolve toward having more negative charges, and that the number of Asp and Glu residues has reached the maximum number possible to dissociate. To address this possibility, we investigated the theoretical net charges (TNCs) of ferritins based on their amino acid compositions. We defined TNC as the difference between the numbers of positively charged residues (arginine and Lys) and negatively charged residues (Asp and Glu). We excluded histidine residues because their pK values are close to the physiological pH, and it is difficult to judge whether they are protonated. As the first step, we calculated the TNCs of ferritins, of which three-dimensional structures were deposited in the Protein Data Bank25. The TNC of EcFtn is − 13. Although the TNCs of ferritins from bacteria and archaea range from − 9 to − 18, ferritins from vertebrates seem to have less-negative TNCs (from − 4 to − 11). Ferritins from plants and diatoms have TNCs from − 11 to − 18. Thus, we conducted a similar mutational experiment on a diatom ferritin, PmFtn, because its TNC is more negative than EcFtn (–16). The mutational sites selected were Asp and Glu residues located at the outer surface for charge-depress mutants, and asparagine and Gln residues for charge-enhance mutants (Fig. 3). The combination of substitutions in a series of mutants is summarized in Table 2. CD spectroscopy and SAXS have confirmed that all PmFtn mutants maintain their secondary and quaternary structures (Figs S5 and S6). A native PAGE result is shown in Fig. 4. The result indicates that the effective negative charge does not increase beyond the WT of PmFtn.

To clarify whether other ferritins from various biological species have the maximum or near maximum negative values, we investigated the distribution of TNCs from the amino acid sequences deposited in the UniProt database26. Figure 5 shows the TNC distributions for ferritins from bacteria, plants, invertebrates, and vertebrates. Bacteria have two different ferritins, bacterial ferritin (gene name is FtnA) and bacterioferritin (gene name BfrB). The former is homologous to the H-chains of vertebrate ferritins, and the latter contains heme at the interface between dyad-related subunits. Although bacteria have DPS in addition to FtnA and BfrB, we excluded DPS here because DPS is known to have positively charged residues at the N-terminal extension or the C-terminal extension for the interaction with DNA20. FtnA and BfrB show peaks at TNCs of − 12 and − 14, respectively. TNC is not necessarily the actual net charge because the existence of His residues and large pK shift of ionizable groups are not considered in the calculation of TNC. However, the peak values of TNC distributions for FtnA and BfrB are close to the TNC of EcFtn, which is proven to have the maximum net charge, suggesting that ferritins of many bacteria may have the maximum or nearly maximum negative charge. Plant ferritins are localized in the chloroplast and have a transit (or target) sequence for localization. In our TNC calculation, therefore, the transit peptide portions were removed based on the prediction by the TargetP-2.0 server27. The mature plant ferritins show a somewhat broad peak at a slightly less-negative TNC of − 11, suggesting that plant ferritins also tend to have the maximal negative charge.

By contrast, the H-chains of vertebrate ferritins (gene name FTH) show a less-negative peak at a TNC of − 8. Furthermore, the L-chains of vertebrate ferritins (gene name FTL) show two peaks at TNCs of − 6 and + 7. By inspecting the relationship between the TNC of FTL and different species, we found that the positive peak in the TNC distribution of FTL originates from avian ferritins. A recent avian genomics project (B10K) produced many avian genomes, resulting in a wealth of avian ferritin sequences28. Although it is known that birds have ferritoids in corneal epithelial cells, but not L-chains19[,29[,30 the gene name of ferritoids in the UniProt database is FTL. Therefore, the data set extracted by the criterion that the gene name is FTL contains both L-chains and ferritoids. Ferritoids have an SV40-type nuclear localization signal and can be localized in the nucleus, whereas ferritin H-chains do not have the signal sequence and are localized in the cytoplasm. Because chicken ferritoid can form complexes with ferritin H-chains, H-chains are thought to be transported to the nucleus of corneal epithelial cells by forming ferritoid–ferritin complexes, and to protect DNA from the UV light- and hydrogen peroxide-induced damage30[,31. The positive TNCs of ferritoids may be necessary for interacting with negatively charged DNA. The TNC of FTL, except for avian ferritoids, has a peak at − 6, a value less negative than that of FTH. The mitochondrial ferritin (gene name FTMT), which is known to be a homo-oligomer, is present in mammalian mitochondria and shows TNC values distributed around the TNC peak of FTH. We further collected the sequences of invertebrate ferritins. Although the TNC distribution of invertebrate ferritins is broad, its peak is consistent with the peaks of FTH and FTMT. Therefore, the negative charge saturation seems to be restricted to bacterial and plant ferritins.

Discussion

Electrokinetic charge of ferritin

Here, we evaluated the charges of EcFtn and PmFtn and their mutant proteins by native PAGE and ζ-potential measurements. Because ζ-potential measurement is principally based on electrophoretic movements of particles, the charge measured in this study is electrokinetic, not the charge of ferritin itself. In the diffusive double layer model of Gouy–Chapman32[,33, electrokinetic charge (Qe) is related to ζ-potential (ζ) as follows:

If a ferritin molecule can be regarded as an impenetrable hard sphere, the mobility in native PAGE and ζ-potential are related to the electrokinetic charge, that is, the charge of ferritin itself (Q) and surrounding ions inside the plane of shear. In the case of ferritin, however, we must consider the ions within the central cavity. If the ions cannot pass through the ferritin shell, ferritin migrates, keeping ions inside the cavity. In this case, the charges on an inner surface are neutralized by counterions and do not contribute to the electrokinetic charge. Several reports on the electrophoretic mobilities and ζ-potential have shown that this is the case. Fan and coworkers11 engineered human H-ferritin variants HFn(+) in which Glu and Asp facing inward were replaced with Lys or Arg to accommodate negatively charged siRNA in the cavity. Because they replaced seven acidic residues with positively charged residues, this mutation is expected to cause a + 14 change in the net charge of the subunit. However, it has been shown that the ζ-potential of HFn(+) is not changed significantly from that of WT H-ferritin, demonstrating that the mutation inside ferritin does not influence the electrokinetic charge. Behera and coworkers34 substituted inward- and outward-facing residues of frog M-ferritin. Although the inward-facing mutants D38A and E135K did not affect the mobility of native PAGE, the mobility of the outward-facing mutant D40A was reduced, and the mobility of the outward-facing mutant K104E was increased. Therefore, ferritin can be regarded as a “nondraining” particle during electrophoresis.

Mutations introduced in the present study should be reflected in native PAGE and ζ-potential results because they are outward-facing mutants except for Q121 of EcFtn (Figs. 1 and 3). Therefore, we conclude that at least the negative charge on the outer surface does not increase beyond the charge of WT EcFtn and PmFtn, even though additional acidic residues are introduced. Furthermore, we consider that this charge saturation is because of a pK shift of some carboxy groups, and that the shift results from the highly negative electrostatic potential at the outer surface of ferritin.

Generality of charge saturation

Here, we have shown that there is a limit to the negative charge that ferritins can bear. Is there a limitation for all proteins? From a physics viewpoint, charge saturation should occur for all macromolecules. To our knowledge, however, there is no report showing net-charge saturation. Lawrence et al.35 created “supercharged” variants of green fluorescent protein (GFP), streptavidin, and glutathione-S-transferase (GST). They attained the TNCs of + 48 and − 30 for GFP, + 52 and − 40 for streptavidin, and − 40 for GST. Although the net charge was not confirmed experimentally for these variants, the number of TNCs of these supercharged proteins is much larger than − 13 and − 16 observed in the present study for EcFtn and PmFtn, respectively. Therefore, the charge saturation observed for ferritins are cage protein-specific phenomena. The dimer of QE mutants showed that migration is dependent on the number of mutations (Fig. 2D), supporting this conclusion.

Effective charge saturation may occur in other symmetrical cage proteins, such as DPS, encapsulin, lumazine synthase, and virus capsids. Unfortunately, we could not find any data in the literature indicating the charge saturation in other cage proteins. Hilvert and coworkers3 introduced four negatively charged residues in the luminal surface of Aquifex aeolicus lumazine synthase (AaLS) to encapsulate its positively charged proteins. AaLS assembles into a virus-like icosahedral cage consisting of 60 subunits. The TNC of AaLS is only − 3. The mutant with four additional negatively charged residues (called AaLS-neg), of which TNC = − 7, was further optimized for encapsulation by directed evolution (AaLS-13)4. The TNC of AaLS-13 is − 12. However, in this case, the assembly was surprisingly changed from a 60-mer of AaLS to a 180-mer of AaLS-neg and 360-mer of AaLS-137. These changes in assembly numbers resulted from the difference in the diameter of the spherical cage (16 nm of AaLS, 29 nm of AaLS-neg, and 39 nm of AaLS-13). Although the charge states of AaLS and its variants are not known, the pK shift, if any, is not significant in the larger cage because the magnitude of the electrostatic potential and charge density at the shell surface decreases as the radius increases. In other words, AaLS may select the quaternary structure change instead of pK shifts to avoid the high free energy of the system. It is known that ferritins from Thermotoga maritima and Archaeoglobus fulgidus dissociate at low salt concentrations36,37[,38. Because salt concentrations reduce the electrostatic potential at the cage surface, these ferritins can form the cage structure only at high salt concentrations. They select the dissociation at low salt concentrations as a strategy to avoid the high free energy of the system.

Implication of the net charge limit for evolutions

The present study has shown that at least EcFtn and PmFtn bear maximally possible negative charges and that ferritins from bacteria and plants tend to have nearly maximum charges. As described above, the large number of negative charges is a disadvantage for the cage stability of ferritins. The tendency that bacteria and plant ferritins have nearly maximum charges suggests that there is a counterbalanced pressure to increase the net charge. Considering the selective pressures for negative charge increments during evolution, a possible factor is their functional advantage. Ferritins are iron-storage proteins. It has been shown that the electrostatic potential, especially around the threefold channel, which is suggested to be a route for Fe2+ entering the inner cavity, is important for Fe2+ transport39–42. Not only the gradient of electrostatic potential at the channel, but also the entire negative potential of the inner cavity can be important for the flux of Fe2+ into the cavity. This hypothesis should be addressed in future work.

Materials and methods

Construction of expression plasmids

EcFtn was overexpressed in E. coli BL21 (DE3) as described previously21. The PmFtn gene was synthesized by Eurofins Genomics (Tokyo) based on the amino acid sequence of a recombinant used in a previous study43. The PmFtn gene was inserted into an expression vector (pET-21a). The mutant genes were constructed using the QuikChange Site-Direct Mutagenesis Kit (Agilent Technology). The DNA sequences were confirmed by Eurofins Genomics. E. coli BL21 (DE3) cells were transformed with these vectors.

Protein expression and purification

E. coli cells harboring the expression plasmid were grown at 37 °C, and protein expression was induced by the addition of 0.1 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside. Cultivation was continued overnight at 37 °C for EcFtn and its mutants, and at 20 °C or 10 °C for PmFtn. Cells were then collected by centrifugation, resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline, and lysed by sonication. The supernatant of the cell lysates was heated at 75 °C for 10 min and cooled rapidly in ice water. The solution was centrifuged, and the supernatant was collected. After polyethyleneimine treatment, the protein was precipitated with ammonium sulfate. The pellet was suspended in 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0) and dialyzed against the same buffer at 4 °C. In the case of EcFtn and its mutants, the dialyzed solution was subjected to Sephacryl S-300 chromatography. The fraction containing the proteins was further purified by Q-Sepharose ion exchange chromatography with a linear NaCl concentration gradient of 0.1–1.0 M. The ferritin oligomers were removed by gel filtration chromatography with a Superose 6 Increase column. During purification of PmFtn and its mutant proteins, tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine was added to buffer solutions at a concentration of 1 mM. To remove bound iron, the fraction containing EcFtn, PmFtn and their mutants was treated with 5 mM sodium dithionite and 10 mM EDTA. Finally, the buffer was exchanged to 20 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) using a Sephadex G-25 column. The purity of proteins was confirmed by reverse-phase chromatography using high-performance liquid chromatography and sodium dodecyl sulfate–PAGE.

Electrophoresis

Native PAGE was performed in 5% or 7% polyacrylamide gels at a constant current of 30 mA at pH 8.044 or pH 9.545. The pIs were determined by isoelectric focusing using a PhastGel IEF 5–8 and a Phast system (GE Healthcare). The samples were dialyzed against distilled water before application. Native PAGE of the reassembled sample was performed as follows. EcFtn and its net-charge mutants were dissociated by mixing the protein solution and 0.5 M phosphoric acid at a ratio of 15:1, resulting in 50 mM sodium phosphate (pH 2.5). The reassembly was initiated by mixing this protein solution and a solution containing 50 mM Tris, 2 mM EDTA/2Na, and 37.5 mM NaOH at a ratio of 1:1, which gave conditions of 25 mM Tris, 25 mM phosphate, and 1 mM EDTA; pH 8.0. Immediately after reassembly initiation, the samples were mixed with a sample buffer for native PAGE and applied.

Zeta potential measurements

Zeta potential was measured using a DelsaMax Pro instrument (Beckman Coulter) at 20 °C. The measurements were performed with a 15-s duration, a 10-Hz electric field frequency, and an applied voltage of 2.5 V. The protein sample prepared in 50 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 8.0) was diluted to a final protein concentration of 0.7 mg/mL. For the salt-containing condition, a 50 mM Tris-HCl buffer containing 500 mM NaCl was used for dilution to yield a final NaCl concentration of 100 mM. All measurements were performed in triplicate, and the results are expressed as the mean ± standard error.

CD spectrum measurements

CD spectra were measured with a Chirascan CD spectrometer (Applied Photophysics) at 25 °C. Near- and far-CD spectra were recorded in cuvettes with 10 mm and 1 mm optical path lengths, respectively. The protein concentration was determined with extinction coefficients at 280 nm of 24,000 M[–1cm[–1 for EcFtn and its mutants and 25,200 M[–1cm[–1 for PmFtn and its mutants. The extinction coefficients were determined by the method of Gill and von Hippel46.

AUC experiments

Sedimentation velocity experiments were performed using a Beckman XL-I analytical ultracentrifuge and An-50Ti rotor. The centrifugation was done at a rotor speed of 30,000 rpm and 20 °C, and sedimentation was monitored at 280 nm at 2 min intervals with a radial step size of 0.002 cm. The solution conditions were 20 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0). Data were analyzed by SEDFIT software47.

SAXS measurements

SAXS was measured at BL6A or BL10C of the Photon Factory at the High Energy Accelerator Research Organization, Tsukuba. The X-ray wavelength was 1.5 Å, and the camera length was 2 m, which was calibrated with a powder diffraction of silver behenate. Scattering profiles were collected by PILATUS3 1 M or 2 M (Dectris). To minimize the effect of X-ray radiation damage on the scattering data, a flow cell was used. The temperature was controlled at 25 °C by circulating water from a thermostat bath. To obtain one-dimensional scattering data, the scattering intensities on two-dimensional images were averaged azimuthally using Sangler48. The radius of gyration, Rg, was obtained using GNOM49.

TNC calculations

The bacterial ferritin sequences were extracted from the UniProt database26 by selecting the data of protein name “ferritin” and gene name “FtnA.” The dataset was further restricted as the sequence length ranged from 130 to 300. The final dataset for bacterial ferritin contains 1884 sequences. The heme-containing bacterioferritin sequences (185) were similarly extracted by gene name “bfrB” instead of “FtnA.” The plant ferritin sequences were extracted from the group with protein name “ferritin” and taxonomic name “Viridiplantae.” Furthermore, the sequence length was limited from 150 to 370. Finally, the data containing the protein name of “ferritin-like” or “diiron containing four-helix bundle family ferritin protein, putative” were excluded. From the sequences thus obtained, transit peptides were removed based on the prediction by the TargetP-2.0 server (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/TargetP-2.0/)27. The number of sequences in this data set is 1311. Invertebrate ferritin sequences were extracted using the following criteria: protein name “ferritin,” taxonomy “Protostomia,” and sequence length ranging from 130 to 300. This data set contains 2482 sequences. Vertebrate ferritins are selected by protein name “ferritin,” gene name “FTH” for H-chains (295), “FTL” for L-chains (283), and “FTMT” for mitochondrial ferritins (87). In these cases, sequence length was also restricted from 130 to 300. For mitochondrial ferritin sequences, signal peptides were removed according to predictions generated by the TargetP-2.0 server. TNCs were calculated using in-house software. The distribution of TNC was calculated as the population of the sequence that has a given TNC within each data set. For example, if FTH sequences of which TNC = − 8 is 146, its population is 146/295 = 0.49.
