Perceived health related quality of life among cancer patients based on insights from Kamuzu central hospital patients, caregivers and staff
Authors: Jonathan Chiwanda Banda, Agatha Bula, Mercy Tsidya, Michael Udedi, Adamson Sinjani Muula
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Patients living with cancer and their families experience several challenges that affect their health-related quality of life (HRQOL). Perceived factors affecting the quality of life (QOL) among patients living with cancer have rarely been studied. We aimed to explore the factors perceived to be associated with health-related quality of life among patients living with cancer attending a National Cancer Centre at Kamuzu Central Hospital in Malawi. Between May and June 2022, we conducted a qualitative study to assess perceptions and lived experiences of patients living with cancer, their caregivers, and healthcare workers working at the cancer centre on health-related quality of life. We purposively selected and interviewed thirteen participants. Interviews were transcribed, coded using NVivo 12, and analyzed using thematic analysis. Two broad categories of emerging themes of quality of life were identified. These included: (a) individual-level factors such as experiences with diagnosis and treatment, changes in physical, psychological, and social health, support received, benefits, and challenges, (b) facility-level factors such as availability of resources, relationship with providers, satisfaction with cancer care, facility organization, facilitators and barriers to improved quality of life. This study has highlighted the importance of physical, psychological, and social factors as key determinants perceived to be affecting health-related quality of life among patients living with cancer and their caregivers at the National Cancer Centre, Kamuzu Central Hospital in Malawi. The QOL challenges experienced by patients and their caregivers indicated that cancer care interventions should consider a holistic approach to cover all dimensions of life in order to improve health-related quality of life among patients living with cancer.

Introduction

Cancer remains a major global public health challenge, with an estimated 19.3 million new cases and nearly 10 million deaths reported in 2020 1. This burden is projected to escalate to 28.4 million new cases by 2040, a 47% increase from 2020—with disproportionately higher growth expected in Low- and Middle-Income Countries (LMICs) (64–95%) compared to High-Income Countries (HICs) (32–56%)1. In Malawi, cancer accounted for approximately 16% of Disability-Adjusted Life Years (DALYs) attributed to non-communicable diseases (NCDs) in 20152. The five most prevalent cancers in the country as of 2020 were Kaposi sarcoma (34.1%), cervical cancer (25.4%), esophageal cancer (12.0%), non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma (5.7%), and bladder cancer (2.9%)3.

Beyond its clinical manifestations, cancer significantly impacts the health-related quality of life (HRQOL) of patients and their families4,5. Following diagnosis, individuals frequently experience denial, emotional distress, impaired concentration, disrupted sleep, loss of appetite, irritability, and recurring thoughts about the future6. Families also confront numerous psychosocial challenges, including breaking the news of diagnosis to relatives, managing household responsibilities, adjusting social roles, and navigating altered life plans7. The World Health Organization (WHO) defines quality of life (QOL) as an individual’s perception of their position in life within the cultural and value systems they inhabit, and in relation to their goals, expectations, standards, and concerns8. QOL is a dynamic construct encompassing physical, emotional, social, and cognitive domains, shaped by reactions to diagnostic outcomes and the psychological stages of sadness, grief, and anger4,9,10.

Global studies have highlighted a variety of factors contributing to diminished QOL among cancer patients and their families11. These include systemic issues such as understaffing, fragmented care continuity, professional incompetence, and disorganized healthcare systems, as reported in Norway12. Additional concerns include inadequate communication and information-sharing, which can result in distressing and unpredictable illness trajectories4,10. Patients in Belgium, Italy, and Ghana have cited debilitating physical symptoms, such as nausea, fatigue, pain, skin conditions, and altered physical appearance as significant contributors to poor QOL10,13. Psychologically, worries about disease progression, future uncertainty, and loss of autonomy are common9. Social support and meaningful engagement with healthcare professionals and family networks have also emerged as vital components for sustaining QOL13,14. In Uganda, men with prostate cancer described the absence of informational support, limited access to healthcare providers, feelings of isolation, and lack of support groups as key factors negatively affecting their HRQL15. However, data on perceived determinants of QOL among cancer patients in Malawi remain scarce16posing challenges to developing targeted interventions for physical, emotional, and social support. This study was therefore conducted to explore the perceived determinants of HRQOL among patients living with cancer at the National Cancer Center located at Kamuzu Central Hospital in Malawi.

Methods and materials

Study design and setting

We conducted a descriptive qualitative study to explore perceptions of health-related quality of life among patients living with cancer, their caregivers, and healthcare workers (HCWs) at the National Cancer Centre of Kamuzu Central Hospital (KCH), a tertiary public care facility based in Lilongwe, the capital city of Malawi. Although we adopted a qualitative descriptive approach, we acknowledged that a phenomenological design would have been more appropriate for capturing the full depth of lived experiences. However, our intent was to generate broad thematic insights rather than pursue an in-depth phenomenological analysis17,18.

At the conduct of the study, the cancer centre was a newly built oncology unit which provided chemotherapy and palliative care services in both admission and out-patient capacities to the referred patients. It served as a main oncology referral for the central and northern regions of Malawi.

Participants and sample size

This study purposively enrolled three distinct participant groups: patients living with cancer, their caregivers, and healthcare workers (HCWs). The inclusion of these groups was intended to provide a comprehensive understanding of health-related quality of life (QOL) from multiple perspectives. Patients offered firsthand accounts of symptom burden and treatment experiences; caregivers contributed insights into the challenges of providing support beyond the clinical setting; and HCWs reflected on service delivery and systemic barriers within oncology care.

Eligible patients were adults (≥ 18 years) with histologically confirmed cancer of any type or stage, attending the clinic. Those who were critically ill or unable to provide informed consent were excluded. Caregivers were defined as adults who provided daily support to cancer patients attending the clinic but were not patients themselves. HCWs included nursing officers, clinicians, and clinic supervisors actively involved in oncology care during the study period.

Data collection

Data collection utilized an interview guide developed by the Principal Investigator (PI) in collaboration with the supervisor. The guide was tailored to each participant group; patients, caregivers and healthcare workers (HCWs), using a contextual model of health-related quality of life (HRQOL)19. This theoretical framework conceptualizes HRQOL as a multidimensional and dynamic construct encompassing physical, psychological, and social domains. It emphasizes how disease and its treatment affect broader aspects of an individual’s life, including their general perception of physical and mental health20. Accordingly, the interview guide included questions addressing both individual-level and systemic or facility-level factors that may influence HRQOL. Individual-level factors focused on physical and psychological dimensions, while systemic factors explored institutional and service delivery challenges.

Prior to participation, individuals were provided with an information sheet and informed consent form to review and sign. Interviews were conducted in a private room arranged by facility staff to ensure confidentiality. Patients and caregivers were interviewed in Chichewa, while HCWs were interviewed in English. All interviews were conducted by a trained bilingual interviewer fluent in both languages.

Data collection was conducted until thematic saturation was achieved within and across participant categories, meaning no new themes emerged from additional interviews21,22. Although the overall sample size was modest, saturation was assessed independently for each group. A total of 13 interviews were conducted; 4 with patients, 4 with caregivers, and 5 with healthcare workers.

Data analysis

Demographic characteristics were summarized and presented descriptively. The audio recordings were transcribed and translated into English by the interviewer and reviewed by the Principal Investigator (PI) for accuracy and completeness. A codebook was then developed through pre-determined themes from the interview guides and the team read two transcripts to further identify common themes and sub-themes emerging from the data. We continued adding new themes emerging from the data during the coding process. All transcripts were double-coded by two authors (JCB and MT) using NVivo version 1.7 and a coding comparison was done. Inter-rater reliability for this study was set at 95% and any differences below 95% were discussed and consensus reached. Data were analyzed using thematic analysis where themes were summarized using memos and matrices. We used the following predetermined themes: physical factors, psychological, social, and healthcare system components of HRQOL to guide the analysis process.

Ethical consideration

Ethical clearance was obtained from the Kamuzu University of Health Sciences Research Ethics Committee (COMREC) bearing approval number COMREC P.05/22/3647. An approval letter permitting us to conduct the study at the site was obtained from the Hospital Director. We obtained written informed consent from participants and those who could not read and write had fingerprint impressions taken upon consenting.

Emerging individual-level themes perceived to determine quality of life

Knowledge of cancer diagnosis, reaction after diagnosis, experience with treatment, change in physical, psychological, and social health, support received, benefits and challenges.

Knowledge about cancer diagnosis

Knowledge of cancer disease included patients’ and caregivers’ understanding of the cancer disease, potential benefits and side effects of the proposed therapy. All four patients and three caregivers demonstrated knowledge of the type and nature of the cancer diagnoses, mentioning specific cancer types and affected organs. Similarly, they could narrate how a patient suffering from cancer could have a chance of being cured from the disease given a timely and appropriate treatment while also accepting the possibility of death due to the disease. One caregiver narrated:

“Cancer is a difficult disease, like in our case, he started in 2019 and he seemed to recover, then it resurfaced. From my experience, it is a difficult disease because it is causing many health problems and his immunity has become weaker than before, his appetite has gone down, body weakness, everything is affected” (Caregiver # 2). “She is suffering from cancer which is in the esophagus and it is affecting her eating habit. She does not eat as before; she has lost appetite and complains of painful swallowing.” (Caregiver # 3). Similarly, patients knew their disease condition. One patient said: “It is cancer involving the breast. I don’t know the specific area in the breast but it affects also other parts around the breast. I have loss weight, experiencing general body weakness, sometimes I vomit a lot particularly during the time I am receiving chemotherapy treatment.” (Patient # 4). Additionally, the patients also knew that cancer was a disease that could be cured, it could recur and there was a chance of a complete cure. One patient indicated: “I was told that cancer is a disease that can sometimes spread, sometimes in can stick to one place, sometimes you can recover and sometimes you can die.” (Patient # 4).

Physical health experiences following cancer diagnosis

These were physical appearances and body changes due to cancer diagnosis; and its treatment and they included the following: weight loss, skin changes, body weakness, body scarring, vomiting, and many others. In the current study, patients reported pain, vomiting, weight loss, fatigue, swelling of body parts, and anemia as presenting symptoms. One patient said:

“This disease has drastically changed my physical health. For example, I don’t have one breast on the left, it was removed. Additionally, I feel strong pain inside the body particularly, where the breast was removed and of course I have lost of weight. This is not my usual weight. Sometimes I even fail to take a bath on my own. I cannot wash clothes to make them look clean on my own. I rely on someone to do it for me. In addition, I experience loss of sensation on two of my fingers on the left.” (Patient # 1).

Another patient indicated unpredictability of body fitness due to sudden changes in disease symptoms. “I cannot work as before because in most cases I am weak. For example, I can be fine in the morning but in the afternoon, I find that I am shivering and that means I am not going to do anything, I have to sleep. I feel hopeless and always in fear about what will happen next. What is more dangerous is that I am losing a lot of blood from my private parts and always changing pads frequently.” (Patient # 2). Caregivers also reported physical challenges experienced by their patients. One caregiver said: “With the cancer disease, the patient is not in good condition. She cannot walk nor take a bath. It is a difficult situation. Even eating is a problem. She is selective in food uptake. The pain is so severe. The leg is swollen and the disease changed her/his quality of life. (Caregiver # 4).

Psychological experiences following cancer diagnosis

These were emotional experiences by the patients, caregivers and families following a cancer diagnosis, and these included: denial, anger, anxiety, distress, and depression among others. Most participants were overtaken by disbelief, denial, fear, and anxiety about the diagnosis because they perceived it as a disease associated with a long period of suffering and that someone may not fully recover and return to normal function again. One patient reported: “I was very disappointed when I was told at the hospital that I have cancer of the skin. It was something that took me time to understand. I had many questions like “What happened to me to suffer from cancer? Is it preventable or not so that I can tell my relatives to take control measures so that they do not suffer from cancer.” I was really disappointed and the diagnosis changed my life.” (Patient # 3).

Another caregiver had a similar comment on the cancer diagnosis: “When I heard that it is cancer, it affected me badly. Considering her age, I was badly affected and this made me fall sick as well because I am very close to her and she is very intelligent at school. So, thinking about the length of the cancer treatment, I was badly affected because I thought that she would be affected in some areas of her education. So, I was affected so much.” (Caregiver # 3).

The feeling of being hopeless was observed from the patients: “I am always living in fear, “maybe I am not going to recover, maybe I am going to die. With the pain that I am experiencing, maybe I will die by next month. So, I am not sure about my future and all my plans ahead are no longer my priority anymore.” (Patient # 1). These stages of emotional reaction to cancer diagnosis were expected reactions considering the deadliness of the disease however, such reactions could be therapeutic when coping with the disease23.

Acceptance to a cancer diagnosis

It was observed that both patients and caregivers required enough time to think over the new disease situation. They needed time of reflection as reported by one patient indicating it took him a long period to accept the disease and re-adjust life expectations and plans23.

One patient said: “I have now accepted the disease condition which I am suffering because even if I do not agree, I cannot change anything. Of course, at the beginning I felt it cannot be me suffering from this cancer and I was always wondering how this has happened to me, but when I started receiving treatment, I can say I am now feeling better.” (Patient # 2). Similarly, one caregiver said: “I was concerned when I heard that she has cancer considering the severity of this disease. The person becomes weak, she cannot do her normal cores. So, I asked her “Did you go early at the clinic?” She said “I went late”. So, I was so worried. However, I believe with the treatment that she is receiving, she can get better.” (Caregiver #3).

Additionally, another patient reported a positive transformation from being completely hopeless to a new level of self-belief following cancer treatment. The improvement in physical symptoms was reportedly being key to gaining confidence of a possible cure to the disease. This was what he said during the interview: “Mh… I should say that during the time the doctor told me that I have cancer, I could not believe it and I was hopeless “I always asked myself questions about whether I will be, okay?” But over the time I started getting treatment from here at the hospital, slowly I am regaining my hope because I am improving. I can differentiate because I was not able to do anything before but now, I can. I am hopeful that I am going to recover” (Patient # 4).

Social life experiences following cancer disease

Patients living with cancer reportedly experienced problems in various aspects of social life, such as family life, relationships with people around them, work, income, leisure activities, and relationships with healthcare providers24. One patient indicated how much his work was affected and no longer being relied upon due to body weakness. His explanation was clear as follows:

“At our neighborhood, it changed very much because people depend on you that “she can buy us sugar, salt, fertilizer” but this disease changed so much that I am not able to do things like those. Similarly, at work, they stopped relying on me regarding my position because of my condition. They just maintain me that “this one is a meter reader but they cannot rely on me much because I am not working most of the days. Even at home, the children relied on me that “Mother is going to wash for us but you find that a child is coming to school but there is no food while I am there. Things that were not happening before. They were finding everything done by me. Yeah, so things changed very much.” (Patient # 1).

Patients reportedly struggled to do the chores that could support a living as before. They were not able to support their families and felt guilt about that experience. On top of failing to contribute to family needs, patients complained of failure to support themselves with usual activities due to disease severity “From the time I have been suffering from this disease, I have stopped working because I am always weak without energy. Before this disease, I was doing business but I can no longer travel long distances such as going to South Africa, Zambia, or Mozambique for business like I used to be doing. So, my life has changed, I look forward to recover so that I can continue doing my business.” (Patient # 2). Due to protracted disease conditions and avoidance of inviting several questions related to their disease from friends, some patients resorted to self-isolation. This was observable by one caregiver who indicated how social interaction has been affected by his patient during the period of suffering from cancer.

“The patient is very worried, he currently isolates himself but previously he was playing football, and working on electronics, now few friends come to see him but previously he had so many friends. He is lonely and has depressed mood because he isolated and always staying along. He does not want to be in a group anymore. When the caregivers are away, it makes him feel more worried. So, his social life is compromised as compared to the past.” (Caregiver # 4).

Support received from healthcare workers, families and friends

Participants reported various types of support they received from family, friends, and the community which included material, spiritual and psychosocial support. One patient reported positive socio-economic support which she was getting from friends such as taking her to the hospital during appointment schedules. They were able to contribute money for transport and other needs. “My relatives and friends have been supporting me in various ways. My fellow businesswomen have been supporting me. They are the ones who have been driving me to the hospital here. They have been contributing money for transportation. They are supportive and the relatives took part.” (Patient # 2). Caregivers narrated being supportive to their patients. Providing daily care, encouraging, and making sure prescription was followed and moral support in prayers. The continuous presence of caregivers was key to a feeling of being loved during the period of suffering, and this was vindicated by strong comments from them. One caregiver said:

“Firstly, when we knew it was cancer, we started telling her the issue of prayer. We started by telling her that “This is not the end of your life, this is a new life and for us to stay in this new life, we need to pray hard.” That is the first thing that we encourage her. We also encourage her the importance of consistent drug uptake and adherence. We encourage her to take drugs and when the time to go to the clinic has come, we encourage her “You need to avail yourself at the clinic at this time.” We make sure she arrives at the clinic at the right time so that she receives drugs at the right time.” (Caregiver # 3).

Emerging facility-level themes perceived to determine quality of life

This included materials, human, and organizational resources affecting health systems’ ability to provide individualized/personalized care25. Availability of resources such as drugs and supplies, availability of facility space and infrastructure, relationship with healthcare workers, experience with treatment, relationship with providers, satisfaction with care, facility organization, support from the facility, barriers to QOL, things which needed to be changed at the health facility are some of the contributory determinants to quality of life.

Drugs and supplies availability

Drugs and supplies were reportedly not always being available at the facility. However, the patients and caregivers understood issues of supply chain problems, and therefore their perception was positive. One patient indicated that although some drugs were out of stock, the management of the facility tried hard to find alternatives so that the service would not suffer. “I can say that the availability of the drugs at the pharmacy is the same because if there is no Brufen, we only find Panadol. If there is no Diclofenac, we find Tramadol. If one type is not available, the other type is available but they are all painkillers.” (Patient # 3).

Facility space for service provision

There were mixed reactions reported by patients and caregivers in terms of space availability at the facility. Others would say it was spacious while others said there was not enough space for patients in the waiting area. Additionally, the perception could have been influenced by the socio-economic background where someone was coming from and hence being subjective. One patient said: “Form the time I have been visiting this hospital for treatment, I have started getting used to the place. There is enough space at least where we can sit while waiting for treatment… [Chuckles]… and there are good beddings and the availability of food. In most cases, we leave the food because we say “we are full.” (Patient # 1). While the caregiver complained about how waiting space was not enough for them, it was clear participants had different receptions as shown here: “They sit here, there is space and sometimes when there are many people, they wait for someone to go inside and they sit on the chair because on Tuesdays there are many people.” (Caregiver # 1).

Relationship with healthcare workers

Participants reported the empathetic relationship that doctors and nurses provided. It was however noted that some staff were harsh. Cancer information was provided accordingly. Patients responded positively by indicating that they were satisfied with the service they received at the facility. The support staff such as watchmen and cleaners could speak politely and they were willing to support whenever necessary however it depended on how they had been approached.

“Ever since I have been coming to this hospital, I can say the hospital staff is friendly, they listen attentively to our problem and they treat us based on our presentation of the problem. All the staff are friendly starting from doctors, nurses, including hospital guards are not harsh, they are polite to us,” We have good relationship with them. For example, when we are new here, we first go there and they can to escort us to the required place. even the clinic aides, when you are sitting at a place where they want to mop, they ask politely; can you move to the other side, I would like to mop.” They don’t shout or insult us.” (Patient # 3).

Sometimes, the perception depends on the personality of the healthcare worker on duty. Others were very friendly while others were harsh. It was narrated that some healthcare workers did not pay attention to their disease status however, only a few of them had such personalities. One caregiver said:

“Our relationship with nurses is good but there are times when they are busy with other things instead of focusing on the patient. You find that they are playing on the phone or chatting with friends, something that is not helpful. In terms of other staff, they are not helpful. For example, today when we were coming, we found them mopping. So, I was surprised “why mopping this time? Why can’t they come early and mop in good time?” So, they send you out so that you give them a chance to mop. “Wait, they have gone to collect the drugs”, so these workers delay the processes.” (Caregiver # 4).

Satisfaction with cancer care

This included patients’ and families’ expectations, preferences, and perceptions of quality of care, including appropriateness, coordination, access, and continuity/transition of care, followed by emotional support and physical comfort. All the participants specifically patients and caregivers reported to have been satisfied with the care they received at the KCH cancer clinic. One patient defined satisfaction in terms of timely acquisition of prescribed treatment by the attending physician. “ I feel satisfied with the treatment I am receiving at this hospital. I cannot complain, they are trying their best and to me, everything is in order”. (Patient # 4). Another patient had similar comments. “I am satisfied because when we talk about the right time, we are considering that you went to the facility early and were diagnosed at the right time. In that way, you can be assisted and it is possible but if you went there late, there is nothing that the doctor can do because they are not God.” (Patient # 1).

Participants commented to have satisfaction with the service provision despite a few delays in getting chemotherapy treatment. The delay in the perception of one caregiver was due to long the distances healthcare workers would travel to the pharmacy to get their supplies and it was taken positively. “We are satisfied although sometimes they delay because drugs are stored in pharmacy but we are not worried because we come here at the facility to get treatment.” (Caregiver # 1).

Facility organization

The organization of the clinic was described in terms of patient flow at the facility, demarcation of the consultation rooms, availability of admission rooms, record keeping, appointment dates for patients etc. This type of organization received mixed responses from participants. Most of the patients had indicated that the cancer clinic was well organized.

“I feel like the management of this facility is not good enough. Every time I come here; I do not find many hospital staff available. Mostly they come around 10 or eleven or they have conflicts with each other, we would say there are poor organization on part of management.” (Patient # 4).

One caregiver had also commented positively about time management by staff members to be good, however, there were certain lower cadres at the hospital which were slow at disbursing laboratory results:

“The organization of this facility is okay; we only complain the time when we are coming for results. When we come for sample collection, the providers are fast because they have many people on the queue and they make sure the assist them to go home in good time but when we come for results, the providers are slow and when we delay in receiving the results, we go to the doctor late and it means we delay in receiving the drugs. This takes much of our time and we leave late in the afternoon. So, I would recommend that those who give the results should be punctual so that we can go to the doctor in good time.” (Caregiver # 3).

However, some participants particularly healthcare workers commented on the non-availability of some infrastructure such as blood bank as affecting the organization of the facility since samples travel a long distance. In the process, some samples go missing thus affecting quality of care given to patients. “Ah… my comment on the clinic setup could be that this clinic is not yet finished as you see it. We don’t have our own theatre; we don’t have our own radiation room… and we also don’t have a laboratory. So, I feel that the setup is still in progress because we are lacking a lot of those facilities as I have said on the laboratory thing, when we want to collect blood, platelets, or other blood products, as well as when we want to send samples, we travel a long distance to the lab which KCH has.” (Healthcare worker #1).

Facilitators to improve quality of life

The commitment and their attitudes towards the cancer patients were important benefits of the cancer clinic. Patients and caregivers showed satisfaction with the care they were getting from the hospital in terms of reception, and food provision at the facility under Government support. They also expressed satisfaction with the willingness of healthcare workers to listen to their concerns. One patient said: “Ah… I think patients benefit a lot more when they come and visit this hospital. The providers are friendly. We can to come and be given drugs and results. We also receive food. These are good things that we experience (Patient # 3). The other patient also added his comment to the service provision satisfaction as reported in the following narrative: “I can say that patients benefit a lot when they come here, the doctors are keen to assist the patients and that the facility is smart because if it is dirty, the caregivers contract diseases. So, this makes many people come and access care from this facility.” (Caregiver # 2).

Barriers to improved quality of life

These were problems or challenges that patients and their caregivers faced in their pursuit of obtaining cancer care. The barriers were categorized as individual, social, environmental, and health systems factors. These included: drug and supply shortages, inadequate human resource for health, lack of kitchens for caregivers because the hospital policy in Malawi only allows provision of free food to patients, lack of radiotherapy services, and lack of laboratory and pharmacy infrastructure specific to cancer center were some the obvious challenges as reported by patients and caregivers. One caregiver narrated the following: unavailability of some drugs, long distance to the clinic, and shortage of clinical staff members. The quality of care was affected by missing specimen samples due to long distances of travel to where they were being processed. There was a wastage of precious time that could have been invested in running other hospital duties “Even the samples themselves, sometimes go missing and patients do not receive them on that day, the patient has to come on another day. All those things are affecting the quality of life of our patients. They move long distances to access the theatre. It is time-consuming; it is not good. Another thing is that we have a hood where we prepare our chemotherapy. So, the hood is very far away from our facility because we move from the cancer center to a place near the pharmacy…. he coughs.” (Healthcare worker #1). Another barrier was described in terms of drug shortages at the facility which affected the quality of care given to patients “Lack of medications is the main barrier because mostly, our patients are prescribed two drugs but you find that they are only given one drug, that is chemotherapy. Sometimes they can be prescribed maybe two drugs but you find that we don’t have both of them or they can be given one drug and that has an effect on the improvement of the patient. On the same medications, some patients go to the pharmacy to receive antiemetics or painkillers but you find that they don’t have and when they go home, they vomit but they don’t have any antiemetics. Later, their quality of life is affected and they end up coming back and being admitted to the hospital due to dehydration and the like. They even end up being admitted to the hospital because they have severe pain simply because they were not given the painkillers.” (Healthcare worker #2,3).

Similarly, a lack of kitchen for caregivers because the hospital does not provide food to them was reported: “There is no kitchen at the cancer centre for caregiver because according to the hospital policy, they are only giving food to patients. Caregivers have to cook on themselves elsewhere. Usually, in other hospitals, guardian shelters are used as kitchen for caregivers but here we do not have guardian shelter. We cook outside there under gmelina trees and we are worried of being attacked by thugs.” (Caregiver #2). Another caregiver complained of transport problems from home during appointment schedules. One caregiver indicated the following: “The challenge is when we come here on our hard-earned transport which is expensive and difficult to find and we find that the doctor who was supposed to provide the care is not available.” (Caregiver # 4).

Things to change for improved quality of care

These were challenges or problems which required to be addressed to improve the cancer service delivery. All participants including healthcare workers agreed that the cancer clinic should have its own surgeons, cancer specialist, radiation facility, pharmacy hood, laboratory, X-ray, and blood bank. The availability of these services would shorten the waiting time for patients while improving service delivery at the facility. One caregiver reported: “This hospital is supposed to change in terms of infrastructure. For instance, there should be a blood bank right here. Pharmacy, and laboratory should be here. This is a big hospital and one should not be going out there to get services from the pharmacy or laboratory.” (Caregiver # 1). Another healthcare worker commented on the need for a cancer center to have its own radiotherapy services because cancer treatment requires multiple treatment modalities.

“So, it’s important that we have our own theatre, our own surgeons, and not moving long distances. So, it would be better if the hood is right here at the cancer centre. The other thing is the radiation centre; we need to have a radiation centre here so that there should be different treatments. We should not only depend on chemotherapy because some cancers respond to radiotherapy” (Healthcare worker # 4).

The issue of having a pharmacy away from the cancer clinic was also mentioned by patients. The unavailability of specific chemotherapy drugs and painkillers was a big challenge in pain control.

“Things that are supposed to change at this facility… for instance, at the pharmacy, we don’t know what their procurement process is like but it would be helpful if they could have stock of all the required drugs because we experience strong pain. We cannot fall asleep unless we take a painkiller. So, if they can try their best, things can go well with us.” (Patient # 1).

Patients complained of a lack of adequate cancer specialists at the facility, as such they were mostly been reviewed by general practitioners most of the time. Some patients and caregivers were not comfortable with the arrangement and felt it was not ideal for their treatment. “The things that I feel should change at this facility, when the patient is seen by the general practitioners, after three months she should be seen by a specialist. What normally happen is that our patients are always seen by junior doctors who are general practitioners. Although they get treatment, we feel it is better to get advice from specialist doctors” (Caregiver # 3).

Discussion

The study aimed to explore perceived factors associated with health-related quality of life among patients living with cancer. Table 1 indicates the sociodemographic characteristics of the respondents of the study. Two broad themes emerged which included the following: (1) Individual-level factors (e.g., type of cancer disease, reaction after diagnosis, experience with treatment, change on physical, psychological and social health, support received, benefits, and challenges) and (2) Facility level factors. The facility-level factors included: availability of resources, experience with treatment, relationship with providers, satisfaction with care, facility organization, and barriers to QOL, things to change at the facility. All four categories of respondents acknowledged the importance of physical, psychological, and social factors impacting the health-related quality of life among patients and their families.

In this study, both patients and caregivers exhibited an understanding of the cancer diagnosis in their narratives, indicating that healthcare providers had delivered adequate information. This suggested that effective communication extended beyond the patient to include family members, ensuring that all parties involved were well-informed about the disease12. Such communication is critical in cancer care, as it fosters collaboration between patients, caregivers, and the healthcare team, ultimately enhancing patient well-being and improving quality of life26. Communicating about concerns and decision-making is important during all phases of treatment and supportive care for cancer26,27. A patient-centered communication approach needs to be developed to reduce the emotional distress to patients and their families following a cancer diagnosis28. The results were also supported by the findings in Belgium and Italy where patients also provided good understanding of the disease condition they were going through10.

Patients and their caregivers received news of cancer diagnosis with strong reactions of disbelief, denial, and shock. Similar reactions were also reported in other studies4,6,10. Denial, anger, sadness, and depression are common reactions to cancer diagnosis6,29. Receipt of a cancer diagnosis is often deemed as bad news because it is widely regarded as synonymous with a death sentence28. This is particularly so because cancer is a life-threating illness often associated with a lot of physical disability such as loss of body functions, change in physical appearance, disruption of family and personal life plans, recurrence or progression of the disease, and high symptom burden6,10,11,30.

Patients in the current study complained of pain, fatigue, weight loss, and loss of usual daily activities as important physical challenges due to cancer and had a negative impact on their quality of life. Similar findings were also reported in other studies12,29,31. The feeling of hopelessness by patients and their caregivers to the cancer diagnosis was obvious in their narratives and impacted negatively to health-related quality of life6,11,12. Socially, patients indicated social detachment being demonstrated by self-isolation from friends, and stopped being relied upon at work or home due to reduced functionality11,29. In terms of psychosocial support, the patients spoke positively regarding the support they were getting from their caregivers and the facility. However, some were self-critical of the continued over-dependency on their caregivers which took away their autonomy and it was negatively impacting on their HRQOL10,12. They felt by being dependent on their caregivers, they were troubling their caregivers12. The role of the partner and wider support systems such as family, friends and religion through church members’ involvement were reported in most studies in South Africa, Malawi and elsewhere as essential components of adaptation to disease and facilitating good HRQOL11,32,33.

In terms of the systemic challenges, the National Cancer Centre where the current study was conducted was a newly established unit still under construction as such there were no social amenities such as a caregiver’s shelter and kitchen for caregivers. This was a challenge to caregivers because it implied walking long distances to the main hospital premises for food preparation and other daily needs. The management should consider expediting the construction of a caregiver’s shelter at the cancer centre.

Both the patients and caregivers, as presented in this study, underwent the process of accepting to the diagnosis and adjusting to life and this process took time. Acceptance doesn’t mean that anyone is giving up34. Acceptance allows you to take control of your life and it is a psychological indicator of the quality of adaptation to life with a disease34,35. An important finding was that patients conceded to have been undergoing the re-adjustment process as a form of acceptance because, on the contrary, denial had been shown to lead into adverse effects such as delays in seeking medical help and noncompliance with treatment36,37.

The study participants were critical of the erratic supply of chemotherapy drugs which implied frequent changing of drug regimens depending on which ones were available at the time of clinical review. This had the potential to negatively affect good quality of life. The experiences of frequent drug stock-outs were also shared by other disease programs in the Ministry of Health in Malawi and that management was engaging on the long term recapitalization of the Central Medical Stores Trust (CMST) as the institution mandated for procurement of drugs and supplies for public hospitals38,39. The cancer center was well organized and respondents were in cognizant that construction to facilitate the expansion of services (e.g., radiotherapy) was still underway. The facility had clear scheduling of appointments and this was highly commended by study participants.

Generally, patients and their caregivers in this study showed satisfaction to the service provided at the facility which implied that the quality-of-service delivery met their expectations and needs40. Patient satisfaction with cancer care is an important indicator of the quality of healthcare systems and it is a key determinant of HRQOL41,42. Normally, satisfied patients are more likely to comply with treatment, continue their use of medical services, and maintain a good relationship with their healthcare workers42.

The facilitators of improved QOL as described in this study such as the commitment and friendliness by staff to serve, and provision of food under government support were important components to leverage on drug compliance by patients which could impact positively on quality of life. Simple kindness as demonstrated by staff members in this study, was instrumental to diffuse negative emotions that were associated with cancer diagnosis and treatment and might eventually help to improve patients’ outcomes43. Similarly, the provision of meal rations at the facility was aimed at addressing the nutritional concerns of patients. Nutrition is a central factor in oncology, influencing the response to, and recovery from cancer diagnosis44.

The common barriers or challenges experienced by patients and their caregivers included long distances traveled to seek medical attention and drug unavailability. They were highlighted as barriers to improved quality of life and it was supported by other studies16,45. High travel costs negatively influence the stage of diagnosis, appropriate treatment, compliance, outcomes, and quality of life among cancer patients16,46,47. The non-availability of radiotherapy facilities was troubling considering that more than 50% of all cancer patients could benefit from radiotherapy services for treatment26. Respondents recommended that cancer center should have their own radiotherapy unit, theatre, surgical oncologists, pharmacy, and laboratory for improved access of services. During the conduct of the study, such services were shared with the main hospital premises, distant from the cancer centre.

Strengths and limitations of the study

The study was limited by a small sample size which affected transferability and depth of understanding of patient-only experiences. Similarly, data collection exercise could not be extended to Queen Elizabeth Central Hospital, (another large cancer care hospital in Malawi) as initially planned in the approved protocol due to logistical challenges hence the findings could not provide comparative analysis between the two major cancer centres16. However, the results in the current study provided a required descriptive overview on the perceived factors affecting quality of life among patients living with cancer and their caregivers. Future studies with larger patient samples are recommended to validate and expand these findings.

Conclusion

Our study complemented existing literature that underscored the need for holistic cancer care addressing not only physical symptoms but also psychological distress and social support in resource-limited settings32,48. The reported drug shortages and infrastructural limitations, in this study resonated with known challenges in Malawi’s healthcare system, underscoring the systemic barriers impacting patient outcomes. Furthermore, patients showed psychological evolution, migrating from denial and hopelessness to renewed hope with treatment, highlighting dynamic coping processes requiring further ongoing psychosocial interventions.
