A comparative simulation study of cluster ensemble algorithms integrated with multiple imputation for clustering with missing data
Authors: Yui Tomo, Funato Sato, Mari Oba
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Since cluster analysis methods usually cannot be applied directly to data with missing values, various approaches have been investigated to handle the problem. Multiple imputation is one of the standard procedures for addressing the problem of missing data. In cluster analysis, instead of Rubin’s rule, cluster ensemble methods have been proposed to be combined with multiple imputation. However, it remains unrevealed which of the cluster ensemble algorithms leads to better performance when integrated with the procedure. Therefore, we conducted numerical comparisons of several algorithms to integrate the results from k-means++ clustering for multiply imputed datasets and also applied the combined approaches to two real datasets. Our results suggest that the non-negative matrix factorization algorithm may be suitable for scenarios with class balance, whereas the greedy and agglomerative cluster algorithms may be suitable for scenarios with class imbalance. Before application to actual datasets, we still recommend performing simulation experiments in scenarios reflecting the characteristics of the datasets and the assumption of missing value mechanisms.

Introduction

Cluster analysis is the task of partitioning a series of observations into several clusters (groups) in a way that the observations in the same cluster tend to be similar. Cluster analysis has been applied in various fields, from medicine to social sciences. Numerous methods have been developed to perform cluster analysis, such as k-means and hierarchical clustering [1, 2]. However, most basic clustering methods cannot be directly applied to real-world datasets because they often include missing values.

Complete case analysis is the approach in which any observations with missing values are merely deleted from the analysis. Although this approach is one of the simplest ways to deal with missing values, it can influence the validity of results and impede subsequent analyses based on the assigned clusters to each observation. For example, if only a limited number of observations remain after the deletion of observations with missing values, the statistical power declines and the association between the assigned clusters and follow-up data might not be detectable. Therefore, we focus on the approaches that include all of the observations, even those with missing values. Multiple imputation is one of such methods [3–5]. This approach consists of two main steps: first, multiple complete datasets by imputing the missing values using probabilistic models and second, the results from each complete dataset into the final result using Rubin’s rule.

While multiple imputation has been used for regression analysis with incomplete data, the approach has recently been extended to cluster analysis. For cluster analysis, instead of Rubin’s rule, cluster ensemble algorithms have been proposed to combine the results [6, 7]. Basagaña et al. [8] employed a relabeling and voting algorithm to combine results from multiple complete datasets. Faucheux et al. [9] used the MultiCons algorithm based on frequent item set mining [10]. Bruckers et al. and Audigier and Niang [11, 12] focused on the formulation of a clustering ensemble algorithm as the solution to the mean partition problem. They applied a direct optimization approach of the target function and the non-negative matrix factorization ensemble algorithm [13]. Other proposed methods to combine multiple imputation results are the incorporation of distance matrices and the integration of cluster centroids [14, 15].

Although various clustering ensemble methods and several comparison results have been presented, it remains unrevealed which algorithm is effective for integrating with multiple imputation to handle missing data [16, 17]. Therefore, the purpose of this study is to compare numerically the performance of several ensemble algorithms. We especially focus on the algorithms to offer approximate or locally optimal solutions to the problem formulated by the mean partition problem.

Methods

Cluster ensemble methods

Cluster ensemble methods integrate multiple results from various cluster analysis algorithms or techniques into a single result. These approaches have been developed to improve clustering quality, providing stability of results [18].

We first formulate the cluster ensemble method based on the mean partition problem. The NMI distance is based on the quantity of mutual information normalized using the entropy. The distance is defined as\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} d(C_{i}, C_{j}):=1 - \text {NMI}(C_i, C_j), \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \text {NMI}(C_i, C_j) = \sum \limits _{s \in C_i} \sum \limits _{t \in C_j} P(s, t) \log \left( \frac{P(s, t)}{P(s) P(t)} \right) /{\max \{H(C_i), H(C_j)\}}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

We note that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H(C):= -\sum _{s \in C} P(s) \log P(s)$$\end{document} is the entropy, where P(s, t) is the proportion of data points simultaneously assigned to cluster s and t, and P(s) and P(t) are the proportions of data points assigned to cluster s and t, respectively. Since the optimization problem (1) using these dissimilarities is known as NP-complete, several algorithms have been developed to offer approximate or locally optimal solutions to this problem.

Greedy algorithm to directly optimize target function

Greedy algorithms are approaches that obtain local solutions via repeated optimization of partial problems. In this study, we focus on the algorithm by [6], which directly optimizes D(C). The procedures of this algorithm are as follows: (i) Provide the data points with the initial labels. (ii) Evaluate D(C) repeatedly relabeling the data point \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x_{i}$$\end{document} with labels different from the initial one. (iii) Replace the label of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x_{i}$$\end{document} such that it gives the minimal target function. (iv) Apply Steps ii and iii repeatedly until the label change does not occur. This algorithm is independent of the definition of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d(\cdot ,\cdot )$$\end{document}, so it is applicable to both the Mirkin and NMI distance.

Combined approach with multiple imputation

The integrated estimate after multiple imputation is obtained by Rubin’s rule in the case of regression analysis. For cluster analysis, the results can be combined via cluster ensemble approaches. The amount of uncertainty of the final result can be quantified via the integrated version of clustering instability defined in [12], which is not considered in this study.

Numerical experiments

Experiment 1: comparison of cluster ensemble algorithms alone

We performed the comparison of the cluster ensemble algorithms alone to obtain some insight into the differences in performance of the algorithms introduced in the prior section. We designed the scenarios in terms of varying the sample size and class balance. While the data generation settings were based on the experiments by [12], our settings were extended from 2 to 3 classes.

(iii) The procedures above were repeated 200 times. k-means++ is an improved version of the k-means algorithm to address sensitivity to initial cluster centers [26]. For data generation, application of the k-means++ method, and implementation of each ensemble algorithm, we used Python 3.9.6, Numpy 1.24.3, scikit-learn 1.6.1, and Scipy 1.10.1 [28, 29]. The implementations of MICE and k-pod were executed using R 4.5.3, mice 3.19.0, and kpodclustr 1.1 [27, 30]. All the scripts for these numerical experiments are available from the GitHub repository https://github.com/t-yui/MI-ClusterEnsembles-Comparison.

Application to real data

To evaluate the practical performance in actual settings, we analyzed two real datasets: (I) the Mayo Clinic primary biliary cholangitis (PBC) dataset and (II) the SUPPORT dataset. It should be noted that the analysis in this section is intended to evaluate the performance of cluster ensemble algorithms integrated with multiple imputation. Therefore, we do not intend to provide clinical insights or medical interpretations.

PBC dataset

The PBC dataset is available in the survival package in R and consists of 418 cases from a clinical trial conducted at the Mayo Clinic between 1974 and 1984 [31].

We used age (age), edema (edema), bilirubin (bili), albumin (albumin), prothrombin time (protime), platelet count (platelet), alkaline phosphatase (alk.phos), aspartate aminotransferase (ast), cholesterol (chol), copper (copper), and triglycerides (trig) as variables for clustering.

Predictive mean matching was employed as the imputation model for the multiple imputation process, except for edema. Since the variable edema is a three-level categorical variable, we applied polytomous regression for its imputation. Furthermore, edema was transformed into dummy variables after the generation of the imputed datasets. Detailed analysis and evaluation procedures were as provided below.

SUPPORT dataset

The SUPPORT (Study to Understand Prognoses Preferences Outcomes and Risks of Treatment) dataset includes 1, 000 randomly selected individuals from the SUPPORT study and was obtained from the Hmisc package in R [32, 33].

We used age (age), number of comorbidities (num.co), coma score (scoma), mean blood pressure (meanbp), heart rate (hrt), respiratory rate (resp), temperature (temp), sodium (sod), creatinine (crea), white blood cell count (wblc), \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{PaO}_2 / \textrm{FiO}_2$$\end{document} (pafi), albumin (alb), bilirubin (bili), blood urea nitrogen (bun), urine output (urine), and activities of daily living score (adls) as variables for clustering.

For all variables, in the multiple imputation process, predictive mean matching was employed as the imputation model. The subsequent analysis and evaluation were performed according to the procedures described below.

Analysis and evaluation procedures

For each dataset, as in Experiment 2, we generated m=30 imputed datasets by multiple imputation with chained equations. After imputation, all variables for clustering were standardized, and k-means++ method was applied to each imputed dataset. Then, cluster ensemble algorithms (GNMI, NMF, or AClu) were applied to the 30 results. We also applied k-means++ for complete-case and k-pod.

The number of clusters was determined by visualizing the elbow plot. Specifically, we set candidate cluster numbers \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k \in \{2,\ldots ,10\}$$\end{document}. For each k, we calculated the inertia of the k-means++ results for each imputed dataset and connected their median values with line segments. The number of clusters k was determined as the point where the change in the slope between the line segment from k-1 to k and the segment from k to k+1 was largest. Here, inertia is defined as the sum of squared distances of each data point to its closest cluster center.

Because no ground-truth cluster labels are available for these real datasets, ARI and NMI were not used in these applications. Instead, each method was evaluated by the silhouette score, the instability of the base clusterings, and visualizations. The instability of the base clusterings was calculated as the average pairwise normalized mutual information distance among the 30 k-means++ results obtained from the imputed datasets. For each cluster ensemble algorithm, the silhouette score was also calculated as the average score across the imputed datasets based on the final cluster labels.

Visualization was performed by the Median Consensus Embedding (MCE) method using t-distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE) as the base visualizations [34, 35]. Specifically, we first applied t-SNE with random initializations to the 30 imputed datasets, then calculated the geometric median of the embeddings using a well-defined distance metric between embeddings, and finally projected the result into a low-dimensional space via the multidimensional scaling [36]. This approach integrates low-dimensional visualization with multiple imputation, just as cluster ensemble methods integrate cluster analysis with multiple imputation.

Discussion

The experiments suggest that in terms of ARI between the true cluster labels, the NMF algorithm may tend to yield better performance in the class balance scenarios. In contrast, in the multiple-imputation settings with class imbalance, GNMI and AClu tended to perform better than NMF. These results are consistent with the results of evaluating ensemble algorithms alone, which indicates that NMF tends to be more robust against the instability of the clustering results to be integrated than AClu and GNMI, and AClu typically demonstrates more stability in scenarios with class imbalance than other algorithms. Therefore, differences in performance when integrated with multiple imputation procedures may be attributable to the characteristics of the type of cluster ensemble algorithms.

The NMF algorithm tends to show lower performance in scenarios with large sample sizes and low instability. This could be because the symmetric non-negative factorization method falls into inappropriate local minima, which leads to the failure to reconstruct the original matrix. Figure 8 shows an example of failure of the NMF algorithm to reconstruct the original matrix in a dataset with n=120 and class imbalanced scenarios. It seems that the GNMI algorithm also falls into inappropriate local minima in scenarios with class imbalance. In our simulation study, we adopted the best solution of the NMF among five implementations for each iteration to address the issue of the selection of initial values. Although this approach may have led to better solutions than NMF with a single initial value, it did not completely overcome the issue.

The k-pod algorithm is commonly used for cluster analysis with incomplete data. Since multiple imputation procedures are computationally intensive, the k-pod algorithm may be better in terms of computational efficiency. However, our results suggest that the performance of k-pod may decline compared to those of the multiple imputation and ensemble approaches in high missing ratio scenarios or MAR missing scenarios. The additional advantage of multiple imputation and ensemble approaches over k-pod is the ability to select clustering algorithms and missing value imputation models in a way that is suitable for the data.

In this study, we compared three ensemble algorithms that are closely related to direct or approximate solutions to the mean partition problem. However, there are many variations of ensemble algorithms. Recent studies have also proposed advanced approaches based on weighting and graph learning. For example, structured graph-based ensemble clustering learns a weighted similarity matrix with an explicit block-diagonal structure and extracts clustering indicators directly [37]. Parameter-free ensemble clustering dynamically weights both base clusterings and resulting clusters and is also intended to mitigate the problem of cluster imbalance [38]. In addition, Zhong et al. proposed a method that removes low-frequency co-associations before applying the normalized cut and selects the final clustering [39]. Zhang et al. recently provided theoretical results on generalization error, excess risk, and consistency for ensemble clustering via trace minimization of normalized co-association matrix, and also proposed an algorithm considering the bias and diversity of base clusterings [40]. The current study was designed to compare algorithms that can be formulated in a common framework for integrating base clustering results across multiply imputed datasets, with particular focus on methods related to the mean partition problem, which has been proven to have consistency under mild conditions on the metric space [20]. By contrast, the performance of the recent methods above when combined with multiple imputation remains to be investigated. Therefore, direct numerical comparisons with these recent approaches are an important topic for future work.

Among the algorithms proposed for combination with multiple imputation, we did not adopt the voting and relabeling algorithm and the MultiCons algorithm. The former involves the manual process of relabeling data points so that the interpretations of the assigned labels are the same across results. This manual process is so labour-intensive that it is impractical to conduct it on many clustering results. The MultiCons algorithm is an ensemble approach based on frequent pattern mining. This algorithm requires substantial computational time. In our preliminary studies, even with smaller sample sizes, such as n=30 or n=60, a single run required hours. Thus, the practicality of these algorithms was deemed limited.

In this study, we used MICE with predictive mean matching, a popular multiple imputation method in various applications. However, it is not always the best choice for cluster analysis. DPImputeCont is a method based on the Dirichlet Process Gaussian mixture model and it may be more suitable [41]. Other imputation approaches can be implemented using the clusterMI package in R, and further simulation experiments on their combination with cluster ensemble methods should be conducted in the future [12, 42].

When applying cluster analysis to real-world datasets, it is important to determine the number of clusters. In Application to real data section, we determined the elbow method to determine it. Other possible approaches include using information criteria to select the number of clusters for k-means to be integrated or other automatic cluster number selection such as X-means [43], and the final number of clusters could be determined by the most frequent number across the imputed datasets. In some actual studies in the applied field, the number of clusters may be predetermined based on the prior knowledge of researchers.

Finally, in actual applications, it is important to consider the missing data mechanisms and select the appropriate methods to address them. The missing data mechanisms investigated in this study are limited, and our scenarios do not cover all possible data scenarios. Therefore, before applying the algorithms to actual data, we recommend assessing their performance via simulation experiments under some data generation scenarios that reflect both the characteristics of the actual data and the assumptions of missing mechanisms.Fig. 8Example of matrix restoration failure via symmetric non-negative matrix factorization (NMF): a original matrix and b recovered matrix

Conclusion

In conclusion, our numerical comparison of cluster ensemble algorithms together with the applications to real datasets provided us with some practical guides for combination with multiple imputation. Our results suggest that, in the simulation settings with multiple imputation, the non-negative matrix factorization algorithm may be suitable for scenarios with class balance, whereas the greedy algorithm and agglomerative clustering may be suitable in scenarios with class imbalance. In the class-imbalanced scenarios, the greedy algorithm often attained the smallest mean rank and agglomerative clustering was often comparable, whereas the performance of the non-negative matrix factorization algorithm could deteriorate. We should also consider that the non-negative matrix factorization and greedy algorithms may fall into inappropriate local minima and provide low performance in some settings. The results from the real datasets were also broadly consistent with these tendencies. Before applying these approaches to actual data in practice, we still recommend performing simulation experiments in scenarios reflecting the characteristics of the datasets and the assumption of missing data mechanisms.
