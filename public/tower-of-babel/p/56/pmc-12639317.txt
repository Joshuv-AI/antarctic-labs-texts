Integration analysis of transcriptomics and proteomics reveals molecular pathways of duck intestinal epithelial cells in response to oxidative stress
Authors: Xiaolong He, Zhenhua Liang, Yan Wu, Jingshu Huang, Yuejin Pu, Jingbo Liu, Jinsong Pi, Hao Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Previous studies have shown that ducks undergo stress when transferred to a cage, and intestinal oxidative damage is induced. The intestinal mucosa is an important target of stress, and the terminal intestinal epithelial cells of the villi are the most strongly affected by oxidative stress. To investigate the molecular mechanism of oxidative damage in duck intestinal epithelial cells (dIECs), damage to dIECs was detected by inducing oxidative stress with different concentrations (0, 50, 100, and 200 μM) of hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) in primary dIECs, and molecular pathways were analyzed using combined transcriptomics and proteomics. The results revealed that oxidative stress significantly increased the reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels and apoptosis rate (P < 0.05), decreased antioxidant activity and intestinal barrier-related gene expression (P < 0.05), and caused nuclear irregularity, mitochondrial damage and vacuolization. A total of 766 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and 566 differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) were screened by transcriptome and proteome sequencing. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses revealed that the DEGs and DEPs were enriched in pathways such as the T-cell receptor signaling pathway, apoptosis signaling pathway, cellular response to tumor necrosis factor, glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, steroid hormone biosynthesis, retinol metabolism, PPAR signaling pathway, FoxO signaling pathway, arachidonic acid metabolism, retinol metabolism, and cytochrome P450 metabolic pathway. In addition, key genes at the intersection of multiple pathways, such as PCK1 and HPGDS, were identified. In conclusion, H2O2-induced oxidative stress resulted in oxidative damage and increased the permeability of dIECs, leading to apoptosis and intestinal barrier damage. Multiomics analyses revealed that cellular homeostasis, immune response and metabolic regulation signaling pathways are important molecular pathways involved in the response of dIECs to oxidative stress. These findings elucidate the molecular biological process of duck intestinal epithelial injury and provide a molecular theoretical basis for research to improve duck intestinal health.

Introduction

The intensive development of animal husbandry has promoted the transformation of duck farming patterns from the traditional semiarid mode to the multilayer cage raising model. However, owing to changes in their breeding mode, ducks face more concentrated stress, such as cage stress, density stress, and heat stress, during cage raising, which easily induce intestinal damage (He et al., 2024). Previous studies involving animal experiments have shown that cage stress can reduce the body weight of ducks, decrease feed intake, lower the egg production rate, damage intestinal tissues, and ultimately lead to intestinal oxidative stress (Zhang et al., 2019b; Zheng et al., 2022; He et al., 2024). Oxidative stress interferes with intestinal immunity and intestinal cell proliferation, thus disrupting intestinal barrier function and leading to a decline in the growth, digestion, and absorption of nutrients in ducks (Ma et al., 2014; Sahoo et al., 2023).

The intestinal mucosa is an important barrier for maintaining intestinal health. The intestinal epithelium, located in the outermost layer of the intestinal mucosa, is the layer that is primarily exposed to and protected against external substances. The intestinal epithelium consists mainly of a single layer of intestinal epithelial cells. Intestinal epithelial cells are interconnected mainly by tight junction proteins to form a protective barrier to resist pathogens and toxins and maintain the homeostasis of the intestinal environment (Groschwitz and Hogan, 2009; Buckley and Turner, 2017). However, oxidative stress induced by various stressors, such as environmental stress, pathological conditions and disease infections, alters the redox state of the intestinal mucosa and leads to oxidative damage to the intestinal mucosa. Intestinal mucosal damage in stressed laying ducks is triggered by a decrease in oxygen partial pressure and an exacerbation of oxidative stress in terminal villous intestinal epithelial cells. Oxidative stress has been widely shown to disrupt intestinal epithelial tight junctions and increase paracellular permeability (Rao, 2008). Oxidative stress attenuates the antioxidant capacity and cell permeability of cell lines (e.g., IPEC-J2) or primary duck intestinal epithelial cells, thereby disrupting intestinal barrier integrity (Zhang et al., 2019a; Li et al., 2022b, 2023). However, the molecular pathways through which duck intestinal epithelial cells respond to oxidative stress damage remain unclear. Owing to the complex microecological network system in the intestines of individual animals, which is affected by numerous factors, such as environment, feeding, and management, it is difficult to achieve homogeneous experimental conditions and accurately focus on the target location, as well as, it is also difficult to conduct direct observations, highlighting these limitations (Jung and Kim, 2022). In vitro cell culture models provide a cost-effective and high-throughput approach for studying intestinal function and disease mechanisms, and provide a controlled and reproducible environment for mechanistic studies (Ponce de León-Rodríguez et al., 2018).

The rapid progress in high-throughput sequencing technology provides a powerful tool for analyzing the molecular mechanisms underlying organismal health and disease. Transcriptomics reveals gene expression regulation and function by capturing the dynamics of RNA under specific physiological or pathological conditions (Wang et al., 2009). Proteomics can focus on overall structural and functional changes in cellular proteins (Gershon, 2003). Numerous factors are involved in the molecular mechanisms of animal health, developmental traits and disease traits. Single-omics research often struggles to reveal the full picture. Owing to post transcriptional regulation and post translational modifications (e.g., phosphorylation), a strict linear correlation does not exist between gene expression and protein expression, and mRNA levels do not reflect changes in protein expression (Saelao et al., 2018; Han et al., 2022). At present, the integration of transcriptomic and proteomic data has become the most commonly used technique for studying complex biological processes. In poultry, Wang et al. (2019) used transcriptomics and iTRAQ proteomics to screen for potential genes and pathways related to chicken feather color, providing a basis for feather pigmentation. Zhang et al. (2020b) integrated transcriptomic and proteomic data to identify several key candidate regulatory genes and pathways involved in the adaptation of Tibetan chicken embryos to hypoxia. Gao et al. (2020) used transcriptomic and proteomic analyses to study the molecular mechanisms of intestinal immunotoxicity caused by aflatoxin M1 and ochratoxin A and reported changes in specific molecular pathways under their synergistic effects. The combined analysis of transcriptomics and proteomics is a practical approach for studying molecular regulatory effects in organisms. Although studies on H₂O₂-induced oxidative stress in intestinal epithelial cells have been reported, most have focused primarily on the protective effects of exogenous compounds (Zhou et al., 2022, 2024), lacking molecular-level analysis of the intrinsic stress response mechanisms in primary duck intestinal epithelial cells (dIECs). Therefore, this study established an oxidative stress model using primary dIECs to investigate the phenotypic changes under oxidative stress. In addition, combined with RNA-seq and 4D-DIA proteomics technologies, an integrated analysis of primary dIECs under oxidative stress was conducted to reveal the molecular pathways of oxidative damage, providing new molecular basis for the intervention of duck intestinal health.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and treatment

Duck eggs from the Nonghu No. 2 green-shell laying duck, a newly developed domestic breed (developed by the Hubei Academy of Agricultural Sciences and Hubei Lihu Poultry & Egg Co., Ltd.), were incubated in a constant temperature incubator for 26 days. According to previous research, duck embryos at 26 days have many advantages in terms of oxidation, including mature organ systems, easily isolable intestines, and a relatively sterile intestinal environment (the incubation period of ducks is 28 days) (Zhang et al., 2019a). Therefore, in this study, 26 days duck embryos small intestine tissues were aseptically isolated. The methods of cell isolation, culture and oxidative stress treatment were referred to those of Zhang et al. (2019a). Briefly, small intestinal tissue was dissected along the medial side and thoroughly wash with precooled PBS containing penicillin-streptomycin-amphotericin B solution (Sigma-Aldrich, Missouri, USA) to remove impurities. The tissue was then sectioned into segments approximately 1 cm. Small intestinal tissues were digested using 1 % type I collagenase (Biosharp, Hefei, China) in a CO2 incubator (METHER, Anhui, China) at 37 °C for 60 min. The obtained cells were cultured by differential adhesion for 90 min in complete medium based on DMEM/F-12 (containing L-glutamine and 15 mM HEPES; Gibco, CA, USA), supplemented with 5 % fetal bovine serum (Gibco, CA, USA), 0.025 mg/mL insulin (Biosharp, Hefei, China), 0.1 mg/mL sodium heparin (Biofroxx, Einhausen, Germany), and 100 ng/mL EGF (Sigma-Aldrich, Missouri, USA). The dIECs were collected and plated for culture. The experiment was divided into four groups, the control group (CON) was treated with complete medium, the low concentration group (HL) was treated with 50 μM H2O2, the medium concentration group (HM) was treated with 100 μM H2O2, and the high concentration group (HH) was treated with 200 μM H2O2 to establish the oxidative stress model. This study follows the same isolation and culture method established in our previous research, in which the cell morphology has been characterized and confirmed to be intestinal epithelial cells (Zhang et al., 2019a).

Cell viability assay

Isolated and purified cells were inoculated into 96-well plates and were treated with different concentrations of H2O2. Each group has 6 replicates, and the experiment is repeated three times. The cell viability was measured in each well at 2, 4, 6, 8, and 10 h. Complete medium wells without cells were used as a blank group. After oxidative stress treatment, 20 μL of CCK-8 solution (Biosharp, Hefei, China) was added to each well, and the cells were incubated for 2 h. The absorbance value at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (PerkinElmer, USA) at the end of the incubation. Cell viability was calculated according to the formula: cell viability (%) = (absorbance value of experimental wells - absorbance value of blank wells) / (absorbance value of control wells - absorbance value of blank wells) × 100. To determine the appropriate time for oxidative stress treatment for subsequent experiments.

Cell proliferation assay

The isolated and purified cells were inoculated into 12-well plates at 1 × 105. Cell proliferation was then detected using the BeyoClick™ EdU kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China), 20 μM EdU of 500 μL working solution was added to each well, and the cells continued to be incubated for 6 h. The cells were then fixed for 15 min at room temperature with the addition of fixative, washed with washing solution for three times plus permeabilization solution for 15 min. After washing the cells with the washing solution, add 200 μL of the click reaction mixture and incubate at room temperature in the dark for 30 min. Following wash the cells with the washing solution, cell nuclei were stained with the Hoechst 33342 reaction solution for 10 min in the dark. EdU-stained cells were visualized using a fluorescence microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).

Detection of intracellular reactive oxygen species

The intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels of treated intestinal epithelial cells were detected using the ROS detection kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China). The intestinal epithelial cells were inoculated in 96-well cell culture plates with 6 replicates per group and repeated three times. The cells were washed once with PBS and incubated with 100 μL of 10 μM DCFH-DA (diluted 1:1000 in serum-free DMEM/F12 medium) for 30 min at 37 °C, and the cells were washed with PBS solution. The cells were photographed under a fluorescence microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan), and the production of intracellular ROS was measured using a microplate reader (PerkinElmer, USA) at an excitation wavelength of 480 nm and an emission wavelength of 530 nm.

Apoptosis assay

The apoptosis rate after oxidative stress was determined using a lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) assay kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). The dIECs were inoculated in 96-well cell culture plates with six replicates per group and repeated three times. The cell wells containing cells untreated with H2O2 were used as wells with maximum cellular enzyme activity, and cells were lysed by adding LDH release reagent 1 h before the end of oxidative stress treatment. A total of 120 μL of supernatant from each well was taken from the cells treated with different concentrations of oxidative stress and transferred to a new 96-well plate. Then, 60 μL of LDH assay working solution was added to each well, and the plate was mixed and incubated for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. Subsequently, the absorbance was measured at 490 nm. The apoptosis rate was calculated according to the formula: apoptosis rate (%) = (Absorbance of treat wells - Absorbance of control wells) / (Absorbance of maximum enzyme cellular activity - Absorbance of control wells) × 100.

Measurement of cell monolayer integrity

The dIECs were inoculated into 12-well transwell plates (diameter 12 mm, pore size 0.4 μm, area 1.12 cm2, LABSELECT, Hefei, China) in the upper chamber for culture. The TEER value of the cell monolayer was measured using a Millicell ERS (Electrical Resistance System) (Merck Millipore, Germany) at 1 d intervals to detect the formation of cell monolayers. When the TEER value of the cell monolayer stabilized and exceeded 500 Ω cm2, it indicated that the monolayer of intestinal epithelial cells was formed. Then, different concentrations of oxidative stress were applied to different groups as described above. The TEER values per well were determined at the end of the coculture using a Millicell ERS. The three measurement points per well were selected and averaged, with three replicates per group. The medium wells without cell inoculation were used as blank group. The TEER were calculated as (measured value - blank value) × effective membrane area, and normalized to the control group.

Transmission electron microscopy assay

Primary dIECs were treated with different intensities of oxidative stress, and then the cells were fixed using 2.5 % electron microscope fixative (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) overnight at 4 °C. A 0.1 M phosphate buffer was rinsed three times and fixed in 1 % osmium tetroxide for 2 h at room temperature in the dark, and then rinsed again three times. The cells were dehydrated in gradient alcohol and acetone solutions, and then osmotically embedded using a mixture of resin embedding agent and acetone solution at 37 °C overnight, followed by polymerization at 60 °C for 48 h. The resin blocks were sectioned using an ultramicrotome (Leica UC7, Wetzlar, Germany), and then stained with 2 % uranium acetate saturated alcohol solution as well as 2.6 % lead citrate solution. After the sections were dried, and photographed using transmission electron microscope (HT7800, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan) for observation.

Transcriptomics sequencing and analysis

Total RNA was extracted from the cells using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The concentration and integrity of sample RNA were evaluated using a Qubit fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and a Qsep400 high-throughput biological fragment analyzer (BiOptic Inc., Jiangsu, China). The mRNA with polyA tails was enriched with Oligo (dT) magnetic beads and cleaved into small fragments using fragmentation reagent. Double-stranded cDNA was synthesized using random hexamer primers and DNA polymerase, and end repair and A tails addition were performed. After DNA magnetic bead purification and fragment selection, PCR amplification was performed. The library was sequenced using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform after passing the quality control of library construction and obtaining 150 bp paired-end reads.

Raw sequencing reads were subjected to comprehensive quality control and filtering using fastp (v0.23.2) to obtain clean reads. The clean reads were then aligned to the duck reference genome (GCF_015476345.1_ZJU1.0) using hisat2 (v2.2.1) to obtain location information and sequence characteristics information. The number of reads for each gene was counted using featureCounts (v2.0.3) for gene alignment. Gene expression levels were estimated by FPKM based on gene length. Differential expression between groups was performed using DESeq2 (v1.22.1), and the probability of hypothesis testing (P-value) was corrected for multiple hypothesis testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg method. The differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were screened for |log2Fold Change| ≥ 1 and P < 0.05. The screened DEGs were mapped to the GO database for molecular function (MF), biological process (BP) and cellular composition (CC) annotation, and to the KEGG pathway database for pathway annotation. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed on the screened DEGs using clusterProfiler (v4.6.0). The GO terms and KEGG pathways were screened for significant enrichment at P < 0.05.

4D-DIA proteomics sequencing and analysis

The cell samples were lysed by adding urea lysate containing 1 mM PMSF and 2 mM EDTA to the cell samples for 5 min, and then samples were centrifuged at 15,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected, and the total protein concentration was determined by using a BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. An equal amount of protein solution was reduced with 10 mM DTT solution (Solarbio, Beijing, China) at 37 °C for 45 min, followed by alkylation with 50 mM iodoacetamide (IAM) in the dark for 15 min. Protein solution was precipitated using prechilled acetone at −20 °C for 2 h. After centrifugation, the protein precipitate was collected and digested with ammonium bicarbonate solution and trypsin at 37 °C overnight, and desalted on a C18 cartridge. The peptides were separated for LC-MS/MS analysis using a reversed-phase column XBridgeTM BEH300 (Waters, Milford, MA, USA). The samples were separated on a nanoElute UHPLC system (Bruker Daltonics, Germany) using 0.1 % formic acid in water (A) and 0.1 % formic acid in acetonitrile (B) as mobile phases. A total of 200 ng of sample was loaded onto the analytical column at a flow rate of 300 nL/min with a 60 min gradient. The peptides were introduced into the timsTOF Pro2 mass spectrometer (Bruker Daltonics, Germany) through a CaptiveSpray nano-electrospray ion source for mass spectrometry analysis. The data were collected in diaPASEF mode. The capillary voltage was set to 1400 V, and the scanning range of the precursor ion was set from 100 to 1700 m/z, the mobility range was 0.7-1.4 Vs/cm². The MS scan range was set from 400 to 1200, with 32 scans per cycle, and totally 64 windows were defined, and an average acquisition period of 1.8 s.

The raw data were processed using DIA-NN (v1.8.1), specifying stricttrypsin as the digestive enzyme (allowing up to 2 missed cuts). The fixed modifications were carbamidomethyl of cysteine, and variable modifications including methionine oxidation and N-terminal acetylation. The database search results were used to construct a spectral library, which was used for re-analysis of DIA data for protein quantitative analysis. False discovery rates for precursor ions and protein level were filtered at 1 %. The differently expressed proteins (DEPs) were filtered by P < 0.05 and Fold Change≥1.5. Functional annotation and enrichment analyses were performed using topGO (v2.48) and clusterProfiler (v4.4.4) for GO and KEGG databases. String (v11.5) was used for protein - protein interaction analysis, and Cytoscape (v3.10.1) was used to visualize the results.

Quantitative real-time PCR analysis

The mRNA of genes related to cellular antioxidant and intestinal barrier after oxidative stress was analyzed by RT-qPCR. Cell RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, CA, USA). RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the HisyGo RT Red SuperMix for qPCR (+gDNA Wiper) reverse transcription kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The RT-qPCR was performed on the Applied Biosystems Quant Studio 3 Real-Time PCR system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) using ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). The RT-qPCR reaction program was pre-denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s, and annealing at 60 °C for 30 s. The relative mRNA expression of the target genes was normalized to the GAPDH level using the 2-ΔΔCt comparative method. To verify the results of RNA-seq, DEGs in the KEGG pathway were selected to verify the reliability of the sequencing results. The primers for all genes are shown in Supplementary Table S1.

Statistical analysis

Use Microsoft Excel 2021 software to statistically analyze the measured data, and perform one-way ANOVA and Tukey multiple comparisons using the SPSS 26.0 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The data are presented as the mean and the standard error of the mean. A significance level of P < 0.05 was used to determine statistical significance, and the results were plotted using the GraphPad Prism 9.5.0 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

Cell Proliferation. The proliferation of dIECs after oxidative stress was determined using an EdU cell proliferation kit, and positive cells stained with red fluorescence were considered proliferating cells. Compared with the CON group, oxidative stress treatment inhibited dIECs proliferations, and the number of proliferating cells was reduced in the oxidative stress-treated group. In addition, in the CON group, more aggregated cell clusters were observed, the number of EdU-positive cells in these cell clusters was greater, and some of the cells in these cell clusters potentially have the presence of stem cell proliferation and division ability. In contrast, few aggregates were present in the oxidative stress-treated group, especially in the HH group, where the number of EdU-positive cells was very low (Fig. 1B).

Intracellular ROS Content. The effects of H2O2-induced oxidative stress on ROS production in dIECs were detected using an ROS kit. Compared with those in the CON group, the intracellular fluorescence and intracellular ROS levels significantly increased after treatment with different intensities of oxidative stress (Fig. 1C) (P < 0.05). The H2O2 treatment resulted in increased oxidative stress in dIECs.

Cytotoxicity. The degree of cellular damage caused by oxidative stress is assessed based on the amount of LDH released from cells in the cell culture medium. As shown in Fig. 1D, the LDH release rate of cells increased gradually with increasing intensity of oxidative stress. Compared with those in the CON group, the LDH release rates in the HM and HH treatment groups significantly increased (P < 0.05), while no significant difference was observed between the CON and HL groups (Fig. 1D). Oxidative stress treatment leads to an increase in cell damage, especially with increasing intensity of oxidative stress.

TEER Assay. Changes in intestinal permeability are manifestations of intestinal barrier dysfunction. To investigate the effect of oxidative stress on dIECs integrity, changes in intestinal permeability after cell treatment were measured. As shown in Fig. 1E, compared with those of the CON group, the TEER values of dIECs significantly decreased after treatment with different intensities of oxidative stress (P < 0.05), indicating an increase in intestinal permeability.

Ultrastructural Changes in the Cells. The effects of oxidative stress treatment on the ultrastructure of dIECs are shown in Fig. 1F. In the CON group, the nuclear structure was relatively normal, with oval nucleus, a relatively uniform distribution of chromatin in the nucleus, and less vacuolization. Under oxidative stress, the cells were wrinkled, the nucleus were irregular changes, heterochromatin increased, mitochondrial cristae were partially disordered or broken, vacuolization increased, and autophagosomes and autolysosomes were observed. Moreover, as the degree of oxidative stress increased, cell morphology and structure changed, the cell membrane was incomplete, the contents overflowed, the mitochondria were swollen and blurred, and vacuolization was severe.

The effect of oxidative stress on the antioxidant capacity of cells is shown in Fig. 2A. Compared with those in the CON group, the mRNA expression levels of GPX4, CAT, and GSR in the HL, HM, and HH treatment groups were significantly lower (P < 0.05). The GPX1 mRNA expression levels were significantly downregulated in the HM and HH groups (P < 0.05). The SOD3 mRNA expression was significantly downregulated in the HM group (P < 0.05), whereas no significant difference was detected among the other groups. The effects of oxidative stress on the expression of genes related to intestinal epithelial barrier function are shown in Fig. 2B. Oxidative stress at different intensities impaired the integrity of the dIEC barrier. Differentially expressed genes of dIECs

Twelve sequencing libraries were constructed using the Illumina sequencing platform for RNA-seq analysis of dIECs after oxidative stress treatment. A total of 619,011,638 reads were obtained, and 6.95 Gb of high-quality data were obtained for each sample after quality control filtering (average of 46,337,404 clean reads). The average percentage of Q20 and Q30 bases was 99.02 % and 97.05 %, respectively. The GC content of each sample ranged from 51.69 to 52.91 %, and the sequencing error rate was 0.01 %. The sequencing data of each sample were aligned with the duck reference genome. A total of 88.11–89.55 % of the clean reads were aligned to the reference genome, and 82.55–83.67 % of the clean reads were mapped to the unique position of the reference genome (Supplementary Table S2). These findings indicate that the sequencing quality and yield of each sample are good.

A total of 766 DEGs were screened in each group, and the total number of DEGs and the number of up- and downregulated genes were determined between the comparison groups (Supplementary Table S3). Compared with those in the CON group, there were 454 DEGs in the HL group, including 324 were upregulated and 130 downregulated genes, and 571 DEGs were found between the HM group and the CON group, of which 409 were upregulated and 162 were downregulated. In addition, 477 DEGs were noted between the HH group and the CON group, with 338 upregulated and 139 downregulated (Fig. 3A). The distribution of DEGs between groups was further demonstrated using a volcano plot, as shown in Fig. 3B. Cluster analysis of the DEGs in each group revealed that the DEGs in the groups had similar expression patterns, whereas the expression patterns of the DEGs in the CON group and the treatment group were significantly different (Fig. 3C). Genes in the same category in different oxidative stress groups had similar trends and might have similar functions. K-means cluster analysis was used to investigate the trends of the DEGs. The results revealed that all the DEGs could be divided into five subclusters. There were 182 and 219 DEGs in subclusters 1 and 2 respectively, whose expression increased during oxidative stress and tended to decrease with increasing intensity of stress. There were 113 DEGs in subcluster 4, whose expression levels gradually increased with increasing stress intensity. Among the 231 DEGs in subcluster 3, the expression levels of these genes decreased after oxidative stress. In addition, the expression levels of 21 DEGs in subcluster 5 increased at low concentrations of oxidative stress but decreased sharply with increasing concentration (Fig. 3D).

GO and KEGG enrichment analysis of DEGs

Discussion

With the transformation of the waterfowl farming industry toward the facility-based model, ducks face multiple stressor challenges when they shift from traditional water surface farming to stacked-cage raising. Previous animal experiments have shown that the cage stress response reduces duck production performance, damages intestinal tissues, and leads to intestinal oxidative stress and inflammation (Zhang et al., 2019b; Gu et al., 2020; Zheng et al., 2022). The intestinal epithelium is the primary layer that defends against external substances. A monolayer composed of intestinal epithelial cells is important for absorbing substances and resisting the external environment. However, due to the complex microecosystem of the animal intestine being affected by numerous factors, research has been limited in terms of the difficulty of focusing on target cells, observing, and achieving homogeneous conditions (Jung and Kim, 2022). Currently, immortalized intestinal cell lines from animals and humans (such as Caco-2, IPEC-1, and IPEC-J2) have been widely used as in vitro models of intestinal epithelial cells to study the effects of oxidative stress on intestinal barrier function (Wijeratne et al., 2005; Paszti-Gere et al., 2011). Ducks belong to the waterfowl. Although immortalized intestinal cells are inexpensive and easy to manage, they may not be suitable for studies on the specific physiology of ducks or the interaction between specific pathogens and intestinal cells (duck plague). Owing to genetic modification, the cell phenotype, function, and responsiveness to stimuli may be altered, leading to differences in results (Kaur and Dufour, 2012). In this study, an oxidative stress model of dIECs was constructed using H₂O₂. After treatment with 200 μM H₂O₂ for 6 h, the cell survival rate was approximately 54.5 %. Therefore, this treatment time was selected for subsequent studies. Similar results were observed in other studies. Zhang et al. (2019a) reported that dIECs viability decreased to approximately 60 % after treatment with 200 μM for 4 h and decreased to approximately 20 % at 500 and 1000 μM. These findings indicate that H₂O₂ at different concentrations induces oxidative stress in dIECs. Moreover, oxidative stress in this study led to a weakened proliferative ability of dIECs, and the number of EdU-positive cells decreased sharply with increasing intensity of oxidative stress. These finding indicate that oxidative stress has adverse effects on cells, but only a small number of proliferating cells were observed in the untreated group. The intestinal epithelium is an actively self-renewing tissue, and cell proliferation and differentiation are driven by crypt stem cells (van der Flier and Clevers, 2009). Intestinal stem cells at the bottom of the crypt differentiate into progenitor cells, which migrate upward along the crypt‒villus axis to proliferate and differentiate into specific epithelial cell types that maintain the function and homeostasis of the intestinal epithelium (Clevers, 2013). Therefore, the low degree of cell proliferation observed in this study may reflect the inherent characteristics of intestinal epithelial cell renewal and proliferation.

ROS levels can be used to assess the intracellular oxidative stress status (Sahoo et al., 2023). Previous studies have shown that IPEC-J2 cell exposure to 0.8 mM H2O2 for 24 h resulted in a significant increase in cellular ROS fluorescence signals and levels (Zou et al., 2016). Similar results were also observed in this study. As the intensity of oxidative stress increased, the ROS level in dIECs increased significantly, indicating the toxic effect of oxidative stress on cells. The increase in LDH release also demonstrated intestinal cell damage caused by oxidative stress. LDH release increases during cell death because of damage to the cell membrane. Transmission electron microscopy revealed that the ultrastructure of cells changed after oxidative stress, with increased vacuolization, damaged mitochondria, and autophagosomes appearance. These findings indicate that the intestinal epithelial cells were damaged to some extent. The in vivo antioxidant defense system plays an important role in alleviating oxidative damage. In this study, the mRNA expression levels of antioxidant-related genes (GPX1, GPX4, SOD3, and CAT) decreased significantly with increasing intensity of oxidative stress. Previous studies have shown that H2O2 reduces the antioxidant enzyme activity of intestinal epithelial cells (Zhang et al., 2019a). Zhang et al. (2023) reported a significant decrease in the expression of antioxidant-related genes (Nrf2 and HO-1) after the induction of oxidative stress in dIECs using H2O2. This finding is similar to the results observed in this study. These findings indicated that H2O2-induced oxidative stress resulted in an impaired antioxidant defense system in dIECs.

The intestinal epithelium is a single-cell layer composed of different types of cells with various functions. Oxidative stress can increase the permeability of intestinal epithelial cells. Changes in the TEER indicate that the transcellular permeability of ions and the intestinal epithelial barrier are disrupted (Paszti-Gere et al., 2014). Studies have shown that dIECs treatments with 50 μM H2O2 for 4 h resulted in a significant decrease in cellular TEER values (Zhang et al., 2023). This study also revealed a consistent effect, indicating a change in intestinal permeability. The intestinal epithelial barrier is composed of junctional complexes such as tight junctions, adherens junctions, and desmosomes, the disruption of which is considered a key mechanism in the development of intestinal barrier disorders (Lechuga and Ivanov, 2017). Oxidative stress disrupts the intestinal barrier integrity of dIECs (Zhang et al., 2023). Studies on IPEC-J2 cell also revealed that H2O2-induced oxidative stress resulted in decreased expression of CLDN1, Occludin, and ZO-1 (Li et al., 2022b). Consistent with the results of previous studies, H2O2-induced oxidative stress in the present study significantly reduced the expression of intestinal tight junction and adherens junction-related genes (CDH1, JAM2, ZO-2, and CLDN3), suggesting that oxidative stress causes intestinal cell damage and apoptosis by disrupting the integrity of the intestinal epithelial barrier.

Gene expression in cells is regulated by various factors, with mRNA serving as an intermediate in gene expression and proteins acting as the ultimate functional executors in organisms. Integrating the differences and complementarity of transcriptomics and proteomics allows for a comprehensive analysis of gene expression levels, providing a more complete understanding of physiological changes in organisms (Shi et al., 2024). In this study, integrated analysis revealed that only 10 DEGs and DEPs showed consistent changes at both the transcriptomic and proteomic levels, indicating a low correlation between transcriptome and proteome data. Similar results have been observed in studies across other species and tissue types (Saelao et al., 2018; Lu et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2023; Shi et al., 2024). The processes of RNA transcription and protein translation are highly complex and can be influenced by multiple factors, including transcriptional regulation, translational efficiency, and post-translational modifications (Saelao et al., 2018; Han et al., 2022). Since mRNA and protein expression levels do not directly correspond, their changing trends are not entirely consistent, leading to a relatively small number of overlapping genes. However, integrating transcriptomic and proteomic data enables the exploration of common changes in functional enrichment between differentially expressed genes and proteins, revealing their molecular functional pathways. In this study, integrated co-enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes and proteins showed that immune response, metabolic regulation, and cellular homeostasis-related pathways were collectively enriched, suggesting these pathways may play potential roles in the oxidative stress response.

The ROS generated intracellularly are important signaling molecules, especially in T cells, that determine the activation and inactivation of T cell-mediated immune responses (Kamiński et al., 2013). Excessive production of intracellular ROS caused by various stressors may inhibit T cell activation by suppressing the expression of T cell transcription factors (Simeoni and Bogeski, 2015). Research has shown that lead exposure in quails stimulates ROS production and disrupts the balance of the antioxidant redox system, inducing oxidative stress and inhibiting T cell activation and differentiation by suppressing T cell receptor signaling (Wang et al., 2022). Intestinal redox plays a crucial role in maintaining the balance among T cell subsets and controlling intestinal balance and inflammation (Prasad et al., 2024). Similarly, previous research has indicated that mitochondrial ROS accumulation inhibits B cell differentiation and immune responses by suppressing the B cell receptor signaling and reducing the expression of CD19 (Ogura et al., 2016). Additionally, cytokine (TNFα) is a core mediator of intestinal inflammation, and TNFα activates the JNK/p38 apoptotic signaling pathway by inducing ROS production, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction and exacerbating intestinal epithelial cell apoptosis (Baregamian et al., 2009). High-throughput sequencing revealed that multiple DEGs and DEPs were significantly enriched in pathways related to cellular senescence, T cell receptor signaling pathway, cellular response to tumor necrosis factor, B-1 B cell differentiation, and apoptotic signaling pathway. The qRT‒PCR validation results also showed that the expression of TNFSF10, IL7R, and CDKN2B genes was significantly upregulated after oxidative stress. This indicates that intestinal epithelial cells alter the expression of immune-related molecules within the cells themselves after suffering from oxidative stress. However, further studies are needed to determine whether the transcriptional changes observed in intestinal epithelial cells can modulate intestinal immune responses, and to further investigate the activation and inhibition mechanisms of immune signaling after oxidative stress in intestinal epithelial cells.

Many genes and proteins are involved in pathways related to immune and metabolic regulation, suggesting that oxidative stress may affect the normal immune metabolic-functions of dIECs. Oxidative stress leads to intestinal ischemia‒reperfusion, generating a large amount of ROS that disrupts the intestinal redox balance and induces intestinal oxidative stress injury (Akbarian et al., 2016; Sun et al., 2020; Li et al., 2022a). High-throughput sequencing of intestinal tissues from transport-stressed ducks revealed that the FoxO signaling pathway is involved in the mechanism of transport stress-induced intestinal injury in ducks (Zhang et al., 2020a). The FoxO family in the FoxO signaling pathway is involved in a wide range of physiological functions such as oxidative stress, cell proliferation and apoptosis, cell metabolism, and DNA damage repair (Webb and Brunet, 2014; Cheng, 2022). DON treatment of IPEC-J2 cells revealed that the DEGs in the FoxO signaling pathway were significantly upregulated, and DON treatment resulted in FoxO3a translocation to the nucleus to induce apoptosis, whereas FoxO3a silencing reduced apoptosis (Kang et al., 2022). FoxO family members translocate to the nucleus and are highly expressed under stress, affecting the cellular redox state and damage repair process through posttranslational modifications such as phosphorylation and acetylation (Klotz et al., 2015). Moreover, FoxO1 influences immune cell recruitment and activation, as well as the development and function of helper T cells and B lymphocytes, thereby regulating mucosal immune function (Graves and Milovanova, 2019). In this study, the DEGs identified after oxidative stress treatment were enriched in the FoxO signaling pathway, which is consistent with the above research results. Meanwhile, key genes within this pathway (such as TNFSF10 and PCK1) were upregulated after oxidative stress, and these genes have been reported to be associated with intestinal inflammation and injury (Ślebioda and Kmieć, 2014; Yin et al., 2024). These results suggest that the FoxO signaling pathway may play a potential role in oxidative stress-induced damage in dIECs. Additionally, Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptors (PPARs), as nuclear receptor factors, play crucial roles in regulating energy metabolism, lipid metabolism, and inflammatory responses. Previous studies have demonstrated that PPAR signaling exerts anti-inflammatory and homeostasis-maintaining functions in intestinal inflammation models (Azuma et al., 2010; Choo et al., 2015). Although the specific role of PPARs in oxidative damage of dIECs remains unclear, the enrichment of this pathway under oxidative stress in our study suggests its potential involvement in maintaining redox homeostasis and regulating inflammatory responses. However, the specific mechanism of action of its FoxO and PPAR pathways and related genes in oxidative stress of dIECs still needs further research to clarify their functional significance in oxidative damage defense and intestinal homeostasis.

Cellular metabolic reactions are necessary for the physiological processes of cells. In this study, DEGs and DEPs in dIECs after exposure to oxidative stress were enriched in multiple key metabolic regulatory pathways, including those involved in steroid hormone biosynthesis and retinol metabolism. Steroids are essential hormones involved in physiological processes such as metabolism and immune function. In the intestinal mucosa, the intestinal epithelial cell layer is among the main cellular sources of steroids. Local immune cell activation and related inflammation can stimulate the synthesis of intestinal steroid hormones (such as anti-inflammatory glucocorticoids) in intestinal epithelial cells to exert anti-inflammatory effects (Ahmed et al., 2019). Research has shown that glucocorticoids reduce the increased permeability of intestinal epithelial cells caused by TNFα and protect the tight junction barrier function of the intestinal epithelium by activating the glucocorticoid receptor (Boivin et al., 2007). Moreover, disorders in intestinal steroid synthesis are associated with intestinal inflammation (Ahmed et al., 2019). Meanwhile, glucocorticoids increase the expression of PPARγ in colonic epithelial cells, whereas the production of cortisol and the expression of steroidogenic factors are significantly reduced in the enterocytes of patients with enteritis, which is directly related to PPARγ dysfunction (Bouguen et al., 2014). The significant enrichment of steroid hormone synthesis in this study may contribute to the regulation of the intestinal inflammatory immune response through PPAR signal transduction-mediated hormone secretion. Vitamin A (retinol) is crucial for epithelial cell growth, differentiation, and proliferation. Studies have shown that retinol and retinoic acid reverse H2O2-induced intestinal damage in dIECs by enhancing intestinal barrier function (Zhang et al., 2023). Wang et al. (2024) reported in a proteomic study of the jejunum of fasted and refed broilers that intestinal retinol metabolism was upregulated after 36 h of fasting followed by 6 h of refeeding, which may be involved in the regeneration of fasting-induced intestinal damage and intestinal cell differentiation. In this study, oxidative stress may have allowed cells to compensate for the enhanced immune response and antioxidant defense through enrichment of these pathways to counteract the adverse effects of excessive free radical production and repair intracellular oxidative damage.

Maintaining internal environmental homeostasis is a highly energy-consuming process, and an energy supply is a crucial prerequisite for resistance to environmental disturbances. In this study, DEGs and DEPs were significantly enriched in the glycolysis and lipid metabolism pathways, indicating that intestinal epithelial cells need to regulate energy synthesis and metabolism to adapt to oxidative stress after exposure to H2O2 stress. Studies have shown that glucose metabolism promotes glutathione synthesis in pancreatic islets through pyruvate carboxylase, limiting oxidative stress during inflammation (Fu et al., 2021). Glucose is the main source of cellular ATP and can be metabolized through the glycolysis and pentose phosphate pathways. Tian et al. (2021) reported that the deletion of TKT (a key enzyme connecting the pentose phosphate pathway and glycolysis) inhibits cellular ATP production through the accumulation of pentose phosphate pathway metabolites and a reduction in glycolytic metabolites, leading to a decreased energy supply in intestinal epithelial cells and the induction of apoptosis and intestinal barrier defects. The significant enrichment of the glycolysis/gluconeogenesis pathway after oxidative stress treatment in this study indicates that oxidative stress disrupts the normal energy supply within cells. Intestinal epithelial cells compensate for the energy supply disorder caused by oxidative stress by upregulating the activity of the glycolysis pathway to meet high energy demands and survive under stress conditions. In addition, significant enrichment of linoleic acid and arachidonic acid metabolism was also observed in this study. Different types of fatty acids affect the immune response and inflammatory processes of intestinal cells. In DSS- and TNBS-induced rat colitis models and a Caco-2 in vitro inflammation model, α-linolenic acid decreased the mRNA levels of proinflammatory genes (IL-6, IL-8, and TNF-α) (Reifen et al., 2015). Conjugated linoleic acid (CLA) also helps to reduce intestinal inflammation and oxidative stress (Putera et al., 2023). These findings indicate that proinflammatory factors in the inflammatory microenvironment may affect the integrity of the intestinal barrier by influencing fatty acid metabolism. Moreover, PPAR transcription factor mediated signal transduction is activated by different types of fatty acids. The CLA regulates the expression of genes related to lipid metabolism and epithelial cell maturation (such as UCP1, PGC-1α, and CD36) by activating PPARγ and PPARδ and inhibits TNF-α expression and NF-κB activation, whereas the protective effect of CLA is weakened in PPARγ-deficient mice (Bassaganya-Riera et al., 2004). Under oxidative stress, dIECs may increase the synthesis of unsaturated fatty acids through the lipid metabolism pathway to adapt to metabolic disorders and immune homeostasis caused by the stress environment, and contribute to the activation of the PPAR signaling pathway. Additionally, this study revealed a significant enrichment of cytochrome P450-related metabolic pathways. Cytochrome P450s are family of heme-thiolate enzymes that participate in the oxidative metabolism and detoxification of various endogenous and exogenous compounds. Mice deficient in NADPH-cytochrome P450 reductase exhibit increased levels of proinflammatory factors and tissue damage in the acute inflammatory response, and the levels of intestinal arachidonic acid metabolites are significantly decreased (Ahlawat et al., 2014). Studies have shown that arachidonic acid metabolites activate PPARα, regulate the expression of lipid metabolism-related genes, and affect the expression of CYP450 enzymes through self-regulation (Ng et al., 2007). Therefore, dIECs under oxidative stress may interact with PPAR and FoxO signaling pathways through metabolic pathways to coregulate intestinal immune inflammatory responses and energy metabolism homeostasis. Further studies are needed to clarify the role of PPAR and FoXO pathways in intestinal oxidative damage.

Conclusions

In conclusion, oxidative stress causes redox imbalance in dIECs, resulting in cellular oxidative damage, and disruption of cellular intestinal barrier integrity, induction of apoptosis and impairment of barrier function. Transcriptomic and proteomic sequencing identified 766 and 566 DEGs and DEPs, respectively, and functional enrichment analysis revealed potential pathways, such as the FoxO signaling pathway, PPAR signaling pathway, glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, steroid hormone biosynthesis, retinol metabolism, arachidonic acid metabolism, retinol metabolism, and cytochrome P450 metabolism. The network diagram revealed key genes such as PCK1 and HPGDS, that are located in multiple pathways. Using integrated transcriptomics and proteomics analysis, this study revealed multidimensional changes in dIECs after oxidative stress damage, reflecting the effects of oxidative stress on the metabolism, immune regulation, and cellular homeostasis of dIECs. These pathway alterations may be adaptive responses and important molecular pathways of action in intestinal epithelial cells in response to oxidative stress. However, these findings need to be explored and resolved in depth.
