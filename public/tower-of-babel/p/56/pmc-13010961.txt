Multi-omics analysis identifies key microbial taxa and host genes controlling abdominal fat deposition in chickens
Authors: Zhong Wang, Xiaoling Yang, Yongxian Yang, Xiaoxia Long, Shijun Li, Liqi Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abdominal fat deposition (AFD) is a complex economic trait in broilers influenced by both genetic and environmental factors, yet its underlying regulatory mechanisms remain largely elusive. To better understand this process, we analyzed a cohort of 205 chickens with phenotypic records and multi-omics datasets, including host genomics, cecal microbiota, and serum metabolomics. Using an integrative approach consisting of genome-wide association studies (GWAS), random forest, and two-part models, we dissected the individual and combined contributions of host genetics and gut microbiota to AFD. Our results identified a host gene–microbiota–metabolite regulatory axis, wherein a novel Z-chromosome gene ENSGALG00010010675 modulated the abundance of cecal Muribaculaceae. This, in turn, altered key metabolites including sorbitol 6-phosphate and gallic acid, thereby promoting AFD. Conversely, two cecal taxa—Blautia and the [Ruminococcus]_torques_group—exerted attenuating effects on AFD by regulating specific serum metabolites, such as 3-hydroxyphenylacetic acid, o-ethyl methylphosphonothioate, and 7-methylguanine. In summary, this study systematically elucidates the complex interactions among host, cecal microbiota, and metabolites underlying AFD in broilers, and identifies key microbial taxa and metabolites as potential targets for reducing excessive fat deposition.

Introduction

Chickens are an important domestic food animal, but modern rearing methods have resulted in excess abdominal fat deposition (AFD), a major challenge confronting the broiler industry. This unwanted fat accumulation not only deviates from the demands of commercial production but also imposes significant adverse impacts on broiler performance and industry profitability. Excessive AFD in broiler chickens impairs feed conversion efficiency, reduces meat quality, increases mortality, and ultimately causes substantial economic losses (Cui et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2018a, 2018b). These adverse effects are closely linked to disrupted lipid metabolism and abnormal fat accumulation in broilers (Chen et al., 2023). This condition arises when dietary lipid intake exceeds the capacity of adipose tissue to store surplus lipids as triglycerides, further triggering ectopic lipid deposition in non-adipose tissues through systemic circulation and subsequent abdominal fat accrual (Geng et al., 2022; Sun et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2025). Accumulating evidence has demonstrated that both host genetics and gut microbiota are pivotal determinants in the regulation of AFD (Chen et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2026; Wu et al., 2024), with their interactive effects jointly shaping the phenotypic variation of this trait.

Host genetics play a fundamental role in AFD regulation, as prior studies have established that AFD is orchestrated by a complex gene network that coordinates all stages of adipogenesis (Cui et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024). Several AFD-associated genes have been identified, including FABP4 (involved in intracellular fatty acid binding and transport), apoB (responsible for cholesterol transport), IGF1 (insulin-like growth factor regulating growth and adipogenesis), L-FABP (liver fatty acid binding protein), TGFB3 (key growth and differentiation regulator), THRSPα (linked to lipogenesis and AFD), FOXC1 (developmental regulator), and GPNMB (promoter of adipocyte differentiation and lipid accumulation) (Abasht and Lamont, 2007; Abdalla et al., 2018; Hu et al., 2016; Sun et al., 2013; Wang et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2022). Heritability estimates for chicken abdominal fat weight (AFW) range from 0.24 to 0.82 (Cahaner and Nitsan, 1985; Guo et al., 2023; Moreira et al., 2018). This wide range indicates that genetic factors only partially explain the phenotypic variation observed in AFD, implying the involvement of non-genetic mechanisms in abdominal fat formation—particularly the gut microbiota, which plays crucial roles in nutrient absorption, growth regulation, and lipid metabolism (Chen et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2023). As a key non-genetic regulator of animal AFD, the gut microbiota includes several obesity-associated taxa: Megamonas rupellensis (Wu et al., 2024), Prevotella copri (Chen et al., 2021) and Fusimonas intestine (Takeuchi et al., 2023) have been linked to obesity in human or pigs. In chickens, previous studies have shown that the duodenal and cecal microbiota account for 24 and 21 % of AFW variation, respectively. Methanobrevibacter spp. and Mucispirillum schaedleri are closely linked to AFW in chickens as were Olsenella, Slackia, Bacteriodes salanitronis, Bacteriodes fragilis, and Parabacteriodes distasonis (Wen et al., 2019; Chen et al., 2023; Xiang et al., 2021). These studies have revealed mechanisms including that the gut microbiota promotes adipogenesis and AFD by generating additional bioavailable energy, thereby enhancing lipid accumulation in abdominal adipose tissue (Cui et al., 2022; Polansky et al., 2015). However, the crosstalk between host genes and gut microbiota in regulating chicken AFD and their integrated regulatory mechanisms remain unclear.

With the rapid advancement of multi-omics technologies, multi-omic dataset integration has become a powerful tool to identify regulatory networks and molecular associations underlying trait formation (Lu et al., 2024). In chicken AFD research, recent multi-omics studies have provided valuable insights: for example, the variant at the chicken rs734209466 functions as an allele-specific enhancer that increases transcription of the IGF-binding protein genes IGFBP2 and IGFBP5 via IRF4 binding, thereby influencing preadipocyte differentiation and proliferation and ultimately modulating AFW in chickens (Shen et al., 2024). Additionally, Cui et al. identified the fatty acid synthase gene FASN, which promotes intramuscular fat accumulation by regulating fatty acid utilization and contributing approximately 40 % of total fatty acids (Cui et al., 2024). Moreover, Chen et al. revealed a tight linkage between the crosstalk of the gut microbiota and fat anabolic/catabolic genes in regulating AFD (Chen et al., 2023). However, the regulatory networks underlying chicken adipogenesis and fat deposition are highly complex, involving genetic, microbial, and metabolic components, and a comprehensive understanding of AFD is still lacking (Shen et al., 2024).

While genetic and microbial factors associated with AFD have been extensively studied, their interplay—along with coordinated effects involving metabolites and cytokines—remains largely unexplored. We therefore hypothesized that host genetic variants, cecal microbes, serum metabolites, and cytokines collectively regulate chicken AFD through intricate interactions, with host genetics indirectly influencing AFW by shaping the cecal microbiota. To test this, we established an AFD-focused chicken population, quantified AFW phenotypes, and performed multi‑omic profiling encompassing the genome, gut microbiota, metabolome, and cytokines. Key AFW-associated microbial taxa and metabolites were identified using a two-part model, followed by microbial genome-wide association studies (mGWAS) to dissect host–microbiota interactions and elucidate the underlying metabolic mechanisms. This study clarifies the multi‑omic regulatory basis of chicken abdominal fat deposition and provides potential functional biomarkers for breeding low-fat broilers.

Materials and methods

Birds, phenotypic determinations and sample collection

Based on the experimental designs of previous multi-omics studies in chickens (Wen et al., 2019; Wen et al., 2021), a total of 205 Qiandongnan Xiaoxiang chickens at 70 days of age (114 males and 91 females, with the sex ratio derived from natural hatching) were selected as the experimental cohort. This breed is a local indigenous Yellow-feathered chicken in Guizhou Province, China. All birds were raised at the Experimental Animal Center of Guizhou University from March to September 2024. The detailed feeding management was as follows: All chickens were housed in three-tiered iron cages, with 4 birds per cage during 0–4 weeks of age and 2 birds per cage during 5–10 weeks of age (mixed sex). A customized commercial diet was provided (diet formulation is shown in Table S1-1). Chickens were fed twice daily at 09:00 and 17:00, with ad libitum access to water, and a 16-h daily photoperiod was applied. According to the Technical Specification for Feeding and Management of Yellow-feathered Broilers (NY/T 1871–2010), the ambient temperature and relative humidity in the chicken house were regulated for different weeks of age using a laboratory environmental control system (Table S1-2). No antibiotics were administered during the experiment. Vaccinations against Marek’s disease, Newcastle disease, infectious bronchitis, fowl pox, infectious bursal disease, and avian influenza were performed following the routine immunization program of the farm (immunization schedule is shown in Table S1-3). Body weight was measured at the same time every week. Body weight on day 1 was recorded as WD1, and body weights at other ages were abbreviated using the same rule; for example, WD7, WD14, WD21, and WD35 represent body weights on days 7, 14, 21, and 35, respectively.

At 70 days of age, blood samples were collected from all 205 chickens via wing venipuncture. Paired whole blood aliquots were prepared: one was allowed to clot at room temperature for serum isolation, whereas the other was immediately stored at −80 °C for subsequent metabolomic and cytokine analyses. Genomic DNA was extracted from the remaining whole blood aliquot for genomic profiling. Following blood collection, birds were euthanized by CO2 asphyxiation in accordance with established procedures (Haetinger et al., 2021) to collect cecal content samples. Meanwhile, abdominal fat was carefully dissected, completely isolated, and weighed to determine AFW. Abdominal fat percentage (AFP) was calculated as: AFP (%) = (AFW / WD70) × 100 %, where WD70 represents body weight at 70 days of age. Approximately 2 g of cecal content was aseptically harvested from a standardized site in the middle segment of the ipsilateral cecum using sterile instruments. All cecal content and serum samples were immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C until further analysis. Whole blood samples for DNA extraction were sent to Majorbio (Shanghai, China) for whole-genome resequencing. Acquisition and quality control of host genomic sequencing data

Genomic DNA was extracted from whole blood samples of all chickens following standardized protocols. Briefly, 200 μL of anticoagulated whole blood was utilized to for DNA isolation using a universal extraction kit (Tiangen, Beijing, China). DNA concentration was determined using a Nanodrop Technologies 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Pittsburg, PA, USA), and extracts were stored at 4 °C until further use. A Nanodrop NE-2000 nucleic acid analyzer was employed to assess both concentration and purity of DNA samples. Samples meeting predefined quality thresholds (OD260/280 ratio between 1.8 and 2.0; OD260/230 ratio ≥2.0) were subjected to whole-genome resequencing. Genomic libraries were constructed using the TruSeq Nano DNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), and high-throughput paired-end sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq X Plus platform. Whole-genome sequencing was conducted at an average sequencing depth of approximately 10 × Clean reads from all individuals were mapped to the Gallus gallus reference genome using BWA-MEME software (Jung and Han, 2022). Single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) calling was conducted on the aligned data following the GATK Best Practices pipeline (McKenna et al., 2010). Functional annotation of SNPs was conducted using SNPEff software (Cingolani et al., 2012), integrated with gene prediction annotations from the reference genome to produce a comprehensive functional annotation dataset.

Cecal microbiota 16s rRNA gene sequencing and data preprocessing

DNA of cecal microbiota was extracted using the Magnetic Soil and Stool DNA Kit (Tiangen, Beijing, China), with procedures strictly followed the manufacturer’s protocols. Post-extraction, DNA concentration and purity were evaluated via Nanodrop-1000 spectrophotometry and 0.8 % agarose gel electrophoresis, respectively. The V3-V4 hypervariable regions of the 16S rRNA gene were amplified using primers 338F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′) under optimized conditions: 55°C annealing temperature and 27 amplification cycles. PCR amplicons were purified using the AxyPrep DNA Gel Extraction Kit (Axygen, Union City, CA, USA) prior to library construction. Paired-end sequencing was subsequently performed on the Illumina MiSeq platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Raw sequencing data were quality-filtered using Trimmomatic (version 0.33) (Martin, 2011). Primers, low-quality reads, sequences with high nucleotide ambiguity, and barcode sequences were identified and removed using Cutadapt (version 1.9.1) to generate clean reads. Paired-end reads were then merged and assembled into contiguous tags using FLASH (version 1.2.11) (Magoc and Salzberg, 2011). Amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) were clustered from high-quality tags using DADA2 (Callahan et al., 2016) and rare ASVs (accounting for <0.005 % of total sequenced reads) were filtered out according to established criteria (Bokulich et al., 2013). Representative sequences of retained ASVs were taxonomically annotated by alignment against the Silva database (Release 132; http://www.arb-silva.de) (Quast et al., 2013).

Serum sample chromatography-mass spectrometry analysis and data preprocessing

Non-targeted metabolomics detection of serum samples was conducted using previously reported methods (Cai et al., 2015; Dunn et al., 2011; Wang et al., 2016). The HPLC system (Thermo Fisher) was equipped with an LC BEH Amide chromatographic column (2.1 mm × 100 mm, 1.7 μm) and coupled with an Orbitrap MS Q Exactive HFX mass spectrometry detector (Thermo Fisher). Raw mass spectrometry (MS) data were converted to mzXML format using ProteoWizard MSConvert before import into the open-source XCMS software. For peak picking, the following parameters were applied: centWave algorithm with m/z tolerance of 10 ppm, peak width range of c (10, 60), and prefilter settings of c (10, 100). For peak grouping, parameters included a bandwidth of 5, m/z window width of 0.025, and minimum fraction of 0.5. Isotope and adduct annotations were performed using CAMERA (Collection of Algorithms for MEtabolite pRofile Annotation). Among the extracted ion features, only variables with >50 % non-zero measurements in at least one group were retained. Metabolite identification was achieved by matching accurate m/z values (<10 ppm) and MS/MS spectra against an in-house database established using authentic reference standards.

Statistical analysis

Genome-wide association analyses (GWAS)

The normality of phenotypic data (AFW, microbial abundances, and metabolite abundances) was verified using the Shapiro-Wilk test via the shapiro.test () function in the built-in stats package of R. A P-value > 0.05 indicated that the data conformed to a normal distribution. GWAS were performed using the AFW, absolute abundances of cecal microbiota, and absolute abundances of metabolites as target phenotypes in PLINK software (Purcell et al., 2007). Additionally, GWAS for these traits were conducted in R software with the ‘rMVP’ package (Yin et al., 2021). For GWAS, we employed the FarmCPU (Fixed and random model Circulating Probability Unification) method. This approach iteratively utilizes a Fixed Effect Model (FEM) and a Random Effect Model (REM) to completely eliminate confounding between the tested markers and the kinship matrix while controlling for false positives, thereby enhancing statistical power.

In the Fixed Effect Model, each SNP marker is tested individually using the following model:yi=∑k=1tMikbk+Sijdj+eiwhere yi is the phenotype of the i-th individual, Mik is the genotype of the k-th pseudo QTN (pseudo quantitative trait nucleotide), bk is its effect, Sij is the genotype of the j-th marker being tested, dj is the effect of the tested marker to be estimated, and ei is the residual.

The analysis proceeds through an iterative process involving both models. Initially, with an empty pseudo QTN set, the FEM is run to obtain P-values for all markers. A P-value substitution procedure is then applied: when a pseudo QTN serves as a covariate, it lacks its own test statistic; therefore, the most significant P-value obtained for that marker from all marker tests is assigned as its final P-value. Subsequently, based on the genome-wide P-values and the physical positions of SNPs, pseudo QTNs are optimally selected within the REM. In this step, the REM constructs a kinship matrix K using the selected pseudo QTNs, with the model specified as yi=ui+ei, where Var(u)=2Kσa2. The optimal combination of pseudo QTNs is determined through maximum likelihood estimation. This optimized set of pseudo QTNs is then updated to the FEM. These steps are repeated iteratively until the pseudo QTN set converges. Upon convergence, the final output from the FEM provides the effect sizes and P-values for all genome-wide SNPs.

Identification of metabolites and cecal microbes associated with AFW using the two-part model

The relationships between microbial/metabolite profiles and AFW were evaluated using the two-part model previously described by Fu et al. (2015). Briefly, a binomial model was first applied to examine associations between the presence or absence of individual ASVs and AFW. For each ASV, samples were assigned a binary indicator (b), where 1 denoted detection and 0 denoted non-detection. The binary model was defined as:y=β1b+ewhere y represents individual AFW values, β1 is the estimated effect of the binary term, b is the binary presence/absence variable, and e denotes the residual error.

Subsequently, a quantitative model was used to assess relationships between feature abundance and AFW, restricted to samples in which the feature was detected and identified as significant in the preceding binomial analysis. Feature abundances were typically log10-transformed prior to analysis. The quantitative model was specified as:y=β2q+ewhere q represents the normalized abundance of the given feature, β2 is the corresponding effect estimate, and e represents residuals.

To integrate evidence from both models, a meta-analysis was performed using an unweighted Z-method to compute a combined meta-P value. The final effect direction and significance were determined based on the smallest P-value among the binomial model, quantitative model, and meta-analysis. The corresponding Z-score was derived from the standard normal distribution. A Z > 0 indicated a positive association between the feature and AFW, whereas Z < 0 indicated a negative association.

Other analytical methods

Microbial co-occurrence networks were constructed using the SparCC algorithm (Friedman and Alm, 2012) which is designed to infer correlation networks from compositional data by accounting for the constant-sum constraint through an iterative log-ratio transformation procedure. Interactions between bacteria (nodes) were determined using absolute sparse correlation coefficients, with taxon pairs showing a correlation coefficient > 0.45 included in the network construction. Cytoscape (version 3.7.1) (Lopes et al., 2010) was used for network construction, visualization and calculation of topological features. Hub microbiotas were identified using the Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC) algorithm in the cytoHubba plugin of Cytoscape (Chin et al., 2014). The Bayesian sparse linear mixed model in GEMMA software (Zhou, 2017) was used for heritability (h²) estimation.

Results

Experimental birds and phenotypes

At 70 days of age, the AFW of Qiandongnan Xiaoxiang chickens was 19.31 ± 9.16 g (n = 205), with a minimum value of 3.3 g and a maximum of 63.4 g (Table S2). The AFP was 2.28 ± 0.95 %. AFP was significantly lower in roosters (2.07 ± 0.93 %, n = 114) than in hens (2.52 ± 0.94 %, n = 91; P = 0.0081), whereas no significant difference in AFW was detected between sexes (P = 0.28) (Fig. 2A). To characterize the relationship between abdominal fat deposition and body weight, Spearman correlation analysis was performed among AFW, AFP, average daily gain (ADG) from hatching to 70 days, and weekly body weights (WD). The results revealed that AFW was significantly and positively correlated with body weight at all weekly time points except for day 1 body weight (WD1) (r = 0.030, P = 0.67), and also with ADG (Fig. 2B and C and Fig. S1). Furthermore, a strong positive correlation was observed between AFP and AFW (Fig. 2D; r = 0.92, P = 2.2e-16). Previous studies have indicated that obesity may be closely associated with inflammatory factors (Chen et al., 2021; Gerhardt et al., 2001). To determine whether inflammation contributes to abdominal fat deposition, we measured the serum concentrations of six cytokines (IFN‑γ, IL‑1β, IL‑6, IL‑17, IL‑22, and IFN‑α) (Table S3) and analyzed their correlations with AFW and AFP. No significant correlations were observed between AFW or AFP and any of these cytokines, suggesting that abdominal fat deposition in chickens during the 0–70 day stage has little association with inflammatory factors. By contrast, several significant correlations were identified between body weight and certain cytokines. IL‑6 was negatively correlated with body weight at 14, 21, and 35 days (WD14, WD21, and WD35). IFN‑γ and IL‑17 were negatively correlated with WD7, and IL‑17 also showed a negative correlation with WD1 (Fig. S1).

Identification of key loci of AFW using GWAS

A total of 29,441,528 SNPs and 29,198,737 Indels were retained for GWAS in 205 chickens. The heritability of AFW was estimated using the GCTA model, and AFW showed moderate heritability (h² = 0.213). No significant loci were detected under the Bonferroni‑corrected significance threshold. However, when the threshold was relaxed to -log10 (P) = 6, a total of 46 variants including 3 Indels were identified to be significantly associated with AFW (Fig. 3 and Fig. S2). QTL annotation revealed that these significant loci were distributed across 10 chromosomes, with 27 QTLs markedly enriched on chromosome 5 that explained 0.122–0.165 of the phenotypic variance. These SNPs were located within or near 25 candidate genes, including KIF21, AETS2, PHF14, RNF138, PM20D2, MID2, HS6ST2, DKK2, TACR3, HS6ST2, RAG2, CLDN9 and some novel genes. All loci on chromosome 5 were located in intergenic regions, and 25 of them were annotated to the gene cluster ENSGALT00010065177–ENSGALT00010065189, which encodes a long non‑coding RNA (lncRNA) (Table S4).Fig. 3Manhattan plot from the genome-wide association study (GWAS) for abdominal fat weight in chickens.Fig 3 dummy alt text

Identification of cecal microbiota associated with AFW

A total of 21,538,325 high-quality reads (averaging 105,065 reads per sample) were generated from cecal content samples and clustered into 9923 ASVs. Taxonomic annotation assigned these ASVs to 19 phyla, 11 of which were present in all samples (Fig. 4A). Phyla with mean relative abundance > 1 % included Firmicutes (52.11 %), Bacteroidota (35.53 %), Proteobacteria (2.73 %), Synergistota (2.17 %), and Verrucomicrobiota (1.85 %). A total of 320 genera of cecal microbiota were identified across all samples (Fig. 4B and Table S5). The five most abundant genera were Bacteroides (20.36 %), Faecalibacterium (5.73 %), Rikenellaceae_RC9_gut_group (5.63 %), Clostridia_UCG-014 (5.61 %), and norank_f_Muribaculaceae (4.06 %, an unclassified family temporarily designated as Muribaculaceae). An interaction network of the chicken cecal microbial ecosystem was constructed at the ASV level, and hub microbial taxa were identified. Among the top 30 ASVs with the highest hub scores, Bacteroides caecigallinarum and Phascolarctobacterium spp. exhibited strong connectivity in the network. Specifically, the ASVs 4, 9, 6, 11 and 3 (ranked 3rd, 4th, 6th, 8th and 16th, respectively) were annotated as Bacteroides caecigallinarum, whereas ASVs 35 and 15 (ranked 1st and 7th) were annotated as Phascolarctobacterium (Fig. 4C). Correlation analyses were performed between cecal microbiota alpha diversity and AFW. The Shannon and Simpson indices were significantly positively correlated with AFW (r = 0.17, P = 0.015 and r = 0.16, P = 0.023, respectively) and AFP (r = 0.17, P = 0.016 and r = 0.15, P = 0.019, respectively) (Fig. 4D). In addition, the Firmicutes/Bacteroidota (F/B) ratio was evaluated in relation to AFW and AFP, showing no significant correlation with AFW (r = 0.12, P = 0.79) but a significant positive correlation with AFP (r = 0.14, P = 0.043)..Fig. 4Cecal microbiota diversity and taxa associated with abdominal fat weight (AFW) in chickens. A two-part model was applied to identify AFW-associated ASVs. Using thresholds of Z score > 2 and P < 0.05, 34 AFW-associated ASVs were detected from the 262 ASVs analyzed (Fig. 4E). Of these, 26 ASVs were positively correlated with AFW (Z score ≥ 0) and 8 ASVs were negatively correlated (Z score < 0). Among the ASVs positively correlated with AFW, four ASVs (18, 22, 27 and 8) were annotated to the Muribaculaceae family, three ASVs (36, 125 and 87) to Faecalibacterium and three ASVs (132, 180 and 210) to Lactobacillus. These observations suggest that Muribaculaceae, Faecalibacterium, and Lactobacillus are positively associated with AFW in chickens. Of note, Spearman correlation analysis revealed that the four Muribaculaceae‑annotated ASVs and three Faecalibacterium‑annotated ASVs were significantly positively correlated with each other (Fig. S3). Other taxa positively associated with AFW included [Eubacterium]_coprostanoligenes_group, Bacilli, and UCG-005 (a member of Ruminococcaceae). Among the 8 ASVs negatively associated with AFW, Blautia (ASV112) and Faecalibacterium (ASV37) were identified, along with other bacterial taxa. Meanwhile, ASV218_f_Lachnospiraceae, ASV255_g_Clostridia_vadinBB60_group, and ASV36_g_Faecalibacterium—all within the top 30 hub microbiota—were significantly associated with AFW (Fig. 4F).

Serum metabolome signatures associated with AFW

Non-targeted metabolomic analysis was conducted on chicken serum, yielding 12,642 and 10,222 metabolite ion features in positive and negative modes, respectively, among which 621 and 530 metabolites were reliably annotated (Fig. 5A; Table S6). A two-part model identified 98 AFW-associated metabolites, including 29 cationic metabolites (21 positively and 8 negatively correlated with AFW, respectively; Table S7) and 69 anionic metabolites (39 positively and 30 negatively correlated). To further prioritize AFW-related metabolites, two subgroups were defined from the chicken study cohort: the top 20 % for AFW (hAFW: 32.7 ± 7.5 g, n = 40) and the bottom 20 % for AFW (lAFW: 7.9 ± 2.2 g, n = 40). A random forest analysis was employed to identify differential serum metabolites between high and low AFW chickens, and 42 differential metabolites were identified between the two groups (17 enriched in the hAFW group and 25 in the lAFW group; Table S8). Intersection analysis of the two-part model and random forest results identified 11 cationic shared metabolites and 17 anionic shared metabolites associated with chicken AFW, respectively. At the Class level, these shared metabolites were mainly categorized into the following classes: 10 metabolites were classified as Glycerophospholipids (GPLs), 4 as Benzene and substituted derivatives, 2 as Carboxylic acids and derivatives, 2 as Phenols and others (Table S9). Among the 28 shared metabolites (Fig. 5C and D), a total of 10 metabolites (35.7%)were GPLs, indicating that GPLs are the most prominent metabolite class associated with AFW. Notably in the positive mode, 8 out of 11 shared metabolites were GPLs, including 1-myristoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine, 1-heptadecanoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine, LPC 18:1, and others. Several nucleotide metabolites also showed significant positive associations with AFW, including 7-methylguanine (a pyrimidine), Ile-Pro (a pyrimidine nucleoside), and xanthosine 5′-monophosphate (a purine nucleotide). Given that cecal microbiota was closely linked to AFW-related metabolites in our previous analysis, we investigated whether these metabolites were regulated by host genetics, cecal microbiota, or both. Several metabolites were co-regulated by both cecal microbiota and host genetics. Specifically, sorbitol 6-phosphate (M261T90, h² = 0.0.885), xanthosine 5′-monophosphate (M211T44, h² = 0.877), gallic acid (M169T38, h² = 0.642), and 1-heptadecanoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (M510T195, h² = 0.604) were highly heritable metabolites and were also significantly associated with specific bacterial taxa, such as the [Ruminococcus]_torques_group, Blautia, and Muribaculaceae. The Z-chromosome locus promotes AFD by regulating muribaculaceae

To investigate whether AFW-associated microbiota are under host genetic control, we estimated the heritability of the 34 AFW-associated microbiota. In total, 15 taxa were heritable (h² ≥ 0.2), accounting for 44.12 % of all tested taxa (Fig. 7A; Table S11). Of particular interest, Muribaculaceae, a key AFW-associated bacterial family, displayed high heritability. Specifically, four ASVs annotated to Muribaculaceae (ASV18, ASV22, ASV27, and ASV8) showed heritability values of 0.950, 0.885, 0.841, and 0.971, respectively. Several microbial taxa with inhibitory effects on AFW were non-heritable, including ASV112_Blautia, ASV179_g_[Ruminococcus]_torques_group, ASV208_g_[Eubacterium]_coprostanoligenes_group, and ASV218_f_Lachnospiraceae. To further dissect the genetic architecture underlying these AFW-associated microbiota, we performed mGWAS using the relative abundances of the 34 ASVs as quantitative traits. At a significance threshold of –log₁₀ (P) = 8.2, we detected 43 significant SNP loci associated with six ASVs. A prominent locus harboring the gene ENSGALG00010010675 was significantly associated with both ASV18 and ASV22, members of Muribaculaceae. This locus was located on the Z chromosome (position 41811941; interval: 41806144–41817738) (Fig. 7C and D), and explained 15.1 % and 10.3 % of the phenotypic variance for ASV18 and ASV22 abundance, respectively. Consistent with microbial interaction analysis in this study, cecal microbial network analysis revealed a potential cross-feeding relationship between Faecalibacterium and Muribaculaceae. Notably, ASV125, ASV36, and ASV87 (all annotated to Faecalibacterium) showed low heritability and no significant associated loci in the mGWAS. Collectively, these results indicated that ENSGALG00010010675 may act as a specific key regulator of Muribaculaceae abundance. The key AFW-inhibitory taxa (ASV112_Blautia and ASV179_g_[Ruminococcus]_torques_group) were non-heritable and showed no significant associated SNP loci in the mGWAS (Fig. 7E), indicating that their abundances are not under host genetic control.

Discussion

The genetic improvement of broiler chickens by reducing excessive AFD is central to modern poultry breeding, as it directly addresses key industry challenges including feed efficiency, carcass quality, and metabolic health (Kochetova et al., 2026). Consumer demand for indigenous chickens has been steadily increasing owing to their distinctive sensory characteristics. However, many indigenous breeds often exhibit exacerbated AFD under intensive commercial rearing systems, which further underscores the urgency of this objective (Li et al., 2020). Here, we identified a novel quantitative trait locus on the Z chromosome that specifically regulates the abundance of Muribaculaceae, and nominated ENSGALG00010010675 as the key functional candidate gene. Muribaculaceae promoted AFD by elevating the systemic levels of sorbitol 6-phosphate, xanthosine 5′-monophosphate, and gallic acid. In contrast, Blautia was identified as a dominant commensal bacterium in the cecum with strong anti-adipogenic effects. Notably, unlike Muribaculaceae, the abundance of Blautia was independent of host genetics, enabling it to inhibit AFD via the production of bioactive metabolites including 7-methylguanine, 4-benzofuranethanamine, and 1-heptadecanoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine. These findings define a critical genetic–microbial axis that modulates abdominal fat development, providing dual strategies targeting both host genetics and the gut microbiota to improve production efficiency in chickens.

The results of phenotypic analysis showed that the AFW of 70-day-old chickens displayed considerable individual variation, ranging from 3.3 to 63.4 g, with a coefficient of variation of approximately 47.4 %, which is consistent with that reported in indigenous yellow chickens from Wen’s Group in China, whose AFW ranges from 3.4 to 97.7 g (Wen et al., 2019). Significant sexual dimorphism was detected in abdominal fat percentage (AFP) rather than in AFW, with cocks exhibiting a lower AFP than hens. This discrepancy might be explained by the higher average body weight of cocks compared to hens at the same age. Despite having similar AFW values, the larger body size of cock results in a lower AFP, implying that body weight could be a key factor influencing AFP (Yang et al., 2024). Unlike the established association between obesity and inflammation in humans and pigs (Chen et al., 2021; Gerhardt et al., 2001), none of the six measured serum cytokines (IFN‑γ, IL‑1β, IL‑6, IL‑17, IL‑22, and IFN‑α) showed significant correlations with AFW in the present study. Given the relatively moderate adiposity (average AFP: 2.28 %) of chickens from hatching to 70 days of age, our data further suggest that inflammation exerts no dominant effect on fat accumulation during this growth period, which is in line with the notion that severe obesity is required to trigger marked inflammatory responses (Renner et al., 2018).

Heritability estimation revealed a moderate heritability estimate for AFW in this study, which aligns with the previously reported heritability range (0.15–0.30) for chicken abdominal fat-related traits (Guo et al., 2023; Moreira et al., 2018). However, other studies have reported a substantially higher heritability of 0.82 for AFW (Cahaner and Nitsan, 1985), likely reflecting breed-specific genetic backgrounds and divergent rearing conditions. This moderate heritability implies that AFW is jointly governed by genetic and environmental factors, underpinned by a complex genetic architecture involving multiple minor-effect loci rather than a limited number of major-effect loci. Our identification of 46 significant AFW-associated SNPs/Indels (including 3 Indels) provides empirical support for this polygenic basis. Notably, 27 of these significant loci mapped to chicken chromosome 5, where they were annotated to a gene cluster (ENSGALT00010065177–ENSGALT00010065189) comprising long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs). Although intergenic regions were once considered functionally inert, a growing body of evidence indicates that they can modulate gene expression through cis- or trans-regulatory mechanisms, particularly via lncRNAs that play pivotal roles in fat metabolism (Zhang et al., 2018a, 2018b). Thus, this lncRNA-related gene cluster emerges as a putative key regulatory module underlying AFW variation, whose functional relevance warrants further investigation.

The gut microbiota is a critical pivotal modulator of host lipid metabolism and fat deposition (Chen et al., 2023). In this study, higher cecal microbial alpha-diversity (Shannon and Simpson indices) and an elevated Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes (F/B) ratio were significantly positively associated with increased AFW and AFP in chickens. In the context of previous studies, early human research linked a higher F/B ratio to obesity (Jumpertz et al., 2011), while subsequent studies reported the opposite (Collado et al., 2008; Turnbaugh et al., 2006), leading to no consistent consensus in the field. The observed positive correlations between cecal microbial diversity, F/B ratio, and AFW in the present study are attributed to enhanced nutrient and energy harvesting by the cecal microbiota, which in turn fuels abdominal adipogenesis. A higher F/B ratio (dominated by Firmicutes) promotes AFD via two interconnected pathways: Firmicutes effectively break down indigestible dietary polysaccharides and fats to increase energy substrate bioavailability, and enhanced fermentation elevates short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) production, which further promotes adipocyte differentiation and triglyceride synthesis (Sun et al., 2025). Similarly, the positive correlation between microbial alpha-diversity and fat traits reflects the enrichment of energy-harvesting microbes under conditions of excess energy intake, with a diverse microbiota facilitating the efficient conversion of dietary energy into abdominal lipids (Xiang et al., 2021).

In the present study, we identified distinct cecal microbiota associated with AFW in chickens, which could be classified into two functional groups: AFD‑promoting bacteria (Muribaculaceae, Faecalibacterium, [Eubacterium]_coprostanoligenes_group, and Sellimonas) and AFD‑reducing bacteria (Blautia, [Ruminococcus] torques_group, and Lachnospiraceae). Compared with previous reports (Xiang et al., 2021), our findings are inconsistent. Such discrepancies may arise from variations in experimental conditions, including breed, diet formulation, and rearing environment. Among the AFD‑promoting taxa, Muribaculaceae and Faecalibacterium were particularly prominent, with multiple AFW‑related ASVs annotated to these two microbiotas. Further mechanistic insights from co‑abundance network analysis and associated metabolite detection suggested a potential cross‑feeding relationship between them, and this cross‑feeding partnership has also been reported previously (Zhu et al., 2024). Muribaculaceae may supply essential nutrients to support the proliferation and metabolic activity of Faecalibacterium. This synergistic interaction implies that the two taxa act cooperatively to modulate fat synthesis and deposition via their metabolic products, as their coordinated abundance dynamics and metabolic crosstalk were closely associated with AFW phenotypic variation. Muribaculaceae, a family within the order Bacteroidales formerly known as S24‑7 (Ormerod et al., 2016) and officially renamed in 2019, has few cultivated isolates to date (Lagkouvardos et al., 2019). However, its core metabolic function involves the degradation of complex polysaccharides (Pei et al., 2023), which converts indigestible dietary polysaccharides into absorbable monosaccharides and enhances host energy harvest. Faecalibacterium, a well‑characterized gut commensal in humans, is a major producer of butyrate (Heinken et al., 2014; Lopez-Siles et al., 2017) and is traditionally linked to anti‑inflammatory effects, improved gut microecology, elevated bacterial diversity, increased abundance of SCFA‑producing bacteria (Lopez-Siles et al., 2017). Nonetheless, the pro‑obesogenic potential of Faecalibacterium has also been observed, with its enrichment reported in the gut of obese individuals (Del et al., 2018). In the present study, we observed a combined pro‑obesogenic effect of Muribaculaceae and Faecalibacterium, which may be explained by their efficient utilization of dietary monosaccharides. This metabolic feature enhances host energy acquisition to a greater degree than the anti‑obesogenic effects exerted by SCFA production, ultimately promoting increased AFD. Consistent with these results, inflammation was not a major driver of abdominal fat deposition in chickens from 0 to 70 days of age, supporting this interpretation. We observed a combined pro‑obesogenic effect of Muribaculaceae and Faecalibacterium, which could potentially be attributed to their efficient utilization of dietary monosaccharides. This metabolic trait enhances host energy harvest to a greater extent than the anti‑obesogenic effects conferred by SCFA production, ultimately contributing to higher AFW. Consistent with our correlation analysis between inflammatory factors and AFW, inflammation does not appear to be a major driver of abdominal fat deposition in chickens from 0 to 70 days of age, further supporting this interpretation. Blautia was identified as a key genus that showed a significant negative association with AFW. As a member of the family Lachnospiraceae (Chanda et al., 2024), Blautia has been consistently recognized in humans and other animal models as an important regulator of lipid metabolism (Benitez-Paez et al., 2020; Hosomi et al., 2022), with the ability to enhance lipid degradation and reduce visceral fat accumulation, thus supporting its conserved anti‑obesity function (Ozato et al., 2019; Ozato et al., 2022). Prebiotic intervention studies have been reported to enrich Blautia, leading to elevated butyrate levels, enhanced intestinal barrier function, and reduced visceral adiposity (Kim et al., 2024; Song et al., 2023). Based on the above results, we infer that Blautia can inhibit adipogenesis by regulating the host’s metabolic signaling pathways through specific microbial metabolites (7-methylguanine, 4-benzofuranethanamine, and 1-heptadecanoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine), thereby reducing AFW in chickens. However, the specific mechanisms underlying the inhibition of fat deposition by these metabolites have not been reported to date, which provides a direction for future research.

Metabolites serve as pivotal mediators in host-microbiota crosstalk, modulating host phenotypic traits by transmitting signals between the gut microbiota and host metabolic pathways (Li et al., 2025). Our metabolomic profiling revealed that AFW-related metabolites mainly comprised glycerophospholipids, organic acids, nucleotides, and dipeptides. Among these, GPLs were the most dominant class, underscoring their critical involvement in chicken fat deposition. Significantly, GPLs exerted divergent regulatory effects on fat deposition rather than acting uniformly. Of the 10 AFW-related GPLs, six were positively correlated with AFW, while four were negatively correlated. This functional duality may arise from structural variations in acyl chain length, saturation, and positional isomerism, which modify their biological activity and metabolic fate in the host (Syme et al., 2016). Such as reducing adipocyte differentiation and stimulating lipolysis, consistent with observations in mouse models (Kim and Chung, 2021). While many studies have reported anti‑adipogenic effects of GPLs (Kawamura et al., 2012; Kim and Chung, 2021), other evidence supports a pro‑adipogenic role via choline phospholipid metabolism and lipid synthesis. For instance, specific GPL species were positively linked to visceral fat accumulation in humans (Syme et al., 2016), and dysregulation of the GPL pathway contributes to abnormal lipid deposition (Sonkar et al., 2019). Beyond molecular structure, GPL subtype, and host species may further shape these contrasting functions by altering choline phospholipid metabolism and gut microbiota‑associated signaling.

We observed that among the identified AFW-related GPLs, 1-heptadecanoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine, 1-oleoyl-2-myristoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine, and LPC 18:0 showed the strongest correlations with the AFW-related microbiota. Such extensive correlations suggest that these GPLs are tightly modulated by the cecal microbiota. These numerical observations collectively support the notion that the biosynthesis, metabolism, or turnover of these GPLs are extensively regulated by the cecal microbiota. In contrast, 1-myristoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine was correlated with only one AFW-related microbiota, implying that this metabolite is less affected by cecal microbiota. This finding is consistent with previous studies demonstrating that gut microbiota is closely involved in the regulation of glycerophospholipids metabolism, as bacterial-derived phospholipids are critical mediators of host-microbe interactions in the gut (Song et al., 2025). LPC 18:0, one of the most correlated metabolites in our study, has been shown to be a metabolite of many gut bacterial strains and its levels in the animal systemic circulation are significantly regulated by gut microbiota (Liebisch et al., 2021). We further performed heritability analysis to dissect the contributions of host genetics and cecal microbiota to these lipid metabolites. Generally, metabolites with moderate-to-high heritability are considered to be under notable host genetic control, whereas non-heritable metabolites are more likely driven by environmental factors such as the microbiota (Chen et al., 2022). In this study, 1-heptadecanoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine and 1-hexadecyl-2-(5Z,8Z,11Z,14Z-eicosatetraenoyl)-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine showed moderate heritability (0.604 and 0.493, respectively), and both were significantly correlated with AFW-related microbiota. These findings suggest that these two GPLs are coordinately regulated by both host genetics and cecal microbiota (Zhang et al., 2023). Among all bacterial taxa detected, ASV25_g_Streptococcus showed the broadest associations with lipid metabolites, implying this taxon represents a functionally important gut bacterium that participates in the regulation of lipid metabolism and GPL homeostasis in chickens.

Compared with their limited involvement in lipid metabolism, cecal microbiota are more extensively engaged in synthesizing specific organic acids and their derivatives (3-hydroxyphenylacetic acid, gallic acid, sorbitol 6-phosphate). The aforementioned AFW-related microbiotas (Muribaculaceae and Faecalibacterium) were positively correlated with these metabolites, while Blautia and [Ruminococcus]_torques_group were negatively correlated. Consistent with this inference, previous studies have shown that gut microbiota utilize p-hydroxyphenylacetic acid (a structural analog of 3-hydroxyphenylacetic acid) for energy metabolism, thereby altering host metabolic processes (Lee et al., 2024). Among these AFW-related metabolites, sorbitol 6‑phosphate displayed the highest Z‑score, suggesting its role as a key core metabolite in cecal microbiota‑mediated fat deposition. To our knowledge, the association between sorbitol 6‑phosphate and lipid synthesis or deposition remains unreported in humans or livestock. Furthermore, two nucleotide derivatives (xanthosine 5′-monophosphate (XMP) and 7-methylguanine) were significantly associated with AFW, suggesting that cecal microbiota regulate host fat deposition not only through organic acid metabolism but also through nucleotide metabolism. As an intermediate in de novo purine synthesis, XMP is directly converted from inosine 5′-monophosphate (IMP), implying that the IMP-XMP purine synthesis pathway is closely related to host fat deposition; accordingly, human studies have identified IMP metabolism as a potential therapeutic target for obesity (Takahashi et al., 2022). In contrast, 7-methylguanine was negatively correlated with AFW, suggesting it may inhibit fat deposition. As a modified purine, 7-methylguanine is involved in the regulation of gene expression and DNA damage repair (Tang et al., 2023), and our further analysis showed it is closely associated with Blautia and [Ruminococcus]_torques_group, implying these two bacterial taxa may regulate intestinal 7-methylguanine levels. Collectively, our results indicate that Muribaculaceae and Faecalibacterium promote AFW by regulating metabolites such as sorbitol 6-phosphate and gallic acid, while Blautia and [Ruminococcus]_torques_group reduce AFW by modulating specific serum metabolites, including 3-hydroxyphenylacetic acid, methylphosphonothioic acid o-ethyl ester, and 7-methylguanine.

Genetics and gut microbiota are widely recognized as two pivotal factors in fat deposition (Wu et al., 2024). Previous studies have reported that the gut microbiota in chickens is largely independent of host genetics, and it often regulates abdominal fat deposition in an autonomous manner (Wen et al., 2019). Consistently, our study also identified that Blautia and [Ruminococcus]_torques_group could independently regulate AFD without being affected by genetic regulation. In contrast, Muribaculaceae abundance, which was positively correlated with AFW, was found to be regulated by the gene ENSGALG00010010675, suggesting distinct regulatory patterns across different microbial taxa in relation to fat deposition‑related traits in chickens. Although our AFW‑focused GWAS did not identify a direct genetic association between ENSGALG00010010675 and AFW, we hypothesize that this gene may exert an indirect regulatory effect by modulating cecal Muribaculaceae abundance. Such modulation could further amplify the microbial impacts on host lipid absorption and fat deposition, thereby ultimately promoting AFD in chickens. The interaction between host genetics and gut microbiota has also been clearly validated in human and pig studies (Yang et al., 2022; Zhernakova et al., 2024). For instance, Faecalibacterium prausnitzii in the human gut is more likely to harbor genomic fragments containing N-acetylgalactosamine (GalNAc) metabolic gene clusters, a trait jointly determined by the human ABO and FUT2 genes (Zhernakova et al., 2024). ABO porcine research shows the gene regulates N-acetylgalactosaminyl transferase (GalNAc) levels to affect abundance, and this GalNAc shapes the gut microbiome via GalNAc-mediated antigen modification (Yang et al., 2022). Collectively, our results imply that both genetically independent and genetically regulated gut microbes participate in the regulation of abdominal fat deposition in chickens. While Blautia and [Ruminococcus]_torques_group function as autonomous regulators of fat deposition, Muribaculaceae is under the control of host gene ENSGALG00010010675, which may indirectly influence AFW by modulating microbial abundance.

Several limitations in this study should be acknowledged. First, the reliance on a single cohort of 205 broilers may limit the generalizability of our findings to other genetic backgrounds or production systems, given that chicken cecal microbiota and AFD are strongly affected by breed, feeding regime, and rearing environment. Further validation in independent and diverse broiler populations is therefore warranted. Second, our study only provides correlative evidence among host genetic loci, cecal microbiota, serum metabolites, and AFD; functional experiments (e.g., gene knockout, microbial transplantation, metabolite supplementation) are essential to verify the causal relationships within this regulatory axis. Additionally, microbial profiling lacked strain-level resolution and functional metagenomic annotation, while metabolomic analysis was limited to serum, potentially overlooking tissue-specific metabolites (e.g., in cecal contents) that may be closely associated with AFD. Future studies should employ more comprehensive strategies to address these limitations and further advance our understanding of the regulatory mechanisms underlying AFD in broilers.

Conclusion

This study reveals two independent regulatory processes that contribute to AFD in chickens, governed by the cecal microbiota and its interaction with host genetics. We identified a cascade connecting host gene, gut microbiota, and metabolites, in which the Z-linked gene ENSGALG00010010675 modulates the abundance of Muribaculaceae, thereby altering key metabolites including sorbitol 6-phosphate, xanthosine 5′-monophosphate, and gallic acid that contribute to AFD progression. In parallel, specific cecal bacterial taxa, including Blautia and the [Ruminococcus]_torques_group, independently attenuate AFD by regulating the levels of metabolites such as 7-methylguanine, 4-benzofuranethanamine, and 1-heptadecanoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine. Collectively, this study advances our understanding of host–microbiota interactions in modulating chicken abdominal fat deposition and provides valuable targets for the genetic improvement of fat-related traits in poultry breeding.
