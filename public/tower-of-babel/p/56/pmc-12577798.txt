Introduction

Subcutaneous emphysema (SE) refers to the presence of air within subcutaneous tissues and may arise from infectious or noninfectious sources Infectious causes, notably gas gangrene (clostridial myonecrosis) and necrotizing fasciitis, are life-threatening and necessitate emergent surgical management combined with antimicrobial therapy[1]. Conversely, noninfectious SE, often secondary to trauma, iatrogenic injury, or pneumatic tools, typically follows a benign course but may mimic severe infections, posing diagnostic challenges[2].

The upper limb is an uncommon location for SE, and cases related to pneumatic tool injuries are especially rare in the literature. High-pressure injection injuries from pneumatic devices introduce air and possibly contaminants into tissue planes, creating a ball-valve effect that traps air within soft tissues[3]. This manuscript presents a case of noninfectious SE following pneumatic injury to the left upper limb, accompanied by a literature review to contextualize clinical features, diagnostic criteria, and management strategies. The patient was treated at a secondary-level academic teaching hospital with access to multidisciplinary support, highlighting the value of coordinated institutional care in managing such cases. The aim is to assist clinicians in differentiating between infectious and noninfectious causes of SE and to guide appropriate therapeutic decisions.

Case presentation

A 37-year-old right-handed man with no significant past medical or surgical history presented to the emergency department after an occupational injury involving a pneumatic blower. The patient reported that the blower tip accidentally penetrated his left hand, causing immediate swelling.

On arrival, vital signs were stable: temperature 36.7°C, heart rate 78 bpm, blood pressure 125/80 mmHg, respiratory rate 16/min, and oxygen saturation 98% on room air. The patient was alert and in no distress. Physical examination of the left upper limb revealed a small puncture wound at the base of the index finger distal to the first metacarpal (Fig. 1). Crepitus was palpable extending from the dorsum of the hand to the mid-forearm. There was no erythema, warmth, tenderness, or fluctuance. Distal pulses were intact with capillary refill in less than 2 s. Neurological examination was unremarkable, with full motor and sensory function preserved.Figure 1.Comparison of affected (left) and unaffected (right) upper limbs demonstrating swelling and crepitus.

Laboratory investigations include the following: white blood cell count 8.1 × 103/μL (normal 4.0–11.0), C-reactive protein (CRP) 3.4 mg/L (normal <5 mg/L), erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) 12 mm/h (normal 0–20 mm/h), sodium 139 mmol/L, potassium 4.1 mmol/L, creatinine 0.65 mg/dL, international normalized ratio 1.05, prothrombin time 13.5 s, and partial thromboplastin time 32.2 s.

Given the absence of systemic symptoms, normal laboratory values, and imaging findings confined to subcutaneous tissue, a diagnosis of noninfectious SE secondary to pneumatic injury was made.

The patient was admitted for close observation. Intravenous ceftriaxone 1 g twice daily was administered prophylactically. The limb was immobilized, and analgesia with paracetamol (1 g every 8 h) and diclofenac (75 mg intramuscularly daily) was given. Serial neurovascular examinations were performed with no evidence of compartment syndrome.

Prophylactic antibiotics were administered to reduce the risk of secondary infection due to potential skin barrier compromise from the high-pressure air injury. While evidence in the literature is mixed regarding routine prophylaxis in similar cases, the patient’s clinical profile and risk factors favored a precautionary approach.

After 24 h of observation, the patient remained afebrile with stable clinical status and no progression of symptoms. He was discharged with outpatient follow-up instructions. At 1-week follow-up, the swelling and crepitus had resolved completely, and the puncture wound healed without complication. The patient expressed satisfaction with the provided treatment.

Literature review and discussion

A structured literature search was performed using PubMed, Scopus, and Google Scholar from inception to July 2025, employing the keywords “pneumatic injury,” “subcutaneous emphysema,” “upper limb,” and “case report.” Reference lists of relevant articles were also manually screened.

Epidemiology and etiology

SE in the extremities is uncommon and frequently underreported. The true incidence of traumatic SE in the upper limb is unknown but is thought to be rising due to the widespread use of pneumatic tools in industrial settings[4].

The etiologies of SE can be divided into two causes:

Infectious causes: Gas gangrene caused by Clostridium species and necrotizing fasciitis due to mixed aerobic and anaerobic bacteria. These conditions are fulminant and rapidly progressive[1,5].

Noninfectious causes: Trauma including penetrating injuries, blunt trauma, high-pressure injection injuries, iatrogenic causes such as arthroscopy or surgery, and spontaneous SE associated with barotrauma or Valsalva maneuvers[6].

High-pressure injection injuries from pneumatic devices are particularly notable for causing SE due to direct introduction of air and other foreign material into tissues. These injuries often appear minor externally but can cause extensive subcutaneous air tracking[7].

Pathophysiology

The ball-valve mechanism is central to the development of SE in traumatic injuries. Air is forced into the subcutaneous space through a skin breach but cannot escape, leading to progressive accumulation and spread along tissue planes[8]. The subcutaneous tissue’s loose connective matrix facilitates air migration, often tracking proximally from the point of entry[9].

In infectious SE, gas is produced by bacterial metabolism in necrotic tissues, usually accompanied by systemic toxicity and extensive soft tissue destruction[5].

Clinical presentation

Patients with noninfectious SE often present with the following symptoms:

Rapid swelling and crepitus localized to the injury site.

Puncture wounds or abrasions at the site of trauma.

Absence of systemic features such as fever, tachycardia, or hypotension.

Normal or mildly elevated inflammatory markers.

Diagnosis

Diagnostic workup includes laboratory tests and imaging:

Laboratory tests: Complete blood count, inflammatory markers (CRP, ESR), blood cultures if infection suspected.

Imaging: Plain radiographs are the initial modality and demonstrate radiolucent streaks of air within subcutaneous tissue. CT imaging can delineate deeper fascial involvement and guide surgical decision-making[11].

Clinical monitoring

Vital for early recognition of deterioration or compartment syndrome

Management

Management depends on the underlying etiology:

Noninfectious SE: Conservative management with limb immobilization, analgesia, prophylactic antibiotics (though debated), and close monitoring is generally sufficient. Surgical intervention is reserved for compartment syndrome or suspected infection[12].

Infectious SE: Requires emergent surgical debridement, broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics, and supportive care including fluid resuscitation and possible hyperbaric oxygen therapy[13].

Recent case series demonstrate excellent outcomes with conservative management in traumatic SE without infection[14,15]. The routine use of hyperbaric oxygen therapy in noninfectious cases remains controversial due to limited evidence[16].

Discussion

Our patient displayed hallmark features of noninfectious SE: rapid, localized swelling and palpable crepitus following a minor occupational injury, without systemic signs or lab abnormalities. Radiographic imaging confirmed air limited to the subcutaneous tissue, with no fascial or muscular involvement – further supporting a benign etiology.

This case highlights an underrecognized occupational hazard associated with pneumatic tools. As these devices become more widely used in industrial settings, clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for noninfectious SE resulting from high-pressure air injection injuries. These cases may appear dramatic but are often self-limiting with conservative treatment.

Crucially, prompt recognition and accurate diagnosis can prevent unnecessary surgical intervention. This case adds to the limited but growing body of literature that supports close observation, limb immobilization, and analgesia as effective treatment in uncomplicated cases. The role of prophylactic antibiotics remains uncertain; more research is needed to guide best practices.

The utility of hyperbaric oxygen therapy in expediting air resorption has been proposed, but its routine application in noninfectious cases is not currently supported by evidence and remains an area for future investigation.

This report describes a single patient with a short follow-up period, which limits the generalizability of findings. However, the detailed clinical course and literature review contribute to the sparse existing evidence on pneumatic-induced SE of the upper limb.

This work has been reported in line with the SCARE 2025 criteria[17].

Conclusion

SE of the upper limb following pneumatic trauma is a rare but important clinical entity. Differentiation from infectious causes is critical for appropriate management. This case, together with the literature review, highlights the typical clinical and radiological features of noninfectious SE and supports a conservative management approach. Increased awareness can improve patient outcomes and reduce unnecessary surgical procedures.
