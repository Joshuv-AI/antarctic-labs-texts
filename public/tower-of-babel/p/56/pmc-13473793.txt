Sex-specific associations of the CALLY index with mortality in hypertension: Stronger relative protection in males but greater absolute benefit in females-a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Ying Xu, Jiping Wang, Qiaoyi Li, Likun Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveInflammation, immunity, and nutrition are established factors in hypertension, but the prognostic role of the C-reactive protein-albumin-lymphocyte (CALLY) index remains undefined. We aimed to clarify its impact on mortality.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study used data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 1999-2010.We identified 9,199 adults with hypertension and linked their records to the National Death Index to obtain mortality follow-up through December 31, 2019. The association between the CALLY index and both all-cause mortality (ACM) and cardiovascular mortality (CVM) was assessed using survey-weighted Cox proportional hazards models. To evaluate potential sex-specific differences, we performed sex-stratified subgroup analyses and quantified additive interactions via the relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI) and attributable proportion (AP).ResultsOver a median follow-up of 11.2 years, 3,259 ACM (35.4%) and 1,111 CVM (12.1%) occurred. Subgroup analyses showed numerically stronger inverse associations in males than females for both ACM (male: HR = 0.82; female: HR = 0.87) and CVM (male: HR = 0.79; female: HR = 0.90), although multiplicative interaction was not significant (ACM: p = 0.13; CVM: p= 0.84). On the additive scale, a significant sex difference was observed for ACM (RERI = 0.14, 95% CI: 0.05–0.24), but not for CVM (RERI = 0.09, 95% CI: -0.07 to 0.26). Nonlinear dose-response patterns were observed for ACM (p < 0.001) but not for CVM (p = 0.072).ConclusionThe CALLY index was independently associated with mortality in hypertensive adults. Although the relative protective effect was numerically stronger in males, additive interaction analysis revealed that females exhibited a significant additional absolute risk reduction associated with higher CALLY levels for ACM. These findings suggest the potential for sex-specific risk assessment using the CALLY index, though given the observational design and the lack of significant multiplicative interaction, these results should be considered hypothesis-generating and do not support therapeutic recommendations.

Introduction

Hypertension affects over 1.2 billion adults worldwide and remains a leading cause of all-cause mortality (ACM) and cardiovascular mortality (CVM).1,2 Despite current therapies, a substantial residual mortality burden persists. This burden reflects the heterogeneity of individual risk among treated patients.3,4 Therefore, more effective risk stratification tools are needed to improve risk prediction and guide targeted interventions.

Emerging evidence recognizes inflammation, nutritional status, and immune dysfunction as interconnected drivers of hypertension progression.5–8 To capture their combined effects, the C-reactive protein-albumin-lymphocyte (CALLY) index was developed as an integrated biomarker. 9 This index has shown prognostic value across a wide range of diseases, including cancers,10–18 chronic inflammatory disorders such as rheumatoid arthritis, 19 chronic kidney disease, 20 chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, 21 as well as acute conditions like sepsis 22 and severe acute pancreatitis. 23

Recent studies have focused on the prognostic value of the CALLY index in cardiovascular disease (CVD). Data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) have confirmed its inverse association with ACM and CVM in patients with established CVD.24,25 However, whether this association extends to the broader hypertensive population remains unknown. These individuals are at an earlier stage of the CVD continuum, where early risk stratification could have greater preventive impact. Unlike patients with advanced CVD who often have multimorbidity and overt inflammation, hypertensive adults represent a group for whom such stratification may be particularly valuable. Moreover, the potential sex-specific nature of this association has not been systematically examined. Therefore, this study aims to evaluate the association between CALLY and mortality in hypertensive adults; and investigate whether this association is modified by sex and other clinically relevant subgroups.

Methods

Data source and study population

This retrospective cohort study used data from six cycles (1999-2010) of the NHANES, a nationally representative program of the U.S. non-institutionalized population. 26 The participant selection process is shown in Figure S1. From 62,160 individuals, we excluded those without hypertension, aged ≤ 20 years, pregnant women, those with invalid sampling weights, and those missing mortality data. After further excluding participants with incomplete CALLY index or covariate data, the final analytic sample comprised 9,199 hypertensive adults.

Hypertension definition

Hypertension was defined according to established criteria, 27 as meeting any of the following: (1) mean systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg; (2) mean diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90mmHg; (3) self-reported diagnosis of high blood pressure; or (4) current use of antihypertensive medication.

Exposure: CALLY index

The CALLY index was calculated as [albumin (g/dL) × lymphocyte count (/µL)]/[C-reactive protein (mg/dL) × 104]. 9 Comprehensive details on laboratory protocols can be accessed via the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention website. 28 Due to its skewed distribution, a natural logarithm transformation (ln) was applied before analysis.

Outcomes: All-cause and cardiovascular mortality

All-cause mortality (ACM) and cardiovascular mortality (CVM) were ascertained from the National Death Index. Follow-up began at baseline and continued until death or December 31, 2019. 29 CVM was defined as deaths from heart disease or cerebrovascular disorders (ICD-10: I00–I09, I11, I13, I20–I51, and I60–I69). 30

Covariates

Covariates were selected based on prior studies.25,31–33 They included age, sex, race, educational level, marital status, poverty income ratio (PIR), body mass index (BMI, kg/m2), waist circumference (WC), smoking history, alcohol use, self-reported history of CVD, diabetes mellitus (DM), cancer, triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL), dietary sodium and total energy intake. The detailed definitions of the variables are provided in Table S1.

Statistical analysis

To account for the complex, multi-stage cluster sampling design of NHANES, sampling weights were applied following the official NHANES guidelines to ensure national representativeness. 34 For the 1999-2002 cycles, the 4-year sample weight (WTMEC4YR) was used; for the 2003-2010 cycles, the 2-year sample weight (WTMEC2YR) was used. To obtain 6-year estimates, weights were calculated as 2/6 × WTMEC4YR for 1999-2002 and 1/6 × WTMEC2YR for 2003-2010.

Participants with missing data on the CALLY index or any covariate were excluded from the analysis. The final analytic sample comprised 9,199 participants with complete data on all study variables. The proportion of missing data for each covariate is presented in Table S2.

The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residual tests, with detailed results and residual plots provided in Table S5 and Figure S3. Weighted multivariable Cox proportional hazards models assessed the associations of ln CALLY and CALLY quartiles with ACM and CVM. Three sequentially adjusted models were constructed: Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 was adjusted for age, race, and sex; Model 3 was additionally adjusted for educational level, marital status, PIR, BMI, WC, smoking status, alcohol use, history of CVD, DM, cancer, TG, TC, HDL, dietary sodium, and total energy intake. The proportional hazards assumption was tested using Schoenfeld residuals, and no significant violations were detected (all p > 0.05). To examine potential nonlinear relationships, restricted cubic splines (RCS) with three knots at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles were used, with the 10th percentile as the reference. All RCS models were adjusted for the covariates in Model 3. The overall association was tested using the Wald test, and nonlinearity was assessed using a likelihood ratio test comparing the model with only the linear term to the model with both linear and spline terms. To formally identify the potential threshold of ln CALLY for all-cause mortality, we further performed a piecewise linear regression model. The inflection point was determined using a recursive algorithm that maximized the model likelihood, and the threshold effect was evaluated using a likelihood ratio test comparing the one-slope and two-slope models.

Subgroup analyses were performed across clinically relevant strata, including age, sex, BMI, smoking status, alcohol use, diabetes, CVD, and cancer history. For each stratum, separate Cox models were fitted, and interaction terms between the CALLY index and each stratifying variable were tested using the Wald test. To formally evaluate whether sex modified the association, we further conducted interaction analyses on both multiplicative and additive scales. For additive interaction analyses, CALLY was dichotomized using a median split. Multiplicative interaction was tested by including a product term (CALLY × sex) in the Cox model. Additive interaction was quantified using the Relative Excess Risk due to Interaction (RERI) and Attributable Proportion (AP) according to Andersson et al., 35 implemented via the interaction R package in R. For additive interaction analyses, CALLY was dichotomized by its median value (≤72.0 vs. > 72.0). RERI > 0 with a 95% confidence interval excluding 0 indicates a positive additive interaction. All interaction models were adjusted for the same covariates as in Model 3.

To assess robustness, we conducted four sensitivity analyses: (1) excluding participants with cancer; (2) 1-year landmark analysis (excluding deaths within the first year) to address reverse causality; (3) excluding participants with CRP > 10 mg/L to reduce transient inflammation; and (4) multiple imputation using chained equations with 5 imputed datasets to address missing data. All sensitivity analyses applied the fully adjusted Model 3.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.2; The R Foundation, Vienna, Austria) and the Free Statistics analysis platform (version 2.3 Beta; Beijing Free Clinical Medical Technology Co., Ltd.). A two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

This study is a secondary analysis of publicly available, de-identified data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (as revised in 2024). The original NHANES protocol was approved by the NCHS Ethics Review Board, and all participants provided written informed consent. As the data are publicly available and fully de-identified, this secondary analysis was determined to be exempt from additional institutional review board (IRB) review and patient consent. This study is reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for cohort studies. 36 The completed STROBE checklist has been provided as supplemental material.

Results

Missing data analysis

Among 11,557 hypertensive participants meeting initial criteria, 2,358 (20.4%) were excluded due to missing covariate data, yielding a final sample of 9,199. Comparison of included and excluded participants showed modest differences in baseline characteristics (Table S3), suggesting that exclusion of participants with missing data is unlikely to have introduced substantial selection bias. The median follow-up duration for the overall cohort was 11.2 years (IQR: 9.4–15.4 years).

Association between CALLY index and mortality

Kaplan-Meier curves showed progressively improved survival across CALLY quartiles (log-rank p < 0.001 for both endpoints) (Figure 1). Weighted Cox regression demonstrated an inverse association between ln CALLY and both ACM and CVM (Table 2). After full adjustment, the HR for ACM was 0.84 (95% CI: 0.81–0.87; p < 0.001), and for CVM was 0.84 (95% CI: 0.78–0.91; p < 0.001). The inverse association remained significant across sequentially adjusted models (p for trend < 0.001 for all models). RCS analysis revealed an L-shaped nonlinear pattern for ACM (p for non-linearity < 0.001) and a linear pattern for CVM (p = 0.072) (Figure 2). Threshold effect analysis identified an inflection point at ln CALLY = 2.85 for ACM (Table S4), below which each unit increase was associated with a 30% lower mortality risk (HR = 0.70, 95% CI: 0.64–0.77), and above which the effect was attenuated (HR = 0.91, 95% CI: 0.85–0.97; likelihood ratio test p < 0.001). Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of the inverse associations between ln CALLY and mortality. Excluding participants with baseline cancer, those who died within the first year of follow-up (1-year landmark analysis), or those with CRP > 10 mg/L did not materially change the results (Tables S6–S8). In all these analyses, each unit increase in ln CALLY remained significantly associated with reduced all-cause and cardiovascular mortality (all p < 0.001), with consistent inverse trends across quartiles (all p for trend < 0.001). Further adjustment for antihypertensive medication use, blood pressure control, eGFR, physical activity, and arthritis (Model 4) also yielded results consistent with the primary analysis (Q4 vs. Q1 for all-cause mortality: HR = 0.62, 95% CI: 0.55–0.70; p < 0.001; for cardiovascular mortality: HR = 0.63, 95% CI: 0.50–0.79; p < 0.001) (Table S9). Similarly, multiple imputation for missing covariate data produced results consistent with the complete-case analysis (Table S10).

Discussion

This study provides the evidence of a significant, inverse, and independent association between the CALLY index and ACM/CVM risks among 9,199 U.S. hypertensive adults. These associations remained robust across stratified and sensitivity analyses, with RCS revealing a distinctive L-shaped dose-response curve for ACM and a predominantly linear association for CVM. The CALLY index integrates CRP, albumin, and lymphocyte count, and aligns closely with hypertension pathophysiology. Elevated CRP reflects chronic inflammation, leading to endothelial dysfunction and vascular remodeling.37,38 Hypoalbuminemia, beyond a marker of nutrition, is increasingly recognized as a sign of persistent systemic inflammation and is frequently comorbid with hypertension.39–43

Serum albumin, a key component of the CALLY index, plays a dual biological role that underpins its prognostic value. Beyond serving as a conventional marker of nutritional status, albumin functions as a critical negative acute-phase reactant and exerts antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and vascular regulatory effects. 44 During systemic inflammation, pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α redirect hepatic protein synthesis away from albumin toward positive acute-phase proteins like CRP, leading to a decline in serum albumin levels that reflects both inflammatory burden and nutritional depletion.39,40,43 This duality makes albumin a unique biomarker that captures both metabolic reserves and chronic inflammatory burden. Accordingly, composite indices that integrate albumin with inflammatory markers-such as the C-reactive protein-to-albumin ratio (CAR) or the CALLY index-offer a more comprehensive assessment of a patient’s pathophysiological status than isolated parameters, effectively bridging nutritional status and systemic inflammation.44,45

Lymphopenia signifies immune dysregulation, which plays a key role in vascular resistance development. 46 Each of these components has been independently linked to adverse CVD outcomes.47–51 The causes of hypertension are diverse, including chronic inflammation, immune disorders, and nutritional imbalances, among others. A comprehensive biomarker that can cover these interrelated pathways may have superior prognostic value compared to a single indicator. Thus, the CALLY index links the onset and progression of hypertension to systemic inflammation-nutrition-immune imbalance, serving as an effective comprehensive prognostic biomarker.

We observed distinct dose-response patterns: an L-shaped association for ACM and a linear relationship for CVM. This difference most likely reflects the pathophysiological characteristic of the different stages. For CVM, the linear association suggests CALLY captures a continuous, modifiable risk gradient for vascular events. However, for ACM, the L-shaped pattern indicates a threshold effect: beyond a certain CALLY level, further increases confer limited additional benefit, as competing non-cardiovascular risks (e.g., frailty, infection) may dominate. This contrasts with the uniform nonlinearity observed in advanced CVD, where severe systemic dysregulation affects all outcomes. CALLY may shift from a continuous vascular risk indicator for primary prevention to a threshold indicator for systemic frailty in secondary prevention.

Mounting evidence supports CALLY’s prognostic utility across established CVD. Han’s NHANES-based CVD cohort and Wen et al.’ expanded NHANES sample (2001-2018) both demonstrated CALLY’s L-shaped nonlinear association with mortality. 24 Real-world studies have further confirmed its predictive value in severe or acute stages of CVD, including ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction, acute coronary syndrome, and heart failure.50–53 In a recent study of patients with heart failure, Candemir et al. demonstrated that a lower CALLY index was significantly associated with prolonged hospital stay, further supporting the prognostic relevance of CALLY in this high-risk population. 54 The present findings extend this evidence in two important ways. First, while previous studies focused on patients with established CVD, we demonstrate that CALLY’s prognostic value also applies to the broader hypertensive population, who are at an earlier stage of the cardiovascular risk continuum. Second, we report a novel sex-specific pattern: although multiplicative interaction was not significant, additive interaction revealed that females had a greater absolute risk reduction associated with higher CALLY levels. To our knowledge, this observation has not been previously described in the CALLY literature and may have implications for sex-informed risk assessment in hypertension.

A key finding from our subgroup and interaction analyses is the sex-specific pattern of the CALLY-mortality association. Subgroup analyses showed numerically stronger inverse associations in males than females for both ACM (HR: 0.82 vs. 0.87) and CVM (HR: 0.79 vs. 0.90, non-significant in females). Additive interaction analyses revealed a significant absolute-scale association for ACM in females (RERI = 0.14, 95% CI: 0.05–0.24; AP = 0.26, 95% CI: 0.08–0.44), but not for CVM. This discrepancy between relative risk reduction (numerically stronger in males) and additive interaction (female association) is not uncommon, as HR addresses multiplicative scales while RERI quantifies additive effects. Collectively, females showed a significant additional absolute risk reduction associated with higher CALLY for ACM-a pattern masked when only relative measures are considered. Biologically, it has been hypothesized that in males, low CALLY may synergize with androgen-influenced pro-inflammatory states, potentially exacerbating vascular injury (Th1/Th17 polarization). 55 In females, estrogen-mediated pathways (PI3K/Akt/eNOS, ACE2/Ang-(1-7)/MasR) 56 and greater regulatory T cell activity, 55 may promote immune homeostasis, potentially explaining the sex-specific patterns observed. Our sex-specific finding is corroborated by Bafei et al., who reported a stronger hs-CRP-mortality association in women (HR = 1.40) than in men (HR = 1.28). 57 However, as these mechanisms were not directly measured in our dataset, these interpretations remain speculative and warrant further investigation.

The RERI of 0.14 indicates that the excess risk reduction attributable to the interaction between female sex and higher CALLY accounts for approximately 14% of the combined protective effect. This suggests that the absolute risk reduction associated with higher CALLY levels is more pronounced in females, although the magnitude is modest and should be interpreted within the context of an observational study. Exploratory subgroup analyses suggested potential effect modification by DM and smoking status, although interaction terms were not statistically significant (DM: p = 0.291; smoking: p > 0.05). The observed patterns warrant cautious interpretation and further investigation.

This study has several strengths. The integration of diverse analytical techniques and extended follow-up reinforce outcome robustness. Use of sampling weights enhances national representativeness to the U.S. population. Results remained stable after rigorous adjustment for multiple sociodemographic and clinical covariates. Also, several limitations warrant acknowledgment. First, single baseline CALLY assessment precludes longitudinal trend analysis. Second, NHANES lacks granular hypertension staging data, limiting evaluation across severity strata. Third, potential selection bias may arise from excluding individuals with missing key variables. Fourth, despite additional adjustment for antihypertensive medication use, blood pressure control, eGFR, physical activity, and arthritis in sensitivity analyses, residual confounding cannot be fully excluded. Specifically, NHANES lacks data on medication adherence, inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-6), and comprehensive inflammatory disorders other than arthritis, which may remain as unmeasured confounders. Fifth, as conclusions stem solely from U.S. hypertensive cohorts, cross-national validation is warranted. Finally, although we excluded participants with CRP > 10 mg/L in a sensitivity analysis to address transient inflammation, residual confounding from unmeasured acute inflammatory states cannot be completely ruled out, as CRP is a single-time measurement and may not fully capture chronic inflammatory burden.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study extends the prognostic value of the CALLY index from established cardiovascular disease to the broader hypertensive population. As a composite marker of inflammation, nutrition, and immunity, CALLY may facilitate early risk stratification. Notably, additive interaction analysis revealed that the inverse association between CALLY and all-cause mortality was more pronounced in males in terms of relative effect, whereas females exhibited a greater absolute risk reduction, though multiplicative interaction was not significant. These findings highlight the potential of incorporating CALLY into sex-specific risk assessment. However, given the observational design, these results should be considered hypothesis-generating and do not support therapeutic recommendations. Prospective studies are warranted to externally validate these findings.
