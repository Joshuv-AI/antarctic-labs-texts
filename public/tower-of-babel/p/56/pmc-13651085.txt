Post-ERCP adverse events in children: a systematic review and meta-analysis of post-ERCP pancreatitis incidence with synthesis of risk factors and prevention strategies
Authors: Xin Wang, Ruiying Li, Wenhai Li, Peng Huang, Hu Yang, Hongxi Guo, Hua Liu
Published: 2026

Background

Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) has been used clinically since 1968 and is now a minimally invasive option for selected pancreatobiliary diseases [1]. In adults, it is widely used for common bile duct stones, biliary strictures, and pancreatitis. In children, ERCP is performed less often and requires attention to smaller anatomy, equipment constraints, and anesthesia risk [2–4]. Improvements in accessories and pediatric endoscopy expertise have expanded its use for choledocholithiasis, chronic pancreatitis, and post-liver transplant biliary complications [5–8].

Pediatric ERCP indications differ from adults and vary across age groups [4, 9]. For instance, choledocholithiasis is common in Western populations, whereas choledochal cysts are more prevalent in Asia [10]. ERCP may also be considered as an adjunct in selected diagnostic dilemmas such as neonatal cholestasis or suspected biliary atresia when non-invasive imaging is inconclusive, although its role must be balanced against procedural risk. Despite high technical success and clinical efficacy in children [11, 12], post-procedural complications remain an important clinical concern. The most common complications are post-ERCP pancreatitis (PEP), bleeding, perforation, and infection [1]. These can prolong hospital stays, increase costs, and, in severe cases, threaten a child’s life [13].

Previous research suggests that post-ERCP complication rates in children are broadly comparable to those in adults, but pediatric anatomy, indications, sedation requirements, and case volume create a distinct risk context. Compared with prior reviews and meta-analyses [4, 5, 13, 14], the present review updates the evidence through June 2025, separates primary-study data from prior pooled estimates, avoids double counting overlapping cohorts, and restricts quantitative synthesis to outcomes with extractable non-overlapping data.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis was reported in accordance with PRISMA 2020 [15]. The protocol was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251084264), in accordance with best practice guidelines to ensure transparent reporting and reduce reporting bias.

Search strategy

We performed a systematic review and meta-analysis to identify, pool, and stratify reported post-ERCP adverse events in pediatric patients. Two reviewers independently searched PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library for literature on pediatric ERCP complications. Any discrepancies in study selection were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer. The search strategy combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and free-text keywords using Boolean operators (AND, OR). Keywords included: “children”, “pediatric”, “ERCP”, “endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography”, “complications”, “adverse event”, “pancreatitis”, “bleeding”, “perforation”, and “infection”.

The search was limited to studies published between January 1, 2015, and June 26, 2025, to capture contemporary pediatric ERCP practice, including changes in therapeutic indications, endoscopic accessories, anesthesia support, and prophylaxis strategies. Earlier evidence was considered through prior reviews only for contextual comparison. Reference lists of eligible studies and relevant reviews were manually checked to identify additional contemporary primary studies.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies were selected using predefined criteria:

Inclusion Criteria:

Study Type: Original pediatric studies, including cohort studies, case-control studies, and randomized or non-randomized interventional studies. Prior systematic reviews and meta-analyses were screened for contextual comparison and citation tracking only. Their pooled results were not entered into the present quantitative synthesis.

Population: Pediatric patients (< 18 years) undergoing ERCP.

Outcomes: Reported incidence, types, risk factors, or management strategies of post-ERCP complications.

Language: English.

Publication Date: January 1, 2015 to June 26, 2025.

Exclusion Criteria:

Population: Adult patients (≥ 18 years).

Intervention: Research unrelated to ERCP complications.

Study Design: Case reports, narrative reviews, editorials, conference abstracts, animal studies, in vitro studies.

Data Availability: Incomplete or unextractable data.

Duplication: Repeated publications.

Data extraction

Data extraction was independently conducted by two reviewers using a standardized form. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer. For each primary study, we collected first author, year, country, study design, sample size, number of ERCP procedures, mean or median age, ERCP indications, procedure type, follow-up duration, adverse-event definitions or reporting windows, event numerators and denominators for PEP, bleeding, perforation, cholangitis or infection, severity classification, risk factors, and management strategies. For PEP, we recorded whether the denominator represented ERCP procedures, patients, first ERCP per child, or a mixed/unclear reporting unit. We retained the denominator unit used by the source rather than converting procedure-level data to patient-level data when repeat procedures per child were not consistently reported. For prior systematic reviews and meta-analyses, we extracted scope, search period, included study types, pooled estimates, and overlap with primary studies to avoid double counting.

Quality assessment

The methodological quality and risk of bias of each eligible source were independently assessed by two reviewers using tools appropriate to study design. Disagreements were resolved by consensus, with a third reviewer consulted when needed.

Non-randomized primary studies were assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS), randomized trials using RoB 2, and prior systematic reviews or meta-analyses using AMSTAR 2 [16–18]. Rather than reproducing checklist content, we summarized each study’s overall judgment and used these judgments to grade confidence across evidence domains.

Risk-of-bias judgments informed the narrative synthesis. Studies with incomplete adverse-event definitions, unclear denominators, overlapping cohorts, or high risk of selection or reporting bias were interpreted cautiously and were not used to support quantitative claims beyond their reported data.

Statistical analysis

Meta-analysis was conducted only for outcomes reported by at least three non-overlapping primary studies with extractable numerators and denominators. Pooled incidences were estimated using random-effects models for proportions, with variance stabilization for rare events where appropriate. Results were expressed as pooled proportions with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Between-study heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran Q and the I-squared statistic. Heterogeneity was interpreted as low, moderate, substantial, or considerable using conventional thresholds, while acknowledging that I-squared may be unstable in rare-event analyses. We prespecified subgroup exploration by age group, ERCP indication, procedure type, pancreatic duct manipulation or contrast injection, sphincterotomy, and operator or center volume. Formal subgroup meta-analysis was not forced when studies did not provide stratified PEP event counts, because doing so would have required ecological assumptions. These variables were therefore summarized as explanatory domains for heterogeneity and risk.

Sensitivity analyses were planned by excluding studies at high risk of bias, studies with unclear adverse-event definitions, and studies with potential cohort overlap. In response to peer review, we also added a post hoc denominator-unit sensitivity summary, separating procedure-level studies from patient-level or first-ERCP-per-child studies and retaining a mixed/unclear category when the source did not permit classification. Risk-factor effect estimates were not pooled when exposure definitions, covariate adjustment, or reporting units differed substantially. These data were summarized narratively and, where appropriate, displayed as reported estimates

Publication bias was assessed visually using funnel plots and statistically using Egger test only for outcomes with at least ten eligible studies. Funnel plots were not generated for rare outcomes with fewer than ten studies because asymmetry tests would be unreliable.

All tests were two-sided. For analyses other than Cochran's Q test for heterogeneity, P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. For Cochran's Q test, P < 0.10 was considered indicative of potential between-study heterogeneity because this test has limited power when relatively few studies are included. Heterogeneity was interpreted together with I² and τ².

AI-assisted tools were used during revision for language editing and formatting checks. The authors verified the data extraction, statistical analyses, interpretation, and final text and take full responsibility for the manuscript.

Results

This systematic review included 65 sources in the qualitative synthesis, consisting primarily of contemporary pediatric ERCP primary studies and selected prior systematic reviews or meta-analyses used for contextual benchmarking [2–14, 19–71]. Prior reviews were not treated as independent primary data and their pooled estimates were not combined with overlapping primary studies. Risk-of-bias summary

Most included primary studies were retrospective or observational, and the overall certainty of evidence was limited by heterogeneous adverse-event definitions, variable follow-up windows, incomplete reporting of denominators for specific complications, and potential overlap among multicenter pediatric ERCP cohorts. Contemporary multicenter studies generally provided stronger evidence for technical outcomes and common adverse events, whereas estimates for rare events such as perforation and cholangitis were less precise. Prior systematic reviews and meta-analyses were useful for benchmarking but were not used as data sources in the present pooled analyses.

Incidence of complications

The available evidence suggested that ERCP indication influenced complication patterns. Children undergoing ERCP for acute recurrent or chronic pancreatitis were repeatedly reported to have higher PEP risk than those undergoing ERCP for choledocholithiasis, although the magnitude of association varied by study design, adjustment strategy, and procedural case mix.

The overall incidence of post-ERCP complications in children varied across studies because of differences in patient populations, procedural indications, adverse-event definitions, and follow-up. Random-effects pooling was performed for PEP because this outcome had the most consistently extractable numerators and denominators. Figure 2 presents the study-level forest plot for PEP incidence after pediatric ERCP. Bleeding, perforation, and cholangitis were less frequent and were summarized descriptively when study-level event data were sparse or inconsistently reported.

PEP

PEP was the most frequent and clinically significant complication associated with pediatric ERCP. In the present synthesis, 15 non-overlapping primary studies contributed extractable PEP event counts, yielding a pooled incidence of 7.3% (95% CI, 5.1% to 10.4%; I-squared = 83.7%) under a random-effects model. Table 1 now reports the study-level numerator, denominator, percentage, denominator unit, and available definition or reporting note for every study contributing to the PEP synthesis. The rows most dependent on percentage back-calculation or source-unit interpretation were rechecked against the original source records for Goetz, Mercier, Perera, and Batibay [24, 60, 63, 72].

The high I-squared value indicates real between-study inconsistency rather than a single uniform pediatric PEP risk. Likely contributors include age distribution, acute recurrent or chronic pancreatitis as the indication, pancreatic duct cannulation or contrast injection, pancreatic sphincterotomy, difficult cannulation, operator and center volume, trainee participation, follow-up windows, and differences in PEP definitions. Denominator unit was another source of variation: some studies reported ERCP procedures, whereas others reported patients or first ERCP per child. Because repeat ERCPs per child were not consistently available, procedure-level data were not converted to patient-level data. In the post hoc denominator-unit summary, procedure-level studies contributed 181 PEP events among 2669 ERCPs (crude rate, 6.8%), patient-level or first-ERCP-per-child studies contributed 20 events among 421 children (4.8%), and the mixed or unclear-denominator study contributed 12 events among 232 observations (5.2%). Heterogeneity persisted after this separation, so the pooled estimate should be interpreted as a contemporary benchmark rather than a single individualized risk estimate.

Most pediatric PEP cases are mild and resolve with conservative management, whereas a minority progress to moderate or severe PEP with prolonged hospitalization, systemic inflammatory response, pancreatic necrosis, pseudocyst formation, or rarely mortality [4, 59, 73]. The Atlanta classification, originally developed for adults, is often applied in pediatric settings but has not been fully validated for pediatric post-ERCP pathophysiology [62].

Visual inspection of the PEP funnel plot suggested possible small-study effects, although Egger regression did not show clear statistical evidence of asymmetry (intercept, -1.98; approximate two-sided P = 0.16). This result should be interpreted cautiously because heterogeneity was substantial and denominators were reported as either patients or procedures across studies (Fig. 3).

Bleeding

Post-ERCP bleeding in children most commonly occurs after endoscopic sphincterotomy (EST). Clinically significant bleeding is uncommon, with an estimated incidence of approximately 0.5% [4]. Bleeding may appear during the procedure, shortly afterward, or several days later. Reported risk factors include coagulopathy, larger sphincterotomy incisions, and vascular anomalies. Most cases respond to endoscopic hemostasis with epinephrine injection or clip placement. Severe or endoscopically refractory bleeding may require angiographic embolization; surgery should be reserved for cases in which less invasive measures fail.

Perforation

ERCP-related perforation is rare but potentially life-threatening, with an estimated incidence below 1% in pediatric patients [4]. Risk factors include difficult cannulation, excessive force during guidewire or endoscope advancement, anatomical anomalies, and strictures. A practical concern is device size. Pediatric duodenoscopes have become less available in recent years, so clinicians may need to use adult-sized equipment in small children, which can increase mechanical risk. Early recognition matters: minor perforations may be treated conservatively, whereas larger defects often require surgery.

Cholangitis

Post-ERCP cholangitis is a recognized infectious complication, typically resulting from incomplete biliary drainage or procedural contamination. Although its incidence in children is less well-defined than that of PEP, it remains clinically important [63]. Risk factors include persistent biliary obstruction, incomplete stone clearance, and prior biliary surgery. Prophylactic antibiotics are commonly considered, particularly in patients with pre-existing cholangitis or immunocompromised status. Management requires prompt antibiotic therapy and restoration of biliary drainage, which may require repeat ERCP or percutaneous intervention.

Other complications

Less common complications of ERCP in children include cholecystitis, bile duct injury or stricture, and events related to sedation or anesthesia [13]. Cholecystitis may arise if the cystic duct is obstructed or manipulated during the procedure. Ductal injury is rare but can lead to later stricture formation. Sedation- and anesthesia-related complications are especially relevant in children because of smaller airway size, higher metabolic demand, and age-specific responses to anesthetic agents. Close monitoring by pediatric anesthesia teams is therefore important for procedural safety [64].

The evidence points to a broad but unevenly reported risk profile for pediatric ERCP. Overall complication rates are usually low, but serious events such as PEP, bleeding, perforation, and cholangitis require close attention. Differences in definitions, follow-up, and reporting explain much of the variability across studies. Better pediatric definitions and prospective datasets are needed before risk estimates for rare outcomes can be pooled with confidence.

Risk factors for complications

Operator background also influenced complication rates. Pediatric series and adult operator-volume data [66, 68, 69] suggest that procedures performed by less experienced operators or outside specialized pediatric endoscopy settings may carry higher procedural risk than procedures performed by experienced pediatric gastroenterologists. These findings support specialized training and referral pathways for complex pediatric ERCP.

Identifying risk factors for post-ERCP complications in children helps guide patient selection, pre-procedural optimization, and intra-procedural risk mitigation. Across the literature, both patient characteristics and procedural variables were associated with adverse events.

Patient-related risk factors

Age: Younger age, particularly infancy and early childhood, has been reported as a possible risk factor for PEP in several pediatric cohorts [65]. Smaller ductal anatomy, pancreatic reactivity, and age-specific inflammatory responses may contribute. However, some multivariable analyses suggest that age may not remain an independent predictor after adjustment for indication and procedural factors. Fig. 5 illustrates the reported age pattern, but the finding should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive.

Age-stratified incidence of PEP in pediatric patients. This bar graph illustrates the estimated incidence of PEP across pediatric age groups and should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive

History of Pancreatitis: A prior diagnosis of acute recurrent pancreatitis or chronic pancreatitis is strongly associated with an elevated risk of PEP in children undergoing ERCP [65]. This increased susceptibility likely reflects underlying pancreatic ductal distortion, ongoing low-grade inflammation, and fibrosis, all of which may amplify the pancreas’s vulnerability to procedural trauma.

Sphincter of Oddi Dysfunction (SOD): Although SOD is more commonly recognized in adults, pediatric patients with suspected or confirmed SOD appear to face a greater risk of PEP. This may stem from impaired pancreatic outflow dynamics and elevated ductal pressures, which exacerbate the likelihood of post-procedural inflammation [65, 66].

Genetic Predisposition: Variants linked to hereditary pancreatitis, including PRSS1 and SPINK1, may increase baseline susceptibility to pancreatic injury [67]. Their relevance to post-ERCP risk remains uncertain, but their presence may help identify children who require closer procedural planning and follow-up.

Procedure-related risk factors

Pancreatic Duct Cannulation and Sphincterotomy: Pancreatic duct instrumentation, including deep cannulation, repeated cannulation attempts, and pancreatic sphincterotomy, has been associated with higher PEP risk in pediatric cohorts [65]. These findings parallel adult multicenter risk-factor data and support minimizing pancreatic manipulation unless it is clinically necessary [74, 75]. Selective biliary cannulation remains an important risk-reduction strategy. Precut Papillotomy: Precut papillotomy, often reserved for cases of difficult biliary access, carries an increased risk of both PEP and duodenal perforation due to greater mechanical trauma to the ampullary complex [68]. Although it can facilitate access in anatomically challenging cases, its use in children should be judicious and ideally limited to experienced operators. Pancreatic Stent Placement: While prophylactic pancreatic stent placement has demonstrated efficacy in reducing PEP in high-risk adult populations, its role in pediatric ERCP remains less well-defined. Improperly selected or malpositioned stents may paradoxically increase the risk of ductal injury, stent-induced pancreatitis, or migration-related complications [65]. Pediatric-specific evidence is limited, and further work is needed to define appropriate stent design and placement protocols in children.

Operator Experience: Lower operator or center volume and trainee involvement may contribute to adverse events, particularly during difficult cannulation or pancreatic duct manipulation. The available pediatric studies do not provide enough stratified event counts for formal pooling by operator experience, but the recurring association supports referral of complex pediatric ERCP to experienced teams and high-volume centers [76].

Procedure Complexity and Duration: Complex procedures, including those involving multiple therapeutic steps, altered anatomy, or prolonged duration, are associated with higher adverse-event risk [68]. These factors probably reflect both the difficulty of the underlying case and cumulative tissue trauma.

Where sufficiently comparable adjusted estimates were available, risk-factor associations were extracted. However, heterogeneity in exposure definitions and covariate adjustment generally precluded formal pooling. Management and prevention strategies

Improving pediatric ERCP safety requires attention before, during, and after the procedure. Practical countermeasures include risk stratification, avoidance of unnecessary pancreatic duct trauma, selective rather than routine prophylaxis, child-sized stent selection when stenting is used, careful NSAID eligibility assessment, and structured post-procedural monitoring. Prevention and management strategies for post-ERCP complications in children

Prevention of PEP

Prevention of PEP remains a central objective in pediatric ERCP practice. Strategies adapted from adult guideline recommendations and limited pediatric data have potential value, but pediatric use requires attention to anatomy, body size, renal function, bleeding risk, and local expertise [71, 77].

Patient Selection: Careful pre-procedural evaluation is essential. The decision to proceed with ERCP should be based on risk-benefit assessment. Children with recurrent pancreatitis or anticipated complex pancreatic interventions should be categorized as high risk and managed accordingly.

Prophylactic Pancreatic Stent Placement: Prophylactic pancreatic stenting is widely used in high-risk adult ERCP and may reduce PEP risk in selected pediatric cases, particularly after pancreatic sphincterotomy or difficult pancreatic duct cannulation [65, 70, 77]. In children, the decision is not simply whether to place a stent, but whether the duct size, papillary anatomy, and operator experience allow safe placement. Stent caliber, length, flange design, and planned dwell time should be matched to the child and the pancreatic duct; adult-sized assumptions may increase ductal trauma, migration, or stent-induced pancreatitis. Current pediatric data are insufficient to recommend one universal stent protocol.

Nonsteroidal Anti-inflammatory Drugs (NSAIDs): Rectal indomethacin is established for PEP prophylaxis in adults, but pediatric evidence remains limited and should not be treated as a direct adult-to-child transfer [78, 79]. Pediatric use should account for age, body weight, renal function, hydration status, bleeding risk, allergy history, and institutional protocols. The available pediatric feasibility work, including intravenous ibuprofen, supports further study but does not define a universal dose or timing strategy for all children [71].

Aggressive Intravenous Hydration: Periprocedural hydration with lactated Ringer solution reduces PEP risk in adults [80]. In children, this approach requires careful fluid monitoring to avoid overload, especially in younger patients or those with comorbid disease.

Minimizing Pancreatic Duct Manipulation: Limiting unnecessary pancreatic duct instrumentation is critical. Strategies such as reducing the number of cannulation attempts, avoiding deep cannulation when not clinically indicated, and using wire-guided cannulation techniques have been associated with lower PEP rates by minimizing ductal trauma [68].

Operator Experience: Endoscopist experience is a consistent determinant of procedural safety. ERCPs performed in high-volume centers by experienced operators are associated with lower PEP and overall complication rates [76]. When trainees participate, supervision should include predefined limits for cannulation attempts, early senior-operator takeover during difficult access, and avoidance of unnecessary pancreatic duct injection. Management of Post-ERCP complications

The management of ERCP-related complications in children is guided by the nature and severity of each event:

PEP: Mild to moderate cases are typically managed conservatively with bowel rest when clinically indicated, intravenous fluids, analgesia, and antiemetics. Early enteral nutrition should be considered as recovery permits. In severe PEP, additional interventions may include antibiotics for infected necrosis, image-guided percutaneous or endoscopic drainage of fluid collections, and, rarely, surgical debridement [81]. Bleeding: Most episodes of post-ERCP bleeding are self-limiting. Clinically significant hemorrhage requires prompt endoscopic treatment, including epinephrine injection, hemostatic clip placement, or thermal coagulation [82]. Blood transfusion may be necessary in patients with hemodynamic instability. Refractory bleeding may require angiographic embolization or surgical control [83].

Perforation: Management depends on perforation type, location, and clinical impact. Minor retroperitoneal perforations, often detected by retroperitoneal air on imaging, may be managed with bowel rest, intravenous fluids, antibiotics, and close observation [84]. Selected defects may be closed endoscopically with clips or stents. Large perforations, especially those with free intraperitoneal air or peritonitis, usually require urgent surgical repair [85].

Cholangitis: Post-ERCP cholangitis requires immediate broad-spectrum empirical antibiotics targeting common biliary pathogens. Definitive management necessitates biliary drainage restoration, preferably via endoscopic stenting or stone extraction; surgical drainage should be considered when endoscopic intervention fails or is contraindicated. Timely management is essential to prevent sepsis and other severe complications [86, 87].

Discussion

Our analysis also identified features associated with severe complications requiring surgical intervention, including prior pancreatitis, prolonged procedure duration, and repeated pancreatic duct cannulation attempts. Children differ from adults not only in anatomy and duct size but also in how complications may present. Children with low body weight may deteriorate faster from fluid shifts or infection, whereas adult risk profiles are more often shaped by cardiovascular comorbidity. Pediatric ERCP therefore requires age-specific risk assessment rather than direct extrapolation from adult practice.

This systematic review and meta-analysis synthesizes contemporary evidence on post-ERCP complications in children. Prior reviews were used for context, while original pediatric studies served as the primary evidence base. The findings support ERCP as an important diagnostic and therapeutic tool in pediatric pancreatobiliary disease, but they also show that PEP remains the main adverse event requiring prevention, early recognition, and standardized reporting. Consistent with prior reports, PEP was the most commonly reported adverse event. The present pooled estimate was 7.3% (95% CI, 5.1% to 10.4%), with substantial between-study heterogeneity (I-squared = 83.7%). Table 4 compares this estimate with prior pediatric ERCP meta-analyses. Usatin 2016 and Hosseini 2023 reported lower PEP estimates of approximately 3% to 4%, whereas Sun 2022 provided an overall therapeutic ERCP adverse-event benchmark rather than a uniformly extractable PEP-only estimate [4, 5, 13]. The higher estimate in the present review is likely related to the contemporary search window, explicit restriction to non-overlapping primary studies with extractable PEP numerators and denominators, high-risk therapeutic indications, pancreatic duct manipulation, and retention of the source-specific denominator unit. Standardized adverse-event reporting using established classifications (e.g., Cotton criteria [88]; revised Atlanta criteria) and pediatric-specific definitions is needed for valid cross-study comparisons.

Although PEP was the most commonly reported adverse event, less frequent complications such as bleeding and perforation also require attention. Bleeding was generally uncommon (approximately 0.5%) and was most often associated with endoscopic sphincterotomy. Perforation was rare, generally reported below 1%, but its severity justifies careful technique, especially in children with smaller and more delicate duodenal and biliary anatomy.

Risk reduction depends on both patient selection and procedural technique. Younger age, prior pancreatitis, and selected genetic predispositions were recurring patient-level risk factors. Procedural factors such as pancreatic duct manipulation (including deep cannulation and pancreatic sphincterotomy), precut papillotomy, and lower operator experience were also associated with higher risk. Individual risk stratification and referral to high-volume pediatric endoscopy centers may reduce avoidable harm [66].

Preventive strategies remain central to reducing post-ERCP morbidity, but pediatric practice requires more detail than simply importing adult ERCP protocols. For PEP, the most actionable measures are careful indication selection, minimizing pancreatic duct manipulation and contrast injection, limiting cannulation attempts, early senior-operator involvement, and selective prophylactic pancreatic stenting when anatomy and expertise make it safe. Stent size and length should be adapted to the child and duct, and NSAID prophylaxis should be individualized by age, weight, renal function, bleeding risk, and local pediatric protocols. For bleeding, perforation, and cholangitis, countermeasures include correction of coagulation abnormalities, careful sphincterotomy, early recognition of perforation, complete biliary drainage, and selective antibiotics when drainage is incomplete or infection risk is high.

Selective prophylactic pancreatic stenting may benefit high-risk pediatric cases, but the evidence remains limited. Intravenous hydration may also help when fluid status can be monitored closely [65, 80].

This study has several strengths: a contemporary search strategy, explicit separation of primary studies from prior reviews, rechecking of percentage-derived PEP rows, and a meta-analytic approach restricted to outcomes with extractable non-overlapping data. Limitations remain. Heterogeneity in indications, adverse-event definitions, follow-up windows, and reporting units limited comparison across studies and prevented pooling of several risk-factor and prevention outcomes. Some studies used procedure-level denominators whereas others used patient-level or first-ERCP-per-child denominators; because repeat procedures per child were not consistently reported, these could not be fully harmonized. Selective reporting, incomplete stratified event counts, and overlap among multicenter cohorts may also affect published estimates.

Future research should prioritize prospective multicenter pediatric ERCP datasets with standardized adverse-event definitions and consistent reporting of numerators, denominators, follow-up windows, and severity. Pediatric trials or well-designed registries are also needed for prophylactic agents, pancreatic stenting strategies, and child-adapted ERCP equipment.

Conclusions

Pediatric ERCP is effective, but adverse events, especially PEP, remain clinically important. A focused meta-analysis of extractable PEP data provides a transparent incidence estimate, while the broader evidence synthesis shows where pediatric data are still thin. Risk reduction in pediatric ERCP will depend on careful patient selection, experienced operators, standardized reporting, and prospective pediatric evidence for prophylaxis.
