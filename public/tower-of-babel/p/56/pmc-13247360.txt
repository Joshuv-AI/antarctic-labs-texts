ANTICIPATE-ing a future without liver biopsy in MASH. Ready for prime time?
Authors: Harendran Elangovan, Jacob George
Published: 2026

Aceituno, L., Banares, J., Pons, M., et al. The ANTICIPATE-NASH Models Stratify Better the Risk of Clinical Events Than Histology in Metabolic Dysfunction-Associated Steatotic Liver Disease Patients With Advanced Chronic Liver Disease. Gastroenterology 170, 385–394 (2026). 10.1053/j.gastro.2025.08.020

With global prevalence estimates exceeding 30%, steatotic liver diseases represent the face of modern clinical hepatology, fomented by an environment that fosters poor nutritional choices and sedentary lifestyles.1 This public health scourge demands strategies focused on the early detection of liver disease, prognostication, and, importantly, disease modification to limit the morbidity and mortality associated with cirrhosis, organ failure, transplantation and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). Historically, liver outcome prediction required liver biopsy and hepatic venous pressure gradient (HVPG) assessment to determine clinically significant portal hypertension (CSPH). However, these techniques are invasive, costly, not readily repeatable, and not applicable at the population level for outcome prediction. Conventional liver biopsy also suffers from reduced sensitivity and reproducibility related to quality control from staining and sampling error, reliance on the inherent subjectivity of pathologist interpretation, interobserver variability, and ordinal reporting standards that can lead to misclassification (Fig. 1).

ANTICIPATE-NASH (non-alcoholic steatohepatitis), a composite scoring system incorporating BMI, platelet count, and liver stiffness assessment by vibration controlled transient elastography (VCTE), has been proposed as a simple, effective and pragmatic alternative that may predict the risk of CSPH and adverse liver outcomes in metabolic liver disease.2 This is reflected in the Baveno VII recommendations.3 In a recent issue of Gastroenterology, Aceituno et al.4 extend the evidence base for this tool, externally validating the superiority of the ANTICIPATE-NASH model over conventional liver biopsy assessment for prediction of adverse liver outcomes in patients with advanced fibrosis (F3 and F4). Impressively, the model was able to nuance risk prediction in a manner that conventional ordinal scales of fibrosis staging could not, identifying subsets of F3 patients with more aggressive risk profiles like those of F4 patients, and vice versa, downstaging some F4 classified patients to more indolent phenotypes. Whilst the analyses are retrospective, the results are nonetheless important and suggest that a simple, non-invasive tool can replace liver biopsy for adverse liver outcome prediction for a highly prevalent disease.

The clinical implications stemming from ANTICIPATE-NASH reflect an underlying philosophical shift towards effective, point of care and, principally, safer tools for risk appraisal in hepatology. While elastography represents a step in the right direction, its availability may be constrained by operator proficiency, patient obesity, and budgetary and geographic factors across health systems. From a population policy perspective, risk appraisal in metabolic liver disease should ideally be blood based, which eliminates the need for sonographic equipment and the operational expertise required for effective utilisation. In this regard, benchmarking ANTICIPATE-NASH performance against validated serum tools for advanced fibrosis such as ELF5 and ADAPT,6 which run on existing widely available diagnostic platforms, would be useful.

As a risk prediction tool for liver-related adverse outcomes, the role of ANTICIPATE-NASH scores in guiding the use of non-selective beta-blockers, endoscopic surveillance, referral for advanced portal hypertension management (e.g. transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt [TIPS] and/or transplant evaluation), and HCC screening pathways is now ripe for further evaluation.

Liver fat accumulation related to metabolic dysfunction is the hepatic manifestation of an underlying failure of systemic metabolic homeostasis.9 Accordingly, it is equally important for hepatologists and other physicians to determine how the ANTICIPATE-NASH score reflects extrahepatic organ risk (particularly cardiovascular disease and cancer) to support holistic patient-centred care.

With an emphasis on anthropometric assessment (i.e. BMI) in risk calculation, questions around whether complications in the lean phenotype, with its predilection towards maladaptive visceral fat dystrophy over excess total body adiposity, are adequately captured would also require clarification in future analyses. In a similar vein, incretin analogues (including GLP-1 receptor agonists, dual and triple agonists, and their combinations) are now ubiquitous in clinical practice, and further assessment is needed to understand how their bariatric and body recomposition effects influence the risk prediction characteristics of the ANTICIPATE-NASH score. This is a pertinent consideration given that early evidence suggests that these drugs may not be as effective in reversing fibrosis and, presumably, portal hypertensive complications once cirrhosis is established.7 Results from the phase II ESSENCE trial and the ongoing LIVERAGE-Cirrhosis trial, which assess the impact of GLP-1-based therapies on liver outcomes, may provide further insight.

Critically, as the authors demonstrate, the addition of histologic appraisal did not enhance the performance characteristics of the ANTICIPATE-NASH algorithm in predicting adverse liver outcomes. Rather than an indictment of histology per se, this likely reflects the limitations of conventional ordinal liver biopsy scoring systems which lack the sensitivity necessary to capture the nuances of biology. Moreover, liver biopsy sampling error can also significantly impact the accuracy of results. The field of liver histopathology is undergoing rapid transformation with the advent of digital pathology and artificial intelligence-augmented tissue assessment, including evaluation of cellular and zonal architecture, and second harmonic generation and/or two-photon excitation fluorescence microscopy, enabling quantitative assessment of liver fibrosis.8 Such innovations support reporting on reproducible continuous scales and may provide novel insights into the histology of the inflamed, steatotic liver. How these evolving histological reporting standards translate to outcome prediction, and how they complement or benchmark against HVPG assessment and non-invasive testing strategies, will help define the role of liver biopsy within the hierarchy of fatty liver disease workflows.
