Engineering plant architecture in the ornamental species Eustoma grandiflorum by knockout of strigolactone biosynthesis
Authors: Kana Yamaguchi, Eri Tomizawa, Noriko Kanematsu, Kimitoshi Sakaguchi, Takashi Kasai, Ryo Ebisawa, Akihiro Hasumi, Kyoko Kawakatsu, Yuriko Osakabe, Keishi Osakabe, Takashi Nakatsuka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key messageThe targeted mutation of the strigolactone biosynthetic gene, CCD8, through genome-editing CRISPR–Cas9 induces dwarfism and enhanced branching in Eustoma grandiflorum.AbstractIn ornamental plants, modifying shoot architecture is a major breeding objective for diverse applications; however, achieving this goal using conventional techniques remains challenging. Strigolactones are plant hormones that inhibit shoot branching. We aimed to generate a strigolactone-deficient mutant of Eustoma grandiflorum using CRISPR–Cas9 genome editing. Four guide RNAs targeting CAROTENOID CLEAVAGE DIOXYGENASE 8 (EgCCD8) were designed, and transgenic E. grandiflorum plants were produced. Three independent lines carried deletions or insertions in all EgCCD8 alleles, indicating high editing efficiency. Two lines exhibited increased branching and pronounced dwarfism. In one of these lines, plant height in T1 null segregants was reduced to 47% of wild-type (WT) levels. Branch number increased 5.0-fold compared with the WT, and flower bud formation increased 1.7-fold alongside enhanced branching. These findings demonstrate that the targeted mutation of CCD8 through genome editing induces dwarfism and enhanced branching without compromising other ornamental traits.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00299-026-03867-w.

Introduction

Ornamental plants are valued for their diverse flower colors and morphologies. Floricultural products for customers are categorized as cut flowers, potted flowers, and bench flowers. Flower use largely depends on inherent species traits. For example, roses, carnations, lilies, chrysanthemums, and gerberas are primarily cultivated as cut flowers; cyclamens and orchids are cultivated as potted plants; and pansies and petunias are cultivated as bench flowers. Lisianthus (Eustoma grandiflorum (Raf.) Shinn.), a member of the Gentianaceae, is cultivated worldwide and has substantial commercial value as a cut flower. In recent decades, commercial breeding has produced numerous E. grandiflorum cultivars with diverse colors and increased corolla lobe numbers. The molecular mechanisms underlying flower color, floral morphology, and flowering in E. grandiflorum have been well characterized (Ishimori and Kawabata 2014; Nakano et al. 2011; Noda et al. 2004; Shimizu et al. 2011; Tomizawa et al. 2021). In addition, a genetic linkage map and whole-genome sequences of E. grandiflorum have been established (Kawakatsu et al. 2021; Liang et al. 2022; Shirasawa et al. 2023).

Shoot architecture is a critical trait in ornamental plants and is influenced by both genetic and environmental factors. Architectural variation arises from differences in plant height and in the number, length, angle, and position of branches (Barbier et al. 2019; Wang et al. 2018). Shoot architecture is regulated by several plant hormones, including gibberellins (GAs), auxins, cytokinins, and strigolactones (Luo et al. 2021; Wang et al. 2018). Maintenance and differentiation of the shoot apical meristem involve coordinated interactions among auxin, cytokinins, and peptide hormones. Modification of shoot architecture has also been a key determinant of yield in cereal crops (Barbier et al. 2019). For example, the success of the Green Revolution was partly driven by the development of high-yielding cultivars of rice (Oryza sativa) and wheat (Triticum aestivum) through the introduction of dwarf/semidwarf alleles such as sd1 (Khush 2001).

Strigolactones are carotenoid-derived plant metabolites that function as signaling molecules both as phytohormones and as exuded compounds in the rhizosphere (Waldie et al. 2014). Mutants defective in strigolactone biosynthesis or signaling exhibit increased shoot branching, including more axillary growth (max) in Arabidopsis thaliana (Sorefan et al. 2003), decreased apical dominance (dad) in Petunia hybrida (Napoli 1996; Snowden et al. 2005), ramosus (rms) in Pisum sativum (Beveridge et al. 1997; Foo et al. 2001), and dwarf (d), and high-tillering dwarf in Oryza sativa (Arite et al. 2007; Ishikawa et al. 2005; Zou et al. 2006). Strigolactone biosynthesis begins with the conversion of all-trans-β-carotene to 9-cis-β-carotene by DWARF27. CAROTENOID CLEAVAGE DIOXYGENASE 7 (CCD7) subsequently converts 9-cis-β-carotene to 9-cis-β-apo-10′-carotenal, which is further processed by CAROTENOID CLEAVAGE DIOXYGENASE 8 (CCD8). The CCD8-catalyzed step forms the methylbutenolide moiety, a structural feature essential for inhibiting axillary bud outgrowth (Dun et al. 2023). In Arabidopsis, D14 functions as a strigolactone receptor, and methyl carlactonoate has been proposed as the active compound mediating shoot branching inhibition (Abe et al. 2014).

CRISPR–Cas9 has emerged as a precise genome-editing tool in plants. The Cas9 endonuclease is directed to a specific genomic locus by a 20-bp single-guide RNA, resulting in double-strand DNA cleavage followed by nonhomologous end joining (NHEJ)-mediated repair. Genome editing has been applied to modify flower color, floral morphology, flowering time, and vase life in chrysanthemum (Chrysanthemum × morifolium, Kishi-Kaboshi et al. 2017), Japanese morning glory (Ipomoea nil, Shibuya et al. 2018; Watanabe et al. 2017; Watanabe et al. 2018), Japanese gentian (Gentiana triflora × G. scabra, Nishihara et al. 2023; Takahashi et al. 2022; Tasaki et al. 2019; Tasaki et al. 2020), torenia (Torenia hybrida, Nishihara et al. 2018; Sasaki and Ohtsubo 2020; Su et al. 2017), and petunia (Petunia hybrida, Abdulla et al. 2024). However, few studies have reported the application of genome editing to improve shoot architecture in ornamental plants.

In this study, CCD8 knockout lines of E. grandiflorum were generated using CRISPR–Cas9, and the growth and development of their T1 progeny were evaluated. The results demonstrate that genome editing-induced strigolactone deficiency has practical potential to modify plant architecture, thereby expanding the utility of ornamental species from cut flower production to bedding and potted applications.

Materials and methods

Isolation of CCD8 from E. grandiflorum

Whole-genome sequences of E. grandiflorum were not available at the start of this study. Therefore, the sequences of CCD8 and CCD7 were obtained by degenerated PCR. Total RNA was isolated from seedlings of E. grandiflorum cv. Light Pink Thumb (Fukukaen Nursery and Bulb Co. Ltd., Nagoya, Japan) 1.5 months after sowing using RNAiso reagent (Takara Bio, Shiga, Japan) and Fruit-mate (Takara Bio). cDNA was synthesized using an RNA PCR kit (AMV) Ver. 3 (Takara Bio). Degenerate primers for amplification of partial CCD8 were designed based on consensus amino acid sequences among other dicotyledonous species, as shown in Supplementary Table S1. The PCR reaction mixture, which had a total volume of 25 µl, contained 0.5 µl cDNA, 400 µM dNTPs, 5 µM of each primer, 2.5 units of ExTaq Polymerase (Takara Bio), and 1 × Ex Taq PCR buffer. The PCR conditions were as follows: pre-heating at 94 ˚C for 2 min, 35 cycles at 95 ˚C for 20 s, 50 ˚C for 40 s, 72˚C for 1 min, and then extension at 72 ˚C for 10 min. The amplified fragments were subcloned into the pGEM-T Easy vector system (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) and transformed into E. coli strain DH5α. White colonies were screened by colony PCR using BioTaq DNA polymerase (Meridian BIOSCIENCE, TN, USA) with M13-M4 and M13-RV primers. Colony PCR products were treated with ExoSAP-IT (Thermo Fisher Science, MA, USA) and sequenced by Fasmac Co. (Kanagawa, Japan). Sequence alignments were performed using CLC Main Workbench version 25.0.2 (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). The 5ʹ- and 3ʹ-RACE were performed using a GeneRacer Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Construction of CRISPR–Cas9 vectors

Target sequences in EgCCD8 genome (accession number LC927686) were designed using the web-based tools Focas (Osakabe et al. 2016) and CRISPRdirect (Naito et al. 2015). Four guide RNAs (gRNAs) (G1–G4) were selected. G1 and G2 targeted exons 1 and 2, respectively, whereas G3 and G4 targeted exon 4 (Fig. 1A). Annealed oligonucleotides corresponding to each gRNA were inserted into the Bsa I-digested pEgP137-2A-GFP (Ueta et al. 2017) by Golden Gate cloning. The pEgP137-2A-GFP vector, which confers hygromycin resistance, contains a double cauliflower mosaic virus (CaMV) 35S promoter driving A. thaliana codon-optimized Streptococcus pyogenes Cas9 (AtCas9) and GFP, and the A. thaliana U6-26 promoter controlling gRNA expression. Transformation of E. grandiflorum

Seeds of E. grandiflorum cv. Light Pink Thumb were sown aseptically on germination medium (MS medium (Murashige and Skoog 1962) containing 0.3% (w/v) gellan gum and 3% (w/v) sucrose with a pH of 5.6) and cultured at 25 °C under a 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod with a fluorescent light intensity of 30 µmol m⁻2 s⁻1. Transgenic E. grandiflorum plants were generated using CRISPR–Cas9 vectors (Fig. 1B) through Agrobacterium-mediated leaf disk transformation, as described by Tomizawa et al. (2021). Briefly, leaf sections (5 mm × 5 mm) cut from 1.5-month-old seedlings were pre-cultured on co-cultivation medium (MS medium containing 0.5 mg L−1 6-benzylaminopurine [BAP] and 100 μM acetosyringone [3',5'-dimethoxy-4'-hydroxyacetophenone], pH 5.7) supplemented with 0.3% (w/v) gellan gum and 2% (w/v) sucrose at 25°C for 5 days. The leaf explants were then soaked in the Agrobacterium suspension for 5 min, transferred to the co-cultivation medium, and co-cultured in the dark at 25 °C for 7 days. After co-cultivation, the explants were transferred to the selection medium (MS medium containing 0.5 mg L−1 BAP, 50 mg L−1 hygromycin, 10 mg L−1 meropenem; Meropen, Sumitomo Dainippon Pharma Co., Osaka, Japan) supplemented with 0.3% (w/v) gellan gum and 2% (w/v) sucrose for 4 weeks to induce callus formation. Subsequently, the calli were transferred to the shoot regeneration medium (MS medium containing 0.8% [w/v] agar, 2% [w/v] sucrose, 0.1 mg L−1 BAP, 50 mg L−1 hygromycin, and 10 mg L−1 meropenem) for 4 weeks. The regenerated shoots were separated from the calli and treated with Rootone (Kincho-engei, Tokyo, Japan) to induce root regeneration. After root regeneration, the transgenic seedlings were transplanted into sterilized soil (Metro-mix 350J, Hyponex Japan, Osaka, Japan) and grown at 22 °C under a 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod with a fluorescent light intensity of 85 µmol m⁻2 s⁻1. After blooming, the transgenic plants were self-pollinated and T1 seeds were collected. The T1 seeds were sown on MS medium supplemented with 50 mg L−1 hygromycin and incubated for 1 month at 25 °C under a 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod. T1 plants were then acclimated and grown under the conditions described above.

At least 15 individuals from each T1 progeny were planted in ϕ7.5 cm pots filled with Metro-Mix 350J and cultivated in a closed greenhouse under natural daylight conditions from April to June 2024.

Detection of CRISPR–Cas9-induced mutations

Genomic DNA was extracted from independent transgenic plants using the NucleoSpin Plant II Kit (MACHEREY–NAGEL, Düren, Germany). To evaluate mutations at the target sites, approximately 500 bp flanking each gRNA target locus was amplified using ExTaq polymerase with the corresponding primer sets (Supplementary Table S1). As a first screening, PCR products were subjected to T7 endonuclease I digestion to detect insertions or deletions. The amplified fragments were denatured at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 95–85°C (-2 °C/s) and 85–25 °C (-0.1°C/s) as an annealing step. The annealed PCR product was then incubated with 1 μL T7 endonuclease I (New England BioLabs, MA, USA) at 37 °C for 15 min. This step was confirmed by electrophoresis. As a secondary screening, positive lines in the T7 endonuclease I assay were sequenced. PCR amplicons were cloned and sequenced as described above.

Evaluation of T1 progeny of genome-edited plants

T1 seeds derived from three EgCCD8-edited lines were sown on germination medium (MS medium containing 3% (w/v) sucrose and 0.2% (w/v) gellan gum, pH 5.6) without antibiotics and incubated for 1 month at 25°C under a 16-h light/8-h dark photoperiod with a fluorescent light intensity of 30 µmol m⁻2 s⁻1. The absence or presence of transgenes in T1 individuals was determined by direct PCR. Approximately 5 × 5 mm leaf segments were homogenized in extraction buffer (100 mM Tris–HCl, 1 M KCl, 10 mM EDTA, pH 9.5) and incubated at 95°C for 10 min. The supernatant was used as a crude DNA template. PCR reactions (25 μL) contained 1 × PCR buffer, 0.4 mM dNTPs, 0.3 μM primers, and 0.5 unit KOD FX Neo DNA polymerase (Toyobo, Osaka, Japan). The primer sets are provided in Supplementary Table S1. Thermal cycling conditions consisted of an initial denaturation at 94°C for 2 min, followed by 35 cycles at 98°C for 10 s, 55°C for 30 s, and 68°C for 1 min. PCR products were separated on 1.5% (w/v) agarose gels and stained with Midori Green Advance (Nippon Genetics, Tokyo).

T1 null segregant plants were acclimatized and cultivated in a closed greenhouse. After flowering, plant height, branch number, fresh and dry weights, floral bud number, and petal size were measured destructively. Leaf chlorophyll content was evaluated using a SPAD-502Plus chlorophyll meter. Petal color was assessed by the L* and C* values, which represent lightness and chroma, respectively, using a CR-20 colorimeter (Konica Minolta, Tokyo, Japan).

Gene expression analysis

For semi-quantitative reverse transcription PCR (RT-PCR) analysis, total RNA was isolated from the flower buds, stems, leaves, and roots of 4-month-old wild-type ‘Light Pink Thumb’ plants, as well as from the shoots and roots of seedlings collected 1.5 months after sowing. For quantitative RT-PCR (RT-qPCR) analysis, total RNA was isolated from the leaves, stems, and roots of guide 1 (G1#1) T0 and wild-type plants at the floral bud formation stage, with three biological replicates. First-strand cDNA was synthesized using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit with a gDNA Eraser (TaKaRa Bio). For RT-PCR, the reaction mixture (50 μL) consisted of 1 × ExTaq buffer, 0.2 mM dNTPs, 0.4 μM of each primer, 2.5 U ExTaq polymerase, and 1 μL template cDNA. The PCR cycling conditions were as follows: 2 min at 94 °C, 26–34 cycles of 20 s at 95 °C, 40 s at 55 °C, 1 min at 72 °C, and final extension for 10 min at 72 °C. The PCR products were electrophoresed on a 1.5% (w/v) agarose gel in TAE buffer and then stained with Midori Green Advance. RT-qPCR was performed using a Thermal Cycler Dice Real Time System (TP850; Takara Bio) and KAPA SYBR FAST qPCR Master Mix (KAPA Biosystems, Wilmington, MA, USA), as described by Nuraini et al. (2020). Transcript levels were normalized to EgACT (AB565514) as the reference gene. The primer sequences used are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Results

Genomic structure of EgCCD8 in E. grandiflorum

To identify EgCCD8 in E. grandiflorum, we performed degenerated PCR using primers from conserved regions of CCD8 amino acid sequences in previously characterized eudicot species. A 260 bp partial EgCCD8 fragment was amplified and used to query the E. grandiflorum whole-genome sequence (Kawakatsu et al., unpublished). The genomic structure of EgCCD8 comprises six exons encoding a 572-amino acid protein. The deduced amino acid sequence showed 83.7%, 79.7%, 79.3%, and 66.2% identity with kiwifruit AcCCD8 (ADP37984; Ledger et al. 2010), P. hybrida DAD1 (AAW59435; Snowden et al. 2005), P. sativum RMS1 (AAS66906; Sorefan et al. 2003), and A. thaliana MAX4 (AT4G32810), respectively (Supplementary Figure S1). Two CCD8 homologs (Egra_v1_Chr36.g41040 and Egra_v1_Chr06.g52510) were identified within the chromosome-level E. grandiflorum whole-genome sequences (Shirasawa et al. 2023). The nucleotide sequence of Egra_v1_Chr36.g41040 fully corresponded with EgCCD8, whereas the deduced amino acid sequence of Egra_v1_Chr06.g52510 showed only 70.2% identity with EgCCD8. Phylogenetic analysis placed Egra_v1_Chr06.g52510 outside of the plant CCD8 clade. Therefore, E. grandiflorum chromosomes were considered to have one copy of EgCCD8. However, the possibility of undetected allelic variation or partial functional redundancy cannot be completely excluded due to limitations of PCR-based genotyping.

A 1,540-bp partial fragment of EgCCD7 was also isolated from E. grandiflorum using degenerate PCR and RACE. The deduced amino acid sequence showed 85.5%, 79.4%, and 77.5% identity with P. hybrida CCD7 (ACY01408), P. sativum RMS5 (ABD67496), and A. thaliana MAX3 (AT2G44990), respectively. Spatial expression analysis revealed that EgCCD8 transcripts accumulated predominantly in roots and stems of plantlets, whereas EgCCD7 transcripts were detected in all examined organs (Supplementary Figure S2). Based on these expression patterns and the pleiotropic functions of strigolactones, EgCCD8, which revealed the restricted and strong gene expression levels, was selected as the genome-editing target. We designed four gRNAs (G1–G4) to target exons 1, 2, and 4 of EgCCD8 (Fig. 1A). These guide RNAs were unique to EgCCD8 (Egra_v1_Chr36.g41040) in the E. grandiflorum genome (Supplementary Figure S3).

Detection of CRISPR–Cas9-induced mutations in E. grandiflorum

A total of 300, 300, 360, and 360 leaf sections were infected with A. tumefaciens harboring the G1, G2, G3, G4 constructs, respectively. We obtained one, one, 19, and 17 lines of hygromycin-resistant shoots, respectively. The presence or absence of the transgene was confirmed by genomic PCR. This generated four T0 transgenic lines: one line each for G1 (G1#1) and G2 (G2#1), and two lines for G3 (G3#1 and G3#2). No transgenic lines were obtained for G4. Genomic DNA extracted from leaves was analyzed to determine mutation status (Fig. 2). The G1#1 exhibited biallelic mutations consisting of a 1 bp insertion (7 /10 clones) and a 3 bp deletion (3/10 clones) at the target site. G3#1 also carried biallelic mutations, including a 4 bp deletion (2/10 clones) and a 26 bp deletion (8/10 clones). G3#2 displayed a homozygous 6 bp deletion (10/10 clones). These three lines lacked the wild-type (WT) EgCCD8 allele. In contrast, no mutations were detected in G2#1 at the target site (data not shown). Phenotypes of T1 progenies in each EgCCD8-genome-editing line

We obtained T1 seeds by self-pollination of each transgenic line. T1 seeds were aseptically germinated on MS medium, and genomic PCR targeting the HPT gene within the T-DNA region was performed to identify null segregants. The proportion of transgene-free individuals in the T1 progeny was 81.5% for G1#1, 84.0% for G3#1, and 78.3% for G3#2 (Supplementary Table S2). In contrast, all T1 individuals derived from G2#1 retained transgenes, suggesting the integration of multiple T-DNA copies.

Eleven T1 individuals from G1#1 were evaluated by sequencing the EgCCD8 alleles (Fig. 2A). T1 progeny from G1#1 showed five EgCCD8 mutation alleles, including three novel 3 bp, 4 bp, and 6 bp deletion alleles that were not detected in the T0 generation. There were homozygous individuals with two copies of the same mutated EgCCD8 allele, as well as heterozygous individuals with different mutated alleles. Frameshift mutant alleles (1 bp insertion, 4 bp deletion, and 5 bp deletion) were assumed to encode significantly shortened and non-functional CCD8 proteins (Fig. 2D). G3#1 T1 progeny inherited either homozygous or heterozygous mutant EgCCD8 alleles, consisting of 4 bp or 26 bp deletions (Fig. 2B). These frameshift mutant alleles seemed to encode EgCCD8 proteins with a truncated C-terminal (Fig. 2D). In contrast, the G3#2 progeny retained only the 6 bp deletion allele of EgCCD8, which was also observed in the T0 generation (Fig. 2C).

Null segregant T1 progeny of G1#1 and G3#1 were further cultivated in a closed greenhouse. The WT and G3#1 T1 progeny showed a uniform phenotype, whereas the G1#1 T1 progeny showed slight variation. WT plants formed one or two primary stems (Fig. 3A). The G3#1 T1 population showed a high frequency of branching (89.7%), whereas the G1#1 T1 population showed a slightly lower branching frequency (Fig. 3B, C; Supplementary Table S3). Mean plant height was 8 cm in G1#1 and 10.3 cm in G3#1, both significantly shorter than the 17 cm observed in the WT (Fig. 3D). Shoot fresh weight was significantly higher in G3#1 than in the WT and G1#1, whereas root fresh weight was significantly lower in G1#1 than in the WT and G3#1 (Fig. 3E, F). The mean number of branches was 5.6 in G1#1 and 9.9 in G3#1, compared with 2 in the WT (Fig. 3G). Stem length in both edited lines was approximately one-third that of the WT. Collectively, EgCCD8 genome editing in G3#1 increased shoot biomass and enhanced stem branching. Differences between RT-PCR (Supplementary Fig. 2) and RT-qPCR results may reflect differences in sensitivity and plant developmental stage. In contrast, EgCCD8 transcripts were not detected in all examined tissues of G3#1, especially in roots. The effects of plant hormones, except strigolactones, were also investigated. In the WT, EgYUCCA and EgPIN4, which are involved in auxin biosynthesis and efflux carrier genes, respectively, were strongly expressed in roots. EgGA2ox, a gene associated with GA metabolism, was strongly expressed in stems and leaves but weakly expressed in roots. In G3#1, expression of EgYUCCA and EgPIN4 was significantly increased in leaves, and EgGA2ox expression was significantly upregulated in roots compared with the WT. These results indicate that EgCCD8 deficiency alters the expression of genes involved in strigolactone and other plant hormone pathways.

Discussion

In floricultural crops valued for their diverse flower colors and shapes, modification of shoot architecture remains a major breeding objective to expand ornamental applications. However, conventional breeding approaches have limited capacity to precisely manipulate plant architecture. Previous studies have clarified hormonal and genetic mechanisms regulating shoot architecture and lateral branch outgrowth and have highlighted the potential of breeding and biotechnological approaches to optimize plant architecture for economic purposes (Teichmann and Muhr 2015; Wang et al. 2018). Semidwarf phenotypes in cereal crops have largely resulted from natural mutations in GA-biosynthetic genes. Targeted modification of the GA pathway has been proposed as a strategy to design plant height ideotypes; however, generating weak alleles is recommended to minimize undesirable pleiotropic effects associated with strong GA disruption (Wang and Wang 2022). Therefore, CRISPR–Cas9 genome editing based on NHEJ-mediated repair is difficult to control when generating weak alleles of GA-biosynthetic genes.

E. grandiflorum is mainly cultivated as a cut flower, although several potted cultivars have been developed. Since potted cultivars are typically dwarf and have limited branching, planting two or three seedlings per pot can increase canopy volume. In the present study, we successfully produced dwarf and multiple-branching E. grandiflorum lines through targeted EgCCD8 mutation using CRISPR–Cas9. Three independent edited lines (G1#1, G3#1, and G3#2) were obtained (Fig. 2). In contrast, G2#1 showed no mutation in EgCCD8, likely due to a 1-bp mismatch between the gRNA and the target sequence (Supplementary Figure S3). The three edited T0 lines carried insertions or deletions ranging from 1-bp insertions to 4- to 26-bp deletions and did not retain the WT EgCCD8 allele (Fig. 2). No base substitutions were detected within the EgCCD8 genome in these edited T0 lines. A previous transient genome-editing assay in E. grandiflorum protoplasts reported up to 72.6% mutation efficiency at the PHYTOENE DESATURASE locus (Li et al. 2024), supporting the high editing efficiency observed in T0 plants in this study. Further improvement of editing efficiency may be achieved by replacing the CaMV35S and Arabidopsis U6 promoters driving Cas9 and gRNA expression, respectively, with endogenous EgUBQ10 and EgU6 promoters (Li et al. 2024). G1#1 and G3#1 plants, in which all EgCCD8 alleles were disrupted, showed dwarf and highly branched phenotypes in the T0 generation. In contrast, G2#1 and G3#2 did not differ phenotypically from the WT. G3#2 carried a 6-bp in-frame deletion in EgCCD8 and displayed a WT-like phenotype (Fig. 2C). This finding indicates that deleting Gly441 and Lys442 did not impair EgCCD8 enzymatic function (Fig. 2D). In G3#1, EgCCD8 transcript levels in roots were significantly reduced compared with those in the WT (Fig. 5). Therefore, the loss-of-function mutant may positively suppress EgCCD8 expression. In contrast, EgCCD7 expression in leaves of G3#1 increased 12.5-fold relative to the WT (Fig. 5). In P. hybrida, Dad1/PhCCD8 expression is upregulated in stems of strigolactone-defective mutants (dad1-1, dad2, and dad3), indicating feedback regulation in this tissue, whereas root expression remains unaffected (Snowden et al. 2005). These findings suggest that feedback regulation of strigolactone biosynthesis in E. grandiflorum may differ from that reported in other species.

A limitation of this study is the absence of direct quantification of strigolactone levels (e.g., LC–MS analysis) and functional complementation experiments such as GR24 application. Therefore, the link between EgCCD8 disruption and strigolactone deficiency is inferred indirectly from phenotypic observations and previous studies.

After self-pollination, we investigated the T1 progeny that inherited the transgene cassette and the edited EgCCD8 alleles. T1 progeny of G1#1, G3#1, and G3#2 included 78.3%–84.0% transgene-free individuals, indicating a high frequency of null segregants (Supplementary Table S2). In plants carrying a single transgene insertion, approximately 75% of T1 seedlings are typically transgene positive. Ledger et al. (1997) reported that the inheritance ratios varied considerably in transgenic E. grandiflorum. We propose two possible reasons: one, segregation distortion associated with the low germination rate (20–30%), and the other, possible false-negative PCR results because the null segregant individuals do not produce amplified fragments. There are also two conflicting reports of the ploidy level in E. grandiflorum: 2n = 2x = 72 (Kawakatsu et al. 2021) and 2n = 6x = 72 (Liang et al. 2022), which complicate genetic analysis. In future studies, it will be necessary to confirm the copy number of the transgene and chimerism in T0 plants.

The G3#1 T1 progeny exhibited three mutation patterns at the EgCCD8 locus: homozygous 4-bp deletions, homozygous 26-bp deletions, and heterozygous combinations of both alleles (Fig. 2B). The G1#1 T1 progeny included three novel deletion alleles of 3 bp, 4 bp, and 6 bp, in addition to the previously detected 1-bp insertion and 5-bp deletion alleles. Sequencing analysis using two independent leaves from G1#1 T0 plants only detected the 1-bp insertion and the 5-bp deletion alleles. We propose two possible explanations: that the residual WT alleles or 1-bp insertion allele was re-edited by CRISPR–Cas9 activity during the reproductive process, or that different genome-edited cells were distributed as a mosaic in G1#1 T0 plants. The G3#1 T1 progeny exhibited more pronounced and stable branching than the G1#1 progeny. G1#1 T1 plants segregated into individuals carrying homozygous frameshift mutation alleles or homozygous/heterozygous in-frame mutation alleles (Fig. 2A; Supplementary Table S3). G1#1 T1 individuals (T1-3, T1-4, T1-9, T1-10) with the 3-bp or 6-bp deletion EgCCD8 alleles showed a very similar phenotype to the WT progeny (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Table S3). Leu90 or Ala91 deficiency did not impair EgCCD8 enzymatic function. These residues may not be critical for CCD8 catalytic activity or structural integrity, which could explain the retention of wild-type phenotypes in in-frame mutants. These findings suggest a strong correlation between the dwarf and branching phenotypes and EgCCD8 functional deficiency by CRISPR–Cas9. Although our results suggest a strong association between EgCCD8 mutations and the observed phenotypes, we cannot fully exclude the potential contribution of somaclonal variation or off-target effects. However, further research using genetic segregation by backcrossing, whole-genome re-sequencing, or complement experiments with GR24 application is needed to exclude any effects from off-target effects or somaclonal variation.

Genome-edited T1 plants showed increased branching and reduced plant height (Fig. 3D and G). Flowering time and developmental progression did not differ from the WT. In tobacco, an allotetraploid species, CRISPR–Cas9-mediated disruption of two closely related CCD8 genes (NtCCD8A and NtCCD8B) resulted in increased branching, reduced plant height, increased leaf and node number, and reduced total biomass (Gao et al. 2018). Similarly, BnD14-genome editing of Brassica napus plants altered shoot architecture and increased total pod weight per plant (Stanic et al. 2021). In contrast, EgCCD8 editing in E. grandiflorum G3#1 did not reduce shoot or root biomass (Fig. 3E, F) but subsequently altered shoot and root architecture (Figs. 3 and Supplementary Figure S4), consistent with observations in ccd8 tobacco mutants.

Strigolactone deficiency in E. grandiflorum induced increased branching, consistent with phenotypes reported in petunia dad1-1 (Napoli 1996), pea rms1, and Arabidopsis max4 mutants (Sorefan et al. 2003). Genome editing-mediated disruption of strigolactone biosynthesis, therefore, represents an effective strategy for changing plant architecture without circumventing side effects. In G3#1, enhanced branching was accompanied by a significant increase in floral bud number (Fig. 4A). An extended flowering period was also observed, likely due to continuous bud formation (data not shown). Since E. grandiflorum typically produces three to five flower buds per branch, the formation of lateral stems in G3#1 appears to increase the floral bud number and promote continuous blooming. These findings suggest that strigolactone deficiency in E. grandiflorum influences not only branching but also flowering persistence, potentially extending postproduction display life.

Strigolactones are key signaling molecules in the establishment of arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) symbiosis in land plants (Akiyama et al. 2005; Kretzschmar et al. 2012). In E. grandiflorum, GA treatment enhances colonization by the AM fungus Rhizophagus irregularis through stimulation of extraradical hyphal branching (Tominaga et al. 2023). GA treatment significantly suppresses strigolactone production while upregulating biosynthesis of characteristic monoterpene glucosides in roots (Tominaga et al. 2023). These findings suggest that EgCCD8 genome editing may influence AM fungal colonization in E. grandiflorum.

Overall, targeted mutation of CCD8 through genome editing-induced dwarfism and enhanced branching without compromising other ornamental traits. Strigolactone biosynthesis, therefore, represents a promising breeding target for architectural improvement in floricultural crops.
