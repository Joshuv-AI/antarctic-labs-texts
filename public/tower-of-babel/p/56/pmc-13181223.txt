Prognostic Value of Pathological Response After Conversion Therapy and Salvage Surgery in Unresectable Hepatocellular Carcinoma
Authors: Qi‐Yu Chi, Xiao‐Di Gao, Mi Wang, Quan‐Yuan Liu, Yong‐Yi Zeng, Lan‐Fang Yang, Zi‐Jian Liu, Shuang‐Jia Wang, Shan‐Geng Weng, Zhi‐Bo Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsTo evaluate the prognostic significance of pathological response in patients with initially unresectable hepatocellular carcinoma (uHCC) who underwent salvage resection after successful conversion with lenvatinib, anti‐PD‐1 antibodies, and locoregional therapy (LPLRT).MethodsThis multicenter retrospective study included 102 uHCC patients who achieved curative resection after LPLRT across four tertiary centers in China. Residual viable tumour (pRVT) ratio was used to assess pathological response. Optimal pRVT cutoffs for overall survival (OS) and recurrence‐free survival (RFS) were identified using X‐tile (a statistical tool that identifies optimal prognostic cutoff values for continuous variables by systematically maximizing the log‐rank statistic). Survival was analysed by Kaplan–Meier and log‐rank tests, and prognostic factors by Cox regression analyses.ResultsAmong 102 patients, 25 (24.5%) achieved pathological complete response (pCR). X‐tile identified pRVT thresholds of 10% and 35% as optimal for predicting OS and RFS, respectively. Patients with pRVT ≤ 10% had superior OS (1‐, 2‐, 3 year: 96.6%, 89.8%, 82.5%) compared with pRVT > 10% (95.5%, 73.0%, 49.4%; p = 0.002). pRVT ≤ 35% was associated with longer RFS (1‐, 2 year: 60.5%, 47.1% vs. 24.0%, 10.0%; p < 0.001). Similar trends persisted after excluding pCR cases. On multivariate analysis, NLR < 2.6, adjuvant therapy, and pRVT ≤ 10% independently predicted improved OS, while age < 60 years was adverse for RFS, and NLR < 2.6 and pRVT ≤ 35% were independently associated with prolonged RFS. No clinical variables predicted pRVT thresholds.ConclusionspRVT refines postoperative prognostic assessment in uHCC after multimodal conversion therapy. Thresholds of ≤ 10% for OS and ≤ 35% for RFS serve as practical cutoffs for postoperative risk stratification.

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the sixth most commonly diagnosed malignancy and the third leading cause of cancer‐related mortality worldwide, representing a substantial global health burden [1]. While surgical resection remains the mainstay of curative treatment for early‐stage HCC with 5 year overall survival (OS) rates ranging from 50% to 70% the asymptomatic nature of early disease often leads to delayed diagnosis. Consequently, approximately 70%–80% of patients are diagnosed at intermediate or advanced stages, rendering them ineligible for curative resection [2, 3, 4, 5]. For these patients, systemic therapy has long been the standard of care. The introduction of targeted agents and immune checkpoint inhibitors has significantly improved clinical outcomes, extending median OS in advanced‐stage HCC to 10.7–23.7 months [3, 4, 5, 6].

In recent years, the therapeutic landscape of HCC has evolved toward multimodal strategies, particularly the integration of systemic therapy with locoregional therapy (LRT). Accumulating evidence supports the synergistic potential of tyrosine kinase inhibitors and anti‐PD‐1 antibodies (PD‐1), especially when combined with LRT, which may enhance tumour control and create opportunities for tumour downstaging in selected patients with advanced HCC [7, 8, 9]. Moreover, emerging evidence from recent clinical trials evaluating transcatheter arterial chemoembolization (TACE) combined with systemic therapies—including immunotherapy and targeted therapy—such as EMERALD‐1 [10], LEAP‐012 [11], and TALENTACE [12], further highlights the capacity of these combinations to reshape the therapeutic landscape of intermediate‐stage HCC and reinforces the clinical rationale for integrating LRT with systemic therapy. In this context, our group has actively explored multimodal conversion strategies in unresectable HCC (uHCC). In a prospective multicenter study [9], we investigated the combination of TACE, camrelizumab, and lenvatinib (LEN), which yielded notable efficacy with an objective response rate of 76.4% and a disease control rate of 85.5%, with a proportion of patients subsequently becoming eligible for potentially curative resection. Moreover, in a real‐world multicenter cohort of uHCC patients with portal vein tumour thrombosis, we further validated the effectiveness of LEN plus PD‐1 combined with LRT (LPLRT), achieving an ORR of 62.1% and allowing selected patients to proceed to curative‐intent surgery [13].

With the increasing acceptance of “conversion therapy”, defined as systemic or multimodal treatment aimed at downstaging initially uHCC to enable potentially curative resection, this approach has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy in recent years; however, its impact on long‐term survival remains to be fully validated. In this context, salvage surgery may become feasible for selected patients who achieve sufficient tumour downstaging after conversion therapy. Accurate assessment of treatment response in these patients is therefore critical for prognostic evaluation. However, radiologic assessment following conversion therapy may not fully capture residual tumour viability or reflect true biological behavior [14]. Accordingly, postoperative pathological response has emerged as a more objective and potentially prognostic indicator of treatment efficacy. In several solid tumours pathological response has been closely associated with recurrence risk and long‐term survival [15, 16, 17, 18]. For instance, in lung cancer, major pathological response (MPR), defined as < 10% residual viable tumour cells, has been widely adopted as a surrogate marker for treatment response and prognosis following neoadjuvant therapy [19, 20]. Of particular relevance, D'Alessio et al. [21] recently validated the prognostic relevance of pathological response in HCC after neoadjuvant immunotherapy and established ≥ 90% tumour necrosis as the threshold for defining MPR.

In contrast, the prognostic significance of pathological response following conversion therapy in HCC remains unclear, and high‐quality evidence from large‐scale studies is limited. While some studies suggest that > 90% tumour necrosis correlates with improved outcomes [22], others propose that ≥ 70% necrosis is a sufficient threshold for favourable prognosis [23]. More recently, a multicenter study [24] reported that the presence of MPR or complete pathological response (pCR) may improve the prognosis of patients initially diagnosed with uHCC who undergo conversion surgery after triple therapy. This study reinforces the relevance of pathological response as a prognostic marker; however, its binary classification of response (MPR/pCR versus non‐MPR) does not fully reflect the continuous spectrum of tumour regression, and the optimal pathological threshold for risk stratification remains undetermined. Taken together with prior inconsistencies in necrosis‐based thresholds, these findings highlight the need to refine pathological response assessment and to establish more precise, quantifiable predictors of long‐term outcomes in uHCC following conversion therapy. Therefore, this multicenter retrospective study aimed to evaluate the prognostic value of pathological residual viable tumour (pRVT) in uHCC patients undergoing successful salvage resection after LPLRT, by modelling pRVT as a continuous variable and determining optimal cutoff thresholds for prognostic assessment.

Patients and Methods

Patients

In this multicenter retrospective study, we enrolled patients with initially uHCC who underwent successful conversion therapy followed by curative resection between January 2019 and December 2023. Eligible patients were treated at four participating centers: the First Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University, Zhangzhou Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University, Mengchao Hepatobiliary Hospital of Fujian Medical University, and the First Affiliated Hospital of Xiamen University. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the institutional review boards of all participating institutions.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients with initially uHCC who underwent curative‐intent resection following LPLRT; (2) initial treatment comprised LEN, PD‐1, and LRT, including TACE, hepatic arterial infusion chemotherapy (HAIC), or a combination of TACE and HAIC; (3) age between 18 and 80 years; and (4) Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) stage B or C at diagnosis. Exclusion criteria included: (1) the presence of other concurrent malignancies; (2) a history of prior surgical treatment for HCC; (3) severe dysfunction of major organs, including the heart, lungs, liver, kidneys, or brain; and (4) insufficient or missing essential clinical or pathological data. To minimize potential confounding effects on pathological assessment, patients who had received any prior LRT (such as TACE or HAIC) or systemic therapy before initiation of the LPLRT regimen were excluded.

uHCC was defined as HCC that was initially unsuitable for curative resection due to extensive bilobar involvement, macrovascular invasion, extrahepatic metastasis (EHM), inadequate liver functional reserve, clinically significant portal hypertension, or insufficient future liver remnant volume.

LPLRT Protocol

LEN was administered orally once daily at a dose of 12 mg for patients weighing ≥ 60 kg or 8 mg for those weighing < 60 kg. PD‐1 s included camrelizumab (200 mg), sintilimab (200 mg), tislelizumab (200 mg), or toripalimab (240 mg), all given intravenously every 3 weeks.

TACE was performed using a conventional protocol. After superselective catheterization of tumour‐feeding arteries, a chemotherapeutic emulsion consisting of lobaplatin and/or pirarubicin mixed with lipiodol was infused, followed by embolization with gelatin sponge particles. TACE was typically repeated every 4 to 6 weeks. For patients receiving HAIC alone, a catheter was inserted into the hepatic artery via the femoral approach. The FOLFOX regimen was infused as follows: oxaliplatin 85 mg/m2 over 2 h on day 1, leucovorin 400 mg/m2 over 1.5 h on day 1, and fluorouracil 400 mg/m2 as an intravenous bolus followed by 2400 mg/m2 continuous infusion over 46 h. HAIC was generally repeated every 3 to 4 weeks. In patients undergoing TACE‐HAIC, TACE was first performed as described above. Upon completion of embolization, the exposed segment of the catheter was securely fixed with surgical silk and covered using a sterile transparent 3 M film dressing, with its external end anchored to the skin in the right lower abdomen and groin region. Digital subtraction angiography was performed to confirm the proper position of the catheter. Once confirmed, patients were transferred back to the ward for immediate administration of the HAIC regimen (FOLFOX regimen). TACE‐HAIC was typically repeated every 4 to 6 weeks.

Treatment strategies were determined at baseline through multidisciplinary team (MDT) evaluation, taking into account tumour burden, liver function reserve, and performance status, in accordance with guidelines for the diagnosis and treatment of primary liver cancer of China. During treatment, patients were closely monitored for therapeutic response and adverse events. Locoregional and systemic therapies were continued, modified, or discontinued based on serial imaging assessments, liver function status, treatment tolerance, and overall clinical condition. All patients provided written informed consent prior to initiation of treatment for the proposed therapeutic plan. Given the retrospective nature of the study and the use of anonymized clinical data, the requirement for additional study‐specific informed consent was waived in accordance with local regulatory policies.

Surgical Procedures

Tumour resectability was evaluated every 6 to 8 weeks following conversion therapy. Once the tumour met the criteria for resectability, surgical resection was considered after MDT discussion. The criteria for resectability were based on our previous study [13]. The objective criteria applied in this study included: (1) adequate future liver remnant (≥ 40% in patients with cirrhosis and ≥ 30% in those without cirrhosis); (2) feasibility of achieving R0 resection with sufficient margins; (3) preserved liver function (Child–Pugh class A); (4) absence of severe treatment‐related adverse events during conversion therapy; and (5) no contraindications to major hepatectomy. Additionally, EHM confined to the hilar lymph nodes was considered resectable when radical resection was deemed technically feasible.

Preoperative management included discontinuation of LEN at least 1 week before surgery and cessation of PD‐1 at least 4 weeks prior to surgery. Additionally, the interval between the last LRT and surgery was maintained at a minimum of 4 weeks. The extent and approach of hepatectomy were determined based on tumour stage and location, residual liver function, and overall patient condition, referring to criteria established in our prior work [25]. Major hepatectomy was defined as resection involving three or more Couinaud liver segments, whereas minor hepatectomy referred to resection of fewer than three segments.

Pathological Examination

All resected specimens were evaluated by experienced pathologists at each center. The assessment of pathological response was performed under strict quality control procedures to ensure inter‐center consistency and reproducibility. Specifically, pathological evaluation was initially conducted by an experienced specialist pathologist, and all cases were subsequently and independently reviewed by a second senior pathologist at the same center. In cases of discrepant assessments, the pathological slides were jointly re‐examined, and the degree of tumour regression was discussed in detail until a consensus was reached. Key pathological features assessed included tumour size, tumour number, surgical margin status, presence of microvascular invasion (MVI), and pathological response to preoperative therapy. A standardized “7‐point sampling protocol” was applied for pathological assessment. Specifically, tissue samples were obtained from the tumour‐parenchyma interface at the 3, 6, 9, and 12 o'clock positions, as well as from within the tumour. Additional samples were collected from peritumoral liver tissue at ≤ 1 and > 1 cm from the tumour edge. For solitary tumours with a maximum diameter ≤ 3 cm, complete sampling of the entire lesion was performed. The number and location of samples were further adjusted based on tumour size and number to ensure comprehensive assessment. Formalin‐fixed specimens were sectioned at a thickness of 5 μm and stained with haematoxylin and eosin for histological examination under light microscopy. Pathological response was quantified as the percentage of necrotic tumour cells relative to the total tumour area. An average value was calculated for each patient based on all available slides. pCR was defined as the absence of viable tumour cells within the entire tumour area, MPR was defined as having ≤ 10% pRVT, whereas patients with pRVT > 10% were categorized as having non‐MPR.

Postoperative Adjuvant Therapy and Follow‐Up Protocol

It was recommended that patients resume oral LEN and intravenous administration of a PD‐1 within 4 weeks after surgery as adjuvant therapy. The total duration of adjuvant therapy was suggested to be 6 to 12 months.

During postoperative follow‐up, if tumour recurrence was suspected, MDT assessment was required to comprehensively evaluate the patient based on the location and nature of the recurrent lesion, hepatic functional reserve, and overall performance status. An individualized treatment strategy was formulated accordingly. Available options included systemic therapies (e.g., chemotherapy, targeted therapy, or immunotherapy), LRT (e.g., TACE, HAIC, TACE‐HAIC, radiofrequency/microwave ablation, or radiotherapy), or repeat hepatic resection.

Patients were followed every 3 months during the first 2 years after surgery and every 6 months thereafter. Each follow‐up visit included imaging examinations (contrast‐enhanced abdominal CT or MRI and chest CT) and laboratory tests, including serum alpha‐fetoprotein (AFP), protein induced by vitamin K absence or antagonist II (PIVKA‐II), and liver and renal function tests. These evaluations were performed to monitor tumour recurrence or distant metastasis. Key outcome data, including the timing and location of first recurrence and the timing and cause of death, were systematically recorded.

Statistical Analysis

To determine the optimal prognostic cutoff for pathological response–related continuous variables, X‐tile software (version 3.6.1) was used. X‐tile is a statistical tool that identifies optimal outcome‐based thresholds by systematically maximizing the log‐rank statistic. Categorical variables were compared using the chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test, depending on the data distribution, while continuous variables were assessed using independent sample t‐tests. The primary endpoints of this study were OS and recurrence‐free survival (RFS). Survival curves were generated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and intergroup differences were assessed with the log‐rank test. OS was defined as the interval from surgery to death from any cause or last follow‐up. RFS was defined as the time from surgery to the date of first recurrence, death from any cause, or last follow‐up. The follow‐up period was censored on March 1, 2025. Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to identify independent predictors of OS and RFS. Logistic regression analysis was applied to identify predictors associated with the pathological response cutoff. Variables with a p < 0.10 in univariate analysis were included in the multivariate models. All statistical tests were two‐sided, and a p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.2).

Results

Patient Characteristics

Among the 319 patients who received LPLRT across four centers in China, 140 (43.9%) achieved successful conversion. Of these, three patients declined surgical intervention, and 137 (42.9%) ultimately underwent salvage hepatectomy. After applying the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, 102 patients were included in the present study (Figure 1). The demographic and clinical characteristics of these patients before LPLRT are summarized in Table 1. The median age of the included patients was 57 years (range, 32–80 years), and 86 patients (84.3%) were male. According to the BCLC staging system, 73 patients (71.6%) were classified as stage C. Within the BCLC stage C subgroup, 66 patients presented with macrovascular invasion, including 58 with portal vein thrombosis and 8 with hepatic vein thrombosis, while 7 patients had EHM, all of which were hilar lymph node metastases. A total of 89 patients (87.3%) had underlying hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection and all received standard oral antiviral therapy. Multifocal lesions were identified in 57 patients (55.9%) on preoperative radiological evaluation.

Association Between pRVT and Survival Outcomes

To further clarify the relationship between residual viable tumour burden and long‐term prognosis, restricted cubic spline analyses were performed to evaluate the association between pRVT, treated as a continuous variable, and survival outcomes, including OS and RFS. This analysis was conducted prior to determining optimal cutoff values in order to assess whether any nonlinear associations existed between pRVT and prognosis. As shown in Figure S1, the RCS models demonstrated a linear relationship between pRVT and both OS and RFS across the observed range of values. Formal tests for nonlinearity revealed no significant deviation from linearity for either endpoint (OS: P for nonlinearity = 0.861; RFS: P for nonlinearity = 0.279). These findings indicate that increasing pRVT was proportionally associated with progressively worse survival outcomes.

Optimal pRVT Cutoff Values for Predicting OS and RFS

Using X‐tile software to analyse the relevant data, the optimal cutoff values of the pRVT proportion for predicting OS and RFS were identified as 10% and 35%, respectively (Figures S2 and S3). Based on the 10% cutoff, the 102 patients were stratified into two groups: pRVT ≤ 10% (n = 60) and pRVT > 10% (n = 42). The baseline characteristics between the two groups were largely comparable, except for a significantly lower incidence of MVI in the pRVT ≤ 10% group compared to the pRVT > 10% group (11.7% vs. 18.6%, p = 0.006). No statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups with respect to age, sex, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status, HBV infection, comorbidities, albumin–bilirubin (ALBI) grade baseline AFP and PIVKA‐II, maximum tumour diameter, tumour number, EHM, macrovascular invasion, BCLC stage, cirrhosis, or presence of satellite nodules (Table 1). Importantly, detailed analyses of conversion therapy intensity and composition demonstrated no significant differences between the two groups. Specifically, the distribution of PD‐1 s (including camrelizumab, sintilimab, tislelizumab, and toripalimab), median duration of LEN treatment, median number of PD‐1 treatment cycles, the choice of locoregional modality, the number of LRT cycles, and TTS (time from last treatment to surgery) were comparable between the pRVT ≤ 10% and pRVT > 10% groups (all p > 0.05; Table S1). To further explore the potential impact of treatment timing on pathological response, nonlinear regression analyses were performed, revealing a trend toward lower pRVT with longer TTS (Figure S4).

Similarly, when stratified by the 35% pRVT cutoff, baseline characteristics remained largely balanced between the pRVT ≤ 35% (n = 76) and pRVT > 35% (n = 26) groups. The only significant difference was a higher incidence of MVI in the pRVT ≤ 35% group (17.6% vs. 12.7%, p = 0.012), while all other variables showed no statistical differences (Table S2). Consistent with the 10% cutoff analysis, treatment‐related variables did not differ significantly between groups stratified by the 35% pRVT threshold (Table S3). These findings suggest that pathological response stratification was not confounded by differences in treatment regimen composition or intensity.

When patients were stratified according to pathological response using either the 10% or 35% pRVT cutoff values, no statistically significant differences were observed between groups with respect to the receipt of adjuvant therapy, the choice of adjuvant regimen (LEN alone vs. LEN plus PD‐1), duration of LEN exposure, or number of PD‐1 cycles (all p > 0.05). Detailed distributions of adjuvant therapy according to pRVT stratification are summarized in (Tables S4 and S5). These findings suggest that differences in postoperative systemic therapy were unlikely to confound the observed associations between pathological response and survival outcomes.

Changes in Tumour Characteristics and Safety

Tumour characteristics before and after LPLRT are summarized in Table S6. The median number of tumours remained at 2 (range, 1–6) pre‐ and post‐treatment, though the change was statistically significant (p < 0.001). Median tumour diameter decreased significantly following treatment (8.6 cm vs. 9.6 cm, p = 0.003), accompanied by marked reductions in serum AFP and PIVKA‐II levels (AFP: 22.0 vs. 374.5 ng/mL, p = 0.001; PIVKA‐II: 117 vs. 2722.5 mAU/mL, p < 0.001). Stratification by a 10% pRVT cutoff showed no significant differences in tumour burden or baseline AFP and PIVKA‐II levels between groups. However, post‐treatment AFP and PIVKA‐II levels were significantly lower in the pRVT ≤ 10% group (p = 0.021 and p < 0.001; Table S7). Similarly, subgroup analysis using a 35% pRVT cutoff revealed comparable baseline tumour characteristics between groups. Yet after treatment, the pRVT ≤ 35% group exhibited significantly smaller tumours and lower AFP and PIVKA‐II levels compared to the pRVT > 35% group (p = 0.034, p = 0.029, and p < 0.001; Table S8). Treatment‐related adverse events (TRAEs) following LPLRT are summarized in Table S9. No grade 5 TRAEs were observed, and all events were manageable with appropriate medical intervention.

Radiological Response and Pathological Correlation

Preoperative radiological tumour response was evaluated using both RECIST 1.1 and mRECIST criteria. In the overall cohort, the objective response rate (ORR) was 48.0% according to RECIST 1.1% and 92.1% according to mRECIST. Notably, no patients achieved complete response (CR) when tumour response was assessed using RECIST 1.1 criteria, whereas 30.4% of patients achieved CR according to the mRECIST criteria. When stratified by pathological response, no significant difference in ORR was observed between patients with pRVT ≤ 10% and those with pRVT > 10% using RECIST 1.1 (p = 0.381). In contrast, mRECIST‐based ORR was significantly higher in the pRVT ≤ 10% group than in the pRVT > 10% group (p = 0.008). Moreover, when assessed by mRECIST, the CR rate was also significantly higher in the pRVT ≤ 10% group compared with the pRVT > 10% group (p < 0.001). Using the 35% cutoff, patients with pRVT ≤ 35% showed significantly higher ORRs than those with pRVT > 35% when assessed by RECIST 1.1 (p = 0.041), with an even more pronounced difference observed using mRECIST (p < 0.001). Consistently, the CR rate assessed by mRECIST was significantly higher in patients with pRVT ≤ 35% than in those with pRVT > 35% (p < 0.001). These findings consistently demonstrate that higher radiological response rates are associated with lower residual viable tumour burden, and that mRECIST more accurately reflects treatment‐induced tumour necrosis and pathological regression than conventional RECIST 1.1. Detailed results are provided in (Tables S10 and S11).

Survival Outcomes After Conversion Surgery

The median follow‐up duration for all patients was 29.2 months. A total of 23 patients (22.5%) died during the follow‐up period, including one postoperative death within 30 days after surgery. Tumour recurrence was observed in 66 patients (65.3%). The median OS was not reached. The 1‐, 2‐, and 3 year OS rates were 96.0%, 81.1%, and 67.1%, respectively. Additionally, the median RFS was 11.2 months (95% CI, 9.6–12.8), and the 1‐, 2‐, and 3 year RFS rates were 45.7%, 32.2%, and 23.0%, respectively (Figure S5A,B).

Prognostic Analysis Based on Different Levels of pRVT

Patients with pRVT ≤ 10% exhibited significantly better OS compared to those with pRVT > 10%. The 1‐, 2‐, and 3 year OS rates were 96.6%, 89.8%, and 82.5%, respectively, in the pRVT ≤ 10% group, whereas the corresponding rates in the pRVT > 10% group were 95.5%, 73.0%, and 49.4% (p = 0.002). The median OS was not reached in the pRVT ≤ 10% group, while it was 35.4 months in the pRVT > 10% group (Figure 3A). Similarly, the pRVT ≤ 10% group demonstrated significantly superior RFS compared to the pRVT > 10% group. The 1‐, 2‐, and 3 year RFS rates were 65.6%, 53.3%, and 37.0%, respectively, versus 27.7%, 13.0%, and 13.0% in the pRVT > 10% group (p < 0.001); the median RFS was 26.6 months and 7.7 months, respectively (Figure 3B).

When using a pRVT cutoff of 35%, no significant difference in OS was observed between the two groups (p = 0.129). However, the RFS remained significantly better in the pRVT ≤ 35% group compared to the pRVT > 35% group. The 1‐ and 2 year RFS rates were 60.5% and 47.1%, respectively, in the pRVT ≤ 35% group, compared to 24.0% and 10.0% in the pRVT > 35% group (p < 0.001); the median RFS was 22.8 months versus 6.7 months, respectively (Figure 3C,D).

A similar trend was observed between patients with pCR and those without pCR. Although no significant difference in OS was detected between the two groups (p = 0.155), the RFS was significantly better in the pCR group, with a 3 year RFS rate of 63.3%, markedly higher than 13.1% in the non‐pCR group (p < 0.001) (Figure S6A,B).

Prognostic Value of pRVT in Non‐pCR Patients

To determine whether the prognostic impact of pRVT is independent of pCR status, we excluded patients who achieved pCR from both the pRVT ≤ 10% and pRVT ≤ 35% groups and reanalyzed survival outcomes. The results showed that, even after excluding pCR cases, patients with pRVT ≤ 10% still demonstrated significantly better OS and RFS compared to those with pRVT > 10% (p = 0.008 and p = 0.030, respectively; Figure 4A,B). Similarly, although no significant difference in OS was observed between the pRVT ≤ 35% and pRVT > 35% groups (p = 0.272; Figure 4C), the RFS remained significantly superior in the pRVT ≤ 35% group (p = 0.006; Figure 4D). These findings suggest that the prognostic value of both 10% and 35% pRVT cutoffs was not solely attributable to the presence of pCR. Instead, pRVT appeared to retain its prognostic significance independently and may serve as a robust indicator of long‐term survival outcomes.

Independent Predictors for OS and RFS

Multivariate Cox regression analysis identified baseline neutrophil‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (NLR) < 2.6 (HR = 0.34, 95% CI: 0.13–0.88, p = 0.026) and pRVT ≤ 10% (HR = 0.29, 95% CI: 0.12–0.72, p = 0.008) as independent protective factors for OS. In addition, the absence of adjuvant therapy (HR = 3.74, 95% CI: 1.58–8.81, p = 0.003) was independently associated with inferior OS (Table 2). Regarding RFS, age < 60 years (HR = 1.88, 95% CI: 1.05–3.38, p = 0.034) was identified as an independent adverse predictor, while baseline NLR < 2.6 (HR = 0.40, 95% CI: 0.20–0.94, p = 0.001) and pRVT ≤ 35% (HR = 0.43, 95% CI: 0.20–0.94, p = 0.034) were both independent protective factors (Table 3).

Predictive Factors Associated With pRVT ≤ 10% and pRVT ≤ 35%

The potential predictors of achieving pRVT ≤ 10% were explored using univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses, with results summarized in Table S6. In the univariate analysis, baseline PIVKA‐II levels < 400 mAU/mL (HR = 0.44, 95% CI: 0.20–0.98, p = 0.045) and absence of macrovascular invasion (HR = 0.45, 95% CI: 0.22–0.93, p = 0.030) were significantly associated with achieving pRVT ≤ 10%. However, neither variable remained statistically significant in the multivariate analysis, suggesting that no independent predictors for pRVT ≤ 10% could be identified. Similarly, analyses of factors associated with pRVT ≤ 35% are presented in Table S7. Neither univariate nor multivariate logistic regression analyses identified any clinical parameters that independently predicted achieving pRVT ≤ 35%. Conversion therapy modality was not significantly associated with achieving either pRVT ≤ 10% or ≤ 35% in univariate or multivariate analyses (Tables S12 and S13).

Discussion

This multicenter study evaluated the prognostic significance of pathological response in patients with initially unresectable HCC who achieved successful conversion with LPLRT followed by curative resection. Lower pRVT was significantly associated with improved survival outcomes. Specifically, pRVT ≤ 10% correlated with prolonged OS and RFS and remained an independent protective factor for OS in multivariate analysis, whereas pRVT ≤ 35% independently predicted longer RFS. Notably, even patients who did not achieve pCR still derived substantial survival benefits when pRVT was low, highlighting pRVT as a pathological marker with unique prognostic value after conversion therapy. By modelling pRVT as a continuous variable and defining clinically relevant thresholds, our findings extend previous observations [24] and support the use of pRVT as a refined marker for postoperative risk stratification after multimodal conversion therapy.

The prognostic significance of pathological response has been recognized across multiple malignancies. Rubbia‐Brandt et al. [16] first reported that histopathologic tumour regression after preoperative chemotherapy predicted improved survival in colorectal liver metastases, a finding subsequently confirmed in several tumour types [16, 17, 19, 20, 22, 23, 26, 27, 28, 29]. In HCC, early evidence was largely derived from LRT‐based therapies; for example, Allard et al. [23] observed that the survival benefit after TACE was primarily limited to patients achieving > 90% tumour necrosis. With the emergence of immunotherapy‐based strategies, the prognostic relevance of pathological response has become increasingly evident. D'Alessio et al. [21] demonstrated that the extent of residual viable tumour after neoadjuvant immune checkpoint inhibition in resectable HCC correlated with postoperative outcomes, suggesting that pathological response may serve as a meaningful surrogate beyond pCR. However, these studies focused mainly on resectable disease, and evidence in patients with initially unresectable HCC undergoing conversion therapy remains limited. Moreover, prior analyses of LEN plus PD‐1–based therapy in uHCC were restricted by small sample sizes and binary response definitions [30]. These limitations underscore the need for refined residual tumour–based thresholds to improve prognostic stratification following multimodal conversion therapy.

Multimodal strategies integrating targeted therapy, immunotherapy, and LRT have emerged as a promising approach to enhance tumour response and survival in HCC [8, 9, 31, 32, 33]. These treatments exert complementary biological effects, including LRT‐induced tumour necrosis and immune activation, anti‐angiogenic effects of LEN, and immune potentiation through PD‐1 blockade [24]. Consistent with this rationale, recent randomized trials evaluating TACE combined with systemic therapies—including immunotherapy and targeted agents (EMERALD‐1 [10], LEAP‐012 [11], and TALENTACE [12])—have demonstrated the clinical potential of these integrated approaches in intermediate‐stage HCC. Rather than merely redefining treatment paradigms, these studies reinforce the concept that combining LRT with systemic therapy can deepen tumour regression and translate into more durable clinical benefit. In our cohort, LPLRT achieved pRVT ≤ 10% in 58.8% of patients, supporting its ability to induce deep pathological tumour regression and providing a biological basis for the favourable outcomes observed after conversion surgery.

Our findings also demonstrated a strong correlation between radiological response and pathological regression, particularly when tumour response was assessed using mRECIST. Unlike RECIST 1.1, which primarily evaluates tumour size, mRECIST incorporates arterial enhancement and therefore more accurately reflects viable tumour tissue after treatment [34]. In our cohort, no patients achieved CR according to RECIST 1.1, whereas 30.4% achieved CR by mRECIST, and mRECIST‐defined responses were significantly associated with lower residual viable tumour burden. This is consistent with the biological effects of LRT‐based therapies, which often induce extensive tumour necrosis without immediate tumour shrinkage. Previous studies have similarly shown that mRECIST better correlates with pathological necrosis and clinical outcomes in HCC treated with LRT [35, 36, 37]. However, although radiological CR may indicate profound tumour regression, imaging alone cannot reliably exclude residual viable tumour or microscopic disease [35, 38, 39]. Furthermore, it must be considered that not all patients achieving radiographic CR will benefit from surgical resection. In selected cases, particularly when operative risks are substantial and the probability of residual disease is low, surgery may offer limited additional benefit or even potential harm [40]. Therefore, although mRECIST provides a useful non‐invasive tool for assessing treatment response, for patients meeting the criteria for curative treatment, surgical resection remains the most reliable method for obtaining pathological confirmation. However, whether surgical resection can provide durable survival benefits for patients who have achieved radiological CR requires further investigation. Future studies integrating advanced imaging techniques and molecular biomarkers may further improve the ability of radiological assessment to predict pathological response after conversion therapy.

pCR has traditionally been considered a surrogate marker of strong tumour sensitivity and favourable outcomes [23, 24, 41]. In our cohort, pCR showed a trend toward improved RFS, although the difference in OS did not reach statistical significance, likely due to limited follow‐up and a low event rate. Importantly, analyses excluding pCR cases demonstrated that pRVT ≤ 10% remained independently associated with improved OS, and pRVT ≤ 35% with prolonged RFS, indicating that residual viable tumour burden provides prognostic information beyond the binary pCR classification. These findings suggest that pRVT may more comprehensively capture the spectrum of pathological response, with potential implications for postoperative risk stratification and management.

As a continuous and quantifiable parameter, pRVT can identify patients with favourable prognosis even in the absence of pCR. Given its ability to reflect residual tumour burden, pRVT also holds potential as a surrogate endpoint in clinical research. This is particularly relevant for trials evaluating multimodal therapies in HCC, where OS requires prolonged follow‐up and may delay treatment evaluation [19]. If validated prospectively, postoperative pRVT could serve as a clinically meaningful surrogate endpoint for OS, facilitating more efficient assessment of emerging therapeutic strategies. Notably, the use of pRVT ≤ 10% as a surrogate endpoint has been proposed in resectable non‐small cell lung cancer, supporting the potential applicability of this concept in HCC [19].

From a clinical perspective, pRVT‐based stratification may inform postoperative surveillance and treatment strategies. Patients with high pRVT, who have a greater risk of recurrence, may benefit from intensified monitoring, whereas those with low pRVT may be managed with standard follow‐up. In addition, pRVT may help identify patients most likely to benefit from postoperative adjuvant therapy, as poor pathological response may indicate residual micrometastatic disease despite curative resection. In such cases, continuation or re‐initiation of systemic therapy may serve as a consolidation strategy, particularly in patients who previously responded to targeted therapy or immunotherapy.

The role of postoperative adjuvant therapy after curative resection remains under active investigation in HCC. The IMbrave050 trial demonstrated that adjuvant atezolizumab plus bevacizumab significantly improved recurrence‐free survival compared with active surveillance in patients with resectable HCC at high risk of recurrence, although overall survival data remain immature [42]. However, updated analyses have shown that the initial RFS benefit was not sustained with longer follow‐up [43], highlighting the uncertainty regarding the long‐term efficacy of this strategy. In our cohort, patients receiving postoperative adjuvant therapy showed better outcomes than those who did not, suggesting a potential benefit in this setting. Importantly, patients with initially unresectable HCC who undergo salvage resection after multimodal conversion therapy represent a high‐risk population with limited evidence guiding postoperative management [24]. Given the possibility of residual microscopic disease despite curative resection, continuation of systemic therapy after surgery may serve as a consolidation strategy, particularly in patients who previously responded to targeted therapy and immunotherapy. Prospective studies are warranted to further clarify the role of adjuvant therapy in the post‐conversion setting.

Multivariate analysis identified baseline NLR < 2.6 as an independent protective factor for both OS and RFS, highlighting the role of systemic inflammation in treatment response and prognosis. Elevated NLR has been consistently associated with adverse outcomes in HCC [44, 45] and may promote angiogenesis and immune evasion, thereby reducing therapeutic efficacy [46]. In addition, age < 60 years was associated with poorer RFS, consistent with reports suggesting more aggressive tumour biology and higher recurrence risk in younger HCC patients [47, 48]. Notably, pRVT demonstrated stronger prognostic value than traditional histopathological markers such as MVI and satellite nodules in our cohort. Although these features are well‐established predictors of recurrence in early‐stage HCC undergoing upfront surgery [49, 50], they did not retain independent significance after adjustment for pRVT, suggesting that pRVT may better reflect residual malignant potential in the setting of conversion therapy.

We further explored potential predictors of low pRVT (≤ 10% or ≤ 35%), but no clinical variables were significantly associated with pathological response in logistic regression analyses. Although prior studies have suggested that markers such as NLR and AFP may predict tumour response [24], this relationship was not observed in our cohort. These findings highlight the complex and multifactorial nature of tumour regression following multimodal therapy, which may not be adequately captured by conventional clinical parameters. Future studies integrating radiomics, molecular profiling, and immunogenomic approaches may improve the prediction of deep pathological response and facilitate more precise patient selection for conversion strategies.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design and absence of an external validation cohort may have introduced selection and information bias. In addition, our findings apply only to patients who achieved successful conversion and subsequently underwent curative resection after LPLRT, and therefore may not be generalizable to the broader uHCC population, particularly those who did not respond adequately to treatment. The upper age limit of 80 years, reflecting real‐world surgical practice and safety considerations, may also limit extrapolation to very elderly patients. Second, pRVT was assessed only in primary tumours using standardized protocols at each center, but without centralized pathological review or evaluation of nodal or tumour‐thrombus disease, which may have led to underestimation of total pathological burden and some inter‐institutional variability in MPR and pRVT assessment. Third, because the cohort consisted predominantly of HBV‐related HCC, the applicability of these findings to other etiologies, including HCV‐ or NAFLD‐related HCC, remains to be established. Finally, the limited number of patients with extrahepatic metastasis (EHM) precluded meaningful subgroup comparisons between EHM and non‐EHM cases. Despite these limitations, our study provides clinically relevant evidence supporting the prognostic value of pRVT in uHCC patients undergoing conversion surgery after multimodal therapy. Prospective multicenter studies are needed to validate these findings and standardize pathological response assessment.

In conclusion, this study is the first to systematically evaluate the prognostic significance of pRVT in patients with initially uHCC who achieved successful conversion through LPLRT. pRVT thresholds of 10% and 35% optimally predicted OS and RFS, respectively: pRVT ≤ 10% was associated with a clear OS benefit, while pRVT ≤ 35% correlated with prolonged RFS after conversion surgery with LPLRT. These findings suggest that pRVT may provide a more refined basis for prognostic judgement in postoperative risk stratification. Future multicenter prospective studies should validate these thresholds, standardize pRVT assessment, and integrate pRVT with radiologic and molecular biomarkers to guide individualized postoperative management and improve long‐term outcomes in advanced HCC.
