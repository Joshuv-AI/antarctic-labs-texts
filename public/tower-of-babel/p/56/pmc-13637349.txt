A comparison of behavioral health service use among dually eligible, Medicaid-only, and Medicare-only beneficiaries
Authors: Mustafa Karakus, Sushmita Shoma Ghose, Cristian Ilie, Peter Lee, Amanda Harris, N Phil Masiakowski, Victoria Nelson, Michael Ilie, Howard Goldman, Timothy B Creedon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDually eligible individuals, enrolled in both Medicare and Medicaid, have complex medical, social, and behavioral health needs and account for a disproportionate share of spending in both programs. They experience higher rates of poverty, disability, chronic conditions, and behavioral health disorders than Medicaid-only or Medicare-only beneficiaries, yet little is known about their behavioral health service utilization.ObjectiveTo compare behavioral health (BH) service use among Medicaid-only, Medicare-only, and dually eligible beneficiaries.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective analysis using claims data. Individuals with a BH diagnosis were classified into mutually exclusive coverage cohorts: Medicaid-only, Medicare-only, and dually eligible. Cohorts were further stratified into diagnostic groups: mental health (MH)-only, substance use disorder (SUD)-only, co-occurring MH/SUD.ResultsWe identified 147,426,385 individuals enrolled in Medicaid and/or Medicare. 8.7% were dually eligible, 55.8% were Medicaid-only, and 35.5% were Medicare-only. Medicare-only beneficiaries had the lowest utilization rates. Medicaid-only beneficiaries showed intermediate levels of use. Within the dually eligible group, adults 18–54, males (for SUD), White beneficiaries, and urban residents had the highest utilization rates.ConclusionsDually eligible individuals exhibit higher BH service utilization, as compared to Medicaid-only and Medicare -only beneficiaries, suggesting elevated treatment needs.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-026-15025-9.Key Words: Medicaid, Medicare, Dually Eligible, Behavioral health

Background

In 2021, 12.8 million Americans qualified for Medicare due to age (65 and older) or disability, and for Medicaid based on limited income [1, 2]. These individuals are known as “dually eligible” and represent a unique population with complex social, general medical, and behavioral health needs [3]. Individuals who are dually eligible for Medicaid and Medicare can either be fully eligible for Medicaid benefits, or partially eligible for Medicaid benefits. Those who are fully eligible typically have lower incomes and higher rates of poverty than those who are partially eligible [1]. Eighty-seven percent of dually eligible individuals earn less than $20,000 annually, compared to only 20% of Medicare-only beneficiaries [2]. Among those who are eligible for full benefits, 45% earn $10,000 or less per year, whereas only 27% of those with partial benefits (receiving Medicare coverage with minimal contribution from Medicaid for mostly copays) earn $10,000 or less [2]. Eighty percent of dually eligible individuals report at least some health-related social need, such as food insecurity, childcare, housing instability, or transportation challenges, compared to 48% of Medicare-only beneficiaries [4].

Individuals who are dually eligible for Medicaid and Medicare have significantly more health concerns than those who are eligible for Medicaid or Medicare only. For example, 51% of the dually eligible population qualifies for Medicare through disability (by qualifying for Social Security Disability Insurance [SSDI] benefits) rather than age, as compared to 14% of the Medicare only population [1]. Individuals who qualify through disability, meaning they cannot work due to their health, tend to be in poorer health and have more complex healthcare needs than those who qualify based on age [5]. In addition, nearly half (47%) of full-benefit dual-eligible beneficiaries qualified for Medicaid through the Supplemental Security Income (SSI) pathway, a federal income assistance program for individuals with disabilities, blindness, or limited income and resources [6]. Forty-four percent of dually eligible individuals report “fair” or “poor” health and 48% have at least one limitation in activities of daily living (ADLs), compared to just 17 and 23%, respectively, among Medicare-only beneficiaries [2]. Among adults aged 65 and older, dually eligible individuals have the highest rate of multiple chronic conditions, affecting nearly 77% of this population [7]. In addition, 27% of the dually eligible population has six or more chronic conditions, compared to 15% of the Medicare-only population [8].

Dually eligible beneficiaries are also disproportionately affected by behavioral health conditions (mental health conditions and substance use disorders), as compared to beneficiaries who are eligible for Medicare or Medicaid only [9–12]. Forty-seven percent of dually eligible individuals report a diagnosed mental health (MH) condition, a significantly higher rate than among Medicare-only beneficiaries (24%) [2], Medicaid only beneficiaries (29%) [13], those with private insurance (29%) [13], and individuals who are uninsured (20%) [13]. Furthermore, approximately 11% of the dually eligible population have a substance use disorder (SUD), compared to 6% among Medicare-only beneficiaries [12, 14]. Despite this elevated prevalence of behavioral health conditions, there is a striking lack of disaggregated data on behavioral health service utilization among dually eligible individuals.

In 2024, the Medicare Payment Advisory Commission (MedPAC) and the Medicaid and CHIP Payment and Access Commission (MACPAC) [1] jointly released a data book highlighting the complex care needs and substantial costs associated with the dually eligible population. Although they make up a small share of Medicare and Medicaid beneficiaries, dually eligible individuals account for a disproportionately large share of spending in both programs. They are more likely to rely on high-cost services such as hospital care, skilled nursing care, and long-term care compared to Medicare-only or Medicaid-only populations [1] reflecting their greater health needs and more intensive service use. Although the MedPAC–MACPAC Data Book offers valuable insights into the dually eligible population, the analyses report healthcare utilization in aggregate, combining physical and behavioral health services without distinction [1].

Understanding how this population accesses and uses MH and SUD services—compared to Medicaid-only and Medicare-only groups—is critical for identifying gaps in behavioral health care, designing targeted interventions, and informing federal and state policies that support integrated and equitable service delivery. Without this insight, efforts to improve overall health outcomes and reduce avoidable costs may fall short.

The objective of this study was to examine differences in behavioral health service use across three insurance eligibility cohorts: Medicaid-only; Medicare-only; and dually eligible. Patterns for these cohorts are compared for those with MH diagnoses only, SUD diagnoses only, and co-occurring MH and SUD diagnoses.

New contributions

While prior studies have compared healthcare utilization among Medicaid-only, Medicare-only, and dually eligible beneficiaries, they have typically examined overall healthcare use. This study disaggregates data to focus specifically on behavioral health (BH) service utilization across these groups. Given the significantly higher rates of BH diagnoses among dually eligible beneficiaries, understanding their BH service use has important implications for policymakers and healthcare funders.

Methods

In this study, we used 2020–2022 Medicaid and Medicare claims data to examine and compare behavioral health disorder prevalence and BH service use rates in 2021 across three insurance cohorts: (1) dually enrolled for Medicaid and Medicare, (2) only enrolled for Medicaid (Medicaid-only), and (3) only enrolled for Medicare (Medicare-only).

Within each cohort, we further categorized individuals into three mutually exclusive diagnostic groups: (1) mental health (MH) only, (2) substance use disorder (SUD) only, and (3) co-occurring MH and SUD. We used national Medicare and Medicaid research identifiable files (RIF) for service year 2021, with condition identification extending into 2020, and service monitoring into 2022.

Medicaid data were drawn from the 2021 Transformed Medicaid Statistical Information System (T-MSIS) Analytic Files (TAF). TAF includes enrollment, claims, and encounter records for beneficiaries in both Medicaid Fee-for-service (FFS) and Medicaid managed care organizations (MCOs). We used the Demographic and Eligibility file and the inpatient (IP), other services (OT), long-term care (LT), and pharmacy (RX) files.

Medicare data were obtained from the CMS Chronic Condition Data Warehouse and included 100% of beneficiaries across both FFS and Medicare Advantage (MA) delivery systems. We used the Master Beneficiary Summary File for enrollment and demographic information, Medicare FFS claims (inpatient, outpatient, carrier, skilled nursing facility, home health, and Part D), and MA encounter and Part D event files.

Dual eligibility status was identified via CMS beneficiary summary data accessed through the Virtual Research Data Center. For dual-eligible beneficiaries, utilization was measured using both Medicare RIF claims/encounter data and Medicaid T-MSIS claims/encounter data, allowing capture of services financed by either program.

Behavioral health diagnostic groups

Behavioral health diagnostic groups were consistently identified across both Medicare and Medicaid datasets using all available diagnosis codes from claims files. For alcohol use disorder (AUD) and opioid use disorder (OUD) these classifications were further supplemented with National Drug Codes (NDCs). Beneficiaries were classified as having a behavioral health condition if they met at least one of the following criteria in 2021:

One inpatient claim with a relevant behavioral health diagnosis,

One inpatient claim with a relevant behavioral health diagnosis,

Two or more outpatient/professional claims with a behavioral health diagnosis,

Two or more outpatient/professional claims with a behavioral health diagnosis,

One pharmacy script for AUD or OUD.

One pharmacy script for AUD or OUD.

All beneficiaries with at least one month of enrollment in their respective coverage program during 2021 were included. In the CMS Medicare–Medicaid eligibility data, a beneficiary is generally considered enrolled for an entire month if they are eligible at any point during that month. The Master Beneficiary Summary File (MBSF) captures enrollment and dual-eligibility status using monthly indicators (01–12) and does not typically provide partial-month detail.

To assess missing demographic information, we examine records with “unknown” values. These reflect beneficiaries who are present in the dataset but lack reported data for specific fields. The proportion of unknown values varies by category: age (0.1%), sex (0.1%), race (2.4% to 16.1%), and geography (0.2% to 1.1%, depending on coverage status). These percentages represent the share of beneficiaries with unreported information in each demographic category. Beneficiaries with behavioral health diagnoses were identified regardless of whether they were newly recorded in 2021 or preceded the year.

Utilization outcomes

Utilization outcomes were restricted to behavioral health–related services. Inpatient admissions, outpatient visits, emergency department encounters, and pharmacotherapy were classified as behavioral health services if they contained a qualifying behavioral health diagnosis, procedure, revenue, or pharmacy code consistent with the study’s service identification framework. Thus, reported utilization reflects behavioral health–specific care among beneficiaries with treated behavioral health conditions, rather than overall healthcare use.

We used procedural codes (International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision, Procedure Coding System, Healthcare Common Procedure Coding System, Current Procedural Terminology) and revenue center codes to identify multiple categories of behavioral health service use (including administration of psychiatric medications). We also used NDCs to identify claims for psychiatric medications dispensed from pharmacies.

We assessed BH service utilization with three annualized measures: (1) the percentage of beneficiaries using services; (2) the number of services per 1,000 enrollees; and (3) the average number of services per user. For dually eligible individuals, we also calculated the number of services per 1,000 enrollees by age, sex, race, and urban/rural status (based on Rural-Urban Commuting Area code).

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the primary analyses across enrollment and coverage subgroups. The study population was stratified by dual-eligibility type, including full dual beneficiaries, who receive comprehensive Medicare and Medicaid benefits, and partial dual beneficiaries, who receive Medicare assistance through Medicaid but do not qualify for full Medicaid benefits. Additional stratifications examined delivery system characteristics, including Medicare Advantage versus Medicare fee-for-service (FFS) enrollment and Medicaid managed care organization (MCO) versus Medicaid FFS enrollment. Analyses also applied a 12-month continuous enrollment requirement. Demographic characteristics, service utilization, and diagnostic patterns were examined across these subgroup analyses, with detailed results reported in Additional File 1.

Because the study uses national administrative data representing a near-census of beneficiaries in 2021 and employs a large-sample approach intended to identify the full population of beneficiaries, the very large sample size would likely render most (if not all) statistical tests significant. Therefore, we did not estimate measures of statistical uncertainty (standard errors) or conduct hypothesis testing of between-group differences, and the reported percentages and utilization rates should be interpreted as observed population values describing utilization patterns during the study period rather than as projections of future program experience.

This study was reviewed by the institutional review board (IRB) at Westat and found to be exempt. This research was conducted according to U.S. Health and Human Services Research Ethics based on the Belmont Report / U.S. Common Rule (45 CFR 46) and consent to participate are not applicable because we are utilizing administrative claims data.

Among dually eligible individuals, 59.3% had a MH-only diagnosis, 4.4% had a SUD-only diagnosis, and 3.7% had co-occurring MH and SUD diagnoses. About 32.6% of dually eligible beneficiaries did not have a current MH or SUD diagnosis.

Overall, dually eligible beneficiaries showed higher rates of MH, SUD, and co-occurring conditions compared to those in the Medicaid-only and Medicare-only cohorts. In comparison to the dually eligible group, 18.5% of Medicaid-only and 30.8% of Medicare-only beneficiaries had a MH-only diagnosis. SUD-only diagnoses were found among 2.1% of Medicaid-only beneficiaries and 0.9% of Medicare-only beneficiaries, while co-occurring MH and SUD diagnoses were present in just 1.6% and 0.6%, respectively. Individuals with no behavioral health diagnosis represent 32.6% of dually eligible beneficiaries, 77.8% of Medicaid-only, and 67.7% of Medicare-only populations. Given this study’s focus on behavioral health service use, we limited our analysis to individuals with a documented mental health or substance use disorder diagnosis, excluding those without such diagnoses even if they may have accessed behavioral health services.

Medicaid-only beneficiaries tend to be younger and lower-income, and they show a more balanced distribution between MH-only and co-occurring diagnoses. MH-only rates are highest in the 55–64 age group (37.6%); co-occurring diagnoses are relatively elevated in the 35–54 (4.0%) and 55–64 (3.4%) age ranges. Unlike Medicare populations, both males and females in this group show comparable rates of co-occurring conditions (1.8% vs. 1.5%, respectively). White beneficiaries have the highest MH (27.0%) and co-occurring (2.7%) rates, while Black and Hispanic beneficiaries show lower behavioral health diagnoses across the board. The urban-rural split is minimal in this group, though slightly higher co-occurrence is seen in urban areas (1.7% vs. 1.4%).

In contrast, Medicare-only beneficiaries, who are predominantly older adults, exhibit a behavioral health profile skewed toward MH-only diagnoses, especially in the 55–64 (45.9%) and 75+ (33.0%) age groups. Co-occurring and SUD-only diagnoses remain low across all demographics in this group, with co-occurring rates below 3.0%. Females (37.0%) and White beneficiaries (32.6%) are most likely to have MH-only diagnoses, but all racial groups in this coverage category show relatively lower SUD and co-occurring prevalence.

Sensitivity analyses applying a 12-month continuous enrollment requirement and stratifying by coverage type showed similar demographic and diagnostic patterns across groups, although some variation in prevalence levels was observed across delivery systems and eligibility types. Notably, full duals generally exhibited higher prevalence of MH, SUD, and co-occurring conditions than partial duals (Additional File 1 Tables A1–A3).

Among beneficiaries with treated behavioral health conditions, dual-eligible beneficiaries had higher behavioral health–related inpatient and outpatient utilization. For individuals with a MH-only diagnosis, 15.2% used inpatient services and 24.3% used specialized outpatient services. In contrast, 16.3% of Medicaid-only beneficiaries and 8.1% of Medicare-only beneficiaries used inpatient services.

Service utilizations by insurance type and by diagnosis, 2021

Dually eligible beneficiaries also had the highest service intensity among those with MH only diagnoses, with 254.1 inpatient services per 1,000 beneficiaries and 963.3 outpatient services, compared to significantly lower rates in the other two insurance groups. Pharmacotherapy for beneficiaries with MH only diagnoses was utilized by 46.9% of dual-eligible beneficiaries, a rate much higher than Medicare-only beneficiaries (23%) and lower than Medicaid-only beneficiaries (90.2%). For SUD-only beneficiaries, dually eligible individuals also had the highest usage of services, with 55.4% using inpatient services and 49.3% using specialized outpatient services. Medicaid-only beneficiaries had the highest rate of inpatient service use (57.9%) and a higher rate of specialized outpatient services (27.1%). Medicare-only beneficiaries had notably lower rates, with only 46.7% using inpatient services and 36.6% using outpatient services. Dually eligible beneficiaries had the highest intensity for both inpatient (82.4 per 1,000 beneficiaries) and outpatient services (149.1 per 1,000). Additionally, 7.7% of dually eligible beneficiaries used medications for alcohol use disorder / medications for opioid use disorder (MAUD / MOUD) services in office, which was higher than the rates for Medicaid-only beneficiaries (7.7%) and Medicare-only beneficiaries (2.1%).

Co-occurring MH and SUD beneficiaries followed similar patterns. Dually eligible beneficiaries had the highest service utilization, with 56.4% using inpatient services and 51.2% using specialized outpatient services. Medicare-only beneficiaries had the lowest service usage in this group, with 46.6% using inpatient services and 37.8% using outpatient services. Medicaid-only beneficiaries had the highest inpatient (58.5%) and outpatient (30.3%) usage. Service intensity was also highest among dually eligible beneficiaries, with 74.2 inpatient services and 136.7 outpatient services per 1,000 beneficiaries.

Overall, dually eligible individuals had generally the highest rates of service use and intensity across all behavioral health conditions. Medicare-only beneficiaries had the lowest service utilization, especially for inpatient and outpatient services. While Medicare provides coverage for a broad range of behavioral health services, it is not subject to such requirements as the Mental Health Parity and Addiction Equity Act as is Medicaid and many commercial health plans. [15]. Accordingly, Medicare coverage may be more limited in some ways than commercial insurance or Medicaid; for instance, Medicare behavioral health coverage is subject to a 190—day lifetime limit on inpatient psychiatric care [16]. Medicaid-only beneficiaries demonstrated high utilization for both mental health and substance use services, particularly for pharmacotherapy.

Sensitivity analyses using continuous enrollment and disaggregating by coverage type yielded consistent overall patterns, with dually eligible beneficiaries maintaining the highest utilization and service intensity. Absolute utilization levels varied across delivery systems and eligibility types, with full duals generally exhibiting higher service intensity and partial duals sometimes showing higher proportions of service use (Additional File 1 Tables A4–A6).

Differences by delivery system (e.g., Medicare Advantage vs. FFS; Medicaid MCO vs. FFS) influenced measured utilization levels but did not alter the overall ordering of utilization across insurance groups.

The findings presented in Table 4 highlight important differences in BH service utilization among the dually eligible population, stratified by age, sex, race, and geography. Age emerges as a key factor in MH and SUD service use patterns. For example, children under 18 with MH-only diagnoses have the highest outpatient use (2,133 per 1,000 beneficiaries), while their SUD and co-occurring conditions show minimal service use, reflecting the relative rarity of SUD in this age group. Young to middle-aged adults (18–54) demonstrate the highest service utilization across nearly all categories—particularly emergency room (ER) visits and inpatient stays for individuals with SUD or co-occurring diagnoses. Utilization rates decline steadily with age, with the lowest levels consistently observed among those individuals aged 75 and older across all diagnosis categories, which may be, in part, attributable to MH stigma for this group [17, 18].

Sex-based differences are also notable. Females with MH-only diagnoses use outpatient and ER services more frequently than their male counterparts. However, for those with SUD or co-occurring disorders, males surpass females in all service types, particularly in inpatient and outpatient care.

Geographic differences reveal that urban residents have higher behavioral health service use across all diagnostic categories, especially in ER and outpatient settings. In contrast, rural beneficiaries show lower service use, particularly in inpatient and ER care related to SUD.

Discussion

This study highlights important differences in behavioral health diagnoses and service utilization among the dually eligible beneficiaries relative to Medicaid-only and Medicare-only populations. In general, dually eligible beneficiaries have higher rates of behavioral health conditions and service utilizations.

This study found that individuals who are dually enrolled in Medicaid and Medicare experience higher prevalence of MH-only, SUD-only, and co-occurring diagnoses than either Medicaid-only or Medicare-only populations, a finding that is consistent with previous literature that report higher health and socioeconomic risk factors in dually eligible populations, including high levels of disability and poverty [2, 9, 10, 11].

Dually eligible individuals also had the most complex utilization patterns, especially for inpatient care, specialized outpatient services, and emergency department (ED) services across all diagnostic categories. The rates of MAUD and MOUD and residential care were also highest among the dually eligible and Medicaid-only groups, further underscoring their elevated treatment needs compared to Medicare-only beneficiaries.

In contrast, Medicare-only beneficiaries, who are typically older, displayed lower prevalence and service utilization across behavioral health conditions. Their diagnostic patterns skewed heavily toward MH-only conditions, with very limited representation in SUD-only and co-occurring groups. These trends are also consistent with previous research [10]. Their engagement with behavioral health services was significantly lower across almost all service categories.

Medicaid-only beneficiaries fell between the other two groups, exhibiting moderate levels of diagnosis prevalence and service utilization, with greater SUD-related treatment use than Medicare-only beneficiaries but lower than dually eligible individuals [8].

Demographic variations within the dually eligible population further reveal interesting patterns. Adults aged 35–64 bear the greatest burden of behavioral health conditions, particularly co-occurring disorders, and exhibit the highest rates of service utilization. Sex-based analyses show that females are more likely to have MH-only diagnoses and use outpatient care more frequently. Males, especially those with SUD or co-occurring diagnoses, are more likely to utilize inpatient and ED services. This may suggest that men are more likely to present with more acute or severe substance-related issues, potentially because they delay seeking care until conditions worsen, requiring more intensive treatment.

Geographic differences were clear, with urban residents accessing services more frequently than their rural counterparts, suggesting access barriers in less densely populated areas. This may reflect better access to healthcare facilities and services in urban areas, or access barriers in rural areas [19, 20].

It is important to note that because the study uses national administrative data representing a near-census of beneficiaries during 2021, reported percentages and utilization rates reflect observed population values rather than sample estimates. These findings therefore describe utilization patterns during the study year and should not be interpreted as projections of future program experience. Changes in policy, eligibility, or delivery systems may influence patterns in subsequent years. Further, while formal confidence intervals were not calculated, observed differences across groups are large in magnitude and consistent across multiple utilization measures, suggesting that interpretation is unlikely to hinge on small random fluctuations.

These findings have important implications for policy and program design. They emphasize that while dually eligible individuals as a whole face significant behavioral health challenges, their experiences and needs vary widely across subpopulations, necessitating targeted and individualized approaches to care. Tailored strategies that address the high and complex needs of the dually eligible population, particularly those with co-occurring conditions, are critical.

As healthcare systems evolve toward integrated and value-based models, improving behavioral health care delivery for dually eligible individuals should remain a central priority. In addition, potential changes to Medicaid may impact service utilization among individuals with complex, above-average needs, particularly those who are dually eligible or rely solely on Medicaid, relative to those covered exclusively by Medicare.

Limitations

This study relies on administrative claims and encounter data from multiple delivery systems, including Medicare fee-for-service, Medicare Advantage, Medicaid fee-for-service, and Medicaid managed care. These systems differ in coding incentives, encounter reporting completeness, and diagnosis field availability. For example, risk-adjustment incentives in Medicare Advantage may increase diagnostic coding intensity, while encounter data in managed care settings may incompletely capture services relative to adjudicated claims. Structural differences in diagnosis reporting across Medicare and Medicaid datasets may also affect measured prevalence. As a result, observed differences across enrollee groups may partially reflect variation in documentation practices rather than underlying clinical differences.

Measurement of utilization among dual-eligible beneficiaries may be affected by variation in Medicaid encounter completeness and state reporting practices, particularly for services financed primarily by Medicaid. In addition, Medicaid-only beneficiaries are more likely to experience enrollment churn, which may limit observability of diagnoses and services occurring outside covered months. In contrast, dual-eligible beneficiaries retain continuous Medicare coverage even when Medicaid eligibility fluctuates. These factors may lead to conservative estimates of utilization among Medicaid-only populations relative to duals. In addition, sensitivity analyses looking at the difference between partial dual eligible beneficiaries and fully dual eligible beneficiaries, further stratified by delivery system showed variation in measured utilization levels, but these findings may possibly reflect differences in coding practices and encounter data completeness rather than underlying differences in care.

The study period overlaps with the COVID-19 pandemic, which disrupted healthcare delivery and accelerated the use of telehealth, potentially affecting absolute utilization levels. However, because all enrollee groups were observed during the same period, relative comparisons are less likely to be driven solely by pandemic-related effects. Finally, dual-eligible beneficiaries represent a heterogeneous population that includes both full- and partial-benefit duals. Consistent with national reporting conventions, these subgroups were analyzed together; future research could examine within-group differences in greater detail.

Conclusion and future directions

This study demonstrates that dually eligible beneficiaries experience disproportionately higher prevalence of behavioral health conditions and markedly greater utilization of inpatient, outpatient, and emergency department services compared to Medicaid-only and Medicare-only populations. These patterns persist across diagnostic categories and demographic subgroups, highlighting the complex and multifaceted behavioral health needs of the dually eligible population. Differences in utilization may reflect variation in clinical complexity, coverage design, access to services, stigma, care preferences, or delivery system characteristics. The findings underscore the critical importance of integrated, data-driven, and inclusive strategies to behavioral health service delivery. Tailored interventions that account for variations by age, sex, race, and geography are essential to improving access, quality, and outcomes for this high-need group. Future research should examine the effectiveness of integrated behavioral health models and explore the impact of structural and policy-level interventions aimed at reducing differences in care access and outcomes.
