Evaluation of the therapeutic potential of Curcumin and Methylprednisolone in mental nerve injury: a histological and immunohistochemical study in rats
Authors: Oya Törün, Onur Kutlu, Nihat Laçin, Selçuk Tunik, Ercan Ayaz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to evaluate the roles of Curcumin and Methylprednisolone on S100 protein and Beta-3 expression levels in healing process of the mental nerve injury. 30 adult Wistar rats were included which were 3 months old and had a body weight of 200 to 250 g. Rats were divided randomly into 3 groups. The left mental nerve of each rat was surgically exposed under sterile conditions. Pressure was applied for 60 s using Yaşargil aneurysm clips, which are commonly used in experimental models due to their consistent and precise pressure application. Group 1 was the control which did not receive any treatment. Rats in the Group 2 were administered 100 mg/kg Curcuma Longa extract; in Group 3 subjects received 2 mg/kg Methylprednisolone intraperitoneally every day for 14 days. After the one-month recovery period, sacrification procedure was performed for histopathological and immunohistochemical evaluation. Mental nerve regeneration was assessed using Beta-3, S-100ß, and Hematoxylin and Eosin parameters. Comparative analyses were conducted using non-parametric statistical methods such as the Kruskal-Wallis and Mann-Whitney U tests. Both curcumin and methylprednisolone significantly increased Beta-3 Tubulin levels compared to the control group (p < 0.05). However, no significant difference was observed between the two treatment groups, suggesting that curcumin and methylprednisolone have comparable effects on nerve regeneration, as indicated by the upregulation of Beta-3 Tubulin, a marker associated with neuronal repair and growth. Immunohistochemical findings reveal that curcumin has a strong effect on S100β expression (p < 0,0001), potentially more pronounced than methylprednisolone. Regarding the clinical significance of the observed S100β upregulation by curcumin, this finding suggests a potential neuroprotective effect. Mental nerve injury is a frequent complication of oral and maxillofacial surgery, and its management and prognosis pose significant clinical challenges. This study highlights the potential of curcumin as an effective therapeutic agent for promoting peripheral nerve regeneration and functional recovery, offering promising implications for the treatment of nerve injuries.Keywords: Curcumin, Peripheral nerve injury, Methylprednisolone, RegenerationSubject terms: Neurophysiology, Preclinical research

Introduction

Peripheral nerve injuries, especially on branches of trigeminal nerve could occur consequently after various maxillofacial procedures, including third molar extractions, orthognathic surgeries, maxillofacial trauma, implant placements, pathological surgeries, or salivary gland surgeries. It was reported in a survey of the iatrogenic trigeminal nerve injury, that mental nerve injuries lead to persistent sensory disturbances in 50–70% of cases post-surgery, compared to 30–50% for lingual nerve injuries, highlighting the clinical burden1. The nerves most affected by such injuries are the inferior alveolar nerve (IAN), lingual nerve (LN), and infraorbital nerve (ION)2. The mental nerve in rodents is comparable in size and function to that in humans, while being easily accessible surgically3,4. Seddon categorized nerve injuries based on the severity of damage into three types: neurapraxia, axonotmesis, and neurotmesis5. Among these, axonotmesis, often resulting from crush injuries to peripheral nerves, leads to degeneration of the distal nerve stump, a process known as Wallerian degeneration. This classification provides a critical framework for evaluating potential treatment strategies, including those targeting mental nerve injuries in experimental models such as rats3,6,7. While there is no universally approved treatment that extends beyond conservative management and surgical interventions for the various types of nerve injury, numerous agents have shown positive and promising effects in enhancing nerve recovery8–10.

Curcumin, another agent under investigation, exhibits neuroprotective and anti-inflammatory effects11. The notion that many modern medicines may not necessarily surpass traditional or generic medicines in terms of safety, efficacy, and affordability has prompted a renewed interest in exploring the therapeutic potential of age-old remedies. Curcumin, the active compound in turmeric (Curcuma longa), is a notable example of such a remedy, with a history deeply rooted in the traditional Ayurvedic system of medicine12. Curcumin is demonstrating a wide spectrum of pharmacological activities, including anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, anticancer, immunomodulatory, neuroprotective, antiproliferative and antibacterial properties, positioning it as a highly promising therapeutic candidate13. It has been demonstrated to modulate apoptotic pathways, protect Schwann cells critical for myelin formation and axonal repair, and reduce oxidative stress in injured nerves14,15.

Methylprednisolone, a corticosteroid with potent anti-inflammatory properties, has shown promise in the treatment of peripheral nerve injuries, including mental nerve injuries3. Its mechanism of action involves suppressing the production of inflammatory cytokines, thereby reducing edema, and minimizing the detrimental effects of inflammation on the healing process. By mitigating these factors, methylprednisolone helps create an environment conducive to axonal regeneration and repair. Additionally, studies in animal models have demonstrated improved motor and sensory recovery following methylprednisolone administration16,17.

Preclinical studies conducted on rats have investigated the potential of various agents to enhance recovery following peripheral nerve injuries3,13,18,19. Among these, curcumin and methylprednisolone have been explored for their ability to mitigate degeneration, promote axonal regeneration, and support functional recovery.

The main aim of our study is to assess the therapeutic potential of curcumin and methylprednisolone in promoting recovery from mental nerve injuries. By comparing their impact on histopathological and functional outcomes, we seek to identify which agent provides superior benefits in reducing inflammation, preserving nerve structure, and accelerating regeneration. The findings aim to contribute to the development of effective treatment strategies for peripheral nerve injuries.

Methods

Ethical statement

This study was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Ege University for experimental research on animals with the 2018-008 approval number.

Study design

All experimental procedures were carried out in strict compliance with the ARRIVE (Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments) Guidelines (PLoS Bio 8(6), e1000412,2010​) to ensure the ethical and accurate reporting of animal research by the same surgeon using standard microsurgical techniques. All animals received humane care in compliance with “The Principles of Laboratory Animal Care” formulated by the National Society of Medical Research and “The Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals” published by the U.S. The animals were housed at a set temperature (24 °C) in a humidity-controlled room with a 12-h light/dark photoperiod. By selecting 30 rats which were 3 months old, male and had a body weight of 200 to 250 g (10 animals per group), the researchers ensure that enough animals are used to provide reliable data without over-exerting animal use. This sample size ensured that sufficient biological variability was being captured, making the results more reliable.

Surgical procedure

The rats were anesthetized by intraperitoneal administration of 100 mg/kg Ketamine HCl (Ketalar, Pfizer) combined with 5 mg/kg Xylazine HCl (Rompun, Bayer, Germany). Articaine solution 0.2 mL was injected subcutaneously for hemostasis. Povidone-iodine (Poviiodex, Kimpa, Turkey) solution was applied to the surgical site to facilitate the antisepsis. Longitudinal 1 cm dermal incision was made in the middle of the neck for mental nerve exposure and sharp dissection was performed. Left mental nerves were exposed in all rats and crush injury was performed on mental nerves with Yaşargil aneurysm clips for 60 s (Fig. 1). After completion of the surgical procedures, the inner tissues were sutured using 4/0 absorbable suture (Vicryl, Ethicon, Brussels, Belgium) to facilitate internal healing and 4/0 silk suture (Doğsan, Turkey) was utilized to secure the outer layer and protect the wound site. Group 1 was the control group and rats in this group were only given 1 ml of saline intraperitoneally for 14 days.

Left mental nerves were exposed in all rats and crush injury was performed on mental nerves with Yaşargil aneurysm clips for 60 s (a). Mental nerve after crush injured (b).

Postoperative care

To manage infection and pain, rats received daily intramuscular injections of enrofloxacin (Baytril-K®, 2.5 mg/kg) and meloxicam (Maxicam®, 1 mg/kg) for five days following the surgery.

Administration of Curcumin and Methylprednisolone

Curcumin was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA) as powder form. Curcumin powder was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and subsequently mixed with 0.9% NaCl. Rats in the Group 2 were injected 100 mg/kg curcuma longa extract intraperitoneally every day for 14 days. In Group 3, rats were administered daily 2 mg/kg Prednol-L 40 mg (GENSENTA, İstanbul) intraperitoneally for 14 days.

Sacrification procedure

After one-month healing period, the sacrification procedure was performed under general anesthesia induced by 100 mg/kg Ketamine HCl (Ketalar, Pfizer) by cervical dislocation to the subjects. Mental nerve tissue was excised for histological analysis and samples were fixed in 10% formalin solution.

Histomorphometry analysis

Histopathological examination and histomorphometry measurements were made in the laboratory of Dicle University Faculty of Medicine, Department of Histology Embryology. Tissue sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for histopathological analysis and subsequently examined using a light microscope. (BX 43, Olympus).

Immunohistochemical analysis

The sections were first transferred to distilled water, and tissue borders were outlined using Dakopen (lot 008877, Thermo Fisher, MA, USA). Subsequently, the sections were immersed in phosphate buffer solution (PBS) and treated with 3% H2O2 solution for 20 min to quench endogenous peroxidase activity. The slices were then washed three times with PBS, each wash lasting 5 min, and treated with a blocking solution (lot 1754084 A, Life Technologies, Frederick, MD, USA) for 10 min to prevent nonspecific antibody binding. The blocking solution was removed, and the sections were incubated overnight at + 4 °C with primary antibodies targeting beta III-tubulin (ab18207, Abcam, Boston, USA) and S100 beta (ab52642, Abcam, Boston, USA) to facilitate immunohistochemical analysis. To detect the immunohistochemical reaction, the sections were treated with the DAB-Plus substrate kit (lot#1636518A, Life Technologies, Frederick, MD, USA) for 4–6 min. Contrast staining was achieved using Gill III Hematoxylin (cat#1051740500, Merck, Darmstadt, Germany), followed by rinsing the sections under running water for 45 s. The slides were then mounted with Entellan mounting medium (lot#1079610100, Merck, Darmstadt, Germany). Images of the stained sections were captured at different magnifications using a Zeiss Imager A2 light microscope. To assess the immunoreactivity of regenerated axons following nerve injury, beta III tubulin primary antibody was applied. The S100ß primary antibody was used to analyze the presence of Schwann cells and the level of inflammation. During the immunohistochemical evaluation, a scoring system was implemented based on the proportion of positively stained cells and areas. The proportion of positively stained cells and areas was evaluated under a Zeiss Imager A2 light microscope at high (20 μm bar scale) and low (50 μm bar scale) magnifications. The scoring was based on the percentage of stained axons (Beta-3 Tubulin) or Schwann cells (S100β) relative to the total field: 0 (< 5%), 1 (5–25%), 2 (26–50%), 3 (51–75%), and 4 (> 75%). Staining intensity was categorized into five levels: no staining (0), weak (1), moderate (2), strong (3), and very strong (4).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using R version 3.2.3 (released on December 10, 2015), with copyright attributed to The R Foundation for Statistical Computing. Non-parametric tests, specifically the Kruskal-Wallis and Mann-Whitney U tests, were applied for comparison. A significance level of (p < 0.05) was considered indicative of a statistically significant result.

Discussion

Peripheral nerves play a crucial role in transmitting motor, sensorimotor and endocrine signals between the central nervous system and the rest of the body. Injuries to peripheral nerves, whether due to trauma, surgery, or pathological conditions, can lead to significant functional impairments, such as loss of sensation, motor dysfunction, or chronic pain20,21. Given its clinical relevance, developing reliable experimental models for mental nerve injuries is essential for understanding the healing mechanisms and testing therapeutic interventions6,7. The mental nerve in rodents is comparable in size and function to that of humans, while being easy to access, thereby making it a more practical choice3,6. As indicated in the literature, a considerable challenge with the crush injury model is the lack of standardized methods of force application19.The Yaşargil aneurysm clips, which provide a consistent compression force of 1.77 N, were used in our study to attempt to resolve this standardization issue. Sarikcioglu et al. underlined the reliability of this approach by demonstrating that Yaşargil aneurysm clips provide reproducible levels of injury in rat optic nerves, thus providing a reliable model for neurological research22,23. Unlike central nervous system injuries, peripheral nerves are not life threatening and have some capacity for regeneration, but this process is often incomplete or inefficient, particularly in severe cases24,25. Various therapeutic approaches have been explored to enhance trigeminal nerve injuries, including surgical repair techniques, growth factors, neuroprotective agents, and anti-inflammatory drugs2,26,27. By targeting processes such as inflammation, oxidative stress, and cellular apoptosis, these treatments aim to create a more favorable environment for nerve regeneration and functional recovery18,28,29.

Curcumin on nerve regeneration

The notion that many modern medicines may not necessarily surpass traditional or generic medicines in terms of safety, efficacy, and affordability has prompted a renewed interest in exploring the therapeutic potential of age-old remedies14. However, its clinical administration encounters significant limitations due to its low solubility in water, instability in the bloodstream, rapid metabolic degradation, poor bioavailability, and limited penetration capacity through the blood-brain barrier (BBB) poses a significant barrier to its clinical use for mental nerve injuries. Nano formulations, such as liposomes, PLGA nanoparticles, and hydrogels, offer promising solutions by enhancing solubility, stability, and targeted delivery, potentially amplifying the regenerative effects observed in the study30,31. Lower doses (e.g., 50–100 mg/kg) are often sufficient to reduce inflammation and oxidative stress, which are key contributors to nerve damage32,33. Higher doses (e.g., 200–300 mg/kg) of curcumin are associated with enhanced neuroprotective effects, including improved schwann cell activity, reduced apoptosis, and accelerated axonal regeneration11,34. The dose of 100 mg/kg is consistent with many preclinical studies and is considered effective in rodents for reducing inflammation and promoting regeneration, therefore we preferred this dose for our crush type mental nerve injury treatment to rats35. Daily administration for 4 to 8 weeks post-injury is common in experimental designs, reflecting the sustained need for curcumin’s effects during the phases of inflammation resolution and axonal regeneration36. However, for acute and subacute phases of nerve recovery, 14-day treatment period, which we administered, aligns with curcumin’s effects on inflammation resolution and axonal repair are most crucial and sufficient37.

S100β is a marker of Schwann cell activity and is involved in their proliferation and migration to the injury site Recent studies suggest that curcumin may exert its effects on nerve regeneration by interacting with S100ß proteins. By modulating the expression and activity of these proteins, particularly S100ß, curcumin could reduce neuroinflammation and glial activation, which are known to impede the regeneration process19. While the exact mechanisms by which curcumin interacts with S100ß proteins and promotes nerve regeneration are still under investigation, current evidence highlights its potential as a therapeutic agent in nerve injury and neurodegenerative disease. Curcumin’s upregulation of S100β likely enhances nerve regeneration by activating Schwann cells to proliferate, migrate, myelinate axons, secrete neurotrophic factors, and reduce excessive scarring38. Our immunohistochemical results show a strong S100β immune reaction in the curcumin group (scored as 2.70), significantly higher than the control (0.60) and methylprednisolone (1.70) groups. This suggests that curcumin enhances Schwann cell activation more effectively than methylprednisolone, potentially due to its broader mechanistic profile (Table 1). Further clinical studies are needed to fully understand the interaction between curcumin, S100 proteins, and the cellular processes involved in nerve regeneration.

Methylprednisolone on nerve regeneration

Production of proteins within the neuron is moderately regulated by corticosteroids at normal physiological conditions29. By reducing the inflammatory cascade and oxidative stress, they may help preserve the structural integrity of the injured nerve, preventing further degeneration of axons and supporting the regeneration process17,39. In rodent models, doses of 2 mg–50 mg/kg intraperitoneally or subcutaneously have been used. These dosages have been shown to reduce inflammation and improve regeneration3,17 and we also preferred 2 mg/kg Prednol-L 40 mg intraperitoneally administration to rats. Our results revealed that methylprednisolone had significantly higher anti-inflammatory efficacy compared to control group (p < 0.05). Emerging evidence from experimental models suggests that corticosteroids could serve as an effective adjunct therapy in peripheral nerve repair, especially when combined with other treatment modalities such as neuroprotective agents or surgical interventions17,40.

Long-term corticosteroid use can lead to dependency, requiring tapering to avoid withdrawal symptoms such as rebound inflammation or adrenal insufficiency. Al- Mossawi et al.41 highlighted that abrupt cessation after 2 weeks of moderate doses (e.g., 2 mg/kg in rats) can increase inflammation by 30–40% within 48 h, complicating postoperative management. Johansson and Bennett16 noted that high-dose methylprednisolone (e.g., 30 mg/kg) in rats delayed axonal regeneration by 10–20% in some models due to inhibited growth factor expression. The limitations of corticosteroids—systemic side effects, delayed healing, variable efficacy, and dependency issues—highlight the need for safer and more versatile alternatives like curcumin42. Our findings, demonstrating curcumin’s potent S100β upregulation and anti-inflammatory effects without the adverse profile of methylprednisolone, support its potential as a therapeutic agent for mental nerve injuries. Curcumin’s effects could complement corticosteroids by addressing oxidative stress and apoptosis, which corticosteroids do not target effectively. The study’s comparable Beta-3 Tubulin levels between curcumin and methylprednisolone groups (mean ranks 18.80 vs. 20.10) (Table 1) indicate similar axonal growth potential, but curcumin’s additional benefits could improve outcomes in chronic or complex injuries where corticosteroids fail.

Conclusion

In this study, we evaluated the effects of curcumin and methylprednisolone on mental nerve regeneration in rats following crush injury. Combination therapy could mitigate inflammation early (methylprednisolone) while promoting long-term regeneration (curcumin). The findings highlight the therapeutic potential of both therapeutics in peripheral nerve injuries; methylprednisolone provides immediate suppression of inflammation and curcumin could serve as a safer and more effective alternative or complementary treatment to corticosteroids in the management of peripheral nerve injuries, addressing a critical clinical challenge in nerve repair.
