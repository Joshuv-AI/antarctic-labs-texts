Seepage analysis of tunnel lining under the synergistic effect of cave-fracture networks in water-rich karst formations
Authors: Guiping Li, Jian Tao, Zhiyong Shen, Jun Wang, Wei Dai, Yantao Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInterconnected cave–fracture networks are widely developed in karst regions. The complex geometric characteristics and strong heterogeneity of these formations not only disturb groundwater flow fields but also alter the in-situ stress state of the surrounding rock, leading to the formation of irregular preferential seepage pathways. These factors pose significant challenges to the long-term stability and construction safety of underground tunnels. In this study, a hydro-mechanical (HM) coupled model is established using COMSOL Multiphysics to investigate the seepage behavior of tunnels under different geological conditions, considering the synergistic effects of cave–fracture systems. The model is validated against classical consolidation problems and fracture seepage cases, and the results show good agreement with analytical solutions and previous studies, indicating its reliability. The results show that increasing fracture density enhances network connectivity, resulting in higher seepage pressure on the tunnel lining accompanied by greater non-uniformity. In addition, larger cave radius, shorter cave distance, and higher cave pressure further increase the seepage pressure on the lining, with caves located above the tunnel exerting a more pronounced influence on the upper section. When a connected seepage pathway forms between the cave, fractures, and lining, the local seepage pressure is more likely to exceed the lining bearing capacity. These findings suggest that targeted anti-seepage measures, such as grouting reinforcement, are necessary in high-risk zones, and provide a basis for the adaptive design of tunnel linings in karst regions.Keywords: Underground tunnel, Karst cave, Seepage stability, Hydro-mechanical coupling, Lining permeabilitySubject terms: Engineering, Natural hazards, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

The karst region in Southwest China, centered around the Yunnan-Guizhou Plateau, represents the world’s largest contiguous expanse of outcropping carbonate rocks, covering approximately 5.1 × 105 km2 and accounting for roughly 5.8% of China’s total land area1. As a strategic national energy base, this region possesses abundant coal reserves. However, during the subsurface exploitation in the karst-developed terrains, engineering activities inevitably encounter cavernous formations accompanied by randomly distributed cave and fractures2,3, as illustrated in Fig. 1. Meanwhile, under the combined perturbations of mining-induced stresses and rainfall infiltration, the complex cave-fracture networks are highly susceptible to triggering geohazards such as water/mud inrush, roof collapse, and lining failure4–6, posing a severe threat to the mine operation and personnel safety. Therefore, in light of the specific geological constraints of karst mining areas, investigating the influence mechanism of cave-fracture development characteristics on the seepage stability of underground tunnels is of profound significance and practical engineering value.

Schematical illustration of underground tunnel construction in karst regions with complex cave-fracture networks.

To date, a wealth of research has been conducted for exploring the stability of underground structures influenced by karst caves. For example, Li et al.7 employed a fluid-solid coupling model test to investigate water inrush mechanisms, revealing distinct stress-seepage evolution patterns and a negative correlation between stress and pore pressure for karst caves located ahead of and alongside the tunnel. Man et al.8 incorporated seepage effects into the stability analysis framework of the tunnel face by transforming groundwater influence into an equivalent body force. Their results demonstrated that both groundwater level and permeability characteristics exert a significant control on tunnel stability and can fundamentally alter the failure mode. An et al.9 conducted a 3D model test to investigate lining response under high hydraulic pressure, revealing that top-positioned cavities induce the most critical stress states. By conducting a large-scale hydro-mechanical model test influenced by a water-filled spherical caves, Di et al.10 conducted a series of physical model tests to systematically investigate the influence of seepage on tunnel face stability in saturated strata. The findings revealed that seepage markedly reduces the critical support pressure, while also modifying the extent of the failure zone and the evolution process of the soil arching structure. Yang et al.11 elucidated the three-phase evolution of water inrush, i.e., from initial fracture extension to the formation of dominant water-conducting channels and eventual structural instability of the rock mass. Liang et al.12 developed a three-dimensional analytical model for tunnel face stability based on the unified strength theory, explicitly accounting for the effect of the intermediate principal stress on the failure mechanism. The results indicated that conventional methods may underestimate stability, whereas the three-dimensional stress state plays a crucial role in determining the required support pressure.

Given the high technical complexity and prohibitive costs associated with physical experiments, numerical simulation has emerged as a crucial instrument for assessing the tunnel stability in karst areas. Ma et al.13 utilized the distinct lattice spring model (DLSM) to numerically investigate the stability of U-shaped tunnel under various karst cave conditions, revealing that right-side caves most significantly impact structural integrity. Yau et al.14 proposed a probabilistic method to assess the impact of spatially variable karst cavities on tunnel lining stability and water inflow, facilitating rationalized risk allocation in geotechnical baseline reports. Xu et al.15 employed the PANDAS code for performing hydro-mechanical (HM) analysis, finding that lateral caves primarily induce compression-shear failure, whereas the caves above the tunnel tend to trigger tensile fracture.

In addition to the influence of internal caves, random fracture networks are typically widespread around tunnels in karst areas. Therefore, the coupled HM behavior of rock masses governed by the fracture network are also one of the key factors affecting tunnel stability16. Li et al.17 developed a numerical model of water inrush utilizing COMSOL Multiphysics and found that fractures render strong anisotropy in the seepage field, thereby promoting concentrated water inrush along dominant flow channels. Xiong et al.18 derived a coupled Darcy-Forchheimer flow model for fractured porous media based on the finite volume method (FVM), highlighting that the fracture density and distribution uniformity are key controlling factors for tunnel seepage in deep-buried water-rich strata. Jia et al.19 developed a non-Darcy HM model for deep tunnels, concluding that high-conductivity fractures drive non-linear flow, while ignoring non-Darcy effects underestimates lining pore pressure. Additionally, a multitude of studies based on discrete fracture network (DFN) models have further clarified that while fracture connectivity is paramount, other parameters such as fracture density, roughness, and length also significantly modulate the seepage response of rock masses by reshaping stress distributions and flow paths20–24.

In summary, although considerable progress has been achieved in interpreting the hydro-mechanical coupling effects under the influence of internal caves or fractures, research on the synergistic interaction within the typical cave-fracture coexisting systems in karst mining areas remains relatively limited. To address the limitations of existing studies, this work takes the Xintian Coal Mine in the mountainous region of Southwest China as the engineering background and develops a hydro-mechanical coupling model that simultaneously accounts for the combined effects of karst cavities with different spatial distributions and stochastic fracture networks. Unlike previous studies that primarily focus on either isolated karst voids or fractured media alone, the novelty of this work lies in the incorporation of a discrete fracture network (DFN) to represent the stochastic nature of fractures, while further considering the coupled interaction between stress redistribution induced by karst cavities and seepage behavior in the surrounding rock. On this basis, the mechanisms by which different fracture characteristics and karst cavity distributions influence the seepage stability of tunnel linings are systematically investigated. In addition, the regulatory effect of grouting reinforcement on seepage pathways is examined. The findings of this study provide a more comprehensive theoretical basis and technical reference for excavation and support design of mining tunnels under water-rich karst geological conditions.

Numerical implementation

The coupled hydro-mechanical (HM) model is implemented in this study using the finite element software COMSOL Multiphysics. Specifically, the Solid Mechanics module is employed to calculate the stress and displacement of the rock and fracture, while the Darcy Flow module is utilized to simulate the fluid transport process within the rock fractures. A fully coupled solution scheme with unconditional stability is adopted for the numerical calculation, where the Solid Mechanics and Darcy Flow models are solved simultaneously within a single iteration. Compared to the segregated methods typically used in multiphysics problems, the fully coupled approach, although computationally more demanding, generally offers superior convergence and requires fewer iterations.

The standard Galerkin method is employed for the spatial discretization of the numerical model, and the direct solver MUMPS with Newton-Raphson iterations is selected for solving the nonlinear equations, configured with a relative tolerance of 0.001, an absolute tolerance of 0.1, an initial damping factor of 1, and a maximum of28 iterations. Note that the convergence parameters adopted in the numerical simulations, including the relative tolerance, absolute tolerance, and damping factor, were selected with reference to the default solver settings in COMSOL Multiphysics, as well as relevant previous studies25,28. To verify the rationality of these parameter choices, additional computations were performed under stricter convergence tolerance conditions. The results indicate that no significant variations were observed in the key outcomes, demonstrating that the selected convergence parameters are sufficient to ensure the stability and convergence of the numerical simulations.

Seepage analysis of tunnel lining under different cave-fracture conditions

Engineering background

The Xintian Mine is located in the northwestern part of Guizhou Province, China, northeast of Gantang Town, Qianxi City, within the karst hilly terrain of the Yunnan–Guizhou Plateau (Fig. 6(a)). The mining area extends approximately 8 km in the east–west direction and 4.5 km in the north–south direction, covering a total area of about 36 km2. The elevation of the mine field ranges from 1165 to 1463 m, and the burial depth of the coal seams varies between 305 and 513 m. The mine utilizes a single-level development method via inclined shafts, with three shafts constructed in the initial stage (Fig. 6(b)). The main geological structures exposed during shaft excavation include the Jiujitan Member, the Yulongshan Member (upper and lower sub‑members), the Shabaowan Member, the Changxing Formation, and the Upper Longtan Formation. Among these, fractures and karst caves are relatively well developed in the Yulongshan Member and the Changxing Formation.

Geological overview of the Xintian Coal Mine: (a) geographical location (modified from Wang et al.29, and (b) distribution of karst caves and fractures (modified from Li et al.30.

The mining area is characterized by a subtropical humid monsoon climate with distinct seasonal precipitation patterns. Rainfall is abundant in summer and scarce in winter, with an average annual precipitation of 1005.2 mm. Numerous gullies, depressions, inactive faults, and surface sinkholes are developed within the region. During the rainy season, surface water such as streams and ponds form and infiltrate downward through sinkholes, depressions, and fault fracture zones. The karst cave and fracture systems around the underground roadways are highly developed, constituting a complex groundwater conduction network (see Fig. 1). Under the coupled effects of high water pressure and excavation-induced disturbance, the lining structure and surrounding rock of the roadways are susceptible to instability.

Geological characteristics and geotechnical properties of surrounding rock

The surrounding rock of the Xintian Coal Mine is predominantly composed of carbonate formations, mainly including limestone and dolomite, which are primarily distributed within the Yulongshan and Changxing Formations. Influenced by karstification, the rock mass exhibits widespread development of cavities and fractures, resulting in a highly complex structure characterized by significant heterogeneity and discontinuity. The orientation, scale, and connectivity of fractures are highly variable, and these discontinuities serve as the main pathways for groundwater flow, exerting a decisive control on the spatial distribution of the seepage field31.

The study area represents a typical karst aquifer system, where groundwater predominantly migrates through interconnected conduits formed by karst cavities and fracture networks32,33. The permeability of the rock mass is strongly governed by the degree of fracture development and exhibits substantial spatial variability. In the numerical analysis, the surrounding rock is simplified as an equivalent porous medium, while a discrete fracture network (DFN) approach is employed to characterize the influence of fractures on local flow33. Model configuration and numerical setup

According to the geological conditions of the Xintian Coal Mine, a numerical model containing cave-fracture networks is established using COMSOL Multiphysics, with the main inclined shaft taken as the research object, as illustrated in Fig. 9(a). The roadway shape is a semi-circular arch, with a height of 3.8 m and a width of 4.4 m36. The lining structure is constructed of C30 concrete, and its early warning threshold for seepage pressure is 480 kPa37.

Model configuration: (a) illustration of model geometry and boundary condition, (b) mesh generation, and (c) layout of monitoring points on the tunnel lining.

Whilst the geometry of karst caves is typically irregular polygonal structures, the cave is idealized as a circular shape to simplify the numerical modeling, following the studies by Xu et al.15 and Yao et al.18. A uniform and constant water pressure is applied at the inner boundary to simulate the stable recharge conditions of the water-filled karst cave5,17. In addition, owing to the lack of statistical data on the actual fracture distribution in the mining area, the trace lengths and orientations of fractures around the tunnel are set to follow normal distributions, in accordance with the study by Jia et al.19. This study analyzes the spatial distribution characteristics of fractures, providing a basis for the statistical representation of fracture networks. The mean values and variances of key fracture parameters, such as length and dip angle, are determined based on the statistical results reported in19, where fracture characteristics were derived from actual engineering geological conditions through systematic statistical analysis. This approach introduces spatial heterogeneity into the model through a randomly generated fracture network, thereby capturing, to a certain extent, the inherent heterogeneity of the rock mass. Consequently, the proposed model maintains computational tractability while achieving a reasonable representation of the heterogeneity of the surrounding rock. The discrete fracture network (DFN) is randomly generated via Matlab programming, with fracture dip angles distributed from 0° to 180° and lengths ranging from 2 m to 25 m, as shown in Fig. 9(a). To eliminate the boundary effects on the numerical results, the geometric dimensions of the model are set to 80 m × 80 m, ensuring that the model boundaries are at least three times the diameter of the central tunnel.

The computational domain is discretized using a free triangular mesh, with a total of 15,759 triangular elements generated (Fig. 9(b)). To evaluate the influence of mesh discretization on the numerical results, a series of comparative simulations with varying mesh densities were conducted. The results indicate that further mesh refinement leads to no significant changes in the primary computational outcomes, demonstrating that the adopted mesh configuration is sufficient to meet the required accuracy. On this basis, and by balancing computational efficiency and precision, the selected mesh is employed as the final discretization scheme. Specifically, a refined mesh is applied at the tunnel lining and the intersections of fractures, while a relatively coarser mesh is utilized for the remaining regions. To monitor the seepage characteristics of the lining structure, ten monitoring points are arranged circumferentially on the lining, including crown (A), spandrels (B, C, J, I), sidewalls (D, H), inverts (E, G), and the center of the floor (F), aimed at capturing the spatial evolution of pore pressure across different tunnel sections, as shown in Fig. 9(c).

The numerical model adopts the plane strain assumption, wherein displacement in the out-of-plane direction is constrained. The bottom boundary is set as a fixed constraint, while the self-weight stress of the overlying strata is applied at the top boundary to simulate a 410 m overburden. Horizontal stresses are applied to the left and right boundaries of the model, respectively, with magnitudes varying according to the burial depth following the relationship38:

For the hydraulic boundary conditions, the water head at the upper boundary is set to 120 m, while the top and lateral boundaries are prescribed as impermeable. The karst cave is modeled as a uniform constant pressure boundary, and the inner contour of the lining is defined as free boundary. The entire model is subjected to gravitational loading. The model parameters used in the calculations are determined based on previous studies19,25 and are summarized in Table 3. In particular, the mechanical and hydraulic parameters are derived based on the geological report of the Xintian Coal Mine, in conjunction with the theoretical frameworks established in previous studies19,28. The normal stiffness (Mn) and shear stiffness (Ms) of fractures are determined according to the empirical relationships proposed by Jia et al.19, in which fracture stiffness is correlated with the Young’s modulus of the surrounding rock matrix, thereby ensuring displacement continuity across the interfaces. The storage coefficients (Ss and Sf)39 are calculated by comprehensively considering the compressibility of both the rock skeleton and the pore fluid. This approach enables an accurate characterization of the transient seepage response in deep, water-rich karst strata.

Notably, this study adopts homogeneous hydraulic parameters for the rock matrix. This is because, while in-situ surrounding rock mass inherently exhibits heterogeneity, seepage in strongly karstified zones typically displays pronounced channelization characteristics. Specifically, fluid flow predominantly occurs along highly permeable fracture-cavity networks, whereas the contribution of the rock matrix, owing to its relatively low permeability, is negligible to the overall seepage field. Consequently, the discrepancies in simulation results induced by matrix heterogeneity can be reasonably ignored, and the surrounding rock is simplified as an equivalent homogeneous medium in this work.

Seepage analysis of tunnel lining under different fracture densities

This section first discusses the influence of different fracture densities on the seepage stability of underground tunnels without karst cave. Five fracture distribution scenarios, ranging from intact rock mass to high-density fractured rock mass, i.e., D = 0.00, 0.01, 0.02, 0.03, and 0.04 m/m2 are investigated. The simulated pore pressure in the surrounding rock, seepage pressure on the lining, and Darcy velocity under different fracture densities are illustrated in Figs. 10(a), (b), and (c), respectively. As can be observed from Fig. 10(a), the increase in fracture density enhances the connectivity of fractures around the tunnel, forming a fracture network that connects to the lining, which consequently elevates the pore water pressure in the surrounding rock mass. The distribution of lining seepage pressure in Fig. 10(b) reveals that, the seepage pressure on the exterior of the lining is relatively uniform and low in the intact rock mass. However, as the fracture density increases, the lining seepage pressure not only rises gradually but also exhibits a highly non-uniform distribution at the fracture-lining intersections. In addition, as shown by the Darcy velocity distribution in Fig. 10(c), the expanding fracture network provides more preferential seepage paths for groundwater compared to the low-permeability matrix. This enhanced connectivity further facilitates the migration of groundwater towards the tunnel, thereby accelerating the Darcy velocity within the DFN as the fracture density increases. The study found that when the fracture density increased from 0.02 to 0.03 m/m², the equivalent permeability increases by an order of magnitude. This phenomenon suggests that the fracture network reaches a percolation threshold, forming penetrating preferential flow channels. This transition provides a mechanical explanation for the abrupt surge in seepage pressure across all monitoring points observed at D = 0.04 m/m2.

To further evaluate the influence of varying fracture densities on the seepage stability of the tunnel lining, the evolution of seepage pressure at different monitoring points, along with the warning threshold pressure of 480 kPa, is collectively illustrated in Fig. 11. The result shows that the seepage pressure of the lining exhibits an overall increasing trend as the fracture density rises from D = 0.00 m/m2 to D = 0.04 m/m2 (Fig. 11(a)). Meanwhile, the dispersion of seepage pressure across various monitoring points intensifies, suggesting that a higher fracture density promotes a more non-uniform pressure distribution of the lining. On the other hand, Fig. 11(b) suggests that higher pore pressures are localized only at monitoring points E and G (i.e., the arch feet) under low fracture density due to gravity-driven fluid migration. However, with the increase of fracture density and the formation of preferential seepage paths, the regions of elevated seepage pressure shift to concentrate at the fracture-lining connection points, which constitute the most critical locations for potential water inrush. Notably, when the fracture density reaches D = 0.04 m/m2, the seepage pressure at all monitoring points exceeds the safety limit of 480 kPa, potentially triggering full-section water inrush failure of the lining structure.

In summary, both fracture density and spatial distribution are pivotal factors influencing the seepage stability of tunnel linings in fractured rock masses. Higher fracture density and connectivity lead to elevated lining seepage pressure, thereby increasing the risk of water inrush. Given a constant fracture density, the water pressure at the fracture-lining interface is inherently higher than in other regions, which further exacerbates the vulnerability of tunnels to water inrush. Consequently, the accurate identification of fracture-lining intersection points and timely anti-seepage interventions such as grouting reinforcement in these zones are crucial for the effective prevention of water inrush hazards in underground tunnels.

In practical engineering, grouting is commonly employed to mitigate water inrush hazards induced by highly permeable fracture channels. By filling fractures and karst cavities, grouting reduces local permeability and weakens the connectivity of preferential flow paths, thereby reconstructing dominant seepage pathways. Previous studies have shown that this process not only decreases seepage intensity but also causes originally concentrated flow paths to shift or become more dispersed33. Accordingly, accurate identification of fracture–lining intersection zones, followed by timely implementation of grouting reinforcement and anti-seepage measures at these critical locations, is of great significance for effectively preventing and controlling water inrush disasters in underground tunnels40.

Seepage analysis of tunnel lining under different lining permeabilities

This section explores the influence mechanism of different lining permeabilities on the seepage behavior of tunnels by adopting a constant fracture density (D = 0.02 m/m2). Four numerical simulations are performed under κl = 1.5E − 14 m2, 0.5E − 14 m2, 1.5E − 15 m2, 0.5E − 15 m2, and 1.5E − 16 m2, respectively, and the corresponding pore pressure in the surrounding rock, the seepage pressure on the lining, and the Darcy velocity are plotted in Fig. 12. The results demonstrate that as the lining permeability decreases, it becomes increasingly difficult for the fracture water to be discharged through the lining. This leads to a progressive increase in the pore pressure within the rock mass surrounding the tunnel (Fig. 12(a)), while the lining simultaneously sustains greater external water pressure (Fig. 12(b)). It is noteworthy that throughout the process of reducing lining permeability, although the direction of fracture seepage remains unchanged, the magnitude of Darcy velocity decreases significantly due to the impediment posed by the low-permeability lining (Fig. 12(c)).

Seepage behavior of the tunnel under various lining permeabilities: (a) pore pressure of the surrounding rock, (b) seepage pressure of the lining, and (c) Darcy velocity.

The evolution of seepage pressure at different monitoring points of the lining with respect to lining permeability are illustrated in Fig. 13. As can be observed, the seepage pressure of the lining increases sharply as its permeability decreases owing to the impedance effect of the low-permeability lining on the water flow (Fig. 13(a)). Figure 13(b) further reveals that, although the seepage pressure at high permeability remains within the safe range, its spatial distribution is markedly heterogeneous. This is because the seepage pressure in such cases is governed primarily by seepage flow through fractures connected to the lining. As the lining permeability decreases, the non-uniformity of the hydraulic pressure distribution gradually diminishes. However, a low-permeability lining impedes the discharge of fracture water flow, thereby substantially increasing the overall hydraulic pressure exerted on the lining. Under such conditions, it is required to increase the anti-seepage strength of the lining for ensuring its stability. Ultimately, a lining permeability of κl = 1.5E − 15 m2 is identified as the critical threshold for the safe seepage pressure of the tunnel lining at the fracture density of D = 0.04 m/m2. Quantitative calculations indicate that as the lining permeability decreases from 1.5E -14 to 1.5E-16m2, the maximum seepage pressure on the exterior of the lining increases by approximately 3.5 times. By establishing a functional relationship between the maximum pressure (Pmax) and the warning threshold (Pthreshold), it can be inferred that under the current fracture conditions, every order-of-magnitude reduction in lining permeability results in an approximately 40% decrease in the anti-seepage safety margin.

Variations of seepage pressure in the tunnel lining under different lining permeability: (a) box plot of seepage pressure variation, and (b) radar plot of seepage pressure distribution.

To further elucidate the mechanisms by which fracture density and lining permeability govern the distribution of lining seepage pressure, a series of orthogonal numerical simulations are conducted. The fracture density (D) is varied across 0.00, 0.01, 0.02, 0.03, and 0.04 m/m2, while the lining permeability (κl) is set at 1.5E − 14 m2, 0.5E − 14 m2, 1.5E − 15 m2, 0.5E − 15 m2, and 1.5E − 16 m2. The evolution of seepage pressure for each configuration is illustrated in Fig. 14. It is observed that at a constant fracture density, lower lining permeability results in a more uniform seepage pressure distribution, albeit with higher magnitudes. By contrast, as the lining permeability increases, the seepage pressure field undergoes significant differentiation, evolving into a non-uniform pattern. The regions of elevated seepage pressure are primarily concentrated at the arch feet on both sides and the junctions between fractures and the lining, aligning with the coupling mechanisms between the gravitational and preferential seepage pathways. On the other hand, under a constant lining permeability, an increase in fracture density leads to a growing trend in lining seepage pressure, although the magnitude of this impact is slightly secondary to the effects induced by variations in the lining permeability itself. This suggests that the hydraulic properties of the lining material serve as the dominant factor governing the distribution of lining seepage pressure compared to the fracture density.

Evolution of lining seepage pressure under various fracture densities and lining permeability scenarios.

In summary, for the practical engineering traversing water-rich formations with varying fracture densities, the lining permeability should be rationally selected based on the degree of fracture development. Specifically, for sections with high fracture density, lining materials with higher permeability are preferred to reduce the seepage pressure levels. Conversely, for low fracture density zones, the lining permeability can be appropriately reduced to smoothen the seepage field distribution and mitigate the concentration of pore water pressure. Such a strategy ensures the long-term structural integrity and operational safety of the lining under complex hydrogeological environments.

Seepage analysis of tunnel lining under various cave radii

To investigate the synergistic effect of cave-fracture networks on the hydraulic behavior of tunnels in karst regions, this section examines the seepage characteristics under different cave radii (i.e., R = 1, 3, 5, 7 m) with a constant fracture density (D = 0.02 m/m2), a cave pressure (P = 0.8 MPa), and a fixed distance (2 m) from the tunnel crown, alongside a no karst cave case being employed for comparison. The simulated pore pressure in the surrounding rock, seepage pressure on the lining, and Darcy velocity under different cave radii are illustrated in Figs. 15(a), (b), and (c), respectively. It can be observed that as the karst cave radius increases, the contact area between the cave and the fractures expands progressively. This leads to the gradual formation of interconnected pathways among the cave, fractures, and the lining, thereby exacerbating the seepage pressure exerted on the lining structure.

Seepage behavior of the tunnel under various cave radii: (a) pore pressure of the surrounding rock, (b) seepage pressure of the lining, and (c) Darcy velocity.

The evolution of seepage pressure for various monitoring points are illustrated in Fig. 15 with respect to the cave radius. It shows that the presence of karst cave substantially elevates the overall pressure of the lining compared to the no cave condition (Fig. 16(a)). As the cave radius increases from 1 m to 7 m, the seepage pressure at all monitoring points exhibits an incremental trend, and the median values gradually approach and eventually surpass the early-warning threshold of 480 kPa. The observed variations are closely associated with the stress field distribution. As the radius of the karst cavity increases, the extent of stress redistribution in the surrounding rock expands, accompanied by intensified local stress concentration. This condition promotes the opening and propagation of pre-existing fractures, thereby enhancing the equivalent permeability of the rock mass. Under such effects, groundwater more readily migrates along fracture networks and karst conduits, leading to an increase in seepage intensity. Figure 16(b) depicts the spatial distribution of seepage pressure across various lining segments under different cave radii. The results indicate that high-pressure zones are primarily concentrated at monitoring points E, G, and J. Specifically, the elevated pressures at E and G are driven by gravity-induced flow, resulting in water pressure accumulation at the arch feet on both sides. By contrast, the high pressure at point J stems from its location within the cave-fracture-lining interconnection zone (see Fig. 15), which facilitates the formation of preferential fracture seepage channels, leading to a localized abnormal increase in water pressure in this region. Therefore, targeted anti-seepage interventions such as grouting reinforcement are recommended in such high-risk areas to guarantee the seepage stability of tunnel lining.

Seepage analysis of tunnel lining under various cave distances

This section investigates the seepage behavior of tunnels under different cave distances (L = 2, 4, 6, and 8 m) with a constant fracture density (D = 0.02 m/m2), a cave radius (R = 3 m), and a cave pressure (P = 0.8 MPa), alongside a no karst cave case for comparison. The simulated rock pore pressure, lining seepage pressure, and Darcy velocity under different cave distances are presented in Figs. 17(a), (b), and (c), respectively. It indicates that as the distance between the karst cave and the tunnel increases, the disturbance of the cave to the pore water pressure around the tunnel gradually diminishes. When L = 8 m, the distribution of the seepage field approaches that observed in the no karst cave case. This is attributed to the fact that a nearby karst cave serves as a dominant water-source boundary, imposing a substantial perturbation to the surrounding seepage field. As the cave distance increases, the hydraulic influence of the cave progressively attenuates, with seepage through fractures in the surrounding rock becoming dominant, leading the seepage field toward a steady-state distribution.

Seepage behavior of the tunnel under various cave distances: (a) pore pressure of the surrounding rock, (b) seepage pressure of the lining, and (c) Darcy velocity.

The evolution of seepage pressure for various monitoring points are illustrated in Fig. 18 with respect to the cave distance. The results show that the seepage pressure on the lining decreases most significantly as the cave distance increases from L = 2 m to L = 4 m, while the rate of seepage pressure reduction tends to level off when the spacing exceeds 6 m (Fig. 18(a)). As the distance between the karst cavity and the tunnel increases, its perturbation effect on the stress field gradually diminishes, and the stress state of the surrounding rock tends to stabilize. At greater distances, the degree of fracture opening is only weakly affected, resulting in negligible changes in permeability. According to Terzaghi’s effective stress principle (Eq. (7)), the stress concentration induced by the proximity of the karst cave to the tunnel leads to a reduction in local effective stress. This, in turn, triggers an increase in the normal displacement of fractures within the elastic thin-layer model. Such “passive opening” of fractures, driven by geometric proximity, constitutes the core physical mechanism for the emergence of localized high-pressure centers (e.g., point J) on the lining surface. Consequently, the influence of the karst cavity on the seepage field progressively decreases. Furthermore, Fig. 18(b) reveals that, the upper region of the lining exhibits higher seepage pressures than the middle to lower regions under the influence of the karst cave, with monitoring point J, located at the spandrel, demonstrating the most sensitive response to changes in cave distance. This provides further evidence that the hydraulically connected pathway formed by the karst cave, fractures, and lining exerts a dominant control over the water pressure acting on the lining.

Seepage analysis of tunnel lining under various cave pressures

The simulated rock pore pressure, lining seepage pressure, and Darcy velocity under different cave pressures (P = 0.8, 1.2, 1.6, and 2.0 MPa) with a constant fracture density (D = 0.02 m/m2), a cave radius (R = 3 m), and a cave distance (L = 2 m), are plotted in Fig. 19(a), (b), and (c), respectively, accompanied by a no karst cave case for comparison. The results suggest that an increase in cave pressure gives rise to a pronounced zone of high hydraulic pressure concentration around the cave, while the pressure gradient gradually dissipates from the cave boundary towards the tunnel periphery. The overall seepage field exhibits a distinctly heterogeneous distribution. Notably, when the cave pressure reaches P = 2.0 MPa, upward Darcy velocity arrows even emerge around the cave, indicating that the intense cave pressure overcomes the influence of gravity. When the internal cave pressure reaches 2.0 MPa, the local hydraulic gradient surpasses the gravity potential gradient. By comparing the pressure term and the gravity term in the Darcy velocity equation (Eq. 3), it is quantitatively determined that within a 5 m radius of the cave, pressure-driven seepage becomes dominant. This explains the observed counter-gravity seepage recharge occurring even in regions below the tunnel level, as illustrated by the upward Darcy velocity vectors.

Conclusions

Under the combined effects of complex hydraulic conditions and external loading, the stability of underground tunnels is significantly influenced by the structural characteristics of the surrounding rock and the distribution of the seepage field. In particular, in karst-developed regions, the complex water-conducting network formed by fractures and cavities often governs groundwater migration, thereby exerting a critical control on the stress state of the lining structure and the overall seepage stability. In this study, a coupled hydro-mechanical model integrating the synergistic effects of stochastic discrete fracture networks (DFN) and water-rich karst cave has thus been established for investigating the seepage behavior of underground tunnels based on the geological conditions of Xintian Coal Mine. The influences of various fracture densities, lining permeabilities, and cave distributions on the seepage stability of lining structures have been systematically elucidated. The main findings obtained in this work can be summarized as follows:

Increased fracture density enhances the connectivity of the fracture network, accelerates fracture water flow, and raises Darcy velocity, resulting in elevated and more uneven seepage pressure on the lining.

In high fracture density zones, high-permeability lining materials are recommended to limit seepage pressure, whereas in low-density areas, reducing lining permeability can help smooth pore pressure distribution and mitigate local pressure concentrations.

Larger karst cave radii, reduced tunnel-cave spacing, and higher cave internal pressures all increase seepage pressure on the lining, with the upper lining region being more significantly affected.

The authors recognize that, despite the simplifications adopted in the present model, the study provides valuable insights into the characterization of local seepage behavior in fractured and karstified rock masses. In the model, the surrounding rock matrix was idealized as a homogeneous medium. Although the discrete fracture network (DFN) captures macro-scale discontinuities, the spatial variability within the matrix itself was not fully considered, which may limit the granularity of the seepage field analysis. Similarly, karst caves were simplified as regular circular geometries with uniform constant pressure boundaries, overlooking the complex morphology of actual karst systems and the dynamic evolution of internal fluid flow. In addition, fluid flow within both fractures and caves was assumed to follow generalized Darcy’s law. Under conditions of well-connected fracture–cave zones or high flow velocities, however, flow may deviate from Darcian behavior, potentially underestimating nonlinear effects and the local distribution of water pressure.

Guided by these limitations, it is necessary to further refine model assumptions and computational methods under conditions closer to in-situ engineering scenarios, thereby enhancing both the precision of the results and their applicability. Future research will focus on introducing irregular cave geometries that better reflect actual geological features to improve the resolution of local seepage and pressure distribution, incorporating non-Darcian flow characteristics in fracture–cave systems to more accurately describe preferential high-velocity flow paths, and integrating field monitoring or experimental data to validate and calibrate the model, ultimately strengthening its reliability in practical engineering applications.
