The test-retest reproducibility of [11C]PK11195 positron emission tomography using Biograph Vision Quadra scanner
Authors: Tanja Sjöros, Markus Matilainen, Virva Saunavaara, Saara Wahlroos, Johan Rajander, Mikko Koivumäki, Eveliina Honkonen, Amelie Luoma, Laura Airas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPositron emission tomography (PET) imaging of the 18 kDa translocator protein (TSPO) is a method to quantify central inflammation and particularly microglial activation, which is increased in progressive multiple sclerosis (MS) among other neurodegenerative diseases. We evaluated the test-retest reproducibility of TSPO-PET imaging using Biograph Vision Quadra and [11C]PK11195 radioligand. Three healthy volunteers and three people with progressive MS underwent two [11C]PK11195-PET scans with a 1–3 day interval. TSPO binding was quantified as distribution volume ratios (DVR) using the supervised clustering algorithm. To evaluate the reproducibility of repeated [11C]PK11195-PET scans, repeatability coefficients (RC) and intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) were calculated.ResultsThe ICCs indicated excellent test-retest reproducibility in distinct cortical regions (0.933–0.985) and white matter (0.980), and good to excellent test-retest reproducibility in deep grey matter structures (0.867–0.988). Additionally, the reproducibility was evaluated lesion-wise in the ≥50mm3 MS lesions (n=59). The test-retest reproducibility was good both at the lesion core (ICC 0.869) and rim (ICC 0.846). Conclusion: The test-retest reproducibility of [11C]PK11195-PET using Biograph Vision Quadra is good to excellent among people with MS. This supports the usability of the ligand in longitudinal studies, including treatment trials.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13550-026-01460-6.

Background

Radioligand [11C]PK11195 binds to the 18 kDa translocator protein (TSPO), which is primarily present in mitochondria of microglia, but is to a lesser extend also expressed in macrophages, monocytes, endothelial and ependymal cells, as well as hypertrophic astrocytes [1].

In multiple sclerosis (MS), disease pathophysiology comprises both adaptive and innate immune system responses. The adaptive immune system is associated with the formation of focal inflammatory lesions in the central nervous system (CNS), whereas the innate immune system is involved in the CNS-compartmentalised diffuse inflammation including activated microglia. People with progressive MS have higher TSPO-binding in several brain areas compared to age-matched control participants [2–7]. TSPO-binding in different brain regions of interest (ROI) can be quantified with pseudo-reference region modelling by avoiding regions with specific binding using a supervised clustering algorithm (SVCA) [8, 9].

In order to reliably estimate the differences between various subject groups or longitudinal changes within a ROI, the test-retest reproducibility of the given data acquisition method needs to be at an adequate level. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) is a valid index to compare the degree of agreement among datasets if their between-subjects variances (BSV) are similar and there is a reasonable spread of subjects with regard to the outcome analyzed, that is, the BSV expresses the variability of the outcome in the population [10].

Previously, [11C]PK11195 binding potential (BPND) in grey matter, quantified using SVCA and ECAT Exact HR 47 scanner, has demonstrated poor reproducibility as examined in six healthy participants with a six-week interval between scans [11]. However, in a group of four people with Alzheimer’s disease using a similar approach and a maximum of six-week interval between scans with ECAT EXACT3D reached good to excellent reproducibility in nearly all of the studied ROI [9]. Moreover, [11C]PK11195 binding in thalamus, white matter (WM), and cortical grey matter (GM) showed excellent test-retest repeatability measured using Siemens Biograph mCT PET/CT among six people with MS with a two-hour interval between scans [12]. However, using the pseudo-reference method, within chronic WM lesions the test-retest variability was large, especially among small lesions or within small ROI [12].

To date, Biograph Vision Quadra has been in clinical use for approximately five years. This scanner provides a long axial field of view (AFOV) of 106 cm. This extended AFOV provides improved sensitivity compared to previous PET/CT systems [13]. Its high sensitivity makes image acquisition as short as 10s feasible for comparative quantitative images, especially in the middle of the AFOV [14]. However, at 10 min or longer acquisition times, good image quality can be reached throughout nearly the whole AFOV [15].

The aim of this study was to evaluate the test-retest reproducibility of brain [11C]PK11195-PET imaging using the Biograph Vision Quadra and SVCA both in a population of people with MS and among healthy controls.

Materials and methods

This prospective test-retest study was conducted at the Turku PET Centre, University of Turku and Turku University Hospital, Turku, Finland. The study was conducted according to Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Wellbeing Services County of Southwest Finland.

Participants

The study participants were recruited between August and December 2023 from the outpatient clinic of the Division of Clinical Neurosciences at the Turku University Hospital, Turku, Finland, based on a confirmed MS diagnosis and willingness to participate in a PET study, or willingness to participate as a healthy control (HC) participant. All participants gave their informed consent before entering the study. The clinical information was available to the researchers analysing the data.

Image acquisition

The TSPO-binding radioligand [11C]PK11195 with half-life of 20.4 min was produced as previously described with some modifications [6]. Briefly, [11C]carbon dioxide was converted into [11C]methyl iodide, which was trapped into a solution of (R)-N-desmethyl-PK11195 (1.0 mg) and 3.0 µl of 1.0 M tetrabutylammonium hydroxide in dimethyl sulfoxide (150 µl). Reaction mixture was heated at 100 °C for 3 min. Tracer was subsequently purified with semipreparative HPLC. The product was formulated with solid phase extraction in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) containing propylene glycol/ethanol (8:2) and sterile filtered through 0.22 μm Millex GV sterile filter with polyethersulfone membrane. Radiochemical purity of produced [11C]PK11195 was more than 99%.

Each participant was imaged twice with a 1–3 day interval. The median injected dose was 104.92 MBq in the test and 102.31 MBq in the retest condition, with no significant difference between time points (p = 0.46). A dynamic 60 min PET scan was started from ligand injection and conducted with Siemens Biograph Vision Quadra (Siemens Healthineers, Knoxville, TN, USA), with time-of-flight (TOF) resolution of 228 ps and spatial resolution of approximately 3.5 mm. Low-dose CT (CareDose 4D, Care kV; ref. mAs 7; ref. kVp 120; collimation 32 × 1.2 mm) was used for attenuation correction. For attenuation correction, CT images were reconstructed with 3-mm slice thickness and 3-mm increment using kernel Br38 (FOV 780 mm; no ADMIRE). Clinical CT reconstructions were generated with a 3-mm slice thickness and 3-mm increment using kernel Br36 and ADMIRE strength 5 with a 650-mm FOV for body images, and using kernel Hr38 and ADMIRE strength 5 with a 350-mm FOV for brain images. PET data were corrected for detector dead-time, background radioactivity, decay, randoms, scatter, and spatial movement. PET data were reconstructed using the vendor’s TrueX + TOF (ultraHD PET) algorithm. The high-sensitivity (HS) reconstruction used four iterations and five subsets, a 440 × 440 matrix, zoom factor 1, and a 4-mm Gaussian filter. Dynamic framing consisted of 2 × 15 s, 3 × 30 s, 3 × 1 min, 7 × 5 min, and 2 × 10 min, yielding a total reconstructed duration of 3600 s (60 min). For the estimation of the [11C]PK11195 distribution volume ratio (DVR), the time-activity curve corresponding to a reference region devoid of specific TSPO-binding was acquired using SVCA [8].

Additionally, MRI was performed with a 3T Ingenia (Philips, Eindhoven, Netherlands). The MRI protocol included T1-, T2-weighted, and fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) sequences. A manually edited semi-automated method was used to draw the T1 and T2 lesion ROI using Lesion Segmentation Tool (LST) in SPM8 (www.statistical-modelling.de/lst.html) and Carimas software (https://turkupetcentre.fi/carimas/) as previously described [16]. Cortical and subcortical ROIs as well as the WM, normal-appearing WM (NAWM), and cortical GM ROI were segmented with the T1 lesion-filled T1 MRI (LST lesion-filling tool in SPM8) with the FreeSurfer 7.3.0 software (http://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu/) as previously described [16]. The DVRs in lesion-related ROI (lesion core, rim, and perilesional area) were analysed by applying the lesion masks of the three participants with MS for each HC in the normalised MNI space. Next, the images were backtransformed into the native space for the statistical analyses. Percentage active voxels in the whole brain and NAWM were calculated using an active voxel threshold DVR > 1.2222. Based on a sample of 16 HC, the threshold was determined by first calculating the 97.5th percentile threshold (represents the upper limit of the 95% confidence interval in a symmetric case) across all cerebral WM voxels for each HC (n = 16), then computing the group-level mean of these individual values to define the final threshold.

Statistical methods

The repeatability coefficient (RC) was calculated as the variance of within-subject differences multiplied by 1.96 [17], using the formula:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{R}\mathrm{C}=1.96\times\:\sqrt{\frac{{{\Sigma\:}({\mathrm{m}}_{2}-{\mathrm{m}}_{1})}^{2}}{n}}$$\end{document}

where m1 and m2 denote the test and retests results, respectively. The RC has the advantage of quantifying reliability measurement error in the same units as the original variable. The agreement between two measurements was calculated with ICC using a one-way random effects model with single measures. Regarding the person-wise lesion-related ROI, the three individual MS lesion masks were used for each of the HC participants leading to six distinct datasets including three people with MS with their individual lesion ROI masks and three HC with a MS lesion mask of one of the MS participants. The ICCs of one randomly chosen dataset with a HC-MS lesion mask combination is presented, and the results of the five other possible combinations are presented in the Additional materials (Additional Tables 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5). Additionally, the Cronbach’s alpha as a measure of internal consistency was determined. The RC was calculated using Microsoft Excel, the ICC and Cronbach’s alpha calculation was conducted with IBM SPSS Statistics 29.0.2.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and the parallelism plots were created using JMP Student Edition 19.0.1 for Windows (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Agreement between test and retest

According to the ICC analyses, in distinct cortical regions the DVR reproducibility varied between 93.3% and 98.5%, indicating excellent test-retest reproducibility (Table 2; Fig. 1). In subcortical regions, the DVR reproducibility varied from 86.7% to 98.8%, similarly indicating good to excellent test-retest reproducibility (Table 2; Fig. 2). Additionally, whole brain DVR showed excellent test-retest reproducibility of 98.4% (95% CI 91.0–99.8%; Table 2; Fig. 3). Furthermore, the internal consistency of the repeated measures was excellent, as evaluated by the Cronbach’s alpha that varied from 0.917 to 0.999 (Table 2). Tentative power calculation

Based on an earlier finding that natalizumab treatment decreased NAWM DVR by 0.03 points [18], we performed a tentative power calculation. According to this, a tentative treatment would decrease NAWM DVR by 0.04 (SD 0.04) points, which would represent a 3% change from the baseline. Based on the current test-retest data, we estimated that in the control group NAWM DVR would decrease by 0.009 (SD 0.004) points. To detect a statistically significant change during the intervention and compared to the control group, we calculated that 15 participants would be needed in both groups (α = 0.05, 1 - β = 0.8).

Adverse events

One participant experienced some lower back pain during the test imaging and therefore declined to participate in the retest imaging. No other adverse events were recorded.

Discussion

In this study we show that [11C]PK11195 positron emission tomography using Biograph Vision Quadra has excellent test-retest reproducibility among people with MS. An ICC above 0.90 is considered excellent, whereas ICCs 0.75–0.90 indicate good reproducibility and 0.50–0.75 moderate reproducibility [19]. The ICCs in the current study indicate excellent repeatability in cortical regions and WM, and good to excellent repeatability in small subcortical regions. Moreover, the ICCs of the lesion-wise analyses indicate good repeatability in lesional and perilesional DVRs. Additionally, based on Cronbach’s alpha, the internal consistency of the test-retest outcomes is excellent.

Many people with MS, especially in the subgroup of relapsing-remitting disease type (RRMS), have very similar TSPO-binding in several cortical and subcortical regions compared to HC [2, 4, 5, 20, 21]. On the other hand, people with progressive MS have higher TSPO-binding in several brain areas compared to people with RRMS [2, 4, 22]. Therefore, in this study both HC and people with progressive MS were included in order for the BSV to approximate the variance of the outcome in the MS population. Thus, the results indicate an excellent test-retest- reproducibility of [11C]PK11195 using Biograph Vision Quadra among people with MS. However, one must be careful when applying these results to groups in which the BSV may be different.

Interestingly, [11C]PK11195 BPND in grey matter demonstrated poor reproducibility (ICC 0.34) in six young healthy male participants with a six-week interval between scans [11]. On the other hand, in four people with Alzheimer’s disease and a similar time interval between scans the reproducibility was good in several brain areas (median ICC 0.878) [9]. TSPO-binding is known to increase with age, and previously we have reported that both healthy men and men with MS have higher DVRs compared to women [23], but whether the within-subject variability is greater among men than women, or among younger participants compared to older, is not known. Additionally, the different scanners, time intervals between scans, and BSVs are plausible sources of the disagreement between these studies. Among the young women imaged within this study, there was no apparent difference in the within-subject variation between scans in comparison to the MS participants. Nevertheless, it is possible that among young (male) participants, the biological variability in [11C]PK11195 binding is more considerable than in older participants or among women, which warrants future research.

The RC can be interpreted as the smallest detectable difference, i.e. the lower limit of measurement change that can be interpreted as a true difference, exceeding measurement error [24, 25]. In the current study, brain DVR RC was 0.013, suggesting that a 0.013 point change or difference in brain DVR can be considered a true difference. However, in order to reliably determine the smallest detectable difference, a larger sample size would be preferred [26].

In the current study, a standard pseudo-reference region modelling SVCA was used in order to produce comparable outcomes with previous data obtained with the ECAT High Resolution Research Tomograph (HRRT). In the future, with the extended AFOV provided by the Biograph Vision Quadra, arterial input function can also be acquired from the dynamic imaging data, using a ROI drawn in the descending aortic artery or inside the left cardiac ventricle. However, this approach warrants further validation with larger study samples. As [11C]PK11195 may also bind to endothelial cells, it is important to have sufficiently large area free of possible endothelium in order reliably obtain input function out of the drawn ROI.

The extended AFOV of Biograph Vision Quadra provides improved sensitivity compared to previous PET/CT systems [13]. However, in the dedicated human brain HRRT point-source resolution varied from 2.3 to 3.4 mm [27]. Based on our unpublished NEMA testing, in Biograph Vision Quadra point-source resolution is about 3.3–3.8 mm. Therefore, in the current study we are reporting lesion rim results using a 3 mm ROI surrounding the lesions, compared to our previous publications using a 2 mm rim obtained by the HRRT. However, the test-retest reproducibility using a 2 mm rim was very similar as was seen in the 3 mm rim.

The main strength of the current study is a short time interval between the two measurements (1–3 days) that enhances the similarity of the conditions under which the repeated measures were taken. The half-life of a 11C-labeled radiopharmaceutical is 20.4 min, which enables repeated measures on subsequent days. Another strength is the inclusion of both healthy participants and people with progressive MS, who are known to have higher brain TSPO-binding compared to healthy individuals and people with RRMS. Weaknesses considering the current analyses are the relatively small number of participants, inclusion of only female participants, and the relatively young age of the HC. Additionally, the participants were positioned in the scanner approximately 10 cm from the scanner end, although higher image precision could be achieved in the middle of AFOV [13]. However, with the image acquisition time of 60 min this effect is most likely negligible. Moreover, two of the six participants of this study had some movement artefacts in the image. This highlights the importance of minimizing head movement during imaging. Thereafter we have paid extra attention to supporting the head of the participant in the scanner, since thermoplastic masks, which are used to minimize head movement for example in the HRRT scanner, are currently not available for the Quadra system. However, despite these shortcomings, the reproducibility of the method using the current data was most satisfactory.

Assessing test-retest reproducibility provides implications for optimizing clinical trial designs. In addition to estimating the anticipated intervention effects, it is important to distinguish the level of change that can be considered as mere noise or measurement error. Therefore, we included tentative power calculations for a tentative trial using the average test-retest difference in NAWM DVR as the comparator. However, these calculations should be interpreted with caution because of several limitations, including differences in scanner sensitivity, noise characteristics, and systematic biases. The DVR variability may differ systematically between scanners. Currently we do not have any intervention data obtained with the Biograph Vision Quadra, thus the power calculation is based on a previous finding acquired using an HRRT scanner. Nevertheless, the RC is a practical application for identifying the smallest clinically significant difference for future studies.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the results of this study indicate good to excellent test-retest reproducibility of [11C]PK11195-PET imaging in people with MS using Biograph Vision Quadra scanner and quantified DVRs as outcome measures. Within small ROI the variability increases and test-retest reproducibility remains within ‘good’ range, whereas using larger ROIs the reproducibility is excellent. The work supports the usability of [11C]PK11195-PET imaging in longitudinal settings, including therapeutic trials addressing glial activation in the context of neurodegenerative conditions.
