Cellular phospho-signaling map of the enigmatic serine/threonine kinase MAST2
Authors: Isha Fathima, Althaf Mahin, Pahal Priyanka, Nazah Naurah Vattoth, Ayishath Nishana, Athira Perunelly Gopalakrishnan, Sowmya Soman, Rajesh Raju
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMAST2 (Microtubule-Associated Serine/Threonine Kinase 2) is an enigmatic serine/threonine kinase that is considered to bridge microtubule-associated cytoskeletal architecture through its phospho-regulatory networks. Yet, MAST2 remains a dark horse in the human kinome, as the molecular details on its structure, upstream regulators, and downstream phosphorylation targets remain unknown.Methods and resultsTo interpret MAST2-linked phospho-signaling dynamics, PubMed-indexed articles were curated based on predefined MeSH terms to obtain global cellular phosphoproteomics datasets. Among 105 class I phosphosites in MAST2 identified across cellular phosphoproteomics datasets, 2 phosphosites, S74 and S148, were represented more abundantly compared to other phosphosites, making them predominant and functionally significant. Expression coregulation analysis, aimed at interpreting phosphosites that consistently show either similar or opposite patterns of expression, was performed by computing the expression patterns of phosphosites in parallel with the predominant MAST2 phosphosite. Their frequencies were then ranked across differential datasets to ensure consistency, and Fisher's exact test was performed to assess the likelihood of the coregulation pattern. Potential biases within the datasets were mitigated using additional cutoffs. Interpreting these datasets, we identified the majority of high-confidence coregulated protein phosphosites of both predominant phosphosites to be involved in transcriptional regulation. This is consistent with reports that a nearby Arg89Gln mutation in MAST2 disrupts its transcriptional regulatory activity. Co-occurrence analysis of phosphosites within MAST2 revealed that these predominant sites tend to co-occur positively and share similarity in expression coregulation patterns with phosphosites in other proteins. Finally, novel upstream kinases that potentially phosphorylate the predominant phosphosites of MAST2, as well as potential downstream substrates that are phosphorylated by MAST2, were also identified from the high-confidence coregulation dataset.ConclusionsWe propose that phosphorylations at S74 and S148 of MAST2 are functionally similar, and that these phosphosites are candidate regulatory sites influencing the transcriptional regulatory activity of MAST2.

Introduction

The microtubule-associated serine/threonine protein kinase 2 (MAST2) is a 205 kDa protein kinase encoded on chromosome 1p34.1 within the AGC family of kinases that carries three conserved protein domains [1]. The AGC family, named after its foundational members Protein Kinase A (PKA), Protein Kinase G (PKG), and Protein Kinase C (PKC), is a major superfamily within the human kinome, where most members require phosphorylation-induced conformational changes to activate their enzymatic activity [2]. MAST2, originally known as MAST205, is the first member of the MAST kinase subfamily to be identified. It was initially isolated by screening microtubule-associated protein antiserum [3]. It carries a serine/threonine kinase domain, a PDZ (Postsynaptic density-95, Disc large, and Zonula occludens-1) scaffolding domain towards the C-terminal end that plays a critical role in protein-protein interactions, and a DUF1908 domain towards the N-terminal end, whose function remains unclear. It is primarily localized in the cytosol and is also found in apical membranes [4].

MAST2 remains a largely underexplored kinase, with most knowledge limited to molecular interactions involving its PDZ domain. Specifically, the PDZ domain binds to the TKV motif of PTEN (Phosphatase and Tensin Homolog), regulating its phosphatase activity, which in turn dephosphorylates PIP3 (Phosphatidylinositol (3,4,5)-trisphosphate), thereby acting as a negative regulator of PI3K/AKT signaling, a pathway central to cell division, survival, and tumorigenesis [5]. Beyond PTEN, the MAST2 PDZ domain also interacts with CDHR2 (Cadherin Related Family Member 2), mediating contact inhibition of proliferation, and with TRAF6 (TNF Receptor Associated Factor 6), where binding promotes IL12 synthesis and suppresses NFKB1 (Nuclear Factor Kappa B Subunit 1) activity to regulate Toll-like receptor (TLR) 2/4 signaling [[7], [8], [9]]. MAST2 has also been shown to interact with SLC9A3 (Solute Carrier Family 9 Member A3) [10]. Furthermore, MAST2 is reported to negatively regulate TFPI (Tissue Factor Pathway Inhibitor) while positively regulating SERPINE1 (Serpin Family E Member 1), PLAU (Plasminogen Activator, Urokinase), and RHBDL2 (Rhomboid Like 2) [11]. Interestingly, current molecular insights into the kinase domain of MAST2 suggest that phosphorylation of PTEN at its C-terminal end by MAST2 reduces PTEN activity, thereby enhancing PI3K/AKT signaling [6].

Although MAST2 appears to be a kinase that links phospho-signaling to microtubule and cytoskeletal organisation, its molecular specificities regarding substrates, interacting partners, and protein complexes are poorly understood. However, overexpression of MAST2 has been reported to have proliferative effects both in vivo and in vitro [12], and it is implicated in several disease conditions such as breast cancer [13], liver cancer [14], and neurodegeneration [15]. Additionally, the Arg89Gln mutant variant of MAST2 is reported to be responsible for inherited thrombophilia [11]. This signifies the importance of studying phosphosites or phosphorylations in MAST2 that regulate its functional significance spatiotemporally and influence the dynamics of signaling mediated by MAST2, which remains underexplored in the dark phosphoproteome. This study aims to map MAST2 phosphosites using global phosphoproteomics datasets and to identify candidate upstream kinases, substrates, and transcriptional associations. We curated and analysed datasets reporting differential regulation of MAST2 phosphosites, identifying representative sites consistently regulated across multiple studies. Based on these, we classified potential upstream kinases, substrates, interactors, and their placement within the associated phospho-signaling hierarchy.

Materials and methods

Assembly of global phosphoproteome datasets to investigate MAST2 phosphosite regulations

The global cellular phosphoproteome datasets were curated and assembled in our laboratory as previously reported [16,17]. This followed a pipeline of PubMed-based literature survey and data curation using the MeSH term “phosphoproteomics” OR “phosphoproteome” NOT “plant” NOT “review”, and fetching human cellular phosphoproteomics datasets with representations of Class-1 phosphosites (localization probability ≥75 %; A-score ≥13) of MAST2. Proteins were mapped to their corresponding gene symbol as per HGNC (Human Gene Nomenclature Committee) and their respective UniProt accessions to maintain uniformity [18]. Further, datasets are classified into qualitative profiles and quantitative differential datasets, where differential expression of individual datasets was calculated based on the statistical cutoffs, p-value <0.05 and fold change ≥1.3 for upregulation and ≤0.76 for downregulation.

Identification of predominant phosphosites representing MAST2 in phosphoproteomes

The class-1 MAST2 phosphosites were extracted from human cellular phosphoproteome profile datasets. The number of qualitative profiles and quantitative differentially regulated datasets where each phosphosite of MAST2 is detected was computed and ranked. MAST2 phosphosites were represented across a wide range of frequencies in the cellular phosphoproteomics datasets, with certain phosphosites being poorly detected in multiple datasets, while others were frequently represented across the phosphoproteomics datasets. Phosphosites of MAST2 that are most frequently represented across multiple profiling and differential phosphoproteomics datasets were considered as predominant phosphosites of MAST2.

Identification and categorization of coregulated protein phosphosites with MAST2 predominant phosphosites

Datasets with representations of Class 1 MAST2 phosphosites with their corresponding PubMed accessions are listed in Supplementary Table 1A and B Quantitative profile datasets representing MAST2 predominant phosphosites were then individually categorized to identify phosphosites in other proteins (PsOPs) that are positively or negatively coregulated in expression. Quantitative profile datasets displaying differential regulation of MAST predominant phosphosites (eg, MAST2-S74) were divided into two main categories: datasets where MAST2-S74 is upregulated (U) and datasets where MAST2-S74 is downregulated (D). From the datasets with S74 upregulation, PsOPs that are upregulated (UU) and downregulated (UD). Similarly, datasets with S74 downregulation (D), PsOPs that are upregulated (DU), and downregulated (DD) were extracted. These PsOPs were then quantified based on their consistency of differential expression frequency to be coregulated with MAST predominant phosphosites, and re-categorized into UUDD (positive coregulated) based on the ratio Ʃ(nUU + nDD)/Ʃ(nUD + nDU) and UDDU (negative coregulated) based on the ratio Ʃ(nUD + nDU)/Ʃ (nUU + nDU). To ensure consistency in the coregulation pattern, a minimum coregulation frequency of 15 % of the predominant phosphosites differential frequency was considered relevant.

To assess the significance and likelihood of the coregulation patterns, a one-sided Fisher's exact test (FET) was applied using a contingency table covering all possible patterns of phosphosite pairs occurrence across the phosphoproteomics datasets (Supplementary Table 2A and B). We followed the FET analysis protocols used by Li et al. (2017) to identify co-occurring phosphosites [19]. FET p-values less than 0.05 are deemed significant for coregulated PsOPs. Further filtering criteria were applied to avoid biases such as over-representation of multi-temporal datasets and over-representation of datasets from a single stimulus type. To ensure this, an experimental code was generated, clubbing experimental conditions across different datasets that have similar stimulus types together, and an experimental code confidence value was calculated as Positive code confidence = (UUDD EXP Code - UDDU EXP Code) and vice versa for negative coregulation. Similarly, we calculated the number of PMIDs from which each phosphosite coregulation was identified, and the PMID confidence value for positive and negative coregulation was calculated. An experimental code confidence and PMID confidence cutoffs of ≥3 were then applied to positive and negative coregulations to avoid potential bias. Those phosphosites that satisfy these filtering parameters were considered as high-confidence coregulated phosphosites along with predominant phosphosites of MAST2.

Co-occurrence analysis of MAST2 phosphosites

Phosphosites that co-occur within a protein are reported to be functionally associated [19]. To investigate the mutual associations of phosphorylation sites within MAST2, we particularly looked into the co-differential regulation pattern of phosphosite pairs within MAST2. Differential datasets with multiple phosphosites of MAST2 detected in the same experimental condition were extracted. For each pair, we separately calculated the UU, UD, DD, and DU frequencies and further assessed their positive coregulation pattern using ∑ (nUU + nDD)/∑ (nUD + nDU) and negative coregulation pattern using ∑ (nUD + nDU)/∑ (nUU + nDD).

Retrieval of MAST2 interacting proteins

The protein-protein interactors of MAST2 were obtained from databases such as HPRD [20], BIND [21], BioGRID [22], and ConsensusPathDb version 35 [23]. Additionally, PubMed-based manual screening was conducted to gather more MAST2 interactors.

Kinase/substrates prediction of MAST2

Since no experimentally confirmed kinases or substrates for MAST2 were available, in-silico prediction tools such as NetworKIN [24], AKID [25], and datasets available in the iKiP-DB database [26] were used to identify potential upstream kinases and substrates of MAST2. Additionally, datasets on MAST2 upstream kinases and substrates predicted by Johnson et al. (2023) [27] using a synthetic peptide library-based screening approach were also fetched, and datasets with a ≥90 % confidence cutoff were used in the current study.

Data visualisation

Cytoscape [28], RAWGraph [29], SRplot [30], and R/Bioconductor package trackViewer (10.18129/B9.bioc.trackViewer) were used to generate figures and visualisations.

The PeptideCutter tool from the protein identification and analysis tools in the ExPASy server was used to identify trypsin-digested peptides of MAST2 [31].

Results

Class-I phosphosites in MAST2 from global cellular phosphoproteome datasets

Phosphoproteomics has evolved considerably over the past decades, owing to technical innovations, enhanced dataset accessibility, and its significance in elucidating cellular signaling and disease development. Aligned with that, Microtubule-associated serine/threonine kinase 2 (MAST2) emerged as a heavily phosphorylated protein kinase, with 104 phosphorylation sites reported in PhosphoSitePlus [32]. However, the molecular relevance of these phosphorylations within MAST2 is currently unknown in the literature. Additionally, none of the 536 human kinases reported in the KinHub database [33] are currently reported as upstream kinases that phosphorylate these phosphosites in MAST2. This led us to explore the phosphosite-specific signaling dynamics of MAST2. We curated and analysed the global cellular phosphoproteome datasets with representations of Class-1 phosphosites from PubMed. MAST2 phosphosite-specific data were taken from 164 human cellular-level articles spanning 842 profiling and 292 differential phosphoproteomics datasets, integrating multiple experimental and temporal conditions (Supplementary Table 1A–B).

The profiling datasets reported phosphorylations at 96 phosphosites in MAST2, while the differential data in total comprised expression patterns of 64 phosphosites. Considering these phosphosites are detected from diverse experimental/biological conditions, we ranked the frequency of detection of each phosphosite across profiling and differential datasets. Interestingly, these phosphosites ranged in frequency from weakly detected to abundantly detected phosphosites of MAST2. As abundantly detected phosphosites could be more reliably associated with the function of the protein, we looked for phosphosites that have the highest frequency of detection in both profiling and differential datasets. The most abundantly detected phosphosites were S74 and S148, with frequencies of 681 and 563 in profiling datasets and 70 and 48 in differential datasets. Hence, we considered them as predominant phosphosites of MAST2. Considering the positions of the predominant sites relative to the kinase domain, recent work from our team has classified MAST2 as a kinase with 'predominant phosphosites outside the kinase domain' (Pokd) [34]. Co-occurrence of phosphorylations within MAST2

Previously, Li et al., 2017 reported that the phosphorylations that co-occur within a protein are functionally associated [19]. So, in our efforts to interpret the functional significance of phosphosites within MAST2, we analysed their co-occurrence patterns within cellular differential phosphoproteomics datasets. From datasets with differential expression of multiple MAST2 phosphosites identified, we classified phosphosites that co-upregulate or co-downregulate as positive co-occurrence, and phosphosites that express opposite expression (one upregulated, the other downregulated) as negative co-occurrence. Further, the scoring of the positive and negative co-occurrence of phosphosite pairs was done across the differential datasets to identify coherence (Fig. 2). Our analysis identified that the MAST2 predominant phosphosites S74 and S148 tend to be positively co-occurring and functionally associated. They were identified to be positively coregulated in 14 differential datasets and negatively coregulated in 3 datasets, showing a systemic inclination to positive co-occurrence, with a ratio of 4.6. Further, we noted that both S74 and S148 showed positive co-occurrence with other phosphosites such as S66, S900, S1364, and S1381 that are outside the kinase domain of MAST2. Interestingly, S204 of MAST2, within its kinase domain, exhibited consistent negative co-occurrence with several phosphosites, such as T182, S900, S1032, and S1381. This negative co-occurrence of S204 implies that phosphorylation at S204 tends to show opposite expression coregulation (one upregulated, the other downregulated) with these phosphosites, and could be functionally opposite in its significance towards MAST2. Further, considering that the predominant phosphorylation sites S74 and S148 of MAST2 tend to be positively co-occurring, we hypothesized that the phosphosite coregulations associated with these phosphosites should be similar to some extent. Our analysis identified that 36 phosphosites in other proteins (PsOPs) showed similarly consistent positive coregulation with MAST2 predominant phosphosites. For instance, phosphorylation at S14 of the Iron-sulfur cluster assembly enzyme ISCU, which is associated with the stabilization of ISCU [35], showed positive coregulation with MAST2 S74 and S148, with coregulation frequencies of 11 and 8, respectively. Additionally, phosphorylation at T434 of the Nuclear pore complex protein NUP214 showed consistently negative coregulation with MAST2 S74 and S148. This similarity in expression coregulation patterns further suggests convergent functional significance for MAST2 predominant phosphosites.

Co-regulated phosphosites in currently known interacting partners of MAST2

To further interpret the association of coregulated phosphosites and MAST2 predominant sites, we assembled the list of proteins that are currently identified to interact with MAST2 from multiple repositories. Among the binary interactors of MAST2, we identified phosphorylation at S207 of Ras association domain-containing protein 1 (RASSF1) to be positively coregulated with S74 and phosphorylation at S369 of Ribosomal protein S6 kinase alpha-1 (RPS6KA1) to be negatively coregulated with MAST2 S74. Similarly, we identified phosphorylation at S582 and S610 of Kinesin Light Chain 2 (KLC2) and phosphorylation at S110 of Beta-2-syntrophin (SNTB2) to be positively coregulated with S148, and phosphorylation at S231 of Heat shock protein HSP 90-alpha (HSP90AA1) to be negatively coregulated with MAST2 S148 (Fig. 4) (Supplementary Table 3B). MAST2 is known to interact with HSP90AA1 in cardiomyocytes and modulate IL12 production in macrophages [39]. Analysis of novel upstream kinases associated with MAST2

Currently, experimentally validated protein kinases that phosphorylate predominant phosphosites of MAST2 are unknown in the literature. However, to identify potential kinases that could phosphorylate these phosphosites in MAST2, we predicted upstream kinases using in silico tools and integrated peptide screening-based kinase substrate data from Johnson et al. (2023) [27]. With the list of potential upstream kinases, we screened the coregulation data for phosphosite coregulations with potential upstream kinases of MAST2. We noted that RPS6KA1, which was initially identified as a binary interactor with negative phosphosite coregulation, is also predicted as an upstream kinase that phosphorylates S74 of MAST2 by Johnson et al. (2023) [27]. The molecular significance of phosphorylation at S369 of RPS6KA1 is currently unknown. However, it is located within the protein kinase C-terminal domain of RPS6KA1, outside its kinase domain. Future studies could provide more clarity on the relevance of this phosphorylation, as the negative coregulation currently observed potentially implies a negative impact of this site on the kinase activity of RPS6KA1. Additionally, Microtubule Affinity-regulating Kinase 2 (MARK2) was identified as a potential upstream kinase capable of phosphorylating S148 of MAST2, where phosphorylation at S486 of MARK2 positively coregulated with MAST2 S148 (Supplementary Table 3C). MARK2 is reported to regulate microtubule-associated proteins and is associated with cancers and neurodegenerative diseases [41]. However, the molecular significance of phosphorylation at MARK2 S486 is currently unknown. Future studies that uncover the impact of these phosphorylations on these potential kinases are required to provide clarity on the relevance of the observed phosphosite coregulations.

Analysis of novel MAST2 substrates

MAST2 is one of the protein kinases for which no experimentally validated substrates have been reported yet. So in our efforts to identify potential substrates of MAST2, we utilised substrates predicted by in silico tools as well as Johnson et al. (2023) [27]. Similar to the upstream kinase analysis, the list of potential substrates was analysed in the coregulation data to identify predicted phosphosites in the substrates that show expression coregulation with MAST2 predominant phosphosites. Among the coregulation data of MAST2 S74, phosphosites from 14 predicted substrates showed positive coregulation, and phosphosites from 2 showed negative coregulation. Among them, BAG cochaperone 6 (BAG6) with phosphosite S964, followed by mammalian microtubule (MT) plus-end binding proteins CLASP2 and 2 with phosphosites S507 and S688, respectively, showed the highest positive regulation frequencies. Similarly, substrate phosphosites of 46 potential substrates of MAST2 showed positive coregulation with MAST2 S148, which includes phosphorylations at S1279 of Zinc finger protein 106 (ZNF106), S335 of Striatin-interacting protein 1 (STRIP1), etc (Fig. 5). Additionally, both MAST2 S74 and S148 were positively coregulated with potential substrates phosphosites S844 and T505 of BBX High Mobility Group Box Domain Containing (BBX) and Jumonji Domain Containing 1C (JMJD1C), respectively. Discussion

MAST2 is a heavily phosphorylated kinase that is understudied in terms of the upstream signals that activate it and the regulatory signals mediated by its protein kinase domain. Currently, MAST2 has been associated with cancers and neurodegeneration [1]. Its clinical relevance extends to association with rabies virus infection, where the viral glycoproteins interact with the PDZ domain of MAST, preventing MAST2-PTEN interaction, inhibiting neurite outgrowth [42]. Our analysis of the cellular phosphoproteomics datasets associated with MAST2 phosphosites identified patterns of consistent phosphosite coregulations with phosphosites in other proteins.

Among the coregulations identified, VCL S290 and ANKS1A S647 were the top positive coregulated phosphosites with MAST2 S74. VCL (Vinculin) is an F-actin binding protein involved in cell matrix and cell–cell adhesion [43], whereas ANKS1A (Ankyrin repeat and SAM domain-containing protein 1A) regulates cell migration and neurite retraction, with deficiency linked to reduced microtubule networks in aged mice [44]. Conversely, HARS2 S67 and USP36 S464 were the top negative coregulated sites. HARS2 (Histidyl-tRNA synthetase 2, mitochondrial) functions in protein biosynthesis [45], while USP36 (Ubiquitin-specific peptidase 36) maintains nucleolar structure by deubiquitinating polyubiquitinated proteins [46]. For MAST2 S148, top positive coregulations included SRRM2 S1124 and RPS6 S236. SRRM2 (Serine/arginine repetitive matrix 2) is a spliceosome component and Parkinson's disease biomarker [47], while RPS6 (Ribosomal protein S6) is part of the 40S ribosomal subunit, where phosphorylation at S236 enhances its activity [48]. Negative coregulations involved FBL S124 and MLPH S337. FBL (Fibrillarin) is a nucleolar protein in ribosome biogenesis, with microtubule-mediated redistribution [49], and MLPH (Melanophilin) is a Rab effector protein mediating melanosome transport via microtubule–actin track selection [50]. However, the absence of phosphosite-specific functional information for most coregulated sites currently restricts deeper interpretation.

Previously, MAST2 was reported to regulate the transcription of TFPI and SERPINE1 genes, where transfection of MAST2-specific siRNA to HEK293 cells increased TFPI promoter activity and reduced that of SERPINE. Additionally, this transcriptional regulation is also linked to Arg89Gln mutation in MAST2, where MAST2 Gln89 overexpression cells showed lowered TFPI promoter activity and increased SERPINE1 promoter activity compared to overexpressing wild-type MAST2 [11]. Considering this site-specific mutation positionally occurs between the two predominant sites identified in the current study (S74 & S148), we analysed the influence of phosphorylations at our predominant sites towards transcriptional regulation. Independent protein level enrichment analysis was done with positively coregulated and negatively coregulated PsOPs of S74 and S148. Positive coregulation data of S74 and S148 of MAST2 showed proteins involved in ‘positive regulation of DNA-templated transcription’ to be enriched. Among them, RSF1 T1305, SUPT7L S108, and TRRAP S2077 commonly showed positive coregulation with MAST2 S74 and S148. RSF1 is a chromatin-remodeling factor that orchestrates p53 transcriptional activity upon DNA damage [36]. While SUPT7L is a subunit of the transcriptional coactivator histone acetyltransferase complex, mediating protein acetylation and transcription factor binding activities [37]. TRRAP, on the other hand, plays a crucial role in DNA repair by recruiting histone acetyltransferase complexes to chromatin to control neural progenitor fate decisions [38]. However, phosphosite-specific information on the coregulated phosphosites of these proteins is currently unknown. Other transcription-associated enriched proteins include transcription factors, co-regulators, and DNA-binding proteins such as JMY, MEF2A, SP1, TFE3, ZNF318, and ILF3, and chromatin remodeling and modification proteins such as BICRA, CHD3, DOT1L, EP400, KAT6A, KMT2B, PHF2, SMARCA1, SMARCA4, SUPT7L, and TRRAP. These coregulation patterns with phosphosites in transcription-associated proteins suggest that phosphorylation at MAST S74 and S148 could potentially be associated with the transcriptional regulation activity of MAST2.

Interestingly, ‘negative regulation of microtubule depolymerization’ was enriched in the protein-level enrichment analysis. Although MAST2 (Microtubule Associated Serine/Threonine Kinase 2) in its name denotes association with microtubules, the direct association of MAST2 with microtubule activity has not yet been reported [1]. In light of this, the enrichment linking MAST2 phosphorylations at S74 and S148 to negative regulation of microtubule depolymerization could be significant. Major proteins that showed enrichment in negative regulation of microtubule depolymerization include microtubule-associated protein 1B (MAP1B), cytoplasmic linker-associated protein 1 (CLASP1), CLASP2, calmodulin-regulated spectrin-associated protein 1 (CAMSAP1), CAMSAP2, etc. CLASP1 and CLASP2 localize at microtubule plus-ends and prevent excessive depolymerization, while CAMSAP1 and CAMSAP2 stabilize non-centrosomal microtubule minus-ends, protecting them from depolymerization. MAP1B also binds along microtubules to limit depolymerization for stabilization [[51], [52], [53]]. Specifically, MAP1B S1016, CLASP2 S455, and CAMSAP2 S1148 were commonly positively coregulated with both MAST2 S74 and MAST2 S148, however, the functional significance of these phosphorylation events specific to the protein is currently unknown.

Considering that MAST2 is one of the protein kinases for which no experimentally validated substrates are currently known, and that the kinases phosphorylating its phosphosites are also unidentified, our study provides meaningful insights into the poorly understood functions of MAST2. In addition to finding that S74 and S148 tend to co-occur and that RPSKA1 and MARK2 are kinases capable of phosphorylating these sites, respectively. The current study also identified 60 potential novel substrates phosphorylated by MAST2, with functional significance ranging from transcriptional regulation to cytoskeleton organization.

Phosphoproteomics and the characterization of cellular phospho-signaling networks coordinated by kinases represent a rapidly expanding area of research [54]. With several human kinases still lacking even a single experimentally validated substrate, the concept of “deciphering the dark phosphoproteome” has gained increasing attention in the scientific literature [55,56]. Recent studies underscore the importance of understanding the functional roles of individual phosphorylation sites [57]. In this context, our in-silico pipeline and methodology aim to provide a novel perspective for interpreting phosphorylation events and cellular signaling patterns, thereby advancing insights into their biological significance [58]. However, we acknowledge that the current study is data-driven and requires future experimental studies to validate the findings. Further, enrichments inferred from published data may exhibit bias towards well-studied proteins, reflecting publication biases inherent in publicly available data. Additionally, future studies that enlighten the nuances of the dark phosphoproteome could give better insights into the significance of the novel phosphosite associations identified in the current study.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study represents the first investigation into the intricate regulatory landscape of phospho-signals governing and governed by the serine/threonine kinase MAST2. Focusing on the predominant phosphosites S74 and S148 that were identified to be perturbed across several experimental conditions, we uncovered a multitude of phospho-signaling dynamics associated with MAST2. Our analysis revealed that phosphorylations at S74 and S148 of MAST2 tend to co-occur positively, and they are candidate regulatory sites influencing the transcriptional regulatory activity of MAST2. Furthermore, we identified high-confidence kinases capable of phosphorylating MAST2 phosphosites, as well as high-confidence substrates that MAST2 may phosphorylate. The phosphosite coregulation data generated through our in-silico pipeline provides a novel perspective for interpreting phosphorylation events and signaling patterns. By mapping the phosphosignaling landscape associated with MAST2, our study establishes a foundation for future experimental investigations into its functional significance.
