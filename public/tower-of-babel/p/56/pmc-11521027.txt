Clinical efficacy of single-channel percutaneous endoscopic nucleotomy with annulus fibrosus suturing for lumbar disc herniation: A retrospective study
Authors: Song Fu, Renhua Lv, Zhenyu Wang, Fengming Wang, Xiaoling Huang, Longqiang Wang, Hao Gao, Wei Zhao, Yanan Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
This retrospective study aimed to investigate the clinical efficacy of single-channel percutaneous endoscopic nucleotomy combined with annulus fibrosus suturing for treating lumbar disc herniation (LDH). The study included 86 patients with LDH treated in our spinal department from August 2020 to January 2022. The suture group consisted of 26 males and 18 females, while the control group (nucleotomy alone) included 25 males and 17 females. All patients were followed for at least 1 year, with follow-up points at 3 days, 3 months, and 12 months postoperatively. Various clinical and radiological indicators were used to evaluate and compare the efficacy and safety of the 2 procedures. No statistically significant differences were observed in age, sex, BMI, surgical segments, degree of disc degeneration, or postoperative length of hospital stay. However, the suture group had a longer operative time (63.28 ± 4.17 minutes vs 54.71 ± 4.89 minutes, t = 8.759, P < .05). At 12 months postoperatively, neither group showed aggravated disc degeneration. There was also no significant difference in excellent rates between the 2 groups (95.45% vs 92.86%, X2=0.265, P = .607). However, the suture group had a lower intervertebral disc height loss rate compared to the control group (22.3% ± 4.6% vs 29.8% ± 6.2%, X2=6.390, P < .001). In conclusion, single-channel percutaneous endoscopic nucleotomy combined with annulus fibrosus suturing is a safe and reliable treatment for LDH. It effectively reduces the recurrence rate, slows disc degeneration and loss of disc height, and achieves excellent early outcomes.

1. Introduction

Lumbar disc herniation (LDH) is a prevalent degenerative spinal disease affecting a significant portion of the population, often causing severe low back pain and radiculopathy.[1] When conservative treatment proves ineffective, open discectomy with nucleus pulposus removal has traditionally been the preferred surgical intervention. This approach, however, presents several limitations, including extensive tissue dissection, elevated complication risks, and extended recovery periods.[2,3] In recent years, minimally invasive techniques like percutaneous endoscopic lumbar discectomy (PELD) have revolutionized the surgical management of LDH. PELD offers several advantages over open discectomy, including smaller incisions, less tissue trauma, quicker recovery, and lower complication rates.[4,5] Notwithstanding these advantages, both open and endoscopic nucleotomy can lead to annular fissures, which are significant factors in postoperative recurrence and discogenic low back pain.[6]

Previous studies have reported nucleotomy recurrence rates ranging from 4% to 18%, with larger annular defects (>6 mm) correlating with higher recurrence rates compared to smaller defects (<6 mm).[7] Consequently, interest in annular repair techniques has grown as a potential strategy to mitigate recurrence risk and enhance long-term outcomes.[8] Although biological repair methods like tissue engineering, stem cell therapy, and gene therapy are still experimental, physical surgical repair techniques, including various forms of annular suturing and sealing, have been successfully used clinically with microendoscopy, large channel endoscopy, or expandable access channels.[9] These techniques aim to restore annulus fibrosus integrity and potentially enhance long-term intervertebral disc stability.[10,11] However, applying annular suturing with small-channel full endoscopy presents unique challenges due to limited channel diameter and field of view, resulting in a lack of clinical evidence. This highlights the need for further research to evaluate the feasibility and effectiveness of endoscopic annular repair techniques in managing LDH.

In this context, this retrospective study aims to investigate the clinical efficacy of single-channel percutaneous endoscopic nucleotomy combined with annulus fibrosus suturing for LDH and to introduce the technical essentials of this innovative procedure. The findings of this study may enhance our understanding of annular repair’s role in improving outcomes and reducing recurrence rates in LDH surgery.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. General information

This observational study included 86 patients diagnosed with LDH who were treated at the Department of Minimally Invasive Spine Surgery, Shandong Wendeng Orthopaedic Hospital, between August 2020 and January 2022. Patients were divided into 2 groups: the suture group (n = 44) underwent endoscopic lumbar discectomy with annular suturing, while the control group (n = 42) underwent simple endoscopic lumbar discectomy. The decision to perform annular suture was based on disc protrusion type, annular fissure size, and surgeon preference. All surgeries used an interlaminar approach. Informed consent was obtained from all patients, with a follow-up of at least 12 months. The suture group included 26 males and 18 females (mean age 38.8 ± 5.2 years; mean BMI 26.38 ± 4.21 kg/m²), while the control group included 25 males and 17 females (mean age 37.9 ± 4.6 years; mean BMI 26.18 ± 4.59 kg/m²). The affected spinal levels were L3 to 4 (n = 3), L4 to 5 (n = 22), and L5-S1 (n = 19) in the suture group, and L3 to 4 (n = 2), L4 to 5 (n = 21), and L5-S1 (n = 19) in the control group. According to the Pfirrmann grading system, the suture group had 24 grade III and 20 grade IV intervertebral discs, while the control group had 23 grade III and 19 grade IV discs. No significant differences were observed between the groups in gender, age, BMI, surgical segment, or degree of disc degeneration (P > .05, Table 1).

The Ethics Committee of Wendeng Orthopedic Hospital (LL20200807) approved this study, which was conducted in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Due to the retrospective nature of the study, the requirement for written or verbal informed consent from patients was waived.

2.2. Inclusive and exclusive criteria

Inclusion criteria: single-segment LDH with a confirmed diagnosis based on consistent clinical symptoms, signs, and imaging findings (e.g., MRI and CT); no significant improvement after at least 3 months of regular conservative treatment; CT showing soft disc herniation without significant calcification or ossification; Annular defect < 10 mm after discectomy, as assessed during the endoscopic procedure; A follow-up period of ≥12 months with complete medical records.

Exclusion criteria: lumbar spondylolisthesis or segmental instability identified by preoperative imaging; previous lumbar surgery; significant annular calcification or ossification; annular defect > 10 mm after discectomy; acute local or systemic infections, tumors, or severe systemic diseases.

2.3. Surgical procedures

Control group: after effective epidural or spinal anesthesia, patients were positioned prone with the pelvis tilted posteriorly to widen the intervertebral space. A 2 cm longitudinal skin incision was made under fluoroscopic guidance at the intersection of the midline of the target intervertebral disc space and the projection of the upper and lower vertebral pedicles. Sequential dilators were inserted after incising the deep fascia. The position of the dilator tip was confirmed under anteroposterior fluoroscopy at the junction of the inferior edge of the superior facet and the superior outer edge of the inferior lamina, with the lateral fluoroscopy view aligned with the target interspace. The working cannula was rotated into position over the dilator. Once the position was reconfirmed fluoroscopically, an endoscope (Joimax 4.2 mm intervertebral foramen endoscopy system, Germany) was introduced. The endoscope provided clear visualization of the inferior edge of the upper vertebral endplate and the inferior articular process. Depending on the protrusion position, size, and lamina inter-space, a portion of the lower edge of the upper lamina and the upper edge of the lower lamina could be removed using a visible ring saw to expose the ligamentum flavum. The ligamentum flavum was treated with 45° forceps, blue forceps, and lamina bone-biting forceps, fully exposing the dural sac and nerve roots. At this point, 10 mg of dexamethasone could be administered intravenously. After protecting the dural sac and nerve roots, any protrusions and loose nucleus pulposus tissue within the intervertebral space were removed. After sufficient decompression, the annulus fibrosus was reshaped with a radiofrequency electrode, and the incision was closed.

Suture group: Suturing was performed after annulus plasty, with the entire procedure visualized under an endoscopic microscope. The annular tear was identified, and suturing was preferably done in the middle of the tear using a 2-needle technique with the STAR-S Suturing System (Beijing 2020 Company). The first needle was inserted 2 to 3 mm from the edge of the tear, and a guidewire with an anchoring device was pushed into the annulus fibrosus by rotating the knob. The second needle implanted a threaded puncture needle on the opposite side of the tear, and a metal guidewire was inserted to push the built-in guidewire into the annulus fibrosus. Once the needles and guidewire were removed, the suture implantation was complete. The tear was then repaired by tying secure knots under arthroscopic guidance using a split-tip suture pusher. To ensure a durable repair, a surgical knot was first tied, followed by 2 square knots. After confirming the suture was secure, any excess suture material was cut off using endoscopic scissors (Fig. 1).

2.4. Efficacy evaluation

The clinical efficacy of the treatment was assessed using the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) and the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) at 3 time points: 3 days, 3 months, and twelve months postoperatively. Lumbar disc degeneration was evaluated preoperatively and twelve months postoperatively using T2-weighted lumbar MRI scans, with the Pfirrmann grading system and intervertebral disc height (IDH) measurement.[12] Patient satisfaction was determined using the excellent and good rate criteria adapted from the Macnab scale. Additionally, perioperative metrics, including operation duration, complication incidence, and postoperative hospitalization duration, were carefully recorded for both groups. Recurrence during the follow-up period was defined according to criteria established by Zhou Yue et al,[13] which include significant relief of radicular neurological symptoms post-surgery, reappearance of radicular symptoms after 1 month, and confirmation via lumbar MRI showing recurrent compression of neural tissues by protrusion at the same intervertebral disc segment and side as before surgery.

2.5. Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS for Windows (version 20.0, IBM SPSS Inc., Chicago). Quantitative data were presented as mean ± standard deviation and compared using the t-test for inter-group differences and 1-way ANOVA for time-point comparisons within groups. Categorical data were compared using the Chi-square test. A P-value of <.05 was considered statistically significant.

3. Results

3.1. Perioperative indicators and recurrence rates

Both surgical procedures were successfully completed (Fig. 2). The mean operative time for the suture group (63.28 ± 4.17 minutes) was significantly longer than that of the control group (54.71 ± 4.89 minutes, P < .05). There were no intraoperative neural injuries or dural tears. Postoperatively, no complications such as intervertebral infection, neurological deterioration, or cerebrospinal fluid leakage occurred. No significant difference was observed between the groups in the postoperative length of hospitalization (P > .05). The suture group exhibited a significantly lower rate of same-segment recurrence (2.27%) than the control group (9.52%) (P < .05). All cases of recurrence were successfully treated with revision PELD via an interlaminar approach (Table 2).

At 12 months postoperatively, lumbar MRI showed reductions in IDH in all patients compared to preoperative values. The IDH loss rate was 22.3% ± 4.6% in the suture group and 29.8% ± 6.2% in the control group, showing a statistically significant difference (P < .05), as shown in Table 4.

The Pfirrmann grade was evaluated based on MRI assessments before and 12 months after surgery. In the suture group, 24 cases were Pfirrmann grade III preoperatively, of which 6 improved to grade II and 18 remained grade III at final follow-up. 3.4. Excellent and good rates

At 12 months postoperatively, based on the modified MacNab criteria, the percentage of excellent and good outcomes was 95.45% (39 excellent, 3 good, 2 fair) in the suture group and 92.86% (36 excellent, 3 good, 3 fair) in the control group. 4. Discussion

This study evaluates the clinical efficacy of single-channel percutaneous endoscopic nucleotomy combined with annulus fibrosus suturing for LDH. Our results demonstrate significant short-term improvements in clinical symptoms, decreased recurrence rates, and decelerated disc degeneration compared to traditional nucleotomy alone.

Existing literature has extensively documented the short-term benefits of both open and minimally invasive lumbar disc nucleotomy in ameliorating patients’ clinical symptoms. Nevertheless, the persistent challenge of recurrence, attributed to residual nucleus pulposus tissue protruding through the annular fissure, remains a significant concern warranting further investigation. Additionally, some patients may experience lingering low back or leg pain, and postsurgical magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) has revealed varying degrees of aggravated disc degeneration. Previous studies have provided valuable insights into the underlying mechanisms driving these suboptimal outcomes. Ambrossi et al[14] conducted a 12-month follow-up study on 156 patients who underwent single-level nucleotomy, with 141 cases available for analysis. During the follow-up, 17 cases (12%) experienced recurrence, and 11 cases (7.8%) required revision surgery. Carragee et al[15] reported a 2-year follow-up on 72 patients after lumbar discectomy, showing reherniation rates of 18% in the limited discectomy group and 9% in the aggressive discectomy group. Hoogland et al[16] studied 262 cases of recurrent LDH treated with revision endoscopic discectomy, with 238 cases available for analysis at a 24-month follow-up. The excellent and good outcome rate was 85.71%, with a recurrence rate of 4.62%. These findings highlight the ongoing challenges in achieving durable outcomes, despite advancements in minimally invasive techniques.

Numerous studies[17–20] have established that surgical factors contribute to residual low back and leg pain, aggravated disc degeneration, and recurrence after lumbar discectomy. These factors include: mechanical irritation of the nerve root caused by the postoperative “volcano-like” annular fissure; surgical trauma or disc disease activating the immune stress response, leading to persistent proinflammatory factor formation within the disc space, which are then released through the annular fissure into the spinal canal, resulting in chemical radiculitis; impaired annular integrity after surgery, reducing intradiscal hydrostatic pressure and leading to progressive disc height loss and accelerated disc degeneration; and disruption of the concentric lamellar structure of the annulus fibrosus, which exhibits limited self-repair capability due to poor vascularization, resulting in weak fibrous scar formation and elevated risk of residual nucleus tissue reherniation. Addressing these underlying mechanisms through effective annular repair holds significant clinical promise.

In light of these limitations, recent domestic and international studies have investigated the potential advantages of annular repair techniques following lumbar discectomy. Bailey et al[21] conducted a 2-year multicenter, single-blind randomized controlled trial involving 750 LDH patients, in which 500 patients underwent annular repair using the X-close device, while the remaining patients served as unrepaired controls. The recurrence rate at 3 and 6 months was significantly lower in the repair group (1.0% vs 5.9%, 2% vs 6.9%), though the difference at 2 years was not statistically significant (6.7% vs 12.1%). Zhu et al[22] reported successful annular repair in 19 cases utilizing bilateral cannulas endoscopy, resulting in good clinical outcomes and no recurrence during short-term follow-up. Li et al[23] reported on 50 non-contained LDH patients who underwent single or double suture annular repair, with no recurrence observed at the 12-month follow-up. Parker et al[24] used the Barricaid annular closure device, which demonstrated no recurrence at 24 months and effective maintenance of disc height. Additionally, Song et al[25] conducted a study on adolescent LDH and found that annular repair not only preserved disc biomechanics but also facilitated the postoperative “rehydration” of the nucleus pulposus, thereby promoting the restoration of disc structural integrity.

This study included 86 LDH patients. Both groups exhibited improvement in VAS and ODI scores at all postoperative time points compared to preoperative scores. The excellent outcome rate at 12 months exceeded 90%. The suture group (n = 44) had a recurrence rate of 2.27%, while the control group (n = 42) had a recurrence rate of 9.52%, indicating a statistically significant difference (P < .05). These findings suggest that repairing the annular defect effectively prevents postoperative recurrence before scar tissue healing. At 12 months postoperatively, MRI revealed a decrease in disc height in both groups, with the suture group (22.3%±4.6%) showing a statistically significant smaller decrease compared to the control group (29.8%±6.2%) (P < .05). These results indicate that closing the annular fissure facilitates the restoration of intradiscal pressure, slowing disc height loss and degeneration. However, it is important to note that annular repair under a small working channel endoscope has a steep learning curve. The suture group had a significantly longer operative time (63.28 ± 4.17 minutes) compared to the control group (54.71 ± 4.89 minutes, P < .05), primarily due to the additional time required for suturing. However, it was observed that with increased surgical proficiency (after 15–20 cases), suturing time gradually decreased.

To attain optimal suturing quality in endoscopic annular repair, several critical factors warrant consideration. First, utilizing the working cannula to safeguard the dura and nerve root on the inner aspect while fully exposing the annulus is crucial for safe suturing. Second, the incision should be strategically placed at the site of maximum annular protrusion and tension, as close to the center as possible, while avoiding the vertebral endplate to ensure sufficient annular tissue for repair. Biomechanical studies[26] have demonstrated that the strength along the collagen fiber orientation is 3 times greater than in the horizontal direction. Therefore, an oblique incision following the nerve root orientation is preferred to avoid nerve injury and ensure optimal repair strength. The incision typically spans 5 to 6 mm in length, with the midportion of the fissure selected for suturing. Needle entry points should be perpendicular to the fissure edges and positioned 2 to 3 mm away.[27] Prior to needle insertion, it is advisable to assess the soft tissue using a probe, select the optimal trajectory, and avoid secondary annular injury by ensuring precise needle positioning. When inserting the needle, it is important to first orient the curved needle tip towards the endplate. Once the outer annulus is penetrated, the needle should be rotated 180°to face the fissure and advanced to the depth mark to incorporate more annular tissue for secure repair. To ensure secure knotting, a surgeon’s knot followed by 2 square knots should be tied. Even tension should be applied when tightening the knot to avoid tearing the annulus. An angled knot pusher can be used to apply perpendicular pressure during knot advancement, and the assistant can simultaneously direct the suture at a 45° angle to facilitate knot advancement while protecting the annular tissue.

This study has several limitations. As a retrospective analysis, there is an inherent risk of selection bias. The sample size is relatively small, and the follow-up period is limited to 12 months, which may not capture long-term outcomes. Moreover, the learning curve inherent to the suturing technique may have impacted operative times and initial outcomes. Future studies should include larger cohorts and longer follow-up periods to validate these findings and assess the durability of the repair over time. Exploring the effectiveness of annular suturing in different patient populations, such as those with varying degrees of disc degeneration or different types of disc herniation, would be beneficial. Investigating the long-term biomechanical and biochemical effects of annular repair will provide deeper mechanistic insights and help optimize surgical techniques. Additionally, studies should explore the potential for combining annular repair with biological therapies, such as stem cell or growth factor injections, to further enhance disc regeneration and function.

5. Conclusion

Single-channel percutaneous endoscopic discectomy with annular suturing is a safe and reliable treatment for LDH. This procedure effectively reduces the recurrence rate, slows disc degeneration, and prevents disc height loss, resulting in excellent early outcomes.
