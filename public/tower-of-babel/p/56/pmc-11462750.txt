The impact of physical activity on the prevalence of edentulism: an analysis of the relationships between active lifestyle and dental health
Authors: XingJin Chen, ChongWen Zeng, XianRun Chen, JiangLing Sun, YaMei Li, Zhu Chen, JuKun Song
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEdentulism is considered the ultimate sign of oral health disease burden. It is a common and frequent clinical disease, mostly seen in the elderly, which has been reported to be closely associated with many diseases. While physical activity is known to benefit many chronic diseases, the relationship between the volume of physical activity and edentulism remains unclear.Materials and methodsThe CHARLS provided the data for this study. A total of 6,480 participants were enrolled in this study, of whom 46.02% were male and 53.98% were female. The edentulism was defined according to self-report and physical activity volume can be represented by metabolic equivalents. Multiple logistic regression, smooth curve fitting, threshold effects analysis, and Cox regression models were used to explore the relationship between physical activity volume and edentulism.ResultsIn the fully adjusted model, physically active participants had a 24% lower risk of edentulism compared to those who were physically inactive. Then, we subdivided participants with adequate physical activity into four levels: 600 ≤ PAV < 1200; 1200 ≤ PAV < 1800; 1800 ≤ PAV < 3000; and PAV ≥ 3000. We found that the risk of developing edentulism was lowest and statistically significant at the level of 1,800-3,000 MET-minutes/week physical activity volume (OR = 0.41; 95% CI, 0.24–0.69, p < 0.001). A U-shaped relationship between physical activity volume and edentulism with an inflection point of 2514 (MET-minutes/week) was observed. The results of the longitudinal study also confirm the benefits of physical activity volume with edentulism, participants with adequate physical activity volume had a 21% lower risk of developing edentulism than participants with inadequate physical activity volume (HR = 0.79; 95% CI, 0.67–0.94, p = 0.008).ConclusionsThis study reveals a U-shaped relationship between physical activity volume and edentulism. Physical activity reduces the risk of developing edentulism in middle-aged and older populations, but it is important to control the appropriate volume of physical activity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-20242-0.

Background

Edentulism is defined as the loss of all-natural teeth in the mouth, which is considered the ultimate sign of oral health disease burden [1]. Edentulism reflects a cumulative burden of oral diseases and conditions that can significantly affect the quality of life, self-esteem, and nutritional status [2–4]. Edentulism is a common and frequent clinical disease, mostly seen in the elderly, which has been reported to be closely associated with many diseases. Studies showed that in the middle-aged and elderly population, a higher prevalence of edentulism was associated with a higher rate of depression [5]. In the Chinese population aged 45 years and older, edentulism is associated with a high risk of many chronic diseases, such as memory-related disorders, hypertension, emotional problems, stroke, and heart disease [6, 7]. In addition to being a risk factor for many diseases, edentulism is also a global public health problem, especially in low- and middle-income countries. It is reported that the prevalence rates of edentulism range from 2.9 to 15.3% in low- and middle-income countries and it is also prevalent globally [8–10]. According to the results of the Fourth National Health Epidemiology Survey 2017, the prevalence of edentulism in the age group 65–74 years was 4.5% [11]. The third (2007) and second (1998) findings were 6.8% and 10.5% respectively [12]. However, a decrease in the prevalence of edentulousness does not equal a decrease in the overall number of edentulous patients. According to the 2013 United Nations World Population Ageing Projections, from 2010 to 2040, the proportion of Chinese people over 60 years of age will increase from 12.4 to 28.1% [13]. Aging significantly increases the risk of developing oral diseases, including edentulism [14]. China, as the country with the largest elderly population in the world, the public health burden caused by edentulism is even heavier [15]. Therefore, it is essential to take effective measures to prevent edentulism and promote oral health in the middle-aged and elderly population.

Physical activity is defined as any bodily movement produced by skeletal muscles that requires energy expenditure according to the World Health Organization 2020, which can be categorized into occupational, sports, conditioning, household, or other activities [16]. Physical activity volume is intensity multiplied by time, making volume and intensity intrinsically linked as nested constructs [17]. Physical inactivity has been identified as the fourth leading risk factor for mortality globally, and one of the most prevalent modifiable risk factors for disease [18, 19]. In the last few decades, a large amount of scientific evidence has confirmed the beneficial role of physical activity as a modifiable risk factor for a wide variety of chronic diseases, including Alzheimer’s disease, depression, rheumatoid arthritis, and coronary artery disease [20–24]. Particularly in the older population, physical activity has a positive impact on the quality of life and physical functioning of older persons and helps to maintain independence in basic daily activities [25]. Maintaining a good level of physical functioning, the absence of physical illness and psychological problems, and maintaining an adequate level of physical activity are important for the physical health and quality of life of older people [26].

Some scholars have studied the relationship between physical activity and oral health. A cross-sectional study based on the NHANES databases showed that the prevalence of periodontal disease was lower in individuals with higher total physical activity and lower total sedentary behavior [27]. It has been shown that physical activity is negatively correlated with the presence and severity of periodontitis, with gender differences. This relationship was significant in females, while there was no significant association in males [28]. In a population-based case-control study, researchers found that leisure physical activity prevented the excessive inflammatory response of periodontitis [29]. Past research has been almost exclusively on physical activity and periodontitis, and there is a lack of research on physical activity and edentulism, which is seen as the worst possible outcome of periodontitis progression.

To date, no research has examined the relationship between physical activity and edentulism in middle-aged and elderly individuals, despite edentulism being a prevalent condition in this population. While physical activity is known to offer numerous benefits for managing chronic diseases, its potential role in preventing or reducing edentulism remains unclear. Given this gap, our study seeks to determine whether physical activity can help lower the incidence of edentulism and, if so, to identify the optimal volume of activity required. With an aging population and a growing emphasis on maintaining oral health, there is an urgent need to propose simple, practical strategies to prevent oral diseases like edentulism, which can significantly impact quality of life. By exploring the relationship between physical activity levels and edentulism, our study aims to provide new insights for improving oral health care among middle-aged and elderly people.

Materials and methods

Data source

Data for this study came from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). CHARLS is a survey of middle-aged and older adults in China, and the data include information on a wide range of socio-economic conditions to individual health status, meeting the needs of aging research. The CHARLS national baseline survey began in 2011, using a multi-stage stratified whole-group sampling method. The baseline sample was conducted in 450 villages and settlements in 150 countries and districts in 28 provinces across the country, and four follow-up surveys were conducted in 2013, 2015, 2018, and 2020. The Biomedical Ethics Review Committee of Peking University approved the CHARLS (IRB00001052-11015), and all research participants signed an informed consent form.

Study participants

This study is based on baseline data from 2011 and combines data from 2013, 2015, and 2018. We selected 17,705 participants from the 2011 baseline data, based on which we excluded 11,225 participants due to (1) no information on edentulism; (2) no information on physical activity volume; (3) age < 45 years; (4) no information on other covariates. The final 6,480 participants were enrolled in the cross-sectional study. In the longitudinal analysis of the same group of participants, we followed them from 2011 to 2018, excluding 1,683 participants due to (1) those who had already lost all their teeth in 2011; and (2) no information on edentulism in 2018. Finally, 4,797 participants were enrolled from 2011 to 2018 (Fig. 1).

Physical activity volume and edentulism

The International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) served as the basis for the questionnaire’s framework to assess participants’ degree of physical activity [30]. In the CHARLS “Health Status and Functioning” questionnaire, participants were asked if they engaged in at least 10 min per week of vigorous physical activity (e.g., lifting heavy loads, digging, plowing, aerobics, fast biking, carrying loads on a bike.); at least 10 min per week of moderate physical activity (e.g. lifting light objects, bicycling at a regular pace, mopping the floor, tai chi, brisk walking); and at least 10 min per week of low physical activity (e.g. walking). If the participant responded “Yes,” the participant was then asked about the number of days and duration of physical activity per week. Since the time of physical activity is not a specific value but a period (≥ 10 min and < 30 min; ≥30 min and < 2 h; ≥2 h and < 4 h; and ≥ 4 h), we borrowed from other scholars’ treatments [31]. By choosing a middle value, we managed to adapt the time range. For example, 20 min were recorded for “≥10 min and < 30 min,” 75 min were recorded for “≥30 min and < 2 h,” 180 min were recorded for “≥2 h and < 4 h,” and 240 min were recorded for “≥4 h.” Vigorous Physical Activity Volume = Days of Vigorous Physical Activity × Duration; Moderate Physical Activity Volume = Days of Moderate Physical Activity × Duration; Low Physical Activity Volume = Days of Low Physical Activity Volume× Duration. Finally, total physical activity volume (PAV) can be represented by metabolic equivalents (MET) [32]. PAV = 8 × Vigorous Physical Activity Volume + 4 × Moderate Physical Activity Volume + 3.3 × Low Physical Activity Volume. According to the IPAQ, if physical activity volume did not reach 600 MET-minutes/week, it indicated inadequate physical activity, and vice versa, it indicated adequate physical activity. We further subdivided adequate physical activity into ≥ 600 and < 1200; ≥1200 and < 1800; ≥1800, and < 3000; ≥3000.

Part D of the CHARLS National Questionnaire, which asked participants about their health status and functional information, included the question “Have you lost all of your teeth?”. Meanwhile, we refer to other literature. In this research, edentulism is identified by a positive response to the question, “Have you lost all of your teeth [33, 34]? .

Covariates

This study also took into account the covariates of sex, age, Hukou status, education, marital status, smoking status, drinking status, sleeplessness, depression, hypertension, diabetes, and cardiovascular disease, which may have an impact on the relationship between physical activity volume and edentulism. The baseline national questionnaire consisted of eight main sections, section A through section H. Section A collected personal information on household members other than the primary respondent and his/her spouse. Section B through section H collected information on basic demographics, family, health and functional status, health insurance, work status, income and expenses, and assets, respectively. Demographic information (gender, age, Hukou status, education, marital status) is from Section B - Demographic Background. Information on smoking status, drinking status, sleeplessness, depression, hypertension, diabetes, and cardiovascular disease is from Part D - Health Status and Functioning. Age was divided into two categories (age < 65, age ≥ 65). The Hukou status is categorized into agricultural Hukou, non-agricultural Hukou, unified residence Hukou, and others. Educational level is categorized into below high school and high school and above. Marital status is categorized as married, separated or divorced, widowed, and never married. Smoking status is categorized into three types: now, ever, and never. Drinking status is divided into three types: severe (more than once a month), moderate (less than once a month), and never [34]. Both sleeplessness and depression are categorized as mild (not more than 2 days per week), moderate (3–4 days per week), and severe (5–7 days per week). Information on hypertension, diabetes, and cardiovascular disease was obtained from the question: “Have you been diagnosed with hypertension, diabetes, or cardiovascular disease by a doctor?” A “Yes” means that there is a corresponding disease [35].

Statistical analysis

This study used the statistical software tools RStudio (version 4.3.3) and EmpowerStats (version 2.0) to extract and merge the data, and Adobe Illustrator (version 2021) to create the figures. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and categorical variables are presented as percentages. To determine if there was a significant difference between different physical activity volume groups and edentulism, chi-square, and Kruskal-Wallis tests were used. First, we used multiple logistic regression to assess the independent relationship between physical activity volume and edentulism. In this study, the crude model does not take into account variables; model I only takes into account sex, age, and Hukou status; model II, based on model I, takes into account education, marital status, smoking status, drinking status, sleeplessness, depression, hypertension, diabetes, and cardiovascular disease. Then, the dose-response relationship and the inflection point between physical activity volume and edentulism were assessed using the smooth curve fitting and threshold effect analysis. Finally, we measured follow-up time and used Cox regression models to explore the effect of physical activity volume on edentulism. Every p-value below 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant.

Participants with adequate physical activity (PAV ≥ 600 MET-minutes/week ) had a lower risk of developing edentulism (PAV < 600: 13.09%; PAV ≥ 600: 7.76%; p < 0.001). The association between physical activity volume and edentulism in the cross-sectional study

The results of multiple logistic regression showed a negative correlation between physical activity volume and edentulism (Table 2). Participants with adequate physical activity volume (PAV ≥ 600 MET-minutes/week) had a 44% lower risk of developing edentulism compared with those with inadequate physical activity volume (PAV < 600 MET-minutes/week) in the crude model (OR = 0.56; 95% CI, 0.46–0.68; p < 0.001). The least adjusted model (OR = 0.73; 95% CI, 0.60–0.90, p = 0.003) and the fully adjusted model (OR = 0.76; 95% CI, 0.62–0.94, p = 0.012) also showed a significant negative correlation between physical activity volume and edentulism. In the fully adjusted model, the risk of developing edentulism was reduced by 24%. Then, we subdivided participants with adequate physical activity into four levels: 600 ≤ PAV < 1200; 1200 ≤ PAV < 1800; 1800 ≤ PAV < 3000; and PAV ≥ 3000. In the fully adjusted model, the risk of developing edentulism was lowest and statistically significant at the level of 1,800-3,000 MET-minutes/week physical activity volume (OR = 0.41; 95% CI, 0.24–0.69, p < 0.001).

Furthermore, we conducted a smooth curve fitting (Fig. 2) and threshold effects analysis (Table 3) to describe the nonlinear relationship between physical activity volume and edentulism. A U-shaped relationship between physical activity volume and edentulism with an inflection point of 2514 (MET-minutes/week) was observed. Before the inflection point, the risk of developing edentulism decreased with increased physical activity volume; after the inflection point, the risk of developing edentulism increased with increased physical activity volume. The log-likelihood ratio test p-value is significant (p < 0.001).

The association between physical activity volume and edentulism in the longitudinal study

During the 7-year follow-up, the prevalence of edentulism was 16.97%. The prevalence of edentulism was 22.64% in participants with PAV < 600 and 15.88% in participants with PAV ≥ 600 (Fig. 3). In a longitudinal study, we further explored the effects of physical activity volume on edentulism (Table 4). In fully adjusted model II, participants with adequate physical activity volume had a 21% lower risk of developing edentulism than participants with inadequate physical activity volume (HR = 0.79; 95% CI, 0.67–0.94, p = 0.008).

Discussion

Both cross-sectional and longitudinal studies based on CHARLES revealed a negative correlation between physical activity volume and edentulism. This association emphasizes the importance of promoting physical activity among middle-aged and older adults, not only for general health but also to maintain oral health. More interestingly, a U-shape association between physical activity volume and edentulism with an inflection point of 2514 MET-minutes/week was found, suggesting that more physical activity is not better and that the risk of edentulism is lowest only within an appropriate range. Strategies that combine improved oral health and physical activity may be beneficial in preventing edentulism in middle-aged and older adults and contribute to their quality of life.

Previous studies have reported the benefits of physical activity volume, one of the most prevalent modifiable risk factors, for many diseases, including oral diseases. A study shows that in physically active individuals, periodontal microbial antibody titers reflecting good oral health tended to increase [36]. Another study found that physical activity is associated with a lower prevalence of periodontitis [37]. In an experimental study, physical activity was found to improve glycemic and inflammatory conditions and reduce the progression of periodontitis in diabetic rats [38]. Overall, previous studies have confirmed the benefits of physical activity for oral diseases. The most widely studied oral disease is periodontitis, with forms of research including experimental studies [39], cross-sectional studies [28], systematic evaluations and meta-analyses [40].

In the present study, physical activity volume and edentulism showed a significant negative correlation. In the multiple logistic regression, when the population with physical activity volume higher than 600 MET-minutes/week was subdivided into several intervals, a statistically significant reduction in the risk of edentulism for the population with physical activity volume in the range of 1800–3000 MET-minutes/week was found. This U-shape relationship was also verified in the smoothed curve fitting and threshold effect analysis. This suggests that, regarding the relationship between physical activity volume and edentulism, increasing the volume of physical activity beyond a certain point does not provide additional benefits. Only an appropriate amount of physical activity can significantly reduce the risk of edentulism. The reasons may be as follows.

From the etiology of the disease. Periodontal disease is the major cause of tooth loss. Periodontitis is a chronic inflammatory disease, and the role of inflammation plays a key role in aggravating periodontitis, resulting in tooth loss [41, 42]. Studies have shown that inflammation is a physiologic response of the body to a noxious stimulus, but when the inflammatory process is sustained, the inflammation becomes detrimental, leading to low-grade chronic inflammation and is associated with skeletal muscle atrophy, loss of strength, and increased functional impairment [43, 44]. Physical activity interventions have been shown to reduce oxidative stress and inflammation at the cellular or tissue level [45]. In addition, moderate-intensity physical activity has been found to decrease levels of the pro-inflammatory cytokine tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α, which is closely related to the body’s immune response, acute phase response and inflammatory response), whereas high-intensity physical activity may increase resting-state levels of pro-inflammatory cytokine Interleukin- 6 (IL-6, which is the most typical cytokine associated with inflammation), which can exacerbate symptoms of inflammation, including illness behaviors [46]. Moderate physical training can lead to a decrease in the TNF-α/IL-10 ratio (IL-10 strongly inhibits the synthesis of IL-6 and TNF-α at the transcriptional level, thereby exerting an anti-inflammatory effect), leading to an improvement in the inflammatory state [47]. This suggests that the intensity of physical activity is important and coincides with our study. The underlying causes need to be explored in more deeply studies.

From lifestyle habits aspect. People who adopt healthy lifestyles are usually more aware of their general health, including oral hygiene [48]. Physical activity, as a healthy lifestyle, helps to prevent systemic diseases that can lead to edentulism, such as diabetes or cardiovascular disease [49, 50].

From the psychological aspect. An active lifestyle improves mental health and reduces stress and anxiety, which in turn benefits not only general health but oral health as well [51].

In a word, physical activity and edentulism are closely related. However, at present, this conclusion only remains in the theoretical aspect, and we hope to further explore the mechanism of physical activity and edentulism through basic research. At the same time, the relationship between physical activity and other oral diseases, such as caries and periodontal disease, can also be explored in future research.

The study has the following advantages. This is the first study to explore the relationship between physical activity and edentulism in a middle-aged and elderly Chinese population. Meantime, the benefits of physical activity volume on edentulism were investigated using both cross-sectional and longitudinal studies, which helped identify an optimal volume of physical activity. Besides, through a rigorous multi-stage probability sampling procedure to select study participants and effective quality control, CHARLS represents the samples of the middle-aged and elderly and meets the scientific needs related to the aging problem and policy research [52].

Despite the many original and meaningful results demonstrated in this study, there are still many limitations of this study. First, the definition of edentulism was obtained from self-reports, which may be subject to bias. Specific dates and causes of total tooth loss were not included in this study because CHARLS does not disclose or collect this information. Second, even after controlling for some relevant confounding variables, we could not completely rule out the influence of other confounding factors on the results. In addition, the article is only based on the Chinese database, and the study is about middle-aged and elderly people, so the conclusions may not apply to other countries and other age groups [53, 54]. Finally, the conclusions are only at the theoretical level and the mechanism of action is not clear, which needs to be confirmed by more fundamental studies.

Conclusion

This cross-sectional and longitudinal CHARLS-based study reveals a U-shaped relationship between physical activity volume and edentulism. Physical activity reduces the risk of developing edentulism in middle-aged and older populations, but it is important to control the appropriate volume of physical activity. Physical activity in the range of 1800–3000 MET-minutes/week may be optimal for reducing the risk of edentulism, which provides a guide to the volume of physical activity in middle-aged and older populations. Meanwhile, it is more important for dentists to prevent oral diseases from occurring than to treat them. The findings of the study may provide dentists and edentulous patients with a simple, implementable method to prevent oral diseases. Thus, encouraging an active lifestyle, along with oral hygiene education, can help reduce the prevalence of edentulous. Public health programs should integrate aspects of physical activity and oral health to promote a holistic approach to health.
