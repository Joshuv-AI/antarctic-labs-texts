First in-vivo multicenter experience with the novel CVAC 2.0 ureteroscope with simultaneous irrigation and aspiration functionality
Authors: Christopher C. Ballantyne, Halle E. Foss, Jackson J. Cabo, Daniel Turin, Victoria S. Edmonds, Kevin M. Wymer, Aaron M. Potretzke, Mitchell R. Humphreys, Karen L. Stern
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeAdvancements in ureteroscopy (URS) technology aim to reduce postoperative residual stone burden. A steerable ureteroscope renal evacuation (SURE) procedure using the CVAC® steerable aspiration catheter (Calyxo Pleasanton, CA) has shown safety and efficacy in a prior prospective, randomized multi-center trial. The newer CVAC 2.0 system is a single-use ureteroscope that combines laser lithotripsy, vacuum, irrigation, aspiration, and stone collection. We report the first in-vivo multicenter experience using the new CVAC 2.0 system.MethodsA multicenter analysis of a prospectively maintained database from two tertiary centers (5 surgeons) of patients who underwent URS with the CVAC 2.0 system between March 2024 to January 2025 was performed. Adult patients with at least one renal stone on CT imaging were included. Patient demographics, intraoperative data, peri-operative complications, and stone-free status based on volumetric and single-dimension stone clearance were recorded. Patients with incomplete imaging or nephrocalcinosis were excluded from volumetric analysis.ResultsA total of 84 cases were included, with 44 eligible for volumetric stone clearance analysis. 81 (96.4%) had no intra-operative complications, 3 had Clavien-Dindo (CD) I and 10 had CDII post-operative complications. Median preoperative stone volume for eligible patients was 835 mm3 (diameter 20.5 mm) and post-operative volume was 14.5mm3 (diameter 3.35 mm), resulting in an absolute stone volume reduction of 796.6mm3 with relative stone clearance of 98.4%. Pearson correlation analysis showed no increase in residual volume as preoperative stone volume increased.ConclusionThe new CVAC 2.0 system is safe and allows effective extraction of stone burden, especially in patients with higher stone volumes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00345-025-05912-x.

Introduction

Ureteroscopy (URS) with laser lithotripsy is one of the most commonly performed procedures in urology and over the past several years technological advancements have been introduced to improve stone-free-rate (SFR) and optimize patient outcomes [1]. A 2021 metanalysis inclusive of 9 studies, reported pooled stone free rates of 88.3% [95% CI: 78.3–98.3%] with conventional ureteroscopy, highlighting that residual fragments are not uncommon [2]. Likewise, the multi-institutional, retrospective, FLEXible Ureteroscopy outcomes (FLEXOR) registry found 21.7% of patients undergoing primary ureteroscopy for renal stones had residual fragments of at least 2 mm with approximately half of these patients ultimately requiring a second procedure [3]. Ghani and Wolf reported a zero stone free rate closer to 55-60% when using cross-sectional imaging [4].

New technologies incorporating suction and aspiration during URS have been developed to further enhance SFR, reduce reliance on stone baskets, and minimize need for reintervention [5]. One such advancement is the Flexible And Navigable ureteral access Sheaths (FANS). FANS utilizes passive and active suction to remove the small dust during laser ablation and when fragments are unable to pass beside ureteroscope, the ureteroscope is withdrawn to end of the sheath removing the fragments [6]. A recent multicenter randomized trial demonstrated a 95% SFR with the use of FANS [7]. Direct in-scope suctioning system (DISS) is another suction device that integrates suction through the working channel of the ureteroscope. This system creates negative pressure through the working channel to evaluate stone fragments after laser lithotripsy to evacuate stone dust [8]. Recent studies have reported SFR of 84.21–96.6% using the DISS technique [8].

Steerable ureteroscopic renal evacuation (SURE) procedure utilizing the first-generation CVAC® radiographically steerable aspiration catheter (Calyxo Pleasanton, CA), has proven to be safe and effective for aspirating large stone volumes (SV) in a single procedure though does not offer direct vision [9, 10]. A recent randomized controlled trial demonstrated that the SURE system was non-inferior for SFR compared to traditional URS [11]. CVAC 2.0 is the second generation of the SURE technology with dual aspiration/irrigation channels built into a 11.9-french (Fr) single-use digital ureteroscope (Appendix A).

In this study, we report the first prospective, in-vivo, multicenter evaluation of CVAC 2.0 ureteroscope. The aim of the study is to describe the intraoperative experience, 30-day post-operative complications, SFR status, and stone volume reduction (SVR).

Materials and methods

This investigator-led, non-randomized, prospective multicenter study was conducted across two tertiary care centers where patients underwent URS with the CVAC 2.0 system from March 2024 to January 2025. Institutional Review Board approval was obtained at each site (IRB #24-007550). All patients were ≥ 18 years old and had at least one renal stone on non-contrast CT (NCCT) imaging, with patient selection based on surgeon discretion. Patients with ureteral stents in place and unstented patients were both eligible. Procedures were performed by five fellowship-trained endourologists. Follow-up imaging was requested for all patients 1–3 months following index URS, unless a staged procedure was planned. Safety and complication data were analyzed for the cohort. For volumetric analysis of stone clearance and SFR, patients needed pre- and post-operative CT scan with 3.75 mm slices or thinner. Exclusion from stone-free and clearance analysis included patients undergoing staged procedures, those with significant nephrocalcinosis, incomplete CT imaging, and those in other study requiring different follow-up imaging modalities (Fig. 1). In order to capture complications in the broader population, patients ineligible for volumetric analysis were included for the safety analysis.

Baseline and intraoperative data were collected for all patients. Pre-operative stone diameter was measured from pre-operative CT scans (3 mm slices or thinner) from the maximal diameter of each ipsilateral renal stone using bone window, then combined for cumulative diameter. Ureteral stones were excluded unless repositioned in kidney prior to treatment. Pre- and post-operative SVs were measured using Quantitative Stone Analysis Software (QSAS) (Mayo Clinic, Rochester, MN), a semi-automated software which requires clinician annotation of the area of interest [12]. After software performed stone segmentation, clinicians reviewed and removed any artifacts on initial analysis.

CVAC 2.0 single-use flexible ureteroscope

The CVAC® 2.0 (Calyxo Pleasanton, CA) is a 11.9Fr single-use digital URS with a 7Fr working channel (Appendix A). It features distinct vacuum and irrigation channels and an integrated stone collector on the handle. The ureteroscope is unique with independent channels that generate continuous flow, enabling simultaneous irrigation and vacuum aspiration of stone fragments during and after laser lithotripsy. A single trigger controls both flow and aspiration, and at baseline, such as during laser lithotripsy, the scope has passive suction, with active aspiration activated via the trigger (Appendix A). The 7Fr working channels allows for large stone fragment evacuation, up to ≤ 2 mm once the laser bridge has been removed. Due to the large working channel, a laser stabilization catheter is inserted to stabilize the 200 or 272 micron laser. After lithotripsy, the laser bridge is removed to allow the 7Fr working channel to aspirate stone fragments.

A 12/14 ureteral access sheath (UAS) of varying length was placed over a wire to the level of proximal ureter in all cases and accommodates the scope. The CVAC 2.0 URS was advanced either over a wire with fluoroscopy or under direct visualization into the renal pelvis. Use of a safety wire next to the UAS was up to surgeon preference. Irrigation was provided by the ThermedX® system or pressure bags set to 150 mmHg, and wall suction was set to 150 mmHg. Choice of laser (Holmium or Thulium Fiber Laser (TFL), ) and laser strategy were based on surgeon preference. Intraoperative and device related complications were recorded. Upon procedure completion, the ureter was carefully inspected upon removal of the CVAC ureteroscope with ureteral injuries graded according to Traxer grading system [13]. All patients had a ureteral stent placed at the end of the procedure with duration determined by surgeon preference.

Outcomes and statistical analysis

30-day complications were evaluated with Clavien-Dindo (CD) classifications. Sepsis events were evaluated using Sepsis-3 criteria. Post-operative CT scans were obtained within 3 months of index procedure in patients eligible for volumetric analysis (Fig. 1). Post-operative cumulative residual diameter and cumulative residual volume (RV) were evaluated. Stone-free data were classified into four grades: Grade A (no RFs), Grade B (Grade A plus ≤ 2 mm fragments), and Grade C (Grade A and B plus 2.1–4 mm fragments), or not stone-free (> 4 mm fragments) [14].

In order to assess for risk of bias in our volumetric analysis, we performed a planned sub analysis comparing demographic, stone burden, and intraoperative variables between patients eligible for volumetric analysis and those who did not have eligible imaging. Comparisons were performed using Wilcoxon (rank-sum) tests for continuous variables and Chi-squared/Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. Pearson correlation analysis was performed to assess interaction between pre-operative SV versus RV and relative reduction in SV [11]. Linear regression was performed on correlation of preoperative SV and with residual SV. Logistic regression was performed to assess associations of preoperative SV parameters, with staged procedures, and 30-day infectious complications. All statistical analysis was performed using R version 4.4.1 (Vienna, Austria).

Results

A total of 84 patients underwent URS with CVAC 2.0 from 03/2024 to 01/2025 across two tertiary centers. Of these, 65% (N = 50) had > 2.0 cm of stone preoperatively, while 29% (n = 22) patients had lower pole stone burden > 1.5 cm, and 49% (n = 41) were pre-stented (Supplemental Table 1). 40 patients were excluded from volumetric analysis: 35 for incomplete imaging (e.g. planned staged procedures, renal ultrasound, or inaccessible post-operative imaging), 2 for unplanned imaging between staged procedures, and 3 for significant medullary nephrocalcinosis (Fig. 1). No significant differences were found in demographic variables and comorbidities between those eligible and ineligible for volumetric analysis (Supplemental Table 2). Likewise, there is no statistical difference between pre-operative SV between the two groups.

44 patients were eligible for volumetric analysis. The pre-operative median cumulative stone diameter was 20.6 mm (16.6–34.2), and the SV was 835.1mm3 (492–1254). Post-operative SV decreased to 14.5mm3 (1.5–84.7), with cumulative residual diameter at 3.35 mm (0-9.7). The absolute SVR was 796.6mm3 (381–1248), corresponding to a relative stone clearance of 98.4% (Table 1). SFR were observed as follows: Grade A SFR 27%, Grade B 39%, and SFR grade C 73% of cases (Table 1). Preoperative stone parameters, including volume, location, and Hounsfield units, did not influence the SFR (Supplemental Table 3).

A total of 10 staged procedures were performed, 9 of which were planned and 1 unplanned. The median pre-operative SV in the staged procedures was 1872mm3 (962–2585) and median cumulative diameter was 60.6 mm (33.90–74.4). Logistic regression analysis revealed that for every increase in 100mm3 of SV there was a 6% higher chance of requiring a staged procedure (OR 1.06 95% CI 1.02–1.13, p = 0.017) (Supplemental Table 4).

Linear regression analysis revealed no significant relationship between preoperative SV and RV (Supplemental Table 5). However, Pearson correlation analysis demonstrated a significant positive linear association between pre-operative SV and the percentage of SV cleared (R = 0.31, p = 0.038) (Fig. 2A). There was no significant correlation between preoperative SV and residual SV (Fig. 2B).

Intra-operative complications occurred in 3 patients (3.6%) across the entire cohort (Supplemental Table 6). These included parapelvic cyst rupture, a grade 2 ureteral injury, and bladder neck bleeding requiring post-operative foley. 30-day complications were recorded in 15.5% (n = 13) of patients, including 10 CDII complications (8 postoperative infections) and 3 CDI I complications (Supplemental Table 6). Patients with positive pre-operative culture were 5.76 times more likely to have a postoperative infection (OR 5.76, 95% CI 1.23–41.3, p = 0.04) (Supplemental Table 7). Pre-stenting and operative time did not significantly affect 30-day infectious complications. No cases of sepsis were reported, and no device malfunctions occurred. Intermittent device clogging did occur but resolved with clearing of the working channel.

Discussion

In this multicenter study, we demonstrated that the CVAC 2.0 ureteroscope is both safe and effective for treating patients with large SVs, building upon the positive outcomes observed with the first-generation CVAC. CVAC 2.0 achieved a clearance of 98.4%, despite a large preoperative stone burden. A SFR of ≤ 2.0 mm fragments (Grade B) was observed in 39% of patients in this study, as compared to 21.7% in patients undergoing conventional ureteroscopy in the FLEXOR registry [3]. In the present study, in 73% of index procedures, patients achieved ≤ 4 mm fragments. RFs > 4 mm has been linked to higher rates of stone events, with RF > 2 mm more likely to grow than smaller fragments [15, 16]. Notably, these studies assessed RF based on two-dimensional measurements without considering SVR or stone clearance, and such methods have been criticized for lacking standardization and consensus [17, 18].

Recent data evaluating the impact of RV on stone events shows a lower retreatment rate with aspiration device (4.3% vs. 16.4%) compared to conventional URS approach [18, 19]. Moreover, aspiration resulted in a median SV of 11.7 ± 25.8 mm3, as opposed to 57.8 ± 124.6 mm3 with conventional URS [18]. This finding highlights the need for further evaluation of the relationship between stone events and SVR or stone clearance, as SVR or RV may offer a more accurate predictor for stone events than two-dimensional RF measurements.

Traditional URS has demonstrated diminishing benefits with increasing stone volumes, which is why percutaneous nephrolithotomy is recommended for stones > 2.0 cm [20–23]. Gauhar et al.. noted lower SFR with FANS when the SV burden > 1500mm3 [14]. To our knowledge, DISS has not been evaluated in large SV, with a recent study by Nedbal et al.. reporting a mean SV of 480mm3 [8]. In contrast, the CVAC 2.0 ureteroscope showed that RV did not increase with increasing volume (R = 0.31, p = 0.038). This positive correlation may be attributed to the larger working channel of the CVAC 2.0 (7Fr). The first-generation CVAC system had similar working channel (7.5Fr), which allowed aspiration of up to 2 mm, compared to DISS PUSEN ureteroscope (Zhuhai, Pusen China) working channel of 3.6Fr which limited to < 250 μm fragments [8, 9, 24]. As urologists become more experienced with CVAC 2.0, we expect the SVR to increase, allowing its application to large SV cases compared to other URS lithotripsy methods.

Safety evaluation of the CVAC 2.0 ureteroscope revealed a 3.6% rate of intraoperative complications and 15.5% 30-day postoperative complications. Notably, there were no instances of sepsis across the entire cohort, and only 8 post-operative infections (9.5%). The sepsis rate is lower than the reported rates following traditional URS of 5.0% [25]. Of the 8 patients who developed infections, 6 had positive preoperative urine culture, a known risk factor for post-operative infection [25]. While the odds ratio of developing infections in positive preoperative cultures was elevated, this finding may be influenced by the small event rate coupled with small sample size.

Among the intraoperative complications, 1 grade 2 injury (1.2%) was noted, which was managed with the placement of a ureteral stent. Importantly no other ureteral injuries were observed. The ureteral injury rates with CVAC 2.0 are comparable to those reported in large multicenter FANS trial, which noted 7 grade 1and 1 grade 2 injuries among 364 patients [14]. The CVAC 2.0 ureteroscope has proven safe in crossing the ureteropelvic junction, similar to the FANS system. Furthermore, 51% of patients in our cohort were not pre-stented prior to placement of the CVAC 2.0 ureteroscope, which demonstrates that the ureteroscope is safe to use in that patient population if the ureter is compliant. We found the CVAC 2.0 scope can pass through a 12/14 sheath without issue, though there may be some impact on mobility given the narrow clearance, particularly when navigating to the lower pole. An important consideration given that there is minimal clearance between the 11.9 Fr scope and the 12/14 Fr UAS wall is that the surgeon must be careful to ensure the outflow (suction) channel of the ureteroscope is unobstructed. The outflow channel of the scope can clog intermittently (especially while the laser cannula is in place) but can be cleared with several different maneuvers, with the details outside the scope of this manuscript. Future work should also aim to determine the optimal sheath size to utilize with the CVAC 2.0 scope to minimize intrarenal pressure.

There are several limitations to this study. The cohort size is relatively small, despite being drawn from two high-volume tertiary centers, which may limit the generalizability of results to other institutions. The CVAC ureteroscope was not compared to other devices such as conventional URS or other suction-based devices, which could have provided more comprehensive comparison. We did not control for laser type or lithotripsy technique, which could influence outcomes. 40 patients were excluded from volumetric analysis due to incomplete imaging or undergoing staged procedures limiting volumetric analysis, although all cases were included for safety analysis. Additionally, about half of our cohort was pre-stented, and a ureteral access sheath used in all cases. This may limit generalizability to a broader population of patients undergoing ureteroscopy, as the large scope size (11.9 Fr) may complicate successful scope placement particularly in patients with more narrow ureters. Despite these limitations, this study is the first to evaluate the CVAC 2.0 ureteroscope and its performance in clinical setting.

Conclusion

In this first reported multicenter study on CVAC 2.0, we evaluated the safety outcomes and stone clearance of the novel ureteroscope. The new device was shown to clear > 98% of pre-operative stone volume, in patients with starting stone volumes over 800 mm3 with 73% being free of residual fragments over 4 mm. No significant adverse events were encountered, and infection rates were low. As experience with the device improves, we expect continued improvement in stone-free-rates and stone clearance.
