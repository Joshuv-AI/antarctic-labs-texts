Effect of transcutaneous auricular vagus nerve stimulation on postoperative pain in patients undergoing thoracoscopic partial lung resection: a randomized, double-blind, controlled clinical trial
Authors: Meng Yuan, Si-Jin Lv, Meng-Ru Wu, Yu-Chen Lu, Min-Hao Zhang, Jian-Hua He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPostoperative pain after thoracic surgery remains common and challenging. Transcutaneous auricular vagus nerve stimulation (taVNS) is a noninvasive neuromodulation technique with potential analgesic effects. This study aimed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of taVNS for postoperative pain management in patients undergoing thoracoscopic partial lung resection.MethodsAdults undergoing thoracoscopic partial lung resection were randomized to active or sham taVNS. The primary outcome was cough pain intensity at 48h post-surgery, assessed by Numeric Rating Scale (NRS). Secondary outcomes included cough pain at 24h and 72h, resting pain, moderate-to-severe pain incidence, opioid consumption, quality of recovery, postoperative pulmonary complications , chest tube duration, hospital stay, postoperative nausea/vomiting, and adverse events.ResultsAmong 119 analyzed patients (active n = 60, sham n = 59), active taVNS reduced cough pain scores at 24h, 48h, and 72h postoperatively, as well as resting pain (p < 0.05). It also lowered the incidence of moderate-to-severe cough pain at 24h and 48h, reduced cumulative postoperative opioid use at 24h and 72h, and decreased rescue analgesia on postoperative day 3 (p < 0.05). Active taVNS was associated with a lower incidence of postoperative pneumothorax (p < 0.05). No serious adverse events occurred.ConclusionPerioperative taVNS was associated with a modest analgesic benefit and reduced postoperative opioid requirements after thoracoscopic partial lung resection. The observed reduction in postoperative pneumothorax requires cautious interpretation, and further multicenter trials are needed to determine its clinical utility.

Introduction

According to the 2022 global cancer statistics, lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer incidence and mortality worldwide [1]. With the widespread use of low-dose computed tomography and high-resolution computed tomography for screening, the detection rate of early-stage lung cancer has increased, often presenting as small pulmonary nodules. For these patients, thoracoscopic minimally invasive surgery has become the primary treatment, offering reduced surgical trauma, less postoperative pain, and faster recovery compared with open thoracotomy [2].

However, acute postoperative pain after thoracoscopic surgery remains common, with approximately 16%–38% of patients experiencing moderate-to-severe pain [3,4]. Inadequate pain control not only increases discomfort and anxiety but also impairs respiratory function and sputum clearance. Current analgesic strategies, such as intravenous patient-controlled analgesia, epidural analgesia, and regional blocks, are effective but limited by opioid-related adverse effects and procedural risks [4–6]. Therefore, safe and effective non-pharmacological interventions have gained increasing attention.

Transcutaneous auricular vagus nerve stimulation (taVNS) is a noninvasive neuromodulation technique that exerts its effects by stimulating the auricular branch of the vagus nerve. Evidence suggests that taVNS produces analgesic effects by modulating pain signal transmission and suppressing inflammatory responses via activation of the nucleus tractus solitarius–vagus nerve pathway and the cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway [7–9]. Previous studies have demonstrated that taVNS reduces postoperative pain and opioid consumption in orthopedic trauma, alleviates uterine contraction and incision pain after cesarean delivery while improving postpartum anxiety and depression, and has also been shown in proctologic surgery to improve pain at multiple postoperative time points and reduce the need for rescue analgesia [10–12]. More recently, studies in thoracoscopic lobectomy indicate that taVNS may help mitigate postoperative pain and decrease the risk of developing chronic pain [13].

Nevertheless, current evidence remains limited, and its clinical significance is unclear. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the analgesic efficacy and safety of taVNS in patients with early-stage lung cancer undergoing thoracoscopic partial lung resection, and to investigate its effects on opioid consumption, postoperative pulmonary complications (PPCs), and postoperative recovery.

Methods

Study design and participants

This randomized, double-blind, controlled trial enrolled adult patients undergoing elective thoracoscopic partial lung resection at the Jiangsu Cancer Hospital. The study was approved by the institutional ethics committee (KY-2025-041-01) and registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2500107199, August 6, 2025; https://www.chictr.org.cn/bin/project/edit?pid=280887), and conducted in accordance with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) 2025 guidelines.

Adults aged 18–65 years with American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status I–III undergoing elective thoracoscopic partial lung resection were eligible. All participants provided written informed consent. Exclusion criteria included clinically significant arrhythmias requiring intervention (e.g. sinus bradycardia <50 beats/min or atrioventricular block), severe cardiopulmonary dysfunction (e.g. NYHA class III/IV, or requiring home oxygen therapy), bleeding tendency, fever, electrolyte imbalance or unstable vital signs, prior thoracic surgery (including both thoracotomy and thoracoscopic surgery), history of epilepsy, skin lesions at the stimulation site, severe psychiatric disorders, vagus nerve stimulation within the past month, history of chemotherapy or radiotherapy, severe diabetic neuropathy, implanted electronic devices (e.g. pacemaker or deep brain stimulator), chronic pain or long-term analgesic use, and participation in other interventional trials.

Patients were withdrawn if extensive pleural adhesions were identified intraoperatively, conversion to thoracotomy occurred, severe intraoperative complications developed, or if postoperative intensive care unit (ICU) admission, reintubation/mechanical ventilation, reoperation for bleeding, or withdrawal of consent occurred.

Intervention

Electrical stimulation was administered to all participants using a transcutaneous electrical stimulation device (tVNS501, RISHENA, Changzhou, China). Stimulation parameters were set as follows: pulse width 200μs, frequency 25 Hz, and a duty cycle of 30 s on/30 s off. The current intensity was gradually increased from 10 mA until the participant perceived a distinct tingling or pricking sensation, and then reduced to a subthreshold level.

Two stimulation sessions were performed: the first on the afternoon before surgery and the second after the start of skin incision during surgery. Each session lasted 30 min. In the active group, electrodes were placed in the left cymba conchae. In the sham group, electrodes were placed on the left earlobe, with otherwise identical procedures.

Randomization and blinding

Participants were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to the active group or the sham group by an independent researcher not involved in data collection or outcome assessment. Allocation was concealed using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes kept by a study coordinator. After enrollment, envelopes were opened sequentially to determine group assignment. Participants, postoperative follow-up staff, and statisticians were blinded to group allocation throughout the study until completion of the final analysis.

Perioperative management

Upon arrival in the operating room, standard monitoring was applied, including electrocardiography, noninvasive blood pressure, and pulse oximetry. Radial arterial catheterization was performed under local anesthesia (2% lidocaine) for invasive blood pressure monitoring. General anesthesia was induced with sufentanil (0.3–0.6 μg/kg), midazolam (0.02–0.075 mg/kg), propofol (1.5–2.5 mg/kg), and rocuronium (0.6 mg/kg). After adequate neuromuscular blockade, one-lung ventilation was established using either a double-lumen tube or a single-lumen tube with a bronchial blocker, with positioning confirmed by fiberoptic bronchoscopy.

Before pleural opening, patients were ventilated with volume-controlled ventilation (tidal volume 6–8 mL/kg, respiratory rate 12–16 breaths/min, inspiratory-to-expiratory ratio 1:1.5–2.0). After pleural opening, lung-protective one-lung ventilation was applied (tidal volume 4–6 mL/kg, positive end-expiratory pressure 4–8 cmH2O), with adjustments to maintain SpO2 ≥95% and end-tidal CO2 within the target range.

Anesthesia was maintained with continuous infusions of propofol (4–8 mg/kg/h), remifentanil (0.15–0.3 μg/kg/min), rocuronium (0.3–0.6 mg/kg/h), and dexmedetomidine (0.2–0.7 μg/kg/h). Additional sufentanil (0.1–0.2 μg/kg) or oxycodone (0.03–0.05 mg/kg) was administered as needed. Dezocine (10 mg) was given intravenously 30 min before the end of surgery. Some surgeons performed local infiltration analgesia at the incision and deep muscle layers using liposomal bupivacaine (266 mg/20 ml), which is currently indicated only for wound infiltration., and its use was recorded.

At the end of surgery, lung recruitment maneuvers were performed, and patients were transferred to the PACU with tracheal intubation. Extubation was performed by an experienced anesthesiologist once standard criteria (Steward recovery score ≥4) were met and the patient was hemodynamically stable.

Postoperative analgesia was provided using intravenous patient-controlled intravenous analgesia (PCIA) with sufentanil (0.6–2.5 μg/kg) with or without oxycodone (0–0.3 mg/kg), combined with ondansetron (8 mg) and diluted with normal saline to 100 mL. The background infusion rate was 0.5–1.0 mL/h, with a bolus dose of 1.5–2.0 mL. Rescue analgesia with subcutaneous morphine (5–10 mg) was administered as needed.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was postoperative pain intensity during coughing at 48 h after surgery, assessed using the numeric rating scale (NRS; 0 = no pain, 10 = worst pain imaginable).

Secondary outcomes included cough pain intensity at 24 and 72 h after surgery; pain intensity at rest; incidence of moderate-to-severe postoperative pain (defined as NRS ≥4) at rest and during coughing at 24, 48 and 72 h; number of patients receiving rescue analgesia and the amount of rescue opioid consumption; cumulative PCIA button presses; time to first PCIA demand; postoperative and perioperative total opioid consumption (expressed as intravenous morphine equivalents); quality of recovery assessed using the 15-item Quality of Recovery scale (QoR-15); PPCs were defined per EPCO criteria (respiratory tract infection, respiratory failure, pleural effusion, atelectasis, pneumothorax, bronchospasm, and aspiration pneumonitis) and assessed within 72 h after surgery [14]; postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV); time to chest tube removal; length of hospital stay; and taVNS-related adverse events.

Sample size calculation

Sample size was estimated using PASS software based on an unequal-variance two-sample t-test (Welch’s t-test), with a two-sided significance level of 0.05 and a target power of 90%. The 48-hour postoperative cough pain score was prespecified as the primary endpoint for sample size estimation because pilot data from 30 patients demonstrated the largest between-group difference and acceptable variability at this time point (mean difference, 1.0 NRS point [4.067 vs. 3.067]; standard deviations, 1.792 and 1.223, respectively). From a clinical perspective, postoperative pain at approximately 48 h remains highly relevant, as most patients still have chest tubes in place and effective coughing is essential for early pulmonary rehabilitation. Allowing for a 20% dropout rate, the required sample size was 64 patients per group (128 in total). Ultimately, 119 patients were enrolled.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 27.0. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± SD or median (interquartile range), and categorical variables as counts (percentages). Postoperative pain scores were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test and generalized estimating equations (GEE) to account for repeated measurements. As a sensitivity analysis, additional GEE models were constructed adjusting for procedure-related variables that could potentially influence postoperative pain, including surgical procedure (wedge resection versus segmentectomy), lymph node dissection status, and number of chest tubes placed. Other continuous variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test or independent-samples t test, as appropriate. Categorical variables were analyzed using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test. Time to first PCIA demand was analyzed using Kaplan–Meier curves with the log-rank test. Multivariable logistic regression was used to identify risk factors for PPCs. All analysis were performed under blinded conditions. A two-sided p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Between September 7 and November 27, 2025, 169 patients scheduled for elective video-assisted thoracoscopic partial lung resection at Jiangsu Cancer Hospital were assessed. Of these, 39 were excluded (34 did not meet inclusion criteria and 3 declined), leaving 130 patients randomized 1:1 to the active stimulation group (n = 65) or sham group (n = 65). Following randomization, 3 patients in each group were excluded intraoperatively due to excessive pleural adhesions. During follow-up, 2 patients in the active group and 3 in the sham group were lost. Ultimately, 119 patients (active: n = 60; sham: n = 59) were included in the final analysis (Figure 1). Given that less than 5% of the data were missing, no imputation was performed for the missing data.

Baseline characteristics and intraoperative data

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were comparable between groups, with no significant differences in age, sex distribution, BMI (body mass index), preoperative anxiety and depression scores (Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale‑Anxiety, Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale‑Depression, Amsterdam Preoperative Anxiety and Information Scale), comorbidities, surgical history, smoking and alcohol use, ASA status or surgical side (Table 1). Intraoperative variables, including anesthesia and surgical duration, analgesic consumption, one-lung ventilation strategy, PACU stay, fluid administration, blood loss, urine output, surgical characteristics, and PCIA regimen and settings, were also similar between groups (Supplementary Tables 1–3).

Primary outcome

At 48 h after surgery, patients receiving taVNS reported a median NRS score of 3 (2–3), compared with 3 (3–4) in the sham group (p = 0.001, Figure 2).

Second outcomes

For cough pain at 24 and 72 h, the active group also had lower pain scores: at 24 h, 3 (3–4) vs. 4 (3–6) (p = 0.004); at 72 h, 2 (1–3) vs. 3 (2–4) (p = 0.002; Figure 2). GEE analysis showed that active taVNS was associated with lower postoperative cough pain (β = −0.734, 95% CI −1.269 to −0.218; p = 0.006; Supplementary Table 4). After adjustment for surgical procedure, lymph node dissection status, and chest tube number, the association remained statistically significant (adjusted β = −0.754, p = 0.005; Supplementary Table 4). Resting pain was also lower in the active group, and GEE analysis showed an association between active taVNS and lower resting pain scores (β = −0.615, 95% CI −1.033 to −0.196; p = 0.004; adjusted β = −0.610, p = 0.003; Figure 2 and Supplementary Table 4).

In addition, the incidence of moderate-to-severe pain during coughing was lower in the active group at 24 h (45% vs. 73%; p = 0.002) and 48 h (22% vs. 46%; p = 0.005) postoperatively, whereas no significant differences were observed for resting pain (Table 2).

No between-group differences were observed in PCIA press counts and perioperative cumulative opioid consumption. However, postoperative cumulative opioid consumption was lower in the active group at 24 h [18 (13–27) vs. 28 (20–32) mg; p = 0.030] and 72 h [61 (43–98) vs. 85 (61–117) mg; p = 0.027; Figure 3], and fewer patients required rescue analgesia on postoperative day 3 (23% vs. 34%; p = 0.016; Table 2). Time to first PCIA use was longer in the active group (2.1 vs. 5.2 h; p = 0.018; Supplementary Figure 1).

QoR-15 scores were comparable between groups at all time points (Supplementary Table 5). The incidence of PONV, chest drain removal time, and length of hospital stay were similar between groups (Supplementary Table 5). The incidence of postoperative pneumothorax was lower in the active group (23% vs. 42%; p = 0.027; Table 3), whereas no significant between-group differences were observed for the other PPC components. Multivariable logistic regression analysis showed an association between taVNS and lower odds of PPCs (aOR = 0.331, 95% CI 0.154–0.713; p = 0.005; Supplementary Table 6, Supplementary Table 7).

Adverse events were mild and self-limiting, including throat discomfort (n = 1) and local skin irritation (n = 2) in the active group, and skin irritation (n = 3) in the sham group, with no serious adverse events reported (Supplementary Table 8).

Discussion

This randomized controlled trial showed that taVNS was associated with lower postoperative pain scores during the first 3 postoperative days after thoracoscopic partial lung resection. Although the absolute reductions in NRS scores were modest, taVNS also reduced the incidence of moderate-to-severe cough pain and postoperative opioid requirements, suggesting a potentially meaningful clinical benefit. Furthermore, taVNS was associated with a lower incidence of postoperative pneumothorax, while no significant differences were observed for most other pulmonary complications.

Post-thoracic surgery pain is multifactorial, involving both incisional and visceral components. Although video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery is less invasive than thoracotomy, nociceptive stimuli such as intercostal nerve irritation, pleural injury, and chest drain-related pain remain common. Our findings are consistent with previous studies by Patel et al. and Xiong et al. which also reported the analgesic efficacy of taVNS [10,11]. Notably, pain scores remained lower in the active group despite reduced opioid use. These findings suggest that taVNS may provide adjunctive analgesic effects in the context of multimodal postoperative pain management.

The analgesic effect of taVNS may be mediated through multiple peripheral and central mechanisms. Peripherally, stimulation of the auricular branch of the vagus nerve activates the vagal anti-inflammatory reflex via the cholinergic pathway, suppressing proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α and thereby attenuating postoperative inflammation-related pain [7]. Centrally, vagal afferent input to the nucleus tractus solitarius (NTS) engages brainstem monoaminergic pathways, including the locus coeruleus–noradrenergic and raphe–serotonergic systems, enhancing descending inhibition of nociceptive transmission [15–17]. Experimental evidence also suggests that taVNS may interact with endogenous opioid signaling and reduce neuroinflammation, thereby limiting central sensitization [18,19]. Beyond direct nociceptive modulation, taVNS may enhance parasympathetic activity, attenuate sympathetic overactivation, and regulate stress-related responses, which could further contribute to postoperative analgesia [19–22]. Functional imaging studies have additionally demonstrated taVNS-associated modulation of key pain-processing regions, including the insula, anterior cingulate cortex, and thalamus [23,24].

Compared with many other surgical procedures, thoracoscopic surgery is associated with a relatively high incidence of PPCs, which can adversely affect recovery [25]. In the present study, taVNS was associated with a lower incidence of postoperative pneumothorax. One possible explanation is that improved analgesia may facilitate deep breathing and effective coughing, potentially supporting postoperative respiratory recovery. Experimental studies have also suggested that vagal activation may modulate pulmonary inflammatory responses and enhance airway clearance [26–28]. However, these potential mechanisms remain speculative in the context of the present study, as no direct measures of pulmonary function or inflammation were obtained. Moreover, although multivariable analysis suggested an association between taVNS and lower odds of PPCs, postoperative pneumothorax was the only individual PPC component that differed significantly between groups. Therefore, whether taVNS confers broader benefits with respect to PPCs remains uncertain and warrants further investigation in larger studies.

The present study demonstrated that taVNS reduced cumulative opioid consumption at 24 and 72 h postoperatively and decreased the need for rescue analgesia on postoperative day 3. Kaplan–Meier analysis further showed that taVNS delayed the time to first analgesic request. Although intraoperative opioid consumption was comparable between groups, postoperative opioid requirements were lower in the active taVNS group, suggesting that the observed opioid-sparing effect was unlikely to be attributable to differences in intraoperative analgesic administration. In the first 48 h postoperatively, the proportion of patients requiring rescue analgesia was similar between groups, while the total rescue opioid dose showed a trend toward reduction in the active group. This may be partly explained by the observation that a small number of patients in the sham group received relatively higher doses of rescue opioids. These findings are consistent with previous reports demonstrating opioid-sparing effects of auricular nerve stimulation [29]. Reduced opioid use may provide multiple clinical benefits, including a lower risk of opioid-related adverse events and attenuation of opioid-induced immunosuppression, potentially improving long-term outcomes in cancer patients [30,31].

However, taVNS did not significantly reduce the incidence of PONV in this study. This may be related to the modest opioid-sparing effect, limited sample size, and relatively few stimulation sessions [32]. Previous study employed repeated stimulation protocol, and the limited stimulation frequency in this study may have been insufficient to influence central emetic pathways [33].

This study has several limitations. First, as a single-center randomized controlled trial in patients undergoing thoracoscopic partial lung resection, the relatively small sample size may limit the generalizability of the findings and warrants validation in larger multicenter studies. Second, mechanistic biomarkers, such as serum inflammatory cytokines, endocrine indicators (e.g. cortisol, catecholamines, adrenocorticotropic hormone), autonomic function indices (e.g. heart rate variability) and pulmonary function, were not assessed, which limits mechanistic interpretation of the observed effects. Third, our 72-hour observation window precluded systematic assessment of constipation, and other recovery-related variables (e.g. dizziness, ambulation time, detailed gastrointestinal symptoms) were not prospectively collected. While we assessed postoperative nausea and vomiting and used the QoR-15 scale to capture global recovery, the absence of these additional metrics limits the comprehensiveness of our perioperative outcome evaluation. Fourth, the stimulation parameters were based on previous literature and were not individually optimized; therefore, whether alternative or patient-tailored stimulation protocols could yield improved outcomes remains unknown. Fifth, regarding postoperative pulmonary complications, postoperative pneumothorax was the only individual PPC that differed significantly between groups. Although a difference was observed in the composite PPC endpoint, this was not consistent across individual components. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and require confirmation in larger studies. Finally, because the study mainly enrolled relatively young patients who recovered quickly and were typically discharged within 4–6 days after surgery, follow-up was limited to 72 h postoperatively. Consequently, the long-term effects of taVNS on chronic postsurgical pain and recovery could not be assessed. Future studies with extended follow-up are needed to clarify the longer-term effects of taVNS.

Conclusion

In summary, perioperative taVNS was associated with a modest analgesic benefit and reduced postoperative opioid requirements after thoracoscopic partial lung resection. It was also associated with a lower incidence of postoperative pneumothorax; however, whether taVNS provides broader benefits for other postoperative pulmonary complications remains uncertain. Overall, taVNS may represent a promising adjunct to multimodal analgesia, but its clinical utility requires validation in larger, multicenter trials.
