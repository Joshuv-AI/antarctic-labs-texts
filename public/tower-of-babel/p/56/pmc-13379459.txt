Profiling circulating Tau and phospho-Tau isoforms in pediatric and adult spinal muscular atrophy identifies selective elevation of pTau-262 in adults
Authors: Leillani L Ha, Sunayana Mitra, Doreen T Ho, Becky Fillingham, James D Berry, Kathryn J Swoboda, Christiano R R Alves
Published: 2026

Dear Sirs,

The development and application of reliable biomarkers have become increasingly critical in neuromuscular disorders as therapeutic interventions expand. Tau, a microtubule-associated protein essential for maintaining axonal structure and transport, has emerged as an informative marker of neuroaxonal injury [1]. Under normal physiological conditions, tau phosphorylation supports microtubule binding and cytoskeletal plasticity. However, dysregulated kinase activity in different diseases can lead to aberrant Tau phosphorylation, including the residues T181 (pTau-181), T217 (pTau-217), T262 (pTau-262), and S396 (pTau-396) [2, 3]. These modifications can impair axonal transport, and promote the formation of neurofibrillary aggregates that contribute to neuronal dysfunction [2, 3]. Elevated pTau species have been well documented in neurodegenerative diseases including amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), a severe neuromuscular condition [4–10]. However, a distinct non-aggregating tau-mediated toxicity at the neuromuscular junction pathology is expected based on pre-clinical studies for other neuromuscular conditions, such as spinal muscular atrophy (SMA), a leading infantile genetic cause of death worldwide [11]. Our understanding of circulating Tau and pTau levels as potential accessible peripheral biomarkers of ongoing neuronal death in SMA is still very limited. Tau represents an attractive candidate biomarker in SMA, although clinical evidence remains limited. Thus, we aimed to determine alterations in circulating Tau profiles in cohorts of both pediatric and adult patients with SMA.

Written informed consent or parental consent were obtained from all participants under Institutional Ethics Review Boards at the University of Utah (protocol 8751) and Massachusetts General Hospital (protocols 2025P001475, 2016P000469, 2021P000822, and 2006P000982). We queried our local databases [12, 13] for all serum and plasma samples available from SMA subjects above 14 years old (Cohort 1: adults) or under 2 years of age (Cohort 2: pediatric) and not receiving any SMN-targeted molecular or gene therapy at the time of sample collection. Moreover, we used a subset from a previously published study from our group including all samples available for pediatric SMA patients treated with gene therapy at our own site (Cohort 3) [13]. We included healthy control subjects for the natural history cohorts (Cohorts 1 and 2) as well as samples collected on site from ALS patients for the adult cohort (Cohort 1). Table 1 and Supplementary Table 1 present the distribution of age, sex, SMA type and SMN2 copies for these three independent cohorts in addition to ambulatory status, disease duration, ventilation status, motor function, symptomatic status and age at treatment/sample collection for specific cohorts.

All blood samples were collected at our hospital clinic and directly transferred to the laboratory on wet ice for immediate processing. Samples were centrifuged for 10 min to isolate plasma or serum, aliquoted, and fast-frozen. The entire process from collection to final aliquot freezing was completed within 20 min, taking less than 30 min total per sample. Aliquots were stored in a -80C freezer with no prior freeze–thaw cycles until they were pooled for tau measurements. Regarding sample storage duration, the adult SMA and ALS cohorts consist of contemporary samples, and no pediatric natural history samples pre-date 2015.

Circulating total Tau, pTau-181, pTau-217, pTau-262, and pTau-396 concentrations were measured using commercially available and validated immunoassays. Different kits required different dilution factors, and all samples were analyzed in triplicate. Any samples with biomarker concentrations falling outside the upper or lower limits of quantification for a given assay were excluded from subsequent statistical analyses. Total Tau levels were measured using Human Tau ELISA Kit (Catalog # NBP2-62749; Novus Biologicals LLC a Bio-Techne Brand, CO, USA) at a 1:4 dilution factor, featuring a lower limit of detection (LLOD) of 4.69 pg/mL, an intra-assay variability of < 5.03%, and an inter-assay variability of < 4.82%. For pTau-181, pTau-217 and pTau-262, a LEGENDplex™ Pre-defined Human Phospho-Tau Panel (Catalog # 741507; BioLegend; CA, USA) was performed at a 1:2 dilution factor, with an average LLOD of 1.89 pg/mL, an intra-assay CV of < 10%, and an inter-assay CV of < 18%. For pTau-396, a Human Tau (Phospho) [pS396] ELISA Kit (Catalog # EEL188; Invitrogen / Thermo Fisher Scientific; MA, USA) was performed at a 1:4 dilution factor, providing an LLOD of 18.75 pg/mL, an intra-assay CV of < 10%, and an inter-assay CV of < 10%. For Cohorts 1 and 2, all analyses were performed using serum samples. For Cohort 3, all analyses were performed using plasma samples due to the limited number of available serum samples. Neurofilament levels analyzed in Cohort 3 are the same data previously reported in our previous studies using a pNF‐H enzyme‐linked lectin assay (ProteinSimple, CA, USA) and a single-molecule array (Simoa) NF-light Advantage Kit (Quanterix, Billerica, MA, US) [13].

Biomarker concentrations are presented as median (interquartile range) with individual observations. Group differences were evaluated using Gamma generalized linear models with a log link, an approach appropriate for positive, right-skewed biomarker concentrations. Healthy controls were used as the reference group, and pre-specified comparisons between adult SMA versus healthy controls and ALS versus healthy controls were performed with false discovery rate correction. To account for potential demographic confounding, additional multivariable Gamma generalized linear models, including age and sex, as covariates were performed for both the adult and pediatric cohorts. For transparency, means ± SD, 95% confidence intervals, fold-change estimates, and exact P values are additionally reported in Supplementary Table 2.

To assess whether circulating Tau levels are altered in adults with SMA, we analyzed serum samples from 17 healthy controls and 19 untreated adult SMA patients (natural history cohort). We also included 20 adults with ALS as a disease control group. Total Tau, pTau-181, pTau-217, pTau-262, and pTau-396 concentrations were quantified in all samples (Fig. 1A). Total Tau levels were comparable between healthy controls and SMA patients, whereas ALS patients showed significantly elevated total Tau compared with both groups (Fig. 1B). No significant differences were observed among the three groups for pTau-181 (Fig. 1C). Interestingly, SMA patients, but not ALS patients, displayed higher pTau-262 levels relative to healthy controls (Fig. 1E). In contrast, ALS patients exhibited increased pTau-217 and pTau-396 levels, a change not observed in SMA (Fig. 1D and F). Neither age nor sex was a significant predictor of any biomarker concentration, and adjustment for these covariates did not materially change the estimated effect of SMA or ALS on circulating tau levels. Together, these results highlight distinct alterations in circulating Tau species across SMA and ALS, with pTau-262 emerging as the principal Tau alteration detected in the adult SMA cohort.

We next examined circulating Tau measurements in a natural history cohort of pediatric SMA patients with 2 or ≥ 3 SMN2 copies and compared them with age-matched healthy controls (Fig. 2A). Overall, no significant differences were observed for any Tau species assessed (Fig. 2A–F). Notably, a small subset of the most severely affected infants with 2 SMN2 copies exhibited elevated pTau-181 (Fig. 2C), pTau-262 (Fig. 2E), and pTau-396 (Fig. 2F) levels that were not seen in healthy controls, although these findings were not consistent across all SMA infants in this group. These isolated observations warrant further investigation in larger prospective cohorts, and these data indicate that Tau alterations are not reliably detected in early life.

Given our previous observations that onasemnogene abeparvovec monotherapy is associated with increased neurofilament levels in symptomatic SMA infants early in life, we next sought to determine whether similar effects would be observed in circulating Tau measurements. We analyzed a subset of eight patients with available samples before treatment and between 17 and 152 days following onasemnogene abeparvovec administration (Fig. 2G). Due to variations in clinical follow-up schedules, these secondary time points were not collected at a uniform nominal interval for all subjects. Among these, four patients received onasemnogene abeparvovec alone, while the remaining four were co-treated with nusinersen. Consistent with the neurofilament changes observed previously (Figs. 2H–J), we detected increased Tau levels after onasemnogene abeparvovec infusion, with more pronounced elevations in patients receiving monotherapy compared with those on combination therapy (Fig. 2K–O). These preliminary observations raise the possibility that circulating Tau dynamics may change during the early post-treatment period. However, given the small cohort size and clinical heterogeneity, these findings should be considered hypothesis-generating and may alternatively reflect differences in treatment timing, baseline disease severity, combination therapy, or biological variability. Additional mechanistic studies will be required to clarify the biological basis of these observations.

We report real world data from a series of Tau measurements in different cohorts of SMA patients. While newborn screening programs and multiple FDA-approved therapies have transformed SMA, there remains an urgent need for sensitive biomarkers to track early disease progression and treatment response. While candidates like circulating SMN protein, electrophysiologic measures, and neurofilaments offer complementary readouts, each has unique limitations [12–14]. In this context, Tau represents a compelling candidate for further consideration.

Our observations highlight several important considerations. First, pTau-262 emerged as a candidate biomarker preferentially associated with adult SMA. Interestingly, while pTau-217 levels did not differ significantly in our cohorts, future SMA biomarker panels might achieve higher sensitivity by utilizing brain-specific isoforms to minimize the impact of non-CNS-derived tau peripheral interference [15]. Second, the absence of consistent Tau alterations in untreated SMA infants likely reflects their distinct developmental physiology. Third, the transient Tau increases following gene therapy parallel neurofilament kinetics. While these observations suggest dynamic changes in circulating Tau following gene therapy, they should be interpreted cautiously given the small sample size and clinical heterogeneity of this cohort. Several confounding variables could alternatively explain these trends, including differences in the timing of treatment initiation, variations in baseline clinical severity, the compounding effects of dual therapeutic approaches, or subtle pre-analytical fluctuations during sample handling.

Our current real-world findings should be interpreted in the context of several important limitations, including the modest sample size, the age imbalance across cohorts, and the lack of an independent validation cohort. Furthermore, the clinically heterogeneous gene-therapy cohort (n = 8) should be considered exploratory. Methodologically, the use of different sample matrices, with serum analyzed in Cohorts 1 and 2 and plasma in Cohort 3 due to the limited availability of treated pediatric samples, precludes direct cross-cohort numerical comparisons. Nevertheless, this study provides a clinically relevant and comprehensive characterization of circulating Tau and phospho-Tau isoforms across pediatric and adult SMA cohorts, identifying pTau-262 as a promising candidate biomarker for adult SMA. Larger, multi-center longitudinal studies incorporating harmonized sample processing and independent validation cohorts will be important to further define the utility of Tau within a multidimensional SMA biomarker framework.
