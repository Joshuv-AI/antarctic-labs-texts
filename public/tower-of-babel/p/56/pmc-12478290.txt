Echocardiographic Markers of Prognosis in Patients Undergoing Ventricular Arrhythmia Ablation
Authors: Lisa Steen Duus, Maria Dons, Morten Lock Hansen, Rene Worck, Martin H. Ruwald, Arne Johannessen, Jim Hansen, Tor Biering‐Sørensen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundRadiofrequency catheter ablation (RFA) plays an increasing role in the treatment of ventricular arrhythmias (VAs). This study aimed to evaluate the echocardiographic and clinical characteristics of patients undergoing first‐time RFA for VAs and to identify risk factors associated with VA recurrence, implantable cardioverter‐defibrillator (ICD) therapy, and mortality.MethodsWe studied patients scheduled for first‐time RFA for VAs from 2011 to 2022 with available transthoracic echocardiography before the procedure. The primary outcome was defined as VA recurrence, and the secondary outcome was defined as appropriate ICD therapy and mortality. Uni‐ and multivariable Cox hazards regression models were used to assess the prognostic value of cardiac structure and function by echocardiography.ResultsA total of 218 patients (mean age 61 years [±15], 62% men, mean LVEF 51% [IQR: 44; 57]) underwent RFA during the study period. Of these, 90 patients (41%) had VA recurrence while 52 patients (24%) developed the secondary outcome during a median follow‐up time of 3.1 years (IQR: 1.1; 6.7 years). Patients with ventricular tachycardia had significantly more right—and left ventricular (LV) dysfunction, compared to patients with premature ventricular contractions. No echocardiographic measures were associated with VA recurrence, while several echocardiographic measures were associated with the secondary outcome. LV mass index, LV ejection fraction, global longitudinal strain, and VA recurrence showed the highest predictive values of the secondary outcome.ConclusionIn patients undergoing RFA for VAs, several echocardiographic measures may identify patients at risk of an adverse outcome.

Introduction

Ventricular arrhythmias (VAs) such as ventricular tachycardia (VT) and premature ventricular contractions (PVCs) are abnormal heart arrhythmias, where an area or substrate in the ventricles either generates a single electrical impulse (PVCs) or several pulses in a row (VT) [1], leading to premature ventricular contraction(s). VAs are associated with increased morbidity and mortality, as these patients are at significantly higher risk of arrhythmia‐induced cardiomyopathy, stroke, heart failure, and sudden cardiac death (SCD) [1, 2, 3, 4]. Treatment options for PVCs and VT include antiarrhythmic medication, implantable cardioverter‐defibrillator (ICD), and radiofrequency catheter ablation (RFA) [5]. RFA plays an increasing role in the treatment of VAs to reduce episodes and clinical symptoms of VT and PVCs.

Echocardiography is the first‐line imaging technique, and according to guidelines recommended with an international class (IC) I recommendation to assess cardiac structure and function in patients with known or suspected VAs (level of evidence C) [6]. Echocardiography is considered an important diagnostic and prognostic tool for the management of patients with arrhythmias. In addition, studies have demonstrated that both conventional and novel echocardiographic parameters can offer prognostic information for predicting SCD in patients with VAs [7, 8].

Only a subset of patients with VAs undergo RFA, so data on this patient population are limited. Gaining a better understanding of the clinical and echocardiographic characteristics in patients with VAs might contribute to the development of more precise risk models and guide therapy approaches. The present study aimed to evaluate the echocardiographic and clinical characteristics of patients undergoing RFA for VAs and investigate the prognostic value of measures of cardiac structure and function obtained by conventional and advanced echocardiography.

Methods

Study Design and Population

This study included 232 patients with VT or PVCs undergoing first‐time RFA performed at the Department of Cardiology, Herlev & Gentofte University Hospital, Denmark. Procedures were performed from 2011 to 2022. Inclusion criteria were age ≥ 18 years and diagnosis of VT or PVCs undergoing first‐time RFA. Patients with no available baseline transthoracic echocardiography (n = 8) or inadequate echocardiographic image quality (n = 6) were excluded. Hence, the final study population included 218 patients.

All patients were required to have a transthoracic echocardiogram performed as part of routine clinical evaluation prior to the index ablation. Baseline demographics, medical history, clinical, and laboratory data were obtained from electronic medical records and a well‐established national database, the Danish Ablation Registry.

Hypertension was defined as having an ICD‐10 code for hypertension (DI109, DI159, DI151, DI152, DI158) or the use of antihypertensive medication, prescribed for the management of hypertension. Diabetes mellitus was defined as having an ICD‐10 code (DE10, DE109A, DE11, DE119A, DE13) or the use of anti‐diabetic medication. Ischemic heart disease (IHD) was defined as ischemic cardiomyopathy, stable angina pectoris, previous myocardial infarction, and/or revascularization procedures. Structural heart disease was defined as ischemic heart disease, hypertrophic—and, dilated cardiomyopathy, and arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy (ARVC).

Catheter Ablation Procedures

All procedures were performed at a single center. Primary RFA procedures were performed with a circular mapping catheter, an electro‐anatomical CARTO mapping system, and an irrigated‐tip contact force‐sensing RFA catheter (64%), an irrigated‐tip RFA catheter (33%), or a non‐irrigated‐tip RFA catheter (3%). Procedures were performed with patients under monitored anesthesia care with sedation or general anesthesia with mechanical ventilation. Prolonged hospitalization after RFA was defined as hospitalization of more than 1 day.

Follow‐Up and Outcome

The primary outcome was specialist‐verified recurrence of VAs, defined as repeated RFA, VA recurrence diagnosed on implantable devices or electrocardiograms, or VA admission. The secondary composite outcome was defined as all‐course mortality or life‐threatening VAs, defined as a VA leading to appropriate ICD‐therapy. Data acquisitions were performed at follow‐up, a median of 6.5 years (IQR: 3.4; 9.3 years) after the RFA procedure. Data on all outcomes were collected through review of electronic patient records. No patients were lost to follow‐up.

Conventional and Novel Echocardiography

All transthoracic echocardiograms were performed according to standard clinical protocols and digitally stored in a remote archive. All examinations were analyzed by a single investigator, blinded to clinical data and outcomes. Analyses adhered to guideline‐recommended acquisition and quantification standards available at the time of image acquisition [9] and were performed during sinus rhythm. Echocardiograms were acquired on commercially available ultrasound systems (GE Healthcare, Vivid series). Analyses were performed with available post‐processing software (EchoPAC BT1.12 software, GE Healthcare, Horten, Norway).

Cardiac chamber quantification recommendations available at the time of analysis were followed for all conventional echocardiographic measurements. Left ventricular (LV) dimensions at end‐diastole were measured in the parasternal long‐axis view. The LV dimensions included the left ventricular internal diastolic diameter (LVIDd), the interventricular septal wall thickness (IVSd), and the diastolic LV posterior wall thickness (LVPWd). The dimensions were used to calculate the LV mass and indexed to body surface area (BSA) to acquire the LV mass index (LVMi) [9]. Left atrial (LA) end‐systolic volume was obtained using the biplane area‐length method and indexed to the body surface area (BSA), resulting in the left atrial volume index (LAVi). LV ejection fraction (LVEF) was measured using the Simpson biplane method from the apical 4‐ and 2‐chamber views and expressed as a percentage. Pulsed‐wave Doppler was performed in the apical 4‐chamber view, at the level of the mitral valve leaflet tips, to obtain transmitral inflow velocities; E‐wave, A‐wave, E/A ratio, and E‐wave deceleration time (DT). Pulsed‐wave tissue Doppler imaging with sampling at the lateral and septal mitral annulus was utilized to measure the early relaxation velocity (e'). From this, the E/e' ratio was calculated.

For the evaluation of myocardial strain, speckle‐tracking echocardiography was performed in the apical 4‐chamber, 2‐chamber, and apical long‐axis views. Two‐dimensional speckle‐tracking was applied on the three apical views during sinus rhythm with a frame rate higher than 40 frames per second, requiring at least 4 out of 6 ventricular segments traceable in each view. LV endocardial border was traced by a semi‐automated function with the placement of two points at the basal level and one point at the apex. The region of interest was manually adjusted, ensuring the enclosure of the entire LV wall. Segments with persistent inadequate tracking were excluded. Global longitudinal strain (GLS) was calculated as the average of the remaining segments.

Statistics

All statistical analyses were performed using STATA version 15.1 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). A p‐value of ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant in all tests. Baseline characteristics were stratified by VA‐type. All variables were checked for normality of distribution using histograms. Student´s t‐test was used for continuous variables with a Gaussian distribution. Wilcoxon rank‐sum test was used for non‐Gaussian distributed variables. Gaussian distributed continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while non‐Gaussian distributed continuous variables are expressed as median with interquartile ranges. Binary and categorical variables are expressed as total numbers and percentages and compared using Chi2 and, alternatively, Fisher´s exact test when expected numbers were below 5.

Univariable Cox proportional hazards regression models for conventional and novel echocardiographic measures were performed to investigate associations with the composite outcomes. Harrell's C‐statistics were calculated to test the prognostic performance. To adjust for potential confounding variables and obtain fitted hazard ratios (HR), multivariable Cox regressions were performed for selected echocardiographic measurements. The multivariable adjustments included age, sex, IHD, hypertension, heart rate during echocardiogram, and underlying VA type.

Cumulative hazard curves were created for selected echocardiographic measurements, divided into high and low‐risk groups based on the median value of the measurement. The difference in outcome between groups was tested using the log‐rank test.

Results

Baseline characteristics stratified by underlying VA are displayed in Table 1. Among the 218 participants, 62 % were men, the mean age was 64 years, 41% had hypertension, mean BMI was 27 kg/m2, 13% were current smokers, and 40% were former smokers. Compared to patients with PVCs, patients with VT were more likely to be male, had higher creatinine levels at admission, and had a more frequent history of prior myocardial infarction (MI), IHD, heart failure, a higher number of out‐of‐hospital cardiac arrests, ICD at admission, and prior direct current cardioversion. Prior to ablation, three patients had a dual‐chamber pacemaker (DDD pacemaker), and thirteen patients had a cardiac resynchronization therapy device with defibrillator (CRT‐D). Regarding ablation procedure, patients with VT had significantly longer ablation times and more often a prolonged hospitalization. Patients with VT had more frequent verified VA recurrence compared to patients with PVCs. Baseline characteristics stratified by structural heart disease are displayed in Table S1. Patients with structural heart disease, as compared to those with idiopathic arrhythmias, were older, more often male, had significantly more comorbidity, and more often VT as underlying arrhythmia.

VA Recurrence and Prognostic Value of Echocardiography

Transthoracic echocardiographic examinations were performed at a median of 30 days (IQR: 5–84 days) prior to index RFA. Compared to patients with PVCs, patients with VT had significantly increased LV dimensions, impaired LVEF, and impaired GLS. Additionally, they presented more pronounced LV diastolic dysfunction and RV dysfunction.

During a median follow‐up of 6.5 years (IQR: 3.4; 9.3 years), 90 patients (41%) had specialist‐verified VA recurrence. The median time to recurrence was 140 days (IQR = [48–434] days). The clinical and echocardiographic characteristics of patients with and without VA recurrence are shown in Table 2. Patients with VA recurrence had lower BMI, were more likely to have an ICD at admission, had prior direct current cardioversion, and experienced a greater incidence of prior out‐of‐hospital cardiac arrest. Additionally, they had a higher prevalence of dilated cardiomyopathy and ARVC compared to patients without VA recurrence. Patients with VA recurrence had VT as underlying VA more often than patients without recurrence, and more often a prolonged admission. No echocardiographic measurements differed between patients with VA recurrence and patients without VA recurrence.

In a subgroup analysis, stratifying patients according to underlying arrhythmia (Table S2), we found that LVMi remained independently associated with clinically relevant VA recurrence in patients with PVCs, after adjustment for clinical and echocardiographic variables. In patients with VT, we found that LVEF was independently associated with specialist‐verified VA recurrence, after adjustment for clinical and echocardiographic confounding variables.

Life‐Threatening VA, All‐Cause Mortality, and Prognostic Value of Echocardiography

Out of the 218 patients, 52 patients (24%) developed the secondary composite outcome, whereof 21 patients received appropriate ICD therapy, 15 patients experienced appropriate ICD therapy and subsequently died, and 16 patients died. Baseline clinical and echocardiographic characteristics stratified according to the secondary composite outcome are shown in Table S3. Among the 52 patients who met the composite outcome, 46 (89%) suffered from VT as an underlying arrhythmia, while only 6 (12%) suffered from PVCs. Those who developed the composite outcome were older, predominantly male, and with a higher prevalence of hypertension, prior MI, and ICD at admission. In addition, more patients suffered from ischemic or dilated cardiomyopathy compared to those who did not meet the secondary composite outcome. Regarding ablation procedure, patients who developed the composite outcome had longer ablation time, more had intraprocedural intracardiac echocardiography (ICE) to evaluate catheter position, and had prolonged admission. Additionally, patients who developed the secondary composite outcome had significantly impaired LV systolic function, with a lower LVEF and GLS. They had higher left ventricular filling pressure, increased LA volume, and increased LV dimensions. Moreover, they had a longer E‐wave deceleration time and impaired RV systolic function as assessed by TAPSE.

Table 4 displays the risk for developing life‐threatening VA or all‐cause mortality. LVEF, GLS, LVEDV, LVMi, LAVi, and VA recurrence remained independently associated with the outcome after adjusting for clinical and echocardiographic variables (Table 4). We found an increased risk of the composite outcome of 9% per 1% decrease in LVEF and an increased risk of 12% per 1% decrease in GLS. Patients who had prior VA recurrence had a more than 4‐fold increased risk of the composite outcome (HR = 3.74 [1.87; 7.47], p < 0.001) compared to patients without VA recurrence. Increasing LVMi showed the highest predictive value (C‐statistic of 0.78), followed by VA recurrence (C‐statistic of 0.74). LVEF and GLS both independently demonstrated a C‐statistic of 0.71.

Discussion

This study investigated the clinical and echocardiographic characteristics of patients undergoing RFA for VAs, as well as potential predictors of VA recurrence, life‐threatening arrhythmia, and mortality. The findings of this study revealed several differences in clinical and echocardiographic characteristics between patients diagnosed with VT and those with PVCs referred for RFA. Remarkably, no significant differences in measures of cardiac structure and function were found between patients experiencing VA recurrence and those without.

Predictors of VA Recurrence

Several echocardiographic measures of cardiac structure and function have been shown to be valuable in predicting and identifying patients at high risk of SCD; however, data are limited when it comes to predicting VA recurrence. ESC guidelines recommend the use of echocardiography to assess LV function and detect structural cardiac abnormalities in all patients with suspected or known VAs [6]. Although measurements such as LVEF are considered keystone parameters, the ability to predict VAs is limited. This is likely due to the complexity of LV function and the arrhythmogenic substrate. In our study, we observed that no echocardiographic parameters could predict VA recurrence in the overall study population. However, when assessing the predictive value within subgroups stratified by underlying VA type, we identified that increasing LVMi remained associated with VA recurrence in patients with PVCs after multivariable adjustment. Similarly, LVEF demonstrated a significant association with VA recurrence in patients with VT in a multivariable model. Notably, the associations of LVMi and LVEF with VA recurrence were evident only after adjusting for confounding variables. This might be attributed to the elimination of significant confounding. Only a few studies have reported echocardiography to be useful in predicting VA recurrence. For instance, Vergara et al. demonstrated that patients with LVEF ≥50% had a lower recurrence rate [10], while Tóth et al. found LVEF to be associated with VT recurrence in male patients with structural heart disease [11]. Another study by Tilz et al. found that procedural failure was the only parameter associated with VT recurrence [12]. Consistent with other studies, we found no echocardiographic parameters to be associated with VA recurrence [12].

Sex has been linked to VA recurrence in various studies, but there is disagreement regarding this association. Tóth et al. reported that male sex was associated with an increased risk of VA recurrence, whereas Frankel et al. found an increased risk of recurrence in females after RFA [13]. Other studies did not identify sex as an independent predictor of recurrence [14]. In our study, we observed a higher incidence of VA recurrence in males. However, after adjusting for age, IHD, hypertension, and heart rate, we found no significant association between sex and VA recurrence. Therefore, the observed higher incidence of VA recurrence in males may be attributed to a higher prevalence of IHD and more pronounced disease progression. Identifying risk factors associated with VA recurrence might be useful in individualizing post‐ablation management. However, our findings, along with previous studies, suggest that echocardiography is unlikely to enhance preoperative risk stratification for VA recurrence. Nevertheless, further investigations involving larger populations are necessary.

Predictors of Life‐Threatening VAs and Mortality

We identified several measures of cardiac structure and function to be associated with life‐threatening VAs and mortality, even after adjustment for age, sex, IHD, hypertension, and underlying VA type. Our study revealed that the distribution of patients referred for RFA of VAs consisted of an equal ratio of VT to PVC patients. Not surprisingly, the clinical profile of VT patients predominantly included males, a higher prevalence of ICDs at admission, IHD, and structural heart disease, which often contributes to a more complex arrhythmogenic substrate. This complexity was further highlighted by procedural characteristics, as VT patients were more likely to undergo ICE during the procedure and experienced longer ablation times. Additionally, two VT patients required hemodynamic support during the procedure. Previous studies have demonstrated that periprocedural hemodynamic instability can occur in up to 11% of scar‐related VT cases and is associated with increased mortality [15, 16]. Moreover, VT patients had a higher likelihood of significant VA recurrence following the procedure. The above‐mentioned factors are widely recognized as prognostic indicators of adverse outcomes and mortality. Enhancing our understanding of the clinical and echocardiographic characteristics associated with these arrhythmias could potentially ease treatment decisions and optimize patient outcomes.

In previous studies, systolic dysfunction, including impaired LVEF, has been recognized as a predictor of adverse long‐term outcomes and increased mortality risk in patients with VAs [16, 17, 18]. In our study, we identified several measures of cardiac structure and function associated with appropriate ICD therapy and mortality in patients with VT or PVCs. Specifically, LVEF, GLS, and VA recurrence exhibited the highest predictive value.

Our findings on echocardiographic structural parameters complement prior research exploring anatomical and electrophysiological predictors of ablation success. A study by Yokokawa et al. [19] showed that in ventricular arrhythmias originating from papillary muscles, smaller papillary muscle mass and larger LV dimensions on cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) were associated with a higher risk of procedural failure. In our study, LVMi was associated with VA recurrence in univariable models; however, this association did not remain significant after multivariable adjustment. Similarly, as studied by Nabar et al. [20] demonstrated that in patients with post‐infarction ventricular tachycardia, larger LV end‐diastolic volumes and end‐systolic diameters, but not ejection fraction, were predictive of poorer survival, underscoring the importance of chamber remodeling as a prognostic factor. This aligns with our findings, where LV mass, volumes, and systolic function parameters were associated with life‐threatening VAs and mortality. Collectively, this emphasizes that precise structural assessments obtained through imaging can inform risk stratification and procedural planning, reinforcing the value of echocardiographic evaluation in patients undergoing catheter ablation for VA.

Our study encompassed both VT and PVC patients, distinguishing it from previous investigations that primarily focused on VT patients alone. In a clinical setting, it is often unclear whether patients solely have PVCs or also possess a substrate for non‐sustained or sustained VT prior to RFA, especially in patients with heart failure or structural heart disease. Moreover, it is recognized that lethal arrhythmias like ventricular fibrillation (VF) and polymorphic VT can arise from short‐coupled PVCs. However, patients with PVCs are typically not considered to be at the same risk of SCD and life‐threatening arrhythmias as patients with sustained VT or electrical storm. Nevertheless, our study demonstrated that several echocardiographic measures of cardiac structure and function were independently associated with the composite outcome regardless of the underlying VA type.

Both non‐invasive and invasive methods exist for risk stratification in patients with VT [10]. The most widely accepted predictor is impaired LV systolic function, as assessed by LVEF. Consistent with other studies, we found that decreased LVEF was associated with higher mortality [10, 16]. Vergara et al. [10] proposed an LVEF cutoff of <30 % to identify patients at increased risk of early recurrence and mortality. In line with our findings, previous studies have also demonstrated that VT recurrence strongly predicts adverse outcomes and mortality [12, 14]. We found that patients experiencing recurrence had a seven‐fold increased risk of appropriate ICD therapy and mortality. Combining clinical and echocardiographic measures of cardiac structure and function may help identify patients who would not typically be considered at high risk of death and life‐threatening arrhythmias. Echocardiographic assessment has the potential to enhance risk stratification for adverse outcomes post‐procedure. Our findings indicate that patients who experience post‐procedure recurrence require closer follow‐up and more aggressive treatment, both in terms of VA and potential heart failure management.

Limitations

This was a retrospective study, which has inherent limitations, including a risk of residual confounding in our analyses. Furthermore, the retrospective design precludes standardized imaging protocols across the entire cohort, and echocardiographic data were acquired as part of routine clinical care rather than within a predefined study protocol. As such, the timing of imaging in relation to ablation and the uniformity of image acquisition may vary. While these limitations are inherent to retrospective analyses, they highlight the importance of prospective studies with standardized echocardiographic protocols to validate and extend our findings, and to further define the role of echocardiography in predicting outcomes after catheter ablation for ventricular arrhythmia.

Cox regression analyses were used, which may be susceptible to the risk of overfitting, reducing the generalizability outside the study population. In our model, we adjusted for six variables, a practice considered appropriate when not building prediction models [21].

The inclusion period extended from 2011 to 2022. RFA techniques have evolved over the last decade, which may influence both prevalence of VA recurrence and adverse outcomes [22]. Moreover, the endpoints utilized in this study were carefully selected based on set criteria but have not undergone formal validation.

Conclusion

This study found no significant associations between echocardiographic measurements and VA recurrence in patients undergoing first‐time RFA for VA. However, both LVMi, LVEF, and GLS were found to be independently associated with life‐threatening arrhythmia and all‐cause mortality. The risk of life‐threatening arrhythmia and mortality was highest amongst patients experiencing VA recurrence.
