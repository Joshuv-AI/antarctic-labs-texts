Influence of Adding Cyclodextrin Derivatives on the Mechanical and Thermal Properties of Glassy Polymers
Authors: Rinsei Toda, Kenji Yamaoka, Yoshinori Takashima
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe use of naturally derived materials for reinforcing and modifying polymer materials contributes to the development of sustainable polymer materials and reduces petroleum consumption. Cyclodextrin (CD) derivatives are promising naturally derived additives for reinforcing and modifying polymer materials. In this study, we investigated the influence of triacetylated γ‐cyclodextrin (TAcγCD) addition and pseudorotaxane formation on the mechanical and thermal properties of poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA) to elucidate the reinforcement mechanism. The incorporated TAcγCD primarily reinforced PMMA by acting as a rigid filler, with the reinforcement behavior following the Guth–Gold equation. Notably, pseudorotaxane formation slightly increased the Young's modulus compared to the value predicted by the equation, suggesting that pseudorotaxane formation may provide a modest additional contribution beyond conventional filler reinforcement. In addition, the glass transition temperature (T g) was maintained upon the incorporation of TAcγCD, indicating that TAcγCD does not act as a plasticizer in PMMA. These findings indicate the potential of CD derivatives as naturally derived additives for reinforcing and modifying glassy polymers, thereby contributing to the development of polymer materials compatible with sustainable societies.Keywords: cyclodextrin, filler effect, glass transition temperature, pseudorotaxane, Young's modulus

Introduction

The reinforcement and modification of polymer materials are essential for improving the heat resistance and mechanical strength of general‐purpose plastics and rubbers, achieving lightweight materials with high strength, and imparting additional functions and durability [1, 2]. Such improvements contribute to extending product lifetimes and reducing material and energy consumption. Filler incorporation [3, 4], plasticizer addition [5], and polymer alloying [6] are powerful, versatile, and readily applicable approaches for reinforcing and modifying polymer materials. However, many conventional additives are derived from petroleum resources and some of them have raised significant environmental and health concerns [7]. Therefore, naturally derived additives, such as cellulose‐based fillers [8, 9, 10, 11] and bio‐based plasticizers [12, 13, 14, 15], as well as bio‐based polymers [16, 17, 18, 19], have attracted increasing attention as sustainable alternatives for improving polymer properties while reducing petroleum consumption. To establish naturally derived additives as practical alternatives to conventional petroleum‐derived additives, elucidating the mechanisms by which these additives reinforce and modify polymer materials is essential.

CDs are cyclic oligosaccharides produced industrially from starch through enzymatic catalysis [20], and their derivatives have emerged as promising naturally derived additives for reinforcing and modifying polymer materials. In particular, mechanically interlocked structures based on cyclic molecules, such as polyrotaxane and pseudorotaxane, can influence the properties of polymers through topological constraints and the motion of cyclic components. Reinforcement using rotaxane structures has been developed mainly for rubbery polymer networks and gels, where the sliding motion of cross‐linking points and the resulting topological network structure play central roles in determining the mechanical properties [21, 22, 23, 24]. In such systems, mechanical improvement is associated with stress‐dissipation effects arising from the force‐induced motion of mechanically interlocked structures integrated into the network structure. Recently, rotaxane structures have also been introduced into thermoset resins and glassy polymers, demonstrating their potential for reinforcing rigid polymer materials in the glassy state [25, 26, 27, 28, 29]. Notably, CD derivatives can modify polymer properties even without being converted into synthetically demanding polyrotaxane structures. β‐CD derivatives incorporated into crystalline polymers enhance the crystallinity and mechanical properties as nucleating agents [30]. TAcγCD can reinforce and toughen polystyrene when simply incorporated as an additive [31]. Although TAcγCD has been demonstrated to modify the properties of glassy polymers, the reinforcement mechanism remains unclear. In addition, the incorporation of CD may influence the mechanical and thermal properties not only through its role as an additive but also through the confinement of segmental mobility by pseudorotaxane formation.

In this study, we aimed to clarify how the incorporation of CD and pseudorotaxane formation affect the mechanical and thermal properties of polymers. We established a straightforward method for introducing pseudorotaxane structures into polymers based on a movable cross‐linking strategy using CD monomers [32]. In this method, pseudorotaxane structures are formed through the statistical threading of growing polymer chains during bulk polymerization in the presence of cyclic molecules. This approach enabled us to evaluate both the effect of CD addition and the role of topological confinement induced by the inclusion of polymer chains in the CD cavities on the polymer properties. Using PMMA as a model glassy polymer, we demonstrated that bulk polymerization in the presence of TAcγCD readily afforded pseudorotaxane structures (Figure 1). The incorporated TAcγCD acts as a rigid filler and reinforces the PMMA matrix, with the reinforcement behavior following the Guth–Gold equation. Furthermore, pseudorotaxane formation led to modest improvements in the mechanical and thermal properties of PMMA, suggesting that the confinement of polymer segments by cyclic molecules may provide additional contributions beyond conventional filler reinforcement. These findings indicate the potential of CD derivatives as naturally derived additives for reinforcing and modifying glassy polymers, thereby contributing to the development of polymer materials compatible with sustainable societies.

Results and Discussion

The formation of the pseudorotaxane structure was confirmed using 2D nuclear Overhauser effect spectroscopy (2D NOESY). We prepared two types of samples with and without pseudorotaxane structures using two different synthetic procedures. A copolymer of methyl methacrylate (MMA) and hexyl methacrylate (HMA) containing pseudorotaxane structures, designated P(MMA‐HMA)‐PR, was synthesized via the bulk polymerization of MMA and HMA in the presence of TAcγCD. The HMA moieties acted as bulky stoppers to maintain the pseudorotaxane structure in the solvent during the 2D NOESY measurements. The cavity size of TAcγCD was estimated from an all‐atom model and was 0.67 nm. The extended chain length of MMA and HMA was determined from the all‐atom model and was 0.65 and 1.27 nm, respectively (Figure S1). Therefore, the extended chain length of HMA is approximately twice the cavity size of TAcγCD and is expected to function as a bulky stopper to prevent TAcγCD dethreading. As a non‐pseudorotaxane reference, P(MMA‐HMA) and TAcγCD were mixed in deuterated chloroform (CDCl3), and the resulting solution was designated P(MMA‐HMA)/TAcγCD.

P(MMA‐HMA)‐PR exhibited NOE cross‐peaks between the α‐methyl protons of MMA (1.1 ppm) and the protons of TAcγCD (4.0–5.3 ppm), indicating that the α‐methyl groups of PMMA and TAcγCD were in close spatial proximity (Figure 2a,c). These results suggest that the P(MMA‐HMA) chains were encapsulated within the TAcγCD cavities in P(MMA‐HMA)‐PR. In addition, diffusion‐ordered spectroscopy (DOSY) provided supporting evidence for the formation of pseudorotaxane structures in P(MMA‐HMA)‐PR through comparison of diffusion coefficients as described in the literature [33]. Free TAcγCD is expected to exhibit a higher diffusion coefficient than threaded TAcγCD because of its smaller molecular size and greater mobility. In contrast, the threaded TAcγCD is expected to exhibit a diffusion coefficient similar to that of the P(MMA‐HMA) axis chains, reflecting their cooperative diffusion as part of the same supramolecular assembly. In the DOSY spectrum (Figure S2), one of the diffusion coefficients of TAcγCD was found to be 6.5 × 10−10 m2/s, which is consistent with the diffusion coefficient of the P(MMA‐HMA) axis chain. The diffusion coefficient of the free TAcγCD peaks was found to be 1.4 × 10−9 m2/s, which was higher than that of the threaded TAcγCD. Therefore, bulk polymerization in the presence of TAcγCD led to the formation of a pseudorotaxane structure. However, only a fraction of TAcγCD cavities are threaded by PMMA chains, whereas some remaining TAcγCD molecules are free. Previous studies reported that the threading efficiency depends on the ring size of the cyclic molecule and the thickness of the axis chain, and that the threading efficiency for TAcγCD is 22%–29.7% [34, 35]. In contrast, P(MMA‐HMA)/TAcγCD did not show such NOE correlations (Figure 2b,d), indicating that no pseudorotaxane structure was formed within the PMMA/TAcγCD system.

Chemical structures of (a) P(MMA‐HMA)‐PR and (b) P(MMA‐HMA)/TAcγCD. 2D NOE spectrum of (c) P(MMA‐HMA)‐PR and (d) P(MMA‐HMA)/TAcγCD in CDCl3 at 5°C.

The formation of pseudorotaxane structures was supported by NMR measurements using the model polymer, P(MMA‐HMA)‐PR. To evaluate the influence of TAcγCD addition on the mechanical and thermal properties of PMMA, we prepared PMMA containing pseudorotaxane structures (PMMA‐PR) and PMMA without pseudorotaxane structures (PMMA/TAcγCD) using the same procedures used for P(MMA‐HMA)‐PR and P(MMA‐HMA)/TAcγCD, respectively. We consider that the pseudorotaxane structures formed during polymerization are kinetically trapped and maintained in PMMA‐PR unless the samples are dissolved in a solvent, because the segmental motion of PMMA chains is restricted when the T g of the polymerizing system exceeds room temperature as MMA polymerization proceeds.

The Young's modulus (E) of PMMA was 2.1 GPa, and the E values of PMMA‐PR and PMMA/TAcγCD monotonically increased with increasing volume fraction of TAcγCD (ϕ CD) (Figure 3a). However, the E value of PMMA‐PR decreased at ϕ CD = 9.1 vol%. PMMA‐PR became turbid at this composition (Figures S3 and S4), suggesting that phase separation occurred between PMMA and TAcγCD in PMMA‐PR. This phase separation may be responsible for the decrease in E at ϕ CD = 9.1 vol%. In PMMA‐PR, only a fraction of TAcγCD molecules is expected to be threaded by PMMA chains, and excess free TAcγCD may aggregate or phase‐separate during polymerization. The difference in transparency between PMMA‐PR and PMMA/TAcγCD at ϕ CD = 9.1 vol% is likely related to the different preparation methods. PMMA‐PR was prepared by bulk polymerization in the presence of TAcγCD, whereas PMMA/TAcγCD was prepared by solvent casting from a homogeneous solution.

ϕ CD dependence of (a) E and (b) T g of PMMA‐PR and PMMA/TAcγCD. The black solid line in panel (a) represents the predicted E based on the Guth–Gold equation.

X‐ray diffraction (XRD) measurements were performed to examine whether TAcγCD formed crystalline domains in the PMMA matrix, because CD crystalline domains can strongly influence mechanical properties [36, 37]. The XRD pattern of pristine TAcγCD powder exhibited multiple sharp diffraction peaks (Figure S5), indicating the formation of crystalline structures known as “cage‐type” packing, in which each cavity is blocked by neighboring CD molecules [38]. For PMMA/TAcγCD, no distinct diffraction peaks attributable to crystalline TAcγCD were observed at either ϕ CD = 7.3 or 9.1 vol%. PMMA‐PR at ϕ CD = 7.3 vol% also showed no apparent crystalline peaks derived from TAcγCD. In contrast, a weak diffraction peak was observed for PMMA‐PR at ϕ CD = 9.1 vol% at 2θ = 10.2°, suggesting partial crysallization of TAcγCD. These results suggest that TAcγCD may partially crystallize within the macroscopic TAcγCD‐rich aggregates that cause sample turbidity in PMMA‐PR at ϕ CD = 9.1 vol%. Therefore, the increases in E discussed in this study, particularly for samples without detectable crystalline TAcγCD peaks, are unlikely to originate from the crystallization of TAcγCD.

To discuss the effect of TAcγCD addition on the E value of PMMA, the experimental results were compared with the values predicted by the Guth–Gold equation [39]. This equation is applicable when the fillers are spherical, well‐dispersed, rigid, and when interfacial interactions between the matrix and fillers are not significant. The experimentally obtained E values of PMMA‐PR and PMMA/TAcγCD were in good agreement with those predicted by the Guth–Gold equation in the range of ϕ CD = 2.7–7.3 vol%, indicating that TAcγCD primarily behaves as a rigid filler in the PMMA matrix and contributes to the reinforcement of the PMMA matrix. Notably, the E values of PMMA‐PR were slightly higher than the predicted values in the range of ϕ CD = 2.7–7.3 vol%. Although this difference was modest, the deviation from the value predicted by the Guth–Gold equation suggests that pseudorotaxane formation may provide an additional contribution to the increase in stiffness. In rubber composites containing nanofillers, the E values can be significantly higher than those predicted by the Guth–Gold equation at high filler concentrations owing to the presence of an adsorbed polymer layer on the filler surface and a percolated filler network. The reinforcement of the rubber composites is attributed to both the rigid polymer chains adsorbed onto the filler surface and the formation of a 3D network of fillers [40, 41, 42]. Therefore, the modest reinforcement of PMMA‐PR suggests that the pseudorotaxane structure suppresses the segmental mobility of the PMMA chains. The small magnitude of this reinforcement may also be attributed to the limited threading efficiency of TAcγCD, which reduces the fraction of PMMA segments confined within TAcγCD.

The T g value was determined from the peak temperature of the loss tangent (tanδ) during the glass transition (Figure S6). The T g of PMMA was 136°C. The T g values of PMMA‐PR did not show a clear monotonic increase with increasing ϕ CD, although several compositions exhibited slightly higher T g values than PMMA without TAcγCD (ϕ CD = 0 vol%) (Figure 3b). In contrast, the T g values of PMMA/TAcγCD were comparable to those of PMMA and did not increase with ϕ CD. These results suggest that pseudorotaxane formation may slightly affect the segmental mobility of PMMA at certain compositions; however, its effect on T g is modest and composition‐dependent. Therefore, the thermal properties of PMMA‐PR should not be interpreted as being significantly enhanced solely by pseudorotaxane formation. Importantly, no decrease in T g was observed for either PMMA‐PR or PMMA/TAcγCD over the examined range of ϕ CD. This result clearly indicates that unlike conventional low‐molecular‐weight plasticizers, TAcγCD does not plasticize the PMMA matrix.

Conclusion

In summary, this study investigated the influences of TAcγCD addition and pseudorotaxane formation on the mechanical and thermal properties of glassy polymers to clarify the reinforcement mechanism. Bulk polymerization in the presence of TAcγCD enabled the introduction of pseudorotaxane structures into PMMA. The incorporated TAcγCD primarily acted as a rigid filler and reinforced the PMMA matrix, with the reinforcement behavior following the Guth–Gold equation. In addition, pseudorotaxane formation slightly increased the Young's modulus compared with the value predicted by the equation, suggesting that pseudorotaxane formation may provide a modest additional contribution beyond conventional filler reinforcement. Importantly, TAcγCD did not act as a plasticizer in PMMA. These findings indicate that TAcγCD has potential as a naturally derived additive for reinforcing and modifying glassy polymers, thereby contributing to the development of polymer materials compatible with a sustainable society.

Experimental Section

Materials

MMA, acetic anhydride, and tetrahydrofuran (THF) were purchased from Nacalai Tesque, Inc. Acetone, methanol, and dichloromethane (DCM) were purchased from FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Co., Ltd. γ‐Cyclodextrin was purchased from Junsei Chemical Co., Ltd. HMA and 1‐hydroxycyclohexyl phenyl ketone (HCPK) were purchased from Tokyo Chemical Industry. CDCl3 with 0.03% tetramethylsilane (TMS) was purchased from Eurisotop, France. MMA was purified by passing through a short column of activated basic alumina to remove the polymerization inhibitors. All other reagents were used as received. TAcγCD was prepared according to a previous report [31].

Preparation

PMMA‐PR, in which PMMA chains thread the cavity of TAcγCD, was prepared by the bulk polymerization of MMA in the presence of TAcγCD. TAcγCD and HCPK were dissolved in MMA. The homogeneous mixture was sonicated for 60 min and poured between two glass plates with a 1 mm thick spacer. Bulk free‐radical polymerization was carried out under UV irradiation with an LED lamp (wavelength, λ = 365 nm) for 24 h (Scheme S1). PMMA‐PR was obtained after the samples were dried at 140°C under vacuum to remove residual monomer. ϕ CD was varied from 0 to 9.1 vol%. The ϕ CD values were calculated using the densities of PMMA and TAcγCD, which were 1.19 and 1.31 g cm−3, respectively. The amounts of reagents used for the preparation of PMMA‐PR are summarized in Table S1. The weight‐average molecular weight (M w) of PMMA‐PR was 209–722 kg/mol (Table S2). To confirm the formation of the pseudorotaxane structure, P(MMA‐HMA)‐PR was prepared using the same procedure (Scheme S2 and Table S3). The HMA moieties act as bulky stoppers to maintain the pseudorotaxane structure. The molar feed molar ratio of MMA/HMA was 99/1.

PMMA/TAcγCD was prepared via solvent casting. PMMA was synthesized via the photo‐initiated bulk free‐radical polymerization using HCPK as a photo‐initiator. PMMA and TAcγCD were dissolved in DCM at a solute concentration of 5 wt.%. The solution was stirred for 1 h at room temperature and poured into a Teflon Petri dish. PMMA/TAcγCD films were obtained after drying at 30°C under atmospheric pressure for 15 h and then 140°C in vacuo for 12 h to completely remove residual DCM (Scheme S3). ϕ CD was varied from 0 to 9.1 vol%. The amounts of reagents used for the preparation of PMMA/TAcγCD are summarized in Table S4. The M w of PMMA used to prepare PMMA/TAcγCD was 211–218 kg/mol (Table S5). As a non‐pseudorotaxane reference for the NOESY measurement, P(MMA‐HMA)/TAcγCD was prepared by dissolving P(MMA‐HMA) and TAcγCD in CDCl3, with the weight concentration of TAcγCD adjusted to be identical to that of P(MMA‐HMA)‐PR (Table S6). P(MMA‐HMA) was prepared using the same procedure as PMMA (Scheme S4). The molar feed molar ratio of MMA/HMA was 99/1.

Nuclear Magnetic Resonance spectroscopy

Proton (1H) nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) and 2D 1H‐1H NOE spectra were obtained at 700 MHz using a Bruker ADVANCE NEO NMR spectrometer at 5°C. P(MMA‐HMA)‐PR was dissolved in CDCl3 at a concentration of 100 mg mL−1. P(MMA‐HMA)/TAcγCD was prepared in CDCl3 at the same concentration and used directly for the NOESY measurements. The mixing time was 500 ms. Chemical shifts were referenced to TMS, which was used as the internal standard (δ = 0 ppm).

Gel Permeation Chromatography

The molecular weight of each polymer was determined by gel permeation chromatography (GPC) using an EcoSEC HLC‐8420GPC (Tosoh Bioscience, Inc.) column (Tosoh TSK gel SuperHZM‐N ×2) and a guard column (KF‐G 4A). The polymers were dissolved in THF at a concentration of 1.5 wt.%. The elution peak was analyzed to determine the relationship between the elution time and the molecular weight of the polystyrene standard sample.

Dynamic Mechanical Analysis Measurements

The viscoelastic properties of the polymers under shear deformation were measured using an MCR302e torsional shear rheometer (Anton Paar). Rectangular specimens with dimensions of 1 mm × 8 mm × 20 mm were used for measurements. Temperature sweep measurements were conducted over the temperature range of 50°C–180°C at a heating rate of 3°C min−1. The measurements were performed at a constant frequency of 1 Hz, while the applied strain amplitude was logarithmically increased from 0.01% to 0.1% during heating. T g was defined as the peak temperature of tanδ during the glass transition.

Tensile Tests

Tensile tests of the polymers were performed using an Autograph AGX‐V2 (Shimadzu) at a deformation rate of 0.01 mm s−1. The strain was determined using a non‐contact extensometer (TRViewX, Shimadzu). All samples were tested using at least three test pieces to calculate the average values of the mechanical properties and their standard deviations. The Young's modulus was calculated from the initial slope of the stress‐strain curve at the strain range of 0.5%–1%.

Density Measurements

The true density of TAcγCD was measured using a gas pycnometer for semi‐solid and solid materials (Ultrapyc 7000, Anton Paar). The sample volume was determined from the pressure change of the helium gas in the chambers according to Boyle's law. The density was calculated from the measured mass and volume, and the reported value represents the average of at least three measurements.

XRD Measurements

XRD measurements were performed using a MiniFlex benchtop X‐ray diffractometer (Rigaku, Japan) with Cu Kα radiation operated at 40 kV and 15 mA. The diffraction patterns were recorded over a 2θ range of 5°–45° at a step size of 0.01°.
