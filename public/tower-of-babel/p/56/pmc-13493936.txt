Frequency-dependent effects of motor thalamus deep brain stimulation on speech and swallowing
Authors: Lilly W Tang, Erinn M Grigsby, Arianna Damiani, Jonathan C Ho, Isabella M Montanaro, Sirisha Nouduri, Scott Ensel, Sara Trant, Theodora Constantine, Gregory M Adams, Denise V Balason, Tracey L Thomas, Kelly Zdrowak, Jordyn Ting, George F Wittenberg, Kevin Franzese, Bradford Z Mahon, Julie A Fiez, Donald J Crammond, Kaila L Stipancic, Jorge A Gonzalez-Martinez, Elvira Pirondini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSpeech and swallowing are complex motor sequences driven by cortico-bulbar communication between motor cortex and cranial muscles. Damage to these pathways impairs strength and control of orofacial muscles, causing chronic dysarthria and dysphagia that increase social isolation, aspiration risks, and mortality. We show that motor thalamus Deep Brain Stimulation (DBS) can improve these functions after traumatic brain injury (TBI). First, we demonstrated that low-frequency DBS (50-80 Hz) facilitates activation of cortico-bulbar fibers, immediately potentiating motor output in n = 8 participants with intact pathways. In a participant who sustained a TBI resulting in chronic moderate dysphagia and severe dysarthria, low-frequency DBS improved facial movement, swallowing efficiency, and speech intelligibility across respiratory, phonatory, and articulatory subsystems. Importantly, these improvements in facial articulator and speech subsystems control were specific to low-frequency (50-80 Hz) stimulation. These findings provide preliminary evidence that motor thalamus DBS can modulate corticobulbar circuitry to improve dysarthria and dysphagia following cerebral lesions.Subject terms: White matter injury, Motor cortex

Introduction

Natural and intelligible speech requires coordinated motor control of four subsystems: respiration, phonation, resonance, and articulation. Similarly, swallowing involves a sequential orchestration of movements from the oral cavity, velopharyngeal port, pharynx, larynx, and esophagus to safely and efficiently transport food and liquid from the mouth through the stomach. Precise and coordinated activation of these systems depends upon the integrity of the cortico-spinal tract (CST), which innervates the muscles of respiration located in the thorax, neck, and shoulder, and the cortico-bulbar tract (CBT), which provides the bilateral innervation of laryngeal, palatal, lingual, and facial muscles1. Cerebral lesions from stroke, traumatic brain injury (TBI), brain tumors, or neurodegenerative disorders can disrupt these tracts and cause deficits in the volitional control of facial, respiratory, and oropharyngeal muscles. This may result in a spectrum of undesirable auditory-perceptual speech characteristics, such as voice breaks, reduced speech intensity, or imprecision in sound production. Any of these motor impairments, taken alone or collectively, can contribute to an overall reduction in speech quality and intelligibility, leading to dysarthria1–4. Similarly, loss of oropharyngeal, laryngeal, and esophageal control can lead to swallowing difficulties, also known as dysphagia. Patients with dysphagia often require alternative means of nutrition and hydration, putting them at elevated risks of aspiration, pulmonary co-morbidities, and even mortality5. To date, over 5 million people in the United States alone live with dysphagia or dysarthria, and the resulting disability burden will only increase as the population ages6–8.

After cerebral lesions, speech and swallow therapy remains the standard treatment for dysarthria and dysphagia, where speech-language pathologists (SLPs) introduce compensatory behavioral techniques to improve speech and swallowing functions9. Yet outcomes remain highly variable because efficacy of these approaches depends on sustained individual effort, retention capacity, and ability to transfer skills beyond the course of therapy10–12. External augmentative or substitutive communication devices13 can palliate unsuccessful behavioral interventions, but these methods do not restore natural speech production. Consequently, many patients continue to face major communication barrier that further exacerbates psychosocial burdens14–17. Importantly, failure to restore swallowing function compromises patients’ access to nutrition, further contributing to a rapid deterioration in health14–17.

In most cases, injury to the CST and CBT as a consequence of stroke or diffuse axonal injuries from TBI is incomplete, leaving some residual connections. Yet, these remaining fibers cannot adequately activate lower motoneurons, leading to weakness and poor control of orofacial musculature. Facilitating the activation of these residual axons could reestablish the missing excitation to restore speech and swallowing motor functions18. This facilitation could be achieved by increasing the excitability of cortical neurons, particularly the facial representation of the motor cortex, thereby enhancing CST and CBT motor transmission and consequently facial and oropharyngeal movements responsible for speech production and swallow coordination. In this regard, previous works have proposed the use of non-invasive neuromodulation interventions, such as transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) and transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS), to boost cortical excitability and treat dysarthria and dysphagia, yet results to date remain inconclusive19–23.

We recently demonstrated that deep brain stimulation (DBS) of direct excitatory connections to the motor cortex selectively and substantially increases the excitability of cortico-spinal motoneurons, thereby producing large potentiation of upper-limb motor function18. Specifically, with intra-operative experiments in non-human primates and human subjects, we showed that low frequency (50–80 Hz) DBS of the motor thalamus enhanced the excitability of the CST by recruiting thalamocortical afferent fibers to the motor cortex. This increased excitability improved arm and hand strength and volitional control in a participant with severe upper limb paresis sustained from TBI. Importantly, these effects appeared immediately when the stimulation was switched ON and vanished when the stimulation was turned OFF. These promising effects may be attributed to the higher selectivity and continuous nature of DBS compared to non-invasive approaches24–26.

The motor thalamus also has direct excitatory connections to the agranular motor cortical areas representing the face, oropharynx, and larynx27–30. Indeed, our previous study in monkeys18 showed that stimulation of the motor thalamus potentiated motor-evoked potentials (MEPs) of not only hand but also orofacial muscles. We therefore hypothesized that a targeted DBS of the motor thalamus could be specifically tailored to enhance voluntary activation and control of facial and oropharyngeal articulators. By specifically stimulating the interface between the ventral intermediate (VIM) and ventral oralis poster (VOP) nucleus, regions known to have direct excitatory projections to the motor cortex, DBS of the motor thalamus may provide immediate improvements in swallowing and speech motor deficits in patients with corticospinal and corticobulbar tract lesions.

However, this hypothesis contradicts established clinical experiences where DBS of subthalamic (STN) and motor thalamic nuclei at clinical-standard frequencies (≥130 Hz) have often been associated with adverse effects such as dysarthria31–34. We posit that these observed outcomes may be attributed to a frequency-dependent mechanism whereby low-frequency stimulation (50–80 Hz) exerts a facilitative effect while high frequencies (≥130 Hz) suppress motor output. To reconcile this inconsistency, we first conducted acute intraoperative testing in human subjects with intact descending motor fibers undergoing DBS implantation in the motor thalamus for treatment of essential tremor (ET). We verified that the facilitatory mechanisms of motor thalamus DBS observed for upper-limb muscles remained valid for facial and oropharyngeal articulators. Specifically, we showed that low-frequency (50–80 Hz) DBS of the motor thalamus potentiated MEPs of facial and oropharyngeal muscles. Importantly, in the same pool of subjects, the same frequencies that resulted in MEP potentiation did not cause dysarthria. This confirmation provided critical mechanistic validation that allowed us to proceed with proof-of-concept testing in one patient with chronic speech and swallowing impairments following bilateral diffuse axonal injuries due to TBI. Specifically, in this participant, we tested the effects of motor thalamus DBS on the potentiation of corticobulbar excitability, amplitude and speed of facial movements, swallowing, and speech functions.

Collectively, this study provides evidence that low-frequency motor thalamus DBS can potentiate speech and swallowing subsystems, paving the way towards a new intervention to improve dysphagia and dysarthria secondary to cerebral injury.

Results

Study design

To test our hypotheses, we conducted a proof-of-concept study comprised of two sub-studies assessing the effects of motor thalamus DBS on the control of facial and oropharyngeal articulators. The first sub-study comprised participants with intact CBT and motor function, who underwent DBS implantation in the motor thalamus for treatment of essential tremor (n = 8 subjects, S01–S08). Specifically, we investigated the mechanisms of motor thalamus DBS and confirmed that low-frequency stimulation did not induce dysarthria. In the second sub-study, we conducted functional testing in a participant with implants in the motor thalamus who sustained CBT lesions (n = 1, TBI01, see Supplementary Fig.1C for lesion details) resulting in profound speech deficits and moderate swallowing difficulties. The participant underwent sessions of facial movements, swallowing, and speech tests to quantify the immediate effects of DBS on oro-motor functions. Assessments included 3D kinematics during facial movement tasks, videofluoroscopic, and audio recordings. In all participants, we individualized DBS parameters to stimulate the motor thalamus at stimulation intensities that produced a transient paresthesia in the facial muscles. No adverse events related to the use of DBS were reported.

Stimulation of the motor thalamus potentiates motor-evoked potentials of facial and oropharyngeal muscles

We previously demonstrated that stimulation of the motor thalamus, specifically at the interface of VIM/VOP nuclei, at low-frequency (50–80 Hz range) increases primary motor cortex (M1) excitability and upper-limb motor output via the CST, resulting in improved hand and arm functions after TBI. In both human and non-human primates, motor thalamus stimulation immediately increased the amplitude of M1 direct cortical stimulation (DCS)-MEPs of contralateral upper-limb muscles18. Notably, sustained potentiation of DCS-MEPs was only present at low-frequencies, whereas high-frequencies produced progressive suppression of DCS-MEPs over time. These frequency-dependent effects suggested that the increased M1 excitability is mediated by thalamocortical synaptic excitatory projections.

Interestingly, in our previous monkey experiments, we also observed a significant potentiation of contralateral facial and oropharyngeal muscle MEPs. Expanding upon these preliminary results, we sought to determine whether targeted motor thalamus stimulation could produce a similar potentiation in facial and oropharyngeal MEPs in human subjects (Fig. 1A) and whether this potentiation would also demonstrate a frequency-dependent behavior. To test this, we leveraged intraoperative electrophysiological recordings and stimulation tests routinely performed in ET patients undergoing DBS implantation in the VIM/VOP (n = 8, subjects S01–S08, 5 males, 3 females, Supplementary Table 1). Bilateral subcutaneous EMG needle electrodes were placed in up to five facial and oropharyngeal muscles associated with speech and swallowing physiology (masseter, orbicularis oris, mylohyoid, cricothyroid, and mentalis, Fig. 1A, B). We then positioned a 6-channel subdural strip electrode (Adtech, Oak Creek, WI, USA) over the facial representation of M1. To verify electrode position, we performed DCS of the subdural electrode and recorded facial DCS-MEPs. The optimal subdural electrode contact was identified as the contact that elicited the largest and most consistent contralateral MEPs across facial and oropharyngeal muscles.

We then recorded facial and oropharyngeal MEPs evoked by M1-DCS alone or paired with VIM/VOP stimulation at 50, 80, 100, and 130 Hz from a macroelectrode (Neuro Omega, Alpha Omega Engineering, Israel) implanted in the same hemisphere (see Supplementary Table 1 for individual parameters of stimulation and Supplementary Fig. 1A for location of stimulation). Because our previous study in upper-limb muscles showed that sustained potentiation was stronger at low-frequency (50–80 Hz), we first compared the DCS-MEPs average amplitude between M1-DCS alone and M1-DCS paired with motor thalamus stimulation at 50 and 80 Hz. Consistent with our previous findings for upper-limb and orofacial muscles in monkeys18, pairing M1-DCS with low-frequency (50 and 80 Hz) motor thalamus stimulation produced an immediate significant increase in the DCS-MEP amplitudes of contralateral facial and oropharyngeal muscles compared to M1-DCS alone (i.e., median increase of 62.4% at 50 Hz and 65.2% at 80 Hz across all recorded muscles, Fig. 1C–E, Supplementary Fig. 2A). Potentiation was observed in all recorded muscles associated with the main articulators for swallowing and speech: jaw (masseter), lips (orbicularis oris), tongue (mylohyoid), chin (mentalis), and larynx (cricothyroid). We then compared the changes in amplitude over time between the MEPs generated from M1-DCS alone and those generated with concurrent motor thalamus DBS. We identified three distinct modulation patterns: (1) no potentiation, (2) sustained potentiation, or (3) gradual attenuation (Fig. 1F). As expected, low-frequency stimulation (50–80 Hz) primarily produced a sustained potentiation of DCS-MEPs (>60% of the cases), whereas high-frequency stimulation (100–130 Hz) led to predominantly attenuation (potentiation constituted less than 50% of the cases, Fig. 1F–G). These findings confirm that the potentiation of descending CBT activity arises from the orthodromic recruitment of cortico-bulbar neurons in M1 through excitatory thalamocortical synapses.

Because speech requires bilateral muscle coordination and the CBT provides bilateral innervation, we also explored the effects of low-frequency (50–80 Hz) motor thalamus stimulation on the ipsilateral facial and oropharyngeal muscles (muscles on the same side of DCS and motor thalamus stimulation)1. Interestingly, pairing M1-DCS with motor thalamus stimulation significantly potentiated the ipsilateral facial and oropharyngeal MEPs (Supplementary Fig. 2A), although the potentiation effect remained significantly stronger for contralateral muscles (on average across all muscles and participants, contralateral potentiation was over 7.4 times greater than it was for ipsilateral counterparts, Supplementary Fig. 2B).

In summary, these findings suggest that targeted low-frequency motor-thalamus stimulation enhances corticobulbar excitability, as evidenced by increased MEP amplitudes of facial and oropharyngeal muscles. These results are consistent with our previous findings of CST and upper limb muscles, further supporting our proposed mechanisms of motor thalamus stimulation18.

Low-frequency stimulation (50–80 Hz) does not disrupt speech motor functions

DBS of the VIM and STN has been associated with stimulation-induced dysarthria in at least 10% of patients35–39. These adverse effects have been proposed to arise from unintentional current spread to the CST and CBT within the internal capsule and from the use of high-frequency stimulation parameters (≥130 Hz). We therefore hypothesize that stimulation at the frequency shown to induce sustained potentiation (50–80 Hz) will not cause dysarthria in people with intact CBT and speech function. Demonstrating that low-frequency motor thalamus DBS does not worsen dysarthria is critical to justifying the application of this approach to improve motor speech functions in people with pre-existing dysarthria.

To test for this, we recruited a subset of ET patients (n = 3, S01, S02, and S04) from those who had undergone acute intraoperative testing after completing bilateral chronic DBS implantation (Medtronic, Sensight leads Percept PC). Participants performed the Assessment of Intelligibility of Dysarthric Speech test (AIDS) by repeating sentences of varying length under three randomized conditions: no stimulation, 57 Hz, and 130 Hz stimulation. Importantly, while participants could perceive a transient paresthesia in the face muscles when stimulation was turned ON, they could not distinguish stimulation frequencies. Participants were, therefore, blinded to the stimulation conditions. Certified SLPs (n = 4), blinded to the stimulation conditions, evaluated the audio samples by perceptual speech measures on a scale of 0 to 100 (0 = most impaired, 100 = least impaired)40–43. We then quantified the change in each perceptual measure between no stimulation and stimulation at 57 Hz and 130 Hz, respectively.

As expected, stimulation at 130 Hz significantly degraded speech, specifically reducing intelligibility, perceived listener’s ease, and overall quality of speech compared to no stimulation. In contrast, stimulation at 57 Hz produced ratings indistinguishable from no stimulation across all perceptual speech measures (Fig. 1H, Supplementary Fig. 3). These results demonstrate that low-frequency DBS of the motor thalamus preserves speech motor functions in individuals with intact CBT, thereby validating the safety profile of our approach. As expected, during testing, we did not observe noticeable improvements in tremor with low-frequency stimulation.

Low-frequency stimulation (50–80 Hz) of the motor thalamus exerts a facilitatory effect, enhancing corticospinal and corticobulbar excitability without impairing normal speech. Therefore, we sought to leverage this low-frequency stimulation to augment motor output in patients who sustained facial and oropharyngeal motor deficits due to corticobulbar cerebral injuries.

Potentiation of motor-evoked potentials persists after CBT lesions

To demonstrate the potential clinical application of this approach, we assessed whether the potentiating effect of low-frequency (50–80 Hz) motor thalamus stimulation persisted after a partial lesion of the CBT. We had the opportunity to test the effect of motor thalamus DBS in a patient who sustained bi-hemispheric diffuse axonal injuries from traumatic brain injury (TBI01), resulting in bilateral upper-limb paresis, severe facial paresis, moderate dysphagia, and profound dysarthria. The patient was implanted with a chronic DBS system (Medtronic, Sensight leads Percept PC) in the VIM/VOP nucleus for off-label indication of tremor management (Supplementary Fig. 1B). Diffusion tractography of his descending motor fibers confirmed the presence of bilateral lesions of the CBT, with more severe injury localized to the right side, paralleling his exhibited motor deficits (Supplementary Fig. 1C). We used transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) to replicate M1-DCS stimulation in a non-invasive chronic experimental paradigm44. Surface EMG was used to collect MEPs from the bilateral masseter during M1 stimulation alone (M1-TMS) and paired with motor thalamus stimulation at low frequency (55 Hz, Fig. 2A, see Supplementary Table 1 for details on stimulation parameters). As expected, the amplitude of TMS-MEPs in TBI01 was significantly potentiated with concurrent motor thalamus stimulation (Fig. 2B, C), demonstrating that primary motor cortex excitability and CBT output were increased despite the cerebral lesion. This potentiation prompted us to further test the effects of stimulation across orofacial movements, speech, and swallowing functions in TBI01.

Stimulation of the motor thalamus increases the range of motion and speed of facial articulators during facial movements

TBI patients who sustain diffuse axonal injuries to CST and CBT often experience facial paresis as a result of muscle weakness1. Therefore, we first evaluated whether the observed potentiation of TMS-MEPs from motor thalamus stimulation at 55 Hz would lead to functional augmentation of overt voluntary facial movements. To test this, TBI01 performed facial motor tasks alternating between neutral and designated facial expressions (tongue in/out, smile, mouth open/close, and lip pucker) as exaggeratedly and as quickly as possible for 15 s (Fig. 2D). Tests were repeated with stimulation OFF and stimulation ON at 55 Hz 3 mA (see Supplementary Table 2 for details on the stimulation parameters).

We recorded videos of TBI01 completing the tasks across three different sessions to perform quantitative kinematic analysis, assessing the amplitude of the movements of the main facial articulators: jaw, cheek, mouth, upper and lower lip, and chin. We then quantified the range of motion and velocity of these facial articulators with low-frequency stimulation ON and OFF. As hypothesized, motor thalamus DBS significantly increased movement amplitude as compared to DBS OFF trials immediately (i.e., increased range between 97.3% and 364.3% in amplitude across all facial articulators, Fig. 2E–F, Supplementary Movie 1). To better evaluate the range of movement, the three most representative facial articulators for each task were then selected to calculate the mouth aperture. DBS ON produced a significant increase in mouth aperture (Fig. 2I) and movement velocity (Fig. 2J) relative to OFF across all four tasks. The potentiating effect was consistently observed over all sessions of testing (n = 3). Finally, we explored the effects of the stimulation on contra- and ipsilateral articulators. Specifically, we compared facial movements for unilateral stimulation of the hemisphere with the more extensive lesion (i.e., right hemisphere, Supplementary Fig. 1C) versus bilateral stimulation. Interestingly, we found that bilateral stimulation resulted in more symmetric (Supplementary Fig. 4A) and larger movements as compared to unilateral stimulation (Supplementary Fig. 4B).

Overall, these results demonstrate that low-frequency motor thalamus DBS enhances voluntary facial motor output in a patient with an injury of the CST and CBT, suggesting the applicability of this approach as a neuromodulation therapy to improve facial paresis.

Stimulation of the motor thalamus improves swallowing functions in a participant with dysphagia

We then sought to investigate whether low-frequency (50--80 Hz) motor thalamus stimulation could improve swallowing motor function. Importantly, loss of strength and coordination of facial and oropharyngeal muscles45 often results in impaired swallowing, characterized by a delay in the transit of a liquid or solid bolus and/or an increase in retention or residue during the oral, pharyngeal, or esophageal phases of swallowing. To assess the effects of low-frequency motor thalamus stimulation on the swallowing function of TBI01, we conducted videofluoroscopic swallow studies46 (modified barium swallow tests) with DBS OFF and ON at 55 Hz. An SLP (n = 1) who was blinded to the stimulation conditions conducted the clinical assessment. Videofluoroscopic study radiology reports were interpreted by blinded SLPs (n = 2) to confirm internal validity across raters and evaluations.

With the DBS OFF condition, TBI01 exhibited moderate oral dysphagia, mild pharyngeal dysphagia, and no deficits in the esophageal phase. The SLPs rated all TBI01’s swallowing trials to be safe, as indicated by PAS scores of either 1 or 2. Importantly, TBI01 was instructed to clear each trial of bolus in a single swallow as much as possible. Therefore, any residue remaining after the initial swallow was evaluated and assigned an efficiency grade. The PAS score and efficiency grade collectively accounted towards a cumulative DIGEST score of either 0 (no impairment) and 1 (mild impairment) (see Supplementary Table 3, for individual trial scores). Low-frequency motor thalamus stimulation did not further reduce PAS scores, as these values were already within the normal and safe range for TBI01. However, stimulation did improve the efficiency grade of swallowing and DIGEST scores such that mild impairments observed in DBS OFF conditions were all restored to no impairment with stimulation ON (Fig. 3A). PAS and DIGEST scores were highly reliable across SLPs, with a weighted Cohen’s kappa of k = 0.22 (fair agreement) for PAS and k = 0.83 (substantial agreement) for DIGEST. Moreover, the SLPs reported a consistent reduction in the number of swallows required to clear a bolus of the matched volume and consistencies with DBS ON compared to DBS OFF (Fig. 3B).

To assess the tongue and lip strength, both of which are critical in bolus propulsion during the oral phase of swallowing, we employed the Iowa Oral Performance Instrument (IOPI)47. The IOPI measures pressure exerted on an air-filled bulb to quantify the maximal strength of the tongue and lips. For this task, TBI01 applied maximal pressure sustained for >2 s on the bulb using either his tongue, left lip, or right lip, respectively. While both lips and tongue showed an increased strength with stimulation ON at 55 Hz compared to OFF, TBI01 experienced significant improvement in the left side, indicative of greater strength and sealing ability of the lip for swallowing (Fig. 3C).

To further evaluate the effects of the stimulation on swallowing efficiency in the pharyngeal phase, we quantified the duration of swallowing from the videofluoroscopic radiology reports and the maximum amount of premature spillage of the bolus for the subsequent swallow (or residues following the initial swallow) of each bolus trial. Importantly, the total duration of swallowing across the pharyngeal phase was significantly reduced with stimulation ON as compared to OFF (p = 0.038, n = 6 tasks), indicating a faster, more efficient swallow (Fig. 3D, Supplementary Movie 2). TBI01 also had significantly less spillage in the vallecula and piriform sinuses when stimulation was ON compared to OFF (p = 0.037, n = 6 tasks, Fig. 3E), highlighting a positive effect of the stimulation on improving deglutition of liquids (Fig. 3F).

These results collectively demonstrate the remediating effect of low-frequency (50–80 Hz) motor thalamus DBS on improving swallowing efficiency, primarily reducing spillage and shortening pharyngeal transit, in a patient with chronic corticobulbar lesions.

Stimulation of the motor thalamus improves respiratory, phonatory, and articulatory control during speech in a participant with severe dysarthria

Since patients with CBT injuries often experience concomitant motor speech deficits, we investigated the effect of low-frequency motor thalamus stimulation on speech production. We had the opportunity to test this on TBI01, who sustained severe chronic speech impairment in the setting of his TBI. Prior to any of the testing sessions, an SLP who was blinded to the stimulation condition performed a clinical speech evaluation of TBI01 with stimulation OFF. TBI01 demonstrated a profound dysarthria characterized by impaired ability to control vocal pitch or loudness, reduced breath support for phonation, and imprecise articulation characterized by distortions of consonants and vowels (most notably bilabial consonant sounds). We collected video and audio recordings across multiple sessions of TBI01 repeating mono-to tri-syllable words that included the full spectrum of American English phonemes and required varying degrees of coordination between lips, soft palate, and tongue (Supplementary Fig. 5).

Since the speech deficits of TBI01 affected respiration, phonation, and articulation, we assessed the effects of low-frequency motor thalamus stimulation across each subsystem. Vocal intensity was increased when low-frequency stimulation was turned ON as compared to stimulation OFF in 72.2% of the words (65.4% of which were statistically significant, Fig. 4A). Importantly, in 35.3% of these significant cases, the increase was above 5 dB, meeting the threshold for a perceptible, clinically relevant change in loudness48, proving that low-frequency motor thalamus stimulation enhanced respiratory support for volume control. Similarly, stimulation immediately and significantly reduced the number of voice breaks (mode: 1 break and 0 breaks, with stimulation OFF and ON, respectively, Fig. 4B), demonstrating an improvement in phonatory control. We further assessed the effects of the stimulation on phonatory integrity by computing Cepstral Peak Prominence-smoothed (CPPS)49,50: a lower CPPS value corresponds to a more dysphonic sound, often presenting as a hoarse, harsh, and/or strained voice quality. We observed a significant increase in the CPPS values with stimulation ON compared to stimulation OFF (median increase: 10.9%, Fig. 4B), further corroborating a significant improvement in phonatory control. We also observed an improvement in articulatory control (Supplementary Movie 3). Patients living with dysarthria often experience deficits in control of the articulators, leading to an omission of consonants and distortion of vowels1. For this reason, we assessed articulatory control separately for consonants by perceptually assessing the presence, distortion, and absence of each phoneme and for vowels by quantifying changes in the main formants (i.e., changes in frequencies of F1 and F2 formants). Interestingly, we found that stimulation ON produced an increase (26.2 ± 13.1%, Fig. 4C) in the perceptual presence of several consonant sounds (/b/, /m/, /f/, /d/, /l/, /g/, and /h/). Additionally, we found a change in the frequencies of the F1 formant, which is closely associated with vertical tongue movement51 (increase for low vowels—/ei/, /Ɛ/ and /ɑ/—and decrease for high vowels—/i/, /ɑɪ/, /ɝ/ and /oʊ/), and in the frequencies of F2 formant, which encodes horizontal tongue movement51 (decrease for back vowels—/ɑ/, /ɔ/, and /ʌ/—and an increase for front vowels—/eɪ/, /ɛ/, and /ɝ/—Fig. 4C). These changes suggest an effect of the stimulation on mouth and tongue control consistent with the improvements observed in the facial expression and swallowing tasks (Figs. 2, 3).

Finally, we assessed whether low-frequency motor thalamus stimulation would reduce the amount of task duration-related fatigue that patients would experience. For this, we computed the time required for TBI01 to complete the repetition of the first five words and the last five words. We reasoned that a participant would generally experience more fatigue at the end of a task than at the beginning of a task and would, therefore, require more time to complete productions at the end of the task as compared to the beginning of the task. If stimulation reduced task duration-related fatigue level, we would expect to observe a smaller increase in time between the first and last five words when the stimulation was ON compared to when stimulation was OFF. Our hypothesis was confirmed: the increase in time between the first and the last five words was significantly smaller for the stimulation ON condition as compared to stimulation OFF, demonstrating that motor thalamus stimulation decreased fatigue level (Fig. 4D).

Overall, our results demonstrate that low-frequency motor thalamus stimulation yields a significant improvement in speech production. These initial findings support further investigation of this neuromodulation approach for dysarthria.

Improvements in facial articulator and speech subsystem control were specific to low-frequency (50–80 Hz) stimulation

While it is difficult to assess whether 130 Hz would cause dysarthria in a person who already experiences motor speech deficits, we still attempted to compare the effects of stimulation at standard DBS high-frequency (130 Hz) and low frequency (55 Hz) in TBI01. Importantly, TBI01 could not perceive or distinguish between these two frequency stimulation parameters, which ensured effective blinding. These experiments allowed us to refine the effects of high- and low-frequency motor thalamus stimulation on orofacial movement and speech functions.

Stimulation at 130 Hz resulted in amplification of facial movement as compared to no stimulation for a few motor tasks. However, stimulation at 55 Hz showed greater and more consistent movement amplification across most tasks and sessions, confirming a stronger efficacy of motor thalamus stimulation at lower frequencies (averaged across all motor tasks, range of movement increased by 28.1% at 55 Hz and 19.4% at 130 Hz, Fig. 5A, B).

Similar to the facial expression tasks, while stimulation at 130 Hz and 55 Hz led to comparable values of speech intensity (Fig. 5D), stimulation at 55 Hz produced better articulation. Overall, low-frequency stimulation resulted in an increased perceptual presence of consonants as compared to no stimulation across a larger number of consonants (40% of tested consonants for 55 Hz versus <30% of tested consonants for 130 Hz). Instead, stimulation at 130 Hz resulted in a larger number of consonants for which the perceptual presence was decreased as compared to no stimulation (>36% of tested consonants for 130 Hz versus 17% for stimulation at 55 Hz, Fig. 5C), providing further evidence on the degradative effects of 130 Hz on speech performances. Moreover, tongue and lip strength measured by IOPI exhibited stronger improvement at 55 Hz compared to 130 Hz: average pressure of 12.9 kPa at 55 Hz, which is a 38.3% increase from no stimulation, versus 9.7 kPa at 130 Hz, i.e., 3.6% increase relatively (Fig. 5E).

In conclusion, these preliminary data indicate that stimulation at 55 Hz most effectively potentiates orofacial motor output, mirroring our prior upper-limb results and further reinforcing our proposed mechanisms of frequency-dependent motor thalamus facilitation18.

Low-frequency (50–80 Hz) motor thalamus stimulation results in a clinically significant improvement in speech intelligibility after TBI

Finally, we sought to test whether the observed effects across respiratory, phonatory, and articulatory control achieved with low-frequency motor thalamus stimulation would translate to any clinically significant improvements in speech intelligibility and quality. For this, we recorded TBI01 speaking phoneme-balanced sentences selected from the AIDS with low-frequency stimulation ON and OFF. We then asked certified SLPs (n = 4), blinded to the stimulation conditions, to compare between stimulation ON and OFF and select the condition with the superior performance across various perceptual speech measures40–43. For >63% of the cases, listeners selected the stimulation ON conditions as the more intelligible, natural, less effortful to understand, and with better articulatory precision (Fig. 6A). We then classified the frequencies of descriptive terminologies within the SLP evaluations. These descriptors of speech quality were classified into two categories: positive (e.g., Precise, Concise, Intelligible) and negative (e.g., Distorted, Difficult, Slow) descriptors. As expected, SLPs showed a preference for using more positive descriptors for sentences pronounced when the stimulation was ON, further supporting that low-frequency DBS of the motor thalamus improves speech quality (Fig. 6B).

We also had the opportunity to perform two additional clinical assessments: the 2nd Edition Frenchay Dysarthria Assessment (FDA-2) to assess patterns of oral motor functions, with particular focus on the following subsections: Respiration, Lips, Palate, Laryngeal, and Tongue52, and the single word section of the AIDS53. For the FDA-2, for each subsection, the SLP (n = 1) provided a score from A–E, representative of normal function to no function, respectively. Importantly, when scored on a 9-point scale with “E” corresponding to 1 and “A” corresponding to 9 across all subsections, the low-frequency stimulation ON condition resulted in a 10-point improvement as compared to stimulation OFF (i.e., from 88 to 98/234 for stimulation OFF and ON respectively, where 234 represents normal functions over all subsections, Supplementary Fig. 6). Specifically, we found that low-frequency motor thalamus stimulation immediately improved reflexes during swallowing (from severe to moderate impairments), paralleling the improvements observed in the videofluoroscopic swallow study (Fig. 6C). Additionally, stimulation improved lip control at rest and during the exaggerated smile (i.e., spread) from severe to mild impairments, suggesting that stimulation reduced facial paresis and paralleled effects captured with the facial expression tasks. Finally, stimulation improved bilateral movements of the palate and elevation as reported by the SLP, but slightly reduced palate maintenance (mild to moderate impairments). For the AIDS, TBI01 was instructed to read aloud words. The audio recordings were then shared with a blinded SLP (n = 1) who was tasked with identifying the word pronounced from a multiple choice of 12 words. Without stimulation, TBI01 presented with a profound speech deficit (<26% of speech intelligibility, 26% of words were correctly selected), which immediately improved when DBS was turned ON (8%, 20%, and 16% improvement for session 1, 2, and 3, respectively, Fig. 6D). Importantly, a 7% change in intelligibility is considered a small clinically significant change; whereas a 15% change in intelligibility is considered a large clinically significant improvement54.

Collectively, these findings demonstrate the feasibility of low-frequency motor thalamus stimulation to enhance speech quality and intelligibility in a participant with chronic profound motor speech deficits.

Discussion

In this study, we report proof-of-concept feasibility that continuous low-frequency (50–80 Hz) DBS targeting the motor thalamus immediately enhances facial and oropharyngeal volitional motor output, leading to clinically significant improvements in speech and swallowing functions in one participant with partial corticobulbar lesions.

Stimulation of the motor thalamus potentiates motor function by selectively augmenting the recruitment of cortico-spinal and cortico-bulbar motor neurons within the primary motor cortex via excitatory synaptic innervations from the VIM/VOP nuclei18. Importantly, due to the short duration of excitatory postsynaptic potentials on the membrane potential (5–20 ms), synaptic-mediated excitatory inputs necessitate a stimulation frequency sufficiently high (>40 Hz)55,56 to induce sustained membrane depolarization, but also sufficiently low (≤130 Hz) to prevent presynaptic attenuation effects that reduce synaptic-mediated excitatory inputs57–59. We have previously extensively shown these mechanisms for arm and hand motor cortex, and here, we demonstrated that a similar mechanism applies to orofacial and pharyngeal motor output. We therefore tested the effects of stimulation frequency on facial movements, swallowing, and speech performances, all of which confirmed that low-frequency stimulation (50–80 Hz) resulted in larger facial movements, more efficient swallowing, and better speech intelligibility/quality as compared to high-frequency stimulation (130 Hz). While our results seem to contradict years of literature reporting on stimulation-induced dysarthria as a side effect of DBS therapy31–34, these adverse effects have been consistently linked with high-frequency stimulation parameters used in clinical settings of 130 Hz or higher. In fact, a few recent studies have explored the use of low-frequency stimulation (e.g., 60 Hz) of the STN to reduce side effects on speech and swallowing60–65. Our results on ET support these previous findings, showing that low-frequency stimulation can significantly mitigate the degradation of speech and support our application of low-frequency motor thalamus stimulation for post-cerebral lesion speech deficits. Yet alternative mechanisms may also contribute to the observed effects of the DBS, including compensation through recruitment of parallel or spared motor pathways. Moreover, while increasing cortical excitability may facilitate residual motor function in disorders characterized by impaired corticobulbar output (e.g., traumatic corticobulbar injury), this approach may not apply to conditions characterized by excessive muscle tone like dystonia or dyskinetic cerebral palsy, where dysarthria and dysphagia arise from fundamentally different network abnormalities66–68. Therefore, future studies are necessary to further elucidate the mechanisms of action and to understand whether this approach could generalize to other neurological disorders.

DBS therapy in patients with ET or Parkinson’s Disease (PD) provides immediate relief from movement disorder symptoms such as tremor, joint stiffness, and rigidity when the device is activated24,69,70. These immediate and robust improvements have been crucial for the acceptance of DBS technology for movement disorders. Therefore, the primary aim of our study was to achieve immediate assistive effects in speech and swallowing upon activating DBS to best facilitate rapid clinical adoption for patients with cerebral lesions. Although our small sample size limits conclusions regarding safety and efficacy, our report offers evidence of significant improvements in dysarthria and dysphagia in a patient with TBI and diffuse axonal injury. In this context, recent clinical trials have shown that the surgical procedure for DBS implantation does not pose significant barriers to its clinical application for cerebral lesions71,72, further supporting the feasibility of our approach.

It is important to acknowledge that our study is not a clinical trial. This is a proof-of-concept of the effects of motor thalamus stimulation on speech motor functions after corticobulbar lesions caused by traumatic brain injury. For this reason, we could not implant participants specifically for this study, but all participants recruited within this study received implantation in the VIM/VOP nuclei of the motor thalamus for alternative clinically indications of tremor management. Importantly, testing completed in participants with ET was adopted as a model for investigating corticothalamic circuitry in humans with intact CST and CBT and speech motor functions. While TBI01 received implantation of DBS for off-label indication of tremors, his tremor developed specifically as a result of his brain injury, distinct from the conventional etiology of ET patients. This unique lesion profile provided the opportunity to evaluate our proposed mechanism, i.e., low-frequency motor thalamus stimulation enhances motor cortex excitability and potentiates residual corticobulbar output. The resulting improvements in his speech and swallowing functions support this mechanistic interpretation. Given TBI01’s distinct etiology and lesion profile compared to intact ET subjects, assessments of speech were adapted and personalized to best assess his deficits. Importantly, stimulation parameters were comparable across participants, and the effects of the stimulation were assessed with quantitative measures and remained consistent across different clinical and behavioral tasks. Moreover, despite the limited sample size of n = 1 participant with speech and swallowing deficits, the significant improvement in swallowing efficiency, speech quality and intelligibility with motor thalamus DBS ON achieved in the setting of profound chronic dysarthria further underscores the potential for more promising recovery for participants with milder impairments. Importantly, the observed amelioration was due to improved production of stop consonants and movement of the tongue, which are the aspects of speech articulation typically most impaired in patients with dysarthria1. In this regard, it is important to highlight that TBI01 was the same participant of our previous work in which we demonstrated that motor thalamus DBS immediately improves arm and hand strength and volitional force control18, suggesting that our approach could be used to simultaneously treat speech and upper-limb deficits. Finally, while the primary data for speech and swallowing dysfunction were derived from one TBI participant, our approach builds on a broader foundation of evidence, including increased face motor cortex excitability in eight neurologically intact participants.

Further studies scaling to a larger cohort with a wider and more heterogeneous spectrum of deficit severity are now needed to validate the observed immediate assistive effects and quantify possible long-term therapeutic effects of stimulation. Indeed, beyond the immediate facilitation observed in our participants, the present findings raise the intriguing possibility that repeated or chronic low-frequency motor thalamus stimulation could engage mechanisms of thalamocortical plasticity. Repeated recruitment of excitatory thalamocortical afferents may promote activity-dependent strengthening of residual corticobulbar projections, potentially enhancing synaptic efficacy and reestablishing functional connectivity over time. Such long-term reorganization has been observed in other DBS paradigms71,72 where stimulation reshapes cortical motor maps and normalizes pathological oscillatory coupling, suggesting that future longitudinal studies should assess whether extended stimulation leads to durable therapeutic gains in speech and swallowing. Moreover, given prior research indicating enhanced outcomes when non-invasive neuromodulation was complemented with speech therapy73–75, it is plausible that motor thalamus DBS could provide a synergistic effect in treating dysarthria and dysphagia when combined with traditional SLP therapy. Refined investigation on the role of stimulation laterality may also be beneficial to understand the necessity of bilateral stimulation. Indeed, while unilateral stimulation exhibited potentiating effects preferentially on contralateral side innervated muscles, bilateral stimulation in TBI01 appeared more effective in enhancing facial movements. These findings suggest that patients with more severe deficits may benefit from bilateral implantation.

In conclusion, speech and swallowing control are a priority for millions of individuals with dysarthria and dysphagia, as underscored by recent advancements in neural engineering76. In this regard, several research teams have developed real-time brain-computer interfaces that detect intended speech from cortical neural signals and output it on a computer screen77–82, building off from decades of brain decoding technologies for upper- and lower-limbs76. Inspired by these efforts, here we engineered a neurotechnology to improve natural speech and swallowing, which patients and SLPs report as a priority83. Given that DBS is an FDA-approved therapy for ET and PD, and the motor thalamus VIM/VOP nuclei are a common clinical target for these pathological conditions24,25, our approach supports further investigation of DBS-based neuromodulation strategies for treating dysphagia and dysarthria in patients with lesions of the cortico-bulbar tracts.

Methods

Ethical considerations

The first sub-study, which included intra-operative procedures in human subjects, was approved by the University of Pittsburgh Institutional Review Board (STUDY21040121, NCT07056361). The second sub-study, which included functional tests in awake human subjects, was approved by the University of Pittsburgh Institutional Review Board (STUDY21100020, NCT07056348). All the recordings were carried out according to CARE guidelines and in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki principles. The participants were informed of the procedure, and they signed an informed consent, which included consent for the use of all data collected during the experiment in scientific publications. Additionally, participants provided written consent to the use of photos, videos, and voice-recordings acquired during the experiments for research purposes and publications. TBI01 was compensated at a rate of $50/hour for their time. TBI01 consented to the publications of Fig. 2, Supplementary Fig. 4 and Supplementary Videos 1, 3.

Human participants

We recruited a total of nine participants for this study, which comprised two sub-studies (see Supplementary Table 1, 2 for clinical details and experiments performed for each participant). Please note that the data comes from the first nine enrolled subjects in our study. Therefore, sex was not considered in the study design nor in the analysis. For the first sub-study, we performed electrophysiological experiments on n = 8 human subjects (S01–S08, 5 males, 3 females, self-reported sex) of age 67 ± 3 (mean ± std) with no evidence of CBT lesions on pre-operative MRI who were undergoing DBS implantation of the motor thalamus to treat medically-intractable asymmetric Essential Tremor (ET) symptoms. In S01–S08, tests were performed using macroelectrode stimulation during DBS implantation with the macro contact positioned in the motor thalamus and specifically between the interface of VIM/VOP nuclei (see Human intraoperative data acquisition and electrophysiology and Supplementary Fig. 1 for details on the stimulation location). Speech and swallowing functions in S01-S08 were intact. S01, S02, and S04 were implanted with Medtronic Sensight leads Percept PC, and after implantation, some speech tests were also performed to assess the effects of stimulation frequency on speech intelligibility (see Functional tests for more details). Stimulation tests were conducted, stimulating all contacts located within the VIM/VOP (i.e., without current steering) to ensure comparability with tests performed in TBI01. In the second sub-study, we had the opportunity to test TBI01, who suffered a traumatic brain injury and had electrodes implanted in the motor thalamus to treat the post-traumatic tremor. Specifically, TBI01 (male in his 40’s) had suffered severe traumatic brain injury resulting in diffuse axonal injury from a motor vehicle accident around 1 year before DBS implantation. Acute imaging immediately following injury identified diffuse axonal injury involving the CST and CBT with bilateral edema of the cerebral peduncles and pons. Consequently, TBI01 suffered from paresis of the right side and left upper extremity muscle weakness and tremor with decreased overall strength, balance, coordination, and endurance. According to SLP evaluations (n = 1), the participant suffered profound motor speech impairment characterized by slow, uncoordinated, and imprecise speech with overall poor comprehensibility in an unknown listeners’ context. The SLP also noted probable dysphonia with reduced vocal intensity and hoarse vocal quality, likely secondary to reported left vocal fold paralysis. Regarding swallowing function, the participant demonstrated oropharyngeal dysphagia characterized by suspected oral phase discoordination and possible airway compromise due to left vocal fold paralysis. The participant was recommended for bilateral DBS electrode implant (Medtronic, Sensight leads Percept PC) in the VIM/VOP nucleus (see Supplementary Fig. 1B for details on the stimulation location) to treat the post-traumatic tremor. Stimulation tests were conducted, stimulating all contacts located within the VIM/VOP (i.e., without current steering) to ensure comparability with the other participants.

Stimulation location

High-resolution T1-weighted anatomical MRI scans were acquired prior to deep-brain stimulation (DBS) implantation, and computed tomography (CT) images (1.25 mm isotropic, GE Medical Systems) were obtained post-implantation. The DBS leads were manually segmented on the CT to generate a binary implant mask. Non-brain tissue was removed from the T1 image using FSL’s brain extraction tool (BET)84. Using the FMRIB’s Linear image registration tool (FLIRT) with spline interpolation and normalized correlation cost, warping fields were found from native space to MNI-152 template space85. The DBS lead masks were normalized to the MNI-152 template using the resulting warping maps from the previous step.

Diffusion MRI and tractography analysis

Diffusion images for TBI01 were acquired on a SIEMENS Prisma Fit scanner using a diffusion sequence (2 mm isotropic resolution, TE/TR = 99.2 ms/2490 ms, 257 diffusion sampling with maximum b-value 4010 s/mm²). The accuracy of b-table orientation was examined by comparing fiber orientations with those of a population-averaged template86. The tensor metrics were calculated using diffusion weighted images (DWI) with a b-value lower than 1750 s/mm².

To quantify the white-matter fibers damage in TBI01, we used an advanced diffusion MRI technique commonly used in patients with white-matter damage that allows for quantification of a decrease in anisotropic diffusion by applying a “tracking-the-difference” paradigm when compared to a normal population template: differential tractography87. Differential tractography was performed by comparing the high-definition fiber tractography (HDFT) of TBI01 against an age-and-sex-matched template constructed from a group of healthy controls of fractional anisotropy (FA). To capture neural injury, we highlighted tracks that displayed a 10% decrease in FA when compared to the template of healthy controls. We then quantified the composition of lesioned tracts based on a population-averaged atlas of human white matter tracts86 for the main fiber tracts: corticospinal tract, corticobulbar tract, and dentato-rubro-thalamic tract.

Motor-evoked potential experiments

In order to demonstrate whether motor thalamus stimulation increases primary motor cortex (M1) excitability and consequently CBT output, we performed an electrophysiological test. Specifically, we stimulated the motor cortex either via direct cortical stimulation (DCS) or using single pulse transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS, MagStim 2002) to generate motor evoked potentials (MEPs) in different facial muscles. We then paired motor cortex stimulation with motor thalamus stimulation and compared the amplitude of the MEPs between the two conditions—with and without motor thalamus stimulation. These experiments parallel previous studies in upper-limb MEPs from our group and others18,44,88. Experiments using DCS were performed during DBS implantation in participants S01-S08. For this, pairs of needle electrodes (Rhythmlink Columbia, SC) were placed subcutaneously in bilateral masseter (MASS), orbicularis oris (ORIS), mylohyoid (MYLO), mentalis (MENT), and cricothyroid (CRICO) muscles to record EMG and evoked MEPs. At the beginning of the surgical procedure, a subdural strip electrode (6-contact platinum subdural electrode, AD-TECH Medical Instrument Corporation, Oak Creek, WI) was placed over the face representation of M1 to perform DCS. The electrode position was optimized using results from DCS-MEPs of the contralateral face muscles. We selected the optimal subdural stimulation contact as the contact that elicited the largest MEPs and recruited the greatest number of contralateral facial and oropharyngeal muscles. DCS of the face representation of M1 was provided using trains of 5 stimulation pulses (0.5 ms) at 400 Hz every two seconds at stimulus intensities up to 15 mA using an intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring system (XLTEK Protektor, Natus Medical, Ontario, Canada). Detailed information on the stimulation parameters used for DCS for each subject is reported in Supplementary Table 1. After implantation of the subdural strip electrode, the surgery continued following standard clinical procedures. Specifically, as part of the standard of care in our institution, DBS was performed using stereotactic techniques and with a robotic-assisted device (ROSA, Zimmer-Biomed, Warsaw, IN, USA). The robot was used to position a platform on which a NeuroFortis head stage (Alpha Omega, Nof HazGilil, Israel) was used to advance up to three microelectrodes, while an Alpha Omega (AO, Nazareth, Israel) Neuro-Omega system was used for thalamic macrostimulation to identify the optimal location for implanting clinical DBS electrodes. Electrophysiology experiments were performed following completion of the clinical procedure by stimulating the macro contact from the microelectrode passing through the VIM/VOP nucleus. Specifically, DCS-MEPs were recorded by stimulating the optimal electrode contact over the face representation of the primary motor cortex (M1), both without and with continuous macrostimulation of the motor thalamus (stimulation from the macro contact of the microelectrode) at 50, 80, 100, and 130 Hz with pulses of 100 us and intensity of 4 mA. Due to limited time available during the surgery for these experiments, only a few sets of stimulation frequencies for the motor thalamus stimulation could be tested per subject (see Supplementary Table 1, for details). Across the eight subjects reported, each frequency was tested in at least two participants.

Experiments using TMS were performed in TBI01, paralleling the intra-operative experiments performed in S01–S08. Specifically, surface EMG was recorded in bilateral masseter muscles. A 70 mm figure-8 coil was held tangentially at 45° AP orientation over the scalp at the optimal position to evoke MEPs in the contralateral masseter. Ten pulses of TMS with a stimulus intensity of 75% of the maximal stimulator output were delivered to elicit contralateral MEP. TMS stimulation was then paired with motor thalamus stimulation at 55 Hz (see Supplementary Table 1 for details on the stimulation parameters). We compared the amplitude of the MEPs between the condition with and without motor thalamus stimulation. All traces were low-pass filtered at 100 Hz to reduce artifacts from the motor thalamus stimulation.

Functional tests

Participants performed a series of functional tests depending on their impairments and the time available. All functional tests were repeated without and with motor thalamus stimulation ON. For each session, the amplitude and frequency of motor thalamus stimulation were selected to be the highest value that the patients could tolerate and that would produce a transient paresthesia in the facial muscles (see Supplementary Tables 2 for details on the tests performed and stimulation parameters for each participant). Tests were initiated only when the participants reported that the paresthesia disappeared. For all tasks, a computer monitor was placed in front of the participants, about 25–30 inches away from their faces, to instruct them on the tasks. Participants were instructed to initiate the trial after a cue that paired a short tonal sound with the appearance of the action prompt on the screen. For tasks with time constraints, participants received feedback on the remaining time during the trial and visual feedback to stop the instructed action after the allotted time. A directional microphone was placed in front of the participant to record both the participant’s speech and the auditory go cue from the monitor without occluding the behavioral task displayed on the monitor. Participants completed all behavioral tasks sitting in a chair. Stimulation ON and OFF conditions were randomized across subjects for S01, S02, S04 and across sessions for TBI01.

Facial tasks

To evaluate the stimulation effect on voluntary facial movement, four facial tasks were designed to evaluate the range of movement and aperture of facial articulators critical to speech and swallowing: 1) protruding tongue in/out; 2) smiling; 3) opening/closing the mouth; 4) puckering the lips. For all tasks, participants were instructed to alternate between a neutral facial expression and the designated facial task as fast and as large as they could within 15 s. No visual feedback was provided to prevent subjects from adjusting their movements between conditions. Video of the facial movements was recorded during the task. TBI01 performed these facial tasks across 3 different sessions.

Single words

We performed tests to measure the effects of stimulation on the four subsystems of speech in TBI01. TBI01 was tasked to repeat single words five times as clearly as possible. We tested words composed of one to three syllables (n = 31 one syllable, n = 18 two syllables, and n = 2 three syllables) and ensured that all American English phonemes were tested in at least one experimental session. See Supplementary Fig. 5 for the full list of tested words and their phoneme composition. TBI01 performed this task across 7 different sessions.

Sentences

We performed tests to measure the effects of stimulation on speech intelligibility and quality in S01, S02, and S04 and in TBI01. Participants were tasked to repeat 5 to 8-word phoneme-balanced sentences selected from the Assessment of Intelligibility of Dysarthric Speech test (AIDS)53 TBI01 performed these tasks across 5 different sessions.

Videofluoroscopic swallow study

Since only TBI01 presented swallowing deficits, a videofluoroscopic swallow study was performed exclusively with this participant. At the time of the test, TBI01 was given the clinical safety recommendation of International Dysphagia Diet Standardization Initiative (IDDSI) Level-7 consistency for food and Level-2 consistency for liquids. Adhering to these reports, the videofluoroscopic swallow study46 (VFSS) consisted of challenging the patient with the following consistencies under both stimulation conditions (ON and OFF) while seated upright, framed by radiography in a lateral plane: 1) 5 mL IDDSI Level-2 liquid; 2) 10 mL IDDSI Level-2 liquid; 3) unmeasured free straw sip of IDDSI Level-2 liquid; 4) 5 mL of IDDSI Level-4 semi-solid; 5) IDDSI Level-7 solid. By standard protocol, trials consisting of swallowing varying volumes of IDDSI Level-2 liquids (5 ml, 10 ml, and unmeasured free straw sip) were repeated twice. However, one trial of the unmeasured free straw sip was excluded from analysis due to failure to capture all complete phases of swallowing. Additionally, the trial of IDDSI Level-7 solid was excluded because the participant misunderstood the instructions and attempted to swallow the solid without mastication. Given the research premise of this assessment, the excluded trials were not repeated to minimize radiation exposure. Notably, the participant was instructed to clear the bolus in a single swallow as much as possible. Therefore, any portion of the bolus remaining after the first swallow was evaluated as premature spillage of the bolus for the subsequent swallow (or residue) as per the DIGEST protocol. VFSS was acquired for each swallow at a frequency of 30 frames per second.

Iowa Oral Performance Instrument (IOPI) Assessment47: The IOPI is a clinically validated handheld device applicable for either assessing or training tongue and lip strength. The anterior tongue strength is the maximum pressure generated by the tongue pressing a standard-sized air-filled bulb against the hard palate. To start, the air-filled bulb is placed longitudinally on the hard palate, posterior to the alveolar ridge. Then, TBI01 was instructed to close their lips and press on the bulb as hard as possible using the front of their tongue for 2 s. To measure lip strength, the bulb was placed between TBI01’s lips either to the left or the right corner of his mouth with instructions to seal his lips as hard as possible for 2 s. Both TBI01 and the SLP operating the IOPI were blinded to the stimulation condition. Stimulation conditions were randomized, collecting a total of 3 trials for each condition across the 3 systems assessed. Pressure measurements are recorded in kilopascals (kPa).

Effects of stimulation parameters

In order to test the effect of varying frequencies, S01, S02, and S04 performed AIDS without and with motor thalamus stimulation at 57 Hz and 130 Hz in a single session each (see Supplementary Table 2 for details on the stimulation parameters). TBI01 performed facial tasks, the single-word tests, and the IOPI assessment without and with stimulation of the motor thalamus at 55 Hz and 130 Hz (see Supplementary Table 2 for details on the stimulation parameters). Facial tasks and single-word tests were repeated in two different sessions. Importantly, all participants were blinded to the stimulation condition and thus were unable to perceive or distinguish between the two frequencies tested. Additionally, we randomized the order of stimulation conditions and the tasks across the sessions to reduce the effects of fatigue. Movement, speech performances, and strength were then compared between the different stimulation frequencies. For TBI01, we also compared stimulation effects in the facial tasks between bilateral stimulation at 55 Hz and unilateral stimulation (right hemisphere with the more extensive lesion, Supplementary Fig. 1C) at 55 Hz.

Electromyographic recordings and analysis for MEPs

MEP trials were recorded for 100 ms following each DCS stimulation burst at a sampling frequency of 6000 Hz using the XLTEK system. To quantify DCS-MEPs and TMS-MEP recorded from triggered electromyographic activity, we computed the stimulation-triggered averages (EMG from 10 ms to 75 ms after stimulation) and calculated the area-under-the-curve (AUC) for each muscle.

Kinematic recordings and analysis

Kinematics of facial tasks were recorded by a GoPro® Camera and analyzed through DeepLabCut, a neural network for 2D and 3D markerless pose estimation based on transfer learning89. All videos were initially processed using Adobe Premiere Pro to standardize framing and lighting across stimulation conditions. Kinematic facial articulators were manually labeled for a minimum of 40 frames per video clip. The labeled facial structures included jaw, cheek, mouth, upper lip, lower lip, nose, and chin. We employed DeepLabCut and a cloud computing cluster (Google Colab Pro) to train a neural network and extrapolate the articulator positions across all frames. Each dataset for each participant was trained for a minimum of 300,000 iterations. Visual inspection from LT and JH confirmed facial articulators automatically labeled by trained DeepLabCut models. Final x- and y-coordinates of each articulator across all tasks were used for kinematic analysis. For each movement repetition, movement initiation and completion were identified manually from visual inspection of the kinematic traces and videos. For each facial articulator, we calculated amplitude and velocity of movement. Additionally, we quantified mouth aperture by calculating the area between three (for videos capturing side profile) or four (for videos capturing front profile) representative facial articulators. For each trial, we then computed the percentage of change in mouth aperture and the percentage of change in mean velocity relative to the average value in stimulation OFF as: Trialvalue(x)−Meanvalue(OFF)Meanvalue(OFF)x 100, where Trial value (x) corresponds to a trial with either stimulation ON or OFF.

Audio recordings and analysis

All voice recordings were collected using a Sennheiser MKE 600 microphone (Sennheiser electronic SE & Co. KG, Germany) that was then amplified through a M-track Duo (48 kHz, 2-channel USB audio interface with 2 Combo Inputs, Crystal Preamps, and Phantom Power, M-audio, Rhode Island, USA) and finally digitally processed, and recorded using the Ripple Neuro Grapevine and Trellis software at a sampling frequency of 30 kHz. Each audio file was then cut into single words using a semi-automatic MATLAB script that removed pauses and non-task-relevant sounds. The cutting process was verified by both auditory and visual inspection before the cut words were saved as both MATLAB structures and WAV files. To determine the beginning and end of individual phonemes, we used the Montreal Forced Aligner (MFA) to train an acoustic model to align phoneme transcripts to audio files. Specifically, we used the CMU ARPABET library to train the model, as it is a dictionary for North American English90. Models were trained, including all sessions. The final output of the model was a grapheme “TextGrids” compatible with PRAAT91. Phoneme alignment was then inspected and confirmed for each WAV file using a custom PRAAT script91. The final grapheme information was added to the MATLAB structures, storing both single words and their individual phoneme traces. All MATLAB analysis used these structures, and all PRAAT91 analysis used the WAV and TextGrid datasets.

Speech assessment

Due to the profound deficits of TBI01, we always excluded the first trial of every single word. This was to reduce any confounding/misleading effects from initiation variability and errors, including coughing, speaking over the tonal go cue, poor breath support, and whispering the word. Each word within a session was considered as a single word. We calculated several speech metrics to assess the effects of the stimulation on speech production.

Speech intensity

To assess changes in speech intensity with motor thalamus stimulation, we first decomposed the audio signal of every recorded word into a spectrogram. The spectrogram was calculated every 5 ms using a 25 ms Hanning window, and the frequency range was limited to 50–10000 Hz. We calculated the total power in decibels for each time step and then applied a second-order 1D median filter to the trial trace. For each trial, we calculated speech intensity as the median value of the smoothed trace.

Voice breaks

To evaluate the effects of the stimulation on respiratory control, we determined voice breaks for each word individually using PRAAT’s voice report function91. We first calculated a pitch object using a filtered autocorrelation method (recommended for voice pathology) over the entire sound bit with the standard settings save for pitch range, which we adjusted to 75–400 Hz, and the voice threshold, which we reduced to 0.25 to better evaluate irregularities in the speech signal. We then created a PointProcess object to define the pulse train for the signal before running PRAAT’s voice report function91 with its standard settings. Voice breaks were defined as any inter-pulse intervals between voiced frames that lasted longer than 16.67 ms.

Cepstral peak prominence-smoothed

In order to assess the effects of the stimulation on phonatory control, we computed the Cepstral Peak Prominence-smoothed (CPPS) for each unique trial of each word, reflecting the harmonic structure of the speech signal and the amplitude of the cepstral peak92,93. We employed a PRAAT script to calculate the power-cepstrogram from each word’s spectrogram, and subsequently derived the CPPS using the “Get CPPS” function with default settings from Hillenbrand et al., 199649. The CPPS values from all words and sessions were concatenated to assess the percent change in voice quality between stimulation OFF and stimulation ON conditions.

Consonant articulation

To determine whether there was improvement in the articulation of consonants, we perceptually assessed the presence and quality of each consonant. Four researchers (EG, LT, IM, and SN) blinded to both the stimulation condition and session listened to the word while being presented with a transcript of the word and its phonemes. The researcher then classified each phoneme as being 1) present, 2) distorted, or 3) fully absent. Next, we calculated the percentage of present and distorted/absent phonemes for stimulation OFF and stimulation ON for each session by concatenating the phonemes across words. To reduce bias, the listeners had to record both the timing and type of error (distortion/absence) that they observed. An error was only counted if at least two of the listeners had identified the exact same timing and type of error.

Formant frequencies

To determine whether there was improvement in the articulation of vowels, we quantified changes in the formant frequencies of formant F1 and F2. We estimated formant frequencies using linear predictive coding (LPC) coefficients. To build the LPC model, we first preprocessed the audio data. This involved extracting individual vowel phoneme segments from the recorded words and applying an all-pole pre-emphasis filter Pz=1−0.63z−1 to enhance higher frequency components and improve the signal-to-noise ratio. Next, we created a spectrogram using a Hamming window, segmenting the data into 15 ms windows with 80% overlap. We then calculated the LPC coefficients using a model order determined by Fs/1000+2 where Fs is the sampling frequency. We identified the first four formants (F1–F4) as the first four roots of the LPC spectrum that had frequencies greater than 90 Hz and bandwidths less than 400 Hz. All formant frequency calculations were performed in MATLAB. We verified the results by comparing them with formants identified using PRAAT’s default settings, confirming consistency and accuracy. Given that F1 and F2 correspond to vowel openness and tongue position51, respectively, we next assessed how these values change with stimulation. For each session, we identified all unique vowels tested to determine the average F1 and F2 values for each trial. Since formant values are calculated in 15 ms intervals over the duration of the sound, each trial of a phoneme produces multiple formant values. This is important because different words may require different durations for the same vowel, and voice breaks during the trial can lead to inaccurate formant estimates. To address these issues and minimize bias in the formant range, we preprocessed the formant data by smoothing and time-warping it to ensure consistency across trials. We smoothed the first two formant signals using a first-order median filter. For time-warping, we applied spline interpolation to resample the data to 100 points for the entire vowel. We then truncated the data to cover 70% around the center of the vowel (i.e., points 15–85), ensuring that only the most stable portion of the vowel sound was used in calculating the averages for each trial. The magnitude of change was calculated as the percent change from the average formant value in the stimulation OFF condition to the ON condition.

Fatigue

To determine whether stimulation affects the endurance aspect of speech, we timed the duration spent in completing the single-word tasks as a measurement of fatigue. We calculated the difference in duration between the first and last 5 words, adjusted for the number of repetitions and the number of syllables per word. A larger time difference suggested increased fatigue from the speech task.

Analysis of videofluoroscopic swallow study

Penetration Aspiration Scale (PAS) and Digesting Image Grade of Swallowing Toxicity (DIGEST) scores were administered to interpret VFSS data by two independent SLPs, blinded to stimulation condition. Importantly, since DIGEST scoring is only evaluated from the first swallow, spillage of bolus remaining after the initial swallow was considered residue even if remnants were cleared by consequent swallows. The quadratic weighted Cohen’s kappa was calculated to assess interrater reliability between the two SLPs scoring for both the PAS and DIGEST. We constructed a confusion matrix consisting of both SLPs' ratings, from which we computed the quadratic weights to create an observed proportion matrix representative of interrater discrepancies. An expected agreement matrix was then computed assuming both SLPs rated randomly. We then computed the weighted discrepancies, kappa (k), between observed and expected matrices. k-value should be interpreted as follows: < 0.20 as poor agreement, 0.20–0.40 as fair agreement, 0.41–0.60 as moderate agreement, 0.61–0.80 as good agreement, and > 0.80 as very good agreement94,95.

In addition to the SLP evaluation of the videos, we performed a quantitative analysis of the swallow duration and amount of premature spillage of the bolus for the subsequent swallow (or residue). Specifically, swallow duration was calculated by the number of frames in the radiology video report from the first observation of modified barium within the oral cavity to the end of the swallow for all trials. Behavioral signs indicative of swallowing completion included clearance of barium through the upper esophageal sphincter and relaxation of the sphincter to its resting state. The Area of Residue was quantified through ImageJ. Because the instructions provided to the subject were to clear the bolus in a single swallow per the DIGEST protocol, the area of residue was calculated after the first swallow. Specifically, one representative frame capture for each phase-matched trial with the maximal visible modified barium bolus was manually labeled as a Region of Interest (ROI) from the videofluoroscopic swallow radiology video report. The total area of ROIs was calculated in Pixel2 and then normalized to the pixel area of the entire frame capture.

Statistical procedures

All statistical comparisons of means presented in this manuscript were performed using the bootstrap method, a non-parametric approach that makes no distributional assumptions on the observed data. Instead, bootstrapping uses resampling to construct empirical confidence intervals for quantities of interest. For each comparison, we construct bootstrap samples by drawing a sample with replacement from observed measurements, while preserving the number of measurements in each condition. We construct 10,000 bootstrap samples and, for each, calculate the difference in means of the resampled data. We employed two-tailed bootstrapping with alphas of 0.05 (95% confidence interval), 0.01 (99% confidence interval), or 0.001 (99.9% confidence interval). The null hypothesis of no difference in the mean was rejected if 0 was not included in the confidence interval of the corresponding alpha value. If more than one comparison was being performed at once, we used a Bonferroni correction by dividing the alpha value by the number of pairwise comparisons being performed. Nonparametric paired t-tests were used specifically for the analysis of duration, residue area of swallowing function, and fatigue of speech function. For the videofluoroscopic swallowing study, due to the varying volume and consistency of content swallowed across each trial (5 ml, 10 ml, free sip of IDDSI Level-2 liquid, and IDDSI Level-4 semi-solid), swallowing time and residue also varied for each trial. Therefore, a comparison between stimulation conditions ON/OFF was paired across trials where the same content was swallowed. Similarly, fatigue analysis evaluates the time duration spent repeating the first and last five words tested within each session. Since the length of the words tested for each session was distinct, the time necessary to complete repetitions of the first and last five words varies. Hence, to evaluate the stimulation effect on speech fatigue, duration was paired within each session where the words tested were consistent between stimulation ON/OFF conditions.
