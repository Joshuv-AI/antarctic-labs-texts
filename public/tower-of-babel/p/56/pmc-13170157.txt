Efficacy and safety of dexmedetomidine in orthognathic surgery: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Ahmad M. Molhem, Hanan M. Qasem, Abdullah Yousef Aldalati, Qussai R. Abuelsamen, Haya B. Abukharmh, Tamara M. Molhem, Rania Anwar Ayasrah, Heider Sultan Sheyyab, Mariam Kawafha, Mohammed Al-mahdi Al-kurdi, Alhussein Hasanat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionOrthognathic surgery improves dentofacial function and aesthetics, but it’s usually associated with postoperative pain, hemodynamic instability and postoperative nausea/vomiting. Dexmedetomidine, a hypotensive agent, is known for its sedative and analgesic properties in various surgical procedures. This study aims to evaluate its efficacy and safety in orthognathic surgery.MethodsWe followed PRISMA guidelines throughout the entire process. We searched the following medical databases: PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, Web of Science and Scopus. Randomized controlled trials and cohorts on dexmedetomidine in orthognathic surgery were screened and retrieved according to predefined criteria. Primary outcomes include blood loss, anesthesia time, operation time, MAP (mean arterial pressure) and heart rate. R studio software was employed for all analyses indicated.ResultsEight studies, including 1,432 participants, were included in the review, with 7 of them contributing to the analysis. Compared to controls, our meta-analysis revealed that patients on dexmedetomidine showed significantly shorter anesthesia time (MD: -40.75, 95%CI [-74.21; -7.29], P = 0.03) and significantly lower heart rate (MD: -12.99, 95%CI [-23.23; -2.75], P = 0.03). A non-significant reduction in blood loss (MD: -119.13, 95%CI [-333.17; 94.92], P = 0.21) and MAP (MD: -2.47, 95%CI [-8.26; 3.31], P = 0.27) was also observed.ConclusionsOur results support the clinical utility of dexmedetomidine in patients undergoing orthognathic surgery. It significantly shortens anesthesia duration and reduces heart rate, with a statistically nonsignificant trend toward reduced intraoperative blood loss.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08177-x.

Introduction

Orthognathic surgery is performed to improve facial symmetry, restore balance, and achieve proper dental occlusion (Class I) in patients with facial deformities. These aesthetic and physiological benefits are achieved by accurately repositioning the maxilla, mandible, and chin [1, 2]. However, the surgical site of the oral and maxillofacial region is highly vascularized and, thus, is frequently associated with excessive bleeding and restricted visibility during maxillary osteotomy. Blood loss, nerve injury, malocclusion, and infection are common complications of orthognathic surgery, significantly influencing postoperative morbidity and mortality [3, 4].

To minimize the need for blood transfusions and reduce associated risks, hypotensive anesthesia is commonly used [5, 6]. Deliberate hypotensive anesthesia (DHA) aims to lower the mean arterial pressure (MAP) of the patient by at least 30% from baseline, with a minimum of 50-60mmHg. The MAP is adjusted according to the patient’s preoperative blood pressure (BP), age, and medical history [5, 7, 8].

Common agents used to induce deliberate hypotension include volatile anesthetics, opioid analgesics (e.g., Remifentanil), direct-acting vasodilators, autonomic ganglion-blockers, beta-blockers (e.g., Esmolol, Labetalol), calcium channel blockers (e.g., Nicardipine), and alpha-2 adrenergic agonists (e.g., Clonidine, Dexmedetomidine). Studies have shown that these agents produce effective and comparable outcomes in DHA, though each has their own advantages and disadvantages [8, 9].

Dexmedetomidine is a selective α-2 adrenoreceptor agonist known for its rapid-acting sedative, analgesic, and opioid-sparing effects. It works by inhibiting norepinephrine release, which lowers blood pressure (BP) and heart rate (HR) through sympatholytic action. Dexmedetomidine’s minimal risk of respiratory depression and short duration of action have made it a practical option to use for DHA during surgical procedures [10–12].

While dexmedetomidine has shown promise in deliberate hypotensive anesthesia (DHA), conclusive evidence of its clinical efficacy in patients undergoing orthognathic surgery remains lacking [13].

This systematic review and meta-analysis aims to evaluate the efficacy of dexmedetomidine in orthognathic surgery through a stratified approach. Specifically, we sought to determine its impact on intraoperative blood loss and hemodynamic stability when used as either a sole hypotensive agent or an adjuvant add-on therapy. To address clinical and methodological heterogeneity, results are stratified by study design and intervention protocol.

Materials and methods

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions and reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [14, 15]. No ethical permission was indicated, as this paper relies exclusively on previously published articles. This systematic review and meta-analysis were registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under the registration number CRD420251079781.

Data sources and search strategy

Studies reporting the efficacy of dexmedetomidine in producing controlled hypotension in patients who underwent orthognathic surgery were searched extensively by two independent authors (A.M. and H.Q.). The literature review was conducted across major electronic databases: PubMed, Scopus, Cochrane, Web of Science (WOS), and Embase covering a time frame until February 2025. Details of the search strategy used are provided in Table S1. Furthermore, we assessed the reference lists of all included studies for any eligible studies possibly missed.

Eligibility criteria

We included both randomized clinical trials (RCTs) and observational studies. The eligibility criteria were constructed via the PICOS framework: Population: patients undergoing orthognathic surgery. Intervention: dexmedetomidine (administered either as a sole hypotensive agent or as an add-on adjuvant therapy). Comparison: any comparator group, including other sedative agents, specific hypotensive agents (e.g., nitroglycerin, clonidine), placebo, or routine standard anesthetic management. Outcomes: intraoperative blood loss, operation time, anesthesia time, postoperative recovery time, and incidence of adverse events.

We excluded review articles, meta-analyses, editorials, conference papers, gray literature, books, case reports, guidelines, animal trials, in vitro studies, conference abstracts and studies in languages other than English.

Study selection

Two investigators (A.D. and Q.S.) conducted the selection process in two stages while being blinded to each other’s work. The first stage was carried out through the Rayan website, which involved preliminary title and abstract screening of the studies collected through the predefined search strategy. Studies that passed the first stage were retrieved and underwent further full-text screening to ensure relevance. In cases of disagreement regarding the papers’ eligibility, a third author was consulted.

Data extraction

Data extraction was conducted using a predetermined data sheet. The extracted data included the following baseline characteristics: first author, publication year, country, study type, number of participants, mean age of patients, disease manifestation, disease duration, study aim, and conclusion. A separate sheet was prepared for efficacy outcomes such as intraoperative blood loss, operation time, anesthesia time, postoperative recovery time and incidence of postoperative adverse events. Two investigators (A.M. and H.S.) independently gathered the relevant data, which were then verified by a third author.

Primary outcomes were intraoperative blood loss, operation time, anesthesia time, MAP, and HR. Secondary outcomes included opioid consumption (fentanyl), and the incidence of adverse events (postoperative nausea/vomiting, bradycardia, hypotension).

For multi-arm trials involving a shared intervention group and multiple control groups (e.g., Shin et al., 2013), data from the control arms were combined (pooled means and standard deviations) to create a single pair-wise comparison, preventing unit-of-analysis errors associated with double-counting the intervention group.

Similarly, for studies reporting repeated measures on the same participants at different timepoints (e.g., hemodynamic measurements at Le Fort I and BSSRO incisions in Sumphaongern et al.), values were averaged across timepoints to generate a single composite mean for analysis, ensuring statistical independence.

Risk of bias and certainty of evidence

Two investigators (A.M. and H.S.) independently assessed the quality of the included studies. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion with a third author. The Cochrane risk of bias (ROB-2) tool was used for RCTs [16], whereas the National Institutes of Health (NIH) tool was used for observational studies [17]. Each study was categorized according to the ROB-2 tool as follows: (1) High risk of bias, (2) Moderate risk of bias, or (3) Low risk of bias. The evaluated aspects included (1) randomization process, (2) deviations from the planned interventions, (3) missing outcome data, (4) measurement of outcomes and (5) selection of reported outcomes. For observational studies, and in accordance with NIH guidance, studies scoring 8 points or higher were considered good quality, whereas studies scoring below 8 points were considered fair quality.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R Studio (Version 2026.01.0-392) with the “meta” and “metafor” packages. To account for the potential instability of variance estimation due to the small number of included studies, we utilized the Hartung-Knapp (HK) random-effects model for all analyses. The random-effects model was selected to estimate the mean distribution of effects across clinically heterogeneous conditions (differing study designs and comparator types) rather than assuming a single fixed effect. Binary outcomes were expressed as risk ratios (RRs), and continuous outcomes as mean differences (MDs) with 95% CIs. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. We evaluated heterogeneity among studies through Higgins’ I² statistic. A leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was employed for studies with heterogeneity values greater than 50%.

To address clinical and methodological heterogeneity, subgroup analyses were performed based on: (1) the role of dexmedetomidine (sole hypotensive agent vs. add-on adjuvant), (2) the study design (randomized controlled trials vs. observational studies), and (3) the type of control treatment (hypotensive agent vs. sedative agent). These stratifications were applied to primary efficacy outcomes (blood loss, anesthesia time, operation time, MAP, and HR) to elucidate source-specific effects. We assessed publication bias across studies, by DOI plot and LFK index model to analyze the relationship between effect size and standard error [18].

Results

Search results

Our search yielded 376 relevant studies. After removing 158 duplicates, 218 records underwent title and abstract screening. This process resulted in 15 reports being included in a subsequent full-text review. All 15 full texts were retrieved and assessed, resulting in the exclusion of seven papers for the following reasons: abstract-only publication (n = 2), non-English language (n = 2), incorrect outcomes (n = 1), and incorrect intervention (n = 2). Finally, 8 studies were included in the systematic review and the meta-analysis (Fig. 1).

Characteristics of the included studies

The review included 8 studies: 6 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and 2 cohort studies, comprising 1,432 participants [8–10, 19–23]. Sample sizes varied across studies, ranging from small controlled trials of 15–36 patients per arm to a large cohort of 1,080 individuals. Across all studies, 704 participants received dexmedetomidine and 728 served as controls. Reported mean ages were primarily in the early to mid-twenties, with minor variation between treatment groups. When available, Body Mass Index (BMI) values fell within a narrow range of approximately 20–25 kg/m². All studies evaluated dexmedetomidine either as a primary hypotensive agent or as an adjunct during orthognathic surgery, with comparators including nitroglycerin, clonidine, nicardipine, remifentanil, and normal saline. Quality assessment

The ROB-2 tool was used for RCTs. Six studies were considered to have a low risk of bias, indicating high quality. However, one study was considered at high risk of bias due to the reported outcomes (Figure S1).

The NIH tool was used for cohort studies. The two studies were considered to have good quality. Table S2.

Meta-analysis

Blood Loss (ml)

The clinical findings regarding blood loss varied across studies. When comparing dexmedetomidine to standard care controls, significant reductions were observed, for example, Sumphaongern et al. [8] reported a dramatic reduction of over 50% in the dexmedetomidine group (401.67 mL vs. 898.33 mL), Hsiao et al. [9] found significantly less blood loss (476 mL vs. 693 mL), and Jin et al. [10] showed reduced blood loss (670.83 mL vs. 732.31 mL). However, when dexmedetomidine was compared to active hypotensive agents, differences were not significant as in Shin et al. [23] that observed comparable blood loss between dexmedetomidine (620.0 mL) and control (742.9 mL), Goswami et al. [19] reported no significant difference between dexmedetomidine (316.66 mL) and control (263.33 mL), and Rummasak et al. [22] found similar blood loss between dexmedetomidine (785.00 mL) and control (695.00 mL).

Six studies reported the mean blood loss. Our analysis revealed that patients receiving dexmedetomidine were associated with a non-significant reduction in blood loss (MD: -119.13, 95% CI [-333.17; 94.92], P = 0.21) compared with controls, with high heterogeneity (I² = 83.4%) (Fig. 2).

A leave-one-out meta-analysis showed that no single study substantially resolved the heterogeneity; omitting Sumphaongern et al. yielded the most notable shift (MD: -55.38, 95% CI [-195.01; 84.25]). The DOI plot showed an LFK index of -1.94, indicating minor asymmetry and a low possibility of publication bias. (Figure S3)

Subgroup analysis was performed according to the type of control treatment. Dexmedetomidine was associated with a non-significant reduction in blood loss compared with hypotensive agents (MD: -127.48, 95% CI [-549.41; 294.45], P = 0.41) (Fig. 3a).

Subgroup analysis was performed according to whether dexmedetomidine was a sole or add-on intervention. Sole dexmedetomidine showed no difference in blood loss (MD: -5.63, 95% CI [-287.37; 276.11], P = 0.94). Add-on dexmedetomidine also showed no significant reduction in blood loss (MD: -242.99, 95% CI [-786.79; 300.82], P = 0.19)(Fig. 3b).

Additionally, subgroup analysis was performed by study design. Dexmedetomidine was associated with a non-significant reduction in blood loss in RCTs (MD: -115.02, 95% CI [-533.05; 303.00], P = 0.45). Similarly, dexmedetomidine demonstrated a non-significant reduction in blood loss in observational studies (MD: -117.99, 95% CI [-1068.42; 832.44], P = 0.36) (Fig. 3c).

Anesthesia time (min)

The clinical findings regarding anesthesia time varied across studies. Sumphaongern et al. [8] reported a significant reduction in the dexmedetomidine group (282.20 min vs. 336.90 min), while Hsiao et al. [9] observed a reduction that did not reach statistical significance (703 min vs. 758 min). Two studies found no significant differences: Rummasak et al. [22] reported similar anesthesia times between dexmedetomidine (270.85 min) and control (277.00 min), and Shin et al. [23] showed comparable times between dexmedetomidine (251.0 min), remifentanil (299.2 min), and nicardipine (288.1 min).

Four studies reported the mean anesthesia time. Our analysis revealed that patients receiving dexmedetomidine had significantly shorter anesthesia time (MD: -40.75, 95% CI [-74.21; -7.29], P = 0.03) than controls, with low heterogeneity (I² = 15.7%). (Fig. 4)

Similarly, dexmedetomidine was associated with a significant reduction in anesthesia time in observational studies (MD: -55.00, 95% CI [-108.32; -1.68], P = 0.04) (Fig. 5c).

Operation time (min)

MAP

The MAP varied depending on whether dexmedetomidine was compared to active hypotensive agents or standard care controls. When compared to active agents, dexmedetomidine showed comparable efficacy. Shin et al. [23] found no significant differences between dexmedetomidine (61.3 mmHg), nicardipine (60.7 mmHg), and remifentanil (61.0 mmHg). In contrast, when compared to standard care or placebo controls, results were mixed. Sumphaongern et al. [8] demonstrated significantly lower MAP with dexmedetomidine (63.4 mmHg vs. 72.6 mmHg, P < 0.001), Jin et al. [10] showed a significant reduction at initial incision (60.2 mmHg vs. 62.5 mmHg, P < 0.001), while Hsiao et al. [9] observed numerically lower but not statistically significant MAP (67.2 mmHg vs. 69.5 mmHg, P = 0.099).

Four studies reported the MAP. Our analysis showed that patients receiving dexmedetomidine were associated with a non-significant reduction in MAP (MD: -2.47, 95% CI [-8.26; 3.31], P = 0.27) compared with controls, with high heterogeneity (I² = 87.2%). (Figure S7)

A leave-one-out meta-analysis showed that the result remained non-significant regardless of which study was omitted; omitting Sumphaongern et al. produced the most stable estimate (MD: -0.64, 95% CI [-3.39; 2.10]). (Figure S8)

Subgroup analysis was performed according to the type of control treatment. Dexmedetomidine was associated with a non-significant reduction in MAP compared with hypotensive agents (MD: -3.60, 95% CI [-58.19; 50.99], P = 0.56). (Figure S9)

Sole dexmedetomidine demonstrated no difference in MAP (MD: 0.45, 95% CI [-1.16; 2.06]). Add-on dexmedetomidine showed a non-significant reduction in MAP (MD: -3.52, 95% CI [-12.81; 5.77], P = 0.24). Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis provides important evidence and a valuable starting point on the clinical use of dexmedetomidine in orthognathic surgery. Findings from six randomized controlled RCTs and two cohort studies, showed that anesthesia time was significantly shorter in dexmedetomidine groups (MD: -40.75 min, 95%CI [-74.21; -7.29], P = 0.03) and heart rate was significantly reduced (MD: -12.99 bpm, 95%CI [-23.23; -2.75], P = 0.03). Our results showed a clinically meaningful but statistically non-significant trend toward reduced intraoperative blood loss (MD: -119.13 mL, P = 0.21) and reduced MAP (MD: -2.47 mmHg, P = 0.27) was also observed. Postoperatively, dexmedetomidine was associated with non-significant trends toward fewer nausea/vomiting events and reduced opioid requirements, though with a higher incidence of manageable bradycardia.

The overall pooled analysis showed a mean reduction in blood loss of 119.13 mL, though this did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.21). The clinical meaningfulness of this absolute reduction must be interpreted within the context of modern orthognathic practice, where baseline blood loss is often mitigated by standard hypotensive techniques and antifibrinolytics. Notably, included studies such as Hsiao et al. and Sumphaongern et al. administered tranexamic acid (TXA) to both study arms; despite this, the dexmedetomidine groups in these trials still exhibited reduced blood loss compared to controls. However, given that the mean blood loss in control groups across several included studies was often below the threshold typically requiring transfusion (e.g., 693 mL in Hsiao et al. and 898 mL in Sumphaongern et al.), a reduction of 119 mL may not drastically alter transfusion requirements for the average patient. Instead, it is likely that this reduction might be more usefully driven by a desire to optimize the quality of the surgical field.

However, the subgroup analysis adds important nuance, that when compared directly to active hypotensive agents like clonidine as in Goswami et al. [19] or nitroglycerin as in Rummasak et al. [22], dexmedetomidine showed no statistical superiority (MD: -127.48 mL). This shows that dexmedetomidine is as effective as other agents in achieving a bloodless field. Conversely, in studies like Hsiao et al. [9] and Sumphaongern et al. [8], where the control group received standard care or placebo, dexmedetomidine demonstrated more pronounced clinical benefits. Thus, its utility is most evident as an upgrade to standard anesthetic protocols rather than a replacement for other effective hypotensive drugs.

The improved surgical field visibility reported in several included studies further supports dexmedetomidine’s utility. For instance, dexmedetomidine provided better surgical field quality and surgeon satisfaction compared to clonidine, though the difference was not statistically significant [19]. This is clinically relevant, as a clearer surgical field can reduce operative complexity and potential complications, such as nerve injury or prolonged surgery. The vasoconstrictive properties of dexmedetomidine may further contribute to this benefit by reducing local blood flow without compromising vital organ perfusion [24].

Dexmedetomidine demonstrated a statistically significant reduction in HR (MD: -12.99 bpm, 95% CI: -23.23, -2.75; P = 0.03), while MAP showed a non-significant trend toward reduction (MD: -2.47 mmHg, 95% CI: -8.26, 3.31; P = 0.27).P = 0.03) and − 13.48 bpm (95% CI: -23.05, -3.9; P = 0.03). The pooled MD for MAP was − 2.47 mmHg (95% CI: -8.26, 3.31; P = 0.27), which did not reach statistical significance. These findings are consistent with the mechanism of action of dexmedetomidine as an α-2 adrenergic agonist, which attenuates sympathetic outflow and may promote hemodynamic stability [25].

Additionally, these finding aligns with the mechanistic insights provided by Shin et al. [23], who utilized Heart Rate Variability (HRV) analysis to compare dexmedetomidine against nicardipine. They demonstrated that while vasodilators like nicardipine induce sympathetic overstimulation and reflex tachycardia, dexmedetomidine preserves autonomic balance through central sympatholysis. This absence of reflex tachycardia is clinically advantageous in orthognathic surgery, as it maintains a lower cardiac output, which Jin et al. [10] and Hsiao et al. [9] cite as a primary driver for the observed reduction in surgical site bleeding.

Notably, the reduction in HR may indirectly contribute to decreased blood loss by lowering cardiac output [26]. The meta-analysis also revealed a significant reduction in anesthesia time (MD: -40.75 min; 95% CI: -74.21 to -7.29; P = 0.03), although operation time did not differ significantly between groups. This discrepancy suggests that, although dexmedetomidine enhances anesthetic efficiency, potentially by reducing the need for additional hypotensive or analgesic agents, its impact on surgical duration may be limited by other factors, such as procedural complexity or surgeon experience [27]. This outcome must be interpreted with caution as anesthesia time is highly dependent on non-pharmacological factors, including institutional extubation criteria, emergence protocols, and workforce logistics. Therefore, the observed reduction in time may reflect protocol differences across studies like Hsiao et al. [20] and Sumphaongern et al. [23], rather than a direct pharmacological benefit of dexmedetomidine facilitating faster emergence.

The postoperative benefits of dexmedetomidine were evident in several domains.

Patients receiving dexmedetomidine experienced fewer episodes of nausea and vomiting. The lower incidence of respiratory depression and delirium in dexmedetomidine groups further underscores its safety advantage over traditional sedatives like midazolam. These outcomes are particularly relevant in orthognathic surgery, where PONV and agitation can complicate recovery [28]. This benefit is likely multifactorial. As observed by Labafchi et al. [21], the opioid-sparing effect of dexmedetomidine directly reduces the emetogenic load on the patient. Furthermore, Rummasak et al. [22] reported significantly lower intraoperative fentanyl requirements in the dexmedetomidine group compared to the nitroglycerin group. While our pooled analysis indicated shorter anesthesia times, this must be balanced against the findings of Ham et al. [20], who noted that despite smoother emergence profiles with less coughing, single-dose dexmedetomidine could delay eye-opening due to residual sedation. Clinicians must therefore titrate the cessation of infusion carefully to balance smooth emergence with timely recovery.

However, the hemodynamic stability provided by dexmedetomidine involves a trade-off. Our meta-analysis indicates a higher incidence of bradycardia, consistent with the drug’s sympatholytic mechanism. While Hsiao et al. [9] and Sumphaongern et al. [8] reported no severe bradycardia requiring intervention in their cohorts, Shin et al. [23] noted significant heart rate reductions compared to nicardipine controls. Clinicians must weigh this risk against the benefits of a bloodless field, particularly in patients with pre-existing conduction abnormalities.

Based on our findings, dexmedetomidine holds significant promise for integration into multimodal anesthetic strategies for orthognathic surgery. Its ability to shorten anesthesia time and reduce heart rate, alongside a consistent, though non-significant, trend toward reduced blood loss, coupled with opioid-sparing effects noted in studies like Labafchi et al. [21] and Sumphaongern et al. [8], supports its utility in ‘fast-track’ or Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) protocols.

Clinically, anesthesiologists should consider dexmedetomidine, particularly for patients where reducing opioid consumption and postoperative nausea is a priority, with no bradycardia-related contraindications.

Future research should shift from comparing dexmedetomidine with placebo or disparate “routine care” controls to designing rigorous, head-to-head randomized trials comparing specific, standardized hypotensive agents (e.g., remifentanil vs. dexmedetomidine). Furthermore, studies focusing on establishing the optimal dosing regimen, specifically comparing the safety profile of sole agent versus add-on protocols, are necessary to reduce the hemodynamic risks identified in this meta-analysis.

Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, there is significant clinical heterogeneity regarding the comparison groups. The included studies compared dexmedetomidine against a diverse array of interventions representing local institutional “gold standards” including nitroglycerin [22], clonidine [19], nicardipine [23], and placebo/standard care [8]. We proceeded with the meta-analysis despite this variability to evaluate the efficacy of dexmedetomidine against the aggregate of traditional anesthetic management strategies.

This diversity reflects the fact that dexmedetomidine is a relatively recent introduction to this specific surgical field, and a single unified comparator has not yet been established. Consequently, our results don’t represent a head-to-head comparison with a single standard but rather a comparison to the heterogeneous routine practice baseline. This analysis shows that.

dexmedetomidine with active hypotensive agents, revealed no significant advantage for dexmedetomidine in terms of blood loss, such as nitroglycerin in Rummasak et al. [21], clonidine in Goswami et al. [18], and nicardipine in Shin et al. [22]. Therefore, the above evidence proves the efficacy of dexmedetomidine as an alternative similar agent not superior in decreasing blood loss.

Second, the exclusion of studies published in languages other than English may have caused language bias. We included studies published in English to ensure strict accuracy of data extraction by the authors who have no formal competence in other languages, and consequently may have missed data from studies not published in English.

In addition, the inclusion of retrospective cohort studies, such as Hsiao et al. [20] and Jin et al. [9], presents the risk of selection bias and confounders that is inherent to a non-randomized design. For example, blood loss and transfusion, the clinical decision in particular, could be confused by unobserved variables, such as surgeon preference, case complexity, institutional differences in triggering transfusion. By contrast, RCTs control for these very rigorously.

Consequently, the pooled estimated effect from observational data requires a greater level of caution than that from randomized data. Additionally, the sample size was relatively small. More large Multicenter randomized controlled trials (RCTs) with uniform doses and outcomes need to be performed to validate these findings. Additionally, the role of dexmedetmidine in combination with other agents, such as tranexamic acid, warrants further exploration to optimize blood conservation strategies.

Additionally, the evidence base is geographically restricted, as every eligible study was performed in Asian countries, specifically in Taiwan, Thailand, India, China, Iran, and Korea. This regional concentration makes a challenge to the generalizability of our findings due to two primary factors: pharmacogenetics and regional healthcare logistics. First, regarding pharmacokinetics, Jin et al. [10] highlight that dexmedetomidine is metabolized via the hepatic cytochrome P450 system. Genetic polymorphisms in these enzymes vary across ethnicities and may influence drug clearance, potentially altering the optimal dosing regimens required to achieve the hemodynamic effects observed in this meta-analysis compared to non-Asian populations [29]. Second, specific institutional constraints within the included studies may not reflect global standards of care. For instance, Hsiao et al. [9] noted that dexmedetomidine was a ‘self-paid’ medication at their institution, which would introduce potential socioeconomic selection bias. Furthermore, Sumphaongern et al. [8] reported that their facility lacked an on-site blood bank which required a more careful transfusion trigger because obtaining blood products took significantly longer. Clearly, logistical factors directly influence outcomes like blood transfusion rates and anesthetic choice, suggesting that our pooled estimates must be interpreted with caution when applied to settings with different resource availability or insurance coverage. Finally blinding of anesthesiologists was not feasible and may have introduced performance bias, although outcomes such as blood loss and hemodynamic parameters are relatively objective, they remain susceptible to measurement variability and clinician driven intraoperative management.

Conclusion

According to this meta-analysis demonstrates that dexmedetomidine was found to reduce anesthesia time and heart rate in patients undergoing orthognathic surgery. Furthermore it also showed a consistent, clinically meaningful trend toward reduced intraoperative blood loss that did not reach statistical significance. MAP reduction was similarly non-significant. Nausea/vomiting and lower opioid requirements were potentially beneficial direction postoperatively without significantly statistically different treatments. Although associated with a higher risk of bradycardia, its benefits support judicious use in selected patients. The certainty of these findings is constrained by the high heterogeneity and the inclusion of observational data; thus, these results should be viewed as low-to-moderate certainty evidence requiring further validation. Further large-scale, high-quality trials with standardized comparators are needed to confirm its effect on blood loss and hemodynamic outcomes.
