Random‐Order Ballistic Training Improves Throwing Distance in Resistance‐Trained Males
Authors: Mohsen Yaghoubi, Sajjad Ramezani, Vahid Valipour Dehnou, Roger Eston, Tommy R. Lundberg, Ali Gorzi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe order of strength or ballistic exercises in complex training with similar movement patterns may affect performance outcomes. The main objective of this study was to investigate whether the order (random vs. progressive block) of ballistic exercise within a complex training protocol influences throwing performance and muscular strength in resistance‐trained males. Twenty resistance‐trained males (age: 21.1 ± 3.2 years; height: 177.1 ± 7.7 cm; and body mass: 76.6 ± 12.9 kg) with a minimum of 3 years of resistance training experience were randomly assigned to either a random‐order, independent weight‐order group (Rnd: e.g., 5,3, and 7 kg–7,3, and 5) or a blocked‐order, progressive weights‐order group (Blc: 3,5, and 7–3,5, and 7 kg) during medicine ball throwing. Subjects performed the training protocol three times per week for 4 weeks in addition to their normal training. Each training session included three repetitions of the bench press at 65% of one‐repetition maximum (1‐RM), followed by medicine ball throws for 10 sets. Both groups improved strength and throwing distance (p = 0.001). However, the Rnd group showed greater improvements (interaction effect) compared to the Blc group in 3 kg (p = 0.001; 52 VS. 115 cm) and 5 kg (p = 0.001; 32 VS. 95 cm), but not 7 kg throw distance (p = 0.10; 40 VS. 70 cm). These findings suggest that coaches and strength practitioners should consider performing ballistic exercises in random order rather than a progressive block ordering when designing complex training protocols as the unpredictable sequence appears to improve neuromuscular adaptations and translates more effectively to throwing performance.

Introduction

Improving muscle strength and power through innovative and advanced training strategies is important for success in many sports. A range of loads and training methods to improve muscle power or rate of force development (RFD) have been investigated to determine the characteristics of effective training programs to improve performance (Bogdanis et al. 2018). Training strategies include high loads (> 85% of 1 repetition maximum (1‐RM)) with low repetition s (Jakobsen et al. 2012; Poulos et al. 2018), lower loads (0%–50% of 1‐RM) with ballistic format (Zaras et al. 2013), plyometric training (Markovic 2007), or combinations of the different methods (Cormie et al. 2011). Although resistance training (Silva et al. 2015) and plyometric training (Ramirez‐Campillo et al. 2020; van de Hoef et al. 2020) may improve strength and power, respectively, the application of complex training sessions within a microcycle has been recommended to further enhance performance (Thapa et al. 2021). Previous research demonstrates that variations in load and training mode can elicit distinct neuromuscular adaptations; however, how the sequence of implementing ballistic and heavy‐load exercises interacts with these adaptations remains insufficiently understood.

Traditional complex training (CT) protocol (Comyns et al. 2007) usually involves resistance exercise with high loads followed by plyometric or ballistic exercises with lower loads (Thapa et al. 2021). This sequence of exercises with a similar movement pattern and in a given period of time induces postactivation performance enhancement (PAPE) (Prieske et al. 2020), a phenomenon in which, during the training period, the intensification of voluntary activation of the muscles increases the need for recruitment of more motor units and thus the potential for power generation by the muscles used in a given movement (Healy and Comyns 2017). Although the effects of CT may vary depending on training history and relative strength levels of the athletes (Freitas et al. 2017; Vlachopoulos et al. 2018), there is evidence that CT leads to significant performance improvements. For example, a CT protocol for more than 8 weeks (2 sessions per week) in soccer players improves running speed, jump height, and change of direction ability (Thapa et al. 2021). Thus, it seems that the effectiveness of CT may be influenced by the type, volume, intensity, and duration of training (Seitz and Haff 2016) and possibly by the characteristics of the subjects. In the current study, resistance‐trained males were selected because they possess sufficient strength and technical proficiency to safely and effectively perform CT exercises, while still maintaining capacity for measurable adaptation. Individuals with moderate training experience are more likely to exhibit potentiation effects due to an optimal balance between neuromuscular efficiency and trainability, whereas elite athletes often display reduced responsiveness because of near‐maximal adaptation levels.

To date, various CT protocols have been used to achieve optimal postactivation performance enhancement (PAPE) of strength–power performance, with varying results (Cormie et al. 2011; Iacono et al. 2019). Although various training approaches have been shown to improve muscle power, a critical gap remains in understanding how exercise sequencing influences outcomes within complex training (CT) protocols. In particular, the effect of ballistic exercise order (random vs. progressive) on neuromuscular adaptations has received little direct investigation and there is little research on the implementation of plyometric or ballistic exercises following resistance training. In addition, most existing protocols employ a fixed progressive order (e.g., gradually increasing load from light to heavy). Introducing a randomized sequence may heighten neural engagement by interrupting anticipatory motor strategies and increasing the need for rapid adjustment to varying mechanical demands. This unpredictability could enhance motor unit recruitment, firing variability, and neuromuscular adaptability, mechanisms consistent with principles of motor learning and variability‐driven plasticity (Elgueta‐Cancino et al. 2022; Wu et al. 2014). Since neuromuscular responses are influenced by external load and exercise sequencing, investigating this factor could offer valuable practical insights for strength and conditioning professionals. Clarifying these effects has direct practical relevance for coaches and strength and conditioning professionals as implementing randomized exercise sequencing may enhance neuromuscular performance without increasing total training volume or session duration.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to compare the effects of ballistic training in random order versus progressive block order following resistance exercises on bench press strength and medicine ball throw performance within a CT protocol.

Materials and Methods

Participants

Twenty resistance‐trained adult males (age: 21.1 ± 3.2 years, body mass: 76.6 ± 12.9 kg, and height: 177.1 ± 7.7 cm) volunteered to participate in this study. Participants were selected using a simple random sampling method from various fitness centers across the city. Recruitment took place between January 2023 and February 2023. A sensitive power analysis was conducted using G × Power software version 3.1.9.2 to calculate the detectable effect size with a sample size of 20 participants, an alpha error probability of 0.05 and a power of 0.8 (1‐β). The analysis yielded an effect size (f = 0.54) for the ANOVA‐based between‐within interaction (Faul et al. 2007; Gorzi et al. 2022). The benefits and risks of this study were explained, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. At the beginning of the study, all the participants' information, including addresses, contact numbers, physical conditions, training history, nutritional status, health condition, medications, and supplements used, were collected in writing from the participants. The inclusion criteria were male sex, aged between 18 and 30 years, and to have at least three consecutive years' experience of structured resistance training (at least three sessions per week). Participants also had to be fully familiar with standard resistance training techniques, including the bench press and medicine ball throws. The exclusion criteria included a history of cardiovascular, musculoskeletal, neuromuscular, or metabolic disorders; a current injury; the use of performance‐enhancing substances or medications; and engagement in other training programs that could interfere with the study protocol. Participants refrained from ingestion of caffeine, alcohol, medication, and supplements 48 h before commencement of testing (Bogdanis et al. 2018). They were asked to follow general nutritional guidelines during the study. Compliance with these restrictions was monitored weekly through self‐report logs documenting diet, recovery, and physical activity, which were reviewed by the research team to ensure adherence throughout the study. The experimental protocol was approved by the scientific committee of the department of sports sciences at Lorestan university and the study was conducted in full compliance with the provisions of the ethics committee of Sports Sciences Research Institute with the code IR. SSRI.REC.1401.1823 and in compliance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki except for preregistration.

Research Plan

Measurements and tests were administered pretest, mid‐test (2 weeks), and posttest, with all subjects completing the protocol consisting of 4 weeks of 3 sessions per week along with their normal training. During the training period, the participants first performed the bench press exercise and then threw 3, 5, and 7 kg medicine balls in random order (Rnd) or progressive blocked order (Blc). Group allocation was performed using computer‐generated random numbers to ensure unbiased assignment. We have selected the bench press and the medicine ball throw because these exercises use almost the same muscles and involve the same movement pattern (Ignjatovic et al. 2012). Both exercises emphasize horizontal pushing mechanics and primarily recruit the pectoralis major, anterior deltoid, and triceps brachii. Electromyographic evidence demonstrates substantial overlap in muscle activation patterns between the bench press and the medicine ball throw (Ebben et al. 2000).

Before the initial testing, all participants took part in a familiarization session. The familiarization session lasted approximately 45 min and was supervised by certified strength and conditioning specialists. It incorporated verbal instructions, visual demonstrations, and real‐time corrective feedback to ensure uniform execution of all test movements. Participants performed multiple practice trials with submaximal loads until consistent technique was achieved. This process was intended to reduce learning effects, minimize interindividual variability, and enhance the reliability of performance assessments across testing sessions. During the initial testing session, participants' height and body mass were measured using a standard wall‐mounted stadiometer and a calibrated electronic digital scale (Danesh Salar Iranian, Tehran, Iran).

In the second testing session, the 1‐RM measurement for the bench press and then the throwing distance measurement of the three weights of the medicine balls were performed under the supervision of a qualified national strength and conditioning coach. For warm‐up, the subjects performed a standard dynamic protocol consisting of 8 repetitions of light weight bench press exercise (estimated ∼40–50% 1‐RM), and throwing medicine balls of 3, 5, and 7 kg at an estimated 60%–80% of their maximum power with a 2‐min rest interval between sets (Blocquiaux et al. 2020). Participants then performed a specific warm‐up protocol of 4 repetitions at 70% 1‐RM and 2 repetitions at 80% 1‐RM to prepare for the bench press exercise with heavy weights (Chaouachi et al. 2011; Kilduff et al. 2008). After the specific warm‐up and 15‐min passive recovery period, subjects performed the bench press and their 1‐RM was calculated based on the Brzycki model, where Mass is the mass of the lifted barbell (kg) and Rep is the number of repetitions of bench press. Here, the mean repetitions for assessing 1‐RM were 4.1 ± 2.4.(Brzycki 1993). 1RM=Mass1.0278−(0.0278∗Rep)

All the training and measurements were done at the same time of the day (6–8 p.m.) in an euhydrated state and 3–4 h after lunch. All training sessions were interspersed with at least 48 h recovery. Participants were asked to avoid any sports activities, resistance training, or plyometric exercises during the recovery periods. Water consumption (about 500 mL) was allowed during each training session, and verbal encouragement was used during the training and tests sessions to maximize the individual effort of the subjects (Poulos et al. 2018).

Training Protocol

Following the pretest, based on their one‐repetition maximum (1‐ RM) and their age, participants were randomly divided into a random throw order group (Rnd: N = 10, age: 21.1 ± 3.4 years, body mass: 78.2 ± 16.2 kg, height: 176.7 ± 7.2 cm, and 1‐RM bench press: 86.8 ± 22.3 kg) and a progressive blocked order group (Blc: N = 10, age: 21.1 ± 3.2 years, body mass: 75.0 ± 8.9 kg, height: 177.4 ± 8.5 cm, and 1‐RM bench press: 86.2 ± 20.3 kg). Each group then performed one of the two different complex training protocols which comprised 3 sessions per week for 4 weeks. Each session involved 10 sets with a 3‐min rest interval between sets (Table 1). Both groups performed a barbell bench press with 65% of 1‐RM (Kilduff et al. 2008) with maximum speed followed by ballistic training in the form of throwing three medicine balls with different weights (3, 5, and 7 kgs) with maximal effort. In the Rnd group, participants performed a bench press (65% 1‐RM, 3 repetitions) (Poulos et al. 2018) and after a 3‐min rest, they threw 3, 5, and 7 kg medicine balls with a 15‐s rest interval in a random order (e.g.: 5,3,7–7,3,5‐ …). In the Blc group, participants threw the balls in progressive weight order of 3, 5, and 7 kg in all 10 sets (Table 1). During the training session, the subjects performed only the designated training program supervised by the researcher. The chosen training volume (10 sets per session) was based on previous contrast training research demonstrating that this workload effectively elicits potentiation without causing excessive neuromuscular fatigue (Kilduff et al. 2008; Poulos et al. 2018). The selected volume was further supported by pilot testing in resistance‐trained males, during which all participants completed the protocol without adverse events or notable declines in movement quality, confirming its appropriateness and safety for ballistic exercise.

Procedure for Medicine Ball Throw

The participant sat on a chair with a straight back so that the lower and upper back were fully supported against the back of the chair and technique and angle of trajectory were considered carefully. Once in position, he was instructed to throw balls with two hands from the front of the chest. During the throws, the participant's lower and upper back were not allowed to be separated from the chair. The rest interval between each throw was 15 s, and each medicine ball was thrown in two sets with a 2‐min rest interval. To determine the exact distance of the throw, an electronic mat (measurement accuracy: 1.00 cm) was used (DSI mat sensor for medicine ball, Danesh Salar Iranian, Tehran, Iran).

Statistical Analysis

After ensuring the normality of the data with the Shapiro–Wilk test, a split‐plot ANOVA (SPANOVA) was used for testing differences between two groups (i.e., group × time interaction). Differences within groups in 1‐RM and throwing performance at various loads were compared using a one‐way repeated‐measures ANOVA. The Bonferroni test was used for pairwise comparisons. Statistical analysis was performed using Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS, version 24). Results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The Pearson correlation coefficient was used to assess the association between maximum strength (1‐RM) and throwing distance (3, 5, and 7‐kg). Cohen's d effect size (ES/η2) was analyzed for repeated measures and confidence intervals (CI) and observed power (OP) was reported. The statistical level of significance was set at P < 0.05 for all analyses. Test–retest reliability was determined using an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC).

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the effects of a specific complex training protocol using a combination of power‐oriented resistance training and variation in the order of applying ballistic exercises with different medicine balls weights (3, 5, and 7 kg). The main finding was that the throwing distance of light and medium medicine balls (3 and 5 kg), but not heavy (7 kg), increased to a greater extent in the random order group compared with the progressive block order group. Thus, athletes engaging in complex training may benefit more from varied nonsequential loading patterns during ballistic exercises, as this approach produced superior improvements in lighter medicine ball throws.

The implementation of the protocol, including 10 sets of complex training with bench press set at 65% 1‐RM, had a significant effect on bench press and medicine ball chest throw records in both the Rnd and Blc groups. In a similar study, performing a 10‐set complex training protocol, including squat exercise set at 65% 1‐RM, led to a significant improvement in the power and vertical jump height of the participants (Poulos et al. 2018). In another study, it was observed that after 6 weeks of complex training in the form of plyometric exercises followed by squat jump at 74% 1‐RM and ballistic exercise at 30% 1‐RM, the jumping speed and reactive jump height of the subjects increased significantly (Bogdanis et al. 2018). However, interestingly, our results suggest that randomized training brings greater improvements than complex training in block form as shown by the interaction between group and time for the 3 and 5 kg loads.

These contradictory results between 7 kg versus 3 and 5 kg loading were supported by the higher correlation of 1‐RM and throwing distance with 7‐kg, compared to 3 and 5 kg, and similar correlation of 3 and 5 kg in both the Blc and random order groups. The 7 kg medicine ball chest throw is closer to strength than speed in the power continuum for resistance‐trained amateurs. It is possible that random‐order throwing had advantages over block‐order throwing at lower loads, but not at higher loads because we used 65% of the 1RM for the strength part of the CT program, which would be considered power‐oriented strength training.

Previous studies have reported that in a complex training protocol, the intensity of resistance used in the first part of training can affect the explosive performance in the second part (performing plyometric or ballistic exercise) (Baker 2003; Poulos et al. 2018), possibly due to some acute neural or mechanical facilitations (Blazevich and Babault 2019). Phosphorylation of the regulatory light chain of myosin and an increase in sensitivity to calcium have been suggested as the main mechanism related to PAPE (Blazevich and Babault 2019). Our results showed that changing the amount of resistance and even the order of different resistances (balls with different weights) in the second stage of the complex training (plyometric or ballistic exercises) can improve power performance. Variable resistance was used in the ballistic movements, which may have stimulated neuromuscular facilitation due to the unpredictable loading and challenging firing rates compared to the progressively blocked conditions with weights. These results were supported by the higher correlation (all 3 time‐points combined) of 1‐RM and throwing distance (light, medium, and heavy) in the Rnd group compared to the Blc group.

It is known that performance of a set of heavy resistance exercise between power training sets will acutely enhance power output in the second power training set, so when training resistances are used in a variable form and with light and heavy weight, they have a significant effect on power output (Baker 2003). This effect has been previously theorized to be due to some combination of acute neural or mechanical adaptations. Studies have also shown that during a complex training program and following the alternating use of heavy and light resistances, descending activity from higher motor centers increases. This results in direct myoelectric strengthening, the simultaneous increase in the motor units firing rate, a decrease in the inhibition of Golgi tendon organ, and a decrease in the central inhibition of Renshaw cells (Baker 2003; Ebben et al. 2000). Based on this mechanism, Yang et al. reported that the strongest explosive performances occur in athletes who use a combination of heavy and light resistances in their training program (Young et al. 1998). In addition, it seems that the random execution of ballistic exercises with different weights may lead to greater neural stimulation in the muscle and greater motor unit recruitment. In the present study, in the second stage of the complex training approach as supported by the greater improvements in the random order group, the neural stimulation might have been more intense and the force–velocity curve challenged differently (Baker 2003; Wilson, Newton, Murphy, and Humphries, 1993). In fact, several subjects in the Rnd group anecdotally reported increased stimulation in the pectoralis major, anterior deltoid, and triceps muscles after each training session and in some cases even the presence of muscle jumps and nervous tics in the hands during sleep.

Based on the findings of previous studies, the more the exercise sequence reflects the actual movement pattern during performance, the greater the improvement in potentiation and increase in potentiation transfer to the power performance of athletes (Prieske et al. 2020). Training protocols in which exercises are performed only in a fixed cycle and with many repetitions may perhaps lead to mental and central fatigue and subsequently to peripheral fatigue, which ultimately reduces the rate of increase in potentiation (Tillin and Bishop 2009). In this regard, many studies have emphasized the balance between fatigue and potentiation and its potential impact on subsequent performance in plyometric activities (Tillin and Bishop 2009). Although we did not investigate this, it appears that during the complex training program employed in this study, randomly weighted patterns in ballistic exercises may favorably improve exercise effectiveness.

Our results also showed that after 4 weeks of complex training, 1‐RM increased significantly in both groups, with no significant difference between the Rnd and progressive Blc groups, which may be due to the power‐oriented nature of resistance training. It can be expected that in a complex training program, combining strength training with plyometric or ballistic exercises will increase 1‐RM and the rate of force development. However, in some studies, the observed changes in maximal strength are more influenced by the first part of the complex training protocol (resistance training) (Baker 2003; Bogdanis et al. 2018). Accordingly, it may be assumed that one of the main reasons for the similar improvement in 1‐RM bench press in the two groups in this study is the use of the same resistance training load based on the percentage of each individual's 1‐RM (65% 1‐RM). Further, all participants performed the exercise routines in a similar rhythm and speed in the first stage of the training program.

As reported in previous studies, individuals with greater relative strength have a greater probability to enhance potentiation, with subsequent improvements in the execution of ballistic and explosive movements (Poulos et al. 2018). In addition, based on the force‐velocity curve as the amount of resistance increases, the speed of movement and power output will decrease (Wilson et al. 1993). Therefore, since the participants in this study were not at the elite level, this is a possible explanation for the better explosive performance when throwing lighter medicine balls.

Practical Applications

These findings provide clear guidance for practitioners looking to enhance explosive upper‐body performance. Even small changes to the order in which exercises are performed can have a significant impact on training outcomes, particularly for trained athletes with an established level of strength. Introducing randomized sequences in the ballistic components of CT could be a practical and time‐efficient way to improve performance without increasing the overall volume or load. Coaches may wish to implement randomized sequences of medicine ball throws after resistance exercises such as the bench press. This approach may enhance neuromuscular stimulation and optimize potentiation, particularly when using moderate loads (3–5 kg). It is particularly useful in high‐performance settings where there is limited scope for improvement.

Limitations

This study has some limitations that should be considered. First, the intervention period was relatively short (4 weeks), which restricts the ability to evaluate the long‐term adaptations resulting from the applied training program. Second, the sample size was limited (n = 20), which may have reduced the statistical power and affected the generalizability of the findings. Third, the participants of the study were resistance‐trained males, which limits the applicability of the results to other populations such as female athletes, untrained individuals, or even elite athletes.

Conclusions

In conclusion, both random‐order and progressive block‐order ballistic training strategies within a CT protocol improved strength and throwing performance. However, the random‐order approach was more effective in enhancing throwing distance when using moderate loads. This suggests that the order in which ballistic exercises are performed can affect the outcomes of training, emphasizing the importance of sequencing when designing programs for resistance‐trained athletes. Further research is needed to investigate the mechanisms behind this performance‐enhancing effect.

In addition, individual athlete characteristics, such as training experience, neuromuscular profile, and fatigue management strategies, may influence the effectiveness of different sequencing approaches. Considering these factors could help coaches and athletes to design training drills more effectively. Furthermore, integrating sequencing variations into complex training may optimize adaptations, improve the transferability of training to the sport‐specific performance, and improve the overall athletic performance. Further research is needed to investigate these contextual and mechanistic factors to better understand how exercise order in a single session of training interacts with the observed long‐term performance‐enhancing effect.
