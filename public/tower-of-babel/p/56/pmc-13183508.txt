Intravenous administration of a selective metabotropic glutamate receptor 2/3 agonist attenuates hypertension in spontaneously hypertensive rats
Authors: Suzu AOKI, Yoshiyasu NAGASHIMA, Ryota TOCHINAI, Shin-ichi SEKIZAWA
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hypertension is associated with impaired central autonomic regulation and remains a major risk factor for cardiovascular diseases. The nucleus tractus solitarius (NTS) in the medulla oblongata plays a critical role in blood pressure control via glutamatergic neurotransmission from peripheral baroreceptors. Previous studies have shown that intracisternal administration of Group II metabotropic glutamate receptor (mGluR2/3) agonists attenuates hypertension; however, the clinical applicability of this invasive approach is limited. The present study investigated whether systemic administration of a selective mGluR2/3 agonist, LY 379268, can modulate central blood pressure regulation and attenuate hypertension. LY 379268 was administered intravenously to spontaneously hypertensive rats (SHRs). In anesthetized conditions, arterial blood pressure and heart rate were continuously recorded and dose–response effects were evaluated. In conscious conditions, LY 379268 were administered into the tail vein of control SHRs or SHRs with intact (Sham) or preoperatively transected baroreceptor information (Sino-Aortic Denervation, SAD), and blood pressure was periodically measured by the tail-cuff method. A dose-dependent reduction in arterial blood pressure without significant changes in heart rate was observed up to moderate doses (10 μg/kg), showing bell-shape response. Similarly, aforementioned dose of LY 379268 i.v. injection decreased blood pressure in control or Sham-SHRs but not in SAD-SHRs in conscious condition. In addition, intravenous Evans blue injection demonstrated increased permeability of the blood-brain-barrier in the NTS. These findings indicate that systemic activation of mGluR2/3 can attenuate hypertension by modulating central autonomic regulation via access to the NTS. Intravenous administration of mGluR2/3 agonists may represent a minimally invasive strategy for targeting central mechanisms underlying hypertension.

INTRODUCTION

Hypertension is a major global health problem affecting more than one billion adults worldwide [37]. Blood pressure generally increases with age, and the etiology of hypertension is multifactorial, involving dietary habits, genetic predisposition, and neuroendocrine regulation [3, 14]. Excessive sodium intake, genetic abnormalities affecting aldosterone secretion, and dysregulation of the renin–angiotensin system contribute to the development of hypertension [2, 22].

Despite diverse etiologies, impaired baroreflex sensitivity is a common characteristic of hypertension in both humans and experimental animal models [4, 27]. Therefore, central neural mechanisms play a critical role in blood pressure regulation and represent an important therapeutic target.

Current antihypertensive treatments include lifestyle modification and pharmacological therapy using diuretics, renin–angiotensin system inhibitors, calcium channel blockers, vasodilators, and centrally acting sympatholytic agents [36]. Although these drugs are effective in lowering blood pressure, most primarily provide symptomatic control and do not directly target central regulatory mechanisms. In addition, centrally acting agents are often associated with adverse effects, limiting their clinical applicability.

Blood pressure is regulated by the integrated actions of local vascular mechanisms, the renal-endocrine system, and central neural pathways. The nucleus tractus solitarius (NTS) in the medulla oblongata serves as the primary central integration site for cardiovascular sensory information transmitted from arterial baroreceptors via the glossopharyngeal and vagus nerves [1, 24, 34]. Glutamate is the principal neurotransmitter mediating synaptic transmission from baroreceptor afferents to NTS neurons [20].

NTS neurons project to the caudal ventrolateral medulla, which inhibits sympathetic premotor neurons in the rostral ventrolateral medulla, thereby reducing sympathetic outflow and arterial pressure [23, 33]. Thus, modulation of glutamatergic neurotransmission within the NTS has a profound influence on cardiovascular regulation.

Metabotropic glutamate receptors (mGluRs) are G protein–coupled receptors that modulate synaptic transmission and neuronal excitability [11]. Among them, Group II mGluRs (mGluR2 and mGluR3) inhibit adenylyl cyclase activity and suppress neuronal excitation [28]. These receptors are abundantly expressed in the NTS and play an important role in autonomic regulation [7, 16].

Previous studies from our laboratory demonstrated that chronic intracisternal administration of a highly selective mGluR2/3 agonist attenuated the development of hypertension and improved baroreflex sensitivity in spontaneously hypertensive rats (SHRs) [18]. However, this approach required invasive intracranial procedures, limiting its translational potential.

The caudal medulla is known to lack a fully developed blood–brain barrier and is anatomically adjacent to the NTS [15], suggesting that systemically administered compounds may access this region without direct intracranial delivery. Therefore, the present study aimed to determine whether intravenous administration of a highly selective mGluR2/3 agonist can attenuate hypertension in SHRs via a minimally invasive route and to elucidate the underlying physiological mechanisms.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals

Male SHRs (SHR/Izm) and normotensive Wistar–Kyoto (WKY) rats (WKY/Izm) were obtained from Japan SLC, Inc. (Hamamatsu, Japan). Rats were 20–24 weeks of age at the time of experiments. Animals were housed under controlled environmental conditions with a 12-hr light/dark cycle (lights on at 08:00) at 24°C, with free access to food and water.

All experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with the Regulations for Animal Experiments of the University of Tokyo and were approved by the Animal Experiment Committee of the Graduate School of Agricultural and Life Sciences, The University of Tokyo (Approval No. P21-019H05).

Drugs

The selective Group II mGluR (=mGluR2/3) agonist LY 379268 was purchased from Tocris Bioscience (Bristol, UK). Phenylephrine hydrochloride, sodium nitroprusside dihydrate and Evans blue dye were purchased from FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corp. (Osaka, Japan). All drugs except Evans blue dye were dissolved in distilled water and stored as an aqueous solution and diluted in physiological saline immediately before use in each experiment. The administration volume was set at approximately 0.16 mL per animal.

Experiments under anesthetized conditions

Rats were anesthetized with urethane (1.5 g/kg), and polyethylene catheters were inserted into the femoral artery and vein for blood pressure measurement and intravenous drug administration, respectively. The former catheter was connected to a pressure transducer (AMPLIFIER CASE 7903, Nihon Kohden, Tokyo, Japan), and electrocardiograms in standard limb leads were also connected to the bioelectrical amplifier (AB-621G, Nihon Kohden). Arterial pressure and ECG signals were recorded using a hemodynamic analysis system (ECG Processor SBP2000, Softron Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) at a sampling rate of 1 kHz. Mean arterial pressure (MAP) was calculated from systolic and diastolic blood pressure using standard methods and heart rate was calculated from ECG signals. After a stabilization period of at least 10 min, baseline cardiovascular parameters were recorded. LY 379268 was administered intravenously at doses of 1.0, 3.0, 10, 30, 100, or 1,000 μg/kg (n=3 per dose). Saline was administered as a control. Arterial blood pressure and heart rate were continuously recorded for 60 min after injection. Comparing cardiovascular responses between hypertensive and normotensive animals, LY 379268 (10 μg/kg) was also administered to WKY rats under the same anesthetic conditions.

Experiments under conscious conditions

Blood pressure and pulse rate (hereafter referred to as heart rate) were measured and recorded using the tail-cuff method with a non-invasive blood pressure monitor (BP-98AL, Softron Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). During measurement, the animal was also restrained inside the tube to reduce stress with an animal warmer (THC-3, Softron Co., Ltd.) to maintain their body temperature at 37°C to ensure stable blood flow from the tail artery. Measurements were taken at the following time points whenever stable blood flow could be detected and recorded: before administration, and 15, 30, 45, 60, 90, 120, 150, and 180 min after administration. At each time point, measurement was repeated five times. Intravenous injections of saline (n=4) or LY 379268 (10 μg/kg, n=8) were performed via tail vein.

In other experiments, SHRs, whose peripheral baroreceptors were surgically denervated, were used. The procedure for peripheral baroreceptor denervation (Sino-Aorticus Denervation, SAD) was described by Hirakawa et al. (1997) [17]. Briefly, after anesthetizing rats with isoflurane (2–3%), a small incision was made in the ventral midline of the neck, and under a stereomicroscope, the aortic depressor nerve, which branches from the superior laryngeal nerve just before it joins the vagus nerve ganglion (from the peripheral side), was identified and bilaterally transected. Next, connective tissue and nerve tissue around the common carotid bifurcation where the carotid bodies are located were removed, and a 5% phenol solution was applied to the area containing the carotid bodies. This procedure took about one hour. The control group underwent sham surgery (Sham), which involved only identifying the aortic depressor nerve. Rats that underwent SAD or Sham surgery were transferred to individual cages with free access to food and water. The SAD group was observed for 3 to 4 weeks and the Sham group for approximately 1 week until body weight recovered to preoperative levels, and then those animals were used for experiments. After the tail-cuff experiments, in some animals baroreflex sensitivities were evaluated under the urethane-anesthetized conditions: measuring blood pressure form the arterial catheter and heart rate from the ECG as described above, drugs, phenylephrine (21 μg/kg) or sodium nitroprusside (50 μg/kg) [18], were administered via the femoral vein after measuring baseline values for 10 min.

Assessment of blood–brain barrier permeability

To assess blood–brain barrier permeability in the NTS, Evans blue dye (80 mg/kg) was administered intravenously to one anesthetized SHR and one anesthetized WKY rat. One hour after administration, brainstem tissue was fixed using the transcardial perfusion method. Under deep isoflurane anesthesia, the thoracic cavity was exposed, an 18 G needle was placed in the left ventricle, the right atrial appendage was excised, and bleeding was achieved by perfusing PBS solution (300 mL, 20 min) through the placed needle. Tissue fixation was performed by subsequent perfusion with 10% neutral buffered formalin solution via the same route (300 mL, 20 min). After perfusion, the extracted medulla oblongata tissue was immersed in 10% neutral buffered formalin solution at room temperature for 24 hr. From the fixed medulla tissue, 100 μm sections were prepared using a vibratome (Neo-LinearSlicer NLS-AT, Dosaka EM Co., Ltd., Kyoto, Japan) from Bregma −14.2 mm to −12.0 mm. These sections were mounted on slides, dried, embedded in a decolorization-resistant mounting medium, and sealed with a cover glass. Subsequently, excitation light was applied to Evans blue-stained tissue using a confocal laser scanning microscope (LSM-700, ZEISS, Oberkochen, Germany), and the resulting red fluorescence was observed (ZEN (blue edition) @ Carl Zeiss Microscopy GmbH).

Data analysis and statistical procedures

Data are presented as mean ± SE unless otherwise stated. For the anesthetized experiment, data were collected every 5min, and the area under the curve (AUC) of mean arterial pressure and heart rate during the 30-min period after injection was calculated. For the conscious experiment, since data could not be obtained at a specific time point for some reason, and therefore comparison was made at the point of greatest blood pressure reduction in the LY 379268 group vs. saline group using Student’s t-test. For each time point, blood pressure and heart rate were calculated from the three measurements excluding the highest and lowest systolic blood pressure readings among the five measurements taken. Comparisons between groups were performed using Student’s t-test or one-way ANOVA with post-hoc Tukey HSD test, as appropriate. Baroreflex sensitivity was determined from the change in mean blood pressure and heart rate before drug administration and 5 sec after administration and the slope was compared using Student’s t-test. A value of P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

DISCUSSION

The present study demonstrated that intravenous administration of the selective Group II mGluR agonist LY 379268 attenuates arterial blood pressure in SHRs. The hypotensive effects were observed under both anesthetized and conscious conditions, occurred without consistent changes in heart rate, and were minimal in normotensive WKY rats. These findings indicate that systemic activation of mGluR2/3 selectively modulates blood pressure regulation in hypertensive states.

Under anesthetized conditions, LY 379268 produced a dose-dependent reduction in arterial blood pressure with a bell-shaped dose–response profile, based on no-hypotensive effect occurred at 1.0 μg/kg or >100 μg/kg. As evident from Fig. 5 as well as the report by Gross et al. (1990) [15], the NTS, a key integrative center for cardiovascular reflexes [10, 19], is presumed to be more susceptible to the effects of intravenously administered drugs than other central nervous system regions due to its incomplete blood-brain barrier. However, at excessively high doses such as >100 μg/kg, mGluRs outside the NTS may be stimulated, potentially negating the antihypertensive effect and/or causing other effects, for example schizophrenia related modulation observed at dose of >300 μg/kg of LY 379268 in rats [6].

The inconsistent heart rate changes by LY 379268 administration suggests that the hypotensive effects were not primarily mediated by cardiac reflexes but rather by modulation of autonomic regulatory mechanisms in various conditions of each animal. In anesthetized conditions, heart rate responses may have been reflected by activation of the baroreflex mechanism, while in conscious conditions the stress caused by handling and/or injection into the tail vein may have some effects on heart rate. In addition, LY 379268 may have activated on mGluR2/3 outside the NTS, for example, chronic administration of LY 379268 is known to promote parasympathetic dominance [18].

In WKY rats, administration of LY 379268 tended to cause less reduction in blood pressure compared to SHR. The strain-dependent difference in responsiveness between SHRs and WKY rats suggests that altered central autonomic regulation in hypertension enhances sensitivity to mGluR2/3 activation, which finely modulates glutamate projection to control blood pressure. This would be supported by possible access of LY 379268 to the NTS even in WKY rats based on Evans blue experiments. Although direct activation of mGluR2/3 within specific nuclei was not examined, these findings support the possibility that intravenously administered LY 379268 accessed dorsal medullary structures and influenced central autonomic regulation. Furthermore, while we cannot rule out species differences in blood-brain barrier permeability in the NTS [5], its potential advantage as a therapeutic target may lie in the increased permeability observed under hypertensive conditions.

Group II mGluRs, mGluR2/3, are generally known to function as autoreceptors and may therefore be localized in glutamatergic presynaptic terminals. Our previous studies demonstrated substantial mGluR2/3 expression in both pre- and postsynaptic neurons within the NTS of normotensive Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats. It remains unclear whether mGluR2/3 expression patterns differ among normotensive strains, such as SD and WKY rats. Comparative analysis of mGluR2/3 expression across rat strains may provide valuable insights and represents an important direction for future studies aimed at elucidating the mechanisms underlying hypertension development.

Peripheral pressure receptors localized in the aortic arch and carotid sinus detect changes in blood pressure and transmit afferent information to the NTS via glutamate synapses [8, 24]. mGluRs are abundant in the medulla, particularly in the NTS [7, 16], and are involved in glutamatergic transmission within the autonomic nervous system [7]. Among these, LY 379268, an agonist for mGluR2/3 (Group II), is known to cause hyperpolarization of second-order baroreceptor neurons, thereby suppressing neuronal excitation [31]. Microinjection of glutamate into the NTS is known to lower blood pressure in normotensive rats [13], likely due to amplification of baroreceptor information by exciting NTS neurons. However, since LY 379268 suppresses neuronal excitability, the blood pressure-lowering effect observed in this study and in previous experiments reported by Hsu et al. (2021) appears inconsistent [18]. Since LY 379268 administration to normotensive WKY rats showed little effect on blood pressure or heart rate, it suggests that mGluR2/3 function may differ in a hypertensive state as described above. If mGluR2/3 function were impaired in a hypertensive state, the membrane potential of NTS neurons may shift toward depolarization. Consequently, while these neurons may become more prone to generating action potentials, their excitability (action potential frequency) may decrease, potentially leading to inadequate blood pressure control. Conversely, exogenously stimulating mGluR2/3 to hyperpolarize the membrane potential of NTS neurons could induce a hypotensive effect by more effectively transmitting afferent information from baroreceptors to higher centers, i.e., by enhancing the aforementioned excitability.

In this study, we prioritized mean arterial blood pressure for analysis. The increase in systolic blood pressure is thought to be caused by age-related arteriosclerosis and increased cardiac output [35], while the increase in diastolic blood pressure is thought to be caused by increased peripheral vascular resistance [30]. In contrast, mean arterial pressure, which is the average of systolic and diastolic blood pressure, is influenced by both cardiac output and systemic vascular resistance [12], and thus we focused MAP for analysis of effects of LY 379268 administration in this study.

In conscious conditions, saline injection via tail vein in SHRs also showed a tendency for blood pressure to decrease 15 min after administration. Although the cause is unclear, it is possible that the relatively strong pain from needle insertion triggered vasovagal reflex [9]. In addition, blood pressure increased after 30 min post-administration in the same group, and it may be due to the rebound effect (off-effects) of the aforementioned reflex or acute stress during blood pressure measurement [32]. Furthermore, it is known that SHRs are prone to various types of stress [21, 25]. In any case, the presence or absence of mGluR2/3 action might significantly influence the outcome. In the saline-treated group, heart rate continued to decrease despite the rise in blood pressure, suggesting this could be due to the functioning of the baroreflex mechanism.

As shown in Fig. 2, we compared the effects of LY 379268 on blood pressure in conscious SHRs at a single time point, which may have introduced some bias. Since a biphasic blood pressure response was observed in SHRs following the administration of saline, it may not be possible to accurately calculate the AUC from data collected only 30 and 60 min after administration, when complete data sets are available. However, the analysis during the 60-min period confirmed that LY 379268 has a hypotensive effect in SHRs without altering heart rate; MBP: +13.2 ± 3.3 [mmHg*hr] in saline (n=4) vs. −10.0 ± 3.0 [mmHg*hr] in LY 379268 (n=8), (t-test: P<0.001); HR: −22.0 ± 24.5 [bpm*hr] in saline vs. −20.6 ± 23.3 [bpm*hr] in LY 379268, (t-test: P=0.971).

The SAD procedure, sino-aortic denervation, blocked afferent information from the baroreceptors to the NTS in the medulla and indeed, to some extent, declined baroreceptor sensitivities based on results of reflex bradycardia induced by phenylephrine administration. Consequently, administration of LY 379268 failed to exert its antihypertensive effect, suggesting that intact afferent input from peripheral baroreceptors is required for the hypotensive effect of LY 379268. Furthermore, tail vein administration of LY 379268 to the Sham group, which did not block afferent information, showed a hypotensive effect peaking 30 min after administration. From this, it can be inferred that the hypotensive effects following intravenous administration of LY 379268 was dependent on afferent inputs from peripheral baroreceptors and came from mGluR2/3 activation caused by LY 379268, suggesting that intravenously administered LY 379268 may access the dorsal medulla, including the NTS, where blood–brain barrier permeability is relatively high.

Although the SAD procedure could eliminate the hypotensive effect of LY379268, it did not reduce baroreflex sensitivity as much as expected. As the source of afferent fibers from baroreceptors in the aortic arch, we were only able to transect the aortic depressor nerves. On the other hand, the main vagus nerve trunk and the recurrent laryngeal nerve, which anastomoses with the superior laryngeal nerve, may contain these afferent fibers. Since we cannot denervate those nerves for survival surgery, our SAD method is not perfect, and this is the limitation of this procedure. One method for complete removal of the aortic baroreceptor involves direct access to the aortic arch; however, this procedure could be unfeasible in SHRs without causing adverse effects via thoracotomy.

Previous studies investigating the role of mGluR2/3 in blood pressure regulation have relied on intracisternal or intracerebral administration [18]. In contrast, the present study demonstrates that intravenous administration of a selective mGluR2/3 agonist is sufficient to attenuate hypertension, suggesting a less invasive approach to targeting central mechanisms involved in blood pressure control. However, since Hsu et al. (2021) demonstrated a greater hypotensive effect (approximately 40 mmHg reduction) with chronic administration of LY 379268 at a dose of 4.0 μg/day, more pronounced antihypertensive effects may be achievable in repeated-dose studies, warranting future investigation [18].

In conclusion, systemic administration of LY 379268 induces sustained hypotensive effects in SHRs without significant effects on heart rate or normotensive animals. These findings provide experimental evidence that peripheral delivery of mGluR2/3 agonists can influence central autonomic regulation and may represent a potential pharmacological strategy for the treatment of hypertension.
