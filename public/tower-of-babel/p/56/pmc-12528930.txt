Mortality rate of stroke and its determinants in Africa: An umbrella review of systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Addisu Getie, Mihretie Gedfew, Tegene Atamenta Kitaw, Gizachew Yilak, Melaku Bimerew
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionStroke is a significant clinical condition characterized by inadequate blood flow to the brain, resulting in cerebral function loss and potential death if not promptly treated. In developed countries, stroke incidence is decreasing due to efforts targeting risk factors like high blood pressure and smoking, though aging populations sustain high overall rates. Globally, stroke ranks among the top causes of death and disability, with Africa experiencing notably high stroke-related mortality rates. Introduction

Stroke is a complex and acute neurological condition that occurs when the blood supply to part of the brain is interrupted or significantly reduced. This deprives brain tissue of oxygen and nutrients, causing rapid cell injury and, without timely medical intervention, can lead to irreversible brain damage, long-term disability, or death. Strokes are broadly classified into two major types: ischemic stroke, caused by obstruction of a cerebral blood vessel, and hemorrhagic stroke, caused by rupture of a blood vessel resulting in bleeding within or around the brain [1]. In high-income populations, the majority of strokes are ischemic, accounting for approximately 80 % of cases. Ischemic strokes result from blood clots or other obstructions that block cerebral arteries, while intracerebral hemorrhages, representing 10–15 % of cases, arise from rupture of small arteries in the brain, often linked to chronic hypertension or vascular abnormalities. Subarachnoid hemorrhage, usually caused by rupture of an aneurysm or vascular malformation, accounts for about 5 % of strokes [2]. Stroke is relatively uncommon in individuals under 40 years old; however, when it occurs, it can have devastating consequences due to its impact on productive years of life. In younger adults, chronic high blood pressure is the most frequent underlying risk factor, contributing to both ischemic and hemorrhagic strokes [3].

Globally, high blood pressure (hypertension) is the single most important modifiable risk factor for stroke, contributing to an estimated 12.7 million cases annually. Chronic hypertension damages blood vessel walls, accelerates atherosclerosis, and increases the risk of both ischemic and hemorrhagic strokes [4]. Although stroke incidence has decreased in some developed countries due to improved blood pressure control and reduced smoking, the overall stroke rate remains high due to population aging [5]. According to the Global Burden of Disease Study 2017, stroke ranked as the second‑leading cause of death and the third‑leading cause of combined death and disability worldwide, as measured by disability-adjusted life-years (DALYs) [6]. This reflects both the high mortality and the substantial long-term physical, cognitive, and social impairments experienced by survivors.

The global burden of stroke has risen sharply over recent decades, driven by population growth, aging, and increasing prevalence of risk factors such as hypertension, diabetes, obesity, and unhealthy lifestyle behaviors [3]. The World Stroke Organization (WSO) Global Stroke Fact Sheet 2022 reports that the worldwide economic burden of stroke exceeds US$721 billion annually, encompassing acute care, long-term rehabilitation, lost productivity, and informal caregiving. Between 1990 and 2019, incident cases of stroke increased by 70 %, while stroke-related deaths rose by 43 % [7]. Africa faces a disproportionately high stroke burden, with an estimated 29 % of stroke patients dying (95 % CI: 23–36 %) [8].

High mortality in Africa reflects limited access to timely diagnosis, acute care, specialized treatment, and rehabilitation services, compounded by a high prevalence of uncontrolled hypertension and other cardiovascular risk factors. Stroke also causes substantial long-term disability, with up to half of survivors experiencing chronic impairments such as hemiplegia, aphasia, cognitive deficits, emotional disturbances, and loss of independence in daily activities [9].

Multiple clinical factors are associated with increased stroke mortality, including hypertension, prior stroke, a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score below 8, hemorrhagic stroke type, clinical diagnosis without imaging, aspiration pneumonia, impaired mobility, reduced oxygen saturation, and post-stroke complications such as pulmonary embolism. Recognition of these predictors is essential for early risk stratification, targeted interventions, and improving outcomes [[10], [11], [12]]. Socioeconomic factors, including older age, lower education level, reduced income, and having a female head of household, have also been linked to worse outcomes [13,14].

While several individual studies have examined stroke mortality in Africa, no comprehensive synthesis of the region's evidence has been conducted. This umbrella review represents the first effort to estimate the pooled prevalence of stroke mortality across Africa and to identify its associated predictors. By synthesizing findings from multiple systematic reviews and meta-analyses, this review aims to highlight common trends, evaluate methodological quality, and identify research gaps. The findings will provide crucial insights to guide healthcare strategies, inform policy decisions, and support targeted interventions aimed at reducing stroke mortality and improving outcomes for African populations. Therefore, the primary objective of this umbrella review is to assess the pooled prevalence of stroke mortality and its associated predictors in Africa.

Methods

This umbrella review systematically synthesizes findings from systematic reviews and meta-analyses on stroke mortality rates in Africa. The review was done following the methodology of an umbrella review of systematic review and meta-analysis studies [15].

Data source and literature search

A systematic search was conducted across multiple databases, including PubMed, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science, Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, Database of Abstracts of Reviews of Effects (DARE), and Google Scholar, to identify systematic reviews and meta-analyses on stroke mortality rates in Africa. The searches included publications from 1 January 2010 to 30 December 2024, with the final search performed on 30 December 2024. The search focused on stroke mortality by region and stroke type, specifically targeting reviews reporting on the prevalence and determinants of stroke mortality. Search strategies were developed using relevant keywords combined with the Boolean operators “AND” and “OR.” Key terms included ‘stroke mortality,’ ‘cerebrovascular accident mortality,’ ‘ischemic stroke mortality,’ ‘hemorrhagic stroke mortality,’ ‘risk factor,’ ‘predictor,’ ‘determinant,’ ‘meta-analysis,’ ‘systematic review,’ and ‘review.’ Two reviewers (AG and MB) independently conducted the searches, with any discrepancies resolved by consensus with the other authors (TAK and GY).

Study screening and selection

All search results were exported to the EndNote X8 citation system to identify systematic reviews and meta-analyses that met the inclusion criteria and to remove duplicate articles. The articles were then screened by title and abstract, and the full text was assessed. Only English-language publications up to June 2024 were eligible for inclusion. To be considered a systematic review or meta-analysis, a study needed to have a defined literature search strategy, appraise included studies using a relevant tool, and follow a standard approach in pooling studies and providing summary estimates. Studies were excluded if they did not report the outcome of interest, were published in languages other than English, or were narrative reviews, editorials, correspondence, abstracts, or methodological studies. While the authors generally agreed on the selection and screening process, any disagreements were resolved through discussion and consensus. This ensures transparency and rigor in our review methodology.

Data extraction

Data from the included systematic reviews and meta-analyses were extracted using a standardized data abstraction form in Microsoft Excel 2016. For each study, information collected included identification details (first author and publication year), the number of studies included in each review, sample size, overall prevalence of stroke mortality, prevalence by region and stroke type, and determinants of stroke mortality. Two reviewers (AG and MB) independently performed the data extraction, and any discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus with the other authors (TAK and GY), ensuring accuracy and consistency of the extracted data.

Study quality and reliability assessment

The methodological quality of the included systematic reviews and meta-analyses was assessed using the AMSTAR tool [16], which consists of 11 questions evaluating the methods used to combine and summarize findings from the included studies. The AMSTAR tool is validated and widely used for assessing the quality of systematic reviews and meta-analyses. Quality scores range from 0 to 11, with scores of 8–11 indicating high quality, 4–7 indicating medium quality, and less than 3 indicating low quality. The quality assessment was independently performed by AG and MB, and any discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus with the other authors (TAK and GY).

Data synthesis

Quantitative methods were employed to summarize the estimates of stroke mortality rates in Africa. For this analysis, Stata version 17.0 software (Stata Corp, TX, USA) was used to determine the summary prevalence of stroke mortality in Africa. Heterogeneity was assessed using Higgin's I2 statistics [17], which categorizes I2 values as follows: less than 49 % indicating low heterogeneity, 50–75 % indicating moderate heterogeneity, and greater than 75 % indicating high heterogeneity. Due to the high level of heterogeneity observed among the studies, the summary prevalence estimates were calculated using the Der Simonian-Laird random-effects model [18], which considers both within-study and between-study variations. Publication bias was assessed through visual inspection of funnel plots and Egger's regression tests [19]. To address publication bias, a trim and fill analysis was conducted.

Results

Study screening and selection

After an extensive database search, we found 2160 studies conducted on stroke mortality in Africa. We then excluded 2111 articles as they were not systematic reviews or meta-analyses. Additionally, 24 duplicate articles were removed. Out of the remaining 25 articles, 16 were excluded after screening the titles and abstracts, evaluating the outcomes of interest, and conducting a thorough full-text review. Ultimately, 9 systematic reviews and meta-analyses [[20], [21], [22], [23], [24], [25], [26], [27], [28]] were included in the final analysis for this umbrella review (Fig. 1).

The PRISMA flow diagram of identification and selection of systematic review and meta-analysis studies done on the mortality rate of stroke in Africa.

Characteristics of the included review studies

In this umbrella review, nine systematic reviews and meta-analyses, comprising a total of 341 primary studies, included data on 170,501 stroke patients admitted to hospitals across Africa. The number of primary studies included within these reviews varied considerably, ranging from 16 [24] to 93 [22] studies per review, reflecting differences in scope and inclusion criteria. Sample sizes of the primary studies also showed substantial variation, ranging from 2392 [24] to 64,256 [20] participants. The publication years of the included systematic reviews and meta-analyses spanned from 2018 [20] to 2024 [22], indicating that the evidence synthesized was relatively recent and up-to-date. Only two of these reviews provided stroke mortality rates stratified by type of stroke (ischemic vs. hemorrhagic) [21,23]. Across the studies, reported hospital-based stroke mortality rates varied, with the highest rate of 27 % observed in a 2024 study [22], and the lowest rate of 14 % reported in two earlier studies conducted in 2018 and 2022 [20,27]. This variability highlights differences in patient populations, stroke types, and healthcare settings across the continent (Table 1).

Methodological quality of included studies

The methodological quality of the included systematic review and meta-analysis studies was assessed using the AMSTAR tool, which evaluates the methodological quality of such studies [16]. The quality scores, out of a possible 11 points, ranged from 9 to 11, with an average score of 10.11 points, indicating a generally high quality. The AMSTAR criteria most frequently met by the studies were related to the disclosure of conflicts of interest. Conversely, the criteria less frequently satisfied were those concerning the scientific quality assessment and documentation of the primary studies, as well as publication bias (Table 2).

Umbrella review of the included studies

The overall pooled prevalence of stroke mortality in Africa was found to be 20.3 % (95 % CI: 17.3–23.2, I2 = 99.51, p < 0.001). Due to the significant heterogeneity among the included systematic reviews and meta-analyses, the pooled stroke mortality rate was calculated using a random-effects model (Fig. 2). A meta-regression was performed to explore potential sources of heterogeneity, with the number of studies and sample size considered as moderators. Findings showed that the number of studies within the included systematic reviews and meta-analyses contributed notably to heterogeneity (coefficient 0.138), while sample size did not appear to play a meaningful role (coefficient − 0.0001).

Umbrella review of systematic reviews and meta-analysis on the mortality rate of stroke in Africa.

Publication bias

In this umbrella review, the funnel plot of the included systematic reviews and meta-analyses showed asymmetry, and Egger's test indicated statistically significant publication bias (p = 0.002) (Fig. 3A). To address this, a trim-and-fill analysis was performed, which identified 14 additional studies and produced a bias-adjusted pooled prevalence of stroke mortality in Africa of 15 % (95 % CI: 10.2–19.7) (Fig. 3B). Potential contributors to this publication bias include selective reporting of significant results, underrepresentation of studies from certain regions, and the tendency to publish positive findings over null or negative results. Such biases may influence the conclusions of the review, potentially leading to overestimation of the prevalence of stroke mortality or the strength of its associated predictors.

Mortality rate of stroke based on region and type of stroke

In this umbrella review, stroke mortality rate was assessed based on the region and type of stroke. The highest prevalence of stroke mortality was reported in Western Africa: 26.99 % (95 % CI 14.4–39.6), whereas the lowest was reported in Northern Africa: 15.00 % (95 % CI 14.7–15.3). The narrow confidence interval for Northern Africa likely reflects the limited number of studies and relatively small variability in the available data from that region, whereas the wider intervals observed in West Africa may be due to larger heterogeneity among included studies, differences in sample sizes, and variable data quality. Based on the type of stroke, the higher mortality rate of stroke was recorded among hemorrhagic stroke patients: 26.1 (24–28.3) (Table 3).

Determinants of stroke mortality

Based on findings from systematic reviews and meta-analyses, one study reported that patients with a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score below 8 had more than a six-fold higher risk of stroke mortality (AOR 6.1, 95 % CI 3.2–12.8), while those with aspiration pneumonia experienced about a three-fold increased risk (AOR 3.0, 95 % CI 1.4–6.4). Other factors associated with increased mortality included older age, vascular disease, female sex, absence of aspirin therapy, and reduced renal function [23]. Diabetes was also linked to worse stroke outcomes, with a 60 % higher risk of poor prognosis at 6 months (AOR 1.6, 95 % CI 1.2–2.2) and nearly double the risk at 12 months (AOR 1.9, 95 % CI 1.3–2.8) [25]. An Ethiopian study further identified hemorrhagic stroke type (AOR 1.8, 95 % CI 1.1–2.9), GCS <12 (AOR 8.5, 95 % CI 4.1–17.8), altered mental status (AOR 5.6, 95 % CI 3.9–23.0), and National Institute of Health Stroke Scale <13 (AOR 16.1, 95 % CI 5.3–49.1) as strong predictors of mortality [27].

Discussion

In recent years, there has been an increase in systematic reviews and meta-analyses examining stroke mortality rates in Africa. While these studies provide substantial evidence for decision-making, the growing number of specific reviews can overwhelm information users. Therefore, this umbrella review was conducted to consolidate findings from existing systematic reviews and meta-analyses on stroke mortality rates in Africa. The review yielded an overall summary prevalence of 20.3 % (95 % CI: 17.3–23.2), which is higher than the rates reported in Europe (4.8 %) [29], the USA (16.3 %) [30], and China (2.6 %) [31]. A stroke mortality rate of 20 % can be considered high, especially when compared to global averages and rates in more developed regions. In many developed countries, improvements in healthcare systems, access to medical facilities, and public health education have contributed to lower stroke mortality rates. In contrast, in Africa and other regions with limited healthcare resources, factors such as delayed access to medical care, inadequate preventive measures, and restricted treatment options contribute to higher mortality from stroke and other medical conditions.

This umbrella review examined stroke mortality rates by region and stroke type. Western Africa had the highest stroke mortality prevalence at 27 % (95 % CI: 14.4–39.6), while Northern Africa had the lowest at 15 % (95 % CI: 14.7–15.3). The narrow confidence interval for Northern Africa likely reflects a smaller number of studies with more consistent results, whereas the wider intervals in West Africa are due to greater heterogeneity, variable sample sizes, and differences in data quality. We also note that these discrepancies should be interpreted cautiously, as they may affect the precision and reliability of regional mortality estimates. Contributing factors may include limited access to healthcare, inadequate medical infrastructure, delayed treatment, insufficient preventive measures, and public health education. A supporting study noted an annual stroke incidence rate in Africa of up to 316 per 100,000 individuals, among the highest globally, with a prevalence rate of 1460 per 100,000 reported in one region of Nigeria, Western Africa, also being among the world's highest [32]. Regarding stroke type, hemorrhagic stroke patients had a higher mortality rate of 26.1 % (95 % CI: 23–28.3). Hemorrhagic strokes are more fatal than ischemic strokes because they involve bleeding within the brain, causing immediate and severe damage. The bleeding increases intracranial pressure, compressing brain tissue and causing further injury. Additionally, hemorrhagic strokes are harder to control and treat, often leading to more complications and a higher risk of death compared to ischemic strokes, which are caused by blood clots blocking blood flow to the brain. Hemorrhagic strokes occur when brain arteries bleed or rupture, resulting in brain tissue death and damage. The estimated 5-year survival rate for hemorrhagic strokes is around 26.7 % [33].

A Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score below 8 was associated with more than a sixfold higher risk of stroke mortality (AOR 6.1, 95 % CI 3.2–12.8), while aspiration pneumonia was linked to approximately a threefold increased risk (AOR 3.0, 95 % CI 1.4–6.4). Lower GCS scores indicate severe neurological impairment and reduced consciousness, often signifying extensive brain damage. This severe impairment can lead to complications such as respiratory failure and increased intracranial pressure, thereby increasing the risk of stroke mortality [34]. Aspiration pneumonia, which occurs when food, liquid, or vomit is inhaled into the lungs, leads to infection. Stroke patients are particularly vulnerable to this condition due to weakened swallowing muscles and reduced consciousness, causing severe respiratory complications that further weaken the patient and increase mortality rates [35].

Strengths and limitations of the study

This umbrella review has several notable strengths, including a comprehensive literature search across multiple databases, standardized data extraction to minimize bias, and the use of robust statistical methods to analyze pooled data. Publication bias was also assessed and addressed through trim-and-fill analysis, enhancing the reliability of the findings. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, significant heterogeneity among the included studies may affect the precision and generalizability of the pooled estimates. Second, the exclusion of non-English publications introduces the potential for language bias. Third, despite measures to mitigate bias, publication bias remains possible, as studies with null or negative results are less likely to be published. Fourth, the number of high-quality studies on stroke mortality in Africa is limited, particularly those reporting outcomes by stroke type, region, or healthcare setting. Notably, studies differentiating stroke mortality across African regions, especially North Africa are scarce, making regional comparisons indicative of literature gaps rather than definitive conclusions. These limitations have important implications for clinical practice and future research. Clinicians should interpret pooled stroke mortality estimates with caution and consider local epidemiological and healthcare contexts. Future research should prioritize large, high-quality prospective studies across diverse African settings, with standardized reporting of stroke type, patient characteristics, and outcomes. Additionally, investigations into modifiable risk factors and effective interventions to reduce stroke mortality are essential to inform evidence-based practice and guide policy decisions.

Conclusion

The findings of this study highlight significant variations in stroke mortality across different regions of Africa, with an overall pooled prevalence of 20.3 % (95 % CI: 17.3–23.2). Western Africa exhibited the highest prevalence at 27 % (95 % CI: 14.4–39.6), while Northern Africa reported the lowest at 15 % (95 % CI: 14.7–15.3). Hemorrhagic stroke patients had a notably higher mortality rate of 26.1 % (95 % CI: 24–28.3). Systematic reviews and meta-analyses identified several factors significantly associated with stroke mortality. These include severe neurological impairment indicated by a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) <8 and the development of aspiration pneumonia. Other contributors to increased mortality included older age, presence of vascular disease, female gender, lack of aspirin treatment, and reduced renal function. Diabetes was also linked to poorer stroke outcomes at 6 and 12 months. Predictors specific to African settings, such as hemorrhagic stroke type, GCS <12, altered mental status, and severity of stroke as measured by the National Institute of Health Stroke Scale, were further highlighted in regional studies. These findings underscore the critical need for targeted interventions and healthcare strategies tailored to regional disparities and specific risk factors to mitigate stroke mortality across Africa.
