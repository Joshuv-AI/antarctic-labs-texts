Approval and delay status of United States -approved new drugs (2010–2023) in European Union, Japan, and China
Authors: Qihui Mo, Jiaqi He, Qixiang Guo, Haibo Wei, Yanxi Liu, Yue Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChina has long been faced with a drug lag. A decade has passed since China launched its pharmaceutical regulatory reforms, and related policies have been continuously optimized, yet their effectiveness remains to be evaluated. This retrospective analysis uses the United States as a reference to compare drug lags among the European Union, Japan, and China, and investigates key factors underlying lags between the US and China.MethodsThis study examined new drugs first approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) between 2010 and 2023. We conducted a descriptive analysis of key characteristics, including approval dates, drug categories, and development strategies. We further investigated factors associated with drug approval lag using the Mann–Whitney U test and analysis of covariance (ANCOVA).ResultsFrom 2010 to 2023, the FDA approved 516 new drugs. Of these, 399, 301, and 266 were authorized by the European Medicines Agency (EMA), the Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices Agency (PMDA), and the National Medical Products Administration (NMPA), respectively. China’s drug approval lag decreased from 1,667 days in 2010–2014 to 635 days in 2020–2023. Furthermore, we found that the EMA’s lag was mainly concentrated in the review phase, the PMDA’s lag was mainly concentrated in the submission phase, and the NMPA showed a substantial lag in both phases. Factors influencing drug approval lag in China include whether conditional approval was granted by the NMPA, whether FDA breakthrough therapy designation was obtained, the region of the Marketing Authorization Holder whether a domestic development strategy in China was adopted, and whether external collaborative research and development was used. From a clinical development strategy perspective, drugs undergoing Multi-Regional Clinical Trial and early bridging trials experience shorter approval lag.ConclusionsSince China’s pharmaceutical regulatory reforms, the number of newly approved drugs has increased, and approval lag has shortened, although it remains longer than that of the EMA and PMDA. We recommend that the NMPA improve review efficiency to reduce approval lag and adopt more favorable policies to encourage earlier development and submission in China, thereby alleviating submission lag.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-026-15081-1.

Background

In China, drug lag—delays in drug approval compared to other countries—has long been a persistent public health issue. This delay exacerbates unmet clinical needs among patients, challenges their access to medications, and negatively impacts public health [1–3]. Drug lag is not unique to China; it also exists in other Asian countries such as Japan and South Korea [4, 5].

China previously grappled with severe “drug lag” challenges. In 2015, it launched reforms to its drug review and approval system. On August 18 of that year, the State Council released the Opinions on the Review and Approval System for Drugs and Medical Devices—a move that officially initiated a comprehensive reform initiative [6]. Subsequently, National Medical Products Administration (NMPA) has conducted self-inspection and verification of clinical trial data, effectively addressing issues such as falsification of clinical trial data and enhancing data authenticity and successfully addressed the backlog in drug reviews through expanding the pool of reviewers and improving review efficiency [7].

In 2017, NMPA joined the International Council for Harmonisation of Technical Requirements for Pharmaceuticals for Human Use (ICH) and was elevated to membership in the ICH Management Committee in 2018. That same year, China formally implemented the ICH E17 guideline, “General Principles for the Planning and Design of Multi-Regional Clinical Trials (MRCTs),” standardizing MRCTs management protocols and design requirements while enhancing the acceptance of MRCTs results [8]. This move has aligned domestic registration standards with international norms, effectively reducing unnecessary duplicate studies, improving drug R&D efficiency, and significantly narrowing the time gap between new drug approvals in China and the US [9].

The revised Drug Administration Law of 2019 changed the approval process for drug clinical trials from a 90-day approval system to a 60-working-day silent approval mechanism. Further, in September 2025, the NMPA introduced a 30-working-day pathway applicable to key innovative drugs, pediatric and orphan drugs, and global simultaneous R&D products (innovative drugs with concurrent MRCTs conducted in China and other major global regions). The policy requires clinical trials to commence within 12 weeks after approval to accelerate the R&D progress and facilitate simultaneous development and submission [10–12]. Meanwhile, facilitating the domestic market entry of overseas-approved drugs serves as a crucial way to improve the availability and accessibility of urgently needed overseas drugs (UNODs) for Chinese patients. To this end, the Center for Drug Evaluation (CDE) issued the relevant clinical technical guidelines in October 2020, clarifying the requirements for clinical research and evaluation for overseas-marketed drugs to enter China, thereby accelerating their R&D and market launch in China [13].

China launched the UNOD policy in 2018 and has released three batches of the list of UNODs to date. The list primarily targets new drugs for rare diseases or life-threatening conditions that have been approved in the United States (US), European Union (EU), or Japan in the past decade but not yet in China [14–16] (Figure S1).

Since the reform of the drug evaluation and approval system, China’s pharmaceutical regulatory framework has undergone significant transformation. A series of proactive policies has been introduced to encourage pharmaceutical companies to conduct simultaneous domestic and overseas R&D and submissions, aimed at reducing drug lags. However, the effectiveness of these reforms in narrowing the time gap for new drug launches compared to the US, EU, and Japan still requires comprehensive evaluation. Furthermore, existing research has primarily focused on the initial outcomes of reforms or the lag in oncology drugs, with a lack of systematic analysis of influencing factors such as clinical development strategies and collaboration models for new drugs [17–19].

This study aims to analyze the approval status of FDA-approved new drugs across different regions and identify factors contributing to the delayed approval of these drugs in China.

Method

Study design

This study analyzed the approval lags and R&D status of new molecular entities (NMEs) and new therapeutic biologics approved by the FDA Center for Drug Evaluation and Research (CDER) from January 2010 to December 2023 in the EU, Japan, and China, and explored the characteristics and influencing factors of drug approval lag across these regions.

Data collection and definition.

The data for this study were sourced from review reports published by the FDA [20], European Medicines Agency (EMA) [21], Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices Agency (PMDA) [22], and NMPA [23], providing regulatory information for these drugs in the EU, Japan, and China. For those not marketed, their development status was obtained from the Insight public database [24]. The data cutoff date for all sources was October 30, 2025.

The extracted information included generic drug names, brand names, indications, Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) 1st-level classifications, New Drug Application (NDA)or Biologics License Application (BLA) submission dates and approval dates, use of expedited programs (Table S1), and clinical development strategies. The data extraction was performed independently by two individuals, and any inconsistencies were resolved by discussion.

Clinical development strategies were classified into four categories: global development strategies, bridging strategies, local development strategies, and other strategies. The global development strategy refers to MRCTs involving Chinese research centers as pivotal studies, which provide pivotal clinical data for NDA in China. The bridging strategy refers to conducting small-scale local clinical trials in China to demonstrate the similarity of drug characteristics between Chinese and non-Chinese populations, thereby extrapolating primary clinical trial data from other countries to the Chinese population. The local development strategy entails conducting all key clinical trials within China. The “Other” category includes drugs submitted on the basis of overseas clinical data without conducting clinical trials in China. For example, in three batches of UNOD lists previously issued in China, these drugs were exempt from clinical trials in China but were still required to provide evidence demonstrating the absence of racial differences [14–16].All above categorizations were based on the evaluation conclusions of the NMPA stated in its review reports [25].

Company nationality was categorized as Chinese or foreign-affiliated based on the headquarters’ location. Company types were classified by market capitalization (small: <$1 billion, medium: $1 billion to <$8 billion, large: ≥$8 billion) [26]. Marketing Authorization Holder (MAH) refers to the first entity registered in the country. If applicants differed between China and the US, the R&D strategy was considered an external collaboration.

Data on the clinical benefit of medicines was obtained from the official public assessments of the Haute Autorité de Santé (HAS). Therapeutic value was graded by HAS using its Amélioration Service Médical Rendu (ASMR) system, which classifies incremental clinical benefit into five levels: ASMR I (major improvement), ASMR II (important improvement), ASMR III (moderate improvement), ASMR IV (minor improvement), and ASMR V (no clinical improvement). All ASMR ratings were extracted from the official public database of HAS.

This study, consistent with previous research methodologies, categorizes drug lag into relative lag and absolute lag. A drug’s absolute lag is defined as having obtained FDA approval but not yet receiving approval from other regulatory agencies [27]. Relative lag is defined as the time gap between FDA approval of a new drug and subsequent approvals by other regulatory agencies and is further subdivided into submission lag and review lag. Submission lag refers to the time interval between an FDA NDA submission and an NDA submission to other regulatory agencies. Review lag is defined as the difference in review duration between the FDA and other regulatory agencies [28]. In this study, drug lag was calculated by subtracting the FDA approval date from the approval dates of the other three regulatory authorities, with the resulting time difference reported in days.

Statistical analysis

This study utilized GraphPad Prism to perform descriptive statistics on key approval features and calculate relevant lag times, using data from the FDA, NMPA, EMA, and PMDA. The review time is calculated by subtracting the NDA submission date from the NDA approval date, with the time unit in days. Continuous variables were presented as median (interquartile range [IQR]), and differences between groups were assessed using the Mann-Whitney U test. Analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was employed to identify factors associated with drug lags. All statistical analyses and graph generation were performed using GraphPad Prism (Version 9.5) and RStudio software, with statistical significance set at P < 0.05.

Result

Between 2010 and 2023, the FDA approved a total of 516 new drugs. As of the data cutoff date, 399 of these were also approved by the EMA, 301 by the PMDA, and 266 by the NMPA (Figure S2). Further analysis shows that between 2020 and November 2025, the NMPA approved 159 of these drugs, accounting for 59.8% of its total approvals; the EMA approved 155 (38.8%), and the PMDA approved 137 (45.5%) (Table 1). We further analyzed the current development status of unapproved drugs across regions. Among them, the number of undeveloped drugs was 143 (27.7%) in Japan and 140 (27.1%) in China, respectively, none of which had initiated clinical trials in the corresponding regions. In addition, several drugs were under marketing authorization application, with China having the largest number at 39 (Fig. 1 and S3).

In terms of expedited programs, the FDA has granted at least one expedited program to 340 drugs (65.9%), with priority review being the most common designation (60.5%); the NMPA has granted at least one expedited program to 155 drugs (58.3%), of which 148 (55.6%) received priority review. The relative lag situation

The submission lags situation

Among the 266 new drugs approved by the NMPA, only 17 were submitted earlier than the FDA, while the remaining 249 were submitted later than the FDA. Among the 399 new drugs approved by the EMA, 116 were submitted earlier than the FDA, with an additional 15 submitted concurrently with the FDA (with a submission time gap of 0 days). Among the 301 new drugs approved by the PMDA, 37 were submitted earlier than their FDA counterparts, while the remaining 264 were submitted later than their FDA counterparts.

In recent years, the submission lag for new drugs has continued to shorten. The median submission lag for new drugs approved by the FDA between 2020 and 2023 was 534 days at the NMPA, 245 days for the PMDA, and 41 days for the EMA. Notably, compared to 1,104 days during the 2015–2019 period, China’s median submission lag has been significantly reduced (Fig. 2).

The approval lags situation

The median approval lag time for drugs approved by the FDA between 2010 and 2014 was 1,677 days [IQR 1,177–2,776] at the NMPA, which was significantly reduced to 635 days [IQR 319–986] for the 2020–2023 period(P < 0.001); however, it still lags behind the median approval lag times of the PMDA (245 days, [IQR 49–400], P < 0.001) and the EMA (215 days, [IQR 38–684], P < 0.001) (Fig. 3).

Among the 399 drugs approved by the EMA, 91 received approval earlier than the FDA, while 215 were approved within one year of FDA approval. In contrast, China approved only 17 drugs earlier than the FDA, with most drug approvals lagging by more than three years (Figure S4).

We classified drugs based on HAS clinical benefit assessments to analyze the impact of clinical benefit level on approval lag in China. The results showed that the approval rates were 67.2% for ASMR II–III and 70.6% for ASMR IV, whereas the approval rate for ASMR V (no clinical benefit) was only 54.0%, indicating that drugs with clinical benefit were associated with higher approval rates in China. However, median approval lags were similar across groups: 1,280 days for ASMR II–III, 1,292 days for ASMR IV, and 1,263 days for ASMR V, suggesting that higher clinical benefit did not lead to shorter approval lags in China (Table S3).

The review lags situation

Overall, the median review times for new drugs by the FDA, EMA, PMDA, and NMPA are 301 days, 431 days, 301 days, and 423 days, respectively, with the FDA and PMDA demonstrating higher review efficiency.

Specifically, the NMPA’s priority review program significantly shortens the review time. The median review time for new drugs granted priority review is only 358 days, compared to 511 days for standard review, representing a statistically significant difference (Figure S5).

Influencing factors of new drug lag

Table 2 lists the key characteristics of drugs approved in China and the impact of different characteristics on the approval lag time for new drugs in China.

Among drugs approved in China, large pharmaceutical companies dominate as MAH, accounting for 77.4% of the total, while small and medium-sized pharmaceutical companies make up only 22.6%. In terms of corporate ownership, 94.4% are foreign-funded companies, with domestic companies comprising just 5.6%. Additionally, in terms of R&D strategy, pharmaceutical products currently adopting a global R&D strategy slightly outnumber those using a bridging strategy, though the difference is not significant. Additionally, it is noteworthy that 36.8% of new drugs enter China through external collaborative R&D models, such as license-in arrangements.

Analysis results indicate that factors contributing to the reduction in new drug lag time include: the MAH in China being a domestic company, not adopting an external collaboration model, obtaining conditional approval from the NMPA, and receiving Breakthrough Therapy Designation (BTD) from the U.S. FDA (all P < 0.05). Analysis of clinical development strategies for drugs with relative lag

Global development strategy

Among drugs approved by the NMPA, 78 were approved in China through MRCTs. Among these, 27 drugs underwent clinical trials conducted simultaneously across multiple global regions, with a median time lag of 1,082 days. Additionally, 51 drugs underwent clinical trials conducted exclusively in the Asian region, with a median time lag of 1,513 days (Table 3). A significant difference was observed between the two groups (P < 0.05).

Among 78 drugs approved based on MRCTs, 10 used Phase II and 68 used Phase III trials. Median approval times were 1,056 vs. 1,321 days, with no significant difference (Table 3).

Bridging strategy

Among drugs approved by the NMPA, 55 received approval in China through bridging clinical trials. Of these, 11 were early-stage bridging trials initiated before the drug’s US launch, with a median lag time of 592 days. The remaining 44 were late-stage bridging trials with a lag time of 1,454 days (Table 3). The lag times between the two groups showed a significant difference (P < 0.05).

Further analysis of the bridging trial phases reveals that 12 drugs underwent Phase III bridging trials, 18 underwent Phase II, and 25 underwent Phase I. The respective lag times were 2,031 days, 972 days, and 1,326 days. Among these, Phase III bridging trials exhibited the longest lag time, potentially attributable to factors such as larger clinical trial scale, patient enrollment duration, and trial complexity. Of the 18 drugs undergoing Phase II bridging clinical trials, 8 received conditional approval, resulting in a shorter lag time compared to drugs undergoing Phase I trials. (Table 3).

Exemption from local clinical trials strategy

Among drugs approved by the NMPA, 38 were exempted from domestic clinical trials in China, with approval supported by overseas clinical trial data. Examples include drugs listed on the “List of Overseas New Drugs with Urgent Clinical Needs” and medications used for antiviral treatment of Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV). These drugs were exempted from domestic clinical trials in China due to their urgent clinical necessity within the country.

Among FDA-approved drugs, 42 fell into the category of China’s UNODs, with 33 having received marketing approval in China. The overall median approval lag time for these drugs was 1,218 days (IQR: 802–2,740 days). However, the majority of this lag occurred prior to the publication of the UNODs list. Following the list’s release, these drugs were rapidly approved in China, with the median lag time shortening to 385 days (IQR: 130–622 days). We found that the implementation of this policy significantly accelerated the access of overseas innovative drugs to the Chinese market (Table S5).

Discussion

This study assesses the approval lags and development status of new drugs first approved by the FDA between 2010 and 2023 in the EU, Japan, and China, and examines the key factors contributing to delays in drug market entry in China.

Our results indicate that, as drug regulatory reforms in China have deepened continuously in recent years, the approval lag between China and the US has narrowed steadily, fully reflecting the remarkable achievements of drug regulatory reform. However, there are still obvious gaps compared with Japan and the EU. It is worth noting that China currently has the largest number of drugs in the NDA submission stage, with 39 in total. Among these, 30 drugs submitted their marketing applications in 2024 or 2025, suggesting that China’s appeal for drugs already approved overseas has continued to grow in recent years.

As shown in Fig. 4, we found that EMA approval lags primarily driven by longer review times, with only minor differences in submission timelines compared to the FDA. For the PMDA, approval lags mainly result from submission lags, while its review times are similar to those of the FDA. In contrast, the NMPA’s submission and review times are significantly longer than those of the FDA, leading to substantial approval lags.

In terms of absolute lag, the proportion of drugs not approved by the NMPA significantly exceeds that of the EMA, but the gap with the PMDA is relatively smaller (48.4% vs. 41.8%). In 2023, the proportion of new drugs approved in the US that experienced absolute lags in China reached 62.3% (33/53). However, we found that after the 2017 drug regulatory reforms, both the number and proportion of drugs approved in China within three years of FDA approval have risen consistently. In 2023, the number of such drugs approved in China reached 20, accounting for 37.8% of the corresponding drugs approved in the same period (Figure S6).

In terms of relative lag, the FDA and EMA consistently maintain a small-time difference. The lag time differences for the NMPA and PMDA have been steadily decreasing in recent years [2]. The median lag for drugs approved by the NMPA between 2010 and 2014 was 1,677 days. The median approval lag for drugs from 2020 to 2023 was 635 days (P < 0.05), reflecting the significant achievements of China’s pharmaceutical regulatory reforms. This finding is corroborated by Luo et al. [30], particularly in oncology, where many drugs achieved simultaneous launch in the US.

In terms of review duration, the NMPA has continuously improved its efficiency by implementing expedited review programs and providing proactive guidance. However, the NMPA’s review times remain relatively long compared with those of the PMDA and the FDA. Therefore, to further reduce approval lags for new drugs in China, efforts should focus on two key stages: first, creating a more attractive regulatory environment and market ecosystem through regulatory reform to reduce submission lags; and second, enhancing review efficiency by strengthening review capacity and providing advance guidance to optimize the review process [9, 31]. In addition, reimbursement policies are a key consideration for companies deciding whether to enter the Chinese market.

Additionally, we analyzed the factors influencing drug approval lags, and the primary factors reducing such lags are the Chinese MAH being a domestic company, the drug not adopting an external collaboration model, and the drug obtaining conditional approval from the NMPA and BTD from the FDA.

Domestic companies acting as MAHs will have shorter drug lag times compared to foreign-funded companies. This is primarily because they often prioritize launching products in China or adopt simultaneous launch strategies in China and the US. This finding is consistent with the results reported by Enya et al. [14]. External collaboration models, such as license-in or Mergers and Acquisitions(M&A), were associated with longer approval lags compared with drugs developed in-house by the same MAH. This may be attributed to the additional time costs inherent in external partnerships. A Japanese study on orphan drugs reported consistent results [26].

Drugs granted FDA BTD and NMPA conditional approval exhibited shorter approval lags. These agents are primarily intended for serious or life-threatening conditions for which there are no effective treatments, and they offer clear clinical advantages over existing therapies. They benefit from priority and rolling review, and they can be approved based on surrogate or interim endpoints or early clinical data. This effectively shortens development and review times, as well as significantly reduces drug lag in China [32].

In recent years, with the implementation of special policies such as those for UNODs, China has steadily increased its flexibility in introducing foreign new drugs through exemptions from domestic clinical trials. However, most of these approvals are conditional, requiring completion of specified post-marketing studies. Nevertheless, such policies have significantly encouraged companies to submit applications. Additionally, we found no significant difference in lag time between drugs undergoing MRCTs and those undergoing bridging studies in China [33]. This contrasts with a Japanese study, which reported a significantly shorter median lag time for the MRCT strategy than for bridging studies (204 days vs. 1,245 days) [6]. This discrepancy may be related to the implementation patterns of MRCTs in China. Some MRCTs are not initiated simultaneously in the initial global R&D phase, but instead as regional clinical trials conducted later solely for the Asian market. As such trials start at a relatively late stage, they cannot effectively shorten approval lags through early, simultaneous global development, as was observed in Japan [34].

Therefore, it is recommended to encourage the inclusion of Chinese or broader Asian populations in the initial phase of global MRCTs for pharmaceuticals, thereby avoiding the need for separate Asian MRCTs or bridging studies at later stages. Only by promoting China’s early and deep involvement in global synchronized R&D processes can we effectively reduce the time lag in bringing new drugs to market in China [35].

Conclusions

Between 2010 and 2023, the drug approval lag at the NMPA has been significantly reduced, but there remains a considerable gap compared to the PMDA and EMA. In addition, the early inclusion of Chinese research centers in MRCTs has helped to shorten the drug approval lag. Therefore, it is still necessary to further improve review efficiency and refine supporting policies to narrow the gap in drug approval timelines in China.

Limitations

When analyzing clinical development strategies, some Chinese drug review reports were unavailable, mainly due to delayed disclosure and a small number of early-approved reports not retrieved. Thus, our analysis of clinical development strategies is based solely on the 177 drugs with available review reports. As such, whether the current findings reflect an overall trend requires further validation and analysis.
