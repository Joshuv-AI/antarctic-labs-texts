Financial difficulty among patients with cancer participating in clinical trials and its association with patient survival and quality of life
Authors: Bishal Gyawali, Nan Chen, Dongsheng Tu, Joseph Pater
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe incidence and worsening of financial difficulty among patients with cancer participating in clinical trials and their relationship with mortality, quality of life, and toxicity outcomes have been understudied. The objective of this study was to understand the incidence of financial difficulty among Canadian patients participating in systemic therapy trials at baseline and after trial participation and to assess the relationship between financial difficulty and clinical outcomes.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included patients participating in 14 systemic therapy trials conducted by the Canadian Cancer Trials Group for adult patients with advanced cancer. Incidence of financial toxicity at baseline and after trial participation were measured using the European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer QLQ Core Questionnaire. The association of financial difficulty with trial characteristics and patient outcomes (mortality, quality of life, and toxicity) was studied.ResultsOverall, 2766 patients from 14 trials were included, of whom 37% had financial difficulty at baseline and 25% experienced worsening of financial difficulty after trial enrollment compared with their baseline status. Baseline financial difficulty but not the worsening of financial difficulty was associated with worse mortality outcomes. Worsening of financial difficulty was, however, associated with poor global quality of life outcomes and increased odds of clinical toxicities.ConclusionsEven in the setting of a public health system in Canada, one-third of patients experience financial difficulty, with one-quarter of them experiencing worsening of financial difficulty with trial participation, and both were related to worse patient outcomes. Targeted policy-level interventions are needed to understand and address the cause of financial difficulty in patients with cancer.

Introduction

Financial toxicity refers to the detrimental effects of the excess financial strain resulting from the diagnosis of cancer on the well-being of patients, their families, and society.1 Financial toxicity is increasingly being acknowledged as an important policy issue in oncology. Unlike other toxicities that affect the patient, financial toxicity affects the family and society at large.2 Furthermore, financial toxicity has been linked with several clinical outcomes, such as medication adherence, treatment discontinuations, poor treatment satisfaction, poor quality of life (QOL), and even poor survival.3,4

Several instruments to measure financial toxicity have been proposed in the literature.5 The COST (Comprehensive Score for Financail Toxicity) is a well-validated instrument developed to measure the burden of financial toxicity in patients with cancer in the United States6 and has subsequently been validated in Japanese patients with cancer.7 Based on a systematic review of tools to measure financial toxicity in patients with cancer, however, the most frequently used disease-specific instrument was the European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer (EORTC) questionnaire.8 One of the questions (question 28) of the EORTC QLQ Core Questionnaire (QLQ-C30) directly asks, “During the past week: Has your physical condition or medical treatment caused you financial difficulties?” to measure financial burden. Because this question specifically focuses on financial difficulties, which is one among many domains encompassed by the term financial toxicity, we refer to this measure as financial difficulty to distinguish it from the broader term, financial toxicity. The responses to this question have previously been used to understand financial difficulty among Italian patients with cancer enrolled in certain clinical trials.4 One advantage of using this EORTC questionnaire to measure financial difficulty is that, unlike other instruments, it can be used retrospectively to understand financial difficulty because these questionnaires will have been administered prospectively during the trial, so the data are already available.

Data suggest that 33% of Canadian patients with cancer experience a self-reported high financial burden.9 It is important to understand whether such financial difficulty translates to worse patient outcomes. The association of financial difficulty with outcomes such as patients’ QOL, toxicities, and survival, however, has not been studied before in the Canadian context. In this study, using the EORTC QLQ-C30 questionnaire in the clinical trials database, we aimed to study the prevalence of financial difficulty among Canadian patients with cancer participating in clinical trials and analyze its relationship with global QOL, toxicities, and survival.

Methods

This study was approved by the ethics board of Queen’s University. It was an individual patient data–level secondary analysis performed by pooling 14 prospective trials conducted by the Canadian Cancer Trial Group from 2000 to 2015, of which 13 were phase 3 trials and 1 was a phase 2 trial. From these trials, we included those patients treated in Canada who were older than 18 years of age. Only clinical trials of drugs used in advanced solid tumors were included; trials of radiation therapy [RT], surgery, or supportive care or trials in hematological malignancies or adjuvant and neoadjuvant setting were excluded to reduce the heterogeneity of the population. An EORTC QOL questionnaire was used in all these trials. All patients with a nonmissing response to question 28 of the baseline EORTC QOL-C30 from all 14 trials were included. When multiple baseline questionnaire responses were recorded by a single patient, which resulted in multiple responses to question 28, only 1 response closest to the patient’s random assignment date was used.

The objectives of this analysis were to study the prevalence of financial difficulty among patients with cancer before they participated in clinical trials (baseline financial difficulty) and worsening of financial difficulty after trial participation. We also aimed to study the relationship between baseline financial difficulty and worsening of financial difficulty with overall survival, global QOL, and severe (grade ≥3) drug toxicities.

Definition and worsening of financial difficulty

Question 28 of the EORTC QLQ-C30 asks, “Has your physical condition or medical treatment caused you financial difficulties?” with the responses coded from 1 to 4 (1 = “not at all,” 2 = “a little,” 3 = “quite a bit,” and 4 = “very much”). For our analysis, financial difficulty at baseline was categorized as “no” if the response to this question at baseline was 1 (not at all), and all other responses were categorized as “yes.” We conducted additional sensitivity analyses coding 1 and 2 as “no” for baseline financial difficulty and 3 and 4 as “yes” for baseline financial difficulty.

Worsening of financial difficulty was defined as any increase in the response score (the higher the response score, the worse the financial difficulty) to this question from baseline to any postbaseline assessments. Thus, only those patients who completed at least 1 postbaseline assessment questionnaire were included in the analyses involving worsening of financial difficulty.

Definition of variables

The global QOL score was calculated as the average of the responses to questions 29 and 30 on the EORTC QLQ-C30, which were coded from 1 (“very poor”) to 7 (“excellent”) and linearly transformed to a scale from 1 to 100, with higher scores representing better performance. Global QOL was defined as having “worsened” if there was a decrease of 10 or more points from baseline without any improvements of 10 or more from baseline on postbaseline assessments, consistent with previous recommendations.10,11 This transformation into a scale of 1 to 100, and use of 10 points in this scale as a cutoff for statistical significance, is based on the recommendations in the EORTC QLQ-C30 scoring manual.12 We chose global QOL rather than individual domains because this single metric encompasses the effect on multiple domains and can have validity across tumor types.

Clinical toxicity was assessed using Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events as the worst grade each patient experienced during the study on any of the toxicity items, which ranged from 0 (“no toxicity”) to 5 (“toxicity-related death”). It was coded as a binary variable (grade ≥3 vs grade <3) in the analysis. Overall survival was defined as the time from random assignment to death from any cause or censored at the last date of follow-up for living patients.

Statistical analysis

The Kruskal-Wallis test was used to assess the relationship between the responses to question 28 at baseline and baseline characteristics, which include trial, line of treatment, age, gender, period of enrolment, and region. These variables were selected because they each can potentially be associated with financial difficulty. For example, age (young patients may have less financial reserve and more financial responsibilities, days away from work may lead to financial difficulty, etc), gender (female patients are known to experience greater financial difficulty in other studies), tumor type, and line of therapy (certain cancers or treatments may require more frequent visits or more intervention), period of enrollment (financial difficulty may have improved or worsened with time), region (some provinces may have different coverage for treatments vs others) can each affect financial difficulty.

The association between baseline financial difficulty and patient outcomes (overall survival, worsening of global QOL, and clinical toxicity) was assessed using multivariate Cox proportional hazard and logistical regression models, respectively, adjusting for tumor type, line of treatment, age, gender, period, and region of enrollment. The hazard ratio (HR) of death and the odds ratio (OR) of worsening global QOL with 95% CIs were reported. The relationship between worsening of financial difficulty and overall survival was assessed using landmark analyses, which removed patients who died or had last information on vital status 28 days before (to remove patients with poor prognosis at random assignment) or 4.5 months before random assignment (maximum planned time to complete all QOL-C30 questions and consistent with a previous study4), with a Cox proportional hazard model adjusting for tumor type, line of treatment, age, gender, period, and region of enrollment. This exclusion of patients who had died within 28 days or 4.5 months was done only for survival analyses (not for other analyses in this study) because patients with poor prognosis at baseline may not live long enough to have survival affected by financial difficulty. The results of this analysis were reported in terms of hazard ratio and 95% CI for death. Logistical regression models adjusting for tumor type, line of treatment, age, gender, period, and region of enrollment were used to assess the association between worsening of financial difficulty as well as worsening of global QOL and clinical toxicity. The results of these analyses were reported in terms of odds ratios and 95% CIs. P values were not adjusted for multiple comparisons.

Results

Baseline financial difficulty and its association with patient characteristics

Overall, 2766 patients from 14 trials met the eligibility criteria and were included in the baseline financial difficulty analysis. One-third (33%) of patients were included from lung cancer trials, followed by patients with colorectal cancer (31%) and ovarian cancer (22%). The majority of the patients were 65 years of age or younger (60%), female (57%), and from the province of Ontario (49%). One-half of the patients were enrolled in first-line trials; the other half were enrolled in second-line or subsequent line therapy trials.

In all, 1754 (63%) patients reported no baseline financial difficulty. Baseline financial difficulty was lower among older patients (older than 65 years of age), patients enrolled in the Canadian Cancer Trials Group CE.6 Clinical Trial for brain cancer, and patients treated in the province of Quebec (Table 1).

Impact of baseline financial difficulty on patient outcomes

All analyses were conducted after adjusting for tumor type, line of treatment, age, gender, period of enrollment, and region (Table 4). After these adjustments, baseline financial difficulty remained statistically significantly associated with an increased risk of death, with a hazard ratio of 1.14 (95% CI = 1.04 to 1.26; P = .006). The odds of worsening global QOL were statistically significantly lower among patients with baseline financial difficulty (OR = 0.79, 95% CI = 0.67 to 0.94; P = .008). Similarly, baseline financial difficulty was not statistically significantly associated with the odds of experiencing grade 3 or higher adverse effects (OR = 1.12, 95% CI = 0.94 to 1.21; P = .21).

Impact of worsening financial difficulty on patient outcomes

Worsening financial difficulty was statistically significantly associated with worsening global QOL (OR = 1.79, 95% CI = 1.47 to 2.18; P < .001) as well as experiencing clinical toxicity (OR = 1.40, 95% CI = 1.14 to 1.70; P = .001), after controlling for other covariates (Table 4). Worsening of financial difficulty, however, was not statistically significantly associated with mortality risk at either of the landmark survival analyses (HR = 0.94, 95% CI = 0.84 to 1.05, P = .26 when 28 days was used as the landmark time and HR = 0.96, 95% CI = 0.85 to 1.09, P = .53 when 4.5 months was used as the landmark time).

Discussion

In this individual patient data–level analysis of 2766 adult patients enrolled in 14 clinical trials in Canada, we found that financial difficulty exists at baseline before enrollment in more than one-third of patients, with financial difficulty further worsening in one-quarter of patients after clinical trial enrollment. Baseline financial difficulty was associated with worse mortality outcomes, and worsening financial difficulty was associated with worsening QOL as well as higher chances of experiencing severe drug toxicities. These data provide important information to address financial difficulty among patients with cancer being enrolled into clinical trials and offers further clarity on the relationship between financial difficulty and patient outcomes.

The incidence of financial difficulty at baseline (ie, at the beginning of trial participation) in our analysis was 37%. This rate is consistent with the reported incidence of financial difficulty among patients with cancer in Canada (range = 33%-40%).13 In Italy, 26% of patients reported baseline financial difficulty at trial enrolment.4 In 25% of patients in our study, however, financial difficulty worsened after trial participation, which is also consistent with the Italian study that reported worsening of financial difficulty in 23% of patients after trial participation. Whether trial participation in itself leads to worsening of financial difficulty is not known but should be explored further because clinical trial participation usually does not entail any direct costs to the patient. Supportive care costs (except for oral drugs) are also covered under public health systems, but the added visits to the cancer center due to trial participation could contribute to higher financial difficulty stemming from the ancillary costs of trial participation. Indeed, hidden costs, such as travel, parking,14 food, and time away from work, could all lead to worsening of financial difficulty among trial participants.1 The absence of comparable data from nontrial participants, however, makes it difficult to ascribe the worsening of financial difficulty to trial participation. Our findings highlight the need to study in further detail whether trial participation leads to worsening financial difficulty and, if it did, investigate the causes to plan interventions.

We found a substantially elevated risk of mortality among trial participants with baseline financial difficulty, even after adjusting for other covariates. Financial difficulty has been known to be associated with increased risk of nonadherence to medications,15 bankruptcy,16 treatment dissatisfaction,17 treatment discontinuation,18 and poor QOL19 in the United States. In a public health system such as Canada’s, where patients do not need to pay for treatment out of pocket, it is surprising that financial difficulty was associated with worse survival. Indeed, in the previously mentioned Italian study, baseline financial difficulty was not associated with survival outcomes.4 The mechanism for this association remains unknown and could imply some underlying relationship between baseline financial difficulty and poor socioeconomic status, a hypothesis that needs to be tested. In a previous study of cancer clinical trial participants in the United States, area-level socioeconomic deprivation was associated with worse survival,20 which is aligned with our own findings. The authors of this study hypothesized that this worse survival may be due to reduced access to supportive care or postprotocol therapy or to differences in health status not reflected by protocol selection criteria. We agree with the authors and advocate that financial difficulty should be included as a parameter under socioeconomic determinants of cancer care. Baseline financial difficulty was associated with lower odds of worsening global QOL in our study, possibly because patients with baseline financial difficulty already have a poor global QOL.

Worsening financial difficulty with trial participation, in contrast, had opposite results to baseline financial difficulty. Worsening of financial difficulty was associated with worsening of global QOL but not with increased risk of mortality. Although there is no rationale for why worsening financial difficulty should necessarily lead to increased risk of mortality, our finding is in contrast to that of the Italian study, which showed increased risk of mortality with worsening of financial difficulty.4 This finding is surprising because both Italy and Canada have public health systems that cover cancer-related treatment expenses. Only 7% of patients in the Italian analysis were from second-line trials, however, and an equal percentage of patients were from adjuvant trials; this is in contrast to our analysis, where there were no adjuvant trials and the majority of patients (50%) were from second-line or subsequent-line trials. Thus, we believe that patients’ survival in our analysis was more profoundly dictated by the poor prognosis of patients overshadowing any potential detrimental effects worsening of financial difficulty could have had on survival. This hypothesis is also supported by our findings that the odds of worsening financial difficulty was higher in second-line trial participants than in first-line trial participants in our study.

Our study has highlighted concerns regarding the high prevalence of financial difficulty among patients with cancer in Canada and its association with increased risk of mortality as well as worsening financial difficulty after trial participation. Clinical trial participants are known to have a higher socioeconomic status, awareness, and overall health status than patients in the real world.21-23 Thus, the magnitude of the problem in the real world is probably higher than what our study reflects. Most importantly, we show that despite publicly funded health-care systems, patients do continue to experience financial difficulty because of cancer treatments, calling for an urgent need to plan policy interventions to address this problem. Indeed, financial difficulty is not only a result of direct treatment costs but also includes costs related to absences from work as well as transportation, accommodation, and food costs.1,5 This finding is consistent with the recent report from the Canadian Cancer Society, which highlighted that even in the Canadian cancer care system, 20% of costs are probably borne by patients and their families.24

Patients older than 65 years of age in our study not only had a lower incidence of baseline financial difficulty but also lower odds of worsening of financial difficulty, probably because older patients have fewer financial needs and feel the burden less; they are usually retired, as well, so being away from work does not affect them directly in terms of lost income. In addition, most medications are available free of cost for older patients, while some medications, especially oral medications, are borne out of pocket for younger patients in many provinces in Canada. Furthermore, young patients have higher expenses related to mortgages, child rearing, etc; thus, subjective experience of financial hardship is higher in younger patients. This finding is consistent with data from the United States showing that younger patients are at a higher risk than older adults of financial hardship.25

Some limitations must be considered when interpreting our results. First, we defined financial difficulty based on 1 question on the QLQ-C30. Although this definition has been used previously to define financial difficulty in the Italian context, whether this definition truly captures the burden and nuances of financial difficulty remains unknown. We have previously defined financial toxicity as a spectrum that includes economic burden not only to patients but also to their family and society1; thus, this 1 question–based assessment fails to capture all the dimensions. For a secondary analysis of clinical trials, however, we believe that this definition provides a good proxy. Future studies that prospectively study financial toxicity should use a definition that adequately captures the subjective burden of the consequences of economic hardships, consistent with the recommendations in a recent review of financial toxicity definitions in the literature.5

Second, as discussed above, clinical trial participants are not necessarily representative of the real-world population, so our results probably underestimate the burden. Third, no causative relationship between trial participation and worsening of financial difficulty can be implied based on our study methods. Indeed, 9% of participants even had improvements in financial difficulty following trial enrollment. These results, based on analysis of patients participating in clinical trials, may or may not apply to patients being treated outside of trials. We used trial participants in this study to take advantage of a large convenience sample to explore the prevalence and impact of financial difficulty on patients with cancer in Canada. Similarly, whether the QOL impact is driven by financial difficulty vs other factors, such as disease progression, cannot be ruled out, although QOL assessments in these trials were made before documented disease progression. Furthermore, QOL analyses in clinical trials are usually complicated because of the higher number of dropouts. In our dataset, overall QOL completion rates were above 60% (see Supplementary material). Because we included all patients with both baseline and at least 1 postbaseline QOL assessment, patients who dropped out due to worsening of financial difficulty will have been captured in our analysis. For patients who dropped out earlier for reasons unrelated to financial difficulty, we believe that it is reasonable to assume that these dropouts are at random and therefore would not affect our conclusions. Finally, our selection of the clinical trials was not systematic or random but was based on the availability of clinical trial data with QOL questionnaire results.

In conclusion, patients with cancer, even in a public health system, experience financial difficulty that can worsen during the course of treatment, even within the setting of a clinical trial. This financial difficulty is associated with poor survival, and worsening of financial difficulty is associated with poor QOL. Thus, interventions are needed to address financial difficulty both at baseline and during the course of treatment or participation in clinical trials.
