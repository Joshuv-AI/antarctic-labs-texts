Providing Choice: Exploring Maternal Perceptions of Infant Feeding When Supplementation Is Required
Authors: Oby Ezeigbo, Jannette Festival, Christine Ou, Jennifer Dion, Heather Christine Rusi, Meredith Brockway
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTExclusive breastfeeding is recommended for the first 6 months of life. However, many full‐term infants require supplementation in the first few days of life, with formula often being the only supplementation option provided. With increased awareness and availability, donor human milk is becoming a viable alternative for supplementation in full‐term infants. This study aimed to understand how having a choice to supplement infants with donor human milk rather than formula informed maternal experiences with infant feeding. Using a qualitative descriptive design, we conducted 15 semi‐structured interviews with mothers who chose to supplement their infant with donor human milk. The semi‐structured interview guide was co‐developed with milk banking associations to elicit feeding goals, perceptions of donor human milk, infant feeding experiences in the first weeks of life and perceived well‐being, health, and feelings around supplementation. Interviews were analyzed using reflexive thematic analysis. Of the 15 participants, over half delivered via cesarean section (n = 8), the majority were primiparous and had no previous breastfeeding experience (n = 12) and most wanted to exclusively breastfeed their infant (n = 11). Four overarching themes were identified: (1) feeding experience, (2) supplementation, (3) maternal well‐being, and (4) feasibility. Choosing donor human milk positively impacted participant mental health, helped relieve stress from the pressure to breastfeed, and provided peace of mind. Donor human milk may provide a feasible intervention that may help to mitigate maternal distress by providing a supplement that is more to similar breastmilk, while breastfeeding is being established.

Introduction

While the World Health Organization (WHO) recommends that infants are exclusively breastfed for the first 6 months of life (World Health Organization 2003), 40%–60% of full‐term infants require supplementation while breastfeeding is being established (Forbes et al. 2018; Francis et al. 2021; Mildon et al. 2022). Hospital protocols around hypoglycemic, weight loss, as well as issues with establishing breastfeeding (latch, perceived insufficient milk) often result in newborn infants requiring supplementation (Bookhart et al. 2022; Mildon et al. 2022). Formula is the standard supplementation option for these infants. However, with increasing awareness and availability of donor human milk (DHM) from accredited human milk banks, access to DHM is now becoming a feasible supplementation option for full‐term infants (Belfort et al. 2018; Bramer et al. 2021). Additionally, commercialized human milk products from for‐profit human milk companies have become increasingly available in hospitals (Rusi et al. 2024; Shenker et al. 2024). Breastfeeding initiation rates in many Western Countries are high, exceeding 90% in Canada and Australia, and 80% in the United States (Public Health Agency of Canada 2022). However, mothers often feel intense pressure to exclusively breastfeed their infants and can experience negative emotions including guilt, failure, and fear of being stigmatized (Fallon et al. 2017) when they are required to supplement their infant with formula. Further, feelings of guilt are amplified when breastfeeding goals are unmet (Jackson et al. 2021) which occurs in over 60% of breastfeeding families (Odom et al. 2013). These feelings of guilt have been associated with negative perceptions of infant feeding experiences and anger towards healthcare workers (Smyth and Hyde 2020). Formula feeding has also been associated with increased rates of postpartum depression and maternal anxiety (Yuen et al. 2022). Adverse maternal mental health conditions reduce overall maternal well‐being and can negatively affect infant development (Rogers et al. 2020).

Evidence indicates that reassuring mothers that their infant is receiving the safest and best possible breastmilk substitute helps to ameliorate negative emotions they may be experiencing (Smyth and Hyde 2020). DHM is more biologically similar to mother's own milk (MOM) than infant formula (Pound et al. 2020). In many countries, provision of DHM for preterm and medically fragile infants is emerging as the standard of care when supplementation of MOM is required as preterm infant have significantly improved outcomes with DHM compared to formula (Quigley et al. 2019). However, families are rarely provided a choice between DHM and formula when supplementation is required in the full‐term postpartum setting. Providing mothers with a choice to supplement with DHM instead of formula may help to alleviate maternal feelings of guilt and failure, which are related to maternal experiences of postpartum depression and stress (Caldwell et al. 2021).

Traditionally, DHM has been restricted to use for infants who are hospitalized due to illness or prematurity. This is mainly due to limited availability (McCune and Perrin 2021) resulting from several factors including logistical challenges related to DHM (Brown et al. 2024) and lack of evidence on the impacts of DHM in the full‐term population (McCune and Perrin 2021). As the momentum of nonprofit milk banking builds and the use of commercialized human milk increases, there are now increasing opportunities to offer families of non‐hospitalized infants DHM in place of formula when supplementation is required. This study emerged as an evaluation of a pilot program in Canada that aims to provide DHM to infants as an option to formula supplementation on a postpartum hospital unit.

Pilot Program

The local milk bank, accredited by the Human Milk Banking Association of North America (HMBANA), developed a pilot project to provide families the option to purchase DHM for their full‐term infant while admitted the postpartum unit at a tertiary care facility in a province in Western Canada. In this healthcare setting, families whose infants require or choose supplementation while in hospital are provided with infant formula, free of cost. The milk bank is a not‐for‐profit organization, that sells pasteurized milk, donated from screened lactating parents, at $18 per bottle on a cost recovery basis. A substantial component of their annual fundraising activities is dedicated to the medical relief program that provides DHM to families of vulnerable infants who do not qualify DHM in the healthcare setting and cannot afford to purchase DHM.

The pilot program offered families the option to purchase DHM supplied from the local accredited milk bank using an online purchasing system. Families could purchase up to 10 bottles at $18 per bottle (120 mL per bottle) and those who could not afford DHM were offered financial support through a medical relief program provided by the local milk bank. Parents who declined to purchase DHM due to financial concerns were offered access through the medical relief program. Access to the medical relief program is based on the honors system and the families will receive one‐two bottles of milk during their hospital stay. To our knowledge, this is one of the first opportunities within Canada for parents to access DHM in a maternity unit caring for healthy, term newborns. The aim of the current study was to understand how having a choice to supplement infants with DHM rather than formula informed maternal experiences with infant feeding.

Methods

We conducted a qualitative descriptive study (Sandelowski 2000; Sandelowski 2010) of 15 mothers who chose to supplement their infants with DHM while admitted to the postpartum unit at a tertiary care center in a province in Western Canada. In adherence to maintaining straight description and remaining close to the data within qualitative descriptive designs, we engaged in naturalistic inquiry, employing an inductive approach with low inference (Kim et al. 2017; Lincoln and Guba 1985; Sandelowski 2010).

Study Design and Population

Parents with full‐term infants who required supplementation after breastfeeding initiation were informed about DHM by their nurse or the unit lactation consultant. If parents were interested in DHM, they were provided with an educational brochure explaining the benefits and safety of DHM and how to access it. Interested parents were directed to the website to purchase DHM and complete intake questions. One of the intake questions asked parents if they were interested in discussing their infant feeding experiences for research purposes. Breastfeeding parents who indicated interest in participating in research were contacted via telephone or email at 4–12 weeks postpartum to be invited for an interview. Maternal recall for breastfeeding demonstrates high reliability, including maternal recall of infant feeding and maternal lactation practices, up to 6‐year postpartum (Li et al. 2020). As such, the interview timeframe of 4–12 weeks postpartum should accurately capture maternal experiences of breastfeeding and supplementation. The participants selected to be interviewed had replied via email to the primary author's invitation to participate. Only parents who purchased DHM through the online system were accessible to researchers to contact. As such, parents who chose not to purchase DHM were omitted from the study. Parents who were not planning on breastfeeding, those with infants admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU), or infants who had a diagnosis that could interfere with breastfeeding (cleft lip or palate, Trisomy 21, or congenital heart disease) were excluded.

Interview Process

From April 2023 to January 2024, 15 interviews were conducted via Zoom. Informed consent was obtained verbally from all participants. The semi‐structured interview guide was co‐developed with the director of the local milk bank with input from directors of the HMBANA (DiCicco‐Bloom and Crabtree 2006).

The interview guide (Appendix 1) consisted of open‐ended questions which captured information about infant feeding goals, experiences with infant feeding, perceptions of DHM, perceived mental health, and feelings around supplementation. The interview guide was modified slightly after half the interviews were conducted to better capture participants' feelings on supplementation. Interviews were audio recorded and transcribed using the Zoom transcription feature. Recruitment and interviews continued until informational redundancy was achieved, with no new topics or concepts emerging.

Analysis

Reflexive thematic analysis was conducted to interpret interview data and identify key themes (Braun and Clarke 2006, 2019; Clarke and Braun 2021). NVivo software (Version 14) (Lumivero 2023) was used to conduct the thematic analysis. Transcripts were reviewed and coded by two researchers. Initial themes were constructed by searching for shared meanings and patterns in the coding, which were then organized into a thematic table. Codes were revisited to refine the themes and ensure that they captured key ideas. Following the coding process, broader categories were developed and discussed to form emergent themes. These themes were organized into a thematic network (Figure 1) to better visualize the phenomenon of interest. The primary author conducted the initial coding, and the supervising author reviewed and verified themes. Ethical Considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the University of Calgary Conjoint Health Ethics Research Board (REB22‐1557) prior to data collection. Prior to each interview, the study was fully explained to each participant with a chance to have all questions about the research answered. Upon having all questions addressed, each participant provided oral consent prior to continuing with the interview. Participant names were converted to pseudonyms and stored on a master list. Participant information and interview files were stored on the secure University server (SharePoint/ShareFile), separate from the master list with identifying information with all files being encrypted and password protected.

Results

Four major organizing themes (1) Feeding Experience, (2) Supplementation, (3) Maternal Well‐being, (4) Feasibility and 14 associated subthemes were identified (Figure 1).

The first three themes explore participant's personal experiences and challenges with breastfeeding and supplementation, emphasizing the impact on maternal well‐being and feelings. The final theme addresses the feasibility and challenges of using DHM as a supplementation option.

Feeding Experience

Many mothers felt unprepared for the challenges of breastfeeding and these challenges were intensified by the pressures to feed their newborns while in the postpartum unit. Difficulties with breastfeeding or meeting hospital feeding guidelines, combined with the requirement to supplement, often resulted in feelings of shame, guilt, and failure.

Challenge of Breastfeeding

Breastfeeding presented considerable challenges for many participants. Some participants were surprised at how difficult learning to breastfeed was or were unaware that there would be as many challenges with a “natural” process. One participant explained that “it (breastfeeding) is probably the most challenging thing I found about motherhood” (Daisy, primiparous). These difficulties were compounded by factors including traumatic or unplanned deliveries or exhaustion in the early postpartum period. Noah (primiparous) shared, “I think my c‐section made a huge difference on my breastfeeding experience…It added that one extra added layer of difficulty in trying to learn how to breastfeed.”

Pressure to Feed

Many participants reported feeling pressure to feed their infants, with their own milk or via supplementation, during their hospital stay. This pressure stemmed from both internal sources, such as personal expectations, and external sources, including healthcare staff and hospital policies. While some participants felt pressured to breastfeed, there was an emphasis from nurses on meeting specific feeding volumes at scheduled times, regardless of the feeding method. One participant said, “the emphasis is put on the volume feeding in the hospital, that you have to meet this feeding schedule” (Grace, primiparous). Betty (primiparous) described her experience as “it felt…really rushed…they came into the room, and they said, in 20 min, you're going to have to feed your kid, and you don't have supply.” Many participants found these schedules and protocols imposed on them increased pressure to feed their infant.

Shame, Fault, Failure

Many participants experienced feelings of failure, shame, and guilt when unable to exclusively breastfeed, produce an adequate milk supply, or they had to introduce supplementation. Some participants described breastfeeding as defining their worth and success as mothers. Participants shared, “I feel like breast milk was defining my worth now as a new mom.” (Lisa, multiparous) and “if your goal is to breastfeed and…you can't…then that could be perceived almost as somehow you're failing” (Maria, primiparous). When having to introduce supplementation, participants expressed feeling discouraged and had internal criticisms of themselves especially if they had an unexpected delivery outcome. Lisa (multiparous) explained, “for me it felt like another shame I had… not only that my delivery did not go well, now I can't breastfeed, so it was a lot of pressure.”

Additionally, some participants expressed difficulties with having to supplement because they felt unable to give their infant “the best” nutrition or provide their own breastmilk for their infants. However, some participants perceived supplementation not as a personal failure, but to ensure their infant was healthy and well‐fed. According to Stacey (primiparous), “if I couldn't produce it's not about me it's about him being healthy and thriving.”

Supplementation

Some mothers did not anticipate the need for supplementation, and when it became necessary, they expressed feelings of stress or disappointment. Jessica (primiparous) shared “I didn't want to have to supplement was the only thing that didn't work out for me.”

In contrast, others were more indifferent, especially if they had already been open to various infant feeding options. While some participants showed readiness to supplement by purchasing formula in advance, many were reluctant to use it due to concerns about its potential negative impact on their infants' health. When supplementation was required, many mothers preferred DHM, viewing it as the closest alternative to breastmilk and more aligned with their feeding goals. They often perceived DHM as a healthier option than formula and were hesitant to expose their infants to formula.

DHM Similar to Breastmilk

Many participants discussed how they preferred to breastfeed or to feed their infants' breastmilk and therefore felt that supplementing with DHM better aligned with those goals, allowing them to more closely adhere to their intended feeding plan. Grace (primiparous) expressed that by giving DHM “we were somehow aligning with the goals that we had, even if it wasn't my breastmilk.”

Participants associated DHM with breastmilk due to the perceived similarities. Many even referred to DHM as breastmilk, although they voiced differences between DHM and mother's own expressed breastmilk. Many participants were more inclined to choose DHM as a supplementation option because they felt that it closely resembled their own breastmilk. Additionally, most participants felt more comfortable providing DHM to their infants since it is a form of human milk and a more natural supplementation option. Daisy (primiparous) shared, “I think the biggest pro to using donor milk was that it was as close to what he would be getting from me, it was closer than what formula is.”

Furthermore, participants wanted to supplement with DHM because they perceived it shared similar health benefits with breastmilk. Many participants specifically highlighted advantages of DHM such as “enhanced immunity,” “improved growth” and “cognitive development,” and the potential to reduce the risk of type 2 diabetes. Jessica (primiparous) expressed that “breastmilk was a lot better option for him, so I think in terms of…health reasons, I wanted him to only have that if possible.”

DHM Healthier Than Formula

Many participants sought to limit or entirely avoid supplementing their infants with formula, citing concerns about potential negative effects. Many believed formula could lead to increased digestive issues and emphasized that infants tend to absorb breastmilk more easily. One participant noted “I think there are things in breastmilk that are not in formula…. and then I have heard they absorb it and take breastmilk better than formula” (Melinda, primiparous). Other participants noted that introducing formula may complicate the transition back to their own breastmilk and breastfeeding.

Furthermore, most participants believed that DHM was healthier and offered better nutritional value for their infants compared to formula. When discussing health benefits, many highlighted the presence and importance of antibodies in DHM, noting that it's a key component that is absent in formula. Jennifer (multiparous) conveyed “I feel like donor milk is still better than formula… It provides more nutrition… at least those antibodies.”

Willingness to Supplement

While some participants purchased formula prior to delivery indicating a willingness to supplement, their readiness to do so was influenced by their knowledge and understanding of supplementation. These participants obtained formula as a security measure due to previous experience or knowledge about having difficulties with milk supply early postpartum. For example, one participant shared that “I did end up obtaining formula anyways just in case, because I've heard of instances where the supply doesn't come in right away” (Shelley, primiparous). Additionally, some participants mentioned that they were aware of and open to supplementation before they delivered their infant. Jennifer (multiparous) said, “because of my first, my son, I didn't have enough milk in the first week, so we already had formula at home.”

Being Required to Supplement

Despite some participants showing willingness to supplement, most did not anticipate needing to supplement. When they were required to supplement, some expressed feeling stressed or disappointed over not grasping breastfeeding immediately. Further, some participants were reluctant to supplement, particularly when it conflicted with their expectations of what their breastfeeding experience would be like. Participant, Melinda (primiparous) expressed, “in some ways it's [supplementation]… has made things a bit stressful.”

However, other participants were open‐minded about supplementation. Some saw it positively, viewing it as a way to “top up” (Jessica, primiparous) their infants after breastfeeding. Others preferred not to restrict their infants to one feeding option, prioritizing the idea that “fed is best.” One participant shared, “we weren't limiting ourselves to… breastmilk only or …only formula. So, we approached it with an open mind” (Jill, primiparous).

Maternal Well‐Being

Most participants appreciated having options when they were required to supplement and reported positive experiences with DHM. Many felt that DHM provided them with time to figure out breastfeeding, offering peace of mind, and alleviating feelings of failure, guilt, and shame around milk production and supply.

Time to Figure It Out

Participants noted that DHM helped bridge their infants before they transitioned to providing their own breastmilk, allowing them more time to navigate breastfeeding and make informed decisions about their feeding plans. Many felt reassured that their infants were still receiving optimal nutrition with DHM, which lessened their disappointment about needing to supplement and gave them more time to address challenges with breastfeeding. DHM provided extra time to adjust and helped normalize these difficulties, thus reducing feelings of failure or shame. Participant Noah conveyed “it helped bridge until I was able to breastfeed, so I needed him to be fed until I could sort my own stuff.”

Peace of Mind

Most participants mentioned that having DHM as a supplementation option was like a safety net, which gave them peace of mind. Many felt a sense of security that their infants were obtaining optimal nutrition when receiving DHM, referring to it as “good food” or “proper food” (Melinda, primiparous). Participants expressed relief at having DHM as a backup, particularly if their infants were already accustomed to breastmilk. Additionally, others noted that DHM helped reduce their stress around breastfeeding and alleviated anxiety about their milk supply, especially for those who desired to exclusively breastfeed. One participant said “…just knowing…he can have… proper food made me feel…less stressed” (Melinda, primiparous) and another participant shared, “I think it was less stressful because it was closer to what I was trying to achieve” (Zuri, primiparous).

Having a Choice

Many participants were thankful to have a choice when infant supplementation was required, primarily on the premise that multiple options are better than one. Most preferred having DHM and formula as supplementation options, rather than exclusively relying on formula. Many noted that having this choice positively affected their mental health. Participant Jessica (primiparous) shared, “I think it helped me in a positive way with the fact that I had a more traumatic birth.”

Some participants discussed the importance of having options due to the various reasons for needing supplementation, noting that the availability of choices helped maintain trust and confidence in their care. According to Shelley (primiparous), “the fact that they [healthcare providers] gave me a choice [in supplementation options] instilled more confidence in them as competent professionals.”

Other participants were indifferent about having a choice regarding supplementation options. While they appreciated the availability of DHM, they were equally content if formula was their only option. One participant shared, “I would have been fine with formula if that was the only option” (Noah, primiparous).

Feasibility

Many participants reported positive experiences with the pilot program and felt reassured by the safety and screening processes for DHM. However, their primary concerns were related to the cost and accessibility of DHM. Additionally, many expressed a desire for greater awareness of DHM, particularly among healthcare professionals.

Cost

Cost was highlighted as a potential barrier to purchasing DHM by all participants. Many noted that exclusively supplementing with DHM would be costly, with some stating they would opt for formula as a more affordable long‐term solution. Participants were also cognizant of the limited options available to lower‐income families who could not afford DHM, with many advocating for financial support for these mothers. One participant expressed that “I'm very lucky that I can afford this right now but there are people who obviously can't afford this, and they wouldn't have a choice” (Daisy, primiparous).

Some participants felt that DHM was worth the cost, even with financial constraints, due to the quality and advantages it provides. For some, the cost was not a deterrent, especially when they viewed purchasing DHM as doing what was best for their infants. Stacey (primiparous) shared “for us it wasn't about the money, it was making the choice of what was best for our baby.”

Accessibility

Many participants highlighted the ease of purchasing and accessing DHM in the hospital. Although participants valued the convenience of readily accessible DHM in the hospital, some encountered obstacles after discharge. These included restrictions such as the 10‐maximum cap on the number of bottles they could purchase. Participants who preferred supplementing with DHM faced difficulties accessing DHM if they lived outside the city. One participant who lives in a different city, stated that “I wish that we could have availability down here where I live to buy it… I would buy that over formula in a heartbeat” (Stacey, primiparous). Additionally, some participants expressed a desire to supplement with DHM more frequently if there were no limitations on the number of bottles they could buy. According to Sam (primiparous), “I would have preferred to supplement with just the donor milk, but he was eating so much I couldn't get more than the 10 bottles.”

Awareness

A few participants learned about DHM and the milk bank through word of mouth or other sources, but the majority were unaware of DHM and wished they had known about it earlier. Zuri (primiparous) expressed, “I didn't really have in my head that it [DHM] would be offered at the hospital so I wasn't really aware that it would be that easy to do something like that.” Most felt that increased awareness of DHM, especially among healthcare professionals would be beneficial as they had not learned about it from their healthcare provider. Lisa (multiparous) expressed, “if you could get more awareness around it is key. I think more education for the nurses around that it's available”. Additionally, some participants emphasized the importance of advocacy and education about DHM before birth, particularly during prenatal visits, so expecting parents can be better informed.

Acceptability

A few participants were initially hesitant about using DHM because the milk originates from various donors. One participant shared “I think before having a baby, I would have been kind of grossed out by the idea of having someone else's breastmilk for my baby” (Stacey, primiparous). Some even anticipated resistance from older generations toward accepting DHM as a supplementation option. One participant shared, “we didn't tell a set of grandparents because we thought they would be quite judgmental about it” (Daisy, primiparous). Others expressed concerns about the hygiene and safety of DHM for their infants but felt reassured by the screening and pasteurization processes. Stacey (primiparous) stated, “the mothers are screened…and its pasteurized and it's coming from a good source… so that also gave me peace of mind.” Further, a few participants wished they had inquired more about the potential risks associated with DHM. One participant expressed, “I wonder if I should have…thought about if there were any risks…I know there's the possibility you might pick up something even though it's pasteurized” (Melinda, primiparous).

Discussion

While DHM may be a beneficial supplementation alternative to formula and act as a bridge for mothers while establishing breastfeeding (Picaud 2022), the potential effects on the initiation, duration, and maintenance of exclusive breastfeeding should be considered first and foremost. In a systematic review of the impact of DHM on breastfeeding rates, DHM was shown to have a positive effect on any breastfeeding rates upon hospital discharge with no impact on exclusive breastfeeding upon discharge (Williams et al. 2016). While one study reported a significant decline in MOM after DHM was provided (Williams et al. 2016), other studies found that DHM supplemented infants were more likely to be exclusively breastfeeding at 4–6 months compared to infants supplemented with formula (Mathias et al. 2023). As such, it is likely that supplementing with DHM likely neither improves nor harms exclusive breastfeeding rates at discharge and may help to improve rates of any breastfeeding, compared to supplementing with formula.

While DHM may present as a beneficial supplementation option compared to formula, support for breastfeeding and providing MOM to infants should remain the priority of healthcare providers when supplementation of breastfeeding is required. This is important for nurses and other healthcare providers who support mothers with lactation and infant supplementation. DHM may be viewed as a more natural and easier supplementation option compared to formula. While DHM is superior to infant formula, it is not equivalent to MOM (Parker et al. 2024). Therefore, nurses and other healthcare providers should be aware of this and ensure their focus of practice is on supporting mothers to express breast milk or breast feed while using DHM as a temporary supplementation option or as a bridge to breastfeeding (Reimers and Coutsoudis 2021). Further research is also needed surrounding nurse's perspectives and decision making around supplementation and supporting breastfeeding.

Having a Choice

The importance of having choice in shaping maternal experiences with infant feeding during the early postpartum period aligns with findings from a recent scoping review exploring facilitators for receiving donor milk (Mathias et al. 2023). Fear of health risks of formula and an understanding of the importance of human milk were cited as motivations for choosing DHM (Mathias et al. 2023). Clinically, an important consideration when counseling families around using DHM, is to emphasize that while DHM is more similar to breastmilk compared to formula; however, it is not a direct replacement as pasteurization reduces many nutritional and bioactive components (Colaizy 2021). Minimal comparisons have been made between health outcomes of infants who are fed DHM compared to formula or MOM. Further research on using DHM to supplement full‐term infants needs to be conducted to better understand the safety profile of DHM use in this population (McCune and Perrin 2021).

Well Being

The findings that participants reported improved well‐being when DHM was presented as a supplementation option are supported by previous findings that provision of DHM helps to take the pressure off while mothers were working to build their own breastmilk supply (Brown et al. 2024). Pressure to breastfeed is a common occurrence that many mothers experience from their families, healthcare professionals and society (Korth et al. 2022). Often, breastfeeding promotion strategies result in increased pressure on mothers. It is evident across studies that providing DHM as a supplementation option consistently alleviates the pressure to provide milk or breastfeed while mothers are trying to establish breastfeeding (Brown et al. 2024; Brown and Shenker 2022). Offering informed, non‐directive supplementation options in postpartum care may improve maternal perceptions of infant feeding experiences. This reinforces the importance of shared decision‐making around infant feeding in early postpartum care.

The positive impact that DHM has on improving maternal well‐being may help to reduce anxiety and improve maternal mental health. Negative experiences with breastfeeding can result in adverse associations with maternal mental health and emotional distress (Yuen et al. 2022). Previous research has revealed that providing DHM as an option to families who are required to supplement reduced anxiety over their baby's health because parents believed that human milk protects infant health and they did not have to worry about the impacts of using formula (Brown et al. 2024). This sense of well‐being also translated into a significant decrease in both anxiety and depression scores after mothers received DHM (Brown et al. 2024). Further, anxiety is associated with earlier cessation of breastfeeding and lower breastfeeding self‐efficacy (Fallon et al. 2016). While this study demonstrates that offering DHM as a supplementation option may help to alleviate the stress that temporary breastfeeding difficulties present, minimal evidence exists on how this translates into quantitative mental health measure. As such, future research should examine if offering DHM as a supplementation option can meaningfully improve maternal mental health outcomes. Additionally, there is inconsistent evidence on how supplementation with DHM in early life impacts breastfeeding outcomes (McClintock et al. 2024). Future research needs to carefully assess breastfeeding exclusivity and duration for infants who are supplemented with DHM in early life to better understand the relationships between DHM supplementation and infant feeding outcomes.

Equity, Accessibility, and Feasibility

Barriers to access highlighted in this study including high cost, limited availability outside urban centers, restrictions on the number of bottles, and low public and professional awareness are also reflected in evidence from the USA, where mothers felt that the cost and accessibility of DHM was a substantial concern and would limit them from accessing it after discharge from the hospital (Kair and Flaherman 2017). Additionally, Brown et al. (Brown et al. 2024) identified privilege as a prominent theme in their research exploring the experiences of UK mothers receiving DHM, where mothers acknowledged that many families do not have the financial ability to supplement with DHM. These challenges reflect broader structural inequities in access to human milk and align with international concerns regarding disparities in DHM distribution (Weaver et al. 2024). Healthcare providers should also be aware of accessibility issues related to DHM distribution while supporting families with infant feeding (Kashyap and Choudhari 2024).

The knowledge and perception of DHM can shape supportive attitudes towards breastfeeding and encourage increased positive public awareness (Shenker et al. 2024). Globally, there have been calls to shift away from a dependence on infant formula and emphasize human milk feeding, through exclusive breastfeeding and increasing accessibility to DHM as a supplementation option (Mathias et al. 2023). However, much work remains in increasing awareness, funding, and global access to DHM as availability remains largely restricted to preterm and critically ill infants and to those who have the means to pay for it. Future research needs to explore the effects of DHM supplementation in other vulnerable infant populations, including certain groups of full‐term infants to identify other infant populations that would benefit from DHM as a supplementation option. Increasing evidence in this area would inform policy and practice guidelines to improve equity in DHM access (Human Milk Banking Association of North America 2023).

Lack of knowledge or experience on the use of DHM by healthcare providers, which was noted as a significant barrier in this study, has previously been cited as a barrier to use (Mathias et al. 2023). This also underscores the importance of incorporating information about DHM in perinatal education classes as well as into the training programs for front‐line health workers who work with breastfeeding families (Mathias et al. 2023). While awareness of DHM is improving in NICU settings, use in full‐term infants remains relatively unexplored (McCune and Perrin 2021). This is likely related to accessibility as many hospitals do not have access or capacity to provide DHM to full‐term infants. Additionally, there are significant gaps in evidence around DHM supplementation in full‐term or non‐hospitalized infants and providers are unequipped to provide evidence‐informed care around supplementation of DHM in these populations (McCune and Perrin 2021). Future research needs to examine the effects of DHM compared to formula supplementation on full‐term or non‐hospitalized infant populations. Additionally, efforts need to be made to educate healthcare providers and to improve awareness of DHM as a potential supplementation option in areas where access to DHM is possible for full‐term infants. Normalizing DHM in postpartum care pathways and establishing clear clinical guidelines on when and how it should be offered may improve both access and acceptance.

Exploring the Future of DHM Supplementation in Postpartum Settings

Globally, there is a growing interest in increasing access to DHM for infants who are not admitted to the NICU. Availability of DHM for non‐NICU populations is a concern around sustainability and wholly depends on the success of individual milk banks to bring in sufficient milk donations to meet demand. As such, focussing on the sustainability and increasing milk donation where possible, needs to be a priority to ensure continued access of DHM for full‐term infants. Supporting breastfeeding and increasing breastfeeding rates will help to improve the supply of lactating people who are able to donate their own breastmilk to accredited milk banks (Doshmangir et al. 2025; Kaech et al. 2022). Additionally, expanding evidence around DHM supplementation for increasingly diverse groups of infants is one way to increase donor awareness and participation (Mahdikhani et al. 2025). The HMBANA Equitable Donor Milk Access Blueprint (Human Milk Banking Association of North America 2023) calls for improved research evaluating outcomes following supplementation with DHM among term infants to increase access and equity. Building on this evidence base, may also compel healthcare agencies and insurance companies to cover the costs of DHM in for certain infant groups (Rose et al. 2022). Finally, expanding the focus to maternal outcomes and well‐being, rather than solely on infant outcomes, may help to improve donors' willingness to pursue milk donations by bringing about understanding of shared breastfeeding experiences (Kaech et al. 2022; Mahdikhani et al. 2025).

Limitations

While our study explored perspectives of mothers who chose to use DHM for their infants, the sample was limited to those who could afford to purchase DHM or were able to access DHM via community support. This sample omitted those who chose not to supplement with DHM, or who may not have accessed the medical relief program. Further research could benefit from understanding the perspectives of those who chose not to use DHM as a supplementation option. Additionally, demographic data collection of who chose and did not choose to supplement with DHM was not available to the researchers as this was not recorded in hospital. Future work in this area should capture demographic features of those who prefer DHM supplementation over formula supplementation.

Conclusion

This study provides a unique lens, considering the impact of DHM supplementation on maternal experiences with breastfeeding. DHM could provide a feasible intervention that may help to mitigate maternal distress by providing a supplement that is more similar breastmilk, while breastfeeding is being established. This appears to be especially meaningful to mothers who aim to exclusively breastfeeding their infants.

To improve continuity of care and reduce emotional stress, DHM should be normalized in clinical care pathways, with clear guidelines on when and how to offer it. Nurses are uniquely positioned to lead this change, not only in bedside care but through prenatal education, early feeding assessments, and health system advocacy. In doing so, they can ensure that all families, regardless of their background, geography, or income, have access to the information and options they need to make empowered and supported infant feeding decisions. The findings from this study can inform development around future postpartum DHM research to increase the body of evidence that can contribute to scaling and spreading provision of DHM to full‐term infants on a national and international level.
