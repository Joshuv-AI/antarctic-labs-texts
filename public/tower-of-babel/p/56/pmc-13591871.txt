Catheter architecture as a determinant of cerebrospinal fluid flow in pediatric hydrocephalus
Authors: Christopher W Roberts, Brandon G Rocque, Leopold Arko IV, Neena I Marupudi, Sandeep Sood, Andrew Jea, Elliot Widd, Bryn A Martin, Carolyn A Harris
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCerebral ventricular catheters (VCs) remain the critical but failure-prone component of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) shunt systems for pediatric hydrocephalus, with proximal obstruction accounting for most malfunctions. Several catheter geometries have been introduced to the market in the US alone, yet none demonstrate long-term reliability. This study investigates how catheter design interacts with patient-specific ventricular anatomy and surgical placement to influence CSF drainage patterns. Three patient-specific hydrocephalic ventricles were modeled from MRI scans, with FOHR values of 0.45, 0.30, and 0.29 and corresponding ventricular volumes of 167.5 (enlarged), 12.5 (moderate), and 20.6 mL (small). Four ventricular catheters with varying architectures from our institutional biobank were reverse engineered using confocal microscopy. Catheters were virtually implanted into the lateral ventricles under frontal, parietal, and occipital placements. Computational fluid dynamic simulations were performed, and mass flow rates were quantified across drainage holes grouped into longitudinal segments. Boundary conditions consisted of a constant CSF efflux rate of 0.35 mL/min applied uniformly across all configurations and a continuous pressure outlet at the proximal catheter outlet, representing valveless drainage. Occipital placement consistently produced valve adjacent dominant inflow across catheter designs, whereas frontal and parietal placements exhibited design-specific flow behaviors when segmental inflow through the catheter drainage holes was measured. In most configurations, segments nearest the catheter valve captured the majority of inflow, accounting for 65–82% of total CSF entry. In frontal placements, smaller ventricular geometries caused valve-adjacent segments in several catheter designs to extend outside the ventricular cavity. As a result, inflow was redistributed toward the remaining tip-adjacent segments, with up to 99.5% of total flow entering through these segments when valve-adjacent holes were excluded. Catheter designs with shorter perforated lengths and more closely spaced drainage holes maintained a greater number of functionally active segments across ventricular geometries, whereas longer perforated spans were more susceptible to anatomical exclusion. These findings demonstrate that ventricular catheter drainage is governed not by catheter geometry alone, but by the interaction between catheter architecture, ventricular morphology, and surgical placement.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12987-026-00834-1.Keywords: Computational Fluid Dynamics, Hydrocephalus, Pediatric Hydrocephalus, Cerebrospinal fluid, Intracerebroventricular catheter, Ventricular shunt, Ventricular shunt architecture

Introduction

The standard treatment for pediatric hydrocephalus relies on cerebrospinal fluid shunt systems [1], which diverts excess cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) from the ventricles to the peritoneal cavity. Despite decades of refinement, CSF shunts continue to face an alarming failure rate of nearly 85%, with ventricular catheter (VC) obstruction representing the most frequent mode of malfunction [2–4]. Extensive prior studies [5–7] show that these obstructions are composed of cellular and tissue elements, including astrocytes, macrophages, glial cells, blood clots, and ingrowth of the choroid plexus that accumulate at the catheter interface. For clinicians, this failure translates to repeat neurosurgical procedures, high revision rates, diminished patient quality of life, long-term neurological consequences, and significant healthcare costs [8–10]. Despite the high VC failure rate, catheter designs remain largely empirical. Currently, several ventricular catheter designs are commercially available, differing in hole size, spacing, number, and arrangement, yet all remain vulnerable to failure. This persistent limitation underscores the need to move beyond trial-and-error in design.

A clinically driven approach is required to analyze how catheter architecture interacts with patient-specific ventricular anatomy and surgical placement to influence long-term function. To improve design, researchers have long relied on benchtop experiments and computational fluid dynamics (CFD) models, seeking to characterize how catheter geometry influences CSF inflow [11–20]. Early investigations by Galarza [13–17] systematically varied hole number, spacing, angle, and tapering and found that valve-adjacent holes dominate inflow, and increasing perforation density can redistribute drainage more evenly. Prior studies [21, 22, 34] simulated tissue-related obstruction by mechanically blocking catheter holes, demonstrating that inflow redistributes into the remaining patent perforations. Subsequent CFD frameworks expanded this work by confirming that catheter hole geometry significantly shapes local shear stresses and inflow distribution, while also suggesting that hole arrangement could be optimized to mitigate valve-adjacent inflow distribution [20]. Building on our prior work [39], which focused on a single catheter architecture, this study investigates how catheter design shapes CSF flow distribution and how these patterns are modulated by ventricular morphology and surgical placement. The present study integrates variable catheter geometry with patient-derived ventricular models and clinically driven VC placements. We compared four distinct catheter designs from our clinical patient BioBank [5, 6, 24] across enlarged, moderate, and small hydrocephalic ventricles. Using frontal, parietal, and occipital trajectories [23], we evaluated how specific design features such as hole size, spacing, and perforation density interact with ventricular morphology and surgical placement to affect flow distribution.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

Patient clinical data were collected at Children’s of Alabama, University of Alabama at Birmingham (Birmingham, AL, USA), Riley Children’s Health (Indianapolis, IN, USA), and Children’s Hospital of Michigan, Wayne State University School of Medicine (Detroit, MI, USA). All analyses were conducted using de-identified imaging data and clinical materials that were collected as part of routine clinical care. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Wayne State University Institutional Review Board (IRB) prior to data collection. Three patient-specific ventricular geometries were used in this study: (1) an 11-year-old male with hydrocephalus secondary to a posterior fossa tumor, representing an enlarged ventricular morphology (FOHR = 0.45, ventricular volume = 167.5 mL); (2) a 15-year-old male with Chiari I malformation and shunt dependency, representing a moderately enlarged ventricular morphology (FOHR = 0.30, ventricular volume = 12.5 mL); and (3) a 19-year-old female with post-hemorrhagic hydrocephalus of prematurity, representing a slit-like ventricular morphology (FOHR = 0.29, ventricular volume = 20.6 mL), who remained under the care of pediatric neurosurgery at the time of imaging.

These cases were chosen to represent a range of different ventricular morphologies and volumes. Full clinical details and collection methodology are described in our previous work [38, 39]. Four ventricular catheters were obtained from pediatric patients undergoing shunt revision procedures (Fig. 1). Although these catheters had varying degrees of visible tissue coverage consistent with obstruction, selection was not based on obstruction status. Instead, catheters were selected by visual inspection to represent a range of geometrical features, including differences in hole number, row configuration, and lumen diameter, to examine how design architecture influences CSF flow. During computer-aided design (CAD) reconstruction, all adherent tissue was digitally removed to preserve the native catheter geometry for computational modeling. These catheters do not represent the full range of commercially designed catheters; instead, they serve as representative examples to illustrate design-dependent flow behavior.

Clinical model development

MRI data were obtained retrospectively from routine clinical imaging protocols. Patients were imaged on GE scanners (1.5 T and 3.0 T) using 2D sequences, including fast-spoiled gradient-recalled echo (FGRE) and single-shot fast spin-echo (SSFSE). Slice thickness ranged from 1.2 to 1.4 mm, with in-plane spatial resolution between 0.55 and 1.17 mm. Acquisition matrices ranged from 256 × 128 to 300 × 128, with flip angles of 30° or 90°, and images were reconstructed over clinically prescribed fields of view (240–300 mm). Ventricular segmentations were semi-automatically generated in 3D Slicer (5.8.1, 2026) and exported as STL files for further processing. The STL surfaces were imported into Rhino 8, where manual editing was performed to remove non-ventricular tissue resulting from thresholding artifacts, particularly brain tissue fragments outside the ventricular cavity. All surface edits were restricted to the exterior of the ventricular models; the intraventricular surfaces defining the fluid domain were not modified. Ventricular volumes were calculated in Ansys Space Claim before and after surface cleanup and confirmed to be unchanged. Sharp edges, intersecting faces, and overlapping surfaces were removed to produce volume domains suitable for meshing. Final models were reviewed by pediatric neurosurgeons and compared with the source MRI to confirm anatomical accuracy.

The complete segmentation and processing workflow has been described in detail in our prior work [38, 39]. Four ventricular catheters from our institutional biobank (Fig. 1) were imaged using confocal microscopy (RS-G4 upright microscope, Caliber ID, Andover, MA, USA) equipped with a Leica HC Fluotar PL 10×/0.32 objective. Image stacks were acquired using a tiled mosaic centered along the catheter length, covering a total imaging area of approximately 20.6 × 5.8 mm. High-resolution surface-intensity thresholding was performed (Fig. 2) to reconstruct a CAD design model that preserved inlet hole depth, spacing, and luminal geometric curvature (Fig. 3). Individual drainage holes were measured along the length of each catheter. Reported values represent the mean hole diameter and spacing for each catheter design, and these averaged measurements were used to define representative catheter geometries. The ventricular catheter segment containing the drainage holes, located within the ventricle, represents the proximal component of the shunt system. Drainage hole locations are described relative to their position along the catheter. Holes and segments nearest the catheter tip are referred to as tip-adjacent, whereas holes and segments located farther from the tip and closer to the valve are referred to as valve-adjacent. Catheter A consisted of four rows with four holes per row, with a lumen inner diameter (ID) of 1.4 mm and outer diameter (OD) of 2.8 mm. The drainage holes measured 1.2 mm in diameter, spaced 3.7 mm apart, with a distance of 21.8 mm from the tip to the most proximal hole. Catheter B featured a larger lumen (ID 1.8 mm, OD 3.0 mm) and two rows of smaller holes (0.9 mm) paired with two rows of larger holes (1.6 mm). The first holes were located 4.5 mm and 6.3 mm from the tip, with subsequent holes spaced 3.5 mm apart. Catheter C had a lumen ID of 1.5 mm and an OD of 2.8 mm, with uniform 0.9 mm-diameter holes positioned 5.3 mm from the tip and separated by 1.0 mm. Catheter D had a 1.3 mm lumen ID and 2.4 mm OD, with 0.55 mm holes positioned 5.3 mm and 6.1 mm from the tip and spaced 0.75 mm apart.

CAD models of explanted ventricular catheters and drainage segment classification

The first hole from each row (Rows 1–4) was grouped as Segment 1, representing the drainage region closest to the catheter tip (Fig. 3). Segment 2 included the second hole from each row; Segment 3, the third; and subsequent segments were defined in this manner, with the final segment corresponding to the drainage region nearest the valve. Catheter placement was performed within the three previously segmented ventricular geometries representing small, moderate, and enlarged shunted hydrocephalus. Each catheter was virtually inserted into the left lateral ventricle using three clinical surgical approaches (frontal, parietal, and occipital). Placement was guided by anatomical landmarks derived from patient MRI data to ensure consistent entry orientation and depth. While catheter orientation and depth were standardized across models, the local distance between the catheter surface and the ventricular wall was not independently controlled, as this spacing is inherently determined by patient-specific ventricular geometry and catheter design. A detailed description of ventricular reconstruction and catheter placement methodology is provided in our prior work [39]. For analysis purposes, drainage holes were considered fully patent when exposed to the ventricular CSF space. Holes located outside the ventricular cavity or in direct contact with the ventricular wall were classified as functionally blocked.

Computational modeling methods

Segmented ventricular models and reconstructed catheter geometries were imported into ANSYS Fluent (Canonsburg, PA, 2024 R2) for numerical simulation. Simulations were conducted using a non-uniform polyhedral volume mesh, with a reduced growth rate applied to optimize element distribution in the ventricular catheters. Local refinement was applied around the catheter lumen and drainage holes, and boundary layer inflation was added to resolve near-wall velocity gradients. The ventricular walls were modeled as non-deformable rigid domains. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) was treated as an incompressible Newtonian fluid [26] (density 998.3 kg/m³, viscosity 0.89 mPa·s) at 37 °C, representing physiological conditions. Boundary conditions consisted of a constant CSF efflux rate of 0.35 mL/min [25, 43, 44] applied at the anatomically defined choroid plexus inlet surface [27] and a continuous pressure outlet at the proximal catheter outlet, representing valveless drainage. The total volume mesh size for the simulations ranged from approximately 2.7 to 2.9 million elements across all catheter models (Fig. 4). A coupled pressure–velocity scheme was employed, utilizing second-order discretization for pressure and second-order upwind schemes for the continuity and momentum equations. Simulations were performed under steady-state, laminar conditions using the Fluent Solver, with a convergence residual criterion set to 1 × 10⁻⁶. Consistent with the prior methodology reported by Khani et al., differences in mesh resolution along a velocity rake were quantified to assess numerical sensitivity [42]. The maximum discrepancy between the medium and fine meshes was 4.64%, indicating that further refinement yielded negligible changes to the computed velocity field. Full details of the numerical verification for the computational model are provided in our prior work [39]. Mass flow rates were measured at each drainage hole across all segments for the four catheter designs. Velocity contours were generated to visualize CSF flow patterns within the catheter inlet holes and lumen.

Results

Ventricular wall distance as a function of catheter segment position

Ventricular wall distance varied with ventricular geometry, surgical trajectory, and catheter design (Fig. 5). Across all catheter designs, enlarged ventricles exhibited the greatest catheter-to-wall distances, generally ranging from 10 to 27 mm depending on the trajectory. In contrast, distances in moderate and small ventricles were substantially reduced and typically remained below 8 mm. For Catheters A and B, occipital and parietal placements produced relatively consistent wall distances across catheter segments within a given ventricular geometry, whereas frontal placement exhibited greater variability. Similar trends were observed for Catheters C and D. Among the catheter designs, Catheter C demonstrated the largest wall distances in enlarged ventricles, particularly under frontal placement, where distances ranged from 23.8 to 27.4 mm. The influence of ventricular geometry was most apparent for frontal placement. In enlarged ventricles, all catheter designs maintained measurable separation from the ventricular wall, whereas moderate and small ventricles frequently exhibited one or more zero-distance measurements, indicating catheter-wall contact at specific segments. In contrast, occipital and parietal trajectories generally maintained measurable separation between catheter segments and the ventricular wall across ventricular geometries, although the magnitude of this separation decreased with ventricular size.

Flow distribution across catheter segments by ventricular size and surgical placement

Flow distribution varied with both ventricular geometry and surgical trajectory (Figs. 6, 7 and 8). Under frontal placement (Fig. 6), enlarged ventricles generally exhibited valve adjacent dominant inflow across all catheter designs, with most flow entering through the drainage segments nearest the catheter valve. As ventricular size decreased, Catheters A and B demonstrated redistribution toward more tip-adjacent segments, particularly in the moderate ventricle, whereas Catheters C and D maintained predominantly valve adjacent inflow profiles. In the small ventricle, Catheter A exhibited a pronounced shift toward Segment 3 (66%), while Catheter D maintained predominantly valve-adjacent inflow, with increased contributions from the more tip-adjacent segments. Under parietal placement (Fig. 7), valve-adjacent inflow patterns were largely preserved across ventricular geometries. Catheters C and D maintained highly consistent flow distributions regardless of ventricular size, while Catheters A and B exhibited modest redistribution in the moderate ventricle, with greater contributions from mid-catheter segments. Despite these shifts, segments near the catheter valve remained the primary contributors to inflow. Under occipital placement (Fig. 8), flow distributions were highly consistent across ventricular sizes for all catheter designs. Inflow remained concentrated within the valve-adjacent drainage segments, with only minor contributions from tip-adjacent segments. Catheter D exhibited the most stable distribution across ventricular geometries, while Catheters A and B showed similarly persistent valve-adjacent dominant flow profiles.

Segmental flow rate as a function of distance from the catheter tip

Segmental flow rates varied with catheter design, ventricular geometry, and surgical trajectory (Supplemental Figures A–C). Across all catheter designs, enlarged ventricles generally exhibited increasing flow rates as distance from the catheter tip increased, with the segments nearest the catheter valve carrying the greatest flow. Under frontal placement (Supplemental Figure A), ventricular size influenced the distribution of segmental flow. For Catheters A–C, moderate and small ventricles exhibited a redistribution of flow away from the segments nearest the catheter valve, with several valve-adjacent segments demonstrating markedly reduced flow rates compared with those in enlarged ventricles. This effect was most evident in segments associated with catheter-wall contact. In contrast, Catheter D maintained a more consistent increase in flow rate with distance from the catheter tip across ventricular geometries. Under occipital placement (Supplemental Figure B), segmental flow profiles were highly consistent across ventricular sizes. For all catheter designs, flow increased progressively toward the valve adjacent drainage segments, and the corresponding segmental flow rates were similar across enlarged, moderate, and small ventricles. Under parietal placement (Supplemental Figure C), Catheters C and D demonstrated nearly identical segmental flow profiles across ventricular geometries. Catheters A and B exhibited modest redistribution of flow in the moderate ventricle; however, the overall trend of increasing flow toward the drainage segments nearest the catheter valve was preserved.

Segmental flow rate as a function of drainage hole to ventricular wall distance

Segmental flow rate varied with drainage hole proximity to the ventricular wall and was influenced by ventricular geometry and surgical trajectory (Supplemental Figures D–F). Under frontal placement (Supplemental Figure D), moderate and small ventricles frequently contained one or more drainage holes with wall distances approaching 0 mm. These locations were associated with markedly reduced flow rates compared with segments that maintained measurable wall separation. This pattern was observed across Catheters A–C and was most evident in segments exhibiting catheter-wall contact. Under occipital placement (Supplemental Figure E), drainage hole-to-wall distances remained relatively stable across ventricular sizes for all catheter designs. Correspondingly, segmental flow profiles were highly consistent, with similar flow rates observed at comparable drainage hole locations across enlarged, moderate, and small ventricles. Under parietal placement (Supplemental Figure F), measurable wall separation was maintained across catheter designs and ventricular geometries. Segmental flow rates generally increased as the drainage hole distance from the ventricular wall increased. Although moderate ventricles exhibited some redistribution of flow for Catheters A and B, substantial reductions in flow associated with drainage hole-wall contact were not observed.

Discussion

Before considering CSF flow through ventricular catheters, a more fundamental question is whether catheter segments remain within the ventricular cavity as ventricular size, insertion approach, and catheter design vary. In enlarged ventricles, all catheter segments remained within the ventricle across catheter types and insertion approaches, indicating that when the ventricular space is quite large, the placement trajectory has minimal influence on catheter positioning (Fig. 5). As ventricular size decreased, the limited ventricular space increasingly displaced the valve- adjacent segments outside the ventricular cavity. This effect was most pronounced with frontal placement, where the segments nearest the catheter valve extended beyond the ventricular cavity into parenchyma. Occipital and parietal trajectories generally preserved intraventricular positioning more consistently in smaller ventricles. Importantly, catheter design also influenced this behavior: while several catheters demonstrated loss of valve-adjacent intraventricular exposure depending on the approach and ventricular size, one design (Catheter D) remained within the ventricle across all tested conditions. These findings indicate that catheter segments do not inevitably exit the ventricle as ventricular size decreases, and that catheter design can influence whether segments remain inside the ventricle. Mechanistically, whether catheter segments remain within the ventricle determines how many drainage segments are exposed to CSF versus embedded in brain tissue, thereby influencing potential inflow. As ventricular size decreases, catheter placement shifts from largely approach-independent to one in which trajectory and catheter design influence intraventricular exposure.

Next, we examined how CSF inflow is distributed across all catheter designs. Occipital placement consistently produced valve-adjacent dominant drainage, regardless of ventricular size (Fig. 8). Vector flow fields of CSF entry into four ventricular catheter designs (A–D), placed in an enlarged ventricle via an occipital approach, are shown in Fig. 9. In these placements, flow concentrated almost entirely in the holes nearest the catheter valve, consistent with prior reports by Galarza et al. [12], Good et al. [51], and Weisenberg et al. [20], who demonstrated valve-adjacent dominant inflow in both simplified ventricular models and patient-specific hydrocephalic ventricles. Our results confirm this observation and extend it across distinct catheter designs. In the occipital trajectory, inflow remained concentrated in the valve-adjacent segments when these perforations remained fully within the ventricular cavity. Thus, in enlarged ventricles where catheter rows are unobstructed, anatomy exerts minimal influence compared to design, and flow continues to follow the path of least resistance. Clinically, however, even in enlarged ventricles, catheters may shift toward the wall over time or encounter progressive ventricular size reduction, introducing wall contact and thereby altering these idealized distributions [28, 29].

By contrast, frontal placement produced the greatest redistribution of inflow, whereas parietal placement resulted in more moderate, design-dependent changes in flow distribution (Figs. 6 and 7). Catheter A, with its large drainage holes over a 21.8 mm perforated span, was highly impacted by ventricle size. In enlarged ventricles, valve-adjacent segments concentrated inflow (Segments 3–4: 96%), but in the moderate frontal model, segments nearest to the catheter valve were blocked by parenchyma outside the frontal horn, eliminating their contribution and forcing flow entirely into Segments 1 and 2 (99.5%). This redistribution aligns with CFD studies of hole obstruction, such as TerMaath et al. [21], which demonstrated that occluded rows force flow into the remaining patent holes, elevating local velocity and shear stress. Our results show that comparable redistribution can also occur via parenchymal and ventricular wall contact, thereby limiting the patency of catheter drainage holes. Ventricular configurations that position drainage holes against parenchyma result in functional occlusion of those rows, with flow subsequently diverted to the available patent perforations (Fig. 10). These findings may be further altered by fluid-structure interactions between the ventricular wall and catheter drainage openings.

Catheter B, with a larger lumen (1.8 mm ID) and a long 27 mm perforated span, featured alternating small and large drainage holes, preferentially concentrating flow in the valve-adjacent rows containing the larger perforations when those holes remained patent. In enlarged ventricles, inflow was concentrated in the segments nearest to the catheter valve (> 75% in Segment 5), reflecting a preference for the larger perforations when CSF surrounded all rows (Fig. 11). In moderate ventricles, however, the valve-adjacent segments were displaced outside the ventricular cavity, forcing flow to shift toward the tip-adjacent segments (Segments 2–3), such that flow entered even the smaller perforations, not due to inherent preference, but because the total inflow had to be distributed across the few remaining patent rows. This indicates that the alternating large- and small-hole design has little effect on flow distribution when all rows remain patent, since inflow preferentially enters the larger-valve adjacent holes. However, once valve-adjacent rows are excluded by ventricular morphology, inflow is forced toward the tip-adjacent segments, where even the smaller holes contribute, making the architecture relevant under constrained conditions. In the small ventricle, where the ventricular cavity shape partially accommodated both the tip and the valve-adjacent segments, flow was more evenly partitioned. However, the valve-adjacent concentration was lower than in the enlarged case. These results show that Catheter B’s performance is determined less by its alternating hole pattern itself and more by whether the ventricular morphology allows the larger valve-adjacent perforations to remain within the cavity and functional.

Contour map of flow distribution in segment 5 large vs. small drainage holes

Catheter C, by contrast, was more consistent with ventricular scaling. With 24 uniformly small holes (0.92 mm) spaced 1.0 mm apart over a 19.8 mm perforated length, this design preserves valve adjacent concentrated inflow across ventricle sizes. Even in moderate and small ventricles, Segments 7–8 consistently captured 65–70% of inflow, with only mild redistribution to the tip adjacent segments. The shorter spacing between perforation patterns ensured that valve-adjacent rows remained within the ventricle under most configurations, stabilizing flow patterns regardless of the approach. Catheter D, which featured the smallest holes (0.55 mm) spaced (0.75 mm) over the shortest span (15.5 mm), produced the broadest distribution of inflow across all designs. Unlike Catheter A or B, which showed higher redistributions, or Catheter C, which remained heavily valve-adjacent dominant, Catheter D maintained segmental inflow across approaches and ventricular sizes, with valve and tip-adjacent contributions often within the same range (Segment 8 35%, Segments 1–3 collectively 38% in the small ventricle).

Across the catheter designs studied, shorter perforated spans with more closely spaced holes preserved more consistent valve-adjacent dominant inflow across ventricular sizes and surgical placements. Because more perforations remained within the ventricular cavity despite geometric changes, flow distribution remained relatively stable. This observation is consistent with prior reports suggesting that shorter, denser perforation patterns are less sensitive to anatomical variability. For example, Thomale et al. [31] reported that perforations nearest the catheter valve in longer catheters often extended into parenchyma and contributed little to inflow, whereas shorter designs preserved functional drainage across perforations. However, prior work by Galarza [13] suggested that smaller or more numerous holes may increase susceptibility to occlusion from debris or cellular ingrowth. Our findings indicate that although uniformly small holes can maintain flow distribution and reduce anatomical sensitivity, the extent to which these designs remain vulnerable to biological occlusion warrants further investigation. Differences in catheter hydraulic resistance may also contribute to the observed flow redistribution behavior. Catheter features such as lumen diameter, drainage hole size, hole density, and perforated length influence the hydraulic resistance of the flow pathway. As a result, these design characteristics [30] may affect how flow redistributes among the remaining patent drainage holes when other segments become excluded. Although the present study was not designed to isolate the effects of hydraulic resistance, these factors likely interact with ventricular anatomy and catheter geometry to influence segmental flow distribution [34]. It should be noted that maintaining a larger number of functionally active drainage holes does not necessarily imply resistance to catheter obstruction. Even for catheters with many small holes, if ventricular and/or choroidal tissue moves into the catheter, only the valve-adjacent catheter lumen would need to be obstructed to restrict CSF egress from the entire catheter.

Segmental flow-rate analysis provided further insight into how ventricular morphology and surgical trajectory influenced drainage patterns (Supplemental Figure A–C). When flow at each catheter segment was mapped as a function of distance from the catheter tip, occipital placement produced nearly identical flow profiles across enlarged, moderate, and small ventricles (Supplemental Figure B). This finding indicates that segments nearest the catheter valve dominated flow behavior with minimal influence from ventricular morphology. Parietal placement introduced minor sensitivity to ventricular size, particularly for longer-span designs (Supplemental Figure C, Catheter B). Frontal placement showed the greatest deviations from this valve-adjacent flow pattern, with progressive redistribution of flow toward tip-adjacent holes as ventricular size decreased. Although these trends were generally consistent with the flow-distribution analysis, segmental flow-rate data showed that the redistribution observed in frontal placements occurred when segments contacting the ventricular wall or extending outside the ventricular cavity were excluded from CSF drainage. To assess whether ventricular wall proximity influenced these patterns, segmental flow was plotted against the distance from the center of each drainage hole to the nearest ventricular wall (Supplemental Figure D–F). In occipital placement, the segment carrying the greatest flow happened to be positioned closer to the ventricular wall (Supplemental Figure E), whereas in parietal and frontal placements, some of the higher flow segments occurred farther from the wall (Supplemental Figure D, F). These patterns indicate that wall proximity does not systematically control flow distribution, but rather reflects the anatomical position of segments that already carry the majority of flow. As ventricular size decreased, segment-to-wall distances generally decreased, causing many drainage segments to lie similarly close to the ventricular wall (Supplemental Figure D–F). Despite this convergence in wall proximity, the valve-adjacent dominant flow pattern largely persisted. Segments closer to the wall did not consistently exhibit reduced flow, and segments farther from the wall did not consistently carry greater flow. These results indicate that near-wall positioning without direct contact has little influence on the baseline flow distribution established by catheter geometry and segment location along the catheter. In frontal placement, however, several catheter designs exhibited drainage holes with zero wall clearance, corresponding to complete loss of flow through those segments (Supplemental Figure D). Holes extending outside the ventricular cavity were effectively removed from the drainage network, thereby redistributing flow to the remaining tip-adjacent holes. Importantly, holes that remained very close to the ventricular wall but retained nonzero clearance continued to conduct flow without a consistent reduction, indicating that, in this hydrodynamic model, wall proximity affected drainage only when the hole was fully occluded.

Hariharan et al. identified contact between the ventricular catheter and the ventricular wall as a robust predictor of obstruction severity and reported that parietal entry was associated with fewer empty holes and a higher prevalence of nonobstructive tissue growth [6]. Computational studies by Lin et al. [11], Galarza et al. 12, and Weisenberg et al. [20] showed that CSF inflow preferentially enters the hole sets nearest the catheter valve, a pattern that was also observed in our simulations when all drainage segments remained patent. These studies suggest that both the catheter’s position within the ventricle and the distribution of inflow across the drainage holes may influence the local environment experienced by individual perforations. Our results extend these observations by demonstrating how ventricular morphology, catheter design, and surgical placement interact to determine which drainage holes remain intraventricular and therefore functionally active in CSF drainage. When valve-adjacent segments were displaced outside the ventricular cavity, inflow redistributed toward the remaining tip-adjacent segments and became concentrated within fewer patent segments. This effect was most pronounced in catheter designs with longer perforated spans and wider hole spacing, which were more susceptible to loss of valve-adjacent intraventricular exposure in smaller ventricles. In contrast, shorter perforated lengths and more closely spaced holes preserved a larger number of functionally active segments across ventricular morphologies.

Prior clinical studies have reported higher rates of shunt malfunction when ventricular catheters are positioned against the ventricular wall or septum pellucidum [6, 40, 41]. Prior literature [47, 48] from the Hydrocephalus Clinical Research Network (HCRN) demonstrated that shunt malfunction rates did not significantly differ between frontal and occipital catheter placements in large pediatric cohorts. However, those studies evaluated overall shunt survival rather than the hydrodynamic behavior of individual catheter perforations. Our simulations demonstrated that catheter placement alters which drainage holes remain within the ventricular cavity and therefore participate in CSF inflow. In particular, frontal placements in smaller ventricles more frequently displaced valve-adjacent holes against parenchyma, whereas occipital placements more consistently preserved valve-adjacent inflow. Importantly, the present study evaluated hydrodynamic behavior under simulated conditions and did not assess catheter obstruction, shunt system malfunction, or long-term clinical outcomes. Therefore, the findings should not be interpreted as evidence that a particular catheter design or surgical trajectory improves shunt survival. Rather, the results demonstrate how ventricular anatomy and catheter architecture influence which drainage holes remain functionally active and how CSF inflow redistributes among those holes. Whether these hydrodynamic differences translate into clinically meaningful differences in obstruction risk or shunt longevity remains unknown and will require future studies that integrate computational modeling with longitudinal clinical outcome data [50].

Although flow distribution characterizes shunt behavior, catheter obstruction is ultimately governed by the local fluid environment. Prior studies have shown that obstruction may be influenced by regional flow patterns, wall shear stresses, and stagnation zones. Lee et al. showed that typical catheter geometries generate these conditions, whereas shear stresses above a critical threshold promote astrocyte detachment [46]. Complementing these findings, Khodadadei et al. showed that astrocytes at the catheter–CSF interface are highly sensitive to physiologically relevant shear stresses, with wall shear stresses exceeding approximately 0.5 dyne/cm² triggering astrocyte IL-6 secretion and inflammatory signaling [45]. Together, these studies indicate that both low- and high-shear environments may contribute to obstruction through distinct but complementary biological mechanisms. Our results demonstrate that when valve-adjacent segments are excluded due to parenchymal contact, flow is redistributed to fewer remaining segments, thereby increasing the local hydrodynamic burden at specific sites [21]. However, because biological tissue interactions were not modeled, the present study cannot determine whether these flow patterns ultimately increase or decrease the likelihood of catheter obstruction.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. While our models assumed idealized catheter positioning, clinical conditions are considerably more complex; surgical variability, ventricular asymmetry, and postoperative shifts can alter where the proximal segment ultimately resides. Janson et al. [35]. demonstrated that catheters positioned within 1 cm of the ipsilateral foramen of Monro had significantly longer revision-free survival. In contrast, suboptimal or poor placements increased the risk of failure by 57–66%. Whitehead et al. [36] found that nearly 33% of catheters migrated from an initially correct, ultrasound-verified position to a different location on the first postoperative scan. Kraemer et al. [37]. demonstrated that chronic suction can deform ventricular tissue into catheter holes, suggesting shunt failure results from pressure-driven tissue interactions rather than static blockage. The sample size of ventricular models was limited, and given the high variability and complexity of ventricular morphology, future work should incorporate a broader range of patient-derived geometries. Similarly, the analysis was limited to four ventricular catheter designs, and external ventricular drains (EVDs) were excluded; future investigations should expand the comparison to include additional catheter types and EVDs. Second, the ventricles were modeled as rigid bodies rather than deformable structures. While this assumption reduces computational demand, future work should incorporate elastic ventricular models to evaluate whether ventricular deformation influences flow distribution. Lastly, the simulations employed simplified boundary conditions, using a steady CSF inflow rate of 0.35 mL/min without incorporating physiological pulsatility or patient-specific variations in CSF dynamics [32, 33, 49]. Although this value is physiologically representative, variations in CSF production and drainage may alter local velocities, pressure gradients, and wall shear stresses within the catheter, potentially affecting the redistribution of flow among drainage holes. Future studies should investigate how pulsatile, patient-specific CSF flow conditions interact with catheter design and ventricular morphology to influence flow behavior and the mechanisms underlying catheter obstruction.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that ventricular catheter flow behavior emerges from the interaction between catheter architecture, ventricular morphology, and surgical placement. While catheter design defines the potential drainage pattern, ventricular anatomy ultimately determines which perforations remain inside the ventricle and are functionally active. Catheters with long perforated spans and widely spaced holes were more susceptible to the effects of smaller ventricular geometries or postoperative ventricular reduction, as valve adjacent segments were more likely to extend outside the ventricular cavity and no longer contribute to CSF drainage. In contrast, shorter designs with higher hole density preserved a greater number of functional perforations across ventricular geometries. These findings suggest that catheter architectures that maintain multiple patent intraventricular perforations may better preserve functional drainage pathways as ventricular morphology evolves after shunting.
