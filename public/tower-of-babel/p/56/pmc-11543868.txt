Lay-up design and experimental study of the multiple corrugated diaphragms of carbon fiber reinforced polymer composite
Authors: Chen Yang, Tao Yu, Angang Cao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Rebound and deformation of stainless steel during processing have brought great difficulties to forming the multiple corrugated diaphragm (MCD), seriously affecting its performance. Carbon fiber reinforced polymer composite (CFRP) provides a new idea for the design and forming methods of MCD due to its excellent performance and designability. This paper uses ABAQUS finite element analysis software to analyze the influence of laying angle and laying angle ratio on the mechanical properties of composite material MCD. Finally, the axial stiffness of the designed CFRP MCD is tested using a flexible coupling stiffness test bench. The findings demonstrate that the design values are consistent with the experimental results within an acceptable margin of error. The investigation validates the feasibility of the proposed laying design method for CFRP multi-waveform membrane discs (MCDs) and establishes a theoretical foundation for their engineering design and practical application.

Introduction

Compared to the traditional film diaphragm couplings, multi-waveform corrugated diaphragm (MCD) couplings have the advantages of high torque-to-diameter ratio, torque-to-weight ratio, and compensation capacity, making them more suitable for high-power and high-speed conditions. The core element of the coupling is a sinusoidal wave-shaped MCD. The problems of rebound and deformation of stainless steel during processing have brought great difficulties to the forming of MCD, seriously affecting the performance of MCD couplings [1,2]. Carbon fiber reinforced polymer composites (CFRP) provide a new idea for the design and forming methods of MCD due to their excellent performance and designability [3,4].

Several studies on the MCDs in different fields were conducted. Mancuso et al. introduced several couplings for gas turbines. The authors mentioned that MCD coupling is more suitable for gas turbines. However, the paper does not introduce the design calculation method of MCD coupling [5]. Li explored the structural strength, fatigue life, and vibration characteristics of MCD by establishing a parametric finite element model [6]. Cao et al. deduced the analytical solution of axial stiffness of MCD with a sinusoidal surface using the theory of mechanics of corrugated annular plates [7]. The authors proposed the calculation method of the fatigue safety factor of MCD coupling. They verified the feasibility of the calculation method through fatigue life tests [8]. Cao et al. also investigated the stamping forming method of stainless steel MCD and mastered some laws of stamping rebound. However, the deformation and rebound problems are still unavoidable [9].

In addition, many researchers have investigated the layup design of CFRP. Lin et al. introduced the basic concepts and properties of CFRP and the design and analysis methods of laminated structures. Simultaneously, different laminate structure types, applications, and future development directions were also discussed [10]. Zhang et al. discussed composite laminate design methods and optimization techniques and their applications in the aerospace, automotive, and energy fields [11]. Yang et al. described how genetic algorithms can be used to optimize the design of composite laminates. A genetic algorithm is an optimization algorithm based on natural selection and genetics principles. This type of algorithm searches for the optimal solution by simulating the evolutionary process [12]. Mondal et al. introduced the influence of fiber orientation and stacking sequence on the mechanical properties of composite laminates, including strength, stiffness, fatigue life, and fracture toughness. The authors proved that fibers' direction and stacking sequence affect composites' strength, stiffness, fatigue life, and fracture toughness [13]. Liu et al. introduced the recent research progress in optimizing composite laminates, including optimization methods, selection of design variables, constraints, and multi-physical field optimization [14].

Sathish et al. explored the fabrication of mechanical and thermal properties of banana-kenaf glass fiber reinforced epoxy composite, a relatively newer hybrid composite. The results show that the properties of hybrid composites with fibers arranged at 45° are better than those of other hybrid composites [15]. Lin et al. proposed a method to optimize the stacking sequence of laminate composite structures. This method can improve the performance and reliability of laminate composite structures [16]. Abouhamze et al. proposed an effective method to optimize the sequence of composite laminates. The authors presented the results of a symmetric angle-ply six-layer cylindrical panel to illustrate the optimization process [17]. Nedri et al. explored the free vibration of the upper composite plate with an elastic foundation using the improved hyperbolic shear deformation theory. The numerical results are presented and compared with those available in the literature. The proposed theory accurately and efficiently predicts the natural frequencies of laminated composite plates [18]. Gohari et al. developed a Levi-type analytical solution procedure to characterize static and dynamic deformation response of smart laminated simply-supported composite rectangular plates induced by inclined piezoelectric actuators under constant electrical voltage and time-dependent electrical voltage with excitation frequency [19]. Then, the authors presented a systematic approach toward localized failure inspection of internally pressurized laminated ellipsoidal woven composite domes. The domes were made of thin glass fiber reinforced polymer (GFRP) woven composite layups [0, 0, 0], [0, 30, 0], [0, 45, 0], and [0, 75, 0]. The analytical results demonstrated that the circumferential regions near meridian φ = 45°in prolate ellipsoidal domes and near meridian φ = 90°in oblate ellipsoidal domes sustain the highest deformation under internal pressure [20].

The above literature review reveals that current research on the lamination design of CFRP focuses on optimization methods and technologies, material selection, lamination structure design, interface characteristic studies, and structural applications. These studies are significant for improving the properties and applications of composites. Optimization methods and technologies are important in designing composite material layups, such as genetic algorithms, neural networks, and particle swarm optimization algorithms. In addition, the research on MCD couplings mainly focuses on structural design level optimization, performance calculation and simulation, and test verification and improvement. Moreover, no literature report on the improvement of materials was found. There are relatively few studies on the lay-up design of CFRP MCD. Therefore, CFRP was introduced into the MCD design, seeking to improve and optimize the performance of MCD through material changes, thereby improving the performance of MCD coupling.

The finite element model of CFRP MCD was established by using ABAQUS software to verify the possibility of using CFRP instead of metal to design and process MCD. Secondly, the influence law of the composite lamination angle and the proportion of different lamination angles on the torque, axial load, and bending moment of the MCD is analyzed. Then, the lamination optimization design of the CFRP MCD is carried out. Finally, the axial stiffness test of the designed CFRP MCD is conducted to verify the feasibility of the above optimization design method. This research provides a theoretical basis for the engineering design and application of CFRP MCD.

Influence of the layering angle

The initial investigation evaluates the impact of varying layup angles on the torque-transfer capacity, axial displacement compensation ability, and angular displacement compensation ability of MCDs [22]. Therefore, seven distinct oblique symmetrical layup configurations with varying layup angles are designed (each comprising eight layers with a default [±0]4S layer). The mechanical properties of MCDs with different layup angles under the influence of torque, axial displacement load, and bending moment are then analyzed via Abaqus.

The effect of torque

The analysis was conducted for MCDs with different oblique symmetrical layup configurations. The maximum torsion angle exhibited under a constant torque varies as a function of the oblique layup angle (Fig. 4).Fig. 4MCD Maximum torsion angle of CFRP MCD under different layering angles and torsional load.Fig. 4

MCD Maximum torsion angle of CFRP MCD under different layering angles and torsional load.

Fig. 4 illustrates that the minimum torsion angle for CFRP MCDs with different oblique layup angles subjected to a constant torque is achieved when the curved film disc adopts an oblique symmetrical layup configuration of [±45°]4S. This phenomenon indicates that this particular layup angle enables the MCD to exhibit a high capacity for torque resistance. Consequently, a 45° layup angle should be considered in the design of MCD layups.

The effect of axial displacement load

Fig. 5 depicts the maximum stress experienced by each layer of CFRP MCDs with various oblique symmetrical layup configurations when subjected to a constant axial displacement load.Fig. 5Maximum stress in each layer of CFRP MCD with different layering angles under axial displacement load.Fig. 5

Maximum stress in each layer of CFRP MCD with different layering angles under axial displacement load.

The maximum stress in each layer for MCDs with oblique symmetrical layup configurations subjected to an axial displacement load varies as a function of the oblique layup angle. The stress is symmetrically distributed along the mid-plane of the layer (the fourth layer) for 0°, 15°, 30°, and 45° layup angles. The highest stress is found at the upper and lower outer surface layers (the first and eighth layers). In contrast, the stress distribution is not symmetrical for 60°, 75°, and 90° layup angles. Lastly, the stress in each layer of the MCD is minimized when [0°]4S is employed as an oblique symmetrical layup configuration (with fibers oriented radially).

The analysis suggests that incorporating a 0° layup can enhance the axial displacement compensation ability of MCDs. Consequently, a 0° layup should be considered in the design of MCD layups.

The effect of bending moment

Fig. 6 presents the maximum stress experienced by each layer of MCDs with various oblique symmetrical layup configurations subjected to a constant bending moment.Fig. 6Maximum stress in an MCD with different layering angles under bending moment.Fig. 6

Maximum stress in an MCD with different layering angles under bending moment.

As depicted in Fig. 6, for MCDs with oblique symmetrical layup configurations subjected to a bending moment, the maximum stress in each layer varies as a function of the oblique layup angle. The stress distribution is asymmetric along the layers and increases progressively from the first to the surface layer. The stress distribution in each layer is relatively uniform for [0°]4S and [90°]4S oblique symmetrical layup configurations. When the layup angle is less than 45°, the stress distribution in each layer is similar.

The analysis suggests that 0°, ±45°, and 90° layup angles should be carefully considered when designing MCD layups to optimize torque transmission and axial and angular displacement compensation capabilities.

The effect of torque

Fig. 7 illustrates the maximum torsion angle exhibited by CFRP MCDs with varying layup angle proportions when subjected to a constant torque.Fig. 7Torsion angle of MCD with different layering angle ratios under the same torque.Fig. 7

Torsion angle of MCD with different layering angle ratios under the same torque.

As depicted in Fig. 7, MCDs with 1, 2, 3, and 4# layups (comprising only 0° (radial) and 90° (circumferential) layup angles) exhibit the largest torsion angles when subjected to a constant torque. The torsion angles of MCDs with these four layups are similar. Hence, when only 0° and 90° layup angles are employed, the torque transfer ability of MCDs is low and does not vary as a function of the ratio of these two angles.

For MCDs with 5–10# layups, the torsion angle decreases as the proportion of a 45° layup increases. Furthermore, the torsion angles of MCDs with 6, 7#, 8, and 9# layups are approximately equal. This observation suggests that the torque transfer ability of MCDs does not vary as a function of the ratio of 0° and 90° layup angles when the proportion of 45° layup is constant. Consequently, the proportion of 45° layup in CFRP MCD layups should be carefully considered to optimize the torque transfer ability of MCDs.

The effect of axial displacement load

Fig. 8 presents the calculated axial stiffness of CFRP MCDs with varying layup angle proportions when subjected to a constant coaxial displacement load.Fig. 8Effect of different ply angle ratios on axial stiffness of CFRP MCD.Fig. 8

Effect of different ply angle ratios on axial stiffness of CFRP MCD.

The analysis reveals that CFRP MCDs with a 4# layup angle ratio (comprising only 0° layup, with fibers oriented radially along the surface of the flexible element) exhibit the highest axial stiffness. In contrast, CFRP MCDs with 5# and 10# layup angle ratios (lacking a 0° layup) exhibit the lowest axial stiffness. This observation suggests that omitting a 0° layup in the design of MCD layups reduces the structure's axial stiffness. Furthermore, the axial stiffness of MCDs increases with the proportion of 0° layup. Consequently, the proportion of 0° layup should be carefully considered in their design to enhance the axial compensation ability of CFRP MCDs while maintaining their strength and torsional stiffness.

The effect of axial displacement load

Fig. 9 presents the calculated change in the angular direction of CFRP MCDs with varying layup angle proportions when subjected to a constant bending moment load.Fig. 9The effect of ply angle ratios on CFRP MCD angle.Fig. 9

The effect of ply angle ratios on CFRP MCD angle.

As illustrated in Fig. 9, the angular compensation ability decreases as the proportion of 0° layup increases for CFRP MCDs with only 0° and 90° layups (i.e., 1, 2, 3, and 4# layups). When a certain proportion of 45° layup is included, the impact of the 0° and 90° layup ratios on the angular compensation ability of CFRP MCDs is insignificant. The angular compensation ability increases substantially for MCDs without a 0° layup angle (i.e., only 45° and 90° layups, such as 5 and 10# layups). This observation suggests that the proportion of 0° layup in MCD layups has a pronounced effect on their angular compensation ability and should be carefully considered in their design. Furthermore, incorporating a 90° layup in the design of CFRP drive shafts can enhance their torsional yield [24,25]. The proportion of 90° layup should be appropriately considered in their design to prevent buckling of MCDs during torque transmission.

The test methods

The outer ring hole of the CFRP MCD was fixed on the measuring fixture, and the inner ring hole was added with a washer. The axial displacement load was applied to the washer with a pressure plate, and the axial displacement load was applied to the CFRP MCD using a displacement loading test bench of 0–4 mm. The loading force was recorded by a force sensor, and the displacement of the pressure plate with a displacement angle of 120° was recorded by a percentage table. The strain gauge collects the strain at the key position, as shown in Fig. 11.Fig. 11Axial stiffness test of the CFRP MCD.Fig. 11

Axial stiffness test of the CFRP MCD.

The 45o strain flowers are glued on the positive and negative surfaces of the CFRP MCD. The tangential, radial, and 45o strain conditions are measured in the three directions of the strain flowers. The positions 01 and 11 of the strain gauge are the same position of the axial projection and the closest to the wheel hub (0 is the front side, and 1 is the reverse side, the same below). Positions 02 and 12 are the same position of the axial projection and the middle of the disk surface. Positions 03 and 13 are the same position of the axial projection and the closest to the wheel rim. Positions 04 and 14 are the positive crest and the reverse crest, and 05 and 15 are the positive crest and the reverse crest. The position for attaching the strain gauge is shown in Fig. 12.Fig. 12Strain gauge locations.Fig. 12

Strain gauge locations.

The axial stiffness of CFRP MCD was determined by establishing a coordinate system with axial displacement of each measuring point as the horizontal coordinate and dynamometer reading (axial force) as the vertical coordinate. The test curve is presented in Fig. 13.Fig. 13Axial stiffness curve of the CFRP MCD.Fig. 13

Axial stiffness curve of the CFRP MCD.

The axial force of a CFRP MCD coupling is divided by the axial displacement to obtain the axial stiffness. Fig. 13 demonstrates that the stiffness value of the CFRP MCD is 712.5 N/mm. The error from the design value is 1.8 %. Stiffness test studies of MCD were conducted in Refs. [7,8,26]. Although the design error value was not explained in each reference, the error in this paper is smaller than that in Refs. [7,8,26]. Moreover, according to the design experience of MCD coupling, the deviation between the test value and the design value is less than 10 %. Therefore, it can be concluded that the axial stiffness test results of the CFRP MCD designed in this paper agree with the allowable error range of the design value, verifying the feasibility of the layup design method of CFRP MCD [7,8,26].

Conclusions

In this study, a finite element model of CFRP MCD was established using ABAQUS software. The effects of layup angle and layup angle ratio on the mechanical properties of CFRP MCD were analyzed, and the layup design was completed. The axial stiffness of the designed CFRP MCD was tested using a flexible coupling stiffness test bench, with results agreeing with design values within the allowable error range. The conducted investigation confirmed the feasibility of the layup design method for CFRP MCD and provided a theoretical basis for its engineering design and application.

Analysis of the influence of different layup angles and proportions on CFRP MCD under various loads yielded the following conclusions.(1)A 45° layup angle enhances torque bearing capacity;(2)A 0° layup angle increases axial compensation ability;(3)A 0° layup angle significantly impacts angular compensation ability. Therefore, CFRP MCD layup design should combine 45° and 90° layups."
