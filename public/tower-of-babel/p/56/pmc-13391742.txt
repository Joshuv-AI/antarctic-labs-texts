Robot-assisted modified unilateral percutaneous vertebroplasty for lumbar Kümmell’s disease: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Song Guo, Zhong Liu, RuiNing Hang, KeWei Li, Xingyu Zhu, XinHua Li, MeiJun Yan, YanBin Liu, Liang Zhu, Qiang Fu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The clinical value of using a spine robot to guide unilateral PVP for lumbar Kümmell’s disease has not been fully established. The traditional bilateral pedicle PVP tends to cause suboptimal cement distribution, longer operation time, and increased exposure to radiation. To compare the safety and effectiveness of spine robot-assisted modified unilateral PVP with conventional bilateral pedicle puncture PVP. We performed a single-blind randomized trial including 110 patients with lumbar Kümmell’s disease. Patients were assigned in equal numbers to robot-assisted modified unilateral PVP or manual bilateral pedicle PVP. The main endpoint was 12-month Cobb angle change. Additional endpoints included AVH, PVH, WA, VAS, ODI, operative indicators, and complications. At all time points after the operation, the robot-assisted group showed significantly higher reductions in Cobb angle, WA, VAS, and ODI and significantly better preservation of AVH and PVH (all P < 0.05). Operative time (47.15 ± 8.26 vs. 69.25 ± 10.94 min), blood loss (10.29 ± 2.87 vs. 25.31 ± 4.43 mL), and fluoroscopy times (9.80 ± 1.59 vs. 26.24 ± 4.74) are significantly reduced in the robot-assisted group. The rate of bone cement leakage and vertebral recollapse also decreased. Robot-assisted modified unilateral PVP has better long-term correction of kyphotic deformation, reduced complications, and higher procedural efficacy compared to utilizing conventional bilateral PVP under the limitation of single-center single-blind trial. It is a more precise and minimally invasive approach for treating lumbar Kümmell’s disease.

Introduction

Kümmell’s disease represents a delayed osteoporotic vertebral collapse with a protracted disease course, often leading to persistent pain, functional impairment, and increased mortality risk [1]. First identified by the German physician Kummell in 1895, the disease is clinically marked by spinal pain resulting from minimal or no obvious trauma in the context of osteoporosis. After an initial asymptomatic period, most patients experience progressive worsening of spinal pain at the same site, eventually developing kyphotic spinal deformity [2]. The pathogenesis of the disease is commonly thought to involve ischemic osteonecrosis following vertebral fractures. Mild trauma can result in small, often undetectable fractures that disrupt the vertebral blood supply due to mechanical vascular injury. This reduction in blood flow impairs the healing process, delaying or even halting fracture repair. Over time, scar tissue develops at the fracture site, further restricting blood supply and perpetuating a harmful cycle of ischemia, necrosis, re-fracture, and ongoing ischemia. This cycle ultimately leads to fracture nonunion, the development of intra-vertebral clefts, and the formation of pseudoarthrosis [3, 4].

For patients diagnosed of Kümmell’s disease who do not have significant neurological deficits and retain relatively intact posterior vertebral walls, many experts advocate for percutaneous vertebroplasty (PVP) as a means to achieve short-term pain relief and improve quality of life [5]. However, research indicates that PVP outcomes in Kümmell’s disease are less promising compared to those for regular osteoporotic compression fractures. Over follow-up, patients may continue to lose vertebral height and develop progressive kyphotic deformity [6]. Liu et al. reported a vertebral recollapse rate of about 3.2% among Kümmell’s disease patients who underwent PVP. This complication has been ascribed to inadequate filling of the cement within the cleft and over-injected cement outside of the non-cleft area, which may additionally aggravate the blood supply of the vertebra [7]. In addition, lumbar Kümmell’s disease vertebra clefts are usually located ventrally to the center of the vertebra and often cross the midline. The anatomical constraints, along with the limited abduction angles of the pedicle, pose significant challenges to achieving adequate cement distribution, even with the use of traditional bilateral pedicle punctures [8, 9]. Such limitations can reduce PVP’s effectiveness. Moreover, bilateral punctures are not only correlated with longer surgery time, but also more cost and radiation exposure, which highlights the disadvantages of this technique for lumbar Kümmell’s disease.

The modified unilateral extra-pedicle approach bypasses pedicle constraints, enabling greater abduction angles (up to 40°-45°) and more flexible cranio-caudal angulation [10]. This technique enables the working channel to directly access the vertebral cleft for optimal cement dispersion, better integration with surrounding bone, and improved axial stress support, restoring vertebral stiffness and balancing stress distribution. Additionally, unilateral puncture reduces operative time, radiation exposure, costs, and cement usage. However, this approach involves longer puncture paths that may traverse the retroperitoneum, vital organs, and neurovascular structures, making manual puncture difficult and risky, especially with the poor imaging quality typical in patients with severe osteoporosis. Jiang indicated using the modified approach for puncture still has drawbacks such as intraoperative difficulty in puncture and increased radiation exposure, which limits the clinical promotion of this technique [11]. The spinal robotic system has gained widespread clinical use in recent years. The system allows precise preoperative CT planning and intraoperative X-ray matching with minimal radiation exposure. Once matching is successful, the robot suggests the optimal position for the external guide sleeve. The surgeon only needs to make a skin incision along the guide sleeve and can quickly and accurately insert the working channel. No fluoroscopic assistance is required during the procedure, significantly reducing the need for repeated intraoperative imaging [12]. Barzilay et al. demonstrated that in 60 osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures treated with robot-assisted vertebral augmentation, intraoperative radiation exposure was reduced by 74% compared to conventional methods, and by 50% compared to navigation techniques [13]. Our prior studies also found that robot-assisted PVP for osteoporotic lumbar fractures significantly improved channel placement accuracy and reduced fluoroscopy usage by 60.68%, alleviating surgical workload [14]. Accordingly, we performed a randomized, controlled, single-blind clinical trial to determine whether robot-assisted modified unilateral PVP offers superior safety and clinical efficacy compared with manual bilateral pedicle puncture PVP in the treatment of lumbar Kümmell’s disease.

Methods

Study design

This single-blind, randomized controlled trial was prospectively registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2200058659) and performed at the Department of Spine Surgery, Shanghai General Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine, between January 2022 and February 2026. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of Shanghai General Hospital (Approval No. [2022]55). The study was conducted in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the International Council for Harmonisation Good Clinical Practice guidelines. All participants provided written informed consent. This trial is reported in accordance with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) statement [15].

Randomization and blinding

After providing written consent, participants were allocated to treatment groups through the Clinical Information Management Suite, ensuring allocation concealment during the process. This study used simple randomization. Randomization was conducted by statistical personnel who generated random allocation tables for the trial groups. A light-proof sealed envelope containing the treatment assignment for each case was prepared alongside the random allocation table. Each envelope was marked with a unique participant enrollment number and contained a treatment method code. Researchers opened the envelopes sequentially according to the participant enrollment order, recording the date, time, and signature of the person opening the envelope, and followed the assigned treatment code to carry out the procedures. Throughout the entire process, the patients were blinded. There was no non-compliance with treatment allocation. In addition, the entire envelope-opening process was supervised by personnel from the Clinical Research Center of our hospital. The envelopes were kept by a statistician from the hospital’s clinical center, who was not involved in the clinical work.

Sample size estimation

We estimated the sample size relying on statistical assumptions predefined prior to experimentation. The Cobb angle was selected as the primary outcome indicator because previous studies have used it to assess treatment effects in Kümmell’s disease [16, 17]. Based on preliminary data, the mean Cobb angle at 12 months postoperatively was estimated to be 8° in the experimental group and 10° in the control group, with a standard deviation of 3°. With a two-sided α = 0.05 and 1-β = 0.9, PASS software calculated that 49 patients were required in each group. After considering a 10% dropout rate, the planned enrollment was set at 55 patients per group, for a total of 110 cases. The allocation ratio between the experimental and control groups was 1:1. The experimental group underwent Mazor robot-assisted unilateral modified PVP, while the control group underwent manual bilateral transpedicular PVP.

Interventions

(1) Experimental group: spine robot-assisted modified unilateral approach PVP

The surgical procedure in the robot-assisted group consisted of two parts: preoperative planning and intraoperative execution. First, preoperative planning: The entry point for the modified PVP approach was selected at the superolateral corner of the upper endplate of the vertebral body, corresponding to the base of Kambin’s triangle. Because this modified approach does not pass through the pedicle, it allows for a greater abduction angle. During preoperative planning, the trajectory can therefore be planned directly toward the cleft in the center of the vertebral body, thereby ensuring sufficient diffusion of bone cement within the cleft. Second, intraoperative execution: After satisfactory anesthetic depth was confirmed, patients were placed in the prone position on a carbon-fiber spinal intraoperative table, and the surgical level was confirmed using a C-arm fluoroscope. Spinous process screws were implanted two levels above the lesion, and their placement was confirmed using fluoroscopy. The robotic frame and bed-mounted rail were then fixed. Anteroposterior and oblique fluoroscopic images were acquired for registration using a 3D marker. The ideal entry point for the guide sleeve was identified using the robot’s navigation system, and the skin was incised along this path. The vertebral cleft was accessed using a powered drill, and a working channel and hollow sleeve were subsequently inserted. The position of the working channel within the cleft was confirmed using fluoroscopy. Bone cement was injected through the working channel under continuous C-arm imaging until satisfactory filling was achieved or until the cement reached the posterior border of the vertebra, the intervertebral disc, or the venous plexus, at which point injection was stopped. The incision was closed. Throughout the entire intraoperative course, vital signs were continuously monitored at regular intervals to ensure patient stability (Fig. 1). All surgeries were performed by an experienced spine surgeon who had independently completed more than 100 spinal robot-assisted surgeries and had already overcome the learning curve of robotic surgery. In contrast, in the control group, the selected puncture trajectory passed through the pedicle. Therefore, the entry point for the conventional puncture approach was located at the 3 o’clock or 9 o’clock position of the pedicle. Because this approach is constrained by the abduction angle of the pedicle, the puncture trajectory must remain within the pedicle and cannot be freely designed. As a result, it is difficult to advance the puncture channel into the vertebral cleft, which may compromise bone cement diffusion and lead to insufficient cement distribution within the cleft.

(2) Control group: manual bilateral pedicle puncture PVP

Bilateral percutaneous manual pedicle puncture was performed under real-time C-arm fluoroscopic guidance. After successful anesthetic induction, a Jamshidi needle was advanced to the 3 or 9 o’clock position of the pathological vertebra through a minimal percutaneous incision. Under lateral fluoroscopy, the needle trajectory was adjusted and advanced along the pedicle until it reached the posterior vertebral wall. Subsequent anteroposterior fluoroscopic views were obtained to verify that the needle tip was positioned at the medial margin of the pedicle silhouette before proceeding with further insertion into the vertebral body. The inner stylet was then withdrawn, a guidewire was placed, and the working channel was established. Bone cement injection was performed as in the experimental group, using polymethylmethacrylate (PMMA) bone cement (Fig. 2). All surgeries were performed by an experienced spine surgeon who had independently completed more than 100 osteoporotic vertebral fracture surgeries and had already overcome the learning curve.

(3) Postoperative management

Patients were instructed to perform functional rehabilitation exercises the day after surgery. Bed rest was advised for four weeks postoperatively, with brace-assisted ambulation. Patients were also prescribed oral anti-osteoporosis medications during their hospital stay and after discharge. Briefly, Denosumab was administered subcutaneously to all patients after surgery. Meanwhile, active vitamin D combined with calcium carbonate was supplemented individually based on each patient’s bone mineral density status. Six months after the initial subcutaneous injection of denosumab, bone mineral density and bone turnover markers were re-examined. The subcutaneous denosumab injection was then repeated, along with continued supplementation of active vitamin D and calcium carbonate thereafter.

Outcomes

Demographic and clinical baseline characteristics, including age, gender, and involved surgical segment, were prospectively recorded via a standardized electronic data capture platform. Effectiveness indicators included Cobb angle (CA), Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) score for quality of life, fractured vertebral height (AVH and PVH), vertebral wedge angle (WA), Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) score for back pain. Safety indicators included the bone cement leakage and recollapse rate. Intraoperative indicators included fluoroscopy frequency, operative time, and blood loss. In this study, an experienced and well qualified surgeon was designated to measure the outcomes, and blinding was applied to control measurement bias.

Primary outcomes

The primary outcome was the Cobb angle on standard lateral X-rays 12 months after surgery. Standard Cobb angle measurement was performed by tracing lines parallel to the superior endplate of the vertebra above the fractured level and the inferior endplate of the vertebra below the fractured level, with the intersection angle between these lines recorded as the Cobb angle (Fig. 3) [18]. Cobb angles were also measured at 3 days, 3 months, and 6 months and at the final follow-up postoperatively.

Secondary outcomes

Standard lateral radiographs were used to measure anterior vertebral height (AVH) and posterior vertebral height (PVH), defined as the vertical distances between the superior and inferior margins of the anterior and posterior borders of the fractured vertebral body. Vertebral wedge angle (WA) was determined from the angle subtended by the superior and inferior endplates of the fractured vertebra (Fig. 3) [19]. Spinal functional status was assessed via the validated Chinese version of the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI), a widely accepted instrument for quantifying back pain-induced limitations in activities of daily living. Each ODI item is rated on a 0–5 Likert scale, and the total score is expressed as a percentage according to the formula: (sum of individual item scores / (5 × number of completed items)) × 100 [20]. Pain intensity was evaluated using the 10-point Visual Analogue Scale (VAS), where 0 represents no pain and 10 indicates the most severe pain imaginable [21]. The incidence of intraoperative bone cement leakage and postoperative vertebral recollapse was documented for all patients in both study groups. Intraoperative outcomes, namely the number of fluoroscopic exposures, operative duration, and estimated intraoperative blood loss, were additionally recorded. Furthermore, cost-effectiveness metrics were compared between the two treatment groups as pre-specified secondary endpoints.

Statistical analysis

Blinded study results and data on safety and efficacy were reviewed by the data and safety monitoring board every six months to monitor for deaths, paralysis events, new fractures, new radiculopathy, or new myelopathy, and infections. SPSS software was used to perform analysis. Normality of the data was assessed. For continuous variables Cobb angle, AVH, PVH, WA, VAS, ODI, blood loss, operation time and use of fluoroscopy, standard deviation and means were determined. Within-group differences between pre- and post-treatment outcomes were determined using paired t-tests. In order to determine differences between the changes in the experimental and the control group, independent sample t-tests were performed. Statistical significance was determined based on the rule that when a P-value of 0.05 or less (two-tailed) is calculated, a statistically significant difference in the analyzed variables can be concluded.

In this study, “±” refers to the standard deviation (SD).

Results

Enrollment and intervention

A total of 182 patients were initially screened for study eligibility, of whom 110 were ultimately enrolled in the clinical trial. The remaining 72 patients were excluded for the following reasons: failure to meet the predefined inclusion criteria, voluntary withdrawal of consent prior to enrollment, cancellation of the scheduled surgical procedure, or other unspecified exclusionary factors. Fifty-five patients were assigned to each group. The control group underwent manual bilateral pedicle puncture PVP, whereas the experimental group underwent robot-assisted modified unilateral approach PVP (Fig. 4). The 12-month postoperative follow-up rate was 92.73%, with 6 patients lost to follow-up and 2 unable to continue participation due to a change in work location. Follow-up duration ranged from 13.2 to 38.4 months, with a mean follow-up time of 25.6 ± 3.78 months. By the final follow-up, an additional 5 patients had been lost to follow-up. After applying all predefined exclusion criteria, 97 patients were retained for the final statistical analysis, comprising 49 patients allocated to the intervention group and 48 assigned to the control group.

Patient characteristics

Baseline demographic data showed that the control group included 24 males and 31 females patients, whereas the intervention group had 23 males and 32 females patients. The mean age of patients in the control and intervention groups was 74.31 ± 4.50 years and 76.04 ± 6.53 years, respectively. For surgical segments, the control group included 32 cases at the L1 segment, 7 cases at the L2 segment, 6 cases at the L3 segment, 5 cases at the L4 segment, and 5 cases at the L5 segment. In the experimental group, there were 33 cases at the L1 segment, 6 cases at the L2 segment, 7 cases at the L3 segment, 5 cases at the L4 segment, and 4 cases at the L5 segment. In the control group, there were 48 patients with stage I Kümmell’s disease and 7 patients with stage II; in the experimental group, there were 47 patients with stage I Kümmell’s disease and 8 patients with stage II. Comparative statistical analyses revealed no significant between-group differences in baseline characteristics, including age, sex, surgical segment, and disease stage (Table 1).

Secondary outcomes

Preoperative AVH and PVH were comparable between the two groups. At 3 days after surgery, both groups showed significant increases in AVH and PVH. However, as follow-up continued, AVH and PVH declined rapidly in the control group and returned to preoperative levels by 3 months. With longer follow-up, the control group showed further decreases in AVH and PVH. In contrast, although the experimental group also showed gradual decreases over time, the decline was slower. At 12 months, AVH and PVH in the experimental group remained above preoperative levels (Table 3; Fig. 5). At the final follow-up, AVH and PVH in the experimental group were still higher than before surgery, whereas the control group showed further decline compared with the 12-month postoperative values.

There was no statistically difference in preoperative WA between the two groups. At 3 days postoperatively, WA in both groups improved significantly. Over time, WA in the control group increased rapidly, returning to preoperative levels by 6 months postoperatively. With further follow-up, WA in the control group continued to rise. In contrast, while WA in the experimental group also increased over time, the rate of increase was much slower. By 12 months postoperatively, WA in the experimental group remained lower than its preoperative level (Table 3; Fig. 5). At the final follow-up, the WA in the experimental group remained lower than before surgery, while the WA in the control group increased further compared to 12 months postoperatively.

Baseline VAS and ODI scores were similar between the two groups. Significant reductions in both outcomes were observed at 3 days after surgery. Although both scores increased gradually over time, the experimental group demonstrated consistently lower VAS and ODI scores than the control group throughout follow-up (Table 3; Fig. 5).

Cost-effectiveness

Hospitalization costs were 34,755 ± 323.65 RMB in the robotic group and 35,335 ± 343.45 RMB in the control group. Although costs were slightly lower in the robotic group, this difference did not reach statistical significance.

Adverse events

In the robotic group, bone cement leakage occurred in 6 patients (6/55), compared with 21 patients (21/55) in the control group. By the final follow-up, no vertebral height recollapse to preoperative levels was observed in the experimental group, whereas 37 patients (37/55) in the control group showed recollapse. Bone cement leakage and vertebral recollapse were significantly less frequent in the experimental group than in the control group. No major complications, including nerve injury, bone cement allergy, or pulmonary embolism, occurred in either group.

Discussion

Current treatment options for Kümmell’s disease include conservative management, minimally invasive vertebroplasty, and open surgical fixation [22–26]. The disease is commonly classified into three stages according to imaging characteristics and vertebral involvement. In stage I, adjacent disc degeneration is absent and vertebral height loss is less than 20%. In stage II, adjacent disc degeneration is present and vertebral height loss exceeds 20%. Stage III is defined by posterior vertebral wall rupture, which may cause spinal cord compression symptoms [27].

Park et al. [28] reported significant short-term pain relief after PVP in 18 patients with stage I or II Kümmell’s disease, supporting the role of PVP as an effective option for pain control in selected patients. However, the choice of puncture technique for PVP is not yet standardized. Some patients undergo unilateral pedicle puncture, while others undergo bilateral pedicle puncture. Given the lack of standardized technical guidelines and consensus, the long-term clinical efficacy of PVP in the treatment of lumbar Kümmell’s disease warrants additional in-depth investigation. There are three major challenges in treating Kümmell’s disease with PVP. First, conventional PVP uses bilateral pedicle punctures, but due to limitations of pedicle anatomy and manual operation, it is often difficult to place the working channel into the fracture cleft. This results in insufficient filling of bone cement, leading to progressive vertebral compression and kyphotic deformity. Moreover, bilateral punctures increase the time required, risks, surgical trauma, and costs. Second, manual punctures lack precision, which increases the likelihood of placing the working channel into the ruptured part of the vertebra. This significantly raises the rate of bone cement leakage. To ensure safety, the amount of bone cement injected is reduced, compromising the surgical effect. Third, Kümmell’s disease patients often have severe spinal deformities and vertebral compression, resulting in poor intraoperative fluoroscopic image quality. This makes it more difficult to puncture and place the working channel, prolonging the operation time and increasing surgical risks [29, 30]. Therefore, for lumbar Kümmell’s disease treatment, careful preoperative evaluation of the affected vertebra is necessary to understand the location of the vertebral cleft and its relationship with the vertebral walls. Preoperative precise planning and intraoperative precise placement of the working channel are needed to ensure sufficient bone cement filling in the cleft, reducing the risk of progressive vertebral compression and kyphotic deformity [4]. Additionally, while ensuring sufficient cleft filling, it is necessary to reduce the amount of bone cement to minimize cement leakage and surgical risks. Improving the accuracy of working channel placement during PVP is an important target for enhancing the treatment effect of lumbar Kümmell’s disease.

With the continuous advancement of medical imaging technology, artificial intelligence, and mechanical-assisted techniques, the robotic spine surgery system has become key equipment for assisting in vertebral localization, puncture, and biopsy [31, 32]. Lee et al. [33] conducted a multicenter study comparing robot-assisted screw placement with manual screw placement. The results showed that robot significantly improved the accuracy of implant placement, reduced complications by 48%, decreased intraoperative radiation by 56%, lowered reoperation rates by 46%, and shortened the average hospital stay by 27%. Robot-assisted PVP has several advantages. First, precise preoperative intelligent planning provides greater surgical freedom, allowing repeated simulations of the surgical approach to avoid critical structures and improve safety. Second, the robotic arm and external guide sleeve reduce surgeon fatigue and hand tremor, ensuring stability during operations. Third, precise planning and stable operation improve the accuracy of working channel placement, avoiding repeated fluoroscopy and reducing intraoperative radiation and surgical time. Fourth, Robot-assisted modified unilateral extrapedicular puncture enables precise working channel placement, avoiding bilateral pedicle punctures, reducing risks, trauma, and costs, and shortening surgical time. In this study, a randomized, single-blind, single-center trial was designed and implemented to investigate the safety and efficacy of Mazor robot-assisted modified unilateral extrapedicular PVP in treating lumbar Kümmell’s disease, compared with conventional bilateral pedicle puncture PVP. We successfully enrolled 55 patients in the control group (bilateral pedicle puncture) and 55 patients in the experimental group (robot-assisted unilateral approach). Statistical analysis showed that baseline characteristics were comparable between the two groups.

Based on previous studies and preliminary experiments, Cobb angle was chosen as the primary outcome measure [34]. The results also showed significant postoperative improvement in Cobb angle in both groups compared with preoperative values. However, as follow-up progressed, Cobb angle in the control group increased rapidly, returning to preoperative levels by 6 months postoperatively and continuing to worsen. In contrast, while Cobb angle in the experimental group also increased over time, the rate of increase was much slower, and by 12 months postoperatively, Cobb angle remained lower than preoperative levels. This suggests that the robot-assisted modified approach, with its larger abduction angle, allows the working channel to reach closer to the vertebral midline, enabling better filling of bone cement into the fracture cleft and achieving superior kyphosis correction. Variations in baseline demographics, medication compliance, and radiographic measurement standards may underlie interstudy differences in postoperative kyphosis correction loss. Compared with the younger cohorts with better bone quality included in earlier reports, our study included many elderly patients with severe osteopenia and advanced disc degeneration. Their more severe baseline deformity may inherently predispose them to greater kyphosis progression and correction loss at 12 months. The favorable mean follow-up Cobb angle of 10° reported previously was derived largely from overseas populations [35]. In our elderly cohort, suboptimal adherence to postoperative immobilization protocols and premature weight bearing may have further accelerated sagittal alignment deterioration and Cobb angle re-progression in some control subjects. In addition, inconsistent imaging planes and measured spinal segments across publications may introduce inherent disparities in Cobb angle quantification. Although a mean 12-month Cobb angle of 10° was adopted from historical data for sample-size estimation, postoperative values greater than 10° have been reported in comparable Kümmell’s disease populations. Ding et al. [36] reported 1-year Cobb angles of 14.41 ± 4.68° and 10.63 ± 4.55° after neutral-position and hyperextension-position vertebroplasty, respectively. Therefore, the value of 13.80 ± 2.01° observed in our control group is clinically plausible. Differences among studies may reflect variations in baseline kyphotic deformity, osteoporosis severity, vertebral level, reduction position, cement distribution, and radiographic measurement methods. The vertebral wedge angle (WA) is another key indicator of kyphotic deformity [16]. The study found no significant difference in preoperative WA between the two groups. Both groups showed significant improvement in WA at 3 days postoperatively. However, as follow-up continued, WA in the control group increased rapidly, returning to preoperative levels by 6 months and continuing to worsen. In the experimental group, WA also increased over time but at a slower rate, and by 12 months postoperatively, WA remained lower than preoperative levels. This further supports the advantages of robot-assisted modified extrapedicular puncture in achieving better kyphosis correction. Regarding AVH and PVH, which reflect vertebral height restoration, both groups showed significant postoperative improvement. However, the AVH and PVH levels in the control group rapidly dropped to preoperative levels by 3 months postoperatively and continued to worsen. In the experimental group, although AVH and PVH also gradually reduced, the reduction rate was much slower, and by 12 months postoperatively, vertebral height remained higher than preoperative levels. This indicates that the robot-assisted modified approach has a superior capability to preserve vertebral height.

VAS and ODI scores reflect changes in symptoms and quality of life. Both groups showed postoperative improvement, suggesting that PVP can relieve symptoms and improve daily function in patients with lumbar Kümmell’s disease [37, 38]. However, follow-up VAS and ODI scores were lower in the experimental group than in the control group. This difference may be related to the shorter operative time, reduced blood loss, and less postoperative wound pain in the experimental group, which may support earlier mobilization and better long-term recovery.

Bone cement leakage is among the most frequently encountered adverse events following PVP in patients with Kümmell’s disease [8]. Our analysis revealed that the incidence of intraoperative bone cement leakage was markedly reduced in the intervention group compared with the control cohort. This may be because robotic assistance allowed more accurate puncture-path planning and a larger external expansion angle, making it easier to access the intravertebral fissure for cement injection. As a result, cement leakage was reduced. By contrast, in the control group, the limited external expansion angle of the pedicle made it more difficult to establish a working channel into the intravertebral fissure in some cases. More cement therefore had to be injected to achieve fissure filling, increasing the risk of leakage.

In addition, osteoporosis is a core factor in the progression of Kümmell’s disease [39]. Imbalanced postoperative compliance with anti-osteoporosis medication between the two groups may confound the results regarding vertebral recollapse and kyphosis progression [40]. In the present study, despite the baseline intergroup imbalance in adherence to anti-osteoporotic medication, multivariate adjustment was performed to control for this confounding factor. Significant intergroup differences persisted after adjustment, further suggesting that robot-assisted surgery independently reduces postoperative vertebral recollapse and attenuates progressive kyphotic aggravation. These clinical benefits is independent of patients’ compliance with anti-osteoporosis regimens.

In terms of intraoperative parameters, patients in the intervention group exhibited markedly reduced operative time, intraoperative blood loss, and total number of fluoroscopic exposures relative to those in the control group, which once again confirmed that robot-assisted modified PVP is a more minimally invasive procedure. Previous studies have shown that robotic assistance can improve puncture accuracy and reduce fluoroscopy use during vertebral augmentation procedures. Tan et al. [41] compared 42 patients who underwent robot-assisted single-level percutaneous vertebroplasty (PVP) with 42 matched patients treated using conventional manual PVP. They found that robot-assisted PVP significantly shortened both puncture time and total operative time and reduced the number of fluoroscopic exposures during puncture and throughout the entire procedure. Lin et al. [42] reported similar findings in patients undergoing multilevel percutaneous kyphoplasty. In their study, TiRobot-assisted PKP significantly reduced the operative time from 57.656 ± 5.445 min to 47.153 ± 7.128 min and decreased the number of fluoroscopic exposures from 51.7 to 34.7. The robot-assisted group also had lower radiation doses, higher puncture accuracy, more favorable cement distribution, and a lower cement leakage rate. Wang et al. [43] likewise demonstrated that TiRobot-assisted vertebral augmentation reduced the mean number of fluoroscopic exposures from 31.53 ± 5.72 to 9.80 ± 1.74, although the reduction in operative time did not reach statistical significance. These findings suggest that reduced needle repositioning and fewer repeated fluoroscopic confirmations are among the most consistent advantages of robot-assisted vertebral augmentation, whereas the effect on operative time may vary according to procedural complexity, the number of treated vertebrae, and the surgeon’s experience with robotic systems. Our findings are generally consistent with those of previous studies. Compared with conventional bilateral transpedicular PVP, robot-assisted modified unilateral PVP significantly reduced the operative time from 69.25 ± 10.94 min to 47.15 ± 8.26 min and the number of fluoroscopic exposures from 26.24 ± 4.74 to 9.80 ± 1.59. These benefits may be explained by the ability of robotic navigation to preoperatively determine an optimal entry point and puncture angle, thereby reducing repeated intraoperative adjustment of the puncture needle.

Notably, the mean operative time of 47.15 min in our robot-assisted group was very similar to the 47.153 min reported by Lin et al. [42], and the mean number of fluoroscopic exposures of 9.80 was also comparable to the 9.80 exposures reported by Wang et al. [43]. Therefore, the procedural efficiency achieved in the present study was comparable to that reported in previous robot-assisted vertebral augmentation studies. Moreover, compared with some reports involving relatively acute osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures, the operative time in our study was not markedly shorter despite robotic assistance [42]. This may be related to the distinctive pathological characteristics of Kümmell’s disease. Unlike acute osteoporotic compression fractures, Kümmell’s disease is characterized by delayed post-traumatic vertebral osteonecrosis, intravertebral vacuum clefts, nonunion, progressive vertebral collapse, and kyphotic deformity. The intravertebral cleft may be surrounded by sclerotic bone, which can increase puncture resistance and make precise needle advancement more difficult. Furthermore, the cleft may extend toward the vertebral cortex or intervertebral disc, increasing the risk of cement leakage. Consequently, repeated anteroposterior and lateral fluoroscopic confirmation is often required to verify the needle tip position and continuously monitor cement filling. Slow and staged cement injection may also be necessary to ensure adequate filling of the intravertebral cleft while preventing cement migration into the disc space, paravertebral tissues, or venous system [3]. These additional safety-related steps may partly offset the time-saving advantage of robotic navigation. Severe osteoporosis and advanced vertebral collapse may also contribute to prolonged operative and fluoroscopic times. Patients with Kümmell’s disease often have more pronounced loss of bone density, cortical disruption, and vertebral deformity than patients with uncomplicated acute osteoporotic fractures. These changes may make pedicle and vertebral landmarks less distinct under fluoroscopy and require more careful confirmation of the puncture trajectory, needle depth, and cement distribution. In addition, severe osteoporosis and greater vertebral compression have been associated with an increased risk of cement leakage in Kümmell’s disease [3], which may prompt surgeons to use smaller cement volumes per injection and perform more frequent fluoroscopic checks. Future studies should record fluoroscopy duration, dose-area product, and actual radiation exposure to both patients and surgeons, and should further analyze these outcomes according to bone mineral density, intravertebral cleft morphology, vertebral collapse severity, and cortical integrity.

Although the spinal robot was used for assisted operations in the experimental group, the cost did not increase compared with the control group. This was because the costs associated with spinal robot-assisted operations were waived, and the anesthesia time in the robot-assisted group was shorter, thereby reducing the corresponding expenses.

Our study has several limitations. First, only patients with stage I or II Kümmell’s disease were included, and patients with stage III disease were not evaluated. However, stage I and II patients are more common in clinical practice and are usually better candidates for PVP, whereas stage III patients often require additional internal fixation and decompression. Second, this was a single-blind study with some follow-up loss and a relatively small sample size, giving it less statistical power than large-scale, double-blind, multicenter studies. Third, the follow-up duration was relatively short, so longer observation is needed to assess long-term clinical efficacy. Finally, this study did not compare PVP with similar interventions such as PKP, which uses balloon inflation before bone cement injection.

Conclusions

In conclusion, robot-assisted modified unilateral approach PVP is a safer and more effective treatment for lumbar Kümmell’s disease, although this study was limited by its single-center, single-blind design. The robot-assisted modified unilateral approach has several advantages compared with traditional bilateral pedicle puncture PVP, including shorter operative time, less intraoperative blood loss, fewer fluoroscopy instances, better maintenance of vertebral height, improved correction of kyphotic deformities, and significant improvements in pain relief and life quality. These results suggest that the robot-assisted modified unilateral approach is a more minimally invasive surgical technique to treat lumbar Kümmell’s disease. However, the sample size of this study was relatively small. Under such a small sample, subgroup analysis, detection of rare adverse events, and generalizability of conclusions are limited. However, the 95% confidence interval for the primary outcome indicator, Cobb angle, was relatively narrow, demonstrating the precision of the study results. Furthermore, multicenter, double-blind randomized controlled trials are needed to confirm the results and establish a standardized treatment protocol.
