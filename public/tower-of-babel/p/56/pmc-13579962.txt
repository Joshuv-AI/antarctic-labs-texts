Primary duct closure after laparoscopic common bile duct exploration in elderly patients with cholecystocholedocholithiasis: a propensity-matched analysis
Authors: Yang Liao, Fei Liu, Nan Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLaparoscopic common bile duct exploration (LCBDE) is a minimally invasive option for treating cholecystocholedocholithiasis. Primary duct closure (PDC) after laparoscopic common bile duct exploration has been shown to be safe. This study aims to evaluate the feasibility and safety of LCBDE + PDC in elderly (≥ 65 years) patients.MethodsThis study included 284 elderly patients who underwent LCBDE(PDC group = 86, T-tube drainage group = 198). We compared the baseline characteristics and perioperative data between the two groups. The Propensity Score Matching (PSM) method was employed to balance the baseline characteristics of the groups and mitigate potential biases.ResultsThe PDC group showed shorter operative time (105 vs. 130 min, P < 0.001), reduced postoperative hospital stays (7 days vs. 8 days, P < 0.001) and total hospital stays (11.5 days vs. 13 days, P < 0.001), and lower costs (3273.6 USD vs. 3848.4 USD, P < 0.001) compared to the T-tube group. The rates of postoperative bile leakage (4.65% vs. 2.02%, P = 0.25) and severe complications (3.49% vs. 3.54%, P = 1) were comparable between the two groups. After PSM, PDC group maintained advantages in operative time (105 min vs. 130 min, P < 0.001) and costs ( 3310.9 USD vs. 3719.4 USD, P = 0.012).ConclusionsOur study demonstrates that LCBDE + PDC is a safe and feasible treatment for elderly patients with cholecystocholedocholithiasis.

Introduction

Gallstone is a common disease of the digestive system, with reported gallstone prevalence ranging from 1.93% to 25.8% [1]. The prevalence of choledocholithiasis among symptomatic gallstone patients varies between 10% and 20% [2]. Choledocholithiasis can result in serious complications, including acute obstructive suppurative cholangitis and acute biliary pancreatitis. These complications present significant health risks and can be life-threatening if choledocholithiasis are not removed promptly [3]. The incidence of cholecystocholedocholithiasis increases with age [1, 4]. In the era of population aging, the rising incidence of cholecystocholedocholithiasis, along with an increase in comorbidities and a decline in physiological function among elderly patients, presents surgeons with increasingly challenging cases. To address these challenges, strategies such as Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) and multimodal analgesia [5] can be implemented to significantly enhance the recovery process for elderly patients. Currently, the primary treatment options for cholecystocholedocholithiasis include laparoscopic common bile duct exploration with laparoscopic cholecystectomy (LCBDE + LC) and endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography followed by laparoscopic cholecystectomy (ERCP + LC). Preoperative ERCP with LC has emerged as the preferred treatment approach, as endorsed by the European Society of Gastrointestinal Endoscopy and the Japanese Society of Gastroenterology [6, 7]. However, the stone clearance rate and complication rate of the two strategies are comparable, and compared with ERCP + LC, LCBDE + LC offers advantages in terms of hospitalization costs and is a single-stage surgical procedure [8, 9]. A meta-analysis [10] has demonstrated that, in comparison to the two-stage method, the single-stage treatment strategy involving intraoperative ERCP combined with laparoscopic cholecystectomy yields superior outcomes. With advancements in surgical techniques, the clinical application of the single-stage treatment plan LCBDE + LC has become increasingly prevalent in areas where medical resources are limited. In traditional laparoscopic common bile duct exploration, the T-tube is inserted into the common bile duct to facilitate bile drainage and potentially eliminate any residual CBD stones via a percutaneous T-tube sinus. However, the use of T-tube drainage may be associated with several complications, including pain at the drainage site, electrolyte disturbances, retrograde biliary tract infections, and biliary peritonitis [11]. Recently, primary duct closure after laparoscopic common bile duct exploration has been considered a safe alternative to T-tube, as it can further reduce surgical injury and significantly enhance patients’ postoperative quality of life [12]. A study [13] demonstrated that compared with T-tube drainage group, the laparoscopic common bile duct exploration with primary duct closure group exhibited advantages in terms of mean surgical time, postoperative hospital stays, hospitalization costs. Although another study [14] confirmed that laparoscopic common bile duct exploration is both safe and effective for elderly patients, it did not strictly differentiate between primary duct closure and T-tube drainage among the included participants. A recent study has shown [15] that compared with the young population, LCBDE + PDC is a safe and feasible option for suitable elderly patients. No studies have yet compared the feasibility and safety of primary duct closure versus T-tube drainage after laparoscopic common bile duct exploration specifically in elderly patients (≥ 65 years old). Currently, there is still no clear consensus regarding the safety and efficacy of LCBDE + PDC specifically for elderly patients with cholecystocholedocholithiasis. This study aims to further evaluate the feasibility and safety of LCBDE + PDC in elderly patients(≥65 years old)compared with T-tube drainage.

Materials and methods

Patients selection

This study presents a retrospective analysis of clinical data from consecutive elderly patients (≥ 65 years old) diagnosed with cholecystocholedocholithiasis, who underwent laparoscopic common bile duct exploration at our hospital between July 2020 and September 2024. The study was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of our Hospital(No. M2025-16). The diagnosis of cholecystocholedocholithiasis was mainly based on imaging studies, including abdominal ultrasound, computed tomography (CT), and/or magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP). Patients were divided into two groups: the primary duct closure (PDC) group and the T-tube drainage group. The inclusion criteria for LCBDE were as follows: (1) the diameter of the CBD was ≥8 mm; (2) no contraindications to general anesthesia; (3) no severe acute cholangitis or moderate to severe acute pancreatitis; (3) no history of biliary surgery and cholecystectomy; (4) the Oddi sphincter not loses its contraction function; (5) no cavernous transformation of the portal vein ; (6) no decompensated liver cirrhosis; (7) Child-Pugh A/B grade; (8) no malignant tumor of the biliary tract; (9) ASA ≤ IV grade. The inclusion criteria for primary duct closure included: (1) no residual stones and intrahepatic stones; (2) no significant inflammatory edema of the CBD; (3) the Oddi sphincter functions normally without stricture; (4) no Mirizzi syndrome; (5) obtain the patient’s consent; (6) the common bile duct has an adequate blood supply and no tissue defect. For patients who are not suitable or refuse to primary duct suture, T-tube drainage is selected. Patient demographics, clinical data, and intraoperative and postoperative outcomes were meticulously collected and analyzed. The management process for choledocholithiasis is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Operative techniques

All patients underwent general anesthesia and received intravenous prophylactic antibiotics. They were positioned supine with the head elevated and the operating table tilted to the left. The laparoscopic common bile duct exploration was performed using a three-port approach by biliary surgeons experienced in laparoscopic techniques. A 10 mm trocar is inserted into the abdominal cavity through a subumbilical incision, followed by the insertion of a camera. Under visual guidance, a subxiphoid port (12 mm) and a right midclavicular port (5 mm) are established. The operator carefully separated the adhesions surrounding the gallbladder, thereby exposing Calot’s triangle and the hepatic hilum. Subsequently, the gallbladder artery and cystic duct were separated and ligated using an absorbable clamp. the gallbladder artery is severed, and the gallbladder is carefully dissected in a retrograde manner from the liver bed and the cystic duct is preserved to to facilitate surgery. A longitudinal incision of about 0.6 to 1.5 cm was made in front of the common bile duct above the duodenum. A 5 mm flexible choledochoscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) was inserted for exploration, and stones were removed by using a nitinol basket. Holmium laser lithotripsy was performed for large stones or stones embedded in the distal CBD. After lithotomy, choledochoscopy was repeated to examine the intrahepatic and extrahepatic bile ducts. If the patient meets the criteria for primary duct closure, for those with a CBD diameter > 1 cm, the incision is closed with interrupted sutures using 4 − 0 Vicryl; for patients with a CBD diameter between 0.8 cm and 1 cm, 5 − 0 Vicryl sutures are used for interrupted closure. In the T-tube group, a T-tube of appropriate diameter (14–22 F) was inserted into the abdominal cavity through a subxiphoid port, with its T-shaped head positioned in the common bile duct, secured with interrupted suture using 4 − 0 Vicryl. The operator injected saline into the biliary tract via a T-tube to assess the tightness of the suture. T-tube cholangiography was conducted 14 days post-surgery. If no residual stones were identified during angiography, the T-tube was removed 8 weeks post-operation. Conversely, if residual stones are identified, the operator would perform choledochoscopic lithotomy through the T-tube sinus. Finally, the operator transected the cystic duct and completely removed the gallbladder. For patients with T-tube drainage, a 0.5 cm port is created approximately 10 cm below the intersection of the right axillary midline and the right rib for the placement of the negative pressure drainage tube. Additionally, the T-tube is inserted through the right midclavicular port. In patients with PDC, a negative pressure drainage tube is subsequently placed into the Winslow hole via the right midclavicular port.

Definitions

According to the definition and classification provided by the International Liver Surgery Study Group [16], biliary leakage is characterized by a bilirubin concentration in the drain fluid that is at least three times the upper limit of the normal serum bilirubin concentration on or after postoperative day 3, or as necessitated by endoscopic, radiological, or operative interventions due to bile collections or biliary peritonitis. The classification of bile leakage includes: Grade A bile leakage does not alter the patient’s clinical management and does not necessitate prolonged drainage. Grade B bile leakage necessitates additional percutaneous abdominal drainage and/or endoscopic nasobiliary drainage (ENBD), with or without stent placement. Grade C bile leakage requires reoperation. Morbidity and mortality are defined as the occurrence of complications or deaths within 30 days after surgery. Postoperative complications are classified using the Clavien-Dindo criteria [17], with a Clavien-Dindo ≥IIIa indicating severe complications. Recurrent stones are defined as the rediscovery of calculi at or after 6 months of surgery.

Postoperative treatment and follow-up

All patients received standard nursing care and antibiotic therapy. All patients’ drainage tubes should remain in place for a minimum of three days. If the daily drainage volume is less than 30 ml and there are no signs of bile leakage, the abdominal drainage tubes may be removed. Follow-up examinations were conducted at 1 month, 6 months, and 12 months after the patient’s discharge. The follow-up data included liver function tests and upper abdominal ultrasound. If necessary, magnetic resonance imaging would also be performed.

Statistics

Statistical data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS, version 27.0, Chicago, IL). We performed propensity score matching (PSM) to mitigate the effects of selection bias and potential confounding factors on the measured outcomes. PSM was conducted at a 1:1 ratio, employing a caliper width of 0.2. The following patient characteristics were included in the model: age, gender, body mass index (BMI), all laboratory data, acute biliary pancreatitis, jaundice, the use of laser lithotripsy, the multiple of CBD stones, diameter of CBD, maximum stone diameter, acute cholangitis, and all comorbidities.

The Student’s t-test was applied to continuous data, with values presented as the mean ± standard deviation. The Mann-Whitney U test was employed to compare continuous variables with skewed distributions, which are reported as median and interquartile range (IQR). The χ² test or Fisher’s exact test was utilized for categorical data. A p-value of < 0.05 was deemed significant.The analysis of results was conducted in accordance with the intention-to-treat principle.

Results

This study enrolled a total of 284 elderly patients. Table 1 presents the demographic data and clinical characteristics of these patients. The PDC group comprised 86 patients, whereas the T-tube group included 198 patients. No significant differences were observed between the two groups regarding gender (P = 0.412), age (P = 0.337), BMI (P = 0.149), ASA grade (P = 0.943), acute cholangitis grade (P = 0.113), incidence of biliary pancreatitis (P = 0.523), jaundice (P = 0.11), or gallbladder pathology type (P = 0.332). In terms of laboratory biochemical data, no statistically significant differences were observed in other indicators, except for albumin. The two groups were comparable concerning the rate of multiple CBD stones (P = 0.851), the CBD diameter (P = 0.174), and the maximum stone diameter (P = 0.145), with no statistically significant differences found. Except for a higher incidence of cerebrovascular disease in the PDC group (P = 0.035), the incidence rates of other comorbidities were similar between the two groups. After PSM, there were 67 patients in both the T-tube group and the PDC group.

The intraoperative and postoperative outcomes of the patients are presented in Table 2. None of the patients required conversion to open surgery. Two patients in each group exhibited negative results for common bile duct exploration (2.33% vs. 1.01%, P = 0.587). There were no statistically differences between the two groups regarding the holmium laser usage rate, estimated intraoperative blood loss, abdominal drainage volume on the first postoperative day, and abdominal drainage volume on the second postoperative day. The PDC group demonstrated advantages in terms of operation time (105 (85–130) min vs. 130 (105–160) min, P < 0.001), postoperative hospital stay (7 (6–9) days vs. 8 (7–10) days, P < 0.001), total hospital stay (11.5 (8–14) days vs. 13 (11–17) days, P < 0.001), and total hospitalization costs ($3273.6 ($2868.4-$3840.4) vs. $3848.4 ($3311.4-$4430.9), P < 0.001). However, compared with the T-tube group, the PDC group had a longer duration of abdominal drainage (5 (4–6) days vs. 4 (3–5) days, P < 0.01). After PSM, there were no differences between the two groups in terms of postoperative hospital stay (P = 0.074) and total hospital stay (P = 0.191).

The complications and management outcomes are summarized in Tables 2 and 3. In each group, four patients experienced postoperative bile leakage (4.65% vs. 2.02%, P = 0.25). In the PDC group, there were three cases of grade A bile leakage and one case of grade B bile leakage; in the T-tube drainage group, there were three cases of grade A bile leakage and one case of grade C bile leakage. In the T-tube group, two patients were transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU) due to inadequate postoperative spontaneous respiratory recovery. Furthermore, in the PDC group, one patient experienced respiratory failure resulting from a pulmonary infection and was subsequently admitted to the ICU for ventilator-assisted breathing. In the T-tube group, two patients were diagnosed with residual stone, which were successfully removed using a choledochoscope through the T-tube sinus. One case developed pulmonary thromboembolism and received low-molecular-weight anticoagulation therapy and interventional thrombolysis. In terms of postoperative pneumonia, there were 4 patients in the PDC group and 11 patients in the T-tube group (4.65% vs. 5.56%, P = 1). The median follow-up time for all patients was 6 months (IQR, 6–12 months). There were four cases of recurrent choledocholithiasis, and one patient experienced biliary stricture. The incidence of severe postoperative complications was comparable between the two groups (3.49% vs. 3.54%, P = 1).

Discussion

For patients with cholecystocholedocholithiasis, laparoscopic common bile duct exploration presents a relatively optimal treatment option, particularly in regions such as ours where medical resources are limited. Traditionally, T-tube drainage is utilized to decompress the common bile duct following LCBDE. This method is believed to alleviate sphincter spasm or edema after bile duct exploration and lithotomy [12], and provide percutaneous sinus channels for cholangiography. However, T-tube placement may lead to various complications, including fluid and electrolyte disturbances, biliary leakage post T-tube removal [11, 13]. Moreover, the presence of T-tubes can significantly inconvenience patients in their daily lives, impose economic and psychological burdens. With advancements in surgical techniques and increased experience in laparoscopic surgery, an increasing number of surgeons are opting for primary duct closure following laparoscopic common bile duct exploration, recommending it as a feasible alternative. Compared to T-tube drainage group, PDC group presents several advantages, including reduced operation time, shorter hospital stays, and lower costs, while maintaining a comparable incidence of long-term complications between the two approaches [11–13, 18]. Elderly people frequently exhibit higher rates of comorbidities and diminished organ reserve, which leads to a reduced tolerance for surgical trauma and general anesthesia. Consequently, this demographic often experiences prolonged postoperative recovery times and an increased incidence of postoperative complications [19]. Consequently, the management of cholecystocholedocholithiasis in this demographic presents considerable challenges. However, the results of this study confirm the safety and efficacy of LCBDE + PDC in elderly patients.

In our study, two elderly patients in the T-tube drainage group experienced residual choledocholithiasis. The overall clearance rate for choledocholithiasis in this study was 99.3% (2 /284). To prevent residual choledocholithiasis, the operator must thoroughly review the patient’s imaging examinations before the procedure to ascertain the location, size, and number of stones. During the operation, it is essential to repeatedly verify the clear of stones in both the intrahepatic and extrahepatic bile ducts, particularly following holmium laser lithotripsy. The surgeon may gently maneuver the nitinol basket into the duodenal lumen through the Oddi sphincter to extract residual stones embedded in the duodenal papilla. Compared to the PDC group, all patients in the T tube group underwent postoperative cholangiography, which facilitated the detection of residual stones. Consequently, this introduced a degree of confounding bias that may have impacted the authenticity of the results.

Bile leakage significantly impacts postoperative recovery in elderly patients. In our study, biliary leakage was observed in 2.82% (8/284) of patients (2.99% after PSM), which is consistent with the reported incidence rates in the literature, ranging from 2.2% to 3.8% [18, 20, 21]. The incidence of bile leakage in the PDC group and the T-tube group was 4.65% and 2.02% (P = 0.25). This indicates that T-tube placement does not significantly reduce biliary leakage compared to primary duct closure. Grade A bile leakage was observed in six patients. Among these, five cases were managed conservatively, while one case required prolonged antibiotic infusion. Grade B bile leakage was reported in one case within the PDC group, which was resolved through endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography and endoscopic nasobiliary drainage (ENBD). In the T-tube group, one patient experienced biliary peritonitis due to accidental T-tube withdrawal post-surgery but recovered successfully following laparoscopic T-tube drainage under general anesthesia and intravenous antibiotic treatment. In our center, primary duct closure is also performed for patients with a common bile duct diameter ranging from 0.8 to 1 cm. To prevent biliary leakage and postoperative biliary stricture, Typically, 5 − 0 absorbable sutures are employed for fine suturing to minimize overall damage to the bile duct. Furthermore, utilizing fewer suture edges can further decrease the risk of postoperative biliary duct stricture. After the insertion of a T-tube into the common bile duct, it is essential to inject water under pressure through the T-tube to evaluate the integrity of the sutures. For novice surgeons, the continuous practice of suturing techniques on an laparoscopic simulator is crucial for shortening the learning curve. To minimize the risk of bile leakage, the operator may apply human fibrin sealant [22] to cover the CBD suture site and suture the serous membranes on the common bile duct wall [23], thereby enhancing the integrity of the suture site. In conclusion, meticulous patient screening and optimized suture techniques are crucial for reducing the risk of bile leakage after primary duct closure .

It seems that it is difficult to avoid T-tube-related complications. In our study, the incidence of T-tube-related complications was recorded at 10.61% (13.43%after PSM). Among these, 15 cases (7.58%) experienced pain at the drainage site, some of these individuals required oral non-steroidal painkillers. Additionally, there were 3 cases of retrograde biliary infection (1.52%). Our center usually performs T-tube angiography 14 days after surgery and then clamp the T-tube. The blind end of the T-tube may harbor bacteria and potentially lead to the formation of sludge, which can result in retrograde biliary tract infection. One patient developed severe biliary peritonitis due to accidental T-tube dislodged (0.5%). In our practice, if there is no stone residual, the T-tube is usually removed 8 weeks after surgery. However, 2 patients (1.01%) developed mild biliary peritonitis after T-tube removal, mainly due to incomplete or interrupted T-tube sinus formation, which was successfully treated with antibiotic infusion and fasting. Especially for elderly patients, the time of T-tube placement can be prolonged to reduce the risk of such complications. In our study, the placement of T-tube was associated with a longer hospital stay, extended surgical time, and increased hospitalization costs compared to primary duct suture. These findings corroborate previous research results [11]. The decision to place a T-tube or to perform a primary duct suture following laparoscopic common bile duct exploration should be regarded as complementary rather than antagonistic. We contend that T-tube drainage remains appropriate for patients with difficult stones (stones > 15 mm, > 3 or impacted) [24], as well as for those with Mirizzi syndrome, a high risk of residual stones, biliary stricture, altered anatomy, or inflammatory edema and stricture at the sphincter of Oddi. LCBDE can be performed via either transcystic or transductal. Laparoscopic transcystic common bile duct exploration (LTCBDE) entails the removal of choledocholithiasis through the cystic duct, This approach minimizes bile leakage and reduces hospital stay; however, it is associated with a lower stone clearance rate [25]. Transcystic duct access may be constrained by various factors such as the location, size, and number of stones, the diameter of the cystic duct, and the surgeon’s experience. At the same time, laparoscopic transcystic common bile duct exploration typically necessitates specialized equipment, including a 3 mm choledochoscope, a biliary balloon dilatation catheter, and holmium laser [26]. In our region, the tense relationship between doctors and patients has led surgeons to exercise caution regarding residual stones. Consequently, they routinely perform laparoscopic transductal common bile duct exploration to optimize the stone removal rate.

The study by Yi et al. [18] demonstrated that patients who underwent primary duct closure exhibited a low recurrence rate of stones ( 4.4%) and no instances of biliary stricture. For surgeons, preventing biliary strictures requires careful patient selection. One of the key selection criteria is to ensure that the common bile duct (CBD) diameter is ≥8 mm in patients undergoing primary duct closure. Furthermore, it is crucial to avoid excessive suture tension or overly wide suture margins, which may result in ischemic bile duct walls. Additionally, minimizing the use of electrocautery for hemostasis during bile duct wall bleeding is essential. In our study, all patients were scheduled to undergo a one-year follow-up. Recurrent choledocholithiasis occurred in one case in the PDC group and three cases in the T-tube group, with no significant difference observed (1.16% vs. 1.52%, P = 1), all of which were successfully treated by ERCP. In the T-tube group, one patient developed a postoperative biliary stricture, which was associated with impacted choledocholithiasis. During the procedure, holmium laser lithotripsy was performed. The postoperative biliary stricture may be attributed to damage caused by the holmium laser to the inner wall of the bile duct. Subsequently, endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) with biliary stent placement was conducted. In our study, patients with mild to moderate acute cholangitis in both the PDC group and the T-tube group were comparable. The research conducted by Wang et al. [11] indicated that LCBDE + PDC is a safe and effective approach for managing choledocholithiasis with mild or moderate cholangitis.

In our study, the PDC group demonstrated a prolonged duration of abdominal drainage tube, a finding that contrasts with previous research [27]. Surgeons often exhibit reluctance to remove abdominal drainage tubes when minimal bile is present, even if this condition does not meet the criteria for bile leakage. This hesitation primarily stems from concerns about the potential for bile leakage, leading to a more conservative approach to drain removal and, consequently, an extended duration of abdominal drainage tube placement.

However, several limitations of our study warrant consideration. First, as a retrospective analysis, it may be susceptible to selection bias. Second, the follow-up period was inadequate, potentially affecting the reliability of long-term results. Therefore, well-designed, multicenter randomized controlled trials with larger sample sizes are essential to validate these findings.

Conclusion

In conclusion, LCBDE with PDC represents a safe, effective, and feasible treatment option for elderly patients with cholecystocholedocholithiasis. When compared to the T-tube group, the PDC group demonstrates superior clinical outcomes, particularly in terms of operation time and overall hospital costs. Furthermore, the incidence of postoperative complications, including severe complications (Dindo-Clavien ≥ IIIa), was found to be comparable between the two groups. For elderly patients with cholecystocholedocholithiasis who fulfill the criteria for primary duct suture, LCBDE with PDC presents a preferred option.
