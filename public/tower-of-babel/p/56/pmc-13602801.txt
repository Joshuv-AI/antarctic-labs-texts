Perceptions of the use of smartwatches for the early detection of cardiac arrhythmias in Saudi Arabia: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Rabab A AlKhalaf, Abdullah M AlKhudair, Walaa H AlMousa, Fatemah S AlHadab, Fatimah Y Alshareet, Norah H AlSultan, Eman Elsheikh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSmartwatches and other wearable technologies have become increasingly popular for health monitoring, offering potential benefits in the early detection of cardiac arrhythmias such as atrial fibrillation (AF). Despite their expanding use, limited data exist regarding public perceptions and usage patterns of these devices in Saudi Arabia. Understanding these perceptions is essential to inform strategies for digital health integration and preventive cardiovascular care.ObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate public perceptions, usage patterns, and factors influencing acceptance of smartwatches for early detection of cardiac arrhythmias among adults in Saudi Arabia.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted between May and October 2023 across five major regions of Saudi Arabia. A total of 459 adults aged ≥ 22 years participated through structured face-to-face interviews using a validated questionnaire adapted from prior studies. Descriptive statistics summarized demographic characteristics and usage trends, while Poisson regression analysis identified determinants of positive perceptions toward wearable technologies.ResultsNearly half of the respondents (46.4%) reported using wearable devices for health monitoring, with smartwatches being the most preferred (42.0%). Among smartwatch users, (35.2%) had received notifications of cardiac arrhythmia through their devices. Most participants (76.5%) expressed willingness to use smartwatches for arrhythmia detection, and (48.1%) strongly agreed that wearable technologies could contribute to the early diagnosis and monitoring of arrhythmia. Younger adults (22–51 years) demonstrated significantly more positive perceptions compared to those > 51 years (p < 0.05). Previous experience with wearables was also a significant predictor of favorable perception (p = 0.015). Educational level showed a positive but non-significant trend (p = 0.061), while gender and cardiovascular history were not associated with perception.ConclusionThe Saudi public shows a high level of awareness and positive perception toward using smartwatches for early cardiac arrhythmia detection. Younger age and prior experience with wearables were the strongest predictors of acceptance, underscoring the importance of digital literacy and user familiarity. Integrating smartwatches into preventive cardiology and public health initiatives—supported by education and clinical validation—may enhance early arrhythmia detection and promote proactive heart health management in Saudi Arabia.Keywords: Smartwatch, Arrhythmias, Public, Heart, Perceptions

Introduction

Wearable fitness trackers have gained significant interest in medicine and have revolutionized healthcare methods provided to patients. These devices track steps and mileage and monitor electrocardiograms (ECGs), heart rates, stress levels, oxygen levels, and sleep patterns. As a result, they have become essential for enhancing the early detection of any changes in a patient’s health [1–4]. The integration of wearable fitness trackers in healthcare has opened new possibilities for remote patient monitoring and personalized care [1]. These devices provide real-time data that can be used to identify potential health issues before they become serious. This has subsequently led to improved patient outcomes and reduced healthcare costs. One example of wearable fitness trackers is smartwatches, which have emerged as a popular alternative to traditional medical ECGs [4, 5]. In particular, interest in using smartwatches to detect cardiac arrhythmia through ECG monitoring applications is increasing [6]. ECG applications utilize a photoplethysmography (PPG) sensor that can calculate the pulse rate from the wrist, allowing patients to identify any abnormalities in the conductance pathway that may cause arrhythmia [5, 7].

Cardiac arrhythmia is characterized by unbalanced electrical activity leading to an accelerated, decelerated, or irregular heartbeat [6]. This global issue significantly contributes to morbidity, mortality, and financial burdens in developed and developing nations [8, 9], with prevalence rates ranging from (1.5%) to (5%) worldwide [10]. Atrial fibrillation (AF) affects an estimated 34 million individuals globally [9]. However, approximately one-third of patients with paroxysmal AF may not exhibit any symptoms, making early detection of these arrhythmias difficult. However, not all individuals are convinced about early cardiac arrhythmia detection through smartwatches, as they still harbor concerns or negative perceptions regarding using them for this purpose [10]. Ultimately, the ability of smartwatches to detect cardiac arrhythmia depends on various factors, including personal experience, scientific evidence, and cultural attitudes toward technology [3, 11]. Additionally, the risk of false positives or negatives can lead to unnecessary anxiety or even a misdiagnosis [12].

The diagnostic accuracy of smartwatches for identifying cardiac arrhythmias was evaluated through a systematic review and meta-analysis by Nazarian and his colleagues (2021). Adhering to PRISMA guidelines, the researchers examined 18 studies, which included 424,371 participants for their investigation. The research used smartwatch PPG signals to investigate the detection capacity of atrial fibrillation along with bradyarrhythmias, tachyarrhythmias, and premature contractions. The assessment demonstrated outstanding diagnostic capabilities through sensitivity rates of (100%), specificity of (95%), and an accuracy rate of (97%). The predictive values from pooling data showed a total of (85%) sensitivity with a perfect (100%) specificity. These results demonstrate how smartwatches can reshape the way we manage heart health. The exceptional diagnostic precision of smartwatch sensors validates their status as crucial medical instruments, helping physicians detect arrhythmia early while saving healthcare expenses and enhancing treatment success. Additional research and proper regulatory procedures are essential to properly facilitate these devices’ clinical smartwatches to demonstrate increasing significance for advancing pathogen recognition and individualized medical service delivery as digital medical instruments advance [10].

Additionally, the research review by Kozieł et al. evaluated smartwatch detection accuracy for cardiac arrhythmias using PubMed database studies between 2015 and 2022. The research data shows that smartwatches succeed in diagnosing AF at superior levels. Research-based on 5,050 participants spanning 16 unique studies yielded a (100%) sensitivity rate in detecting arrhythmias. According to these studies, at least (25%) of participants were correctly diagnosed with the condition, but the overall certainty of their assessments found between (68%) to (100%) of cases to be accurate. The research team did not include specific data points because of inadequate PPG signal quality. Data collected from smartwatch PPG sensors demonstrated comparable diagnosis accuracy to single-lead ECG measurements [13].

A systematic review of smartwatch use in arrhythmia detection work was carried out by Bogár et al.. The research search produced 758 results, and we selected 57 articles distributed across 33 case reports and case series coupled with 24 cohort studies. Atrial fibrillation detection using Apple Watches emerged as the main focus in most studies, but investigations also included alternative arrhythmias, which could lead to circulatory collapse. Research data indicates underground systematic growth of smartwatches as medical tools in arrhythmia detection and patient care. Medical devices integrated into existing clinical protocols would allow early medical intervention while improving treatment effectiveness, monitoring, and care timeliness. Testing has shown that enhanced cardiac patient outcomes can result from depending on smartwatches for cardiac care [14].

Cardiac arrhythmias, especially AF, rank as significant causes of mortality and morbidity worldwide. Healthcare advancements and widespread smartphone usage in Saudi Arabia enable researchers to investigate firsthand how residents view and practice health monitoring through smartwatches. However, insights into Saudi population knowledge levels and their reception and adoption challenges regarding these technologies remain limited. Strategies to improve cardiovascular health outcomes through integrated healthcare frameworks require knowledge of public perceptions and factors influencing smartwatch adoption [15].

Consequently, this study explores Saudi Arabian public perceptions of smartwatch utilization for predicting arrhythmias. Research findings about awareness and usage patterns while identifying potential barriers will deliver essential insights to healthcare policymakers, clinicians, and technology developers. The collected data will help establish focused public awareness programs while increasing confidence in wearable health devices, making their incorporation into preventive healthcare programs possible to decrease cardiovascular diseases in Saudi Arabia.

Aim of work

The aim is to investigate public perceptions regarding the use of smartwatches for early detection of cardiac arrhythmia and assess the current utilization among adults in five key regions of Saudi Arabia.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional study was conducted from May to October 2023 in five major regions of Saudi Arabia. The research explored the usage of wearable technologies and public perceptions regarding smartwatches for early cardiac arrhythmia detection, addressing the integration of wearable technology into public health strategies.

A total of 585 responses were gathered, of which 459 participants met the inclusion criteria.

Additionally, to avoid biases in the sample and ensure diversity, data collection occurred at various locations, including universities, hospitals, shopping malls, gyms, and streets. This approach helps to capture responses from different demographics and environments within Saudi Arabia.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was determined using the Raosoft calculator based on a (95%) confidence level, a (5%) margin of error, and a target population size of 1,338,585. The minimum required sample size was 385 participants. To enhance reliability and representation, 585 responses were collected, with 459 included in the final analysis.

Questionnaire

Data were collected through structured face-to-face interviews using a modified version of a validated questionnaire from two prior studies [11, 12]. Initially in English and Turkish, the questionnaire was professionally translated into Arabic and underwent a back-translation process by certified translators to ensure linguistic accuracy—a pilot test involving 30 participants assessed reliability and validity. Adjustments were made based on participant feedback. The Cronbach’s alpha value confirmed internal consistency.

The questionnaire included three main sections:

Demographics: Collected data on age, gender, place of residence, nationality, marital status, occupation, and educational level.

The instrument ascertained smartwatch ownership/use and experience with irregular-heartbeat notifications. It did not include an item on active use of the on-demand ECG feature (e.g., recording a single-lead ECG), and therefore ECG-app engagement cannot be quantified from the present data.

Data collection and management

Trained researchers conducted the interviews, which lasted approximately three minutes per participant, using a standardized script to ensure consistency and minimize interviewer bias. Data were anonymized and stored securely in Google Forms, and participation was voluntary. Measures were taken to protect participants’ confidentiality.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS), version 28 on Windows 10. Descriptive statistics summarized demographic data and wearable technology usage. Inferential statistics, such as chi-square tests and logistic regression, analyzed associations between demographic factors and perceptions. Missing data were handled using pairwise deletion to maximize the use of available data. A P value of < 0.050 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Ethical approval

This project was assessed and certified by King Faisal University’s ethics committee (KFU—REC-2023-MAY-ETHICS907). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Results

Table 1 presents the demographic characteristics of the study participants. A total of 459 respondents were included, with a predominance of females (62.7%) compared to males (37.3%). Most participants were young adults aged 22–31 years (59.5%), while only (7.4%) were older than 51 years, indicating that the study sample was largely composed of younger individuals. Most respondents were Saudi nationals (95.0%), reflecting the study’s regional focus, and representation was fairly distributed across the five major regions of the Kingdom, with the eastern region contributing the largest proportion (24.8%). Nearly half of the participants were single (48.4%), while (45.3%) were married. Educational attainment was generally high, as (80.2%) of respondents held a university degree or higher, suggesting good digital literacy and potential familiarity with technology. More than half of the sample (56.0%) were employed, (21.0%) were unemployed, and (20.0%) were students, indicating a diverse occupational background. Overall, the demographic distribution shows that the study cohort consisted predominantly of young, educated Saudi adults with balanced regional representation, which is appropriate for exploring perceptions of emerging health technologies such as smartwatches.

Table 2 summarizes participants’ patterns of use and interest in wearable technologies for health monitoring. Nearly half of the respondents (46.4%) reported having used wearable devices for health-related purposes, while a slightly higher proportion (53.6%) had never used such technologies. Among those familiar with wearables, smartwatches were by far the most commonly used or preferred device (42.0%), followed by smartphones (11.3%) and smart wristbands (8.1%). Figure 2 illustrates the distribution of respondents’ preferences and usage of different wearable technologies for health monitoring. Smartwatches were identified as the most commonly used or preferred device, accounting for (42.0%) of participants, highlighting their dominant role in personal health tracking. In contrast, more than one-quarter of respondents (26.8%) reported not using any device or application, while (11.8%) indicated plans to start using one in the future, reflecting a growing potential for adoption. Variety of wearable technology among respondents

Table 3 presents participants’ responses regarding the use of wearable technologies for monitoring heart diseases. The vast majority of respondents (83.9%) expressed a strong desire to regularly record and monitor their health-related indicators, reflecting a high level of health awareness and interest in proactive self-care. Only a small proportion of participants (10.0%) reported having cardiovascular-related health issues, yet more than three-quarters (76.5%) expressed willingness to use smartwatches specifically for monitoring and detecting arrhythmia, indicating broad acceptance of such technology even among those without known heart conditions. Among current smartwatch users, (35.2%) reported receiving irregular-heartbeat notifications. Because such alerts are primarily generated by passive PPG screening, this percentage should not be interpreted as active use of the device’s ECG recording feature; our questionnaire did not capture ECG-app use. However, the majority (64.8%) had not received any alerts, which may reflect the low prevalence of arrhythmia in the general population or variations in device sensitivity. Overall, these findings underscore a growing public readiness in Saudi Arabia to integrate wearable technologies into cardiac health monitoring, positioning smartwatches as valuable tools for early detection and prevention initiatives.

Previous experience with wearable technologies also emerged as a significant factor (RR = 1.05, p = 0.015), suggesting that hands-on exposure to such devices enhances user confidence and reinforces positive attitudes toward their usefulness in health monitoring. In contrast, gender (p = 0.349) and presence of cardiovascular-related health issues (p = 0.233) were not significantly associated with perception, implying that attitudes toward wearable technologies are generally consistent across sexes and health status. The effect of educational level showed a positive trend (RR = 1.03, p = 0.061) but did not reach statistical significance, indicating that while education may influence awareness and understanding of wearables, its impact may be attenuated in a population with high overall educational attainment.

Overall, the regression results suggest that younger age and prior use of wearable technologies are the strongest determinants of positive perception toward smartwatches and similar devices in the Saudi population. These findings highlight the importance of promoting early exposure and digital literacy initiatives, particularly targeting older adults, to foster broader acceptance and integration of wearable health technologies in preventive cardiology and population health management.

Discussion

This cross-sectional study of adults across five regions of Saudi Arabia shows substantial penetration and generally favorable perceptions of wearable technologies for cardiovascular monitoring. Nearly half reported prior use of wearables (46.4%), smartwatches were the most common device (42.0%), and three quarters expressed willingness to use smartwatches for detecting arrhythmia (76.5%). In Poisson regression, younger age groups had significantly higher relative risk of positive perceptions than adults > 51 years, and previous wearable use was also associated with more favorable views; gender and the presence of cardiovascular-related health issues were not significant determinants, while higher education trended positive without reaching conventional significance. Taken together, these findings suggest that prior experience and generational differences—rather than clinical need or sex—are the primary drivers of positive perceptions in this setting, a pattern aligned with recent adoption studies and guideline commentary on growing encounters with consumer wearables in clinical practice [16, 17].

International data continue to show enthusiasm for wearables coupled with age-related gradients. Observational and intervention studies indicate that younger adults adopt and sustain use at higher rates, while older adults weigh usability and simplicity more heavily and benefit from structured onboarding (e.g., larger displays, coaching, and clinician reinforcement) [18–20]. Our age gradient parallels findings from the Pulse watch program and related trials in older/high-risk cohorts demonstrating that smartwatch-based AF screening is feasible, but adherence improves with tailored interfaces and training [21–23]. The strong association we observed between prior use and positive perception mirrors technology-acceptance pathways in which direct experience increases perceived ease of use and usefulness, reinforcing intention to continue use [24]. Conversely, the non-significant role of gender is consistent with surveys showing that once access and exposure are accounted for, sex differences in overall acceptance often diminish [24].

Perception that smartwatches can assist with early arrhythmia detection was widespread and consonant with an expanding evidence base on diagnostic performance. Recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses report high accuracy for AF detection using smartwatch PPG and single-lead ECG, although estimates vary by algorithm, signal quality, and adjudication methods [18–20]. Importantly, population-level performance differs by feature: irregular rhythm notification (background PPG screening) can show high specificity but modest sensitivity in pragmatic settings, while on-demand ECG features yield higher sensitivity/specificity for AF classification—highlighting the need for confirmatory testing and clinical pathways [19, 20]. Our finding that (35.2%) of smartwatch users reported an irregular-heartbeat notification underscores real-world engagement but should not be equated with confirmed AF prevalence, as positive predictive value is driven by pretest probability and the confirmation process [18–21]. These nuances are emphasized in recent ACC/AHA/HRS guidance and expert analyses encouraging clinicians to develop workflows that triage consumer-device alerts to definitive ECG confirmation and appropriate follow-up to avoid unnecessary anxiety or downstream testing [16, 17]. Our survey distinguished ownership/use and alert experience but did not measure active ECG-app engagement. As irregular rhythm notifications largely reflect passive PPG screening rather than user-initiated ECGs, future surveys should include item-level questions on ECG recording frequency and contexts of use, ideally paired with objective device logs and clinical adjudication.

The education gradient in our data trended toward significance (p = 0.061) but did not cross the threshold, a nuance that aligns with mixed literature. While higher education can facilitate adoption through greater health/digital literacy, the consumerization of wearables and Arabic-language/localized interfaces may attenuate education effects in digitally saturated environments. Given that (~ 80%) of our sample reported university education or higher, a ceiling effect and reduced variability are plausible, and broader educational spread may reveal clearer gradients in future work. Our null finding for cardiovascular-related health issues suggest perceived utility is driven more by general health engagement than current disease burden, echoing studies where prevention-oriented users adopt wearables even without established conditions [24].

Regionally, our results complement emerging Saudi evidence on digital-health uptake and attitudes toward AI-enabled wearables. Recent Saudi studies report predominantly positive perceptions of wearables’ role in proactive care and teleconsultation, tempered by concerns about data accuracy, technical failures, and reduced human interaction—barriers that mirror our respondents’ emphasis on trust and actionable integration with care [25]. Broader Saudi digital-health landscape reports show rapid growth in wearable adoption and virtual-care use, especially among younger adults, suggesting favorable conditions for integrating validated arrhythmia-detection pathways into routine care [26]. Together with local adoption research on smartwatches, these trends highlight the importance of culturally attuned messaging, privacy assurances, and clinician-mediated pathways to build trust and sustain engagement [27].

From an implementation standpoint, our age-stratified findings support international recommendations to tailor onboarding, device selection, and follow-up. Studies in older adults emphasize simplified interfaces, reminder systems, and periodic human support, which improve confidence and sustained use [21–23]. Clinician-mediated introduction and periodic review of uploaded tracings align with guideline advice to “close the loop” between consumer alerts and clinical confirmation, ensuring benefits while limiting false-alarm burden [16–18]. Given the high proportion of respondents who wish to track indicators regularly (83.9%), there is an opportunity to embed wearables into preventive cardiology programs with attention to equitable access and reimbursement models for review time—a theme gaining traction in recent policy and implementation analyses [16, 17].

Finally, interpretation should recognize known sensing and algorithmic limitations: motion artifacts, skin-perfusion variability, and threshold settings can yield both false positives and negatives; performance depends on feature type (background PPG vs. on-demand ECG), device generation, and user adherence [18–20]. Contemporary guidance recommends confirmatory diagnostics and clinician oversight, which dovetails with our proposed pathways for Saudi settings [16–18]. Future Saudi and international research should pair perception surveys with objective device logs and clinical adjudication, and test whether smartwatch-enabled community screening improves time-to-diagnosis and outcomes in pragmatic, equity-focused designs [18–21].

Limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and does not capture temporal changes in perceptions or use patterns. Second, the sample was obtained by convenience sampling across public venues, which introduces selection bias and limits generalizability beyond adults who were available and willing to participate; non-response bias cannot be excluded. The age distribution was skewed toward younger adults and the educational profile toward university graduates, raising the possibility of ceiling effects for education and underrepresentation of older adults (> 51 years), who are a clinically important target for arrhythmia screening. Third, all variables were self-reported, which may be subject to recall and social desirability bias and could lead to differential or non-differential misclassification. We handled missing data using pairwise deletion; if data were not missing completely at random, this approach may have introduced additional bias.

Measurement constraints also warrant caution. Although we ascertained smartwatch ownership/use and receipt of irregular-heartbeat notifications, we did not directly measure active use of the on-demand ECG feature (e.g., single-lead ECG acquisition). Because irregular-rhythm notifications typically arise from passive photoplethysmography (PPG) screening, our data cannot distinguish passive alerts from user-initiated ECG recordings, nor can they estimate adherence to ECG functionality. In addition, devices were analyzed as a broad category; we did not stratify by brand/model, firmware, sensor suite, or algorithm generation, and we did not verify device outputs against clinical standards. Consequently, we could not account for heterogeneity in accuracy or functionality across devices, nor could we adjudicate reported notifications with confirmatory ECGs. Finally, while several associations reached statistical significance, other effects (e.g., education) trended toward significance and may have been underpowered given subgroup sizes.

These limitations underscore the need for longitudinal and/or pragmatic designs that pair surveys with objective device logs and clinically adjudicated outcomes; sampling strategies that ensure adequate representation of older adults and lower-education groups; and device-level analyses that account for model-specific features and software versions.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates a high level of awareness and favorable perception toward the use of smartwatches for early detection of cardiac arrhythmias among adults in Saudi Arabia. Nearly half of participants reported using wearable devices for health monitoring, with smartwatches emerging as the preferred choice. Younger age and prior experience with wearables were significant predictors of positive perception, highlighting the role of familiarity and generational differences in technology acceptance. Although education showed a positive trend, it did not reach statistical significance, suggesting widespread accessibility of wearable technologies across educational levels. These findings underscore the growing public readiness to integrate smartwatches into preventive cardiovascular care. Future initiatives should focus on enhancing accessibility, user education, and clinical integration—particularly for older adults—to fully leverage the potential of wearable technologies in improving early arrhythmia detection and overall heart health in Saudi Arabia.
