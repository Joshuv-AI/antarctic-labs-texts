A traditional Cameroonian spice, Syzygium guineense curtails leukemia tumor growth through G2-M cell cycle arrest and predicted NLRP3 inflammasome pathway
Authors: Francine Tankeu Nzufo, Constant Anatole Pieme, Rajesh Kumar Meher, Jean-Bosco Jouda, Ninon Geornest Eudes Ronauld Etsassala, Trupti Pradhan, K. Nirmal Kumar, Subrata Sen, Madan Barkume, Satyanshu Kumar, Showkat Ahmad Mir, Jyoti Kode
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundS. guineense DC. var. macrocarpum (Engl.) F. White. plant has been a traditional Cameroonian spice used as medicine to treat several health conditions such as liver cirrhosis, vaginal infections, and cancer.ObjectiveThis study aims to investigate the anti-cancer effects of Syzygium guineense plant parts on leukemia preclinical models.Material and methodsCrude extracts and fractions of S. guineense were tested to inhibit the proliferation of human leukemia cells and reduce tumor volume in leukemia xenograft KG1 in immunodeficient mice. Further active fractions were evaluated for the mechanism of action using cell cycle analysis. Inflammasome markers, key targets of chronic inflammation, were screened from phytoconstituents obtained by LC-MS analysis. Of these, putative compounds having potential anticancer efficacy were assessed for kinetic binding with Molecular docking using PyRx-Python Prescription 0.8, and molecular dynamics simulations were performed by GROMACS 22.04.ResultsBark extract B2 and leaf fractions L4/L5 exhibited significant (P < 0.001) anticancer activity against leukemia cells in a dose-dependent manner. Mechanistically, in L4/L5 arrested HL-60 cells at G2/M phase (population increased to >25 % at drug concentration up to 80 μg/ml compared to 8.8 % in control cells), while B2 caused S phase arrest (population increased to 56.2 % at drug concentration up to 100 μg/ml compared to 45.8 % in control) in HL60 cells. L4/L5 treatment led to apoptosis induction in these cells. B2 (P=0.035) and L4 (P=0.096) significantly reduced the tumor xenograft KG1 in the animal model. HRMS allowed the identification of Betulinic Acid/Oleanolic Acid in L4. Computational prediction and GROMACS simulations of these compounds revealed stable binding patterns between the component and target proteins NLRP3 and NEK-7 over 100 ns.ConclusionOur study has provided interesting leads that Cameroonian spice S. guineense fractions/extract exhibited anti-leukemia activity, which could be mediated by inhibiting the innate immune inflammasome pathway. The study emphasizes the importance of S. guineense plant parts that have the potential to be developed for novel targeted therapeutics for leukemia.

Introduction

Cancer is the second leading cause of death worldwide, with about 10 million deaths recorded globally in 2020 [1]. Leukemia ranks 13th in terms of incidence and represents about 3.1 % of all cancer deaths worldwide [2]. Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a hematologic malignancy characterized by immature myeloid cell proliferation and bone marrow failure [3]. It accounts for about 85 % of acute leukemia cases [4]. In 2020, a total of 474,519 thousand new cases were diagnosed, and 311,594 thousand deaths from leukemia were registered worldwide [5]. Although targeted therapy has made great progress over the last decades, the overall prognosis of AML remains grim, with a 5-year survival rate of less than 40 % for patients younger than 60 years with current forms of treatment. Besides, these chemotherapeutic agents also induce undesirable side effects and increase the risk of developing therapy-related cancers, thus highlighting the urgent need for novel therapeutic strategies.

Recently, inflammasome NLRP3 has gained great interest and appears as one of the main current therapeutic targets in the field of immuno-oncology owing to its crosstalk between innate and acquired immunity. It has been shown to play a pivotal role in the development and onset of several types of cancers, including acute myeloid leukemia [6]. A growing body of evidence shows that activation of inflammasome NLRP3 promotes cell proliferation, chemotherapy resistance, and apoptosis inhibition in acute myeloid cells and primary leukemia cells, which is further correlated with poor prognosis [7]. Inhibitors of NLRP3 are thus potential treatments for AML.S. guineense belongs to the Myrtaceae family. Ethnopharmacological studies demonstrated that S. guineense has been used in African traditional medicine to manage several diseases including cancers [8], [9].

S. guineense belongs to the Myrtaceae family. Ethnopharmacological studies demonstrated that S. guineense has been used in African traditional medicine to manage several diseases including cancers [8], [9].

It has been shown to possess several biological activities, including antioxidant [10], anticancer [11], and antimalarial [12]. Few reports demonstrated that betulinic acid, a phytochemical isolated from S. guineense, also possesses anticancer properties and is currently undergoing clinical trials for the treatment of melanoma[13]. In a previous study, we showed that tannins of S. guineense inhibit Wnt signaling and proliferation of colon cancer cell lines and colon cancer organoids through inactivation of the Wnt pathway [11]. This work aims to assess the antitumor effects of S. guineense in vitro and in vivo models of acute myeloid leukemia.

Material and methods

Chemicals and reagents

The chemicals used in our study were bought from Sigma Chemical Co. (St. Louis, MO, USA), Santa Cruz Biotechnology (Santa Cruz, CA) and Invitrogen (Carlsbad, CA, USA).

Plant harvesting and extract preparation

Leaves and barks of S. guineense (Willd.) S. guineense DC.var. macrocarpum (Engl.) F. White. were collected in the Centre Region of Cameroon in November 2014 by Dr. Ghogue Paul, a botanist on duty at the National Herbarium of Cameroon. Our collected samples were brought back to the National Herbarium of Cameroon. They were identified by comparing them to the elements observed on the samples collected by the botanist Satabié Benoit no. 761; this sample is preserved at the herbarium under collection reference no. 49885/SRF/Cam.

The air-dried leaves and barks were ground and extracted as described previously [10]. The aqueous and water-ethanol extracts were lyophilized while the ethanolic ones were dried in an oven at 50 °C. The resulting crude extracts were labeled as follows L1 (crude ethanol extract from leaves); L9 (crude aqueous extract from leaves); L10 (crude aqueous ethanol extract from leaves); B1 (crude ethanol extract from barks); B2 (crude aqueous extract from barks) and B3 (crude aqueous ethanol extract from barks).

The crude ethanolic extract of leaves (L1, 237.8 g) was subjected to vacuum chromatography over silica gel (200–300 mesh), using hexane, ethyl acetate, and methanol by increasing the polarity of solvent mixtures. All the collected sub-fractions (200 mL) were grouped into four fractions according to thin layer chromatography monitoring and labeled as follows: L4 (12.34 g): Hexane (Hex)/ethyl acetate (EtOAc) 6/4 (v:v); L5 (18.14 g): (Hex/EtOAc 4/6 (v:v); L6 (18.62 g): Fraction (EtOAc: methanol MeOH (7/3); L7 (15.71 g): pure MeOH.

L2 and L3 were also obtained from L1 during vacuum chromatography at (hexane-ethyl acetate (8/2) and were washed and dried. Fraction L4 was re-subjected to column chromatography on silica gel (200–300 mesh) eluted with hexane-ethyl acetate (95/5, v/v) giving a compound called L8. Thus L2, L3, and L8 were compounds.

Preclinical animal models

Non-Obese Diabetic/Severe Combined Immunodeficiency (NOD-SCID) male mice aged six to eight weeks in-house bred, weighing 20g body weight were included in the study. The room temperature was maintained at 23 ± 2 °C, and the relative humidity was between 50 and 60 %. The animals received a standard diet and free access to autoclaved water. They received human care, and all efforts were made to minimize animal suffering before and during experiments.

In vitro cytotoxicity activity

Cytotoxic effect of S. guineense plant extracts on leukemia cell lines

The cytotoxicity of the S. guineense (crude extracts and fractions) against leukemia cell lines HL-60 and K562 was assessed using sulforhodamine B (SRB) assay [14]. In brief, the cell lines (procured from ATCC, USA) were grown in RPMI 1640 medium containing 10 % fetal bovine serum and 2 mM l-glutamine. Growth inhibition (GI50), anti-proliferative (TGI), and cytotoxic (LC50) parameters of crude extracts and fractions were determined.

Effect of crude extracts and fractions of S. guineense on cell cycle

The effect of crude extracts and fractions of S. guineense on cell cycle and ploidy using propidium iodide was done using the previously described method [15]. Samples were acquired on FACS Calibur (BD Biosciences, San Vose, USA) and analyzed by ModFit LT 4.1 (Verity Software House, Maine, USA). The resulting DNA distributions were analyzed for the proportions of cells in the G0/G1, S- phase, and G2/M phases of the cell cycle and apoptosis.

In vivo efficacy evaluation of S. guineense extracts

All procedures were performed in compliance with the standard operating procedures approved by the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee of ACTREC-TMC (Proposal # 01/2015) and adhered to CPCSEA guidelines (Registration Number: 65/GO/ReBiBt/S/99/CPCSEA).

Determination of maximum tolerated dose (MTD)

Non-Obese Diabetic/Severe Combined Immunodeficiency (NOD-SCID) mice, six per group in-house bred, weighing 20g on average were included in the study. Fractions exhibiting significant anti-proliferative activity viz. L4, L5, and B2 were selected for the in vivo study. Details of doses administered and the toxicity criteria considered are provided as supplementary material.

Antitumor activity in vivo

For the in vivo antitumor effect, male NOD-SCID mice aged 6–8 weeks, weighing 18–22g were used. The tumor was induced by transplanting acute myeloid leukemia KG-1 cells to NOD-SCID mice according to the method described previously [14,16].

The groups consisted of Tumor-bearing control (Group A), Adriamycin-treated group as Positive control (Group B), L4 fraction treated groups at two dosing protocols (Groups C and D), L5 fraction treated group (Group E and Group F), and B2 fraction treated group (Group G). Dosing protocols were set as 100mg/kg or 200 mg/kg daily for 5 days a week for 4 weeks. Tumor measurements were carried out to determine tumor growth and tumor volume using a digital Vernier caliper. The drug dosing was initiated after the tumor reached 4–6 mm in size. Mice were observed for 30 days for body weight, tumor volume, and mortality. The data were represented as relative Tumor volume (RTV in cc), T/C (ratio of test versus control), and survival. Details of the calculations of the tumor RTV are provided in the Supplementary material.

Liquid Chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) analysis of fractions L4, L5, and crude extract of bark

Peak identification by high-resolution mass spectroscopy (HRMS)

HPLC-MS conditions were as described in previous works [17,18]. Details to obtain high-resolution mass spectra are provided in the supplementary material.

Statistical analysis

Data were represented as the mean ± standard error (SE). One-way ANOVA (analysis of variance) test was used to compare the efficacy in the treated groups and the untreated control for the in vivo efficacy study, using IBM SPSS Statistics 21 software. The significance was considered at p ≤ 0.05.

In silico assessment of the binding potential of active compounds with innate inflammasome markers

To understand the mechanistic action of Syzygium extracts, we shortlisted two active compounds from the phytochemical profile and processed them for computational analysis. The molecular docking was conducted using the PyRx software. The 3D structure of NEK7 serine/threonine-protein kinase PDB-ID 6S75 existing in the quaternary structure composed of four identical chains was sourced from https://www.rcsb.org/ [19]. Only chain A was kept in the 3D X-ray crystallographic structure and chains B, C, and D were removed for further in-silico investigations. In chain A the missing amino acids near the binding loop were filled using swiss-model https://swissmodel.expasy.org/ [20]. The molecular docking simulations of the phytochemical Betulinic Acid and Oleanolic Acid with NEK7 were adopted from previous studies [21]. The details of the dockings are given in the supplementary material.

Molecular dynamics simulations

To validate the binding stability of potential compounds with NLRP3/NEK7 immune target, molecular dynamics simulations were performed by using GROMACS 22.04 according to the procedure described in earlier works [[22], [23], [24]]. The details of the molecular dynamic simulations are added in the supplementary material.

Maximum tolerated dose and in vivo efficacy study

The MTD study was conducted to determine the maximum dose of compounds that animals can tolerate safely and ascertain a safer use of the plant extract without compromising the survival of animals.

The results showed that B2, and fractions L4 and L5 were well tolerated by mice at 300, 600, and 900 mg/kg bw (Supplementary Tables S3–S6). Indeed, there were no changes in water and food consumption (data not shown), and no behavioral changes in mice during the study period were observed, with no changes in the body weight in all the groups during the study period. Interestingly, the body weight decrease was minor and below 4 %, considered as toxicity criteria according to NCI MTD guidelines. As L4/L5 fractions and B2 extract, dosed above 900 mg/kg as a single dose exhibited less solubility, it was considered that compounds may be well tolerated up to 2000 mg/kg as per standard NCI criteria.

Molecular dynamics simulations

The molecular dynamics simulations allow us to examine the interactions of the NLRP3 protein with natural compounds Betulinic Acid and Oleanolic Acid and compare these to the effects of a known NLRP3 inhibitor, MCC950. Using the GROMACS platform, interactions were analyzed over a prolonged simulation period of 100 ns, focusing specifically on the dynamics and stability of the protein-ligand complexes. Initially, each ligand-Betulinic Acid, Oleanolic Acid, and MCC950-was superimposed onto the initial frame of the NLRP3 protein complex. This setup closely monitors and quantifies the root mean square deviations (RMSD) of the ligands from the starting structure throughout the simulation. The MCC950 exhibited RMSD values parallel to those of OA, the backbone RMSD, showed a range of 0.35–0.46 nm for Betulinic Acid, 0.25–0.3 nm for Oleanolic Acid, and 0.28–0.35 nm for MCC950, suggesting levels of stability and conformational across the complexes (Fig. 6B). Throughout the simulation, the root mean square fluctuations (RMSF) were tracked. For the compactness of the protein-ligand complexes, the radius of gyration (RoG) was calculated, and the RoG remained between 2.38 and 2.45 nm across the simulations. The consistency of RoG values after 50 ns suggests stable compactness in the secondary structures of the complexes (Fig. 6D). Lastly, principal component analysis (PCA) was employed to reduce the dimensionality of the simulation data, helping to identify stable secondary structures. The PCA highlighted a denser clustering of data points for the MCC950-bound complex compared to those of Betulinic or Oleanolic Acids, suggesting a potentially more stable or favorable interaction profile (Fig. 6F).

Additional target NEK7 was complexed with Betulinic Acid/Oleanolic Acid and simulated to explore their potential as multi-target inhibitors. Throughout a 100 ns simulation. During the simulation, RMSD values for Betulinic Acid ranged from 0.15 to 0.25 nm, demonstrating moderate stability within the binding site of NEK7. In contrast, OA exhibited higher RMSD values, ranging from 0.42 to 0.6 nm, indicating a greater degree of molecular mobility till 20 ns then a more stable interaction with the NEK7 was observed (Fig. 7A). Further analysis of the backbone RMSD confirmed these findings, with Betulinic Acid showing tighter control (0.2–0.3 nm) over structural deviations than Oleanolic Acid (Fig. 7B). This stability is crucial for the efficacy and specificity of potential inhibitors. The RMSF across the amino acid chain, observing variations from 0.1 to 0.4 nm (Fig. 7C). These fluctuations provide insights into the flexibility of the protein structure in response to ligand binding, essential for understanding the dynamic behavior of NEK7 in physiological conditions. Additionally, the interaction strength between the ligands and NEK7 was assessed through the formation of hydrogen bonds. Betulinic Acid formed four hydrogen bonds, while Oleanolic Acid formed three, indicating a slightly stronger interaction of Betulinic Acid with the protein, which may correlate with its more stable RMSD values (Fig. 7E). The compactness of the protein-ligand complexes was quantified by calculating the radius of gyration (RoG), which remained between 1.95 and 2.05 nm throughout the simulation (Fig. 7D). This consistency in RoG values indicates a stable compact structure, suggesting that the secondary structures of NEK7 remain largely intact upon binding with these ligands. Principal component analysis (PCA) was employed to further reduce the complexity of the simulation data, helping to pinpoint stable configurations and interactions. PCA revealed dense clustering of data points when NEK7 was bound with both Betulinic Acid and Oleanolic Acid, suggesting robust binding modes and interactions that could be critical in the context of NEK7 inhibition (Fig. 7F). This detailed simulation provides valuable insights into the structural dynamics and interaction potential of Betulinic Acid and Oleanolic Acid with NLRP3 and NEK7, highlighting their prospects as multi-target inhibitors and validating the experimental models of the present study.

Discussion

In this work, we presented the growth inhibitory effects of crude extracts and fractions of Syzygium guineense on the acute myeloid leukemia cell line HL60 as well as their antitumor effectiveness in vivo on acute myeloid leukemia xenografts of human AML cells in NOD-SCID mice. Mechanistically, cell cycle arrest in G2/M and S phase and apoptosis induction have been demonstrated. We hypothesize that the extracts being complex mixtures, contain several compounds responsible for these observed biological effects.

The chemical composition of S. guineense extracts was unraveled to identify bioactive derivatives in these extracts. L4 fraction identified one major compound Oleanolic Acid (OA; m/z 457.3414) [26]. have reviewed Oleanolic acid's importance as triterpenoid saponin has anti-cancer efficacy.

Furthermore, triterpenoids have been shown to act through cell-cycle arrest and induction of apoptosis. Additionally, Betulinic Acid was reported to induce ROS-dependent cell cycle arrest in human leukemic cells [27] and exhibit anticancer properties in various types of cancer [28]. Fraction L5 rather increased the proportions of cells at the S phase. Maslinic Acid (m/z 473.3611) and its derivative were shown to be the major components in fraction L5. Maslinic Acid was shown to exert antileukemic effects by potentiating key immune markers and promoting NK cell cytotoxicity in leukemic mouse models [29].

Fractions L4 and extract B2 showed better anticancer efficacy in animal models. B2, the crude bark extract could not be analyzed on LC-MS as separating distinct peaks on chromatograms is a big challenge. While leaf fraction L4 could be well fragmented on LC-MS and identified OA or Betulinic Acid, few studies have reported low water solubility of Betulinic Acid, which limits their pharmacological effects in permeating biological membranes [30]. Yet it was interesting to note that, in our study, 50 % of L4-200 group mice showed significant tumor reduction in KG1 xenograft-bearing mice. Furthermore, compounds such as phenolic acids, notably Syringic acid, would also be involved in the anticancer effect [31]. Regarding the crude extract of barks, no compounds could be identified by tracing the obtained base peaks from the LC-MS analysis. However, our previous works [10] on this extract revealed a high total phenolic content and successful identification of syringic acid, and apigenin by high-performance Liquid Chromatography. Therefore, the antitumor effect observed in vivo can be attributed to those compounds [31]. demonstrated that syringic acid significantly decreases the tumor volume in colorectal tumor rats compared to the untreated group. Similarly, Apigenin has been shown to inhibit cancer cell migration and invasion both in in vitro cancer cells and in cervical cancer xenografts [32].

Betulinic Acid has been shown to attenuate osteoarthritis-chronic inflammatory conditions by limiting NLRP3 inflammasome activation and decreasing associated IL-1β maturation/secretion [33]. Hence, we thought to explore the interaction of Betulinic Acid with innate immune inflammasome pathway markers NLRP3 and NEK7.

Oleanolic Acid and its derivatives are commonly plant-derived triterpenoids with broad therapeutic roles in various inflammatory conditions including cancers [16]. OA has been shown to induce apoptosis in human leukemia cells through caspase activation and poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase cleavage [34]. Similarly, Oleanolic Acid was also shown to alleviate diabetes-induced cardiomyopathy by regulating the NLRP3 inflammasome signaling pathways [35].

In our study leaf fraction L4, having phyto-actives Oleanolic Acid/Betulinic Acid inhibited leukemia cell proliferation, which was found to be through cell cycle G2/M arrest. Corroborating with this data Oleanolic Acid/Betulinic Acid derivative inhibited the proliferation of glioma cells [36] and leukemia cells by regulating G2/M cell cycle proteins and gene expression in vitro [27].

The molecular dynamics simulations shed light on the interactions of NLRP3 and NEK7 proteins with Betulinic Acid and Oleanolic Acid, alongside the NLRP3 inhibitor MCC950. Simulations revealed stable binding of the compounds to NLRP3 and NEK7 over 100 ns, reflected in RMSD and RMSF analyses. Notably, MCC950 exhibited tighter clustering and more hydrogen bonds with NLRP3. NEK7 simulations showcased moderate stability with Betulinic Acid and increasing stability with Oleanolic Acid over time. These findings validate the multi-target inhibitory potential of the compounds, supporting experimental models and emphasizing their therapeutic promise in leukemia.

Interestingly, we noted that fraction leaf ethanolic extract derived from another habitat in Cameroon i.e. S. guineense DC.var. macrocarpum exhibited no significant change in body weight and survival at 900 mg/kg bw after the acute toxicity study indicating the safety of this plant fraction. corroborating with our study 80 % methanol extract of S. guineense leaves from Ethiopia exhibited no toxicity on body weight, behavior, and gross pathology of rats, at 5000 mg/kg bw [37]. Another sub-chronic toxicity study using 70 % ethanolic extract of fresh leaves from S. guineense Wall. in rats at doses up to 1000 mg/kg demonstrated no alteration in body weight and histological sections of kidneys and liver compared to the control group at necropsy [38]. Since S. guineense leaf extract consumption has shown efficacy leads, doses should be evaluated using standard regulatory guidelines and cautiously.

Conclusion

From our study, we concluded that leaf and bark extracts of Syzygium guineense, a commonly available spice in Cameroon have the potential to be developed as NLRP3 inhibitors for affordable improved leukemia therapeutics.
