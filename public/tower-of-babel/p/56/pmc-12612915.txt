Natural Amino Acid-Derived Iron(III) Catalysts for Green Oxidation Chemistry Applications
Authors: Karie E. Sanford, Raja Shekhar Kondrapolu, Paul Omituyaki, Taiwo-Oluwanifemi A. Fashina, Md Mohsin Patwary, Anindya Ghosh, Ambar B. Rangumagar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Iron-complex peroxide activators make up a group of catalysts developed to perform green oxidation chemistries. They are among the most efficient, environmentally friendly oxidants, but several structural challenges limit their wider applications. In this study, we altered the traditional synthesis of these iron complexes by using natural amino acids, thereby increasing the hydrolytic stability and planarity of the iron-complex structures, which improved their reactivity. Three different iron complexes were synthesized (1-A, 1-B, and 1-C) and characterized through multiple techniques, including Electrospray Ionization-Mass Spectrometry (ESI-MS). 1-C was used for dye degradation studies, demonstrating its ability to remove ∼98% of synthetic dyes from water within 20 min under optimized conditions. The degradation followed an overall second-order kinetics with an apparent rate constant (k app = 8.5 × 105 M–1 min–1) consistent with values reported in previous Fe-TAML/H2O2 studies. Total Organic Carbon (TOC) analysis in the byproduct indicated a 30% mineralization by the end of the degradation with the remaining 70% converted to products identified by Compact Mass Spectrometry (CMS) analysis. The rapid oxidative degradation of dyes by 1-C was attributed to the formation of high-valent iron-oxo species, as supported by UV–vis and CMS analyses.

Introduction

Environmental sustainability, driven by industrial revolutions and population growth, is a global priority. Urbanization and consumer demand strain natural resources, escalating pollution. Sustainability ensures meeting current needs without compromising the future through renewable energy, reduced reliance on fossil fuels, and pollution control. Scientists lead efforts for a cleaner, healthier planet. −

Rapid industrial growth, fueled by population increase and economic expansion, results in significant air, soil, and water pollution. , Sectors like textiles and leather contribute vividly colored wastewater due to synthetic dye usage, posing a major threat to water bodies. , The complex textile processing involves excessive synthetic dye consumption with unused dyes flushed into water during cleaning, comprising a substantial pollution source. These pollutants, due to their insolubility and durability, harm aquatic life and pose potential health risks. − Developing safe and sustainable dye removal methods is essential for protecting natural water sources. While oxidation processes, commonly used in various industries including bleaching, offer the potential for color removal, traditional methods often involve toxic chemicals. − Eco-friendly alternatives, such as hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) and oxygen, can mitigate the environmental impact of persistent pollutants, , including polychlorinated dibenzo-dioxins and furans, which are associated with oxidation chemistry.

H2O2, a widely used oxidizing agent, is favored for its environmentally friendly nature, as it decomposes into water and oxygen. However, its inefficiency at ambient temperatures and lack of selectivity pose challenges, necessitating higher concentrations and energy demands. , Green chemistry, promoting safer and sustainable chemical processes, offers principles to address such issues. , Waste prevention is a key principle, aiming for safe initial designs that minimize postproduction cleanup. When H2O2 interacts with metal ions, particularly iron ions, it triggers Fenton-type chemistry, a process that generates highly reactive hydroxyl radicals used for medical purposes, such as wound cleaning; however, these radicals lack selectivity for practical applications. , The Fenton chemistry requires stoichiometric amounts of metal ions − and strong acidic conditions, posing environmental concerns.

To harness H2O2’s potential as a green oxidant, catalysts such as iron-complex activators have been designed to avoid the nonselective Fenton route. These catalysts (iron-tetraamido macrocyclic ligand, Fe-TAML) utilize iron-oxo or iron-peroxo intermediates, offering selectivity and efficiency in various applications. ,,− This innovation represents a promising substitute to chlorine-based oxidants like chlorine gas or chlorine dioxide, which raise notable environmental worries due to the creation of chlorinated byproducts and possible toxicity concerns, highlighting both environmental safety and effectiveness. , Achieving this alternative intermediate involves designing a catalyst with specific ligands that control the electronic geometry of the metal, making higher oxidation states more accessible. ,, The ligands deprotonate secondary amides, striking a balance between strong electron donation and resistance to self-oxidation, thereby ensuring the catalyst’s stability. Additionally, the tetradentate ligand design provides coordination spots on the metal ion for H2O2, enhancing its efficiency and adaptability for various applications. ,,,, These design features make the catalyst easy to synthesize, adaptable, selective, and highly efficient in activating H2O2. This innovation offers a promising alternative to chlorine-based oxidants, , emphasizing environmental friendliness and efficacy.

Iron complexes are prone to ligand demetalation and oxidative decomposition during catalysis. Early generations of Fe-TAML undergo proton- or buffer-induced removal of the iron center at acidic to moderate pH. Ghosh et al. demonstrated that the Fe-TAML complex undergoes acid- or buffer-catalyzed demetalation with pseudo-first-order kinetics, and that modifying the ligand tail to a CF2 group dramatically suppressed this deactivation by over 10 orders of magnitude. Another challenge with the iron complex is the operational inactivation via oxidative self-destruction, intramolecular or intermolecular attack of high-valent Fe-oxo species against the macrocycle itself, especially at strongly alkaline pH. The foundational Handbook of Green Chemistry by Collins and Khetan lays out the balance between catalytic activity and stability, highlighting that although Fe-TAMLs maintain high turnover across a broad pH (notably near pH 10), they eventually degrade through ligand oxidation or demetalation, limiting long-term function.

Several studies have attempted different strategies, including the introduction of electron-withdrawing substituents on the amido ligands to enhance oxidative resilience, designing sterically protected macrocycles that shield the metal center from self-destructive pathways, and employing fluorinated or alkylated derivatives to improve hydrolytic stability in alkaline solutions, but none have used natural amino acids to synthesize the Fe-TAML.

Synthetic dyes have been extensively employed in the textile, paper, and food industries, but their effluent release into water bodies poses a considerable environmental problem, as these are highly stable, toxic, and resistant to biodegradation. Concentrations of dye in industrial effluent are commonly in the tens or hundreds of milligrams per liter range, and regulations governing levels acceptable to be discharged into water specify limits for producing units (mg/L). Thus, laboratory experiments are generally performed at similar orders of magnitude concentrations, so that the results obtained can represent real physical settings. In this study, the chosen dye concentrations are consistent with reported wastewater values and provide a relevant basis for evaluating catalytic degradation performance. ,

Additionally, several advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) have been studied for dye removal including Fenton chemistry, electrochemical oxidation, photocatalysis, and ozonation. The Fenton process and its variations, which produce hydroxyl radicals in acidic environments, are the most commonly used of these. Despite the wide applications of the Fenton process, it is faced with some drawbacks, such as sludge production, limited pH tolerance, and secondary waste generation. Several catalytic systems, including transition-metal complexes and metalloporphyrins, have been explored for dye degradation, but relatively few studies have reported the use of bioinspired or amino acid-based ligands for pollutant removal. ,

This work represents an initial step toward developing novel catalysts directly from natural amino acids, which has not been reported for environmental applications. Unlike conventional methods that rely on synthetic derivatives, this approach employs natural amino acids as starting materials, offering a more environmentally friendly pathway to catalyst synthesis. , Notably, the use of natural amino acids in the synthesis introduces two chiral centers into the catalyst advancement, which were not observed in previous versions of the compound. These chiral centers enhance its structural versatility and improve its efficiency, expanding its potential applications in green chemistry. The novelty of this work is also demonstrated by the catalyst (1-C)’s efficiency in degrading dyes through the highly valent metal-oxo pathway under alkaline conditions (pH 9.5), which is in contrast to the Fenton process. This study provides a sustainable and environmentally friendly approach to pollutant degradation, thereby contributing to the advancement of green catalytic methods of waste treatment.

Materials and Methods

General

All reagents and solvents were purchased from commercially available sources and used without further purification. Ultrahigh-purity nitrogen gas (N2) and argon (Ar) were obtained from Air Gas Company. Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR) spectra were recorded on a JEOL 400 MHz spectrometer for 1H NMR and 13C NMR spectra using deuterated chloroform (CDCl3) or dimethyl sulfoxide-d 6 (DMSO-d 6) as solvents. Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectra were recorded with a Nicolet IS5 FT-IR spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) using the Attenuated Total Reflectance (ATR) mode. This analysis was conducted across the 4000–600 cm–1 spectral range to identify the molecular vibrations within the compounds. Electrospray Ionization Mass Spectrometry (ESI-MS) was obtained using an Agilent 100 series MSD VL instrument. Negative ion mode ESI-MS was employed as a supplementary technique to characterize the ligands (NAA-AML, V) and catalysts (Fe-NAA-AML, 1), with samples dissolved in acetonitrile. Ultraviolet–visible (UV–vis) spectra were obtained with a PerkinElmer Lambda 365 UV–vis spectrometer over the 200–800 nm range. This was applied to monitor dye degradation reactions by measuring changes in the absorbance of the dye solutions over time. Additionally, the use of UV–vis spectroscopy enabled the determination of metal complex properties and the assessment of the effects of pH, catalyst dosage, and reaction time on the degradation efficiency. UV–vis evaluated the catalyst’s stability in aqueous solution. The Advion Expression Compact Mass Spectrometer (CMS), atmospheric pressure chemical ionization (APCI), with the source system parameters set at 250 °C and low fragmentation methods in both positive and negative modes, and a Shimadzu TOC-L Total Organic Carbon (TOC) and Total Nitrogen analyzer equipped with an ASI-L autosampler were used to analyze degradation products. Data analysis and plotting were conducted using OriginPro.

Synthesis of the Ligands (V) and Iron-Complex (1)

Three ligands were synthesized from readily available amino acids (l-valine, l-leucine, and l-phenylalanine), following literature procedures with minor modifications. , Synthesis step details are given in the Supporting Information and depicted in Figure , and each intermediate and final product was characterized.

Schematic representation of the catalyst synthesis with their structures. From left to right, 1-A, 1-B, and 1-C (Fe-NAA-AML).

Catalysts were synthesized based on literature methods with adjustments, , as outlined in Figure , and confirmed using standard techniques. Briefly, the ligand (1.0 equiv) was dissolved in 25 mL of anhydrous tetrahydrofuran (THF) in a 100 mL Schlenk flask. The solution was cooled to 0 °C, and then n-butyllithium (4.1 equiv) was added dropwise. The reaction mixture was stirred for 15 min at a temperature of 0 °C. Subsequently, the mixture was allowed to warm to room temperature and stirred for an additional 15 min. Anhydrous iron­(II) chloride (FeCl2) (1.1 equiv) was added, and the solution was stirred for 18 h at room temperature (rt) until it turned a deep brown color. Upon exposure to air, the solution changed from brown to orange-red. After filtration, the solvent from the filtrate was removed under a low pressure. The resulting orange-red solid product was dissolved in a minimal amount of DI water, while maintaining a pH of around 8.5. The water was removed using a liquid nitrogen trap for 3 h.

Preparation of Catalyst Stock Solution

For the dye degradation studies, a 10:1 dye-to-catalyst molar ratio was employed, necessitating the preparation of a catalyst stock solution. The stock solution of catalyst 1-C (1.81 × 10– 3 M) was prepared by dissolving 1 mg of the catalyst in 10 mL of deionized water. Appropriate volumes of this stock solution were then used to achieve the desired catalyst concentration for the degradation of various dyes while maintaining the 10:1 ratio.

Dye Degradation

Dye degradation experiments were conducted using catalyst 1-C. Various studies, including control, molar ratio optimization, pH variation, and kinetics, were performed. , Methylene Blue (MB, 9.6 mg/L) and Indigo Carmine (IC, 14.0 mg/L) dyes prepared in phosphate buffer (pH 10) at 25 °C were used to investigate their degradation behavior. The absorbance at the dye’s maximum wavelength (λmax) was monitored over time to track the degradation process. To optimize the system, various ratios of catalyst 1-C, MB, and H2O2 were tested in pH 10 buffer. Using the optimized ratio, the dye degradation was further studied across a range of pH values to identify the optimal pH condition for maximum catalytic activity. A total of five dyes were examined for supplementary data, as depicted in Figure .

Names and structures of the different dyes utilized for the research.

Analyses

Given that the dye molecules undergo degradation in the solution, it is crucial to investigate the resulting products. Hence, various analyses were conducted to comprehend the mineralization process of dye degradation using 1-C. These analyses included TOC, nitrate analysis, and CMS.

For TOC and nitrate analyses, aliquots were collected after 20 min of IC dye degradation and submitted to Arkansas Analytical Laboratories (Little Rock, AR). TOC measurements were performed to evaluate the distribution of carbon, distinguishing between residual organic intermediates and the fraction released as CO2 or carbon monoxide (CO). Nitrate analysis was carried out to quantify the nitrogen species liberated from the dye molecules upon mineralization.

To further comprehend the mineralization process of the dye degradation facilitated by the 1-C/H2O2, postreaction products were extracted from the aqueous solution using an organic solvent (5% methanol in dichloromethane solution). After solvent removal, the residue was dissolved in Acetonitrile (ACN) to examine the degradation products using Advion Expression CMS. This approach allows for very rapid analysis.

Stability Test of Catalyst in Water

The stability of the natural amino acid-derived iron­(III) catalysts in water was investigated to assess their suitability for degrading textile dye pollutants. , To evaluate stability, each catalyst solution was monitored in a UV–vis spectrophotometer for over an hour. Baseline calibrations were conducted with water as the solvent, and samples were scanned within the 300–700 nm range. This method facilitated the determination of the wavelength of maximum absorbance for each catalyst, monitoring dye degradation over time, and assessing reaction kinetics. ,

The maximum absorbance (λmax) value of the catalyst was utilized to evaluate the catalyst’s stability in water over time. The solid catalysts exhibited a vibrant red-orange color, which transitioned to pale-yellow-orange when dissolved in water, indicating absorbance in the visible region. UV–vis spectra for catalysts 1-A, 1-B, and 1-C are depicted in Figures S10a – c respectively. Each catalyst displayed a peak absorbance in the visible region, with λmax at 369 nm and a broad shoulder extending from 420 to 500 nm, corresponding to the solution’s color. Consistency in absorbance over time indicated stability, whereas fluctuations suggested structural changes that could impact the catalytic function. Figure S10d illustrates the stability plots.

Hydrolytic stability is hypothesized to depend on the electron density around the amides and the planarity of the catalyst. Catalyst 1-C showed the highest stability, maintaining steady absorbance at λmax. In contrast, catalysts 1-A and 1-B exhibited fluctuations, likely due to the chiral centers affecting planarity, which led to electron density buildup and potential demetalation. Catalyst 1-C’s aromatic side chain may contribute to stability by maintaining planarity or redistributing electron density. Its superior stability led to its selection for dye degradation trials.

Additionally, the stability of those synthesized catalysts can be explained based on their energy-minimized structural models, as shown in Figure S11. In the energy-minimized structures, the deprotonated nitrogens in 1-A and 1-B adopt a puckered conformation due to significant steric congestion. This conformation limits lone-pair delocalization between the nitrogen atoms and the amide carbonyl groups, rendering the nitrogens highly basic and prone to protonation, even in aqueous environments, which leads to catalyst demetalation. This behavior is not observed in the regular Fe-TAML catalyst. Among the three structures, 1-C is the most planar and most closely resembles Fe-TAML, although it still does not match its degree of planarity. This greater planarity may account for 1-C’s comparatively higher stability in water.

Nevertheless, all three structures (1-A, 1-B, and 1-C) possess hydrogen atoms within their frameworks that can interact with the oxidized iron complexes. Such interactions promote degradation in the presence of hydrogen peroxide, further contributing to the loss of catalytic activity. Taken together, the limited hydrolytic and oxidative stability of these catalysts likely explains their inferior oxidation performance relative to that of Fe-TAML. Single-crystal X-ray diffraction analysis would have been ideal to confirm the ring conformations; however, suitable crystals could not be obtained.

Furthermore, the catalytic activity of 1-A, 1-B, and 1-C toward IC degradation facilitated the selection of the most stable and efficient catalyst for further studies (Figure S12). Catalysts 1-A and 1-B exhibited limited and declining performance over the 30 min reaction period due to hydrolysis in water, resulting in only partial dye degradation. In contrast, 1-C demonstrated superior activity and resistance to hydrolysis under aqueous alkaline conditions, making it more suitable for sustained pollutant removal. Its structural robustness in alkaline mediaa critical factor for AOPs that often operate in nonacidic environmentstogether with earlier screening results showing higher catalytic efficiency, supports the choice of 1-C for further degradation studies.

Optimization of the Dye Degradation Reaction

Conducting control studies is essential for accurately evaluating the catalyst’s effectiveness in dye degradation. These studies were performed to determine whether degradation occurred from catalyst 1-C alone, H2O2 alone, or their combined action.

Figure S13a illustrates the control study, which involves only catalyst 1-C (0.1 equiv) and dye (1 equiv) over 30 min, demonstrating no significant decrease in absorbance. Figure S13b shows the control study with only H2O2 (10 equiv) and dye (1 equiv), indicating a slight initial decrease in absorbance, followed by a negligible change. These results suggest that neither the catalyst alone nor H2O2 alone has a substantial effect on the dye. Thus, a combination of both is required for effective dye degradation in this project.

Additionally, the degradation behavior of IC in a homogeneous aqueous medium at pH 10 phosphate buffer was monitored using UV–vis spectroscopy. As shown in Figure , minimal degradation (<10% over 30 min) was observed when IC was treated alone, with only the iron complex (IC + 1-C), or with H2O2 (IC + H2O2). These results indicate that neither the oxidant nor the catalyst, when used individually, can effectively initiate dye degradation under the tested conditions. This finding is consistent with previous reports, where the absence of a cooperative catalytic system limits the formation of reactive oxidizing species necessary for efficient pollutant degradation. ,

Control studies for the degradation of IC dye at pH 10 and 25 °C.

In contrast, the system containing both 1-C and H2O2 exhibited a rapid and pronounced degradation, achieving over 80% removal within the first 10 min and nearly complete degradation (∼98%) by 20 min. This accelerated process highlights the strong catalytic synergy between H2O2 and the iron complex, driven by the in situ generation of high-valent iron-oxo species that promote oxidative cleavage of dye molecules and disrupt chromophore units. , These results underscore the potential of iron-complex catalysts as environmentally friendly agents in advanced green oxidation processes for effective dye degradation, particularly for the decolorization of wastewater. ,

After establishing that the catalyst and H2O2 alone are insufficient for dye degradation, various molar ratios were investigated using MB dye in a pH 10 phosphate buffer at 25 °C. The ratios studied were 0.01c:1d:1p, 0.01c:1d:10p, 0.1c:1d:1p, and 0.1c:1d:10p. Figure S14 illustrates the results. At 0.01c:1d:1p (Figure S14a), a minor degradation of 6.4% ± 0.35% was observed. Increasing H2O2 to 10 equiv (0.01c:1d:10p, Figure S14b) raised degradation to 17.0% ± 0.56%. Elevating the catalyst to 0.1 equiv (0.1c:1d:1p, Figure S14c) notably increased degradation to 32.7% ± 0.04%. Finally, at 0.1c:1d:10p (Figure S14d), both catalyst and H2O2 were increased 10-fold, resulting in significant degradation of 95.1% ± 0.62% within 30 min.

The substantial increase in dye degradation observed with the 0.1c:1d:10p molar ratio highlights the synergistic effect of higher catalyst and H2O2 concentrations rather than the effect of increasing either one of them. Figure a summarizes these findings, indicating the optimal ratio for dye degradation.

(a) Molar ratio optimization studies for MB dye degradation at pH 10, (b) pH’s impact on MB dye degradation using the 0.1c:1d:10p molar ratio at 25 °C, (c) effect of pH on the initial rate of MB dye degradation using the 0.1c:1d:10p molar ratio at 25 °(C, and d) degradation of other dyes using the 0.1c:1d:10p molar ratio at pH 9.5 and 25 °C.

Following the identification of the 0.1c:1d:10p ratio as optimal, pH studies were conducted to assess its impact on the degradation efficiency. These investigations, conducted at 25 °C using MB dye, revealed peak degradation at pH 9.5 to 10, with degradation percentages ranging from 98.0% ± 0.06% to 98.2% ± 0.45%. As the pH deviates from the optimal range, dye degradation decreases. At lower pH, H2O2 may struggle to dissociate, hindering reactive intermediate formation. At higher pH values, OH ligands likely occupy catalyst coordination spots, preventing H2O2 coordination. Figure b presents a summary of these pH study results.

Kinetic Studies

The initial rate of degradation of MB was monitored at its maximum absorbance (λmax = 660 nm) by varying the concentrations of MB, H2O2, and 1-C catalyst at pH 9.5. In each experiment, the concentration of one reagent was varied while the others were held constant (MB: 1 × 10–5 M, 1-C: 1 × 10–6 M, H2O2:1 × 10–4 M). These studies were conducted to analyze the impact of changing concentrations of catalyst 1-C, MB, and H2O2 on the initial rate of dye degradation, revealing insights into the overall reaction rate. The effect of varying H2O2 concentrations (with dye and catalyst concentrations held constant) showed a clear first-order relationship, as depicted in Figure a. Similarly, Figure b illustrates a first-order correlation between catalyst concentration and reaction rate when dye and H2O2 concentrations were held constant, whereas Figure c indicates a zero-order relationship with respect to dye concentration and reaction rate, with H2O2 and catalyst concentrations held constant. In other words, the initial rate of the reaction does not depend on the amount of dye in the solution.

Average initial degradation rates of dye at pH 9.5 with varying concentrations of a) H2O2, b) catalyst 1-C, and c) dye, while maintaining other concentrations constant (dye: 1E–5 M, catalyst 1-C: 1E–6 M, H2O2: 1E–4 M).

The apparent rate calculated at an optimum pH of 9.5 (k app = 8.5 × 105 M–1 min–1) is consistent with previously reported Fe-TAML/H2O2 studies. Chahbane (2007) reported values in the range of 105 to 106 × 105 M–1 min–1 for the degradation of orange II dye in alkaline pH, using an Fe-TAML catalyst. The observed rate constant is attributed to optimized reaction conditions, improved catalyst dispersion and stability, and the homogeneous nature of the reaction medium, which enhances mass transfer. Overall, the kinetics suggest that the degradation process is catalyst-controlled and oxidant-limited, ,,

A similar kinetic pattern was observed by Omituyaki (2023) in biosorption studies, where linear pseudo-second order behavior governed uptake rates of pollutants, suggesting direct surface and complex interaction pathways.

Hydrogen Peroxide (H2O2) Utilization

To determine the amount of H2O2 utilized during the degradation process using MB, the concentration of H2O2 was observed to decrease significantly. Figure spectra (as shown by the red line) demonstrate that H2O2 was actively consumed as the initial concentration of H2O2 was 0.10 mM, and after the degradation, only 0.018 mM of H2O2 remained, implying that approximately 83% of the H2O2 was utilized. This is consistent with a previous study using Fe-TAMLs, in which H2O2 acts as the terminal oxidant to generate high-valent iron-oxo intermediates that drive substrate oxidation.

High-Valent Iron-Oxo Species Studies

High-valent iron-oxo species are generally considered the primary reactive intermediates in aqueous-phase contaminant degradation, often exhibiting greater activity and selectivity. The generation of high-valent iron-oxo species during catalysis was further corroborated by UV–vis spectroscopy. Figure S18 compares the UV–vis spectrum of the 1-C catalyst in the presence of H2O2 with that of the catalyst alone, with the inset showing the color change of the solution upon H2O2 addition. The introduction of H2O2 to the 1-C catalyst markedly reduced the characteristic iron­(III) charge-transfer band and produced a broader absorption in the visible region. These spectral changes are attributed to the oxidation of the iron­(III) complex to high-valent iron-oxo intermediates, likely (Fe­(IV)O) and/or (Fe­(V)O), consistent with earlier observations in Fe-TAML/peroxide systems.

Additionally, the formation of high-valent iron-oxo intermediates was supported by CMS analysis. The chromatogram (Figure S19) and the corresponding mass spectrum (Figure S20) obtained from APCI positive mode CMS of the reaction mixture revealed a peak at m/z 567.24 [M + H]+, confirming the presence of the iron-oxo intermediate.

Conclusions

Iron­(III)-complex activators, essential for H2O2 activation in oxidation processes, were synthesized from natural amino acids for the targeted degradation of synthetic dyes in water. Three catalysts (1-A, 1-B, and 1-C) were successfully synthesized, with 1-C exhibiting superior reactivity and stability. Its enhanced stability was supported by the energy-minimized structural model, which revealed that the aromatic side chain promotes planarity and redistributes electron density. Catalyst 1-C was employed for dye degradation, achieving ∼98% efficiency for MB and IC under an optimized pH of 9.5 within 40 and 20 min, respectively, thereby highlighting its robustness and high catalytic potency. Kinetic studies revealed a second-order rate law, with an apparent rate (k app = 8.5 × 105 M–1 min–1) dependent on both 1-C and H2O2 concentrations. The degraded product analysis indicated that ∼30% of carbon was released as CO2/CO, while the remaining 70% formed intermediate products, as confirmed by CMS. The rapid degradation of the dyes was facilitated by high-valent iron-oxo species, as confirmed using UV–vis and CMS analysis.

Overall, 1-C demonstrated high efficiency in removing persistent pollutants from water, highlighting its potential for environmental remediation applications.
