Matters arising: single-versus double-layer uterine closure at the time of cesarean delivery and risk of uterine scar niche
Authors: Gabriel Moreira Lino, George Alessandro Maranhão Conrado
Published: 2025

In a systematic review and meta-analysis of 11 randomized controlled trials, Dominoni et al. reported that single-layer closure of the uterine incision was superior to double-layer closure at follow-up periods of at least six months [1]. Nonetheless, some issues in their review process may have led to inaccurate conclusions.

First, they used risk difference as the effect measure in the meta-analysis. However, meta-analyses of risk differences are known to be statistically inconsistent, which is why the Cochrane Handbook advises against this approach [2]. Instead, results should be transformed into risk differences afterwards.

The authors also included mistaken data from the 2CLOSE trial [3], which actually had ultrasound assessments at three months of follow-up rather than six. They based their analysis on a secondary report of the trial, in which many patients declined participation. In both the original study and the three-year follow-up of menstrual symptoms, no difference was found between closure techniques.

To clarify these issues, we conducted a reanalysis of the six-month follow-up data, excluding the 2CLOSE trial. Notwithstanding the initial results, the reanalysis using relative risk as the effect measure shows no appreciable difference in the prevalence of niche (RR 0.78, 95% CI 0.61–1.01; n = 6 trials, 1254 women; I2 = 82%). Further, the inclusion of multiple trials at high risk of bias, along with the high heterogeneity, is a major limitation to the analysis. In a subgroup analysis excluding studies at high risk, not only is the heterogeneity nonsignificant, but the results also show little to no difference between the closure techniques (RR 0.99, 95% CI 0.90–1.09; n = 3 trials, 790 women; I2 = 21%; Fig. 1).

After transforming to risk difference, we again found no difference between closure techniques, even less so when considering a threshold for clinical meaningfulness of 1% (RD − 11.20%, 95% CI − 19.80 to + 0.50%).

The investigation of the origin and prevention of uterine niches remains complex. Other surgical factors, such as suture material, locking sutures, and labor-related variables, have been suggested to play a role, but no high-quality evidence supports their influence [4]. The recent NICEST trial [5] reported a reduction in the risk of large niches with double-layer closure, a finding that should be confirmed.

Further research into interventions for uterine wound healing is crucial, with careful attention to bias detection and outcome definitions. Whether there is an optimal surgical approach is still uncertain.
