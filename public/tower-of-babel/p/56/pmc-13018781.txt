Diagnosis and management of paediatric magnet ingestion: a systematic review of clinical practice guidelines
Authors: Andrey Nezhentsev, Jonathan J Neville, Nigel J Hall
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe widespread use of small and powerful neodymium magnets in consumer products has led to a significant rise in paediatric magnet ingestion. The ingestion of multiple magnets poses serious risks, including bowel obstruction and perforation. Evidence-based Clinical Practice Guidelines (CPGs) are necessary to inform clinicians of the safest and most effective management strategies across a range of healthcare settings.AimThis systematic review aims to summarise existing CPGs for diagnosing and managing paediatric magnet ingestion, evaluate the supporting evidence, highlight variation and consensus, and identify areas requiring further research.MethodsMEDLINE, Scopus, the Cochrane Library, Web of Science and Embase were searched to identify CPGs. Study characteristics, investigation and management recommendations, and indications for conservative management, endoscopy, surgery and discharge with and without follow-up were extracted. Two reviewers independently assessed CPG quality using the Appraisal of Guidelines for Research and Evaluation (AGREE) II instrument.Results25 CPGs were included. Recommendations varied for diagnostic imaging, management of single and multiple magnet ingestion and the use of conservative, endoscopic or surgical interventions. However, there was consensus that: abdominal X-ray is the preferred first-line imaging modality, conservative management is recommended for asymptomatic single magnets, endoscopic removal is recommended for prepyloric asymptomatic multiple magnets and surgery for symptomatic multiple magnets. AGREE II appraisal revealed poor scores in CPG Applicability, Stakeholder Involvement and Rigour of Development, but strong scores for Editorial Independence. Existing CPGs for paediatric magnet ingestion are based on low-quality evidence or expert consensus.ConclusionsThis review highlights inconsistencies and areas of consensus in current CPGs for paediatric magnet ingestion. Variations underscore the need for standardised, evidence-based guidelines to optimise care for affected children. Future research should focus on addressing gaps in evidence and consensus to ensure consistent management across healthcare settings.

Introduction

Small and powerful neodymium magnets are used in a wide range of consumer products, including wireless headphones, magnetic phone cases, jewellery, alternative health products and cabinet locks. Since the early 2000s, desktop toys containing such magnets have also gained popularity as stress-relieving, creativity-enhancing and educational gadgets. This has led to a rising incidence of paediatric magnet ingestion, with 1316 cases reported in the USA in 2019—an increase of 408% from 322 cases in 2017.1 In the UK, the incidence was at least 2.4 per 100 000 children and young people per year.2 This represents a significant public health concern, with regulators imposing recalls and restrictions on these products, citing the need for safer product design to prevent accidental ingestion.3 4

While single magnets typically pass through the gastrointestinal tract without causing harm, the ingestion of multiple magnets can cause serious complications. Their mutual attraction may lead to the formation of clusters within the gastrointestinal tract, sometimes across separate loops of bowel, with the intestinal wall trapped in between. Bowel obstruction, perforation and fistula formation secondary to pressure necrosis have been reported.47 Due to the increasing frequency of magnet ingestion many groups have developed Clinical Practice Guidelines (CPGs) to assist clinicians when assessing and treating patients. Treatment options include observation, laxatives to accelerate the passage of the foreign bodies, endoscopic removal or surgery.8 There are apparent differences observed between CPGs, suggesting uncertainty about the best approach to diagnosing and managing paediatric magnet ingestion.

This systematic review aims to summarise published CPGs, explore the evidence behind them, identify areas of consensus and variation, gaps in knowledge and avenues for further research.

Methods

Search strategy

A search was conducted to identify published CPGs (online supplemental data 1). CPGs are statements that include recommendations intended to optimise patient care, which are informed by a systematic review of evidence, and an assessment of the benefits and harms of alternative care options.9

Inclusion criteria

Citations were uploaded onto Rayyan.10 Titles and abstracts were screened independently by AN and JJN. Disagreements were resolved by a third reviewer (NJH). Full-text records were then retrieved to assess their eligibility. Where the titles and abstracts did not provide sufficient information for assessment against the inclusion criteria, the full-text article was evaluated.

Data extraction

The following characteristics were extracted from each CPG: authors, year of publication, location of publication and the target age group. The following recommendations were extracted: first-line diagnostic investigation, treatment recommendations for the ingestion of one or multiple magnets, treatment recommendations based on the anatomical location of the magnet(s) and whether the patient was symptomatic. Multiple magnets were defined as >1 magnet or (≥1 magnet + ≥1 magnetisable object). The indications for conservative management, endoscopy, surgery, discharge with follow-up and discharge without follow-up were recorded. The recommended conservative management and surgical approaches were also recorded. If a CPG did not comment on a particular aspect of diagnosis or management, this was recorded as ‘No Guidance’.

Quality appraisal

The CPGs were assessed independently by AN and JJN using the Appraisal of Guidelines for Research and Evaluation (AGREE) II tool, which ensures consistent guideline evaluation. The AGREE II instrument has 23 items across six domains: Scope and Purpose, Stakeholder Involvement, Rigour of Development, Clarity of Presentation, Applicability and Editorial Independence. Items are rated on a 7-point Likert scale from ‘strongly disagree’ to ‘strongly agree’, and domain scores are standardised to a percentage, with higher scores indicating better adherence. Median scores were calculated for each domain. This quality assessment system is used frequently in critical appraisals of CPGs.1113

Data synthesis

The data was synthesised qualitatively. The inter-rater variability of AGREE II scores was calculated by measuring the Pearson correlation coefficient, with 95% CIs. Analysis was conducted in GraphPad Prism (V.10.3.1, Boston, Massachusetts, USA). A p value of <0.05 was considered significant. To aid interpretation, we also summarised areas of agreement across CPGs. In line with commonly used thresholds in Delphi methodology,14 we defined ‘consensus’ as ≥75% of included CPGs making the same recommendation.

Results

Search characteristics

The initial search in March 2024 identified 2847 results, of which 1825 were duplicates (figure 1). A total of 984 studies were excluded based on their title and abstract; two records could not be retrieved. 38 were deemed not to be eligible after retrieval, and 22 studies were excluded as they had published only limited recommendations, such as providing only one suggestion regarding paediatric magnet ingestion. A further 15 only cited previously published CPGs without adding new recommendations, and 1 study had audited a previously published CPG. A total of 25 CPGs met the inclusion criteria and all were included in this review.

CPG characteristics

The characteristics of the CPGs are summarised in online supplemental table 1. CPGs were based on different study types: 17 case reports or case series, 4 literature reviews and 4 were groups or expert opinions. All studies were retrospective. The 17 case reports/series and 1 literature review based their CPGs on individual patient data—a median of 36.5 patients were included.

Diagnostic imaging

22 CPGs provided guidance for first-line diagnostic imaging, with consensus that a plain film abdominal X-ray (AXR) is recommended. Of these, approximately a quarter provided no further guidance, while the remaining advice varied, including anteroposterior AXR (27%), anteroposterior and lateral AXRs (18%), AXR and chest X-ray (14%), AXR or ultrasound (9%) or a kidney, ureter and bladder X-ray (5%). Second-line diagnostic imaging was advised by 28% (7/25) of CPGs if there was diagnostic uncertainty after the first-line imaging. Of these, 71% recommended a lateral AXR, and 29% advised a low-dose CT scan.

Single magnet ingestion

Asymptomatic patients

Most CPGs recommended, with consensus, conservative management for asymptomatic patients who ingested a single magnet, regardless of location (figure 2). However, consideration of endoscopic removal of a single magnet was suggested by some CPGs, particularly for prepyloric locations, with approximately 50% recommending it for the oesophagus and stomach. If the magnet was not progressing on serial imaging, some CPGs recommended considering surgical removal.

Clinical Practice Guidelines for single magnet ingestion.

Symptomatic patients

CPG recommendations also varied for symptomatic patients after swallowing a single magnet figure 2. 25% recommended initial conservative management for a single symptomatic magnet in the oesophagus, and about 50% beyond the oesophagus. Endoscopic retrieval was more commonly suggested for prepyloric magnets and surgical retrieval for those beyond the pylorus, although an endoscopic approach was recommended in some CPGs for symptomatic single magnets felt to be in the colon (online supplemental table 2).

Multiple magnet ingestion

Asymptomatic patients

Like recommendations for single magnets, there is variation in CPGs for patients who ingest multiple magnets. For asymptomatic patients (figure 3), initial conservative management was more commonly recommended for postpyloric magnets, while there was consensus that endoscopy should be considered for prepyloric locations. 11 CPGs recommended intervention if magnets did not progress on serial imaging. Three recommend intervention if the magnets were seen to be adherent to each other on imaging (online supplemental table 3).

Clinical Practice Guidelines for multiple magnet ingestion.

Symptomatic patients

For symptomatic patients with multiple magnet ingestion, an interventional as opposed to conservative approach was recommended by most CPGs for magnets in most anatomical locations, with consensus that surgical removal should be considered (figure 3). When the magnets are prepyloric, approximately half of CPGs advised considering endoscopic removal, while 36% recommended considering surgery initially. For postpyloric magnets, the majority recommended considering surgical removal. Even in the presence of symptoms, a minority of CPGs recommended initially trialling conservative management for multiple magnets in the small intestine (online supplemental table 3).

Conservative management

Where conservative management was deemed appropriate, usually based on magnet location or absence of symptoms (figures2 3), some CPGs specified further conditions for continuation of conservative management. Most CPGs recommended serial X-rays to monitor magnet progression, with the timing of subsequent X-rays varying between 4-hourly to a single repeat AXR in 14 days. One CPG further advised that subsequent AXRs must be taken in the same position (ideally supine and anteroposterior), to aid interpretation.15 Five CPGs suggested patient admission and five advised prescribing laxatives to aid magnet progression (online supplemental table 4).

Endoscopic removal: indications and timing

Among the 19 CPGs which advised endoscopic removal, 12 provided no specific guidance regarding timing. Of those that did, the guidance varied (table 1). Most CPGs advised considering endoscopy based on magnet location or symptoms (figures2 3). However, two CPGs also advised endoscopy if there was no progression on serial AXRs, one if there were signs of obstruction on an AXR and one if there were no signs of small bowel obstruction or intestinal perforation on AXR.

Surgical removal: indications, timing and approach

The most common indications for surgical removal of magnets were a lack of progression or symptom development, with other criteria, such as specific imaging findings and time since ingestion noted in some CPGs (online supplemental table 5). 52% (13/25) gave recommendations on the surgical approach, with 54% (7/13) of these advising laparoscopy. One also suggested simultaneous endoscopy.16 Three mentioned either open or laparoscopic approaches, while another17 noted the risk of laparoscopic instruments being attracted to magnets. Three others recommended laparotomy in specific scenarios: a distended abdomen or critical illness,18 mapping laparotomy after 48 hours19 and exploratory laparotomy for postpyloric magnets.20

Discharge from the emergency department

36% (9/25) of CPGs provided discharge guidance, with 7 recommending discharge after a single magnet ingestion (online supplemental table 6). One recommended discharge if the ingestion was accidental, there was no risk of further magnet ingestion and the child was tolerating oral intake.21 One CPG stated that patients who swallowed multiple magnets could be discharged if they were asymptomatic, the magnets were postpyloric, endoscopic removal failed and progression was seen on serial AXRs.22

CPG quality

CPGs scored worst in Applicability, with a median score of 13%, followed by Stakeholder Involvement (17%), and Rigour of Development (24%). CPGs scored best for Editorial Independence, with a median score of 71%, followed by Scope and Purpose and Clarity of Presentation (both 61%) (figure 4). A strong correlation was observed between each raters’ scores for all CPGs (online supplemental table 7).

Discussion

This systematic review critically appraises the quality of CPGs for paediatric magnet ingestion. Most CPGs were based on small case series or expert opinion, often with limited case numbers, leading to variability in recommendations.

This review identified 25 CPGs for the management of paediatric magnet ingestion published between 2009 and 2023. Many were not informed by patient data, and fewer than half analysed more than 20 cases, highlighting the reliance on expert consensus. The resulting variability presents both challenges and opportunities: CPGs can improve patient outcomes by (1) incorporating evidence-based medicine into practice, (2) creating clear decision-making tools to enhance patient safety, (3) reducing over-investigation and overtreatment and thus also improving resource allocation, (4) reducing health inequalities by standardising care and (5) supporting shared decision-making by simplifying a complex clinical scenario. For clinicians, the lack of consensus on critical aspects of paediatric magnet ingestion—such as when to opt for conservative management versus invasive procedures—can complicate the balance between timely intervention and avoiding overtreatment.23 24 This hinders clinical decision-making by introducing uncertainty and leads to inconsistencies in care when identical cases are managed differently based on the guideline followed. Nonetheless, recognising these discrepancies highlights priorities for research and quality improvement.

Some consensus was evident: AXR is consistently recommended as the first-line imaging modality; conservative management is suggested for single asymptomatic magnets; endoscopic removal is often considered for multiple prepyloric asymptomatic magnets; and surgery for multiple symptomatic magnets. However, guidance diverged on single symptomatic and multiple asymptomatic cases, with both conservative and surgical management recommended in the same scenarios (figures2 3). Future research should clarify: (1) which patient groups can be discharged home with safety-net advice, (2) whether laxatives hasten the passage of ingested magnets, (3) evidence-based durations for trialling conservative management before intervention, (4) indications for endoscopic removal based on AXR magnet location(s) and (5) optimising timing of serial AXRs to balance radiation exposure and resource use, while either safely monitoring the magnets’ passage or conversely reliably identifying cases in which magnets have not progressed. Ideally, seeking resolution to these uncertainties would involve the use of patient data to ensure that subsequent iterations of CPGs are evidence-based where at all possible.

Quality appraisal of CPGs revealed consistently low scores for ‘Stakeholder Involvement’ (median 17%), highlighting the need for future guidelines to involve those who benefit from and use the CPGs, such as emergency physicians, endoscopists, paediatric surgeons, patients and their parents/carers. This would enhance their relevance and practicality, addressing issues including resource implications, implementation barriers and monitoring criteria, which were often overlooked. The low ‘Applicability’ score (median 13%) reflects these shortcomings, underscoring the need for multidisciplinary input and large multicentre studies or registries to improve guideline development for this rare clinical scenario.

Limitations of this review include bias due to possible omission of unpublished or grey literature guidelines, particularly hospital protocols and restriction to English-language publications.

This systematic review has identified significant variability in current clinical practice guidelines for the management of paediatric magnet ingestion. Most recommendations were based on expert consensus or small case series, with quality appraisal highlighting limitations in stakeholder involvement, applicability and methodological rigour. These findings demonstrate that current guidance lacks a robust evidence base and that future work should prioritise collaborative, consensus-driven, multicentre studies to resolve areas of uncertainty and support the development of more consistent, evidence-informed CPGs.
