Health benefits and value addition of bovine colostrum (BC): a comprehensive review of its nutritional and functional potential
Authors: Sawera Riaz, Ali Imran, Musarrat Rasheed, Aleeza Amjad, Amar Shankar, Gauri Chauhan, Zunaira Basharat, Fakhar Islam, Usman Naeem, Catherine Tamale Ndagire
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The present review tries to explore composition, processing technologies, bioactive components, and clinical applications of BC, laying especial emphasis on their nutritional and therapeutic potentials. BC is highly bioactive secretion enriched with macronutrients and a wide spectrum of bio-functional molecules such as immunoglobulin (IgG, IgA, and IgM), lactoferrin, growth factors (IGF-1, TGF-β), proline-rich polypeptides, cytokines, and bioactive peptides. These mutually act in immune modulation, gastrointestinal integrity, epithelial repair, and antimicrobial defense. Advanced processing technologies, such as freeze-drying, spray-drying, and low-temperature vacuum drying, are important for maintaining structural stability and ensuring the biological activity of heat-sensitive immunoglobulins, peptides, and probiotics that assure extended shelf life and industrial scalability. Recent knowledge in omics-based proteomics and transcriptomic has unraveled new components like microRNAs, osteopontin, milk fat globule membrane proteins, and lactoperoxidase, which have come to bear upon a critical role in the regulation of immune response, epithelial protection, and anti-inflammatory signaling. These molecular understandings of BC bring its functionality as an active therapeutic ingredient for clinical nutrition, sports recovery, and management of conditions where immune fitness is compromised. Clinical evidence has established the effectiveness of BC in soothing irritable bowel syndrome, ulcerative colitis, infectious diarrhea, metabolic dysfunction, regeneration of tissues, wound healing, and anti-aging properties. BC applications continue into functional foods, nutraceuticals, and pharmaceutical formulations, and currently, new and innovative encapsulation and delivery systems are being developed, which enhance its bioavailability and targeting activities. In this context, bovine colostrum is emerging as an attractive bio-functional ingredient for an expanding application in human health and with commercial viability, considering present consumers’ trends toward natural products with immune-enhancing and regenerative features.

Literature search and selection methodology

Literature search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was done based on the PRISMA guidelines to ensure that the synthesis is comprehensive and evidence-based. All scientific literatures related to the nutritional composition, bioactive components, health-promoting effects, and industrial value addition of BC were targeted in the search strategy. Major scientific databases, including PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, ScienceDirect, and Google Scholar, would be systematically searched for publications from 2000 to 2025. Complementary searches across all diverse subject areas would also be made in SpringerLink, Taylor & Francis Online, and Elsevier repositories to cover major scientific databases comprehensively. Searches for relevant materials were conducted using specific keywords in various combinations. The searches included a variety of relevant keywords and their combinations or use of Boolean operators, such as “bovine colostrum,” “colostral proteins,” “bioactive peptides,” “immunoglobulins,” “colostrum supplementation,” “nutritional value,” “functional food,” “immune modulation,” “athletic performance,” “gut health,” and “value addition.” Grey literature, including industry reports, conference proceedings, and doctoral theses, was also reviewed with a view to incorporating into the review emerging industrial trends and practical applications.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Pre-screening inclusion and exclusion criteria were established to ensure scientific stringency. Original data and comprehensive review studies on nutritional, biological, and therapeutic properties of bovine colostrum were included. In vitro, in vivo, and clinical studies investigating BC for its bioactive compounds with immunomodulatory properties, antioxidant properties, and functional food or nutraceutical applications were included. The language was restricted to English, and the studies published in peer-reviewed journals were included. In addition, studies that were exclusively focused on nonbovine colostrum (e.g., human, ovine, caprine), studies with incomplete methodological details, nonscientific reports, and those whose content was not related to the nutritional or functional potential of BC were excluded from the final analysis.

Selection and Short-listing procedure

The process of selection was performed in a methodical and transparent manner. All the citations retrieved were transferred to EndNote, version X9, which was useful in reference management and duplicate removal automatically. The screening was done in two stages: First, title and abstract screening to exclude non-relevant or non-scientific material; second, full-text assessment of the remaining articles was done in detail to confirm their eligibility against the study’s inclusions. Two independent reviewers were in charge of screening and selecting, since this would reduce the bias and make it more objective. In cases of disagreement after discussion, a consensus was reached, and another third reviewer was consulted where necessary.

Data extraction and synthesis

The relevant data was systematically extracted from included studies and organized in thematic categories. The information extracted ranged from the nutritional composition of BC to its bioactive and functional properties antimicrobial, immunomodulatory, antioxidant, gut-protective effects and technological and value-added applications: microencapsulation, spray-drying, pasteurization, incorporation into dairy or nutraceutical formulations.

Rationale for the application of bovine colostrum

Colostrum is the first secretion by mammals immediately after parturition, a nutrient-rich biofluid providing the neonate with very essential immunological and growth-supporting factors. However, although colostrum can be of human, goat, buffalo, or camel origin, bovine colostrum has received the most significant scientific, clinical, and commercial attention. Justification for the use of bovine colostrum particularly relies on its special composition profile, functional bioactivity, scalability, and documented compatibility with human physiology. Bovine colostrum is particularly rich in immunoglobulins, especially IgG, growth factors such as IGF-1 and TGF-β, a number of AMPs, cytokines, lactoferrin, lysozyme, oligosaccharides, and all essential vitamins and minerals. Because of this, the IgG concentration in BC was found to be nearly 100 times higher than in human colostrum, thus providing better passive immunity and enhanced antimicrobial defense. Moreover, many molecular structures of such bioactive components are highly conserved among species, which allows the bovine-derived molecules to effectively interact with human receptors and physiological systems. This cross-species bioactivity underlines the therapeutic utility of BC in view of immune modulation, gut integrity, and tissue repair (Yalçıntaş et al., 2024).

Among the sources of colostrum available, bovine colostrum is unique for a number of practical and ethical reasons. In addition, dairy cows produce, in the first days after birth, a considerable amount of colostrum that is renewable and well beyond the nutritional requirements of the calf. This surplus can be collected without any compromise in animal welfare to provide a sustainable and ethically acceptable source of bioactive material. On the other hand, human colostrum cannot be obtained in an ethically or logistically feasible way. The production of other animal colostrums is low, such as goat and camel, and without the intense compositional and clinical data supporting BC use. The other important reason for choosing bovine colostrum is that it has already been proven safe and able to be scaled. Moreover, according to the regulatory agencies in several countries, bovine colostrum has been recognized as a generally safe and natural nutraceutical ingredient (Silva et al., 2024). Transitionally, bovine colostrum represents a very specific link between nutrition and medicine, wherein relatively low doses of bioactive compounds are sufficient for nutrition supplementation in athletes, the elderly, immunocompromised patients, and individuals with gastrointestinal disorders. This combination of potent biological activity, abundant availability, and well-documented clinical efficacy makes BC the most practical and scientifically justified choice among all the types of colostrum. In general, there are different types of colostrum in different mammalian species. Bovine colostrum provides the greatest immunological potency, biochemical richness, large-scale availability, ethical sourcing, and verified safety profile. Therefore, it has solid scientific validation and is capable of being adapted to food and pharmaceutical industries, with which it reinforces its position as the preferred colostrum source for human health applications.

Composition

Protein

Bovine colostrum is a very rich source of high-order proteins, major contributors to its nutritional and therapeutic values. Colostrum contains a wide array of bioactive proteins such as immunoglobulins (IgG, IgA, IgM), lactoferrin, lactoperoxidase, α-lactalbumin, β-lactoglobulin, casein fractions, and several other growth factors like insulin-like growth factors (IGF-1 and IGF-2) and transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) (Silva et al., 2024). Immunoglobulins, contributing to about 70–80% of the total proteins in bovine colostrum, improve passive immunity, protect against microbial infections, and maintain gut mucosal defense. Furthermore, lactoferrin and lactoperoxidase have showcased their ample antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory properties, respectively, which would definitely help in the inhibition of pathogenic bacteria and modulation of oxidative stress. Whey proteins like α-lactalbumin and β-lactoglobulin have all the essential amino acids that contribute to muscle synthesis; thus, they play an important role in tissue repair mechanisms (Robbers et al., 2021). Caseins provide not only slow-releasing amino acids but also participate in mineral binding and nutrient absorption. Altogether, these protein components turn bovine colostrum into a functional bioactive matrix in immune health and intestinal integrity, enhancing recovery and growth, and make it a valuable ingredient in functional foods, sports nutrition, and clinical formulation (Yalçıntaş et al., 2024).

BC contains similar nutrients to mature bovine milk. The macronutrient profile and growth factor, immunoglobulins, and other immune factor content, however, changes dramatically. BC contains higher total protein content than mature milk, mainly because of its higher levels of immunoglobulins and casein. Total protein concentration in BC constitutes about 15% of day one BC (weight/weight) as shown in Table 1. The percentage gradually falls until it reaches approximately 3% - - less than that found in mature milk. Protein constituents can be divided into two categories: whey proteins (soluble protein components) and caseins (insoluble proteins). Both of these components offer nutritional and bioactive properties to one degree or another. In cow milk and cheese, casein is the predominant phosphoprotein that constitutes about 75% of all proteins. 1-Casein is the main protein fraction in bovine milk and cheese (Playford & Weiser, 2021). BC contain 100 times more bovine trypsin inhibitor than mature normal milk (Godden et al., 2019). Immunoglobulins, Lactoferrin, α-lactalbumin, β-lactoglobulin, lactoperoxidase, glyco-macro-peptide, and several growth factors, including the EGF-receptor ligand betacellulin are present as whey proteins (Robbers et al., 2021) (Table 2).

Fats and lipids

BC, or first milk, contains more fat and higher levels of palmitic, palmitoleic, and myristic acids than mature milk. These facts are ideal for developing calves but have been associated with long-term negative health consequences. It is controversial how much dairy fats contribute to heart disease. Evidence suggests that these fatty acids act as signaling molecules and control the lipogenesis process of the liver as dietary fats. The depletion of these lipids reduces the levels of certain fat-soluble vitamins, including vitamins D and A, in BC (Yu & Satyaraj, 2025). The ability to efficiently separate the fats from the aqueous fraction of milk would be of interest to the consumer, as this allows them to reduce or completely remove these fats from BC before downstream processing and ultimately production into low-fat or fat-free products made from BC. Approximately 7% of BC is fat. The most important part of this fat is milk fat whey globules. Fat is the main lipid composition of BC. It is useful to health because it contains elements like polyunsaturated fatty acids (ω−3, ω−6), short-chain fatty acids, and conjugated linoleic acid (CLA) a variety of different non-essential components are found in the lipid fraction, including gangliosides and phospholipids. The main fatty acids are palmitic and oleic acids they respectively account for 40% and 21% total (O’Callaghan et al., 2020). Immune defense, as we know it, is a common development of integration between innate and adaptive processes. Numerous constituents of BC can influence these two different paths. Lactoferrin

In human colostrum and BC in cattle, lactoferrin is an iron-binding glycoprotein of 80 kDa, but in the latter, its levels are 90% lower than those found at human concentrations. However, despite these differences between species, iron in BC more readily passes into breast-fed calves. Lactoferrin has many effects such as increasing iron-absorbing performance, bacteriostatic action, anti-endotoxin activity, immunity-improving ability through its effect on cytokines, and enhancing the growth of epithelial cells in both the gut and liver (Zhao et al., 2019). Immune-regulating potential microRNA elements are also available in BC. These components are in microvesicles that, at least at the passage through the gastrointestinal tract, are relatively stable; they can influence lymphoid cell function in the intestine (Van Hese et al., 2020).

Lactoferrin (LF), a versatile glycoprotein with approximately 80 kDa molecular mass and 700 amino acids, exhibits two alpha-helix-connected lobes (N and C) capable of binding various metal ions (Cu2+, Zn2+, Mn3+, and ferric ion). Bovine colostrum contains around 0.80 mg mL− 1 of lactoferrin, a cationic glycoprotein that binds iron. It is one of the main proteins in the milk serum of all mammals, which has a variety of uses, including antibacterial, antifungal, antiviral, antiparasitic, anticancer, and immunomodulatory (anti-inflammatory) properties. Lactoferrin obtained from bovine colostrum possess antibacterial activity by preventing the growth of bacteria, viruses, yeasts, and protozoa that cause disease. It also helps to preserve intestinal permeability and stability by preventing pathogens from adhering to epithelial cells. Furthermore, some research indicates that lactoferrin from bovine colostrum can boost the proliferation of cells called osteoblasts, which are involved in the production of bones, as well as the release of certain growth factors from these cells. Moreover, it has been established that it contributes to the intestinal absorption of iron and the stimulation of phagocytes and immunological responses. Intestinal tissue, monocytes, macrophages, neutrophils, lymphocytes, platelets, and some bacteria all express lactoferrin receptors. Supplements containing lactoferrin from cows are hypothesised to boost immunity and affect immune cell activity through several antioxidative properties. BC contains the highest proportion of this protein, four times more than mature milk. In addition to this, Parkinson’s disease originates from the death of dopaminergic cells, often induced by oxidative stress and glial dysfunction. Lactoferrin, a versatile glycoprotein present in brain regions, demonstrates a protective effect by reducing oxidative damage and initiating receptor-mediated transcytosis across the blood–brain barrier. This dual action suggests the potential use of BC-derived LF in transporting therapeutic agents for Parkinson’s treatment (Van Hese et al., 2020).

Lactoperoxidase

Bovine colostrum includes a substantial quantity of lactoperoxidase, a basic glycoprotein that catalyses the oxidation of thiocyanate to form intermediate compounds possessing antibacterial effects.102 In mature cow milk, lactoperoxidase concentrations vary from 13 to 30 mg L − 1, whereas in BC, it is between 11 and 45 mg L − 1.36 Three to five days after parturition, it reaches its highest concentration in BC, which is initially quite low. Additionally, bovine colostrum has stronger lactoperoxidase catalase activity than mature milk. Moreover, lactoperoxidase activity produces harmful oxidation products that inhibit bacterial metabolism by oxidizing proteins’ vital sulfhydryl group. Both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, including Streptococcus mutans, Salmonella typhimurium, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Listeria monocytogenes, and Staphylococcus aureus, were impacted. Additionally, the lactoperoxidase system inhibits the poliovirus, HIV, and vaccinia virus (Van Hese et al., 2020).

Health benefits of bovine colostrum

Immune system enhancement

Probably the biggest gain for the body is the strengthening of the immune system. Immunoglobulins in BC help build passive immunity among newborns. The antibodies contained in BC will neutralize bad pathogens, preventing infections that trigger respiratory and intestinal diseases. There are other components of BC as well, including proline-rich polypeptides (PRPs), which stimulate immune responses either by boosting or suppressing the immune system depending on what it needs. BC’s immune components exhibit potential for treating a range of diseases. While it seems unlikely that the bovine colostrum components are absorbed into human systemic circulation that conclusion no longer holds (Playford & Weiser, 2021). Gastrointestinal health and gut barrier function

BC provides great importance to gut maturation and protection. In contrast to casein, whey proteins are more soluble. In the stomach, they are quickly mixed with gastric juice and empty rapidly from the stomach into the small intestine where they can be digested and absorbed (Buttar et al., 2017).

Antimicrobial and antiviral properties

The antimicrobial properties of colustrum contribute to serving as a natural barrier to infection. The combined action of lactoferrin, lysozyme, and lactoperoxidase combat bacterial pathogens like Escherichia coli and Staphylococcus aureus. Role in metabolic health and growth promotion

BC is required for optimum development and metabolic function. Insulin-like growth factors, particularly IGFs in BC, enhance muscle protein synthesis and contribute to the development of lean body mass. These growth factors have regulatory effects on glucose metabolism by enhancing insulin sensitivity, therefore reducing the risk of disorders related to metabolic syndrome. Other amino acids, such as glutamine found in it, contribute toward the repair of tissues and promote muscle recovery, therefore BC is very beneficial for athletes and individuals undergoing physical rehabilitation. It helps protect against upper respiratory tract infections, gastrointestinal illnesses, and inflammation. The antiviral and antibacterial properties of BC, directed by compounds such as lactoferrin, are able not only to carry out effective elimination of pathogens but also prevention thereof. It can be used effectively for treating viral diseases like COVID-19 where in that instance both immune balance support and viral inactivation are provided (Duman & Karav, 2023). Industrial drying of Bovide colostrum

Freeze-drying of bovine colostrum

Freeze-drying, sometimes referred to as lyophilization, is among the best methods of preserving the structure and functionality of bovine colostrum components, such as immunoglobulins, bioactive peptides, and probiotic cultures. In this process, colostrum is frozen at subzero temperatures, followed by removal of water by sublimation under reduced pressure. Unlike other drying techniques that depend on heat, freeze-drying is under low thermal tension and, therefore, exposes the thermolabile bioactive compounds to minimal denaturation. One of the most important advantages of freeze-drying BC is that the process preserves the very labile immunoglobulins, IgG, IgA, and IgM, sensitive to heat and pH changes; these are highly important in giving passive immunity against GI infections, especially when BC is used for infant formulae or functional supplements (Silva et al., 2024). It has been estimated that freeze-dried BC retains more than 85–95% of IgG activity from the original, but high-temperature drying methods such as spray-drying may cause partial degradation. The absence of liquid water during sublimation avoids hydrolytic reactions, while low processing temperatures minimize unfolding and aggregation of protein molecules. Besides immunoglobulins, bioactive peptides and growth factors, lactoferrin, insulin-like growth factors (IGF-1 and IGF-2), and transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) retain their biological activities after freeze-drying (Serra et al., 2023). These components have significant activities in cellular growth, immune modulation, and antioxidant defense, and their stability is crucial regarding the therapeutic value of nutraceuticals from BC. The soft nature of lyophilization means that the peptide structure and disulfide bonds remain intact, preserving their binding affinity and biological activity. The process creates a dry, porous matrix that embeds the probiotic cells within a protective environment against both oxidative and osmotic stress during storage. It was evident that more than 80–90% survival rates after freeze-drying may be achieved by adding cryoprotectants such as trehalose, skim milk powder, or maltodextrin to ensure functional delivery in colostrum-based health supplements. Further, freeze-dried powder is highly soluble, with great stability and good reconstitution properties that make it ideal for use in formulations such as infant formula, medical nutrition products, and sports supplements. Translation of the method’s ability to keep proteins and peptides in their native conformation means greater bioavailability and efficacy upon rehydration. While relatively time and energy-intensive, the gold standard of freeze-drying preserves biochemical integrity and bioactivity of functional constituents in colostrum unmatched by other methods for long-shelf-life, high-value colostrum powders with minimal nutrient losses (Yalçıntaş et al., 2024).

Spraying-drying and its optimization parameters

Among the various techniques, spray-drying represents the most commonly used method for large-scale production of bovine colostrum powder because of its lower cost, high scalability, and efficient process. In this technique, liquid colostrum is atomized into fine droplets and subjected to a stream of hot air, followed by rapid water evaporation and the generation of dry particles. Even though it is a very common process, the major problem associated with spray-drying is the need to balance the efficiency of drying against the loss of thermosensitive bioactives like immunoglobulin, enzymes, and probiotics. Temperature control is usually a critical optimization parameter in this respect (Silva et al., 2024). This has generally been done within inlet air temperatures between 140 °C and 180 °C and outlet temperatures between 70 °C and 90 °C, depending, again, on feed composition and required powder characteristics. The technique of microencapsulation does not only protect bioactives from oxidation and moisture, but it also allows self-controlled release and better dispersibility in reconstituted products. Advanced modifications in spray-drying developments have been developed to improve powder flowability, reduce stickiness, and enhance particle stability, such as multi-stage drying systems and fluidized bed agglomeration.

In addition, spray-dried colostrum powder microstructure, which involves particle size, porosity, and surface morphology, depends on some parameters: feed solid concentration, atomization pressure, and airflow rate. These factors, once optimized, will guarantee a well-shaped and uniform spherical particle with good solubility and poor hygroscopicity. An appropriate relative humidity and correct air velocity during the drying process will avoid caking and nutrient oxidation during storage. From an industrial point of view, spray-drying represents a high-throughput, continuous operation suitable for large-scale colostrum processing. If properly optimized, the process can retain up to 70–80% of immunoglobulin activity, while microbial load significantly reduces and extends shelf life. While freeze-drying retains bioactivity at higher levels, spray-drying, from an economic, adaptability, and compatibility viewpoint with encapsulation systems, remains the method of choice for commercial purposes (Yalçıntaş et al., 2024).

Perspective of bovine colostrum MicroRNAs of Bovida colostrum: immune modulation and protection of the epithelium

Recent data revealed the presence of miRNAs in bovine colostrum and positioned them as one of the most important epigenetic regulators contributing to their immunomodulatory and development-enhancing roles. Encapsulated within EVs, such as exosomes, these small, non-coding RNA molecules are highly stable and could resist enzymatic degradation, which enables functional transfer into recipient cells within the gastrointestinal tract. Colostral miRNAs may affect the gene expression of immune signaling, epithelium protection, and inflammatory modulation upon ingestion. For example, miR-148a and miR-21 modulate T-cell differentiation, macrophage activation, and cytokine balance toward immune tolerance and suppression of inflammatory responses. Besides, miRNAs in bovine colostrum have been implicated in improvement of intestinal barrier integrity due to the regulation of tight junction proteins and epithelial cell proliferation. Lately, there is a growing body of evidence that these miRNAs may cross species barriers and contribute to host immune programming and gut homeostasis. Taken together, the presence of miRNAs in bovine colostrum adds a new gene-regulatory dimension to its bioactivity and opens perspectives beyond traditional therapeutic protein and peptide mechanisms (Yalçıntaş et al., 2024).

Bioactive peptides and minor proteins

Unraveling the Functional Landscape In addition to the major immunoglobulins and lactoferrin, bovine colostrum is rich in bioactive peptides and minor proteins that play an important role in growth, immunity, and protection of tissues. These include several recently identified proteomic analyses: among these are osteopontin, milk fat globule membrane proteins, and lactoperoxidase, each expressing different physiological functions. Osteopontin is a phosphorylated glycoprotein showing anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory capacities in modulating macrophage activity, cytokine release, and cell adhesion mechanisms (Silva et al., 2024). It also favors neonatal intestinal maturation and has been implicated in bone remodeling and tissue repair. Antiviral, antibacterial, and neurodevelopmental properties have been exerted by MFGM proteins such as butyrophilin, xanthine oxidose, and mucin-1, among the structural and functional components. These proteins are important in the formation of stable emulsions and may also function as biological barriers against pathogens. One of the best-characterized antimicrobial enzymes is lactoperoxidase, which catalyzes the oxidation of thiocyanate ions to yield reactive intermediates that can theoretically prevent bacterial growth. Besides that, the proteolytic processing of casein and whey proteins during digestion results in the formation of bioactive peptides with antihypertensive, antioxidant, and immunostimulatory properties that extend the physiological effect of colostrum beyond nutrition alone (Yalçıntaş et al., 2024).

Integration of omics-based findings: new perspectives in colostrum research

The use of omics technologies, including proteomics, metabolomics, and transcriptomics, has really revolutionized the complex bioactivity of bovine colostrum. Utilizing proteomic profiling, more than one thousand different proteins and peptides have been identified from bovine colostrum, which determines low-abundance bioactives implicated in cell signaling, regulation of oxidative stress, and mucosal immunity (Silva et al., 2024). Metabolomic analyses have pointed out some active metabolites, such as short-chain fatty acids, amino acid derivatives, and oligosaccharides, which are highly implicated in gut microbial balance and energy metabolism. Finally, transcriptomic studies have demonstrated the coordinated expression of immune-related genes influenced by colostral components and reinforce the hypothesis that bovine colostrum acts like a molecular blueprint for early immune system programming. These omics-enabled strategies further allow the identification of novel therapeutic candidates, including peptide fragments possessing antiviral activity against respiratory and enteric pathogens, and miRNA signatures that may be used as biomarkers of immune resilience. All these together help bridge nutritional science with precision health and place bovine colostrum as a biofunctional matrix with multi-dimensional benefits into immunity, metabolism, and epithelial health (Yalçıntaş et al., 2024).

Clinical uses of colostrum from bovines

Exercise performance and body composition

In 1997, the first research examining the impact of BC supplementation on exercise performance was finished. It revealed significant increases in immunoglobulin concentration in serum and explosive muscular power (Mero et al., 1997). Given that vigorous exercise might impair immunity for several hours following training, this discovery is pertinent (Shing et al., 2009). Lean body mass and weightlifting performance, athletic performance in male and female athletes, speed in elite cyclists with dose-dependent effects, and recovery in runners have all been shown to significantly improve in subsequent, well-controlled studies when compared to whey protein concentrate. In contrast to whey protein complex supplementation, (Duff et al., 2014) found that bovine colostrum supplementation (60 g/d of colostrum) during resistance training is advantageous for enhancing leg press strength and decreasing bone resorption in both male and female older individuals. Both the colostrum-supplemented group and the whey protein-treated group showed improvements in upper body strength, muscular thickness, lean tissue mass, and cognitive function (Duff et al., 2014). The precise mechanism behind these notable advancements remains unclear despite this progress. Observed variations are unlikely to be a result of protein digestibility or amino acid supplementation alone, as human studies usually employ whey protein with a similar protein concentration. The immune system may be directly impacted by bioactive substances and/or their metabolites, as bovine colostrum immunological components are probably not giving humans passive immunity (Shing et al., 2009). The idea that supplementing with bovine colostrum might enhance leukocyte activity in relation to adaptive immunity is presently not well supported (Shing et al., 2009). Despite reports of a 79% rise in IgA in runners given 12 g/day for 12 weeks and a 33% increase in saliva IgA following supplementation of BC at 20 g/day for 2 weeks, these findings were not replicated in other research. Additionally, in vitro culture models of intestinal epithelial cells demonstrated that BC supplementation reduced exercise-induced intestinal permeability (Marchbank et al., 2011). Further research is necessary to understand the underlying mechanism and explain inter-individual variations and unexplored discrepancies between the increasing number of studies on BC supplementation in relation to body composition and athletic performance, given the safety profile and generally positive prior research from well-controlled studies.

GI inflammation and permeability caused by NSAIDs

In order to address the symptoms of acute pain, chronic inflammation, and degenerative joint illnesses, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory medications (NSAIDs) are the most often recommended medication (Allison et al., 1992; Henry & McGettigan, 2003). NSAIDs have been linked to intestinal and stomach problems, including peptic ulcers and damage to the small and large intestines. NSAID side effects include stricture development, increased intestinal permeability, and blood and protein loss (Allison et al., 1992; Sigthorsson et al., 1998). Bleeding, perforation, and inflammation are among the negative GI tract symptoms that affect about 2% of NSAID users. While acid suppressants and prostaglandin analogs are used to lessen the damage that NSAIDs cause to the stomach, they are insufficiently successful in avoiding damage to the small intestine. Therefore, further ways to lessen these adverse side effects are required. Because BC contains growth factors such α-IGF-1, β-IGF-1, transforming growth factor (TGF), and epidermal growth factors (EGF), some study indicates that it might be an option. Together with evidence of decreased GI permeability linked to exercise (Marchbank et al., 2011), these growth factors have the capacity to promote the GI tract’s healing process (Playford et al., 2000). A clinical study evaluating the potential of bovine colostrum to change intestinal permeability with concurrent indomethacin supplementation was conducted on seven male volunteers (ages 26 to 38) who were taking NSAIDs or who had conditions that could affect intestinal permeability (such as celiac disease or prior intestinal surgery). After a baseline permeability test, these subjects in this crossover trial were given 125 mL of bovine colostrum or a whey placebo orally three times a day for seven days. Intestinal permeability was reevaluated at the conclusion of the trial period, and a 2-week “washout” interval was conducted in between the crossover. When indomethacin and whey placebo were taken together, permeability increased almost three times, but when bovine colostrum and indomethacin were taken together, there was no discernible increase in permeability (Playford et al., 2001). Documentation of the molecular characterisation of the mechanism causing these effects supports these conclusions. The effectiveness of NSAIDs may not be impacted by co-administration of a lactoferrin-containing protein source, such as bovine colostrum, as Mir et al. (Mir et al., 2009) showed that bovine lactoferrin can function as a carrier for NSAIDs by binding to these molecules, albeit with a much lower affinity than the protein targets for NSAIDs. Although further research is needed to show that these substances retain their intended effectiveness when taken in combination with bovine colostrum, there is mounting and steady evidence that bovine colostrum may be used to help control the possible negative effects of NSAIDs.

Bovine colostrum’s applications in particular clinical populations

Because of its potential to promote GI integrity and health, bovine colostrum has sparked human supplementation research. Numerous gastrointestinal disorders linked to either acute or chronic infections have been studied to see if bovine colostrum might reduce the symptoms of these disorders or infections. Though not all conditions show promising treatment avenues, there are recurring themes related to the improvement of GI symptoms and decreased inflammation associated with each, making it challenging to separate the mechanisms underlying these findings given the diverse populations and disease etiologies.

Colitis ulcerative

In a modest proof-of-concept experiment, ulcerative colitis an inflammatory bowel illness linked to colon ulcers and persistent inflammation (Klotz, 2000) was examined as a possible target for a bovine colostrum enema therapy. Given the high levels of immunoglobulins, growth factors, and antimicrobial peptides present in BC, the authors justified this strategy (Khan et al., 2002). Fourteen individuals with active mild to moderate colitis were compared in this pilot trial. Six patients got an albumin placebo twice daily for four weeks, whereas eight patients received 100 mL (10%) solution of bovine colostrum. Seven out of eight patients in the bovine colostrum-treated groups reported improvements in the symptom score, which included patient well-being, abdominal pain, rectal bleeding, temperature, anorexia/nausea, bowel frequency, stool consistency, abdominal tenderness, and the presence of extra-intestinal manifestations (Khan et al., 2002). Despite the limited study size, the results indicate a substantial decrease in symptom ratings, suggesting the need for bigger sample size follow-up research in a comparable population.

Enterocolitis with necrotization

One of the leading causes of death for prematurely born children and one of the most prevalent morbidities linked to preterm delivery is necrotizing enterocolisis (NEC) (Niño et al., 2016). The effects of either human or bovine-derived colostrum on preterm baby growth and NEC outcomes have been the subject of several research. In one clinical study, 86 low birthweight babies were given bovine colostrum supplements at doses of 2 g four times a day for babies weighing 1,000–1,500 g and 1.2 g four times a day for babies weighing less than 1,000 g at birth. When bovine colostrum was administered instead of a placebo, no appreciable changes in the incidence of NEC, sepsis, or death were noted (Balachandran et al., 2017). The cumulative results in the literature indicate that neither bovine nor human colostrum had an impact on the incidence of severe NEC, mortality, culture-proven sepsis, feed intolerance, or length, according to Sadeghirad et al.‘s meta-analysis of the use of colostrum in preterm infants (Sadeghirad et al., 2018). Due to certain restrictions, such as the use of commercial bovine colostrum supplements and the small patient population, NEC patients show no effects (Playford et al., 2001; Mir et al., 2009). It will be challenging to defend the ongoing use of human or bovine colostrum with preterm newborns in the hopes of improving these outcomes in light of these findings. These results should not, however, be used to limit the use of human colostrum in preterm newborns because additional advantages have been shown for these babies (Snyder et al., 2017).

Diarrhea in travelers

One of the most frequent causes of “traveler’s diarrhea,” which is linked to travel to tropical and semitropical areas worldwide, is an acute infection with enterotoxigenic Escherichia coli (ETEC) (de la Cabada Bauche & DuPont, 2011). Given that ETEC is one of the main causes of neonatal calf diarrhea and that bovine colostrum is essential for passive immunity in defending the newborn calf against environmental pathogens (Kolenda et al., 2015), researchers have been curious to see if the same benefits can be shown in people who are susceptible to traveler’s diarrhea. In a double-blind, placebo-controlled ETEC challenge study, the effectiveness and dose response of taking 400 mg of bovine colostrum protein as a tablet showed a dose-dependent and significant improvement in preventing the development of diarrhea among volunteers. Hyperimmune bovine colostrum is rich in immunoglobulins that target 14 strains of ETEC. In comparison to the placebo, 90.9% protection was provided by a 400 mg dosage of hyperimmune bovine colostrum protein given three times a day with a bicarbonate buffer (Otto et al., 2011). In the ETEC challenge trials, bicarbonate buffer enhances the protective effects of hyperimmune colostrum protein; nevertheless, the difference was not statistically significant. Compared to the placebo group, an estimated 58.3% protection against diarrhea symptoms was provided by as low as 200 mg taken three times a day without buffer (Otto et al., 2011). Apart from ETEC, a considerable percentage of traveler’s diarrhea and newborn calf diarrhea are caused by viruses (Kolenda et al., 2015; de la Cabada Bauche & DuPont, 2011). Consuming three daily servings of 100 mL of hyperimmune bovine colostrum targeting human rotavirus for three days resulted in a modest but significant reduction in the duration of diarrhea and the total amount of stool produced among male infants aged 6–24 months, according to a double-blinded, placebo-controlled study by Mitra et al. (Mitra et al., 1995). These results are corroborated by another investigation that found that isolated immunoglobulins from hyperimmune cow colostrum had a comparable impact in acute rotavirus infection (Sarker et al., 1998). A clinical trial comparing hyperimmune and bovine colostrum in children with shigellosis caused by Shigella dysenteriae (S. dysenteriae) did not find any improvements among patients compared to concurrent antibiotic therapy, even though neither study looked at the effects of BC from cows that had not been immunized against the target pathogen. However, hyperimmune bovine colostrum has shown promise in reducing diarrhea caused by Clostridium difficile (C. difficile) in preclinical investigations in various biological models (e.g., gnotobiotic pigs). While S. dysenteriae invades epithelial cells (Schroeder & Hilbi, 2008), possibly avoiding hyperimmune bovine colostrum immunoglobulins, and ETEC and C. difficile use secreted toxins to cause epithelial damage (Mirhoseini et al., 2018; Kachrimanidou & Malisiovas, 2011) these findings collectively may indicate that infectious mechanisms of pathogenesis shape the ability of hyperimmune bovine colostrum to influence disease progression. Safety and possible risks of bovine colostrum

While bovine colostrum or BC is highly valued both for its nutritional and functional health benefits, careful considerations about its safety and possible risks use it in concentrated or supplement forms. Generally, BC is considered safe for healthy individuals; however, source quality, processing methods, dosage, and individual health may affect the safety profile of BC.

1. Allergenic Potential and Lactose Intolerance Bovine colostrum is rich in milk proteins like casein and β-lactoglobulin, which can also elicit allergic reactions in sensitive people against dairy products. Symptoms might include skin rashes, gastrointestinal problems, or respiration issues. Furthermore, lactose intolerance could be experienced as symptoms of bloating, diarrhea, or stomach ache if unprocessed or nonhydrolyzed colostrum preparations are ingested, as colostrum naturally contains lactose. Though some kind of advanced processing tries to break down lactose or denature allergic proteins, those with milk allergies should be cautious or take physician advice before use (Jensen et al., 2022).

2. Microbial Infection and Sanitary Issues The microbial safety of bovine colostrum basically comes from hygienic handling in collection, storage, and processing. Fresh colostrum is highly perishable and contaminates easily with pathogenic microorganisms such as Escherichia coli, Salmonella spp., and Listeria monocytogenes, particularly if sourced from non-pasteurized or poorly managed dairy operations. Thus, pasteurization or appropriate heat treatment is an important step that will ensure microbial safety without affecting the integrity of bioactive compounds. For this purpose, modern preservation methods of freeze-drying and spray-drying at controlled temperature are preferred for minimizing microbial load and extending shelf life (Ntuli et al., 2023).

3. Bioactive Content Variation and Standardization of Products One of the major safety issues is the large variability of different bioactive compounds, including immunoglobulins, lactoferrin, and growth factors, between colostrum batches. Bioactive concentrations are influenced by factors such as cow breed, diet, parity, and time of collection post-parturition. This may lead to inconsistencies in potency and the delivery of different efficacies for commercial products. Furthermore, without standardized quality control systems in some markets, this could raise concerns about product labeling, adulteration, and unverified health claims. Generally, few regions strictly regulate colostrum products, which makes it necessary to indicate the use of GMP and test the standardization of bioactive composition (Ramani et al., 2024).

4. Immunological and Hormonal Considerations Bovine colostrum is rich in growth factors such as IGF-1 and TGF-β that support tissue growth and the repair of most tissues. However, excessive intake of concentrated colostrum supplements may be associated with abnormal stimulation of growth pathways, raising theoretical long-term metabolic or endocrine consequences in certain cases, particularly in individuals with hormone-sensitive conditions or metabolic disorders. While current evidence does not indicate significant adverse hormonal outcomes at standard doses, supplementation should be supported with further long-term safety trials (Yalçıntaş et al., 2024).

5. Xenoreactive Immune Responses While bovine immunoglobulins are a source of passive immunity, they represent a specific species and may not directly integrate into the host human immune system. In rare cases, high consumption of bovine-derived immunoglobulins might cause immune sensitization or interfere with indigenous immune responses. This particularly relates to people with immunodeficiency or those undergoing immunotherapy and should be preliminarily discussed with healthcare professionals before supplementation (Tables 4 and 5) (Mukherjee et al., 2023).

Conclusion

BC appears to be an excellent bovine colostrum with multi-potential bioactive that supports immune health, gut integrity, and tissue regeneration. Poor in immunoglobulins, growth factors, and antimicrobial peptides, the established benefits in gastrointestinal disorders, inflammation, and metabolic dysfunctions have already been proved. Safety and therapeutic efficacy are ensured by available evidence, although supplementation of more standardized clinical trials is required for optimization of dosages and validation of long-term effects.

BC’s growing role in functional foods, nutraceuticals, and pharmaceuticals underlines its importance as a natural immunomodulant ingredient. Overcoming the challenges of quality control, sustainable sourcing, and processing standardization will be important in its large-scale application. With continuous research and technological developments, bovine colostrum will be one of the staples of future functional nutrition and therapeutic development.
