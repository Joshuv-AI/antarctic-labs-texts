Effective and sustainable methylene blue removal from wastewater using palm kernel cake
Authors: Reddy Prasad DM, Wan Syafera Haizul Akma, Duduku Krishnaiah, C Jayakumar, Nabisab Mujawar Mubarak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis research evaluates unmodified palm kernel cake (PKC), an agricultural by-product of palm oil processing as an inexpensive and sustainable adsorbent in the removal of methylene blue (MB) in aqueous solutions. Experiments on batch adsorption were carried out at 298–313 K, 5–20 mg/L of initial MB concentration, and 2–8 pH. The highest removal of 81% was obtained at pH 8 within 90–120 min. The Langmuir isotherm (R2 = 0.982) provided the best fit, yielding a theoretical maximum monolayer capacity (qm) of 151.5 mg/g; the experimentally measured adsorption capacity (qe,exp) was 4.8 mg/g under the tested conditions. Pseudo-second-order kinetics (R2 = 0.999) best described the adsorption rate, indicating intraparticle diffusion controlled physical adsorption confirmed by an activation energy of 37.8 kJ/mol. Thermodynamic analysis revealed a spontaneous (ΔG° = − 3.98 to − 4.25 kJ/mol), endothermic (ΔH° = 1.47 kJ/mol) process. The adsorption process is mainly physical, which depends on the electrostatic attraction, hydrogen bonding, π–π stacking, and intraparticle diffusion as shown in the Weber-Morris, Boyd, FTIR, and SEM analyses. PKC maintained a high adsorption efficiency of 96.5% even after four regeneration cycles, which showed great reusability and high potential of sustainable treatment of textile wastewater.Keywords: Methylene blue, Kinetics, Isotherm, Regeneration, Adsorption, PKC, MorphologySubject terms: Environmental sciences, Chemical engineering

Introduction

The textile industry uses dyes to colour fabrics, and the hot wastewater (50–60 °C) containing these dyes is often dumped into water sources. This temperature range is critical as it governs dye adsorption efficiency and the sorption capacity of cleaning materials in practical applications. As noted in previous studies1,2, highly coloured effluents discharged from textile industries pose significant ecological threats when released into aquatic environments. Such effluents are highly toxic to fish and other aquatic organisms because they contain several organic materials and toxic chemicals3. Unfortunately, very small amounts of dye can be easily seen in water, and this is the case of synthetic dyes, many of which are carcinogenic and mutagenic which can greatly harm aquatic life and food chains4. Consuming MB can quickly lead to serious health problems: fast heart rate, vomiting, shock, blood abnormalities, blue or yellow skin, paralysis, and tissue death5.

Inhalation of MB can cause episodes of rapid or laboured breathing, while ingestion results in a burning sensation, nausea, vomiting, and gastritis6,7 Also, dyes are the cause of skin irritation and may lead to allergic dermatitis1. Therefore, removal of colour from effluents is therefore critical to mitigate their adverse impact on aquatic life.

The different types of industries today produce a higher volume of industrial effluent, which necessitates the development of proper and inexpensive ways of treating wastewater prior to discharge8. Some of the most widely applied techniques for the treatment of wastewater containing dyes and other organic contaminants are adsorption, chemical oxidation, flocculation-coagulation, and biodegradation6,9. For removing dyes, adsorption is often preferred because it is simple, readily available, effective at removing color, inexpensive, and can handle strong dye solutions10,11.

Though activated carbon is effective in water cleaning, its high cost and challenging regeneration process necessitate the exploration of more accessible adsorbent alternatives12–14. Consequently, many other adsorbent materials have been investigated in view of their effectiveness in the removal of dyes in effluents15,16. For dye removal, researchers have investigated the use of agricultural by-products and natural materials such as wheat straw, bamboo, cashew nutshells, coconut fibers, and even sludge from sugar production17–19. However, some of these adsorbents exhibit low dye adsorption capacity, hence the need to search for better adsorbents.

The primary novelty of this manuscript lies as follows: (1) Palm Kernel Cake (PKC) is an underexplored, abundantly available agricultural by-product from Southeast Asian palm oil mills that has received limited systematic attention as an MB adsorbent. (2) Unlike most comparative studies that employ chemically or thermally activated materials, this study demonstrates that raw, unmodified PKC achieves a remarkably high adsorption capacity of 151.5 mg/g, which is competitive with many modified biosorbents. (3) The comprehensive diffusion mechanism analysis combining Weber-Morris, Boyd, and Arrhenius models provides mechanistic insight into particle-diffusion control that is rarely reported for palm-based unmodified adsorbents. This work thus contributes evidence that agricultural waste valorization without energy-intensive activation is a viable sustainable pathway for textile wastewater remediation.

Materials and methods

Palm Kernel Cake (PKC) was supplied from oil palm mill in Felda Kemahang, Kelantan, Malaysia and used as adsorbent without any treatment20. In this study, palm kernel cake was used in its natural form, without applying any chemical or physical modifications. MB was chosen as the substance to be adsorbed, and all solutions were prepared using distilled water, following the method outlined by9. This approach allowed the adsorption capabilities of PKC to be explored in a simple manner.

Preparation of MB solutions

Methylene blue (MW = 373.9 g/mol; C.I. No. 52015) was used as the model dye. and identification as C.I. No. 52015. Distilled water was used to dissolve MB for preparing its stock solution. Different concentrations of MB solutions were prepared by diluting the stock solution.

Preparation and characterization of PKC adsorbent

The palm kernel cake (PKC) used in this study was sourced from the Felda Kemahang Palm Oil Mill in Kelantan, Malaysia. Three kilograms of dry PKC was collected for research. The material underwent an 8 h solid–liquid extraction process using hexane, followed by drying in an oven maintained at 60 °C for 12 h. The treated PKC was sealed with airtight polymer bags until further use.

After drying, the PKC samples were sieved using a BUHLER MLU 300 to produce powder particles between 0.6 and 0.71 mm in size. It was sealed in a container at room temperature for further experimental purposes. Adsorption tests were conducted before applying any chemical or physical modification to the material. The PKC samples were analysed for structural features and surface area specifics using SEM equipment5.

Batch kinetic studies

To test the dye-cleaning ability of palm kernel cake (PKC), experiments were conducted. Varying amounts of PKC, specifically 1, 2, 3, and 4 g, were added to flasks containing 200 mL of MB solutions. Dye solutions were made at 5, 10, 15, and 20 ppm. The pH of the solutions was adjusted to 2, 4, 6, and 8 using dilute HCl and NaOH solutions, and measured with a pH meter (Eutech Instruments, pH 700).

The flasks were sealed and placed in a rotary shaker, gently shaken at 120 rpm at three different temperatures 298, 303, and 313 K. Once the adsorption process reached equilibrium, i.e., when the dye concentration in solution remained constant, the remaining MB in the solutions was measured using a double-beam UV/Vis spectrophotometer (UNICO SQ-4802) at a wavelength of 668 nm. The amount of MB left was then determined by comparing the instrument’s readings to a pre-established calibration curve. Regeneration studies

Regeneration experiments were carried out to assess the reusability of the PKC adsorbent. After each adsorption cycle, the methylene blue (MB)-loaded PKC was separated by filtration and subjected to desorption using 0.1 N HCl and 0.1 M NaOH as eluents. The desorption process involved 2 h of contact with continuous agitation at 120 rpm, followed by solid–liquid separation through filtration, washing with distilled water until neutral pH, and oven drying at 60 °C for 12 h. The regenerated PKC was then reused for subsequent adsorption cycles. A total of four adsorption–desorption cycles were performed, and the desorption efficiency was calculated as (amount desorbed/amount adsorbed) × 100%. The regeneration efficiency was evaluated by comparing the adsorption capacity of regenerated PKC with that of fresh PKC.

Effect of initial concentration, contact period, and solution pH on MB adsorption

In all the experiments, about 81% of the MB dye was eliminated in the first 90 to 120 min. This can be explained by the rapid adsorption because the palm kernel cake (PKC) has a porous structure (more holes) and hence, it has a high surface area, active sites (where the dye might reside), and a host of functional groups, which provide easy accessibility to the cations (positively charged particles). The equilibrium adsorption capacity (qe,exp), or the total amount of dye the PKC could absorb, increased with higher initial MB concentrations. Higher dye levels in the water at the beginning result in more dye being absorbed by the PKC because generating a larger concentration gradient as the driving force for adsorption.

When looking at all the starting amounts of MB, the equilibrium adsorption (qe,exp) was seen to be the lowest at a pH of 2, increased at pH 4, and reached its highest point at pH 8. This pattern occurs because the surface of the palm kernel cake (PKC) develops more negatively charged areas as the pH gets higher. At pH 2, the PKC surface becomes less negatively charged due to the presence of hydronium ions. This reduces the attraction of the positively charged MB cations and creates repulsive forces, making it harder for the dye cations to bind to the PKC.

Adsorption isotherms

At a steady adsorption point (equilibrium), adsorption isotherm demonstrates the distribution of MB molecules in the liquid and the palm kernel cake (PKC). This data is used to compare them with various mathematical models to come up with the most appropriate model to be used in the real world, as was the case with Hameed et al. in 20073.

The understanding of the interactions between the substance under absorption (adsorbate) and the substance performing the absorption (adsorbent) is important in correctly predicting the process of adsorption, as suggested by Bulut and Aydin23. The analysis of the experimental data of adsorption models allows the investigator to realize the process of sorption, the surface properties of the adsorbent, and its ability to attract the adsorbate. Various mathematical equations are available to help match experimental results with theoretical expectations at the point of adsorption equilibrium.

The adsorption phenomena were analysed using Langmuir, Freundlich, and Temkin isotherm models because each model delivers unique information. Under the Langmuir model, adsorbate molecules establish a single flat layer that attaches to fixed adsorption points distributed evenly over a surface area at uniform locations. The Freundlich model acknowledges multiple surface energy levels because adsorption energy changes due to increased surface area. The Temkin model assumes that the heat of adsorption decreases linearly with surface coverage, owing to surface heterogeneity24.

The R2 correlation coefficients are computed to evaluate the degree to which these isotherm models fit the experimental data3. The exponential distribution of adsorption sites frequently observed on non-uniform surfaces can be empirically explained by the Freundlich isotherm with Eq. (7).

Ce, expressed in (mg/L), represents the dye concentration at equilibrium state. The amount of dye that the PKC retained at this stable point is indicated as qe is in (mg/g). The adsorption is described by the Freundlich model, which depends on the constants of Kf and n. The PKC’s ability to hold dye is indicated by Kf, while the dye-PKC attraction is indicated bn. As shown in Fig. 1, these constants, Kf and 1/n, can be graphically obtained by plotting the logarithm of qe against the logarithm of Ce.

This lets one assess the relevance of every model in the framework of this adsorption study. The Langmuir isotherm implies a complicated adsorption mechanism that produces a single layer of MB on the palm kernel cake surface. The Langmuir maximum adsorption capacity (qm) of PKC, representing the total quantity of dye retained per unit mass of adsorbent, increased progressively with rising solution pH. Consistently below 1, the RL values, which reflect how favourable the adsorption is, fell with higher initial MB concentrations. This suggests that the PKC efficiently absorbed MB over a broad spectrum of pH levels and dye concentrations.

The Langmuir model often fits adsorption data better than the Temkin model when adsorption occurs on a homogeneous surface with identical sites and results in monolayer coverage. This is because Langmuir assumes uniform energies of adsorption and no interaction between adsorbed molecules, aligning well with single-layer adsorption processes. Therefore, Langmuir provides more accurate predictions and a physical basis for parameters under such conditions26.

It should be noted that this value represents the model-derived saturation capacity, whereas the actual experimentally measured equilibrium adsorption capacity (qe, exp) was 4.8 mg/g, corresponding to the initial MB concentration range of 5–20 mg/L employed in this study. Comparison of the adsorption capabilities of various adsorbents suggests that PKC stands out as one of the most efficient solid wastes from palm oil mills for MB adsorption.

Table 3 shows the numbers that describe how quickly the dye was absorbed, using two different models: pseudo-first order and second order. It also includes the calculated amount of dye absorbed at equilibrium (qe, cal) and how well the models fit the data (R2). The pseudo-second order model fit the data extremely well, with R2 values close to 1 (R2 ≥ 0.999). While often associated with chemisorption, this model can also describe physical adsorption processes involving multiple interaction sites or heterogeneous surfaces. The high R2 value indicates that the rate-limiting step follows second-order kinetics, which could involve physical interactions such as electrostatic attraction between negatively charged PKC surface groups and positively charged MB molecules. On the other hand, the pseudo-first order model didn’t fit the data as well, with R2 values much lower (R2 ≤ 0.773).

The pseudo-second order (PSO) kinetic model can predict adsorption data more successfully than the pseudo-first-order model because of the assumptions. PSO model assumes that the rate of adsorption would be governed by the chemical interactions or exchange of electrons between the adsorbate and adsorbent. This is in line with the behaviour of most adsorption systems particularly where monolayer coverage is done on homogeneous surfaces. The PSO model, as opposed to the pseudo first order model, better represents the rapid initial adsorption as well as the slow approach to equilibrium, and this is why it is the model of choice in most adsorption kinetic investigations28.

Most importantly, the intercept values (C) of the three stages are not zero and they are in the range of 0.486 to 0.619 mg/g. The Weber Morris model shows that when the intercept is not zero then intraparticle diffusion is not the only rate-limiting step. The positive C values indicate that there is a role played by the diffusion within the boundary layer (film resistance) in the total adsorption mechanism. This observation validates that the resistance mechanisms of MB adsorption on PKC are multi-resistance mechanisms, which act concomitantly, in line with the heterogeneous porous structure observed in SEM analysis.

Fourier transform infrared analysis

Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was employed to identify the functional groups present on the PKC surface with the help of VERTEX70 and V-100 ATR unit. With this methodology, IR-observable functional groups were obtained in the 500–4000 cm−1 wavenumber regions.

Figure 9a, b shows the FTIR spectra of PKC before and after the adsorption, with the corresponding wavenumber of the peaks. Although the overall FTIR spectra of PKC before and after MB adsorption appear similar, a notable increase in peak intensity is observed at 1814 cm⁻1 following adsorption, indicating successful uptake of MB onto the PKC surface. Specifically, at 1814 cm−1, a wavenumber associated with C–H bending in aromatic compounds, the peak’s intensity rose from 75% before adsorption to 94% following methylene blue uptake. Given that methylene blue’s chemical composition includes three benzene rings, its adsorption directly contributes to this observed increase in the FTIR peak intensity at 1815 cm−1, thereby inferring the capacity of PKC to adsorb methylene blue. Broadly, an examination of the FTIR spectra recorded from 4000 to 500 cm−1 also revealed the appearance of a novel peak in the 3000–3500 cm−1 range after adsorption, which is characteristic of O–H stretching35. The presence of this O–H stretch at this wavenumber commonly suggests the incorporation of water molecules into the PKC structure, a result consistent with previous findings by Said et al.2.

Proposed adsorption mechanism

The adsorption of methylene blue (MB) onto palm kernel cake (PKC) is governed by a combination of physical forces, primarily driven by electrostatic attraction, intraparticle diffusion, and surface complexation, as supported by the convergent evidence from thermodynamic, kinetic, FTIR, and pH analyses conducted in this study.

Electrostatic attraction (dominant force)PKC is a lignocellulosic compound that contains carboxyl (–COOH), hydroxyl (–OH) and carbonyl (C=O) functional groups, which are analyzed using FTIR. These surface groups become deprotonated at a pH of 4 or above and this makes the PKC surface highly negatively charged. Methylene blue is a cationic dye with the permanent positive charge on the sulphur-aromatic ring structure. This complementary charge leads to a powerful electrostatic interaction between the molecules of MB and the PKC surface that is the major driving force of adsorption. This is consistent with the observed increase in adsorption capacity with increasing pH (maximum at pH 8), where PKC surface charge density is highest36.

Regeneration of PKC

To determine the practicality and potential for repeated use of PKC as an adsorbent, desorption tests were conducted to remove MB dye from PKC that had already undergone adsorption. For these experiments, 0.1N HCl and 0.1 M NaOH served as the elution agents to facilitate dye removal from the PKC surface. The findings showed that, with an increase in the number of regeneration cycles, there was a gradual decline in the percentage of dye desorbed, as depicted in Fig. 11. Despite this decrease, the regenerated PKC maintained high efficiency over repeated use: after four consecutive adsorption and desorption cycles, the adsorption capacity of PKC declined by only 3.5% compared to its initial performance. As illustrated in Fig. 11, these results highlight the strong reusability and durability of PKC, indicating its suitability as an effective and long-lasting adsorbent for the removal of MB dye from aqueous solutions16.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that unmodified palm kernel cake (PKC) is an effective, low-cost, and sustainable adsorbent for methylene blue (MB) removal from aqueous solutions. Optimal adsorption of 81% was achieved at pH 8 within 90–120 min. The Langmuir isotherm (R2 = 0.982) provided the best fit, yielding a theoretical maximum monolayer capacity (qm) of 151.5 mg/g, while the experimentally observed equilibrium capacity (qe,exp) was 4.8 mg/g under the tested concentration range of 5–20 mg/L. Pseudo-second-order kinetics (R2 = 0.999) confirmed that particle diffusion is the rate-limiting step, consistent with the calculated activation energy of 37.8 kJ/mol. Thermodynamic parameters confirmed spontaneous (ΔG° = − 3.98 to − 4.25 kJ/mol), endothermic (ΔH° = 1.47 kJ/mol) physisorption with increasing thermodynamic favourability at higher temperatures. The suggested adsorption mechanism is mainly powered by an electrostatic force between the negatively charged PKC surface and the cationic MB which is complemented by the force of hydrogen bonding, π–π stacking, and intraparticle diffusion, which is shown through FTIR, SEM, Weber-Morris and Boyd. The adsorption capacity of PKC retained 96.5% after four regeneration cycles with the experiment showing that it is highly reusable. These results confirm that PKC is a promising cleaner and eco-friendly alternative to traditional adsorbents in the textile wastewater treatment process, which valorizes agricultural waste without the need to modify it energy-intensively.

Future work

Future research work needs to encompass more physicochemical characterization of PKC such as the BET surface area, XRD, TGA, and determination of point of zero charge (pHpzc) to enhance the mechanistic insight. Measuring of zeta potential and considering of MB pKa values would help in a better understanding of pH-dependent adsorption behaviour. The theoretical Langmuir capacity should be experimentally validated using higher initial MB concentrations. Continuous flow adsorption experiments with columns would be helpful in increasing the practical applicability. Moreover, study of chemically or thermally activated PKC, co-existing pollutant systems and actual textile wastewater experiments would be more extensive evaluation of the industrial potential of PKC.
