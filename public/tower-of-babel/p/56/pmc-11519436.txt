Efficacy of brachial plexus block in reducing radial artery spasm during interventional aneurysm surgery
Authors: Feng Feng, Nan Li, Zijian Zhao, He Liu, Le Qi, Zhenfeng Zhang, Haoran Wang, Chao Lin, Deng Zhang, Feng Han, Yifan Xu, Liwei Wang, Lei Cao, Jingjin Li, Hua Lu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryRadial artery spasm (RAS) occurs in around 50% of patients who undergo interventional aneurysm surgery through the radial artery approach, leading to suboptimal long-term recovery for some. We suggested using brachial plexus block (BPB) to enhance surgical impact on the radial artery, and we confirmed that preconditioning with BPB effectively reduces RAS occurrence and improves prognosis. A total of 177 patients were randomly assigned to either the BPB group or the control group. The main outcome measure was diagnosing RAS incidence through intraoperative angiography, characterized by defects at vessel edges during radial artery angiography. In the control group, RAS occurred in 62.5% (55 out of 88), while it only appeared in 9.0% (8 out of 89) in the BPB group—a significant absolute difference (−53.5 percentage points). The findings suggest that BPB may be an effective strategy to reduce intraoperative RAS incidence in patients undergoing transradial access (TRA) for intracranial aneurysm interventional surgery.Subject areas: Health sciences, Medicine, Medical specialty, Internal medicine, Cardiovascular medicine

Introduction

The safety and effectiveness of cerebrovascular angiography and treatment through transradial access (TRA) have been confirmed by numerous studies.1,2,3 However, the relatively narrow nature of the radial artery (RA) poses a risk of vascular endothelium damage during sheath insertion, potentially leading to radial artery spasm (RAS), stenosis, or occlusion. This will have a significant impact on subsequent medical procedures that necessitate radial access, including radial angiography, coronary artery bypass grafting, and hemodialysis.4,5,6 For these reasons, surgeons often opt to alleviate RAS during procedures by administering vasodilator medications like nitroglycerin.6,7,8,9,10 However, nitroglycerin administration carries the risk of severe hypotension which may lead to life-threatening complications such as cardiovascular and cerebrovascular accidents. Moreover, with its short half-life of 1–4 min, nitroglycerin administered intra-arterially through the sheath may not provide sufficient vascular protection throughout the perioperative period.11,12

Brachial plexus block (BPB) exhibits sympathomimetic resection effects, facilitating vasodilation of upper limb blood vessels and enhancing blood flow without inducing hypotension.13,14,15 Furthermore, BPB has demonstrated the ability to improve functional arteriovenous fistulas patency at 3 months and 1 year.16,17 It may prevent RAS and improve outcomes of upper extremity arteries in patients undergoing intracranial aneurysms embolization via TRA. However, there is a lack of sufficient reports on the preventive application of BPB in such surgeries. Therefore, we conducted a multicenter randomized controlled study to investigate the effect of BPB on RAS in patients undergoing intracranial aneurysm embolization via TRA.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 348 patients admitted for intracranial aneurysm embolization via TRA were assessed for eligibility from June 2023 to December 2023, and a total of 203 were enrolled. Of these, 177 patients met the criteria for inclusion in the intention-to-treat population (88 assigned to the control group and 89 assigned to the BPB group) (Figure 1).

Primary outcome

The incidence of RAS was 62.5% (55 of 88 patients) in the control group and 9.0% (8 of 89 patients) in the BPB group, showing an absolute difference of −53.5 percentage points (95% confidence interval [CI], −65.2 to −41.8; p < 0.001). After arterial sheath placement, 24 cases (27.3%) showed mild spasm (Video S1 in supplemental information),18 19 moderate (21.6%) (Video S2 in supplemental information), and 12 severe (13.6%) (Video S3 in supplemental information) using angiography in the control group, while in the BPB group, 6 patients (6.7%) showed mild spasm, 1 moderate (1.1%), and 1 severe (1.1%). There was a significant statistical difference in the degree of RAS between the two groups (p < 0.001) (Table 2; Video S4 in supplemental information).

Secondary outcomes

The rate of nitroglycerin use was 9.0% (8 of 89) in the BPB group and 62.5% (55 of 88) in the control group (relative risk, 0.14; 95% CI, 0.07 to 0.28; p < 0.001). The percentage of patients with hypotension was 12.4% (11 of 89) in the BPB group and 46.5% (41 of 88) in the control group (p < 0.001). The incidence of acute embolic events was observed in 39 patients (44.3%) after the surgery in the control group, whereas only 18 patients (20.2%) experienced such events in the BPB group (p < 0.001). The satisfaction score of surgeons in the BPB group was significantly higher than that in the control group (p < 0.001). The duration of RA puncture insertion in the control group was marginally longer compared to that in the BPB group (p = 0.044) (Table 2).

The occurrence of unfavorable RA for repeated transradial intervention (TRI) was 35.2% (31 of 88) in the control group and 9.0% (8 of 89) in the BPB group (absolute difference, −26.2%; 95% CI, −37.9 to −14.6; p < 0.001) (Table 2). The incidence of radial artery occlusion (RAO) (Video S5 in supplemental information), intima-media thickness (IMT) ≥0.5 mm and internal diameter (ID) < 1.5 mm (Video S6 in supplemental information), moderate and diffuse stenosis (Video S7 in supplemental information), and severe stenosis (Video S8 in supplemental information) in the BPB group was significantly lower than that in the control group (p < 0.05) at a one-month follow-up (Table 2).

The analysis revealed significant group and time interaction effects on the diameter (F = 161.613, p < 0.001), peak systolic velocity (PSV, F = 24.303, p < 0.001), end diastolic velocity (EDV, F = 80.247, p < 0.001), time-averaged maximum velocity (TAMAX, F = 64.187, p < 0.001), pulsatility index (PI, F = 18.477, p < 0.001), and resistance index (RI, F = 59.440, p < 0.001) of RA from 1 day before surgery to 1 month after surgery (Table 3). And compared to the control group, the diameter, PSV, EDV, and TAMAX were significantly higher at 30 min, 24 h, and 1 month post-surgery (Figure S1 in supplemental information). Conversely, PI and RI were lower than those of the control group with significant statistical differences observed (p < 0.05). The brachial artery (BA) exhibited comparable hemodynamic alterations in a similar manner (Table 3; Figure S2 in supplemental information). The diameter, PSV, EDV, and TAMAX of ulnar artery (UA) showed a significant increase in the BPB group compared to the control group at 30 min after BPB. Additionally, PI and RI exhibited a significant decrease. However, there was no statistically significant difference between the two groups, and they returned to their preoperative levels one month after surgery (Table 3; Figure S3 in supplemental information).

Supplementary analyses

Significant group ∗ time interaction effects (F = 9.200, p < 0.001) and a main effect of time (F = 4.921, p < 0.001) were observed for mean arterial pressure (MAP) from the initiation of BPB or control group intervention to the completion of surgery. Additionally, MAP was significantly lower in the control group compared to the BPB group at 1, 3, 5, and 10 min after angiography (Figure S4; Table S1 in supplemental information).

The incidence of postoperative puncture site complications and BPB complications did not show any statistically significant differences between the two groups (Table S2 in supplemental information).

Discussion

In this multicenter study, 177 patients scheduled for intracranial aneurysm embolization via TRA were randomized into either the BPB group or the control group. The incidence of RAS in the BPB group was significantly lower compared to that in the control group (9% vs. 62.5%, p < 0.001). Moreover, the utilization rate of intraoperative nitroglycerin, occurrence of hypotension, and acute embolic events were significantly reduced in the BPB group compared to the control group. Most notably, the rate of unfavorable RA for repeated TRI was significantly lower in the BPB group at one-month follow-up after surgery (9% vs. 35.2%, p < 0.001). By comparing the hemodynamic parameters of the upper-extremity arteries measured by ultrasound at different time points after BPB, there was an increase in the diameter of RA, BA, and UA. Additionally, there was an enhancement in blood flow velocity (PSV, EDV, and TAMAX), accompanied by a reduction in vascular resistance (PI and RI).

In awake patients undergoing transradial cardiac intervention, the diagnosis of RAS primarily relies on upper limb pain, with an approximate incidence rate of 10%–30%.7,18 The commonly employed methods for alleviating intraoperative radial spasm involve the administration of vasodilator medications such as nitroglycerin, verapamil, nicorandil, etc., and nitroglycerin is the most frequently utilized option.7,8,10,19,20 Nitroglycerin, despite its efficacy in relieving RAS, exhibits a limited duration of action and may induce a transient rise in intracranial pressure as well as a decline in MAP, thereby augmenting the susceptibility to myocardial and cerebral infarction along with other potential damage to vital organs.11,21,22 In response to the circulatory fluctuations induced by nitroglycerin, anesthesiologists would use vasoconstrictor drugs such as norepinephrine to combat hypotension, but this may worsen RAS.23 The duration of ultrasound-guided BPB with ropivacaine can be extended to 2–6 h or even longer, thereby adequately meeting the surgical requirements and significantly reducing the incidence of intraoperative RAS without affecting intracranial pressure. In this study, the baseline characteristics of both the groups were comparable, and the rate of RAS in the BPB group was significantly lower compared to that in the control group (9% vs. 62.5%, p < 0.001). Due to a higher rate of nitroglycerin usage in the control group, the incidence and duration of hypotension were significantly greater in the control group compared to the BPB group, particularly at 3 to 5 min post-administration of nitroglycerin, despite the timely administration of norepinephrine, and postoperative MRI showed that the incidence of cerebral embolism in the control group was also significantly higher than that in the BPB group. Therefore, the utilization of BPB may be considered a more favorable alternative to nitroglycerin.

The recent literature has reported a significantly higher incidence of acute embolic events following TRA surgery compared to the transfemoral access surgery.24 In this study, the incidence of acute embolic events was significantly lower in the BPB group compared to the control group (20.2% vs. 44.3%, p < 0.05). It is noteworthy that in the control group, 2 patients exhibited severe cerebral infarction and limb movement disorders; while such occurrences were absent in the BPB group. The occurrence of cerebral infarction may be directly augmented by perioperative hypotension, while the elevation of inflammatory cytokines could further exacerbate cerebral ischemia-reperfusion injury.25 BPB not only mitigates the incidence of intraoperative hypotension but also attenuates the expression of inflammatory cytokine IL-6, thereby potentially exerting a favorable protective effect and preventing the occurrence of acute embolic events. Additionally, BPB induces vasodilation in the blood vessels of the upper limb, leading to an augmentation in blood flow velocity. This may mitigate thromboembolism resulting from friction between the sheath and vascular wall, consequently reducing the incidence of acute embolic events.

Due to the extensive utilization of TRA in clinical practice, the rate of unfavorable RA for repeated TRI is also a matter of concern for patients undergoing intracranial aneurysm interventional surgery. The findings of our study demonstrate that BPB effectively minimize the rate of unfavorable RA for repeated TRI. The long-term protective effect of BPB on the RA may be attributed to the following mechanisms. (1) The control group patients may experience early-stage local intimal exfoliation and edema, followed by long-term hyperplasia.26 BPB may alter sympathetic tone and inhibit intimal hyperplasia.27 (2) In the control group, local blood vessels exhibited increased stiffness and resistance, potentially associated with reduced local blood flow velocity, interruption of blood flow, and platelet aggregation and activation; these significantly elevate the risk of microthrombus formation and vascular stenosis. The administration of BPB may attenuate microthrombus formation and exerted potent vascular protection. (3) During the diastolic phase of the cardiac cycle, blood is propelled forward by vascular walls contraction, which results in the formation of diastolic blood flow. The forward diastolic blood flow is believed to potentially aid in restoring arterial flow at the puncture site early on, promoting nourishment to the damaged intima and reducing long-term injury.19 BPB could improve vascular tone, decrease vascular resistance, and increase antegrade blood flow, thereby nourishing the intima.

This is a randomized controlled trial to assess the clinical application and efficacy of BPB in the RA approach. Our study establishes a solid foundation for interventional surgery using the RA approach and may encourage further investigation into the impact of BPB on this vascular technique. We hope that more anesthesiologists will acquire proficiency in lateral BPB technology and widely implement it in clinical practice, thereby benefiting a larger number of patients.

Conclusion

Our study provided evidence that BPB significantly reduced the incidence of intraoperative RAS and improved the outcomes of patients undergoing intracranial aneurysm embolization via TRA; thus it could be considered as an effective strategy for protecting RA in TRIs.

Limitations of the study

Certain limitations of this study should be acknowledged. Firstly, the follow-up only encompassed a one-month postoperative period, with no subsequent evaluation conducted, which may warrant further exploration in our experimental cohort. Secondly, there are multiple mechanisms implicated in RAS including mechanical stimulation, augmented nitric oxide release, heightened inflammatory cytokines secretion (e.g., IL-1, IL-6, TNF-α, ect.), discharge of vasoconstrictive agents (such as endothelin, leukotrienes), alterations in calcium channel activity, etc. In this study, BPB exhibited RA dilation and reduction in IL-6 levels; the specific mechanisms have not been further studied. Thirdly, patients who underwent BPB may require several hours for the recovery of upper limb motor function after surgery. Whether blocking only the sympathetic nerve (such as stellate ganglion) can reduce RAS without affecting postoperative movement needs further study.

Experimental model and study participant details

Study design

This prospective, multicenter, randomized controlled trial were conducted at four hospitals from June to December 2023 in Jiangsu and Zhejiang Province, China. Method details

Randomization and blinding

This study was conducted at four centers (The First Affiliate Hospital with Nanjing Medical University, Nanjing, China; Huzhou Central Hospital, Huzhou, China; Xuzhou Central Hospital, Xuzhou, China; Nanjing Jiangbei Hospital, Nanjing, China). Patients were stratified by center, using a stratified block randomized design. A password-protected web-based computer random sequence generator was employed to generate random sequences. Sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes were prepared by a colleague independent of the research team for assigning patients to the BPB or the control group. Each center independently generated random sequences, and patients were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to the two groups. Eligible patients were allocated a study number and corresponding sealed envelopes upon expressing willingness to participate. The envelopes were opened by the anesthesiologist's assistant immediately before the procedure. Anesthesiologists, surgeons, and follow-up personnels were blinded to randomization.

Interventions

The patients underwent upper extremity ultrasound Doppler examination upon admission. During the examination, the patient assumed a supine position with their forearms resting parallel to their sides and their hands slightly extended. The measurements were collected at four distinct time points: baseline (the day prior to the surgical procedure), 30 minutes after brachial plexus block (BPB), 24 hours post-surgery, and one month following the surgery. The patients underwent standard intraoperative monitoring after undergoing routine fasting for 8 hours prior to surgery. The BPB group was given 20 ml dose of 0.15% ropivacaine using ultrasound-guided supraclavicular approach, while a 20 ml dose of normal saline was given in the control group 30 minutes prior to surgery (Figure S5 in supplemental information).

The two groups were administered the identical anesthesia induction protocol, as outlined below. Propofol at a dose of 1-2 mg/kg, fentanyl at a dose of 3-5 μg/kg, and cisatracurium at a dose of 0.15 mg/kg. Tracheal intubation will be performed with the administration of 60% oxygen and mechanical ventilation. The intraoperative anesthesia was sustained using propofol and remifentanil. The intraoperative anesthesia management maintained the systolic blood pressure within 20% of the baseline value, while ensuring a heart rate between 60 and 100 beats per minute. In cases where the mean arterial pressure (MAP) dropped below 60 mmHg, intermittent intravenous administration of norepinephrine (10 μg) was administered. Additionally, if the heart rate fell below 50 beats per minute, atropine was given intravenously at a dose of 0.5 mg.

After the induction of general anesthesia, all patients underwent radial artery puncture performed by the same surgeon. Firstly, the patient's palm was positioned upward with the upper limb abducted at an angle of 20-30 degrees. A small plastic pillow was placed under the patient's wrist to elevate it by 5-8 cm and maintain a state of hyperextension. The surgeon carefully selected a puncture point located 1cm proximal to the radial styloid process and gently palpated for the strongest pulsation of the radial artery using his left index finger, middle finger, and ring finger in a far-to-near direction. Once the puncture point was determined, the needle tip was directed against blood flow at an angle between 30°-45° relative to the skin surface before being slowly inserted. Upon observing blood return within the needle hub, it was advanced by 1-2 mm to secure its position while ensuring continuous pulsatile blood flow through its distal end as evidence of successful puncture. All patients received TERUMO RS∗A60G16SQZ Fr.6 radial sheath tube during this procedure, with recording of puncture time; if unsuccessful, reasons for failure were documented.

After a successful arterial puncture and sheath insertion, additional assessment was conducted through radial artery angiography. The severity of RAS was classified as severe, moderate, or mild based on the degree of stenosis observed on radial arteriography: greater than 75% (Figure S6C in supplemental information and Video S1), 25-75% (Figure S6B in supplemental information and Video S2), and lower than 25% (Figure S6A in supplemental information and Video S3), of the ID of blood vessels, respectively.6 In addition, if the combined spasm length exceeded 2 cm, the severity increases by 1 grade (Figure S6D in supplemental information and Video S4).

It is worth noting that prior to the study, anesthesia investigators underwent training in brachial plexus block (BPB) using a model. BPB was performed on 3-5 patients under the guidance of the principal investigator, strictly adhering to the procedural guidelines. The trainer should pay attention to the following considerations: ensuring proper adjustment of the ultrasonic probe for needle placement in a flat plane, preventing nerve and blood vessel damage caused by needle tip, and aspirating local anesthetic before injection to avoid blood contamination. The satisfaction rating criteria for surgeons included the stability of patients' vital signs during the perioperative period and the incidence of postoperative complications.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the incidence of RAS, assessed through radial artery angiography following arterial catheter insertion. The severity of RAS was categorized as severe, moderate, or mild based on the degree of stenosis observed on radial arteriography: > 75%, 25–75%, and < 25% of vessel diameter, respectively.6 Additionally, if the combined spasm length exceeded 2cm, the severity would increase by one grade.

The secondary outcomes included the incidence of intraoperative nitroglycerin use, intraoperative hypotension, inflammatory cytokines levels, embolic events after surgery, RA puncture duration, surgeon satisfaction scores and unfavorable RA for repeated trans-radial interventions (TRI) at 1 month after surgery. When the radial sheath was inserted, upper limb angiography was performed, and nitroglycerin 100 ug was administered if any vasospasm in the upper limb was observed on the angiography. The administration of nitroglycerin was documented in each group. Intraoperative hypotension was defined as a mean arterial pressure (MAP) below 60 mmHg.28 24 hours after the surgery, blood samples were collected to test eight inflammatory cytokines. The occurrence of acute embolic events was confirmed through head magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) conducted on the day following surgery. The duration of RA puncture was defined as the interval between the initiation of RA puncture and the insertion of the radial sheath tube. The satisfaction of surgeons is assessed using a scale ranging from 0 to 10, where 0 represents the lowest level of satisfaction and 10 indicates the highest. The upper extremity arterial vessels (include the radial, brachial, and ulnar arteries) were evaluated before the surgery, 30 minutes after BPB or control interventions, 24 hours after surgery, and the ultrasonography at 1month post-surgery was performed by a blinded anesthesiologist, who was unaware of the group assignments. RA was considered unfavourable for repeated-TRI if it exhibited any of the following criteria: radial artery occlusion (RAO), intima-media thickness (IMT) ≥ 0.5 mm and diameter < 1.5 mm, moderate and diffuse stenosis (> 10 mm in length), or severe focal or diffuse stenosis (regardless of length).19

Quantification and statistical analysis

The primary objective of this study was to investigate the influence of BPB on the occurrence of intraoperative RAS, which exhibits a prevalence ranging from 15% to 50%.5,6,29 The prevalence of RAS during intracranial aneurysm embolization under general anesthesia (GA) exceeds 30%, thus, approximate incidence of 30% was for the primary outcome. The estimated sample size required to achieve a statistical power of 90% for detecting an absolute between-group difference of 20% or greater in the primary outcome was determined to be 158 patients, assuming a 2-sided type 1 error rate of 5%. Assuming 20% of patients to be lost to follow-up, we aimed to enroll about 200 patients with available data for assessment of the primary outcome.

Data analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 27.0. Continuous variables are represented as means (standard deviations), while the categorical variables are expressed in terms of frequencies (percentages). Normality of the data was assessed, and assuming a normal distribution, a two-tailed Student's t-test was employed to compare continuous variables and a χ2 test or Fisher's exact test was used to compare categorical variables. For data that did not follow a normal distribution, Mann-Whitney U tests were employed. Repeated measures ANOVA was employed to compare continuous variables at different time points, followed by the Bonferroni correction test for multiple comparisons. A significance level of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
