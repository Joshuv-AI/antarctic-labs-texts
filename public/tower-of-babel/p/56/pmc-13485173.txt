Impact of the invasive diatom species Cymbella janischii on riverine microbial biofilm communities and a potential role of bacterially produced zeatin
Authors: Eldrin D. L. R. Arguelles, Kana Mugikura, Shinya Sato
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe diatom Cymbella janischii is an invasive species in Japan, causing nuisance blooms by forming thick mats in rivers. To date, there are no documented studies on the microbiome associations in C. janischii mats or the processes that drive bloom formation. This study used metabarcoding of diatoms, bacteria, and fungi to identify key species and assess the effects of C. janischii blooms on the benthic microbial communities. C. janischii blooms reduced diatom and bacterial species diversity, while fungal diversity remained stable. In addition, the diatom Nitzschia paleacea and the bacterium Flavobacterium sp. were observed to co‐occur and vary in abundance, indicating a possible ecological link that may affect mat structure or function. Metagenomic predictions of bacterial functions showed that compared to benthic stones without visible C. janischii mats, mat‐associated bacteria had enriched pathways related to the metabolism of carbohydrates, nucleotides, and amino acids, along with zeatin biosynthesis. Zeatin is a cytokinin phytohormone that stimulates plant growth and development. In vitro exposure of C. janischii to varying zeatin concentrations confirmed its growth‐promoting effects, inducing cell proliferation and stalk formation. This study shows that zeatin stimulates the growth of C. janischii. The findings of this study provide new insights into microbiome diversity, identifying key taxa associated with C. janischii mats to help better understand bloom formation.

INTRODUCTION

Invasive species in rivers and their impact on local ecosystems have become a major concern (Amalfitano et al., 2015; Litchman, 2010; Suescún et al., 2021). Although recent research has focused on various macroinvasive species such as animals and plants, microbial invasion is less understood and is biased toward organisms that could directly affect human health (Litchman, 2010). Understanding microbial species invasions, encompassing both pathogenic and free‐living organisms, is crucial for understanding the relationship between biodiversity and ecosystem function (Acosta et al., 2015; Suescún et al., 2021). Detecting and evaluating non‐native microorganisms is difficult because of their small size, the difficulty in their cultivation, their limited taxonomic resolution, and disagreements on species definitions (Amalfitano et al., 2015). Despite these issues, understanding how invasive microbes establish and spread is vital since they influence biogeochemical cycles and affect key environmental processes in ecosystems (Amalfitano et al., 2015; Litchman, 2010). Several factors such as enhanced competitive ability and lack of natural enemies can cause the rapid expansion of invasive organisms in new habitats. Their aggressive expansion can decrease local microbial community diversity, alter nutrient cycling, and affect ecosystem productivity, impacting ecosystems such as rivers (Boylen et al., 1999; Gallardo et al., 2016; Stiers et al., 2011). Among diatoms, invasive species can be highly disruptive, as shown by the spread of Didymosphenia geminata in rivers across North America, Chile, Argentina, New Zealand, and parts of Europe (Blanco & Ector, 2009; Kilroy et al., 2009; Kilroy & Novis, 2018). In early stages of spread, the cells of this species remain embedded within the periphyton community and are not readily visible; however, as growth progresses, colonies expand into dome‐shaped aggregates that can merge into extensive mats of varying thickness, particularly in rivers with stable flow regimes (Bahls, 2007; Khan‐Bureau et al., 2016; Whitton et al., 2009). These mats, commonly referred to as “rock snot,” grow rapidly under certain environmental conditions, driven primarily by the prolific production of stalk material composed of extracellular polymeric substances (EPS). This stalk overproduction allows the colonies to spread across large areas of the riverbed, where they can alter the sedimentary bottom structures and negatively affect other native aquatic organisms (Khan‐Bureau et al., 2016; Kilroy & Novis, 2018). Although D. geminata is now widespread, it is native to cold, nutrient‐poor waters typically found in rivers and streams as well as temperate environments characterized by warm summers and cold winters (George & Baldigo, 2015).

Another invasive diatom species in Japan, Cymbella janischii, was first observed in 2006 in the Chikugo River. Since then, it has expanded rapidly beyond Kyushu to multiple prefectures across Japan, including Oita, Gunma, Gifu, Kanagawa, Ishikawa, Tochigi, and more recently, Shiga (Kato‐Unoki et al., 2022; Mugikura et al., 2022; Suzawa et al., 2011; Suzawa & Suzawa, 2021). The cells of C. janischii have the ability to produce long mucilaginous stalks from the valve apex for substrate attachment. These stalks enable the species to form dense mats similar to those produced by D. geminata, earning it recognition as a nuisance invasive diatom (Kato‐Unoki et al., 2022; Mugikura et al., 2022). Within roughly the past 17 years, this diatom has formed massive growth in several river systems, indicating a marked expansion of its distribution and ecological influence. These blooms are characterized by dense colonies in which cells attach to substrates through mucilaginous stalks, forming thick mats that can dominate the periphytic community. The invasion of C. janischii directly affects sectors such as commercial fisheries, recreation, and tourism. The mechanism that drives the massive bloom formation of C. janischii remains unknown, raising concerns for aquatic ecosystems protection and conservation.

Generally, diatom growth on benthic surfaces is made possible by adhesion mechanisms, which include the formation of specialized structures like stalks and mucilage pads (Hoagland et al., 1993). Production of EPS also provides a protective matrix enabling diatom growth that leads to an increase in population density. Competitive relationships with other microorganisms further shape the community composition of benthic diatom assemblages in rivers (Bohórquez et al., 2017; Ma et al., 2023). The occurrence and spatial distribution of invasive diatom species in rivers may also be influenced by bacterial communities in benthic substrates. Ecological relationships among microbes create regulatory networks that operate during algal blooms (initial bloom formation, duration, and collapse stages; Sun et al., 2017; Tan et al., 2015). In algal blooms, the metabolic function and structure of bacterial communities may also influence the ecological function of microalgae at different bloom stages (Amin et al., 2015; Chen et al., 2021). Based on these previous findings, it appears that studying the complexity of relationships within microbial communities is essential for understanding the mechanisms behind algal blooms. Given the potential risks posed by bloom‐forming algae, it is imperative to conduct studies about the biological mechanisms driving their invasion, and the ecological impacts on various trophic levels.

As a contribution to understanding Cymbella janischii blooms, this study aimed (1) to characterize the composition and diversity of diatom, bacterial, and fungal communities associated with C. janischii bloom mats collected from multiple river systems in Japan, using high‐throughput sequencing to profile microbial assemblages. In parallel, we (2) assessed and compared microbial diversity between bloom and non‐bloom sites to determine the extent to which C. janischii blooms alter microbial community structure and potentially disrupt the ecological balance of riverine biofilms. From these community and diversity data, we then (3) identified key co‐occurring taxa and (4) predicted potential metabolic exchanges between C. janischii and its associated microbiota through metagenomic functional inference, which were subsequently (5) validated via in vitro assays testing the growth‐promoting effects of selected metabolites on unialgal cultures of C. janischii.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Sample collection

Samples were collected from 15 rivers in Japan from 2022 to 2023 (Table S1). At each river, two types of samples were obtained for comparison—a visible Cymbella janischii mat colony sample and a control sample from stone surfaces without visible mats (see the Bioinformatic Analysis section for details)—for a total of 30 samples. Both samples were collected within a 10‐m distance, with the assumption that the two microbial communities developed under similar conditions, sharing most abiotic factors such as light irradiance, temperature, and flow rate; the major difference between the two sample types was the presence or absence of a C. janischii mat. For each sample type, three relatively large stones were selected that were expected to have remained stable at the same location and been exposed to approximately the same colonization period, even under high flow conditions. For each of the three stones per condition, samples of the C. janischii mat and the corresponding control samples from nearby stones without visible mats were collected by scrubbing the entire stone surface with a toothbrush. The resulting three samples collected for each condition were then pooled into separate tubes and preserved in RNAlater solution (Lader, 2001) at an approximate RNAlater:sample ratio of 10:1. The preserved samples were kept at room temperature for approximately 2–4 h (travel time) to allow RNAlater to penetrate prior to freezing at −20°C. To prevent cross‐contamination, all sampling procedures were conducted using nitrile gloves, sterile collection tubes, and a new toothbrush for each sample. Samples were transported to the laboratory and stored at −20°C until genomic analysis.

Microscopy

Light microscopy (LM) observations were carried out using an Axio Imager M2 photomicroscope fitted with differential interference contrast optics and an Axiocam MRc5 digital camera (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). For the growth experiment using zeatin (see the In Vitro Analysis of Growth Promotion of Cymbella janischii by Zeatin section for details), observations and image documentation were conducted using an IX73 Inverted Fluorescence Microscope (Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). For scanning electron microscopy (SEM), the colony was first fixed with 4% glutaraldehyde for 2 h under 4°C. The material was then passed through a graded ethanol dehydration series, substituted with t‐butyl alcohol, and freeze‐dried using JFD‐300 (JEOL, Tokyo, Japan). Dried samples were coated with osmium in an osmium coater (Neoc‐AN, Meiwafosis, Tokyo, Japan). The specimen was then observed using a SEM SU1510 (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan) at an accelerating voltage of 10 kV and a 5 mm working distance.

Genomic DNA extraction, amplification, and next‐generation sequencing

Samples for DNA extraction were transferred into 2‐mL MP Lysing Matrix A tubes containing 1.4‐mm ceramic garnet spheres (MP Bio Irvine, California, United States). Homogenization was performed with a FastPrep‐24 Classic bead beating grinder and homogenizer (MP Bio). Genomic DNA was subsequently isolated using the NucleoSpin Plant II kit (MACHEREY‐NAGEL, Düren, Germany) according to the manufacturer's protocols. The total DNA in each sample was eluted in 50 μL of the low salt elution buffer PE (5 mM Tris/HCl, pH 8.5) provided with the kit and stored at −80°C for further polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification.

The PCR reactions were performed using a LifeECO thermal cycler (Bioer Technology, Hangzhou, China) with different primer sets chosen for their broad taxonomic coverage. Diatom community was analyzed by targeting a 312‐bp region of the rbcL gene using a primer set consisting of three forward and two reverse primers (Vasselon et al., 2017). Bacteria were amplified from the V3–V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene using primers 341F and 805R (Klindworth et al., 2013; Logue et al., 2016). Fungi were amplified from the ITS2 rRNA region using primers gITS7 and ITS4 (Ihrmark et al., 2012; White et al., 1990). The complete primer sequences and PCR cycling conditions are provided in Table S2. Each PCR reaction had a final volume of 25 μL and contained 2X TaKaRa LA Taq Master Mix, 10 μM of each primer, and 1 μL of template DNA. Amplicon size and integrity were verified using agarose gel electrophoresis. First‐round PCR products were purified with VAHTS DNA Clean Beads (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) at a 1.0X ratio. DNA concentrations were measured with a Synergy H1 microplate reader (Agilent Technologies, California, United States) using the QuantiFluor dsDNA System (Promega, Wisconsin, United States). A second PCR was conducted to generate libraries. Quantification of the prepared libraries was also carried out using Synergy H1 and QuantiFluor dsDNA systems (Wari et al., 2025). Quality confirmation of the prepared libraries was performed using Fragment Analyzer and the dsDNA 915 Reagent Kit (Agilent Technologies). Paired‐end sequencing (2 × 300 bp) was conducted using the MiSeq system and MiSeq Reagent Kit v3 (Illumina, California, United States).

Sequence analysis

The microbial sequence data were processed using the QIIME 2 (v2023.2.0) following the “Moving Pictures” pipeline (Bolyen et al., 2019; Caporaso et al., 2010). Briefly, paired‐end reads were joined and merged for downstream analysis. The reads were then subjected to demultiplexing into their respective samples using the DEMUX plugin (in QIIME 2). The resulting split library output was filtered for chimeric sequences, denoised, and dereplicated into 100% amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) using DADA2 (v 1.37.0; Callahan et al., 2016). The ASV representative sequences were assigned taxonomies using a trained classifier with the q2‐feature‐classifier and classify‐sklearn plugins (Bokulich et al., 2018; Pedregosa et al., 2011). Trained classifiers based on the Diat.barcode (version 11; Rimet et al., 2019), SILVA rRNA release v138.1 reference (Bokulich et al., 2018; Quast et al., 2013), and UNITE version 9 databases (Abarenkov et al., 2022) were made for diatoms, bacteria, and fungi, respectively, using a naïve Bayesian method (Bokulich et al., 2018; Rimet et al., 2019). All ASVs that were taxonomically assigned to mitochondria and chloroplasts were removed from the dataset. The ASV read counts were normalized by dividing them by the total number of reads in each sample, converting the counts into proportions. The sequence reads corresponding to each sample of this study have been deposited in National Center for Biotechnology Information Sequence Read Archive (NCBI SRA) for public access (Bioproject number PRJNA1476665).

Bioinformatic analysis

We first analyzed the dataset containing all 30 samples, referred to as the “ALL” dataset, to assess the microbial community compositions of diatoms, bacteria, and fungi. We also performed principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) on the ALL dataset using Bray–Curtis distances calculated from normalized ASV data with R (https://www.R‐project.org/, accessed on 23 September 2023). The analysis was used to assess the relatedness or similarity of the samples, with the relative abundance of Cymbella janischii in each sample mapped onto the plots to visualize its effect on microbial community composition. To avoid indirect influence on beta‐diversity, C. janischii reads were excluded from the diatom dataset prior to normalization and distance calculation (Bray & Curtis, 1957; Lozupone & Knight, 2005; Vázquez‐Baeza et al., 2013).

Although samples with and without Cymbella janischii mats were visually distinguished by the naked eye and collected separately at each site, the initial metabarcoding analysis of all 30 samples revealed that up to 12.42% of C. janischii reads were present even in the “control” stone samples (i.e., those without visible C. janischii mats). To further evaluate the effect of the presence or absence of C. janischii on microbial communities, we created a subset of the dataset, referred to as “PAIRED,” which included only seven selected rivers. In this subset, each river contained two contrasting conditions: one sample with C. janischii sequence reads >1%, designated as mat (+) JANI, representing bloom conditions, and one sample with C. janischii sequence reads ≤1%, designated as benthic (−) JANI, representing non‐bloom conditions. Rivers in which both sample types exceeded the 1% threshold were excluded from the PAIRED dataset, as they could not provide a clear bloom versus non‐bloom comparison. The 1% threshold was defined based on the relative abundance of C. janischii within the diatom dataset only and not across the combined diatom, bacterial, and fungal datasets. This approach allowed us to specifically examine microbial community differences in relation to C. janischii dominance, while avoiding confounding effects from rivers that lacked a true contrasting pair. We note that absolute biomass was not measured in this study; instead, the relative abundance of C. janischii reads was used as a proxy for bloom versus non‐bloom conditions.

For the PAIRED dataset, alpha‐ and beta‐diversity were assessed using rarefied data. For this purpose, the number of sequencing reads subsampled from each sample was used as the sample depth, allowing fair comparison across datasets. The rarefaction depth was chosen based on the lowest sequencing depth among samples, ensuring that diversity estimates were not biased by unequal sequencing effort (Shirazi et al., 2021). Microbial alpha‐diversity indices, including number of observed ASVs, Pielou's Evenness, and Shannon diversity, were calculated using the q2‐diversity plugin and the phyloseq package in R (Vázquez‐Baeza et al., 2013). Differential abundance analysis was assessed using linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) to identify significant differences in the abundances of the microbial taxa between mat (+) JANI and benthic (−) JANI samples within each sampling area (Segata et al., 2011). The LEfSe analysis was performed using the Hutlab Galaxy web application (huttenhower.sph.harvard.edu/galaxy/), incorporating linear discriminant analysis (LDA) to estimate effect size at log(10) values >2 (Segata et al., 2011).

Co‐occurrence networks of diatom, bacterial, and fungal samples were analyzed with the PAIRED dataset using the iNAP platform (Feng et al., 2022). Connector hubs are highly connected nodes in a network that links to a large proportion of other nodes across multiple modules, thereby serving as important bridges between different parts of the network. Peripheral nodes, by contrast, have relatively few connections, mostly restricted to nodes within the same module, representing a small fraction of the total possible links. SparCC (Sparse Correlations for Compositional data) analysis (Friedman & Alm, 2012), a method designed to mitigate spurious correlations that arise when using relative rather than absolute abundance data, was used to determine pairwise relationships among ASVs. The networks were visualized with Cytoscape ver. 3.10.0 (Shannon et al., 2003). Predictive metagenome analysis was done using the Phylogenetic Investigation of Communities by Reconstruction of Unobserved States (PICRUSt2; Douglas et al., 2020) pipeline in the QIIME2 plugin; PICRUSt2 is a bioinformatics tool used to predict the functional potential of bacterial communities based on 16S rRNA gene sequencing data. Functional abundances were predicted using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG). The software STAMP (Statistical Analysis of Metagenomic Profiles) was further used in conducting statistical tests and visualizing the profile of feature abundance (ASVs and KEGG ortholog pathways) generated by PICRUSt2 (Parks et al., 2014).

In vitro analysis of growth promotion of Cymbella janischii by zeatin

Trans‐zeatin was selected for experimental validation from the list of enriched metabolic functions (PICRUSt2 analysis in Figure S1) because it is a well‐known cytokinin with established roles in regulating algal growth, biofilm formation, and microbial interactions (Arkhipova et al., 2005; Žižková et al., 2017). Its enrichment in Cymbella janischii‐associated bacterial communities suggested a potential mechanistic link between diatom–bacteria interactions and bloom dynamics. Although other metabolites were detected, trans‐zeatin was prioritized because of prior evidence demonstrating that cytokinins influence both photosynthetic efficiency and microbial colonization processes, making it a strong candidate for functional testing.

The concentration gradient of 0–300 μM used in this study was selected to capture a wide spectrum of possible responses, ranging from physiologically relevant to supra‐physiological levels. Previous studies have employed comparable or higher cytokinin concentrations to evaluate algal and microbial responses. For example, Stirk et al. (2002) reported cytokinin activity in cyanobacteria and microalgae using assays up to 500 μM, and Hussain and Hasnain (2009) tested bacterial cytokinin effects in the range of 24.64–74.94 μM. Similarly, Mazepa et al. (2021) demonstrated zeatin effects on Desmodesmus subspicatus within a 0–200 μM range. Using an extended gradient up to 300 μM therefore allowed us to assess both subtle growth‐promoting effects at low concentrations and potential inhibitory or saturation effects at higher doses, thereby providing a more comprehensive understanding of zeatin's role in diatom–bacteria interactions.

Individual cells of Cymbella janischii from the Kanna River were isolated using a Pasteur pipette. Following isolation, cells were first grown on 6‐well plates and subsequently transferred into 100‐mL flasks containing Wright's cryptophyte (WC) medium, pH 7.5 (Guillard & Lorenzen, 1972). The non‐axenic diatom strain was cultivated at 10°C with a 16:8 h light:dark photoperiod using fluorescent white light at 120 μmol photons · m−2 · s−1.

For the in vitro growth analysis, about 50 cells of Cymbella janischii were inoculated per well in 2 mL of sterile WC medium supplemented with varying concentrations of trans‐zeatin (0, 50, 100, 150, 200, 250, and 300 μM; Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical Corporation, Osaka, Japan). Zeatin stock solutions were prepared in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and added such that the final DMSO concentration in all treatments, including the control, was 0.10% (v/v). Zeatin and medium were added only once at the start of the 21‐day cultivation period without further supplementation.

Cell counting was conducted after detaching cells from stalks to facilitate accurate observation. Cultured samples were first treated with 0.1% domestic bleach (Pipeunish, SC Johnson, Yokohama, Japan) for 2 h to detach cells from the mucilaginous stalks. The colonies grow three‐dimensionally, which makes accurate counting difficult, as not all cells lie within a single focal plane. Therefore, prior to cell counting, cells were detached from the stalks by treatment with domestic bleach, allowing them to settle at the bottom. The samples were then thoroughly mixed to ensure even distribution across the bottom surface. Following this preparation, cell counts were performed using an inverted light microscope at 40× magnification by examining 10 randomly selected microscopic fields from a 6‐well‐plate culture. The average cell count per field was determined and used to estimate total cell abundance within the well. The field of view (FOV) area was calculated assuming a circular FOV, and the total well area was derived based on a standard 6‐well plate diameter of 35 mm. The total cell count per well was estimated by scaling the observed cell counts to the full well area. Finally, the cell concentration per mL was determined by dividing the total cell estimate by the culture volume.

To measure the stalk length, colonies of Cymbella janischii were resuspended in WC medium with 10 μL of ConA‐FITC solution (100 μg · mL−1 in WC medium, pH 7.5), allowed to incubate for 1 h in the dark at room temperature, and washed three times with WC medium. The IX73 inverted fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) was used for observation and documentation. Cymbella janischii stalks were measured only when an entire stalk from tip to base was in a focal plane (stalk was flattened and laid horizontally against the bottom of the culture vessel). Stalks lacking cells at their tips were excluded from the measurements to avoid potential bias in assessing treatment effects, as these regions did not provide reliable information for evaluating cellular responses. If stalk bifurcations were observed at the point of cell division (where each daughter cell continues to produce a stalk), then the average length of the two stalks was taken to obtain the final stalk length. In addition, if a C. janischii colony was composed of two or more stalks, then the average length of the stalks was also taken as the final stalk length of the colony. Stalk length measurements were performed using six colonies for each treatment from fluorescent images using ImageJ version 1.53 t (Schneider et al., 2012).

Statistically significant differences among experimental treatments were validated using Statistica version 13.0. All images used for cell counting and stalk length measurements are provided in Figure S2.

RESULTS

Observation

In some rivers, blooms of Cymbella janischii were observed, forming thick, mat‐like colonies that could be grabbed by hand (Figure 1a). The cells of C. janischii had mucilage stalks (Figure 1b–d), which were secreted from the apical pore fields located at the valve apices (Figure 1d). Notably, an enlarged SEM view of the cell revealed a large number of rod‐shaped bacteria attached to the surface of C. janischii.

Microbiome sequencing and profiling

For the ALL dataset (30 samples collected from 15 sites), a total of 678,447; 622,091; and 1,019,193 sequences were collectively generated after joining and quality filtering for diatoms, bacteria, and fungi, respectively. There were 426 diatom, 1564 bacterial, and 781 fungal ASVs, each assigned to reference sequences in the database using a trained classifier. The dominant ASVs for each microbial community were obtained by calculating the total relative abundance of each ASV across all samples and arranging them in descending order. Diatom community composition was classified into one phylum, four classes, 20 orders, 34 families, and 154 species (Figure 2). The bacterial community composition with relative abundance of at least 0.1% was classified into 26 phyla, 54 classes, 148 orders, 248 families, and 334 genera. The fungal community composition was classified into 10 phyla, 38 classes, 104 orders, 289 families, and 253 genera. Microbial community composition similarities among samples were analyzed using PCoA. We observed high similarity (clustering) in the bacterial communities in samples with higher relative abundance of Cymbella janischii (darker color in Figure 3), but this pattern was not observed in the diatom and fungal communities, indicating that as the abundance of C. janischii increased, the bacterial communities exhibited greater similarity.

To further investigate the influence of Cymbella janischii bloom, a subset of samples (PAIRED dataset) was defined based on a predefined threshold applied to the normalized diatom dataset. Specifically, samples were categorized as mat (+) JANI when C. janischii relative abundance exceeded 1%, and as benthic (−) JANI when it was ≤1%. This threshold‐based classification was established independently of Figure 2. The figure serves as a visual confirmation of this separation, where samples with visibly elevated C. janischii abundance existed within the diatom community, while benthic samples remain below 1%. Only rivers with clear contrasting pairs (one sample above and one below the threshold) were retained, allowing clearer comparison between bloom and non‐bloom conditions. This approach reduced ambiguity in classification and allowed for a more precise assessment of microbial community differences associated with C. janischii dominance.

Microbial diversity

For the PAIRED dataset (14 samples from seven sites, subsampled from the ALL dataset), a total of 113 diatom, 220 bacterial, and 163 fungal ASVs were identified across samples. Mat (+) JANI samples had fewer unique ASVs (10 diatom, 34 bacterial, and 37 fungal ASVs) than benthic (−) JANI samples (38 diatom, 45 bacterial, and 63 fungal ASVs; Figure 4). The microbial community diversity of mat (+) JANI and benthic (−) JANI samples was evaluated by examining the number of ASVs along with Pielou's evenness and Shannon diversity (Figure 5). For the number of ASVs across all microbial communities, no significant differences were observed between mat (+) JANI and benthic (−) JANI samples (Mann–Whitney U test: diatoms, U = 37, p = 0.128; bacteria, U = 35, p = 0.201; fungi, U = 35, p = 0.209). In contrast, all of the communities showed significant differences in Pielou's Evenness index, with values being significantly lower in Mat (+) JANI samples compared to benthic (−) JANI samples (Mann–Whitney U test: diatoms, U = 45, p = 0.007; bacteria, U = 44, p = 0.011; fungi, U = 43, p = 0.017). For Shannon diversity, diatom and bacterial communities among the samples exhibited significant differences (Mann–Whitney U test: diatoms, U = 43, p = 0.017; bacteria, U = 41, p = 0.038), while no significant difference was detected in the fungal community (Mann–Whitney U test: fungi, U = 36, p = 0.165).

In this study, the term phylotype refers to a taxonomically assigned ASV, representing a unique DNA sequence that corresponds to a specific taxonomic group. The analysis of diatoms revealed one phylotype (Nitzschia paleacea) in mat (+) JANI and 14 phylotypes in benthic (−) JANI. For bacteria, 38 phylotypes were identified in mat (+) JANI and 39 in benthic (−) JANI, with the most differentially abundant bacterial groups in mat (+) JANI belonging to the phylum Bacteroidota. In terms of fungi, one phylotype (an unidentified Chytridiomycota) was abundant in mat (+) JANI, while three phylotypes were abundant in benthic (−) JANI.

Co‐occurrence network analysis

Co‐occurrence network analysis was performed to better understand the relationships of microorganisms associated with Cymbella janischii bloom (Figure 9). The network consisted of 90 nodes and 316 edges, and eight connector hubs and 82 peripheral nodes were identified. The connector hubs included several taxa of diatoms (Navicula cryptotenella and Nitzschia linearis), bacteria (Novosphingobium sp., Fluviicola sp., Arenimonas sp., and an uncultured Verrucomicrobiaceae), and fungi (uncultured Alphamycetaceae). Positive relationships were observed between Flavobacterium sp. and Nitzschia paleacea with C. janischii, indicating cooperative or synergistic relationships. In contrast, a negative relationship was observed for Melosira varians and Sellaphora nigri, indicating competitive or antagonistic behavior involving C. janischii. The unidentified Chytridiomycota that was a differentially abundant ASV in the mat (+) JANI sample was a peripheral node, showing no direct relationship with C. janischii.

DISCUSSION

Ecological impacts of Cymbella janischii blooms on riverine microbial diversity and community structure

The study assessed the impact of Cymbella janischii blooms on microbial communities in several rivers in Japan. Cymbella janischii blooms had a significant negative effect on diatom and bacterial evenness (Pielou's evenness) and diversity (Shannon diversity) in mat (+) JANI samples compared with benthic (−) JANI samples, indicating that bloom conditions led to uneven species distribution and reduced community diversity. However, fungal metabarcoding revealed no significant reduction in fungal richness (number of ASVs) and diversity between mat (+) JANI and benthic (−) JANI samples, indicating that C. janischii blooms had a lesser impact on the fungal community. Significant differences were observed only in fungal community evenness, suggesting that mat (+) JANI and benthic (−) JANI samples may have had similar numbers of species, but their relative abundances differed markedly, with a shift toward a few more dominant taxa in one community. Consistent with the LEfSe results (Figure 8), fungal communities associated with C. janischii mats may be dominated by a limited number of taxa, particularly chytrids, whereas benthic (−) JANI samples supported a relatively more diverse set of fungal phylotypes. The abundance of chytrids in mat (+) JANI may indicate potential ecological interactions with the C. janischii mat, while the higher number of abundant phylotypes in benthic samples suggests a more heterogeneous fungal community structure in benthic (−) JANI samples.

The decrease in diatom and bacterial diversity with Cymbella janischii blooms is consistent with the trend of invasive organisms causing ecological disturbances that threaten ecosystem balance (Li et al., 2022). Such invasion‐driven factors likely explain the reduced alpha diversity of river benthic diatom and bacterial communities in habitats invaded by C. janischii. The minimal impact of C. janischii on fungal diversity observed in the current study aligns with Schmitz et al. (2023), who reported no difference in soil fungal species richness between invaded and native riparian habitats, suggesting only minor effects from the two invasive plant species (Fallopia japonica and Impatiens glandulifera; Marino et al., 2022; Schmitz et al., 2023; Sun et al., 2017). The observed differences in alpha‐diversity indices between mat (+) JANI and benthic (−) JANI samples can be explained by the contrasting nature of the sampled communities. All mat (+) JANI samples were specifically collected from stones covered with C. janischii mats, which were strongly dominated by C. janischii itself together with a limited set of diatom taxa closely associated with this invasive species. Such dominance is a hallmark of biological invasions, where an invasive species monopolizes space and resources, leading to the suppression of other taxa (Gallardo et al., 2016; Ricciardi & MacIsaac, 2011). This ecological imbalance was reflected in the alpha‐diversity indices: Shannon diversity values were lower in mat samples due to the reduced richness and uneven distribution of taxa, and Pielou's evenness was likewise diminished because the community was disproportionately structured around C. janischii and its associated diatoms. Previous research documented comparable declines in alpha diversity within aquatic microbial communities affected by invasive primary producers (Kelly & Hawes, 2005; Litchman, 2010). On the contrary, the benthic (−) JANI samples, which lacked visible C. janischii colonies and supported a wider range of algae and periphyton, exhibited higher diversity and greater evenness. These patterns illustrate how invasive species can restructure microbial assemblages in riverine ecosystems by reducing both taxonomic diversity and the equitable distribution of species within the community (Strayer, 2010).

The findings of this study showed that bacterial communities associated with Cymbella janischii blooms exhibited high similarity in species composition at different sampling areas, suggesting that the bacterial communities were not simply a subset of the natural benthic assemblages but rather contained selected microbial flora uniquely associated with C. janischii. The existence of mat‐specific microbial associations may also result from the extensive mucilaginous stalk structure of C. janischii mats that serves as a rich source of organic substances for other microorganisms. Additionally, PICRUSt2 analysis suggested that other growth factors, such as cytokinins (zeatin) and biotin (vitamin B7), could also support the growth of microorganisms associated with mat (+) JANI samples. The differences in the unique and dominant taxa between mat (+) JANI and benthic (−) JANI samples collectively suggest that a complex interplay of environmental factors and microbial (bacterial and diatom) associations, including competition, symbiosis, and the production of metabolites, likely plays a significant role in structuring these microbial communities.

Differentially abundant and co‐occurring bacterial species in mat (+) JANI and benthic (−) JANI samples were observed in this study. Bacterial groups belonging to the phylum Bacteroidota were differentially abundant in mat (+) JANI samples. A great variety of polysaccharides produced by diatoms can be hydrolyzed by the vast repertory of carbohydrate‐active enzymes (CAZyme) found in the order Flavobacteriales of the phylum Bacteroidota (Francis et al., 2021; Tran et al., 2023). Bacteroidetes are often observed on diatom‐rich detritus; they degrade complex polymers and colonize solid substrates rapidly (Tran et al., 2023). Flavobacterium spp. were reported to exhibit a symbiotic and growth‐promoting relationship with Navicula muralis and other diatom species by providing nutritional benefits (Jolley & Jones, 1977; Riquelm et al., 1988; Suminto, 1996). In this study, Flavobacterium sp. was identified as a differentially abundant and co‐occurring bacterial taxa associated with mat (+) JANI samples, showing a positive relationship with Cymbella janischii. This potential mutualistic relationship suggests that Flavobacterium sp. may provide nutrients, such as dissolved organic matter, vitamins, and micronutrients, that support the bloom of C. janischii. In addition, Gramella forsetii, a bacterium also belonging to Flavobacteriaceae, also enhanced the growth of Thalassiosira rotula and provided a microenvironment conducive to colonization by other bacteria (such as Pseudophaeobacter sp.) on diatom surfaces (Tran et al., 2023). Bruckner et al. (2008) showed that Bacteroidota strongly influenced the carbohydrates (polysaccharides) produced by diatoms, drastically decreasing the content of soluble polysaccharides and increasing the level of bound polysaccharides (capsule) in C. microcephala.

The bacterial metabarcoding results of Cymbella janischii blooms in this study showed similarities to findings by Suescún et al. (2021), which showed Bacteroidetes was dominant in Didymosphenia geminata blooms in Patagonian aquatic ecosystems. Specifically, Flavobacterium sp. and members of Comamonadaceae were reported as dominant bacteria associated with blooms of D. geminata. This finding aligns with the results of the current study on the bacterial diversity of C. janischii mats, with some relative abundance differences (Figure 2). These bacterial taxa are reported to have metabolic versatility that allowed them to co‐exist during blooms (Eiler & Bertilsson, 2007; Suescún et al., 2021). The bacterial‐metabolite association might be critical to the dynamics of C. janischii blooms, underscoring the need for further studies on the specific chemical processes involved.

Phytohormone‐mediated interactions in Cymbella janischii blooms: Evidence for zeatin‐driven growth enhancement

In this study, the predictive metabolic functional profile of bacterial communities in mat (+) JANI samples revealed enriched pathways related to carbohydrate and amino acid metabolism, indicating higher carbon turnover and increased utilization of carbon sources by bacteria. This enriched carbohydrate metabolism may be indicative of bacterial adaptation to the organic‐rich conditions of the mat, in which polysaccharides, cellulose, and other complex carbohydrates are likely more abundant because of the accumulation of extracellular polymeric substances from the diatoms (Li et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2022). Additionally, the increased amino acid metabolism suggests that nitrogen cycling and protein degradation could also be intensified, supporting microbial growth and potentially facilitating nutrient exchange within the community. This metabolic activity, along with enriched pathways related to the metabolism of terpenoids and polyketides, suggests that bacterial communities play supportive roles in Cymbella janischii blooms by performing growth‐promoting functions. Additionally, mat (+) JANI samples had enriched pathway related to the biosynthesis of zeatin, a class of adenine‐derived cytokinin phytohormones. In plants, zeatin promotes cell division, axillary shoot formation, and root formation and prevents leaf and shoot senescence (Haddadi et al., 2013). Zeatin produced by bacteria may enhance the growth of their host, such as microalgae, by stimulating cell division and promoting nutrient uptake (Fierli et al., 2022; Tsavkelova et al., 2006).

Currently, reports on the effects of phytohormones on microalgae are limited (Fierli et al., 2022). In our study, we present a documented in vitro growth experiment of Cymbella janischii with zeatin, in which the control treatment—consisting of a unialgal culture of C. janischii—showed lower growth compared to treatments with zeatin, supporting its growth‐enhancing effect. The observed effect may result from a direct action of zeatin on the diatom and/or indirectly through bacteria present in the culture. It has also been shown to promote growth in plants, with concentrations of 20 mg · L−1 (~93 μM) in loquat (Eriobotrya japonica) and 20–40 μM in rice (Oryza sativa; Xing et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2023). These concentrations are comparable to those observed in C. janischii for this study. The ability of C. janischii to respond to relatively higher concentrations of zeatin than those typically reported to plants may reflect potential differences in cytokinin signaling, uptake efficiency, and metabolic processing between diatoms and higher plants. Therefore, further investigation is needed to clarify the underlying mechanisms involved. The findings of this study are also similar to those from previous studies in which exogenous application of phytohormones, such as indoleacetic acid, gibberellic acid, methyl jasmonate, and abscisic acid, to diatoms such as Nitzschia sp., Stauroneis sp., and Phaeodactylum tricornutum led to positive growth effects (Fierli et al., 2022). These phytohormones caused dose‐dependent and species‐specific responses, enhancing biomass yield and production of both fucoxanthin and eicosapentaenoic acid in all three diatoms. At higher concentrations (200–300 μM), both growth and stalk formation of C. janischii were consistently lower compared to the 150 μM zeatin treatment. Growth inhibition at elevated zeatin concentrations was also reported by Masekesa et al. (2016), who evaluated the effects of zeatin (0.46–1.83 μM) on shoot induction in sweet potato cultivar Brondal. Their study found that 0.91 μM zeatin resulted in the highest number of shoots, indicating an optimal concentration range for promoting growth, beyond which inhibitory effects were observed. The present results suggest that bacterial‐derived zeatin may contribute to C. janischii bloom formation at appropriate concentrations, while potentially suppressing growth at higher concentrations. This supports the idea that bacterial communities play a significant role in regulating these blooms.

Rather than simply showing an effect on Cymbella janischii growth, these results point to a broader ecological role for bacterial‐derived zeatin. Our findings suggest that zeatin can act as a signaling compound that supports the persistence and bloom formation of C. janischii. The observed increase in stalk formation indicates that its influence extends beyond cell division. It may also enhance EPS production and substrate attachment, which are key processes underlying the development and stability of benthic diatom mats. This interpretation is supported by studies showing that phytohormones can regulate biofilm formation and structural development in microalgae, including effects on EPS production and surface adhesion (Fierli et al., 2022; Xie et al., 2024). Because benthic diatoms commonly exist within microbe‐rich biofilms, growth hormones such as zeatin may facilitate communication across biological groups, linking bacterial metabolic activity with diatom proliferation and community organization (Underwood & Paterson, 2003). This interpretation is consistent with previous studies showing that bacteria can shape diatom growth through the release of vitamins, phytohormones, and other signaling molecules, which influence population structure and dynamics (Costas‐Selas et al., 2024; Croft et al., 2005; Khalil et al., 2024). Together, these observations support the idea that phytohormones extend beyond their traditional roles as intracellular regulators in higher plants. Specifically, zeatin and other bacterial‐derived phytohormones can also act as environmental signaling compounds within microbial biofilms, highlighting the importance of diatom–bacteria interactions in biofilm development and bloom ecology (Van Tol et al., 2017).

Structural and ecological roles of extracellular polysaccharide matrices in shaping Cymbella janischii communities

In the case of Didymosphenia geminata mats, the stalk (extracellular polysaccharide matrix) is capable of trapping particulate matter, soluble nutrients, and trace metals and acts as a diffusion barrier that promotes retention of hydrolyzed substrates and extracellular enzymes that are beneficial for epiphytic organisms such as diatoms (Freeman & Lock, 1995; Somanader et al., 2022). The present observations suggest that Cymbella janischii mats may form similar relationships to those seen in D. geminata mats. In line with this, the co‐occurrence network analysis of C. janischii identified it as a peripheral node, exhibiting a positive relationship with Nitzschia paleacea, suggesting that mutualistic association or environmental conditions exist that favor their coexistence. Melosira varians and Sellaphora nigri showed negative relationships with C. janischii, suggesting the existence of a competitive association where either species may inhibit the growth of the other. Melosira varians, a filamentous diatom known to produce allelopathic substances, may also impact C. janischii, as these compounds inhibit the growth of the diatom Nitzschia palea and, to a lesser extent, the green alga Desmodesmus quadrispina, reducing their growth by up to 59% at a concentration of 500 μg · mL−1 (Leflaive et al., 2008). The competitive associations among these diatoms can occur in either direction, highlighting the complexity of their relationships. These findings, combined with the structural role of polysaccharide matrices in creating microenvironments within diatom mats, underscore the intricate relationships between C. janischii and its associated diatom communities.

The fungal community associated with mat (+) JANI samples showed an unidentified Chytridiomycota as the most differentially abundant fungal taxon. The dominance of fungal taxa belonging to Chytridiomycota during algal blooms was also documented with other microalgal species, including the dinoflagellate Noctiluca scintillans and the cyanobacteria Microcystis and Planktothrix (Marino et al., 2022; McKindles et al., 2020; Rohrlack et al., 2015; Sun et al., 2017). Chytrids such as Malassezia, Rozella, and Dicellomyces were documented to be dominant fungal genera during a bloom of Noctiluca scintillans. The association network of Noctiluca scintillans blooms documented a total of 36 fungal taxa that exhibited relationships with the dinoflagellate, revealing that Malassezia and Rozella displayed a direct positive correlation (Sun et al., 2017). Rhizophydium was also observed as one of the most abundant chytrid species in samples obtained from blooms of Microcystis aeruginosa in two areas of the Laurentian Great Lakes (Marino et al., 2022). These reported chytrid species are considered opportunistic and may play multiple roles in algal blooms, including acting as parasites (negative relationship) or as nutrient producers (positive relationship) by producing vitamins and iron as well as breaking down organic matter. Chytrids exhibit both positive and negative relationships with bloom‐forming algae, depending on the species and environmental conditions (Bacic et al., 1998; Sun et al., 2017).

CONCLUSIONS

Cymbella janischii blooms caused a reduction in diatom and bacterial diversity in rivers. In addition, C. janischii mat (+) JANI samples harbored bacterial communities that were significantly different from those in benthic (−) JANI samples. Co‐occurring and differentially abundant microbial species were identified in C. janischii mat (+) JANI samples, including diatoms (Nitzschia paleacea) and bacteria (Flavobacterium sp.), suggesting that these organisms have a potential ecological relationship with C. janischii during blooms. The bacterial metabarcoding analysis of C. janischii blooms revealed a dominance of Bacteroidetes, aligning with findings by previous studies in Didymosphenia geminata blooms, with Flavobacterium sp. and an unidentified genus from the Comamonadaceae family being similarly prevalent, although with differences in relative abundance. Metagenomic predictions of bacterial functions suggest that mat‐associated bacteria enhance pathways related to carbohydrate, nucleotide, and amino acid metabolism, as well as zeatin biosynthesis. In vitro growth experiments with C. janischii dosed with zeatin confirmed its growth‐promoting effects (cell proliferation and stalk formation). These results provide new insights into microbiome (diatom, bacteria, and fungi) diversity and identify key taxa and metabolites associated with mats, which are crucial in understanding bloom formation in C. janischii.
