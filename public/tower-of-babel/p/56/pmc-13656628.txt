Fear-related psychological experiences in people with Parkinson’s disease: a systematic review of qualitative studies
Authors: Xiaofei Wu, Lilu Wang, Jing Li, Xue Kong, Jinhua Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFear is a multifaceted and pervasive phenomenon among people with Parkinson’s disease. Although some qualitative studies have explored fear-related psychological experiences in this population, there remains a lack of comprehensive integration of these insights. This study aimed to synthesize existing qualitative research on fear-related emotional experiences in people with Parkinson’s disease, and to provide a theoretical basis for developing effective psychological interventions in clinical practice.MethodsA comprehensive computer-based search was conducted across 11 databases, including PubMed, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, CINAHL, PsycINFO, Embase, Scopus, CNKI, Wanfang, VIP, and SinoMed, to identify relevant qualitative studies. The methodological quality of the included studies was appraised using the 2020 Joanna Briggs Institute Critical Appraisal Checklist for Qualitative Research. A thematic synthesis approach was then employed to synthesize the findings.ResultsA total of 17 studies were included in the systematic review. Three themes related to fear-related psychological experiences in people with Parkinson’s disease were identified: (1) multidimensional manifestations, (2) triggering contexts, and (3) coping strategies, comprising 12 categories in total.ConclusionsFear in people with Parkinson’s disease is a multifaceted phenomenon that spans physical, psychological, and social domains. These fear experiences arise from the interaction of multiple factors, and individuals show marked differences in their responses and coping strategies, resulting in diverse patterns of psychological adaptation. The findings underscore the need for healthcare providers to recognize fear as a central element of the Parkinson’s disease experience and to deliver personalized support.Trial registrationThe study protocol was registered in PROSPERO (Registration No. CRD420251246323).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40359-026-05181-2.

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder worldwide, with a prevalence that increases markedly with advancing age [1]. Traditionally, clinical research and interventions have focused on the motor symptoms of PD, such as tremor, rigidity, and bradykinesia. However, with the deepening understanding of the disease, non-motor symptoms, like neuropsychiatric disturbances, have gradually become the focus of attention in clinical practice due to their persistent harm to patients’ quality of life [2].

The progression of PD is marked by substantial individual variability, with considerable differences in symptom profiles, rates of deterioration, and functional outcomes across people with PD [3, 4]. This inherent unpredictability makes it difficult for people with PD to anticipate how the disease will affect their physical functioning, independence, and long-term quality of life, thereby fostering a profound fear of the future. Fear, as a common emotional experience, is typically triggered by perceived threat of physical harm and closely linked to self-protective behavior [5]. People with PD often worry about the progressive loss of their abilities, declining independence, diminished capacity to fulfill work or family roles, and the possibility of becoming a burden to caregivers or society [6, 7]. Within this context, fear is no longer a transient reaction to a single threat but a persistent, multidimensional psychological experience embedded throughout the disease trajectory [8]. This persistent fear not only intensifies psychological distress but may also interact bidirectionally with motor symptoms, creating a self-reinforcing cycle in which fear aggravates symptom fluctuations, while symptom instability further heightens fear [9]. Over time, this vicious cycle can undermine treatment adherence, restrict social participation, and ultimately exert a s lasting negative impact on patients’ overall health and quality of life [10–12].

Despite growing recognition of the clinical importance of fear in PD, existing research has notable limitations. Many quantitative studies rely on standardized scales to assess levels of fear or anxiety [13–15]. Although such instruments provide valuable numerical estimates, they are often unable to capture patients’ subjective experiences, contextual meanings, and deeper psychological needs. Moreover, several qualitative studies have explored fear-related experiences among people with PD from different perspectives. Existing research has primarily described fears associated with falling, loss of independence, disease progression, medication-related concerns, and social embarrassment [16–18]. These studies suggest that fear is not merely a transient emotional response to isolated events, but rather a complex psychological experience deeply embedded within the lived experience of the disease. However, the findings of existing qualitative studies remain fragmented and have not yet been systematically synthesized to provide a comprehensive understanding of the nature, underlying mechanisms, and coping processes of fear-related experiences in PD. Therefore, a qualitative synthesis is needed to integrate the available evidence and generate a more coherent conceptual understanding of fear-related psychological experiences among people with PD.

Systematic review of qualitative studies offers a rigorous approach to integrating diverse findings, overcoming the limitations of individual qualitative studies, and extracting overarching themes [19]. Therefore, this study employs the meta-synthesis approach of qualitative research to systematically synthesize existing qualitative evidence, extract the manifestations of fear-related psychological experiences among people with PD, and comprehensively elaborate on their emotional formation mechanisms and coping strategies. The findings will provide a robust theoretical to guide the development of precise and effective psychological interventions in clinical practice.

Methods

This review was reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines and Enhancing Transparency in Reporting the Synthesis of Qualitative Research (ENTREQ) [20, 21]. The study protocol was registered in PROSPERO (Registration No. CRD420251246323).

Search strategy

A comprehensive computer-based search was conducted across 11 databases, including seven English-language databases (PubMed, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, CINAHL, PsycINFO, Embase, and Scopus) and four Chinese-language databases (CNKI, Wanfang, VIP, and SinoMed). All databases were searched from their inception to November 11, 2025. A combination of MeSH terms and free terms was used. The English search terms included “Parkinson Disease, Parkinson*, PD,” “fear, worry, psychological distress, uncertainty, anxiety,” and “qualitative research, qualitative, content analysis, descriptive research, ethnography, focus group, grounded theory, narrative, phenomenology, thematic analysis, interview.” The detailed search strategies for the English-language databases are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion and exclusion criteria were developed according to the PICoS framework recommended by the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Library of Evidence-Based Medicine, as shown in Supplementary Table 2. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Population (P): people diagnosed with PD; (2) Interest of Phenomenon (I): fear or fear-related subjective psychological experiences among people with PD, including interrelated experiences of fear, anxiety, worry, uncertainty, and psychological distress; (3) Context (Co): home settings, community environments, or relevant healthcare institutions; (4) Study design (S): qualitative studies, including phenomenology, grounded theory, and other qualitative approaches, as well as the qualitative components of mixed-methods studies. Exclusion criteria included: (1) studies for which the full text was unavailable or data were incomplete; (2) duplicate publications; (3) articles not published in Chinese or English; and (4) studies with a quality rating of Grade C.

Study screening and selection

Two researchers with formal training in evidence-based nursing independently conducted the literature search and screened studies strictly according to the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Duplicate records were removed using EndNote X21, after which titles and abstracts were reviewed to exclude clearly irrelevant studies. Full texts were then examined to determine final eligibility. Any discrepancies arising during the screening process were resolved through discussion between the two primary reviewers or, when necessary, by consulting a third reviewer for further evaluation.

Quality assessment

Two researchers independently assessed the quality of the included studies using the 2020 Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist for Qualitative Research. This checklist comprises ten items, each rated as “Yes,” “No,” or “Unclear.” All assessments were conducted independently by the two reviewers, and any discrepancies were resolved by consulting a third researcher.

Data extraction and analysis

Data were extracted from all included studies, covering the year of publication, the first author, country, research methodology, study population, phenomenon of interest, and main findings.

A thematic synthesis approach was used to synthesize the qualitative evidence. Two researchers with formal training in qualitative methodology independently and repeatedly read the findings of each included study. They conducted iterative analysis and interpretation of the original results, followed by coding and comparison of extracted statements and themes. Similar findings were grouped together to form new categories. Finally, based on the logical relationships among these categories, they were further integrated into higher-order, more concise synthesized themes. This process ensured a rigorous and transparent synthesis of the qualitative evidence.

Main findings

Through repeated reading, analysis, and comparison of the original data from the 17 included studies, the findings were summarized into three themes and twelve subthemes, as seen in Fig. 2. Supplementary Table 3 includes a selection of illustrative quotes from participants in the primary studies.

Fig. 2Themes and subthemes

Themes and subthemes

Theme 1: multidimensional manifestations

Category 1: immediate fear from loss of physical control

Fear of falling is one of the most common and tangible fear experiences among people with PD. Many patients describe an intense sense of dread, stemming from the awareness that a fall can occur suddenly, unpredictably, and at any moment in daily life [7, 16, 22, 26, 31, 32]. Beyond the immediate physical pain of falling, patients are particularly fearful of the potential severe consequences, such as fractures, traumatic brain injuries, or prolonged immobility [16, 31, 32]. Anticipating these outcomes amplifies their fear, posing not only a threat to physical health but also deepening concerns about losing independence and increasing reliance on caregivers. In addition, when people with PD are confronted with sudden freezing of gait, constant numbness, or abrupt intensification of tremors, they also experience an immediate sense of fear [7, 16, 25–28, 31, 32].

Category 2: anticipatory fear regarding disease progression

At the time of diagnosis and in disease progression, many people with PD experience an intense sense of anticipatory fear. Upon first receiving the diagnosis, patients often feel profound sadness, shock, and even denial [3, 23, 25, 27, 28, 31]. These reactions create a significant psychological burden and immediately raise concerns about their future quality of life. As the disease advances, the focus of fear gradually shifts toward concerns about progressive physical disability and cognitive decline [7, 17, 22, 25, 27, 32]. People with PD are acutely aware of the unpredictable trajectory of PD, which makes fear of the future more persistent and difficult to alleviate. Compared with the early stage of PD, many begin to notice marked declines in physical function, like slower movements, worsening tremors, and increasing problems with balance [26–28, 31]. These changes substantially reduce their ability to perform daily activities, leading many people with PD to fear further deterioration in mobility, reliance on walking aids, and even the eventual loss of independent ambulation. In addition, the perception that the disease is continually worsening fuels ongoing worries about severe functional impairment, prolonged bedridden states, and complete dependence on others for care. The incurable and progressive nature of PD also evokes a pervasive fear of death and dying [24, 29]. For many people with PD, this future-oriented fear is not only enduring but recurrent, becoming a significant psychological burden that runs throughout the entire course of the illness.

Category 3: social fear triggered by visible symptoms

People with PD commonly experience social fear due to the visible motor symptoms. Manifestations such as tremor, bradykinesia, postural abnormalities, and masked facial expression heighten their sensitivity to others’ perceptions, creating a persistent fear of appearing inappropriate in public or being misunderstood and stigmatized [3, 7, 17, 23, 27, 29–32]. These concerns often evoke feelings of shame, tension, and avoidance, prompting them to adopt self-concealment strategies and intentionally limit social interaction to avoid potentially embarrassing or judgmental situations. Communication difficulties further exacerbate social distress. With the emergence of symptoms such as hypophonia, dysarthria, and slow speech rate, people with PD worry about not being understood or about disrupting conversational flow, which may be perceived as impatience by others, leading them to voluntarily withdraw from verbal interactions [7, 17, 22, 23, 29, 31]. With disease progression, the combined effects of physical disability and emotional distress further diminish their capacity to maintain social relationships, ultimately contributing to increasing social isolation [16, 17, 27, 30, 31].

Category 4: existential fear linked to role diminishment

As the disease progresses, people with PD are increasingly confronted with declining work capacity and shifting family roles. The gradual loss of functional ability often leads to decreased job performance, early retirement, or unemployment, which not only weakens important social connections but also contributes to financial instability [7, 17, 22, 25, 27, 31]. Meanwhile, worsening physical symptoms force many people with PD to rely on family members even for basic daily activities. This gradual transition from independent living to reliance on others often arouses a profound fear of becoming a burden to others [3, 7, 17, 22, 26–28, 31, 32]. When people with PD feel unable to fulfill expected social and family roles, they may experience diminished self-worth and existential doubt, and over time these persistent concerns can deepen feelings of helplessness and worthlessness.

Theme 2: triggering contexts

Category 1: inherent unpredictability of disease

The unpredictability of symptoms, combined with limited knowledge about PD, directly triggers patients’ fear of losing control over their condition. Daily fluctuations of PD symptoms are a common source of fear [7, 22, 25, 26, 28–30, 32]. Motor manifestations such as balance impairment, wearing-off effects, on–off phenomena, and freezing of gait undermine patients’ sense of bodily predictability and give rise to persistent fear. Limited understanding of the disease further amplifies these worries [25, 27, 28]. People with PD not only struggle with immediate physical discomfort but also remain trapped in ongoing anticipation and worry about the next unexpected symptom episode.

Category 2: treatment process and medical decision-making

Currently, medication remains the most effective treatment for PD, yet many people with PD experience fears related to both its efficacy and side effects. The unpredictability of medication response often leaves people with PD in a state of persistent uncertainty [18, 22]. Even those who benefit from treatment may doubt its effectiveness when confronted with daily symptom fluctuations. In addition, the long-term side effects of medication, such as dyskinesia or hallucinations, place people with PD in a therapeutic dilemma, forcing them to balance symptom management against the burden of adverse reactions [18, 30]. Efforts to control these side effects often require additional medications, further increasing the complexity and burden of treatment.

Deep brain stimulation (DBS) is also an optional treatment for PD, but many people with PD struggle with fears about potential brain tissue damage, adverse effects, and intraoperative risks [22, 24, 30]. The uncertainty surrounding treatment outcomes makes decisions about whether and when to undergo DBS a significant source of fear.

Furthermore, awareness of the limitations of current treatments often triggers deep fears about the future [18, 24]. People with PD clearly recognize that existing medications can only offer temporary symptom relief and cannot slow the progression of the disease. This stark realization often traps them in a state of uncertainty and fear, intensifying anxiety about an unpredictable and increasingly challenging future.

Category 3: physical environmental

External physical environments can easily trigger feelings of vulnerability in people with PD. Everyday settings such as uneven pavements, narrow walkways, slippery or irregular surfaces, and stairs or escalators are commonly perceived as potential hazards, as their inherent challenges directly trigger fear of falling [16, 32, 33]. For those with a history of falls, similar settings can elicit conditioned fear responses, leading to excessive caution or restricted mobility. Additionally, in unfamiliar environments or crowded public spaces, people with PD often experience heightened feelings of insecurity, with many reporting a particular fear of being bumped into by others [17, 31, 32].

Category 4: social interaction situations

Social interactions in public settings often trigger fear responses among people with PD. Given the visible nature of both motor and non-motor symptoms, people with PD frequently remain highly vigilant, worrying that their physical manifestations will be noticed and judged by others [22, 23, 31]. Internalized stigma and heightened sensitivity to social evaluation exacerbate this fear and further erode their self-confidence. Many people with PD remain acutely aware of how they may be perceived by others, even in the absence of overt discrimination, they are prone to internalizing negative self-perceptions, which reinforces feelings of shame, fear, and social vulnerability [3, 23]. In addition, during social interactions, people with PD often encounter other people living with PD, which can intensify catastrophic expectations about their own future [3, 22–24, 26]. Witnessing more advanced symptoms in others often heightens fear of disease progression and deepens concerns about experiencing similar outcomes over time.

Theme 3: coping strategies

Category 1: defensive and avoidant response

When confronted with the physiological changes and social exposure brought on by the disease, many people with PD adopt avoidance as their initial coping response. Behavioral avoidance is the most direct manifestation of this coping pattern. Fears of falling, appearing impaired in public, or becoming a burden lead people with PD to deliberately restrict or abandon certain activities and environments, such as avoiding crowded places or slippery surfaces, withdrawing from social or physical activities, and give up previously enjoyed hobbies [3, 16, 26–28, 30, 31]. Although such avoidance may temporarily reduce fear, it gradually narrows their activity space, accelerates physical decline through disuse, and deepens social isolation. Emotional suppression is also common. Many people with PD choose to conceal their fears and vulnerabilities from family members, remain silence around topics related to the illness. They avoid discussing the future, disease progression, or their own emotional experiences [17, 23, 25, 28, 30, 31]. Prolonged suppression and avoidance can hinder psychological adaptation to the illness and intensify feelings of isolation and helplessness.

Category 2: actively seeking family and social support

In contrast, other people with PD adopt more adaptive strategies by actively seeking emotional support and psychological comfort from family members, friends, or community resources [3, 17, 26, 27]. Family support often serves as a crucial source of emotional regulation, providing reassurance, companionship, and practical assistance when fears arise. This ongoing sense of being cared for and understood helps alleviate feelings of loneliness and insecurity. Social support from community resources further expands their coping repertoires. Some people with PD actively participate in support groups, peer activities, or online communities, which offer opportunities to share experiences and exchange advice [3, 24–27]. Hearing others with similar symptoms have encountered the same fears can reduce psychological burden and foster a renewed sense of courage to continue living with the disease.

Category 3: enhancing disease knowledge and self-management ability

To more effectively manage the fear arising from the uncertainty of the disease, some patients actively seek knowledge about their condition to enhance their sense of self-efficacy. Some turn to the internet, educational programs, or patient support groups to better understand the illness, which helps them regain a sense of control over daily life [3, 25]. In terms of treatment and self-management, people with PD often adhere to regular medication schedules, particularly by carefully timing doses before key activities to help maintain physical stability [7, 18]. Some also actively engage in various forms of physical exercise, such as yoga, walking, or stationary cycling, and maintain regular outdoor activities [7, 25, 27, 29]. Through ongoing exploration and practice of diverse lifestyle strategies, people with PD learn to adapt to the evolving challenges of the disease, progressively enhancing their sense of agency and psychological resilience.

Category 4: mental adjustment and cognitive restructuring

In confronting the persistent fear and uncertainty associated with the disease, some people with PD employ a variety of psychological adjustments to reframe their understanding of illness and future, thereby maintaining an inner sense of stability and control. They attempt to manage uncertainty by cultivating positive mindset and an optimistic outlook when encountering challenging situations. Some choose to openly disclose their diagnosis, actively acknowledge the presence of the disease, and make efforts to coexist with it as part of a newly reconstruct sense of self [23, 25, 27–30]. To reduce the emotional burden of unpredictability, some people with PD often shift their focus back to the present moment, prioritizing what matters most, and taking action on the things they wish to do now rather than postponing them [16, 24, 28, 30]. Additionally, they employ a variety of cognitive and emotional regulation strategies to sustain psychological balance, including deliberate attention management, reframing experiences from a positive perspective, fostering hope, and repeatedly affirming acceptance [7, 24, 26, 27].

Discussion

This study conducted a systematic review of 17 qualitative studies and revealed the complexity of fear experiences among people with PD. Rather than a single emotional response, fear emerges as a multidimensional psychological experience grounded in the disease and its impact on bodily functioning, shaped by various triggering contexts, and managed through different coping strategies.

Fear in people with PD is a multidimensional and persistent experience that spans the entire course of the illness and arises from interacting physical, medical, and social factors. At the bodily level, episodes such as falls, gait freezing, and other moments of losing control undermine patients’ sense of capability and restrict daily participation. At the disease-management level, unpredictable symptom trajectories, fluctuating medication responses, and uncertainty surrounding major clinical decisions such as the choice of undergoing deep brain stimulation generate continuous stress and anticipatory worry. At the social level, visible bodily changes, fear of negative evaluation, and concerns about becoming a burden contribute to shame, role erosion, and withdrawal from social activities. These challenges are further amplified by environmental hazards such as slippery floors and narrow or uneven spaces, as well as by pressures encountered in public interactions, leading to heightened vigilance and avoidance behaviors. Together, these interwoven factors shape an ongoing, cross-dimensional fear experience that affects not only symptom-related functioning but also personal identity, dignity, and meaning in life, highlighting the importance of clinical interventions that attend to both symptom control and deeper existential concerns.

Our study showed that many people with PD experience fear because of the unpredictability of symptoms, insufficient understanding of disease progression, uncertainty regarding medication effects, and limited explanations from healthcare professionals. This finding suggests that fear may be partly sustained by insufficient disease-related knowledge and uncertainty in interactions with the healthcare system. Therefore, systematic health education should be considered a core component of nursing interventions. Multiple studies have shown that continuous, staged, and repeatable educational programs help patients better understand the fluctuations of common motor symptoms in PD, non-motor symptom manifestations, and the possible side effects of drug treatment, thereby reducing fear that arises from the unknown [34, 35]. Effective education enhances patients’ knowledge and awareness, strengthens their confidence in decision-making and in interactions with healthcare providers, and ultimately fosters a more positive attitude and improved self-management behaviors [36, 37]. However, some research also points out that people with PD often feel that medical support is insufficient [22, 23, 27]. They report that healthcare professionals do not provide them with adequate emotional support or sufficient explanations about their disease. This lack of support not only heightens patients’ fear of disease progression but also weakens their trust in the healthcare system. Therefore, clinical practice should place greater emphasis on strengthening communication, emotional support, and disease-related education to better meet patients’ needs and reduce fear.

Our findings indicated that fear of losing physical control is often triggered or intensified by environmental challenges, especially in situations where people with PD feel physically unsafe or unable to predict their own movements. This suggests that fear is not only an internal emotional response, but also a context-dependent experience shaped by the interaction between motor symptoms and the surrounding environment. Accordingly, targeted environmental modification should be implemented in both home and public settings. Research shows that practical measures, such as improving lighting, reducing clutter, installing anti-slip devices, upgrading flooring materials, and providing user-friendly assistive walking or support equipment, can significantly reduce fall risks associated with unsafe environments [16, 38, 39]. These modifications help patients regain a greater sense of control and safety during daily activities.

In addition, this review found that social fear and stigma are closely associated with visible symptoms of PD, such as tremor, abnormal gait, reduced facial expression, speech changes, and freezing of gait. These symptoms may expose people with PD to unwanted attention, misunderstanding, negative evaluation, or stigmatization, leading to embarrassment, avoidance, and social withdrawal. This finding suggests that interventions should address both the internal cognitive appraisal and the external social support. Evidence suggests that cognitive behavioral therapy can effectively reduce social anxiety and avoidance behaviors among people with PD [40]. Group-based psychological or rehabilitative programs can also be beneficial. Activities such as dance, singing, musical improvisation, or comedic performance allow patients to engage in shared experiences, enhance emotional expression, strengthen social participation, and gradually diminish fears of negative evaluation [41]. Our synthesis further indicates that peer support groups play a crucial role in reducing social fear [24]. By interacting with others facing similar challenges, patients can build new social connections and gain emotional support, understanding, and psychological comfort from shared experiences. For social fear caused by communication difficulties, speech and language therapy and the lee silverman voice treatment can help improve vocal capacity and functional communication skills, thereby reducing the embarrassment and helplessness in social interactions [41–43]. At the societal level, community-based health education and public awareness campaigns are equally important [23, 44]. By improving the public’s understanding of PD and reducing misconceptions or stigma, a more inclusive and respectful social environment can be fostered, allowing patients to rebuild self-confidence and gradually overcome social withdrawal driven by shame or fear.

Our study revealed that fear in people with PD involves deeper existential concerns, including weakened personal roles, loss of autonomy, increased dependency, and diminished self-worth. This finding suggests that fear is not only related to physical decline, but also to perceived threats to identity, family roles, social participation, and life meaning. Therefore, psychosocial and existentially oriented interventions should be incorporated into nursing care. Narrative therapy can support patients in reconstructing the meaning of their illness experience, reaffirming life purpose, and strengthening self-identity [45]. Family-centered communication and relational support can further help patients recognize their ongoing value within the family and society, enabling them to better navigate the emotional challenges associated with role transitions.

It is also important to recognize that the sources and manifestations of fear in people with PD vary across different stages of the illness, which means that interventions should be aligned with their disease trajectories. In the early stage, fear often centers on the emotional impact of receiving the diagnosis and on anticipatory concerns about the future. Supportive strategies such as emotional reassurance, psychoeducation, and adjustment training can help them navigate the initial period of cognitive and emotional reconstruction. In the middle stage, fear is closely linked to symptom fluctuations, motor complications, and increasing challenges in social interactions [33]. Interventions that enhance physical control strategies and strengthen coping skills for social situations may help alleviate fear. In the late-stage, fear often shifts toward deeper concerns about dependency, functional decline, and the erosion of life meaning [46]. Practices such as mindfulness, existential support, and life review therapy may offer valuable assistance in addressing these profound anxieties [47, 48]. Naturally, these interventions should be adjusted according to individual needs. By tailoring interventions to disease stage, care providers can more effectively respond to the evolving landscape of fear experienced by people with PD.

Our research indicates that people with PD utilize a variety of coping strategies for fear, primarily falling into passive avoidance and active adaptation, consistent with previous literature [49]. While passive strategies such as behavioral avoidance and emotional suppression, may offer temporary relief from fear, they ultimately exacerbate social isolation, erode the social support system, and intensify feelings of loneliness and helplessness [12]. Conversely, active adaptive strategies, including proactively seeking social support, enhancing disease knowledge and self-management skills, and cultivating a positive mindset, have been shown to significantly improve their coping efficacy, psychological resilience, and quality of life [50]. Clinically, shifting people with PD from avoidance to adaptation is crucial and requires a multidimensional support system. Caregivers require training and emotional support to enhance family support quality, communities should establish peer support groups to foster social connection, and healthcare professionals should implement early psychological screening, psychoeducation, and individualized guidance to promote adaptive coping strategies.

Conclusion

The results reveal that fear in PD is a multidimensional and complex phenomenon, encompassing not only concerns about disease progression and loss of symptom control, but also social fear and diminished self-identity stemming from bodily changes. These experiences of fear do not arise in isolation but are the results of the interplay of disease characteristics, treatment experiences, social environments, and physical surroundings. Furthermore, people with PD exhibit distinct individual differences in their responses and coping strategies to fear, reflecting diverse patterns of psychological adaptation across the course of the illness. By integrating patients’ subjective perspectives into care, healthcare providers can better address unmet psychological needs and promote a more holistic and responsive approach to managing PD.

Implications of the study

The findings of this review indicate that fear-related experiences in people with PD are multidimensional. Therefore, in clinical practice, targeted interventions should first be implemented according to different manifestations and sources of fear. For example, health education and shared decision-making may help alleviate uncertainty related to disease progression and treatment., rehabilitation training and environmental modifications may reduce fears of bodily loss of control and falls, peer support and communication skills training may improve social fear and experiences of stigma, and narrative interventions together with family support may address existential concerns. Second, dynamic, individualized, and person-centered care plans should be developed according to disease stage, symptom burden, social context, and coping resources. Finally, multidisciplinary collaboration should be strengthened, involving neurologists, nurses, psychologists, rehabilitation therapists, speech and language therapists, occupational therapists, social workers, and family caregivers, to provide more precise and comprehensive support for people with PD.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this review employed a broad conceptualization of fear-related experiences, acknowledging that fear, anxiety, uncertainty, worry, and psychological distress frequently coexist and overlap in the experiences of people with PD. While this approach allowed us to capture the complexity of patients’ emotional responses, it may have reduced the conceptual specificity of the findings regarding fear as a distinct emotional construct. Second, limiting inclusion to studies published in English or Chinese may have resulted in the exclusion of relevant research reported in other languages. Third, although the included studies reflected diverse cultural backgrounds, the synthesis may not have fully captured how sociocultural factors such as family structure, stigma, and healthcare systems shape the expression and management of fear across different populations. Finally, as this review synthesized existing cross-sectional qualitative evidence, it was not possible to prospectively examine how fear changes over time or how it interacts with the progression of motor and non-motor symptoms. Future research should prioritize high-quality, longitudinal, and culturally sensitive qualitative studies to better understand the dynamic nature of fear in PD and to inform more targeted and effective interventions.
