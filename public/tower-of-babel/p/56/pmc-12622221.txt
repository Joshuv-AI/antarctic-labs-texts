Diagnostic validity of alcohol demand and monetary delay discounting in relation to alcohol use disorder
Authors: Peter Najdzionek, Michael Amlung, Lawrence Sweet, James MacKillop
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundA reinforcer pathology approach to alcohol use disorder (AUD) proposes that high alcohol reinforcing value (high alcohol demand) and overvaluation of immediate rewards (high discounting of future rewards) are critical determinants of problematic drinking. Applied to clinical settings, these indicators have not been examined as potential assessments for use in clinical practice. Toward that end, the current study examined whether reinforcer pathology indicators accurately classify individuals with AUD from recreational drinkers without AUD at levels that would meet clinical accuracy benchmarks.MethodsIn a case-control sample of 267 Canadian adults (180 meeting DSM-5 criteria for AUD), receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were constructed using reinforcer pathology indicators from an alcohol purchase task and a monetary delay discounting tasks.ResultsAnalysis of the ROC curves revealed that three alcohol demand indices from the alcohol purchase task [i.e. Intensity (observed consumption when free), Omax (observed maximum expenditure), and α (a derived index of price-sensitivity)] significantly differed by groups and met established clinical benchmarks for diagnostic differentiation. While delay discounting significantly differed between groups, it did not meet benchmarks as a clinical differentiator.ConclusionsThis study provides evidence that behavioral economic measures of alcohol valuation accurately diagnostically discriminate individuals with AUD from recreational drinkers. Future work should consider utilizing other behavioral economic indices and validating these results in more diverse populations.Keywords: alcohol use disorder, alcohol demand, delay discounting, reinforcer pathology, classification

Introduction

Alcohol use in North America is highly prevalent with over 200 million US adults having tried alcohol during their lifetime and 166 million endorsing alcohol use within the past year (Center for Behavioral Health Statistics and Quality, 2023). Unsurprisingly, problematic alcohol use is pervasive and consequential, with 31% of US fatal motor vehicle accidents involving a driver exceeding the legal blood alcohol content limit (Stewart, 2023). 11% of US adults met criteria for a diagnosis of alcohol use disorder (AUD) within the past year (Center for Behavioral Health Statistics and Quality, 2023), a condition highly comorbidity with other psychiatric disorders such as anxiety (Lai et al., 2015), depression (Lai et al., 2015), posttraumatic stress disorder (Debell et al., 2014,), and personality disorders (Helle et al., 2019). Beyond acute adverse outcomes and psychiatric disorders, alcohol is a category 1 carcinogen and has been implicated in throat, lung, and breast cancer (Shield et al., 2022). Collectively, these consequences contribute to an estimated financial cost of 2.6% of a country’s gross domestic product based on a meta-analysis of the societal cost of alcohol (Manthey et al., 2021) and underscore the public health importance of understanding alcohol use and misuse.

Reinforcer pathology is a contemporary framework for understanding AUD and other substance use disorders. Rooted in behavioral economics, and specifically the integration of operant learning theory and microeconomics, reinforcer pathology posits addiction is a result of dysfunction in distinct behavioral processes: high delay discounting and a high demand for substance. Delay discounting is a behavioral economic index of choice, encapsulating the extent to which rewards lose perceived value based on their delay in time (Madden & Bickel, 2013). By analyzing responses to a series of binary questions (e.g. would you rather have $30 today or $60 in a week?), an individual’s temporal discounting function can be empirically quantified using a hyperbolic discounting equation (Mazur, 1987), and it is well established that individuals with AUD tend to discount future rewards more steeply, preferentially opt for smaller immediate rewards (Claus et al., 2011; Petry, 2001; Halcomb et al., 2022). Meta analyses have consistently found that delay discounting is associated with increased alcohol consumption and alcohol-related problem severity, with larger effects sizes in clinical samples (MacKillop et al., 2011; Amlung et al., 2017).

Elevated demand for a substance, the second component of reinforcer pathology, exemplifies dysfunctional overvaluation of a substance (Acuff et al., 2023a; Bickel et al., 2014). From this perspective, an individual’s value of alcohol can be evaluated via an operant task with escalating levels of effort required for alcohol access (Bigelow, 2001) or a hypothetical alcohol purchase task (APT) that assesses alcohol consumption at an array of increasing prices (Murphy & MacKillop, 2006). In both cases, responses at different costs can be plotted as a demand curve and several indices reflecting different valuations of alcohol can be derived (Martínez-Loredo et al., 2021). The most common indices are: (i) Intensity (i.e. amount of alcohol consumed when free or at a minimal price), (ii) Omax (i.e. maximal expenditure on alcohol), (iii) Pmax (i.e. price of alcohol at which Omax occurred), (iv) Breakpoint (i.e. the price which fully suppresses consumption), and (v) α, the rate of change of demand elasticity, which is a calculated measure of price sensitivity. In a large number of studies, APTs have indicated heavy alcohol use is associated with higher alcohol demand indicators (e.g. increased expenditure or increased consumption despite increasing prices), as supported by recent meta-analysis of 50 APT studies (Martínez-Loredo et al., 2021).

While reinforcer pathology is a theoretical framework for understanding the etiology of addiction, there is a growing interest in whether these indices of reinforcer pathology are associated with clinical diagnoses and outcomes. For example, alcohol demand and consumption has been shown to decrease following brief motivational interventions (Colby et al., 2018; Gex et al., 2022), and APT indices have been found to predict alcohol consumption post-intervention (MacKillop & Murphy, 2007; Gex et al. 2022). Episodic future thinking (Atance & O’Neill, 2001), a behavioral intervention to extend a person’s time horizon, has been shown to reduce alcohol demand (and occasionally delay discounting) in both AUD (Athamneh et al., 2022; Patel & Amlung, 2020; Snider et al., 2016) and college-age drinking populations (Voss et al., 2022). When considering the etiology of AUD, reinforcer pathology indicators predict future alcohol consumption (Petzold et al., 2023), and subsequently decrease as problematic drinking decreases (Acuff et al., 2023b). When considered in sum, alcohol demand and delay discounting may inform the development and treatment of AUD.

To date, however, relatively little research has examined how reinforcement pathology indicators may help classify or categorize problematic drinking. Minhas et al., (2020) used latent profile analysis to find three subgroups within a heavy episodic drinking sample, finding subgroups characterized by heavier drinking exhibited higher alcohol demand, substance-related reinforcement, and steeper delay discounting. Another recent publication used logistic regressions and receiver operating curves to determine whether reinforcement pathology indicators can accurately distinguish high intensity drinkers from heavy episodic drinkers (Savage et al., 2023). While the regressions found differences in demand Intensity and α between drinking profile, the receiver operator characteristic (ROC) curves suggested only Intensity could accurately distinguish between groups (Savage et al., 2023).

Indeed, ROC curve analysis is an established methodology for assessing a binary diagnostic test. Originating from signal-detection theory (Tanner & Swets, 1954), ROC curve analysis seeks to distinguish true cases from false classifications (Nahm, 2022). Its relative simplicity has led to its incorporation across multiple biomedical domains such as anesthesiology (Jung et al., 2019), radiological medicine (Wang et al., 2020; Lee et al., 2020), genomics (Sui et al., 2021), general test validity (Osman et al., 2001; Annunziata et al., 2020), and most recently the diagnostic validity of behavioral economic indicators (Savage et al., 2023). ROC curves metrics elucidate the accuracy and optimal classification value of a predictor. As an example, Savage et al. (2023) found Intensity can reliably classify different problematic drinkers, with the optimal distinguishing cut-off value of 7.5 drinks being 71% accurate. This demonstrates how utilizing ROC curves and reinforcer pathology indicators may provide insight into problematic drinking behaviors.

Despite these promising findings, no previous studies have investigated whether reinforcer pathology indicators are sufficiently robust to distinguish individuals with AUD from recreational drinkers. To address this, the current study investigated whether reinforcer pathology indicators may be viable clinical measures for identifying AUD using standard benchmarks for diagnostic assessment. Using indicators from two well-established measures (monetary delay discounting and APT), the diagnostic classification utility of empirical behavioral economic indicators was evaluated in adults with AUD and demographically-matched low-risk drinkers using ROC curves. In line with reinforcer pathology theory, we hypothesized that reinforcer pathology indicators would significantly differentiate individuals with AUD from current drinkers without AUD and meet established benchmarks for classification discrimination. Specifically, based on previous meta-analyses of reinforcer pathology indicators (MacKillop et al., 2011; Amlung et al., 2017; Martínez-Loredo et al., 2021) and their relationship with substance use, we predicted rates of monetary discounting (i.e. delay discounting k values) and APT indicators (i.e. Intensity, Omax, Breakpoint, and α) would accurately differentiate the two groups of drinkers. We did not hypothesize that Pmax would be discriminating because its null association with alcohol misuse in the recent meta-analysis (Martínez-Loredo et al., 2021). Finally, as previous studies have consistently found two principal components capture a majority of variance among APT indicators (Cui et al., 2021; Minhas et al., 2021; MacKillop et al., 2009), we conducted a principal component analysis (PCA) of APT indicators to both replicate previous findings and test whether consolidated components reflecting shared variance were superior discriminators of AUD compared to the individual indicators via ROC curves.

Materials and methods

Participants

To maximize sample size, a case-control study design was constructed from two separate brain imaging research projects with overlapping inclusion/exclusion criteria, with a final sample of 180 adults meeting Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders-V criterion for AUD (AUD+) and 87 recreational drinkers without AUD symptoms (AUD–). All participants were recruited from the Hamilton, Ontario community via physical and digital advertisements. Both studies were approved by the Hamilton Integrated Research Ethics Board (#7458 & #4551). The first sample consisted of 99 individuals who met diagnostic criteria for moderate or severe alcohol use disorder (AUD+; >3 symptoms) and exceeded the National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism (NIAAA) weekly drinking guidelines (≥14 drinks for men, ≥7 drinks for women) and 90 alcohol drinkers matched on age, sex, education, and income, who exhibited no diagnostic criteria for AUD and did not exceed NIAAA guidelines. The second project used a within-subjects design to study 159 heavy drinkers who exceeded the NIAAA weekly alcohol consumption guidelines (see Marsden et al., 2023 for additional details). A subsample of 90 participants also met criteria for moderate or severe AUD and were included in the current harmonized AUD case cell. For both samples, complete inclusion and exclusion criteria are found in Supplemental materials. Precautions were taken to ensure: (i) the combined sample did not include overlapping participants (i.e. each participant was a unique individual), and (ii) all reported behavioral measures were methodologically identical (i.e. standardized administration with identical questions). Though both parent studies used magnetic resonance imaging methods, all assessments reported were administered in a conventional setting outside of the scanner. From the total sample, 12 participants were excluded for missing APT data or response patterns that suggest low attention or effort (see APT Processing for specifics); the final combined sample contained 267 participants (180 AUD+, 87 AUD–). The final sample was primarily comprised of Caucasian adults with at least some postsecondary education and a median annual income of $60 000–$90 000 Canadian Dollars; full demographic characteristics are found in Table 1.

Measures

Demographics

Participants disclosed key demographic information such as age, sex at birth, income, and educational attainment using a self-report form.

Alcohol involvement measures

Drinking frequency and quantity were assessed using a 28-day timeline follow back (TLFB) interview in which participants recalled their number of standard alcohol drinks consumed each day within the past 28 days (Sobell & Sobell, 1992). Processed TLFB data yielded three measures: a volumetric measure of alcohol consumed (average drinks per week), and two frequency of alcohol consumption measures in percent drinking days (percentage of days in which alcohol was consumed), and percent heavy drinking days [percentage of days with heavy alcohol consumption, as defined by NIAAA (>4 drinks for men, >3 drinks for women)]. Alcohol-related consequences were assessed using the Drinker Inventory of Consequences (DrInC) total score (Miller et al., 1995). The DrInC tallies consequences related to alcohol consumption such as physical consequences (e.g. hangovers, injuries resulting from alcohol consumption) and social relationship consequences.

Alcohol purchase task

The APT measures a participant’s self-reported consumption of alcohol standard drinks across a spectrum of prices (Murphy & MacKillop, 2006); in this study, monetary prices ranged from $0–$40 Canadian Dollars per drink (i.e. $.00, $.25, $.50, $1.00, $1.50, $2.00, $2.50, $3.00, $5.00, $6.00, $7.00, $8.00, $9.00, $10.00, $11.00, $12.00, $13.00, $14.00, $15.00, $16.00, $18.00, $20.00, $22.00, $24.00, $26.00, $28.00, $30.00, $35.00, $40.00). Prices were presented in ascending order. Although no alcohol was consumed, participants were instructed to estimate their consumption as if they would receive the drinks and consume them in a single episode (i.e. cannot save drinks for another time). The responses produce consumption and expenditure curves, which were further analyzed into five behavioral demand indicators: (i) Intensity, the quantity of drinks consumed when free; (ii) Omax, the maximal expenditure; (iii) Pmax, the price at which Omax occurs; (iv) Breakpoint, the price which fully suppresses consumption; and (v) α, the rate of change in demand elasticity (demand elasticity is an index of price sensitivity).

Delay discounting

Two 5-trial adjusting delay discounting tasks were employed to estimate monetary discounting rates (k) for $100 & $1000 (Koffarnus & Bickel, 2014). Participants were asked whether they would prefer a smaller immediate reward, or a future reward 100% larger than the immediate reward (e.g. would you prefer $50 now, or $100 in 3 days); the future reward timeframe ranged from 1 hour to 25 years in accordance with Koffarnus & Bickel’s protocol. The delay length was iteratively increased or decreased over five choices to approximate a participant’s effective delay 50 (ED50) value. Two control questions with logical correct answers (e.g. “would you prefer $50 now or $100 now?”) were included per task, to gage participant attention and ensure quality control. The inverse of these values (i.e. 1/ED50) is equivalent to the k value derived from the hyperbolic discounting function (e.g. Mazur, 1987). See Koffarnus and Bickel (2014) for additional details on task design.

Data processing and statistical analyses

Quality control

APT quality control processing began by plotting demand curves using the observed values and using the three-criterion algorithm outlined in Stein et al. (2015) to identify and remove nonsystematic purchase task data. Participants with trend violations (inadequate decreases in demand despite increasing prices; delta = 0.025), bounce ratio violations (increases in consumption despite increasing price; i.e. an instance of bounce violation occurred when consumption was 25% higher than consumption at the preceding price, participants were excluded if bounce violations occurred ≥10% of all price points), or any reversal from zero violations (demand despite previous cessation), were removed from the sample. After conducting quality control, seven participants were removed for bounce violations (four individuals from AUD group), and five participants were removed for incomplete data (all from AUD group). Winsorization occurred at the price level, where outlying consumption values (criterion z > 3.99) were reduced to one value above the highest non-outlining value. Breakpoint, Intensity, Omax, and Pmax were observed values based on participants’ responses. The variable α was derived from fitting the data using the exponentiated demand curve model found in Koffarnus et al. (2015) using the R package “PThelper” (Belisario et al., 2023). The bounding parameter (k) was empirically calculated by log10 transforming the mean average consumption at the lowest and highest price points across all participants (k = 1.8). R-squared values suggested the model fit the overall sample data well (R2 = 0.99). Both observed demand and derived (i.e. α) demand indicator outlying values (criterion z > ±3.99) were winsorized to 1 above the next highest non-outlying value (or 0.001 in the case of α). Figures containing consumption, expenditure, and demand curves are provided in supplemental materials. Shapiro–Wilk tests revealed all APT indicators were not normally distributed, the indicators were transformed via base 10 logarithm as suggested by Box–Cox transformation analyses.

For delay discounting, all participants correctly answered two control questions. Participant k values were transformed using base-10 logarithm to address distribution skew. Pearson correlation revealed delay discounting rates were very highly correlated (r = .856, P < .001), so the two were consolidated (AvgDD) by averaging the untransformed k values, then normalizing the data via a base-10 logarithm.

Statistical analyses

Preliminary demographic differences were explored with Student’s t-tests and Chi-squared tests. Independent-samples two tail t-tests were conducted to assess whether reinforcer pathology indicators (APT: Intensity, Breakpoint, Omax Pmax, α; delay discounting k values) differed between groups. Similar analyses of covariance (ANCOVA) were conducted to examine the robustness of findings when incorporating income as a covariate.

ROC curves were constructed to assess the diagnostic accuracy of the reinforcer pathology indicators predicting AUD. These graphs plot the true positive rate (proportion of correctly classified AUD cases) against the false positive rate (proportion of non-AUD individuals incorrectly classified as AUD) at every value of the reinforcer pathology indicators; a diagonal line through the center represents when a test cannot distinguish between the two conditions (overall accuracy is 50%). The area under the ROC curve (AUC) reflects the indicator’s overall ability to distinguish between problematic and recreational drinkers, with higher values indicating better predictions; an indicator with an AUC ranging from 0.70 to 0.79 has acceptable distinguishing ability, 0.80 to 0.89 is excellent, and >0.90 is outstanding (Mandrekar, 2010). The maximum value of Youden’s Index, a value relying discriminatory power, provides a cutoff point where a diagnostic test would be most accurate (Youden, 1950). Further diagnostic information at Youden’s Index such as specificity (true positive rate), sensitivity (true negative rate), positive predictive power (probability a positive test results is accurate), negative predictive power (probability a negative test results is accurate), and overall diagnostic accuracy are also reported. ROC curves were constructed with both untransformed & transformed behavioral indicators; the resulting metrics were nearly identical. We decided to report untransformed measures (except for conventional delay discounting k values) to facilitate interpretation of Youden’s Index; transformed metrics are found in supplemental materials.

For the PCA, consistent with previous literature (MacKillop et al., 2009; Cui et al., 2021), an oblique rotation (direct oblimin) approach allowed multivariate components to correlate, and components which met Kaiser’s criterion (eigenvalue >1) were retained. Individuals’ component loading scores were extracted and utilized to create additional ROC curves of the retained factors using the previously described methods.

All statistical analyses were conducted in RStudio, using R scripting (version 4.3.0). Additional packages used include “psych” (demographic analyses and PCA), “pROC” (constructing ROC curves), “GPArotation” (rotation of PCA), “PThelper” (APT analysis), and “ggplot2” (visualizations).

Results

Comparisons between individuals with AUD and controls

All descriptive statistics and preliminary group differences are in Table 1. As expected, independent samples t-tests revealed individuals with AUD drank more often, engaged in higher rates of risky drinking, and incurred more adverse drinking consequences than non-AUD drinkers. All behavioral economic indicators significantly differed between groups [APT indicators: ps < .001, ds > (−1.22 − +1.18); DD indicators: ps < .05, ds = .28, .30, .31], except for Pmax (P = .732), as anticipated. Despite no significant income differences, these contrasts were repeated using ANCOVAs to address potential influences of income; all significant reinforcer pathology indicators remained significant (ps < .001).

Receiver operating characteristic curves for alcohol valuation indicators

ROC analysis revealed some alcohol demand indicators can accurately determine AUD status (Table 2, Fig. 1). For α, Intensity, and Omax, each exhibited AUCs >0.80 and were >70% accurate at the maximum Youden’s Index, meeting criteria for “good” indicators with discriminatory ability (Carter et al., 2016). Breakpoint and Pmax were unable to properly differentiate AUD from non-AUD, with Pmax performing at levels indicating nearly random classification (AUC = 0.46).

Discussion

This study investigated the clinical value of reinforcer pathology indicators in distinguishing adult drinkers based on AUD status. As a group, individuals with AUD exhibited statistically significantly higher alcohol demand and delay discounting, and the ROC curve analyses of reinforcer pathology indicators revealed alcohol demand indices Intensity, Omax, and α accurately discriminated between AUD+ and AUD– groups according to established benchmarks. Furthermore, APT data condensed via PCA yielded the highest accuracy at 79% for the Amplitude factor. Together, these findings suggest elevated alcohol demand assessed with the APT may be a valid marker of AUD with potential clinical utility.

These APT findings are consistent with previous research investigating problematic alcohol use and provide novel extensions to prior research. A previous meta-analysis concluded alcohol demand indices such as Intensity, Omax, and α are robustly associated with negative alcohol-related outcomes, with Intensity being the index most strongly associated with hazardous drinking, heavy drinking, and alcohol-related problems (Martínez-Loredo et al., 2021). In the current study, Intensity exhibited adequate discrimination, but both Omax and α had higher diagnostic accuracy. Considering these three indicators comprised the Amplitude component, the overall best performing indicator, one interpretation is that evaluating alcohol demand at the level of latent behavioral economic variables (i.e. PCA components) may offer more resolution in screening for problematic alcohol use. On the other hand, calculating a composite score is both substantively more complex and less interpretable than the individual indicators, and improvement was only marginal, suggesting the individual indicators may in fact be more viable. This warrants examination in future studies. Surprisingly, the optimal classification value for Intensity (7.5 drinks) was identical to a different recent reinforcer pathology study using ROC curves to differentiate high intensity drinkers from heavy episodic drinkers (Savage et al., 2023). Intensity values above 7.5 may be a generalizable cut-off for problematic and unhealthy drinking patterns, although further validation is necessary.

Contrary to our hypothesis, delay discounting rates could not accurately classify individuals with AUD from recreational drinkers. Despite significant delay discounting differences between AUD+ and AUD–, it is possible the effect size difference was not large enough to accurately differentiate AUD status. Considering the 5-trial delay discounting task only has 32 potential outcomes, increased resolution using a task such as the adjusting amount delay discounting (Frye et al., 2016) may increase temporal discounting’s discriminatory power. Alternatively, it is possible that nonmonetary delay discounting might be more applicable than monetary discounting when predicting AUD status. Historically, alcohol discounting tasks have revealed larger effect sizes than monetary discounting (Petry, 2001). More recently, novel studies investigating alcohol and monetary cross-commodity delay discounting have found heterogeneous reward delay discounting is strongly associated with alcohol consumption and problematic alcohol use (Taylor et al., 2023; Moody et al., 2017). Evaluating whether alcohol-based delay discounting or cross-commodity delay discounting can distinguish between AUD individuals and non-AUD individuals is a logical direction for future work. While our hypothesis was not supported, delay discounting remains clinically relevant, as samples with addictive disorders (Albein-Urios et al., 2014; Madden et al., 1997; Białaszek et al., 2017) and nonsubstance related psychiatric disorders (Amlung et al., 2019) discount at steeper rates compared to healthy samples. The transdiagnostic relevance of monetary delay discounting may decrease its viability as a diagnostic test for AUD or other specific individual diagnoses. Alternatively, incorporating delay discounting with other reinforcer pathology measures may further improve predictive models, another direction for future study.

It is worth noting that our APT principal components analysis results are somewhat inconsistent with previous literature. Historically, the Persistence factor has comprised of Breakpoint, Omax, Pmax, and α, with the Amplitude factor driven by Intensity and Omax (MacKillop et al., 2009; Bidwell et al., 2012; Cui et al., 2021). Our findings revealed that α principally loaded on Amplitude, however, this pattern is not entirely unexpected, as it has been observed previously (Cui et al. 2021; Minhas et al., 2021). Indeed, one study evaluating factor loadings for both the alcohol and cigarette purchase task found both patterns of factor loading (α loading onto Persistence factor for APT, and α loading onto the Amplitude factor for cigarette purchase task; Cui et al. 2021). It is also worth noting that the derivations of α have varied across studies and could potentially affect the relations among indices (Minhas et al., 2021). Clarifying whether there is a consistent generalizable latent structure of demand indices is a priority in future studies.

While this study investigated two components of reinforcement pathology, there’s an important third component, which was absent from the current study, alternative reinforcers. Alternative reinforcers encapsulate how environmental factors may influence substance use disorder. Delay discounting and substance demand are often evaluated using closed economy paradigms (Kearns, 2019), where participants have constrained or limited options; in reality, we exist in a world where choices are abundant. Understanding this context, an increasingly popular area of research investigates the reinforcing value of alternative reinforcers other than substances (Bickel et al., 2021; Acuff et al., 2023a). This can be examined with cross-commodity delay discounting (Pritschmann et al., 2021) or evaluating hedonic activities (Acuff et al., 2019) as two examples. While the data were unavailable for analysis within our sample, a lack of alternative reinforcers is nevertheless associated with substance use disorders (Bickel et al., 2014, MacKillop, 2016; Acuff et al., 2023a). Future work should include alternative reinforcement measures and evaluate its diagnostic predictive value.

Although further work is needed before the APT validated for the clinician’s assessment toolkit, it nonetheless has several advantages to the traditional assessment measures. When using any self-report data, potential personal biases may obscure accurate reports on face-valid measures, such as the Alcohol Use Disorder Identification Test (Saunders et al., 1993) and asking about consuming alcohol at various prices may avoid positive impression management from individuals. By engaging with neutral language and abstract questions, the APT may also negate (or at least, lessen) stigmatizing aspects associated with reporting alcohol misuse (e.g. reporting impaired control or interpersonal problems). Additionally, considering α is an opaque-derived behavioral economic metric, it would be minimally affected by any personal biases. For these reasons, the APT should be considered as a promising future clinical measure for clinicians.

Strengths and limitations

One strength of this experiment is the sample. By combining two samples with overlapping criteria, this study assembled 267 clinically diagnosed participants, providing high statistical power and increasing confidence in the results. Furthermore, the sample was broadly distributed across ages, increasing confidence that the results will generalize to the target AUD population, not just commonly studied subgroups, such as university students or emerging adults in general. There are also some limitations. While the sample was well-balanced for sex at birth, it lacked representation from underrepresented groups. Future work should consider validating these results with larger datasets with minority populations, since experiences of discrimination increase the likelihood of developing AUD (Glass et al., 2020; Fish & Exten, 2020). Validating these results in vulnerable minority populations would help assess the generalizability of our results. Future work might also consider evaluating additional measures of reinforcer pathology such as alternative reinforcers (Weinsztok et al., 2024; Bickel et al., 2024), which was unavailable in this dataset. Lastly, regarding APT processing, it is understood that generating individual demand curves may bring unique challenges and limitations (Kaplan et al., 2021), alternative methods of demand curve processing (such as mixed-model effects) may be useful to investigate.

Conclusions

This study investigated whether core measures from reinforcer pathology theory were promising clinical measures for identifying AUD. Analysis of ROC curves revealed the alcohol demand measures Intensity, Omax, and α were indeed viable individual indicators and the most accurate and discriminative measure was the Amplitude component from a PCA, itself an aggregate of Intensity, Omax, and α. Delay discounting was associated with AUD status but did not adequately discriminate between the groups. These results extend previous literature implicating reinforcer pathology indicators with AUD, and provide initial evidence that the APT may have promise as a clinical assessment.
