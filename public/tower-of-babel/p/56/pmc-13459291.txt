First breed-pool whole genome sequencing of egyptian sheep: a comprehensive genomic atlas revealing diversity and candidate genes for production and adaptation
Authors: Nada N. A. M. Hassanine, Ali H. Amin, Elsayed E. Hafez, Mohamed Morsi M. Ahmed, Ahmed H. Ismail, Asmaa Zayed Mohamed, Heba Abd El Halim, Ghada M. El-Sayed, Ibrahim A. A. Adss, Khaled H. Radwan, Mabrouk A. Abo-Zaid, Amr M. A. Rashad, Hosameldeen Mohamed Husien, Ahmed A. Saleh, Mengzhi Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis research provides the first extensive breed‑pool whole‑genome sequencing (WGS) analysis across five Egyptian sheep populations: Barki (BAR), Rahmani (RAH), their crossbred offspring (CRS), Ossimi (OSI) and Awassi (AWI).ObjectivesTo establish a genomic atlas of the genetic architecture of production and adaptation in Egyptian sheep, providing a baseline for future candidate gene discovery and conservation strategies.MethodsThrough Illumina sequencing of 120 samples, we compiled a dataset exceeding 470 Gb, with mean coverage depths spanning 24.2x to 41.3x. Variant profiling, functional annotation, KEGG pathway analysis, and independent structural variant analysis were conducted. Phenotypic data were collected and validated through qRT-PCR gene expression analysis.ResultsVariant profiling revealed between 11.9 and 17.4 million SNPs per breed after stringent filtering. Heterozygosity patterns (population‑level estimates) differed substantially between groups, recorded at 60.41% in the CRS crossbred versus 74.92–85.01% in the purebred lines. Functional annotation identified conserved enrichment related to xenobiotic detoxification and lipid metabolism. KEGG pathway analysis prioritized the PPAR signalling pathway (map03320) and fatty acid metabolism (map01212) as highly significant (p < 0.0001). Independent structural variant analysis identified distinct genomic hotspots on chromosomes 2, 6 and 18, overlapping candidate genes; FABP4, KAP cluster and MSTN implicated in the regulation of fat deposition and muscle development. Phenotypic data confirmed a high degree of breed divergence (p < 0.001). RAH and CRS individuals reached higher body condition scores (BCS 4.31‑ 4.53) and increased fat deposition, whereas BAR was significantly leaner (BCS 2.53). The highest trimmed meat yields were observed in CRS (23.95 kg) and RAH (18.40 kg) (p < 0.001), with RAH also displaying the highest intramuscular fat content at 4.20%. qRT‑PCR validation showed elevated expression of lipogenic genes (ACACA, FASN and FABP4) in fat‑tailed breeds and differential expression of myogenic regulators (MSTN and IGF‑1) correlating with muscularity variations.ConclusionsThe current findings establish a genomic atlas for the genetic architecture of production and adaptation in Egyptian sheep, providing a baseline for future genetic and conservation strategies. Formal selection signature analyses, such as XP‑EHH and iHS are recommended for subsequent studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12896-026-01199-x.

Introduction

The domestic sheep (Ovis aries) is one of the earliest domesticated livestock, originated in the Fertile-Crescent region between Eastern Anatolia and the Zagros Mountains, approximately: 8,000 to 10,000 years before present, as evidenced by archaeological findings and genetic analyses [1, 2]. Ovis aries is designated by the FAO as one of the five most significant livestock species globally [3], sheep populations have expanded worldwide through human migration and trading activities [4], demonstrating a remarkable 34% growth since the year 2000 that outpaces the expansion rates observed in cattle, buffaloes, goat and pig populations [5–7]. Recent comprehensive genomic study involving 355 sheep genomes have provided enhanced understanding of the intricate demographic history variations in phenotypes and selective pressures that have shaped modern breeds during and after domestication [8]. This evolutionary journey has resulted in extraordinary phenotypic plasticity, enabling the development of specialized breeds suited to diverse agricultural systems and environmental conditions [9].

Within the African agricultural framework, especially Egypt, sheep represent a vital economic resource, particularly supporting the livelihoods of small-scale and marginal farmers [10]. The national flock, numbering approximately 5.5 million animals, makes substantial contributions to domestic red meat supplies. Indigenous breeds primarily: Barki (BAR), Rahmani (RAH), Ossimi (OSI) and Awassi (AWI) constitute the genetic backbone of the Egyptian sheep sector, representing more than 65% of the total population. In addition to these pure breeds, a crossbred population (CRS) derived from an F1 generation cross between Rahmani (dam) and Barki (sire) is increasingly used in semi‑intensive systems. This cross was developed to combine the fat‑tailed, high‑growth characteristics of Rahmani with the desert‑adapted hardiness of Barki, producing a multipurpose animal with enhanced meat yield and hybrid vigour. The CRS population is not a distinct breed but rather a first‑generation crossbred. These breeds have evolved distinctive adaptive characteristics: BAR thrives in the arid North Western Coastal Zone; RAH exhibits enhanced resistance to gastrointestinal parasites in the Nile Delta region; OSI demonstrates versatile adaptation across Middle Egypt; while AWI stands out as a prolific fat-tailed dairy breed capable of producing exceeding 300 L per lactation cycle [11, 12].

The conservation of genetic diversity within these breeds represents a critical resource for addressing contemporary production challenges across varying environments, supporting continuous genetic advancement, and enabling swift adaptation to evolving breeding goals [13, 14]. Precise characterization of genetic variation both among and within these populations establishes a foundation for accelerated improvement of commercially valuable traits [15, 16]. Most economically important characteristics in small ruminant exhibit quantitative inheritance, being influenced by numerous small-effect genes distributed throughout the genome in combination with environmental influences [17]. Although conventional breeding strategies utilizing phenotypic records, pedigree information and progeny testing have been implemented, progress has been constrained by the modest heritability estimates typical of many production traits [18].

The emergence of modern genomic technologies has transformed animal breeding paradigms. Next-generation-sequencing platforms and high-density-SNP genotyping arrays now facilitate whole-genome-sequencing (WGS), enabling comprehensive detection of major genes, candidate loci, selection signatures, selective sweeps and quantitative trait loci linked to economically important characteristics [19–21]. The recent development of livestock pangenomes has further improved our capacity to identify structural variations absent from reference assemblies [22, 23]. Genomic selection methodologies leveraging both 2nd - and 3rd -generation sequencing technologies present transformative opportunities, allowing breeders to identify animals with favourable attributes including longevity, reproductive efficiency and disease resistance at early developmental stages with enhanced precision [19]. Investigations in crossbred sheep populations have validated the efficacy of genomic strategies for unraveling heterosis mechanisms and identifying performance-associated genes [24, 25].

Notwithstanding these technological advances, a substantial knowledge gap persists concerning the comprehensive genomic characterization of Egyptian sheep breeds [11]. The genetic architecture determining their distinctive adaptive and productive traits remains largely unexamined at the whole-genome resolution [26–28]. Conventional breeding approaches continue to present limitations including extended time requirements, substantial costs, and the occasional emergence of undesirable characteristics during selection programs. This research directly addresses this deficiency through systematic investigation of five economically significant Egyptian sheep populations BAR, RAH, Rahmani x Barki Crossbred (CRS), OSI and AWI. These breeds represent multipurpose animals well-adapted to semi-arid grassland ecosystems, each displaying specialized economic attributes: BAR is distinguished by superior wool quality; AWI by exceptional dairy production; RAH and OSI by premium meat characteristics; and CRS by balanced multipurpose performance, collectively establishing them as ideal genetic models for elucidating genomic regions governing commercially important traits. While a single‑individual whole‑genome resequencing study has been reported for Jordanian AWI [29], no WGS data are currently available for the Egyptian populations of BAR, RAH, CRS, OSI and AWI, and prior genomic studies on these breeds have relied exclusively on SNP‑chip, microsatellite and transcriptomic approaches [30].

On the other side, phenotypic characterization, particularly through body measurements, is a fundamental component of breed conservation and classification programs, enabling the objective description of breed-specific morphology for effective management and utilization [31]. As morphological conformation is a heritable indicator of production potential and adaptation [32], its systematic documentation is essential for maintaining breed integrity under selection.

On the molecular level, qRT-PCR offers a powerful methodological approach for profiling gene expression patterns in response to environmental challenges in livestock, capitalizing on its notable sensitivity, reproducibility, and capacity for high-throughput-analysis [33]. This technique proves especially valuable for examining crucial production and reproduction in sheep, enabling the characterization of various candidate genes [34, 35]. Egyptian sheep breeds face significant environmental stressors that negatively impact growth efficiency, meat quality, and overall production. Therefore, expression profiling of key genes regulating myogenesis (e.g., MSTN and IGF-1) and lipogenesis (e.g., ACACA and THRSP) is essential to understand the molecular basis of these production traits. This approach can uncover the molecular adaptations that help maintain carcass composition, meat yield, and quality under these stressful conditions, moving beyond general resilience to explain specific production outcomes [36–38].

The main purpose of this work is to address the lack of comprehensive genomic and phenotypic characterization of these Egyptian sheep breeds through an integrated approach. The specific objectives were; (a) to conduct WGS of major Egyptian sheep populations for detection of selection signatures and candidate genes; (b) to study genetic variation across the studied breeds at the genomic level and assess its association with production and adaptive traits, while simultaneously evaluating live body measurements and formulating body indices; (c) to evaluate morphological traits, production levels, growth performance, meat production and quality, and carcass characteristics; (d) to investigate candidate genes associated with meat production; (e) to screen insertions/deletions (InDels) and single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs); and (f) to functionally validate the genomic associations identified through WGS by employing qRT-PCR to measure expression levels of candidate genes across different genotypes showing strong trait associations.

Materials and methods

Ethical compliance

The animal-related work in this study was overseen and approved by Alexandria University, Faculty of Agriculture (Al-Shatby), Egypt (No. AU082209203101 & No. AU082209203103) and Yangzhou University, College of Animal Science and Technology, China (No. 202512051). All experimental methods adhered to the FASS Guide (2010) and the AVMA Euthanasia Guidelines (2020). The research was also conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines.

Animals

This study constitutes a principal segment of a larger research program focused on the systematic evaluation of production traits in five ovine populations. The overarching project encompasses a total cohort of 1,262 animals: Barki (BAR, n = 366), Rahmani (RAH, n = 189), a Rahmani x Barki crossbred (CRS, n = 442), Awassi (AWI, n = 143) and Ossimi (OSI, n = 122).

The genetic foundation of these populations was established using progenitor stock sourced from five geographically discrete regions in northern Egypt to ensure a representative genetic pool. These origins are precisely documented as: Alexandria Governorate (31.206208°N, 29.919704°E), the El-Hammam district within Matrouh Province (30.833132°N, 29.397580°E), a second locale in Matrouh Province (31.353910°N, 27.235560°E), Salloum Plateau (31.573626°N, 25.159220°E), and Sakha City (31.090560°N, 30.943543°E).

All animals used in this study were the offspring of the original progenitor stocks, raised under uniform conditions at a privately owned commercial farm “Elmabrok Farm” located in El‑Hammam, Matrouh Governorate, Egypt (30.833132°N, 29.397580°E). The farm operates under a semi‑intensive system and maintains all five studied breeds (BAR, RAH, CRS, OSI and AWI) under consistent nutritional and environmental conditions.

All animals were housed under a standardized semi-intensive, with barn access to open ranges and uniform nutrition and climate. For genomic work, we randomly collected and sampled adult individuals (26.50 ± 0.75 months) from groups born within a three-week period to control for age and seasonal variation.

Sample collection

Blood samples

For the genomic analysis, 120 animals (24 individuals per breed) were randomly selected. Venous blood (5 mL) was aseptically drawn from the jugular vein into EDTA vacuum tubes (Pspark, U.K.) and immediately transported on ice for next step.

Tissues samples

For downstream molecular analysis via qRT-PCR, skeletal muscle tissue samples were collected. Specifically, samples were obtained from the Longissimus dorsi muscle (rib-eye area), a standard tissue for studying meat quality and metabolic gene expression in ruminants (see Sect. 2.5.3).

Immediately following humane euthanasia according to approved protocols, a ~ 100 mg tissue segment was dissected from the Longissimus dorsi of each animal under aseptic conditions. The samples were promptly snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen to prevent RNA degradation and stored at -80 °C until RNA extraction.

A balanced subset of animals (n = 20 total) was selected for this analysis, comprising four unrelated male individuals from each of the five genetic groups: BAR (n = 4♂), RAH (n = 4♂), CRS (n = 4♂), Ossimi (OSI, n = 4♂), and Awassi (AWI, n = 4♂). The sample collection procedure is illustrated in Supplementary File (1), Fig. S1A & B.

Molecular and genomic analyses

DNA isolation, quality control, and processing

High-molecular-weight genomic DNA was isolated from individual whole-blood samples using a commercial silica-membrane-based kit (Tiangen Biotech, Beijing, China). Samples were obtained from a balanced cohort of randomly selected males representing the five genetic groups under study: BAR (n = 24♂), RAH, (n = 24♂), CRS (n = 24♂) OSI (n = 24♂) and AWI (n = 24♂).

DNA integrity was verified by ethidium-bromide-stained agarose gel-electrophoresis and visualization under UV light [39], (ChemiDoc™ XRS+ with Image-Lab™ Software, Bio-Rad, USA). Purity and concentration were assessed using spectrophotometry (NanoDrop™ 2000/2000c, Thermo-Fisher-Scientific, USA); samples with an A260/A280 ratio of ~ 1.82 and adequate yield were processed further, while others were re-extracted.

Library preparation and sequencing

For each of the five breed pools, sequencing libraries were constructed using 100 ng of high-quality genomic DNA. DNA was fragmented to a target size of 350 bp using a Covaris LE220 ultrasonicator. Library preparation was performed with the NEBNext Ultra-II-FS-DNA Library Prep Kit (NEB), including end-repair, adapter ligation with unique dual-indexed adapters (IDT for Illumina), and size selection (300–500 bp) using AMPure XP Beads. The final libraries were amplified with 6-cycle PCR. Pooled libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (S2 flow cell) with a 2 × 150 bp paired-end configuration, generating an average of 600 million reads per breed pool, achieving a mean coverage of > 30x per breed. Raw sequencing data have been deposited in the NCBI.

Pooled sequencing design

For each breed, DNA from 24 individuals was equimolarly pooled prior to library preparation and sequencing. Pooled whole‑genome sequencing (pool‑WGS) is a well‑established strategy for population‑level variant discovery and allele frequency estimation [40, 41]. This approach is particularly suitable for breed‑scale genomic characterisation when individual‑level genotyping is not required. The heterozygosity values reported throughout this study are population‑level estimates derived from allele frequency spectra within each pool, not individual‑level genotypes.

Bioinformatic processing

Quality control & alignment

Raw reads were processed using fastp (v0.23.1.) (https://github.com/OpenGene/fastp) to trim low-quality bases (Phred < 20), remove adapters and discard reads with > 5% Ns or post-trim length < 50 bp. Clean reads were aligned to the Ovis aries reference genome (ARS-UI_Ramb_v3.0; GCF_016772045.2) using BWA-MEM (v0.7.17.) (https://bio-bwa.sourceforge.net). Duplicate reads were marked with Picard (v2.27.1.) (https://broadinstitute.github.io/picard), and coverage depth distribution was visualized with mosdepth v0.3.3 (https://github.com/brentp/mosdepth), generating the genome-wide-coverage-plot. However, this reference was selected as it is the highest-quality, chromosome-level assembly available and allows for direct comparison with the vast majority of contemporary genomic studies in Ovis aries.

Variant detection

For variant calling, the Illumina short‑read sequencing data (2 × 150 bp paired‑end) generated from each breed pool were used. SNPs and small InDels were called using GATK HaplotypeCaller (v4.2.6.0.) (https://gatk.broadinstitute.org/hc/en-us) in GVCF mode, as recommended for pooled sequencing data. Hard filtering was applied with the following thresholds: QD ≥ 8, MQ ≥ 40, FS ≤ 20, DP ≥ 15, MAC ≥ 6, ExcessHet ≤ 10, consistent with best practices for sheep genomics [42]. Structural variants (SVs) were identified via DELLY (v1.1.6.) (https://github.com/dellytools/delly) with a minimum mapping quality (MAPQ > 20) to ensure high-confidence alignments. The copy number variants (CNVs) were detected using CNVkit v0.9.8 (https://cnvkit.readthedocs.io/; 500-bp bins, p < 0.001). All reference based alignments used the Ovis aries reference genome ARS-UI_Ramb_v3.0 (GCF_016772045.2). A complete list of software versions and parameters is provided in Supplementary File (2), Table S1.

Functional annotation & analysis

All variants (both SNPs and InDels) were annotated with SnpEff (v5.1d.) using the sheep annotation database (ARS-UI_Ramb_v3.0). Candidate genes were prioritized based on their functional impacts on economically important traits, for example, roles in lipid metabolism (FABP4 and LEP) and muscle development (MSTN and IGF-1). Following annotation, population genetic diversity and differentiation were summarised using the fixation index (FST) and nucleotide diversity (π), calculated in non‑overlapping 10‑kb windows with VCFtools (v0.1.16.). The 10‑kb window size was chosen to provide a genome‑wide overview of population structure while maintaining sufficient resolution for comparative purposes. These statistics were calculated solely for descriptive purposes to characterise population genetic variation and were not used for outlier detection or selection sweep identification. Pathway enrichment for genes associated with key metabolic and production traits (e.g., KEGG map03320: PPAR signalling pathway and map01212: Fatty acid metabolism) was performed using KOBAS (v3.0.) on the full annotated variant set. For SNP‑based KEGG enrichment, the background gene set was restricted to genes carrying at least one SNP in our dataset to avoid over‑representation of large gene families that could produce artefactual enrichment signals (e.g., olfactory transduction).

Data validation & availability

Key variants were validated via Sanger sequencing (20 SNPs; 100% concordance) and qPCR for CNVs. Genome-wide variant density was visualized using Circos v0.69-9. All data are publicly accessible: Raw sequences: NCBI SRA (accession no. SRX29559823, SRX29559824, SRX29559825, SRX29559826 and SRX29559827), and available in Supplementary File (3).

Gene expression profiling by qRT-PCR

Candidate genes for qRT‑PCR validation were selected using a two‑fold approach. First, genes located within or near structural variant hotspots (identified from the WGS data) and genes belonging to significantly enriched KEGG pathways (e.g., PPAR signalling, fatty acid metabolism) were prioritized. Second, candidate loci were cross‑referenced with peer‑reviewed literature documenting their functional roles in sheep muscle development, such as MSTN, IGF‑1 and CAPN3) and lipid metabolism, such as ACACA, FASN, FABP4, LEP and ADIPOQ [43–47]. Only genes with both genomic evidence and literature support were included in the final panel. So, based on genome sequencing results (Sect. 2.4) and aligned with recent studies, the candidate genes for this study were selected through a convergent approach. Initial analysis of sequencing data highlighted genetic variants and pathways associated with muscle and fat metabolism. These candidate loci were cross-referenced with the established literature on meat quality determinants in ruminants to prioritize a final panel of genes with known functional relevance.

The selected genes focused on two fundamental biological pathways: skeletal-muscle-development/myogenesis and lipid-metabolism/ lipogenesis). For myogenesis, we analysed key regulators including myostatin (MSTN), a known inhibitor of muscle growth [37], alongside the anabolic factors insulin-like-growth factor 1 and 2 (IGF-1 [48] and IGF-2 [37]). Furthermore, we analysed calpain 3 (CAPN3), a protease linked to myofibrillar turnover and meat tenderness and ribosomal protein L7 (RPL7) [49].

For lipid metabolism, genes central to de novo fatty acid synthesis were prioritized: acetyl-CoA-carboxylase (ACACA [37]) and fatty acid synthase (FASN). While, Transcriptional-Regulator-Thyroid-Hormone-Responsive (THRSP) was selected for its role in coordinating lipogenic expression [37]. Adipokine signalling was evaluated through leptin (LEP [50]) and adiponectin (ADIPOQ [49]), while fatty acid binding protein 4 (FABP4 [51]) was included for its role in adipocyte differentiation. Housekeeping

Genes

RNA extraction and cDNA synthesis

Total RNA was isolated from meat tissues (n = 4 per breed) using the RNeasy® Mini Kit (Qiagen). RNA integrity was verified electrophoretically (RIN > 8.0) and quantified via NanoDrop™ 2000 (Thermo Scientific). First-strand-cDNA was synthesized from 500 ng RNA using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied-Biosystems™) with oligo (dT) primers. Reactions (20 µL volume) were incubated at 25 °C (10 min), 37 °C (120 min), and 85 °C (5 min).

qRT-PCR analysis

Expression of target genes were quantified using PowerUp™ SYBR™ Green-Master-Mix (Applied-Biosystems) on a QuantStudio™ 5 system. Reactions (20 µL) contained: 10 µL 2× SYBR Green Mix, 0.8 µL each primer (10 µM), 2 µL cDNA (50 ng/µL) and 6.4 µL nuclease-free H2O. Amplification parameters: 95 °C (2 min); 40 cycles of 95 °C (15 s), 60 °C (30 s), 72 °C (30 s); melt curve 60–95 °C (+ 0.3 °C/sec). GAPDH [51] and ACTB [33] served as reference genes, with expression normalized via 2−ΔΔCT [44]. Four biological replicates and four technical replicates were analysed per group. To control for false positives arising from multiple comparisons across the 12 target genes and two treatments, p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure to maintain a False Discovery Rate (FDR).

Phenotypic traits

Body Condition Score (BCS)

The body condition of all 1,262 animals in the study cohort was evaluated using a standardized Body-Condition-Score (BCS) system. Assessments were performed independently by three experienced evaluators, following established protocols by Morand-Fehr and Hervieu [45] and Russel et al. [46]. The Scores were determined according to Saleh et al. [47, 52]. Table S2.

Body Measurements (BM)

A total of 100 adult animals/ each breed were measured under uniform management and breeding conditions for 25 linear-body-measurements (LBM) was recorded for each animal: Neck-Length (NL), Wither-Height (WH), Sternum-height (SH), Shin-bone (SB), Canon-bone-Circumference (CBC), Body-Length (BL), Heart-Girth (HG) or Body-Depth (BD), Chest-Circumference (Ch.C), Rump-Height (RH), Thigh-Circumference (TC) or Hock-circumference (HC), Tail-Length (TL), Chest-Width (CW), Shoulder-Width (SW), Bicoastal-diameter (Bic.D), Rump-Width (RW), Slump-Rump (SR)/ Rump-Length (RL), Pin-Bone (PB), Chest-depth (CD), Abdominal-Girth (AG), Back-High (BH), Scapulo-ischial-length (SIL), Rumb-Depth (RD), Neck-Girth (NG), Muzzle-diameter (MD), Body-weight (BW), according to Saleh et al. [31]. All measurements were taken by trained personnel using calibrated tools (measuring tape, stick and digital scale) with animals properly restrained on a level surface to ensure accuracy and reproducibility.

A summary of the number of individuals used in each specific analysis is provided in Table S3.

Carcass-trait analysis and meat-composition

Experimental animals and slaughter-protocol

A subset of animals (n = 20) was used for the assessment of carcass characteristics and meat composition. This subset comprised four male individuals from each of the five sheep groups: BAR (n = 4♂), RAH, (n = 4♂), CRS (n = 4♂) OSI (n = 4♂) and AWI (n = 4♂). All animals were slaughtered at a standardized age of 27 months.

These 20 animals were also used for tissue sample collection for gene expression analysis. Animals were humanely euthanized following standard procedures. Prior to slaughter, animals were fasted for 18 h with free access to water. Initial live weight and fasted live weight were recorded. Animals were first rendered unconscious by intravenous injection of sodium thiopental (20 mg/kg body weight; Abbott Laboratories, North Chicago, IL, USA) administered via the jugular vein. Complete unconsciousness was confirmed by loss of pedal and corneal reflexes. Subsequently, animals were euthanized by exsanguination via severance of the carotid arteries and jugular veins while under surgical anesthesia. This two-step method ensures minimal stress and pain, conforms to the AVMA Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals (2020 edition; https://www.avma.org/KB/Policies/Documents/euthanasia.pdf), and allows collection of high-quality tissue samples for gene expression analysis.

Post-slaughter-processing and physical-carcass-assessment

Following exsanguination, non-carcass components (head, skin, feet, pluck, liver, digestive tract, pancreas and spleen) were removed and weighed. The weight of the digestive tract content was determined by difference to calculate the empty-live-weight (ELW).

The carcass was then systematically dissected. Key fat depots including pelvic, kidney, omental and mesenteric fat were isolated and weighed individually to determine total internal fat. Carcasses were split, and the Longissimus dorsi (loin eye) muscle area was measured. The major wholesale cuts were subsequently separated into dissectible lean meat, subcutaneous and intermuscular fat and bone. Each component was weighed to calculate the percentage of lean, fat and bone in the carcass, as well as lean-to-fat and lean-to-bone ratios according to Sen et al. [53] and Santos et al. [54].

Chemical-composition analysis

Meat samples for chemical analysis were obtained from the Longissimus dorsi muscle (Fig. S1). A 100 g sample from each animal was trimmed of external fat and connective tissue, freeze-dried and homogenized. Proximate analysis was performed according to standard methods according to AOAC [55], and Madruga et al. [56]. Moisture content was determined by weight loss after oven-drying, crude ash by combustion in a muffle furnace (600 °C for 8 h), and crude protein content via the Kjeldahl method (using a nitrogen-to-protein conversion factor of 6.25). Total intramuscular fat (ether extract) was quantified using a Soxhlet apparatus.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analyses were performed using SAS software (Version 9.4, SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Statistical significance was declared at p < 0.05 unless otherwise stated for specific tests.

All collected phenotypic variables (body measurements, carcass traits, body condition scores, and chemical composition data) were initially tested for normality of distribution using the Shapiro-Wilk test (PROC UNIVARIATE). All data met the assumption of normality (W ≥ 0.90). Subsequently, the effect of breed on the measured traits was analysed using the General-Linear-Model (PROC-GLM) procedure. For each trait, the following model was applied:1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{Y}_\mathrm{ijk}= \mu+\mathrm{B}_\mathrm{i} + \mathrm{e}_\mathrm{ijk}$$\end{document}

Where: Yijk is the observed value of the dependent variable for an individual animal, µ is the overall population mean, Bi is the fixed effect of the ith breed (i = BAR, RAH, CRS, OSI and AWI), and eijk is the random residual error. When the overall model indicated significant effects (p < 0.05), differences among adjusted least-squares means (LSMeans) for main effects were compared using Duncan’s Multiple Range Test.

Functional annotation of genes within candidate selection regions and associated significantly differentiated variants was performed. Enrichment analysis for Gene Ontology (GO) terms and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways was conducted using KOBAS (v3.0.). Significance was assessed using Fisher’s exact test with a Benjamini-Hochberg False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction (q < 0.05).

Relative gene expression levels of target genes, normalized to stable reference genes (GAPDH and ACTB), were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method. Statistical analysis of expression data was performed on ΔCT values. The effects of breed on gene expression were analysed using a one-way analysis of variance (PROC-GLM) followed by Duncan’s Multiple Range Test for mean separation.

The current WGS generated a high-resolution genomic resource for the five studied sheep breeds. Comprehensive sequencing statistics revealed robust data quality and notable inter-breed differences, as shown in Table 2. Variant discovery and population genetic diversity

Variant calling across the studied sheep breeds captured substantial genetic diversity, with total SNP counts ranging from 11.9 million in OSI to 17.4 million in RAH after stringent filtering (Table 3). Heterozygosity values reported are population‑level estimates derived from allele frequency spectra within each breed pool. These population‑level heterozygosity estimates clearly delineated population histories, with pure breeds such as RAH and BAR showing higher values (85.0% and 83.8%, respectively), while the CRS crossbred displayed an intermediate level (60.4%) a classic genetic signature of a first‑generation hybrid. The conserved transition/transversion (Ti/Tv) ratio across breeds (~ 2.3) aligns with expected mammalian genomic patterns. The genomic distribution of both SNPs and InDels was remarkably conserved, with intronic regions harbouring the majority of variants (38.8% of SNPs and InDels), followed by intergenic regions, as detailed in Table 4.

Population genetic differentiation and diversity were further quantified using FST and nucleotide diversity (π) calculated in 10-kb windows across the genome. Pairwise FST values between breeds ranged from 0.18 (between BAR and RAH) to 0.35 (between BAR and AWI), reflecting moderate to high genetic differentiation consistent with the distinct ecological histories of these breeds. Nucleotide diversity (π) ranged from 0.0021 in OSI to 0.0028 in RAH, with BAR showing intermediate diversity (π = 0.0025), suggesting that the pure breeds maintain substantial genetic diversity while the crossbred (CRS) exhibited expected intermediate values. Full FST and π statistics are provided in Table S4.

Functional classification using Clusters of Orthologous Groups (COG) revealed a strong and consistent signal across investigated breeds. This pattern mirrors findings in other indigenous livestock, where selection often acts on genes governing energy storage and environmental interaction. The uniformity of this COG profile between variant types and across breeds indicates a shared functional architecture under selective pressure.

Structural variant hotspots and candidate gene localization

Analysis of SVs demonstrated a non-random genomic distribution, characterized by prominent hotspots on chromosomes 2, 6, and 18. As illustrated in Fig. 9, a Circos plot integrating structural variant and copy number variant density with gene and GC content data effectively highlights these regions of increased genomic dynamism. Worth mentioning, these hotspot regions consistently coincided with genomic segments harboring key candidate genes associated with major production traits.

For example, a major cluster on chromosome 2 is located in proximity to FABP4 gene, a known regulator of fat metabolism. On chromosome 6, significant clusters were found surrounding KAP gene family, which is responsible for determining wool keratin structure. Additional candidate genes identified within these and other dynamic regions include MSTN (GDF-8) on chromosome 2, a known influencer of muscle growth and yield; BMPR-1B on chromosome 6, a major gene associated with prolificacy; and ASIP on chromosome 18, a primary determinant of coat color patterns.

Structural variations occurring within the regulatory or coding sequences of these genes are positioned to directly affect phenotypes related to fat deposition, muscle development, litter size and wool quality. These findings establish a set of priority candidates for functionally validating the predictions arising from the genomic analysis. Further details on significant SVs and CNVs overlapping candidate gene regions are provided in Table S5 and Table S6, respectively.

Carcass-traits and meat-quality

Post-slaughter analysis revealed significant breed differences in key production metrics (Table 7). CRS and RAH produced the highest yields of trimmed meat (23.95 ± 0.13 kg and 18.40 ± 0.42 kg, respectively; p < 0.001). The dressing percentage was highest in RAH and CRS (~ 53.5%; p = 0.001), and the dissected fat weight was greatest in RAH (3.72 ± 0.06 kg; p = 0.002).

Concerning the chemical analysis of the Longissimus dorsi muscle, RAH had the highest intramuscular-fat (IMF) content (4.20 ± 0.10%; p < 0.001), a key determinant of juiciness. On the other hand, CRS excelled in crude protein content (22.35 ± 0.15%; p = 0.001), indicating superior lean muscle yield. While, the lean-to-fat ratio was highest in AWI (58.60 ± 1.30; p < 0.001), highlighting its efficient meat production.

Gene expression analysis

The qRT-PCR analysis of Longissimus dorsi muscle from 20 animals (n = 4 per breed) was performed to functionally validate genomic predictions. Expression levels of selected 19 candidate genes were normalized to the stable reference genes GAPDH (Figs. 10, 11 and 12). The results reveal breed-specific transcriptional profiles that provide a mechanistic link between the enriched genomic pathways and the key production traits described in Sect. 3.5.

Expression of myogenesis-related genes demonstrated variation across breeds. The expression of MSTN (Fig. 10a) and GDF8 (Fig. 10b) was notably elevated in RAH and CRS populations, showing 1.67-fold and 1.38-fold variation, respectively. Conversely, IGF-1 (Fig. 10c) and IGF-2 (Fig. 10d) showed higher expression in BAR and OSI, varying 1.51-fold and 1.74-fold. Components of the somatotropic axis, including GH (Fig. 10g) and its receptor GHR (Fig. 10f), were most active in CRS and RAH (1.85-fold and 2.05-fold), while CAPN3 (Fig. 10h) was most highly expressed in RAH and AWI (1.34-fold). The expression of DLK1 (Fig. 10e) and RPL7 (Fig. 11a) also varied among breeds, with 1.78-fold and 1.35-fold changes, respectively.

Regarding lipogenesis and fat metabolism, gene expression patterns strongly aligned with fat deposition phenotypes. Critical enzymes for de novo fatty acid synthesis, ACACA (Fig. 11b) and FASN (Fig. 11c), showed elevated expression in the fat-tailed RAH and AWI breeds (1.54-fold and 1.26-fold). The expression of SCD (Fig. 11d) and LPL (Fig. 11e) was also highest in RAH (1.58-fold and 1.28-fold). Notably, FABP4 (Fig. 12b), a central regulator of intracellular fatty acid transport, was most abundant in RAH and AWI (1.31-fold variation). The expression of adipokine genes; LEP (Fig. 11h) and ADIPOQ (Fig. 12c) was highest in CRS and RAH, respectively (2.40-fold and 1.39-fold variation). The expression of CIDEA (Fig. 11f), THRSP (Fig. 11g) and CPT1 (Fig. 12a) also showed significant breed-specific profiles, with fold changes of 1.64, 1.55 and 1.36.

The coordinated upregulation of lipogenic genes (e.g., ACACA, FASN and FABP4) in RAH and AWI is consistent with their enhanced fat deposition phenotype, while the distinct profiles of myogenic genes correlate with differences in muscularity and lean growth among the breeds. These findings provide correlative evidence linking breed specific genomic signatures to phenotypic outcomes.

Discussion

This study establishes the first integrated genomic atlas for five sheep breeds; BAR, RAH, CRS, OSI and AWI by synthesizing WGS, detailed phenotyping and tissue-specific gene expression profiling. The present findings demonstrated that the distinct production and adaptation profiles of these breeds arise from a complex interplay of population genetic history, selection on key metabolic pathways, and the differential expression of candidate genes, providing a cohesive model from genotype to phenotype. These results are consistent with and reinforced by recent research in other breeds. Gene expression studies have similarly identified key candidates like THRSP for fat deposition [37] and co-expression of RPL7, CPT1, FABP4 and CAPN3 across tissues for superior growth [49]. Furthermore, the utility of WGS resources for trait mapping, as demonstrated for rib eye area in Hu sheep [57] and for building foundational variant databases in Duolang and Greek sheep [58, 59], validates the genomic approaches employed here. The high accuracy of imputing to WGS using multi-breed references [60] further supports the robustness of our analytical framework.

Genomic diversity and population structure

The substantial variation in heterozygosity and SNP density among the breeds (Table 3) offers a clear genomic signature of their distinct histories. The high heterozygosity observed in the pure breed: BAR and RAH suggests these populations retain considerable genetic diversity, which is a favourable asset for health, production and adaptive potential, and long-term selection. In contrast, the intermediate heterozygosity and uniquely high SNP count in CRS are classic genomic hallmarks of a first-generation cross, capturing the combined genetic variation of its parental lineages. The significant elevated GC content specifically in the BAR breed (Table 2) may indicate a greater genomic divergence, possibly reflecting its long-term adaptation to the harsh desert conditions of the Northwestern Coastal Zone, which could have selected for or preserved unique SVs. This aligns with broader studies identifying genetic mechanisms for adaptation to extreme arid environments in native sheep populations [61].

Selection signatures and environmental response pathways

The functional annotation of genetic variants revealed a powerful and consistent narrative of adaptation. The strong enrichment of COG related to lipid metabolism and defence mechanisms (Figs. 2 and 3) points to a foundational genomic architecture shaped by selection for energy storage and environmental interaction. This is sharply refined by GO and KEGG pathway analyses, which identified fatty acid metabolic process, response to xenobiotic stimulus, and the PPAR signalling pathway as top enriched pathways (Fig. 8). These findings provide a direct molecular explanation for cardinal breed phenotypes: the prolific fat-tailed and high intramuscular fat traits of RAH and AWI are underpinned by enhanced lipid synthesis and storage pathways, while the enrichment of xenobiotic response and drug metabolism pathways likely reflects adaptation to regional flora and foraging environments.

The genomic architecture identified in the Egyptian breeds aligns with and is contextualized by broader selection patterns observed in global sheep populations. The framework established by Fonseca et al. [62], which distinguishes between adaptation sweeps (e.g., for thermoregulation) and production sweeps (e.g., for reproduction), provides a genome-wide parallel to our pathway analyses. Specifically, our finding of enriched PPAR signalling and lipid metabolism pathways in the RAH and AWI breeds mirrors their discovery that genes at the intersection of adaptation and production sweeps are strongly linked to lipid metabolism, suggesting a universal genetic mechanism for balancing these traits.

Furthermore, candidate genes identified in other adapted breeds offer direct parallels to our candidates. For instance, the selection signatures for heat stress and arid adaptation found in Iranian breeds, such as CORIN, CPQ and PLCB4 genes [63] functionally resonate with the enrichment of xenobiotic response and metabolism pathways in our breeds, particularly the desert-adapted BAR. Similarly, the identification of wool/hair follicle genes (LHX2, COL17A1 and IRF2BP2) in other studies [63, 64] supports the biological relevance of the hair follicle development terms enriched in our genomic data, connecting genotype to a key local economic phenotype.

This comparative view confirms that the distinct selection pressures evident in the studied breeds favouring lipid storage in RAH/AWI, arid resilience in BAR, and heterosis in CRS are not isolated events but part of a conserved evolutionary playbook. The fine-scale genetic structure we observe within and between the sheep breeds [65] and the co-selection of lipid metabolism for both productivity and adaptation [62] validate the integrated model, demonstrating how localized breeding and natural selection have sculpted a shared genomic foundation into diverse, specialized breeds.

Structural variant hotspots and candidate genes

The current analysis identified non-random SV hotspots on chromosomes 2, 6 and 18 (Fig. 10). These regions are of high interest as SVs can have major phenotypic effects by altering gene dosage or regulation. The localization of these hotspots near candidate genes like FABP4 and KAP genes directly connects genomic SVs to the core traits of fat deposition and carcass characteristics. This is supported by external research where FABP4 polymorphisms were associated with fatty acid composition and carcass weight [51], and where SVs have been shown to have significant impacts on production traits and convergent evolution in small ruminants [66]. This suggests that the distinctive phenotypes of these breeds may be driven not only by sequence changes within genes but also by larger-scale genomic rearrangements affecting their regulation.

Gene expression profiles bridge genomic signatures to physiology

The expression data are consistent with the pathways identified under selection, providing correlative evidence linking breed-specific genomic signatures to their characteristic phenotypes. For instance, the higher expression of lipogenic genes (ACACA, FASN and SCD) in the adipose-rich RAH and AWI breeds is a direct transcriptional correlate of the enriched PPAR signalling pathway found in their genomes and their higher dissected fat weights (Table 7). This focus on lipid metabolism mirrors selection signatures in other fat-tailed or high-intramuscular fat breeds where genes like FABP4 and SCD are key candidates [51].

Worth mentioning, breeds like CRS, which showed a favourable heterotic blend for meat yield, exhibited expression profiles favorable to lean growth. This included the upregulation of myogenic genes such as MSTN and IGF-1 and CAPN3, aligning with muscularity traits. This pattern of tissue-specific gene expression coordinating growth and composition is echoed in research on growth performance in other breeds, where expression of genes like; CAPN3 and RPL7 in metabolic tissues is linked to protein accretion and muscle development [49]. Furthermore, genomic insights into rib eye muscle area (REA) in specialized meat breeds like Hu sheep have identified candidate genes (ALS2, PLXNA4, DPP6 and COL12A1) regulating muscle development through cytoskeleton dynamics and extracellular matrix integrity [57], providing a parallel genetic framework for muscularity.

In the desert-adapted BAR breed, the genomic selection for xenobiotic response and environmental stress pathways represents an adaptation signature. This finds parallels in other harsh-climate breeds where specific genes are under selection for heat tolerance, such as PIK3C2B, PLCB4, FAM107B and USP43 for heat stress response, and CORIN and CPQ for adaptation to hot, arid environments [67–71]. This coherent cascade from genomic variant to pathway enrichment to differential gene expression establishes a credible mechanistic chain leading to the observed phenotypic variation.

Integrative phenotypic outcomes, candidate gene

The phenotypic characterization confirmed the expected breed specializations predicted by the integrated genomic and expression model: RAH and AWI excelled in fat-related traits, CRS showed superior meat yield, and BAR displayed a leaner, desert-adapted conformation (Tables 5, 6 and 7).

CRS breed, in particular, demonstrates the value of crossbreeding, combining the genetic diversity of its parents into a heterotic blend that enhances production traits. For conservation, the high genetic diversity preserved in purebreds like BAR and RAH remains a valuable resource [25, 72]. The candidate genes and pathways identified in this study, such as FABP4 and SCD for fat metabolism, CAPN3 for muscle development, and key stress-response genes for adaptation offer tangible targets for marker-assisted selection. The identification of specific, breed-relevant genetic markers, as demonstrated for FABP4 in Sonid sheep [51] and for rib eye area in Hu sheep [57], provides a clear roadmap for applying such strategies. This enables the enhancement of desirable traits such as lean growth, favorable fat deposition or environmental resilience without compromising the genetic integrity of these unique breeds.

Comparative genomic context for adaptation to harsh climates

The strong selection signatures for xenobiotic response and environmental stress pathways in the desert-adapted BAR breed find clear parallels in genomic studies of other breeds from harsh climates [25, 72]. In this aspect, research on heat-tolerant populations has identified specific candidate genes under selection for thermoregulation and arid adaptation. Key genes associated with the cellular heat stress response include PIK3C2B, involved in energy homeostasis [73]; PLCB4, linked to oxidative stress and energy metabolism [74]; FAM107B, implicated in heat-shock induction [75, 76]; and USP43, which functions in the ubiquitination process during thermal stress [76]. For adaptation to hot, arid environments, CORIN (involved in blood pressure and volume regulation) [67–69] and CPQ (linked to proteolytic functions under water deficit) [70, 71] are frequently highlighted. NBEA gene, also noted for its role in body temperature regulation under heat stress [77], exemplifies a gene with potential pleiotropic effects across stress response and other traits.

Comparative genetic architecture of economic traits

The breed-specialization observed in the studied sheep breeds is reflected in the selection signatures for production traits identified in breeds worldwide. For wool production, the genomic enrichment for hair follicle development terms in breeds like BAR and AWI aligns with studies pinpointing genes that influence fiber structure. These include COL17A1, important for the dermal-epidermal junction in primary follicles [78]; LHX2, a crucial transcription factor for secondary hair follicle development [79]; PIK3R4, associated with wool diameter [80]; KRTAP20-2, linked to fiber curvature [81, 82]; and PTPN3 and NBEA, both associated with wool crimp traits [80].

For meat production, the genomic and expression profiles favoring lean growth in breeds such as CRS characterized by the upregulation of myogenic genes (CAPN3 and RPL7) and distinct patterns of lipogenic genes (FABP4 and SCD) resonate with findings in specialized meat breeds. A key parallel is found in Hu sheep, where genomic insights into REA revealed significant positive genetic correlations with body weight and average daily gain, underscoring its economic value [73]. That study further identified candidate genes (ALS2, PLXNA4, DPP6 and COL12A1) that regulate muscle development through cytoskeleton dynamics and extracellular matrix integrity [57], providing a complementary framework for understanding the genetic control of muscularity.

All animals in this study originated from a single commercial farm in Matrouh Governorate, which may not fully represent the genetic diversity of each breed across Egypt. Future studies should build upon the present findings by validating the qRT-PCR expression patterns in larger, independent cohorts across multiple geographic locations in Egypt. CRS population represents a specific Barki x Rahmani F1 cross under a single management system; therefore, other crossbreeding strategies should be examined to assess generalizability. Future work employing individual-level WGS and formal selection signature analyses such as XP-EHH and iHS is recommended to directly link specific genetic variants to production and adaptation traits.

Conclusions

This study provides a foundational genomic atlas for five economically vital sheep breeds BAR, RAH, CRS, OSI and AWI by integrating WGS, comprehensive phenotypic characterization, and tissue-specific transcriptomic data. The present analyses demonstrated that the distinct production and adaptive profiles of these breeds are rooted in unique population structures, strong selection signals in core metabolic and environmental response pathways, and breed-specific expression of key candidate genes. The high heterozygosity of the pure breeds such as BAR underscore their value as a reservoir of genetic diversity for conservation. On the other hand, CRS breed exemplifies the measurable benefits of heterosis for meat yield. Pathway analyses consistently highlighted the importance of PPAR signalling and fatty acid metabolism. Additionally, SVs hotspots were identified near candidate genes, such as: FABP4 and MSTN, offering a direct genetic basis for divergent phenotypes in fat deposition and carcass traits. So, the validation utilising qRT-PCR associated these genomic signatures to the transcriptional mechanisms underlying trait variation, providing correlative evidence that links breed specific genomic signatures to phenotypic outcomes, though formal causal inference requires dedicated association studies. The present study offers more than a catalogue of genetic variants, side by side functional understanding of Egyptian sheep genomics, laying the groundwork for breeding strategies.
