Serial Vascular Responses of Balloon-Expandable Stent With Biodegradable Film-Type Graft in a Rabbit Iliac Artery Dissection Model (BioGard Study)
Authors: Sang Min Park, Kyung-Chan Choi, Byeong Han Lee, Sang Yol Yoo, Christopher Y. Kim
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Author's summaryArterial dissection or rupture during coronary or peripheral angioplasty often unexpectedly occurs but it presents substantial risks, including high mortality rates. Current conventional covered stents encounter constraints related to supply, size, and delivery, and it also related to future poor outcomes due to heavy thrombotic burden. A novel film-type biodegradable graft stent addresses these limitations. Assessing its procedural feasibility, immediate and long-term efficacy, and safety in treating artery rupture in a rabbit iliac model, the present study shows promise in overcoming challenges posed by unexpected severe arterial dissection during vascular interventions.

INTRODUCTION

Arterial dissection or rupture during percutaneous coronary and peripheral transluminal angioplasty rarely occurs but is usually associated with lethal cardiovascular events.1)2) In the case of significant blood volume extravasation in coronary artery interventions, the mortality rate approaches 20–30%.3)4)5) In this hyperacute emergent clinical situation, covered stents have been considered as a primary measure to prevent further serious arterial events.6)7)8) However, the current available stents with fabric-based graft material are quite limited due to several issues including short supply and limited size availability. Additionally, most graft stents are excessively bulky for rapid delivery to the ruptured vessel. Furthermore, unfavorable vascular anatomies with complex lesions are hostile to rescue angioplasty with current graft stents.9)10) Even though the device was barely implanted to address an emergency situation, it remains associated with unfavorable future vascular events, including acute thrombotic occlusion and chronic in-stent restenosis due to exposed durable graft material, and higher bleeding risk due to prolonged multiple antiplatelet therapies.11)12) Moreover, these graft stents block blood flow to side branches and can cause severe iatrogenic ischemia in branched vessels.9)10) To overcome these challenges, the balloon-expandable stent with biodegradable film-type graft for treating arterial dissection was invented and evaluated in regards to its procedural feasibility, immediate- to long-term vascular responses in a rabbit iliac artery rupture model.

METHODS

Ethical statement

Animal care and all experimental procedures were approved by the Guidelines on Animal Experiments of Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Laboratory Animal Center of Osong Medical Innovation Foundation (Approval No. 2012-0158). They were also performed in accordance with the Guidelines of the Korean Government Animal Protection and Management Law.

Animals

New Zealand white male rabbits were obtained from the Central Animal Laboratory from Japan SLC, Inc. (Hamamatsu, Japan). The animals were bred in a pathogen-free environment under barrier conditions. Regular mouse chow diets were provided, and water was freely accessible in controlled temperature facilities.13) Aspirin (2 mg/kg) was administered orally for two weeks before and after device deployment.

Engineering profile of stent with biodegradable graft for arterial dissection

General morphology and mechanical properties of the device

The test device consists of a bare metal stent covered by a polycaprolactone (PCL) film, and a balloon catheter. The device is an open-cell design having 9 S-shaped primary cells and 3 bridges in one circumference. The bare metal is composed of Cobalt-Chromium alloy L605. The size of the device is 3.0–3.5 mm in diameter and 24–28 mm in length. The strut thickness is approximately 78 µm. Magnified photography reveals a uniform PCL film membrane between the stent struts (Figure 1A and B). PCL is a bioresorbable, semi-crystalline polymer with a low glass transition temperature (−60°C), which can be degraded by hydrolytic mechanisms under physiological conditions.14) The tensile property of PCL is suitable for graft material because it is very flexible at body temperature and its biocompatibility has also been approved by the US Food and Drug Administration.15)16)

General morphology and shapes of biodegradable graft for arterial dissection at each processing stage.

(A) Miro-photography before crimping. (B) Micro-photography after expansion. (C) SEM before crimping. (D) SEM after expansion.

SEM = scanning electron microscopy.

One weight % of PCL in dichloromethane was applied abluminally on the bare metal stent by spraying. Subsequently, the graft stent was dried at room temperature for 1 hour and then immersed into ethanol for 30 seconds. Following this, it underwent a three-minute ultrasonic wash using ethanol and was then vacuum-dried overnight. A scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was performed before and after the expansion of the device (Figure 1C and D). On SEM, the thickness of the PCL films is about 11.1 µm and 2.4 µm before and after expansion, respectively. As the flexible PCL film was expanded, the thickness gradually decreased without significant micro-cracks or fissures noted at the surface of the PCL membrane between struts on SEM (Figure 1E and F).

Test for in vitro degradation of polycaprolactone graft

The test was conducted in a custom-made closed loop of continuous flow using i) a peristaltic pump, ii) a multi-channel flow distributor, iii) a reservoir, and iv) a water bath with temperature controller (Figure 2A). The fluid, phosphate buffer saline with a pH of 7.4, circulated with a flow rate of 5 L/min. The pump flow and distributing channel system diverted the flow into each of the 6 devices at the flow rate of 833.3 mL/min which is consistent with extreme testing conditions. The circulation medium was replaced once every 2 weeks for 6 months, after which the graft was released from the in vitro model and analyzed.

In-vitro degradation test of biodegradable graft film (polycaprolactone).

(A) Circuit of degradation test composed of PBS (pH 7.4 at 37°C) water bath, a peristaltic pump (flow rate: 5 L/min), and latex tube for graft stents. (B) Degradation status at 1 month. (C) Degradation status at 3 months. (D) Degradation status at 6 months.

PBS = phosphate-buffer solution.

In vitro degradation test of extended PCL graft demonstrates that the film gradually degraded as time went on. At six months, about 30% of the covered film had visually eroded (Figure 2B, C, and D). It was confirmed that PCL could be degraded by a hydrolytic mechanism under physiological conditions.

The foreshortening of the device was about 3.7%. This is higher than what is typically seen with CoCr bare metal stents without the PCL graft film. It would be reasonable to interpret that the force to return to the original shape of elastic PCL film led to higher stent length reduction when the device was expanded. The radial force is usually measured as a force required to produce a 50% reduction in stent diameter. The radial forces between the device and bare metal stent were not different (data not shown). The dislodgement force of the device was 6.4N. It was lower than that of conventional stents (Supplementary Table 1).

Experimental procedure and clinical follow-up

After creating a rabbit iliac artery dissection model through excessive balloon dilation, 18 rabbits successfully treated with the devices were enrolled in the experiment. At specific time intervals, 1–2 rabbits undertook micro-CT and were sacrificed for histological examination. To evaluate the long-term clinical vascular response of the device, one rabbit was raised for up to one year, and follow-up angiography was performed (Figure 3).

Induction of iliac arterial dissection and sealing with biodegradable graft for arterial dissection

Twenty-two rabbits at 20 weeks old (average weight of 3.0±0.5 kg) were enrolled in the experiment. The rabbits were anesthetized with ketamine (10 mg/kg single dose) and xylazine (5 mg/kg single dose) via intramuscular injection and isoflurane (2.5% continuous inhalation) via respiratory cone mask using a previously established protocol.17)18) Continuous oxygen saturation, hemodynamic profile including blood pressure and heart rate, and electrocardiographic monitoring were maintained over the whole procedure. After administration of subcutaneous 2% lidocaine over the right carotid artery area, an incision was made, and the vessels were exposed under general anesthesia. The right-sided carotid artery was then punctured and a 10 cm length 5-Fr (human radial) sheath inserted into descending the thoracic aorta under fluoroscopic guidance with the Artis-zeeTM angiography system (Siemens Health Care, München, Germany). Two rabbits died of hypovolemic shock due to acute blood loss via the vascular rupture of access site and hyperacute pelvic hematoma before rescue angioplasty using the device. After performing baseline peripheral angiography with 50% diluted contrast and advancing a coil-type 0.014 guidewire (Runthrough®, Terumo, Japan) into the vessel, a human coronary balloon catheter (3×15 mm, Genoss, Suwon, Korea) was delivered to one of the common iliac arteries and inflated to 16–20 atm for 10 seconds to obtain arterial dissection. Sequential angiography was performed to confirm arterial dissection of Ellis type 2 or 3 which were defined by considerable contrast extravasation.19) Another two rabbits died from ventricular arrhythmia during balloon hyperinflation for dissection formation. A total of 18 iliac artery dissections were obtained. As soon as a serious arterial dissection was confirmed on angiography, a test device (3.0×24 mm) was immediately delivered to the injury site and inflated at 10 atm for 10 seconds. If continued significant extravasation was noted on follow-up angiography, adjuvant ballooning with the same one at higher pressure was performed. When the significant blood extravasation was still noted after prolonged ballooning, another device was inserted in an overlapping manner with the previous stent according to the operator's decision. Immediately after the deployment of second device, most lesions were completely sealed, except for just one case exhibiting insignificant contrast spilling, resembling a minor type 1 endoleak at the proximal edge of the device due to incomplete coverage. When treatment of the ruptured vessel was successfully attained, the rabbits were transferred to the recovery room to undergo ligation of the right carotid artery and suture of the wound over the vascular access site. Rabbits received pain killer (ketoprofen 0.06 mL/kg for 7 days), prophylactic antibiotics (cephalexin 0.15 mL/kg for 7 days), and oral antithrombotics (aspirin 2 mg/kg for 7 days).13) Quantitative vascular analyses were performed by standard techniques with automated edge-detection algorithms (CASS-5, Pie-Medical, Maastricht, The Netherlands) in the angiographic analysis core lab.

In-vivo micro-computed tomography with ex-vivo graft stent computed tomography scanning and angiographic follow-up

After the index procedures, the surviving rabbits were raised and underwent micro-CT at 2, 4, 8 weeks and 3, 6, 9, and 12 months. At every stage, micro-CT scans were performed using the Phion 1.0 (NanoFocusRay Co., Ltd., Iksan, Korea) with constant source conditions of 90 kV and 10 mA.20) The pre-and post-enhanced micro-CT images were sent to the core lab and reviewed and measured by an independent researcher (Yoo SY). After obtaining micro-CT measurements, all animals were sacrificed for histopathologic examination by injection with 20 mL of potassium chloride through the left carotid artery. Next, the treated arteries were harvested and fixed with pressure-perfusion at 110 mmHg in 10% neutral buffered formalin overnight. Before tissue preparation for the histopathological exam, ex-vivo graft stent CT scanning and three-dimensional reconstruction was performed to evaluate the deployed status of graft stent with whole flesh vascular specimen using SkyScan1173 (Bruker-CT, Kontich, Belgium). At 1 year, follow-up angiography with the same initial setting was performed in one animal.

Preparation for histopathological examination

Each tissue specimen was separated and fixed in buffered neutral formalin (Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) solution for two weeks, and the tissue sample was dehydrated with ethanol concentration. After the tissue was fixed and embedded, the pellets are placed in a UV embedding system (KULZER EXAKT 520; Kulzer, Dormagen, Germany) to cure the resin for a day. The cured specimens were attached to the slides with an adhesive press system and polished to 40±5 μm in thickness and were dyed with the hematoxylin-eosin. Measurement of the tissue morphology was performed using an optical microscope (BX51; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) with a SPOTTM Insight 2Mp scientific CCD digital Camera system (Diagnostic Instruments Inc., Sterling Heights, MI, USA) and Image Pro plusTM (Media Cybernetics Inc., Rockville, MD, USA). All the histopathological stained slides were referred for semiquantitative analysis under light microscopy by an independent pathologist (Choi KC). Microscopic observations were taken at 12.5 times magnification for whole tissue specimens. To evaluate the morphometric feature of the device and vascular morphology, each specimen was examined at 40-, 100-, and 200-times higher magnification. To quantify the peri-strut inflammatory reaction, the grading was performed according to previous literature published by Kornowski et al.21): 0 (minimal inflammatory response around strut), 1 (few inflammatory cells around strut), 2 (mild to moderate inflammation, can extend into but do not efface surrounding tissue), and 3 (dense and thick peri-strut aggregate of inflammatory cells, effacing surrounding tissue).

Statistical analysis

The data were presented as mean value ± standard deviation. A comparison among different treatment groups is performed by the Kruskal-Wallis test in the case of non-normally distributed continuous variables. A value of p<0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analysis was performed with software (SPSS Version 25; Chicago, IL, USA) on demand.

RESULTS

Index procedure: iatrogenic arterial dissection and implantation of the device

Eighteen rabbits with iliac artery dissection with huge blood extravasation were treated using the device based on peripheral angiography (Figure 4A and B, Supplementary Videos 1 and 2). As soon as vascular rupture was noted, the devices were immediately deployed to seal up the ruptured vessels. Follow-up angiography demonstrated all the dissected vessels were successfully covered by the devices (Figure 4C, Supplementary Video 3). A total of 21 study devices were deployed at 11 right iliac arteries and 7 left iliac arteries. Two devices were implanted at three lesions: One long-length rupture was not covered by one device due to dissection propagation. Two lesions required a deployment of an additional device due to continued extravasation after first device deployment and prolonged balloon tamponade.

Angiographies at index procedure and 1-year follow-up.

(A) Initial angiography. (B) Immediate after balloon dissection. (C) Immediate after the device deployment. (D) Follow-up angiography at 1 year.

After recovery from anesthesia, one rabbit walked with a pronounced limp for one day. At two days post-procedure, six rabbits died of unexplained causes. An autopsy revealed that 5 rabbits had peritonitis as the primary cause of death (three gastric and two duodenal perforations). One rabbit had myocardial necrosis around the coronary artery possibly due to iatrogenic wire injury during the intervention. There were no signs of traumatic vascular injury observed around the aorta or pelvic stented area upon close visual inspection.

Vascular imaging analysis: serial micro-computed tomography with ex-vivo graft stent computed tomography scanning and 1-year follow-up angiography

Serial micro-CT scans conducted just before sacrifice at 2, 4, and 8 weeks, and as well as 3, 6, 9, and 12 months showed no significant stenosis (Figure 5). There were no significant differences in diameter stenosis or lumen gain observed between each stage (Table 1). The ex-vivo graft stent CT with three-dimensional reconstruction images performed at 0 days and 1 month showed two overlapping test devices with partial underexpansion. Considerable late lumen loss due to neointimal hyperplasia was noted at the proximal part of the device at nine months (Supplementary Figure 1A and B). Cross-sectional CT imaging of the devices performed at 1 year showed no significant obstruction or strut exposure (Supplementary Figure 1C).

Serial histopathological examination

Serial histopathologic examination revealed various vascular responses at each stage (Figure 6). The histologic images at low and medium magnification (40× and 100×) obtained immediately after the device deployment at day 0 depicted vascular rupture with disruption across all three vascular layers. Linear red thrombi were observed along the inner wall of the graft stent. At 2 weeks, low power field histology demonstrated reduced but still existing red thrombi lining the luminal side of graft stent and partial neointimal coverage on graft stent. On a medium power field, some neointimal hyperplasia overlying stent with PCL film-type membrane is partially present. In contrast, mixed partial neointima, red thrombi, and unhealed vascular disruption with a rugged dissection flap were still observed at the distal part of the graft stent. High-power field imaging (×200) revealed that PCL membrane between the struts sealed off the ruptured vascular structure, which in turn prevented further extravasation at the dissected vessel area. It also demonstrated decreased, but still present, mural hematoma and red thrombi, and dense and thick inflammatory cell infiltration (Grade 2–3). At 1–2 months, low power field histologic images demonstrated considerable neointimal coverage of the entire graft stent. Middle power field magnification revealed gradual resorption of PCL film and some red thrombi between stent struts. At 3 months, high-power field images demonstrate marked resorption of PCL material and some lining of inflammatory cells on the surface of neointima and native vessel media (Grade 1–2). After 6–9 months, The PCL membrane became scarcely visible. Small amount of red thrombi was still noted only around peri-strut area. At one year, final histologic images with low power fields displayed substantial neointimal hyperplasia without significant luminal narrowing. Under high-power field magnification, only minimal PCL graft material was visually noted between the outer side of the struts and native vessel media accompanied by mild peri-strut area inflammatory response (Grade: 0–1).

Serial histologic examination. Low power field at 0 days and 2 weeks show dissected vessel layers were covered by the test device. As time goes, neointimal hyperplasia was not noted for up to 12 months. High power field images demonstrated that polycaprolactone film gradually decreased at 9 months and was not observed at 12 months.

DISCUSSION

Arterial dissection or perforation rarely occurs unexpectedly during a percutaneous coronary or peripheral arterial intervention, but it leads to very poor clinical outcomes.4)22) Severe coronary arterial injuries causing cardiac tamponade often demand emergency pericardiocentesis. In critical cases where hemodynamic compromise is life-threatening, cardiac surgery becomes inevitable and is associated with a very high mortality rate exceeding 20%.4)5)23) Arterial perforation usually results from balloon or stent oversizing with or without high inflation pressure during the intervention. A deeply engaged catheter or hydrophilic and stiff guidewire also can result in severe vascular tear.3) In addition, serious complications are more common in complex procedures such as treatment of chronically total occluded lesions or during atherectomy of heavily calcified and/or tortuous lesions.2)24)25) In the urgent clinical situation, prolonged balloon inflation could be firstly considered. However, balloon tamponade is not always successful and may aggravate ischemia due to blood flow obstruction. An immediate, more definitive treatment such as covered stent deployment should be considered to reduce further adverse events. It should be performed as soon as possible when prolonged ballooning is not effective.

Since the first covered stent was invented in the early 1990s, it has been continuously developed for coronary or peripheral vascular rupture.26)27) However, there are still several hurdles in clinical use. Primarily, few types of covered stents are available. Graft stents are not easily accessible in many catheterization rooms because their restricted sizes in terms of diameters and lengths. Additionally, the commercialized graft material (polytetrafluoroethylene, PTFE) is durable and exposed to the bloodstream in vivo for a very long time. As for the most widely used covered stent, it has a two-layered bare metal stent with a sandwiched PTFE graft which adversely affects deliverability due to its notably bulky profile with very low flexibility.10)12)27) Moreover, even with successful graft stent deployment, adverse clinical outcomes including stent thrombosis can occur due to delayed endothelialization over the persistently exposed durable graft in the vessel. These clinical situations inevitably necessitate the intensive life-long antiplatelet therapy which cause severe gastrointestinal or intracranial bleeding complications.8)11)23)28)

Because the test device has thin bare metal stents with flexible film-like biodegradable graft material, it could improve procedural success and overcome the shortcomings of current existing treatment modalities. Unlike covered stents with durable PTFE grafts, the test device would be advantageous for its deliverability and improved vascular apposition because of its slim profile and thin graft material properties. Although the present animal study was an experiment involving a small number of subjects, there were no cases of procedure failure.

No major clinical or angiographical adverse events were observed during the study period except for the first two days. Serial histologic exams demonstrated a favorable vascular response characterized by an inflammatory reaction and smooth muscle cell proliferation and migration to the intima layer, as previously been demonstrated.11) Neointimal formation over the stent struts at the injured vessel is stimulated by proatherogenic cytokines such as transcription factor nuclear factor kappa B, interleukin 1β, and tumor necrosis factor α which usually stops at 4–8 weeks after vascular injury, as previously described.12)21)29) In one rabbit raised for one year, the pathological response of the device was not associated with luminal obstruction due to neointimal hyperplasia or thrombosis.

The PCL graft is relatively safe because it comprises biocompatible material. PCL film was gradually resorbed and was not visible in the 1-year histologic slide. Based on the erosion test of nano-fiber matrices in vitro, PCL membrane would be degraded at a much slower rate because of its high crystallinity and hydrophobicity compared to other biodegradable polyesters. Therefore, it could prevent extravasation for a sufficient period during the acute peri-complication setting.30) Another potential advantage of the PCL membrane is that it is relatively easy to penetrate with a standard 0.014 guidewire which may be beneficial in helping to preserve major side branches.15)

There are several limitations in the present study. The enrolled number of animals is relatively small. Also, there is no control group. The results should be carefully interpreted when extended to other vascular territories because the arterial healing process may differ in different vascular beds. Although graft material was made from a biodegradable polymer and was degraded in the ex-vivo bench test, its resorption pattern in the arterial wall could not be achieved in actual conditions. This device was primarily designed for medium-sized animal experiments involving iatrogenic vascular rupture. Further research may be warranted in conditions beyond current experimental parameters such as calcified lesions. Because the vascular response of our graft stent to the rabbit iliac artery dissection model might not be the same as that of humans, our findings should not be extrapolated to the clinical setting.

In conclusion, the use of the balloon-expandable stent with a biodegradable film-type graft in treating arterial dissection demonstrates clinical feasibility, offering ease in delivery and deployment. The procedure is relatively safe and its success rate is high. Serial pathological and angiographic examinations confirm patency in the immediate post-procedural, mid-, and long-term periods. Future human studies are warranted to validate these findings and elucidate the clinical outcomes of the study.
