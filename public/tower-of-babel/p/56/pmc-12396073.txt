Evaluation of Sexual Function and Vaginal Prolapse Relapse in Patients Undergoing Laparoscopic Sacropexy Versus Pectopexy
Authors: MÓNICA ISABEL HERRMANN, BRITTA GRÜNE, MARC SÜTTERLIN, SEBASTIAN BERLIT, MAREN JULIANE WENK
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background/AimThis study aimed to compare sexual function and clinical outcomes in patients undergoing laparoscopic sacropexy (LSP) versus laparoscopic pectopexy (LPP) to treat vaginal cuff or uterine prolapse.Patients and MethodsA cross-sectional explorative cohort study of 46 patients who underwent LSP (n=23) or LPP (n=23) in our tertiary care hospital was conducted. The patients’ postoperative sexual function was evaluated (female sexual function index, FSFI), and prolapse stages were determined with the pelvic organ prolapse quantification system (POP-Q).ResultsBoth groups showed comparable baseline parameters. Mean age was higher in the LSP group (66.76 vs. 61.30 years, p=0.14). The operation time was slightly longer in the LPP group (118 vs. 109 min, p=0.12). Uterus preservation was more common in LPP (p=0.02). There were a few low-grade complications [two patients LPP, one patient LSP, Clavien-Dindo-Classification (CDC) I and II, p=1.00], but no complications of ≥CDC III. Postoperative POP-Q results were comparable for both groups (p>0.05 in all parameters). The FSFI showed similar postoperative results in both groups with a slightly higher total score for the LPP cohort, even though not significant (1.60 vs. 1.40 LSP vs. LPP cohort; p=0.35). A multiple linear regression analysis was performed, however no factors influencing postoperative sexual function were identified.ConclusionA comparable efficacy of apical fixation was found for LSP and LPP, complications were low in both groups, and the FSFI-results were comparable with slightly better results in the LPP group. Therefore, LPP seems to be a viable and safe alternative in the treatment of POP.

Introduction

Pelvic organ prolapse (POP) is a condition that significantly impacts the quality of life (QoL) of affected women worldwide (1). Characteristic symptoms include pain, bowel, and voiding dysfunction. While the physical and psychological consequences of POP have been studied extensively, little is known about its effects on sexual function (2). Sexual dysfunction in women with POP can become a significant problem, leading to a complex interplay between physical discomfort, emotional distress, and altered self-esteem (3).

Laparoscopic sacropexy (LSP) combines the suspension of the middle compartment with the benefits of minimally invasive surgery (4). Typical limitations and risks encountered upon performing LSP are producing narrowness in the pelvis by placing a mesh between sacrum and uterus, cervix or vagina, causing potential defecation problems (5). Also, the operation can be challenging in obese patients due to enlarged sigmoid colon. Changes in sexual function have rarely been reported in the literature and information is frequently confounded by procedures that are performed simultaneously such as perineorrhaphy (5). There are conflicting results in different studies showing an increase of dyspareunia (6) but also a decrease or resolution of preexisting dyspareunia (7).

Since 2011, laparoscopic pectopexy (LPP) has emerged as a viable alternative for uterine/vaginal vault fixation, offering potentially fewer complications particularly in obese patients (8,9). The technique involves securing a mesh on both sides of the descending lateral parts of the iliopectineal ligament to suspend uterus, cervix or vagina. Studies have shown that LPP can improve QoL and sexual function (10,11).

With the increasing number of younger patients undergoing POP surgery and the rising awareness regarding QoL and sexual function, it is crucial to inform patients specifically which outcomes can be expected postoperatively and to support patient-centered and individualized decision-making (12,13). Therefore, the objective of this study was to compare LSP with LPP in terms of not only perioperative outcomes and complications, but also efficacy, and postoperative sexual function.

Patients and Methods

After institutional ethics review board approval, patient data were collected from the hospital’s database, using the operation and procedure codes for LSP and LPP. A cross-sectional explorative cohort study was performed. Female patients who underwent LSP or LPP at our tertiary care center from January 1, 2016, to December 31, 2021, were provided questionnaires upon consenting. Exclusion criteria were age <18 years, previous radiation of the pelvis, previous gynecological tumors and patients with malformations.

Questionnaires. All included patients completed the German version of the validated Female sexual function index (FSFI) questionnaire (14) assessing sexual function. It contains 19 questions and assesses sexual function in the last four weeks, covering six different domains: desire, arousal, lubrication, orgasm, satisfaction, and pain. Each domain is scored on a scale of 0/1 to 5. The total score ranges from a minimum of two to a maximum of 36 points. Higher scores indicate better sexual function. Additionally, a self-administered questionnaire on demographic, general, and health care data (14 questions) was completed.

Parameters. The following parameters were retrospectively collected from the patients’ electronic medical records: Age, body mass index (BMI), parity, American Society of Anaesthesiologists (ASA) score, time since surgery, history of incontinence, history of POP, treatment of POP before and after surgery, and state after hysterectomy. In addition, the following perioperative parameters were recorded: Blood loss, operation time, additional procedures, simultaneous laparoscopic supracervical hysterectomy (LASH), complications within the first 30 days after surgery [Clavien Dindo Classification (CDC) and Comprehensive Complication Index (CCI)], length of hospital stay, and reoperation due to recurrent POP in the course.

Pelvic organ prolapse quantification (POP-Q) measurement. If POP-Q results were not postoperatively documented in the patients file, patients were invited to a voluntary vaginal examination with evaluation of POP-Q stages at least three months postoperatively.

Surgical technique. Standard endoscopic equipment was used for all surgeries. LASH was performed when indicated, alongside elevation of the vagina/cervix to POP-Q level I. Procedures often performed additionally were adnexectomy and occasionally, anterior or posterior colporrhaphy for concomitant cysto- or rectocele. Postmenopausal patients were advised to use vaginal oestrogens preoperatively and estriol cream postoperatively, along with pelvic floor exercises starting four weeks postoperatively.

LSP: All patients were treated with a Parietex™ flat mesh (Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN, USA), which was placed using a tunnelling technique to minimize peritoneal opening, then fixated on the promontory using the ProTack™ system (Covidien, Dublin, Ireland) with permanent helical titanium tacks. It was then covered with peritoneum to isolate it from abdominal organs. The mesh's distal “y”-shaped end was attached to either the cervical stump (ventral and dorsal attachment), uterus wall (ventral attachment), or vaginal apex, using non-absorbable sutures.

LPP: A 3×15 cm monofilament mesh (DynaMesh-PRP™, DynaMesh, Osnabrück, Germany) was fixed in a hammock-like fashion to the iliopectineal ligaments also using non-absorbable sutures. The cervical stump (ventral and cranial), the ventral uterine wall or the vaginal apex was elevated to a tension-free position for fixation using the same non-absorbable sutures.

Statistical analysis. Descriptive statistics were carried out to present the baseline and perioperative characteristics. Quantitative data are expressed as mean and standard deviation (SD) or median and interquartile range (IQR), and categorical data are expressed as absolute and relative frequencies. We performed group comparison using the Pearson’s Chi square test or Fisher’s exact test (if necessary) for categorial variables and Wilcoxon Mann Whitney test for ordinal scaled variables. Predictive factors for better postoperative sexual function were identified using a stepwise multiple linear regression analysis. All statistical analyses were carried out with JMP version 14 [Statistical analysis system (SAS) Institute, Cary, NC, USA]. The level of significance was set at 0.05.

Ethical approval. The study has been approved by Ethics committee II University Medicine Mannheim (Nr. 2022-511).

Results

Patient enrolment. From 01/2016 to 12/2021, 111 female patients were operated with LSP or LPP in our tertiary care hospital and received the questionnaire. A total of 60 patients initially responded. Of those, 14 withdrew their consent in the course or were no longer responsive (lost to follow up), resulting in a total of 46 analyzable patients (LSP: n=23, LPP: n=23, response rate 41.4%). POP-Q measurement data of 32 patients were suitable for analysis (LSP: n=14, LPP: n=18). Patient enrolment is shown in Figure 1.

Baseline characteristics. The mean age of patients in the LSP-group was slightly higher than that in the LPP-group (66.74±11.88 years vs. 61.30±12.45 years, p=0.14). All preoperative parameters were comparable (p>0.05), except for time since surgery (2.78±1.44 years for LPP versus 3.74 ±1.39 for LSP, p=0.02). Fifteen (LPP) and ten (LSP) patients underwent preoperative therapy in terms of pessaries, physiotherapy, or pelvic floor exercises. Seven (LPP) and eight (LSP) patients underwent further pelvic floor treatment postoperatively, with more patients in the LSP-group requiring surgery for recurrence than in the LPP-group (6 vs. 4 patients, p=0.61). A multiple linear regression analysis was performed to evaluate possible factors influencing sexual function. Specifically, the age of the patient, BMI, and postoperative POP-Q-stages were examined. None of these factors showed a significant influence in the regression analysis.

Discussion

In this study, we analyzed the outcomes of LSP and LPP in a cohort of 46 patients. The mean age of patients was slightly higher in the LSP-group compared to the LPP-group. The LSP-group also had a longer follow-up duration. Preoperative conditions were similar between groups, and perioperative parameters revealed comparable ASA scores, reflecting similar preoperative health.

Operation time was somewhat longer for LPP. When interpreting the results, it should be noted that significantly more additional procedures were performed in the LPP-group than in the LSP-group, which is very likely the reason for the longer operating times in the LPP-group. Unfortunately, the operating time for LPP alone cannot be reproduced. After some initial learning curve associated with LPP as a newer technique, most studies, such as the study by Heusinkveld (15), show a shorter operation time for LPP, averaging 138 min compared to 158 min for LSP. It is noteworthy that the operation times in our cohort, although including additional procedures, were below these numbers with an average of 118.70 min (LPP) and 105.70 min (LSP). A shorter operation time would be expected because there is no need to open the deep pelvic space, perform ureterolysis, or mobilize/ligate vessels to perform LPP. One also does not need to perform extensive removal of adhesions or mobilisation of the colon, saving valuable operating time (15).

LASH was performed based on clinical indications or explicit patient request. Notably, a significant difference was found between the two groups regarding hysterectomy rates. This disparity could be due to the better feasibility of uterine preservation while performing LPP. The bilateral fixation may facilitate a more physiological positioning of the uterus and vagina, potentially influencing the decision to preserve the uterus (9). Additionally, the “y”-shaped mesh is classically placed on the front and back cervical/ vaginal surface after subtotal/total removal of the uterus. Patients are increasingly inclined to preserve the uterus when there is no indication for its removal. This trend is common across Europe and has been incorporated into German medical treatment protocols (16,17).

Complication rates were low and minor across both groups, and blood loss was similar for both techniques. It should be considered that the complications could have been caused by the additional procedures and not the LPP or LSP itself, which cannot be determined with certainty. LPP carries a lower risk of bleeding-related complications as it avoids the highly vascularized sacral venous plexus (15). Heusinkveld et al. found an overall complication rate of 6.0% for LPP vs. 16.5% for LSP, even though not statistically significant (p=0.65). This aligns with our results: less deep dissection in the pelvis and no need for colon mobilization leads to less risk of intraoperative complications such as bleeding, nerve damage or thromboembolic episodes (18).

Postoperative POP-Q assessments showed no significant differences between groups, though LSP demonstrated better anterior outcomes, while FSFI scores were slightly better in the LPP-group across all domains, although without statistical significance. Postoperative assessment of the middle compartment showed no significant differences between the LPP and LSP groups, indicating that LPP is an equally effective option for apical fixation in the treatment of POP (8,9). However, distinct recurrence patterns were observed: posterior compartment defects were more prevalent in the LPP group, whereas anterior compartment defects were more common in the LSP group. These patterns likely reflect the anatomical anchoring points of each procedure. In LPP, the anterior pelvic fixation may lead to a redistribution of tension that leaves the posterior compartment more vulnerable (2). This is supported by the integral theory of Petros and Ulmsten, which was first described in 1990 (19). It should be mentioned that the POP-Q examination was conducted at different postoperative time points and by different surgeons, which represents a possible bias factor.

According to the patients, a recurrent prolapse occurred in 2.0% of patients subjected to LPP compared to 6.3% of patients subjected to LSP (p=0.66). In the study presented by Nygaard et al., the median reoperation rate after abdominal sacrocolpopexy was 4.4% (range=0-18.2%) during the follow-up intervals of 6 months to 3 years. (5) Maher et al. reported that 6% of women required repeat prolapse surgery after sacral colpopexy and 14% after vaginal procedures (4).

POP is a complex condition, and sexual dysfunction in women is similarly multifaceted (20). Results regarding the improvement of sexual function after POP-repair are inconclusive (21,22). Helström et al. found that vaginal repairs can worsen sexual function, likely due to nerve and blood supply damage, and changes in lubrication (21). However, Sarlos and colleagues observed improved sexual function after LSP, probably because it avoids vaginal dissection and usage of foreign materials (22). Regarding the FSFI scores in our study, we can generally observe a higher score after LPP in every domain, although statistical significance was not reached. The fact that more and different additional interventions were performed in the LPP-group represents a confounding factor. It cannot be ruled out that the changes in sexual function are caused by LASH or colporrhaphy and not by LPP or LSP itself. To allow for a better comparison in the future, future studies should be prospective and attempt to randomize more equal cohorts. In general, there is very little data concerning female sexuality and POP surgery in the literature. Given Jr. and colleagues studied the role of vaginal length regarding sexual function after performing sacropexy, which seems to be better obtained compared to transvaginal sacrospinous fixation (23,24). This is not surprising as the vaginal length remains unchanged if the uterus is preserved. Fatton et al. have shown that although surgery to correct prolapse improves sexual function, it also increases risks of de novo dyspareunia. Therefore, it is important to screen and counsel patients for sexual issues already before surgery (25,26).

In our study, the mean total FSFI score was 16.60 for the LPP-group and 14.07 for the LSP-group (p=0.35). A comparison with normative values for healthy women is difficult, as these can vary in different population groups and cultural contexts. A later study by Wiegel et al. introduced a clinical cut-off value of 26.55, suggesting that women below this value are at risk of sexual dysfunction. However, this cut-off value is intended for clinical screening and does not represent an average value for healthy individuals (27). In addition, the women in our study cohort were not screened preoperatively for sexual dysfunction, which allows only limited conclusions to be drawn. Most women seeking treatment for POP have several problems concerning sexuality, such as avoidance, negative emotions during intercourse, decreased sexual arousal and poor orgasm quality (28,29). Urinary, pelvic and defecatory symptoms seem to be strongly associated with decreased sexual function, while this does not correlate with the degree of prolapse (29). This emphasizes the need for prospective studies on sexual function before and after LSP and LPP.

This study's retrospective design is a limitation, as some complications may have been caused by additional operative procedures or may have been overlooked if patients sought treatment elsewhere, either in other hospitals or outpatient settings. In addition, the cohort size was small and the response rate not as high as expected. There is a risk of selection bias by soliciting volunteers for the vaginal examination. Additionally, the higher number and greater variety of concomitant procedures performed in the LPP group represent confounding factors that must be taken into account when interpreting the study results.

Nevertheless, our study provides a comprehensive insight into several aspects of postoperative outcomes that are generally underreported and highlights the importance of patient-centered care and counseling to identify individual treatment aspects and patient needs. Patient-related outcome measures are essential nowadays, as symptoms and anatomical findings can differ, and the focus should ultimately be on curing symptoms. Larger prospective studies with a long-term follow-up are warranted to better understand the differences and advantages of both procedures to further improve counselling and optimal patient selection.

Sexual function remains a complex issue to evaluate and analyse. Due to its nature and multifactorial influences, surgical intervention may alleviate symptoms that are directly related to anatomical changes and body image. In general, many women who visit the urogynaecology practice have some form of sexual discomfort and this should be openly addressed during the consultation. A woman's desire to remain sexually active or to become sexually active again should be respected and play a role in the choice of method. This also applies to women of advanced age.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that both LSP and LPP are effective and safe apical fixation techniques with comparable perioperative outcomes, low complication rates, and similar postoperative FSFI scores. LPP showed a slight, although nonsignificant advantage in postoperative sexual function. LPP, particularly in experienced hands, may offer specific benefits, including reduced bleeding risk and suitability for high-risk patients such as those with obesity. Despite the retrospective design and limited sample size, this study provides valuable insights into POP management that can be used to counsel patients individually.
