Perioperative anesthetic challenges in neuromyelitis optica: balancing safety and relapse risk with neurotechnology
Authors: Summayya Anwar, Ali Hamza, Haris Afridi, Muhammad Talha, Suleman Khan, Aizaz Anwar Khalid, Asim Shah, Asraf Hussain
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

Neuromyelitis optica (NMO) is a rare autoimmune disorder that primarily affects the central nervous system, particularly the optic nerves and spinal cord, through injury to astrocytes mediated by aquaporin-4 antibodies[1]. NMO, although different from multiple sclerosis, can lead to severe and often irreversible disabilities, which have necessitated the development of specific perioperative considerations. Surgical interventions for these patients are rarely needed, but when they are necessary, they present great challenges for anesthesiologists. They must carefully balance providing adequate anesthesia with the risk of potentially triggering a disease exacerbation[2].

According to the case report, recent perioperative experiences in anesthesia management for lower-segment cesarean section in neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder emphasize the importance of avoiding relapse triggers through careful anesthetic planning, multimodal analgesia, and close neuromuscular monitoring[3]. Perioperative stress, fluctuations in body temperature, and certain anesthetic agents can precipitate new neurological deficits in NMO patients[4]. The anesthesiologist’s objective is therefore twofold: to provide sufficient sedation and analgesia for surgical tolerance while preventing central nervous system instability. This challenge is complicated by the limited availability of evidence-based perioperative guidelines specific to NMO, with most recommendations extrapolated from broader demyelinating disease literature[2].

Advances in neurotechnology offer a promising solution to one of the most critical gaps in NMO perioperative management: precise, real-time monitoring of anesthetic depth. Conventional parameters, such as heart rate, blood pressure, and clinical signs, are indirect and can be misleading in patients with autonomic dysregulation or baseline neurological deficits[5]. Modern brain-monitoring modalities, including bispectral index (BIS) analysis, entropy monitoring, and processed electroencephalography (pEEG), provide direct neurophysiological feedback on cortical activity, enabling individualized anesthetic titration[5]. BIS quantifies EEG patterns into a numerical index correlating with consciousness levels. While validated in general surgical populations, its utility in NMO warrants focused study, particularly considering altered neurophysiological baselines in demyelinating disorders[5].

Although uncommon, intraoperative neurological flare-ups in NMO can be catastrophic. A rise in body temperature may worsen conduction block in already demyelinated axons, and drops in blood pressure or oxygen saturation can compromise spinal cord blood flow, intensifying symptoms. Incorrect anesthetic dosing, whether excessive or insufficient, carries its hazards. Too much can overly suppress central nervous system activity; too little can leave the patient at risk of awareness, both situations triggering stress responses and possible immune activation. Continuous neurophysiological monitoring allows the anesthesiologist to fine-tune drug delivery in real time, limit sudden depth changes, and keep circulation and oxygenation stable. Evidence from high-risk surgical populations shows that pEEG-guided anesthesia can also lower the incidence of postoperative cognitive problems and delirium[6]. For patients with NMO, whose nervous systems are particularly fragile, these advantages may help preserve function and improve recovery.

Surgery in NMO demands both caution and precision. A comprehensive protocol should begin with a baseline neurophysiological evaluation, followed by tailored depth monitoring and close collaboration between anesthesiology and neurology teams. Preoperative planning needs to account for recent disease activity, current immunosuppressive treatment, and any relevant Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) findings. Intraoperatively, combining BIS or entropy monitoring with standard vital sign tracking can give a fuller picture of anesthetic depth. After surgery, ongoing neurological checks are essential, with early imaging if any new deficits appear. Building multicenter databases that collect perioperative and monitoring data from NMO cases could help shape evidence-based guidelines and encourage wider adoption of these tools. Incorporating neurotechnology enhanced by artificial intelligence and supported by emerging case-based evidence offers a means to deliver individualized care that avoids depth extremes and reduces relapse risk. Making such monitoring a standard part of perioperative management could be a crucial step toward lowering complications and protecting patient outcomes.
