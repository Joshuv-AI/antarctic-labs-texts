First-line selpercatinib for a patient with RET fusion-positive pulmonary large cell neuroendocrine carcinoma
Authors: Tatsuru Eihuku, Seiichi Miwa, Tomoko Oshima, Hisano Ohba, Masahiro Shirai
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPulmonary large cell neuroendocrine carcinoma (LCNEC) is an uncommon variant of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), known for its aggressive behavior. This includes rapid progression, widespread metastases, and resistance to conventional chemotherapy, all of which contribute to a dismal prognosis. Consequently, managing pulmonary LCNEC remains a significant challenge. In this case report, we describe the successful use of selpercatinib, RET (rearranged during transfection) kinase inhibitor, as a first-line treatment in a patient with advanced pulmonary LCNEC harboring a RET fusion gene. Although RET fusion genes are exceedingly rare in pulmonary LCNEC, this case underscores the importance of early genetic testing in patients with pulmonary LCNEC to tailor targeted therapies effectively.Keywords: Large cell neuroendocrine carcinoma, Non-small cell lung cancer, RET rearrangements, Selpercatinib

Introduction

Pulmonary large cell neuroendocrine carcinoma (LCNEC) is a rare and highly aggressive variant of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [1]. It is characterized by its aggressive behavior, including frequent recurrence and metastasis, which often leads to a poor prognosis [1]. As a result, the management of pulmonary LCNEC poses significant challenges [2]. In recent years, various genetic abnormalities have been identified in NSCLC, particularly in adenocarcinoma. These abnormalities include mutations in the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), rearrangements in anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) and ROS1, mutations in BRAF, and rearrangements in the rearranged during transfection (RET) gene [3]. These discoveries have catalyzed the development of targeted therapies, which have shown promising outcomes in the treatment of advanced NSCLC [3]. Among these genetic alterations, RET rearrangements are relatively rare, occurring in only 1–2 % in NSCLC cases [[4], [5], [6]]. Selpercatinib, a selective RET kinase inhibitor, has demonstrated significant efficacy in patients with advanced or metastatic NSCLC harboring RET gene fusions [7,8].

In this report, we present a case with pulmonary LCNEC who developed systemic metastases harboring a KIF-5B-RET fusion gene. Despite a poor performance status, the patient exhibited a favorable response to selpercatinib, leading to sustained disease stability. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first case report documenting the use of first-line selpercatinib therapy in a patient with advanced pulmonary LCNEC with a RET rearrangement.

Case presentation

A woman in her seventies presented to a nearby hospital with a history of back pain lasting several weeks. A chest X ray showed mediastinal lymphadenopathy and an opacity in the right lower lung field (Fig. 1A). Chest computed tomography (CT) identified mediastinal lymphadenopathy and atelectasis of the middle lobe. Consequently, she was referred to our hospital.

Chest X ray showing mediastinal lymphadenopathy and mild shadows in the right lower lung field prior to referral (A). Contrast chest and abdominal computed tomography (CT) (coronal image) demonstrating mediastinal lymphadenopathy, atelectasis of the middle lobe secondary to a mass involving a tumor and enlarged mediastinal lymph node, and multiple ill-defined liver legions at the time of referral (B).

Her medical history included hypothyroidism, hyperlipidemia and chronic gastritis. There was a familial history of gastric cancer in her mother and grandfather. She had never smoked and drank alcohol socially.

Upon evaluation, her temperature was 36.7 °C, blood pressure 144/76 mmHg, heart rate 77 beats per minute, and the oxygen saturation 97 % while breathing ambient air. There were no signs of anemia nor jaundice. Superficial lymph nodes were not palpable. Breath sounds were diminished in the right lung. Point tenderness was noted on some vertebrae. Laboratory findings highlighted an elevated lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) level (497 U per liter, reference range: 124–222) and an increased carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) level (248.8 ng per milliliter, reference value: <5). A CT scan of the chest and abdomen, following intravenous contrast administration, revealed mediastinal lymphadenopathy, atelectasis of the middle lobe secondary to a mass involving a tumor and enlarged mediastinal lymph node, and multiple ill-defined liver legions (Fig. 1B). Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) with contrast revealed edema in the dura mater (Fig. 2A) and legions in the base of the left brain (Fig. 2B). Subsequent bronchoscopy and transbronchial biopsy led to a pathological diagnosis of LCNEC confirmed by hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining showing poorly differentiated atypical cells with abundant cytoplasm (Fig. 3A) and positive immunohistochemistry for CD56 (Fig. 3B), chromogranin A (Fig. 3C), and synaptophysin. Program death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression was negative in less than 1 % of tumor cells. Furthermore, KIF5B-RET fusions were identified using the Oncomine Dx Target Test.

Contrast brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) indecating edema in the dura mater (A)(arrow) and abnormal legions in the base of the left brain (B) (arrowhead).

Transbronchial biopsy showing poorly differentiated atypical cells with abundant cytoplasm by hematoxylin and eosin staining (A), and positive immunohistochemistry for CD56 (B) and chromogranin A (C).

Following the biopsy, 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/CT (18F-FDG-PET/CT) indicated multiple metastases including in the liver, bone, adrenal glands and brain, and revealed a lung abscess, a complication of the transbronchial biopsy (Fig. 4A and B). The final diagnosis was stage Ⅳb pulmonary LCNC with a RET mutation.

18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/CT (18F-FDG-PET/CT) (A: maximum intensity projection image, B: fused axial image) indicating multiple metastases including in the liver, bone, adrenal glands and brain, and revealing a lung abscess, as a complication of the transbronchial biopsy.

Ten days post-referral, she was admitted to our hospital due to fever from lung abscess and disease progression with deteriorated performance status (PS) (Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) scale:4), left ptosis and visual loss. Serum CEA (556.3 ng per milliliter) and neuron specific enolase (NSE) (618 ng per milliliter, reference value: <16.3) was markedly elevated. Following abscess management with percutaneous transthoracic tube drainage and antibiotics, treatment with selpercatinib (120mg twice daily) was initiated, resulting in rapid clinical improvement (Fig. 5). However, dose adjustment was necessary due to elevated aminotransferase levels such as alanine transaminase (ALT) and aspartate transaminase (AST) (Grade 3).

Clinical course: Selpercatinib decreases serum CEA and NSE levels. CEA: carcinoembryonic antigen, NSE: neuron specific enolase.

Seven weeks into the treatment, a temporary halt in selpercatinib administration due to further aminotransferase elevation resulted in rapid disease progression. The treatment was resumed, allowing for an increase in aminotransferase levels. After twelve weeks of treatment, although selpercatinib-related pneumonitis was suspected, the condition spontaneously improved after the treatment was stopped for several days. Subsequent radiographic evaluations showed a reduction in the size of the lung lesion, mediastinal lymph nodes, and liver metastases. Her PS improved from 4 to 1, with a significant decrease in serum tumor marker levels. Although left ptosis improved, the visual loss remained unchanged.

Four months after initiating selpercatinib, with disease progression under control, she was discharged home.

Nine months into the selpercatinib treatment, despite stable brain metastases on MRI, she opted for additional therapy, undergoing gamma knife radiosurgery with doses of 12Gy and 30Gy to the 50 % isodose lines for cerebral falx and left cavernous sinus, respectively.

Nineteen months following the initiation of selpercatinib, she remained alive, demonstrating sustained disease control.

Discussion

Pulmonary LCNEC is recognized for its aggressive behavior, including rapid progression, widespread metastasis, and resistance to conventional chemotherapy. In our report, we describe the early detection of RET rearrangements in a patient with advanced pulmonary LCNEC with multiple metastases. This patient was successfully treated with selpercatinib as first-line therapy. Similarly, Arora et al. [9] reported an Asian female patient with RET fusion-positive advanced pulmonary LCNEC responded well to selpercatinib; however, in their case, selpercatinib was administered after initial platinum-based chemotherapy.

A metanalysis by Lin el [4]. on NSCLC found RET fusion genes in only 1.4 % of patients with adenocarcinomas and even less frequently in 0.3 % of patients with non-adenocarcinoma. This mutation tends to occur predominantly in younger Asian females (less than sixty years old) who are non-smokers [4]. Our case aligns demographically except for age. Additionally, genomic research by Kim et al. [10], involving 467 cases of pulmonary LCNEC, did not detect any RET fusions, underscoring the rarity of such genetic anomalies in pulmonary LCNEC.

Selpercatinib, an ATP-competitive, highly selective small-molecule RET kinase inhibitor with effective central nervous system penetration, has shown promise. In a recent clinical randomized trial comparing selpercatinib with platinum-based chemotherapy with a PD-1 inhibitor in untreated patients with advanced RET fusion–positive NSCLC, selpercatinib showed superior efficacy [8]. However, of the 261 patients included in the study, only four non-adenocarcinoma cases were documented, with just one receiving selpercatinib.

Elevated liver-function values, particularly ALT, AST, and bilirubin are recognized adverse events of selpercatinib [7,8]. Most cases can be managed with dose adjustments without necessitating treatment discontinuation [8]. In our case, elevated transaminase levels necessitated either dose adjustments or a temporary suspension of selpercatinib treatment. However, pausing selpercatinib led to rapid disease progression. Consequently, we decided to continue the treatment, closely monitoring transaminase levels and symptoms, allowing for some increase in transaminase levels. The elevation of transaminase levels during selpercatinib treatment could potentially reflect the effectiveness of selpercatinib, particularly in case with liver metastasis. Effective targeting and destruction of tumor cells in the liver by selpercatinib may release cellular contents, including transaminases, into the bloodstream – a phenomenon, akin to tumor lysis syndrome, which could result in elevated enzyme levels.

Conclusion

Although RET rearrangements are infrequent in pulmonary LCNEC, comprehensive genetic analysis remains crucial, especially oncology increasingly embraces precision medicine. Identifying actionable mutations such as RET can significantly influence treatment decisions and outcomes in these challenging cases.
