Large-scale multiomic analysis identifies non-coding somatic driver mutations and nominates ZFP36L2 as a driver gene for pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma
Authors: Jun Zhong, Aidan O’Brien, Minal B Patel, Daina Eiser, Michael Mobaraki, Irene Collins, Li Wang, Konnie Guo, ThucNhi TruongVo, Ashley Jermusyk, Sudipto Das, Maura O’Neill, Courtney D Dill, Andrew D Wells, Michelle E Leonard, James A Pippin, Struan F A Grant, Tongwu Zhang, Thorkell Andresson, Katelyn E Connelly, Jianxin Shi, H Efsun Arda, Jason W Hoskins, Laufey T Amundadottir
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe identification and characterisation of somatic cancer driver mutations in the non-coding genome remains challenging.ObjectiveTo broadly characterise non-coding driver mutations for pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC).DesignUsing mutation calls from whole-genome sequence data in PDACs and genome-scale maps of accessible gene regulatory regions in normal-derived and tumour-derived pancreatic samples, we analysed enrichment of non-coding mutations in gene regulatory regions relevant to normal-derived and tumour-derived pancreatic contexts. Functional follow-up of potential driver mutations was performed using chromatin interaction analyses, massively parallel reporter assays (MPRA) and targeted analysis of selected non-coding somatic mutations (NCSMs).ResultsWe first created genome-scale maps of accessible chromatin regions (ACRs) and histone modification marks (HMMs) in pancreatic cell lines and purified pancreatic acinar and duct cells. Integration with whole-genome mutation calls from 506 PDACs revealed 314 ACRs/HMMs significantly enriched with 3614 NCSMs. Chromatin interaction analysis identified 416 potential target genes and MPRA revealed 178 NCSMs impacting reporter activity (19.45% of those tested). Targeted luciferase validation confirmed negative effects on gene regulatory activity for NCSMs near ZFP36L2 and CDKN2A. For the former, CRISPR interference identified ZFP36L2 as a target gene (16.0–24.0% reduced expression, p=0.023–0.0047), and growth inhibition after overexpression of ZFP36L2 (4.1–14.1-fold reduction, p=6.0×10–4 − 3.2×10–3) implicates a possible tumour suppressor function.ConclusionOur integrative approach provides a catalogue of potential non-coding driver mutations and nominates ZFP36L2 as a novel PDAC driver gene with a likely tumour suppressor function.Keywords: MUTATIONS, PANCREATIC CANCER, GENE REGULATION, CHROMATIN, EPIGENETICS

Introduction

Driven mainly by diagnosis at a late stage and a lack of effective treatment modalities, pancreatic cancer is characterised by an overall 5-year survival rate of only 13% and is predicted to become the second leading cause of cancer-related deaths in the USA by the end of this decade.1,3 This highlights an urgent need for further research into the aetiology of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) to improve outcomes for this cancer of unmet need. Cancer develops due to somatic and germline genetic factors exerting their influence within the cellular context of environmental factors.4 Identification of driver mutations can significantly enhance our understanding of tumourigenesis and facilitate precision oncology.5 Thanks to large-scale whole exome sequencing studies, protein-coding somatic driver mutations for PDAC (eg, in KRAS, TP53, SMAD4, CDKN2A) have been well characterised.6,8 The discovery of recurrent non-coding mutations in cis-regulatory elements (CREs) implicated in the expression regulation of genes associated with oncogenesis, such as in the TERT promoter,9 10 TAL1 super-enhancer11 and PAX5 enhancers,12 has highlighted an emerging role of somatic mutations in non-coding regulatory regions contributing to carcinogenesis. Although most somatic mutations occur in non-coding regions of the genome, identifying driver mutations and their target genes has remained largely elusive.

Non-coding somatic mutations (NCSMs) can disrupt transcription factor (TF) binding sites that impact the activity of gene regulatory elements including promoters and enhancers.13 These elements are often highly tissue or cell type-specific, with publicly available annotations of PDAC-relevant elements limited in scope and sample size.14 15 With this in mind, we generated genome-wide maps of accessible chromatin regions (ACRs) and histone modification marks (HMMs) in pancreatic tumour-derived and normal-derived pancreatic cell lines, as well as from pancreatic acinar and ductal cell populations purified using flow cytometry. We integrated these elements with NCSMs identified in 506 PDAC genomes16,18 to identify potential drivers, while adjusting for background mutational rates. To characterise the functional consequences of NCSMs, we applied massively parallel reporter assay (MPRA)19 to identify mutations associated with altered transcription regulatory activity. Subsequently, we used genome-scale high-resolution promoter-focused Capture-C (PCC)20 and H3K27ac HiChIP-seq (HiChIP)21 to identify chromatin interactions that link NCSMs to putative target genes. Our work provides new insights into the potential role of NCSMs in PDAC development and establishes an effective framework for applying a multi-omics approach to unravel complex CREs in their relation to cancer biology.

Results

We leveraged an integrative multiomics approach, using genome, epigenome, chromatin interactome and transcriptome datasets for PDAC to identify candidate non-coding driver mutations for further analysis. Putative drivers were then characterised by MPRA, investigation of disrupted TF binding sites and alternative splicing analysis that ultimately prioritised a set of NCSMs most likely to represent non-coding driver mutations (study design outlined in figure 1A).

Quality control and whole-genome annotation of somatic mutations from 506 patients with PDAC

Whole genome somatic mutation calls from PDAC tumours were obtained from the International Cancer Genome Consortium (ICGC-PACA-AU and ICGC-PACA-CA)16 17 and the Pancreatic Cancer Translational Research Initiative (PanCuRx).18 Following quality control, samples from 506 patients remained (see Methods), with a mean somatic mutation count of 6883 (range 102–46 628). The somatic mutation rate in the combined dataset averaged 2.22 mutations per Mb and 98.95% of somatic mutations were in non-coding regions of the genome (figure 1B), of which 59.71%, 31.42% and 6.55% localised to intergenic, coding intronic and non-coding RNA intronic regions, respectively (figure 1C). The most frequently mutated protein coding genes across the three studies were similar, including KRAS, TP53, CDKN2A and SMAD4 (online supplemental figure 1–3).

We first annotated somatic mutations using the FunSeq2 pipeline and ENCODE (The Encyclopedia of DNA Elements) data across all available cell types and tissue samples (figure 1D). The top recurrently mutated non-protein-coding regions (annotated by ENCODE) (online supplemental table 1) included enhancers at chr6:68,590,900–68,601,300 (mutations observed in 40 out of 506 patients with PDAC; 7.91%) and chr17:57,913,400–57,927,100 (33 patients; 6.52%), and two lincRNAs, TSIX and XIST on chrX:73,012,040–73,049,066, (35 patients; 6.92%). The most frequently recurrent NCSMs (identified in ≥10 patients, or in 1.98–2.77% of patients, n=24 NCSMs total) are listed in online supplemental table 2.

Mapping pancreatic accessible chromatin regions and histone modification marks

To identify enrichment of non-coding mutations in gene regulatory elements that are most relevant to normal-derived and tumour-derived pancreatic tissues, we performed ATAC-seq and histone ChIP-seq analysis in two normal-derived (HPDE-E6E7, hTERT-HPNE) and six tumour-derived (COLO357, KP-4, PaTu8988T, SU.86.86, MIA PaCa-2, PANC-1) pancreatic cell lines (table 1). Moreover, we reanalysed data generated in fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) purified primary pancreatic acinar (n=3 for ATAC-seq, n=2 for ChIP-seq) and ductal (n=4 ATAC-seq, n=3 ChIP-seq) cell populations obtained from donor pancreas with no reported pancreas dysfunction.22 This identified 28 346–258 494 ACRs in primary pancreatic cell populations and cell lines (table 1, online supplemental tables 3 and 4, online supplemental figure 4 and 5, see Methods). Likewise, we mapped genomic regions with HMMs typically enriched at promoters, enhancers and repressed chromatin, including H3K4me1 marks (n=73 249–267 643), H3K4me3 marks (n=27 693–174 233), H3K27ac marks (n=86 479–279 075) and H3K27me3 marks (n=20 848–48 886) in the same cell lines and pancreatic exocrine cells (table 1). We then used ChromHMM23 to model eight chromatin states (online supplemental figure 6) and ROSE2 to map super-enhancers (SE, n=851–2107) (table 1). In total, we identified 2 929 610 ACR and HMM annotations that were used to assess enrichment of somatic PDAC mutations.

Identification of non-coding somatic mutations significantly enriched in ACRs/HMMs

To identify non-coding gene regulatory elements enriched with NCSMs as compared with the background mutational rate, we applied ActiveDriverWGS24 and DriverPower25 (functional adjustment by DANN26 and Eigen27) to the epigenetic annotations mapped in: (1) FACS purified pancreatic acinar cells, (2) FACS purified pancreatic duct cells, (3) normal-derived pancreatic cell lines and (4) PDAC-derived cell lines. This identified a total of 314 ACRs/HMMs significantly enriched with somatic mutations (FDR≤0.05) across these cell types (table 2, online supplemental table 5). Where ACRs/HMMs overlapped coding sequences, we observed significant enrichment of somatic mutations in known protein coding driver genes including KRAS, TP53, CDKN2A and SMAD4 (online supplemental table 6).

NCSM-mediated alterations of gene regulatory activity

To functionally assess if the 988 NCSMs highlighted by our enrichment analysis above alter gene regulatory activity, we performed MPRA in two human pancreatic cancer cell lines (PANC-1 and MIA PaCa-2) and a human embryonic kidney cell line (HEK293T). Following quality control (Methods, online supplemental figure 7), 184 out of 915 testable mutations (20.11%, see Methods) exerted significant differential transcriptional activity between mutant (MT) and wild-type (WT) alleles in PANC-1 cells, 118 (12.90%) in MIA PaCa-2 cells and 300 (32.79%) in HEK293T cells (FDR≤0.05) (figure 2A–C). Subsequently, ‘MPRA-significant’ mutations were further deemed ‘MPRA-functional’ when either the MT or WT allele demonstrated an average RNA/DNA TPM ratio that diverged substantially from a null distribution derived from the activity of all scrambled control sequences. Using this approach, 87 (9.51%), 50 (5.46%) and 117 (12.79%) MPRA-functional NCSMs were identified in PANC-1, MIA PaCa-2 and HEK293T cells, respectively. In total, 178 unique NCSMs (19.45% of those tested) were MPRA-functional in one or more cell lines (figure 2D, online supplemental table 10).

We selected the most significant MPRA-functional NCSM across the three cell lines (at chr2p21 near ZFP36L2, MPRA FDR-adjusted p value rank 1, 7 and 1 in PANC-1, MIA PaCa-2 and HEK293T cells, respectively) and two mutations at chr9p21.3 in the last intron of CDKN2A (MPRA rank 10–18) for validation using individual luciferase assays (figure 3). The MT at chr2p21 (310 bp downstream of ZFP36L2, chr2:43,449,231 C>T) exhibited reduced gene regulatory activity in PANC-1 (MPRA log2(MT/WT) = −0.49, p=4.81×10−170), MIA PaCa-2 (MPRA log2(MT/WT) = −0.38, p=8.44×10−49) and HEK293T cells (MPRA log2(MT/WT) = −0.80, p<1×10−308), which replicated by luciferase assays (PANC-1 cells: MT-T allele vs WT-C allele fold change in the forward orientation=0.36, p=2.95×10−7; fold change in reverse orientation=0.33, p=3.42×10−6; MIA PaCa-2 cells: MT-T allele vs WT-C allele fold change forward=0.36, p=3.99×10−5; reverse=0.39, p=8.73×10−6) (figure 3A and B). At chr9p21.3, two mutations were noted in an HMM in the last intron of CDKN2A. The former (chr9:21,968,244 G>C) was associated with reduced MPRA activity in PANC-1 (log2(MT/WT) = −0.36, p=3.53×10−25), MIA PaCa-2 (log2(MT/WT) = −0.55, p=4.29×10−31) and HEK293T (log2(MT/WT) = −0.44, p=2.66×10−28) cells. Nominating candidate target genes for NCSMs

To identify putative target genes for mutations enriched in non-coding gene regulatory regions, we used proximity to transcriptional start sites (TSS) and chromatin interaction analyses. Of the 988 enriched NCSMs, 36 were directly located in promoters (see Methods) of 26 target genes (figure 4A and D) including ZFP36L2 (encoding ZFP36 Ring Finger Protein Like 2, 6 mutations), MIR21 (microRNA 21, 3 mutations) and CPA3 (Carboxypeptidase A3, 1 mutation) (online supplemental table 11). Subsequently, we generated chromatin interaction maps in MIA PaCa-2 and PANC-1 cells using high-resolution PCC20 and observed 161 and 194 NCSMs in gene regulatory elements that physically interacted with the promoters of 166 and 183 potential target genes, respectively (figure 4B and E, online supplemental table 12). These included strong interactions (CHICAGO score ≥15, see Methods) with the promoters of SNHG1 (small nucleolar RNA host gene 1) and SLC3A2 (Amino acid transporter heavy chain SLC3A2) at chr11q12.13 and several core histone genes (H2BC21, H2AC20, H2AC21) at chr1q21.2. Similarly, using H3K27ac HiChIP-seq (HiChIP) data generated in purified pancreatic acinar and ductal cell populations, and in PANC-1 cells,28 29 we identified 164, 137 and 122 NCSMs in regulatory elements that physically interacted with the promoters of 99, 124 and 107 target genes, respectively. These included strong interactions (PET counts≥100, see Methods) with the promoters of CPB1 (Carboxypeptidase B) and AGTR1 (Type-1 angiotensin II receptor) at chr3q24, MIR21 (microRNA 21) at chr17q23.1 and SLC12A7 (Solute carrier family 12 member 7) at chr5p15.33 (figure 4C and F, online supplemental tables 13 and 14). In summary, 416 potential target genes were identified, in which NCSMs localised to promoters or gene regulatory elements that physically interacted with gene promoters. Six target genes (MTMR11, HIST2H2BC, BOLA1, ANP32E, SLC3A2 and RALGDS) were common among both the PCC and H3K27ac HiChIP-seq interaction data across all pancreatic cell populations and cell lines (online supplemental figure 8).

Identifying target genes for NCSMs at chr2p21 near ZFP36L2

The MPRA-functional, luciferase validated, NCSM at chr2p21 (chr2:43,449,231 C>T) localised to an ACR downstream of the ZFP36L2 gene (figure 5A), and to HMMs tagging promoters and enhancers. A nearby NCSM (chr2:43,449,213 ->AA) within this enriched ACR was also MPRA-functional in the three cell lines (log2(MT/WT) = −0.08 to −0.12, p (FDR corrected) = 0.01 to 5.4 x10−8, MPRA rank 46–64 (listed in purple text in figure 2a–c, online supplemental table 10) but was not validated with luciferase assays due to a smaller effect size. Using the chromatin interaction data sets described above, we identified five potential target genes for this enhancer: LINC01126, THADA, LINC02580, LINC01819 and HAAO (figure 5B). To identify genes regulated by this enhancer, we targeted it with CRISPR inhibition and measured the expression of these five genes, and ZFP36L2, as the two mutations are only ∼4.5 kb away from its TSS, possibly limiting detection of chromatin interactions. Three of the four guides resulted in a significant reduction of ZFP36L2 expression in PANC-1 cells (16.0–24.0% knockdown, p=0.005–0.023; the fourth guide showed a marginal reduction of 15.7%, p=0.072) and one of four guides in MIA PaCa-2 cells (22.5% knockdown, p=0.0081; three other guides showed 8.9–21.0% reductions, p=0.057–0.26) (figure 5C) indicating that it may represent a target gene for this enhancer. We also noted smaller reductions in one of the three tested THADA isoforms in PANC-1 cells (0–11.9%, p=0.015–0.98) and MIA PaCa-2 cells (6.6–14.1%, p=0.005–0.12) indicating that it may also be regulated by this enhancer. Significant effects were not seen on the expression of the other genes tested (online supplemental figure 9).

Mutations were also located directly in the 5’UTR (in five or 0.99% of all patients in our study), coding region (in nine patients; 1.78%) and the 3’UTR of ZFP36L2 (four patients; 0.79%). Coding mutations included frameshift (five patients), non-synonymous (three patients) and stop-gain (one patient) mutations indicating probable deleterious effects on the protein (figure 5A). Coding mutations in ZFP36L2 were not observed in tumours from patients with PDAC harbouring mutations in the downstream enhancer.

Due to the presence of (1) MPRA and luciferase functional NCSMs in a downstream enhancer that regulates expression of ZFP36L2, and (2) predicted deleterious ZFP36L2 coding mutations in tumours from the ICGC-PACA and PanCuRx consortia, we hypothesised that ZFP36L2 may represent a tumour suppressor for PDAC. To test this hypothesis, we used lentiviral transduction to overexpress ZFP36L2 and assessed cell proliferation in vitro. This resulted in a strong reduction in cell proliferation in both PANC-1 (fold change=−5.4, p=3.2×10−3) and MIA PaCa-2 cells (fold change=−2.1, p=6.0×10−4 (figure 5D, online supplemental figure 9C), consistent with a possible tumour suppressor function.

The MPRA-functional, luciferase-validated mutation at chr2:43,449,231 C>T is predicted to disrupt TF binding motifs for multiple members of the Krüppel-Like (KLF) family of TFs, including KLF1, KLF2, KLF5, KLF9 and others (figure 5E, online supplemental table 16). As the motif for KLF9 showed the strongest predicted disruption (52.25-fold), we validated this prediction using surface plasmon resonance and noted 3.17-fold reduced binding of recombinant KLF9 protein to the mutated (T) allele (figure 5F). We furthermore assessed the binding of KLF2, KLF5, KLF9 and RUNX2 to chr2:43,449,231 using an electrophoretic mobility shift assay and observed weaker binding to the mutated (T) as compared with the WT allele for all four proteins, confirming the predicted binding effects of the mutation (online supplemental figure 9D). Combined, our results indicate that an enhancer regulating ZFP36L2 expression can be disrupted by somatic mutations in PDAC. Furthermore, KLF9 (and likely other members of this family of TFs) may regulate expression of ZFP36L2 via this enhancer.

To investigate likely gene and protein expression changes downstream of ZFP36L2, we overexpressed the gene in two pancreatic cancer cell lines (PANC-1 and Mia PaCa-2 cells) and performed RNA-sequencing and global proteomics analysis followed by gene set and pathway enrichment analyses (see online methods). This highlighted a strong positive correlation between ZFP36L2 messenger RNA (mRNA) expression and genes important for chaperone cofactor-dependent protein refolding (normalised enrichment score (NES)=2.30, p=4.02 × 10−6) including HSPA6 which was the most upregulated gene (log2 fold change 2.53, p=2.7 × 10−31). DNA repair genes were also positively correlated with ZFP36L2 expression (NES=1.84, p=1.96 × 10−6), while genes important for cytoplasmic translation (NES=−2.48, p=1.00 × 10−10) and aerobic respiration (NES=−2.24, p=5.75 × 10−8) were negatively correlated at the mRNA level (online supplemental table 17). At the protein level, negative correlation was noted between ZFP36L2 expression and genes involved in cell-matrix adhesion (NES=−2.52, p=1.90 × 10−8) and aerobic respiration (NES=−2.11, p=1.08 × 10−6) (online supplemental table 18). This implies that loss of ZFP36L2 function, either via coding mutations or non-coding mutations that result in reduced expression, may impact several pathways that are important for pancreatic homeostasis and carcinogenesis.

Identification of splicing mutations

We next investigated NCSMs in splice sites across the genome (using ANNOVAR annotations) and identified 361 patients (71.34%) with at least one splice site mutation considered damaging and possibly pathogenic. Of these, 30 patients (5.93%) had at least one recurrent mutation impacting splice sites in known tumour suppressor genes, including TP53, CDKN2A and SMAD4 (online supplemental table 19). Splice site mutations in CDKN2A (chr9:21,968,244 G>T/C) and SMAD4 (chr18:48,593,559 T>A/G) appeared to negatively affect splicing as indicated by RNA-seq reads showing disrupted normal splicing (figure 6A, B, D and E; online supplemental figure 10A and B). Specifically, exon 2–3 splicing was disrupted in patients with CDKN2A splice acceptor mutations, and exon 10 skipping events were observed in patients with SMAD4 splice site mutations (in frame). We confirmed the impact of predicted splicing mutations for both CDKN2A and SMAD4 using exon trap assays in MIA PaCa-2 and PANC-1 cells (figure 6C and F; online supplemental figure 10C). The observed exon-skipping patterns were consistent with the predicted effects, suggesting a direct causal relationship. Notably, coding mutations in CDKN2A or in SMAD4 were not observed in tumours from the patients with PDAC harbouring splicing mutations in these two genes. Of note, the two NCSMs in a splice acceptor site in the last intron of CDKN2A (chr9:21,968,244 G>T/C) negatively affected gene regulatory activity in both the MPRA and luciferase assays (as described above).

Discussion

The importance of somatic coding mutations in cancer initiation and progression has been well established since the discovery of a mutated HRAS oncogene over 40 years ago.30 While most driver mutations have historically been identified in protein coding regions of the genome, non-coding somatic drivers are increasingly considered important components of oncogenesis.31 The vast size of the non-coding genome, combined with ambiguous effector mechanisms, complicates this search. In this study, we present a comprehensive integrative analysis to nominate candidate non-coding somatic driver mutations for PDAC and identify putative target genes. Our strategy employed three main steps. We first characterised the PDAC-relevant chromatin landscape by mapping ACRs and HMMs in tumour-derived and normal-derived pancreatic cell lines and purified pancreatic acinar and ductal cell populations, thus greatly expanding the breadth and scope of previously generated datasets for this organ. Subsequent enrichment analysis prioritised gene regulatory elements that may be somatically implicated in PDAC development. In the second step, we applied MPRA to identify functionally relevant somatic mutations in these elements, showing that a sizeable proportion exerted significant disruptions to cis-regulatory activity. Finally, we applied an integrative approach to link mutated regulatory elements to putative target genes via chromatin interaction analysis and comparative transcriptomics, providing a valuable resource for understanding the downstream effects of non-coding mutations on gene regulation and possible mechanisms by which NCSMs may contribute to PDAC.

Our framework nominated mutations downstream of ZFP36L2 as candidate non-coding drivers. These mutations decreased cis-enhancer activity for the encompassing gene regulatory element, which appears to drive ZFP36L2 expression, possibly via members of the KLF and RUNX TF families. Additional mutations were located throughout the ZFP36L2 gene in close to 4% of patients included in this study. The fact that most of the coding mutations result in frameshifts or premature stop codons further supports a role for ZFP36L2 as a pancreatic cancer driver gene with a possible tumour suppressor function. This gene encodes mRNA decay activator protein ZFP36L2, a zinc-finger RNA-binding protein of the tristetraprolin family that regulates mRNA stability by binding and promoting the decay of adenylate-rich and uridylate-rich element-containing mRNAs and plays important roles in controlling cell cycle progression, DNA damage repair and immune responses.32 33 While most studies indicate a tumour suppressor role for ZFP36L2,34 35 one study has indicated that this gene may be overexpressed in PDAC where it drives cell proliferation and invasion in vitro, supporting a possible oncogenic role.36 Our results showing growth inhibition in pancreatic cancer cell lines in vitro after overexpression lend support for a tumour suppressor function for ZFP36L2 in PDAC, in line with most published studies in other cancer types. Furthermore, our pathway enrichment analysis indicated that ZFP36L2 may play important roles in protein folding as well as response and repair of DNA damage, which could affect how cells respond to extracellular factors that influence endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, the unfolded protein response and DNA damage.37 38 ZFP36L2 loss may also affect cellular plasticity in the pancreas by influencing acinar to ductal metaplasia, possibly in inflammatory contexts, and increase the likelihood of pancreatic intraepithelial neoplasia and malignant transformation.39

Three pan-cancer studies have investigated potentially functional non-coding driver mutations in 2250,40 265817 and 394915 tumours by whole-genome sequence, including PDAC. These studies used multi-tissue HMMs and TF annotations generated by ENCODE and the National Institutes of Health Roadmap Epigenomics Consortia for enrichment analysis and nominated target genes based on the closest genes. Coding mutations in ZFP36L2 were identified in 0–8.3% of tumours across different organs (including 1.8–6.41% of PDAC), lending support to a possible tumour suppressor function. Non-coding driver mutations were not described, but ZFP36L2 promoter mutations were enriched in ovarian cancer (8.63%).15 Our results indicate that expression of this gene is also influenced by NCSMs in PDAC in an enhancer downstream of the gene. Further work is warranted to investigate the role of ZFP36L2 in PDAC development, particularly in an in vivo setting.

Two studies have specifically focused on non-coding driver mutations for PDAC. The first assessed enrichment in ENCODE defined gene regulatory elements and then focused on mutations within gene promoters (±2 kb of TSS).41 While non-coding mutations in promoters were identified, specific mutations were not followed up with functional experiments. More recently, an analysis of CREs marked by H3K27ac in PDAC cell lines and patient-derived organoid samples was used to detect putative CRE drivers enriched for significant NCSMs, followed by STARR-seq reporter assays for candidate functional mutations, nominating 43 potential functional mutations.42

Beyond mutations that mediate their effect by disrupting active enhancer elements, we identified splicing mutations in CDKN2A and SMAD4, occurring in patients without coding mutations in each gene. Experimental validation supported a direct causal relationship between these NCSMs and aberrant splicing, highlighting them as likely drivers that impact two well-known PDAC tumour suppressor genes. Surprisingly, mutations in a splice acceptor site in the last intron of CDKN2A influenced both splicing and gene regulatory activity. While effects of somatic mutations on splicing are already known at other splice sites in this gene, a possible gene regulatory effect at this genomic location requires further follow-up to better define its function and target gene(s).

Our study provides important advantages as compared with previous studies. First, we focused our search for non-coding driver mutations on genome-wide ACR and HMM maps derived from pancreatic-derived cell lines and primary pancreatic acinar and duct cell populations. As compared with previously available datasets with only small numbers of pancreatic cell lines and bulk pancreatic tissue samples, the maps we generated should represent a more refined picture of gene regulatory elements in the pancreas. To our knowledge, this is the largest whole-genome non-coding driver mutation analysis for PDAC. Specifically, we used enrichment methods25 that outperform other methods for non-coding somatic driver discovery. Importantly, we applied MPRA to functionally assess the effects of somatic non-coding mutations on gene regulatory activity and conducted genome-scale chromatin interaction analyses to link non-coding mutations to target genes. Despite these strengths, our approach is not without limitations. Foremost, given the inherent challenges of enrichment analyses for the mostly non-recurrent somatic non-coding mutations our work (and that of previous investigations) has identified, our analysis remains underpowered to characterise potentially important NCSMs in personal genomes. Likewise, differential expression analysis in tumours with and without specific mutations is underpowered due to the low frequency of most mutations and mutated gene regulatory elements. Furthermore, as gene regulation depends on the cellular context, developmental stage and environmental stimuli, future studies incorporating such conditions when mapping non-coding gene regulatory elements are likely to enhance discovery of non-coding driver mutations. Simulating inflammation and ER stress might be important next steps for such an approach.

In conclusion, by creating maps of non-coding gene regulatory elements in pancreatic cell lines and purified exocrine cell populations, we have nominated and functionally assessed novel candidate driver mutations for PDAC and identified likely target genes. While non-coding mutational hotspots do not appear to be common in PDAC, it is likely that important gene regulatory elements are targets for mutations that may drive cancerous growth. Our findings contribute to the understanding of the molecular mechanisms driving PDAC and provide avenues for further research with potential implications for patient care and outcomes for this cancer of dismal prognosis. Future follow-up studies of the catalogues of candidate somatic driver mutations highlighted here, incorporated with non-coding germline risk variants,43 epigenetic drivers44 and artificial intelligence-powered approaches,45 may uncover more of the dark matter important for carcinogenesis in the non-coding genome.
