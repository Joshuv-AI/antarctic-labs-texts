Does HPV vaccination during periconceptional or gestational period increase the risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes?—An updated systematic review and meta‐analysis based on timing of vaccination
Authors: Jiawei Zhang, Zhirun Lian, Xiaoou Xue, Jun Li, Yuying Zhu, Nian Huang, Wei Xie
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccine is crucial in preventing cervical cancer, and a significant number of women in 135 countries worldwide may have unknowingly received the vaccine during peri‐pregnancy or pregnancy due to a lack of regular pregnancy testing. Previous studies on the safety of pregnancy outcomes with vaccination before and after pregnancy have not reached definitive conclusions. Thus, we subdivided the vaccination time frame and conducted an updated study to further examine whether exposure to the HPV vaccine during pregnancy or the periconceptional period increases the likelihood of adverse pregnancy outcomes.Material and MethodsThe clinical trials and cohort studies published before August 1, 2023, were retrieved from PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials. The Newcastle–Ottawa Scale and Cochrane risk of bias assessment tool were adopted to evaluate the risk of bias in the included studies. In addition, the quality assessment was carried out using the Review Manager 5.4 Software, and a meta‐analysis was conducted using the Stata 16 Software.ResultsEleven studies were located. The results showed that receiving 4vHPV during the periconceptional or gestational period had no relationship with an increased risk of spontaneous abortion, stillbirth, preterm birth, birth defects, small for gestational age, and ectopic pregnancy. Neither receiving 2vHPV nor 9vHPV was associated with a higher risk of stillbirth, preterm birth, birth defects, small for gestational age, and ectopic pregnancy; however, receiving 2vHPV during the period from 45 days before last menstrual period (LMP) to LMP and 9vHPV during the period from 90 days before LMP to 45 days after LMP seemed to be related to an increased risk of spontaneous abortion (RR = 1.59, 95% CI: 1.04–2.45, RR = 2.04, 95% CI: 1.28–3.24).ConclusionsIn conclusion, the likelihood of an elevated risk of spontaneous abortion caused by HPV vaccination during the periconceptional or gestational period could not be completely ruled out. Given the lack of evidence, further research is needed to examine the effect of HPV vaccination on spontaneous abortion.Keywords: human papillomavirus vaccine, meta‐analysis, pregnancy outcome, systematic review, vaccine safety

Key message

HPV vaccination during the periconceptional or gestational period is not associated with an increased risk of stillbirth, preterm birth, birth defects, small for gestational age, or ectopic pregnancy. However, vaccination during this period does not rule out the possibility of an increased risk of spontaneous abortion, and the evidence for a causal relationship between HPV vaccination and the risk of spontaneous abortion is not yet sufficient.

INTRODUCTION

As of September 2023, 1 135 countries worldwide have included human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccination in their national immunization programs, and tens of millions of women around the world have received HPV vaccines, including bivalent human papillomavirus vaccine (2vHPV), quadrivalent human papillomavirus vaccine (4vHPV), and nonavalent human papillomavirus vaccine (9vHPV). HPV vaccination is recommended as a routine vaccination for female children aged 11–12 in many countries, and it is also recommended that women who are below 26 years old and have not yet received HPV vaccination should be vaccinated. 2 In this case, many women of childbearing age receive HPV vaccines prophylactically without regular pregnancy testing. As a result, some women have received HPV vaccines without knowing that they had already gotten pregnant, or they had unintended pregnancies after receiving HPV vaccines.

Because adequate and well‐controlled studies on pregnant women rarely provide evidence on the safety of HPV vaccination during pregnancy, the World Health Organization (WHO) and vaccine manufacturers recommend not receiving HPV vaccines during the gestational period. 3 Besides, although the U.S. Food and Drug Administration classifies HPV vaccines as a pregnancy drug class B drug, 4 , 5 pregnant women are still concerned about the possible adverse effects of HPV vaccine on pregnancy, such as spontaneous abortion (SAB), stillbirth, and birth defects. Any degree of adverse pregnancy events can lead to concerns about the behavior of HPV vaccination in these women, which in turn reduces the potential benefits of HPV vaccines in preventing cervical cancer and its precancerous lesions. 6 , 7 To date, many studies have analyzed the association between HPV vaccination during the periconceptional or gestational period and the risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes (APOs), but no certain conclusions have been made. 8 , 9 , 10 Based on the meta‐analysis published by Tan 8in 2019, 2vHPV vaccination 45 days before pregnancy may elevate the risk of SAB. In two other meta‐analyses with unclassified time windows, 9 , 10 no association was found between HPV vaccination during the periconceptional period or pregnancy and APOs including SAB, stillbirth, preterm birth, birth defects, and SGA, implying that more studies are required to evaluate the impact of HPV vaccine exposure during this particular periconceptional period or pregnancy on APOs.

Given the conflicting evidence and the updated data, we conducted a systematic review and meta‐analysis of evidence from previous clinical studies to elucidate whether exposure to HPV vaccine is related to an elevated risk of APOs, to inform antenatal counseling for HPV‐exposed populations.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

We performed a systematic review and meta‐analysis of the effects of HPV vaccination during the periconceptional or gestational period on APOs following the Cochrane Manual 11 and PRISMA guidelines, 12 and this study has been registered with PROSPERO (CRD42022373835).

Literature retrieval and study selection

The literature retrieval was conducted in such electronic databases as PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and Central Register of Controlled Trials. To avoid missing out information, the following terms were used: “human papillomavirus vaccine” or “HPV vaccine” and “gestation” or “pregnancy.” The retrieval time was from the establishment of these databases to 1 August 2023. The detailed retrieval strategy is shown in Table S1. In addition, a manual search of the reference lists of the included papers was conducted to get access to other relevant publications.

We included all clinical trials and cohort studies that assessed periconceptional or gestational exposure to HPV vaccines and that reported available outcome data and the time interval between HPV vaccination and pregnancy. The pregnancy outcomes were recorded. For multiple publications of the same study, the most recent publication or the version with comprehensive data was adopted. Only English studies were searched and included, while unpublished or non‐peer‐reviewed studies were excluded. In addition, the following studies were eliminated: case reports, studies without a control group or with non‐pregnant women as the control group, and studies only focusing on the immunogenic characteristics or intrinsic adverse events of anti‐HPV vaccines.

Literature screening and data extraction

Two authors read and independently screened the studies, extracted relevant data, and cross‐checked the results. Disagreement, if any, was discussed between the two investigators or addressed through consultation with a third investigator. When screening literature, the investigators first read the titles, and after excluding obviously unrelated literature, they further read the abstracts and full texts to determine whether the articles should be included or not. Risk of bias assessment

The risk of bias in the included studies was examined by two independent researchers, and the results were cross‐checked by them. The risk of bias in cohort studies was assessed using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale. 13 In this scale, studies were assessed against the following domains: the selection of participants, the comparability of study groups, and assessment of exposure. Each relevant study was graded on a 0–9 scale. A higher score indicates higher study quality. The methodological quality of the included randomized controlled trials (RCTs) was assessed using the Cochrane risk of bias assessment tool. 11 The risk of bias assessment was performed from the following six perspectives: selective bias, performance bias, detection bias, attrition bias, reporting bias, and other biases. Specifically, the risk of bias in each study was determined as “Low,” “High,” or “Unclear.”

Data statistics

Given the variability in the timing of vaccination across studies, four time windows were considered: (1) −90 days to +45 days of last menstrual period (LMP), HPV vaccination between 90 days before and 45 days after LMP. (2) −45 days to LMP, vaccination within 45 days before LMP. (3) During pregnancy, vaccination after LMP until the end of pregnancy. (4) −90 days to the end of pregnancy, vaccination from 90 days before LMP to the end of pregnancy (Figure 1). We analyzed the effects of HPV vaccination on pregnancy outcomes in different time windows.

Exposure time window for HPV vaccination in the periconceptional or gestational period. LMP, last menstrual period.

Every efficacy analysis was conducted in the intention‐to‐treat (ITT) population, involving participants who were all randomly assigned. Statistical analysis was performed using the Stata 16 software. The effect size of the binary variable was presented as relative risk (RR), and 95% CI was calculated for each effect size. The χ2 test was performed to analyze the heterogeneity among the included studies. The heterogeneity was quantitatively determined by I 2. 14 If the heterogeneity was not significant (I 2 < 50%), a fixed effects model was used for meta‐analysis. Otherwise, a random effects model was adopted. Sensitivity analysis was performed to explore sources of heterogeneity. We performed Egger's test to assess the publication bias.

Association of 2vHPV vaccination with APOs

We performed a meta‐analysis of the four different exposure times mentioned above. The results showed that 2vHPV vaccination during −90 days to the end of pregnancy had no association with an increased incidence of SAB (RR = 1.12, 95% CI: 0.91–1.39), stillbirth (RR = 0.66, 95% CI: 0.15–2.94), preterm birth (RR = 0.77, 95% CI: 0.44–1.33), birth defects (RR = 0.84, 95% CI: 0.33–2.16), and ectopic pregnancy risk (RR = 1.32, 95% CI: 0.22–7.88), and 2vHPV vaccination during pregnancy seemed not related to a higher risk of SAB (RR = 0.85, 95% CI: 0.45–1.61). We found that vaccination with 2vHPV during −90 days to +45 days of LMP did not increase the incidence of SAB (RR = 1.22, 95% CI: 0.94–1.60), stillbirth (RR = 0.83, 95% CI: 0.28–2.42), preterm birth (RR = 1.05, 95% CI: 0.69–1.61), birth defects (RR = 0.85, 95% CI: 0.47–1.53), SGA (RR = 0. 85, 95% CI: 0.47–1.53), and ectopic pregnancy (RR = 1.31, 95% CI: 0.22–7.82). However, vaccination with 2vHPV within the period from −45 days to LMP seemed to be related to a higher risk of SAB (RR = 1.59, 95% CI: 1.04–2.45) with a statistically significant difference (p < 0.05) (Figure 4A–F).

DISCUSSION

To the best of our knowledge, the present study analyzed the most up‐to‐date RCTs and cohort studies examining the link between HPV vaccination during the periconceptional or gestational period and APOs.

This study demonstrated that vaccination with 4vHPV during the periconceptional or gestational period had no association with an increased risk of APOs, including SAB, stillbirth, preterm birth, birth defects, SGA, and ectopic pregnancy. Vaccination with 2vHPV or 9vHPV during periconceptional or gestational also had no association with a higher risk of stillbirth, preterm birth, birth defects, SGA, and ectopic pregnancy. However, vaccination with 2vHPV during −45 days to LMP and vaccination with 9vHPV during −90 days to +45 days of LMP did not rule out the likelihood of an elevated risk of SAB. Given the paucity of literature incorporating both 2vHPV and 9vHPV, findings in the current research should be interpreted with caution.

An issue of particular interest in interpreting the results is the risk of SAB following HPV vaccination in early pregnancy. The concentration of antigens in the HPV vaccine of various valences is very low, whereas adjuvants account for a larger proportion of the total vaccine components. Therefore, the safety of adjuvants to mothers and fetuses should be considered when HPV vaccines are vaccinated for pregnant women. The adjuvants for 2vHPV, 4vHPV, and 9vHPV are different. The 4vHPV and 9vHPV adjuvants are aluminum salt adjuvants (light‐based aluminum phosphate sulfate). Although aluminum‐adjuvanted vaccines have been reported to be safe for a long term, 26 studies have found that aluminum adjuvants may cause autoimmune diseases and neurotoxicity, and their safety in pregnant women and fetuses needs to be further evaluated. 27 , 28 The 2vHPV, however, contains a more complex AS04 adjuvant system, which a hybrid adjuvant consisting of aluminum adjuvant and TLR4 agonist MPL (3‐Odesacyl‐4′‐monophosphoryllipid). 29 MPL is a potent immune system stimulator that can induce high antibody and T‐cell responses, while aluminum adjuvant can prolong MPL‐activated cytokine responses at the local immune level. 30 Pregnancy loss has been associated with an abnormal maternal immune response and increased T‐cell activation during early pregnancy and proinflammatory response. 31 When a vaccine containing the AS04 adjuvant is injected into the body, it firstly recruits blood and tissue innate effector cells to the injection site. AS04 transiently induces local NF‐κB activity and cytokine production to activate antigen‐specific T cells through TLR4 signaling, thereby inducing T‐cell immune responses and altering the immune system in pregnant women. This may be one of the reasons for the increased incidence of SAB. However, other studies revealed that other ASO4‐containing vaccines were not associated with the risk of miscarriage. Therefore, the underlying mechanism still needs to be further explored. 32

Recent studies have shown that HPV vaccination may have potential benefits not only for HPV‐associated infections and precancerous lesions but also for pregnancy outcomes, 33 including reduction of preterm birth rate 34 , 35 , 36 and risk of SGA. 35 However, considering that exposure to HPV vaccine in the periconceptional period or during pregnancy does not exclude an increased risk of SAB, HPV vaccination during this special period should be given deep consideration to avoid risks and gain benefits. Our study supports the current WHO recommendations for HPV vaccination that it is not recommended to receive HPV vaccines during pregnancy, and if pregnancy occurs after the first dose, it is recommended that the remaining doses should be postponed until the end of pregnancy. 37 Governments should promote universal access to vaccination for women before childbearing age, and women of childbearing age may consider pregnancy testing before vaccination.

The main strength of the current research is that it begins with a meta‐analysis that addresses a controversial and important clinical question about the association between HPV vaccination during the periconceptional or inter‐pregnancy period and APOs. This study classified the type of vaccine exposed and the time window of exposure based on the original literature to ensure the completeness and authenticity of the data analysis. However, there are limitations in our study. Firstly, most of the retrospective studies were conducted by HPV vaccine manufacturers for regulatory purposes with more or less involvement of the vaccine manufacturers. There was the presence of publication bias, and most of the studies had limited adjustment for confounding factors such as age, number of doses, and time of pregnancy at baseline, as well as for important factors such as mothers' history of smoking, alcohol use, drug use, and pregnancy history. All these differences may have contributed to the heterogeneity among studies. In addition, many cases of potential SAB were excluded by chart review and adjudication, because the gestation did not reach 6 weeks or intrauterine pregnancy could not be confirmed. Second, the classification of the time window for vaccination was inconsistent or unspecified across studies, reducing the accuracy of subgroup analyses, and more rigorous grouping is needed in the future. Third, the definition of pregnancy outcomes varied across the included studies, which may have contributed to some analytical errors. Finally, the control group varied. We found that, in addition to placebos, many studies chose women who received HPV vaccine in non‐pregnancy and peri‐pregnancy or who received other vaccines, such as hepatitis A vaccine, as controls. The clinical studies that used aluminum salts and hepatitis vaccines as controls ignored the impact of adjuvants. In fact, they only compared the safety of the antigenic components of the vaccine, instead of the safety of the vaccine itself, which may lead to a certain selection bias. Women who were vaccinated during pregnancy or gestation may have differed from those who were vaccinated during the distal window of exposure and those who were vaccinated with other vaccines, in terms of the risk of SAB or adverse birth outcomes.

CONCLUSION

According to studies published to date, HPV vaccination during the periconceptional or gestational period is not associated with an increased risk of stillbirth, preterm birth, birth defects, SGA, or ectopic pregnancy. However, vaccination during this period does not rule out the possibility of an increased risk of SAB, and the evidence for a causal relationship between HPV vaccination and the risk of APOs is not yet sufficient. Researchers should focus on the association between HPV vaccination during the periconceptional or gestational period and APOs. Considering the current stage of research and the immunogenicity of the vaccine, women of childbearing age should be cautious about receiving HPV vaccine during these particular periods. In addition, physicians should pay attention to the pregnancy status of women before any vaccination and improve the collection of data on pregnancy outcomes for future studies.
