Extracorporeal Membrane Oxygenation–Assisted Ablation of Extremely Refractory Purkinje Ventricular Fibrillation
Authors: Daniel Alyesh, Nicholas Palmeri, Lukas Cerbin, Alexandra Smart, William Choe, Ryan Jordan, Jehu Mathew, Samantha Smith, Madison Keith, Sri Sundaram
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFascicular ventricular arrhythmias represent a spectrum of disease ranging from premature ventricular contraction, idiopathic ventricular tachycardia to idiopathic Purkinje ventricular fibrillation (VF). In the most severe cases, VF can become refractory to many therapies and require emergency ablation with hemodynamic support.Case SummaryWe present a case of a 42-year-old man with no significant past medical history who presented with refractory VF that required over 100 shocks and eventual extracorporeal membrane oxygenation–assisted ablation.DiscussionExtreme presentations of Purkinje VF are rare with few multicenter reports in the literature informing prognosis and best practices. This pathophysiology is postulated to involve the Purkinje-myocardial junction.Take-Home MessagesTimely recognition of refractory VF and the need for advanced hemodynamic support is critical for yielding a good outcome. This case highlights the importance of bystander cardiopulmonary resuscitation and highly coordinated care among specialties yielding many lessons learned and an excellent outcome.Key words: ECMO, fascicular VT, ventricular fibrillation

History of Presentation

A 42-year-old man with no significant past medical history was admitted to the hospital following an out-of-hospital cardiac arrest. He had awoken his wife with agonal breathing. She promptly checked his pulse, began cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR), and called 911. He was intubated in the field, defibrillated, and transported to the hospital. In the emergency room, his physical exam revealed a healthy-looking man who was intubated and sedated with unremarkable cardiopulmonary exam. His neurologic exam was nonfocal, and when sedation was weaned, he followed commands, thus cooling/normothermia was not pursued.

He was admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) and started on intravenous amiodarone. After several hours without further arrhythmia, he was extubated.

Differential Diagnosis

At this time, the differential diagnosis included myocardial infarction, myocarditis, vasospasm, and acute inflammatory infiltrative cardiomyopathy such as sarcoidosis.

Investigations

His echo revealed a structurally normal heart. However, his high-sensitivity troponin peaked at 17,123 ng/L, and he underwent cardiac catheterization which revealed nonobstructive coronary artery disease.

Management Course

Upon returning to the ICU from cardiac catheterization, he developed further recurrent ventricular fibrillation (VF) requiring shocks and intubation. This was despite the ongoing continuous infusion of amiodarone. He was shocked approximately 100 times with continuous high-quality CPR performed. He was started on a continuous lidocaine infusion, in addition to the amiodarone, but his arrhythmia was refractory.

He was transferred to a tertiary medical center for emergency cannulation of veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO). After successful cannulation in the operating room, with the cardiothoracic surgery team, he was transferred to the ICU where he was evaluated by the electrophysiology service. At this time, the patient remained in persistent VF with ECMO circulatory support. The electrophysiology team performed multiple 360J defibrillations, but a unifocal premature ventricular contraction (PVC) from a suspected site of origin at the left posterior fascicle toward the apex (Figure 1) led to reinitiation of VF. This site of origin was suspected given the right bundle morphology, superior axis, and early precordial transition. As all medical therapies had failed, the decision was made to take the patient to the electrophysiology laboratory for attempt at emergency VF ablation. A cardiac magnetic resonance imaging was considered but with a unifocal site of origin and the patient's overall extremis, and it was forgone in favor of immediate pursuit of ablation.

The site of origin was suspected to be the left posterior fascicle toward the apex given the right bundle morphology, superior axis, and early precordial transition.

Right common femoral arterial and venous access was obtained via a modified Seldinger technique with ultrasound guidance. An intracardiac echocardiography catheter (ICE) was then advanced into the right atrium and flexed into the right ventricular outflow tract. An initial ICE survey revealed an estimated LVEF of <10%. The initial plan was to perform mapping and ablation via a retrograde aortic approach. However, further ICE imaging revealed a soft, layering thrombus on the aortic valve (Figure 2). An impella was considered for left ventricular (LV) venting, but with the pre-existing thrombus, adequate support from veno-arterial ECMO, and the desire to map an unobstructed LV cavity, it was not pursued.

After a discussion with cardiac perfusion, a 20,000-unit bolus of heparin was administered, and a transseptal puncture was performed with ICE guidance, BRK-1 needle, and SL1 sheath. Activated clotting time of 350 was targeted for the remainder of the procedure. Mapping was performed with the EnSite X mapping system (Abbott Laboratories). The SL1 sheath was then exchanged for a large curl Agilis sheath (Abbott Laboratories). The advisor HD Grid mapping catheter (Abbott Laboratories) was then advanced into the LV across the mitral valve, to the junction of the distal septum and the posteromedial papillary muscle. This was approximately the location of the distal left posterior fascicle.

Several more 360J defibrillations were then performed, with the mapping catheter in place with re-initiation of VF. Highly fractionated signals were noted at the junction between the posteromedial papillary muscle and the inferoseptum (Figure 3) during mapping of the triggering PVC before subsequent VF. Radiofrequency ablation (RF) was performed with a Tactiflex Catheter (Abbott Laboratories) and delivered at this location for up to 35 W and 1 minute on lesions with ICE guidance. These lesions, however, would initially trigger fascicular ventricular tachycardia (VT) that eventually self-terminated (Figure 4, Video 1). Following these lesions, a 30-minute waiting period was pursued. At the end of 30 minutes, consolidation lesions were delivered at the original site with similar RF settings as previously described. Unfortunately, re-initiation of VF occurred with catheter manipulation at the prior ablation site. Defibrillation was required to return to normal rhythm. Further mapping was then performed with each subsequent reinitiation of VF. A site of origin more basal and superior on the septum was identified. At this time, a high-density map of the fascicular system was created (Figure 5). Further RF at up to 35 W and 1 minute for lesions was then delivered more proximally in the fascicular system to block the posterior fascicle and sufficiently denetwork the Purkinje-myocardial junction in this region (Figure 6). Following these lesions, no further VF was induced. Notably, with the restoration of sinus rhythm, it was appreciated that the ejection fraction was already improving with gradual dissolution of the thrombus on the aortic valve observed throughout the case. At this time, the venous sheaths were removed with the arterial sheath maintained. The patient was then returned to the ICU.

On postoperative day (POD) #3, ECMO was decannulated, and on POD #4, the patient was extubated. Clinically, the patient noted an initial visual field defect that resolved by discharge. On POD #6, the patient went to the operating room for repair of groin pseudoaneurysm related to the ECMO cannulation site. A subcutaneous implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) was placed on POD #8 just prior to discharge on POD #9. Prior to discharge, the patient's ejection fraction had recovered at 65%, and his vision improved to normal. He was discharged on verapamil and statin therapy.

Outcome and Follow-Up

At 17-month follow-up post ablation, the patient has had no further VT or VF. Verapamil has been weaned off, and he remains only on statin therapy. His ejection fraction has completely normalized, and he recently completed a 7-kilometer road race with his daughter.

Discussion

Purkinje ventricular arrhythmias represent a spectrum of disease ranging from PVCs idiopathic VT to idiopathic Purkinje VF. The presentation of malignant VF can be more sporadic and is often detected in cardiac implantable electronic devices with intermittent discharges.1 However, in the most severe cases, VF can become refractory to all therapy and require emergency ablation with hemodynamic support. The pathophysiology of Purkinje VT involves macro-re-entry within the fascicular system that often responds to calcium channel blocker therapy. These VTs can originate anywhere within the left-sided fascicular system from the anterior, mid, and posterior fascicles, with re-entry possible throughout these fascicles often involving the septum. Mapping and ablation of these arrhythmias are also possible although they can be difficult to induce at the time of ablation. Targeting of a P potential (a sharp diastolic potential) that characteristically has an earlier activation during VT than the Purkinje signal during sinus rhythm yields a successful ablation. Entrainment with concealed fusion at the site of the P-potential can be diagnostic.2

However, malignant VF involves pathology not only within the fascicular system but also distally at the Purkinje myocardial junction. These arrhythmias can originate at the junctions of the left- or right-sided diseased fascicles and the myocardium. In the case of our patient, mapping the site of origin of the initiating PVC was insufficient for rendering VF noninducible, and a “de-networking” ablation was required.3 This concept was described by Imnadze et al3 as it can be important to disengage the affected Purkinje system from the myocardium as it is believed that this is a larger area than a focal point and often involves the Purkinje myocardial junction. Based on observational experience, broader application of ablation of abnormal potentials can prevent the recurrence of VF in these cases.4,5

Given the gravity of this case and the patient's young age, we elected to implant a subcutaneous ICD. In idiopathic fascicular VT without structural heart disease, ICDs are often not employed given the success rate of ablation.6 However, in cases of idiopathic VF, even with curative ablation an ICD is indicated.4 We waited 6 months before weaning the patient's calcium channel blocker in the absence of any events.

Conclusions

We present an extremely refractory case of VF requiring excessive shocks and resuscitative care that ultimately required ECMO-facilitated emergency VF ablation. Intracardiac echocardiography was critically important as it revealed a soft thrombus that would have proven catastrophic if unrecognized and allowed to embolize. This led to a different method of LV access and improved safety. The patient had a superb outcome related to prompt and seamless interprofessional coordination of care.
