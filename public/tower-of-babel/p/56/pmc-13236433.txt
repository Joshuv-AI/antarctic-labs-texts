Response to “Considerations in Imaging-Based Assessment of Steatotic Liver Disease to Enhance Harmonization, Longitudinal Interpretation, and Clinical Implementation”
Authors: Shin Mei Chan, Vitor F. Martins, Kathleen Marsh, Kang Wang, Jake T. Weeks, Aiguo Han, Meng Yin, Kathryn J. Fowler, Claude B. Sirlin, Cheng William Hong
Published: 2026

We thank the authors for their thoughtful letter [1] regarding our review, “Imaging Evaluation for Steatotic Liver Disease” [2]. Their careful reading and suggestions strengthen the manuscript’s clinical applicability and translational impact. We respond below; many points underscore key future directions as imaging takes an increasingly crucial role in metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) management.

REPORTING CHECKLIST FOR QUANTITATIVE ULTRASOUND (QUS)

We agree that harmonization and minimum reporting standards are essential for quantitative ultrasound in clinical and research settings, particularly given inter-system variability. The American Institute of Ultrasound in Medicine–Quantitative Medical Imaging Coalition (QMIC) Pulse-Echo Quantitative Ultrasound (PEQUS) Biomarker Committee and other groups are working towards standardization of liver fat biomarkers. While formal guidance is pending, phantom-based calibration has been proposed to estimate backscatter coefficient with reduced dependence on scanner settings. Patient data are compared with a reference phantom of known acoustic properties to correct for system-dependent effects. Adoption remains limited by cost, workflow, and vendor constraints, highlighting the need for pragmatic interim approaches [34].

Consistent reporting remains essential. Developing methods to directly incorporate patient factors (fasting status, body habitus, vendor/model), acquisition parameters (transducer type, frequency, region-of-interest [ROI] size/location), and quantitative outputs may improve interpretation and clinical context. Transparent reporting supports longitudinal assessment and inter-site comparability.

COMPUTED TOMOGRAPHY (CT) CONSIDERATIONS

No universally accepted CT threshold for hepatic steatosis exists, despite frequent identification in routine practice. Published criteria for both nonenhanced and enhanced CT vary, and technique- and patient-related confounders limit generalizability. Opportunistic screening (systematic evaluation of imaging obtained for other indications) may help detect unrecognized steatosis, but protocol heterogeneity, potential false positives, and nonstandard reporting remain barriers. Standardized reporting is essential if such pathways are developed.

On unenhanced CT, scanner and reconstruction parameters (e.g., tube voltage, beam filtration, reconstruction algorithm) may shift measured attenuation. ROI placement that includes vessels, bile ducts, or lesions may confound assessment. Iron overload, edema, fibrosis, cirrhosis, coexisting infiltrative disease (e.g., amyloidosis), and inflammation may increase measured attenuation and yield false-negative results. On contrast-enhanced CT, contrast dose, injection rate, and scan timing can affect enhancement and liver-spleen differences [5]. Focal fat sparing is suggested to be the most specific finding, and warrants future study for opportunistic screening [6].

LONGITUDINAL FOLLOW-UP AND TREATMENT RESPONSE

Noninvasive imaging biomarkers are increasingly used for longitudinal assessment and treatment-response evaluation, particularly with the recent approval of resmetirom and semaglutide. In practice, vibration-controlled transient elastography (VCTE) will likely remain widely used because of accessibility and lower cost.

Standardized VCTE technique and reporting are essential for longitudinal interpretability. Attention to patient preparation, probe selection, quality, and measurement location can meaningfully reduce both inter- and intra-observer variability (Table 1). The Non-Invasive Biomarkers of Metabolic Liver Disease (NIMBLE) consortium has sought to define thresholds for meaningful change, proposing values of 30.7% for shear-wave elastography and 35.6% for VCTE, although evidence is based on a relatively small cohort (40 participants) and larger multicenter validation is needed before routine clinical adoption [7]. It remains unclear how cross-sectional precision translates to longitudinal change.

MRI-based biomarkers provide complementary information and have served as key clinical trial endpoints. MRI-proton density fat fraction (MRI-PDFF) remains the most sensitive and specific technique for quantifying hepatic steatosis. Magnetic resonance elastography (MRE) has high accuracy for fibrosis staging versus histology; however, no validated thresholds for treatment response have been established. NIMBLE data suggest that changes of 0.75 kPa (MRE) and 1.2%–1.6% (MRI-PDFF) may exceed measurement variability in MASLD, and the QMIC MRE Profile indicates that a ≥19% change is consistent with true stiffness change at 95% confidence, suggesting cross-sectional precision could inform the interpretation of longitudinal change, although further research is needed to determine how cross-sectional precision translates to longitudinal precision [78]. Further work is required to harmonize response metrics across platforms and clinical contexts.

Importantly, no imaging biomarker reliably captures hepatic inflammation. Some studies report correlations between MR-based metrics and inflammation, but this remains an early area requiring future validation [9].

SELECT POPULATIONS

Several modality-specific limitations and mitigation strategies are summarized in Table 2. For VCTE in patients with high body mass index (BMI), the extra large (XL) probe is generally recommended, but no single BMI cutoff has been established for choosing probes. Probe selection should follow device guidance and skin-to-liver distance. BMI contributes to discordance between MRE and transient elastography, with MRE often providing more accurate fibrosis assessment [10]. In the setting of iron overload, PDFF and R2* maps can be obtained in a single breath-hold; however, severe iron overload increases the likelihood of non-diagnostic MRE.

CONCLUSIONS

We appreciate the authors’ feedback, which reinforces the need for standardized, transparent, and context-aware reporting in MASLD imaging. Continued harmonization efforts and clearly defined thresholds for meaningful change will improve the accuracy, reproducibility, and clinical utility of quantitative liver assessment.
