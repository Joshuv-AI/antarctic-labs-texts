Do 3D-printed craniofacial fracture models improve learning outcomes in maxillofacial trauma? A multicenter cluster-randomized trial
Authors: Olina Rios, Aurélien Louvrier, Robin Pradel, Astrid Aisenfarb, Charlotte Helmer, Marc-Olivier Gauci, Charles Savoldelli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTeaching maxillofacial traumatology is challenging, as it requires not only theoretical knowledge but also advanced visuospatial skills. Conventional educational resources such as CT scans and diagrams offer limited support for understanding complex three-dimensional fracture patterns. Three-dimensional (3D) printing has emerged as a promising educational tool, yet its pedagogical impact in maxillofacial surgery remains insufficiently investigated.MethodsThis prospective, multicenter, cluster-randomized controlled trial was conducted at three French medical faculties (Nice, Tours, and Grenoble). Medical students were randomized to receive either a standard lecture alone (control group) or the same lecture supplemented with full-scale 3D-printed skull models representing 18 isolated craniofacial fractures derived from real patient CT scans (intervention group). Knowledge acquisition was assessed using identical pre- and post-tests consisting of a 25-item multiple-choice questionnaire. Additional variables included demographic data, prior exposure to 3D-printed models, video-game practice, and visuospatial ability assessed by a mental rotation test. The primary outcome was learning gain, defined as the difference between post- and pre-test scores. Student satisfaction was evaluated in the intervention group.ResultsA total of 112 students were included (73 intervention, 39 control). Baseline characteristics and pre-test scores were comparable between groups. The mean learning gain was significantly higher in the intervention group than in the control group (+ 5.56 ± 4.68 vs. +3.62 ± 4.06, p = 0.024), corresponding to a moderate effect size (Cohen’s d ≈ 0.45). The adjusted analysis of post-test scores showed a favorable but nonsignificant trend toward higher performance in the intervention group (p = 0.074). The item-level analysis revealed consistent improvement across all the questions in the intervention group, whereas gains were partial in the control group. Student satisfaction was high, with 91.8% of participants reporting that the 3D-printed models improved their understanding of maxillofacial trauma.ConclusionsThe integration of 3D-printed craniofacial fracture models into trauma teaching significantly enhances student learning and is associated with strong learner acceptance, although the confounder-adjusted analysis of post-test scores showed only a favorable, statistically nonsignificant trend. Three-dimensional educational media effectively complements traditional lectures by facilitating spatial reasoning and reducing cognitive load. This multicenter cluster-randomized study provides encouraging, although preliminary, support for the broader incorporation of 3D printing into trauma education, with potential extension to other surgical disciplines and future integration with immersive technologies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s41205-026-00337-7.

Background

Maxillofacial traumatology is taught in France during the second cycle of medical studies, with head and neck anatomy -covered in the first cycle- serving as a prerequisite. Traditional teaching methods rely primarily on iconographic resources such as two- and three-dimensional computed tomography (CT) scans, photographs of dry skulls, patient images, diagrams, and descriptive texts. However, these tools remain largely screen-based and rarely provide a fully tangible three-dimensional understanding of craniofacial fracture patterns. Facial trauma is inherently complex, involving interactions between bone topography, biomechanical mechanisms of injury, and the aesthetic and functional consequences of fractures. Its comprehension therefore requires not only theoretical knowledge but also advanced visuospatial reasoning, which is difficult to foster using conventional educational resources alone [1].

Visuospatial ability refers to the capacity to perceive, manipulate, and mentally transform objects in space. Among these skills, mental rotation -the ability to imagine the orientation of a three-dimensional object from multiple viewpoints- is particularly relevant in surgical disciplines [2]. Surgeons rarely benefit from complete 360° visual access to anatomical structures; instead, they must integrate partial visual, tactile, and radiological information to construct accurate internal representations of complex anatomy. For undergraduate medical students, acquiring these skills represents a significant cognitive challenge when learning craniofacial trauma.

Over the past decade, digital technologies have progressively transformed surgical training and planning. Three-dimensional (3D) printing, in particular, enables the faithful reproduction of patient-specific anatomical structures, opening new perspectives for medical education [3, 4]. Several studies have demonstrated that 3D-printed models can improve the understanding of anatomy and surgical procedures compared with conventional teaching tools [5]. Nevertheless, in maxillofacial surgery, educational applications of 3D printing remain relatively limited despite encouraging preliminary results [1, 6–9].

One of the key advantages of 3D-printed models lies in their multisensory nature. By combining visual and tactile (haptic) inputs, physical models facilitate spatial encoding, reduce cognitive load, and enhance memorization compared with strictly visual approaches [10]. Furthermore, visuospatial abilities are not fixed traits and can be trained over time. Activities such as drawing, origami, video gaming, or surgical simulation have been shown to improve mental rotation performance [11]. Integrating educational strategies that actively engage these skills into medical curricula may therefore potentiate learning outcomes, particularly when coupled with three-dimensional physical supports [12].

Objectives

The TMAX TRAUMA 3D (Teaching MAXillofacial TRAUMAtology with 3D-printed models) project is a prospective, multicenter, cluster-randomized controlled trial designed to evaluate the pedagogical impact of 3D-printed craniofacial fracture models in undergraduate medical education. The primary objective was to compare the learning gain in maxillofacial traumatology between students receiving a standard lecture alone (control group) and those receiving the same lecture supplemented with full-scale 3D-printed skull models representing isolated craniofacial fractures (intervention group). Secondary objectives were to assess student satisfaction with the use of 3D-printed models during teaching sessions and to explore factors potentially influencing learning outcomes, including prior exposure to 3D printing and visuospatial abilities.

Materials and methods

Study design and settings

The TMAX TRAUMA 3D study was a prospective, multicenter, cluster-randomized controlled trial conducted at three French medical faculties (Nice, Tours, and Grenoble) between May 2024 and February 2025. This study aimed to compare two teaching modalities for maxillofacial traumatology delivered during standard undergraduate medical courses.

Students were allocated to one of two groups according to cluster randomization: (i) a control group receiving a conventional lecture alone (Group B), and (ii) an intervention group receiving the same lecture supplemented with 3D-printed craniofacial fracture models (Group A). Randomization was performed at the group level within each lecture amphitheater to ensure logistical feasibility and avoid contamination between groups during model handling.

The same standardized lecture was delivered at all participating centers by an experienced maxillofacial surgeon using an identical slide deck and predefined teaching script. Each session lasted 90 min and covered the anatomy, classification, and diagnostic principles of maxillofacial trauma.

The trial was reported in accordance with the CONSORT extension for cluster-randomized trials in education (CONSORT-Ed) and the Template for Intervention Description and Replication (TIDieR) checklist to ensure transparency and reproducibility of the educational intervention.

3D models design

A dedicated pedagogical library of 3D-printed craniofacial fracture models was developed specifically for this study. The objective was to provide full-scale, anatomically realistic representations of isolated (“pure”) facial fractures in order to facilitate focused learning and avoid confounding by associated injuries. Each model corresponded to a single fracture pattern derived from real patient imaging data.

Source CT scans were retrospectively identified from the radiology database of the University Hospital Center of Nice, France. Inclusion criteria were adult patients presenting with an isolated craniofacial fracture, absence of major comminution, and sufficient image quality to allow accurate 3D reconstruction. All DICOM datasets were anonymized in accordance with the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR).

Segmentation was performed using Mimics Medical 25.0 (Materialise NV, Leuven, Belgium). Bone structures were segmented semi-automatically and refined by manual editing to remove artifacts, fill defects, and smooth surfaces while preserving fracture lines. The resulting models were exported in STL format and processed in Cura 5.2 (Ultimaker B.V., Utrecht, Netherlands, RRID: SCR_021235) for slicing. The workflow is presented in Fig. 1.

Digital workflow for reconstruction, processing and 3D printing of educational models

All models were printed at a 1:1 scale using fused deposition modeling (FDM) on an Ultimaker S5 Pro Bundle (Ultimaker B.V., Utrecht, Netherlands). Printing parameters were standardized as follows: white polylactic acid (PLA) filament for the main structure; polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) for soluble supports; layer height of 0.15 mm; infill density of 15–20%; and tree-shaped, automatically generated supports. Although water-soluble PVA support materials are associated with higher material costs and longer postprocessing times compared with breakaway supports, they were deliberately selected for the fabrication of those full-scale fractured skull models. The presence of fine fracture lines, thin bony fragments, and complex undercuts inherent to maxillofacial trauma anatomy makes manual removal of breakaway supports potentially harmful, with a risk of fragment detachment or surface alteration. In contrast, PVA dissolution allows noninvasive support removal, preserving delicate anatomical details critical for both educational accuracy and tactile exploration. This trade-off was therefore considered justified in the context of trauma teaching models, where anatomical fidelity outweighs postprocessing convenience.

The average printing time ranged from 18 to 24 h per model. Postprocessing included dissolution of PVA supports, washing, drying, and minor surface refinements. The final set consisted of 18 full-size craniofacial fracture models, representing isolated fracture patterns of the midface, mandible, and orbit. Each skull was labeled according to fracture type and assembled into a portable carrying case for use across participating centers. The detailed composition of the library is presented in Fig. 2.

Cost and logistical considerations

The production workflow was designed to remain compatible with routine in-house hospital manufacturing. Material costs per model were low, limited primarily to PLA and PVA filaments, with an estimated consumable cost of approximately forty euros per skull. Beyond printing time, the most time-consuming steps were segmentation and postprocessing, requiring on average 1 to 2 h of operator time per model. Once manufactured, the models require no maintenance and can be reused indefinitely for teaching sessions. Their robustness and portability facilitated circulation between lecture halls and institutions, making the library suitable for multicenter educational use.

Participants

Eligible participants were undergraduate medical students enrolled in the third to sixth years of the French medical curriculum who attended the scheduled maxillofacial traumatology lecture at each participating faculty. Students were included if they were present in the lecture amphitheater at the beginning of the session and completed the pre-test assessment. The exclusion criterion was limited to late arrival preventing completion of the pre-test. Participation was voluntary, and no financial or academic incentives were provided. All the data collected were anonymized.

Intervention (TIDieR framework)

The educational intervention was described in accordance with the Template for Intervention Description and Replication (TIDieR) checklist, the intervention consisted solely of the addition of physical 3D-printed craniofacial fracture models during the lecture. Students in the intervention group (Group A) were provided with full-scale 3D-printed skull models corresponding to the fracture types presented on the slides and were allowed to manipulate them during the explanations. Students in the control group (Group B) attended the same lecture without access to physical models. All other aspects of the teaching sessions –including content, duration, instructor, and teaching materials– were identical between groups, as detailed in the study design and settings section. The study design is illustrated in Fig. 3. To ensure educational equity, students from Group B were allowed to handle the 3D-printed skulls after completing the post-test evaluation.

Study design

Randomization

Randomization was performed at the cluster level within each lecture amphitheater. Prior to the teaching session, the amphitheater was divided into two spatially distinct sections (left and right). Two volunteer students drew sealed envelopes to determine which section was allocated to the intervention group (Group A) and which to the control group (Group B). This procedure ensured allocation concealment until the beginning of the lecture while maintaining logistical feasibility and minimizing contamination between groups, as physical handling of the 3D-printed models occurred exclusively on the intervention side.

Evaluation tools

Assessment was performed using a standardized pre-test and post-test administered immediately before and after the teaching session. The pre-test collected demographic data (gender, year of study), prior exposure to maxillofacial traumatology teaching, frequency of video game practice, and previous contact with 3D-printed models. Visuospatial ability was assessed in the pre-test using an extract of the Vandenberg & Kuse Mental Rotation Test. This test consisted of a fixed number of items requiring students to identify, among four alternatives, the two 3D objects corresponding to a reference object presented at a different orientation. This assessment was included to verify baseline comparability between groups with respect to visuospatial skills.

Knowledge acquisition was evaluated using a multiple-choice questionnaire (MCQ) specifically designed for the study. The questionnaire comprised five MCQs, each including five items, for a total of 25 items covering anatomy, fracture classification, and diagnostic principles of maxillofacial trauma. The full text of the MCQ, identical for the pre-test and post-test, is provided in Appendix.

The same MCQ was administered in both the pre-test and post-test in order to measure learning progression. Each correct item was awarded one point, with no negative marking. The total scores therefore ranged from 0 to 25. Learning gain was defined as the difference between post-test and pre-test scores. In addition, students in the intervention group completed a post-test Likert-scale questionnaire assessing their perceived usefulness of the 3D models for understanding maxillofacial traumatology.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the learning gain in maxillofacial traumatology, defined as the difference between post-test and pre-test total MCQ scores. Learning gain was chosen as the main endpoint to account for baseline knowledge and to directly reflect knowledge acquisition attributable to the educational intervention. Secondary outcomes included: (i) post-test MCQ scores adjusted for baseline performance and prior exposure to 3D-printed models; and (ii) student satisfaction with the use of 3D-printed models, assessed in the intervention group using a Likert-scale questionnaire. Exploratory analyses were also conducted to examine the influence of potential covariates -such as gender, video game practice, visuospatial ability, and prior contact with 3D-printed models- on post-test performance.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline characteristics and assessment scores. Categorical variables were compared between groups using Pearson’s χ² test. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Between-group comparisons of pre-test, post-test scores, and learning gains were performed using independent-samples t tests. When appropriate, Welch’s correction was applied after assessment of variance homogeneity using Levene’s test. Within-group pre-test and post-test comparisons were conducted using paired t tests. Item-level analyses were performed for each MCQ using the same statistical approach to explore question-specific performance patterns.

To account for baseline differences and potential confounding factors, univariate analyses were first conducted to identify variables associated with post-test scores. Variables that reached statistical significance (p < 0.05) were then included in a multivariate analysis of covariance (ANCOVA), with post-test score as the dependent variable and pre-test score and prior exposure to 3D-printed models entered as covariates. All tests were two-sided, and a p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using Python version 3.10 (Python Software Foundation, https://www.python.org/), with the SciPy library [13]. Code execution was conducted using the Mu Editor environment (Mu 1.1, https://codewith.mu/).

Results

Population

A total of 116 students attended the maxillofacial traumatology sessions across the three participating centers. Four students were excluded because they arrived after the start of the lecture and were unable to complete the pre-test. The final study population therefore comprised 112 students. Among these, 73 students were allocated to the intervention group (Group A: lecture supplemented with 3D-printed models) and 39 to the control group (Group B: lecture alone). The distribution of students by center and group is detailed in Table 1. The largest proportion of participants were recruited in Nice (n = 85), followed by Grenoble (n = 19) and Tours (n = 8).

No statistically significant differences were observed between the intervention group (Group A) and the control group (Group B). Knowledge score and learning gain

Intragroup analysis

Within-group comparisons of pre-test and post-test scores are presented in Tables 7 and 8. In the intervention group (Group A), students showed a statistically significant improvement for all five MCQs between the pre-test and the post-test (all p < 0.01). This indicates consistent knowledge gains across all assessed domains when the lecture was supplemented with 3D-printed models. In the control group (Group B), significant improvements were observed for MCQ1, MCQ3, and MCQ5 (p < 0.05), whereas MCQ2 and MCQ4 showed only nonsignificant trends toward improvement (p ≈ 0.06). These results suggest that while the conventional lecture led to partial knowledge improvement, the addition of 3D-printed models was associated with more uniform gains across all question categories.

Adjusted analysis

Univariate analysis revealed that the pre-test score (p < 0.0001) and prior contact with 3D-printed models (p = 0.014) were significant predictors of post-test performance (Table 9). No significant associations were detected for gender, frequency of video game practice, or success at the mental rotation test. In the multivariate analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) adjusted for pre-test score and prior exposure to 3D-printed models, students in the intervention group achieved a mean post-test score 1.17 points higher than those in the control group (95% confidence interval: − 0.11 to 2.46). This difference showed a favorable trend toward improved performance in the intervention group but did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.074).

Students’ satisfaction

In the intervention group, the vast majority of students reported positive perceptions of the 3D-printed models. Overall, 91.8% of participants agreed or strongly agreed that the use of 3D-printed skull models improved their understanding of maxillofacial traumatology (Fig. 9). Qualitative feedback collected through free-text comments was globally positive and emphasized appreciation of the course and the clarity provided by physical models during explanations.

Discussion

This study showed that, compared with conventional lectures alone, incorporating 3D-printed craniofacial fracture models into undergraduate teaching of maxillofacial traumatology was associated with a significantly greater learning gain. Students exposed to physical models achieved a mean improvement of + 5.56 versus + 3.62 points in the control group (p = 0.024), corresponding to a moderate effect size. Although adjusted analysis of post-test scores, accounting for pre-test performance and prior exposure to 3D-printed models, showed only a favorable, statistically nonsignificant trend toward higher performance in the intervention group (p = 0.074); this apparent discrepancy is examined in detail below. The 1.17-point adjusted difference nonetheless remains potentially meaningful in high-stakes assessments such as the French national ranking exam (EDNi), where a gap of less than one point may translate into substantial changes in student ranking [14].

This apparent discrepancy between a statistically significant unadjusted learning gain and a nonsignificant confounder-adjusted post-test comparison warrants careful interpretation. Learning gain is a paired, within-subject metric that inherently controls for each student’s own baseline performance, thereby removing between-subject variability and increasing statistical power. In contrast, the ANCOVA compared adjusted between-group means of post-test scores while controlling for pre-test score and prior exposure to 3D-printed models, both of which were strong predictors of post-test performance (pre-test score: r = 0.63, p < 0.0001). Because pre-test score already accounted for a substantial share of outcome variance, the residual between-group difference attributable to the intervention became proportionally smaller and more sensitive to the unbalanced group sizes (73 vs. 39 students) and to between-center heterogeneity, particularly the limited sample recruited in Tours. The 95% confidence interval of the adjusted difference (-0.11 to 2.46) remained close to excluding the null value and was directionally consistent with the unadjusted learning-gain analysis, suggesting an underpowered rather than a null effect. The two analyses should therefore be regarded as complementary rather than contradictory: the paired learning-gain analysis is more sensitive to the individual-level effect of the intervention, whereas the adjusted post-test comparison provides a more conservative, confounder-controlled estimate that is more vulnerable to sample size and clustering effects. This distinction is further addressed in the Limitations section with regard to the unit of analysis used for cluster randomized data.

These findings are consistent with previous studies reporting improved learning outcomes when three-dimensional supports are used in medical education [4, 5, 15, 16]. In maxillofacial surgery specifically, prior work has suggested that 3D-printed models enhance the understanding of craniofacial anatomy and trauma mechanisms [1, 7, 8, 10, 17]. Our results extend this literature by providing evidence from a prospective, multicenter, cluster-randomized controlled trial, a design that remains uncommon in surgical education research.

The educational benefit observed is likely related to the multisensory nature of physical 3D models. By combining visual and haptic inputs, such models facilitate spatial encoding and reduce the cognitive load associated with interpreting 2D images of complex anatomical structures [10]. Maxillofacial trauma requires the integration of fracture lines, spatial relationships and biomechanical considerations into a coherent mental representation. The physical manipulation of models may therefore support the construction of more robust internal representations, particularly for undergraduate students with limited prior exposure to craniofacial pathology. At the item level, improvements in the intervention group were more consistent across MCQs, whereas gains in the control group were partial. Although individual MCQs did not reach statistical significance in intergroup comparisons, this homogeneous pattern of improvement supports the overall learning gain observed at the total score level. Intragroup analyses further revealed that all five MCQs improved significantly in the intervention group, whereas only three improved in the control group. Together, these findings suggest that 3D-printed models contribute to more uniform acquisition of knowledge across different aspects of maxillofacial traumatology.

Notably, some students exhibited negative learning gains. These findings should not necessarily be interpreted as loss of knowledge but may reflect contextual factors such as test-related stress, attentional variability, or an “overconfidence–readjustment” phenomenon between pre- and post-tests [18]. Such fluctuations are frequently reported in the educational literature and should be interpreted with caution. Beyond individual gain values, the overall group-level trend remains the most relevant indicator of an educational intervention’s effectiveness. Importantly, the educational benefit of 3D-printed models appeared to be independent of prior exposure to video games or baseline visuospatial performance, as assessed by the mental rotation test. While visuospatial abilities are known to influence surgical learning [10, 12], our results suggest that physical models may help mitigate interindividual differences by providing external spatial scaffolding. This is consistent with evidence that visuospatial skills can be trained and supported through targeted educational tools [11].

Several strengths of this study should be highlighted. First, its prospective, multicenter, cluster-randomized design enhances internal validity and limits selection bias. Second, the intervention relied on a comprehensive library of full-scale, patient-derived fracture models representing isolated craniofacial injuries, rather than simulated fractures on generic skulls. This approach ensured anatomical realism while allowing focused pedagogical analysis. Third, methodological rigor allowed adjustment for relevant confounders, including baseline knowledge and prior exposure to 3D printing. Finally, the manufacturing workflow was simple, reproducible, and compatible with in-house hospital production, supporting the transferability of this educational approach to other institutions and disciplines.

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, randomization at the amphitheater level resulted in unbalanced group size. This pragmatic approach was chosen to ensure feasibility and avoid contamination between students during model handling, but future studies could consider separate sessions or pre-allocation to improve balance. Second, the number of participants varied across centers, particularly in Tours, which may limit generalizability. This reflects local scheduling constraints (e.g., the maxillofacial trauma course in Tours had already been delivered earlier in the academic year; in Grenoble, teaching is organized in elective “blocks,” leading many students to have already attended the topic in a previous block). Third, although the teaching content was standardized, minor intercenter variations in interaction and questioning cannot be entirely excluded. Fourth, the use of identical MCQs for pre- and post-testing introduces a potential training effect, which should be acknowledged. However, this effect was identical in both groups and is unlikely to explain the between-group difference in learning gain. Fifth, although our multivariable model adjusted for baseline knowledge and prior exposure to 3D-printed models, residual confounding related to individual learning preferences (visual, auditory, reading/writing, kinesthetic) cannot be entirely excluded. However, evidence suggests that fixed “learning styles” are not a reliable basis for tailoring pedagogy, and that active, multisensory learning strategies are more effective across learner profiles [19]. Sixth, a nonspecific intervention effect (Hawthorne effect) may have contributed, as handling physical models can increase engagement independently of cognitive impact [20]. Seventh, although randomization was performed at the cluster level (amphitheater subsection) to preserve logistical feasibility and to avoid contamination between groups during model handling, the statistical analyses trated individual students as independent observations. This approach does not formally account for the intra-class correlation that may arise from shared exposure to the same instructor, seating arrangement, physical proximity to the 3D-printed models, and peer interaction within each cluster. Ignoring this clustering structure may underestimate standard errors and could artificially inflate the statistical significance of between-groups comparisons, including the primary learning-gain analysis. This limitation should be weighed against the consistency of the effect direction observed across the item-level, intragroup, and adjusted analyses, which partially mitigates but does not eliminate this concern. Finally, only immediate post-course outcomes were assessed; long-term retention was not evaluated.

Future directions emerge from this work. Beyond lectures, 3D-printed models could be integrated into tutorials or OSCEs to assess applied diagnostic reasoning. Permanent university libraries of models may also support autonomous learning. Longitudinal assessments at 6–12 months would be valuable to evaluate knowledge retention, as prior studies suggest that 3D models may enhance long-term memory [21, 22]. Finally, combining physical 3D models with digital tools such as virtual or augmented reality may lead to synergistic, multimodal educational strategies [23], particularly for complex anatomical regions such as the craniofacial skeleton.

Conclusion

This multicenter cluster-randomized controlled study showed that the integration of 3D-printed craniofacial fracture models into undergraduate teaching of maxillofacial traumatology was associated with a significantly greater learning gain compared with conventional lectures alone, together with a favorable, although statistically nonsignificant, trend on the confounder-adjusted analysis of post-test scores. By facilitating spatial understanding and reducing the cognitive load associated with interpreting 2D-images, physical 3D models effectively complement traditional teaching methods. Their high level of learner acceptance, combined with a simple and reproducible in-house manufacturing workflow, provides encouraging support for their broader incorporation into medical curricula, although confirmation in larger, adequately powered cohorts is warranted. Beyond lecture-based teaching, the use of 3D-printed models holds promise for tutorials, skill-based assessments, and self-directed learning. Wider implementation across surgical and medical disciplines -potentially in combination with immersive technologies- may further strengthen their educational impact.
