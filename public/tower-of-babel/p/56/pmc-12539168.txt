Characterization of archaeal promoters using explainable and web-based CNN model
Authors: Muhammad Shujaat, Shi-Qing Mao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundArchaea, the third realm of life, shows unique genetic and metabolic adaptations that enable its survival in harsh environments. Despite its biotechnological and evolutionary importance, limited research has been done on archaeal promoter prediction compared to bacteria and eukaryotes. Current tools have shortcomings such as relying solely on DNA duplex stability feature encoding, high false-positive rates, low precision and lack of publicly accessible archaeal promoter prediction tool.ResultsTo overcome these limitations, we present “iProm-Archaea”, a CNN-based tool for precise archaeal promoter prediction. Our method systematically assesses different feature encoding schemes and finds K-mer (K = 6) as the best representation to capture promoter motifs. For model training, we utilized experimentally validated archaeal promoters from Sulfolobus solfataricus, Haloferax volcanii, and Thermococcus kodakarensis. And T. Kodakarensis KOD1 for independent testing. To enhance interpretability, we incorporated explainable artificial intelligence and performed shapley additive explanations to uncover the most influential motifs contributing to the model’s predictions. iProm-Archaea achieves 92% accuracy on training data and 89% on an independent test dataset, outperforming state-of-the-art models. Cross-organism analysis reveals limited generalizability to prokaryotic and eukaryotic promoters, underscoring the distinct regulatory architecture of archaea. To facilitate practical use, we have developed a user-friendly webserver. Moreover, we leverage iProm-Archaea and annotate 5,86,455 archaeal promoters from 478 unannotated archaea genomes.ConclusionsThis work advances archaeal genomics by offering a robust, domain-specific tool for archaeal promoter detection. The development of a user-friendly webserver enhances practical accessibility for researchers. Our work not only improves prediction performance but also enhances the understanding of archaeal gene regulation to facilitate future biotechnological and evolutionary studies in this underexplored domain of life.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12121-8.Keywords: Archaeal promoters, Machine learning, Gene regulation, Convolutional neural networks (CNN), Computational biology tools, Explainable AI

Introduction

Archaea, the third domain of life, was discovered by Carl Woese and George Fox during the ribosomal RNA (rRNA) sequence analysis in late 1970 s [1]. Compared to other organisms such as bacteria and eukaryotes, archaea have unique genetic and metabolic characteristics that permit them to thrive in severe environments, including hydrothermal vents, acidic hot springs, and high-salinity habitats [2]. Their ability to survive in such conditions makes them significant in biotechnology, industrial applications, and evolutionary studies [3].

Single-celled organisms rely on precisely regulated cellular methods to assure survival, including the accurate transcription of RNA at the appropriate time. This process is driven by RNA polymerase (RNAP), a key enzyme essential for the transmission of genetic information in all living organisms. Archaeal transcription holds significant similarities with eukaryotes [4]. This exhibits their close evolutionary relationship, some evidence even suggests eukaryotes may have originated from archaea [5]. Notably, the core transcriptional machinery is highly conserved between these domains, with the eukaryotic system representing a more specialized version of its archaeal counterpart. For example, while archaea use a single RNAP for all transcription, mammals and plants require three and five distinct RNAPs, respectively [4, 6, 7]. The binding of RNAP to DNA is performed by promoter sequences, which mark transcription start sites (TSS). Archaeal promoters typically consist of three key binding sites for basal transcription factors: the TATA-box Binding Protein (TBP), Transcription Factor B (TFB), and Transcription Factor E (TFE). These proteins collectively guide RNAP to its target location, ensuring accurate transcription initiation [8, 9].

Promoters play a vital role in gene transcription, making their accurate prediction essential for interpreting gene expression patterns and understanding genetic regulatory networks. Traditional experimental methods for identifying promoters, such as mutational analysis [10] and immunoprecipitation assays [11, 12] have been used but they are costly and time-consuming. Advances in next-generation sequencing (NGS) [13] have enabled the sequencing of more genes across various organisms, allowing computational analysis of their genetic elements [14, 15]. Additionally, the decreasing cost of whole genome sequencing due to NGS innovations has led to an increase in available sequencing data. This abundance of data has encouraged researchers to develop computational models for promoter prediction. These predictors can be classified into three main categories based on their approach: signal-based [16], content-based [17], and GpG-based methods [18, 19].

Promoter prediction has been extensively studied in bacteria and eukaryotes, with numerous computational tools developed for these domains [19–27]. In contrast, archaeal promoter prediction remains relatively understudied, with limited tools available despite its importance. Many previous promoter predictor tools utilized Machine Learning (ML) classifiers such as SVM, RF, and RNN for classification. However ML models require hand-crafted features, and which hinder and limits it performance. To overcome this limitation researcher developed CNN based prediction tools which out-perform ML classifiers.

(Martinez, et al. 2021) explores the structural and energetic features of archaeal promoters across three model organisms: Haloferax volcanii, Thermococcus kodakarensis, and Sulfolobus solfataricus. By analyzing DNA duplex stability, enthalpy, curvature, and bendability, the researchers identified distinct signals at transcription factor binding sites (TBP, TFB, TFE) regardless of the presence of conserved TATA boxes. The findings suggest that structural parameters, rather than sequence alone, are critical for promoter identification. The method was validated using upstream regions of 13 other archaea, demonstrating its potential [28]. (Martinez, et al. 2022) utilized the ML and statistical models to predict archaeal promoters by analyzing DNA structural features. Model results highlight inconsistencies in model performance, particularly the significant drop in accuracy and recall for shuffled sequences, indicating a strong dependence on sequence order rather than true promoter-specific features. Precision is notably lower for H. volcanii and T. kodakarensis, suggesting potential false-positive issues. The reliance on sequence structure hints at the need for improved feature extraction methods to ensure robust promoter prediction [29]. (Martinez, et al. 2023) introduces an Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI)-based approach using SVM classifier to annotate archaeal promoter regions. They utilized DDS encoding scheme to represent 3935 known promoter sequences. The study’s strengths include its interpretable AI framework, robust validation, and scalability, but it lacks comparison with advanced models like deep learning, potentially overlooking key sequence features [30]. Additionally, the absence of a user-friendly tool limits practical application. While the work advances archaeal genomics, broader benchmarking and accessible implementation could strengthen its utility.

The following limitations are identified in the aforementioned archaea promoter prediction tools. (I) Previous studies exclusively employed the DDS feature encoding scheme, and an optimal feature encoding scheme selection was not conducted. (II) The absence of a publicly accessible webserver for Archaea promoter prediction poses a considerable challenge for many experimental scientists in practical applications. (III) The current proposed tools necessitate enhancements to reduce the number of false positive values in promoter prediction. (IV) Cross-organism analysis of promoter tools was not conducted, which indicates that promoter structures vary among organisms. Consequently, there is a necessity for promoter prediction tools in archaea. (V) There remains potential for enhancement in the accurate prediction of promoters in archaea.

This study addresses existing limitations in archaeal promoter prediction through several key advancements. First, we constructed high-quality benchmark datasets to ensure robust model training. We then systematically evaluated multiple feature encoding methods and different algorithms, including traditional ML and CNN architectures. Through rigorous comparative analysis, we determined that k-mer encoding combined with a CNN framework delivered optimal performance. This approach led to the development of “iProm-Archaea”, a predictive tool designed to distinguish archaeal promoters from non-promoters. To assess generalizability, we performed cross-organism validation using promoter sequences from diverse prokaryotic and eukaryotic species. Model performance is evaluated by using standard metrics mentioned in Sect. 2.3. These parameters are commonly employed in computational biology [31–34]. We also performed additional validation including five-fold cross-validation and independent dataset. Finally, we implemented a user-friendly web server based on this framework.

Benchmark dataset

Selection of a benchmark dataset is a crucial step in ML-based models, as it serves as the backbone for model construction. Training and validation dataset are comprised of experimentally validated promoters of three different archaea organisms, Haloferax volcanii, Sulfolobus solfataricus, and Thermococcus kodakarensis. We obtained these datasets from Prokaryotic Promoter Database (PPD) [35] and [27]. We selected a region referred to as the core promoter, spanning from − 80 to + 20 relative to the TSS, as this area is known for its association with promoter activity [36, 37]. We have utilized the same negative dataset which was previously utilized by (Martinez et al.) [27]. To evaluate our classification model on independent data, we used a separate set of experimentally verified promoter sequences from the archaeon T. Kodakarensis KOD1 obtained from the PPD. Feature engineering

Feature extraction plays a pivotal role in building optimal machine learning models, particularly in bioinformatics. Machine learning algorithms cannot directly operate on raw protein sequences; thus, converting sequences into fixed-length feature vectors is essential. However, biological sequences vary in length and effectively transforming them into vectors without losing key patterns or sequence-order information remains a significant challenge in computational biology. Over the years, researchers developed several feature extraction techniques to represent DNA, RNA, and protein sequences. These methods can be divided into five types: amino acid composition [38], autocorrelation [39], pseudo-amino acid composition [40], profile-based features [41], and predicted structural features [42]. Each of these techniques has its own benefits and drawbacks, and selecting the appropriate combination of features is vital for developing accurate and robust models. By taking advantage of these feature extraction techniques, machine learning models can efficiently capture the biological intricacies of DNA sequences, enabling better predictions and functional insights.

In this research, we have performed an extensive analysis of feature engineering to identify the optimal features for building a robust, state-of-the-art model. Unlike most previous studies, which typically rely on simpler feature encoding schemes such as one-hot or DDS encoding, we recognize that such approaches may overlook critical information necessary for building a high-performance model. By comprehensively evaluating and selecting the most informative features, we improve the model’s ability to capture the intricate patterns and relationships within sequences, thereby enhancing its accuracy and robustness. This comprehensive analysis ensures that the chosen features contribute to the model’s predictive power, setting it apart from existing tools in the field. In this study, we included One-hot [22], NCP [43], DDS [44], K-mer [45], Pseudo-EIIP and Pseudo-DNC [46, 47]for feature evaluation, following are their detail expressions.

One-hot feature encoding scheme

One-hot encoding is among the widely utilized encoding scheme in state-of-the-art bioinformatics applications [22, 31, 32, 48]. This method represents each nucleotide in a DNA sequence as a four-dimensional binary vector, where only one element is active (1) and the others are inactive (0). Through this encoding scheme, any DNA sequence can be transformed into a two-dimensional numerical matrix suitable for computational analysis.

Nucleotide chemical properties (NCP)

DNA nucleotides contain unique chemical properties [43], which are categorized into three characteristics (1) hydrogen bond strength, (2) base structure (purines vs. pyrimidines), and (3) functional group composition. Hydrogen bonding patterns differ significantly between base pairs, such as AT pairs form relatively weak hydrogen bonds, whereas CG pairs create stronger hydrogen bonds. Structurally, purine contains two aromatic rings, while pyrimidines possess a single-ring structure. Furthermore, the nucleotides can be distinguished by their functional groups - A and C contain amino groups (-NH₂) and G and T feature keto groups (= O). To evaluate the performance of the constructed model, we employed widely utilized validation methods such as k-fold cross-validation and independent testing. These methods ensure a robust assessment of the model’s predictive ability and generalizability.

In 5-fold cross-validation, the benchmark dataset is randomly divided into five equal-sized subsets. Each subset takes a turn as the test set, with one subset used for validation and the remaining three for training. This process is repeated five times, with each subset serving as the test set once, and the final performance is averaged across all runs. We propose a CNN based model called “iProm-Archaea” which stands for identification of Promoter sequences in Archaea. We performed grid search hyperparameter tuning to select model layers and their values. Details of each parameter are provided in Supplementary File V1 (Table s2). The model comprises two 1D convolutional layers, each of which uses 16 filters with a kernel size of 2 and ReLU activation to determine local sequence patterns. To avoid overfitting, both convolutional layers use L2 weight regularization (strength 1e-5). After each convolution, max pooling minimizes dimensionality, first with a pool size of 2 and then with 4. Both with a stride of 2. The pooled features are flattened and regularized using a dropout layer with the rate = 0.22, followed by a dense layer (100 neurons, ReLU activation, L2 regularization: 1e-4) and another dropout layer (rate = 0.25). For binary classification, the output layer generates probability scores using a single sigmoid-activated neuron. The model is trained using Adam optimization and binary cross-entropy loss. To solve class imbalance, class weights are calculated dynamically during training to balance promoter/non-promoter representation. The training involves 5-fold cross-validation, which divides data into training and validation folds. If validation loss reaches a limit, an early stopping callback will end training. Each fold trains for 20 epochs with a batch size of 32, and the training progress is tracked discreetly. The model is built using Keras/TensorFlow. Explainable artificial intelligence (XAI)

We employed Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) analysis to explain the decision-making process of iProm-Archaea. SHAP calculates the contribution of individual features in model prediction. Since we utilized 6-mer encoding scheme, SHAP values were calculated to determine which kmers have the most significant influence on promoter classification. For every input sequence SHAP breaks down the model’s output into all possible 6-mers, showing how each motif either increases or decreases the predicted probability of being a promoter relative to the baseline. Figures 7 and 6 show the SHAP analysis. This SHAP analysis highlights the strong influence of AT-rich motifs especially those containing TATA or TTT repeats on model prediction, which is biologically relevant since AT-rich regions (like TATA-boxes) are common in promoter sequences, particularly in archaea and eukaryotes. The prominence of motifs like TTTAAA, AATATA, and TATATA supports the model’s learning of biologically meaningful promoter signals. The beeswarm plot further reveals that these motifs generally have a positive SHAP impact when present at high values (red), indicating their role in classifying sequences as promoters. Overall, the model appears to correctly attribute importance to biologically relevant motifs, validating both its predictive performance and interpretability.

Performance on cross-organism datasets

To examine iProm-Archaea’s cross-domain applicability, we utilized experimentally validated promoter sequences from prokaryotes and eukaryotes retrieved from the EPDnew [51] and RegulonDB [52] databases. These sequences were fed into iProm-Archaea, a model trained only on archaeal promoter data. The dataset information and performance parameters are summarized in Table 6. It shows that iProm-Archaea has limited prediction accuracy when applied to non-archaeal promoters. This mismatch reveals fundamental differences in promoter design across archaea, bacteria, and eukaryotes. Underlining the need for domain-specific prediction tools. While existing tools [25, 26, 31, 32] are successful in their respective areas, our findings show that archaeal promoters have unique structural and regulatory properties that generalist approaches cannot capture. Therefore, developing specialized models like iProm-Archaea is critical for advancing archaeal genome study. We performed motif analysis on cross-organism dataset and archaea promoters to find conserved motifs in different organisms using MEME [53] suit. Webserver

In the field of bioinformatics, publicly accessible databases and web servers play a crucial role in supporting biomedical researchers by facilitating experimental analyses. To assist users in identifying archaea promoters efficiently, we have developed a freely accessible web server. This user-friendly platform allows researchers to input DNA sequences directly into a textbox or upload sequence files in FASTA format through a file selection dialog box. The prediction results will be presented in a clear table format, including details such as the serial number, FASTA ID, and predicted class, Detailed usage instructions are available on the web server’s page, ensuring ease of use for researchers with varying levels of technical expertise.

The webserver also plots the DDS and correlation score between predicted promoter and experimentally validated archaea promoters. Which further helps to analyze the data. Webserver is freely accessible at https://ipromarchaea.alqaswaservices.com/ Fig. 9 depicts webserver input and Fig. 10 shows outcomes of webserver. By providing this accessible and comprehensive tool, we aim to streamline the identification and classification of archaea promoters, empowering researchers to focus on deeper biological insights and experimental validations.

Conclusion

In this research we proposed an archaeal promoter prediction tool called “iProm-Archaea”. To develop this tool, we performed extensive feature encoding analysis and evaluated their performance on different ML and CNN classifiers. Based on results we selected the 6-mer feature encoding scheme and a two-layer CNN model. iProm-Archaea outperforms current state of the art archaeal promoter prediction tools and achieved 92% accuracy. To enhance interpretability, we incorporated XAI using SHAP to uncover the most influential motifs contributing to the model’s predictions. This analysis exhibits strong biological relevance, highlighting AT-rich motifs such as TATA-like elements that are known to play critical roles in archaeal transcription. By utilizing iProm-Archaea we annotate archaeal promoters from 478 archaea genomes. We conducted cross-organism analysis of iProm-Archaea to check its predictability and results exhibits that the structure of promoter is different in cross-organisms and hence specific tool for archaea promoter is required. The trained models and datasets used in this research are publicly available at https://github.com/PromoterTools/iPromArchaea. To provide easy access to biologist we have developed a freely accessible webserver. We expect iProm-Archaea to serve as a valuable resource for archaeal genomic research and bioinformatics applications.
