Engineered zwitterion-nanodelivery for precision targeting of brain metastases
Authors: Haibao Peng, Youming Zeng, Yang Huang, Chenyu Lin, Zeyu Lei, Peixin Sun, Jiahui Yang, Shaoqi Sheng, Yuxin Huang, Xiaohui Li, Jiaxu Zhao, Rui Zeng, Cheng Li, Wanqi Yang, Xiangjie Di, Sisi He, Yongsheng Wang, Hanting Yang, Wuli Yang, Yudan Chi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The central nervous system is a critical reservoir for cancer metastasis, underscoring the urgent need for effective strategies for targeted drug delivery that minimize systemic side effects. Here, we present a zwitterionic polycarboxybetaine (PCB) designed to achieve selective accumulation within the tumor vasculature of brain metastases, thereby facilitating the rapid release of therapeutics without relying on external stimuli in preclinical models. PCB selectively targets the betaine-γ-aminobutyric acid transporter 1 (BGT1) at the blood-brain-tumor barrier (BBTB), enabling localized enrichment of nanoparticles within the tumor vasculature. The intrinsic mechanical flexibility of PCB activates the Piezo1 ion channel in endothelial cells, resulting in calcium ion influx and enhanced calpain activity, which together facilitate the degradation of VE-cadherin. The burst-release mechanism improves vascular permeability and compromises the integrity of the tumor vascular barrier, allowing for the swift release of encapsulated therapeutics and promoting extensive drug distribution within the tumor microenvironment. Notably, PCB encapsulating either osimertinib or cisplatin demonstrates therapeutic efficacy, intratumoral targeting, and favorable biological safety in disease models. These findings indicate that this zwitterion-functionalized platform effectively surmounts the challenges posed by the BBTB, supporting improved drug delivery and therapeutic efficacy in brain metastases.

Introduction

Brain metastasis constitutes the most common type of intracranial neoplasm, arising predominantly from primary malignancies such as lung cancer, breast cancer, and melanoma1–3. The condition is characterized by a poor prognosis and high mortality rates4. Clinically, brain metastasis is characterized by distinct features including marked intracranial contrast enhancement and elevated cerebrospinal fluid protein levels5. However, therapeutic penetration into brain tissue remains critically limited, with brain-to-blood ratios reported at less than 2%6–8. A major obstacle in the treatment of brain metastasis is the restricted permeability of many pharmacological agents across the blood-brain barrier (BBB)9, which has considerably hampered the development of effective systemic therapies10. Even with intrathecal administration, the diffusion of therapeutic agents into brain parenchyma is frequently limited to only a few millimeters11, complicating treatment delivery to deeply seated tumors or larger metastatic lesions12.

The blood-brain tumor barrier (BBTB) represents a specialized adaptation of the BBB in brain tumors13. Unlike the intact BBB, the BBTB exhibits increased permeability, potentially enabling the passage of larger molecules, including antibody-drug conjugates (ADC)14, monoclonal antibodies15, and other macromolecular therapeutics16. While the altered characteristics of the BBTB present new opportunities for chemotherapy and targeted therapeutic delivery17, they also pose challenges due to the heterogeneous architecture of brain tumors, which often results in uneven drug distribution within the tumor microenvironment. These complexities necessitate further exploration to optimize delivery strategies and enhance therapeutic efficacy in treating brain metastasis18.

Nanomedicines have offered a promising avenue for advancing cancer therapies by enhancing treatment precision while minimizing off-target effects19,20. Biocompatible nanocarriers allow for targeted delivery and controlled release of therapeutics, effectively addressing critical challenges posed by biological brain barriers21. Engineered nanoparticles optimize pharmacokinetic profiles to prolong systemic circulation and enhance targeting efficacy at tumor sites22. Notably, nanoparticle platforms have been developed for medulloblastoma, utilizing low-dose radiotherapy-induced P-selectin expression on tumor vasculature to facilitate BBB penetration23. Similarly, fluorination improves BBB penetration of cylindrical polymer brushes by increasing hydrophobicity and reducing surface energy24. Additionally, cationic lipid-polymer hybrid nanoparticles, in conjunction with focused ultrasound-mediated BBB disruption, have achieved efficient siRNA delivery to brain tumors25. However, the targeted release of nanomedicines within brain metastasis tumors remains a formidable challenge, particularly in ensuring deep tissue penetration independent of external stimuli.

In this work, we present zwitterion-functionalized nanoparticles designed to enhance targeting and penetration within brain metastases. The nanoparticles incorporate polycarboxybetaine (PCB), whose extended circulation and selective interaction with the betaine/γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) transporter 1 (BGT1) on BBTB endothelial cells promote vascular retention26,27. They combine BGT1-mediated retention with Piezo1-dependent endothelial mechanotransduction to achieve selective accumulation and mechanotransduction-triggered burst release within the tumor microenvironment. In normal vasculature, the nanoparticles accumulate without burst release, reducing neurotoxicity. This strategy promotes tumor-directed delivery and broad distribution of therapeutics within the brain metastasis microenvironment, improving treatment efficacy.

Results

Zwitterionic nanoparticles accumulate in brain metastases

Zwitterionic PCB nanoparticles were synthesized through a reflux precipitation polymerization process utilizing carboxybetaine methacrylate (CBMA) as the monomer (Fig. 1a). Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis revealed that the synthesized PCB nanoparticles exhibited an average hydrodynamic diameter of 105.5 ± 2.3 nm, indicating stable colloidal properties in aqueous media (Supplementary Fig. 1a). In comparison, PEGylated nanoparticles (designated as POE) produced using a similar aqueous precipitation method yielded an average hydrodynamic size of 149.8 ± 2.1 nm, as we reported previously28. PEGylation is a widely employed modification that extends the circulation time of therapeutic agents29. The zeta potential of PCB nanoparticles was measured at −6.0 ± 1.0 mV, comparable to that of POE nanoparticles (−5.6 ± 0.6 mV), suggesting similar stability against aggregation in solution (Supplementary Fig. 1b). To evaluate the capability of zwitterion-functionalized nanoparticles to enhance accumulation in brain metastasis, we established metastatic cell lines from breast cancer (4T1) and lung cancer (LLC), termed 4T1-BM and LLC-BM, through three rounds of in vivo selection (Supplementary Fig. 1c). We then intravenously injected near-infrared dye Cy5-loaded zwitterionic PCB nanoparticles (PCB-Cy5) into luciferase-expressing 4T1-BM mice. Real-time monitoring of Cy5 fluorescence imaging revealed substantial accumulation of PCB-Cy5 at the metastatic sites within 1 h, peaking at 24 h post-injection, indicating effective tumor penetration (Fig. 1c). In contrast, the control Cy5-loaded POE nanoparticles (POE-Cy5) displayed lower fluorescence signals over the 48-hour observation period. Mice treated with free Cy5 showed only faint fluorescence in the brain, which diminished gradually during the same timeframe.

Ex vivo analyses of organ-specific nanoparticle accumulation in various tissues-including the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, kidneys, and tumor-bearing brain-were conducted to further validate these in vivo observations. Notably, fluorescence signals persisted for up to 48 h, demonstrating sustained fluorophore retention in brain metastatic tissues. (Fig. 1d). The fluorescence signal of PCB was markedly higher at the tumor site compared to free Cy5, indicating enhanced retention of the nanocarriers within brain metastasis. Quantitative biodistribution analysis revealed that PCB nanoparticles treatment resulted in 6.2% accumulation of nanoparticles in brain, approximately 3.4-fold greater than that seen with POE nanoparticles (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Fig. 1d). We extended these studies by administering PCB-Cy5 and POE-Cy5 via tail vein injection in LLC-BM models (Fig. 1f). At 48 h post-injection, PCB-Cy5 demonstrated superior accumulation at brain tumor site compared to POE-Cy5 (Fig. 1g). In healthy mice, free Cy5 was rapidly cleared and became undetectable within 12 h post-injection, while strong fluorescence signals from PCB nanoparticles persisted for up to 48 h, demonstrating their enhanced circulation time (Supplementary Fig. 1e). Consistent with previous reports, prolonged circulation may serve as a prerequisite for their subsequent accumulation30. These findings demonstrate that PCB-Cy5 achieves superior accumulation in brain metastasis compared to POE-Cy5, likely due to its enhanced surface properties of biocompatibility and retention.

Seeking to confirm PCB-Cy5 accumulation in metastatic sites, we utilized 4T1-BM cells expressing the fluorescent protein tdTomato (4T1-BM-TD) (Fig. 1h). Whole-brain imaging demonstrated notable tdTomato expression in 4T1-BM-TD cells, with PCB-Cy5 fluorescence co-localizing with tumor fluorescence. Confocal imaging further revealed that fluorescence intensity of Cy5 in tumor tissues from mice administered PCB-Cy5 was approximately 20-fold greater than that of surrounding non-tumoral brain tissue (Fig. 1i, j). In contrast, POE-Cy5 displayed limited accumulation in the brain with minimal co-localization with tumor tissues, indicating lower enrichment at the tumor sites (Supplementary Fig. 1f). These results demonstrate that PCB nanoparticles exhibit prolonged circulation and enhanced accumulation in brain metastases, potentially via an enhanced permeability and retention (EPR)-mediated process across the BBTB.

PCB nanoparticles exhibit burst release from tumor vasculature

To investigate the distribution and dynamics of PCB nanoparticles within the tumor microenvironment, we utilized two-photon microscopy (2PM) to image the brain surface in 4T1-BM mouse models expressing green fluorescent protein (4T1-BM-GFP) (Fig. 2a). Longitudinal time-lapse 2PM imaging facilitated the visualization of PCB-Cy5 extravasation, revealing rapid and transient aggregation within the tumor vasculature (Fig. 2b). Dextran labeling revealed that PCB nanoparticle fluorescence co-localized with the vasculature, indicating a robust association with the endothelium (Supplementary Fig. 2a). Three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction confirmed the deposition of these nanoparticles in subendothelial spaces (Fig. 2b). Notably, we observed PCB nanoparticles aggregation occurring swiftly within the subendothelial space, accompanied by a consistent increase in fluorescence intensity (Fig. 2b), indicative of localized accumulation. Subsequently, we conducted long-term observations of nanoparticles release dynamics from blood vessels in mouse models within brain metastasis to assess the efficacy of PCB nanoparticles in traversing the BBTB and facilitating nanoparticles extravasation over extended periods. In contrast to healthy mice, where spontaneous bursting events of PCB nanoparticles were infrequently observed (Supplementary Fig. 2b), 4T1-BM-GFP model treated with PCB nanoparticles exhibited pronounced tumor vascular extravasation, resulting in a widespread distribution within the tumor interstitial space (Fig. 2c). This “burst-release” describes the mode of nanoparticle extravasation into tumors subsequent to PCB aggregates formation, with each event typically occurring within minutes (Supplementary Video 1). To elucidate the mechanism underlying PCB nanoparticles-induced burst release, bio-TEM imaging was performed (Fig. 2d), suggesting that PCB aggregates may localize between the basement membrane and endothelial cells. This finding prompted an investigation into whether adherens junctions between endothelial cells were affected by PCB nanoparticles burst release31,32. Immunofluorescence (IF) staining demonstrated that PCB nanoparticles exposure disrupted endothelial junctions, resulting in VE-cadherin dissociation (Fig. 2e and Supplementary Fig. 2c, d). Additionally, PCB-induced decreases in actin fibres expression in endothelial cells may be a key mechanism contributing to vascular dysfunction (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Fig. 2e). Western blot analysis revealed that VE-cadherin and Actin expression were downregulated in the PCB-treated group, consistent with the IF results (Supplementary Fig. 2f, g). Collectively, these findings indicate that PCB nanoparticles-mediated burst release results from endothelial junctional gap formation driven by VE-cadherin degradation and cytoskeletal contraction (Fig. 2g).

Prolonged circulation underpins burst-release dynamics

Prolonged circulation is a critical determinant of the resistance of hydrophilic polymers to nonspecific protein adsorption in vivo33. DLS analysis indicated that both PCB and POE nanoparticles maintained stable size, polydispersity index, and zeta potential over seven days, affirming their resistance to protein adsorption (Supplementary Fig. 3a, b). PCB nanoparticles labeled with Cy5 using both amide condensation and thiol-maleimide chemistry showed no significant differences in hydrodynamic size or zeta potential (Supplementary Fig. 3c, d). In addition, we assessed the stability of the two formulations and found that both retained more than 87% of their fluorescence intensity after 7 days of incubation, with changes remaining within 6% during the first 3 days. To investigate the colloidal stability of zwitterionic nanoparticles, we assessed the hydrodynamic diameter of PCB nanoparticles across a range of NaCl concentrations. PCB nanoparticles exhibited exceptional stability, with only a minimal diameter increase of 15 nm observed at NaCl concentrations up to 2 M (Supplementary Fig. 3e).

To clarify the mechanism of prolonged circulation, we synthesized blocked PCB (b-PCB) nanoparticles through surface modification of carboxyl groups (Supplementary Fig. 3f) to elucidate the effects of zwitterionic structure on nanoparticles stability. In 4T1-BM models, PCB nanoparticles exhibited higher accumulation in tumors compared to POE nanoparticles after repeated dosing, despite POE’s favorable circulation properties (Fig. 3a). Dissection and tissue distribution analysis revealed enhanced localization of PCB nanoparticles in tumors (Supplementary Fig. 3g). PCB nanoparticles displayed stable circulation half-lives of 10.3 h after the first dose and 10.2 h after the second, without significant evidence of accelerated blood clearance (ABC) effects (Fig. 3b). Conversely, both POE and b-PCB nanoparticles demonstrated progressively shorter half-lives upon repeated dosing, suggesting immune activation34. To examine the impact of prolonged circulation on the local accumulation and release of nanoparticles, we performed in vivo 2PM imaging. Following b-PCB nanoparticles treatment, our findings indicated reduced nanoparticles aggregation within tumor vasculature and the absence of burst events, attributed to compromised long-circulation properties (Fig. 3c). In healthy mice, serum IgM and IgG levels following PCB treatment showed no significant differences compared to PBS treated controls, with no detectable immune response observed, collectively indicating a lack of PCB-induced immune activation (Fig. 3d). In contrast, treatment with b-PCB and POE nanoparticles resulted in elevated levels of both IgM and IgG, suggesting these compounds elicit an immune response that may contribute to the diminished blood retention observed after the second dose. This immune activation appears to correlate with decreased nanoparticles blood retention, suggesting a link between immune response and accelerated clearance35.

Additionally, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations further elucidated the hydration properties of PCB and POE oligomers (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Fig. 3h). The number of hydrogen bonds formed by PCB remains consistently higher than that of POE throughout the simulation, indicating stronger hydration of PCB (Fig. 3f). PCB-water hydrogen bonds exhibited longer lifetimes (τHB), reduced donor-acceptor (D-A) distances, and narrower hydrogen donor-acceptor (H-D-A) angles compared to POE nanoparticles (Fig. 3g and Supplementary Fig. 3i-l), indicating stronger interactions with water. The radial distribution functions (RDFs) of water oxygen atoms around PCB heavy atoms illustrated a continuous hydration shell centered around the quaternary nitrogen (Fig. 3h), with the first hydration shell enveloping PCB molecule and comprising tightly bound water at PCB oxygen site. This structured hydration shell was consistently observed throughout the simulations, indicating high order and stability of the water molecules around PCB headgroups (Fig. 3i). Therefore, robust hydrogen bonding and a well-defined hydration shell enhance superhydrophilic properties of PCB nanoparticles34.

PCB nanoparticles target tumor vasculature via BGT1

Each repeating unit of PCB contains a betaine residue, with CBMA, its monomer, recognized as a betaine derivative. Betaine, a methylated derivative of glycine, plays a critical role in cellular osmoregulation and metabolic processes36. It serves as a substrate for the BGT1, which is expressed at the BBTB24. Sequence alignment reveals high conservation of the BGT1 protein across humans, mice, and rats (Supplementary Fig. 4a). IF analysis indicated BGT1 elevation within the tumor vasculature of 4T1-BM-TD mice (Fig. 4a, b). Consistently, Western blot results confirmed the upregulation of BGT1, aligning with the trends observed in the IF analysis (Supplementary Fig. 4b, c). To further define endothelial transcriptional changes associated with brain metastasis, we analyzed publicly available single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) datasets (GSE223503 and Human Brain Cell Atlas v1.0). SLC6A12 (BGT1) was selectively upregulated in endothelial cells within the brain metastasis microenvironment. Violin and dot plot analyses showed that SLC6A12 expression was both more prevalent and higher in magnitude in metastasis-associated endothelial cells compared with those from normal brain tissue (Supplementary Fig. 4d, e). We conducted MD simulations to further determine the binding affinity of CBMA for BGT1. The CBMA-BGT1 complex was simulated within a phospholipid bilayer in a salt solution (Supplementary Fig. 4f). Throughout the trajectory, numerous hydrogen bonds formed between the small molecule and protein (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Fig. 4g). Notably, analysis of the 1000 ns trajectories revealed substantial variations in the conformational properties between the initial and later stages (Fig. 4e). Binding energies in subsequent stages (two to four) were lower than in the initial stage, indicating enhanced stability of the small molecule-protein interactions, with energies stabilizing at −25 kJ/mol over a 250 ns period (Fig. 4f). Additionally, shifts in the hydrogen-bonding network of the BGT1-CBMA complex were observed (Supplementary Fig. 4h), elucidating discrepancies in conformational characteristics. These findings indicate that CBMA can specifically recognize and bind BGT1, thereby potentially promoting PCB nanoparticles internalization into endothelial cells. To experimentally validate the role of BGT1 in mediating PCB nanoparticles transport across the BBTB, in vitro studies were performed using brain-derived endothelial cells (bEnd.3). BGT1 expression in bEnd.3 cells was confirmed by IF staining (Supplementary Fig. 4i).

An in vitro BBTB model was established by co-culturing bEnd.3 cells with primary mouse astrocytes on transwell inserts to investigate PCB nanoparticles penetration. Treatment with 4,4’-Bis(benzyloxy) diphenylazide (BPDBA), a specific inhibitor of the BGT1 transporter, as well as exposure to betaine and GABA-both known substrates of BGT1- reduced PCB penetration across this in vitro model (Fig. 4g). In bEnd.3 cells, BPDBA, betaine, and GABA all inhibited PCB cellular uptake, with GABA exerting a greater inhibitory effect than betaine (Fig. 4h, i). This difference can be attributed to the stronger binding affinity of GABA for BGT1, thus confirming that the transendothelial transport of PCB across the BBTB is mediated by BGT1. We utilized BPDBA in 4T1-BM mice to verify whether BGT1 mediates nanoparticle accumulation. PCB accumulation in tumors was inhibited in BPDBA-treated mice. Confocal imaging showed that Cy5 fluorescence intensity in tumor tissues from control mice was approximately 4.3-fold higher than that observed in BPDBA-treated mice. (Supplementary Fig. 4j, k). Consequently, BGT1 emerges as a critical mediator of PCB uptake and its enhanced capability to traverse the BBTB.

PCB nanoparticles flexibility modulate mechanical forces at the BBTB

Mechanical cues play a crucial role in disease progression, and optimizing the elasticity of biomaterials can enhance drug delivery efficacy37,38. To assess the impact of nanoparticle stiffness on BBTB penetration, PCB nanoparticles with different cross-linking densities (12.5%, 25%, 37.5%, and 50%) were synthesized in this study by tuning the proportion of the glutathione-responsive crosslinker N,N’-bis(acryloyl)cystamine (BAC) (Fig. 5a)39. DLS and TEM analyses showed particle diameters ranging from 91.9 to 180.9 nm (Supplementary Fig. 5a, b), while atomic force microscopy (AFM) confirmed monodisperse, spherical morphologies (Fig. 5b). The Young’s modulus, assessed in liquid phase, served as an indicator of nanoparticles stiffness, revealing values of 2.9 ± 1.7 MPa, 10.8 ± 1.9 MPa, 36.5 ± 3.3 MPa, and 72.9 ± 9.6 MPa for the different cross-linking percentages (Fig. 5c). Rigid SiO₂ nanoparticles served as controls, exhibiting a Young’s modulus of 84.5 ± 33.2 GPa (Supplementary Fig. 5c). Among the formulations, 25% PCB exhibited the highest accumulation in the lower transwell chamber, indicating superior BBTB penetration (Fig. 5d). Consistently, IF imaging revealed the strongest signal for 25% PCB (Fig. 5e), and time-dependent permeability assays further supported its enhanced transendothelial transport (Supplementary Fig. 5d). Piezo1, a mechanosensitive ion channel, is activated by mechanical stimuli and modulated by cytoskeletal remodeling40, regulating calcium influx and calmodulin activity to affect cell adhesion and cytoskeletal function41,42. In the 4T1-BM model, PCB treatment increased Piezo1 protein expression (Fig. 5f), with IF analysis revealing elevated Piezo1 levels in endothelial cells following exposure to PCB nanoparticles across all stiffness variants (Supplementary Fig. 6a). Consistent with Piezo1 activation by membrane deformation43, PCB nanoparticles induced a marked increase in intracellular Ca²⁺ levels in endothelial cells, an effect abolished by the selective Piezo1 inhibitor GsMTx444, whereas rigid SiO₂ nanoparticles of comparable size, serving as high-Young’s modulus controls, failed to elicit a similar response (Fig. 5g, h and Supplementary Fig. 6b, c). Increased Ca²⁺ influx was accompanied by activation of calpain, a Ca²⁺-dependent protease implicated in VE-cadherin cleavage45. Calpain activity was elevated following PCB treatment and was suppressed by GsMTx4, while rigid SiO₂ nanoparticles had no detectable effect (Fig. 5i and Supplementary Fig. 6d). Accordingly, VE-cadherin expression was reduced in PCB-treated endothelial cells, with the 25% crosslinked formulation producing the most pronounced downregulation, whereas SiO₂ nanoparticles did not alter VE-cadherin levels (Fig. 5j, k and Supplementary Fig. 6e, f). Together, these results indicate that soft PCB nanoparticles activate endothelial Piezo1 channels, triggering Ca²⁺ influx and calpain-mediated VE-cadherin degradation46.

To definitively assess the requirement of endothelial Piezo1 for nanoparticle delivery, we compared the intratumoral accumulation of PCB nanoparticles in control versus endothelial cell-specific Piezo1-knockout (Piezo1EC-KO) mice. These Piezo1EC-KO mice were generated by intravenously administering an adeno-associated virus (AAV) expressing Cre recombinase under the control of the endothelial-specific Tie1 promoter (pAAV-TIE1p-NLS-Cre) into Piezo1flox/flox animals. Further analysis revealed that fluorescence intensity in control tumors was approximately 10-fold higher than that observed in tumors from Piezo1EC-KO mice (Supplementary Fig. 6g, h). Together, these results indicate that endothelial Piezo1 deletion drastically diminishes PCB nanoparticle accumulation at tumor sites, thereby supporting a critical role for Piezo1 in facilitating nanoparticle extravasation.

GsMTx4-mediated Piezo1 inhibition was used to evaluate nanoparticle accumulation in LLC-BM-bearing mice (Supplementary Fig. 6g, h). Tumor lesions were delineated using pan Cytokeratin (PanCK) fluorescence47, which allowed confirmation of PCB-Cy5 accumulation at metastatic sites. While whole-brain imaging showed that PCB-Cy5 fluorescence was highly coincided with PanCK-positive tumor areas in control mice, this accumulation was markedly suppressed by GsMTx4. Specifically, confocal imaging revealed that Cy5 fluorescence intensity in tumor tissues treated with PCB-Cy5 alone was roughly 7.2-fold greater than that in mice pre-treated with GsMTx4.

PCB nanoparticles serve as a targeted carrier for brain metastasis

The third-generation tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) osimertinib has been intensively studied for its efficacy in targeting activating mutations in the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) and anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK)48,49 in metastatic non-small cell lung cancer50. However, prolonged osimertinib administration poses significant challenges, including increased drug efflux from target cells and uneven distribution in the central nervous system, leading to diminished EGFR inhibition and suboptimal therapeutic responses48.

We developed LLC-BM cells harboring the EGFR-L860R mutation (LLC-BM- EGFR-L860R), a variant closely related to the clinically prevalent L858R mutation and implicated in TKI resistance49. To characterize drug accumulation in intracranial lesions, we employed desorption electrospray ionization mass spectrometry imaging (DESI-MSI) on LLC-BM-EGFR-L860R model brain tissues at various time points after administering a single dose of osimertinib (30 mg/kg) (Fig. 6a). Osimertinib was efficiently encapsulated in PCB nanoparticles, with a loading capacity of 13.1% and an encapsulation efficiency of 50.3%. DESI-MSI allows for the direct detection of molecular species in tissue without chemical labeling, providing high-sensitivity, spatially resolved profiling of metabolites and biomolecules. The osimertinib peak was detected, and the corresponding spectral data were processed and annotated using MS-DIAL to ensure accurate identification (Fig. 6b). Post-DESI-MSI, we performed hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining on the same tissue section for coregistration with mass spectrometric analysis. Notably, PCB nanoparticles-based osimertinib delivery (PCB-Osi) showed brain accumulation within 1 h, reflecting its prolonged circulation and reduced systemic clearance. PCB-Osi showed pronounced enrichment at tumor sites, surpassing both POE nanoparticles-based osimertinib (POE-Osi) delivery and free osimertinib (Fig. 6c). To evaluate the glutathione (GSH)-responsive release behaviour in the tumor microenvironment, we synthesized non-degradable PCB nanoparticles (n-PCB) that remain stable under elevated GSH concentrations. Cell viability assays demonstrated that the blank PCB nanoparticles exhibited negligible cytotoxicity across bEnd.3, 293 T, LLC-BM-EGFR-L860R and 4T1-BM cell lines, underscoring its favorable biocompatibility (Supplementary Fig. 7a). In contrast, PCB-Osi, formulated with osimertinib, exhibited dose-dependent cytotoxic effects in LLC-BM cells expressing the EGFR-L860R mutation (Supplementary Fig. 7b). Bioluminescence imaging analyses confirmed the superior anti-tumor efficacy of PCB-Osi (Fig. 6d, e), with median survival rates markedly improved relative to control groups (Fig. 6f). Because n-PCB is non-degradable, it cannot efficiently release the encapsulated drug and therefore showed no significant therapeutic benefit. Histological analysis confirmed that tumor volumes were reduced in mice treated with PCB-Osi compared with those receiving free osimertinib, PBS or POE-Osi (Supplementary Fig. 7c). This research underscores the potential of PCB-based nanomedicines to augment therapeutic efficacy in treating brain metastases, highlighting an advancement in targeted cancer therapies.

Radiotherapy is routinely combined with systemic therapies to improve intracranial disease control, in part by increasing BBB permeability and enhancing intratumoral drug delivery51. Given these synergistic effects, combining PCB nanoparticles with radiotherapy may offer a more effective strategy for treating lung cancer brain metastases. Compared to the PBS and radiotherapy-only groups, the PCB-Osi group showed tumor shrinkage by day 15 post-treatment (Supplementary Fig. 7d, e). Consequently, the combination of radiotherapy and PCB-Osi exhibited enhanced anti-tumor efficacy in the LLC-BM-EGFR-L860R model compared to the respective monotherapies.

Cisplatin was effectively encapsulated within the PCB structure, achieving a 7.5% loading capacity and 67.5% encapsulation efficiency. Histological analyses of major organs, including liver and kidney at both acute (24 h, Supplementary Fig. 8a) and 25 days (Supplementary Fig. 8b) time points, showed no detectable tissue damage or structural abnormalities by H&E staining, confirming that the zwitterionic carrier effectively reduced systemic toxicity induced by CDDP. We conducted serum biochemical analyses in mice treated with PCB nanoparticles to assess hepatic and renal function, including key parameters such as liver enzymes (alanine aminotransferase; AST, aspartate aminotransferase, AST) and renal markers (urea nitrogen, UN and uric acid, UA), alongside other relevant metabolites (Supplementary Fig. 8c). No significant differences were observed among the three groups (PBS, POE and PCB), indicating that PCB nanoparticles possess excellent biosafety. This research emphasizes the potential of PCB-based nanomedicines in enhancing the efficacy of chemotherapeutics while mitigating systemic side effects in cancer treatment.

Given the intended intracranial application of PCB nanoparticles, we conducted comprehensive evaluations of their neural safety profile. Immunohistochemical staining of brain sections revealed no detectable immunoreactivity for pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, in mice treated with PCB nanoparticles, comparable to the PBS control group (Supplementary Fig. 8d). Notably, mice treated with free CDDP exhibited body weight loss, indicative of systemic toxicity commonly associated with cisplatin. In contrast, those receiving PCB-CDDP maintained stable body weight, underscoring the capacity of the zwitterionic nanocarrier to mitigate off-target toxicity (Supplementary Fig. 8e). In addition, immunofluorescence staining for ionized calcium-binding adapter molecule 1 (IBA1, a microglial marker) and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP, an astrocytic marker) demonstrated comparable glial morphology and distribution between PCB-treated and control groups (Supplementary Fig. 8f, g), excluding the occurrence of nanocarrier-induced neuroinflammation. Collectively, these findings confirm the excellent neural biocompatibility of PCB nanoparticles, underscoring their translational potential for intracranial therapeutic applications.

Discussion

The process by which nanomedicines target brain metastatic tumors is a dynamic, spatiotemporally regulated phenomenon that unfolds in three distinct stages (Fig. 7e). Initially, during extended circulation within the bloodstream, PCB forms a hydrated layer that effectively shields them from immune recognition, thereby delaying clearance by the mononuclear phagocyte system and renal pathways. This prolongation enhances systemic retention and promotes targeted delivery. In the second stage, PCB nanoparticles exhibit selective binding to the receptor BGT1, enabling accumulation at tumor-associated vasculature and traversal across the BBTB. Notably, prolonged circulation time or higher concentrations of PCB nanoparticles in the bloodstream serve as a prerequisite for their subsequent accumulation, which relies on both BGT1-mediated vascular retention and the EPR effect. The third stage is characterized by the mechanical properties of PCB-specifically their softness and stiffness-as these factors regulate the activity of mechanotransducing proteins at the vascular barrier. The force generated during nanoparticles rupture allows for deeper infiltration into the tumor microenvironment.

Unlike conventional nanoparticle systems, this formulation integrates redox responsiveness with tunable mechanical properties. The disulfide-containing crosslinker confers glutathione-responsive degradability, enabling tumor-associated reductive triggering of nanogel disassembly and facilitating payload release at the tumor site. Additionally, the crosslinked nanogel exhibits controllable mechanical stiffness, allowing it to exert mechanotransductive effects on the vascular endothelium53. By varying the crosslinking density, we were able to modulate nanogel stiffness and identify a formulation with a favorable balance of structural integrity, redox responsiveness, and mechanotransductive activity. These features distinguish the present formulation from conventional non-responsive or mechanically non-optimized nanoparticle systems.

PCB nanoparticles exhibit remarkable long-circulation characteristics and demonstrate specific receptor recognition on tumor blood vessels in the brain, facilitating aggregation and burst release at targeted sites. The PCB nanogel formulation features a modular design that offers broad translational potential as a drug delivery platform. In addition to the payload used in this study, it may be adapted to carry other chemotherapeutics, small-molecule drugs, or targeted therapeutics. Moreover, the platform could be conjugated with antibodies to expand its application in targeted delivery and multifunctional therapeutic design. This strategy may also be relevant to other central nervous system diseases where delivery across brain vascular barriers remains a major challenge, such as Alzheimer’s disease and stroke. However, we recognize that broader applicability may be limited by disease-dependent differences in BGT1 expression, which could affect the targeting efficiency of PCB-mediated delivery.

RNA interference (RNAi) and CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing represent promising therapeutic modalities for managing brain tumors. Future applications of PCB nanoparticles may involve loading RNAi and CRISPR-Cas9 specifically to tumor sites while sparing normal tissue, thereby enhancing the safety profiles of these combined approaches54. Achieving drug passage through the BBTB while protecting the integrity of the BBB poses a significant challenge in nanomedicine. Moreover, the efficiency of drug loading may be influenced by the structural and physicochemical properties of the polymers employed, necessitating optimization tailored to the specific drugs used to enhance loading efficiency. Further research is essential to elucidate the mechanisms driving burst release at tumor vasculature, particularly beyond the role of the mechanosensitive protein Piezo1. Piezo1’s regulation of calcium channel dynamics and its interplay with calmodulin, adhesion proteins, and cytoskeletal components warrant in-depth exploration to better understand burst release mechanism55. From a materials science perspective, fine-tuning the mechanical strength, size and viscoelastic properties of nanoparticles can improve the precision of burst release, thereby maximizing their therapeutic efficacy. In future studies, tumor spheroids and patient-derived organoid models may serve as powerful platforms to further characterize the intratumoral penetration behavior of PCB nanoparticles. Insights gained from the penetration function of PCB nanoparticles in these 3D systems may not only deepen our understanding of their transport mechanisms but also guide the future optimization of nanoparticle design.

Differences in therapeutic outcomes between lung and breast cancer brain metastasis models likely reflect both host and tumor-specific factors. The models use distinct mouse strains with varying tolerances and survival, and the treatments differ: osimertinib in lung metastases is prone to acquired resistance, whereas breast metastases respond to alternative modalities56. Moreover, lung and breast brain metastases exhibit divergent vascular and microenvironmental features, highlighting the need for tumor-specific strategies to optimize drug delivery and efficacy. In clinical practice, radiotherapy remains a cornerstone for managing lung cancer brain metastases and is frequently combined with systemic therapies to enhance intracranial disease control57. The integration of PCB nanoparticles with radiotherapy may represent a particularly effective strategy, as these nanoparticles not only deliver chemotherapeutics but also modulate endothelial mechanosensing and compromise vascular integrity, potentially amplifying radiation-induced vascular permeability and facilitating drug penetration into intracranial lesions. Future studies are warranted to evaluate whether this combinatorial approach can overcome the limited efficacy observed in lung metastasis models and improve the translational potential for patients with brain metastatic disease.

We have successfully developed a zwitterionic PCB system that traverses the BBTB to facilitate the chemotherapeutic delivery, specifically cisplatin, as well as targeted therapies such as osimertinib, directly to brain metastasis. This innovative delivery platform, which utilizes burst release mechanisms, promises improved safety profiles, enhanced delivery efficiency, and superior tumor penetration. Ongoing investigations are focused on optimizing aggregation in aberrant vascular structures. Although our designed nanoparticles proficiently recognize BBTB receptor in tumor vasculature, the burst release mechanism remains random and difficult to control. Future studies will determine whether material properties can be adjusted to regulate this release more effectively. An important path is to engineer materials that specifically target abnormal blood vessels, enhancing the safety of brain-targeted drug delivery strategies. The advancement of translational research, particularly through the development of humanized organ models, will be critical in addressing the challenges that persist in the field of nanomedicine in cancer.

Ethical statement

All animal experiments were performed under the guidelines, evaluated, and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Fudan University (No. 202503015S).

Cell lines

Lewis lung carcinoma (LLC), murine mammary carcinoma (4T1), brain-derived endothelial cells (bEnd.3), and human embryonic kidney cells (HEK293T) were purchased from the Cell Bank of the Shanghai Institute of Biochemistry and Cell Biology. EGFR mutated LLC-EGFR-L860R cells were kindly provided by Professor Ying Mao at Fudan University Affiliated Huashan Hospital. To facilitate the identification of tumor cells in vivo, tumor cells were stably transfected with luciferase (Luc). 4T1 were stably transfected with green fluorescent protein (GFP) for intravital two-photon imaging. To identify tumor cells for confocal images, 4T1 were stably transfected with tdTomato (TD). LLC, bEnd.3, and HEK293T cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. 4T1 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. All cell lines were maintained in a 37 °C incubator with 5% CO2 and were routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination every month.

To generate brain parenchymal metastatic derivative cell, we utilized previously described techniques58. 5 × 104 parental cells suspended in 100 μL PBS were injected into left cardiac ventricle of anesthetized mice. Tumor burden was assessed daily through bioluminescence imaging (BLI) using IVIS® Spectrum In Vivo Imaging System (PerkinElmer). Once brain lesions were localized by BLI, we euthanized the mice and excised the brain. Tumor tissue was mechanically minced and incubated in a culture medium composed of a 1:1 mixture of DMEM and Ham’s F12, supplemented with 0.125% collagenase III and 0.1% hyaluronidase. The samples were maintained at room temperature under gentle agitation for 4–5 h. Following enzymatic digestion, the cell suspension was subjected to brief centrifugation, resuspended in 0.25% trypsin, and further incubated at 37 °C for 15 min in a water bath. The cells were then transferred into culture medium, which was refreshed daily. After three consecutive rounds of selection, the resulting cell population was designated as “BM.”

Synthesis of PCB nanoparticles

We constructed zwitterionic particles using zwitterionic monomers (carboxybetaine methacrylate, CBMA) crosslinked with an environmentally responsive crosslinker (N,N’-bis(acryloyl) cystamine, BAC). PCB nanoparticles were synthesized via reflux precipitation polymerization, a method known for its efficiency and lack of surfactant requirements59. Zwitterionic particles that exceed a critical chain length precipitate from the solution, forming initial nuclei. These nascent nuclei, being unstable, aggregate to form stable, mature particles. These mature particles then grow larger by incorporating additional oligomers and monomers from the solution60.

In a reflux precipitation polymerization to synthesize PCB, 150 mg CBMA, 50 mg BAC and 4 mg AIBN were dispersed in acetonitrile through ultrasonication. After aerating nitrogen for 15 min, the reaction mixture was heated at 90 °C for 1 h, with stirring at 160 rpm. After cooling down to room temperature, the resulting mixture was purified by centrifugation (75,600 g, 20 min) followed by three consecutive washes with deionized water to obtain the PCB. The nanoparticles with different crosslinking rates (12.5% PCB, 25% PCB, 37.5% PCB and 50% PCB) were prepared by changing the weight ratio of CBMA and BAC ranging from 7:1 to 1:1. The n-PCB nanoparticles were prepared by replacing the crosslinker BAC with MBA using the same procedure.

Synthesis of poly(oligo(ethylene glycol) methyl ether methacrylate) (POE) nanoparticles

The POE nanoparticles were prepared using aqueous precipitation polymerization. Briefly, 220 mg OEGMA-188 and 180 mg OEGMA-500 were dissolved in 29 mL deionized water. 5 mg SDS and 40 mg BAC were added, and the mixture was dispersed by ultrasonication. After being injected with nitrogen for 15 min, the mixture was heated at 70 °C with stirring at 160 rpm. And 15 min later, 1 mL KPS aqueous solution (8 mg/mL) was added. After a further 2 h of reaction, the emulation cooled to room temperature was centrifuged (75,600 × g, 20 min) and washed with water three times to obtain the POE.

Synthesis of silicon dioxide (SiO2)

The SiO2 nanoparticles are prepared by the seed-mediated growth method through the hydrolysis of TEOS under basic conditions. 1.5 mL TEOS, 3 mL ammonia solution (25-28%), 1 mL deionized water, and 50 mL ethanol are added to a flask and stirred at 40 °C for 3 h. Subsequently, 1.0 mL TEOS is added to the system, and the hydrolysis continues for another 3 h. Finally, the system is diluted with deionized water, and ethanol is removed by rotary evaporation to obtain SiO2 nanoparticles.

Synthesis and characterization of non-zwitterionic (b-PCB) nanoparticles

A mixture of CBMA (1 g, 4.65 mmol) and 1-bromoethane (0.65 g, 6.00 mmol) in 5 mL acetonitrile was vigorously stirred at 40°C for 48 h. After removal of acetonitrile, the mixtures were washed with the absolute ethyl ether three times to remove the unreacted 1-bromoethane. Evaporated under vacuum at 50 °C to obtain b-CBMA. The b-PCB nanoparticles were prepared by replacing the monomer CBMA with b-CBMA using the same procedure.

Preparation of fluorescent labeled nanoparticles

Use of amide condensation to label the PCB nanoparticles with Cy5 (PCB-Cy5-NH2). 25 mg PCB nanoparticles were resuspended in 5 mL acetonitrile. 85 mg DMTMM was added with magnetic stirring for 10 min. Subsequently, 15 μL 4-methylmorpholine and 0.5 mL dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) solution of sulfo-Cy5 amine (1 mg/mL) were added. After stirring at room temperature for 12 h, the obtained Cy5-labeled nanoparticles were purified by centrifugation (75,600 g, 15 min) and washed with deionized water three times. The Cy5-labeled PCB (PCB-Cy5-Mal), Cy5-labeled POE (POE-Cy5) and Cy5-labeled b-PCB (b-PCB-Cy5) were carried out through the thiol-maleimide reaction. 25 mg PCB/POE nanoparticles and 0.3 mg TCEP in 5 mL deionized water were stirred at room temperature for 12 h under nitrogen atmosphere. The reagents were mixed with 0.6 mg sulfo-Cy5 maleimide for another 12 h. The resulting mixture was centrifuged and washed with deionized water three times to obtain PCB-Cy5-Mal/POE-Cy5. The b-PCB-Cy5 was prepared by using the same procedure. POE-Cy7 was obtained by replacing the sulfo-Cy5 maleimide with sulfo-Cy7 maleimide. Cy7-labeled PCB (PCB-Cy7) was prepared using the same method.

Preparation of drug-loaded nanoparticles

10 mg nanoparticles were resuspended in 3 mL CDDP aqueous solution (1 mg/mL). The mixture was magnetically stirred at room temperature for 48 h. The CDDP-loaded nanoparticles obtained were purified by centrifugation (25,000 rpm, 15 min, Avanti JXN-26, Beckman) and washed with deionized water three times. 10 mg nanoparticles were dispersed in 10 mL deionized water. 1 mL of osimertinib solution in DMSO (1 mg/mL) was added dropwise under vigorous stirring at room temperature. After 48 h, the mixture was centrifuged and washed with deionized water three times to obtain Osi-loaded nanoparticles.

The drug-loaded content (DC) and encapsulation efficiency (EE) of the nanoparticles were calculated using the following formulas:

DC (%) = (weight of loaded drug)/ (total weight of drug-loaded nanoparticles)

EE (%) = (weight of loaded drug)/ (weight of initially added drug)

The CDDP content and Osi content were measured by using a UV absorbance spectrum (Perkin-Elmer Lambda750) at 301 nm and 320 nm respectively.

Releasing profiles of CDDP-loaded nanoparticles

CDDP release from nanoparticles was assessed in PBS at pH 7.4 and 6.5, with varying GSH concentrations. A 14,000 Da dialysis membrane sealed 5 mL of CDDP-loaded nanogels (10 mg PCB-CDDP) in 95 mL of PBS at 37 °C under oscillation. At specified intervals, 1 mL of solution was removed and replaced with fresh medium. CDDP release was quantified by UV-Vis at 301 nm, expressed as a percentage of the total drug loaded.

Characterization of nanoparticles

The size, appearance and morphology of nanoparticles were examined under a transmission electron microscope (Hitachi’s HT7800) and a scanning electron microscope (Zeiss Gemini SEM500 FESEM). Hydrodynamic diameter, size distribution and Zeta potential were determined using dynamic light scattering on a Zetasizer Nano ZS (Malvern Instruments, Ltd.). The Young’s modulus was tested by an atomic force microscope (Bruker Dimension Icon). The fluorescence of fluorescent labeled nanoparticles was detected by a Spark microplate reader (TECAN).

In vitro cytotoxicity of nanoparticles

In vitro cytotoxicity of nanoparticles was evaluated using bEnd.3, HEK293T, 4T1-BM, LLC-BM-EGFR-L860R cells. Cells were seeded in 96-well plates (5 × 103 cells/well) for 24 h. The culture medium was replaced with 100 μL fresh medium containing PCB, POE or n-PCB nanoparticles at a final concentration of 0, 12.5, 25, 50, 100, 200, 400 or 800 μg/mL. After incubation for 48 h, cell viabilities were tested by cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay.

IC50 of drug-loaded nanoparticles

4T1-BM cells were plated in a 96-well plate (5 × 103 cells/well) for 24 h. The culture medium was removed and replenished with 100 μL fresh medium containing PCB-CDDP, POE-CDDP, n-PCB-CDDP or free CDDP at different concentrations of CDDP as 0, 2.5, 5, 10, 25, 50 μg/mL. After 48 h incubation, cell viabilities were tested by CCK-8 assay.

In vivo pharmacokinetics

BALB/c mice were injected intravenously (i.v.) with 100 μL of PCB-Cy5 or POE-Cy5 (equivalent Cy5 dose of 50 μg). 30 μL of blood was taken from the retro-orbital sinus of mice at different time points (0, 0.25, 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 24 and 48 h) post-injection. On day 7, mice were given an additional injection with the same dose as the first injection. Blood was collected with the same procedure as described above. Each blood sample was immediately dissolved in 150 μL of PBS buffer containing 50 μg of EDTA·2 K. The diluted samples were transferred to a 96-well plate and the concentration of nanoparticles of each blood sample was qualified according to the fluorescence intensity measured by a Spark microplate reader (TECAN).

Immunogenicity of nanoparticles

IgM and IgG levels were detected by ELISA method to evaluate the immunogenicity of nanoparticles. BALB/c mice were injected i.v. with PBS, PCB, or POE nanoparticles (2 mg/mL, 100 μL) (n = 6). On day 5, blood was collected from the retro-orbital sinus and centrifuged (1500 g, 15 min) to obtain serum for IgM detection by using ELISA Mouse IgM Kit (n = 3). On day 7, the second injection with the same dose as the first injection was given to the remaining mice. On day 12, blood was extracted to assess IgG level with the same process as the first injection by using ELISA Mouse IgG Kit (n = 3).

In vitro BBTB transwell assays

Mouse primary astrocytes were isolated from neonatal C57BL/6 mice. Cerebral cortices were collected, with meninges and blood vessels removed, followed by mincing, enzymatic dissociation, filtration, and centrifugation. Cells were cultured in DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin on poly-D-lysine-coated flasks. After reaching confluence, non-astrocytic cells were removed by shaking, and the adherent cells were used as primary astrocytes61. Astrocyte cells (2 × 105 cells/well) were seeded on the underside of the membrane in transwell inserts (Corning), adhering for 24 h. Subsequently, bEnd.3 cells were seeded on the upper inserts of the transwell chamber. Media were changed every other day. The integrity of the cell bilayer was evaluated by the trans-epithelial electrical resistance (TEER) values (Millicell-ERS 2, USA). The cell monolayers with TEER values higher than 200 Ω/cm2 were used for the permeability evaluation. PCB-Cy5 (40 μg/mL) was added to the upper chamber, and the FBS-free medium was added to the lower chamber. After incubation for a certain time (4, 8, 12 and 24 h), the Cy5 intensity in the medium of the lower chamber was measured by a microplate reader and the transport ratio was calculated based on a pre-established calibration curve. For the inhibition experiment, BPDBA (10 μM), GABA (5 mg/mL) or betaine (5 mg/mL) were added to the upper chamber. After 1 h, PCB-Cy5 (40 μg/mL) was added to the upper chamber.

Fluorescent intracellular Ca2+ imaging

BEnd.3 cells were cultured in glass bottom dishes (1 × 105 cells/dish) for 24 h. Then, the cells were treated with SiO2 (100 μg/mL), PCB (100 μg/mL), GsMTx4 (5 μM), SiO2 (100 μg/mL) + GsMTx4 (5 μM) or PCB (100 μg/mL) + GsMTx4 (5 μM) for 4 h. Cells were then incubated with Fluo4-AM (2 µM) for 30 min. Cells were washed with PBS three times and then incubated with balanced salt solution at 37 °C for 20 min. Fluo4-AM fluorescence was then detected using an Olympus FV3000 confocal microscope at excitation wavelength of 494 nm and emission wavelength of 516 nm.

Calpain activity assays

Calpain activity was determined using calpain activity assay kit. The bEnd.3 cells were exposed for 4 h to SiO₂ (100 μg/mL), PCB (100 μg/mL), GsMTx4 (5 μM), SiO₂ (100 μg/mL) + GsMTx4 (5 μM), or PCB (100 μg/mL) + GsMTx4 (5 μM). After treatment, cells were collected and lysed in extraction buffer. Protein concentrations were quantified and normalized using the BCA method. Reaction mixtures containing standardized protein samples and calpain were incubated at 37 °C in darkness for 60 min. Fluorescence intensity was measured using a Spark microplate reader (TECAN).

Western blot analysis

The bEnd.3 cells incubated with SiO2 (100 μg/mL), or PCB (100 μg/mL) were lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors and incubated at 4 °C for 20 min and were then cleared by spinning at maximum speed for 10 min. Protein concentrations were run on Bis-Tris gels by SDS-PAGE and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes. Membrane was blocked in 5% non-fat milk for 1 h at room temperature and then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Proteins were incubated on the following day with horseradish peroxidase conjugated secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. Chemi luminescence was captured on gel imaging system (Gel DocTM XR, BioRad). The following antibodies were used for immunoblotting: Rabbit anti-VE-cadherin (1:1000), Mouse anti-GAPDH (1:10000), Rabbit anti-SLC6A12 (1:1000), Mouse anti-Beta Actin (1:10000), Mouse anti-Alpha Tubulin (1:10000), Goat anti-Rabbit IgG H&L (HRP) (1: 3500), Goat anti-Mouse IgG H&L (HRP) (1: 3500).

Cell uptake assay

BEnd.3 cells were cultured in glass bottom dishes (1 × 106 cells/dish) for 24 h. Subsequently, the cells were pretreated with BPDBA (10 μM), GABA (35 mM) or betaine (40 mM) for 1 h. Then, PCB-Cy5 (Cy5 concentration: 10 μg/mL) were added. After 3 h incubation of PCB-Cy5, the cells were rinsed with PBS and stained with DAPI (blue) for 5 min. The fluorescence images were obtained using an Olympus FV3000 confocal microscope.

In vivo two-photons microscopy (2PM) imaging

For 2PM imaging, a cranial window was established using a skull-thinning procedure. 2PM imaging was employed to examine the in vivo penetration behavior of PCB nanoparticles. 3 × 104 4T1-BM-GFP cells were stereotactically injected into the mouse brain at a depth of 500 μm. Two weeks after the tumor implantation, mice bearing 4T1 brain metastases tumor were anesthetized with avertin and immobilized to a head holder with a 37 °C temperature-controlled pad. PCB-Cy5 (Cy5 dosage 1 mg/kg) were injected via tail vein. 2PM imaging was performed using an Olympus FVMPE-RS microscope equipped with a 25× objective (1.05 NA, water) and two lasers (INSIGHT X3-OL, 690-1300; MAITAI eHPDS-OL, 690-1040 nm). The following wavelengths were used for excitation: 850 nm for GFP, 1200 nm for Cy5. The three-dimensional fluorescence and reconstruction images were processed with Imaris (v.10.0.1).

Biodistribution of nanoparticles

The 4T1 brain metastasis tumor bearing mice were randomly grouped and injected with PCB-Cy5, POE-Cy5 or free Cy5 (Cy5 dosage: 1 mg/kg) via tail vein. At predetermined timepoints (0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24 and 48 h) post intravenous injection, the mice were anesthetized with 3% isoflurane during the imaging acquisition process. Fluorescent imaging was conducted using the IVIS near-infrared fluorescence imaging system, with an excitation wavelength of 647 nm and an emission wavelength of 670 nm. Image analysis was performed with Lumia III software, maintaining a consistent fluorescence scale for all samples.

Ex vivo imaging and tumor penetration behavior of PCB nanoparticles were assessed through immunofluorescent analysis. Briefly, mice were euthanized for 48 h post-injection for in vivo distribution studies, after which major organ including the heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, and brain were harvested and imaged using the IVIS® Spectrum near-infrared fluorescence imaging system. Frozen brain tissues were sectioned and subjected to immunofluorescent staining, with cell nuclei labeled using DAPI (blue), followed by imaging with an Olympus FV3000 confocal microscope.

Immunofluorescence

Mice were deeply anesthetized using avertin and subjected to transcardial perfusion using cold PBS. Immediately following PBS perfusion, after which the brains were extracted and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at 4 °C for 12 h. Subsequently, the brains were submerged in a 30% sucrose solution for 48 h at 4 °C, embedded in Tissue-Tek 4583 OCT Compound, and rapidly frozen on dry ice in a predetermined orientation. Coronal sections of 10 μm thickness were obtained using a cryostat microtome (CM1950, Leica). Brain sections were permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS for 30 min, followed by blocking with 5% normal donkey serum in PBS for 1 h at room temperature. Primary antibody incubation was carried out overnight at 4 °C in TBS containing 5% donkey serum. After washing with PBS, sections were treated with fluorescently conjugated secondary antibodies in TBS for 1 h at room temperature, followed by three washes in TBST. Nuclei were stained with 1 μg/mL DAPI, and slides were mounted using Fluoro-Gel medium. Imaging was performed with an Olympus FV3000 confocal microscope.

In vivo anticancer effect

The 4T1-BM model was used to evaluate the anticancer effect of CDDP-loaded nanoparticles. Mice bearing luciferase-expressing 4T1-BM tumors were randomly divided into five groups (n = 6 mice per group) at day 10 post-implantation, followed by receiving an i.v. injection of free CDDP, POE-CDDP, PCB-CDDP and n-PCB-CDDP (5 mg CDDP equiv./kg) or PBS every three days for a total of three doses. Tumor progression was assessed through BLI, using the IVIS Spectrum In Vivo System. The relative photon flux was calculated as I/I0 (I0 is the bioluminescence intensity at the 10th day). On day 25, the treatment was terminated and one mouse from each group was sacrificed for histological analysis. Brain tumor tissues were further stained with TUNEL and finally evaluated with confocal images to investigate the apoptosis of tumor cells induced by CDDP. Additionally, the body weight of each mouse was recorded every two days, and survival data were analyzed using the Kaplan-Meier method.

The LLC-BM-EGFR-L860R model was used to evaluate the anticancer effect of Osi-loaded nanoparticles. Ten days post-tumor implantation, mice harboring luciferase-expressing LLC-BM-EGFR-L860R tumors were randomly allocated into five groups (n = 6 per group) and administered intravenous injections of free Osi, POE-Osi, PCB-Osi and n-PCB-Osi (15 mg Osi equiv./kg) or PBS every three days for a total of three doses.

Molecular docking and dynamics simulations of targeting mechanism

The structure of BGT1 was predicted by ColabFold62 (v.1.5.2). The 3D structure of CBMA was obtained from PubChem (PubChem CID: 11806082). Molecular docking was generated via Autodock Vina63 (v.1.1.2). Autodock Vina yielded 9 favorable outputs, exhibiting affinities ranging from −5.2 to −3.7 kcal/mol. The model with the lowest binding free energy (affinity = −5.2 kcal/mol) was judiciously chosen for subsequent molecular simulations.

Following docking, the structure and force fields for the lipid-protein-ligand system were generated utilizing the CHARMM-GUI64 (v.3.7). The ligand’s force field parameters were derived using the CHARMM General Force Field (CGenFF) program65,66. To achieve system neutrality, Na+ and Cl- ions were introduced in a controlled manner, resulting in a final NaCl concentration of 0.15 M. The system comprised protein, ligand, DMPC lipids, TIP3P water, and NaCl. MD simulations were conducted using the GROMACS67 (v.2023.3). Preceding the MD production, rigorous energy minimization and a sequence of six equilibrium steps were executed to obviate any steric clashes or inappropriate geometry of the system. The energy minimization process was performed utilizing the steepest descent algorithms until the maximum force was reduced to <1000 kJ /mol/nm. The system then underwent equilibration for ~2 ns, with temperature stabilization at 303.15 K. This was initially maintained for 250 ps under an NVT ensemble using the V-rescale thermostat. Subsequently, the system was transitioned to the Berendsen thermostat and maintained at a pressure of 1 bar through the Berendsen barostat in a semi-isotropic coupling type (NPT ensemble) for the remaining 1.7 ns. Subsequent MD simulations were conducted for a total duration of 1000 ns, with trajectory coordinates recorded at 10 ps intervals. During equilibration, the hydrogen bonds were judiciously constrained using LINCS algorithm. Electrostatic interactions over long distances were computed using the particle mesh Ewald (PME)68 method, applying a cutoff of 12 Å. Short-range van der Waals interactions were smoothly transitioned to zero from 10 Å to 12 Å. MM-PBSA calculation for this MD system were carried out with g_mmpbsa software69 to quantify the binding affinity between CBMA and BGT1.

Molecular dynamics simulations of prolonged circulation mechanism

The computational studies were conducted using atomistic molecular dynamics within the GROMACS67 (v. 2023.3) simulation package, employing the General Amber force field (GAFF2) molecular mechanics parameters and the TIP3P water model. Two oligomer systems of size ~5.5 nm made of different monomers were simulated. Each oligomer structure was positioned within an 8 nm cubic simulation box and hydrated with approximately 14,000 water molecules. Following multi-stage energy minimization procedures involving thousands of iterations, the systems underwent sequential equilibration in the isothermal-isobaric ensemble prior to executing 20 ns productive simulations under NPT ensemble conditions. Thermal regulation at 298 K was achieved through Nose-Hoover thermostat implementation, while pressure maintenance at 1 atm employed the Parrinello-Rahman method during equilibration phases. The cutoff scheme of 1.2 nm was established for short-range non-bonded interactions, complemented by Particle Mesh Ewald methodology70, a 0.1 nm grid spacing was applied for the long range electrostatic interactions. Bond constraints for hydrogen-containing covalent bonds were enforced through the LINCS algorithm71. Hydrogen bonding events were identified using geometric criteria requiring donor-acceptor distances <0.35 nm and hydrogen-donor-acceptor angles ≤30°. The temporal evolution of hydrogen bond networks was quantitatively characterized through the Luzar-Chandler kinetic formalism72.

Biocompatibility analysis

BALB/c mice received intravenous injections of PBS, PCB, or POE (2 mg/mL, 100 μL) on days 1, 4, and 7. On day 8, blood was collected for analysis. Complete blood counts were performed on 100 μL of whole blood in EDTA-K2 tubes using an ADVIA 2120i hematology analyzer (Siemens). Serum biochemistry was assessed from 100 μL of clotted blood, centrifuged at 3000 × g for 20 min, using an ADVIA XPT chemistry analyzer (Siemens).

Desorption electrospray ionization mass spectrometry imaging (DESI-MSI)

Mice bearing LLC-BM-EGFR-L860R were randomly grouped and injected with osimertinib, POE-Osi or PCB-Osi (30 mg osimertinib equiv.kg−1). After a predetermined period (1, 2, 4 h), the mice were anesthetized with avertin and perfused with cold PBS. The brains were collected, rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen, and embedded in a block of 2.5% (w/w) PVP (average mol wt. 58000) and 7.5% (w/w) HPMC (viscosity 4000 mPa.s) hydrogel. Coronal sections of 10 μm thickness were obtained using a Leica CM1950 cryostat microtome, with subsequent thaw-mounting onto superfrost slides. Mounted sections dried under nitrogen stream and stored under −80 °C until analysis.

DESI imaging experiments were conducted using a 2D DESI platform (Prosolia Inc., USA) integrated with G2-XEVO (Waters Corporation, USA). The sprayer assembly was configured with the following geometric parameters: 1 mm nozzle-to-surface distance, 5 mm emitter-to-inlet spacing, and 0 mm inlet-to-sample clearance. Electrospray ionization was achieved using a Methanol/Water/Formic Acid (98:2:0.01 v/v/v) mobile phase delivered at 2 μL/min (Waters I-Class UPLC system) under 3.5 kV capillary. Nebulization was maintained with 0.45 MPa nitrogen pressure. Instrument parameters included positive ionization mode (m/z 100-1200), The resolution of the MS system was ~22,000. The source temperature was set to 120 °C. Spatial resolution was optimized to 50 μm through coordinated scan velocity (100 μm/s) and angular parameters (65° incident angle). Acquisition setup, processing and visualisation of imaging data were performed using high definition imaging (HDI) Version 1.4 in combination with Masslynx Version 4.2 (Waters Corporation, USA).

Statistics and reproducibility

IBM SPSS Statistics software (version 20.0.0) was used for statistical analysis. No data were excluded from the analyses. No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. The investigators were not blinded to allocation during experiments and outcome assessment. n = 3 independent experiments in TEM imaging (Fig. 1b), confocal fluorescence imaging (Figs. 4h, 5d- e, g), and western blotting (Fig. 5j). n = 3 mice in confocal fluorescence imaging (Figs. 2e- f, 4a, 5f), two-photon microscopy imaging (Figs. 2b-2c, 3c). n = 5 mice in confocal fluorescence imaging (Fig. 1i). Unless otherwise specified, the data shown are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Data between groups were compared using two-tailed unpaired t test or one-way ANOVA with multiple comparisons. Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05. Sample sizes (n) and P values are described in the figure graphs and legends.
