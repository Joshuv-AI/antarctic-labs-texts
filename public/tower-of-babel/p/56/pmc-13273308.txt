A high-throughput fluorescent DNA cleavage assay for discovering bacterial DNA gyrase poisons
Authors: Matthew Dias, Thirunavukkarasu Annamalai, Yuka Otsuka, Sophia Leng, Trisha Chapagain, Shomita Ferdous, Justin Shumate, Louis Scampavia, Timothy P Spicer, Fenfei Leng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAntimicrobial resistance is a major global public health threat, yet no new antibiotic class has been introduced in over 30 years. Fluoroquinolones (FQs), a type of bacterial DNA gyrase poison, are potent antibiotics which stabilizes gyrase–DNA cleavage complexes and therefore cause lethal DNA double-stranded breaks. However, rising FQ resistance combined with adverse effects highlight the need to identify new chemical scaffolds for gyrase poisons. Discovery efforts in this area have been limited by the lack of robust high-throughput screening (HTS) assays. Here, we describe a fluorescence-based, T5 exonuclease (T5E)-assisted DNA cleavage assay that exploits the formation of multiple gyrase–DNA cleavage complexes on a single plasmid. When stabilized by a gyrase poison, these complexes are denatured with sarkosyl and processed by proteinase K to generate small linear DNA fragments, which are selectively degraded by T5E, thereby decreasing fluorescence upon SYBR Green staining. This assay was miniaturized into an automated 1536-well ultra-HTS format and used to screen 7415 compounds, identifying numerous potent gyrase poisons, predominantly FQs. We also discovered a new gyrase poison, Pyr-AMC. Biochemical assays and molecular dynamic simulation demonstrate that a single Pyr-AMC molecule binds the hydrophobic gepotidacin-binding pocket of gyrase, stabilizing cleavage complexes, and trapping gyrase-mediated DNA cleavages.

Introduction

Bacterial DNA gyrase, a type IIA DNA topoisomerase, is an essential enzyme for bacteria [1–6]. It consists of two distinct subunits, GyrA and GyrB, which form an active tetrameric A2B2 complex [2, 7]. GyrA carries an active tyrosine residue required for DNA cleavage and religation during DNA supercoiling activity [2]. GyrB contains an ATPase domain that is essential for supercoiling activity [3]. The absence of DNA gyrase in human cells makes this enzyme an attractive target for the discovery and development of novel antibiotics [8–10]. Presently, there are two main classes of gyrase inhibitors: catalytic inhibitors and poisons [10, 11]. Catalytic inhibitors, such as novobiocin, primarily target the ATP-binding site of GyrB, thereby inhibiting the enzyme’s supercoiling activity [12–14]. Unfortunately, novobiocin was withdrawn from the US market due to an unfavorable efficacy and safety profile [12]. Although several pharmaceutical companies have synthesized a vast array of compounds with anti-GyrB ATPase and antibacterial activities over the past three decades [12], no synthetic GyrB inhibitor has been successfully developed as antibiotics to date [12].

Gyrase poisons, such as fluoroquinolones (FQs), target the active tyrosine residue of GyrA by stabilizing the gyrase–DNA cleavage-complex intermediates during the DNA supercoiling cycle [15–19]. This poisoning mechanism has made FQs some of the most effective antibiotics available [20–23]. At present, FQs remain among the most widely used antibiotics. Their annual consumption often rivals or exceeds that of other major classes in specific clinical settings [24, 25]. Unfortunately, the emergence of bacterial resistance to FQs [15, 26–33], coupled with the need for scaffold diversity to overcome existing chemical limits [10, 34–36], has created an urgent need for the development of new, more effective antibiotics [28]. Furthermore, the use of FQs is associated with serious side effects [37], including tendonitis and tendon rupture [38–40], peripheral neuropathy [41], hyperglycemia [42], and aortic complications [43–45]. Consequently, the FDA has issued multiple warnings regarding FQ use and has implemented “black box” warnings on all FQs [46, 47], leading to more judicious prescribing patterns despite their continued high volume of global use.

In light of these challenges, there is an evident need to discover novel chemical scaffolds that target bacterial DNA gyrase to safely treat infections effectively. Examples of this ongoing effort include novel bacterial topoisomerase inhibitors (NBTIs), such as gepotidacin and zoliflodacin [48, 49]. These NBTIs, some of which are recently approved or are under the final stage review by FDA for the treatment of uncomplicated urinary tract infections (uUTIs) in female individuals aged 12 and older [50], also poison gyrase, albeit via targeting a different site on gyrase–DNA complexes [49, 51]. These new gyrase poisons offer promise in the fight against the “superbugs” that are resistant to nearly all existing antibiotics [52], thus helping to prevent a future pandemic of untreatable bacterial infections [53].

To address this unmet need, one effective approach for discovering new DNA gyrase poisons is high-throughput screening (HTS) of libraries containing thousands or even millions of drug-like compounds. Surprisingly, there has been a lack of specialized HTS assays dedicated specifically to the identification of DNA gyrase poisons. Instead, labor-intensive and time-consuming agarose or polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis-based assays have typically been used to identify or confirm these poisons [54, 55]. Conversely, several HTS assays are available to identify catalytic inhibitors that target the DNA supercoiling activity of gyrase [54, 56–59]. We recently established a miniaturized and automated ultra-high-throughput screening (uHTS) assay based on the supercoiling-dependent fluorescence quenching (SDFQ) using double labeled plasmids for DNA topoisomerases [60]. Using this method, we screened the NIH’s Molecular Libraries Small Molecule Repository, containing 370 620 compounds, and identified ∼3000 DNA gyrase inhibitors [54]. Although most of these newly identified inhibitors are catalytic inhibitors, agarose gel-based DNA cleavage assays also confirmed that several are poisons [54]. We also developed another method, a T5 exonuclease (T5E) AT-hairpin-based HTS assay, to identify gyrase catalytic inhibitors [56]. This assay is based on the ability of T5E to completely digest supercoiled plasmid pAB1 containing an AT hairpin structure while sparing relaxed pAB1 [56]. This HTS assay can also be converted into a miniaturized, automated uHTS format for DNA topoisomerases.

In addition to bacterial DNA gyrase, other DNA topoisomerases are critical drug targets, as these enzymes are essential to all living organisms [1–3, 8]. Currently, all clinically relevant drugs targeting DNA topoisomerases act as poisons [8, 61]. For example, doxorubicin and etoposide are poisons of human DNA topoisomerase IIα [8], while camptothecin and its analogs kill cancer cells by poisoning human DNA topoisomerase I [8]. As mentioned previously, FQs such as ciprofloxacin (CFX) are poisons against bacterial DNA gyrase and topoisomerase IV [8, 15, 21]. Furthermore, a recent study demonstrated the effectiveness of cyanotriazoles as trypanosomal DNA topoisomerase II poisons, which are capable of selectively eliminating the parasites responsible for Chagas disease and African sleeping sickness [62]. These DNA topoisomerase II poisons are promising candidates for the development of new therapeutics for these diseases [62]. Thus, it is critical to focus on discovering poisons of DNA topoisomerases, including DNA gyrase.

In this article, we present a novel fluorescence-based, T5E-assisted DNA cleavage assay for discovering bacterial DNA gyrase poisons. This assay has been successfully adapted into a miniaturized, automated uHTS format in 1536-well plates. Using this uHTS platform, we screened two compound libraries containing a total of 7415 compounds and identified numerous potent gyrase poisons, most of which are FQs. We also discovered a new bacterial DNA gyrase poison, Pyr-AMC. Combined biochemical and molecular dynamics (MD) simulation studies show that a single molecule of Pyr-AMC binds to the hydrophobic gepotidacin-binding pocket of bacterial DNA gyrase, stabilizes gyrase–DNA cleavage complexes, and consequently induces gyrase-mediated DNA damage.

Materials and methods

Proteins, plasmids, and other reagents

Escherichia coli DNA gyrase and T5E were purified as described previously [63, 64]. Plasmid pBR322 was purchased from Gene Universal Inc. (https://www.geneuniversal.com/). After bacterial genomic DNA in the pBR322 sample was digested by T5E, pBR322 was purified by phenol extraction and isopropanol precipitation. The final pBR322 sample was dissolved in 10 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0 and dialyzed against 10 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0. Lambda DNA HindIII digest was purchased from New England Biolabs, Inc (Ipswich, MA). Relaxed pBR322 was prepared using variola DNA topoisomerase I purified in our lab [65]. CFX, sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), and sarkosyl [sodium lauroyl sarcosinate (SLS)] were purchased from Sigma–Aldrich, Inc. SYBRTM Green I was bought from Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc. L-Pyroglutamic acid 7-amido-4-methylcoumarin (Pyr-AMC) was purchased from Gold Biotechnology, Inc. NSC (National Service Center) compounds were obtained from NCI DTP program (https://dtp.cancer.gov; please see Supplementary Table S1 of reference [56] for details). A 50-compound library was described previously [56]. Bovine serum albumin (BSA) and proteinase K were purchased from Worthington Biomedical Corporation. LOPAC compound library was purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc. The 6K compound library was a compound collection at the UF Scripps. Vendors and sources of other chemical compounds were shown in Supplementary Tables S1–S3.

Agarose gel-based, E. coli DNA gyrase mediated DNA cleavage assay

First, 250 ng of relaxed (Rx) or supercoiled (Sc) plasmid pBR322 and 20 nM of E. coli DNA gyrase were mixed and incubated in 1 × gyrase-mediated DNA cleavage buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8, 50 mM KAc, 10 mM MgCl2, 2 mM dithiothreitol (DTT), 1 mM ATP, 0.1 mg/ml BSA) at 37°C for 15 min in the presence of an inhibitor or a compound. Following incubation, 0.2% SDS was added to the reaction mixtures to denature the topoisomerase-inhibitor-DNA complex and 0.1 mg/ml proteinase K was added to digest the topoisomerase, by incubating for an additional 30 min at 37°C. DNA samples were analyzed in 1% agarose gels containing 0.5 µg/ml ethidium bromide in 1 × TAE buffer and photographed under ultraviolet light. CFX was used as a positive control for E. coli DNA gyrase-mediated DNA cleavage assays.

A fluorescence-based, T5E-assisted DNA cleavage assay to identify poisons for E. coli DNA gyrase

The fluorescence-based, T5E-assisted DNA cleavage assays were performed in 20 μl of 1 × gyrase-mediated DNA cleavage buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8, 50 mM KAc, 10 mM MgCl2, 2 mM DTT, 1 mM ATP, 0.1 mg/ml BSA) containing 250 ng of relaxed or supercoiled pBR322 and 0.05 mg/ml (137 nM) of E. coli DNA gyrase in the absence or presence of a potential gyrase poison. For positive controls, 50 μM of CFX was used. For negative control experiments, 1% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was used. After 60 min of incubation at 37°C, 0.125% of SLS (final concentration) was added to denature the drug-gyrase–DNA complexes. This “denaturing” step is a requirement in both gel-based and fluorescence-based gyrase poison assays to ensure that the drug-induced breaks are preserved for detection. Then 0.5 mg/ml of proteinase K was added to the reaction mixtures to digest DNA gyrase at 37°C for 30 min. After the DNA samples were diluted 100-fold using 1 × NEB buffer 4 [20 mM Tris–acetate, pH 7.9, 50 mM KAc, 10 mM Mg(AC)2, and 1 mM DTT], 30 μl of each DNA sample was transferred to a 384-well plate. Then, 200 nM of T5E was added to digest DNA fragment for 2 h at 37°C. SYBR Green was added a final 1 × concentration to all DNA samples. Fluorescence intensity was measured using a Biotek microplate reader with excitation wavelength of 497 nm and emission wavelength of 525 nm.

Fluorescent DNA cleavage uHTS assay

The fluorescent DNA cleavage uHTS assay was performed in 4 μl of 1 × gyrase DNA cleavage buffer (35 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, 24 mM KCl, 4 mM MgCl2, 2 mM DTT, 0.1 mg/ml BSA, and 2% glycerol). The following is the procedure: (i) 50 nl of a compound (column 5–44) or DMSO (for controls; column 1–4 and 45–48) was pre-spotted to a 1536 well plate (Corning 7234; plate A). (ii) Then, 2 μl of 136 nM E. coli DNA gyrase was dispensed to each well of plate A. For positive control wells (column 1–4), 50 μM CFX was added. (iii) 2 μl of 25 ng/μl plasmid pBR322 was dispensed to all wells. (iv) After centrifugation at 1200 revolutions per minute (rpm; 330 × g of Relative Centrifugal Force (RCF)) with a 204 mm radius rotor for 1 min, plate A was incubated at 37°C for 2 h. (v) After the incubation, 1 μl of 0.55% SLS and 2.3 μg/μl of proteinase K was added to each well in plate A. (vi) After centrifugation at 1 200 rpm (330 × g of RCF) with a 204 mm radius rotor for 1 min, plate A was incubated at 37°C for 30 min. (vii) Then, 4 μl of 50.4 nM T5E in 1 × NEB buffer 4 was dispensed to each well in a different 1536 well plate (plate B) and then 30 nl of reaction mixture was transferred from each well of plate A to the wells of plate B, using a pintool. Plate B was incubated for 2 h at 37°C. (viii) After the incubation, 1 μl of 5 × SYBR Green was added to each well of plate B. Plate B was centrifuged at 1200 rpm (330 × g of RCF) with a 204 mm radius rotor for 1 min. (ix) The plate was incubated at room temperature for 30 min in the dark and read on the PHERAstar FSX (BMG) measuring fluorescence (excitation@485nm, emission@Em520). To calculate assay robustness in terms of Z’, we used wells containing E. coli DNA gyrase, pBR322 and 50 μM CFX as a positive control and E. coli DNA gyrase, pBR322, and DMSO as a negative control.

MD simulation

To obtain a predicted binding model of Pyr-AMC with E. coli DNA gyrase, docking was performed using the online server lab.chaidiscovery.com [67]. The model was generated using the structural components from PDB ID: 8QQI, which includes two GyrA subunits, two GyrB subunits, and the associated DNA fragment, along with the SMILES representation of Pyr-AMC. Using the CHARMM-GUI web interface, the gyrase–DNA–ligand complex was solvated in a cubic water box containing TIP3 water molecules [68]. The system was neutralized and ionized to a final concentration of 0.12 M KCl and 0.02 M MgCl₂. All-atom MD simulations were performed with GPU versions of NAMD 3.0 [69] using the CHARMM36m force field [70]. Long-range electrostatic interactions were computed using the particle mesh Ewald method [71]. The system was initially energy-minimized for 10 000 steps, followed by 250 ps of equilibration at 310 K with a 2 fs integration time step. Subsequently, a 500 ns production run was carried out under constant pressure (1 atm) and temperature (310 K) conditions. Pressure control was maintained using the Nosé–Hoover Langevin piston method [72] with a piston period of 50 fs and a decay time of 25 fs, while temperature coupling was achieved via a Langevin thermostat with a friction coefficient of 1 ps⁻¹.

Results

A novel fluorescence-based, T5E-assisted DNA cleavage assay for DNA gyrase poisons

Figure 1 shows the principle and experimental strategy for the fluorescence-based, T5E-assisted DNA cleavage assay designed to identify bacterial DNA gyrase poisons. Under our experimental conditions, we hypothesize that multiple DNA gyrase molecules bind simultaneously to a single plasmid, forming stable complexes that result in multiple cleavage sites on an individual molecule (Fig. 1A). This hypothesis is supported by the high affinity of E. coli DNA gyrase binding to DNA with a dissociation constant (Kd) of 10−10 M, yielding a stable noncovalent complex with a half-life of about 60 h [73]. At the high enzyme concentrations (68 or 150 nM used in our assays), multiple E. coli DNA gyrase molecules are expected to occupy the plasmid concurrently although a sequential interaction mechanism between gyrase and DNA remains a possibility. While the relaxed (Rx) plasmid is shown for demonstration, both relaxed and supercoiled (Sc) plasmids serve as viable substrates. DNA gyrase poisons, such as CFX, stabilize these gyrase–DNA complexes and shift the equilibrium toward the cleaved state. SDS or SLS then denatures the gyrase protein. Because the protein is covalently linked to the DNA at the 5′ ends via the active-site tyrosine, denaturing the protein prevents it from religating the DNA. This denaturation converts a transient, drug-stabilized intermediate into a permanent, measurable double-stranded break. Following proteinase K digestion of the gyrase, fragmented linear DNA molecules are generated. T5E, which selectively digests linear and nicked DNA while leaving Sc and Rx plasmids intact [56], is then used to degrade the fragmented DNA. This step effectively “enhances” the signal decrease of the remaining DNA in the presence of a gyrase poison (Fig. 1B). The resulting reduction in plasmid concentration can be quantified using SYBR Green staining or Quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction (qPCR). Furthermore, this SYBR Green fluorescence-based format is amenable to HTS of large compound libraries.

A novel fluorescence-based, T5E-assisted DNA cleavage assay for discovery of bacterial DNA gyrase poisons. (A) The principle. (B) The experimental strategy. This novel and unique assay can be developed into an automated miniaturized HTS assay to discover/identify bacterial DNA gyrase poisons by screening compound libraries that contain thousands or millions of drug-like compounds. Signal loss occurs exclusively when a poison induces DNA linearization, allowing for enzymatic degradation.

The genesis of the fluorescence-based, T5E-assisted DNA cleavage assay originated from observations made during our agarose gel-based, bacterial gyrase-mediated DNA cleavage experiments. Although it is well-known that increasing DNA gyrase concentrations leads to higher yields of linear DNA, we found that in the presence of 50 μM CFX and a high concentration of E. coli DNA gyrase (e.g. 150 nM), all pBR322 plasmid molecules were converted into small DNA fragments (Fig. 2A). The fragmentation of pBR322 was contingent upon the presence of CFX (compare lane 1 to lanes 2–6 of Fig. 2A) and correlated with the CFX concentration (Fig. 2B).

Through a DNA gyrase titration experiment, we noted a progressive increase in the production of linear DNA with increasing concentrations of E. coli DNA gyrase in the presence of 50 μM CFX (Fig. 2C). Remarkably, we observed DNA smears extending from the linear band and traversing the supercoiled band when 26.8 and 53.6 nM of DNA gyrase are used (lanes 4 and 5 of Fig. 2C), resulting in a significant reduction of the supercoiled DNA bands. As anticipated, and consistent with Fig. 2A, the supercoiled plasmid DNA template disappeared entirely when 150 nM of DNA gyrase was used (lanes 8 and 9 of Fig. 2C). These findings provide strong support for our hypothesis that the presence of two or more gyrase molecules on a single plasmid leads to the formation of multiple gyrase–DNA cleavage complexes. These complexes, stabilized by CFX, were subsequently denatured by SDS or SLS to yield gyrase–DNA covalent complexes. Following proteinase K digestion of the gyrase, fragmented linear DNA molecules were generated (Fig. 1A). These results serve as the foundation for the proposed fluorescence-based, T5E-assisted DNA cleavage assay (Fig. 1B).

In a typical gyrase-mediated DNA cleavage assay, SDS or SLS is used to denature drug-gyrase–DNA complexes [54, 74]. Our results demonstrated that even low concentrations of SDS completely inhibited T5E activity (Fig. 2D). In contrast, relatively high concentrations of SLS (e.g. 0.015%) did not significantly affect the activity of this enzyme (Fig. 2E). Therefore, we selected SLS to denature the drug-gyrase–DNA complexes in all subsequent assays. Indeed, in the presence of SLS, 50 nM of T5E successfully digested 250 ng of lambda DNA HindIII digest within 30 min (Fig. 2F).

A series of experiments was conducted to determine the optimal conditions for the fluorescence-based, T5E-assisted DNA cleavage assay for E. coli DNA gyrase (Fig. 3). In the presence of CFX, the fluorescence intensity of the DNA samples progressively decreased as the concentration of E. coli DNA gyrase was increased, reaching a plateau at 100 nM (Fig. 3A). In contrast, varying the concentration of E. coli DNA gyrase did not significantly change the fluorescence intensity of the samples in the absence of CFX (Fig. 3A). Furthermore, the fluorescence intensity was found to be dependent on the concentration of CFX (Fig. 3B). T5E significantly enhanced the fluorescence loss of the DNA samples in the presence of CFX (Fig. 3C and D). Diluting the DNA samples 100-fold effectively mitigated the inhibitory effects of SLS on T5E digestion (Fig. 3E). Beyond reducing SLS inhibition, this 100-fold dilution is critical for maintaining the DNA concentration within the linear dynamic range of SYBR Green. At lower dilutions, the fluorescence signal in control reactions becomes saturated, which significantly compresses the dynamic range and reduces the Signal-to-Background (S/B) ratio. Our results in Fig. 3E demonstrate that the S/B ratio is maximized to 8.6 at a 100-fold dilution.

Moreover, this dilution step serves the purpose of reducing the concentration of tested compounds, thereby minimizing potential background fluorescence interference with SYBR Green. Both 1 × and 2 × SYBR Green provided good results for the HTS assays (Fig. 3F). These experiments helped establish the optimal conditions for the HTS assay including 137 nM E. coli DNA gyrase, 50 μM CFX (for positive controls), 200 nM T5E, a 100-fold sample dilution into plate B, and 1 × SYBR Green. The assay demonstrated tolerance to up to 2% DMSO without a significant change in signal. Finally, a titration experiment showed that CFX caused progressive DNA cleavage with an estimated EC50 value of 1.48 μM (Fig. 3B), which is consistent with previously published results [60].

To validate our assay, we conducted a preliminary experiment using 10 compounds: five FQs and five other antibiotics (novobiocin, ampicillin, polymyxin B, sulfanilamide, and kanamycin). The results are shown in Supplementary Figs S1A and S1B. Notably, the fluorescence intensity of DNA samples was significantly lower in the presence of FQs. In contrast, the fluorescence intensity remained notably higher in the presence of the other antibiotics, including novobiocin. As expected, novobiocin, a known DNA gyrase catalytic inhibitor that targets the ATPase subunit [65, 75–77], did not induce a decrease in fluorescence intensity. These findings demonstrate the feasibility of our assay, which is specifically tailored for the identification of DNA gyrase poisons.

Screening a 50-compound library by the fluorescence-based, T5E-assisted DNA cleavage assay

Next, we screened a 50-compound library assembled in our laboratory [56] using this assay at a final compound concentration of 50 μM in duplicate. The screen yielded the following performance statistics: a Z′-factor of 0.53, a signal-to-background (S/B) ratio of 7.3, and 8 hits (Supplementary Fig. S2, Table 1, and Supplementary Table S1). A Z′-factor >0.5 suggests that the screen is statistically robust. We selected a 50 μM concentration for this screen to be consistent with the 50 μM of CFX used as the positive control. Additionally, we sought to determine whether the intrinsic fluorescence of certain library compounds interfered with the assay, as previous HTS assays for DNA topoisomerase inhibitors have been significantly affected by compounds with strong autofluorescence [54, 56]. Our results clearly demonstrated that the fluorescence from these compounds per se did not interfere with the SYBR Green signal (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Table S1).

Among the eight hits, seven were quinolones, specifically levofloxacin, CFX, oxolinic acid, enrofloxacin, norfloxacin, lomefloxacin, and nalidixic acid, along with doxorubicin. Quinolones are well-known as potent gyrase poisons [20, 21], and it was therefore expected that they would be identified in this HTS assay. Interestingly, although nalidixic acid has a reported IC50 value significantly higher than 50 μM [78], the fluorescence intensity of the DNA samples in its presence was considerably lower than that of the negative controls. In essence, this HTS assay is capable of identifying gyrase poisons even if they possess a low IC50 against E. coli DNA gyrase.

The only nonquinolone hit was doxorubicin, a DNA intercalator that binds tightly to DNA [79]. Agarose gel-based DNA cleavage assays demonstrated that doxorubicin did not induce gyrase-mediated DNA cleavage, thereby excluding it as a gyrase poison (Supplementary Fig. S3A and B). Consequently, doxorubicin represented a false positive in this assay. A plausible explanation is that the high affinity of doxorubicin for DNA leads to the quenching of SYBR Green fluorescence. To investigate this further, we repeated the fluorescence-based DNA cleavage assay at a concentration of 20 μM and again observed the quenching of the SYBR Green signal (Supplementary Fig. S3A). Subsequently, we performed a titration experiment by adding varying concentrations of doxorubicin to 200 ng of Rx pBR322 in a 1 × gyrase DNA cleavage buffer. After diluting the reaction mixtures 100-fold and adding 1 × SYBR Green, the results presented in Supplementary Fig. S3C confirmed that high concentrations of doxorubicin indeed causes the quenching of SYBR Green fluorescence.

In this pilot screen, we observed that several compounds, including ethacridine, echinomycin, topotecan, and suramin, caused notably low fluorescence signals in the DNA cleavage assays when conducted at 50 μM. However, when the final compound concentration was reduced to 20 μM, these compounds no longer induced a reduction in the fluorescence intensity of SYBR Green. This observation suggests that they do not function as gyrase poisons (Supplementary Fig. S3A). This dose-dependent shift indicates that at 50 μM, these compounds reach a threshold concentration sufficient to interfere with the SYBR Green signal through fluorescence quenching, a phenomenon characterized for doxorubicin in Supplementary Fig. S3C. Further validation through agarose gel-based DNA cleavage assays confirmed that these compounds indeed do not possess gyrase poisoning properties (Supplementary Fig. S3B).

Establishing a miniaturized, automated uHTS assay and screen the LOPAC library

Based on the fluorescence-based, T5E-assisted DNA cleavage assay, we have established a miniaturized, automated primary uHTS assay [80] in a 1536-well format to identify bacterial DNA gyrase poisons. After a series of experiments, 4 μl of 68 nM of E. coli DNA gyrase, 12.5 ng/μl of pBR322, 0.11% SLS, 0.46 μg/μl proteinase K, and 50 nM of T5E were found optimal and chosen for the assay (Supplementary Fig. S4). Four incubations are needed: the first incubation is for E. coli DNA gyrase binding and cleaving plasmid pBR322 for 2 h at 37°C, the second incubation is for SLS denaturing gyrase–DNA cleavage complexes followed by proteinase K digestion for 30 min at 37°C, the third incubation is for T5E to digest the fragmented linear DNA molecules for 2 h at 37°C, and the fourth incubation is for SYBR Green binding to DNA in the dark at room temperature. We compared two different 1536 well plates: medium-binding (Greiner 789176-F) and nonbinding (Corning 7234) plates, and found that nonbinding plate showed less well to well variability, and good Z’ = 0.77, so they were used in the screens (Supplementary Fig. S5A). We also found that addition of SLS and proteinase K together did not significantly affect S/B ratio and Z’ value, and combined them for dispensing as one reagent during the screens (Supplementary Fig. S5B). Although the assay could tolerate up to 4% DMSO without any significant change in signal, 1% of DMSO was used. The fluorescence intensity was measured at the excitation wavelength of 485 nm and emission wavelength of 520 nm.

With the establishment of the final assay conditions, we screened two full 1536-well plates to compare pre-spotting the compounds to the wells versus adding compounds to the reaction mixtures using the pintool. Our results showed that the pre-spotted plate had less variability on well to well basis across the entire plate with similar Z’ statistics for both plates (Supplementary Fig. S6). Therefore, we decided to pre-spot compounds to the wells moving forward.

To validate the uHTS readiness of the DNA cleavage assay, we screened the LOPAC®1280 library containing 1 279 pharmacologically active compounds nominally at 12.4 μM using the 1536-well plate uHTS assay. Supplementary Fig. S7, Table 1, and Supplementary Table S2 show the results. The following are screening statistics: Z’ = 0.60 and 9 hits with >50% inhibition, yielding a hit rate of ∼0.7%. The hits include 4 known gyrase poisons (finafloxacin, lomefloxacin, ofloxacin, and gemifloxacin) with >95% average response with a very small standard deviation amongst replicate test wells of the same drugs (Supplementary Table S2). The other five hits ranged from 50% to 65% average response with a large standard deviation between replicate wells, except for CGS-15943 (Sigma catalog number C199) which had a 54% response and 2% standard deviation. Agarose gel-based DNA cleavage assays showed that these were false positives. Although the LOPAC®1280 library carries two additional DNA gyrase poisons, nalidixic acid and oxolinic acid, their IC50 values against E. coli DNA gyrase are known to be >30 μM significantly higher than the 12.4 μM used in the HTS assays [78]. In this case, their binding to the gyrase–DNA complex is insufficient to cause the complete DNA degradation required for hit detection in the HTS assays. Therefore, it is not surprising that they are not found as hits since 12.4 μM of compounds was used in the uHTS assay.

6K compound Library HTS identifies a new bacterial DNA gyrase inhibitor

Next, we screened a 6K compound library that contains 6 135 compounds assembled at the UF Scripps HTS Center [80]: the MicroSource Discovery Systems (962 compounds), the NIH Clinical Collection 2 (236 compounds), the Pathogen Box (362 compounds), Cayman Chemical Libraries (1040 Compounds), the Clinically Relevant Compound Library (879 Compounds), the Porco Compound Library (954 Compounds), the Prestwick Chemical Library (850 compounds), and the Tocriscreen 2.0 Micro Compound Library (852 compounds) (Fig. 4A). The uHTS assays were performed using a nominal compound concentration of 19.6 μM. The screen results are summarized in Fig. 4B, Table 1, and Supplementary Table S3. Specifically, 32 compounds demonstrated >50% average response indicating significant loss of plasmid DNA with a hit rate of 0.52% (Table 1). Among these hits, 23 compounds showed an average response >95%, 4 compounds showed responses between 90% and 95%, another 4 between 70% and 90%, and 1 compound showed a 54% average response. Interestingly, after inspecting their chemical structures, 30 compounds out of these 32 hits are FQs (Supplementary Table S3). Only two compounds were not FQs. One compound is mitoxantrone, a known DNA topoisomerase II poison [81] with a 54% average response.

The other compound in the library was initially identified as Pyr-AMC, yielding an average response of 102% ± 0.48. Pyr-AMC is a known fluorogenic substrate for testing proteinase activity; while it is typically nonfluorescent until enzymatically cleaved, its fluorescent properties in our HTS buffer may have contributed to the screening signal [82, 83]. This result prompted a detailed investigation into its biochemical activity against bacterial DNA gyrase. As shown in Fig. 5, Pyr-AMC is a novel E. coli DNA gyrase poison that induces both DNA nicks and double-stranded DNA cleavage (Fig. 5B and C). Notably, this compound did not inhibit gyrase’s catalytic activity (Fig. 5D), suggesting it acts as a “pure” poison. While Pyr-AMC does not cause the complete degradation of pBR322 observed with more potent FQs (Fig. 5), a follow-up fluorescence titration (Fig. 5F) confirmed that it partially stabilizes the cleavage complex, a finding consistent with our gel-based results.

Subsequent examination of the library compound (PubChem SID 104169961) revealed that while early database versions identified this compound as Pyr-AMC, the most recent update (version 3) identifies it as norfloxacin, a potent FQ. This re-identification explains the high response observed in the initial HTS assay. However, because our follow-up validation was conducted using Pyr-AMC based on the initial database entry, this study led to the serendipitous discovery that Pyr-AMC is, in fact, a new bacterial DNA gyrase poison. Although it is a significantly weaker poison than FQs, stabilizing only partial cleavage and requiring higher concentrations, the gel-based assays (Fig. 5B–D) definitively demonstrate gyrase-mediated DNA breakage without catalytic inhibition. These results confirm Pyr-AMC as a new chemical scaffold for gyrase poisoning and validate the sensitivity of our HTS methodology in identifying noncatalytic inhibitors.

Discussion

We have developed a novel fluorescence-based, T5E-assisted DNA cleavage assay for identifying bacterial DNA gyrase poisons. This assay is based on the observation that a potent gyrase poison, such as CFX, in the presence of a high concentration of E. coli DNA gyrase, can convert circular plasmids (e.g. pBR322) into small linear DNA fragments. The likely mechanism is that multiple DNA gyrase molecules simultaneously bind to a plasmid, form multiple gyrase–DNA cleavage complexes on the same plasmid. These complexes can be stabilized or trapped by a gyrase poison like CFX. SLS is then used to denature gyrase–DNA cleavage complexes to yield denatured gyrase–DNA covalent complexes. Subsequent digestion of DNA gyrase by proteinase K produces small linear DNA fragments. Because T5E selectively digests linear and nicked DNA, it was used to completely degrade these DNA fragments, thereby “enhancing” the fluorescence signal loss of the cleavage products after SYBR Green staining (Fig. 1).

We have converted this assay to a miniaturized, automated primary uHTS assay in the 1536-well format and conducted uHTS screens against two compound libraries, the LOPAC®1280 library and the 6K Compound Library with excellent screening statistics Table 1). This uHTS assay offers several unique advantages. First, it is fully automated and requires only 4 μl of reaction volume per well containing 68 nM E. coli DNA gyrase and 12.5 ng/μl pBR322 per assay with a total run time of just a few hours. These features allow rapid and cost-effective screening of large compound libraries. For example, we screened two libraries comprising a total of 7414 compounds in <1 day using limited resources (Table 1). The assay is now ready for HTS of much larger libraries such as the UF-Scripps Drug Discovery Library (UF-SDDL), which contains ∼650 000 compounds [84], when sufficient resources become available.

Second, this HTS assay selectively identifies gyrase poisons rather than catalytic inhibitors, a significant advantage over other topoisomerase HTS assays [54, 56]. This selectivity is critical because, as demonstrated by Kreuzer and Cozzarelli [85] and confirmed by Steck and Drlica [86], gyrase poisons do not merely inhibit the enzyme’s catalytic activity; instead, they stabilize the covalent gyrase–DNA intermediate. These stabilized complexes result in lethal double-strand breaks that are rapidly bactericidal. In contrast, simple catalytic inhibitors often result only in bacteriostatic effects, which may be more easily bypassed by the cell. Our results show that 44 gyrase poisons were identified across the three libraries screened, with no catalytic inhibitors detected. While most of these hits were FQs, future HTS campaigns could utilize an FQ-resistant DNA gyrase to specifically identify non-FQ gyrase poisons.

Third, the uHTS assay is highly effective at identifying potent DNA gyrase poisons, yielding an average response of >90% with very small standard deviations between test replicates (Supplementary Tables S1–S3). None of the top hits were false positives, demonstrating the assay’s robustness when potent gyrase poisons are present in the compound library. Six false positives were observed in the LOPAC®1280 screen, each showing average responses between 50% and 60% with large standard deviations in their replicate well activity. These results suggest potential issues with the quality of these compounds (Supplementary Table S2). The LOPAC®1280 dataset also shows a broad range of activity (−45% to 50%), further indicating possible concerns about promiscuously active compounds in drug repurposing libraries.

Fourth, this fluorescence-based DNA-cleavage HTS assay can be adapted to uHTS formats to identify poisons of other DNA topoisomerases, such as human topoisomerase IIα and topoisomerase I, given that anticancer drugs such as etoposide, doxorubicin, and topotecan act as poisons against these enzymes [61]. Additionally, this assay can be combined with two previously established HTS assays, the T5E AT-hairpin assay and the SDFQ-based assay, to enable efficient discovery of DNA topoisomerase catalytic inhibitors and poisons [54, 56].

The most intriguing discovery is the identification of Pyr-AMC as a new bacterial DNA gyrase poison. Both agarose gel and fluorescence-based DNA cleavage assays demonstrate that Pyr-AMC is indeed an E. coli DNA gyrase poison (Fig. 5). It leads to single-stranded DNA nicks and double-stranded DNA breaks in the presence of bacterial DNA gyrase with significantly more DNA nicks at the high DNA gyrase concentrations (Fig. 5C). This mechanism of action is similar to that of gepotidacin where a single gepotidacin molecule binds to a pocket formed by the two GyrA subunits, located midway between the two scissile DNA bonds where the DNA is cleaved [87, 88]. This poisoning mechanism is vital, as the conversion from growth inhibition to cell death is a distinct event that can be decoupled by specific mutations; thus, the ability of Pyr-AMC to stabilize these breaks suggests it acts as a true lethal poison. This location is distinct from the quinolone resistance-determining region, the binding site used by FQs [32].

We have performed a MD simulation to further understand how Pyr-AMC binds to E. coli DNA gyrase–DNA complex and causes the DNA damage. The dynamics of the gyrase–DNA-Pyr-AMC complex during the 500-ns simulation is shown in Supplementary Video S1. Indeed, the MD simulation shows that a single molecule of Pyr-AMC binds to the gepotidacin-binding site of E. coli DNA gyrase (Fig. 6) with the Pyr group interacting with the DNA and the AMC group occupying the hydrophobic pocket away from the DNA (Fig. 6). There are two hydrogen bonds formed between Asp82 of one GyrA subunit and the Pyr group (Fig. 6B). Nevertheless, the Pyr-AMC molecule, especially the Pyr group, in the binding pocket undergoes large movement during the MD simulation (Supplementary Fig. S8). The Pyr group does not directly interact with base pairs of the DNA molecule for most of the simulation time, indicating weak binding of the Pyr-AMC molecule to the gyrase–DNA complex. This is consistent with our results where Pyr-AMC does not significantly inhibit the gyrase catalytic activities. Pyr-AMC offers a promising novel chemical scaffold for the development of gyrase poisons although it does not have potent antibacterial activities.

While robust, this fluorescence-based HTS assay has certain technical limitations. First, the method requires a higher concentration of bacterial DNA gyrase relative to other HTS platforms to ensure that circular plasmid DNA substrates are fully converted into linear fragments in the presence of a potent poison; this increased protein requirement may elevate costs when screening very large compound libraries. Second, the assay is specifically optimized to identify potent gyrase poisons that achieve near-complete substrate conversion at the tested concentration. Consequently, the platform may lack the sensitivity required to detect low-potency inhibitors or those that only partially stabilize the gyrase–DNA cleavage complexes under HTS conditions. Finally, as with most fluorescence-based assays, there is a risk of signal interference from inherently fluorescent small molecules. However, the implementation of a 100-fold dilution step significantly mitigates this interference, enabling the accurate identification of potent gyrase poisons.

In summary, we have established a novel fluorescence-based, T5E-assisted DNA cleavage assay to discover bacterial DNA gyrase poisons and converted it to a miniaturized, automated uHTS assay. Using this uHTS assay, we have screened multiple compound libraries and identified many potent bacterial DNA gyrase poisons, although most are FQs. We also discovered a new bacterial gyrase poison, Pyr-AMC. Our gel-based DNA cleavage assays and MD simulation show that a single molecule of Pyr-AMC binds to the hydrophobic gepotidacin-binding pocket of bacterial DNA gyrase, stabilizes the gyrase–DNA cleavage complex, and thus greatly interfere with the gyrase’s normal functions.
