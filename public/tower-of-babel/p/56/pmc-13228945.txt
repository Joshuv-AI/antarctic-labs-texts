Expenditures attributable to infant lower respiratory tract infection due to respiratory syncytial virus in the US: A real-world study using the PharMetrics Plus Database, 2015–2023
Authors: Ahuva Averin, Amy W. Law, Mark Atwood, Erin Quinn, Derek Weycker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWhile several studies have reported the episodic expenditures attributable to lower respiratory tract illness due to respiratory syncytial virus (RSV-LRTI) among US infants, published estimates vary widely. Moreover, it is unknown whether expenditures due to infant RSV-LRTI vary relative to the RSV season or COVID-19 pandemic. A new study using a US healthcare claims database (2015–2023) was thus undertaken to estimate infant episodic expenditures attributable to RSV-LRTI requiring hospitalization or ambulatory care only, overall as well as by age at infection and gestational age in weeks (wGA) at birth. Analyses were also conducted relative to the RSV season and COVID-19 pandemic. Mean expenditures were $23,024 for hospitalized episodes, $2,298 for emergency department (ED) episodes, and $410 for other ambulatory episodes. Across care settings, mean expenditures nominally decreased with increasing by age at infection and birth wGA. Mean expenditures – irrespective of care setting – across subgroup analyses defined on RSV season and COVID-19 pandemic were largely consistent with results from overall analyses. In conclusion, findings from this study indicate that expenditures attributable to infant RSV-LRTI remain high, especially in the hospital and ED settings.

Introduction

Lower respiratory tract illness (LRTI) due to respiratory syncytial virus (RSV; RSV-LRTI) is relatively common in children aged <2 y.1,2 Younger infants, those born prematurely, and those with underlying health conditions (e.g., congenital lung or heart disease) who are infected with RSV are particularly susceptible to disease-related morbidity resulting in hospitalization and, in the most severe cases, premature death.2,3

Until 2023, only palivizumab – a short-acting, multi-dose monoclonal antibody recommended for high-risk infants (i.e., based on underlying health conditions and prematurity) – was available to prevent RSV in infants.4 Due to the high costs associated with monthly dosing and exclusive eligibility criteria, most infants remained unprotected.5–7 Three newer interventions – RSVpreF, nirsevimab, and clesrovimab – are currently available in the United States (US) to protect infants against RSV-LRTI.8–10 Nirsevimab, a single-dose monoclonal antibody immunization, received approval from the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in July 2023 and was recommended in August 2023 by the Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices (ACIP) for use in otherwise unprotected infants aged <8 months, as well as for select children aged 8–19 months who are at increased risk of severe disease.8 Maternal vaccine RSVpreF was FDA approved and recommended by the ACIP in August and September of 2023, respectively, for administration among pregnant women between 32 and 36 weeks of gestation.9 In June of 2025, clesrovimab, an alternative monoclonal antibody, was approved and recommended by the ACIP also for use among unimmunized infants aged <8 months.10

A 2023 study estimated the episodic cost of infant RSV-LRTI – overall, by age at infection, and by gestational age in weeks (wGA) at birth – using data from the Merative MarketScan Commercial Claims and Encounters (CCAE) Database.11 Findings suggested that episodic expenditures due to RSV-LRTI among commercial-insured US infants may be higher than previously reported, especially for episodes requiring hospitalization (mean hospitalization cost, adjusted to 2023 USD: $28,945).11 Due to variation in findings across published studies, as well as lack of available data evaluating whether expenditures differ relative to the RSV season and COVID-19 pandemic, a follow-on real-world evaluation using more recent data from another healthcare claims database comprising commercial insurance plans was conducted.11–13

Methods

Study design and data sources

A retrospective observational cohort design was employed, utilizing data from the IQVIA PharMetrics® Plus Database spanning January 1, 2015, through December 31, 2023 (“study period”). The study data source comprises fully adjudicated medical (i.e., facility and professional service) and outpatient pharmacy claims from participating commercial health insurance plans covering 175 million US persons. From medical claims, data include service dates, places of service, diagnoses, and procedures; from outpatient pharmacy claims, data include dispensing date, quantity, and days supplied. Amounts paid (i.e., reimbursed) by health plans are captured in both medical and pharmacy claims. For all health plan enrollees in the database, healthcare enrollment data and select demographic and eligibility information are available.

Study population

All infants aged <12 months with available claims information indicating in-hospital birth without conflicting information on gestational age and ≥1 episode of medically attended RSV-LRTI during their first year of life were included in the study population. For infants with ≥1 episode of RSV-LRTI, only the first episode was included in analyses. Infants were excluded if they had missing healthcare expenditure data.

Episodes of RSV-LRTI were identified based on: hospitalizations with evidence of RSV (based on ICD-10 codes B97.4, J21.0, J20.5, J12.1) and LRTI (Supplement Table 1); and ambulatory encounters for RSV and LRTI on the same date or within ±10 d. Episodes were characterized based on care setting (i.e., hospital, emergency department [ED], outpatient clinic [OC]). The algorithm employed to identify RSV-LRTI is consistent with the methodology detailed in a previously published analysis.11 Episodes were further stratified by whether the episode start date was during the RSV season: in-season (i.e., October–March) or out-of-season (i.e., April–September), as well as by timing in relation to the COVID-19 pandemic: “pre-pandemic” (January 2015–February 2020), “pandemic” (March 2020–June 2022), or “post-pandemic” (July 2022–December 2023).

Study measures

Baseline characteristics of infants in the study population included age at the time of RSV-LRTI (i.e., 0–<1, 1–<2, 2–<3, 3–<6, 6–<12 months), sex, gestational age (wGA) at birth (i.e., ≤32, 33–36, ≥37), geographical region, seasonal timing of RSV-LRTI, timing of RSV-LRTI relative to the COVID-19 pandemic, birth hospitalization length of stay, high-risk status (i.e., yes/no, based on presence of comorbid conditions defined in Supplement Table 2), and use of palivizumab. Expenditures per episode of RSV-LRTI were tallied based on paid amounts for all hospitalizations with evidence of RSV-LRTI and all ambulatory claims with a diagnosis code for RSV or LRTI (irrespective of position) during the episode. In the case that a hospital admission extended beyond the 30 d window, episode duration was truncated at 30 d, and the full cost of hospitalization was included in the episode. Paid amounts were inflated to 2023 US dollars based on the medical care series of the Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers.14

Statistical analyses

Baseline characteristics were analyzed descriptively and reported categorically as counts and percentages. Episodic expenditures due to RSV-LTRI were calculated on an overall basis, as well as for subgroups defined on age at the time of RSV-LRTI infection and wGA at birth and during periods defined relative to the RSV season and COVID-19 pandemic. Study measures were summarized using means and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs; calculated using non-parametric bootstrapping).

Results

Study population

Among 3.1 million infants in the source population, 112,272 infants aged <12 months had evidence of RSV-LRTI between January 2015 and December 2023 (Supplement Table 3). Of these, 22,792 qualified based on a diagnosis of RSV-LRTI in acute-care hospital (inpatient) setting and 87,881 qualified based on a diagnosis of RSV-LRTI in an ambulatory setting (ED: 18,935; OC: 68,946) and had total episodic expenditures >$0 (1,599 excluded).

Baseline characteristics

Among patients included in the study population, 58.1% were aged <6 months at infection (mean [SD] age: 5.5 [3.1] months), 55.6% were male, and 89.0% were born full term (≥37 wGA) (Supplement Table 4). Most RSV-LRTI episodes (78.9%) were in-season, and 49.7% occurred during the pre-pandemic period, 18.9% occurred during the pandemic period, and 31.4% occurred during the post-pandemic period. Only 1.2% of infants were high-risk.

Overall, out-of-season expenditures were nominally higher than in-season expenditures ($24,932 [22,808–28,018] vs. $22,578 [22,003–23,224], respectively). Discussion

To supplement previously published findings on the cost of RSV-LRTI, we used the IQVIA PharMetrics® Plus Database to estimate episodic expenditures attributable to medically attended RSV-LRTI by age at infection and wGA at birth, as well as within periods defined by timing of the episode relative to the RSV season and the COVID-19 pandemic. Study findings suggest that episodic expenditures due to RSV-LRTI were highest among children infected at a younger age and children born prematurely (i.e., <37 wGA). No large descriptive differences in mean episodic expenditures were observed across subgroups based on seasonality and timing of infection relative to the COVID-19 pandemic. This suggests that infants who experience RSV-LRTI outside of the typical season – an occurrence which became relatively common during the years immediately following the COVID-19 pandemic – accrue similar episodic costs to infants who contract RSV-LRTI during the season.

Compared with expenditures reported for commercial-insured infants in a published evaluation by Averin et al., which used claims from the Merative MarketScan CCAE Database, overall episodic expenditures of RSV-LRTI in the present study were somewhat lower for hospital and OC settings (by 20% and 11%, respectively) and higher for the ED setting (by 21%).11 However, similar trends in episodic expenditures of RSV-LRTI by age at infection and wGA (i.e., higher expenditures among younger and premature infants) were observed in both studies. We hypothesize that some of the difference in expenditures across studies are related to differences in database design. Specifically, amounts paid by patients were not tallied in the analyses using the PharMetrics Plus Database, whereas they were tallied in the analyses using the Merative MarketScan CCAE Database. However, it is unlikely that this discrepancy in results is attributable to this singular difference, thus other factors, such as the health plans contributing to the respective databases, may also drive differences.

In a 2024 RSV costing study by Clopper et al., which was based on observational findings from the New Vaccine Surveillance Network (NVSN), overall costs of hospitalization among infants – irrespective of insurance status and type – for acute respiratory infection due to RSV (estimated using cost-to-charge ratios) were markedly lower than expenditures reported in the present study.12 Mean overall costs of hospitalization and ED encounters were 69% and 212% lower, respectively, compared to mean overall plan paid amounts in the present study. However, the Clopper et al. study was subject to several noteworthy limitations, including: small sample size, missing patient data, and large variations in costs across hospitals (range of mean hospitalization cost in 2023 USD: $7,387–$21,407). This study also reported that overall costs were higher for Medicaid versus commercially insured infants, a finding that is unusual in US costing studies.15–19

Another 2024 cost study utilizing claims data for privately insured, non-capitated health plan enrollees from the Merative MarketScan database reported more comparable, albeit higher, costs than those reported herein, with patterns in episodic cost by wGA that were largely consistent with those observed in the present study.13 For infants <1 y of age, the overall mean cost per episode was 23% and 63% higher (vs. overall) for hospital and outpatient settings (i.e., ED/OC), respectively. By wGA, mean episodic cost in 2023 USD for hospitalized infants born <32 wGA was $70,670 and $25,264 for infants born ≥37wGA, versus $48,073 and $20,387 in the present study. Differences in study design (e.g., inclusion of both amounts paid by plans and patients [Tran et al.]) may contribute to the higher reported costs.

While healthcare claims databases provide information on large numbers of patients with specific diagnoses who receive care for specific conditions, several limitations from the use of such databases should be noted. First, the algorithms employed to identify RSV-LRTI encounters (i.e., based on diagnosis codes) have not been formally evaluated against a “gold standard” within the study data sources.20 We note, however, that a recent validation study suggests algorithms for identifying RSV hospitalizations using ICD-10 diagnosis codes are reasonably sensitive and highly specific.21 Prior literature also indicates that the sensitivity of RSV diagnosis codes is higher among children in hospital settings and among younger children (<5 y), as well as in studies that were inclusive of diagnosis codes for both RSV and acute respiratory infections (i.e., as considered herein).22–24 We also note that findings for smaller subgroups (e.g., infants born at <32 wGA) should be considered within the context of their sample size. Additionally, results do not represent the total economic burden of RSV-LRTI–related care because only expenditures (i.e., amounts paid by health plans for serviced rendered) were tallied and other costs (e.g., out-of-pocket payments, travel, productivity loss) are not captured in healthcare claims data. Finally, the study database is not nationally representative, and thus caution should be used when generalizing study results to other populations and settings.

Conclusions

Consistent with previously published studies, the cost of RSV-LRTI remains high, with the greatest economic burden carried by infants who are younger at time of infection and infants who were born prematurely. Timing of infection in relation to the RSV season and the COVID-19 pandemic did not appear to have an impact on RSV-LRTI attributable expenditures.
