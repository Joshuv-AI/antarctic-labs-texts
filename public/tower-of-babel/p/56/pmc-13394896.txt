Epithelial YPEL3 Modulates CD8+ T‐Cell Infiltration and Tumor Progression Through CREB1–CXCL16 Signaling in HNSCC
Authors: Yalun Li, Yumei Li, Caiyu Sun, Congxian Lu, Gang Chen, Tianshu Zhou, Bei Zhang, Xicheng Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTYippee‐like 3 (YPEL3) has been implicated in the regulation of apoptosis, proliferation, and invasion in various cancers, but its role in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) remains unclear. Here, we integrated pan‐cancer data, TCGA/GEO transcriptomes, DNA methylation profiles, and single‐cell RNA sequencing to explore YPEL3's expression, prognosis, and immune relevance in HNSCC. YPEL3 was downregulated in tumor tissues and correlated with better overall survival, particularly in advanced‐stage patients. Functional assays confirmed that YPEL3 suppressed proliferation, migration, and invasion while promoting apoptosis in vitro; its knockdown accelerated tumor growth in vivo. Bulk transcriptomics and TIMER analysis revealed that high YPEL3 expression was associated with increased stromal and immune infiltration, including CD8+ T cells. Single‐cell analysis identified an epithelial subcluster (Epi.C3) enriched for YPEL3, expanded in tumors, and linked to poor prognosis. Mechanistically, YPEL3‐high epithelial cells secreted CXCL16, interacting with CXCR6 on CD8+ T cells to promote immune engagement. YPEL3 regulated CXCL16 via CREB1 but not TFAP2C. Co‐culture assays showed that epithelial YPEL3 modulated the CXCL16–CXCR6 axis in T cells. In vivo, YPEL3 loss was associated with reduced CD8+ T‐cell infiltration and impaired CD8+ T‐cell effector function, as indicated by decreased Granzyme B, Perforin, IFN‐γ, and TNF‐α. Together, these findings identify YPEL3 as a tumor suppressor and an important mediator of epithelial–immune crosstalk in HNSCC, linking epithelial differentiation‐associated states to antitumor immunity through the CREB1/CXCL16/CXCR6 axis.

Introduction

Head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC), originating in the epithelial tissues of the upper aerodigestive tract, is ranked as the sixth most prevalent cancer globally [1]. The preeminent risk factors for HNSCC are tobacco use [2] and alcohol consumption [3]. However, recent years have seen a growing acknowledgment of HPV infection as a substantial contributory factor [4]. Despite a global decline in the overall incidence of HNSCC, the number of cases attributable to HPV is on the rise, particularly among younger populations in the United States and Europe [5, 6]. Although there have been significant enhancements in cancer treatment, such as the integration of cisplatin‐based chemotherapy with locoregional radiotherapy and the adoption of EGFR‐targeted antibody therapies [7], the 5‐year survival rate for HNSCC patients continues to be unsatisfactory. It has been suggested that tumors positive for HPV may be considered a distinct entity in HNSCC, with a prognosis that is generally more favorable, evidenced by higher overall survival, better disease‐free survival, and lower rates of local recurrence [8, 9, 10]. Currently, an incomplete understanding of the pathogenic processes involved in HNSCC hampers the enhancement of treatment outcomes. It is therefore imperative to significantly advance our knowledge of tumor initiation and to identify new biological markers that can optimize prognostic evaluations.

Yippee‐like 3 (YPEL3), originating from the Drosophila Yippee gene, forms part of the protein family characterized by a putative zinc finger motif, where there is notable conservation of cysteine and histidine [11]. YPEL3 exhibits highly dynamic subcellular localization changes during the cell cycle [12]: it is distributed in a salt‐and‐pepper pattern within the nucleus and an unidentified perinuclear structure during interphase, and it associates with centrosomes during mitosis. Initial studies identified YPEL3 as SUAP, a small unstable apoptotic protein that prompts IL3 removal in myeloid precursor cell lines [13]. Additionally, studies have established that under genotoxic stress, YPEL3 acts as an inducible target of the tumor suppressor protein P53 [14]. This expression is notably reduced in cells from breast [15], lung [16], colon [17], ovarian [13], and nasopharyngeal carcinomas [16]. Furthermore, arrested cell cycle and inhibited epithelial‐mesenchymal transition were also observed in cultured nasopharyngeal carcinoma cells after YPEL3 overexpression [16]. These findings from in vitro cell models and tumor samples indicate a tumor‐suppressive role for YPEL3. Although research into YPEL3 is comprehensive, its precise contributions and mechanisms to HNSCC are still largely undefined.

Recent advances in single‐cell transcriptomics have highlighted the heterogeneity of epithelial and immune compartments in HNSCC, as well as the importance of epithelial‐immune interactions in shaping tumor behavior and therapeutic response. Chemokine‐mediated communication, such as the CXCL16–CXCR6 axis, plays a critical role in T‐cell recruitment and function within the tumor microenvironment. However, whether YPEL3 contributes to such epithelial‐immune crosstalk has not been explored. Given the observed association between YPEL3 expression and immune infiltration in bulk transcriptomic analyses, we hypothesized that YPEL3 might influence tumor immunity by acting within specific epithelial subpopulations to regulate T‐cell interactions.

In this study, we systematically investigated the expression pattern, prognostic significance, and biological function of YPEL3 in HNSCC using integrated analyses of TCGA, GEO, and single‐cell RNA‐seq datasets, together with in vitro and in vivo validation. We examined the associations of YPEL3 with clinicopathological features, survival outcome, DNA methylation, and tumor immune infiltration, and further explored its cellular context in the HNSCC microenvironment. In particular, single‐cell transcriptomic analysis was used to identify YPEL3‐associated epithelial subpopulations and their potential interactions with CD8+ T cells. Functional assays were performed to determine the effects of YPEL3 on malignant phenotypes and apoptosis in HNSCC cells, whereas mechanistic analyses were conducted to clarify its role in regulating the CREB1/CXCL16 axis. Finally, xenograft and immune‐related assays were used to evaluate the impact of YPEL3 on tumor growth and antitumor immunity. These findings provide new insight into the role of YPEL3 in HNSCC progression and tumor‐immune communication.

Materials and Methods

Pan‐Cancer Analysis of YPEL3

YPEL3 expression in normal and tumor tissues was analyzed using data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) [18], the Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE) [19], and the Genotype‐Tissue Expression (GTEx) database [20] through the online platform HOME for Researchers. Translational expression and tissue distribution of YPEL3 were further examined using the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) database [21].

Single‐Cell RNA‐Seq re‐Analysis and Cell–Cell Communication

Publicly available HNSCC scRNA‐seq datasets (GSE181919) were processed in Seurat (v4). Cells with < 200 or > 6000 genes or > 15% mitochondrial transcripts were excluded. Clustering was performed using the top 30 PCs (resolution 0.5–1.0), and UMAP was used for visualization. Major lineages were annotated by canonical markers. Epithelial cells were re‐clustered into four subsets (Epi.C1‐C4). Differential expression was tested with Wilcoxon rank‐sum (adjusted p < 0.05), and YPEL3 expression was summarized by average scaled expression and percentage of positive cells. Subcluster proportions were compared across sample states using non‐parametric tests. Cell–cell communication was analyzed with CellChat, focusing on epithelial‐immune interactions. Significant ligand‐receptor pairs were inferred (p < 0.05), highlighting the CXCL16–CXCR6 axis from YPEL3‐high Epi.C3 cells to CD8+ T cells. For survival analysis, an Epi.C3 gene signature was derived from marker genes and applied to TCGA bulk RNA‐seq by ssGSEA. Patients were dichotomized by median score and analyzed by log‐rank test and Cox regression.

Analysis of Immune Infiltration

Through the TIMER interface (https://cistrome.shinyapps.io/timer/), a detailed and organized analysis of immune infiltrations across diverse cancer types is facilitated [22] Our preliminary analysis involved assessing the link between YPEL3 expression and tumor purity, as well as examining the infiltration by tumor‐associated immune cells, including B cells, CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, neutrophils, macrophages, and dendritic cells. Following this, the SCNA module was used to analyze the correlation between variations in gene copy number and the infiltration of immune cells within tumors.

Lentivirus‐Mediated Cell Transduction

AMC‐HN‐8 and Cal 27 cells were infected with lentiviral vectors carrying YPEL3 cDNA or shRNA (Shanghai GeneChem, Shanghai, China). The vector contained an enhanced green fluorescent protein cassette under the CMV promoter. Cells were infected at a multiplicity of infection of 10 according to the manufacturer's protocol. After 72 h, cells were selected with 2.5 μg/mL puromycin for 48 h and examined using an Olympus IX71 microscope.

Cell Co‐Culture Assay

To investigate the effect of epithelial YPEL3 expression on T‐cell function, Cal 27 and AMC‐HN‐8 cells transduced with YPEL3 overexpression vectors, shRNA, or corresponding controls were seeded in 6‐well plates (2 × 105 cells/well). After 24 h, human peripheral blood T cells were activated with anti‐CD3/CD28 Dynabeads (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and IL‐2 (100 U/mL) for 48 h, then added to epithelial cells at an effector‐to‐target ratio of 2:1. Cells were co‐cultured for 48 h in RPMI‐1640 containing 10% FBS and 50 U/mL IL‐2. T cells were then collected by gentle aspiration and passage through a 40‐μm cell strainer to remove epithelial cells. RNA was extracted from purified T cells using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen), and CXCR6 and CXCL16 mRNA levels were quantified by qRT‐PCR.

Construction of HNSCC Mouse Model

All animal experiments were approved by the Ethics Committee of Yantai Yuhuangding Hospital (Approval No. 2024793). For the immunodeficient model, 5‐week‐old male nude mice were housed under specific pathogen‐free conditions and acclimatized for 1 week. SCC7 cells (1 × 106) transduced with control shRNA (sh‐NC) or YPEL3‐targeting shRNA (sh‐YPEL3) were injected subcutaneously into the dorsal flank. Mice were randomly assigned to two groups (n = 5/group). Tumor volume and body weight were measured every 2 days, and tumor volume was calculated as (width)2 × length/2. Mice were euthanized on day 10, and tumors were excised, photographed, and weighed.

For the immunocompetent model, 5‐week‐old male C57BL/6 mice were subcutaneously inoculated with SCC7‐sh‐NC or SCC7‐sh‐YPEL3 cells (1 × 106 cells/mouse). Tumor growth and body weight were monitored every 2 days, and mice were euthanized on day 14. Tumors were harvested for further analyses: histologic, immunofluorescence, and flow cytometric analyses.

CD8 + T‐Cell Functional Assays

CD8+ T cells were co‐cultured with tumor cells under different YPEL3 modulation conditions (NC, sh‐YPEL3, and YPEL3 overexpression). Tumor cell apoptosis was assessed by Annexin V/7‐AAD staining followed by flow cytometry according to the manufacturer's instructions. To evaluate cytotoxic effector molecules, CD8+ T cells were collected after co‐culture, fixed, permeabilized, and stained intracellularly for Granzyme B and Perforin, followed by flow cytometric analysis. For cytokine analysis, intracellular IFN‐γ staining was performed after stimulation in the presence of a protein transport inhibitor. IFN‐γ production was also assessed by ELISPOT, and cytokine concentrations in culture supernatants, including IFN‐γ and TNF‐α, were measured using ELISA kits according to the manufacturers' protocols.

Results

Expression Profile of YPEL3 Across Multiple Cancer Types and HNSCC

Pan‐cancer analysis of TCGA data showed significant upregulation of YPEL3 in CHOL, KIRC, LIHC, and THCA (Figure 1A). Conversely, YPEL3 was downregulated in HNSC, KICH, LUSC, PRAD, and UCEC (Figure 1A). IHC data from the HPA database showed robust YPEL3 expression in normal tissues adjacent to tumors, whereas HNSCC tissues showed a significant reduction in YPEL3 expression (Figure 1B). Following bioinformatic analysis, we conducted IHC staining to further validate these observations. Consistently, our IHC staining showed weaker YPEL3 staining in HNSCC tissues than in adjacent normal tissues (Figure 1C), and quantitative IHC scoring confirmed this decrease (Figure 1D). In paired samples, relative YPEL3 expression was also lower in tumor tissues than in adjacent tissues (Figure 1E). These findings indicate that YPEL3 is downregulated in HNSCC.

Genetic and Epigenetic Profiling of YPEL3 Expression and Its Prognostic Significance in HNSCC

To investigate the genetic and epigenetic features associated with YPEL3 expression in HNSCC, we performed mutation and methylation analyses. Figure 2A,B show the mutation profiles of HNSCC cases with high and low YPEL3 expression, respectively, highlighting the most frequently mutated genes in each group. Notably, TP53, TTN, and FAT1 were among the most frequently mutated genes in both groups, although their mutation frequencies differed. In the high YPEL3 expression group (Figure 2A), TP53, TTN, and FAT1 were mutated in 60%, 38%, and 19% of cases, respectively. In the low YPEL3 expression group (Figure 2B), the corresponding frequencies were 70%, 35%, and 24%. Overall, the low‐YPEL3 group exhibited a more altered mutational landscape, suggesting a genomically more aggressive phenotype.

Genetic and epigenetic features of YPEL3 in HNSCC. (A, B) Somatic mutation landscapes of HNSCC samples with high (A) or low (B) YPEL3 expression. (C) Heatmap of DNA methylation levels at YPEL3‐associated CpG sites with clinicodemographic annotations. (D, E) Kaplan–Meier analyses of overall survival according to methylation status at cg16348385 (D) and cg27106909 (E).

DNA methylation patterns across multiple CpG sites associated with YPEL3 were analyzed using MethSurv. The heatmap revealed heterogeneous methylation patterns across six CpG sites together with clinicodemographic annotations (Figure 2C). Specific CpG sites, including cg12400121 and cg26709300, displayed distinct methylation levels across samples, while cg16348385 and cg27106909 showed relatively lower methylation in a subset of cases, supporting a potential inverse association between methylation and YPEL3 expression.

The prognostic impact of YPEL3 methylation at cg16348385 and cg27106909 was further analyzed (Figure 2D,E). Patients with higher methylation at either cg16348385 or cg27106909 showed significantly worse overall survival than those with lower methylation, as indicated by log‐rank p‐values of 0.0033 and 0.0042, respectively. The hazard ratios (HR > 1) further supported the association between higher methylation and poorer prognosis. These findings suggest that methylation status at specific YPEL3‐related CpG sites may serve as a prognostic indicator in HNSCC.

Prognostic Impact of YPEL3 Expression on Overall Survival in HNSCC Across Clinical Stages

Kaplan–Meier survival analysis of the TCGA cohort showed that patients with high YPEL3 expression had significantly improved overall survival compared with those with low YPEL3 expression (HR = 0.73, 95% CI: 0.56–0.95, p = 0.021; Figure 3A). After stratification by clinical stage, the survival advantage remained significant in patients with stage III–IV disease (HR = 0.67, 95% CI: 0.49–0.93, p = 0.015; Figure 3C), whereas no statistically significant difference was observed in stage I–II disease (HR = 0.60, 95% CI: 0.28–1.30, p = 0.192; Figure 3B). These results suggest that the prognostic relevance of YPEL3 may be more evident in advanced‐stage HNSCC.

(B, C) Kaplan–Meier analyses of overall survival in patients with stage I–II disease (B) and stage III–IV disease (C). (D, E) Forest plots of univariate (D) and multivariate (E) Cox regression analyses. (F) Nomogram integrating YPEL3 expression, pathologic T stage, pathologic N stage, and pathologic stage for prediction of 1‐, 3‐, and 5‐year overall survival. (G) Calibration curves of the nomogram.

To address the potential confounding effect of HPV status, we further analyzed TCGA‐HNSC cases with available HPV annotation. In the HPV‐annotated subset (n = 124), high YPEL3 expression showed a trend toward improved overall survival, but the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.22; Figure S1A). After further stratification by HPV status, no significant association between YPEL3 expression and overall survival was observed in either the HPV‐positive subgroup (n = 42, p = 0.35; Figure S1B) or the HPV‐negative subgroup (n = 81, p = 1.00; Figure S1C). Thus, the survival association was not retained after HPV stratification, possibly because of the limited subgroup size.

Univariate Cox regression analysis showed that pathologic stage, T stage, and N stage were significantly associated with overall survival, whereas YPEL3 showed a protective trend that did not reach statistical significance in the forest plot shown here (HR = 0.852, 95% CI: 0.667–1.088, p = 0.200; Figure 3D). In multivariate Cox regression, N stage remained an independent prognostic factor (HR = 1.432, 95% CI: 1.152–1.780, p = 0.001), whereas YPEL3 did not retain statistical significance (HR = 0.806, 95% CI: 0.619–1.050, p = 0.111; Figure 3E). Therefore, YPEL3 appears to be associated with favorable survival, but its independent prognostic value requires further validation. An exploratory nomogram integrating YPEL3 expression with pathologic T stage, N stage, and overall stage was constructed to estimate the 1‐, 3‐, and 5‐year overall survival probabilities of HNSCC patients (Figure 3F). Calibration curves showed acceptable agreement between predicted and observed survival probabilities at 1, 3, and 5 years (Figure 3G), supporting the potential utility of this model for risk stratification.

Association of YPEL3 Expression With Immune Cell Infiltration in the HNSCC Tumor Microenvironment

The association between YPEL3 expression and various immune microenvironment scores, including StromalScore [23], ImmuneScore [24], and ESTIMATEScore [25], was analyzed. Patients with high YPEL3 expression showed significantly higher ImmuneScore and ESTIMATEScore, whereas StromalScore did not differ significantly from that in the low‐YPEL3 group (Figure 4A), indicating a more immune‐enriched tumor microenvironment. YPEL3 expression was positively associated with multiple immune cell types, including NK CD56bright cells, pDCs, B cells, cytotoxic cells, CD8 T cells, and T cells, whereas negative correlations were observed for neutrophils, Th2 cells, and Tgd cells. These findings were supported by enrichment analysis showing that the high‐YPEL3 group showed higher enrichment scores for several immune cell subsets, including CD8 T cells, cytotoxic cells, B cells, NK‐related subsets, pDCs, and T cells, consistent with a more active antitumor immune contexture (Figure 4C and Table S1). Spearman correlation analysis confirmed significant associations between YPEL3 expression and the enrichment of NK cells, pDCs, CD8+ T cells, cytotoxic cells, B cells, and T cells (Figure 4D). Scatter plots showed positive correlations of YPEL3 expression with CD8 T cells (Figure 4D), NK cells (Figure 4E), and cytotoxic cells (Figure 4F).

Given the known impact of HPV on the HNSCC immune microenvironment, HPV‐stratified analyses were performed. As shown in Figure S2, HPV‐positive tumors exhibited significantly higher CD8+ T‐cell infiltration scores than HPV‐negative tumors (p = 2.5 × 10−6), consistent with the immune‐active phenotype of HPV‐associated HNSCC. YPEL3 expression was also significantly higher in HPV‐positive cases (p = 0.00014), whereas CXCL16 expression did not differ significantly between the two groups (p = 0.32). Multiplex immunofluorescence (mIF) staining further assessed the clinical relevance of YPEL3 during HNSCC progression. In early‐stage laryngeal squamous cell carcinoma (Stage I/II), YPEL3 expression was relatively high and accompanied by abundant CD8+ T‐cell infiltration. In contrast, advanced‐stage hypopharyngeal cancer (Stage IVA/IVB) exhibited reduced YPEL3 expression with decreased CD8+ T‐cell density (Figure 4G). Matched primary tumors and metastatic lymph node lesions revealed that metastatic sites displayed lower YPEL3 and CXCL16 expression, accompanied by reduced CD8+ T‐cell infiltration. Together, these findings suggest that high YPEL3 expression is associated with increased immune cell infiltration in the HNSCC microenvironment.

Single‐Cell Transcriptomic Profiling Reveals a YPEL3‐High Epithelial Subcluster (Epi.C3) That Communicates With CD8 + T Cells

To further dissect the cellular context underlying the association between YPEL3 and immune infiltration observed in bulk analyses, we performed single‐cell transcriptomic profiling of HNSCC samples from GSE181919. UMAP clustering identified major cell types, including epithelial cells, T/NK cells, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, myeloid cells, plasma cells, smooth muscle cells, mast cells, and B cells (Figure 5A,B). Examination of YPEL3 expression across these lineages revealed predominant expression in epithelial cells (Figure 5C). Subclustering of the epithelial compartment identified four epithelial subsets (Epi.C1‐C4) (Figure 5D). Analysis of cellular composition showed that the proportion of Epi.C3 was highest in adjacent non‐tumor tissues and progressively decreased across lesion progression and lymph node metastasis (NL → LP → CA → LN; Figure 5E), suggesting loss of this epithelial state during HNSCC progression. Pathway enrichment analysis indicated that Epi.C3 was associated with epithelial differentiation‐ and adhesion‐related programs, including focal adhesion, ECM‐receptor interaction, adherens junction, tight junction, and cornified envelope pathways (Figure 5F). Survival analysis demonstrated that patients with high infiltration of the Epi.C3 population had significantly better overall survival than those with low infiltration (Figure 5G), indicating that Epi.C3 represents a clinically favorable epithelial state. Potency and pseudotime analyses supported transcriptional continuity among epithelial subsets (Figure 5H,I), providing a developmental framework for subsequent characterization of this population.

We next sought to define the molecular identity of Epi.C3. YPEL3 expression was significantly enriched in Epi.C3 relative to the other epithelial subsets (Figure 6A) and increased along pseudotime (Figure 6B). Notably, CDH1 was also preferentially expressed in Epi.C3 (Figure 6C) and exhibited a similar increasing trend along pseudotime (Figure 6D). Therefore, Epi.C3 is not merely as a “YPEL3‐high” cluster, but as a differentiated epithelial subpopulation characterized by a keratin‐rich epithelial program, relatively high EPCAM and CDH1 expression, low expression of EMT‐associated markers such as VIM and SNAI2, and selective enrichment of YPEL3 and CXCL16 (Figure S3A–F). These findings indicate that Epi.C3 represents a differentiated, low‐EMT epithelial state, supporting its biological relevance during tumor evolution.

YPEL3 Inhibits HNSCC Cell Growth and Promotes Apoptosis

To investigate the role of YPEL3 in HNSCC cells, we performed gain‐ and loss‐of‐function assays. Targeted modulation of YPEL3 expression through YPEL3‐specific shRNA or an overexpression plasmid led to a marked decrease or increase in YPEL3 levels in AMC‐HN‐8 and Cal‐27 cells at the transcriptional (Figure 7A,B) and translational (Figure 7C) levels. Growth analysis using the CCK‐8 assay showed a marked increase or decrease in proliferation of AMC‐HN‐8 and Cal‐27 cells (Figure 7D). Consistent results were obtained from the colony formation assay (Figure 7E). These results support a growth‐suppressive role for YPEL3 in HNSCC cells.

YPEL3 inhibits HNSCC cell growth and promotes apoptosis. (A, B) qRT‐PCR analysis of YPEL3 mRNA expression in AMC‐HN‐8 cells (A) and Cal 27 cells (B) after YPEL3 knockdown or overexpression. (C) W Western blot analysis of YPEL3 protein in AMC‐HN‐8 and Cal27 cells. (D) CCK‐8 assays of cell viability in AMC‐HN‐8 and Cal27 cells. (E) Colony formation assays. (F) Western blot analysis of Caspase‐3 and Caspase‐9 after YPEL3 knockdown or overexpression. (G) Flow cytometric analysis of apoptosis by Annexin V/7‐AAD staining. Statistical significance is indicated as ***p < 0.001.

Next, apoptosis‐related proteins and apoptotic cell death were examined. Western blot analysis revealed that YPEL3 knockdown led to decreased expression of Caspase‐3 and Caspase‐9 in both AMC‐HN‐8 and Cal‐27 cells, whereas YPEL3 overexpression elevated their expression (Figure 7F). This was supported by flow cytometric analysis using Annexin V/7‐AAD staining, which demonstrated a significantly increased proportion of apoptotic cells upon YPEL3 overexpression and a reduced apoptotic population following YPEL3 knockdown (Figure 7G). These results suggest that YPEL3 promotes apoptosis in HNSCC cells, supporting its tumor‐suppressive role.

YPEL3 Suppresses Migration, Invasion, Adhesion, and EMT‐Associated Changes in HNSCC Cells

We assessed the migratory, adhesive, and invasive abilities of AMC‐HN‐8 and Cal 27 HNSCC cells with YPEL3 silencing or overexpression. Compared with their respective control groups, YPEL3 silencing significantly enhanced migration, invasion, and adhesion in AMC‐HN‐8 cells (Figure 8A–D) and Cal‐27 cells (Figure 8E–H), whereas YPEL3 overexpression exerted the opposite effects in both cell lines. Taken together, these findings indicate that YPEL3 acts as a suppressor of the migratory, invasive, and adhesive phenotypes of HNSCC cells.

To further investigate whether these phenotypic changes were associated with EMT‐related alterations. The expression of Slug, E‐cadherin, Vimentin, and Snail were examined by western blotting. YPEL3 knockdown increased Slug and Snail expression while reducing E‐cadherin levels in both AMC‐HN‐8 and Cal‐27 cells, whereas YPEL3 overexpression decreased Slug and Snail and increased E‐cadherin expression (Figure 8I). Changes in Vimentin were comparatively less concordant across the different conditions (Figure 8I). Overall, these data suggest that YPEL3 restrains the aggressive phenotype of HNSCC cells and is associated with a more epithelial, less invasive cellular state.

YPEL3 Regulates CXCL16 Through CREB1 and Modulates the CXCL16–CXCR6 Axis

To investigate how YPEL3 regulates CXCL16, we examined CXCL16 levels in HNSCC cells after modulation of YPEL3. qRT‐PCR showed that CXCL16 mRNA levels were significantly decreased after YPEL3 knockdown and increased after YPEL3 overexpression in both Cal27 and AMC‐HN‐8 cells (Figure 9A). Western blot analysis confirmed corresponding changes at the protein level (Figure 9B). YPEL3 overexpression increased, whereas YPEL3 silencing reduced, CXCL16 expression in both cell lines (Figure 9C). To identify candidate transcription factors involved in this regulation, we integrated transcription factor prediction with co‐IP‐associated candidates and identified CREB1 and TFAP2C as shared regulators of CXCL16 (Figure 9D). Functional validation showed that overexpression of either CREB1 or TFAP2C increased CXCL16 mRNA expression, although the effect of CREB1 was stronger and more consistent, particularly in Cal27 cells (Figure 9E). Moreover, dual‐luciferase reporter assays showed that CREB1 significantly enhanced the luciferase activity driven by the CXCL16 promoter, supporting that CREB1 positively regulates CXCL16 transcription (Figure S4). Consistent with this, modulation of YPEL3 expression produced a clear and consistent effect on CREB1 mRNA levels in both Cal27 and AMC‐HN‐8 cells (Figure 9F), whereas the changes in TFAP2C were comparatively less consistent between the two cell lines (Figure 9G). At the protein level, western blot analysis and quantitative densitometry showed that both CREB1 and phosphorylated CREB1Ser133 were upregulated by YPEL3 overexpression and downregulated by YPEL3 knockdown in both cell lines (Figure 9H–J). Together, these findings indicate that CREB1, rather than TFAP2C, is the principal transcription factor linking YPEL3 to CXCL16 regulation.

Given the observed epithelial‐immune interactions in single‐cell analysis, we next tested whether epithelial YPEL3 affected T‐cell signaling through the CXCL16–CXCR6 axis. Co‐culture assays revealed that T cells cultured with YPEL3‐overexpressing Cal 27 or AMC‐HN‐8 cells exhibited increased CXCR6 expression, whereas co‐culture with YPEL3‐silenced cells significantly reduced CXCR6 expression (Figure 9K). In parallel, CXCL16 expression in T cells followed a similar trend, being elevated with YPEL3 overexpression and decreased with YPEL3 knockdown (Figure 9L). Multiplex immunofluorescence of matched human primary tumors and metastatic lymph node lesions showed that metastatic lesions tended to display lower YPEL3 and CXCL16 expression together with reduced CD8+ T‐cell infiltration, compared with the matched primary tumors (Figure S5). Collectively, these results indicate that YPEL3 regulates CXCL16 expression through CREB1, and that YPEL3 status in epithelial cells modulates the CXCL16–CXCR6 axis in T cells, thereby suggesting that YPEL3 influences epithelial–immune communication in HNSCC.

YPEL3 Suppresses Tumor Growth and Enhances CD8 + T‐Cell Infiltration and Function

To evaluate the functional role of YPEL3 in vivo, we established two xenograft tumor models using SCC‐7 cells with stable knockdown of YPEL3. In the immunodeficient model, YPEL3 knockdown markedly promoted tumor growth, as evidenced by increased tumor burden and accelerated tumor volume expansion compared with the control group (Figure 10A,B). In the immunocompetent model, similar results were observed, with YPEL3 knockdown significantly enhancing tumor growth relative to controls (Figure 10C,D). These findings indicate that YPEL3 exerts a tumor‐suppressive effect in vivo under both immune‐deficient and immune‐intact conditions. We next examined whether YPEL3 is associated with CD8+ T cells; we next examined whether YPEL3 influences intratumoral CD8+ T‐cell infiltration and function. Immunofluorescence staining showed that CD8+ T‐cell infiltration was reduced in YPEL3‐silenced tumors compared with control tumors (Figure 10E). In addition, flow cytometric analysis demonstrated that YPEL3 knockdown decreased, whereas YPEL3 overexpression increased, the proportions of Granzyme B+ and Perforin+ CD8+ T cells (Figure 10F), indicating corresponding changes in cytotoxic effector function. Consistently, ELISPOT assays revealed that YPEL3 overexpression enhanced, whereas YPEL3 knockdown reduced, the number of IFN‐γ‐secreting immune cells (Figure 10G). ELISA further showed that IFN‐γ and TNF‐α levels were significantly decreased in the YPEL3‐knockdown group but increased in the YPEL3‐overexpression group (Figure 10H,I). Moreover, Annexin V/7‐AAD‐based apoptosis assays showed that CD8+ T cells co‐cultured with YPEL3‐overexpressing tumor cells induced more tumor cell apoptosis, whereas YPEL3 knockdown attenuated this effect (Figure S6). Together, these data indicate that YPEL3 not only restrains tumor growth in vivo but also promotes CD8+ T‐cell infiltration and enhances their cytotoxic and cytokine‐producing capacities, thereby strengthening antitumor immunity.

Discussion

HNSCC remains a major global health burden, and its treatment relies on surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy [26, 27]. Identifying molecules that influence both tumor progression and the immune microenvironment is therefore of considerable clinical importance. In this study, we found that YPEL3 was dysregulated across multiple cancers and significantly downregulated in HNSCC. Higher YPEL3 expression was associated with better overall and progression‐free survival, indicating its potential prognostic value. Moreover, YPEL3 expression was positively correlated with immune infiltration, especially CD8+ T cells and other effector immune populations. Single‐cell analysis further showed that a YPEL3‐enriched epithelial subcluster was linked to favorable prognosis and communicated with CD8+ T cells via the CXCL16–CXCR6 axis, suggesting that YPEL3 may participate in epithelial‐immune crosstalk within the HNSCC microenvironment.

YPEL3 is an evolutionarily conserved member of the YPEL family and is widely considered a tumor suppressor in cancer. Its downregulation in multiple malignancies has been linked to enhanced proliferation, motility, and impaired apoptosis [28]. Our functional data further support a tumor‐suppressive role for YPEL3 in HNSCC. Loss of YPEL3 promoted malignant phenotypes, including enhanced proliferation, migration, invasion, and adhesion, whereas restoration of YPEL3 exerted the opposite effects and increased apoptosis. These findings are in line with previous studies describing YPEL3 as a suppressive factor in cancer progression. Beyond its effects on tumor cell behavior, we also observed an association between YPEL3 expression and tumor mutational burden in HNSCC, a feature that has been linked to immunotherapy response and clinical outcome. Together, these results suggest that YPEL3 may be relevant not only to tumor progression but also to the broader biological context that shapes therapeutic responsiveness.

Currently, immunotherapy is a topic of significant interest and frequent discussion in oncology. Critical determinants of immunotherapy response, including the presence of tumor‐infiltrating lymphocytes, assessment of tumor mutational burden, and evaluation of microsatellite instability status, are essential for predicting clinical outcomes in cancer treatment [29]. The results of our study show a consistent link between YPEL3 and the infiltration of six diverse immune cell populations (NK cells, pDCs, T cells, CD8+ T cells, cytotoxic cells, and B cells) in HNSCC. In line with these bulk analyses, our single‐cell investigation provided mechanistic insights by identifying a YPEL3‐enriched epithelial subcluster (Epi.C3) that expands in normal tissues, gradually decreases in proportion with tumor progression, and whose lower infiltration levels are associated with poorer patient prognosis. Notably, Epi.C3 cells exhibited strong communication with CD8+ T cells through the CXCL16–CXCR6 axis, suggesting that YPEL3 contributes to epithelial‐immune crosstalk within the tumor microenvironment. This finding helps to explain the positive association between YPEL3 expression and immune infiltration observed at the transcriptome level, while also revealing that specific epithelial populations may shape the quality of immune responses in HNSCC. Furthermore, the reduction of CD8+ T cell infiltration upon YPEL3 knockdown in vivo supports this model, highlighting the potential of YPEL3 as both a tumor suppressor and a regulator of anti‐tumor immunity.

In addition, identifying specific genetic mutations can aid in predicting the prognosis and treatment outcomes for patients. Studies have shown that a higher somatic tumor mutational burden in HNSCC is associated with increased immunotherapy efficacy and favorable overall survival [30, 31]. We have documented a correlation between YPEL3 expression and tumor mutational burden in HNSCC. This comprehensive evidence supports the potential function of YPEL3 in reducing tumor proliferation by affecting TMB. High TMB status in tumors is associated with pronounced immune cell infiltration, rendering them more responsive to immune checkpoint inhibitor therapies. Further functional assays substantiate the role of YPEL3 deficiency in enhancing HNSCC cell growth and influencing key cellular processes such as migration, adhesion, and invasion. Our data, together with previous reports, suggest that YPEL3 downregulation is likely a multifactorial event rather than a passive consequence of tumor progression. The YPEL3‐low subgroup showed more frequent TP53 mutation, consistent with YPEL3 being a p53‐responsive gene. In addition, hypermethylation of CpG sites in the 5′UTR and first exon was associated with poor prognosis, implicating epigenetic silencing in YPEL3 loss. Prior studies also suggest that EMT‐related transcriptional and post‐transcriptional mechanisms may repress YPEL3 expression. Together, these findings support a multilayered regulatory model for YPEL3 downregulation, although the causal relationships remain to be experimentally validated.

Our subsequent mechanistic experiments further elucidated how YPEL3 regulates immune‐related signaling in HNSCC. By integrating transcription factor prediction with functional validation, we found that YPEL3 modulates CXCL16 expression primarily through CREB1, while TFAP2C did not respond to YPEL3 alteration, suggesting that CREB1 serves as the critical transcriptional mediator. Although CREB1 is a well‐known transcription factor, its role in directly regulating CXCL16 has not been well characterized. In this study, we provide functional evidence supporting a CREB1 → CXCL16 regulatory relationship, which may contribute to a better understanding of CXCL16 regulation in this context. Importantly, co‐culture assays demonstrated that modulation of YPEL3 in epithelial cells significantly influenced CXCL16 and CXCR6 expression in T cells, confirming that epithelial YPEL3 status can directly shape T‐cell signaling. These findings complement our single‐cell results, which identified a YPEL3‐enriched epithelial subcluster (Epi.C3) that engages CD8+ T cells via the CXCL16–CXCR6 axis, and are consistent with our in vivo observations showing reduced CD8+ T‐cell infiltration upon YPEL3 depletion. Together, these results provide a coherent mechanistic framework in which YPEL3 functions not only as a tumor suppressor but also as an active regulator of epithelial–immune crosstalk through the CREB1–CXCL16–CXCR6 pathway. This dual role highlights YPEL3 as a promising biomarker and potential therapeutic target, particularly in the context of immunotherapy for HNSCC.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, functional blocking experiments were not performed to directly establish the causal requirement of the YPEL3‐CXCL16 axis in CD8+ T‐cell infiltration. Second, although our data implicate CREB1 in YPEL3‐dependent CXCL16 regulation, direct evidence of CREB1 binding to the CXCL16 promoter is still lacking. Third, T‐cell functional characterization was not exhaustive, as exhaustion and proliferation analyses were not included. Finally, further in vivo studies are needed to confirm the immunological and therapeutic relevance of the YPEL3‐CXCL16 axis in the tumor microenvironment.

In summary, YPEL3 is downregulated in HNSCC and its higher expression is associated with better survival. Functionally, YPEL3 suppresses malignant phenotypes, promotes apoptosis, and is enriched in an epithelial subpopulation associated with favorable prognosis. Mechanistically, YPEL3 appears to enhance epithelial‐immune crosstalk through the CREB1‐CXCL16‐CXCR6 axis, and in vivo it is associated with reduced tumor growth and increased CD8+ T‐cell infiltration (Figure 11). These findings identify YPEL3 as a tumor‐suppressive and immune‐relevant regulator in HNSCC, with potential value for prognostic assessment and immunotherapeutic targeting.
