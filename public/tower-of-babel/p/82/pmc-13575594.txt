Institutional Learning Curve in Esophagectomy: Technical Standardization of Gastric Conduit Formation and Conduit‐Related Outcomes in 187 Consecutive Patients
Authors: Mayank Tripathi, Reshma R. Balachandran, Kumar Vineet, Dhaval Vadodaria, Vaibhav Kushwaha, Mohd Irfan Ansari, Piyush Shukla
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundLearning curves in esophagectomy are often described in terms of procedural volume, but institutional maturation also reflects progressive standardization of reconstruction, operative choreography, and team‐based decision‐making. Gastric conduit viability remains central to safe esophageal reconstruction, with conduit ischemia, torsion, and tension contributing substantially to leak and necrosis. For descriptive temporal comparisons, the cohort was divided into two pragmatic phases corresponding to institutional practice before and after routine formalization of the conduit protocol: up to 2022 (n = 101) and 2023–2025 (n = 86). During program maturation, a standardized gastric conduit protocol was formalized, emphasizing preservation of conduit vascularity, controlled conduit geometry, minimal omental bulk, cervical‐first dissection, torsion‐free transposition, and selective pyloric management. The primary outcome was composite major conduit‐related morbidity, defined per patient as major anastomotic leak, major conduit necrosis, or conduit‐related re‐exploration. Secondary outcomes included overall leak, vocal cord palsy, chyle leak, Clavien‐Dindo grade, 30‐day mortality, R0 resection, and lymph node yield. Outcomes were interpreted within the broader context of institutional maturation rather than as a simple calendar‐era comparison.ResultsComposite major conduit‐related morbidity declined from 8.9% in the earlier phase of the program to 2.3% in the later phase. Within this composite, major conduit necrosis declined from 5% to 0%, while overall leak rates remained stable. Thirty‐day mortality was 4.3%, and most complications were minor. Oncologic adequacy was preserved, with R0 resection in 94.7% and a median lymph node yield of 25. Improvements in pathological complete response and recurrence over time paralleled broader changes in neoadjuvant treatment intensity and lymphadenectomy and should not be attributed solely to conduit protocolization.ConclusionsInstitutional maturation in esophagectomy was associated with transition from experience‐dependent practice to reproducible technical standardization of gastric conduit formation. This learning curve coincided with disappearance of major conduit necrosis without compromising oncologic adequacy. Standardization of conduit construction may represent an important and exportable quality‐improvement step in developing high‐volume esophageal cancer programs, especially in resource‐variable settings.

Introduction

Esophagectomy remains one of the most demanding operations in oncologic surgery, requiring a balance between radical resection and safe reconstruction. While the oncologic component of the operation is critical, postoperative outcomes are often determined just as much by the quality of the gastric conduit and the integrity of the esophagogastric anastomosis. Conduit ischemia, torsion, excessive bulk, poor geometry, and anastomotic tension remain major drivers of leak, necrosis, and re‐exploration after esophagectomy. Consequently, conduit construction is not merely a reconstructive step but a core determinant of surgical quality.

Reports on esophagectomy learning curves have traditionally focused on case volume, operative duration, and perioperative outcomes. However, institutional maturation involves more than accumulation of numbers. It reflects a transition from variable, experience‐dependent practice toward reproducible, team‐based execution, with increasing consistency in operative choreography, tissue handling, conduit design, and perioperative judgment. In that sense, the true learning curve in esophagectomy is as much technical and organizational as it is numerical.

In our program, this maturation culminated in the formalization of a standardized gastric conduit formation strategy. The protocol emphasized preservation of the right gastroepiploic arcade and right gastric arterial contribution, avoidance of pedicle skeletonization, construction of a geometry‐controlled conduit of uniform width, minimal omental bulk, cervical‐first esophageal mobilization, tape‐guided torsion‐free transposition, and a selective rather than routine pyloric intervention strategy. These steps were not conceived as isolated technical preferences, but as a structured attempt to reduce variability in conduit construction and make safe reconstruction reproducible across the unit.

A parsimonious predictive model for pathological complete response from this same cohort has been reported previously [1]. The present study addresses a different question: how a maturing high‐volume esophagectomy program translated accumulated experience into technical standardization, and whether that transition was associated with improved conduit‐related outcomes.

Importantly, several aspects of treatment evolved contemporaneously within the program, including increased use of neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy and greater adoption of extended lymphadenectomy. For this reason, we deliberately frame the present analysis not as a simplistic era comparison, but as an examination of institutional learning and technical standardization within a real‐world, evolving esophageal cancer practice. The principal question was not whether later years were better than earlier years in general, but whether maturation of operative technique—particularly gastric conduit construction—was associated with safer reconstruction without sacrifice of oncologic adequacy.

We therefore analyzed 187 consecutive esophagectomies performed between 2019 and 2025 to characterize the institutional learning curve, describe the standardized gastric conduit technique, and evaluate its association with conduit‐related morbidity while assessing whether core oncologic quality metrics such as R0 resection and lymph node yield were preserved.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

We conducted a retrospective cohort study of 187 consecutive patients who underwent esophagectomy for locally advanced esophageal cancer at a high‐volume tertiary cancer center in India between 2019 and 2025. The study was designed to examine the institutional learning curve in esophagectomy, with particular emphasis on the progressive standardization of gastric conduit formation and its association with conduit‐related morbidity. Data were derived from a prospectively maintained institutional esophageal cancer database.

Patients

All patients who underwent esophagectomy after neoadjuvant therapy during the study period were included. The cohort represented a real‐world mix of histologies, tumor locations, surgical procedures, and perioperative pathways encountered in a developing high‐volume esophageal cancer program. Patients found to be inoperable at exploration were retained in descriptive analyses where relevant, but conduit‐related outcome analyses focused on patients in whom reconstruction was undertaken. Clinical, operative, pathological, and follow‐up variables were extracted from the institutional dataset.

Definition of Institutional Phases

For descriptive temporal comparisons, the cohort was divided into two pragmatic phases corresponding to institutional practice before and after routine formalization of the conduit protocol: up to 2022 (n = 101) and 2023–2025 (n = 86). These phases were used as practical summaries of institutional maturation, while recognizing that the learning curve was continuous rather than strictly binary.

Concept of Institutional Learning Curve

For the purposes of this study, the institutional learning curve was defined not merely by increasing case volume, but by the transition from variable, experience‐dependent operative practice to a more reproducible and protocolized approach to conduit construction and reconstruction. This maturation included refinement in tissue handling, preservation of conduit vascularity, control of conduit geometry, reduction in technical variability during transposition, and greater consistency in team‐based operative choreography.

A formalized conduit‐construction protocol was adopted during the later phase of the program and served as one visible marker of this maturation process. However, the study does not assume that all temporal changes were caused by protocolization alone; rather, protocolization is interpreted as one component of a broader institutional learning process.

Technical Standardization of Gastric Conduit Formation

1. Vascular preservation: Maintenance of the right gastroepiploic arcade with avoidance of excessive pedicle skeletonization, along with preservation of the Right gastric artery whenever oncologically and technically feasible, or at minimum preservation of its descending branch coursing toward the pyloric antrum, with the aim of protecting distal antral inflow and the submucosal vascular plexus.2. Minimal omental bulk: Creation of a slim omental cuff along the greater curvature to facilitate transposition and reduce compression within the posterior mediastinum and thoracic inlet.3. Controlled conduit geometry: Construction of a tubular gastric conduit of approximately 5–6 cm in width using linear staplers along a pre‐marked line, followed by oversewing of the staple line where appropriate.4. Selective pyloric strategy: Routine pyloromyotomy was avoided; pyloric dilatation was used selectively [2].5. Cervical‐first sequencing: Cervical esophageal mobilization and transection were performed first to improve orientation and avoid traction‐related difficulty during neck dissection.6. Torsion‐free transposition: A tape‐guided push‐pull technique was used to ensure correct conduit orientation and avoid twist during posterior mediastinal passage.7. Tailored anastomotic construction: Cervical esophagogastric anastomosis was performed using stapled or hand‐sewn technique depending on conduit reach, stump length, and tissue condition (Table 1).

Variables Collected

The following variables were analyzed: age, sex, BMI, ASA, ECOG performance status, tumor location and histology, neoadjuvant therapy type, operative approach and procedure performed, extent and field of lymphadenectomy, pathological response, margin status, lymphovascular invasion, perineural invasion, nodal status, lymph node yield, postoperative complications, recurrence, and follow‐up outcomes.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was major conduit‐related morbidity, defined as the occurrence of any of the following on a per‐patient basis: major anastomotic leak, major conduit necrosis, or conduit‐related re‐exploration [3].

Secondary outcomes included any anastomotic leak, any conduit necrosis, vocal cord palsy, chyle leak, delayed anastomotic stricture, overall postoperative morbidity graded by Clavien–Dindo classification, 30‐day mortality, and oncologic adequacy metrics including R0 resection rate and lymph node yield.

Statistical Analysis

Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables as median with interquartile range or range, as appropriate. Comparisons across phases of institutional maturation were performed using the chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables and non‐parametric methods for continuous variables when indicated. Temporal grouping was used pragmatically to reflect phases of program development and technical standardization, but findings were interpreted in the context of a broader learning curve rather than as a pure era‐based comparison. Statistical significance was defined as a two‐sided p value < 0.05.

Results

Cohort Characteristics and Operative Profile

A total of 187 consecutive patients underwent esophagectomy during the study period. The cohort was evenly distributed by age, with 94 patients (50.3%) younger than 60 years and 93 (49.7%) aged 60 years or older. There was a male predominance (62.6%). Most patients had normal BMI (57.2%), while 26.7% were underweight. The majority were ASA class II (71.1%) and had ECOG performance status 1 (89.8%). Tumors were predominantly located in the lower esophagus (63.1%), and squamous cell carcinoma was the commonest histology (67.4%), followed by adenocarcinoma (31%) (Table 2). Most patients received neoadjuvant chemotherapy rather than chemoradiotherapy (86.1% vs. 13.9%). Transthoracic esophagectomy was the most frequently performed procedure. The cohort reflected a predominantly open esophagectomy practice, with selective use of minimally invasive approaches during program maturation. Standard lymphadenectomy was performed in 64.2% of patients, and the median lymph node yield was 25 (IQR 18–35; range 2–89). R0 resection was achieved in 94.7%, and pathological complete response was observed in 27.3%.

Program Maturation and Evolving Practice

As the program matured, treatment and operative patterns also evolved. Use of neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy increased from 2% in the earlier phase to 28% in the later phase (p < 0.001), while extended lymphadenectomy increased from 25% to 43% (p = 0.006). Procedure mix also shifted over time, although the balance between open and minimally invasive surgery remained broadly similar. These changes provide important context and indicate that later outcomes should be interpreted within a broader process of institutional development rather than as isolated era effects [4].

Conduit‐Related Morbidity

Across the entire cohort, anastomotic leak occurred in 21 of 187 patients (11.2%), including 14 minor leaks (7.5%) and 7 major leaks (3.7%) [5]. Conduit necrosis occurred in 6 patients (3.2%), comprising 1 minor event (0.5%) and 5 major events (2.7%). Vocal cord palsy occurred in 12 patients (6.4%), chyle leak in 6 (3.2%), delayed anastomotic stricture in 5 (2.7%), and re‐exploration in 5 (2.7%). Thirty‐day mortality was 4.3%. Most complications were minor, with Clavien–Dindo grade I events accounting for 82.4% of the cohort. The clearest signal across the learning curve was seen in major conduit necrosis, which declined from 5% in the earlier phase to 0% after technical standardization (p = 0.06, trend). In contrast, the overall distribution of anastomotic leak remained stable (p = 0.51). Vocal cord palsy was similar between phases (7% vs. 6%; p = 0.50), and the increase in chyle leak from 1% to 6% did not reach significance (p = 0.10). Thirty‐day mortality decreased numerically from 6% to 2%, without statistical significance (Table 3). The CUSUM plot for the composite endpoint of major leak, major conduit necrosis, and conduit‐related re‐exploration showed an early upward accumulation of adverse events followed by clear flattening in the later phase of the series, consistent with progressive institutional stabilization and improved control of major conduit‐related morbidity [6].

Oncologic Adequacy

Oncologic quality was preserved throughout the learning curve. R0 resection was achieved in 177 of 187 patients (94.7%), and median lymph node yield remained 25. Pathological complete response increased from 16% to 43% (p < 0.001), while recurrence decreased from 33% to 14% (p = 0.003) across phases of program maturation. These favorable oncologic signals paralleled broader shifts in neoadjuvant treatment intensity and lymphadenectomy and should therefore be interpreted as markers of overall institutional evolution rather than direct effects of conduit standardization alone (Table 4).

Summary of the Learning‐Curve Signal

Viewed as a whole, the cohort demonstrates a transition from early institutional experience to a more mature and standardized esophagectomy practice. This transition was characterized by stable leak rates, elimination of major conduit necrosis, low mortality, and preserved oncologic adequacy. These findings support the concept that the institutional learning curve in esophagectomy is expressed not only through accumulated case volume, but also through conversion of operative experience into reproducible technical behavior.

Discussion

The principal finding of this study is that institutional maturation in esophagectomy was associated with a transition from experience‐dependent reconstruction to a more reproducible and standardized conduit‐formation strategy, and that this process coincided with disappearance of major conduit necrosis without compromise in oncologic adequacy. Across 187 consecutive patients, leak rates remained acceptable, mortality was low, and both R0 resection and lymph node yield were maintained. The most meaningful technical signal was therefore not a universal reduction in all complications, but disappearance of the most serious conduit‐related failure event.

Learning curves in esophagectomy are commonly described in terms of case numbers or operative duration, yet the maturation of an esophageal program is broader and more subtle. It includes refinement of tissue handling, standardization of operative choreography, reduction in technical variability, and more consistent decision‐making during conduit construction and transposition. In our unit, this evolution ultimately became codified into a structured conduit protocol emphasizing vascular preservation, geometry control, minimal omental bulk, careful orientation, and selective pyloric intervention. The novelty of this work lies less in the generic concept that standardization improves outcomes, and more in the description of a specific, low‐cost, anatomy‐based, teachable conduit strategy that appears particularly relevant to resource‐variable settings.

Among conduit‐related outcomes, the reduction of major conduit necrosis is probably the clearest marker of technical maturation. Anastomotic leak is multifactorial and influenced by perfusion, tension, tissue quality, prior therapy, nutritional status, and local healing [5]. Conduit necrosis, by contrast, more directly reflects conduit viability and is therefore a cleaner surrogate for the quality of conduit construction and transposition [7, 8]. The decline in major necrosis from 5% to 0%, despite stable overall leak rates, is therefore biologically plausible and consistent with the idea that technical standardization improved conduit viability more than it could influence every determinant of leak. Importantly, this finding is best interpreted as an association within a maturing program rather than as proof of isolated protocol effect.

Several technical principles likely contributed to this effect. Conduit vascularity was deliberately prioritized by preserving the right gastroepiploic arcade and, whenever feasible, the Right gastric artery, or at minimum its descending branch toward the pyloric antrum. In our view, this branch is particularly important in supporting distal antral and pyloric perfusion and in maintaining the submucosal vascular plexus of the future conduit [9]. Conduit geometry was standardized by constructing a tube of relatively uniform width [10], avoiding both narrow ischemia‐prone conduits and bulky conduits that compromise smooth transposition. Omental bulk was minimized to facilitate posterior mediastinal passage and reduce compression at the thoracic inlet. Finally, torsion was addressed explicitly through tape‐guided transposition and maintenance of consistent conduit orientation. Together, these steps likely reduced technical variance and improved conduit viability.

The absence of a parallel reduction in leak rate should not be viewed as a failure of the protocol. Leak after esophagectomy reflects a broader interaction between conduit perfusion, anastomotic tension, tissue quality, prior radiotherapy, inflammation, and host healing factors. It is therefore entirely possible to improve one major determinant of reconstruction while observing stable leak rates, particularly when baseline leak rates are already acceptable and when case mix evolves over time. In our cohort, the later phase of the program also saw greater use of neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy and more extensive lymphadenectomy, both of which may influence tissue characteristics and perioperative complexity. The most appropriate interpretation is therefore that technical standardization improved conduit safety without fully overcoming all factors contributing to anastomotic failure. The numerically higher chyle leak rate in the later phase likely reflects the concurrent increase in extended lymphadenectomy rather than any adverse effect of conduit protocolization.

Although fluorescence perfusion assessment is increasingly advocated during esophagectomy, its practical utility may be more limited in 3‐field transthoracic esophagectomy with a cervical anastomosis, where conduit length is often already at its functional limit and the surgeon is effectively fighting for every additional centimeter [11, 12]. In that setting, even if indocyanine green identifies a marginal conduit tip, the scope for meaningful revision may be small, often no more than 1–2 cm, and may be insufficient to materially alter the reconstructive plan. For this reason, we view fluorescence perfusion imaging as a potentially useful adjunct, but not a substitute for disciplined conduit construction. In long‐reach cervical reconstruction, preservation of vascular inflow, including the right gastric contribution where possible, controlled conduit geometry, minimal omental bulk, and torsion‐free transposition may be more consequential than intraoperative detection of a problem that offers limited room for correction.

A major strength of this manuscript is that it deliberately frames the findings as an institutional learning‐curve phenomenon rather than a simplistic era comparison. Multiple non‐technical variables changed over time, including neoadjuvant treatment intensity, procedure mix, and lymphadenectomy extent. Improvements in pathological complete response and recurrence in the later phase likely reflect broader oncologic evolution and should not be attributed to conduit standardization alone. By separating reconstructive outcomes from overall oncologic maturation, the present analysis avoids overclaiming causality and offers a more credible account of how a high‐volume esophagectomy program evolves [13].

Another important message is that improved conduit safety did not come at the expense of oncologic quality. R0 resection was maintained at 94.7%, and median lymph node yield remained 25, suggesting that greater attention to vascular preservation and reconstruction did not compromise resectional thoroughness. This is important, because one potential criticism of technically protective strategies is that they may weaken oncologic intent. Our data do not support that concern. Instead, they suggest that sound reconstruction and oncologic rigor can coexist within a mature, protocolized esophagectomy program.

These findings also have practical relevance for developing high‐volume programs in resource‐variable settings. Much of the contemporary discussion around conduit safety emphasizes adjuncts such as fluorescence imaging, advanced minimally invasive platforms, and other technologies. While these tools are valuable, they are not universally available and may not always solve the core problem in long‐reach cervical reconstruction. In contrast, the technical principles described here—respect for vascular anatomy, preservation of the right gastric contribution where possible, consistent conduit width, avoidance of torsion, disciplined sequencing, and team‐level standardization—are low‐cost, transferable, and teachable. The broader lesson may therefore be that meaningful quality improvement in esophagectomy does not always begin with technology; it can begin with standardization [14].

This study has several limitations. It is retrospective and single‐center, and the learning curve coincided with evolution in multiple oncologic and operative practices, limiting causal inference. The analysis was not designed to compare open and minimally invasive esophagectomy, and the predominantly open nature of the cohort should be understood in that context. Routine fluorescence‐based perfusion assessment was not used. In addition, the presented CUSUM analysis was descriptive and non‐risk‐adjusted, and therefore does not account for changing case mix over time, including increased use of neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy and more extensive lymphadenectomy. The total number of composite major conduit‐related events was low, which precluded reliable multivariable modeling without substantial risk of overfitting. Future work should incorporate larger datasets, multivariable modeling, and risk‐adjusted learning‐curve analytics to strengthen inference.

Despite these limitations, the findings remain clinically meaningful. They suggest that the learning curve in esophagectomy is not solely a matter of volume accumulation, but also one of deliberate conversion of operative experience into reproducible technical behavior. In our program, that conversion was associated with disappearance of major conduit necrosis, stable leak rates, acceptable mortality, and preserved oncologic adequacy [15]. That combination makes a compelling case for viewing conduit standardization as an integral component of institutional maturation in esophageal cancer surgery.

Conclusion

Institutional maturation in esophagectomy was associated with transition from experience‐dependent reconstruction to a more reproducible and standardized approach to gastric conduit formation. In this cohort of 187 consecutive patients, that maturation coincided with disappearance of major conduit necrosis, while leak rates remained stable, perioperative mortality remained low, and oncologic adequacy was preserved. These findings suggest that the learning curve in esophagectomy is expressed not only through increasing procedural volume, but also through deliberate conversion of operative experience into disciplined technical standardization.

In long‐reach cervical reconstruction, meticulous preservation of conduit vascularity—including the right gastric artery whenever feasible, or at minimum its descending branch toward the pyloric antrum—together with controlled conduit geometry, minimal omental bulk, and torsion‐free transposition, may be more important than reliance on adjunctive perfusion imaging alone. Standardized conduit construction therefore appears to be an important, practical, and exportable quality‐improvement step for high‐volume esophageal cancer programs, particularly in resource‐variable settings.
