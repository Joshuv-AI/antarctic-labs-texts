Disease Elimination Is Not Eradication: Rethinking “Disease-Free” Certification in Global Health amid the Global Resurgence of Measles, Polio, and Cholera
Authors: Adanze Nge Cynthia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Disease elimination, as defined by the World Health Organization (WHO), denotes the sustained reduction of pathogen transmission to zero within a defined geographic area through ongoing public health intervention. In practice, however, certification of elimination status has frequently operated as a terminal designation, reshaping funding and signalling that a problem has been resolved. This perspective argues that such a conception is both scientifically untenable and operationally hazardous. Drawing on three concurrent, well-documented resurgences, measles across the Americas and Europe, circulating vaccine-derived poliovirus (cVDPV) in high-income settings, and accelerating global cholera mortality, this paper identifies three mechanistically distinct but institutionally convergent pathways through which disease control gains erode: immunological erosion driven by unvaccinated birth-cohort accumulation, virological re-emergence via vaccine-derived pathogen variants in under-immunised populations, and structural collapse of water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) infrastructure in conflict-affected settings. Across all three, a shared governance failure is evident: post-certification frameworks reward achievement rather than mandate sustained maintenance. Four targeted reforms are proposed, pathway-sensitive post-certification monitoring, quantitative threshold-triggered status reviews, integration of structural resilience metrics, and reform of public-facing elimination communication. Disease elimination is better understood as a condition of managed equilibrium than as a milestone: it is not secured by certification, it is continuously earned.

Introduction

In 1980, the World Health Organization (WHO) certified the global eradication of smallpox, a watershed moment in the history of public health [1]. Yet the institutional legacy of that achievement has been double-edged. The certification model that smallpox bequeathed to subsequent disease control programmes carries an implicit promise that modern epidemiology cannot keep: that a disease, once eliminated, stays eliminated. The past decade’s epidemiological record refutes that promise. Diseases previously controlled to near-zero transmission are re-establishing themselves in countries and regions once regarded as public health successes. These are not isolated failures of implementation; they are structurally patterned events that expose systematic vulnerabilities in how global health governance conceptualises and manages the post-certification period.

The WHO technical definition of elimination is explicit on this point: elimination requires ongoing intervention to be sustained [2]. Yet the political economy of global health certification tends to operate otherwise. Elimination designations carry significant institutional weight, shaping how funding is prioritized, how governments communicate risk, and how international organisations allocate technical resources [3]. The practical consequence is that the surveillance architecture and political commitment required to maintain elimination are frequently withdrawn at the precise moment they are most needed: immediately after the achievement of certification, when vigilance is falsely perceived as no longer necessary. Frenk and Moon have identified this institutional dynamic, the tendency to mistake the control of a health problem for its resolution, as a defining challenge of global health governance [3].

This perspective examines three contemporaneous and mechanistically instructive resurgences: measles in the Americas and Europe, circulating vaccine-derived poliovirus (cVDPV) in high-income countries, and cholera in conflict-affected settings. Each illuminates a distinct pathway through which disease control gains can be reversed. Crucially, each also exposes a specific structural gap in existing post-certification governance. The argument advanced here is not that current WHO and regional verification mechanisms are absent, the Regional Verification Commissions (RVCs) for measles and rubella, and the International Health Regulations (IHR 2005) framework for re-evaluation, represent genuine institutional achievements [4, 5], but that they contain addressable gaps that require targeted, evidence-grounded reform. The stakes are not merely academic. As the evidence reviewed here demonstrates, the costs of mischaracterising elimination as an endpoint rather than a maintained condition are measured in outbreaks, in disability-adjusted life-years, and, increasingly, in lives.

The Governance Trap: When Certification Becomes Closure

A structural tension lies at the heart of disease elimination governance. Certification is necessarily a discrete event: either a country meets the criteria or it does not, at a specified point in time, following a defined verification process. Yet the epidemiological condition that certification is intended to validate, sustained absence of transmission, is inherently dynamic. Population immunity wanes, birth cohorts accumulate susceptible individuals, vaccine platforms carry their own re-emergence risks, and the infrastructure sustaining disease control can deteriorate faster than routine monitoring captures. This tension is not resolved by existing frameworks. The IHR Emergency Committee on poliovirus has maintained poliovirus as a Public Health Emergency of International Concern (PHEIC) continuously since 2014 [6], a recognition that elimination metrics and resurgence risk can coexist. But the broader governance architecture for other diseases has not internalised this lesson. Status reviews remain heavily discretionary and, critically, politically sensitive: governments with reputational investment in disease-free designations carry structural incentives to delay re-evaluation processes that may result in reclassification [7, 8]. The result is a predictable pattern: surveillance investment contracts after certification, funding for maintenance activities competes unfavourably against other priorities, and early signals of deterioration go undetected until a resurgence is already underway. Fine and colleagues described herd immunity as a ‘rough guide’ rather than a fixed threshold, a caution that applies equally to elimination status itself [9]. What follows is a detailed examination of three pathways through which this governance trap is realised.

Three Pathways of Disease Control Vulnerability

Immunological Erosion: Measles in the Americas and Europe

Measles demands population immunity of approximately 95% to interrupt transmission, reflecting its basic reproduction number (R₀) of 12–18, among the highest of any known human pathogen [10]. The United States declared measles eliminated in 2000, and national vaccination coverage broadly held at threshold for two decades [11]. What this aggregate figure concealed, however, was the sub-national accumulation of susceptible birth cohorts in communities where vaccination coverage had drifted below the threshold required to prevent local transmission. Measles vaccination coverage among kindergarten-aged children began declining before the COVID-19 pandemic, indicating a structural erosion that predated, and was subsequently amplified by, pandemic disruption [12]. National MMR coverage among kindergartners, which stood at 95.2% in the 2019–20 school year, fell to 92.7% by 2023–24 and further to 92.5% in 2024–25, with approximately 286,000 kindergartners unprotected and only ten states meeting the 95% herd-immunity threshold [13].

The epidemiological consequences became unmistakable. By the close of 2025, the United States had recorded 2,287 confirmed measles cases across 45 jurisdictions, with 90% of cases linked to discrete outbreak clusters, and three fatalities, a striking escalation from the 285 cases and 16 outbreaks recorded in all of 2024 [14]. The overwhelming majority involved unvaccinated or vaccination-status-unknown individuals, confirming that immunological gap accumulation, not viral evolution, drove re-emergence [11]. In Europe, 2024 recorded the highest measles case count in the WHO European Region in more than 25 years [15]. In November 2025, the Pan American Health Organization (PAHO) formally announced that the Americas had lost the collective elimination status first achieved in 2016 [16].

A persistent complicating factor is vaccine misinformation. The retracted and thoroughly refuted claim linking the measles-mumps-rubella (MMR) vaccine to autism has proven durable in digital information environments, where algorithmic amplification sustains minority viewpoints at disproportionate scale [17]. The resulting hesitancy clusters operate below the resolution of national coverage statistics, which is precisely why sub-national monitoring, rather than aggregate reporting, is the appropriate governance response. The pandemic amplified a structural vulnerability that national-level metrics had failed to detect.

Virological Re-emergence: Vaccine-Derived Poliovirus in High-Income Settings

The global polio eradication programme’s reliance on live attenuated oral poliovirus vaccine (OPV) has introduced a re-emergence pathway with no analogue in measles control. In under-immunised populations, the attenuated Sabin strains used in OPV can revert to neurovirulence and re-acquire transmissibility, generating circulating vaccine-derived poliovirus (cVDPV) outbreaks that are epidemiologically indistinguishable from wild-type transmission in their capacity to cause paralysis.

In June 2022, paralytic poliomyelitis caused by cVDPV type 2 was confirmed in an unvaccinated adult in Rockland County, New York, the first domestically acquired case in the United States since 2013 [18]. Environmental sequencing linked the strain to virus detected concurrently in London’s sewage surveillance network and wastewater in Jerusalem, demonstrating that three countries formally designated polio-free were connected by poliovirus circulation that clinical surveillance had not detected. Between January 2023 and June 2024, WHO and partners identified 74 cVDPV outbreaks across 39 countries, resulting in 672 confirmed paralytic cases [19]. European environmental surveillance in late 2024 detected cVDPV2 in wastewater from cities across Germany, Finland, Poland, Spain, and the United Kingdom, in communities where sub-national vaccination coverage had drifted below the levels required to prevent spread [20].

The Rockland County case is paradigmatic of the governance gap this paper identifies. The affected county’s vaccination coverage had been a documented concern; environmental surveillance, the tool that would have detected pre-clinical circulation, was not routinely integrated into post-eradication monitoring. Aylward and Alwan, analysing Syria’s 2013 wild poliovirus resurgence, demonstrated that vaccination coverage gaps in conflict-affected populations represent a predictable vulnerability that post-eradication frameworks must explicitly address [21]. The lesson, that certification does not eliminate the epidemiological conditions required for resurgence, applies with equal force to immunologically similar populations in high-income settings where OPV has been used.

Structural Collapse: Cholera and the Limits of Certification-Centric Thinking

Cholera presents the most structurally complex case, and in doing so extends the argument of this paper beyond certification complacency alone. Unlike measles or poliovirus, Vibrio cholerae transmission is a function not of population immunity but of the sustained integrity of water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) infrastructure. Control of cholera reflects the strength of public health systems and civil infrastructure rather than any intrinsic biological protection within a population. No formal WHO cholera elimination certification framework comparable to the RVC process for measles and rubella currently exists. The countries most severely affected in the present resurgence, Yemen, Syria, Lebanon, and Haiti, were therefore never formally certified as cholera-free. Their experiences are nonetheless directly relevant. They demonstrate that disease control gains, whether formally certified or informally achieved, can be rapidly reversed when the infrastructure sustaining them collapses. It merits noting that oral cholera vaccine (OCV) represents a complementary, though not primary, tool in cholera control: while a single-dose OCV campaign was shown to reduce incidence by approximately 38% in high-burden settings, OCV delivery itself depends on the same functional health infrastructure whose collapse drives resurgence [22]. In Haiti, the 2022 resurgence following declared freedom from cholera illustrates precisely this dynamic: infrastructure failure disabled both WASH systems and vaccine delivery simultaneously [23]. This is not a failure of certification per se, it is a failure of governance frameworks to treat structural resilience as a co-equal determinant of disease control. Legros has documented how the resurgence of cholera in settings with previously functional Water Sanitation and Hygiene (WASH) infrastructure follows a predictable trajectory once conflict or governance failure disrupts water safety systems [24]. When infrastructure fails, the epidemiological condition that ‘elimination’ was meant to describe simply ceases to exist, with or without a formal designation.

WHO classified the global cholera resurgence as a Grade 3 emergency in January 2023, its highest internal level, and the risk has remained very high due to expanding outbreaks, geographic spread, and resource constraints. From January to August 2024 alone, 371,517 cases and 2,527 deaths were reported across 28 countries in five WHO regions, with the Eastern Mediterranean Region most severely affected [25]. No certification framework mitigates structural vulnerability when the systems required to sustain disease control fail. The cholera case therefore broadens the reform agenda: governance frameworks must incorporate structural resilience as a leading indicator, not merely a retrospective explanatory variable (Table 1).

Reforming the Post-Certification Governance Architecture

The evidence reviewed here does not argue against disease elimination as a public health goal. The eradication of smallpox remains the most consequential public health achievement in recorded history, and progress toward elimination has prevented hundreds of millions of cases of vaccine-preventable disease. The argument is more precise: that the governance frameworks governing what happens after elimination is achieved or substantially progressed contain specific, addressable structural gaps. Four reforms follow directly from the typological framework presented above.

First, post-certification monitoring must be disease-specific and pathway-sensitive. The current verification architecture applies broadly similar expectations across diseases with fundamentally different re-emergence mechanisms, a category error with tangible epidemiological consequences. For measles, the appropriate response is continuous sub-national coverage assessment, early identification of hesitancy clusters, and maintained rapid-response capacity that can be activated without awaiting formal reclassification. For poliovirus, it requires context-appropriate integration of environmental and wastewater surveillance, not as a universal mandate irrespective of local capacity, but as a calibrated tool prioritised in settings with documented immunity gaps or a history of OPV use. The Rockland County case illustrates starkly what occurs when environmental surveillance is absent in precisely such a setting. For cholera and analogous infrastructure-dependent diseases, it means incorporating WASH resilience assessments into disease control monitoring frameworks as leading, rather than lagging, indicators.

Second, status review should incorporate quantitative threshold triggers alongside discretionary review cycles. At present, re-evaluation of elimination status is heavily discretionary and politically sensitive. Governments with invested reputational stakes in disease-free designations carry structural incentives to delay initiating review processes that may result in reclassification. WHO and regional bodies could strengthen existing RVC processes by adopting predefined epidemiological indicators, coverage falling below defined sub-national thresholds, sustained environmental pathogen detection, or case counts exceeding specified limits within a defined period, that automatically trigger formal review with publicly reported findings, independent of whether the affected country has requested reassessment. The objective is not punitive reclassification but the depoliticisation of the reassessment process: transforming re-evaluation from a reputationally fraught political event into a technical procedure governed by objective criteria.

Third, structural and institutional resilience metrics should be integrated into post-certification frameworks alongside biological transmission indicators. A country may sustain near-zero disease transmission while simultaneously approaching a point of institutional fragility, through conflict escalation, governance deterioration, or fiscal crisis, at which the infrastructure sustaining disease control is at imminent risk of collapse. A tractable set of WASH integrity assessments, health system resilience indicators, and governance stability metrics, integrated into existing review cycles, would enable proactive risk identification and targeted international support before resurgence onset rather than emergency response after. Frenk and Moon’s framework for global health governance explicitly calls for mechanisms that address ‘systemic’ as well as ‘technical’ vulnerabilities [3]; the cholera resurgence is a case study in what the absence of such mechanisms costs.

Fourth, the public communication of elimination status must better reflect its conditional and actively maintained nature. The terms ‘disease-free,’ ‘eliminated,’ and ‘eradicated’ carry an implicit finality inconsistent with the epidemiological reality they describe. The analogy to clinical remission rather than cure is not merely rhetorical: oncologists have long understood that remission requires active monitoring, that relapse is predictable under defined conditions, and that communicating false certainty about cure has measurable consequences for patient behaviour and adherence to surveillance. The same logic applies to disease elimination. Reframing elimination as an actively maintained epidemiological condition would more accurately represent what has been achieved, better support the ongoing political and behavioural engagement required to sustain it, and, critically, reduce the reputational disincentive for governments to report early signals of deterioration. These four reforms are proposed as complements to, not replacements for, existing WHO and regional verification mechanisms.

Conclusion

Disease elimination is best understood not as a milestone but as a dynamic epidemiological equilibrium requiring continuous governance to maintain. The three resurgences examined here, measles, circulating vaccine-derived poliovirus, and cholera, each illuminate a mechanistically distinct pathway through which disease control gains can erode: immunological attrition, virological reversion, and structural collapse. These pathways are biologically different but institutionally convergent: all three were shaped, at least in part, by the withdrawal of the sustained investment and vigilance that elimination requires after the moment of certification or achievement. The COVID-19 pandemic did not create these vulnerabilities; it exposed and amplified pre-existing structural weaknesses that governance frameworks had failed to address.

As with any perspective, several limitations warrant acknowledgement. The three cases examined, while mechanistically distinct and representative of dominant resurgence pathways, are not exhaustive; other diseases and settings may exhibit different or overlapping vulnerability patterns not captured here. The analysis draws on publicly reported surveillance data, which are subject to under-ascertainment, reporting lags, and cross-country comparability constraints. The reforms proposed are conceptually grounded but their practical feasibility will vary substantially by institutional context, resource availability, and political will.

The reforms proposed here, pathway-sensitive monitoring, depoliticised threshold triggers for status review, integration of structural resilience metrics, and honest public communication about the conditional nature of elimination, are tractable, evidence-based, and commensurate with the problem’s scale. They do not require dismantling existing verification mechanisms; they require strengthening and extending them to reflect the full complexity of sustained disease control. Certification marks an achievement. What comes after certification determines whether that achievement endures. Until governance frameworks treat elimination as a condition to be continuously maintained rather than a problem to be declared solved, the epidemiological history of the past decade will continue to repeat itself.
