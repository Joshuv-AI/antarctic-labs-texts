Improved Detection of Myositis‐Specific Autoantibodies Using Luciferase Immunoprecipitation Systems Assay: Comparison With Line Blot and Conventional Immunoprecipitation
Authors: Peter D Burbelo, Iago Pinal‐Fernandez, Maria Casal‐Dominguez, Xin Tian, Shijinqiu Gao, Neha Gupta, Eric W Robbins, John A Chiorini, Christopher A Mecoli, Lisa Christopher‐Stine, Livia Casciola‐Rosen, Andrew L Mammen, Sonye K Danoff, Anthony F Suffredini, Julio A Huapaya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveMyositis‐specific autoantibodies guide the diagnosis and classification of idiopathic inflammatory myopathies, but current immunoassays vary in accuracy, particularly for autoantibodies associated with interstitial lung disease. To compare the performance of the luciferase immunoprecipitation systems (LIPS) assay with line blot and immunoprecipitation (IP) and to evaluate whether antibody‐specific thresholds modify line blot concordance.MethodsWe analyzed 279 sera with line blot results from the Johns Hopkins Myositis Center. LIPS was performed for anti–Jo‐1, anti–PL‐7, anti–PL‐12, anti–MDA‐5, anti‐EJ, and anti‐Ro52 autoantibodies. Line blot results were evaluated using standard thresholds (≥36 and ≥71 arbitrary units [AU]). Assay performance was evaluated against IP (n = 141). In patients without IP (n = 138), clinical diagnoses were examined.ResultsLIPS showed excellent agreement with IP (κ = 0.90, area under the receiver operating characteristic curve 0.935), outperforming line blot. Line blot cutoffs influenced performance: ≥36 AU increased sensitivity (92.9%) but reduced specificity (73.8%), whereas ≥71 AU improved specificity (92.9%) but lowered sensitivity (82.8%). Antibody‐specific thresholds modified concordance but remained quantitatively inferior to LIPS. In patients without IP, concordance between LIPS and line blot was 89% at ≥36 AU and 83% at ≥71 AU. LIPS‐positive cases were enriched for antisynthetase syndrome and dermatomyositis. Among discordant cases (line blot positive and LIPS negative), most were clinically classified as inclusion body myositis (5 of 11). For anti‐Ro52, discrepancies were most frequent at titers <36 AU.ConclusionLIPS provides quantitative discrimination, strong concordance with IP, and improved performance compared with line blot, particularly at low to intermediate titers. By reducing misclassification and complementing antibody‐specific thresholds, LIPS has the potential to enhance clinical stratification in patients with myositis.

INTRODUCTION

Myositis‐specific autoantibodies (MSAs) play a critical role in the diagnosis and classification of idiopathic inflammatory myopathies (IIM). 1 , 2 These antibodies define clinical subgroups with distinct clinical trajectories, including interstitial lung disease (ILD), which remains a major driver of morbidity and mortality. 3 In clinical practice, immunoassays such as enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA) and immunoblots are widely used, with the line blot being one of the most common tests. 4 However, these assays often report qualitative results and are prone to variability in sensitivity and specificity, particularly for non–Jo‐1 autoantibodies. 5 This limitation is most apparent at low or moderate autoantibody titers, in which the clinical significance is uncertain, and both false positives and false negatives are frequent, leading to potential underdiagnosis or overdiagnosis. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9

Luciferase immunoprecipitation systems (LIPS) is a highly quantitative assay with a wide dynamic range, using Renilla luciferase–tagged antigens to measure autoantibodies. 10 It has demonstrated excellent performance in multiple autoimmune and infectious diseases, providing greater sensitivity and a broader dynamic range than conventional immunoassays. 11 , 12 , 13 We recently showed that LIPS can accurately detect and quantify broad levels of antisynthetase and anti–MDA‐5 autoantibodies, highlighting how autoantibody levels may serve as biomarkers for treatment response in patients with autoimmune ILD. 14 Here, we extend these findings in a larger, clinically well‐characterized cohort by directly comparing LIPS, line blot, and immunoprecipitation (IP) assays to assess whether LIPS improves diagnostic accuracy, particularly in the clinically ambiguous low to moderate titer range in which conventional assays have limitations. 15 , 16

MATERIALS AND METHODS

An analysis of archived serum samples was conducted from patients with suspected IIM enrolled in a natural history study at the Johns Hopkins Myositis Center. Line blot testing (EUROLINE Autoimmune Inflammatory Myopathies 16 Ag IgG, Euroimmun, Lubeck, Germany) was performed for research purposes, with titers classified as low positive (≥15 arbitrary units [AU], 1+), moderate positive (≥36 AU, 2+), and strong positive (≥71 AU, 3+). Patients with borderline titers (8–14 AU) for MSAs were excluded. Patients were assigned to a specific autoantibody group if line blot assay was positive for an MSA by line blot (≥15 AU). LIPS testing was performed for the corresponding MSAs. LIPS testing was performed as previously described using Renilla luciferase–tagged antigens, with predefined antigen‐specific thresholds. 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 Antibody binding is detected via luminescence light units (LU), with signal intensity proportional to the levels of antibodies. In an exploratory manner, a myositis‐associated autoantibody (anti‐Ro52) was also analyzed and compared to titers from the line blot. Data from IP, the reference standard for the measurement of MSAs, were included for comparison to the line blot and LIPS assays. IP testing was performed using the Oklahoma Medical Research Foundation (OMRF) myositis panel, which incorporates IP along with complementary confirmatory methods for antibody designation. Additional confirmatory IP testing for selected cases was performed at Johns Hopkins University using an in‐house assay. Discrepancies in results, potentially related to differences in the timing of sample acquisition, are reported. Other assays were incorporated when available through chart review. These included a commercial line blot myositis panel ordered through Quest Diagnostics (Secaucus, New Jersey), a laboratory‐developed ELISA performed at Johns Hopkins Hospital. Results from outside laboratories for which the specific assay methodology could not be determined were categorized as unspecified. Patients with co‐positivity by line blot for other autoantibodies were classified as “undefined” and reviewed separately.

To assess diagnostic agreement, the performance of LIPS and line blot assays was compared with IP as the gold standard. The LIPS assay was analyzed as a binary result (positive vs negative) based on previously published thresholds. 14 Line blot was evaluated using established cutoffs for defining positive results (≥36 and ≥71). Agreement metrics included sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, Cohen's κ‐coefficient, and the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC). ROC curves were generated for LIPS, with line blot operating points (≥36, ≥71, and antibody specific) overlaid as discrete markers. AUC was estimated nonparametrically. To compare the performance of two diagnostic tests, McNemar's test was used to assess differences in sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy, DeLong's test was applied to compare AUCs, and Hotelling's T 2 test was used to compare κ‐coefficients. 17 , 18 For patients without IP data, ILD status and clinical diagnoses were summarized by LIPS positivity and compared with line blot titers at thresholds of ≥36 and ≥71 to illustrate the effect of thresholding on classification. Importantly, clinical diagnoses had been previously established through multidisciplinary clinical evaluation and retrospective chart review and were not based solely on antibody test results. Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5, R Foundation for Statistical Computing) and the R packages pROC, multiagree, and DTComPair.

Ethics approval

This retrospective study used de‐identified samples and was deemed exempt from review by the institutional review board (IRB00455938).

Agreement between LIPS and IP with clinical context of discordant cases

Across all autoantibodies with concomitant IP data (n = 141, 99 positive and 42 negative), LIPS demonstrated the highest agreement with IP (κ = 0.90, accuracy 95.7%, AUC 0.935), correctly classifying 135 of 141 cases (98 true positives, 37 true negatives, 5 false positives, and 1 false negative) (Table 1). In contrast, line blot performance was more threshold dependent than LIPS; at a threshold of ≥36, sensitivity was high (92.9%) and specificity was moderate (73.8%), reducing its accuracy (87.2%, κ = 0.686) compared to IP. Using a threshold of ≥71, specificity improved (92.9%) but sensitivity declined (82.8%), yielding similar accuracy (85.8%, κ = 0.691) yet remaining quantitatively inferior to LIPS across sensitivity‐specificity balance, κ coefficient, and AUC. Using antibody‐specific cutoffs (≥36 for Jo‐1/MDA‐5; ≥71 for PL‐7/PL‐12/EJ) modestly improved line blot agreement (κ = 0.781) but still remained inferior to LIPS. Overall, LIPS performance was significantly superior compared to line blot results (Table 1; Figure 1A). ROC analysis confirmed these trade‐offs, with LIPS achieving a greater AUC (0.935) compared to line blot results (0.834–0.893; Figure 1B). Detailed per‐antibody results are provided in Supplementary e‐Table 1. Comparisons of sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive values, and negative predictive values are presented in Supplementary e‐Table 2 and Supplementary e‐Figure 2.

Most apparent discordances between LIPS and IP were attributable to differences in sample timing rather than assay performance. The median interval between LIPS testing and IP was one day (interquartile range 0–819). When analyses were restricted to samples collected within 30 days of IP testing, only one LIPS‐IP discordant result remained; when restricted to samples collected within 90 days, two LIPS‐IP discordant results remained. Four Jo‐1 cases were LIPS positive but IP negative; in three of these, IP had been performed 8 to 13 years earlier, and all had ELISA or commercial line blot results at other time points consistent with Jo‐1 positivity. Another case was positive for Jo‐1 by both LIPS and ELISA, positive for PL‐12 by LIPS, and positive only for PL‐12 by IP, suggesting true dual antibody positivity. For anti–MDA‐5, one case was LIPS positive and IP negative but clinically consistent with anti–MDA‐5, again favoring the LIPS result. After these considerations, only a single unexplained discordance remained, underscoring the robustness of LIPS concordance with IP.

Agreement between LIPS and line blot in patients without IP results

Among patients without IP results (n = 138), 87 tested positive and 51 negative by LIPS. Applying the stricter line blot thresholds described above, 83 patients had titers ≥36 and 69 had titers ≥71. Among patients who were LIPS negative, 40 had line blot titers <36 and 46 had titers <71. At the ≥36 cutoff, LIPS and line blot were concordant in 89% of cases (11% discordant), whereas at the ≥71 cutoff, concordance fell to 83%. In essentially all LIPS‐positive cases, a clinically plausible corresponding diagnosis was present (antisynthetase syndrome [AS], dermatomyositis [DM], and overlap syndromes), with no misclassification of inclusion body myositis (IBM). By contrast, among discordant cases (line blot positive but LIPS negative), 5 of 11 patients were clinically diagnosed with IBM. These diagnostic distributions, summarized in Table 2, reinforce the clinical specificity of LIPS and illustrate how antibody‐specific thresholds modify interpretation of line blot results by shifting sensitivity‐specificity trade‐offs; however, these effects are antibody dependent and do not uniformly improve performance across antibodies, with persistent misclassification for some specificities. Patient‐level patterns of discordance between LIPS and line blot across both thresholds are presented in Supplementary e‐Table 3.

Anti–Jo‐1

Of 136 line blot–positive samples, 123 (90%) were LIPS positive (range 42,000−11 million LU), whereas 13 (10%) were LIPS negative (9,000–24,000 LU). Among the patients who were LIPS positive, 117 were confirmed by another method (56 by IP, 9 by commercial line blot, and 70 by ELISA). Four samples were discordant between LIPS and IP, although the time between testing was highly variable (Table 1). In patients categorized as “undefined” by line blot but positive by LIPS (n = 15), 11 had DM, 4 had polymyositis (PM), 11 had ILD, and 5 were IP confirmed.

Among the 13 LIPS‐negative cases, IP testing was available for 2, with 1 discordant result (Table 1). In a subset of patients, ELISA results showed partial concordance (Supplementary e‐Figure 1). Line blot titers in this group were generally low (15–30), except for one patient with a high titer of 120. From a clinical perspective, this group included four patients with IBM, four patients with myositis overlap syndromes, two patients with AS, two patients with DM, and one patient with PM. Of note, 10 of these patients were categorized as “undefined” by line blot.

Anti–MDA‐5

Of 56 line blot–positive samples, 37 (66%) were LIPS positive (range 500,000–7,000,000 LU) and 19 (34%) were LIPS negative (<25,000 LU). Among patients who were LIPS positive, 28 had IP data, with 27 positive for anti–MDA‐5. One discordant sample was LIPS positive but IP negative; however, the IP panel did not specifically include MDA‐5, and the patient was clinically consistent with anti–MDA‐5. Notably, 13 patients who were LIPS positive had low to moderate line blot titers (16–70), 10 of whom had ILD. Among 13 patients categorized as “undefined” by line blot but positive by LIPS, 12 had DM (one amyopathic), 1 had lupus overlap, 10 had ILD, and 4 were IP confirmed.

Among 19 patients who were LIPS negative, 15 had confirmation by another method (10 IP and 5 commercial line blot). Three of these had line blot titers between 36 to 64, including one with ILD (anti–Jo‐1+ by OMRF). Clinically, this group included three patients with IBM, four patients with overlap syndromes, one patient with necrotizing myopathy, eight patients with DM, two patients with AS, and one patient with undifferentiated myopathy; only seven patients had ILD. Eleven patients in this group were categorized as “undefined” by line blot.

Anti–PL‐7

Of 49 line blot–positive samples, 13 (27%) were LIPS positive (range 1–4 million LU) and 36 (73%) were LIPS negative (4,000–50,000 LU). All patients who were LIPS positive had confirmation by at least one additional method, with some undergoing multiple confirmatory assays (7 IP, 4 commercial line blot, 1 ELISA, and 2 unspecified). One patient had a low line blot titer of 18 and was correctly identified by LIPS; this patient had PM without ILD, showing that LIPS can detect true positives even when the line blot is weak. Only one patient who was LIPS positive was categorized as “undefined” by line blot, and this case was confirmed as positive by IP.

Among 36 patients who were LIPS negative, 18 had confirmation by another method (15 IP, 4 commercial line blot, and 1 unspecified). Four patients in this group had moderate to strong line blot titers (67–175), three did not have ILD, and two had additional negative confirmatory testing, indicating that even strong line blot titers can be false positives. Clinically, this group included 12 patients with DM (1 amyopathic), 14 patients with IBM, 5 patients with PM, 1 patient with polymyalgia rheumatica, 1 patient with statin‐induced necrotizing myopathy, 1 patient with undifferentiated myopathy, 4 patients had ILD. Fifteen were IP negative. Except for one patient with negative results, all were categorized as “undefined” by line blot.

Anti–PL‐12

Of the 31 line blot–positive samples, 14 (44%) were LIPS positive (150,000–2.2 million LU) and 17 were LIPS negative (3,000–22,000 LU). All patients who were LIPS positive had confirmation by at least one additional method, with some undergoing multiple confirmatory assays (11 IP, 6 commercial line blot, and 1 unspecified). One patient with a low line blot titer of 16 was confirmed positive by both LIPS and IP and had ILD, demonstrating that LIPS can detect clinically meaningful positives even at weak line blot titers. No patients who were LIPS positive were categorized as “undefined” by line blot.

Among patients who were LIPS negative, 12 had confirmation (9 IP, 1 commercial line blot, and 2 unspecified). Three patients had high line blot PL‐12 titers (66–87 AU). One of these patients had ILD but was also negative for anti–PL‐12 by IP. A second patient had mild ILD and was negative for PL‐12 by another line blot test. The third patient did not have ILD, was negative by another line blot, and was clinically diagnosed with overlap syndrome. These cases were therefore classified as false positives by line blot, although lung‐limited or early AS cannot be definitively excluded in patients with ILD who lacked IP confirmation. Clinically, this group included four patients with DM, six patients with IBM, two patients with PM, one patient who was amyloid, two patients with overlap syndromes, one patient with necrotizing myopathy, one patient without myopathy, and four patients had ILD. All patients in this group were categorized as “undefined” by line blot.

Anti‐EJ

Of seven line blot–positive samples, three (43%) were LIPS positive (2–7 million LU) and four (57%) were LIPS negative (17,000–31,000 LU). All patients who were LIPS positive had confirmation by another method (1 IP and 2 unspecified). Line blot titers in this group were strongly positive (135–194). No patients who were LIPS positive were categorized as “undefined” by line blot.

Among the patients who were LIPS negative, IP confirmation was available for two cases, both concordant. Line blot titers ranged from 15 to 27. Clinically, this group included two patients with IBM and two patients with overlap syndromes, and all were categorized as “undefined” by line blot. Despite the small sample size, LIPS results were concordant with IP where available, supporting its reliability for this rare autoantibody.

Anti‐Ro52

When comparing LIPS anti‐Ro52 levels with line blot titers, discrepancies were concentrated at low and borderline values (Figure 2). Of note, in contrast to MSAs, no IP‐based gold standard exists for anti‐Ro52. Among 118 patients with line blot–negative results (<8 AU), the majority were also negative by LIPS, although six had high LIPS levels (110,000–2,000,000 LU). Of the eight patients with borderline titers (8–14 AU), only one demonstrated a high LIPS level (1.3 million LU). In the low‐positive range (15–35 AU; n = 15), LIPS confirmed eight patients as positive, most with very high levels (125,000–3 million LU), whereas the remainder were negative. By contrast, 24 of 28 patients with moderate positives (36–70 AU) were confirmed by LIPS, and among 106 patients with strong positives (≥71 AU), nearly all were concordant, with only 3 discrepant negatives (line blot titers of 80, 119, and 169). Overall, LIPS would have reclassified 21 patients (14 to positive, and 7 to negative). These findings suggest that although line blot is generally reliable at moderate to high titers (≥36 AU), results below this range are more uncertain, and LIPS may provide additional clarity in such cases.

DISCUSSION

Our study demonstrates strong concordance between the diagnostic performance of LIPS and IP (κ = 0.90) across testing for anti–Jo‐1, anti–PL‐7, anti–PL‐12, anti–MDA‐5, and anti‐EJ autoantibodies. Importantly, LIPS showed superior performance compared to line blot, particularly in the low to intermediate autoantibody titer range, in which misclassification was most common. LIPS identified autoantibodies in patients with IIM who would have been missed by line blot at low titers and would have excluded false positives at higher titers, especially for anti–PL‐7 and anti–PL‐12, many of whom did not have ILD. In light of these findings, LIPS could help decrease both underdiagnosis and false positives of clinically relevant autoantibodies associated with ILD. Although line blot titers of cutoff values ≥71 were generally more reliable, false positives still occurred, underscoring the limitations of fixed thresholds. Analysis of patients without IP further supported these findings. In this cohort, patients who were LIPS positive were enriched for AS, DM, and overlap syndromes with frequent ILD, whereas patients who were LIPS negative were primarily IBM. These diagnostic patterns highlight the greater clinical specificity of LIPS and illustrate that although antibody‐specific cutoffs (≥36 for Jo‐1 and MDA‐5; ≥71 for PL‐7, PL‐12, and EJ) modify line blot interpretation, potential misclassification remains. Overall, LIPS provided the most consistent alignment with IP and clinical phenotype, reinforcing its value for accurate classification and patient stratification. Of note, the quantitative nature of LIPS for detecting MSAs may facilitate earlier recognition of pulmonary involvement, particularly in patients with low‐level autoantibody positivity. In addition, LIPS may help refine diagnostic classification in clinically ambiguous cases and support longitudinal monitoring in therapeutic trials or in the assessment of treatment response. 14

Our analysis of anti‐Ro52 further illustrates these threshold‐dependent challenges. Discrepancies with LIPS were most frequent at line blot titers <36 AU. Although no gold standard exists for anti‐Ro52 antibody detection, the broader dynamic range and quantitative output of LIPS support is its greater interpretive value, 14 , 19 especially at low to borderline titers (<36 AU), in which line blot appears prone to misclassification. The quantitative resolution of LIPS clarified interpretation in 21 cases and highlighted that line blot is most reliable at titers >36 AU. These findings suggest that although line blot adequately captures Ro52 at moderate to high titers, LIPS may provide useful information in cases with low to moderate titers.

These findings build upon prior studies that have highlighted variability in myositis autoantibody testing across platforms. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 20 Shinoda et al 16 reported poor specificity for line blot (κ = 0.23), compared to RNA‐IP, which improved (κ = 0.75) with stricter thresholds (going from 1 to 2+). In our study, we demonstrate that even 2+ (titer ≥36) can be poorly discriminative for some autoantibodies, particularly non–Jo‐1 autoantibodies. Mecoli et al also found that antibody‐specific thresholds improved agreement with IP (≥15 for anti–Jo‐1, ≥36 for anti–PL‐12/EJ, and ≥71 for anti–PL‐7), but discordance persisted; only a fraction (n = 5 of 19) of discordant cases fit clinical AS. 21 Espinosa‐Ortega et al observed good agreement between line blot and IP for anti–Jo‐1 (κ = 0.69) but only moderate agreement for a small number of patients with anti–MDA‐5 (κ = 0.49). 15 Loganathan et al showed high concordance between ELISA and IP across multiple autoantibodies but reported reduced sensitivity for anti–MDA‐5. 22 Another study on myositis‐specific antibodies in neurologic disorders also identified false‐positive results in more than 1 of 10 patients with nonmyositis diseases when the standard low (≥15) thresholds were used. 23 Our work confirms and extends these observations in a larger, clinically well‐characterized cohort, establishing LIPS as a reliable alternative for autoantibody detection in IIM‐ILD.

These data have important clinical implications. Patients classified as “undefined” by line blot may have a clear antibody positivity by LIPS, with clinical features consistent with AS. LIPS may therefore refine diagnostic classification when results are ambiguous. For clinicians, line blot titers in the 15 to 70 range or multiple antibody positivity should prompt confirmatory testing with another method such as IP. Although some undefined cases may represent true dual positivity, without contemporaneous IP, this remains uncertain, underscoring the need for future work to distinguish true seropositivity from artifactual multiple reactivity.

Our study has several strengths. It represents one of the largest head‐to‐head evaluations of LIPS, line blot, and IP across multiple clinically relevant myositis autoantibodies. We included both patients with IP data and those without, enabling assessment of assay concordance as well as clinical enrichment patterns in the non‐IP cohort. The limitations include its retrospective design, variability in assay timing, and small numbers for rarer antibodies such as EJ. Further, the anti–Jo‐1 analysis was characterized by extreme prevalence imbalance, with very few true negative samples, a setting in which Cohen's κ‐coefficient may substantially underestimate agreement despite high observed concordance (overall agreement 91.4%). Accordingly, κ values should be interpreted in the context of sensitivity and specificity, particularly in antigen groups with marked prevalence imbalance. Discordant results may reflect long gaps between collections or true longitudinal changes in autoantibody levels. Additionally, this referral‐center cohort was enriched for antibody‐positive cases, and availability of IP may have been influenced by clinical and logistical constraints, which should be considered when interpreting concordance metrics. This limits generalizability to unselected screening populations. Finally, as LIPS was performed in 2025 on sera stored since 2016, our findings also highlight the stability of autoantibodies over time. Future work should evaluate LIPS prospectively in multicenter cohorts, incorporate longitudinal sampling, and test whether integration of LIPS into diagnostic workflows improves clinical outcomes. In particular, linking LIPS‐based classification to ILD progression, treatment response, and survival will be critical to determine its clinical use. Such studies could inform future diagnostic algorithms and guidelines, in which reflex testing with LIPS may provide a more accurate and clinically meaningful assessment of myositis autoantibody status.

LIPS demonstrated strong concordance with IP across multiple myositis autoantibodies and provided clearer discrimination than line blot at low to intermediate titers, in which misclassification was most common. Antibody‐specific thresholds (≥36 for Jo‐1 and MDA‐5; ≥71 for PL‐7, PL‐12, and EJ) modified line blot interpretation by shifting sensitivity‐specificity trade‐offs and may improve clinical interpretability in practice; however, residual false positives persisted, particularly among non–Jo‐1 antibodies. By offering quantitative, high–dynamic‐range output, LIPS complements existing assays, reduces diagnostic uncertainty, and has the potential to enhance patient stratification and ILD risk recognition in IIM as it becomes more broadly available.

e‐Figure 1. Heatmaps of autoantibody‐specific assay concordance and ILD status by LIPS quartiles. Each panel displays patients ordered by LIPS status, with negatives shown first, followed by positives divided into quartiles (Q1–Q4) from lower to higher signal. Rows (top to bottom) are: LIPS (quartiles), IP, Line blot (Euroimmun) titer (+/++/+++), Line blot (myositis panel), ELISA, Proximal weakness, ILD status, and Diagnosis. Color coding is as follows: LIPS negatives are soft blue, and Q1–Q4 progress from yellow through orange to red, reflecting increasing intensity. Line blot titers are shown as yellow (+), orange (++), and soft red (+++), with ≤14 (negative) in soft blue and borderline results in grey (if present). Binary assays (IP, ELISA, myositis panel, ILD, Proximal weakness) are soft red for positive, soft blue for negative or absent, and white for not done or missing. Diagnosis categories are displayed in fixed colors: Other = grey, IBM = blue, Antisynthetase syndrome = red, Dermatomyositis = orange, and Overall = purple. Vertical dividers mark quartile boundaries, and panel titles indicate the LIPS positivity cutoff used for binning (30k for Jo‐1 and MDA‐5; 100k for PL‐12, PL‐7, and EJ).

e‐Figure 2. Additional Performance Measures of LIPS and Line Blot Compared with Immunoprecipitation. Bar plots display assay performance across four metrics: sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV), using immunoprecipitation as the reference standard. Line blot results are shown using a cut‐off of ≥36 (blue), a cut‐off of ≥71 (green), and antibody‐specific cut‐offs (orange), compared with the LIPS assay (red). Error bars represent 95% confidence intervals. Statistical comparisons were made against the LIPS assay: *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001; ns = not significant.
