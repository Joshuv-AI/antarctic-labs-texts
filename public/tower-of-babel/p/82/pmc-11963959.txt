Integrating Social Determinants of Health in the Management of Cardiovascular‐Kidney‐Metabolic Syndrome
Authors: Ayana K April‐Sanders
Published: 2024

There is abundant evidence demonstrating a strong association between cardiovascular disease (CVD) and metabolic conditions such as obesity and type 2 diabetes, as well as chronic kidney disease (CKD). Individually, these conditions are highly prevalent. According to the American Heart Association's (AHA) 2024 heart disease and stroke statistics update, CVD was responsible for nearly 20 million deaths globally in 2020. 1 In a global assessment of CKD burden, there was a 41.5% increase in mortality rates across all age groups between 1990 and 2017. 2 Additionally, diabetes affected approximately 10.5% of adults aged 20 to 79 years worldwide in 2021 (equivalent to 536.6 million people), with projections suggesting an increase to 12.2% (783.2 million people) by 2045. 3 Together, heart disease, stroke, kidney disease, and diabetes contributed to >1 million deaths (29%) in the United States in 2021 4 and have halted nearly 5 decades of declining CVD mortality rates. 5 Addressing these significant public health challenges is critical, especially as new therapeutic options emerge to manage these conditions.

Epidemiologic data provide substantial evidence suggesting that the co‐occurrence of ≥1 of these conditions can multiply cardiovascular health and mortality risks. Given the intricate overlapping relationship between cardiovascular, metabolic, and renal disease, the AHA presidential advisory introduced the term cardiovascular‐kidney‐metabolic (CKM) syndrome to describe how heart disease, kidney disease, diabetes, and obesity interact negatively, leading to poor health outcomes. 5 This new concept is expected to help health care professionals stage CKM syndrome, predict its cardiovascular effects, and manage, prevent, or even reverse it throughout life, from childhood to adulthood. Evidence backing this advisory is detailed in another scientific statement. 6 Together, these publications establish a framework aimed at improving CKM health comprehensively and equitably across the population. Furthermore, they have paved the way for a new cardiovascular risk calculator that incorporates CKM health considerations for the first time. 7

As research continues to highlight the interconnected and mutually reinforcing relationship between CVD, CKD, and metabolic disorders, there remains debate on the extent to which these conditions converge into a syndrome, suggesting a shared underlying pathophysiology. Previously, the interplay among these conditions lacked clear parameters, complicating treatment strategies aimed at addressing the spectrum of CKM syndrome. Therefore, the AHA's presidential advisory and accompanying scientific statement aimed to unify diverse specialty‐specific recommendations into a consensus definition of CKM syndrome, along with tools for its detection, prevention, and management. CKM syndrome is categorized into stages as follows 5 :

Stage 0: No CKM risk factors

Stage 1: Excessive or dysfunctional adipose tissue

Stage 2: Presence of metabolic risk factors and CKD

Stage 3: Subclinical CVD in CKM syndrome or its risk equivalents

Stage 4: Clinical CVD in the context of CKM syndrome

Defining CKM syndrome and its staging provides clarity on how therapeutic interventions can be effectively used and supports efforts in prevention and treatment throughout an individual's life. Alongside staging, it is recommended that screening and risk assessments be initiated early in life and conducted regularly as CKM syndrome stages progress. The AHA's Predicting Risk of cardiovascular disease EVENTs (PREVENT) equation, developed for adults aged 30 to 79 years, offers 10‐ and 30‐year estimates of total CVD risk. 7 This equation incorporates estimated glomerular filtration rates as a predictive factor and adjusts for the competing risk of non‐CVD death. Moreover, the model enhances its predictive accuracy by including CKM factors such as urine albumin‐to‐creatinine ratio and hemoglobin A1c, as well as social determinants of health such as the social deprivation index when available.

Managing and preventing CKM syndrome primarily targets excess body fat and insulin resistance, which are key drivers of its harmful effects. The advisory emphasizes lifestyle changes and weight management as initial treatment strategies. 5 Early introduction of medications such as sodium‐glucose contransporter‐2 (SGLT2) inhibitors and glucagon‐like peptide‐1 (GLP‐1) receptor agonists is also recommended to mitigate CVD risk. Educating patients and providing support for adopting healthier lifestyles are additional measures that can enhance CKM health outcomes both at an individual and population level. 8

The advisory underscores that effective management of CKM syndrome hinges significantly on patient access to care, socioeconomic resources, and seamless integration across health systems. Racial and ethnic disparities in CKM syndrome risks are largely influenced by social determinants of health (SDOH). Highlighting this, the AHA advisory emphasizes the integration of SDOH into CKM care, advocating for screening to identify social barriers, assess high‐risk individuals, and tailor clinical management accordingly. In this issue of the Journal of the American Heart Association (JAHA), Li et al explored the association between social risk profile (SRP) and CKM multimorbidity in US adults aged 20 to 79 years using National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey data spanning 1999 to 2018. 9 Using the newly defined criteria, 5 they classified participants into 4 CKM stages based on severity. CKM measures and social risk profiles were assessed through questionnaires and physical examinations. The study identified 8 SRP characteristics aligned with the Healthy People 2030 framework: employment status, poverty/income ratio, food security, educational attainment, health care access, health insurance coverage, housing stability, and marital status. SRP scores were categorized into 4 categories, with higher scores indicating greater social resources. Multinomial logistic regression, adjusted for demographic and behavioral factors, examined how SRP levels and individual measures correlated with CKM stages.

The study revealed that metabolic disorders were the most prevalent CKM condition (86.6%), followed by CVD (10.3%) and CKD (9.9%). CKM syndrome affected 3% to 4% of participants and showed no significant variation by SRP score. A key finding was that lower SRP scores were associated with increased odds of CKM stages: stage 1 (odds ratio [OR], 1.34 [95% CI, 1.06–1.70]), stage 2 (OR, 2.03 [95% CI, 1.06–1.70]), stage 3 (OR, 5.28 [95% CI, 3.29–8.47]), and stage 4 (OR, 5.97 [95% CI, 4.20–8.49]). The study also explored individual social risk factors' impact on CKM syndrome, finding that unemployment, lower poverty/income ratio, and food insecurity were strongly associated with CKM stages, whereas regular health care access did not significantly influence stages 2 to 4. Results varied across sex and racial and ethnic subgroups, with women and White individuals with low SRP scores having higher odds of CKM stage 4 compared with men and Black individuals with low SRP scores (women: OR, 11.36 [95% CI, 7.32–17.61]; men: OR, 3.26 [95% CI, 1.89–5.62]; White individuals: OR, 8.11 [95% CI, 4.73–13.93]; Black individuals: OR, 4.12 [95% CI, 2.08–8.16]; all P for interaction <0.05). The study's findings underscore the significant role of cumulative social disadvantage in the understanding and management of CKM syndrome. Adults with lower SRP scores showed an increased likelihood of developing multimorbid conditions involving CVD, including CKM syndrome. Factors such as education level, food security, and economic status independently impacted the staging of CKM syndrome, highlighting the relevance of addressing SDOH to enhance CKM health equity initiatives.

Li et al examined various SDOH factors, but certain SRP domains raise concerns about their construct validity. For instance, marital status might not fully capture social and community contexts or differentiate between low and high SDOH. Housing security was categorized based on ownership, potentially overlooking unsafe conditions or financial risks among homeowners. Similarly, classifying government insurance as low SDOH without distinguishing uninsured status needs clarification. Mental health indicators such as depression or stress, crucial for understanding CKM health outcomes, 10 were not included. Future research should enhance the scope of SDOH measures, addressing gaps such as community resources, social support, and environmental factors not covered in the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey, to provide a more comprehensive assessment.

Improving CKM health requires identifying and addressing patient and population social needs in clinical and community settings. Prevention and screening strategies should consider age, sex, and racial and ethnic backgrounds. Li et al found that women and White individuals with low SRP had higher odds of CKM stage 4 than men or Black individuals, 9 although the study's cross‐sectional design limits understanding of temporal trends. Future longitudinal studies should assess the impact of interventions on CKM outcomes. 11

Screening for SDOH in clinical populations is essential for CKM prevention and treatment. Several tools exist to assess social needs, such as financial strain, education, personal safety, and the need for assistance. 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 However, collecting data is futile without integrating screening tools, evaluating their impact on clinical outcomes, and implementing effective interventions. A review showed that interventions improved access to resources and reduced social distress but had mixed effects on health outcomes and costs. 18

To address gaps in CKM syndrome research, we need to integrate SDOH screening and data collection into electronic health records and clinical workflows efficiently. Research should measure both health outcomes and health care costs to identify cost‐effective interventions. When referring patients to social services, it is crucial to consider the agency's capacity to support various social needs, which can differ by community and policy. 19 For example, addressing food‐related needs is typically easier than tackling housing or transportation issues. Interventions should also mitigate structural racism and other barriers without stigmatizing patients or overburdening clinicians. 18

Another challenge is finding and motivating physicians to care for patients with CKM with social needs. US medical practice is highly specialized, with cardiologists, endocrinologists, and nephrologists often unwilling to manage issues outside their specialties, relying on primary care physicians to coordinate referrals. However, primary care physicians face their own challenges, because specialists often limit new referrals and have long wait times. Treating cardiovascular disease, metabolic disorders, and chronic kidney disease separately is complex and costly, especially for patients with adverse SDOH. Consolidating these conditions as CKM syndrome offers a unified approach for prevention and management, enhancing our understanding of the condition's underlying mechanisms. To advance CKM syndrome beyond a conceptual framework, we must develop cohesive and effective management strategies that address the chronic social burdens present in our health care system.
