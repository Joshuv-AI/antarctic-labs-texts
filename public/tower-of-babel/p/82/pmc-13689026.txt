Advances in predictive factors for pathological complete response after neoadjuvant therapy in locally advanced rectal cancer
Authors: Xin Lei, Yujia Han, Xiaozhou Wang, Jingyi Hou, Wei Cheng, Xi Yu, Lijie He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFor locally advanced rectal cancer (LARC), neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (nCRT) combined with total mesorectal excision has emerged as the standard therapeutic approach, as delineated in contemporary clinical guidelines. Pathological complete response (pCR) serves as a pivotal metric for assessing nCRT efficacy, with strong correlations to long-term survival outcomes and organ preservation strategies such as the “watch-and-wait” approach. However, inter-individual variability in treatment responses results in only approximately 20% of patients attaining pCR following nCRT. Accurate identification of these patients is imperative not only for optimizing therapeutic efficacy but also for substantially augmenting patient survival benefits. This article conducts a systematic review of domestic and international literature to synthesize the current research landscape regarding pCR predictive factors in LARC patients post-nCRT across five dimensions: patient-specific characteristics, tumor clinicopathological features, hematological markers, imaging modalities, molecular biomarkers, and multidimensional integrated predictive models. The review emphasizes the synergistic potential of integrating traditional clinical parameters with emerging radiomics, multi-omics biomarkers, and artificial intelligence algorithms. It seeks to furnish a theoretical foundation and reference framework for the development of personalized precision treatment strategies, optimization of therapeutic pathways, and maximization of organ preservation opportunities in clinical practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13014-026-02891-0.Keywords: Locally advanced, Rectal cancer, Neoadjuvant therapy, Pathological complete response

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most common malignant tumor globally and the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality [1]. It is estimated that in 2022, there were nearly 20 million new cancer cases worldwide, with approximately 9.7 million cancer-related deaths. Among these, the new cases and deaths from colorectal cancer accounted for about 9.6% and 9.3% of the total, respectively. Notably, the incidence of colorectal cancer has been rising annually and shows a clear trend toward affecting younger populations [2–5]. Locally advanced rectal cancer (LARC) typically refers to rectal cancer in which the tumor has infiltrated through the full thickness of the intestinal wall (T3 stage) or deeper, and/or is accompanied by regional lymph node metastasis (N+). Due to its specific anatomical structure and high risk of recurrence, surgery alone often fails to achieve satisfactory therapeutic outcomes.

Traditionally, neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (nCRT) combined with total mesorectal excision (TME) [6] has been the standard treatment for locally advanced rectal cancer (LARC) [7]. Recently, total neoadjuvant therapy (TNT) has emerged as the preferred standard of care to further improve clinical outcomes [8]. Among the indicators for assessing treatment efficacy, pathological complete response (pCR) is regarded as the “gold standard,” which is associated with a significantly lower local recurrence rate and a markedly improved 5-year disease-free survival rate [9, 10]. Crucially, if the likelihood of achieving pCR could be precisely predicted by clinical response prior to surgery, these patients would be excellent candidates for organ preservation and non-operative management (such as the “Watch and Wait” strategy) following neoadjuvant therapy, thereby avoiding radical surgery and its associated complications. In addition, another critical benefit of predicting treatment response is the early detection of poor responders, enabling clinicians to timely intensify or adjust their planned therapy. Therefore, accurately predicting treatment response holds profound clinical importance for optimizing therapeutic pathways and achieving personalized medicine.

Despite the clear benefits of identifying pCR candidates, relying on single modalities often yields limited predictive accuracy. The integration of multi-omics data, advanced imaging techniques, and artificial intelligence offers promising avenues to overcome these limitations. In this article, we systematically review the evolving landscape of predictive factors for pCR following nCRT in LARC. We place particular emphasis on the synergistic value of combining traditional clinical parameters with novel radiomic and genomic biomarkers. Such advancements are pivotal for transforming clinical decision-making, minimizing unnecessary toxicity, and maximizing organ preservation opportunities for patients with LARC (Fig. 1).

Prediction factors of pCR based on patient characteristics

Age

Accumulating evidence identifies age as an independent predictor of pCR following nCRT in LARC (Table 3). Studies by Lin et al. and Mehraj et al. corroborate the significant influence of age on treatment outcomes [11, 12]. Notably, Zhang et al. highlighted that patients younger than 40 years exhibit significantly lower pCR rates [13]. This reduced responsiveness in younger cohorts is often attributed to more aggressive pathological features and advanced clinical staging at presentation, which collectively contribute to a poorer prognosis [14, 15].

Patient characteristics predictive factors

Blood glucose levels

The correlation between pre-treatment blood glucose levels and pCR remains a subject of debate. While one multivariate analysis suggested that non-diabetic patients with pre-nCRT glucose levels ≤ 6.1 mmol/L are associated with higher pCR rates, contrasting findings were reported by Lin et al., who found no significant association between glycemic status and pCR [11]. This discrepancy underscores the need for further investigation into the metabolic predictors of nCRT efficacy.

Gender

Evidence concerning the impact of gender on pCR is conflicting. A univariate analysis by Michele et al. indicated a potential association between female gender and improved pCR rates [11]. However, studies by Huang et al. and Lin et al. found that gender did not emerge as a significant independent predictor [11, 16]. These discrepancies suggest that gender alone may lack sufficient predictive value for risk stratification unless adjusted for other confounding variables.

Other clinical and socioeconomic factors

Beyond the factors mentioned above, several traditional clinicopathological variables appear to have limited predictive value. Lin et al. reported no significant correlation between pCR rates and race, body mass index, smoking status, alcohol consumption, or comorbidities such as hypertension [11]. However, socioeconomic factors may play a critical role. Eisar et al. observed that lack of health insurance is associated with reduced pCR rates, hypothesizing that this disparity stems from inadequate access to comprehensive, guideline-adherent treatment protocols [17]. This finding highlights the importance of addressing systemic barriers to optimize therapeutic outcomes.

Tumor-specific characteristics and impact on treatment response

Clinical staging

Clinical T and N staging serve as established predictors of pCR rates in patients with LARC, with lower stages consistently correlating with superior outcomes (Table 4). Eisar et al. observed that reduced clinical T and N stages were associated with higher pCR rates [17]. This finding was substantiated by Hajer et al., whose multivariate analysis confirmed T2 status and pre-treatment N0 classification as independent predictors of pCR [18]. Additionally, Asif et al. identified node-negative status as a robust predictor of pCR [12]. Other studies have also reported a significant correlation between the pre-nCRT N stage and the achievement of pCR. Collectively, these investigations indicate that advanced clinical stages are associated with diminished pCR rates. This trend is likely due to biological factors inherent to advanced tumors, such as higher proliferative activity, richer stromal components, and reduced sensitivity to chemoradiotherapy.

Tumor clinicopathological predictive factors

Tumor size

Tumor dimensions, encompassing length and circumference, serve as critical determinants of pCR in LARC. A tumor length of less than 5 cm has been identified as an independent predictor of achieving pCR following nCRT, whereas endoscopic sizes exceeding 6 cm are significantly associated with non-response. Similarly, smaller tumor circumferences correlate with higher pCR rates, a finding hypothesized to stem from the biological characteristics of smaller, mass-type tumors; these likely possess a richer blood supply that facilitates better penetration of chemoradiotherapeutic agents and exhibit more uniform cell proliferation, rendering them more susceptible to complete eradication [19].

Beyond measurements, the circumferential involvement ratio and tumor volume are vital metrics for assessing treatment response. Patients with low circumferential involvement demonstrate significantly higher pCR rates, confirming this ratio as a key indicator of tumor burden [20, 21]. While some studies found no direct link between pre-treatment size and outcome, they noted that the degree of post-treatment shrinkage effectively predicts pCR [22]. Furthermore, tumor area (the product of length and maximum depth) and Gross Tumor Volume (GTV) serve as independent predictors, with smaller pre-treatment values being favorable. Although specific GTV thresholds vary across studies, cutoffs around 37–40 cm³ have been proposed to distinguish patients likely to achieve pCR [23–25].

Tumor location

Tumor location, defined by both the distance from the anal verge and the circumferential position, significantly influences pCR rates. While the distance from the anal verge is an independent predictor, studies suggest a non-linear relationship where tumors located 6–8 cm from the verge achieve the highest pCR rates, whereas those situated very low (< 4 cm) or high (> 8 cm) are less likely to respond completely, likely due to variations in anatomical characteristics and treatment sensitivity [19, 26]. Additionally, Wang et al. analyzed 486 LARC patients treated with nCRT followed by total mesorectal excision and found that anterior wall tumors had the highest pCR rates, significantly surpassing those of lateral and posterior wall tumors [27].

Differentiation grade and histological type

Multiple studies have demonstrated that well-differentiated tumors are strong predictors of pCR [17, 28]. A systematic review and meta-analysis, encompassing 17 clinical studies, demonstrated a significant correlation between differentiation grade and pCR rates, with moderately to well-differentiated tumors showing superior Tumor Regression Grades and higher pCR rates [29]. Nadeem et al. reviewed 108 cases comparing mucinous adenocarcinoma, signet ring cell carcinoma, and conventional adenocarcinoma, finding that conventional adenocarcinoma exhibited the highest tumor regression rate [30].

Invasion and budding

Primary perineural invasion and lymphovascular invasion significantly impact treatment response; notably, the absence of these features has been identified as one of the strongest predictors of pCR [31, 32]. Furthermore, tumor budding and poorly differentiated clusters serve as hallmarks of invasive growth in colorectal cancer and act as independent prognostic factors. Research indicates a biological continuum from larger to smaller tumor clusters, with tumor budding exhibiting greater invasive potential compared to poorly differentiated clusters [33].

Tumor microenvironment (TME)

The TME serves as the dynamic “soil” for tumor growth and is intricately linked to treatment resistance and response. Recent research highlights intratumoral microbiota as potential independent predictors of response to nCRT in LARC, where interactions between microbes and cancer-associated fibroblasts mediate therapeutic outcomes. Notably, significant differences in microbial alpha and beta diversity have been observed between patients achieving pCR and those who do not, offering a novel reference for predicting treatment efficacy [34].

Immune cell composition within the TME further dictates nCRT success, with specific biomarkers serving as robust spatial predictors. Studies indicate that the upregulation of CD8, IL17RE, LAG3, and OX40, alongside high levels of HLA-DR/MHC-II and stromal B cells, correlates strongly with favorable outcomes [35, 36]. Conversely, the enrichment of CD4 + FoxP3+ regulatory T cells (Tregs) hinder post-treatment immune activation; successful therapy is typically characterized by increased densities of CD3+, CD8+, and dendritic cells coupled with a marked decrease in Treg populations, confirming that suppressing Treg activity is crucial for achieving pCR [37].

Relationship between tumor and surrounding tissues

The invasive nature of tumor cells, characterized by the breach of tissue boundaries into adjacent structures, blood vessels, or lymphatics, serves as a critical marker of malignancy and a prerequisite for metastasis, directly influencing pCR rates in LARC. For instance, Osman et al. demonstrated that patients with persistent extramural vascular invasion (EMVI) positivity following nCRT exhibited significantly lower pCR rates compared to those who converted to EMVI-negative status, highlighting the prognostic value of dynamic MRI assessments of EMVI and mesorectal fascia involvement [38]. Although current research on the predictive accuracy of EMVI and MRF status remains limited, these features represent promising indicators for evaluating treatment response, warranting further validation to optimize clinical decision-making.

Hematological indicators

Serum tumor markers

Serum tumor markers, valued for their ease of detection, play a significant role in monitoring treatment efficacy, with Carbohydrate antigen 19−9 confirmed as an independent predictor of pCR through multivariate regression analysis [11]. The predictive utility of carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) remains more nuanced; while some studies identify a baseline CEA level below 5 µg/L as a strong independent predictor of pCR following nCRT [13]. This discrepancy suggests that future analyses should prioritize evaluating the dynamic changes in CEA levels throughout the treatment course rather than relying solely on baseline measurements to accurately forecast patient outcomes.

Inflammation-related indicators

Blood inflammatory markers are easily accessible, minimally invasive, and cost-effective, making them a promising tool for future prediction of pCR. Xu et al. found that a pre-nCRT neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) ≥ 3.1 predicted a poor tumor response [39]. Similarly, a univariate analysis that baseline NLR and platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) levels were significantly lower in the pCR group compared to the non-pCR group [40]. In a prospective study of 34 LARC patients, Shahram et al. observed correlations between pCR and both a PLR > 1.8 during nCRT and an NLR > 3.21, supporting the dynamic monitoring of inflammatory indices to predict efficacy [41]. The serum albumin levels (SAL) > 3.5 mg/dL and an NLR < 5 was positively correlated with pCR in LARC patients following nCRT. Consequently, hypoalbuminemia and a high NLR could serve as indicators for adopting more aggressive nCRT and postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy strategies.

Other indicators

Beyond traditional markers, emerging indicators like circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) and serum metabolomics offer new avenues for predicting pCR. Tatiana et al. found that patients with negative pre-treatment ctDNA or rapid ctDNA clearance during therapy achieved higher pCR rates, although ctDNA alone showed limited specificity and no significant correlation with long-term prognosis [42]. Complementing this, Jia et al. utilized liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry to identify 15 core metabolic markers, including sphingosine and tryptophan derivatives, which were closely linked to treatment response and hold promise for developing personalized strategies for LARC patients [43].

Treatment-related factors

Radiation dose and regimen

Regarding radiation dose and treatment regimens, dose escalation has shown promise, with Carolina et al. reporting that a mean dose of 54.3 Gy achieved higher pCR rates compared to standard dosing [44]. In terms of protocol optimization, short-course radiotherapy followed by consolidation chemotherapy not only yields superior pCR rates over long-course chemoradiotherapy but also reduces treatment duration and costs while improving patient adherence [8]. Furthermore, combining nCRT with immunotherapy, such as the PD-1 inhibitor Sintilimab, has demonstrated significantly enhanced pCR rates with a favorable safety profile, highlighting the potential of integrated therapeutic strategies [45].

Interval to surgery

The time interval between the completion of neoadjuvant therapy and surgery is a critical, controllable factor affecting pCR. Research consistently indicates that a longer interval correlates with higher pCR rates. Eisar et al. similarly identified that delaying surgery for more than 6–8 weeks after the completion of radiotherapy is associated with pCR [17]. These findings provide a rationale for optimizing surgical timing in clinical practice as appropriately extending the interval allows for a sufficient window for tumor regression.

Radiomics and artificial intelligence: a new paradigm for non-invasive pCR prediction

Traditional imaging techniques

Pelvic Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) remains the gold standard for preoperative assessment of LARC, with subgroup analyses by Jason et al. indicating that 3T MRI offers superior performance compared to 1.5T systems [46]. High-resolution T2-weighted imaging effectively delineates tumor invasion depth and relationships with surrounding tissues, while key parameters such as T stage, N stage, circumferential resection margin status, and extramural vascular invasion serve as critical predictors of pCR (Table 5).

Hematological markers and imaging predictive models

Complementing structural imaging, Zhang et al. demonstrated that morphological changes, specifically reductions in maximum tumor area (MTA) and maximum tumor length (MTL) following nCRT as well as the pre-treatment distance from the anal verge (DTA), are significantly correlated with pCR with high sensitivity and specificity [22]. Furthermore, Diffusion-Weighted Imaging (DWI) provides functional assessment by quantifying tumor cell density through the Apparent Diffusion Coefficient, while endorectal ultrasound serves as a valuable supplementary tool for staging mid-to-low rectal cancers, although its predictive accuracy remains heavily dependent on operator experience.

Emerging functional imaging technologies

Amide Proton Transfer-weighted (APTw) imaging represents a novel MRI technique that reflects tumor microenvironment characteristics by detecting endogenous mobile proteins, peptide concentrations, and intracellular pH. In a study of 145 LARC patients, multiple histogram parameters of the primary tumor, specifically APTw-10%, APTw-90%, minimum, maximum, and median values, were significantly higher in patients achieving pCR compared to non-pCR patients [47]. The parameters were negatively correlated with Tumor Regression Grade (TRG), suggesting that APTw imaging offers a unique perspective for predicting pCR by revealing underlying tumor metabolic activity and microenvironmental features.

Complementing MRI, Contrast-Enhanced Ultrasound (CEUS) assesses tumor activity through microcirculatory blood perfusion. Bai et al. retrospectively analyzed 83 LARC patients and reported significant differences in Peak Intensity (PI) and Area Under the Curve between pCR and non-pCR groups, confirming CEUS as a valuable tool for predicting neoadjuvant treatment response and prognosis [48]. Furthermore, Transrectal Ultrasound-Guided Core Biopsy (TRUS-TCB) has emerged as a promising strategy. In a Phase II prospective trial that combining TRUS-TCB with conventional assessments significantly improved pCR diagnostic accuracy while maintaining a low complication rate of only 1.6%, thereby offering a viable approach to avoid overtreatment and enhance sphincter preservation [49].

Beyond these modalities, other emerging techniques such as [68Ga]Ga-FAPI-04 PET/MRI and [18 F]FDG PET/CT are gaining attention for their utility in predicting pCR. Recent studies have highlighted the potential of these molecular imaging methods, noting that while contrast-enhanced MRI showed relatively low sensitivity (55.56%) in comparison, these advanced PET-based techniques provide complementary metabolic information that may refine preoperative assessment strategies [50].

Radiomics and deep learning

Radiomics enhances prediction accuracy by extracting high-throughput features from medical images to quantify tumor heterogeneity. Li et al. analyzed 165 radiomic features from MRI scans obtained before and after neoadjuvant radiotherapy in LARC patients, identifying seven key features to construct a model capable of estimating pCR rates [51]. Similarly, histogram analysis of APTw imaging has shown promise; parameters such as skewness, kurtosis, and entropy were positively correlated with Tumor Regression Grade. A joint model combining these imaging features with clinical parameters demonstrated high sensitivity and specificity, underscoring its significant predictive value [47].

Building on these findings, Tian et al. developed and compared four single-modality radiomics models, two delta-radiomics models, and four multi-modal fusion models [47]. Their comparative analysis revealed that the multi-modal fusion model, which integrated post-treatment Diffusion-Weighted Imaging (DWI) features with delta changes in both DWI and T2-weighted images (DWI-post + DWI-delta + T2-delta), achieved the highest performance in predicting pCR. This highlights the superiority of combining multiple imaging sequences and temporal changes over single-modality approaches.

Deep learning algorithms offer further improvements in predictive efficacy by automatically extracting complex features and minimizing human error associated with manual segmentation. However, research in this domain remains in its early stages. To ensure robust clinical application, future studies must focus on validating these models with larger, multi-center sample sizes to confirm their generalizability and reliability across diverse patient populations.

Molecular biomarkers and emerging predictive factors such as the gut microbiota

Genomic and transcriptomic biomarkers

Recent advances in genomic and transcriptomic profiling have established a more precise framework for predicting treatment outcomes in colorectal cancer (Table 6). A global large-scale multi-omics study introduced the CRPS classification system, comprising five subtypes that offer superior prognostic accuracy compared to the traditional CMS classification, thereby facilitating the identification of molecular subtypes associated with pCR. Beyond classification systems, specific signaling pathways play a critical role in treatment sensitivity; for instance, oleic acid may reduce pCR rates by activating the PI3K/Akt pathway, whereas deoxycholic acid exhibits anti-tumor effects by inhibiting the MAPK pathway [52]. While some studies suggest that mutations in genes such as BRAF, NRAS, APC, or TP53 are linked to lower pCR rates compared to wild-type tumors, others report conflicting findings regarding the predictive value of PI3K and MAPK pathway mutations [53–55]. Similarly, the impact of KRAS mutations remains debated, with some evidence indicating that wild-type status correlates with higher pCR rates and that specific variants like G12V and G13D may influence tumor regression [56, 57]. Additionally, low EGFR expression and high Ki-67 levels have been identified as favorable indicators for achieving pCR [58].

Molecular biomarkers, gut microbiota, and multidimensional predictive models

Microsatellite instability (MSI) status serves as a pivotal molecular indicator, particularly for immunotherapy response. Tumors characterized as MSI-High (MSI-H) or mismatch repair deficient (dMMR) generally demonstrate heightened sensitivity to immune checkpoint inhibitors. Zhang et al. reported promising pCR rates in dMMR/MSI-H LARC patients treated with neoadjuvant PD-1 inhibitor monotherapy [59]. The deeper insights into the immune microenvironment of dMMR patients receiving neoadjuvant PD-1 blockade, revealing that overexpression of HLA-DQA1 andHLA-DQB1, along with a higher density of pre-existing CD8 + T cells, strongly correlates with pCR [60].

Beyond genetic mutations and immune markers, the presence of cancer stem cells appears to influence outcomes. Zhang et al. observed that younger LARC patients often harbor a higher load of CD133 + tumor stem cells, a factor associated with reduced pCR rates [13]. While individual markers like specific gene mutations or MSI status provide valuable clues, integrating these diverse factors into comprehensive models is essential for accurately stratifying patients and optimizing personalized treatment strategies.

Metabolomic biomarkers

Research indicates significant alterations in energy, lipid, and bile acid metabolism during colorectal cancer progression, with diagnostic models based on key metabolites achieving sensitivity and specificity exceeding 90%. These metabolites likely influence pCR by modulating tumor cell proliferation and chemoradiotherapy sensitivity, serving as potential non-invasive biomarkers. For instance, Wang et al. analyzed serum samples from 165 LARC patients and identified disturbances in phenylalanine and essential amino acid metabolism as fundamental drivers of varied responses to nCRT [61]. Similarly, Lv et al. tracked dynamic changes during nCRT, identifying eight specific metabolites, including L-norleucine, betaine, and hypoxanthine, as robust biomarkers capable of predicting treatment sensitivity [62].

The utility of plasma metabolites is further enhanced by their ease of sampling and high stability, making them ideal candidates for clinical application. Buckley et al. demonstrated this potential by analyzing pre-treatment serum from 52 rectal cancer patients, uncovering 16 metabolites significantly associated with pathological response; notably, 13 of these also correlated with overall survival, linking early metabolic signatures to long-term outcomes [63]. To harness the complexity of such massive datasets, the integration of machine learning algorithms has become indispensable. These advanced computational tools facilitate the precise screening of key biomarkers from high-throughput metabolic data, thereby accelerating the translation of metabolomic findings into reliable, personalized predictive tools for clinical practice.

Gut microbiota biomarkers

The gut microbiota significantly influences tumor treatment response by modulating the immune microenvironment and metabolic outputs. This offers a novel perspective for predicting pCR in LARC. Beneficial shifts in microbial composition are often observed following neoadjuvant immunochemoradiotherapy (nICRT). Responders typically exhibit increased abundance of Lactobacillus, Eubacterium, Lachnospiraceae, and Blautia wexlerae [64, 65]. This suggests a protective metabolic role. In contrast, non-responders frequently harbor higher levels of Bacteroides, Prevotella, Porphyromonas, Coriobacteriaceae, and Fusobacterium. Teng et al. further linked Bacteroides-mediated nucleotide biosynthesis to treatment resistance [66]. They also identified decreased microbial diversity and elevated uric acid as potential prognostic markers post-nCRT.

Distinct microbial signatures at both the phylum and genus levels further differentiate treatment outcomes. Fan et al. reported that dominant phyla like Proteobacteria, Actinobacteria, Firmicutes, Thermi, and Bacteroidetes are present in both responders and non-responders [67]. However, specific taxa serve as key discriminators. The pCR group is characterized by the presence of Thermi and the overexpression of Alloprevotella rava. The non-pCR group is marked by Sphingomonadaceae, particularly Sphingomonas and Acinetobacter, as well as bacteria such as Porphyromonas asaccharolytica. These configurations range from the enrichment of butyrate producers and Lactobacillus to the depletion of pathogenic genera. Consequently, analyzing gut microbiota composition provides a promising and non-invasive strategy for stratifying patients and optimizing neoadjuvant therapy protocols.

Construction of multi-dimensional prediction models and prospects for clinical translation

The efficacy of a single predictive factor is often limited, making prediction models that integrate multi-dimensional information a research trend. Currently developed models are mainly based on the integration of clinical parameters with blood indicators, or clinical pathological features with imaging indicators, demonstrating predictive efficacy significantly superior to that of single indicators.

Gut microbiome characteristics and enterotypes exhibit distinct variations that can distinguish patients with a favorable response to nCRT [68]. An integrated model constructed using baseline Clostridium sensu stricto 1 levels, the fold change of Enteromonas, blood herpesvirus entry mediator levels, and lymphocyte count has been developed to predict nCRT efficacy. Lu et al. developed predictive models based on clinical parameters, blood parameters, and a combination of both [69]. Upon comparison, they found that the combined clinical-blood parameter model demonstrated the best performance.

The LARC patients from three centers who had complete pre-treatment clinical data and T1-weighted and T2-weighted MRI scans [70]. After extracting and screening tumor lesion features, they constructed multilayer perceptron (MLP) models using these features either alone or in combination with clinical variables. Their results indicated that the radiomics model outperformed the pure clinical model, while the combined clinical-radiomics model achieved the best predictive performance for both clinical complete response (cCR) and pCR. Similarly, Lu et al. developed and compared integrated models that incorporated clinical factors, MRI-based radiomics features, and deep learning algorithms to demonstrate their effectiveness [71]. They found that a model combining contrast-enhanced ultrasound radiomics scores with CA19-9 levels yielded an AUC significantly higher than that of the radiomics or clinical models alone.

Discussion

The pursuit of pCR in LARC following nCRT represents a pivotal goal in modern oncology, serving as a surrogate for long-term survival and a prerequisite for organ preservation strategies like “watch-and-wait.” This review synthesizes current evidence across clinical, imaging, hematological, and molecular domains, revealing a clear paradigm shift from reliance on single static indicators toward dynamic, multidimensional integrated models.

While traditional clinicopathological factors such as clinical T/N stage, tumor size, and differentiation degree remain foundational, their predictive power is often limited by subjectivity and low specificity. For instance, while early cT/cN stages correlate with higher pCR rates, they fail to account for the biological heterogeneity of tumors within the same stage. Similarly, conventional MRI, though the gold standard for staging, struggles to distinguish between residual viable tumor cells and post-treatment fibrosis or inflammation, leading to both over- and under-staging. The emergence of functional imaging (e.g., DWI, APTw, PET/MRI) and radiomics has begun to address these gaps by quantifying tissue cellularity, metabolic activity, and microstructural changes that are invisible to the naked eye. However, the lack of standardized acquisition protocols and the “black box” nature of some radiomic features pose challenges for widespread clinical adoption.

Perhaps the most transformative insight from recent literature is the potential of liquid biopsy and multi-omics profiling. The ctDNA dynamics offer a real-time, non-invasive window into tumor burden and molecular evolution, potentially outperforming static baseline markers like CEA. Furthermore, the integration of genomics (e.g., MSI/MMR status), metabolomics, and gut microbiota analysis highlights the critical role of the tumor microenvironment and host-systemic factors in treatment response. The finding that specific gut microbial signatures (e.g., Lactobacillus vs. Fusobacterium) can modulate radio-chemosensitivity opens new avenues for therapeutic intervention, suggesting that microbiota modulation could be a viable strategy to convert non-responders into responders. Nevertheless, most of these biomarkers are currently derived from single-center studies with small sample sizes, necessitating large-scale, prospective validation before routine clinical implementation.

The future of pCR prediction lies not in isolating a single “magic bullet” marker but in the synergistic integration of heterogeneous data sources. Artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning algorithms are uniquely positioned to fuse clinical demographics, longitudinal imaging features, blood-based biomarkers, and genomic data into robust predictive nomograms. Early studies demonstrate that such multimodal models significantly outperform any single modality in terms of Area Under the Curve. However, to translate these research findings into clinical practice, future efforts must focus on: (1) conducting multi-center, prospective trials to ensure model generalizability across different populations and treatment protocols; (2) establishing standardized data collection and processing pipelines to reduce variability; and (3) developing user-friendly, interpretable AI tools that can be seamlessly integrated into clinical workflows.

Conclusion

In summary, while significant progress has been made in identifying predictors of pCR in LARC, no single factor currently possesses sufficient accuracy to guide clinical decision-making in isolation. The convergence of advanced imaging, liquid biopsy, multi-omics, and AI offers a promising pathway toward truly personalized medicine. By accurately stratifying patients based on their likelihood of achieving pCR, clinicians can optimize treatment intensification for poor responders and safely de-escalate therapy or pursue organ preservation for good responders, ultimately improving both oncological outcomes and quality of life for patients with locally advanced rectal cancer.
