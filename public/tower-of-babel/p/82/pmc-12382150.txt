Clinical profile, atrophy and inheritance patterns of pathogenic MAPT gene mutations in Frontotemporal dementia detected using whole exome sequencing: a single-center first report from India
Authors: Subasree Ramakrishnan, Faheem Arshad, B. S. Keerthana, Susan Bosco, Arun Gokul Pon, V. H. Ganaraja, Deekshitha Madhusudhan, R. Mahima, Gautham Arunachal, Karthick Kulanthaivelu, Suvarna Alladi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background/ObjectivesFrontotemporal Dementia (FTD) is one of the common causes of early-onset degenerative dementia and is a clinically and pathologically heterogeneous group of neurodegenerative disorders. Globally, Microtubule Associated Protein Tau (MAPT), progranulin (GRN), and Chromosome 9 open reading frame 72(C9orf72) are the common FTD genetic mutations. However, they have not been reported from India, and only one progranulin (PGRN) mutation has been reported so far. This study aims to describe the clinical features and radiological patterns of seven patients of FTD harbouring pathogenic MAPT mutations from an Indian cohort of Frontotemporal dementia, using whole-exome sequencing (WES) for the first time.MethodsSubjects with dementia fulfilling the criteria for frontotemporal dementia were recruited from a teaching university hospital in South India. All of them underwent detailed clinical evaluation, neuroimaging, and genetic analysis by Whole Exome Sequencing (WES).ResultsAmong 86 patients with FTD who underwent WES, seven had MAPT mutations. Notably, two are novel variants.ConclusionIn the Indian context, pathogenic MAPT in FTD is being reported for the first time and notably from a single center by WES. Identifying pathogenic MAPT genes is important in planning mutation-specific clinical trials and understanding ethical and cultural differences in genetic FTD inheritance.

Introduction

Frontotemporal dementia (FTD) is one of the most common causes of presenile degenerative dementia before the age of 65 years [1]. Over the past two decades, the three common FTD genes identified globally are Microtubule-associated protein tau (MAPT), progranulin (PGRN), and chromosome 9 open reading frame 72(C9orf72) [1, 2].

Advanced genetic studies in FTD from India are very limited, and earlier detection methods and techniques mostly reported negative results, even in the presence of family history [3, 4]. Whole-exome and whole-genome sequencing in large FTD patient populations are mandatory for FTD gene discovery [5]. Individuals diagnosed with FTD-spectrum disorders often have a family history of dementia or other neurodegenerative disease (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sim$$\end{document}40% of cases). It has been estimated that around one-third of FTD is familial and inherited in an autosomal-dominant manner, a much higher proportion than AD (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$< 1\%)$$\end{document}, highlighting the importance of genetics in the aetiology of FTD [6]. Genetic forms have distinct clinical presentations, pathologies, and neuroanatomical patterns compared to sporadic FTD.

MAPT is located on chromosome 17q21.31. In 1998, autosomal dominant mutations in the microtubule (MT)-associated protein tau (MAPT) gene that encodes tau were reported in affected individuals from nine of the 13 families and linked with FTDP-17 [6, 7]. Accumulation of misfolded microtubule-associated protein tau (MAPT) is the hallmark of tauopathies. The MAPT gene has 2 haplotypes (H1 and H2). H1 haplotypes are found in all ethnic groups, but H2 haplotypes are primarily found in Europeans and Southwest Asian and are linked to a reduced risk of developing neurodegenerative disorders. The genetic profiles of some tauopathies, i.e., Frontotemporal dementia, Parkinson’s disease, etc., might therefore reveal differences across ethnic groups [8].

In the Western literature, patients with MAPT mutations account for 5–30% of FTD cases [2, 9, 10]. Most of the pathogenic mutations are either coding mutations affecting microtubule-binding repeats and their flanking region (exons 9–13) or variants in exon 10 and intron 10 that cause differential splicing and a relative increase in 4R tau. Most mutations in other exons are associated with predominant 3R pathology [11]. Few pathogenic mutations outside exons 9–13 have also been reported [12, 13].

Over 100 MAPT mutations have been discovered, with the majority presenting with clinical features of behavioural change, personality changes, subsequent emergence of semantic deficits, memory decline, and motor features like Parkinsonism [14, 15]. The behavioral and personality abnormalities include disinhibition, apathy, loss of empathy, impulsive and/or compulsive behaviour, lack of personal hygiene, hyperorality, excessive use of alcohol or other drugs, and aggressiveness [16]. The cognitive symptoms commonly observed include inattention and executive dysfunction. Semantic impairment and episodic memory difficulties are other symptoms [17–20]. The phenotype and genotype correlation vary among different cohorts and ethnicities. There is high inter- and intrafamilial variability in the phenotype of affected individuals, even in patients with the same variant [21, 22]. Parkinsonism associated with MAPT is characterized by the absence of resting tremors and poor/no responsiveness to levodopa. The age of symptom onset and duration is variable, with an average age of onset of 49 years and duration of disease of 8.5 years [23]. Neuropathology and neuroimaging can also reveal diverse pictures similar to phenotypic variability.

MAPT mutations are associated with symmetric atrophy of the anterior temporal lobe and lesser atrophy of the orbitofrontal and lateral prefrontal cortices [24–27]. MAPT mutations are associated with an atrophy rate intermediate between those of GRN and C9orf72 [28]. In asymptomatic MAPT mutation carriers, abnormalities of brain structure, connectivity, and white matter tract integrity may be detectable several years before symptom onset. [16] Medial temporal lobe atrophy is reported often in MAPT mutations affecting the splicing of exon 10, while lateral temporal lobe involvement is noted in mutations in the coding region. However, there are no reports of MAPT mutations presenting as FTD from the Indian continent. In this study, the clinical, radiological patterns, cognitive profile, and pedigree analysis of seven patients of FTD harboring pathogenic MAPT mutations identified using whole-exome sequencing for the first time and from a single center using whole-exome sequencing (WES) are being described. Understanding the nature of clinical heterogeneity and ethnical differences in genetic FTD, especially from the South Asian cohort, is important for biological insights, treatment options, and future directives.

Methods

The FTD subjects fulfilling international consensus criteria for FTD and FTD overlap syndromes were enrolled in a cohort of frontotemporal dementia from the cognitive disorder registry of a university referral hospital during the period 2016 to 2022 [30, 31]. Patients were interviewed with a structured questionnaire format. Detailed family history, including the presence of mental sub normality, learning disability, psychiatric illness, seizures, movement disorders, weakness of limbs, memory or cognitive disorders, and similar illnesses, was documented. A three-generation pedigree analysis was drawn and analyzed.

Patients underwent a comprehensive mental status examination, documentation with scores of revised Addenbrooke Cognitive Examination-III (ACE-III), Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) scale, Neuropsychiatry Inventory (NPI) scores and Tulia apraxia scale. [32–35]. Detailed clinical neurological examinations were documented in a pre-designed proforma. Patients were clinically diagnosed by cognitive neurology experts, and the overlap syndromes, if any, were documented. Subjects underwent structural imaging using a 3 Tesla MRI, including structural imaging for grey matter volumes and densities, along with advanced imaging sequences like DTI and resting state fMRI sequences, in a subset of patients. When MRI was contraindicated or not feasible in dementia subjects, plain CT brain was performed to rule out any structural causes and understand the pattern of atrophy. The workup for secondary causes of dementia included evaluation of serum Vitamin B 12, thyroid function test, biochemical assay, complete hemogram, HIV and VDRL status, electroencephalography, autoimmune and paraneoplastic profile, and cerebrospinal fluid evaluation.

Subjects with FTD who consented to blood sampling underwent genetic testing by Next Generation Sequencing. Whole-exome sequencing libraries were prepared using 37 Mb capture probe sets (including protein-coding genes and the mitochondrial genome by Twist Biosciences kit, USA, and were sequenced on the Illumina NextSeq 550 platform using 2x150 bp read chemistry (Illumina: San Diego, CA, USA). Reads from the sequence output were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38) using Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (BWA). Variants to the reference were called using the Genomic Analysis Tool Kit (GATK; http://www.broadinstitute.org/gatk) to produce variant files in VCF format. Highly confident variants with depth \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(DP> 10x)$$\end{document} and Phred score \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(GQ> 20)$$\end{document} were selected for annotation, and further analysis was performed using VarSeq (Golden Helix, Inc., USA) to obtain potential and clinically relevant variants. Pathogenicity of the variants was assessed based on American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) guidelines. [36]. This study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Ethical Committee No/NIMHANS/(BS & NS Division)/24th meeting/2020 dated June 11, 2020. Repeat Prime PCR was not performed.

Results

In our FTD registry of approximately 450 patients, 86 patients of FTD underwent whole exome sequencing. A family history of dementia or psychiatric illness was present in 24 out of 86 (27.9%). There were seven likely pathogenic/pathogenic variants detected in MAPT. Descriptions of these patients are given below.

Discussion

This study highlights the clinical presentation, phenotype, and genotype correlation of seven MAPT pathogenic variants from a single-center university referral hospital in an Indian cohort. Hereby, we also describe two novel variants detected for the first time from a cohort of Frontotemporal dementia subjects from a single university hospital referral center in South India. Earlier studies from India in FTD have not reported MAPT and C9orf72 mutations in dementia subjects, despite a positive family history stating various genetic and environmental factors adding to FTD heterogeneity. [37] Aswathy et al, 2016 found one truncating mutation in the PGRN from an Indian patient, which was pathogenic [38]. Das et al, 2013 screened 81 FTD patients for mutations of MAPT and PGRN and found 11 non-pathogenic SNP loci in MAPT and three in PGRN but did not get any pathogenic variants [3].

The current study identified seven MAPT likely pathogenic/pathogenic variants using whole-exome sequencing, which is one of the largest series from a single center in India reported so far. With Next Generation Sequencing being carried out more often recently, newer genes are being detected, as in the case of FTD overlap syndrome with MND, a novel TBK1 variant was reported from Bangalore, India [39]. The same group had also reported a rare variant of CSF1R masquerading as frontotemporal dementia [40] and genes of primary microgliopathy presenting as degenerative dementias [41].

FTD is a highly heritable disease, even more than AD, and is often noted in those with behavioural FTD and FTD-ALS [1, 2]. Family history in FTD is reported in 20 to 50% of cases, whereas family history as low as 9.5% in behavioral FTD has been reported from the Asian population [42]. More than half of FTD patients with a strong family history have no mutation detected, implying that further genetic influences await discovery in FTD. In our cohort, two out of seven with MAPT mutation had a positive family history. MAPT was the most common gene mutation detected in our cohort, and two out of seven likely pathogenic/pathogenic variants were found to be novel. A high clinical heterogeneity exists in FTD due to MAPT mutations without a clear phenotype-genotype correlation in most cases. In our cohort, the age range was from 25 to 68 years. There are only seven cases of behavioural variant FTD presenting before age 30 reported in the literature due to the MAPT mutation. [46] Two patients had a family history. All had bilateral frontotemporal atrophy. One among the seven cases had asymmetric atrophy, and two had associated non-specific white matter signal changes. All of them had behavioural, psychiatric features, cognitive decline, and two had associated Parkinsonism. Interestingly, three presented with rapid cognitive decline. One patient had non-fluent aphasia.

This is one of the largest and first reports on pathogenic MAPT mutations in FTD from India with clinical, neuroimaging features, and genetic diagnosis, and this will pave the way for future FTD genetic studies and clinical trials. Misdiagnosis, underreporting by families, cultural factors, and publication bias have all been reported as causes for the lack of genetic data from Asian countries, and multicenter international collaborative research will be a way forward [52]. Conclusions

This is the largest series of MAPT genetic mutations detected for the first time from a single center in the Indian FTD series and exemple the need for more WES in presenile dementia subjects and has expanded our knowledge on the spectrum of mutations identified in South Asia in genetic FTD, and has implications in clinical trials, given the ethnic, environmental, and genetic heterogeneity. Novel variants of MAPT mutations identified and not reported in available databases highlight the need for more genetic testing by WES in FTD.
