Disease-Modifying Drugs for Alzheimer’s Disease: Bending the Curve
Authors: Faheem Arshad, Suvarna Alladi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is the most prevalent form of dementia worldwide and the leading cause of disability in the aging population. While several disease-modifying therapies, particularly anti-amyloid monoclonal antibodies, have recently gained approval, their clinical accessibility remains limited, especially in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). A major prerequisite for initiating these treatments is a biomarker-based diagnosis, typically involving cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis or amyloid/tau positron emission tomography (PET) imaging. However, these biomarkers are often costly, invasive, or unavailable in resource-constrained settings, emphasizing the urgent need for affordable, scalable, and validated biomarkers to enable early and accurate diagnosis and intervention. This review critically examined the current landscape of approved disease-modifying drugs for AD, their efficacy, safety profiles, and implementation challenges in LMICs. It also explored emerging therapeutic strategies beyond amyloid- and tau-targeting agents, including plasma exchange, synaptic modulators, and gene-based approaches. Finally, it discussed the future trajectory of AD therapeutics, emphasizing the necessity of strengthening healthcare systems and developing globally inclusive research frameworks to ensure equitable access to disease-modifying treatments.

Introduction

Dementia is an emerging global public health priority, ranking as the seventh leading cause of death and one of the major causes of disability and dependency among the elderly worldwide.[1] Currently, more than 55 million people are living with dementia globally, and this number is expected to increase threefold by 2050.[2] In India, recent studies suggest that 7%–9% of individuals over the age of 60 are living with dementia, accounting for nearly 9 million people. The prevalence is notably higher among women and rural populations, with numbers expected to double by 2036.[3] Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is the most prevalent form of dementia worldwide.[4] Previously thought to be incurable, emerging disease-modifying options are receiving approval, although they are not yet widely available. Over the past two decades, disease-modifying drugs for AD have not demonstrated efficacy in clinical trials. However, these trials have provided valuable insights into targeting multiple pathways within the amyloid cascade despite their lack of success.[5]

Amyloid cascade and anti-amyloid drugs

The underlying pathobiology of AD is complex, and the precise mechanisms of its onset remain unclear. The Alzheimer’s Association Work Group has updated the diagnostic and staging criteria for AD to reflect a shift towards integrating both clinical presentation and underlying biology.[67] These revised criteria aim to align patient classification more closely with clinical trials targeting the underlying biological mechanisms of AD. The core pathological cascade of AD involves extracellular amyloid-β deposition and intracellular hyperphosphorylated tau aggregation, which form senile plaques and neurofibrillary tangles, respectively. This process triggers neuroinflammation, synaptic dysfunction, and neuronal loss. Additionally, vascular contributions to AD further modulate disease expression and progression.[8] All these neurobiological changes precede clinical symptoms and drive the trajectory of cognitive decline. Clinically, AD is associated with memory impairment, often accompanied by deficits in visuospatial ability, executive dysfunction, language difficulties, and behavioral disturbances.[9] The final diagnosis of AD was traditionally based on clinical features and supportive neuroimaging, often confirmed only postmortem.[9] Treatment options have been limited to symptomatic therapies such as cholinesterase inhibitors and memantine, which offer modest cognitive benefits.[10]

However, with the advent of biomarker-based diagnostic methods, including positron emission tomography (PET) imaging for amyloid and tau, as well as cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and blood biomarkers,[11] AD is now defined as a biological disease. This shift enables early and accurate diagnosis and more precise intervention. This paradigm shift from symptomatic to disease-modifying drugs has changed the landscape of therapeutic options for AD and expectations from nihilism to hope. Remarkable progress has been made in AD therapeutics in recent years. Disease-modifying drugs in the form of monoclonal antibodies (MABs) have been used as a therapeutic strategy for AD by selectively targeting key pathogenic factors, notably amyloid-beta (Aβ) and tau protein. These drugs directed against Aβ are classified according to their binding affinity for distinct conformational states of Aβ, including monomer, fibril, protofibril, and plaque-associated forms of Aβ, as well as pyroglutamate Aβ.[12] First-generation MABs that targeted the nontoxic monomeric form of Aβ, such as solanezumab, bapineuzumab, and crenezumab, did not demonstrate clinical benefits for AD in clinical trials.[131415] By contrast, second-generation MABs, including aducanumab, lecanemab, donanemab, and gantenerumab, preferentially bind to pathogenic Aβ species and aggregated forms [Figure 1] have shown that reducing Aβ deposition can slow cognitive decline in AD.[161718]

Aducanumab

The 2021 approval of aducanumab marked the end of a two-decade gap during which no new disease-modifying drugs for AD were approved, primarily due to the inability to demonstrate reductions in Aβ plaque burden in clinical trials. Aducanumab (Aduhelm) is a human MAB that specifically binds aggregated Aβ, including soluble oligomers and insoluble fibrils. In two identically designed, yet discordant, phase III trials (ENGAGE and EMERGE), aducanumab demonstrated a 0.39 point reduction [95% CI (0.09–-0.69), P = 0.012] on the Clinical Dementia Rating Scale-Sum of Boxes (CDR-SB) at 18 months compared with placebo in EMERGE, whereas ENGAGE yielded a nonsignificant result favoring placebo.[16] In EMERGE, amyloid-PET imaging revealed a substantial reduction cerebral Aβ plaque burden in the high-dose group, prompting the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) to grant accelerated approval of aducanumab for the treatment of AD dementia.[19] The trials enrolled participants with mild cognitive impairment.

Lecanemab

Lecanemab (Leqembi), a humanized IgG1 MAB, became the second disease-modifying drug approved by the US FDA for mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and early AD. It selectively targets soluble Aβ protofibrils. Its accelerated approval in January 2023 was supported by evidence showing that amyloid plaque reduction, confirmed through amyloid PET imaging, correlated with moderate cognitive decline. In the phase III CLARITY AD trial, lecanumab lowered cerebral amyloid burden and slowed functional decline by 27%, with a statistically significant change in the CDR-SB with a mean difference of 0.45 [95% CI (0.23–0.67); P < 0.001] compared to placebo after 18 months in individuals with early AD.[17] While its long-term impact on disease progression remains uncertain, FDA approval has accelerated scientific and clinical interest in MAB-mediated amyloid reduction as a therapeutic approach for AD. Despite this momentum, the high cost of therapy poses a significant barrier. Notably, lecanemab is the first MAB to demonstrate a significant clinical benefit by pharmacologically targeting the underlying pathological mechanisms of AD.

Donanemab

The third anti-amyloid drug approved by the US FDA in July 2024 is donanemab (Kinsula) for early symptomatic AD, which encompasses MCI and mild dementia due to AD, with confirmed amyloid pathology. This humanized IgG1 MAB specifically targets deposited amyloid plaques containing a pyroglutamate form of Aβ (p3-42), thereby facilitating the clearance of existing amyloid burden from the brain rather than solely preventing new plaque deposition or the expansion of existing plaques.[20] In the TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 2 trial, donanemab demonstrated significant benefits across multiple endpoints, including a smaller decline in the integrated Alzheimer Disease Rating Scale (iADRS) score at 76 weeks (−6.02 with donanemab vs −9.27 with placebo), representing a 35% slowing of disease progression. In addition, there was a slower change in the CDR-SB score change (1.20 with donanemab vs 1.88 with placebo), corresponding to a 28.9% [95% CI (18.41–39.44), P < 0.001] slowing of clinical progression in the low/medium tau pathology subgroup,[18] which demonstrates the most significant difference in improvement compared to controls. Notably, 47% of participants in the low/medium tau group receiving donanemab showed no clinical decline at one year, compared to 29% in the placebo arm (P < 0.001).

The approval of donanemab has renewed hope in the therapeutic landscape of AD. Anti-amyloid MABs are increasingly recognized as a viable passive immunotherapy approach to reduce cerebral amyloid burden. However, these agents are associated with amyloid-related imaging abnormality (ARIA), the most common adverse effect in this drug class. The reported incidence of ARIA was 41.7% with aducanumab compared to 10.2% in the placebo group. For lecanemab, ARIA-edema/effusion (ARIA-E) occurred in 12.6% of patients and ARIA-hemosiderosis/microhemorrhages (ARIA-H) occurred in 17.3%.[17] For donanemab, ARIA-E and ARIA-H rates were 24.0% and 26.8%, respectively.[18]

Current status of MABs

Aducanumab has been withdrawn from commercial use since November 2024, following a strategic decision to discontinue its development in favor of newer agents. Clinicians now face challenges in managing patients who were previously on aducanumab, especially regarding the appropriate timing and criteria for transitioning to lecanemab. Owing to aducanumab’s approximately 24-day half-life, experts recommend a washout period of 4–5 months before initiating lecanemab, although no standardized protocol currently exists. Decisions about reinitiation, including the use of follow-up amyloid PET scans, repeat magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) safety screenings, and consideration of amyloid status, remain largely clinical judgments, underscoring significant gaps in post-aducanumab treatment pathways.[21] Lecanemab has received approval in more than 15 countries, including the United States, Japan, China, South Korea, and the United Kingdom, and is currently under regulatory review in 14 additional countries, including India. In January 2025, the US FDA approved a maintenance IV regimen (10 mg/kg every 4 weeks) following an 18-month initiation phase. A weekly subcutaneous autoinjector is also under review.[22] For donanemab, the TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 3 study is underway to assess the efficacy of the drug in preclinical AD.[23] In addition, to optimize safety, the TRAILBLAZER-ALZ-6 study evaluated a modified schedule that significantly reduced the risk of ARIA by 41% at 24 weeks and 35% at 52 weeks.[24]

Furthermore, the therapeutic landscape for AD is entering a transformative era. Building on recent regulatory approvals of MABs targeting Aβ-amyloid, a new wave of next-generation anti-amyloid and anti-tau therapies is advancing through phase III clinical trials in 2025. These emerging therapies offer diverse mechanisms of action, ranging from high-affinity antibodies and oral amyloid aggregation inhibitors to tau propagation blockers, and aim not only to modify disease pathology but also to improve safety, delivery, and accessibility. Among these emerging therapies, remternetug, a next-generation N3pH-Aβ MAB targeting pyroglutamate-Aβ is currently in the phase III TRAILRUNNER-ALZ3 trial.[25] It is being tested for both intravenous and subcutaneous route, aiming to reduce treatment burden while maintaining efficacy. An oral molecule, valiltramiprosate (ALZ-801) is in the phase III APOLLOE4 trial; it inhibits the formation of soluble neurotoxic amyloid aggregates in the amyloid cascade, focusing on early AD patients who are APOE4/4 homozygotes.[26] E2814 is an investigational MAB targeting the microtubule-binding region (MTBR) of tau, developed to inhibit tau propagation. It is being evaluated in the phase II/III Tau NexGen Trial for Dominantly Inherited Alzheimer’s Disease (DIAD) and in a phase II study 202 for early sporadic AD, which also includes anti-amyloid therapy (lecanemab) to assess safety, tolerability, and disease- modifying effects.[27] Posdinemab, a phosphorylated tau-directed MAB that blocks tau aggregation and propagation, is being evaluated for the treatment of early AD in the phase IIb AuTonomy trial.[28] In addition, drugs like semaglutide, a peptide-1 receptor agonist, are being studied for their disease-modifying and neuroprotective effects in AD through multimodal mechanisms in the EVOKE and EVOKE+ phase III trials for patients with early symptomatic AD.[29] As these therapies advance, they signal a new paradigm shift toward personalized and mechanism-driven approaches, with the potential to substantially alter the trajectory of AD, especially in the early stages.

Newer approaches to treatment of AD

As our understanding of the underlying pathology of AD continues to evolve, it has become increasingly clear that amyloid and tau accumulation represent only a part of a complex, multifactorial neurodegenerative process. This insight has driven the development of novel therapeutic strategies that extend beyond amyloid- and tau-targeted approaches. Emerging approaches now target diverse pathophysiological mechanisms including synaptic dysfunction, neuroinflammation, microglial dysregulation, blood-brain barrier compromise, and gut-brain axis alterations [Table 1]. Innovations such as antisense oligonucleotides and gene-based therapies are being integrated into disease-modifying frameworks. A phase I first-in-human trial is evaluating continuous intracranial delivery of Brain-Derived Neurotrophic factor (BDNF) via AAV2 in participants with MCI and AD.[30] Further expanding the range of therapeutic options for AD, plasma exchange with albumin replacement is emerging as a cost-effective and mechanistically novel approach. This strategy is based on the “peripheral sink” hypothesis, which suggests that removal of circulating Aβ, particularly the albumin-bound fraction, can shift the equilibrium across the blood–brain barrier, thereby promoting Aβ efflux from the brain into plasma.[31] In line with this concept, the Alzheimer’s Management by Albumin Replacement (AMBAR) trial, a phase IIb/III study, demonstrated slower functional and global cognitive decline in moderate AD.[32] This transition reflects a deeper understanding of AD as a multifactorial neurodegenerative disorder and underscores the urgency for multimodal therapeutic strategies capable of intervening early and altering the disease trajectory.

Equitable access to AD diagnosis and treatment

Maximizing the therapeutic potential of anti-amyloid agents requires the integration of biomarker-guided clinical pathways within healthcare systems to enable early detection of AD[33] and timely intervention during the initial stages of pathology. Furthermore, ensuring equitable access to disease-modifying therapies for AD could encourage anti-amyloid drug manufacturers to adopt pragmatic and sustainable pricing strategies that reflect the true therapeutic value to patients and society while considering the capacity of healthcare systems to deliver broad coverage. The decision to initiate anti-amyloid therapy involves complex considerations, given the multifaceted benefits and risks, as well as the variability in evidence and treatment effectiveness observed across clinical trial settings and patient populations.

Emerging evidence increasingly supports the application of blood-based biomarkers as reliable alternatives to amyloid PET imaging and CSF biomarkers for both the diagnosis of AD and the longitudinal monitoring of therapeutic responses.[3435] Studies have demonstrated that blood-based biomarkers closely correlate with established imaging and CSF biomarkers, offering scalable, less invasive, and cost-effective tools suitable for both high- and low-resource settings.[3637] Recent studies from low- and middle-income country (LMIC) contexts, including multicentric cohorts in Latin America, Africa, and Asia, highlight the feasibility and diagnostic relevance of these biomarkers in resource-constrained settings, supporting their integration into global dementia strategies.[363839] This paradigm shift holds promise for enhancing diagnostic equity in LMICs, where access to advanced neuroimaging and lumbar punctures is often limited. Such advancements will facilitate patient recruitment and improve trial quality by confirming the presence of the target disease. Progress in identifying precise biological targets, optimizing pharmacological mechanisms, and validating biomarkers for accurate diagnosis and therapeutic monitoring is driving significant progress in AD drug development. Concurrently, innovative clinical trial designs, including adaptive protocols and biomarker-guided participant selection, enhance trial efficiency and sensitivity to treatment effects. These integrated approaches collectively accelerate the translation of disease-modifying drugs from research to clinical practice.[40] Continued investment from governmental sources, advocacy groups, philanthropies, and biotechnology and pharmaceutical companies is critical to capitalize on this growing knowledge base and advance therapeutics for patients who require new therapies.[33]

Future directions

With the evolution of these disease-modifying drugs, sufficient evidence has shown that they can attenuate cognitive decline by reducing amyloid levels in the brain through different mechanisms. However, despite the approval of these drugs for AD, their use in LMICs remains a challenge due to high costs and potential side effects. It raises the question of whether patients with dementia in LMICs, who often have a high burden of vascular factors, can tolerate these US FDA-approved drugs. Furthermore, the genetic architecture complicates the clinical phenotypic spectrum of dementia subtypes. Dementia exhibits a strong genetic component, with Genome-Wide Association Studies (GWAS) in the Indian population identifying several key variants.[41] Among these, the apolipoprotein E (APOE) gene is particularly significant; it encodes a protein responsible for binding and transporting low-density lipids and has a major influence on the risk of developing late-onset AD.[42]

Ensuring equitable access to timely and accurate AD diagnosis—irrespective of ethnicity, socioeconomic background, language, or geographic location—is essential for enabling individuals with AD to receive appropriate treatment, support, and care. There is a need for consensus on whether clinical trials should be conducted to evaluate the efficacy of these agents in patients with dementia in LMICs, particularly those with a high vascular burden. Alternatively, the direct adoption of US FDA-approved drugs without robust evidence of their effectiveness across diverse cohorts raises critical concerns about their generalizability and clinical applicability. Urgent and concrete actions in global healthcare systems are necessary to prepare for a future where anti-amyloid drugs are readily accessible to everyone who could benefit from treatment. While the approval of these novel drugs marks a milestone in the AD therapeutic landscape, it has also raised more difficult questions[43] than answers for LMICs.
