Improving the yield and biological activity of sage essential oil using NADES: in vitro and in silico insights
Authors: Ümit Erdoğan, Sabri Erbaş, Bülent Dede
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the efficacy of Natural Deep Eutectic Solvents (NADESs) as innovative green pretreatments for the hydrodistillation of sage (Salvia officinalis L.) leaves, focusing on essential oil (EO) yield, phytochemical modulation, and biological activity. Three choline chloride (ChCl)-based NADESs were synthesized and characterized via ATR-FTIR. The results revealed that NADES pretreatments significantly enhanced extraction efficiency, with the ChCl: Malic acid pretreatment achieving the highest EO yield of 2.53% (v/w) compared to 2.31% for the water-based control. GC-MS analysis identified up to 24 volatile compounds, showing that NADES applications notably increased the proportion of oxygenated monoterpenes, reaching a maximum of 78.45% in ChCl: Citric acid-treated oils. In vitro assays demonstrated that specific NADES-treated EOs possessed superior antioxidant capacities, and all extracted EOs exhibited potent, dose-dependent cytotoxic activity against SH-SY5Y human neuroblastoma cells. Mechanistic insights from molecular docking identified β-caryophyllene as a lead bioactive constituent, displaying strong binding affinities toward the VEGFR2 and Keap1 domains. A 100 ns molecular dynamics simulation of the VEGFR2-β-caryophyllene complex confirmed robust structural stability, as evidenced by consistent potential energy, stable root mean square deviation values, and a compact radius of gyration. These findings establish NADES-assisted hydrodistillation as a superior, sustainable technology for producing high-quality sage essential oils with enhanced therapeutic potential.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-62529-9.

Introduction

Sage (Salvia officinalis L.) is a member of the Lamiaceae family, which encompasses over 900 species worldwide and is native to the Mediterranean region1. The aerial parts of sage are primarily used in culinary applications for their aromatic properties. However, the plant is also significant in traditional medicine for its therapeutic effects on various disorders and diseases2 and its established antimicrobial activity. In addition to their common use in the pharmaceutical industry, sage leaves and their essential oils (EOs) are used to flavor many food products and to enhance the aroma of perfumes and cosmetics3,4. Among Salvia species, S. officinalis is particularly valued for its rich essential oil content stored in glandular trichomes, making it a suitable model for studies of extraction efficiency and compositional variability5,6.

In recent years, EOs obtained from medicinal and aromatic plants have gained considerable attention from both producers and consumers, owing to their status as natural products and their demonstrated benefits for food and human health. Moreover, driven by consumers’ growing interest in natural ingredients and concerns about potentially harmful synthetic additives, the global demand for essential oils is rising7. Today, they are mainly evaluated for their biological activities, including antioxidant, anticancer, antimicrobial, wound-healing, analgesic, insecticidal, antidiabetic, and anti-inflammatory effects8–13. However, the efficiency and selectivity of extracting these bioactive volatile compounds remain highly dependent on the extraction technique employed14,15.

EOs are volatile compounds with complex chemical compositions that occur in diverse plant parts, including flowers, leaves, stems, bark, wood, roots, seeds, fruits, rhizomes, and gum or oleoresin exudates16. They typically contain between 20 and 60 distinct chemical compounds, including terpenes (mono-, sesqui-, and diterpenes), alcohols, acids, esters, epoxides, aldehydes, ketones, amines, and sulfides17. However, the composition of EO components varies by plant species and extraction method, resulting in substantial differences in volatility, stability, and solubility18. Hydrodistillation, organic solvent extraction, and supercritical carbon dioxide extraction are widely employed methods for obtaining EOs19. Moreover, pretreatment techniques, including ultrasonics, microwave, and enzymatic methods, are frequently used to increase essential oil yield and reduce extraction time20. Therefore, optimizing extraction strategies is crucial for preserving and enhancing the functional properties of essential oils15.

Natural Deep Eutectic Solvents (NADESs), a newly emerging class of solvents, are increasingly attractive for achieving sustainable development and effective extraction of EOs, particularly because they offer an inexpensive, non-toxic alternative with unique physicochemical properties. NADES are composed of hydrogen bond donors (HBD) and hydrogen bond acceptors (HBA) obtained from primary metabolites such as sugars, sugar alcohols, organic acids, amino acids, and amines, which are commonly found in living cells21 and are combined in defined molar ratios with specific amounts of water22. As the term “eutectic” indicates, the combination of HBA and HBD yields a lower melting point for the NADES than for either component, allowing the mixture to remain liquid at ambient temperature23,24. NADESs with varying molar compositions provide distinct structural matrices and exhibit strong solubilizing capacities for plant metabolites, thereby facilitating enhanced diffusion through plant cell walls during NADES-assisted extraction25. Therefore, the enhanced solubility and extraction capacity can be attributed to the presence of molecules that, while fulfilling their primary functions in the cellular medium, also enable storage stabilization and solubility of secondary metabolites by emulating the internal cellular environment, thereby facilitating their interaction and potential extraction with compounds of interest, such as EO compounds26,27. Consequently, NADESs could form strong intermolecular hydrogen bonds with target substances in plants, thereby significantly enhancing the dissolution of cellulose, a key component of plant cell walls28. Previous reports also indicate that NADESs enhance extraction yields and can be used to isolate specific secondary metabolites29–31. In addition to improving the extraction of bioactive compounds, NADESs are nearly completely biodegradable, sustainable, and renewable solvents because of their bio-based composition and cost-effective ease of preparation32, thereby broadening their applicability for extracting valuable compounds from various plant parts for use in the food, pharmaceutical, and cosmetic fields33,34. Despite these advantages, the application of NADES for extracting volatile compounds, such as essential oils, remains relatively unexplored, particularly when combined with conventional hydrodistillation techniques35,36.

Although previous studies have evaluated the effectiveness of various hydrodistillation techniques and their effects on the chemical profile and quality of sage essential oil, no studies have assessed the efficacy and potential of using NADESs as pretreatment solvents for the hydrodistillation of sage leaves. While NADESs have been extensively employed in recent literature, the vast majority of these studies focus on conventional solid-liquid extractions targeting non-volatile polar compounds, such as polyphenols and flavonoids. Unlike previous studies that use deep eutectic solvents solely as static extraction media, the present study leverages their tunable polarity and extensive hydrogen-bonding networks under active distillation conditions to selectively modulate the volatile phytochemical profile35,36. This functional distinction is particularly important because volatile compounds are sensitive to extraction conditions, and improving their recovery while preserving their bioactivity remains a major challenge in essential oil research15,37. It is hypothesized that NADES pretreatment can enhance cell wall disruption, improve mass transfer, and selectively enrich bioactive volatile compounds35,38. Therefore, the present work investigated the effects of different NADESs as cosolvents on essential oil yield, characterized the sage EOs, and evaluated their antioxidant and anticancer activities. Furthermore, changes in chemical composition were correlated with these biological activities, thereby providing evidence for the functional implications of NADES-assisted extraction39. To the best of our knowledge, this study not only represents the first application of NADES-assisted hydrodistillation for S. officinalis, but it also provides a novel mechanistic perspective, supported by in silico molecular docking and dynamics simulations, on how these green designer solvents can dynamically alter terpene proportions and enhance subsequent bioactivities. Ultimately, this study can encourage the use of NADESs in the hydrodistillation of spice plants, offer an innovative, green alternative to classical water-steam distillation, and serve as a valuable reference for targeted volatile extraction.

Materials and methods

Plant material

Sage leaves were harvested from the garden at the Rose and Aromatic Plants Application and Research Center of Isparta University of Applied Sciences (Isparta, Türkiye) in June 2025, air-dried in a shaded area away from direct sunlight, and a voucher specimen was deposited at the Isubü-Faculty of Agriculture herbarium (reference number SL-062025). The leaves were subsequently pulverized into a powder, passed through a 100-mesh sieve, and hydrodistilled to obtain sage leaf essential oil.

Chemicals

Choline chloride (ChCl; ≥98% purity), L-malic acid (MA; 99%), citric acid monohydrate (CA; ≥99.5% purity), copper (II) chloride dihydrate (CuCl2. 2H2O; ≥99% purity), neocuproine (Nc; ≥98%), and 2-diphenyl-1-picril-hydrazyl (DPPH; C18H12N5O6) were purchased from Sigma Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Propylene glycol (PG; CH3CH(OH)CH2OH; ≥99% purity) was obtained from Carlo Erba reagents (Carlo Erba, Milan, Italy).

Preparation and characterization of NADESs

The synthesis of the NADESs was carried out by heating, as previously reported40. Choline chloride (ChCl)-based solvents were combined with propylene glycol (PG), malic acid (MA), and citric acid (CA) at predetermined molar ratios (Table 1). NADESs were prepared at a 1:4 molar ratio by mixing 0.02 mol of ChCl with 0.06 mol of the respective second component. To reduce viscosity, 40% distilled water (w/w) was added to the NADES. The mixtures were sonicated in an ultrasonic bath and heated to 50 °C until they became a clear, uniform liquid. NADES stability was confirmed by the system remaining liquid after 72 h at room temperature. The molar ratios, abbreviations, and component names used for each prepared NADES are summarized in Table 1. An Attenuated Total Reflection Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (ATR-FTIR) spectrometer (Jasco Co., Tokyo, Japan) was used to characterize the structural properties of NADESs from their infrared spectra. FTIR spectra were recorded over 400–4000 cm⁻¹ with a resolution of 2 cm⁻¹ at 25 °C. The physicochemical characteristics of NADESs were also assessed with respect to density, refractive index, and viscosity.

NADES-assisted hydrodistillation of sage leaves

The NADES-based extraction procedure for essential oil from sage leaves consisted of two sequential processes: ultrasonic-assisted NADES pretreatment and traditional hydrodistillation. In the first stage, 125 mL of various NADESs (N1, N2, and N3), with water content adjusted to 40% by weight (yielding a solid-to-liquid ratio of 1:5), were combined with 25 g of accurately weighed powdered sage leaves in a sealed 500 mL bottle. The mixtures were sonicated in an ultrasonic bath for 10 min at 30 °C. In the next step, the mixture was diluted with 1 L of deionized water at a liquid: solid ratio of 40:1 (g/g). The pretreated plant material was subjected to traditional hydrodistillation to extract EO using a Clevenger-type apparatus. During the extraction procedure, the round-bottom flask containing the leaves and solvent/cosolvent was placed on a heating mantle, and the temperature was maintained at the boiling point for approximately 3 h. The EOs obtained by hydrodistillation were collected, desiccated with anhydrous Na₂SO₄ to remove moisture, and stored at 4℃ for subsequent analysis. The control labeled N0 contained 125 ml of distilled water instead of NADES in its pretreatment and was then mixed with 25 g of ground sage powder. The other steps were the same. Gas chromatography/mass spectrometry (GC/MS) analysis of EOs

The volatile chemical composition of sage essential oil was analyzed using a Trace 1300 gas chromatograph. This system was equipped with an ISQ7000 Single Quadrupole Mass Spectrometer from Thermo Scientific. The approach and instrumental conditions for GC-MS are outlined as follows. The inlet temperature was set at 250 °C. Chromatographic separation was achieved using a Trace GOLD TG-624SilMS GC column (60 m × 0.25 mm I.D. × 1.4 μm film thickness, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, Massachusetts, USA). Helium served as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 1.5 mL/min, with a split ratio of 1:5. The GC oven was held at 35 °C for 3 min. The temperature was then increased at 2 °C/min to 100 °C and held for 1 min. It was then increased at 5 °C/min to 120 °C, with a 1 min hold at 120 °C. The temperature was next increased by 5 °C/min to 200 °C and held for 1 min, then increased by 5 °C/min to 220 °C, with a final hold of 1 min at 220 °C. The detector temperature was set at 280 °C. The analysis runtime was 64 min. Chromatographic analyses were performed with Xcalibur software. The chromatograms of essential oils subjected to GC-MS analysis are depicted in Supplementary Material (Figs. S1-S4). The QMS operated in electron ionization mode, scanning from 33 to 400 m/z. Compounds were identified by comparison with Saturn spectra recorded in Wiley 1n.l, the NIST 0.5 (National Institute of Standards and Technology) database, and Adams41. Parent molecules and qualifying ions were selected for each found compound. Peak regions for each compound were determined by integrating the peaks. Peaks were verified using library spectra gathered from the instrument. The peak area of each compound was divided by the total peak area in the spectrum to determine its percentage content.

In vitro antioxidant activity assays of EOs

The total antioxidant activity (TAC) of the obtained EOs was estimated using the DPPH free radical scavenging and cupric reducing antioxidant capacity (CUPRAC) assays, as described by Apak et al.42, with minor modifications. Before testing, 0.5 mL of each oil sample was diluted with acetone at a 1:10 (v/v) ratio. CUPRAC and DPPH reagents were prepared in 95% ethanol43. For the CUPRAC test, 1 mL of the diluted oil sample was added to the CUPRAC solution (1 mL each of 10 mM Cu(II), 7.5 mM Nc, and 1 M NH4Ac), and the mixture was diluted to a final volume of 4.1 mL with ethanol (95%). After incubating the reaction mixture at room temperature for 30 min, the absorbance at 450 nm (attributed to Cu(Nc)₂⁺ production) was measured relative to the reference solution. The reference solution contained ethanol (95%) instead of the sample. A calibration curve was constructed by preparing 1 mL aliquots of trolox (TR) solutions at concentrations of 12.5, 25, 37.5, 50, 62.5, and 75 µg ml⁻¹ (Absorbance = 0.0162 x C (mg TR) − 0.0232, R²: 0.9998), with TAC results expressed as TR in milligrams per gram of the oil (Eq. 2).

All experiments were performed as three independent biological replicates and measured in milligrams of TR per gram of oil.

Cell culture cytotoxicity assay

The human neuroblastoma SH-SY5Y cell line (American Type Culture Collection) was cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Altrincham, UK) with 10% fetal bovine serum (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), and 1% L-glutamine (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere of 95% air and 5% CO2. When cell cultures reached 80% confluence, they were rinsed with 1X Dulbecco’s PBS (Phosphate-Buffered Saline) (Cegrogen Biotech; Stadtallendorf, GERMANY) and detached with 1 × 0.25% Trypsin-Ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) (Thermo Scientific; Waltham, MA, USA). Cells were then counted with Trypan Blue and a Thoma hemocytometer before analysis.

Cell viability was evaluated using the 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)−2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay. SH-SY5Y cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 103 cells in 100 µL of DMEM per well and incubated overnight before treatment. The MTT Cell Proliferation Assay Kit (Cistron Biologics MTT Assay Kit, Türkiye) was used to assess SH-SY5Y cell viability. Cells were treated with 50, 100, and 200 µg/ml of different concentrations of EOs obtained from N0, N1, N2, and N3 treatments, followed by 24- and 48-h incubation periods. After incubation, absorbance was measured at 490 nm using a Microplate Photometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Altrincham, UK). All measurements were performed as three independent biological replicates, and cell viability was determined as the percentage of viable cells relative to the control (untreated) group.

Molecular docking studies

Molecular docking simulations were performed to investigate binding interactions between the major constituents of S. officinalis essential oil and two biologically relevant protein targets involved in oxidative stress regulation and cancer-related signaling pathways. Four compounds identified as major components of the essential oil, namely 1,8-cineole, α-thujone, camphor, and β-caryophyllene, were selected as ligands for docking analysis. The three-dimensional crystal structures of vascular endothelial growth factor receptor-2 (VEGFR2) and the Kelch domain of Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (Keap1) were retrieved from the Protein Data Bank (PDB) with the accession codes 2XIR and 5FNQ, respectively (https://www.rcsb.org). Before docking calculations, the receptor structures were prepared by removing crystallographically bound water molecules and any co-crystallized ligands from the PDB files. Polar hydrogen atoms were added, and appropriate atomic charges were assigned to the receptor structures. The chemical structures of the selected ligands (1,8-cineole, α-thujone, camphor, and β-caryophyllene) were optimized using molecular mechanics methods implemented in Gaussian 09 software44. Docking simulations were conducted using AutoDock Vina 1.2.7, which predicts the preferred binding orientations of ligands within the receptor binding pocket using an empirical scoring function to estimate binding affinities45. The missing amino acid residues in the protein structures were completed using the MODELLER program46. To define the search space, grid boxes were centered on the receptors’ active regions. The DeepSite module was used to identify the xyz coordinates of the proteins’ binding sites47. For the VEGFR2 protein (2XIR), the grid center coordinates were set to x = 21.0, y = 26.2, z = 38.8 to ensure coverage of the kinase domain binding pocket. For the Keap1 Kelch domain (5FNQ), the grid box was centered at x = 14.81, y = 64,69, z = 30.22, encompassing the ligand-binding interface responsible for Nrf2 recognition. The grid dimensions were adjusted to cover the active sites of both receptors adequately. During the docking procedure, multiple ligand conformations were generated within the defined search space, and the binding pose exhibiting the lowest binding energy was selected as the most favorable interaction conformation. The docking results were evaluated based on predicted binding energies and intermolecular interaction patterns. Molecular docking results were visualized using UCSF Chimera 1.19 for 3D representations and BIOVIA Discovery Studio Visualizer v21.1.0.20298 for 2D interaction diagrams48,49. While conventional re-docking of the co-crystallized ligand with RMSD assessment was not explicitly performed as a static validation step, the reliability of the predicted binding poses was rigorously evaluated dynamically. The precise identification of the binding pockets using the DeepSite module ensured accurate grid positioning, and the structural validity of the selected docking conformations was ultimately verified through subsequent 100 ns molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. The sustained stability of the complex throughout the MD trajectory served as the primary validation of the docking protocol.

Molecular dynamics simulations

Although oxygenated monoterpenes such as 1,8-cineole and camphor are quantitatively the most abundant constituents in the extracted essential oils, relative chemical abundance does not necessarily dictate molecular potency at the target receptor level. Based on the static molecular docking results, β-caryophyllene emerged as the most promising bioactive lead, demonstrating the highest binding affinities toward both VEGFR2 and Keap1 targets. Consequently, to validate the most potent interaction model while optimizing computational resources, the VEGFR2-β-caryophyllene complex was exclusively selected for further evaluation. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations of this top-scoring complex were conducted using the YASARA molecular modeling software to assess its dynamic structural stability50. Before the simulation stage, the protein structures were prepared by optimizing the hydrogen-bonding network and adjusting the protonation states of ionizable residues according to physiological conditions (pH 7.4) using a pKa prediction approach51,52. The simulation systems were generated in an explicit solvent environment using the TIP3P water model. To ensure electrical neutrality and mimic physiological ionic strength, appropriate numbers of Na+ and Cl− ions were added to the simulation box to obtain a salt concentration of approximately 0.9% NaCl. The AMBER14 force field was employed to describe the protein atoms, while ligand parameters were generated using the GAFF2 force field together with AM1-BCC atomic charges53–55. Before the production run, each system was subjected to energy minimization using 5000 cycles of simulated annealing combined with the steepest-descent algorithm, thereby removing unfavorable steric contacts and generating energetically relaxed starting structures. Long-range electrostatic interactions were treated using the Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) method, with an 8 Å cutoff for non-bonded interactions56,57. Subsequently, the equilibrated systems were subjected to 100 ns MD simulations in the NPT ensemble, maintaining a constant temperature of 298 K and a pressure of 1 atm. The integration of atomic motions was performed using time steps of 2.5 fs for bonded intra-actions and 5.0 fs for non-bonded interactions, ensuring stable and accurate trajectory propagation58.

The resulting trajectories were analyzed to assess the structural stability and dynamic behavior of the complexes. Several structural parameters were monitored throughout the simulation, including Root Mean Square Deviation (RMSD), Root Mean Square Fluctuation (RMSF), Radius of Gyration (Rg), Solvent Accessible Surface Area (SASA), hydrogen-bonding patterns, and total potential energy. These analyses provided insights into the conformational stability and persistence of interactions in the protein–ligand complexes during the simulation period.

Data analysis

All data obtained were analyzed using the randomized plot experimental design with the GLM procedure in SAS 9.059. Before the analysis of variance (ANOVA), the necessary assumptions, including the normality of the data distribution and the homogeneity of variances, were evaluated and found to be met. Subsequently, the means were compared using the Least Significant Difference (LSD) test at a 0.05 probability level. The mean values of the examined characters according to the NADES applications were calculated using multivariate principal component analysis (PCA) in RStudio with the “gglott2” and “factoextra” packages60. Furthermore, hierarchical clustering analysis (HCA) and the corresponding heat map of the essential oil components obtained by distillation after NADES application were generated using the ClustVis online tool61. Unit variance scaling was used for normalized and approximate data.

Results and discussion

NADES characterization

The vibrational spectra of the synthesized NADESs and their pure components were examined using ATR-FTIR spectroscopy. Next, the necessary NADESs were identified based on their chemical keys and functional groups. The corresponding FTIR results are depicted in Fig. 1(a-c). FTIR spectra indicated that some characteristic functional groups corresponded to C–N stretching at 952 cm− 1, C–C and C–O stretching in the range of 1380–1040 cm− 1, -C–H bending at 1476 cm− 1, C=O stretching due to carboxylic acid around 1700 cm− 1, N-H stretching bands of choline chloride (ChCl) appearing around 3200 cm− 1, and broad spectra observed between 3300 and 3400 cm− 1 corresponding to free O-H stretching vibrations coupled by hydrogen bonds. FT-IR spectra of N1 (choline chloride-propylene glycol, 1:4) are shown in Fig. 1a. The FT-IR spectra of propylene glycol (PG) showed a broad absorption peak at 3320 cm− 1, which is due to the OH groups in the PG. Pure choline chloride has several functional groups, notably a vibrational band at 3220 cm− 1 for OH stretching and bands at 3014 and 2850 cm− 1 for N–H stretching. In N1, the OH stretching band linked to the ChCl OH(Ch+)–Cl− hydrogen bond at 3220 cm− 1 appears to have vanished. However, the strong OH peaks observed in both PG and ChCl appear broader in N1. This change is attributed to the interaction between the alcohol portion of PG and the chloride ion, which localizes charge on ChCl40. Moreover, the asymmetric C-N bands at 952 cm− 1 have shifted to 950 cm− 1 in N1. The FT-IR spectra of malic acid (MA) and citric acid (CA), as displayed in Fig. 1(b-c), display broad absorption bands near 3400 cm− 1, which are attributed to the presence of carboxyl (COOH) and hydroxyl (OH) groups in both compounds. In the spectrum profile of MA, the C=O stretch band was observed at 1701 cm⁻¹, whereas for CA, it appeared at 1724 cm− 1. Analysis of Fig. 1 (b-c) indicated that the bands corresponding to OH functional groups in the N2 and N3 spectra were significantly broadened, attributable to alcohol groups derived from the carboxylic acids present. However, only minor alterations were observed in the other functional groups of the individual components in N2 and N3, none of which were significant. Specifically, the comparison of the FT-IR spectra of the pure components with those of NADESs revealed vibrational shifts to higher or lower frequencies, indicating non-covalent interactions among the molecules and that the observed alterations in various functional groups were not due to chemical reactions40.

The physicochemical properties of the synthesized NADES, including density, viscosity, and refractive index, were evaluated. The corresponding measurements are provided in Table 1. The densities of NADESs varied from 1.053 to 1.217 g/cm3. These values exceeded the density of distilled water (N0), which was used in place of NADES in the present work (0.997 g/cm³), as well as the densities of solvents used in traditional extraction systems. Along with density measurements, the refractive indices N0, N1, N2, and N3 were simultaneously measured, yielding values of 1.333, 1.412, 1.420, and 1.419, respectively. Viscosity, another parameter, is a critical physicochemical property that influences solvent selection in extraction processes. Most NADES have higher viscosity at room temperature compared to other common organic solvents62. Adding water as a cosolvent can overcome the negative effects of high viscosity on the extraction process63. Table 1 indicates that N1 at 6.46 mPa.s, N2 at 8.64 mPa.s, and N3 at 9.05 mPa.s have higher viscosity than N0, which is 1.09 mPa.s. Table 1 shows that, based on evaluation across all parameters, N1 (choline chloride:1,2-propylene glycol) and N0 (distilled water) exhibited partially similar results.

Essential oil yield

EOs obtained from dried sage leaves by hydrodistillation, employing water as the solvent and three different NADES pretreatments, yielded clear, pale-yellow oils with variable yields (Fig. 2). As depicted in Fig. 2, hydrodistillation of sage leaves using water without NADES treatment (N0) produced 2.31% (v/w) essential oil, a yield consistent with yields of sage essential oil from various European countries reported in the literature64. Employing NADESs N1 and N2 as cosolvents yielded superior EO yield results compared to the conventional water-based method (treated with N0), whereas N3 exhibited a diminished oil yield (Fig. 2). NADES, because of their high solubility and penetration ability, enable more effective extraction of phytochemicals from plant cellular matrices than traditional solvents, thus resulting in higher EO yields65. The essential oil extracted with N2 (choline chloride: malic acid) had the highest yield at 2.53% (v/w), followed by N1 (choline chloride-propylene glycol) at 2.50%. However, the data showed no statistically significant difference between N1 and N2, but a considerable difference with N3. The unique physicochemical properties of the individual ingredients in NADES mixtures, such as polarity, solubility, density, and viscosity, lead to differences in extraction efficiency across NADES mixtures66. In solid-liquid extraction, solvents with slightly higher density than water typically exhibit greater relative mass, as well as higher heat capacity and thermal conductivity. Moreover, a high refractive index typically indicates a high solvent density, which contributes to a more stable contact interface and enhances diffusion67. These properties allow them to penetrate more deeply into the plant’s solid matrix, transfer heat to it more quickly, and thus facilitate softening of cell walls and improve extraction of cellular contents. The higher oil yield observed with N1 and N2 during the hydrodistillation of sage leaves could be attributed to these properties. Although N3 exhibits properties similar to other NADESs, the essential oil yield obtained with N3 remained lower than that achieved with N1 and N2 during the hydrodistillation of sage.

Phytochemical profile of sage essential oil by GC–MS

Table 2 displays the volatile compounds identified by GC/MS in all essential oils obtained using three different NADES treatments (N1, N2, and N3), as well as in the essential oils from the control group without NADES treatment (N0), along with their corresponding % area. Analysis of GC/MS data indicated that the use of different extraction media influenced extraction yields and led to variability in EO composition (Table 2, Figs. S1-S4). Sage essential oils extracted using NADES contained 20–23 volatile chemical compounds, whereas the NADES-free control group exhibited 24 identified compounds. A total of 17 components, including α-pinene, camphene, β-myrcene, p-cymene, eucalyptol, sabinene, terpinolene, α-thujone, β-thujone, camphor, (-)-borneol, isoborneol, isobornyl propionate, β-caryophyllene, aromadendrene, viridiflorene, and viridiflorol, were consistently identified across all treatments.

The groups of sage essential oil compounds were classified as monoterpene hydrocarbons (MH), oxygenated monoterpenes (OM), sesquiterpene hydrocarbons (SH), and oxygenated sesquiterpenes (OS) based on their chemical structures. OM was more abundant in sage essential oils compared to other classes. Moreover, across all groups, OM was highest in N3 (78.45%), followed by N2 (76.16%) and N1 (72.68%), and lowest in N0, the untreated group (72.16%). The observation that the N3 treatment resulted in the lowest overall essential oil yield, despite achieving the highest enrichment of oxygenated monoterpenes (78.45%), highlights the strong chemical selectivity of this specific NADES. This inverse relationship can be attributed to the physicochemical properties of the ChCl: Citric acid mixture. Specifically, N3 possesses the highest viscosity (9.05 mPa.s) among the evaluated solvents, which likely impedes optimal mass transfer and limits the total volume of compounds that can diffuse out of the plant matrix. However, the pronounced hydrogen-bond donor capacity of the tricarboxylic citric acid creates a highly polar microenvironment. This network is highly selective for oxygenated compounds, which readily form non-covalent hydrogen bonds with the solvent, while concurrently suppressing the extraction of non-polar hydrocarbon constituents. As a result, the total extracted oil volume decreases, but the relative percentage of oxygenated monoterpenes within that volume increases significantly.

Following OM, SH accounted for 17.42%, 16.82%, and 11.93% of the total essential oil composition in the untreated group (N0) and in the essential oil-treated groups N1 and N2, respectively. However, SH was represented as the class, accounting for 10.18% of the N3 group. The relative amounts of MH in the N0, N1, N2, and N3 groups were 9.15%, 9.46%, 11.26%, and 10.91%, respectively. The OS class remained at poor levels across all groups. Out of the twenty-five compounds identified in sage leaves, 1,8-cineole (26.23–23.30%), α-thujone (23.74–20.43%), camphor (20.50–19.03%), and β- β-caryophyllene (4.94–8.93%) were present in large amounts (Table 2). The use of NADES combinations in essential oil extraction resulted in higher concentrations of p-cymene, 1,8-cineole (eucalyptol), α-terpinene, sabinene, α-thujone, terpinolene, isoborneol, viridiflorene, α-amorphene, and γ-terpinene compared to water. Meanwhile, α-terpinene was present only in essential oils extracted using N2 and N3. On the other hand, sabinene hydrate was determined only in essential oils extracted with water. Only N0 and N1 exhibited an enhanced ability to isolate α-humulene.

However, it should be noted that thujone and camphor, which are present in the essential oil of S. officinalis, may be toxic at high doses68. Moreover, the ISO 9909 standard limits the amounts of these compounds in sage essential oils: thujone (18.0 to 43.0%), camphor (4.5 to 24.5%), and trans-thujone (3.0 to 8.5%)69. Additionally, it is worth noting that in the European Union herbal monograph on Salvia officinalis L., folium, the presence of thujone in sage leaf preparations is limited to a daily intake of 6.0 mg/person70. As shown in Table 2, GC/MS analysis indicated that the thujone and camphor compounds in sage essential oils from all groups meet the ISO 9909 standard. Furthermore, according to the European Medicines Agency, adherence to recommended camphor dosages and specified maximum thujone limits indicates that sage leaf does not pose a safety risk to adults.

The heat map of S. officinalis essential oils obtained after NADES applications is shown in Fig. 3, which clearly indicates significant and selective effects on both molecular classes and individual components. Hierarchical clustering analysis reveals that acidic NADES systems, particularly N2 and N3, are distinct from N1 and the control group (N0), and that these systems reshape the essential oil composition. The suppression of some oxygenated monoterpenes (OM) and the relative increase in certain monoterpene hydrocarbons (MH) in acidic NADES indicate that solvent polarity and hydrogen-bond interactions selectively affect component solubility. It is particularly noteworthy that the characteristic components of sage, α/β-thujone, 1,8-cineole, and camphor, show different trends depending on the type of NADES; the decrease of these compounds in some NADES environments and their relative increase in others reveal a significant rebalancing in the essential oil composition. While a more stable, but limited, extraction profile is observed in the N1 system, the selective effect of acidic NADES on these oxygenated monoterpenes supports the critical role of solvent-component interactions. These findings demonstrate that choline chloride-based NADES, thanks to their hydrogen-bond donor-acceptor structure and tunable polarity, can selectively dissolve specific components and molecular classes, thus directing the chemical profile of the essential oil35,38,71.

These findings are in strong agreement with recent literature highlighting how external processing parameters profoundly alter the extraction capacity and chemical profiles of volatile bioactive compounds. Recent studies have demonstrated that physical parameters and modern processing techniques significantly reshape the essential oil composition of Lamiaceae species by modifying the recovery of key monoterpenes and oxygenated terpenoids72. Similarly, it has recently been reported that tailored processing conditions for S. officinalis directly govern the ratios of key constituents (such as thujone and limonene), thereby enhancing their antioxidant properties, a phenomenon validated by computational molecular docking73. While these contemporary reports largely focus on post-harvest drying methodologies, our results confirm that integrating specific NADES formulations as active cosolvents during hydrodistillation enriches essential oils with oxygenated monoterpenes (e.g., 1,8-cineole and camphor) in a highly targeted manner. This comparison not only situates our findings within the current scientific consensus on volatile profile modulation but also underscores the novelty and high efficiency of NADES-assisted extraction for the robust valorization of S. officinalis.

To better understand these extraction dynamics, it is essential to consider the underlying mechanisms of NADES pretreatment in the context of recent green extraction advancements28,33. Deep eutectic solvents primarily facilitate extraction through profound structural disruption and targeted solvation. The extensive hydrogen-bond donor and acceptor networks within the NADES system, such as interactions between choline chloride and its respective cosolvents, effectively compete with the intrinsic inter- and intramolecular hydrogen bonding in plant cellulose and lignin. This competitive solvation leads to enhanced cell wall swelling and disruption, thereby exposing the internal secretory structures of S. officinalis. Following this structural breakdown, mass-transfer kinetics govern the release of volatiles. While highly viscous NADES formulations may induce mass-transfer resistance that limits the total macroscopic oil volume, their tunable polarities create a highly selective microenvironment. This tailored polarity preferentially solubilizes polar and moderately polar constituents, facilitating the selective enrichment of oxygenated monoterpenes over non-polar hydrocarbons during the subsequent hydrodistillation phase. Consequently, the distinct physicochemical properties of each NADES composition act collectively to disrupt the botanical matrix and selectively shape the final phytochemical profile, a concept increasingly supported by recent computational and experimental natural product research25,30.

Antioxidant activities of EOs

Many EOs have recently gained attention for their potential as natural agents in food preservation, owing to their antioxidant properties. For this purpose, the present study evaluated the antioxidant capacity of sage essential oils. The effect of NADES applications on the antioxidant activity of sage essential oils was measured in vitro using the DPPH and CUPRAC assays (Fig. 2). It was found that NADES not only significantly improves essential oil yield but also positively influences antioxidant activity by modifying the composition of their volatile components. Across all groups, sage essential oils obtained from leaves treated with N3 showed the highest antioxidant activity in both methods, while those treated with N1 showed the lowest. More specifically, both DPPH and CUPRAC assays consistently showed that the N3 treatment exhibited the highest antioxidant capacity, whereas the N1 treatment showed the lowest. These distinct activity trends seamlessly align with the variations in the oxygenated monoterpene content observed among the extraction groups, as detailed in Fig. 2. The antioxidant capacity of essential oils is attributed to monoterpene hydrocarbons (MH), oxygenated monoterpenes (OM), sesquiterpene hydrocarbons (SH), and oxygenated sesquiterpenes (OS)74,75. Among these classes, OM in essential oils is more significant than in other classes in terms of its effect on antioxidant capacity, primarily because it contains numerous functional groups, such as alcohols, aldehydes, ketones, and ethers, each of which is represented by several variants75. Table 2 indicates that the essential oils produced by N2 and N3 have a higher OM area than those obtained from N1 and N0 (the untreated control group). While MH compounds are typically reported to exhibit minimal or no antioxidant activity, exceptionally, three monocyclic constituents in this class, namely, terpinolene, α-terpinene, and γ-terpinene, and, to a lesser extent, sabinene, a bicyclic compound, demonstrated high antioxidant capacity comparable to that of α-tocopherol according to the TBARS assay method in a previous study75. In the present study, the essential oil extracted with N1 lacked highly active antioxidant monoterpenes (such as α-terpinene and γ-terpinene, which were entirely undetected) and had a lower overall OM content compared to the acidic NADES groups. Conversely, N2 and N3 contained all of these potent components in significantly greater quantities (Table 2). Taken together, the chemical information indicates that both the DPPH and CUPRAC assays logically explain why N2 and N3 exhibited superior antioxidant capabilities, whereas N1 showed the lowest antioxidant performance among all treatments.

Anticancer activities of EOs

The effects of sage essential oil on the survival of SH-SY5Y human neuroblastoma cells were evaluated using an MTT cell survival assay. SH-SY5Y cells were exposed to essential oils at concentrations of 50, 100, and 200 µg/ml, obtained from all experimental groups, for durations of 24 and 48 h (Fig. 4). Relative to DMSO, all essential oils obtained from sage leaves treated with N0, N1, N2, and N3 significantly reduced SH-SY5Y cell survival in a dose- and time-dependent manner. Specifically, cell survival rates decreased by approximately 10.4% to 31.5% after 24 and 48 h of exposure to 200 µg/ml essential oils (Fig. 4). The major components of sage essential oils, specifically 1,8-cineole (eucalyptol), α-thujone, and camphor, which constitute the majority of the essential oil, are likely responsible for its cytotoxic activity. A previous study demonstrated that a 48-hour treatment with a mixture of α-thujone, 1,8-cineole, and camphor, comprising approximately 65% of the oil volume, exerted an antiproliferative effect on A549 and H226 lung cancer cell lines76. Another study revealed that S. officinalis essential oil at concentrations of 100 µg/mL and 200 µg/mL significantly reduced cell viability in MCF7, LNCaP, and HeLa cell lines following a 48-hour incubation77. Moreover, treatment with a mixture of the three major components of the essential oil at the same concentrations and for the same incubation period also resulted in decreased cell viability in these cell lines. Consistent with previously reported findings, the present study confirms that the essential oil’s biological activity is attributable to its constituent compounds. However, it is important to note that the cytotoxicity assessment in the present study was limited to a single cancer cell line (SH-SY5Y). The absence of a normal, non-cancerous cell line control is a limitation of this study. Future in vitro and in vivo investigations incorporating healthy cell lines are essential to thoroughly evaluate the selectivity, safety profile, and potential off-target toxicity of these essential oils.

The clustering of EO, DPPH, and CUPRAC parameters along PC1 indicates that NADES applications increase essential oil yield and enhance antioxidant activity. The literature reports that oxygenated monoterpenes such as 1,8-cineole, camphor, and thujone found in sage essential oil make significant contributions to both antioxidant and biological activities39,78. The positioning of dose and duration groups of anticancer (AC) applications along this axis in PCA indicates that essential oils obtained under more effective NADES conditions exhibit higher antioxidant capacity and, therefore, stronger anticancer potential by regulating oxidative stress. In contrast, the decomposition of major components such as camphor, thujone, and cineol along PC2 shows that NADES applications not only increase yield but also selectively alter the essential oil composition. The placement of antioxidant parameters and individual components on different axes reveals that the anticancer effect is not attributable to the increase in a single component, but rather to the balance of the entire phytochemical profile and synergistic interactions among components. Indeed, the literature reports that Salvia officinalis essential oils exhibit a dose-dependent antiproliferative effect that is composition- and concentration-dependent77. In conclusion, PCA and NADES systems play a role in both increasing extraction yield and directing composition. The optimum anticancer effect is achieved by striking a balance between antioxidant capacity and component composition.

Molecular docking analysis

To gain mechanistic insight into the antioxidant and anticancer activities of Salvia officinalis essential oils, molecular docking studies were performed against two biologically relevant protein targets: the tyrosine kinase domain of VEGFR2 (PDB ID: 2XIR), associated with angiogenesis and cancer progression, and the Kelch domain of Keap1 Kelch (PDB ID: 5FNQ), a key regulator of the Nrf2-mediated antioxidant pathway. The four major essential oil constituents identified by GC-MS analysis, 1,8-cineole, camphor, α-thujone, and β-caryophyllene, were selected as ligands for docking simulations. Vascular endothelial growth factor receptor-2 (VEGFR2) is a critical mediator of angiogenic signaling pathways. While classically associated with endothelial cells, VEGFR2 is also expressed on various tumor cells, including human neuroblastoma cells, where it promotes tumor cell survival and proliferation through autocrine signaling loops. Therefore, evaluating VEGFR2 interactions provides a highly relevant mechanistic rationale for the targeted cytotoxicity observed against the SH-SY5Y cell line in our in vitro assays, and also assesses broader anti-angiogenic potential. Inhibition of its binding site is a well-established strategy for suppressing cancer cell survival.

The docking results demonstrated that all tested ligands bound within the active site of VEGFR2, with binding energies ranging from − 5.5 to − 7.5 kcal/mol (Table 3). Among the evaluated compounds, β-caryophyllene displayed the strongest binding affinity (− 7.5 kcal/mol), followed by α-thujone (− 6.5 kcal/mol), camphor (− 5.9 kcal/mol), and 1,8-cineole (− 5.5 kcal/mol). The interaction profile of β-caryophyllene was characterized predominantly by hydrophobic π-alkyl interactions with residues within the binding pocket, particularly with HIS171 (Figs. 6, 7 and 8). Similarly, α-thujone and camphor formed multiple alkyl interactions with hydrophobic residues, including VAL43, LEU180, ALA61, VAL94, and PHE192, indicating favorable accommodation within the kinase domain. These specific interaction patterns highlight the critical importance of the hydrophobic core within the VEGFR2 binding cleft. The extensive network of alkyl and π-alkyl contacts, particularly the repeated involvement of residues like HIS171, PHE192, and VAL43, plays a pivotal role in anchoring the non-polar terpenoid scaffolds. Unlike some conventional synthetic inhibitors that rely heavily on directional hydrogen bonding, the stabilization of these essential oil constituents is predominantly driven by shape complementarity and favorable van der Waals forces within the hydrophobic pocket. This hydrophobic matching is essential for competitively blocking the binding site, thereby providing a clear structural rationale for the observed antiproliferative effects.

The Keap1-Nrf2 signaling axis represents a primary cellular defense mechanism against oxidative stress. Under basal conditions, Keap1 binds to Nrf2, promoting its ubiquitination and degradation. Compounds that occupy the Kelch binding pocket may disrupt this interaction, thereby stabilizing Nrf2 and enhancing the expression of antioxidant genes. Docking simulations against the Keap1 Kelch domain revealed binding energies ranging from − 5.5 to − 7.1 kcal/mol. Consistent with the VEGFR2 results, β-caryophyllene exhibited the highest affinity (− 7.1 kcal/mol), followed by camphor (− 5.9 kcal/mol), 1,8-cineole (− 5.6 kcal/mol), and α-thujone (− 5.5 kcal/mol). β-Caryophyllene interacted mainly through hydrophobic alkyl contacts with ALA46, suggesting stable accommodation within the Keap1 binding groove. In contrast, camphor and α-thujone formed conventional hydrogen bonds with residues such as VAL192 and VAL145, as well as carbon-hydrogen interactions with GLY144 and GLY191. Although hydrogen bonding may enhance specificity, the overall binding energies indicate that hydrophobic stabilization plays a dominant role in ligand recognition within this pocket. The formation of strong conventional hydrogen bonds (e.g., short distances of 1.94–1.97 Å with VAL192) by the oxygenated monoterpenes (camphor and α-thujone) provides critical directional specificity, anchoring them effectively into the Keap1 active site. Conversely, the superior binding affinity of the larger sesquiterpene, β-caryophyllene, demonstrates that broad hydrophobic surface matching (such as interactions with ALA46) can effectively drive complex formation even in the absence of polar contacts. This dual-binding modality, in which oxygenated compounds utilize targeted hydrogen bonds and hydrocarbons rely on expansive hydrophobic coverage, illustrates the diverse molecular mechanisms by which the synergistic mixture of essential oil components can disrupt the Keap1-Nrf2 interaction and induce the observed antioxidant responses. The stronger interaction of β-caryophyllene with the Keap1 domain is particularly noteworthy in light of the experimental antioxidant results. The N3-treated essential oil, which exhibited the highest antioxidant capacity in both DPPH and CUPRAC assays, may partly owe its enhanced activity to increased molecular interactions between β-caryophyllene and the Keap1 regulatory site. By potentially weakening the Keap1-Nrf2 interaction, β-caryophyllene could facilitate activation of antioxidant response elements, thereby contributing to the observed radical-scavenging activity.

The docking analysis consistently identified β-caryophyllene as the most promising bioactive constituent among the tested terpenoids, demonstrating the highest binding affinity toward both VEGFR2 and Keap1 targets. This dual-target interaction profile suggests that β-caryophyllene may simultaneously influence oxidative stress regulation and proliferative signaling pathways. Importantly, NADES pretreatment was experimentally shown to modify essential oil composition and the relative abundance of terpenes. Therefore, alterations in the proportions of β-caryophyllene and other monoterpenes may directly affect their molecular interactions with biologically relevant protein targets. In this context, the enhanced antioxidant performance of certain NADES-treated groups can be partially rationalized at the molecular level through improved binding interactions within the Keap1 regulatory domain. Overall, the docking results provide mechanistic support for the experimentally observed antioxidant and cytotoxic activities of S. officinalis essential oils and offer a plausible structural explanation for the biological effects observed in the present study.

In synthesis, the integration of GC-MS profiles, biological assays, and computational models reveals a cohesive structure-activity relationship, a multiparametric approach increasingly validated by recent comprehensive essential oil studies79,80. The NADES treatments, particularly N3, successfully modulated the phytochemical profile by enriching the essential oils with oxygenated monoterpenes and key bioactive sesquiterpenes like β-caryophyllene. This specific chemical modulation directly correlates with the enhanced antioxidant capacity observed in the DPPH and CUPRAC assays, which may collectively contribute to the dose-dependent cytotoxicity against SH-SY5Y cells. Furthermore, the molecular docking and dynamics simulations substantiate these empirical data by demonstrating how these enriched constituents can effectively interact with critical regulatory targets like VEGFR2 and Keap1. This strong correlation between experimental bioactivities and computational binding affinities provides a plausible, multi-target mechanistic basis for the observed in vitro efficacy, perfectly aligning with contemporary state-of-the-art structure-activity investigations of natural products81,82.

Molecular dynamics simulation analysis

Potential energy of system

The thermodynamic stability of the VEGFR2-β-caryophyllene complex during the 100 ns molecular dynamics simulation was evaluated by monitoring the total potential energy of the solvated system (Fig. 9a). The potential energy rapidly reached equilibrium during the early stage of the trajectory and remained highly stable throughout the production run. The energy values fluctuated within a narrow range from − 717,500 to − 721,500 kJ/mol, with an average of approximately − 719,500 kJ/mol. Importantly, no systematic upward or downward drift was observed during the simulation. Such a stable energetic profile indicates that the system remained in a well-equilibrated minimum-energy state. The absence of significant fluctuations confirms that the selected force-field parameters adequately represented intermolecular interactions in the NPT ensemble at 298 K and 1 atm. This energetic stability provides a reliable foundation for interpreting subsequent structural and dynamic analyses of the VEGFR2-β-caryophyllene complex.

Solvent Accessible Surface Area (SASA)

The structural compactness and solvent exposure of the VEGFR2-β-caryophyllene complex were evaluated using surface area analyses over the 100 ns simulation trajectory (Fig. 9b). Three surface parameters were monitored: Van der Waals surface area (SurfVdW), molecular surface (SurfMol), and solvent-accessible surface area (SurfAcc). The Van der Waals surface area remained highly stable throughout the simulation, fluctuating between approximately 29,500 Å² and 30,000 Å². This stability indicates that the internal atomic packing and overall molecular volume of the complex remained largely unchanged during the trajectory. Similarly, the molecular surface and solvent-accessible surface areas exhibited consistent profiles throughout the simulation. The SurfAcc values fluctuated slightly between 15,500 Å² and 16,200 Å², averaging approximately 16,000 Å². The absence of significant expansion in solvent-accessible surface area suggests that the VEGFR2 protein preserved its native globular architecture without undergoing unfolding or large-scale conformational changes. These findings indicate that the hydrophobic binding pocket remained effectively shielded from solvent exposure while accommodating the β-caryophyllene ligand.

Hydrogen bond analysis

The hydration stability of the VEGFR2-β-caryophyllene complex was assessed by analyzing solute–solvent hydrogen bonds during the simulation (Fig. 9c). Hydrogen bonds formed between the protein and surrounding TIP3P water molecules were monitored throughout the 100 ns trajectory. The system maintained a stable hydrogen-bonding network, with an average of approximately 565 hydrogen bonds observed during the simulation. The number of hydrogen bonds fluctuated moderately between approximately 540 and 600, reflecting natural dynamic interactions between the protein surface and the solvent environment. The persistence of this extensive hydrogen-bonding network indicates that the protein surface remained well-hydrated and structurally stable during the simulation. Such hydration stability supports the maintenance of the native conformation of the VEGFR2 kinase domain and contributes to the overall structural integrity of the protein–ligand complex.

Radius of Gyration (Rg)

The global structural compactness of the VEGFR2 protein in the presence of β-caryophyllene was examined using the Radius of Gyration (Rg) analysis (Fig. 9d). The Rg parameter reflects the distribution of the protein’s mass about its center of gravity and is commonly used to assess overall folding stability in molecular dynamics simulations. Throughout the 100 ns simulation, the Rg values remained highly stable, fluctuating between approximately 20.2 Å and 20.8 Å, with an average value of about 20.55 Å. These minimal variations indicate that the VEGFR2 protein maintained its compact globular conformation without undergoing significant structural expansion or unfolding. The stable Rg profile suggests that the binding of β-caryophyllene did not induce major conformational rearrangements in the protein structure. Instead, the protein maintained its structural integrity while accommodating the ligand within the binding pocket.

Root Mean Square Deviation (RMSD)

The structural stability of the VEGFR2-β-caryophyllene complex was further investigated through Root Mean Square Deviation (RMSD) analysis of the protein backbone and C-alpha atoms (Fig. 9e). RMSD provides a quantitative measure of the deviation of the simulated structure from the initial reference structure over time. Following an initial equilibration phase over the first 5 ns of the simulation, the RMSD values stabilized and exhibited relatively small fluctuations throughout the remainder of the trajectory. The RMSD values for C-alpha and backbone atoms primarily fluctuated between 2.0 Å and 2.5 Å, indicating a stable structural configuration of the protein. The RMSD values calculated for all atoms remained below 3.25 Å and reached equilibrium after approximately 30 ns. These values fall within the typical stability range reported for globular proteins, suggesting that the VEGFR2 structure remained structurally stable during the simulation and that the binding of β-caryophyllene did not induce significant conformational deviations.

Root Mean Square Fluctuation (RMSF)

Residue-level flexibility within the VEGFR2 structure was examined using Root Mean Square Fluctuation (RMSF) analysis (Fig. 9f). RMSF measures the average fluctuation of individual amino acid residues around their mean positions during the simulation. The RMSF profile indicated that most amino acid residues exhibited relatively low fluctuations, generally remaining below 2.0 Å. This finding reflects a high degree of structural rigidity across the majority of the protein backbone. The highest fluctuations were observed at residue 140 (approximately 5.7 Å) and within the C-terminal region (approximately 7.9 Å). These regions correspond to flexible loops and terminal segments that typically display higher mobility in protein structures. Importantly, residues located within the catalytic and ligand-binding regions showed comparatively low fluctuations, indicating that the binding pocket remained structurally stable during the simulation. This observation suggests that β-caryophyllene helps maintain the structural stability of the VEGFR2 kinase domain throughout the simulation.

Beyond their fundamental thermodynamic stability, these collective MD parameters carry profound biological significance for the inhibitory potential of β-caryophyllene. The sustained low RMSD and RMSF values, particularly within the core catalytic cleft, indicate that the ligand acts as a robust structural anchor. By restricting the conformational plasticity typically required for the kinase’s activation loop dynamics, β-caryophyllene effectively locks the VEGFR2 receptor in an inactive conformation. Furthermore, the steady Rg and SASA profiles confirm that the protein does not undergo localized unfolding or solvent exposure events often associated with allosteric activation. This dynamic restriction competitively hinders the conformational shifts essential for ATP binding and subsequent auto-phosphorylation cascades. These observations are highly consistent with previously published molecular dynamics studies of natural multi-target inhibitors, which similarly report that sustained hydrogen-bonding networks and restricted RMSF fluctuations in the kinase domain strongly correlate with enhanced in vitro anti-angiogenic and apoptotic activities83. Recent computational investigations further support our findings, demonstrating that sesquiterpene scaffolds capable of maintaining a compact Rg and stable solvent-exclusion (SASA) within the hydrophobic clefts of receptor tyrosine kinases are highly predictive of strong, dose-dependent cytotoxicity in tumor cell lines84.

Conclusion

The findings of this study demonstrate that the integration of Natural Deep Eutectic Solvents (NADESs) as a pretreatment for the hydrodistillation of sage (Salvia officinalis L.) leaves represents a highly effective and eco-friendly strategy for enhancing both essential oil (EO) yield and biological functionality. The physicochemical characterization of the synthesized ChCl-based NADESs using ATR-FTIR confirmed the presence of successful hydrogen-bonding interactions between the components. At the same time, the assessment of oil yields revealed that N2 (Choline chloride: Malic acid) and N1 (Choline chloride: Propylene glycol) pretreatments significantly outperformed the conventional water-based method (N0), achieving yields of 2.53% and 2.50%, respectively. GC-MS analysis further illustrated that NADES applications significantly modulated the volatile profile of the EOs, notably increasing the concentration of oxygenated monoterpenes (OM), with N3-treated oils reaching the highest OM level at 78.45%. This phytochemical modulation directly translated into enhanced biological activities: EOs extracted with N3 exhibited superior antioxidant capacity in both DPPH and CUPRAC assays, while all NADES-treated oils demonstrated potent, dose- and time-dependent cytotoxic effects against SH-SY5Y human neuroblastoma cells, reducing cell survival by up to 31.5% at maximum concentrations. Mechanistic insights provided by molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations highlighted β-caryophyllene as the most influential constituent, exhibiting the strongest binding affinities toward both the VEGFR2 kinase domain (− 7.5 kcal/mol) and the Keap1 Kelch domain (− 7.1 kcal/mol). The 100 ns MD trajectory provided robust evidence for the stability of the VEGFR2-β-caryophyllene complex, characterized by consistent potential energy values around − 719,500 kJ/mol, stable RMSD values between 2.0 Å and 2.5 Å, and a compact structural integrity as indicated by the steady Radius of Gyration (~ 20.55 Å) and SASA profiles. These structural findings provide a theoretical rationale for the observed in vitro cytotoxic and antioxidant results, suggesting that the altered terpene proportions achieved through NADES extraction may facilitate favorable molecular interactions with biological targets. However, because these biological findings are restricted to in vitro studies, statements regarding the pharmaceutical and therapeutic potential of these essential oils must be interpreted with caution. Ultimately, this research presents NADES-assisted hydrodistillation as a promising green framework for the extraction of volatile compounds from medicinal plants; yet, extensive in vivo and preclinical validations are required before considering any clinical or therapeutic applications.
