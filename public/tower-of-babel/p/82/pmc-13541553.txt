Permanent left ventricular pacing in a patient with post-metallic tricuspid valve replacement complete heart block- a case report
Authors: Mohd Iqbal Dar, Sheikh Mohamad Tahir, Aamir Rashid, Ajaz A Lone
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDegenerative complete heart block is a relatively common condition and is usually treated with endocardial pacing. Iatrogenic conduction disturbance is often seen after some cardiac surgical procedures. In a significant number of cases, it is irreversible and needs implantation of a pacemaker device. In this case report, we present a case of a young, severely sick patient who presented with tricuspid valve endocarditis with large vegetation. The patient was managed with surgical removal of the vegetation and replacement of the tricuspid valve with a metallic valve. Postoperatively, the patient developed complete heart block. In view of the presence of a metallic tricuspid valve, the patient was successfully managed with the implantation of a dual-chamber pacemaker using a CRT-P generator with a quadripolar lead in the posterior cardiac vein and an atrial lead in the right atrial appendage.Keywords: Complete heart block, Permanent pacemaker, Infective endocarditis, Metallic tricuspid valve, Left ventricular pacing

Introduction

There has been a steady increase in the implantation of various Cardiac implantable electronic devices (CIED) worldwide in recent years. This has been mainly driven by the increased incidence of various conduction disturbances due to an ageing population, widening indication for CIED implantations, and increased detection of various cardiac rhythm disorders, especially in developing countries [1]. Although the degenerative conduction disorders/complete heart block (CHB) contribute to the majority of these indications, postoperative/iatrogenic heart block also forms a significant part of this cohort. Cardiac pacing is especially challenging in these cases due to associated comorbidity and often altered anatomy [2].

Case report

A 32-year-old male presented with complaints of high-grade fever of 3 weeks' duration. Patient had a history of intravenous drug abuse for the past several years. On evaluation, the patient was found to have multiple large tricuspid valve (TV) vegetations with severe tricuspid regurgitation. The patient also had multiple septic emboli to both lungs, causing bilateral pneumonia. Multiple blood cultures taken did not grow any organism, probably because the patient had received multiple antibiotics before the hospital admission. The patient was put on empirical antibiotic therapy for infective endocarditis. The condition of the patient, however, did not improve despite continuing the treatment for 2 weeks. The patient was taken for surgical removal of tricuspid vegetation with replacement of the TV. In view of the younger age of the patient, a metallic valve was implanted.

Postoperatively, the patient developed CHB. The patient was managed with temporary epicardial pacing and observed for 2 weeks for reversal of heart block. Patient also received a course of steroids. The heart block did not reverse, and the patient was taken for permanent pacemaker (PPM) implantation.

In view of the presence of a metallic TV, right ventricular (RV) endocardial pacing was not preferred. A levo-phase coronary sinus (CS) angiogram was done, which showed the presence of a good-sized posterior cardiac vein (Fig. 1A).

Levo-phase CS angiography showing a good-sized posterior coronary vein (A), depicted with the red arrow. Selective angiography of the posterior coronary vein (B), depicted with the red arrow.

During the device implantation procedure, the CS was canulated with a CS guiding catheter and the posterior vein was selectively hooked and wired (Fig. 1B).

A quadripolar left ventricular (LV) lead, Attain Performa S MRI Sure Scan 4598 88cm from Medtronic (Medtronic,710 Medtronic Parkway, Minneapolis, MN, USA) was taken and placed in the posterior cardiac vein (Fig. 2A). A tinned atrial lead was placed in the right atrial (RA) appendage (Fig. 2B).

Placement of a quadripolar LV lead in the posterior cardiac vein (A), depicted with a red arrow. Placement of tinned RA lead in the right atrial appendage (B), depicted with red arrow.

LV lead showed good pacing parameters in the proximal three pacing poles with a threshold of less than 1mv, while the distal-most pole pacing caused phrenic nerve stimulation even at low pacing output of 1.5mvs. After fixing the leads in the pacemaker pocket, a cardiac resynchronization therapy with a pacemaker (CRT-P) generator, Solara Quad CRT-P MRI Sure Scan from Medtronic (Medtronic,710 Medtronic Parkway, Minneapolis, MN, USA) was connected to the LV and atrial leads. The RV lead port in the CRT-P generator was plugged with the lead-port plug (Fig. 3). Soon after connecting the Medtronic generator, it was realised that the Medtronic CRT-P generator doesn't have LV lead sensing, as this device uses the RV lead to sense the cardiac electrical activity. Using this device in this configuration without the RV lead can lead to R on T wave phenomenon in addition to unnecessary pacing.

CRTP generator with leads connected to the corresponding ports. RV port was closed with a lead-port plug.

Subsequent to this, a new CRT-P generator, Enitra 8 HF-T QP from Biotronic (Biotronic SE&KG, Woermannkehre, Berlin, Germany) was chosen, as this device has an independent LV sensing function too. The RA and LV leads were connected to the respective ports after plugging the RV port with the lead port plug, and the procedure was completed (Fig. 4). Discussion

Postoperative heart block is a well-established complication following cardiac surgery, resulting from structural or functional injury to the atrioventricular (AV) node, His bundle, or its branches. This complication occurs frequently in cardiac procedures that involve the anatomical region containing the AV node or His bundle, including perimembranous septum repair, aortic valve surgery, TV surgery, mitral valve surgery, and complex congenital heart repairs. In these procedures, even minimal deviations in suture placement or patch orientation can cause injury to the compact AV node, His bundle, or its branches [2,3].

Various pathophysiological mechanisms have been postulated for postoperative heart block. It includes direct trauma leading to transection of the AV nodal axis, ischemia due to disrupted blood supply, local oedema and inflammation, and extensive debridement in aortic valve surgeries or direct prosthetic valve impingement, like in mitral and TV surgeries [3,4].

Early CHB), which occurs immediately after cardiac surgery, is often reversible and typically resolves to normal conduction as local oedema and inflammation subside. Current recommendations suggest waiting 7–10 days for heart block resolution. However, recent data indicate that a shorter waiting period of 3–5 days may be appropriate, particularly in high-risk surgeries involving the perimembranous ventricular septum, aortic valve, and TV. If heart block persists beyond this period, recovery is unlikely, and PPM implantation is indicated. Similarly, late-presenting CHB occurring months or years after cardiac surgery represents an irreversible conduction disturbance and mandates PPM implantation [4,5].

Patients who undergo TV intervention are particularly vulnerable to conduction disorders due to its proximity to the AV nodal conduction axis. Conduction disorder in TV surgery can occur due to multiple mechanisms, including direct injury causing transection of the conduction system, disruption of blood supply to the node, local oedema/inflammation or impingement of the prosthesis on the conduction system. The incidence of CHB after a TV intervention is high and varies from 10 to 30 % depending upon the type of surgery on the valve. The incidence after a bioprosthetic TV replacement is estimated to be 10 – 15 %, while the incidence after a metallic valve and transcatheter valve replacement approaches nearly 30% [6,7].

Pacing therapy after a TV replacement needs special attention. While endocardial RV pacing can be attempted in case of bioprosthetic TV after accepting a small risk of valve dysfunction, endocardial RV pacing after metallic prosthetic TV is not recommended [8]. An endocardial pacing lead through a metallic valve can lead to severe valvular dysfunction. In addition, due to the constant banging of metallic parts of the valve on the transvenous endocardial lead, pacing failure can occur due to lead dysfunction. Earlier, all these cases would receive an epicardial RV pacing. Although successful in the majority of cases, the concerns regarding the long-term outcomes of epicardial pacing, especially in younger patients, regarding the chronic threshold increase, remain prohibitive of this approach [8,9].

Other approaches to delivering pacing therapy in patients with an underlying prosthetic TV, as described in a few case reports, include achieving LV pacing via the coronary vein or using an intracardiac pacemaker device (bioprosthetic TV only). In cases utilising the coronary venous sinus approach, operators have used a bipolar lead in the CS branches and connected it to a single- or dual-chamber pacemaker [8]. Bipolar LV lead use in recent times has decreased due to its non-availability and advent of better-performing quadripolar LV leads. A few case reports also demonstrated use of His bundle for achieving ventricular pacing in these cases. His bundle pacing is not, however, the preferred technique due to its higher pacing and sensing failure rates [10].

Although in the published literature so far, there have been a few case reports of using a quadripolar LV lead and an CRT-P generator for pacing, all of these case reports have used the therapy in a single chamber mode only [11,12]. Our case report is probably the first case describing implantation of CIED to achieve a dual chamber pacing, post-metallic TV replacement using a quadripolar LV lead in the branch of the CS and connecting it to a CRT-P generator. The advantages of this approach of using a quadripolar lead with a CRT-P generator in these cases are multifold. Firstly, there are multiple poles available for pacing, which may help in averting the phrenic nerve stimulation, which is a common occurrence in LV Pacing. Second, there is the availability of multiple poles and vectors for pacing, which can be very useful in case a particular pole develops a higher pacing threshold. This may be particularly important for younger patients with longer life expectancy and who are expected to need pacing for decades.

A few important considerations, however, need to be kept in mind while administering this kind of therapy. Firstly, not all the CRT- P devices have LV sensing function. LV sensing in this mode of therapy is of paramount importance. In case no intrinsic rhythm is sensed, it can lead to unnecessary ventricular pacing and substantially increase the risk of life-threatening arrhythmias. Out of the widely used CRT pacemaker devices currently available, only the Biotronik (Biotronik SE&KG, Woermannkehre, Berlin, Germany) CRT-P device models, including Enitra 8 HF-T QP have true LV sensing capability and can be used for this therapy in this configuration. Regarding the programming in these devices for this kind of therapy, the device needs to be kept in DDDR mode with LV sensing and pacing set to LV only from bi-ventricular pacing [13].

Conclusion

In case of a metallic tricuspid valve, implantation of a quadripolar LV lead in a branch of the CS, and an atrial lead in the RA and connecting them with a CRT-P generator is a feasible novel approach to achieve dual chamber pacing.
