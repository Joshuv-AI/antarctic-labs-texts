Molecular epidemiology and risk factors associated with Babesia bigemina infection in cattle
Authors: Abdelfattah Selim, Mohamed Marzok, Hattan S. Gattan, Mohammed H. Alruhaili, Hesham Ismail, Adel I. Almubarak, Abdelrahman M. Hereba
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bovine babesiosis caused by Babesia bigemina is an important tick-borne disease that affects cattle health and productivity in tropical and subtropical regions. The present study aimed to determine the prevalence of B. bigemina in cattle and to identify associated risk factors in three governorates of the Nile Delta region, Egypt. A total of 400 blood samples were collected randomly from cattle and examined using nested PCR assay. The overall prevalence of B. bigemina prevalence was 8% (32/400). The prevalence did not differ significantly according to locality or sex (P > 0.05). However, significant variations were observed in relation to breed, age, season, tick infestation, and management practices. Mixed-breed cattle showed the highest prevalence (11.4%), followed by Friesian (5.2%) and native breeds (1.8%). Older animals (> 4 years) exhibited the highest infection rate (24%). Seasonal variation indicated that the highest prevalence occurred during summer (13.6%). Management-related factors also influenced infection rates; cattle receiving regular antiprotozoal treatment had a lower prevalence (2.4%) compared with untreated animals (11%). Tick-infested cattle showed higher infection rates (9.8%) compared with non-infested animals (3.5%), while the absence of acaricide application was associated with increased infection risk. Multivariable logistic regression analysis identified mixed breed OR = 6.96; 95% CI: 2.08–60.48), age over 4 years (OR = 7.01; 95% CI: 2.07–23.73), summer season (OR = 4.16; 95% CI: 1.10–15.71), lack of antiprotozoal drug use (OR = 6.04; 95% CI: 1.28–28.44), tick infestation (OR = 3.16; 95% CI: 1.09–10.23), and absence of acaricide application (OR = 2.37; 95% CI: 1.28–6.32) as significant predictors of infection. These findings highlight the important role of vector control and proper management practices in reducing the burden of bovine babesiosis. Continuous surveillance and integrated tick control strategies are essential for improving cattle health and productivity in endemic areas.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-59394-x.

Introduction

Babesia bigemina is considered one of the most important species of the genus Babesia infecting cattle and water buffaloes, particularly in tropical and subtropical regions worldwide1. This intraerythrocytic protozoan parasite is present in the bloodstream of infected animals and is responsible for significant economic losses in livestock due to reduced productivity and disease-associated mortality2–4.

Taxonomically, Babesia species belong to the phylum Apicomplexa, a diverse group of obligate intracellular parasites that includes more than one hundred recognized species infecting a wide range of vertebrate hosts5–9. Members of the genus Babesia are primarily transmitted by ticks and are widely distributed across different geographical regions10,11. Ticks are considered the second most significant group of arthropod vectors after mosquitoes, affecting a wide range of mammals and birds12,13. They represent a major concern in veterinary medicine due to their substantial impact on livestock health and productivity, leading to considerable economic losses worldwide14. Several tick genera, including Haemaphysalis, Amblyomma, Rhipicephalus, Ixodes, Dermacentor, and Hyalomma, are commonly reported to infest cattle and play an important role in the transmission of various tick-borne pathogens including Babesia species15–17. In addition, the prevalence of Babesia bigemina in Boophilus annulatus ticks was 66%18,19.

Babesia infection in cattle is associated with substantial economic losses in the livestock industry20,21. These losses arise from increased mortality, decreased productivity, reduced feed intake and feed conversion efficiency, abortion, and the costs associated with tick control and disease prevention programs22,23.

Clinically, infected cattle commonly exhibit signs such as fever, hemoglobinemia, hemoglobinuria, anaemia and jaundice which are characteristic manifestations of Babesia spp. infections24,25.

Field diagnosis of bovine babesiosis is often initially based on the observation of characteristic clinical signs combined with the detection of tick infestation on affected animals26. In cases of heavy parasitemia, confirmatory diagnosis can be performed through microscopic examination of Giemsa-stained thin blood smears, which allows visualization of the parasite within erythrocytes27–29. Although microscopy is widely used in field conditions due to its simplicity and low cost, its sensitivity is limited, particularly in carrier animals or during the chronic phase of infection when parasitemia levels are low.

In recent years, the development of molecular diagnostic techniques based on DNA amplification has significantly improved the detection and identification of piroplasm infections. Techniques such as PCR and reverse line blot (RLB) hybridization enable the detection of Babesia DNA with high sensitivity and specificity, even in cases of very low parasitemia. These molecular methods also provide valuable tools for epidemiological studies, allowing accurate identification of circulating species and a better understanding of the distribution and transmission dynamics of Babesia infections in cattle populations30–33.

In Egypt, infections caused by Babesia species have been reported to induce severe clinical disease in cattle, leading to considerable economic losses in the livestock sector34. Several previous studies have documented the occurrence of Babesia bovis and Babesia bigemina infections in cattle populations across different regions of the country18,35,36. However, updated epidemiological data regarding the current status and distribution of Babesia spp. infections remain limited. In particular, comprehensive investigations focusing on the associated risk factors are still needed to better understand the epidemiology and transmission dynamics of these infections in cattle populations.

Therefore, the aim of the present study was to investigate the prevalence of B. bigemina infection and to identify the associated risk factors in cattle reared across three governorates in northern Egypt.

Materials and methods

Ethical statement

All experimental procedures involving animals were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards and guidelines approved by Ethical Committee of the Faculty of Veterinary Medicine, Benha University (Approval number: FVTM02.04.2025). All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations by Ethical Committee of the Faculty of Veterinary Medicine, Benha University. The questionnaire-based data collected from cattle owners were included under the same ethical approval obtained for the study procedures from the Ethical Committee of the Faculty of Veterinary Medicine, Benha University. Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection. All methods were carried out in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines and regulations.

Study area

This study was conducted from January to December 2025 in three governorates located in the Nile Delta region of northern Egypt: Kafr El-Sheikh (31.11° N, 30.94° E), Gharbia (30.87° N, 31.03° E), and Menofia (30.60° N, 30.99° E), Fig. 1. The Nile Delta is one of the most important agricultural regions in Egypt and is characterized by intensive livestock production, dense rural populations, and environmental conditions that favor the persistence and transmission of tick-borne diseases.

Fig. 1Map showing the governorates included in the present study.

Map showing the governorates included in the present study.

Kafr El-Sheikh lies in the northern part of the Nile Delta and borders the Mediterranean Sea, with an agricultural landscape dominated by irrigated farmlands and livestock farming. Menofia is located in the central Nile Delta and consists mainly of fertile agricultural lands intersected by irrigation canals and branches of the Nile River. Gharbia, also situated in the central Nile Delta, is considered one of the most intensively cultivated governorates in Egypt, supporting dense agricultural and livestock production systems.

The climate in these governorates is generally Mediterranean-influenced, characterized by hot summers and mild winters, moderate to relatively high humidity, and limited rainfall occurring mainly during winter. Average summer temperatures range between approximately 30–36 °C, while winter temperatures typically vary between 10 and 20 °C. These environmental and climatic conditions provide a favorable habitat for ticks and may contribute to the persistence and transmission of tick-borne pathogens affecting cattle.

Sample size and sampling

Animals were selected from the study areas using a simple random sampling method to ensure representative coverage of the districts. The required sample size was calculated using the standard formula described by Thrusfield [38]:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:n=\frac{{1.96}^{2}\cdot\:P(1-P)}{{d}^{2}}$$\end{document}

In the formula, n represents the calculated sample size, P denotes the expected prevalence (50%), d is the desired absolute precision (5%), and 1.96² corresponds to a 95% confidence level. Based on this calculation, 400 blood samples were randomly collected from cattle across the selected governorates to ensure adequate statistical power and reliable prevalence estimates.

Approximately 5 mL of whole blood was aseptically drawn from the jugular vein of each cattle into sterile EDTA-containing vacutainer tubes. All samples were properly labeled, stored at -20 °C, until subsequent molecular analysis.

Molecular analysis

Extraction of DNA

Genomic DNA was extracted from whole blood samples using the DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit (Qiagen®, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The concentration and purity of the extracted DNA were assessed using nanodrop (Analytika Jena, Germany), and the samples were stored at − 20 °C until further molecular diagnostic procedures.

PCR assay

Conventional PCR followed by nested PCR (nPCR) was performed to detect B. bigemina DNA using primer sets previously described by Figueroa et al.37. All extracted DNA samples were subjected to both PCR assays.

PCR reactions were carried out in 0.2 mL microtubes with a final reaction volume of 25 µL. Each reaction mixture contained 1 U of GoTaq Hot Start Taq Polymerase (Promega), 8.5 pmol of each primer (BiIA: 5′-CATCTAATTTCTCTCCATACCCCTCC-3′ and BiIB: 5′-CCTCGGCTTCAACTCTGATGCCAAAG-3′), 0.2 mM of each deoxynucleotide triphosphate (dNTP), 3.5 mM MgCl₂, 5 µL of 5× Green GoTaq Flexi Buffer (Promega), and 3 µL of template DNA with a concentration ranging from 20 to 100 ng/µL. Ultrapure water was added to adjust the final reaction volume.

Positive and negative controls were included in each PCR run to ensure the reliability and specificity of the assay. In the negative control, genomic DNA was replaced with DNase-free ultrapure water while maintaining the same reaction components.

Nested PCR targeting 16s rRNA gene was performed using the same reaction mixture, except that 2 µL of the primary PCR product was used as the template instead of genomic DNA. The nested PCR employed the specific primers BiIAN (5′-CGCAAGCCCAGCACGCCCCGGTGC-3′) and BiIBN (5′-CCGACCTGGATAGGCTGTGTGATG-3′) to increase the sensitivity and specificity of B. bigemina detection with amplicon size 278 bp in first step and 170 bp in second step.

PCR amplification was carried out using a thermocycler (SensoQuest, Germany). The thermocycling program for both steps consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 2 min, followed by 30 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 1 min, annealing at 54.2 °C for 1 min, and extension at 73 °C for 1 min, with a final extension at 73 °C for 7 min.

Amplified PCR products were analyzed by agarose gel electrophoresis using a 2% agarose gel containing Unisafe Dye (20,000×; Uniscience) in a horizontal electrophoresis system. A 100 bp DNA ladder was loaded in the first lane as a molecular size marker to determine the size of amplified fragments. Electrophoresis was conducted at 140 V and 400 mA for approximately 60 min, and DNA bands were visualized under ultraviolet illumination. Amplicons of approximately 278 bp were considered positive for B. bigemina in the primary PCR reaction, whereas fragments of approximately 170 bp were regarded as positive in the secondary nested PCR assay.

Questionnaire

Information for each animal was obtained directly from the owners at the time of sampling using a structured questionnaire. The questionnaire captured animal-related variables, including location, breed (Native, Friesian, or Mixed), sex (male or female), age (≤ 2, 2–4, > 4 years), season (summer, autumn, spring, or winter), use of antiprotozoal drugs (none, occasional, or regular), presence of ticks (assessed by observation), and acaricide application practices.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS software (version 24.0, IBM, USA). A chi-square (χ²) test was performed to assess differences in prevalence. Initially, univariable logistic regression was performed for each variable to identify potential risk factors associated with B. bigemina infection. Variables with a P-value < 0.1 in the univariable analysis were further included in a multivariable logistic regression model used to determine independent predictors of infection. Results were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05. For each significant variable, odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. The goodness-of-fit of the final multivariable model was evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test.

Results

The overall prevalence of B. bigemina among the examined cattle was 8% (32/400). The prevalence did not vary significantly (P > 0.05) with respect to locality or sex. Among the studied governorates, the highest prevalence was recorded in Gharbia 10.3% (15/145), whereas the lowest prevalence was observed in Menofia 5.6% (7/125). Regarding sex, female cattle showed a slightly higher prevalence (8.2%) compared with males (6.7%), although this difference was not statistically significant (Table 1).

The prevalence of B. bigemina infection varied significantly according to several risk factors. A significantly higher prevalence was observed in mixed-breed cattle (11.4%) compared with Friesian (5.2%) and native breeds (1.8%). With regard to age, older animals (> 4 years) exhibited the highest prevalence (24%) compared with age group (2–4 years) and age group below 2 years with significant difference (P < 0.0001). Seasonal variation was also observed, with the highest prevalence recorded during the summer season (13.6%) compared with the other seasons (Table 1).

Management-related factors showed a significant association (P < 0.05) with infection rates. Regular application of antiprotozoal drugs markedly reduced the prevalence to 2.4%, (, whereas the absence of antiprotozoal treatment was associated with a higher prevalence (11%). In addition, the presence of ticks on animals was associated with a higher prevalence of B. bigemina infection (9.8%). Similarly, cattle raised without the use of acaricides showed a higher prevalence (10.2%) compared with animals that received regular acaricidal treatment (Table 1).

Multivariable logistic regression analysis identified several factors that were significantly associated with Babesia bigemina infection (P < 0.05), including breed, age, season, use of antiprotozoal drugs, tick infestation, and acaricide application. Mixed-breed cattle were more likely to be infected with B. bigemina (OR = 6.96; 95% CI: 0.80–60.48) compared with other breeds. In addition, cattle older than 4 years had significantly higher odds of infection (OR = 7.01; 95% CI: 2.07–23.73) than younger animals (Table 2).

Season also played an important role, as summer was associated with a significantly increased risk of infection (OR = 4.16; 95% CI: 1.10–15.71) compared with the other seasons. Management practices further influenced infection risk; cattle that did not receive antiprotozoal drugs were more susceptible to infection (OR = 6.04; 95% CI: 1.28–28.44) than animals that received regular treatment. The presence of ticks and the absence of acaricide application were among the strongest predictors of infection. Cattle infested with ticks showed higher odds of B. bigemina infection (OR = 3.16; 95% CI: 1.09–10.23). Similarly, animals not treated with acaricides were at an elevated risk of infection (OR = 2.37; 95% CI: 1.28–6.32) compared with those receiving regular acaricidal control (Table 2).

Discussion

Tick-borne infections caused by B. bigemina represent a major constraint to cattle production in many tropical and subtropical regions, where they are associated with substantial economic losses38. Despite their importance, the epidemiological status of these infections in Egypt remains insufficiently characterized. Limited surveillance, inadequate estimation of prevalence, and the lack of effective preventive and control strategies have contributed to the continued neglect of bovine babesiosis in several regions of Egypt39.

Therefore, the present study provides an overview of the occurrence of B. bigemina infection in cattle and investigates the potential risk factors associated with its transmission under field conditions. Understanding the epidemiological patterns and identifying key risk determinants are essential for developing effective control strategies and improving the health and productivity of cattle populations.

The overall prevalence of B. bigemina infection in the present study was 8%, which is consistent with the findings reported by Abdela et al.40, who documented a prevalence rate of 9.8% in cattle from southwestern Ethiopia. The prevalence of bovine babesiosis recorded in the present study was higher than the rates previously reported by other studies, such as 1.5% and 0.6% reported by Sitotaw et al.41. However, the prevalence observed in this study was lower than that reported in the Teltele district, Borena Zone (16.9%) by Alemu Hamsho et al.42. Such variations in prevalence rates among different studies may be attributed to several factors, including differences in the sensitivity of diagnostic methods, sampling strategies, environmental conditions, and the extent of tick control practices implemented in different regions43–46.

The epidemiology of bovine babesiosis is strongly influenced by the ecology and distribution of tick vectors responsible for parasite transmission47. In addition to biological transmission by ticks, limited mechanical transmission may also occur, although the probability of such transmission is considered low for B. bovis and relatively higher for B. bigemina48. Therefore, the predominance of B. bigemina infection observed in the present study is not unexpected.

The prevalence rate detected in the present study was lower than that previously reported in Egypt by Rizk et al.49 (12.6%), but it was nearly comparable to the results (12.66%) obtained using conventional PCR methods in the Middle Nile Delta of Egypt50. In contrast, lower prevalence rates of 5.30% and 3.97% were documented in cattle from the western Nile Delta and Upper Egypt, respectively51.

Globally, higher prevalence rates of B. bigemina infection have been reported in several tropical and subtropical regions. For instance, nested PCR assays revealed prevalence rates of 26.7% in Brazil52, 18.8% in the Philippines53, 29% in Pakistan54, and 78.5% in Portugal55.

The variation in prevalence rates observed among different studies may be attributed to several factors, including differences in animal sex, breed susceptibility, ecological conditions, farm management practices, and the inconsistent use of antiprotozoal and acaricidal treatments56,57. Furthermore, environmental factors such as temperature and humidity can significantly influence the survival, reproduction, and geographical spread of tick vectors, thereby facilitating the expansion of tick-borne diseases into new areas58. These factors collectively contribute to the considerable diversity in the reported prevalence of bovine babesiosis across different regions.

Age has frequently been reported as an important risk factor associated with the prevalence of tick-borne diseases (TBDs) in cattle51.

Similarly, the findings of the present study demonstrated a clear age-related pattern, where the risk of B. bigemina infection increased progressively with advancing age. The highest prevalence of bovine babesiosis was observed in adult and older animals, which were 1.3 and 7 times more likely, respectively, to be infected compared with young animals.

These findings are consistent with several previous studies conducted in different regions of Ethiopia59,60 as well as studies reported from other countries61, which also demonstrated a higher prevalence of babesiosis among older cattle. However, this observation contradicts the findings of Fereig et al.62, who reported that younger animals were more susceptible to Babesia infection compared with adults.

The lower prevalence detected in young animals in the present study may be explained by several factors. Young animals are often less exposed to tick vectors due to more restricted grazing and management practices, which limit their contact with infested pastures. In contrast, older animals are exposed to vectors for longer periods over their lifetime, increasing their cumulative risk of infection. Furthermore, young animals in endemic areas may benefit from passive immunity acquired through colostrum, which can provide temporary protection during the early stages of life. As a result, infections in young animals are often mild or transient., with limited clinical manifestations24,39.

Animal breed and the irregular use of antiprotozoal drugs were identified as potential risk factors associated with B. bigemina infection. The higher prevalence observed in Friesian cattle compared with native breeds may be attributed to the longer historical exposure of native animals to the parasite, which may enable them to develop a stronger protective immunity against infection. In contrast, exotic or crossbred cattle are often more susceptible to tick-borne diseases due to lower adaptation to local environmental conditions and endemic pathogens. Similar observations have been reported for Babesia gibsoni infection in previous studies49.

The presence and availability of tick vectors represent one of the most important risk factors for the transmission of Babesia infections in cattle48. In the present study, multivariable logistic regression analysis demonstrated that the risk of bovine babesiosis was significantly higher in tick-infested cattle (9.8%) compared with non-infested animals (3.5%). These findings highlight the critical role of ticks in the epidemiology and transmission dynamics of B. bigemina.

A similar association between tick infestation and increased prevalence of babesiosis has been reported by Costa et al.63, who also identified tick infestation as a significant risk factor for infection. The higher infection rate among tick-infested animals may be attributed to the direct role of ticks as biological vectors, facilitating the transmission of Babesia parasites during blood feeding. Consequently, effective tick control measures, including the regular use of acaricides and proper farm management practices, are essential strategies for reducing the risk and spread of bovine babesiosis in endemic regions64,65.

Ecological conditions, management practices, and irregular use of antiprotozoal drugs were identified as significant risk factors for Babesia infection in the multivariable logistic regression model. The higher prevalence observed in cattle from the Nile Delta region may be attributed to high humidity and favorable environmental conditions that support the survival and distribution of tick vectors. Environmental factors such as temperature and moisture influence tick abundance and activity, although their exact relationship remains complex66.

The lack of regular acaricide application was significantly linked to a higher prevalence of B. bigemina infection in cattle. This observation emphasizes the crucial role of effective tick control strategies in reducing the transmission of tick-borne pathogens. Cattle that were not routinely treated with acaricides were more susceptible to tick infestation, thereby increasing their risk of exposure to Babesia parasites.

These findings are consistent with the study of da Silva Casa et al.67, who reported that irregular or inadequate use of acaricides contributes to the persistence of tick populations and consequently elevates the risk of babesiosis in livestock. Therefore, the adoption of regular and well-managed acaricide treatment programs is essential as an effective preventive measure to minimize the occurrence and spread of Babesia infection in endemic areas68.

Conclusion

The present study provides updated information on the molecular prevalence and epidemiological determinants of B. bigemina infection in cattle in the Nile Delta region of Egypt. The overall prevalence of infection was 8%, indicating that bovine babesiosis remains present among cattle populations in the studied areas. Several factors, including breed, age, season, tick infestation, and management practices, were identified as significant risk factors associated with infection. Older animals, mixed breeds, tick-infested cattle, and animals raised without regular antiprotozoal treatment or acaricide application showed a higher risk of infection. These findings emphasize the critical role of tick control programs, regular use of acaricides, and proper herd management practices in reducing the transmission of B. bigemina. Strengthening epidemiological surveillance and implementing integrated vector control strategies are essential steps to minimize the impact of bovine babesiosis and improve cattle productivity in endemic regions of Egypt.
