Prediction Model for Tight Gas Wells with Time-Dependent Mechanism and Stress Sensitivity Effect
Authors: Yuhu Bai, Suran Wang, Bingxiang Xu, Dongsheng Li, Weipeng Fan, Jiaqi Wu, Baosheng Jiang, Shijun Huang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
In the production process of tight gas wells, reservoir fluid distribution and gas–water relative permeability vary with time. However, traditional models fail to handle the time-dependent mechanism and stress sensitivity effect in the reservoir, leading to significant errors in the dynamic analysis results. To address this issue, this article presents a prediction model for fractured well production in tight gas reservoirs. It is based on a three-dimensional embedded discrete fracture model (EDFM), which considers the influences of the time-dependent mechanism and stress-dependent reservoir permeability. Transient flow equations are treated by using the finite volume method to obtain the solution of the model. The accuracy and reliability of the model are verified by comparison with the results of the commercial simulator Eclipse and the field application. Based on the model’s solution, this study emphasizes the analysis of the impact of the time-dependent mechanism and reservoir stress sensitivity on gas well productivity. Simulation results show that the time-dependent relative permeability curve can decrease the level of irreducible water saturation and promote the migration of irreducible water, resulting in an increase in water permeability and a decrease in gas permeability. This effect will reduce the period of stable gas production and increase the level of water production. Besides, reservoir stress sensitivity will reduce daily water production and accelerate gas well decline. It is necessary to control the production pressure difference reasonably during the production process to effectively reduce the negative impact of stress sensitivity effects. The results indicate that when the relative permeability curve and the reservoir permeability are constant, the real gas production capacity of the reservoir will be strengthened. The application of field case studies shows that the theoretical model exhibits stronger adaptability, achieves better fitting results, and can guide the compilation and adjustment of development plans for water-bearing tight gas reservoirs. These findings provide insights into understanding the effects of the time-dependent mechanism on gas production rates in tight gas reservoirs. Furthermore, this study offers useful guidance for the prediction of field-scale gas production.

Introduction

Tight sandstone reservoirs typically exhibit poor physical properties, and hydraulic fracturing is key to enhancing the productivity of tight gas wells.1−3 Research indicates that hydraulic fracturing often results in a complex fracture network modification body, making the degree of fracturing a significant factor limiting the productivity of tight gas wells.4−7 These reservoirs usually have high initial water saturation, and gas–water two-phase flow commonly occurs during exploitation.8−10 Numerous studies suggest that changes in the pressure gradient affect fluid distribution and gas–water flow behavior in the reservoir, and the gas–water relative permeability curve exhibits significant time-variation effects.11−14 Additionally, research suggests that tight sandstone reservoirs exhibit strong stress sensitivity, which has a serious negative effect on the flowing capability of gas and water, directly influencing the stable production period of gas wells.15−17 Therefore, the seepage parameters of the hydraulic fracture, the time-dependent mechanism, and stress sensitivity in the reservoir are key factors impacting the accurate production prediction of tight gas wells and should be given priority in productivity evaluation models.18,19

Currently, methods for predicting the productivity of tight sandstone gas wells mainly include analytical, semianalytical, and numerical simulation methods. Analytical methods are typically based on the steady-state seepage theory, employing the source and sink theory to establish tight gas well productivity models.20−23 These methods often introduce two-phase pseudopressure to linearize equations and employ conformal transformation and the superposition principle to derive the productivity equations of water-producing gas wells.24,25 As analytical models disregard the impact of nonlinear seepage parameters, this results in significant computational errors. Semianalytical methods, primarily founded on the assumption of a linear flow model, can effectively characterize fracture modification bodies, are computationally convenient, and are widely applied. However, these methods are only suitable for the productivity prediction of single-phase problems and cannot accurately depict artificial fracture parameter characteristics.26−28 For the gas–water two-phase flow that occurs during the development of tight gas reservoirs, due to the severe nonlinearity of the mathematical model itself, semianalytical methods are no longer applicable. In general, current analytical and semianalytical methods struggle with handling the nonlinear flow problem of gas–water two-phase flow. Numerical simulation methods can explicitly represent artificial fracture parameter characteristics and manage multiphase fluid flow issues. However, conventional numerical simulation methods are complex in preprocessing fractures and do not consider the complex spatial distribution and flow characteristics of three-dimensional fractures.29−33 To achieve higher simulation accuracy, fractures typically require mesh refinement, leading to a large quantity of meshes. This results in lower time efficiency when thousands of case analyses are done. Moreover, during the exploitation process of tight gas reservoirs, reservoir fluid distribution and gas–water relative permeability will vary with time. However, existing numerical simulation methods usually set the gas–water relative permeability curve as a fixed value by neglecting the time-dependent mechanism, ultimately leading to significant errors in the dynamic analysis results.

In conclusion, the difficulties in accurately predicting the productivity of tight sandstone gas wells mainly manifest in three aspects: the characterization and simulation of three-dimensional discrete fractures; the characterization and simulation of gas–water two-phase flow and time-dependent mechanisms; and reservoir stress sensitivity. To address these issues, based on theoretical and experimental results, this paper first establishes representation models to characterize the time-dependent mechanism and reservoir stress sensitivity. Next, a three-dimensional embedded discrete fracture model is derived to incorporate the characterization of the hydraulic fracture. Thus, we construct a three-dimensional embedded discrete fracture model for a three-dimensional reservoir simulation in fractured tight gas reservoirs, considering the combined influences of the time-dependent mechanism and reservoir stress sensitivity. Then, we accurately and stably solve the model using the finite volume method, verifying the accuracy and reliability of the model by comparing it with Eclipse computation results and field application. Last, based on the theoretical model, we analyze the impact of the time-dependent mechanism and stress sensitivity effects on the productivity of tight gas wells.

Methodology

Physical Model Assumption

A well at the center of the gas reservoir is fractured to create an artificial fracture. The hydraulic fracture is symmetric with the wellbore and is arbitrarily distributed, with a half-length of xf, a height of hf, and a width of wf. In this study, the fracture is regarded as a three-dimensional discrete fracture with a finite conductivity, as shown in Figure 1. The basic assumptions are as follows: (1) the stress sensitivity effect of the reservoir is considered; (2) the time-dependent mechanism in the reservoir is taken into account; (3) changes in the gas volume coefficient, viscosity, and compressibility factor with pressure are considered; (4) fluid flow in the matrix and fractures adheres to isothermal Darcy’s law; (5) the effects of gravity and slippage are disregarded.

Mechanisms and Models

Time-Dependent Mechanism and Representation Model

The mechanisms of water encounter differ between conventional and tight reservoirs. Conventional reservoirs, which are characterized by higher permeability and good connectivity, often form a distinct gas–water interface due to gravity segregation and geological structures. In contrast, tight gas reservoirs, known for their low porosity and permeability, are primarily influenced by capillary forces that affect the pore-level distribution of gas and water. Numerous visualization experiments demonstrate that gas exerts a peeling effect on the water film covering the rock surface. This peeling effect intensifies with an increasing pressure difference and a gas flow rate. During the actual production process of tight gas wells, pressure gradients within the reservoir fluctuate at different locations and times, resulting in a time-variant fluid distribution and gas–water relative permeability. In this paper, we derive theoretical calculation formulas for irreducible water saturation and the gas–water relative permeability curve based on fractal theory. Basic Calculating Procedure of the Proposed Model

The proposed mathematical model is discretized using the finite volume method and solved with the fully implicit method. The calculation procedure of the proposed model is shown in Figure 4. The computational process is as follows: (1)Complete the initialization of the grid and input relevant reservoir property parameters, including reservoir size, grid information, porosity, initial permeability of the reservoir, and the initial relative permeability curve.(2)Input the parameters controlling the temporal variation of irreducible water saturation and gas–water relative permeability.(3)Input the boundary conditions and production well conditions.(4)Initialize the pressure and saturation values.(5)Couple the flow equations for the gas and water phases, using the fully implicit method to calculate the pressure and saturation values for each grid.(6)Compute the pressure gradient values for each grid.(7)Recalculate the irreducible water saturation values and end point values of the relative permeability curve for each grid, updating the values for the next time step.(8)Calculate the pressure sensitivity factors for each grid, and update the permeability for the next time step.(9)Determine whether the calculations have converged. If not, make the necessary adjustments and repeat the calculations.(10)Output the calculation results.

When comparing the production indicators of the two models over a 20-year period, the results indicate that the gas–water production curves obtained using the model proposed in this paper are essentially consistent with the numerical simulation results. The average relative error calculated in this example is less than 5%, suggesting that the proposed model can be employed for the analysis and prediction of production data. The process of establishing a three-dimensional fracture using commercial numerical simulation software is relatively complex. The use of localized refinement or nonuniform grids may lead to an increase in the number of grids and significant differences in size between grids, thereby substantially reducing calculation efficiency. In this paper, the finite volume method is adopted to discretely solve the model, ensuring local material conservation and calculation accuracy. Additionally, in the EDFM model, fractures are embedded into the grid in various ways; global rectangular fractures are directly generated without the need for grid refinement processing, thereby enhancing computational efficiency. To obtain the calculation results shown in Figure 7, the proposed method developed in this study was used to calculate on the same platform, and the time was less than 70 s, while Eclipse’s calculation time was more than 400 s. Furthermore, this study emphasizes the handling of the time-varying characteristics of gas–water relative permeability. Since current commercial numerical simulation software does not consider the time-dependent mechanism, this study addresses this challenge. Therefore, in practical applications, the new model will provide more accurate evaluations of gas well productivity and water production prediction.

Effect of the Time-Dependent Mechanism on the Productivity

It can be observed that after considering the time-varying effect of gas–water relative permeability, the stable production period of the gas well decreases from 269 to 89 days and the average water–gas ratio increases from 0.39/104 m3 to 0.74/104 m3. The average daily water production of the gas well over 20 years of simulated production increases from 0.11 to 0.18 m3/day, a rise of 64%, while the degree of gas extraction decreases from 39.09 to 34.45%, a reduction of 4.64%. These results indicate that without considering the time-varying effect of relative permeability, the predicted stable production period is overly extended and the predicted water production is far lower than the actual water production of the gas well. This implies that the time-varying effect significantly influences the production of gas wells. In summary, in the production process, the time-varying effect of the gas–water relative permeability can decrease the irreducible water saturation, enabling some irreducible water to transform into mobile water. This change increases the water phase permeability and decreases the gas phase permeability, ultimately leading to a shortened stable production period, increased water production, and a reduced extraction degree. Therefore, the impact of the time-varying effect of gas–water relative permeability should be considered when formulating gas well rotation and adjustments to the development plan.

Effect of the Reservoir Stress Sensibility on the Productivity

Based on the reservoir and fracture parameters in Table 2, and combined with the results of reservoir core stress sensitivity experiments, this section analyzes the impact of reservoir stress sensitivity on the productivity of tight gas wells. The stress sensitivity coefficient ranged between 0 and 0.03 MPa–1. Figures 11 and 12 show the productivity change curves under different stress sensitivity coefficients. It can be observed that the larger the stress sensitivity coefficient, the worse the stable productivity of the gas well and the lower the gas recovery. After considering the effect of stress sensitivity, the stable production time of the gas well decreases from 89 to 49 days and the gas recovery drops from 34.45 to 31.55%, a decrease of 2.9%. For tight gas reservoirs, a reasonable production pressure difference should be maintained during the production process to reduce the impact of stress sensitivity on productivity.

Effect of the Initial Water Saturation on the Productivity

In some tight gas reservoirs, the initial water saturation can reach up to 55%, causing significant water production issues that severely restrict the productivity of gas wells. Using realistic geological parameters and our productivity evaluation model, we analyzed the gas and water production trends of tight gas wells under different initial water saturations (40, 45, 50, 55%). As can be seen from Figures 13 and 14, there is a positive correlation between initial water saturation and water production volume and water–gas ratio. The higher the initial water saturation, the more the trend of water–gas ratio decline caused by flow becomes dominant and the water–gas ratio in the middle and late stages remains almost constant. The higher the initial water saturation, the poorer the stable productivity of the gas well and the lower the degree of gas extraction.

Effect of Key Flow Parameters of the Hydraulic Fracture on the Productivity

Figure 15 through Figure 18, respectively, reflect the impact of fracture conductivity and fracture half-length on the productivity of tight gas wells. It can be observed that the greater the fracture conductivity, the larger the initial production, the longer the steady production time, the slower the decline of production, and the greater the degree of gas extraction. From Figures 15 and 16, it can be seen that as the fracture conductivity increases from 5 to 30 D·m, the steady production time of the gas well lengthens, and the average water–gas ratio increases from 0.63/104 m3 to 0.74/104 m3, and the average daily water production of the gas well simulated over 20 years increases from 0.128 to 0.18 m3/day. Although the water/gas ratio increases, the degree of gas extraction increases from 27.54 to 34.45%, an increase of 6.91%. At the same time, it can be observed that the continuous increase of fracture conductive ability to 50 D·m does not significantly change the productivity, mainly because the fracture conductive ability is approaching infinite conductivity. In addition, the fracture half-length has a significant impact on initial production and steady production time but a minor impact on the water–gas ratio. From Figures 17 and 18, it can be observed that with the increase in fracture half-length, the steady production time of the gas well lengthens, the decrease in production slows down. As the fracture half-length increases from 100 to 200 m, the degree of gas extraction increases from 34.45 to 40.07%, an increase of 5.62%, but the water–gas ratio changes very little. Overall, the fracture half-length indirectly reflects the size of the reservoir modification volume and also reflects the well control reserves and the range of mobilization. The larger its value, the larger the linear flow area of the fracture and the greater the range of mobilization. Therefore, in the development of high water content tight gas reservoirs, the degree and range of modification should be maximized during hydraulic fracturing construction to enhance gas well productivity.

Field Application

This paper takes a water-producing tight gas well in a basin as an example to illustrate the application effect of the model. The well was put into production through hydraulic fracturing in September 2019. As of May 2022, it has achieved a cumulative gas production rate of 342.5 × 104 m3. The production dynamics curve is shown in Figure 19, and the basic data for this well are shown in Table 3. The effective thickness, porosity, and gas saturation were all derived from the well-log interpretation results. Based on the experience of evaluating tight gas hydraulic fracturing, the model sets the fracture half-length at 100 m and the fracture conductivity at 30 mD·m. The new model proposed in this paper was used to fit and predict the gas–water production data of this well, as shown in Figure 20. It can be seen from the fitting results that the overall fitting effect of the theoretical curve and the measured curve is relatively good. Although there is a certain deviation in the fitting results, they are all within the allowable range of engineering error. Predicting the gas–water production of this well, the cumulative gas production over 20 years is estimated to be 1310 × 104 m3, and the cumulative water production is about 3020 m3.

Conclusions

This paper presents a new methodology to investigate the combined effects of time-dependent mechanisms and stress-dependent reservoir permeability on the transient rate response for a fractured well in tight gas reservoirs. The main conclusions of this work are as follows:(1)The production prediction model established in this paper can rapidly and accurately evaluate the production capacity of water-producing tight gas wells and predict their water production.(2)The time-dependent mechanism can decrease the irreducible water saturation and promote the migration of irreducible water. This leads to an increase in water permeability and a decrease in gas permeability, ultimately reducing the period of stable gas production and increasing water production.(3)The effect of stress sensitivity on the reservoir leads to a loss of permeability and an increase in fluid flow resistance, which affects the productivity of tight sandstone gas wells. During the production process, it is necessary to control the production pressure difference reasonably to effectively reduce the negative impact of stress sensitivity.(4)The key flow parameters of fractures have a significant impact on the dynamic production of gas and water. Since water production from tight gas reservoirs severely affects the productivity of gas wells, under the same reservoir and fracture parameters, the productivity under the condition of gas–water two-phase flow is significantly lower than that under the condition of single-phase flow.
