Intestinal Obstruction Secondary to Strangulated Richter Type Obturator Hernia: A Case Report
Authors: Suresh Maharjan, Samrat Shrestha, Deepika Rijal, Sabin Ghimire, Kiran Bishwakarma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObturator hernia is a rare internal hernia causing significant mortality and morbidity in elderly age groups. Diagnosis of obturator hernia is challenging because of vague atypical presentations. This case report describes a woman in her late 80s who presented with colicky right lower abdominal pain, abdominal distension, and obstipation, diagnosed with a right obturator hernia, computed tomography (CT) scan. An elderly female presented with chief complaints of right lower abdominal pain and abdominal distension. On examination, the abdomen was distended with generalized tenderness and rebound tenderness. CECT abdomen and pelvis showed dilated small bowel loops with a transition point caused by herniation through the right obturator foramen. Emergency laparotomy revealed a right strangulated obturator hernia. Resection of the gangrenous ileal segment with ileo‐ileal anastomosis and primary closure of the defect was done. Obturator hernia occurs through the obturator canal, which is mostly seen in elderly females. Obturator hernia usually presents as partial bowel obstruction because of a high frequency (41%–100%) of Richter's herniation of the small bowel into the obturator canal. Diagnosis can be delayed because of its nonspecific clinical presentation. CT scan is the most accurate and sensitive way to diagnose the obturator hernia. Obturator defects can be repaired by various methods including simple suture closure, closure of the obturator with adjacent tissue, and mesh placement during laparotomy. Obturator hernia is a rare abdominal hernia with delayed diagnosis because of vague atypical clinical presentation. Early diagnosis and prompt surgical treatment are essential to reduce morbidity and mortality.Keywords: case report, Hannington–Kiff sign, Howship–Romberg sign, intestinal obstruction, obturator hernia

Introduction

An obturator hernia accounts for 0.07%–1% of all hernias and 0.2%–1.6% of all cases of intestinal obstruction [1]. Obturator hernias are more common on the right side because of the presence of a sigmoid colon on the left [2]. Elderly thin‐built women have larger obturator canals with less preperitoneal adipose tissue, leading to herniation of abdominal content when intraabdominal pressure rises [3]. It is a significant cause of morbidity and mortality, especially in the elderly [4, 5, 6]. Obturator hernias are diagnostic dilemmas preoperatively since their symptoms are nonspecific. Obturator hernia accounts for 13%–40% of mortality among abdominal hernia [7]. Strangulated obturator hernia remains a life‐threatening condition, with mortality rates as high as 70% in untreated cases [6]. Signs and symptoms are nonspecific, including nausea, vomiting, and abdominal pain, along with pain in the groin or medial thigh. This case has been reported according to SCARE guidelines 2023 [8].

Case History and Examination

An elderly female in her late 80s with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) presented to our emergency department with complaints of colicky right lower abdominal pain, abdominal distension for 7 days, and obstipation for 5 days. She had no history of vomiting and had never undergone any abdominal surgery. On examination, the patient was frail with a BMI of 17 kg/m2. A vital examination revealed a blood pressure of 97/50 mmHg, a pulse rate of 116/min, a respiratory rate of 22/min, an oxygen saturation of 91% in room air, and afebrile. Abdominal examination showed a distended abdomen with generalized tenderness and rebound tenderness with sluggish bowel sounds. Hernial orifices were intact, and per‐rectal examination was nonsignificant. Laboratory findings were within normal limits.

Investigation and Treatment

A plain abdominal X‐ray showed multiple air‐fluid levels with dilated small bowel loops. Contrast‐enhanced computed tomography (CECT) of the abdomen and pelvis showed dilated small bowel loops with a transition point caused by herniation through the right obturator foramen (Figures 1 and 2). A diagnosis of a right‐sided strangulated obturator hernia was made, and an emergency exploratory laparotomy via lower midline incision was performed. This approach was chosen because of the emergent nature of the case and delayed presentation with the suspicion of a gangrenous bowel segment. Intraoperative findings include a right strangulated obturator hernia (around 75% circumference) containing ileum (Figure 3) with 400 mL of serous intra‐peritoneal collection. Approximately 5 cm of the ileum, 70 cm proximal to the ileocecal junction, was non‐viable. Resection of the gangrenous ileal segment with isoperistaltic ileo‐ileal anastomosis was done. The obturator defect (Figure 4) was closed primarily with a non‐absorbable polypropylene suture. The contralateral obturator canal was examined, and the obturator hernia was ruled out.

Outcome and Follow‐Up

A liquid diet was initiated on the 3rd postoperative day (POD), and a soft diet started from the 5th POD. The patient was discharged on the 7th POD. On a 2‐week follow‐up, the patient was recovering well with a healthy wound. On 1‐month, 3‐month, and 6‐month follow‐up, the patient showed no symptoms and/or signs of intestinal obstruction or recurrence.

Discussion

Arnaud de Ronsil described an obturator hernia in 1724, and Henry Obre was the first to successfully repair it in 1851 [9]. Obturator hernia (also referred to as little old ladies' hernia) occurs through the obturator canal, which is 2–3 cm long and 1 cm wide, and contains the obturator nerve and vessels [1, 10]. Greater pelvic width, larger obturator canal, and age‐related increased tissue laxity in elderly females predispose to obturator hernia [5]. Three main clinical manifestations of obturator hernia: most common: intestinal obstruction (80%), followed by anteromedial thigh pain; Howship‐Romberg sign (50%), and rarely a palpable mass in the inguinal region [4, 6, 11, 12]. Richter's herniation (41%–100%) in obturator hernia commonly leads to partial bowel obstruction [1]. The patient, an elderly woman in her late 80s with COPD and a low BMI, presented with colicky right lower abdominal pain, abdominal distension, and obstipation, symptoms that are typical of intestinal obstruction but nonspecific. This case aligns with previous findings that obturator hernia can mimic other causes of small bowel obstruction. If clinicians have a high index of suspicion, two physical examinations can be employed: Hannington–Kiff and Howship–Romberg signs. Anteromedial thigh pain worsening on adduction, extension, and medial rotation of the thigh defines the Howship–Romberg sign, which is due to compression of the cutaneous branch of the obturator nerve [13]. The Hannington–Kiff sign refers to an absent adductor reflex in the thigh because of obturator nerve compression [13, 14]. These signs are not always present, and their absence does not exclude the diagnosis. This underscores the need for a high index of suspicion in elderly females presenting with small bowel obstruction and unexplained thigh pain [12]. This case exemplifies how a thorough history and physical examination, along with a high index of suspicion, can guide the diagnosis despite the absence of these hallmark signs.

Early accurate diagnosis of obturator hernia is rare and therefore associated with a high mortality rate [6]. Most of the cases (2/3rd) of obturator hernia are diagnosed on exploratory laparotomy done for intestinal obstruction [15, 16]. The condition is often missed on initial presentation, leading to delays in treatment. CECT is the gold standard for diagnosis, as it provides clear visualization of the hernia sac, bowel involvement, and the obturator canal.

Surgery is the mainstay of treatment, with various approaches such as abdominal, inguinal, retropubic, obturator, and laparoscopy being the most common techniques [9]. A low midline abdominal approach is preferred for establishing a diagnosis, better exposure, avoiding concomitant obturator vessel injury, and facilitating bowel resection if necessary [1]. Defects can be repaired by simple suture closure, closure of the obturator with adjacent tissue, and mesh placement in an uncontaminated surgical field [17]. In the case of a contaminated surgical field requiring bowel resection, simple closure of the defect with one or two interrupted nonabsorbable sutures is preferred [18, 19]. In this case, following resection anastomosis, simple suture closure of the hernia defect with a nonabsorbable polypropylene suture was done. Laparoscopic techniques are increasingly favored for their minimally invasive nature and reduced postoperative recovery time. The use of mesh for defect repair is generally reserved for uncontaminated surgical fields, as it provides durable reinforcement and reduces the likelihood of recurrence.

The recurrence rates of obturator hernias vary on the basis of the surgical repair method employed. Non‐mesh (primary suture) repair: A systematic review and meta‐analysis found that hernia recurrence was significantly higher in patients who underwent repair without mesh, with a relative risk (RR) of 0.31. Mesh repair: The same study indicated that mesh repair was associated with lower recurrence rates without an increase in postoperative complications [20]. In this case, resecting the gangrenous ileal segment followed by anastomosis and simple suture closure of the hernia defect with nonabsorbable polypropylene suture was used. The decision to avoid mesh placement due to the contamination from the gangrenous bowel and to perform simple suture closure was appropriate for ensuring a safer outcome.

In this case, the patient's advanced age, low BMI, and nonspecific presentation with abdominal pain and distension were typical of intestinal obstruction, but these symptoms alone did not immediately suggest obturator hernia. The patient's age and frailty, combined with a low BMI, likely contributed to the formation of the hernia. The clinical presentation lacked the characteristic signs of obturator hernia, which made diagnosis difficult, particularly when Howship–Romberg or Hannington–Kiff signs were absent. Despite these challenges, the timely use of CECT played a pivotal role in confirming the diagnosis. It was crucial in visualizing the hernia sac, its contents, and the bowel's involvement, ultimately guiding the successful surgical intervention.

Conclusion

Though obturator hernia is rare, it should be kept in mind if emaciated elderly women present with features suggestive of intestinal obstruction with no past surgical history. Thorough history taking and physical examination provide diagnostic clues for suspected obturator hernia. A CT scan is valuable to establish a preoperative diagnosis. Early diagnosis and appropriate surgical intervention are crucial to prevent complications like bowel strangulation and necrosis, improving patient outcomes and reducing mortality. This report underscores the need for thorough clinical evaluation and the use of advanced imaging techniques in diagnosing rare but potentially life‐threatening conditions like obturator hernia. This case emphasizes the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for obturator hernia in elderly patients presenting with intestinal obstruction, even in the absence of specific physical findings.
