Diagnostic efficacy of endoscopic ultrasound-guided fine needle aspiration cytology in pancreatic lesions
Authors: Pranita Mohanty, Priyadarshini Dehuri, Jimmy Narayan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEndoscopic Ultrasound (EUS) guided needle aspiration cytology (FNAC) is a rapid, reliable diagnostic procedure used in various benign and malignant pancreatic lesions. The present study was aimed to determine the diagnostic accuracy of endoscopic ultrasound-guided needle aspiration cytology (EUS-FNAC) in diagnosing pancreatic lesions. To the best of our knowledge, the first study of this kind in eastern India. The current study was a hospital-based prospective study. The study was conducted in a tertiary care hospital of eastern India over a period of two year. A total of 67 cases of radiologically detected pancreatic lesions were studied. Pancreatic lesions were sampled by EUS-FNA and cytology reporting was done basing on Papanicolaou Society for assessment of cytology of pancreatobiliary system (PSC)as well as new WHO system of reporting 2022. A simultaneous needle biopsy(FNAB)was done to reach at a final histopathology diagnosis. The Cytology reports were compared with the corresponding histology reports as the latter is considered to be the gold standard. Diff-Quik / PAP/H&E stains were routinely used for cytology, and H& E stain was used for corresponding histology sections. In morphologically challenging cases, immunohistochemistry was also done. The SPSS statistics software (version 16) was used for Statistical analysis. The study comprised a total of 67 pancreatic lesions cases with a mean age of 55.5 years and age range of 30–80 years. In both sexes, maximum cases were observed between 51 and 60 years. 42cases were males, whereas 25 cases were females (M: F = 1.7:1). The pancreatic head was the most involved site [29(43.28%) cases]. Among PSC categories; Category II (negative for malignancy) cases were highest in number(n = 27),then were the Category VI( positive or malignant ) i.e. (n = 25).Sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV and diagnostic accuracy were 84.6%, 60%, 58%, 85.71%&70.14% respectively. Whereas reanalysis done by WHO categories revealed; Sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV and diagnostic accuracy as87.5%, 82.8%, 82.3%, 87.8% &85.07%. EUS-FNA is an effective diagnostic procedure for differentiating malignant and benign pancreatic lesions. Introduction

Endoscopic Ultrasonography (EUS) is considered to be a reliable tool for locating and assessing the extent of lesions in the pancreas. However, it cannot distinctly differentiate benign from malignant or tumor from inflammatory lesions. Pancreatic lesions are especially challenging as they present with variable and overlapping clinical features like dyspepsia, abdominal pain, jaundice, etc.; in both benign conditions as well as potentially lethal lesions that need early interventions. Amongst all, pancreatic cancer constitutes a major cause of mortality &morbidity. Pancreatic cancer constitutes 3.2% of all malignancies with a mortality of 8.2% of all cancer deaths according to the National Cancer Institute data1. The most important advantage of EUS-FNAC is its ability to conduct wider sampling and provide a more representative area of the lesion, which makes it superior to other cytological procedures that often fail to sample the lesion2. However, to establish a definitive diagnosis and provide specific management, histology correlation is mandatory either by a small biopsy (FNAB) or by resection specimen3,4. With this aim the present study was conducted and to the best of our knowledge, the first study of this kind in eastern India.

Materials & methods

The present study was a hospital based prospective study conducted over a period of two year in a tertiary care teaching hospital with the collaboration of department of pathology and gastroenterology.Radiologically identified space-occupying lesions in the pancreas and bile ducts were included in the study irrespective of age and sex. Patients with high cardiovascular risk/on anticoagulation or with homeostatic disorders/non-cooperative patients/untraceable patients were excluded from the study.

Total 67 cases of pancreatic lesions were included in the study. A written consent was taken for the procedure(both FNAC & FNAB) and for the collection of the demographic data as per hospital protocol. Radiology, EUS findings, clinical symptoms and investigatory results of participants were obtained either directly or from hospital records and database system. Under sedation, EUS was performed by gastroenterologist with the help of GF – UC140P-AL5 linear Echo endoscope. Aspiration was performed using a 22-gauge FNA needle. Maximum five passes of a pancreatic mass were given to ensure adequate sampling followed by a rapid on-site evaluation (ROSE) of cytology was performed by a cytopathologist. Once aspiration is done the aspirated material were smeared immediately and air dried for subsequent Diff-Quik stain and rest of the aspirated material were fixed in 90%alcohol for H&E and Papanicolaou stain. Cellblocks were prepared wherever required and in cystic lesion of pancreas aspirated fluid was sent for both biochemical and marker study (in suspected carcinoma cases).

In the same setting immediately after FNAC, Concurrent needle biopsy (core biopsy/FNAB) with 19G needle was also performed on all the cases for final diagnosis. The patients were kept under observation for two hours to prevent any unwanted complications5. Biopsy is considered as the gold standard and that reduces the false negative/false positive cases. Hence biopsy from FNAB associated with available cell block/resected sample biopsy was considered for the final histopathology diagnosis.

Sample adequacy in both the procedures (FNA & needle biopsy) was assessed correlating with the type of lesion in radiology findings. For example, if a mucinous cyst is aspirated smear showing scattered benign epithelial cells in a stringy mucinous background may be adequate in a single pass; whereas in case of solid pancreatic tumors like pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, minimum 50 tumor cells are adequate in H&E sections of needle biopsy for confirmation and follow up IHC markers. Then the needle biopsy materials were transferred into a formalin jar for fixation followed by paraffin embedding (FFPE) and routine H&E staining for subsequent histopathology examination. Whereas cellblocks were first processed in plasma-thrombin/thromboplastin clot method before passing through the routine fixation and embedding.

Reporting was done by two blinded pathologists to check the inter observer variability and final decision on cytology was taken by a 3rd senior cyto pathologist in case of discrepancies. Pancreatic lesions were reported on the basis of the Papanicolaou Society of Cytopathology guidelines (PSC) 2014, under six diagnostic categories: I –nondiagnostic, II- negative for malignancy, III – atypical, IV- neoplastic: other, V – suspicious for malignancy, VI- positive for malignant6. Recently WHO has introduced seven tier reporting system for pancreaticobilliary cytopathology i.e. category I- VII with introduction of pancreatic neoplasm low & high grade as category IV & V respectively. It has also replaced SPN and NET Grade I into malignant category (category VII). WHO category VI & VII corresponds to PSC category V& VI. In the present study all 67 cases were also reanalyzed by seven tier new WHO categories (2022).The cytology diagnosis was compared with the final histopathology diagnosis interpreted from needle biopsy materials. Appropriate IHC staining including PanCK, CK7, CK20, synaptophysin, chromogranin, INSM1, ki67 etc. were done on histology sections of morphologically challenging/suspicious of malignant cases wherever possible on automated Ventana BenchMark ULTRA.

The SPSS statistics software (version 16) was used for Statistical analysis. Mean ± standard deviation was derived from continuous variables, and percentages were expressed for categorical.

variables. The t-test and χ 2 test were done between groups for comparison. The sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV with 95% CIS were assessed for the predictability of the procedure. AUROC was evaluated to estimate the overall accuracy of EUS-FNA. Odds ratios with 95% CIS represented the data. A significant ‘p’ value was considered as < 0.05.

Institutional ethical committee approval was obtained by IEC/IMS &SH (SOA) with regd no.- ECR/627/Inst/OR/2014/RR – 20. And prior informed consent was taken from all participants/or their legal guardian(s). Human Ethics were not violated as no new experimental procedure done & this work was a part of their usual procedure for treatment purpose. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations and all experimental protocols were approved by a named institutional and/or licensing committee.

Histology results revealed 23 cases (34.32%) as malignant neoplasm (true positive), 16cases (23.88%) as non-neoplastic, which were reported in cytology as malignant (false positive), and 24 (35.82%) were benign in nature (true negative).

Discussion

Endoscopic Ultrasonography (EUS) is a reliable tool for locating and assessing pancreatic lesions, but it is particularly challenging in a given setting of overlapping clinical features and differentiating between benign verses malignant lesions, or tumor verses inflammatory conditions. However radiology alone is not diagnostic always but an adjunct to FNAC& biopsy. Usually benign lesions in EUS show a hypoechoic rounded lesion with nomal parenchyma of pancreas and may have prominent pancreatic duct, no periportal/peripancreatic lymphadenopathy. Whereas a malignant lesion is usually heterogeneously hypoechoic and abuts surrounding structures, e.g. adenocarcinoma of pancreatic head may abut gastroduodenal Artery with/without perilesional lymphadenopathy (Fig. 9). EUS-FNAC offers the advantage of wider sampling, making it superior to other cytological methods and also provides better resolution for small lesions compared to CT(computed tomography); shortest trajectory to the lesion; multiple targets during single procedure (to grade and stage the patient); but should be correlated with histology through needle biopsy or resection specimens for definitive diagnosis and management2–4. Like in the present study, cases diagnosed as small cell carcinoma and grade I NETs were dealt with chemotherapy without undergoing aggressive surgery whereas cases of SPN, Pseudocysts and serous cystadenoma underwent surgical removal of the tumor mass following diagnosis by concurrent EUS FNAC and needle biopsy.

Many recent studies have examined the accuracy of EUS-FNA for both abdominal and mediastinal lesions. For instance, Al-Zubaidi et al. studied that the accuracy and sensitivity for pancreatic lesions were lower than for lymph nodes. In his study, the primary diagnosis was obtained via EUS FNAB in 49% (29/59) of patients with clinically suspicious lesions7.

Wu et al. study, included 23 cases of pancreatic lesions, and the largest number of cases were found from the pancreatic body (9 cases, 36.0%), followed by the pancreatic head, tail and neck. They found a median age of 61.5 years, with 61.1% male and 38.9% female8. Ilyas et al. conducted a retrospective analysis to assess the accuracy of EUS FNAC in 60 patients using the PSC system of pancreatic-biliary cytology. Their study revealed that 48% of lesions were located in the head, 40% in the body, and 12% in the tail of the pancreas that was in alliance with the present study9. Current study compares similarity with other studies regarding occurrence, PSC categories as well as the demographic data including age, sex & complain. The common complaints included in the current study were abdominal pain, jaundice, abdominal distension, and weight loss. No complication such as perforation, bleeding, or infection were reported while performing or following the procedure. While considering different PSC categories; Ilyas’ et al. reported Category VI (19 cases) as the most common type followed by the category II and Category III (1 case) was the least common one9. In contrast, Sung et al. observed Category IV (Benign) – 0% as least common pattern, and Category VI – 87.9% as most common one in a study of 1,017 pancreatic cases10. Whereas in the present study, least common was Category III (atypical)and most common entity was Category II (negative for malignancy) followed by Category VI (positive for malignant) though the difference between number of cases among the latter two were marginal. This observation almost goes along the findings of Ilyas’ et al. study.

In the context of diagnostic accuracy, Baek et al. assessed the diagnostic accuracy of EUS-FNAC in 191 patients with pancreatic lesions. Their histology result showed 14.7% negative for tumor, 4.7% benign lesions, 8.9% atypical, 68.1% malignant neoplasm, and 3.6% tissue insufficient for diagnosis11.

In the present study, the most significant discrepancies were found in16 false positive cases those were mostly included in Cat-III, IVB, V, VI. Factors contributing to the majority of 16 false positive cases were possibly - drying artifacts in diff-quik smear, inadequate biopsy, very small lesions, hemorrhage in the background, necrotic centers, cellular smears in certain benign conditions, poor sample quality and the technical expertise. 8 cases of SPN, 5 cases of papillary serous cystadenoma and 3 cases of neuroendocrine hyperplasia were included in these false positive category. In eight cases of SPN the probable cause of misdiagnosis in cytology was due to mild anisonucleosis, myxoid/hemorrhagic stroma or fibrovascular core and underscored it as malignant. So also five cases of papillary serous cystadenomam is diagnosed in FNAC mostly due to hemorrhagic background, acellular fibrous tissue fragments, destruction of delicate epithelial lining cells by smearing process and few Papillary fronds. Though histological, follow-up IHC study and accompanied radiology suggested rest three cases as neuroendocrine hyperplasia FNAC was in favor of well differentiated PNET (grade 1) as then radiology correlation was lacking that led to an interpretational error.

Four nondiagnostic cases (false negative) in cytology were later found to be malignant in histology of which three cases were pancreatic ductal carcinoma –NOS and one case-small cell NEC. The main reasons for misdiagnosis in these cases were insufficient tumor components in FNAC samples compared to needle biopsy samples, that led to false-negative results in cytology.

Among the 27 benign nonneoplastic cytology diagnoses; 17cases were chronic pancreatitis followed by7cases - pseudocyst, 2 cases - granulomatous peripancreatic lymphadenitis, one case - lymphoepithelial cyst, rest benign tumors included are SPN and serous cystadenoma. Whereas four cases classified as false negatives as they turned out to be malignant comprised- one case of small cell NEC (high grade) and three cases of ductal adenocarcinoma. Despite these discrepancies, follow-up cases were classified as true positives, reinforcing the importance of cytology in diagnosing malignancy.

A study by Kumar et al. on pancreatic lesions identified adenocarcinoma as the most common diagnosis (24.4%, n = 11), followed by neuroendocrine tumors (NET) (15.6%, n = 7), and solid pseudopapillary neoplasms (SPN) (4.4%, n = 2)12. While EUS-FNA can be challenging, especially when dealing with deep-seated organs like the pancreas and stomach, its diagnostic value remains high. Invasive and costly procedures, such as thoracotomy or laparoscopy, are considered secondary options. EUS-FNA has a distinct advantage in accessing these deep lesions.

The accuracy of EUS in diagnosing gastrointestinal/retroperitoneal lymph nodes has been reported to range from 65% to 100%, with specificity nearing 100% in most studies. However, sensitivity varies between 85% and 93%13. Several factors can influence the diagnostic yield of pancreatic lesions, such as technical issues, needle size, number of passes, and the experience of cytopathologists. Marta Nicola et al. emphasized that these factors remain crucial, and the procedure is still operator-dependent14,15. When all the 67 cases were reanalyzed by WHO categories; the sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV and diagnostic accuracy of the EUS FNA technique were found to be 87.5%, 82.8%, 82.3%, 87.8% & 85.07% respectively that showed an increased trend in comparison to the PSC categories of the current study. It had also seen to be well align with the Kundu et al. study. Their study revealed risk stratification in total 230 cases and the overall sensitivity and specificity, PPV, NPV & diagnostic accuracy of PSC was found to be 68.2% and 96.2%, 95.6%, 71.8% & 81.0% when categories V and VI were taken as positive and 78.9% and 93.3%, 94.9%, 73.7%, 84.5% for WHO when categories VI and VII were taken as positive18.

That confirms the fact that EUS-FNA is an efficient diagnostic tool for pancreatic lesions whether it is PSC or WHO reporting system but clearly WHO reporting was found to be better as per above studies. Yet larger cohort studies are awaited for affirmation for this new reporting system19,20.

However, limitations remain, including a relatively high false-negative rate, as demonstrated by Woolf et al., who reported a 23% false-negative rate due to sampling errors21. In the current study, the false-negative rate was relatively lower at 5.97%. Additionally, complications such as bacteremia were observed at similar rates in patients undergone EUS with or without FNA, and prophylactic antibiotics are recommended to prevent endocarditis in these patients22.

The major limitations of the study were multifactorial such as the physicians’ expertise in extracting the samples for both the procedures of FNAC and needle biopsy was of utmost importance along with the smearing & staining pattern of the yielded material by the pathologist. The third and most important one was the interpretation according the PSC reporting system with interobserver reliability. Nevertheless, a cohort study with large sample size would have been a great insight for the august readers.

Conclusion

EUS FNA is a reliable, cost-effective, and accurate tool in assessing neoplastic and nonneoplastic pancreatic lesions despite its complex anatomy. An additional immunocytochemistry/immunohistochemistry along with the needle biopsy can add more diagnostic importance, particularly in cases of NET/SPN hence expedite the patient specific treatment.
