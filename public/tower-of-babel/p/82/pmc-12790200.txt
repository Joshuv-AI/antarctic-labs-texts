Congestive Heart Failure as Initial Manifestation of Biventricular Arrhythmogenic Cardiomyopathy With MYBPC3 and DSG2 Variants
Authors: José Martín Alanís-Naranjo, Sergio Alfonso Patrón-Chi, Daniel Campuzano-González, Miguel Ángel Cruz-Marmolejo, Javier De la cruz-Pelayo, Gabriela Meléndez-Ramírez, Aloha Meave-González
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundArrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy (ACM) is typically linked to variants in desmosomal genes (eg, DSG2), whereas MYBPC3 variants are associated with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. Concurrent variants from both pathways are rare and poorly characterized.Case SummaryA 35-year-old man who presented with congestive heart failure fulfilled the criteria for biventricular ACM. Genetic analysis identified 3 heterozygous variants, in DSG2 (c.136C>T, p.Arg46Trp and c.806T>C, p.Ile269Thr) and MYBPC3 (c.529C>T, p.Arg177Cys; variant of uncertain significance). The patient was treated with guideline-directed therapy, remained clinically stable with reduced premature ventricular complex burden and improved biventricular function on cardiac magnetic resonance, and was listed for heart transplantation.DiscussionConcurrent DSG2 and MYBPC3 variants represent an uncommon genetic profile in ACM, contributing to variable phenotype and adverse outcomes. This case highlights the value of genetic testing combined with advanced imaging in refining the characterization of inherited cardiomyopathies.Take-Home MessagesConcurrent genetic variants may influence phenotype and prognosis. Comprehensive testing and longitudinal follow-up are essential for risk stratification and personalized care.

Arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy (ACM) is a genetic cardiac disease characterized by progressive fibrofatty replacement of the ventricular myocardium, commonly associated with variants in the DSG2 gene.1,2 In contrast, MYBPC3 variants are predominantly linked to hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM).1, 2, 3 These inherited cardiomyopathies are primarily caused by variations in the desmosomal and sarcomeric genes, highlighting each condition's distinct genetic background.2,3 The precise pathogenic contribution of some gene variants remains unclear, and the coexistence of multiple distinct genetic alterations associated with cardiomyopathy remains poorly understood, with limited clinical evidence.1,2Take-Home Messages•Patients harboring multiple pathogenic genetic variants can present with variable and unpredictable cardiomyopathy phenotypes, underscoring the importance of integrating genetic, imaging, and clinical data.•Comprehensive genetic testing and longitudinal genotype-phenotype correlation are critical for accurate risk stratification and personalized management of inherited cardiomyopathies.

The presence of desmosomal gene variants may contribute to an increased arrhythmic burden in cardiomyopathies.2,4 Multiple HCM-associated gene variants have been linked to earlier clinical presentation, including the onset of heart failure, as well as to poorer cardiac and overall outcomes.1,2,4 Reports of cardiomyopathies involving concurrent genetic variants remain exceptionally uncommon.1,4

This case report presents an adult-onset biventricular ACM phenotype manifested as congestive heart failure, in addition to the identification of heterozygous variants of the DSG2 and MYBPC3 genes, suggesting a potential genetic contribution to the disease phenotype.

Case Presentation

A 35-year-old man presented with a 5-month history of progressive lower extremity edema, abdominal distension, and decreased urinary output. No comorbid conditions or familial history of cardiovascular disease were identified.

Upon admission, the patient showed tachycardia (100 beats/min), blood pressure of 123/69 mm Hg, respiratory rate of 20 breaths/min, and oxygen saturation of 89% on ambient air. Physical examination revealed abdominal distension with shifting dullness, bilateral pitting edema of the lower extremities, bilateral bibasilar crackles, and a holosystolic murmur audible in the mitral and tricuspid areas.

Electrocardiography demonstrated sinus tachycardia, biatrial enlargement, and premature ventricular complexes (PVCs) (Figure 1A). Chest radiography showed cardiomegaly and bilateral pleural effusion. Based on these findings, the case fulfilled the criteria for definite biventricular ACM, supported by the presence of a pathogenic DSG2 variant, consistent with the ACM phenotype. The patient was managed with guideline-directed medical therapy comprising bisoprolol 10 mg once daily, dapagliflozin 10 mg once daily, spironolactone 25 mg once daily, and sacubitril-valsartan 100 mg twice daily, all of which were well tolerated without any reported severe adverse effects.

Clinical status remained stable after 2 years of follow-up, with a marked decrease in PVC burden. Cardiac magnetic resonance demonstrated a reduction in biatrial and biventricular volumes and greater improvement in biventricular systolic function (Figure 4, Table 2, Video 2, Video 3, Video 4, Video 5). Discussion

ACM is predominantly associated with pathogenic variants in desmosomal genes, whereas HCM is primarily linked to alterations in sarcomeric genes. The implementation of next-generation sequencing has incorporated genetic testing into routine clinical practice, serving as a key tool in the diagnosis and classification of inherited cardiomyopathies.2 In this case, 2 variants in a desmosomal gene and 1 variant in a sarcomeric gene were identified. Among these, only the p.Ile269Thr variant in DSG2 has been previously reported in the Latin American population6 and is commonly associated with ACM (Table 1).

DSG2 encodes a cadherin-family protein essential for calcium-dependent cell-to-cell adhesion and the structural integrity of cardiac desmosomes and intercalated discs. By interacting with desmocollin-2, it forms organized adhesive complexes that enable mechanical coupling between cardiomyocytes. Pathogenic variants in DSG2 are a major cause of ACM, often leading to reduced protein expression or impaired function, which disrupts adhesion and intracellular signaling through altered desmosomal protein interactions. Although less frequently reported, MYBPC3 variants have also been identified in individuals with ACM.1

The MYBPC3 gene encodes cardiac myosin-binding protein C, an essential sarcomeric component involved in the regulation of cardiac contractility through interactions with myosin, titin, and actin. The protein modulates thick-thin filament coupling and controls myofilament sliding velocity, contributing to effective myocardial contraction.3 Most carriers (76%) present during early to mid adulthood (18-60 years of age), whereas pediatric cases are less common.3

Pathogenic and likely pathogenic variants are present in approximately 1 in 578 individuals for arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy and 1 in 149 for HCM. DSG2 represents 9% of variants associated with arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy, whereas MYBPC3 is the most frequently affected gene in HCM, accounting for 52% to 54% of cases; some pathogenic and likely pathogenic variants are observed across multiple cardiomyopathy subtypes.2

Cardiomyopathy or heart failure without prior ischemic heart disease (phenotype positive) is more frequently diagnosed in HCM genotype-positive individuals (OR: 3.03; 95% CI: 1.98-4.56; P = 5.8 × 10−7). Ventricular arrhythmias occur more often in ACM genotype-positive individuals (OR: 6.20; 95% CI: 2.30-14.38; P = 3.3 × 10−4), including those without overt phenotype (genotype-positive/phenotype-negative: OR: 5.846; 95% CI: 1.976-14.398; P = 0.001). Heart failure is also more common in HCM genotype-positive individuals (OR: 1.733; 95% CI: 1.086-2.661; P = 0.015), particularly in carriers of MYBPC3 variants (OR: 2.55; 95% CI: 1.72-3.68; P = 4.5 × 10−6). All-cause mortality does not differ significantly between ACM or HCM genotype-positive individuals (P = 0.100).2 The MYBPC3:c.529C>T, (p.Arg177Cys) variant was predicted to be deleterious by SIFT score (0.01, threshold ≤0.05), probably damaging by PolyPhen-2 (score = 0.48), and with a CADD-Phred score of 24.7.7,8 The combined results of these algorithms strongly suggest that the variant may have functional consequences.

In patients with ACM, coinheritance of pathogenic variants has been associated with earlier onset of ventricular arrhythmias, heart failure, and death, suggesting a possible additive effect contributing to more aggressive disease and poorer outcomes.4 Individuals carrying variants linked to both ACM and HCM often exhibit variable clinical expression, with reported cases involving compound desmosomal with sarcomeric variants presenting with phenotypes consistent with either ACM or HCM.1,4

Cardiomyopathy exhibits marked phenotypic variability, even among carriers of the same pathogenic or likely pathogenic variant. Accurate and periodic reclassification of associated genes is essential for improving risk stratification and early identification of high-risk individuals.9

Detailed phenotypic characterization remains essential in patients with cardiomyopathies, highlighting the challenges that clinicians face in translating genetic findings into precision medicine. Advances in nucleic acid sequencing have facilitated the identification of genetic variants across the genome and their incorporation into clinical practice. However, the phenotypic variability of a single mutation presenting as distinct cardiomyopathy phenotypes remains under-recognized.10

In this case, 3 genetic variants were identified: 2 involving the desmosomal gene DSG2 (c.136C>T, p.Arg46Trp and c.806T>C, p.Ile269Thr) and 1 missense variant of uncertain significance in the sarcomeric gene MYBPC3 (c.529C>T, p.Arg177Cys). Despite the presence of a MYBPC3 variant, the patient did not meet the phenotypic criteria for HCM. Nevertheless, the clinical presentation was marked by congestive heart failure, a feature commonly observed in individuals carrying pathogenic variants associated with HCM.

The main limitation of this report is the absence of family genetic analysis, as sibling testing was not possible owing to economic constraints. Consequently, the phase of the DSG2 variant remains unresolved, precluding definitive confirmation of compound heterozygosity. Although this limits the strength of the genetic interpretation, the coexistence of two DSG2 variants together with a MYBPC3 variant in the clinical context of biventricular ACM provides relevant insight and contributes to the expanding spectrum of this disease.

Conclusions

The coexistence of desmosomal and sarcomeric gene variants is infrequently reported in patients with cardiomyopathies and presents diagnostic and interpretative challenges owing to marked phenotypic variability. This heterogeneity underscores the importance of detailed genotype-phenotype correlation and longitudinal follow-up. Further research is essential to elucidate the clinical implications of variant interplay in the context of inherited cardiomyopathies.
