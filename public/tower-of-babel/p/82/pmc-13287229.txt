Patients’ Experiences of Obstructive Sleep Apnea and Tirzepatide Treatment: An Exit Interview Study
Authors: Shraddha Shinde, Chisom Kanu, Oralee J. Varnado, Jo Bednarik, Ellen B. Dennehy, Carla Dias-Barbosa, Natalie Taylor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundQualitative interviews can supplement clinical trial results by providing in-depth insights into participant experiences. In the recent SURMOUNT-OSA (NCT05412004) phase III trials, tirzepatide demonstrated favorable efficacy and safety in patients with moderate-to-severe obstructive sleep apnea and obesity. To explore participants’ obstructive sleep apnea and treatment-related experiences, qualitative exit interviews were held with a subset of trial participants.MethodsOne-on-one qualitative telephone interviews were conducted as participants exited the trial. Participants described their obstructive sleep apnea symptoms, impacts, and sleep quality before and after participation in the trial. They further reported on the meaningfulness of treatment-related changes, experiences, and satisfaction. Interview transcripts were analyzed using ATLAS.ti.ResultsSeventy US-based and 12 Germany-based participants (tirzepatide n = 49, placebo n = 33) had a mean age of 52.7 years. Most were male (61.0%), non-Hispanic (87.8%), and White (91.5%). The “worst” obstructive sleep apnea symptoms experienced overall included snoring and temporary breath cessation, and the “worst” impacts were sleep-related impairment and sleep disturbance. Greater proportions of tirzepatide-treated than placebo-treated patients reported improvements in key symptoms and impacts of obstructive sleep apnea and considered them to be meaningful. Tirzepatide-treated participants also reported improved sleep quality and daytime functioning. Treatment satisfaction was higher among tirzepatide-treated participants than placebo-treated participants. The most common treatment-related likes were ease of treatment administration, weight reduction, and obstructive sleep apnea symptom improvement.ConclusionsThese findings highlight the range of symptoms and impacts experienced by individuals with obstructive sleep apnea and obesity, and support a positive effect of treatment with tirzepatide.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40271-026-00808-3.

Key Points for Decision Makers

Introduction

Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is a common sleep disorder that affects more than 900 million adults worldwide [1, 2]. Characterized by recurrent pharyngeal collapse during sleep, OSA results in transient apnea or hypopnea (ceased or reduced airflow, respectively). This leads to temporary oxygen desaturation and may be connected with arousal from sleep [3]. Disturbed breathing impacts sleep quality and may lead to excessive daytime sleepiness [4]. Downstream effects on health and quality of life include increased risk of cardiovascular [5, 6] and metabolic disease [7, 8], as well as impacts on daily, cognitive, and neurobehavioral functioning, and other aspects of well-being [4]. Obstructive sleep apnea is likely both underdiagnosed and under-recognized [9]. To diagnose OSA, apnea and hypopnea events are scored during sleep via in-clinic or at-home polysomnography and are reported as the apnea-hypopnea index (AHI; average number of apneas and hypopneas per hour slept). The AHI serves as a clinical marker of OSA severity, with a value of 5 to < 15 classified as mild, 15 to < 30 as moderate, and ≥ 30 as severe [10].

Obstructive sleep apnea management involves a multi-modal approach that ranges from positional therapy to surgical interventions [10, 11]. Positive airway pressure (PAP) remains the most effective and commonly used treatment [11]; however, its efficacy depends on sustained use, and treatment refusal and poor adherence present major issues [11]. As obesity is recognized to be a major reversible OSA risk factor [12], weight reduction constitutes an important treatment option and has been recommended in practice guidelines, either as a single therapy or as a supplement to PAP [13, 14]. Historically, bariatric surgery was the only effective means to achieve major weight reduction in patients with OSA in whom dietary attempts at weight control proved ineffective [14]. Recent advances in incretin-based pharmacotherapies represent a promising new avenue for achieving long-term weight reduction and treating OSA [12].

Tirzepatide is a long-acting glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide receptor and glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonist. Recently, two randomized, double-blind, phase III trials, conducted under the SURMOUNT-OSA master protocol (NCT05412004), demonstrated the safety and efficacy of tirzepatide for the treatment of adults with moderate-to-severe OSA and obesity [15, 16]. In participants either unwilling or unable to use PAP (Study 1) or those who had used PAP for at least 3 months at enrollment and planned to continue using PAP while in trial (Study 2), tirzepatide significantly reduced AHI and body weight, improved cardiometabolic risk factors associated with OSA, and improved sleep-related patient-reported outcomes [15, 16].

While tirzepatide was associated with clinically meaningful changes across multiple outcomes in participants in the SURMOUNT-OSA clinical trials, there is limited qualitative information about the lived experience of adults with OSA and obesity, and how tirzepatide treatment affects their day-to-day lives. Qualitative research, recommended by the US Food and Drug Administration for in-depth assessment of patient-focused outcomes in clinical trials [17], can supplement information from the SURMOUNT-OSA clinical trials; it can provide profound insights into participant experiences, offer a nuanced understanding of the heterogeneous OSA disease burden, and supplement quantitative results by determining whether reported changes are truly meaningful to patients [18]. In this study, qualitative exit interviews were conducted with a subset of participants in the SURMOUNT-OSA clinical trial participants to gain an understanding of the OSA disease burden from the patient perspective and to explore trial participant experiences with tirzepatide.

Methods

Study Design

This was a cross-sectional, qualitative, exit interview study involving one-on-one telephone interviews with a subset of US-based and Germany-based participants enrolled in Study 1 (participants unable or unwilling to use PAP) and Study 2 (participants who had used PAP for at least 3 months at enrollment and planned to continue using PAP) of the SURMOUNT-OSA clinical trials [15, 19]. The exit interview study was conducted outside of Study 1 and Study 2 following a separate protocol.

Study Setting and Participants

Participants were recruited by site staff from 11 US-based and four Germany-based SURMOUNT-OSA clinical trials sites during or after their primary outcome visit (week 52) [see Fig. S1 of the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM) for the timeline of SURMOUNT-OSA clinical trials]. During the SURMOUNT-OSA clinical trials, participants were randomized 1:1 to tirzepatide or placebo.

A priori target sampling quotas were established to achieve an approximately even distribution of participants across SURMOUNT-OSA Study 1 and Study 2 and across treatment arms (tirzepatide vs placebo). Because interviewers were blinded to treatment assignment, site staff recruited participants to meet these targets to ensure representation of experiences across study groups. Recruitment continued until the predefined sample size (approximately 80 participants) and distribution targets were achieved.

Eligible exit interview participants were adults (aged ≥18 years) with moderate-to-severe OSA and obesity who had participated in SURMOUNT-OSA Study 1 or Study 2 in the USA or Germany and had completed the safety follow-up visit (week 56) following the primary outcome visit. They had to read, speak, and understand the native language of the country in which the site was based (English or German) sufficiently to participate in a discussion of their OSA symptoms and impacts. They had to further provide written informed consent and be available for an audio-recorded interview lasting up to 90 minutes within approximately 12 weeks of completing the safety follow-up visit. Participants were excluded from the exit interview study if they had withdrawn from Study 1 or Study 2 or had conditions that could interfere with their ability to complete the study interview.

Quantitative Data

Baseline sociodemographic and clinical study characteristics as well as scores of patient-reported outcome measures collected during the SURMOUNT-OSA clinical trials were summarized for this subgroup using descriptive statistics (mean, median, standard deviation [SD], interquartile range, minimum, and maximum for continuous variables; frequency and percentages for categorical variables). Additional sociodemographic variables (e.g., marital status, current living/domestic situation, employment status, and highest level of education) not collected as part of Study 1 and Study 2 were collected at the beginning of the exit interviews and were also summarized using descriptive statistics.

Qualitative Data

One-on-one telephone interviews in English or German were conducted by qualitative researchers, each with a Master’s level education or higher and training in qualitative research methods. Four researchers interviewed US-based participants, and one researcher interviewed Germany-based participants. Interviewers and interviewees were unaware of treatment assignment (blinded to treatment vs placebo at the time of interview) and were not known to each other prior to the study.

To ensure consistency between interviews, the researchers used a semi-structured interview guide. The interview guide was informed by previous qualitative work on OSA (unpublished data) and was developed and revised with input from clinical and qualitative research experts. The guide was designed to capture experience and severity of pre-trial OSA symptoms and impacts. Participants were given time to answer spontaneously before being probed on eight predetermined common OSA symptoms and eight impact domains. The guide further focused on treatment-related symptom and impact changes, including symptoms such as snoring, breathing difficulties, and night-time awakening, and impacts on daily performance, emotional and psychological well-being, relationships, and sleep quality. Themes within the guide explored treatment-related changes meaningful to trial participants, as well as treatment expectations and satisfaction. The guide included open-ended questions, allowing participants to share their experience spontaneously, as well as specific probes, follow-up questions, and closed-ended questions. All interviewers were trained on using the interview guide, received feedback on their first or second interview from an interview monitor, and consulted with a senior researcher as needed throughout the data collection process.

Participants rated several aspects of their experience using structured Likert scales. Five-point scales were used to assess symptom severity at pre-trial and week 52 (‘none’ to ‘very severe’); sleep quality at pre-trial and week 52 (‘poor’ to ‘excellent’); treatment satisfaction at week 52 (‘very satisfied’ to ‘very dissatisfied’). Three-point scales were used to assess symptom and impact change at week 52 (improved, unchanged, worsened) and the meaningfulness of symptom and impact changes (‘not at all meaningful,’ ‘somewhat meaningful,’ ‘very meaningful’). Interviews were audio-recorded, translated (from German), certified (for translations), transcribed, and deidentified via removal of any personal identifiable information.

Qualitative Data Analysis

Qualitative data analysis was performed according to a qualitative data analysis plan and utilized the qualitative analysis software ATLAS.ti©. Cleaned and certified interview transcripts were imported into ATLAS.ti©. Data were coded deductively using a coding dictionary with predefined codes and inductively from new themes and concepts emerging during the transcript analysis. The final coding framework and coding dictionary included all inductive and deductive codes. Coding was completed iteratively and quality checked by a project lead and qualitative data manager to ensure intercoder agreement (ICA; the total number of code discrepancies between both coders divided by the sum of the total number of coded concepts identified by each coder).

Mixed-Methods Approach

A mixed-methods approach, in which unblinded quantitative data were linked to qualitative data, was used to explore participants’ experiences based on treatment assignment (tirzepatide vs placebo) and study group allocation (Study 1 vs Study 2).

Data Saturation

Concept saturation is defined as the point when no new relevant or important information emerges and collecting additional data will not likely add to the understanding of how study participants perceive concepts of interest [20]. The number of participants needed to reach saturation depends on the complexity of a given topic and the diversity of experiences within a specific population of interest. While the number of participants needed to reach saturation cannot be prespecified, we anticipated (based on experience) that a subset of a sample of approximately 80 participants, would be sufficient to reach concept saturation.

To assess concept saturation, all interview transcripts were ordered chronologically and grouped into sets of eight to ten transcripts. A saturation grid was used to track symptom and impact domain concepts, including both spontaneous mentions and concepts elicited through structured probes. Concepts derived from each transcript group were compared with those reported by the previous transcript groups, and saturation was considered achieved once no new codes emerged in a transcript group.

Results

Sociodemographic and Clinical Characteristics at Baseline

Fifteen participating sites identified 112 eligible SURMOUNT-OSA participants and invited them to join the exit interview sub-study. Of these, 82 participants (70 US based and 12 Germany based) consented and completed the interview, resulting in a participation rate of 73.2%. Reasons for non-participation included Study 1 or Study 2 discontinuation prior to week 52, declined consent (because of e.g., work schedule, extended travel, relocation, issues with interview length, or a general unspecified lack of interest), or loss to follow-up after several attempts to schedule the interview.

Most participants (n = 72) were interviewed within 2 weeks of their safety follow-up visit; the safety follow-up visit occurred 4 weeks after the primary outcome visit (week 52) (Fig. S1 of the ESM). To maximize the study sample size, the initial 2-week exit interview timeframe was widened at the onset of data collection; later interviews were conducted within the target timeframe. In total, ten interviews were conducted 4–51 days (up to 12 weeks) after the target timeframe. The interviews took place between 16 October, 2023 and 5 April, 2024.

Participants had a mean age of 52.7 years. Most were male (61.0%), non-Hispanic (87.8%), and White (91.5%). 72.0% of participants had received at least some college education. Forty participants were from Study 1 (tirzepatide: n = 22; placebo: n = 18), and 42 from Study 2 (tirzepatide: n = 27; placebo: n = 15) (Table 1). Study results for participants in the exit interview study were similar to those observed for the full cohort in the SURMOUNT-OSA program (Table S1 of the ESM). By week 52, the tirzepatide-treated exit interview participants experienced a greater reduction in AHI compared with those on placebo in Study 1 (tirzepatide: −71.0%; placebo: −4.7%) and Study 2 (tirzepatide: −66.0%; placebo: +1.3%).

Qualitative Study Results

Coding and ICA

All (N = 82) interview transcripts were coded, and 10% (n = 8) were dual coded (selected at random to evaluate ICA across all coding). The average ICA, assessed for 9.8% of transcripts (n = 8/82), was 80.3%.

Saturation

Sixteen pre-trial OSA symptom concepts and 44 impact concepts emerged during the interviews. Saturation was assessed for all concepts. Most concepts (symptoms: n = 10/16, 62.5%; impacts: n = 34/44, 77.3%) were reported in transcript group 1 of 10. Concept saturation for pre-trial OSA symptoms was achieved after transcript group 7 of 10 (Table S2 of the ESM). Concept saturation for pre-trial OSA impacts was reached after transcript group 3 of 10 (Table S3 of the ESM).

Pre-trial OSA Symptoms

All (N = 82) participants reported on OSA symptoms and impacts experienced in the month prior to participation in the SURMOUNT-OSA clinical trial program. Four of the eight common pre-trial OSA symptoms included in the interview guide, snoring (n = 76, 92.7%), nocturia (n = 62, 75.6%), awakening with dry mouth (n = 60, 73.2%), and temporary breath cessation (n = 57, 69.5%), were reported (spontaneously or probed) by 70% or more of the total exit interview sample (Table 2 and Table S4 of the ESM). The other four, gasping/choking (n = 32, 39.0%), difficulty remembering things (n = 30, 36.6%), morning headache (n = 25, 30.5%), and difficulty breathing (n = 22, 26.8%) were reported (spontaneously or probed) by approximately a third of participants. The most common treatment-related likes were ease of treatment administration (tirzepatide: n = 8/22, 36.4% Study 1 and n = 11/27, 40.7% Study 2; placebo: n = 5/17, 29.4% Study 1 and n = 6/15, 40.0%), weight reduction (tirzepatide: n = 10/22, 45.5% Study 1 and n = 11/27, 40.7% Study 2; placebo: n = 2/17, 11.8% Study 1 and n = 3/15, 20.0% Study 2), and OSA symptom improvement (tirzepatide: n = 6/22, 27.3% Study 1 and n = 4/27, 14.8% Study 2; placebo: n = 3/17, 17.6% Study 1 and n = 0/15, 0.0% Study 2).

Discussion

This qualitative exit interview study provides in-depth insights into OSA symptoms and impacts from the patient perspective, and complements results from the recent SURMOUNT-OSA clinical trial program demonstrating the safety and efficacy of tirzepatide for the treatment of adults with moderate-to-severe OSA and obesity [15, 21]. By reporting on 16 participant-reported pre-trial OSA symptoms and 44 pre-trial impacts (across eight impact domains), the study illustrates the heterogeneity of symptoms and impacts experienced by individuals with OSA and obesity. Except for temporary breath cessation, all symptoms were reported by a larger proportion of participants who were unwilling or unable to use PAP pre-trial than by those who had used PAP pre-trial and continued to use it in-trial.

While previous publications discuss the variability of OSA [22, 23], there is limited qualitative information on patients’ perceptions of symptoms, impacts, and meaningful treatment effects. Eight common OSA symptoms were anticipated in the qualitative interview guide, but eight more were spontaneously reported by exit interview participants, providing finer insights into the OSA experience. Additionally, patients reported a variety of cognitive and mood disturbances; these impacts associated with OSA and obesity may be misunderstood or minimized [24]. Greater awareness of patient complaints may be useful to patients and practitioners alike, facilitating successful diagnosis [25], and enabling a more nuanced understanding of OSA disease concepts.

The burden and impact of snoring were highly apparent in patient reports. While snoring may be regarded as a benign annoyance rather than a potential health concern, our study shows how patients perceive it to directly impair their sleep, cause pain, and adversely affect relationships with partners. This clinical complaint is not only a potential diagnostic marker for OSA or other health concerns, but a serious concern for patients that warrants further investigation and intervention.

As well as providing insights into OSA symptoms and impacts experienced prior to the SURMOUNT-OSA clinical trials, the exit interviews yielded valuable information on how patient perceptions changed during the trials. Most tirzepatide-treated participants reported meaningful improvements in all common OSA symptoms and impacts, felt their treatment expectations had been met, and were satisfied with the study treatment, even while reporting the experience of side effects as a treatment dislike. More improvements were observed among tirzepatide-treated participants who were unwilling or unable to use PAP pre-trial than among those who had received PAP pre-trial and continued to use it in-trial; however, the level of treatment satisfaction was comparable between the two groups.

Participants indicated that improved sleep quality, feeling less tired, and weight reduction contributed to their treatment expectations being met. These perceptions were consistent with significant improvements in AHI and body weight amongst the larger SURMOUNT-OSA clinical trial populations [15]. Further, the descriptions of improved sleep quality and sleep-related daytime functioning add depth to Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) sleep disturbance and sleep-related impairment improvements measured during the core clinical trial [15]. Cai et al. recently characterized patient-defined goals for OSA treatment [26]; key treatment goals included improving sleep quality and reducing daytime sleepiness, snoring, and health risks. Three of these were reported to have been met by tirzepatide-treated exit interview participants.

Positive descriptions of tirzepatide-associated weight reduction mirror those reported in another qualitative study examining patient experiences in clinical trials of tirzepatide (for treatment of type 2 diabetes mellitus) where trial participants’ most enthusiastic descriptions of treatment outcomes focused on weight reductions [27]. Qualitative interviews generate in-depth insight into patient experiences and can provide a more nuanced understanding of heterogeneous disease burdens [18, 28]. Data saturation was achieved, demonstrating that the sample size was sufficient to capture the full range of symptoms and impacts relevant to this study population and to address the study research questions.

A limitation of this study is that participants’ perceptions of baseline symptoms and impacts (i.e., at 1 month prior to SURMOUNT-OSA clinical trial entry) relied on retrospective recall and may thus have been subject to recall and memory biases. It is unknown whether participants’ perceptions would have been more positive or negative with a shorter recall period. Although some responses may have been potentially influenced by these biases, all participants were able to provide meaningful answers to the interview questions. Furthermore, because only participants who completed the SURMOUNT-OSA trials were eligible to participate in the exit interviews, the sample may reflect individuals with more favorable treatment experiences, potentially introducing a selection bias.

While the exit interview sub-study sample was generally representative of the entire SURMOUNT-OSA clinical trial population [15], participants were interviewed in the context of a clinical trial, and—owing to this possible selection bias—the results may not be fully generalizable to the general OSA population. As a further limitation, the overall study population included participants of multiple races but was weighted towards White and highly educated participants in the USA. The interviews were conducted only with English-speaking participants based in the USA (n = 70) and German-speaking participants in Germany (n = 12); our findings may thus not necessarily represent the lived experience of patients with OSA in other cultures and health systems. Future research including a more diverse sample of patients with OSA could explore linguistic and cross-cultural nuances of OSA and its treatment.

Conclusions

These qualitative interview results supplement SURMOUNT-OSA clinical trial data by providing in-depth insights into how tirzepatide treatment improved the symptoms and impacts experienced by individuals living with moderate-to-severe OSA and obesity. Greater proportions of tirzepatide-treated than placebo-treated participants reported improvements in key OSA symptoms and impacts, with higher treatment satisfaction observed among tirzepatide-treated participants. The study suggested that tirzepatide may have alleviated the physical and psychosocial challenges of individuals with moderate-to-severe OSA and obesity, underscoring its potential as a valuable addition to current treatment options.
