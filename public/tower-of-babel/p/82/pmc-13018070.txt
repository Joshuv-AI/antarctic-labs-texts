Whole Lung Irradiation in Ewing Sarcoma with Pulmonary Metastases: Critical Appraisal of Evolution, Evidence, and Future Pathways in Precision Oncology
Authors: YuanYou Yang, Lu Xie, Jie Xu, Xin Sun, Gang Ren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Opinion StatementBased on the cumulative evidence, we advocate for the continued use of whole lung irradiation (WLI) as an integral component of multimodal therapy for Ewing sarcoma patients with pulmonary metastases, particularly in those with isolated lung involvement. Modern cardiac-sparing techniques, such as intensity-modulated radiotherapy and proton therapy, should be prioritized to mitigate acute cardiopulmonary toxicity, despite the unknown long-term implications of the low-dose bath. Dose prescription should be individualized according to age, risk stratification, and response to chemotherapy, with 15–18 Gy in 1.5 Gy fractions representing the current standard. For patients achieving a rapid complete radiographic response, the decision to omit WLI must be balanced against the proven survival benefit and should ideally be guided by emerging biomarkers, pending prospective validation. Concurrent administration of WLI with novel systemic agents, including tyrosine kinase inhibitors and immune checkpoint inhibitors, is not recommended outside clinical trials due to the risk of synergistic pulmonary toxicity. Lifelong surveillance for secondary malignancies and cardiopulmonary late effects is mandatory. We believe that the field must now move beyond empirical application toward biologically adapted, risk-adapted precision radiotherapy.

Introduction

Ewing sarcoma is the second most common malignant bone tumor in children and adolescents, with a global incidence of approximately 1.5 per million. It is sensitive to both chemotherapy and radiotherapy, and is mainly treated with surgery, radiotherapy combined with multi-drug chemotherapy [1, 2]. Approximately 25% of patients have metastases at initial diagnosis, the lungs being the most frequent site; the 5 year survival rate for patients with isolated pulmonary metastases historically ranges from 30% to 50% [3–5]. whole lung irradiation (WLI) has been a pivotal intervention, demonstrating clinical benefit across multiple studies [6–8]. Nevertheless, the integration of WLI into contemporary risk adapted and biologically guided treatment paradigms remains hotly debated. Key controversies include: (i) which patients derive sufficient benefit beyond the mere presence of lung metastases; (ii) the management of overlapping toxicities with novel systemic agents; (iii) the validation of long-term safety for modern dose sparing techniques that may trade acute high dose toxicity for chronic low dose exposure; and (iv) the absence of pediatric specific normal tissue complication probability models to guide organ at risk constraints. This article systematically examines the mechanistic basis, historical evolution, technical advancements, clinical efficacy, toxicities, and optimal timing of WLI, while critically appraising the strengths and limitations of existing data—much of which derives from non-randomized or retrospective settings with inherent selection bias. The overarching goal is to synthesize a framework that moves WLI from a largely empirical tool toward a truly personalized, evidence-based component of precision oncology. To this end, we conducted a literature search of PubMed and Embase databases up to March 2025, using keywords related to Ewing sarcoma, pulmonary metastases, and whole lung irradiation. We focused on synthesizing evidence from clinical trials, major retrospective studies, and key reviews. Consequently, this article explicitly weighs the strengths against the limitations of current data, provides a nuanced, evidence-based framework to guide personalized clinical decision making, and prioritizes the most urgent questions for future translational and clinical research.

Mechanism of Action of Whole Lung Irradiation

The anatomical features of the lung, such as the dense capillary bed and the intersection of lymphatic-venous circulation, provide a favorable microenvironment for tumor metastasis [9–11]. The mechanism of action of WLI includes dual effects on tumor cells and their microenvironment. WLI induces direct deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) damage via ionizing radiation, leading to double-strand breaks and subsequent cell death through p53-dependent apoptosis or mitotic catastrophe [12, 13]. In addition, radiation can damage tumor vascular endothelium and induce anoxic necrosis, and it can also upregulate proinflammatory cytokines (e.g., tumor necrosis factor-alpha [TNF-α], interferon-gamma [IFN-γ]) and enhance antigen presentation capacity, thereby potentially inducing immunogenic cell death (ICD) and systemic antitumor immune responses [14–16]. It is crucial to note that while these immunomodulatory effects are promising, their clinical significance and contribution to the therapeutic efficacy of the relatively low dose WLI regimen used in Ewing sarcoma remain largely speculative and require dedicated investigation. WLI employs low dose fractionated regimens based on the “4R” radiobiological principles (repair, redistribution, reoxygenation, repopulation) to balance tumor killing with normal lung tissue protection [17].

Origin and Development of Whole Lung Irradiation

Whole lung irradiation (WLI) emerged in the mid-20th century, driven by early hematogenous metastasis of Ewing sarcoma, where lung metastases are a major cause of death [18]. In 1969, Margolis et al. reported that cobalt-60 irradiation of at least one lung plus local boost effectively controlled lung metastases in Ewing sarcoma and Wilms tumor [19]. Fernandez et al. (1974) noted 57% of Ewing sarcoma patients had lung as the first metastatic site (78% in autopsies), supporting prophylactic lung irradiation [20]. Mintz (1976) documented contralateral lung failure after unilateral prophylactic irradiation, proposing bilateral prophylactic lung irradiation [21]. The 1990 IESS-I study showed prophylactic WLI improved 5-year disease-free survival from 24% to 60% in nonpelvic Ewing sarcoma, establishing WLI as standard adjuvant therapy for localized disease [22]. The 1993 CESS study found dose-dependent survival benefit of WLI post-induction chemotherapy in newly diagnosed Ewing sarcoma with lung metastases [7]; the Children’s Oncology Group (COG) later refined risk-stratified dosing [23]. With the intensification of chemotherapy, WLI shifted from universal prophylaxis to selective consolidation for overt lung metastases. The 2019 R2Pulm trial demonstrated similar survival between conventional chemotherapy plus WLI and intensive chemotherapy, confirming WLI’s role in consolidating lung metastases [5]. This evolution highlights a pivotal shift from a “one size fits all” approach to a more selective, response adapted strategy. However, it also introduces a critical dilemma: the R2Pulm trial’s finding of “similar survival” raises the question of whether WLI can be safely omitted in patients who achieve a complete radiographic response to intensive chemotherapy. This dilemma underscores the pressing need for predictive biomarkers—such as circulating tumor DNA clearance or metabolic response on PET—to identify patients who may safely forgo WLI. Recent advances in precision radiotherapy have greatly enhanced WLI safety [24–26].

Technical Progress of Whole Lung Irradiation

Traditional anterior-posterior (AP) field irradiation delivers high heart dose, resulting in high congestive heart failure incidence in pediatric patients with persistent cardiac effects over 15 years [27, 28]. Cardiac-sparing techniques reduce heart dose while maintaining target coverage. Kalapurakal et al. [29] showed cardiac-sparing intensity-modulated radiotherapy (CS-IMRT) significantly lowered heart and substructure doses vs. AP fields: left ventricle V95% decreased from 45.2% to 12.3% (P < 0.001) with target coverage of 98.4% (P = 0.008). Sha et al. [26] compared Helical Tomotherapy (HT), IMRT, and intensity-modulated proton radiotherapy (IMPT), finding HT achieved the best target conformity (CI = 0.89) among photon techniques, while IMPT provided superior heart protection (heart V5 28% vs. 65%, P = 0.001) and 60% lower mean breast dose vs. AP fields (P < 0.01). Cunningham et al. [30] confirmed IMPT reduced mean heart dose to 4.5 Gy (vs. IMRT 12.3 Gy, P < 0.001), though maximum heart dose improvement was limited. Wong et al. [25] used IMPT to reduce bilateral breast V50% from 15% to 0.5% vs. AP fields (P = 0.012). For thoracic primary tumor patients, high primary site doses increase lung exposure. Yang et al. [31] applied dose-painting IMRT (DP-IMRT) with simultaneous boost in 11 children, reducing average doses vs. sequential IMRT + AP: spinal cord (15%, 3.2 Gy), esophagus (15%, 3.3 Gy), lung (10–15%, 2.0–4.5 Gy), liver (19%, 2.1 Gy), and heart (31%, 5.8 Gy), while improving lung target dose homogeneity (0.89 vs. 1.11). For combined thoracoabdominal irradiation, Chaballout et al. [32] used IMRT to lower maximum stomach and liver doses from 2062 cGy and 2237 cGy to 1118 cGy and 1334 cGy, respectively, sparing abdominal organs. However, these advanced techniques also present new challenges. IMPT, while dosimetrically superior, is costly and has limited accessibility, potentially exacerbating healthcare disparities. Its use also introduces uncertainties related to range sensitivity and interplay effects with respiratory motion. Similarly, deep inspiration breath-hold (DIBH) is highly patient-dependent and not feasible for all pediatric patients. Perhaps most importantly, the widespread adoption of CS-IMRT has led to the “low-dose bath” phenomenon, where a larger volume of lung, breast, and other tissues receives low-dose radiation (e.g., increased V5). The long-term oncogenic and functional consequences of this exposure pattern, particularly in adolescent females (as highlighted by the CCSS study on breast cancer risk), remain a major unknown and represent a paradigm shift from the historical high-dose, small-volume toxicity profile. Despite these important caveats, there is no doubt that CS-IMRT and IMPT represent a major advancement over traditional AP fields. When available and appropriately applied, they significantly enhance WLI safety by reducing immediate and high-grade cardiopulmonary toxicity. The choice of technique should be guided by equipment availability, patient-specific factors, and a careful consideration of the evolving risk-benefit profile, weighing acute organ protection against potential long-term stochastic risks.

Respiratory motion is a major challenge: lung tumor movement ranges 9.1–18.5 mm [33–36], averaging 14.7 mm craniocaudally for lower lung tumors [37], and liver motion can be 5–50 mm craniocaudally [38], causing targeting inaccuracies. While CS-IMRT, DP-IMRT, HT, and IMPT use four-dimensional computed tomography (4DCT) for coverage, specific motion management is required to avoid target volume enlargement. American Association of Physicists in Medicine (AAPM) Task Group Report 76 describes techniques including respiratory gating, breath-hold, abdominal compression, and real-time tracking [39]. Among these, abdominal compression and breath-hold are relatively simple. Demoor et al. [40] compared free breathing vs. DIBH in 17 WLI patients, finding DIBH significantly reduced liver dose: V5 73.80% vs. 86.74% (p < 0.05), V20 5.70% vs. 26.44% (p < 0.05), and mean dose 12.3 Gy vs. 15.9 Gy (p < 0.006). DIBH is useful for capable patients.

Though rarely reported in WLI, abdominal compression is widely used in thoracic/abdominal SBRT, reducing target/organ motion and normal tissue dose [41–44], suggesting potential WLI benefits. Partoomchart et al. [45] found abdominal compression significantly increased ipsilateral, contralateral, and total lung volumes in left-sided breast cancer radiotherapy (p < 0.001), which may lower lung V20, V30, and mean dose, reducing radiation pneumonitis risk. Thus, for patients unable to perform DIBH, abdominal compression may mitigate motion and pneumonitis risk. Implementing DIBH or abdominal compression may enable safe dose escalation to residual lesions while respecting OAR constraints, especially for inferior lung tumors with > 15 mm motion. Image guided radiotherapy can further enhance accuracy and reduce side effects.

Timing of Whole Lung Irradiation

In the era of prophylactic irradiation, WLI is typically administered after local treatment of the primary tumor and chemotherapy [22]. For patients with newly diagnosed pulmonary metastases in studies like CESS81, CESS86, and EICESS92, WLI followed neoadjuvant chemotherapy, local primary tumor treatment, and consolidation chemotherapy [46]. Notably, in the AEWS1221 trial, patients with thoracic primary tumors received concurrent primary and whole lung radiotherapy without concurrent chemotherapy, followed by consolidation chemotherapy with or without the IGF 1R monoclonal antibody ganitumab. DuBois et al. (2023) reported a higher pneumonia incidence in the experimental group (2.7% vs. 0.7%), including the only grade 5 event; all four patients with grade 3 + pneumonia in this group had received thoracic radiotherapy. This suggests that IGF 1R monoclonal antibodies may increase the risk of grade ≥ 3 pneumonia even when administered after WLI [47].

In the high-dose chemotherapy (HDCT) era, to mitigate toxic overlap, protocols like ISG/SSG-IV and AIEOP/ISG EW-2 [48] mandated a minimum 8-week interval between WLI and autologous hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (ASCT) following Busulfan-Melphalan (BU-MEL) HDCT. The incidences of grade 1–2 and grade 3 adverse events were 16.2% and 5.9%, respectively. Scobioala et al. [49] analyzed toxicity risk factors in HDCT and WLI regimens. While radiation doses of 8–12 Gy and 15 Gy showed significance in Pearson correlation, they did not in Cox regression analysis. Intervals of 30–60 and 60–90 days between HDCT and WLI significantly affected lung function indices, indicating that completing chemotherapy before WLI helps reduce pulmonary toxicity.

For pulmonary recurrence, studies EICESS-99 and Euro-EWING 99 [50] showed WLI after chemotherapy and resection of residual lung lesions significantly reduced lung recurrence and improved PFS and OS, without observed impact on lung function from surgery. Duverge et al. [51] administered WLI at a median of 11.4 weeks after second-line chemotherapy and Bu-Mel HDCT for recurrent pulmonary Ewing sarcoma. Thus, to reduce toxicity, WLI after induction chemotherapy, local treatment, and consolidation chemotherapy is generally recommended. When primary tumor and whole lung fields overlap, the concurrent field scheme from AEWS1221 can be considered. Despite varying approaches, no clear evidence exists for a difference in oncologic outcomes between concurrent and delayed radiotherapy for metastatic and primary sites [52].

Some receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK) inhibitors (e.g., pazopanib, sorafenib, regorafenib, anlotinib, lenvatinib, cabozantinib) have shown clinical efficacy against osteosarcoma and Ewing sarcoma [53]. A phase Ib/II study by Xu et al. reported promising efficacy and acceptable toxicity for anlotinib combined with vincristine and irinotecan in refractory Ewing sarcoma [54]. However, a phase I trial by Zhu et al. [55] evaluating concurrent chemoradiotherapy with anlotinib in stage III non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) was terminated early due to severe pulmonary toxicity, including two treatment related fatal events and two cases of grade 3 radiation pneumonitis. This stark warning underscores the danger of extrapolating adult solid tumor data to the pediatric Ewing sarcoma population without dedicated safety trials.

Regarding immunotherapy, a meta-analysis by Ma et al. [56] of 26 trials (2670 patients) found that combining immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) with radiotherapy increased respiratory adverse effects, with significantly higher grade 1–5 pneumonia incidence in patients with thoracic tumors. Bi et al. [57, 58] found that for patients receiving chest radiotherapy with prior or concurrent ICIs, the factors influencing radiation pneumonitis may change, and normal tissue dose constraints may require adjustment, making the pneumonitis risk difficult to predict.

Therefore, although RTK inhibitors and ICIs show encouraging signals in Ewing sarcoma, their concurrent use with WLI requires cautious, stepwise evaluation in dedicated early phase pediatric trials. This represents a major and urgent knowledge gap. The extrapolation of toxicity data from adult NSCLC to pediatric Ewing sarcoma is fraught with uncertainty, given profound differences in biology, concomitant chemotherapy backbones, and pulmonary reserve.

Prescription Dose for Whole Lung Irradiation

Early studies used empirical doses; e.g., Margolis et al. [19] applied 15–25 Gy fractionated irradiation with high cardiopulmonary toxicity. The CESS study [7] showed that patients with lung metastasis receiving ≥ 15 Gy WLI had a ~ 20% higher 5-year survival rate than those with lower doses (p < 0.05), indicating a dose-dependent effect. The Children’s Oncology Group (COG) proposed a risk-stratified strategy: 12–15 Gy for low-risk and 15–18 Gy for high-risk patients [23], which became the basis for modern dose optimization. The R2Pulm trial [5] confirmed that 15 Gy WLI combined with chemotherapy achieved an 8-year event-free survival (EFS) rate of 43.1% without increasing severe toxicity. For post-chemotherapy residual disease, 18–24 Gy local boost can improve control rate, though no standard recommendations exist—appropriate doses should be selected based on residual lesion characteristics and OAR parameters to balance pulmonary toxicity risks and benefits. Spunt et al. [59] reported that boosting residual lesions to 30–33 Gy after WLI yielded lung failure rates similar to surgical resection with acceptable toxicity. Motosue et al. [60] found that among 48 patients (median WLI dose 12 Gy, 10.5–18 Gy; 35.4% received median 18 Gy local boost, 8.5–36 Gy), long-term follow-up showed local dose correlated significantly with lung function decline. Halalsheh et al. [61] noted 5 patients with post-induction residual lesions received median 21.9 Gy boost (18–39.9 Gy) (median WLI 16.5 Gy, 3–16.5 Gy) without mentioning boost-related pulmonary toxicity. WLI is challenging for chest primary tumors due to high primary site dose requirements. Yang et al. [31] treated 11 chest primary lung/pleural metastasis patients (5 Ewing Sarcoma Family of Tumors [ESFT], 6 Rhabdomyosarcoma [RMS]) with DP-IMRP simultaneous boost: 15–16.8 Gy (0.54–0.88 Gy/fraction) for whole lung/half chest and 41.4–54 Gy (1.8 Gy/fraction) for primary tumor/postoperative bed. Median follow-up 27 months showed 3-year lung relapse-free survival rates of 61% (all), 40% (ESFT), and 81% (RMS), comparable to higher fractionated dose studies. Given ESFT and RMS radiation sensitivity, lower fractionated doses may suffice for tumor control; though toxicity was unreported, very low fractions may reduce normal tissue injury theoretically.

EURO EWING 2012, an international multicenter phase III open-label randomized trial comparing two induction/consolidation chemotherapy regimens, administered WLI to patients with isolated lung/pleural metastases post-consolidation. Per its Radiotherapy Guidelines (v1 Jun 2013), patients with complete response (CR) in lung/pleural lesions received prophylactic WLI: 15 Gy/10 fractions for < 14 years and 18 Gy/12 fractions for ≥ 14 years, with 54 Gy boost recommended for primary chest wall/rib tumors. Brennan et al. [62] reported 47-month median follow-up outcomes: 3-year EFS (61% vs. 67%) and overall survival (OS) (74% vs. 82%) showed no statistical differences between groups. Toxicity data focused on chemotherapy-related effects without mentioning radiotherapy-related toxicities (e.g., radiation pneumonitis). Despite limited radiotherapy toxicity details, 3-year OS > 74% supports the regimen’s viability when balanced against chemotherapy-dominant adverse events.

A critical analysis reveals a persistent tension: while higher doses (≥ 15 Gy) are associated with better disease control, they unequivocally increase toxicity. The risk stratified approach is logical but is based on relatively crude clinical parameters (age, presence of residual disease). The field lacks a biologically guided dose prescription model that integrates tumor genomics (e.g., STAG2, TP53 status), metabolic response on PET, or clearance of circulating tumor DNA. Furthermore, the practice of boosting residual lesions, while intuitive, is supported by low level evidence (retrospective, non-randomized) and carries a significant risk of severe late lung injury, as noted by Ronchi et al. [63]. In conclusion, WLI doses for Ewing sarcoma with pulmonary metastasis require individualization based on risk stratification, chemotherapy response, and technical conditions, with cardiopulmonary protection within 12–18 Gy to optimize efficacy and safety. Current National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) and COG guidelines recommend 15 Gy (< 14 years) vs. 18 Gy (≥ 14 years) and 12 Gy (< 6 years) vs. 15 Gy (≥ 6 years), both at 1.5 Gy/fraction [64]. Since grade ≥ 2 late lung injury is more common in patients receiving a residual lesion boost [63], maximal safe resection of residual lung lesions before WLI is recommended to reduce the risk of severe late toxicity. Dose Constraints for Organs at Risk (OARs)

Due to the low prescribed dose, OAR constraints are often overlooked, but many organs may still be exposed to low-dose radiation with corresponding side effects, so limiting OAR doses remains important. Kalapurakal et al. [65] recommended that the Dmax of the spinal cord, heart, and liver should be < 107%, 110%, and 110% of the prescribed dose, respectively, with the average dose of each heart segment controlled at 4.5–11.8 Gy. Atkins et al. [66] and RTOG 0617 [67] indicated that left anterior descending coronary artery (LAD) V15 (Volume receiving ≥ 15 Gy) ≥ 10% was significantly associated with increased adverse cardiac events and mortality, advising limiting LAD V15 to < 10%. In whole lung irradiation, lung V5-V15 is fully covered, leaving no room for low-dose parameter optimization; however, Quantitative Analyses of Normal Tissue Effects in the Clinic (QUANTEC) [68] requirements should still be referenced for residual lung lesion boosts. Nagayama et al. [69] found that 12–13.5 Gy whole-body irradiation caused 6.5% thyroid dysfunction, while Morgan et al. [70] reported low thyroid dysfunction incidence with WLI average dose 6.7 Gy and maximum dose 12.4 Gy. Multiple studies [71–74] confirmed that esophageal mean dose (Dmean), maximum dose (Dmax), and V5-V65 (5 Gy increments) are significantly associated with esophagitis, and Dmean ≤ 11 Gy, V10 ≤ 30%, and V20 ≤ 15% significantly reduce grade 1–2 esophagitis. Shen et al. [75] found that gastric low-dose volume parameters V10-V50 (5 Gy increments) were significantly correlated with ≥grade 2 acute radiation gastritis, with cut-off values of 79.1%, 74.2%, 72.5%, 60.1%, 46%, 43.8%, 35.7%, 22.3%, and 12.9%, respectively. These organ low-dose parameters are referenceable for WLI. It is crucial to recognize that most OAR constraints used in WLI planning are extrapolated from adult oncology or different disease sites. Their validity for predicting late toxicity in the unique context of pediatric Ewing sarcoma, often combined with cardiotoxic chemotherapy (e.g., anthracyclines), is not prospectively validated. This represents a significant weakness in current treatment planning standards and underscores the need for pediatric-specific normal tissue complication probability models.

Data on combined whole abdominal irradiation are limited. The OVAR-IMRT-02 study [76, 77] evaluated late toxicity, Quality of Life (QOL), and oncologic outcomes in International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) stage III ovarian cancer patients receiving consolidation whole abdominal irradiation (30 Gy/20 fractions) after cytoreductive surgery and taxane/carboplatin chemotherapy. Kandula et al. [78] treated 30 abdominal neuroblastoma patients with radical radiotherapy (median dose 21.6 Gy, 21–30.6 Gy) and followed them for a median 53 months (5.4–106.1.4.1 months). Two patients failing ipsilateral kidney dose constraints (D100% [Dose received by 100% of the target/organ volume] ≤ 14.4 Gy) developed ipsilateral renal dysplasia/atrophy on imaging (no laboratory abnormalities due to contralateral kidney compensation), and two failing liver constraints (D50%≤9 Gy) had no acute/delayed liver toxicity. The prescribed doses in these studies are close to those recommended for Ewing sarcoma and rhabdomyosarcoma whole-abdominal irradiation [52], so their dose constraints are referenceable for WLI combined with abdominal irradiation (Table 3).

Efficacy of Whole Lung Irradiation

Whole lung irradiation (WLI) significantly improves local control and survival in Ewing sarcoma. The efficacy of WLI is supported by decades of clinical research, as chronologically highlighted in Table 4. The IESS-I study showed that prophylactic WLI delays disease progression and enhances disease-free survival in local Ewing sarcoma by eradicating potential lung metastases, while Marinova et al. [79] noted it delays pulmonary metastasis progression for at least 4 years. For metastatic patients, Paulussen et al. [3] (CESS study, 100 Ewing sarcoma patients with lung/pleural metastases) found WLI reduced lung recurrence by 50% (20% vs. 40%, p = 0.046) and increased 5-year EFS by 11% (38% vs. 27%, p = 0.0022). Paulussen et al. [8] (EICESS study, 171 stage IV patients) reported WLI significantly benefited EFS in those with isolated lung metastases (40% vs. < 0.05) and even bone/bone marrow metastases (27% vs. 0, p = 0.0001). Elghazawy et al. [33] showed WLI further improved 5-year EFS (42.2% vs. 18.2%, p = 0.004) and lung-specific EFS (56.9% vs. 27.3%, p = 0.06). Grewal et al. WLI also improves overall survival (OS). Spunt et al. [59] found 5-year OS was similar between patients with incomplete induction chemotherapy remission and those with complete remission post-WLI (30.0%±12.5% vs. 41.7%±13.0%, P = 0.53), indicating WLI compensates for poor chemotherapy response. Bolling et al. [6] (EICESS-99, 99 patients with exclusive pulmonary metastases) showed WLI doubled OS (70.7% vs. 29%, p = 0.012). Reiter et al. [81] confirmed WLI significantly improved 5-year OS regardless of post-induction lung metastasis remission (85.2% vs. 55% and 59.8% vs. 24.6%,<0.01). For lung recurrence, Scobioala et al. [50] reported WLI increased 3-year OS by 14% (47% vs. 33%, p = 0.007). In the intensive chemotherapy era, Bu-Mel-HDCT plus WLI achieved 71.4% 10-year OS in pulmonary recurrence patients [51].

Despite these compelling data, a critical appraisal of the evidence is warranted. The survival benefits are largely derived from retrospective comparisons or subset analyses of clinical trials not primarily designed to test WLI. This introduces significant selection bias, as patients selected for WLI often have better performance status, limited metastatic burden, or favorable response to chemotherapy—all of which independently predict superior outcomes. Furthermore, the magnitude of benefit appears heterogeneous; it is most pronounced in patients with isolated lung metastases and less clear in those with combined skeletal disease or poor chemoresponse. These data confirm WLI’s key role in Ewing sarcoma comprehensive treatment, improving disease control and long-term prognosis multidimensionally. However, the definitive magnitude of its independent contribution—and the identification of subgroups most likely to benefit—requires prospective validation in trials with a contemporary chemotherapy backbone and modern radiotherapy techniques.

Toxicities of Whole Lung Radiotherapy

Whole lung irradiation (WLI)-induced toxicity is closely related to radiation dose, chemotherapy combination, and patient age. The spectrum of WLI toxicities has shifted with technological advancements. While modern techniques mitigate classic high dose effects (e.g., severe cardiotoxicity), they may alter the profile toward more chronic or stochastic risks—a trade off that is poorly quantified to date.

Radiation Pneumonitis and Fibrosis

Radiation pneumonitis and fibrosis are typical complications, with significantly increased risk when combined with chemotherapy. Ronchi et al.’s [64] review (14 studies, 640 WLI patients) reported grade ≥ 3 acute pulmonary toxicity incidence of 0.0–12.2%, including 12 (1.8%) cases of severe pneumonia. Grade ≥ 2 late lung injury is more common in high-risk patients with a history of boost radiotherapy, thoracic surgery, chemotherapy, or smoking. Scobioala et al. [50] (227 Ewing sarcoma patients with lung metastases) found 9% interstitial pneumonia incidence.

Esophagitis

Esophagitis in WLI is rarely reported. Scobioala et al. [50] noted dysphagia in only 4 patients (1.8%) in their review. Abate et al. [48] (68 Ewing sarcoma patients with lung metastases receiving high-dose chemotherapy plus WLI via Three-Dimensional Conformal Radiation Therapy [3DCRT], IMRT, or Volumetric-Modulated Arc Therapy [VMAT]) reported grade 1–2 esophagitis incidence of 5.9% and grade 2–3 odynophagia of 4.3%.

Cardiac Toxicity

Traditional WLI techniques cause cardiac complications (e.g., congestive heart failure [CHF], myocardial infarction) in childhood cancer survivors due to unavoidable high-dose cardiac irradiation [27, 28]. Green et al. [27] found a 1.6-fold increased CHF risk (P = 0.037) per 10 Gy WLI dose increment. Cardiac sparing IMRT and proton therapy have largely eliminated significant acute and subacute cardiotoxicity in recent studies [30, 49, 51, 82]. However, the long term (decades) cardiac outcomes for patients treated with modern CS IMRT—which trades high dose for low dose exposure—are entirely unknown. The concern shifts from overt cardiomyopathy to potential late coronary artery disease, a risk underscored by studies linking LAD dose to cardiac events [66, 67]. This potential shift in the toxicity profile further exemplifies the modern “low dose bath” dilemma.

Pulmonary Dysfunction

Common post-WLI lung function abnormalities include restrictive ventilation dysfunction (decreased including forced vital capacity [FVC], forced expiratory volume in 1 s [FEV1], total lung capacity [TLC]) and impaired diffusing capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide [DLCO]). Motosue et al. [60] found 18.8% had active respiratory symptoms post-WLI, with significant decreases in FVC, FEV1, and TLC (< 0.05); boost irradiation was associated with reduced FEV1/FVC, FEF25%−75%, residual volume (RV), and RV/TLC (all < 0.03), while pulmonary wedge resection lowered abnormal RV/TLC risk (P = 0.002). Weiner et al. [83] observed 50% of children had FEV1 decline and 30% moderate-to-severe TLC reduction post-WLI. Ellis et al.’s [84] prospective study showed transient pulmonary function decreases 6–12 months after WLI plus doxorubicin, with partial recovery at 2 years, indicating reversible early radiation-induced lung injury.

Thyroid Toxicity

Thyroid dysfunction post-WLI is rare. Duverge et al. [32] (7 WLI patients, median follow-up 16.3 years) reported 1 case of hypothyroidism (2.8 years post-WLI) and 1 case of thyroid cancer (7.7 years post-WLI). Morgan et al. [70] (22 Wilms tumor patients post-WLI) found 2 with elevated Thyroid-Stimulating Hormone (TSH) (1 resolved spontaneously, 1 with simple goiter). Abate et al. [48] (68 patients post-WLI plus high-dose BU-MEL, median follow-up 86 months) reported 1.5% grade 2 hypothyroidism.

Chest Wall and Skeletal Muscle Dysplasia

Thoracic and spinal dysplasia are common in children post-WLI, likely due to radiation-induced growth plate inhibition. Attard-Montalto et al. [85] (8 long-term Wilms tumor survivors post-WLI) found all had reduced thoracic volume, 4 had scoliosis, and 4/5 females had breast hypoplasia. Benoist et al. [86] (48 Wilms tumor patients) reported 10 developed spinal curvature (10°–30°) post-WLI, with significantly reduced thoracic spine sagittal and frontal diameters.

Secondary Malignancy and Death Risk

WLI plus chemotherapy may increase secondary malignancy and death risk. Scobioala et al. [49] (9 studies, 227 patients) summarized 3.1% developed secondary malignancies (e.g., acute myeloid leukemia [AML], myelodysplastic syndromes [MDS]) and 10% treatment-related mortality (mainly attributed to high-dose chemotherapy [HD-CT] rather than WLI). Moskowitz et al. [87] (1230 female childhood survivors in the CCSS study post chest radiotherapy) found a dramatically increased breast cancer standardized incidence ratio (SIR = 43.6; 95% CI 27.2 70.3) with a 30% cumulative incidence by age 50 (95% CI 25 34). This risk is paramount and arguably the most severe long term consequence. It is important to emphasize that even with modern techniques that reduce high dose exposure, the low dose bath to breast tissue (a consequence of CS IMRT and, to a lesser extent, IMPT) may still confer significant risk. This concern must be central to risk benefit discussions, especially with adolescent females and their families.

Radiation Myelitis

No relevant reports on WLI exist in the literature.

Appraisal of Unresolved Controversies and Clinical Dilemmas

The synthesis of efficacy and toxicity data within the context of technological evolution reveals several unresolved controversies that define the current clinical and research landscape.

Patient Selection: Who Benefits Most from WLI?

Despite proven efficacy, the absolute survival benefit of WLI varies considerably across subgroups. The EICESS studies showed dramatic benefit for patients with isolated lung metastases [3, 8], yet the marginal gain for those with concurrent bone metastases or poor chemotherapy response remains unclear and may be offset by increased toxicity. This calls into question the routine “one size fits all” application and underscores the need for biology based refinements in patient selection. The R2Pulm trial further complicates this by showing that intensive chemotherapy alone can yield survival outcomes similar to conventional chemotherapy plus WLI [5], posing a direct challenge to the indispensability of WLI in all responders and highlighting the urgent need for biomarkers (e.g., circulating tumor DNA, PET response) to identify patients who can safely forgo WLI.

The Efficacy-Toxicity Trade-off in the Modern Era

While advanced techniques (CS-IMRT, IMPT) have dramatically reduced high-dose cardiac exposure [29, 30], they have inadvertently created a new paradigm characterized by the low dose bath phenomenon: the exchange of a small volume of high dose radiation for a large volume of low dose radiation. The long-term clinical impact of this bath—particularly on pulmonary function, secondary solid tumors (especially breast cancer) [87], and perhaps even late cardiovascular disease—is unknown and represents a modern, unresolved trade off. Furthermore, the survival benefit of WLI in patients already achieving a complete radiological remission with intensive chemotherapy must be critically weighed against its guaranteed, albeit reduced, pulmonary toxicity and the stochastic risk of secondary malignancies. This trade off is at the heart of personalized decision making today.

The Evidence Hierarchy: Limitations of Existing Data

The majority of evidence supporting WLI stems from retrospective series, subset analyses of clinical trials, or single-arm studies. The lack of prospective randomized trials specifically designed for WLI in the metastatic setting is a fundamental limitation. This often leads to selection bias in reported outcomes and hampers the ability to establish definitive causality between WLI and long-term toxicities. Furthermore, data on the combination with targeted agents and immunotherapy are alarmingly sparse, relying largely on extrapolation from other cancers—a risky practice given the unique biology and treatment context of Ewing sarcoma.

Conclusion

WLI plays a key role in treating Ewing sarcoma with pulmonary metastasis by improving local control and survival rates. Historical and contemporary evidence consolidated in this review supports its use, particularly in patients with isolated pulmonary disease. Application of precision techniques (e.g., CS IMRT, IMPT) significantly reduces normal tissue dose and the risk of acute high grade toxicity, although radiation pneumonitis, lung function impairment, and secondary malignancies remain serious concerns. This review identifies that the central challenge is no longer whether WLI works, but for whom the benefits clearly outweigh the risks in the context of modern multimodal therapy, and how to deliver it most safely with evolving technologies. The journey from a one size fits all prophylactic measure to a nuanced, precision oncology tool is underway but incomplete. Individualized dose adjustment, biologically guided patient selection, and lifelong pulmonary and cardiac surveillance are essential for optimizing treatment regimens and respiratory motion management. Future efforts must move beyond technical refinement to resolve the fundamental controversies in patient selection, validate the long-term safety of modern dose distributions, and establish safe, evidence-based protocols for combining WLI with the next generation of systemic therapies. This will advance WLI toward a truly individualized, evidence-based component of multimodal integrated therapy.
