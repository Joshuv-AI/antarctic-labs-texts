Predicting Recovery After Joint Bleeding in Persons With Bleeding Disorders
Authors: Gijs Aertssen, Lize F. D. van Vulpen, Wouter Foppen, Flora H. P. van Leeuwen, Merel A. Timmer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionJoint bleeds are burdensome and recovery differs from bleed to bleed. Identifying predictors of recovery could enable personalized treatment and monitoring; aiming to prevent long‐term joint deterioration, and facilitate faster return to activities.AimTo identify predictors of time to recovery after joint bleeding in people with haemophilia.MethodsRegular care data on recovery of joint bleeds were collected from people with haemophilia who attended the Van Creveldkliniek between 2016 and 2025. The primary outcome was time to recovery, defined as the normalization of all clinical symptoms. Kaplan–Meier survival curves and Cox proportional hazard regression were conducted. Independent variables were as follows: trough level, joint status, cause of the bleed, joint location, limitation in ROM, effusion on ultrasound, time between start of symptoms and start of treatment, treatment days and emicizumab treatment.ResultsSixty‐four bleeds in 44 individuals were included. Most of the patients had haemophilia A (87.9%). Median time to recovery was 32.5 days. Joint bleeds without limitation in range of motion (<15 degrees limitation in flexion or extension) at presentation recovered 2.3 times faster (p = 0.007) than bleeds with a limitation. Patients with severe haemophilia A on emicizumab prophylaxis recovered 2.7 times faster than those on factor‐prophylaxis (p = 0.027).ConclusionJoint bleeds with significant limitation in ROM at presentation needed more time to recover. Patients with severe haemophilia on emicizumab showed shorter recovery after joint bleeding. Follow‐up should be personalized and extended beyond one month.

Introduction

Haemophilia is a rare, X‐linked inherited disease caused by a deficiency or dysfunction of coagulation factors VIII or IX. It is characterized by spontaneous and trauma‐related bleeding most notably in joints. Treatment strategies to stop acute bleeding and prevent ongoing synovial proliferation include the administration of the missing coagulation factor, (partial) immobilization, unloading the joint using aids, such as crutches or a sling, and rehabilitation to regain full functioning and participation [1]. However, recovery still differs from bleed to bleed. While some bleeds resolve quickly, allowing patients to resume normal daily activities and sports soon after the bleed, other bleeds require a longer recovery period. Our recent study of 30 joint bleeding episodes showed that recovery took longer than a month in almost half of the cases, ranging from 0.3 to 5 months [2].

Factors predicting time to recovery are not described in literature, leading to estimated and general follow‐up periods. Timely identification of joint bleeds at risk of ongoing synovial proliferation is crucial, as it increases the risk of repeated bleeding and eventually leads to osteochondral damage [3, 4, 5]. Moreover, identifying these factors could optimize treatment: coagulation factor therapy may be intensified, additional anti‐inflammatory medication may be considered, and an adjusted rehabilitation programme may be needed for patients either at high risk of persistent synovial proliferation or at low risk of prolonged recovery. For example, by using a respectively more strict or more permissive approach, altering duration of immobilization and monitoring frequency.

Therefore, this study aims to identify predictors for time to recovery after joint bleeding in people with haemophilia. We hypothesize that traumatic bleeds in weightbearing and arthropathic joints take longer to recover. Furthermore, prompt treatment after onset of the complaints and higher trough levels of clotting factor could limit the recovery period. Clinical characteristics associated with major bleeding, like a greater limitation of range of motion (ROM), more swelling and more effusion on ultrasound could be predictors of prolonged recovery.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Population

In this single‐centre retrospective cohort study, regular care data of joint bleeds were extracted from medical records of people with haemophilia or Von Willebrand disease who attended the Van Creveldkliniek, University Medical Center Utrecht, between April 2016 and April 2025. This clinic is the largest haemophilia centre in the Netherlands and offers multidisciplinary care to a large, historically well‐documented patient cohort. Patients have personalized treatment plans based on the severity of their disease, pharmacokinetics and personal preferences, including on demand therapy, prophylactic treatment with coagulation factor or non‐factor replacement therapy in the case of haemophilia A (emicizumab). Patients were included if they had an ultrasound confirmed joint bleed in an ankle, knee or elbow. The other inclusion criteria were as follows: examination within 7 days after onset of complaints and availability of follow‐up data until full recovery. Joint bleeds in joints replaced with a prosthesis were excluded. Patients could be included multiple times with distinct bleeds. The study was approved by the institutional Medical Research Ethics Committee (19‐665/C).

Standard Care

All included joint bleeds were treated in line with the World Federation of Haemophilia (WFH) guidelines for management of haemophilia [6, 7]. Patients received factor replacement therapy and/or desmopressin according to their predetermined treatment plan to achieve the target peak factor VIII or IX levels of 60% for at least two consecutive days. According to the local protocol, joint bleed recovery was followed up after 1 week and monthly thereafter with both physical and ultrasound examination until full recovery. These examinations were performed by specialized physiotherapists or haematologists who were trained according to the Haemophilia Joint Health Score (HJHS) and Haemophilia Early Arthropathy Detection with UltraSound (HEAD‐US) protocols [8, 9]. Subsequently, treatment was adjusted based on these clinical and ultrasound findings. Reduced joint loading (partial or full immobilization) was recommended during the acute and sub‐acute phase of a bleed (estimated duration between 4 and 21 days [10]), followed by rehabilitation under supervision of a physiotherapist to regain pre‐bleed functionality. Cyclooxygenase2‐specific non‐steroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs were administered in the case of persistent synovial proliferation [6].

Patient Characteristics

The following characteristics were extracted from the electronic patient records: age, bleeding disorder (haemophilia A, B or Von Willebrand disease), severity (mild, moderate and severe), and treatment regimen (emicizumab, factor prophylaxis, or on demand). Trough level and joint status prior to the bleed were also extracted. Trough levels were categorized in low (<5%) and high (>5%). Patients on emicizumab prophylaxis and with mild haemophilia (baseline factor levels) were categorized as high trough level, patients with moderate or severe haemophilia A on on‐demand therapy or factor prophylaxis were categorized as low through level, as factor levels of 5% will never be exceeded. Exact through levels of patients with haemophilia B were calculated for the moment of onset. Joint status prior to the bleed was determined based on the last reported, joint specific HJHS (range 0−20), HEAD‐US (range 0−8) and/or Pettersson score (range 0−13) [11]. Joint status was defined as arthropathic if HEAD‐US or Pettersson was >0, or HJHS was >1. Abnormalities on HEAD‐US or Pettersson are considered intra‐articular abnormalities even though they may or may not be haemophilia related, there for a cut‐off of >0 was used. As positive scores on HJHS may be due to extra‐articular findings, like muscle atrophy, a cut‐off of >1 was used.

Joint Bleed Characteristics

For each joint bleed: cause of the bleed (trauma or spontaneous), joint location (upper or lower extremity), time between start of symptoms and start of the treatment (<24 or >24 h) and treatment days (≤ 3 or >3 days of administration of clotting factors) was extracted. Physical examination consisted of the following: (1) warmth of the joint on palpation (absent/present) in comparison to the contralateral side, (2) joint swelling on palpation (absent/present), (3) limitations in gait measured by inspection (normal gait, asymmetric gait, no weightbearing) and (4) limitation in active ROM of the joint (flexion/extension, dorsiflexion/plantar flexion) compared to the contralateral side (in the case of healthy joints) or previous measurements, measured with a goniometer. Limitation in ROM was dichotomized based on the minimal clinically important change (MCIC) of short‐arm goniometry with visual interpretation [12]: no limitation in ROM (<15 degrees of limitation in flexion or extension) or limited ROM (>15 degrees of limitation in flexion or extension). Ultrasound examination consisted of 1) degree of joint effusion (no/mild or severe), 2) degree of synovial hypertrophy (no/mild or severe). Examinations were performed using two ultrasound scanners (Esaote, MyLab 25 Gold; Mindray TE7), with a 7.5–12 MHz linear transducer.

Primary Outcome

The primary outcome measure and dependent variable was time to full recovery of the joint bleed in days. Full recovery was defined as normalization of all clinical and ultrasound findings. In joints with pre‐existing abnormalities established during previous routine clinical and ultrasound assessment, normalization to the pre‐bleed joint status was considered as full recovery.

Analysis

Patient and joint bleed characteristics were reported as medians with ranges, or frequencies with percentages. Time to full recovery was reported as median with ranges, summarized in a cumulative incidence curve and used as dependent variable in the Kaplan–Meier survival analysis. Univariable and multivariable Cox Proportional Hazards (PH) regression analyses were used to identify predictors of time to recovery at presentation. Independent variables were: trough level, joint status, cause of the bleed, joint location, limitation in ROM, effusion on ultrasound, and time between start of symptoms and start of treatment. To correct for potential interval censoring, sensitivity analyses were performed using non‐parametric Turnbull and parametric Weibull curves with the last known outpatient visit where the participant was not yet fully recovered was used as left bound. Lastly, a secondary multivariable Cox PH regression analysis was performed with the independent variable treatment days added to the primary analysis. This variable was not measured at presentation and was therefore analysed secondarily. p‐values <0.05 were considered significant and <0.10 considered a trend. All analyses were performed in Rstudio (2025) with use of packages ‘Survival’ and ‘icenReg’.

Subgroup Analysis

To evaluate the predictive value of non‐factor prophylactic therapy compared to prophylactic therapy with coagulation factor, a subset of the data consisting of only patients with severe haemophilia A was used. Univariable and multivariable Cox PH regression analyses were performed with factor versus non‐factor treatment and limitation in ROM included as independent variables.

Results

Patient and Joint Characteristics

After data extraction, 69 bleeds were eligible for inclusion. In 5 bleeding episodes, the patient objected to participate. This resulted in inclusion of 64 bleeds in 44 individuals. Their median age was 15 years. Most of the patients had haemophilia A (87.9%) without arthropathy of the included joint (85.9%). Half of the included patients had severe haemophilia (50.0%). Two thirds (64.1%) of all included patients were categorized as having high (>5%) baseline trough levels. Factor levels of patients with mild haemophilia A ranged from 5% to 28%, with a median of 10%. Patient and joint characteristics are summarized in Table 1.

Joint Bleed and Treatment Characteristics

Bleeds were mostly traumatic (64.0%) and localized in the ankle (67.2%). Physical examination showed swollen (90.9%), in ROM limited (86.3%) and half of the time also warm (56.1%) joints at presentation. In lower‐extremity bleeds, gait abnormalities were also highly prevalent (88.1%). Median start of treatment of the bleed was on the same day as the start of the complaints (range 0–7) and did not significantly differ between patients with severe and non‐severe haemophilia (Wilcoxon p = 0.534). It consisted of a median of four treatment days to coagulation factor (IQR = 1–7). Median recovery time of a joint bleed was 32.5 days (IQR = 20.5–54.75). Pairwise correlation between baseline characteristics of joint bleeds did not exceed 0.5, indicating a low risk of multicollinearity. Survival Analysis

Univariable Cox PH analysis showed a significant faster recovery in patients without limitation in ROM at presentation (p = 0.042), as shown in Table S1 in the Appendix. The primary multivariable Cox PH analysis identified limitation in ROM as significant predictor for recovery as well. Joints without limitation in ROM (<15 degrees limitation to flexion or extension) recovered 2.3 times faster (p = 0.007) than joints with a limitation in ROM (>15 degrees limitation). A sensitivity analysis using Turnbull and Weibull curves and p‐values, showed very small differences in comparison to the Cox PH regression analysis. Most effects were comparable in size and direction, indicating only potentially small effects of interval censoring. The only differences between the models were observed for upper/lower extremity bleeds and for less/more than 3 treatment days. In contrast to the results of the Cox PH analysis, significant Weibull p‐values were observed for these variables in univariable analyses, indicating that upper extremity bleeds recovered faster than lower extremity bleeds and that patients with >3 treatment days to coagulation factor recovered slower. When the variable ‘treatment days’ was added to the multivariable Cox PH model, it lost significance. The Kaplan–Meier, Weibull and Turnbull curves and the results of secondary multivariable analyses are presented in Figures S2.

Discussion

Summary of Findings

This study shows that a limitation in ROM of >15 degrees in flexion or extension in ankle, knee or elbow at presentation is a significant predictor of a longer time to recovery after a joint bleed in people with haemophilia. For patients with severe haemophilia A, prophylactic treatment with emicizumab is related to a faster recovery.

Comparisons With Literature

In this study, the sample of our previous cohort was doubled [2], yet the cumulative incidences of days to full recovery are similar. The alignment of cumulative incidence between these cohorts supports the validity of the findings and enhances their reproducibility.

Our findings align with the WFH guidelines, which classify minor joint bleeds as; minimal limitation in ROM, mild pain, and no swelling. Major bleeds are classified as clear limitation in ROM, severe pain, and swelling. Limitation in ROM now seems the most important symptom. Besides, major bleeds should be treated >3 days with coagulation factor. In our study, major bleeds recovered more slowly despite more treatment days, consistent with this classification. The suggested slower time to recovery when having >3 treatment days is explained by the causal relationship of symptoms of a bleed influencing the number of treatment days. Swelling, present in 90.9% of bleeds, cannot significantly predict time to recovery, likely due to its dichotomous assessment and inability to differentiate between mild and severe swelling reliably. Similar limitations apply to the ultrasound scoring of effusion. Pain, the last symptom in the WFH classification, was not assessed, but could be included in future research.

A potentially slower recovery of bleeds in weightbearing joints was hypothesized. Although weightbearing joints were not identified as predictor for prolonged recovery in the primary analysis, they were when correcting for interval censoring. Since the group sizes are small and the primary analyses yield non‐significant p‐values and wide confidence intervals, the observed differences cannot be fully attributed to the weightbearing nature of these joints. Despite these remarks, it is in line with treatment guidelines of acute joint bleeds that unloading the joint expedites recovery. Arthropathic joints were hypothesized to recover more slowly as well, but in this study no significant difference was observed. A possible explanation is that patients with chronically painful arthropathic joints already exhibit reduced physical activity and mechanical loading of these joints in daily living with a positive effect on recovery. Lastly, no evidence was found of a shorter recovery in the case of higher baseline trough levels or prompt treatment after joint bleeds.

Patients with severe haemophilia A treated with emicizumab recovered faster compared to patients treated with factor prophylaxis. Emicizumab treatment provides stable haemostatic levels resulting reduced annual bleeding rate [13, 14]. Its haemostatic potential is compared to a factor VIII trough level of 15% or more [15]. However, higher trough levels were not associated with faster recovery in our study. Furthermore, limitation in ROM was not a significant predictor of recovery in the subset with severe haemophilia A patients, neither in the univariable nor in the multivariable model, despite being significant in the overall cohort. This suggests that major and minor bleeds in patients on emicizumab recover equally fast, although a larger sample size is needed to confirm this.

Strengths and Limitations

A strength of this study is the relatively large and complete dataset on acute bleeds in people with haemophilia with follow‐up until full recovery. Although the retrospective design of the study limited us to the variables and follow‐up intervals used in regular care, all patients were treated and assessed according to the local protocols in a standardized and very structured way. The results are therefore easy to interpret and applicable in a clinical setting. However, due to the retrospective design, we were limited in the selection of variables; objective and reliable pain scores and exact time between onset of symptoms and start of treatment were lacking [6].

A potential limitation, was the presence of interval censoring in the time to event data. Interval censoring occurs when the specific time of event (recovery in our case) is unknown, but the interval of time in which the event occurred is known. The wider the intervals, the greater the risk of potential bias. As in the current study, the intervals are based on the clinical protocols, patients with longer recovery periods have increasingly longer intervals between follow‐ups. As recovery is a gradual process, it is of less relevance if a bleed has recovered on an exact day, but of way more relevance in which interval. For example, before 30 days or after 60 days. Moreover, the sensitivity analysis to correct for interval censoring revealed only a minimal effect.

Clinical Relevance

We recommend using the degree of limitation in ROM at presentation to estimate the time to recovery of a joint bleed. The clear threshold of 15 degrees limitation to flexion or extension (MCIC of short‐arm goniometry, indicating a true limitation in ROM) increases the clinical applicability of the results. Clinicians can use this for educated considerations about time to recovery and to adjust treatment plans and follow‐up periods. Joints without distinct limitation in ROM, especially if also minimally painful, minimally swollen, non‐weightbearing and in patients with non‐factor prophylaxis, are expected to recover faster. In that case, fewer and shorter follow‐up intervals can be used. Also, it can be hypothesized that the major bleeds indeed need more intensive (more days) treatment with clotting factor, in accordance with the WFH guideline.

Recommendations for Future Research

Future research should follow‐up larger amounts of patients on non‐factor prophylaxis with acute joint bleeds. Highly significant findings of these patients recovering faster and showing less limited and swollen joints could lead to further improvements of the quality of life in people with haemophilia. In addition, more potent emerging therapies (such as ultralong half‐life products [16, 17]) that achieve higher factor levels may result in even shorter recovery times. Besides, future research should aim to prospectively define clinical thresholds for safely increasing therapy intensity during rehabilitation. In particular, limitation in ROM may serve as a valuable indicator, enabling more permissive treatment modalities and reducing rebleeding risk. Lastly, future research should investigate how weightbearing influences rehabilitation after acute joint bleeds. In this study, the retrospective design and small sample size limited causal conclusions, yet the results suggest that non‐weightbearing joints recover faster. As unloading the joint remains the standard therapeutic approach during joint bleeding, it imposes a significant burden on patients. To define stepwise load progressions, patients must be monitored continuously throughout the bleeding episode, using repeated, real‐world measurements of joint loading and activity in daily living.

Conclusion

Joint bleeds with significant limitation in ROM at presentation needed more time to recover. Patients with severe haemophilia on emicizumab showed shorter recovery after joint bleeding. Follow‐up should be personalized and extended beyond one month.
