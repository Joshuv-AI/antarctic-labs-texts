Psychometric properties of the 12-item short form health survey version 2 among general Thai samples: a Rasch analysis
Authors: Krittaphas Kangwanrattanakul
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAlthough the 12-item Short Form Health Survey version 2 (SF-12v2) is suitable for measuring health status in the general Thai population, it has been evaluated using classical test theory. Rasch analysis, however, offers a psychometric testing method that converts ordinal scales to interval-level data without breaching parametric assumptions. Thus, this study aimed to assess the measurement properties of Thai SF-12v2 and SF-6D items derived from it among the general Thai population.MethodsRasch analysis was performed on 300 participants randomly selected from a pool of 1200 general Thai population. The initial SF-12v2 items, physical component summary (PCS), mental component summary (MCS), and SF-6D items were evaluated for overall fit to the Rasch model using the Chi-square test and several key elements of Rasch measurement properties, including unidimensionality, reliability (internal consistency), local independence, ordering thresholds, targeting, and item invariance (differential item functioning [DIF]).ResultsThe two-domain subtests of the SF-12v2 showed an acceptable fit to the Rasch model (χ2[12] = 20.16, p = 0.06), unidimensionality, satisfactory reliability (PSI = 0.72), local independence, and no significant DIF. Although five items initially misfitted the Rasch model, these issues were resolved in the subtest analysis. Individual PCS and MCS were not recommended for measuring health status due to inadequate reliability. The SF-6D items demonstrated a good overall model fit (χ2[20] = 23.18, p = 0.28), unidimensionality, local independence, and good internal consistency (PSI = 0.73). However, all scales showed poor targeting for the person-item threshold distribution.ConclusionsRasch analysis supports the use of two-domain subtests of the SF-12v2 questionnaire for between-group analysis in the general Thai population. However, it does not support the use of individual PCS and MCS due to poor reliability. The SF-6D, achieving satisfactory reliability, was endorsed for eliciting utility scores for between-group analysis.KEYWORDS: Rasch analysis, psychometric properties, SF-12v2, Thai population, SF-6D

Background

Health-related quality of life (HRQoL) has been recognised as an important outcome because it assesses the impact of health intervention on an individual’s health status and functioning through multiple dimensions, including physical and mental health (Remick et al., 2020; Yin et al., 2016). Generic HRQoL questionnaires are designed to measure health concepts relevant to various populations, enabling the comparison of HRQoL levels across various health conditions and healthy samples (Churruca et al., 2021). An example of a generic HRQoL measure is the 12-item Short Form Health Survey version 2 (SF-12v2), which is asserted to be suitable for measuring HRQoL levels across different populations (Frieling et al., 2013; McEvoy et al., 2019; Mols et al., 2009).

The SF-12 is recognised as a concise version of the SF-36, including 12 items that cover 8 dimensions: (1) Physical functioning (PF: 2 items), (2) role limitations due to physical problems (RP: 2 items), (3) bodily pain (BP: 1 item), (4) general health perception (GH: 1 item), (5) vitality (VT: 1 item), (6) social functioning (SF: 1 item), (7) role limitations due to emotional problems (RE: 2 items), and (8) mental health (MH: 2 items) (Turner-Bowker & Hogue, 2014). The SF-36, being more extensive, often imposes a significant burden on participants, particularly in large population surveys. To alleviate this burden, the SF-12 was developed as a shorter version, allowing participants to complete the questionnaire in less than 2 min (Turner-Bowker & Hogue, 2014).

To date, the SF-12v2 is preferred over the SF-12v1 due to modifications that include changes in the number of response options in the RP, RE, MH, and VT dimensions, as well as the rewording of two items (Fleishman et al., 2010; Ware et al., 2002). Although the SF-12v2 provides scores for eight dimensions, the two summary component scores: physical component summary (PCS) and mental component summary (MCS), are more commonly reported in health surveys (Fleishman et al., 2010; Ware et al., 2002). Previous evidence supports the use of SF-12v2 for measuring HRQoL levels across both general and diminished health samples (Bhandari et al., 2018; Kim et al., 2014; Młyńczak & Golicki, 2022; Montazeri et al., 2011; Shah et al., 2018). Additionally, the SF-12v2 items were used to compute the utility index for the SF-6D questionnaire, where the eight dimensions of the SF-12v2 were reclassified into six dimensions: physical functioning, role limitations, social functioning, pain, mental health, and vitality (Brazier & Roberts, 2004). Studies have suggested that SF-6D is a valid, reliable, and applicable instrument for eliciting utility indices in both general populations and therapeutic contexts (Engel et al., 2014; Khanna et al., 2007; Pogue et al., 2022; Xu et al., 2021; Zhao et al., 2019). However, these previous studies employed the classical test theory (CTT) approach, which primarily relies on ordinal summation scores or Likert scales. This approach assumes interval scale data; however, a summation of ordinal scale scores does not automatically transform into interval scale data, leading to potential violations of data assumptions (Stucki et al., 1996).

Rasch analysis, a more modern statistical approach based on item-response theory, is introduced to transform the ordinal scale of polytomous items into interval-level data without violating data assumptions (Medvedev & Krägeloh, 2022). Rasch analysis offers several advantages over the CTT approach, including determining item and person reliability and assessing how each participant responds to each item (Chang et al., 2014; Tennant & Küçükdeveci, 2023). It assists in scale refinement by indicating item location to determine item hierarchy based on difficulty levels on the logits scale. This can also be used to evaluate a person’s ability to endorse each item, identifying whether a person’s ability is located above the most difficult items or below the easiest items, which further indicates the ceiling or floor effects of the scale (Belvedere & de Morton, 2010). Furthermore, it can provide a single, linear scale for both items and persons, allowing for the estimation of measurement error and identification of misfitting items, thereby improving the reliability and validity of the instrument. Rasch models also support the comparison of scores across diverse samples and time points. However, Rasch analysis requires the measurement scale to meet important assumptions, such as unidimensionality and local independence (Kaplan & Hays, 2022; Medvedev & Krägeloh, 2022; Tennant & Küçükdeveci, 2023), before it can be performed. Given these promising aspects, previous studies have investigated the psychometric properties of SF-12v2 among participants with health conditions using Rasch analysis such as lung cancer (Soh et al., 2021), Parkinson’s disease (Hagell & Westergren, 2011), and stroke (Westergren & Hagell, 2014).

In Thailand, a previous study investigated the psychometric properties of SF-12v2 and SF-6D derived from SF-12v2 among general Thai populations (Kangwanrattanakul, 2021a, 2021b) and patients with chronic diseases (Sakthong & Munpan, 2017). However, this study relied primarily on the CTT approach. No previous studies have employed Rasch analysis to investigate the psychometric properties of these instruments in the general Thai population. Therefore, this study investigated the psychometric properties of SF-12v2 and the SF-6D derived from SF-12v2 for the general Thai population.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a dataset from a previous study entitled: ‘Psychometric properties comparison between EQ-5D-5L and EQ-5D-3L in the general Thai population (Kangwanrattanakul & Parmontree, 2020).’ It was a cross-sectional survey study involving 1,200 individuals from the general Thai population across five provinces in Thailand, including Chiang Mai (North), Khon Kaen (Northeast), Chonburi (East), Nakhon Si Thammarat (South), and Bangkok (the capital city of Thailand). Details regarding the selection of these provinces, and the classification of areas (rural/metropolitan) are documented elsewhere (Kangwanrattanakul & Parmontree, 2020). Participants were included in the study if they: (1) were between 18 and 70 years old and (2) could read and understand the data collection process, as assessed by the interviewers or researcher. Nevertheless, participants were excluded if they: (1) had an acute or life-threatening disease, (2) had cognitive impairment, or (3) had a disability.

Data collection

All convenient participants were asked to complete the self-administered SF-12v2 questionnaire at their home residences with the assistance of local village leaders who approached each participant. Interviews were conducted with participants who could not read the questions and response options due to eyesight problems. Interviewers were allowed to read the questions and response options aloud without explaining any meanings to ensure that all participants completed the questionnaires based on their understanding. All data collection was performed between July and September 2019.

Before each interview, participants were given an information sheet explaining the study’s objectives and the entire data collection process. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant, indicating their voluntary participation in the study. Participants were assured that their involvement was anonymous and that their data would be kept confidential. They were also informed that they could withdraw from the study at any time without providing a reason, incurring any cost, or facing any consequences. Ethics approval was obtained from the Burapha University Institutional Review Board (BUU-IRB: 108/2562). Official permission to use the SF-12v2 was granted by OPTUM (QM051842).

Instrument

SF-12v2

The SF-12v2 consists of 12 items for 8 dimensions: PF (2 items), RP (2 items), BP (1 item), GH (1 item), VT (1 item), SF (1 item), RE (2 items), and MH (2 items). It asks participants to rate their health status over the past 4 weeks. Response options range from 3 to 5 levels across 8 dimensions. These eight scales are used to compute both PCS and MCS (Ware et al., 2002). Furthermore, the SF-12 items are reclassified to develop the SF-6D questionnaire, which excludes the general health item and combines the two role limitations items, resulting in seven items covering six dimensions: (1) physical functioning (1 item), (2) role limitations (2 items), (3) social functioning (1 item), (4) pain (1 item), (5) mental health (1 item), and (6) vitality (1 item).

Data analyses

RUMM2030 was employed to randomly select a subsample of 300 participants from a total of 1,200 individuals representing the general Thai population. This sample size is deemed sufficient for conducting Rasch analysis on the SF-12v2 and SF-6D, ensuring reliable interpretation of RUMM fit statistics while reducing the likelihood of Type I error (Hagell & Westergren, 2016).

Descriptive statistics (frequencies, percentages, mean, standard deviations [SD]) were employed to report sociodemographic factors where appropriate. Participant characteristics were reported for the total sample and Rasch analysis groups: total samples (n = 1200) and Rasch analysis (n = 300). The Chi-square test was employed to investigate statistically significant differences in sociodemographic factors between the two groups. In this study, Rasch analysis was performed on the SF-12v2 items as the baseline analysis. The SF-12v2 items were further categorised into three domain subtests: PCS, MCS, and SF-6D. Each domain subtest was also investigated using the Rasch analysis.

The item-trait interaction was investigated for overall fit to the Rasch model using Chi-square statistics to indicate the homogeneity of hierarchical ordering from item difficulty along the continuum of person ability (Robinson et al., 2019). A satisfactory fit to the Rasch model was achieved when a non-significant Chi-square statistic (p > 0.05) was obtained (Robinson et al., 2019; Soh et al., 2021). Furthermore, individual item and person fit were investigated, and they were deemed an acceptable fit to the Rasch model if their fit residuals were within a range of ±2.5 (Robinson et al., 2019). Finally, overall item and person fit were judged to be satisfactory if their residual mean and SD values were zero and ≤1.5, respectively (Soh et al., 2021; Twiss et al., 2016).

As mentioned, unidimensionality should be examined before performing the Rasch analysis. In this study, PCS, MCS, and SF-6D items were investigated to determine if they measured only one underlying construct. The approach to unidimensionality testing proposed by Smith was confirmed if the significant t-test <5% and/or the lower bounds of confidence interval (CI) for the significant t-test were <0.05 for the two most dissimilar subtests of items/items identified from residual correlations (Smith & Jr, 2002; Tennant & Küçükdeveci, 2023).

Local dependency was investigated using the Yen’s Q3 statistic in the form of residual correlation matrix in order to determine whether responses to each item were independent of responses to other items within the same questionnaire set. Local dependency was identified if the residual correlation coefficients between two items exceeded the critical value of 0.2 above average residual correlation coefficients (Christensen et al., 2017). Nevertheless, super items or domain subtests for highly correlated items were created to explore the higher-order structure of the instrument and absorb the local dependency of items (Tennant & Küçükdeveci, 2023). If the domain subtest demonstrated unidimensionality, it indicated the absence of local trait dependency (Tennant & Küçükdeveci, 2023).

The response threshold was examined to assess whether participants could discriminate between response categories for each SF-12v2 item. A threshold map was used to identify disordered thresholds in the SF-12v2 items.

A person-item threshold distribution map was constructed to represent both person ability and item endorsement on the same logit scale. This map illustrates the extent to which individuals were able to endorse each SF-12v2 item, thereby identifying the ceiling and floor effects of the instrument. Effective targeting was indicated by a mean location logit score of zero in the person-item threshold distribution (Pallant & Tennant, 2007; Vaganian et al., 2022).

Differential item functioning (DIF) analysis was conducted to determine if SF-12v2 items remained invariant in measuring the underlying construct HRQoL across predefined sociodemographic subgroups (Robinson et al., 2019; Tennant & Küçükdeveci, 2023). The subgroups included sex (male/female), age (≤43 / > 43 years), marital status (living as single/married), an education level (≤secondary school/college), and health status (healthy/unhealthy). The DIF analyses were examined using analysis of variance (ANOVA) on residuals. If ANOVA showed a significant difference due to personal factors (p < 0.05), the DIF items were further split by that personal factor and anchored by non-DIF items. Paired t-tests were then employed to investigate the mean differences (Tennant & Küçükdeveci, 2023), and the effect size was calculated from the mean differences divided by their SD to determine the magnitude of the mean difference. An effect size <0.1 was considered indicative of a small DIF, warranting no further action on the DIF item (Rouquette et al., 2019; Tennant & Küçükdeveci, 2023).

The person separation index (PSI) was assessed to determine how well the SF-12v2 could distinguish between different levels of person ability for endorsement, thereby differentiating levels of the construct of interest (Tennant & Küçükdeveci, 2023). An acceptable PSI level exceeds 0.7, indicating that the scale is reliable for HRQoL measurement (Dabaghi et al., 2020; Esakki et al., 2018). According to HRQoL measurement guidelines, a PSI level of > 0.7 suggests reliability for between-group analysis, whereas a PSI of ≥0.85 indicates that the measure is suitable for pre–post HRQoL measurement (Tennant & Conaghan, 2007).

All Rasch analyses were performed using RUMM 2030 software, whereas all other analyses were conducted using STATA 17 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA). A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Table 2 presents the Rasch model fit statistics for individual SF-12v2 items, PCS, MCS, and SF-6D items. Among the SF-12v2 items, GH and VT1 were the most difficult to endorse, whereas BP and PF1 were the easiest. Although SF-12v2 showed a high PSI (0.81) and achieved unidimensionality (6.00% significant t-test; lower bound CI, 3.53), it did not fit the Rasch model (χ2[72] = 270.68, p < 0.01), and local dependence was present (Table 3). DIF was also detected for the PCS subtest against age and health status; however, the effect size for both personal factors was <0.1. Therefore, no further action was required for the PCS subtest.

As shown in Table 3, the individual PCS subtest (Analysis 3) did not achieve an acceptable fit to the Rasch model (χ2[30] = 104.01, p < 0.01) and presented with local dependency. However, it showed acceptable reliability (PSI = 0.72), strict unidimensionality (1.67% significant t-test; lower bound CI, −0.80), and no disordered threshold (Figure 1). To resolve these issues, three super items were created, which resulted in an acceptable fit to the Rasch model (χ2[15] = 24.49, p = 0.06) (Analysis 4) and local independence while maintaining unidimensionality (6.67% significant t-test; lower bound CI, 4.20). No DIF was detected against any sociodemographic factors. However, the revised model failed to achieve acceptable reliability (PSI = 0.65).

The 6-item MCS also failed to achieve an acceptable fit to the Rasch model (χ2[36] = 86.61, p < 0.01), had poor reliability (PSI = 0.62), and exhibited local dependency (Table 3). Despite this, it demonstrated unidimensionality (7.00% significant t-test; lower bound CI, 4.53) but showed a disordered threshold for RE2 (Figure 2). Similar to the PCS subtest, creating three super items improved the fit to the Rasch model (χ2[18] = 28.57, p = 0.05), maintained unidimensionality (1.34% significant t-test; lower bound CI, −1.13), and resolved local dependency. No DIF was detected against sociodemographic factors, but the reliability remained below acceptable levels (PSI = 0.66).

Threshold map for mental component summary and SF-6D.

The 7-item SF-6D also misfitted the Rasch model (χ2[35] = 110.85, p < 0.01), had poor reliability (PSI = 0.64), and exhibited a disordered threshold for RP2 (Figure 2). However, it showed unidimensionality (3.33% significant t-test; lower bound CI, 0.87) and local dependency (Analysis 7). Creating four super items led to an acceptable fit to the Rasch model (χ2[20] = 23.18, p = 0.28) and improved reliability (PSI = 0.73). This model maintained unidimensionality (4.00% significant t-test; lower bound CI, 1.53) and local independence (Analysis 8). The disordered threshold was resolved, and no DIF was detected against sociodemographic factors.

Poor person-item threshold distribution was observed for the 12-item SF-12v2 and the two-domain subtests, with a person-item mean of 2.12 ± 1.19 and 1.78 ± 0.80, respectively (Figure 3). A small proportion of respondents (4.01%) achieved the highest possible scores on the two-domain subtests, which is below the commonly accepted threshold for ceiling effects. In addition, poor person-item threshold distribution was found for the PCS-3, MCS-3, and SF-6D-4 super items, with means of 3.01 ± 1.34, 2.77 ± 1.13, and 2.79 ± 1.41, respectively (Figure 4). However, no ceiling effects were observed for these three measures.

Discussion

This study provides new evidence for the psychometric measurement of Thai SF-12v2 in the general Thai population using Rasch analysis.

The present Rasch analysis revealed that the initial SF-12v2 had five misfitting items (RP1, RP2 for the PCS, and RE1, RE2, and MH1 for MCS), whose residuals exceeded ±2.5, and it failed to achieve local independence, but it presented with unidimensionality. No disordered thresholds were observed for any of the 12-item SF-12v2. However, the misfitting items only appeared in the baseline model, where all 12 items were treated as a unidimensional construct. This contrasts with previous studies (Hagell & Westergren, 2011; Westergren & Hagell, 2014), in which Rasch analyses showed no misfitting items for the SF-12v2 questionnaire in individuals with particular health conditions. This discrepancy might imply that Thai participants perceive and understand these items differently compared with participants from other countries. Notably, most misfitting items pertain to limited role activities due to physical or emotional problems. Although most general Thai population reported themselves as healthy, some may have responded differently to these items because they reported various physical and emotional problems. Conversely, in previous studies involving individuals with specific health conditions, a more consistent response pattern to the SF-12v2 items might have resulted in no misfitting items in the Rasch model. Some of the misfitting items might be attributed to cultural or linguistic nuances that were not fully captured during the initial translation, or to changes in perceptions toward those items over time. Therefore, future cognitive debriefing with the target population is an essential step to ensure that linguistic equivalence and cultural appropriateness remain intact. Nevertheless, once the misfitting items were assigned to their respective components to generate two-domain subtests (PCS and MCS), the issue of misfitting was resolved. Each subtest showed an acceptable fit to the Rasch model, achieved unidimensionality, and demonstrated local independence. This indicates that Rasch analysis supports the use of a higher-order construct with two-domain subtests for HRQoL measurement in the general Thai population.

The Rasch analysis also revealed that the individual component summaries (PCS and MCS) demonstrated an acceptable fit to the Rasch model when subtests were created for both scales, with no significant DIF against sociodemographic factors and no local dependency. However, the 6-item MCS showed a disordered threshold for item RE2, where inspection of the category probability curves indicated that participants were more likely to choose ‘some of the time’ (level 3) over ‘most of the time’ (level 2). Consequently, the threshold for level 3 was lower than that for level 2, indicating a disordered threshold. A possible explanation for this is that the response frequency for level 2 was very low (9 responses), which is below the recommended minimum of 10 responses needed to stabilise the response category (Smith Jr et al., 2003). Nevertheless, this threshold disordering was not meaningful when the MCS subtests were created (Maritz et al., 2019).

Similar to the PCS and MCS, the SF-6D also met expectations of unidimensionality, model fit to the Rasch model, and local independence without significant DIF against sociodemographic factors when subtests were created. Again, a disordered threshold was detected for item RP2, where participants were more likely to choose ‘none of the time’ (level 5) over ‘a little of the time’ (level 4), resulting in the threshold for level 5 being lower than that for level 4. However, this threshold disordering ceased to be a major concern when the SF-6D subtests were generated (Maritz et al., 2019).

The PSI value was used to determine the reliability of the measures (Tennant & Küçükdeveci, 2023). In this study, PSI showed acceptable reliability for the initial SF-12v2, the 2-domain subtests for SF-12v2, and the four super items for SF-6D, with values of 0.81, 0.72, and 0.73, respectively. These values exceed the cutoff for acceptable reliability (>0.70), indicating that these measures can be used to assess HRQoL levels for between-group analysis (Dabaghi et al., 2020; Esakki et al., 2018). However, their values were lower than the acceptable cutoff for pre–post HRQoL measurement (Tennant & Conaghan, 2007). Unlike previous studies (Hagell & Westergren, 2011; Soh et al., 2021; Westergren & Hagell, 2014), which showed that both the individual PCS and MCS had satisfactory reliability for patients with lung cancer, Parkinson’s disease, and stroke (PSI > 0.70), this study revealed that their reliability was slightly below the acceptable level (PSI = 0.65 for the PCS and PSI = 0.66 for the MCS). Possible explanations for this discrepancy include the presence of misfitting items within both scales (PCS and MCS) and issues related to translation validity.

No ceiling effects were detected for any subscales (PCS, MCS, and SF-6D). The person-item distribution showed poor targeting, indicating that the scales’ range of measurement did not fully cover the health states reported by the participants. Specifically, the person-item distribution graphs for the MCS, PCS, and SF-6D were more likely to represent better health states, as there was a significant gap between person ability and item location located at the end of the spectrum scale. This suggests that the scales could not adequately distinguish individuals with poor health states. Similar to the earlier Rasch analysis for the Thai WHOQOL-BREF, poor targeting was also observed across all WHOQOL-BREF domains for the general Thai population (Kangwanrattanakul & Krägeloh, 2024). In this study, the general Thai population was relatively healthy, as those with acute or life-threatening diseases were excluded. This could explain why no floor effects and poor targeting were observed. In contrast, previous Rasch analysis involving samples with lung cancer (Soh et al., 2021), Parkinson’s disease (Hagell & Westergren, 2011), and stroke (Westergren & Hagell, 2014) showed well-targeted person-item distributions, as the items in the PCS and MCS could effectively distinguish health states along the entire spectrum for those impaired health samples, resulting in good targeting. The poor targeting of the SF-12v2 and SF-6D may limit their ability to capture meaningful health differences in populations with diverse health statuses. Individuals with severe health conditions may score similarly to those with milder conditions or without comorbidities, resulting in limited variation in HRQoL scores. Researchers and practitioners should be aware of this limitation and consider supplementing these instruments with disease-specific measures to better capture variations in health status across different populations. As a result, the psychometric performance of the SF-12v2 should be reinvestigated in both patient groups and in a general Thai population with varied health conditions using Rasch analysis.

Some limitations should be noted. First, this study recruited relatively healthy participants within a limited age range (20–70 years), which may restrict the generalizability of the findings. Future research should include a broader range of age and health conditions to better represent the general Thai population and enhance the external validity of the results. Second, the poor reliability detected for both the PCS and MCS subscales suggests that translation validity and cognitive debriefing should be performed for the misfitting items to ensure that participants fully understand them. Third, this study did not measure whether the measurement properties could change over time, as data were collected cross-sectionally at only one-time point.

Conclusions

Rasch analysis supports the use of two-domain subtests generated from the SF-12v2 questionnaire for measuring HRQoL levels in between-group analyses. These subtests achieve acceptable reliability, unidimensionality, an acceptable fit to the Rasch model, and local independence without significant DIF against sociodemographic factors. However, individual PCS and MCS are not recommended for use as they fail to achieve acceptable reliability. Although five items (RP1, RP2, RE1, RE2, and MH1) misfit the Rasch model, creating two-domain subtests resolves these misfitting issues, warranting further investigation into the reasons behind the misfitting. Similar to the two-domain subtests, Rasch analysis also supports the use of SF-6D items to elicit utility scores for economic analyses, especially for between-group analyses, due to their satisfactory reliability.
