Midventricular Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy With Apical Aneurysm Presenting as Persistent ST-Segment Elevation Mimicking Acute Myocardial Infarction
Authors: Andreas Kyvetos, Ioannis Liatakis, Panagioula Niarchou, Sophia Prapa, Anastasia Xintarakou, Konstantina Alexopoulou, Grigorios Trikas, Georgios Patavoukas, Efstathia Prappa, Athanasios Trikas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundST-segment elevation on electrocardiography is classically associated with acute transmural myocardial ischemia due to coronary artery occlusion and is central to the diagnosis of ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction. However, persistent ST-segment elevation may also reflect nonischemic structural heart disease.Case SummaryA 62-year-old woman presented with chest pain and widespread ST-segment elevation with T-wave inversion. Coronary angiography showed no obstructive disease. Echocardiography and cardiac magnetic resonance revealed midventricular hypertrophic cardiomyopathy with systolic midcavity obstruction and a left ventricular apical aneurysm. Nonsustained ventricular tachycardia was documented, and an implantable cardioverter-defibrillator was placed for primary prevention.DiscussionThis case illustrates a rare, high-risk hypertrophic cardiomyopathy phenotype in which persistent ST-segment elevation mimics acute myocardial infarction.Take-Home MessagesMidventricular hypertrophic cardiomyopathy with apical aneurysm can present with persistent ST-segment elevation and T-wave inversion, mimicking myocardial infarction. Electrocardiography is a valuable diagnostic and surveillance tool in this phenotype, as characteristic ST-segment abnormalities may precede or complement imaging findings.

History of Presentation

A 62-year-old woman presented to the emergency department with an episode of precordial chest pain. Additionally, she reported exertional dyspnea and occasional palpitations but denied syncope or typical exertional angina. Her functional status corresponded to NYHA functional class II. Physical examination revealed a hemodynamically stable patient with normal and clearly audible first and second heart sounds, a grade 2/6 systolic murmur, and absence of an S4 gallop. No clinical signs of heart failure were detected.

Electrocardiography demonstrated ST-segment elevation in the inferior leads II, III, and aVF, as well as in the anterior precordial leads V3-V6, all accompanied by negative T-wave inversion (Figure 1). Past Medical History

The patient's medical history was notable for arterial hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, and active smoking. Chronic medications included metoprolol, enalapril, fenofibrate, metformin, omeprazole, and long-acting insulin glargine. There was no family history of sudden cardiac death (SCD).

Differential Diagnosis

Given the presentation with chest pain, widespread ST-segment elevation involving both inferior and anterior leads, and elevated cardiac biomarkers, the differential diagnosis included acute coronary syndrome, Takotsubo cardiomyopathy, myocarditis, and midventricular obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) with apical aneurysm.1

Investigations

Transthoracic echocardiography revealed marked hypertrophy of the midventricular segments of the left ventricle (maximum wall thickness: 15 mm), with systolic midcavity obliteration and a dynamic mid- to late-systolic mean intraventricular gradient of 20 mm Hg (Figure 2), findings characteristic of midventricular obstructive HCM.1,2Figure 2Continuous Wave Doppler Showed a Dynamic Mid- to Late-Systolic Mean Intraventricular Gradient of 20 mm Hg

A left ventricular apical aneurysm was identified using contrast enhancement (Video 1). The patient underwent urgent coronary angiography, which revealed no hemodynamically significant coronary artery stenosis. Cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) confirmed midventricular hypertrophy and apical aneurysm formation (Figure 3) and demonstrated midwall late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) within the hypertrophied segments, consistent with myocardial fibrosis. Ambulatory 24-hour Holter monitoring documented an episode of nonsustained ventricular tachycardia (Figure 4). Management

Although the estimated 5-year risk of SCD calculated using the HCM Risk-SCD model was relatively low (2.5%), this value did not fully capture the patient's overall risk profile.1,2 Current American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology guidelines emphasize that risk assessment in HCM should extend beyond prediction models and incorporate key clinical and imaging features.3

Although the precise extent of LGE was not formally quantified as a percentage of left ventricular mass, its presence within hypertrophied segments further contributed to arrhythmic risk assessment. In addition, the presence of a left ventricular apical aneurysm and the documentation of nonsustained ventricular tachycardia significantly increased concern for malignant ventricular arrhythmias. After shared decision-making and careful consideration of the patient's risk profile, a permanent transvenous implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) was placed for primary prevention of SCD.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient remained clinically stable after ICD implantation, with no recurrence of chest pain or documented arrhythmic events during hospitalization. She was discharged with plans for close outpatient follow-up. Given the inherited nature of HCM, first-degree relatives were counseled and enrolled in ongoing clinical surveillance, including serial clinical evaluation and cardiac screening. A detailed family pedigree was obtained to support longitudinal risk assessment and guide continued family screening.

Discussion

ST-segment elevation on electrocardiography is classically associated with acute transmural myocardial ischemia due to epicardial coronary artery occlusion, and it remains central to the diagnosis of ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction.1 However, when clinical presentation, biomarker kinetics, or coronary angiography findings are discordant, alternative nonischemic etiologies must be considered. The early troponin profile in our patient also contributed to diagnostic clarification. Although high-sensitivity troponin I was elevated at presentation, the 1-hour increment was modest (300-329 pg/mL), without the marked early rise typically observed in acute coronary occlusion due to plaque rupture.1 Such a pattern is more consistent with chronic or subacute myocardial injury rather than acute plaque rupture with transmural ischemia. HCM represents an important nonischemic cause of ST-segment elevation, particularly in specific morphological subtypes.1,4,5

Midventricular obstructive HCM is an uncommon phenotype, accounting for approximately 3% of HCM cases.6 In this variant, myocardial thickening is localized to the midventricular region, leading to dynamic obstruction within the left ventricular cavity and substantial elevation of systolic pressures in the distal chamber. The resulting increase in mechanical load and stress at the apex contributes to a sustained mismatch between myocardial oxygen delivery and metabolic demand.7

The condition is further worsened by compromised coronary perfusion resulting from extravascular compression of intramyocardial vessels, which promotes repetitive ischemia, microvascular dysfunction, fibrotic remodeling, and ventricular structural deterioration. Coronary artery spasm has additionally been suggested as a contributing factor in apical aneurysm development.7

Left ventricular apical aneurysm is now recognized as a marker of adverse prognosis in HCM and is associated with ventricular arrhythmias, thromboembolic events, and SCD.1, 2, 3 Persistent ST-segment elevation—particularly in the anterior precordial leads and often accompanied by T-wave inversion—represents a characteristic electrocardiographic pattern in HCM patients with apical aneurysm and may precede definitive imaging diagnosis.8

In the present case, widespread ST-segment elevation involving both inferior and anterior leads closely mimicked an extensive acute myocardial infarction and appropriately prompted urgent invasive evaluation. The absence of obstructive coronary artery disease, together with persistent ST-segment elevation and negative T waves, directed attention toward an underlying structural cardiomyopathy and highlighted the diagnostic value of electrocardiography beyond its traditional role in acute coronary syndromes.

Risk stratification in HCM requires a comprehensive and longitudinal approach that integrates clinical history, ambulatory rhythm monitoring, and multimodality imaging.2 Although the calculated 5-year risk of SCD in this patient was low (2.5%), current prediction models do not incorporate several emerging high-risk markers, as highlighted in the recent American Heart Association guidelines, including systolic dysfunction, apical aneurysm, and myocardial fibrosis detected by LGE.2,3

CMR is particularly valuable when conventional assessment does not clearly define risk. In addition to providing more precise measurements of left ventricular hypertrophy, CMR can identify apical aneurysms and quantify LGE, reflecting myocardial fibrosis.2

Consequently, exclusive reliance on the numerical risk estimate may underestimate the true arrhythmic susceptibility in selected patients. Importantly, this case highlights the discordance between a low calculated HCM Risk-SCD score and the presence of high-risk imaging markers, reinforcing the need for individualized risk assessment beyond prediction models. In this context, the coexistence of an apical aneurysm, documented nonsustained ventricular tachycardia, and CMR-demonstrated fibrosis supported the decision for primary prevention ICD implantation following shared decision-making.

Conclusions

Midventricular obstructive HCM with apical aneurysm represents a distinct and clinically significant phenotype that may present with widespread ST-segment elevation, mimicking acute myocardial infarction.
