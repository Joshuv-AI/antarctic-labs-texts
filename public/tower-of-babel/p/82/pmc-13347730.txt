Ventilator-Associated Pneumonia in Newborns: Distribution of Causative Agents and Antibiogram
Authors: Zeynep Uze Okay, Cansu Tatar Atamanalp, Berker Okay, Burcu Cebeci, Dilek Kurnaz, Hakan Cakir, Derya Buyukkayhan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesTo characterize pathogen distribution and antibiograms in neonatal ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) and to examine clinical correlates of recurrent growth and colonization.MethodsThis was a single-center retrospective cohort study conducted between September 1, 2020, and September 1, 2025. Among 152 neonates with tracheal aspirate culture (TAC) growth, 61 met the VAP criteria; 48 with complete clinical and antibiogram data were analyzed. Recurrent growth was defined as a second positive TAC with a different species obtained at another time point, and colonization was defined as the same organism in ≥3 consecutive TACs. Demographics, inflammatory biomarkers, blood cultures, and EUCAST susceptibilities were recorded. Group comparisons were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test and Fisher’s exact test; temporal susceptibility trends were assessed using Spearman’s rho.ResultsGram-negative organisms predominated. The most frequent first TAC isolates were Klebsiella pneumoniae (33.3%) and Escherichia coli (18.8%); K. pneumoniae also predominated among second isolates (45.0%). Blood cultures were positive in 33.3% of cases, with 37.5% TAC–blood species concordance. Compared with non-gram-negative cases, gram-negative infections had higher C-reactive protein levels (7.9 vs. 1.1 mg/L; p=0.029) and procalcitonin levels (3.13 vs. 0.22 ng/mL; p=0.045) but a lower neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (0.93 vs. 2.37; p=0.017). NICU length of stay was longer in patients with gram-negative growth (63.0 [29.0–111.5] vs. 17.5 [10.>25–45.75] days; p=0.023); the Hodges–Lehmann median difference was +39.5 days (95% CI +13.0 to +69.0), with a moderate effect size (Cliff’s δ=0.36). Recurrent growth occurred in 41.7% of cases and was associated with a longer intubation-to-growth interval (12.0 [5.0–15.75] vs. 4.5 [3.0–8.25] days; p=0.002) and colonization (p=0.002). Susceptibility to amikacin, colistin, and tigecycline was relatively preserved, whereas resistance to third- and fourth-generation cephalosporins, piperacillin–tazobactam, carbapenems, and fluoroquinolones was common. Overall susceptibility declined over the years (ρ=−0.95; p=0.001). In multivariable logistic regression, no variable independently predicted adverse prognosis.ConclusionIn our tertiary NICU, neonatal VAP is predominantly caused by multidrug-resistant gram-negative organisms, particularly Klebsiella pneumoniae and Escherichia coli. These infections are associated with higher inflammatory markers and prolonged hospitalization. Recurrent growth, defined as the isolation of a different species at another time point, highlights the role of airway hygiene and adherence to ventilator bundle practices. Given the decreasing susceptibility trends, maintaining the efficacy of agents such as amikacin, colistin, and tigecycline through rational antibiotic use is crucial.

Mechanical ventilation is an indispensable component of modern neonatal intensive care units (NICUs), improving survival among preterm and critically ill neonates.[1] However, this lifesaving intervention also carries the risk of serious complications, such as ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP).[2] VAP is typically defined as a lower respiratory tract infection occurring at least 48 hours after the initiation of mechanical ventilation.[1,3] It is associated with substantial morbidity and mortality, particularly in preterm and low-birth-weight infants whose immune systems are not yet fully mature.[4] VAP also significantly increases NICU length of stay (LOS), the use of broad-spectrum antibiotics, and health-care costs.[1,2]

The distribution of pathogens causing VAP in neonates and their antibiotic resistance profiles can vary across centers and over time within the same center. [1,4] In recent years, the rise in VAP cases due to multidrug-resistant (MDR) gram-negative (GN) bacteria, such as Klebsiella pneumoniae, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Acinetobacter baumannii, has become a major global threat to NICUs.[1,4,5] These resistant microorganisms diminish the effectiveness of empirical antibiotic therapy, complicate clinical management, and may adversely affect neonatal outcomes.[4]

Diagnosing VAP in neonates is challenging because clinical and radiographic findings are nonspecific. Although biomarkers such as C-reactive protein (CRP) and procalcitonin (PCT) are helpful, they do not provide a definitive diagnosis on their own.[6,7] Contemporary studies from 2024–2025 highlight lung ultrasound as a complementary tool for early diagnosis and monitoring in suspected VAP; however, its use in practice remains limited and operator-dependent.[6,8]

The 2024 update from the Internation-al Society for Infectious Diseases recommends standardizing core practices such as head-of-bed elevation, optimization of sedation, daily assessments of readiness for ventilator weaning, oral care, hand hygiene, and antibiotic stewardship.[2,9] In this context, regular monitoring of local surveillance data in each NICU—identifying the most frequent pathogens and their antimicrobial susceptibility patterns—enables evidence-based updates to empirical treatment protocols.[1,2] In tertiary NICUs, continuous monitoring of local antibiograms is particularly important because resistance patterns may vary between hospitals and over time. Regular evaluation of these trends allows timely updates to empirical treatment strategies and supports antibiotic stewardship efforts within each unit.

The aim of this study was to evaluate the demographic characteristics, causative microorganism profiles, antibiotic susceptibility rates, and clinical outcomes of neonates diagnosed with VAP in a tertiary-level NICU. Our hypothesis was that, in our unit, VAP caused by MDR GN bacteria was associated with a more pronounced inflammatory response, prolonged LOS, and the need for broader-spectrum antibiotic therapy.

Methods

Study Design and Sample

This was a retrospective cohort study conducted in a tertiary-level NICU. The study was conducted in a tertiary-level, level IIIb neonatal intensive care unit with a total capacity of 24 incubators (12 level III and 12 level II beds). All cases in which growth was detected on TAC in the NICU between September 1, 2020, and September 1, 2025, were screened.

During the study period, a standard ventilator care bundle was implemented in the NICU, including head-of-bed elevation, oral care, hand hygiene, and daily weaning assessment; however, formal compliance monitoring was not routinely recorded.

A total of 152 neonates with positive TAC results were identified during the screening period. Based on clinical, radiologic, and laboratory data, 61 cases met the diagnostic criteria for VAP and were included in the study cohort. Cases with missing clinical records, antibiograms, and/or key outcome variables were excluded, yielding a final analytic sample of 48 patients.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (i) management under mechanical ventilation; (ii) a clinical picture consistent with VAP developing ≥48 hours after endotracheal intubation; (iii) growth of at least one pathogen on TAC at the time of VAP evaluation; and (iv) follow-up within the NICU.

Exclusion criteria were as follows: (i) lower respiratory tract infection other than VAP; (ii) mechanical ventilation for <48 hours; (iii) critical missing data in key variables, such as cultures reported only as “growth present” or “gram-positive cocci observed” without organism identification, or cultures without an antibiogram; and (iv) improper collection and/or storage of TAC specimens.

Data Sources and Variables

Sources: Data were obtained from the electronic hospital information system (HBYS), nurse and physician observation forms, and the laboratory information system.

Demographic/clinical variables: Sex, nationality, gestational age (weeks), birth weight (g), postnatal day at TAC sampling, postnatal age at intubation, time from intubation to culture positivity (days), total NICU LOS (days), and prognosis (discharged, transferred, or deceased) were recorded.

Tracheal aspirate sampling procedure: All tracheal aspirate samples were collected by attending physicians with the assistance of NICU nurses under sterile conditions using a closed suction system. Sampling was performed in the morning before routine suctioning. A sterile suction catheter was gently advanced through the endotracheal tube, and secretions were aspirated into a sterile mucus trap without saline instillation. Samples were delivered to the microbiology laboratory within 30 minutes for culture and susceptibility testing.

Microbiologic variables: The first isolated microorganism (first isolate), blood culture result and TAC–blood culture concordance (yes/no), second isolate, recurrent isolation, and colonization were recorded.

Treatment variables: Pre-culture initial antibiotic therapy (empirical), second pre-culture therapy, if any, and final therapy after culture and antibiogram results were recorded.

Laboratory variables: Complete blood count parameters, including leukocytes, hemoglobin, hematocrit, platelets, neutrophils, and lymphocytes; CRP; PCT; blood gas parameters, including pH, pCO2, pO2, bicarbonate, and base excess; and biochemical parameters, including sodium, potassium, chloride, calcium, glucose, and lactate, were recorded.

The neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) was calculated as the absolute neutrophil count divided by the absolute lymphocyte count.

Cases with missing critical variables, such as incomplete clinical records, missing antibiogram data, or unverified outcomes, were excluded from the analysis. For included cases, occasional missing laboratory values were handled by pairwise deletion without imputation.

Definitions

VAP: VAP was defined as a condition developing ≥48 hours after the initiation of endotracheal intubation, characterized by a new or progressive pulmonary infiltrate plus growth on TAC and at least two of the following: episodes of tachypnea, bradycardia, or apnea; increased volume or purulence of tracheal secretions; increased oxygen requirement; and/or impaired gas exchange.[1,3]

First isolate: Growth of a pathogen on the first TAC obtained during VAP evaluation.

Second isolate: The next positive TAC obtained at a different time point, yielding a species different from the first isolate.

Recurrent growth: Recurrent growth was defined as a second positive tracheal aspirate culture yielding a different bacterial species obtained at a distinct time point during the same mechanical ventilation episode.[10]

Colonization: Colonization was defined as the detection of the same microorganism in ≥3 consecutive TAC samples obtained ≥7 days apart.[10,11]

GN classification: The following microorganisms were considered GN: Klebsiella spp., Escherichia coli, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Enterobacter spp., Acinetobacter spp., Serratia spp., Stenotrophomonas maltophilia, and Proteus, Citrobacter, Morganella, and Haemophilus species; all others were classified as non-GN.

Pre-culture initial antibiotic therapy: The empirical regimen started for suspected VAP before culture results became available.

Final therapy: The targeted regimen guided by culture and antibiogram results.

Adverse prognosis: Adverse prognosis was defined as death or transfer to the pediatric ICU/palliative care, that is, any patient who was not discharged.

MDR definition: According to the Magiorakos criteria, resistance to ≥3 antimicrobial classes was flagged as an “MDR proxy,” mapped to the antibiotic-class panel used in this study. [5] MDR classification was applied per isolate based on the resistance pattern of each cultured organism.

TAC–blood culture concordance: A species-level match within the same episode was considered “concordant.”

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics are presented as mean±standard deviation or median (interquartile range [IQR]) for continuous variables and as number (n) and percentage (%) for categorical variables. Normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Between-group comparisons for two independent, non-normally distributed samples were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Associations between variables were examined using Spearman’s rank correlation. Factors associated with an adverse prognosis were evaluated using multivariable logistic regression. Statistical significance was set at p<0.05 (two-sided). For major proportions, 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using the Wilson score method. For length of stay, effect size was summarized as the Hodges–Lehmann median difference with a 95% confidence interval (bootstrap, 5,000 resamples); for ordinal effects, Cliff’s δ was reported.

Ethics Statement

The institutional ethics committee approved the study in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethics approval was obtained from the institutional review board before the conduct of the research (approval No.: 197/2025; date: October 1, 2025). Owing to the retrospective design, informed consent was not required.

Results

Patient and Specimen Characteristics

A total of 48 neonates with VAP were included. Twenty-nine (60.4%) were male. Thirty-three (68.8%) were citizens of the Republic of Türkiye, while the remaining 15 (31.2%) were migrants. The mean gestational age was 31.71±5.07 weeks, and the median birth weight was 1407.50 g (IQR 1009.50–2811.25). The median postnatal day at the time of positive TAC was 10 (IQR 5.0–16.25). The median interval from intubation to culture positivity was 6.5 days (IQR 4.0–13.0). Isolation of different microorganisms at two distinct time points occurred in 41.7% of patients. Colonization was observed in 9 patients (18.8%).

Microbiological Findings

First isolates, ordered by frequency, were as follows: Klebsiella pneumoniae, 16 (33.3%); Escherichia coli, 9 (18.8%); Pseudomonas aeruginosa, 4 (8.3%); Enterobacter aerogenes (currently classified as Klebsiella aerogenes), 4 (8.3%); and, each in 2 (4.2%) cases, Acinetobacter baumannii, Serratia marcescens, methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), coagulase-negative staphylococci (CoNS), Streptococcus agalactiae, and Staphylococcus haemolyticus. Single cases (1 [2.1%] each) of Haemophilus influenzae, Streptococcus pyogenes, and alpha-hemolytic streptococcus were identified.

Second isolates: Klebsiella pneumoniae, 9 (45.0%); Escherichia coli, 4 (20.0%); Stenotrophomonas maltophilia, 4 (20.0%); Enterobacter aerogenes, 2 (10.0%); and MRSA, 1 (5.0%).

Blood cultures: Growth was detected in 16/48 patients. The most common organism was Klebsiella pneumoniae (n=6), followed by methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus epidermidis (MRSE; n=3), methicillin-susceptible Staphylococcus epidermidis (MSSE; n=2), Enterococcus faecium (n=2), and single cases of Escherichia coli, Enterobacter aerogenes, and Staphylococcus haemolyticus. Among the 16 patients with both TAC and blood culture growth, species-level concordance was observed in 6 (37.5%).

Interval from intubation to culture positivity (J, days): J was significantly longer in patients with recurrent isolation [median 12.0 days (IQR 5.0–15.75)] than in those with a single isolation (median 4.5 days [IQR 3.0–8.25]; Mann–Whitney U test, p=0.002). There was no significant difference in J according to colonization status (p>0.05). However, recurrent isolation and colonization were strongly associated (Fisher’s exact test, p=0.002).

Interpretation of Antibiograms

Among GN organisms, particularly Klebsiella pneumoniae, Escherichia coli, and Enterobacter spp., susceptibility to amikacin, colistin, and tigecycline was high, whereas widespread resistance to third- and fourth-generation cephalosporins, piperacillin–tazobactam, carbapenems, and fluoroquinolones was observed. For Acinetobacter baumannii and Pseudomonas aeruginosa, colistin susceptibility remained comparatively well preserved relative to other agents. In MRSA/MRSE isolates, susceptibility to vancomycin and linezolid was 100%. Treatment and Prognosis

Neonates with GN isolates had a NICU LOS of 63.0 days (IQR 29.0–111.5), whereas those with non-GN isolates had a NICU LOS of 17.5 days (IQR 10.25–45.75); the difference was statistically significant (Mann–Whitney U test, p=0.023). The Hodges–Lehmann median difference was +39.5 days (95% CI: +13.0 to +69.0), and the effect size was Cliff’s δ=0.36 (moderate) (Fig. 3). For the entire cohort, the median length of stay was 51.5 days (IQR 23.0–108.5).

Discussion

Our findings reaffirm the predominance of GN pathogens and their association with prolonged hospitalization, while also demonstrating a temporal decline in overall susceptibility and relatively preserved activity for agents such as amikacin, colistin, and tigecycline. The dominance of MDR GN bacteria, particularly Klebsiella pneumoniae and Escherichia coli, as VAP etiologies aligns with reports from many centers in Türkiye and worldwide, where GN enteric bacilli constitute the main component of the NICU VAP flora.[1,4,12] The notably high proportion of Klebsiella pneumoniae in both first and second isolates underscores the endemic and persistent nature of this pathogen in our unit. In our cohort, higher CRP/PCT and lower NLR in GN infections suggest that systemic inflammatory responses in neonatal VAP may be heterogeneous and exhibit age-specific patterns.[1,4]

The most concerning finding of our study is the widespread antibiotic resistance among GN pathogens. We observed high resistance rates to broad-spectrum agents, including third- and fourth-generation cephalosporins, piperacillin–tazobactam, and especially carbapenems. The significant year-to-year decline in overall susceptibility indicates that this resistance escalation is a dynamic and ongoing threat. Carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae are a major problem in NICUs worldwide and are often associated with limited therapeutic options.[13,14] By contrast, the relative preservation of susceptibility to amikacin, colistin, and tigecycline in our cohort suggests that these agents remain viable options for MDR GN infections. Nevertheless, indiscriminate use of these “last-line” antibiotics could accelerate resistance and further narrow future treatment choices.[15] Accordingly, empirical therapy protocols must be tailored to local resistance data.[2,9] Emerging evidence also suggests that quality-improvement initiatives targeting >90% compliance with ventilator care bundles in selected NICUs can reduce VAP rates.[16]

Although tigecycline susceptibility appeared relatively preserved in our isolates, tigecycline use in neonates is off-label and should be approached with caution. Tigecycline carries a boxed warning for increased all-cause mortality and is generally recommended to be reserved for situations in which alternative treatments are not suitable.[17,18] Furthermore, regulatory product information does not recommend its use in younger children (e.g., those younger than 8 years) because of safety concerns, underscoring that, in the neonatal setting, tigecycline should be considered only as salvage therapy after multidisciplinary evaluation and when no safer effective alternatives exist.[19]

From a clinical standpoint, the association between GN etiologies and prolonged length of stay, along with the absence of a clear difference in adverse prognosis, is consistent with the heterogeneous findings in the literature.[1,4] VAP has been reported to increase antibiotic exposure and costs, with potential long-term effects on survival and neurodevelopment.[20] Our observation of extended hospitalization aligns with the concept that GN bacteria, particularly owing to their lipopolysaccharide structures, elicit a more potent pro-inflammatory response, contributing to a more severe clinical course.[21] In keeping with this hypothesis, inflammatory markers such as CRP and PCT were significantly higher in the GN VAP group in our study. The tendency for PCT to rise more prominently in GN bacterial infections has been demonstrated previously and may provide an early clue to the pathogen class.[22] Conversely, the lower NLR observed in the GN group could reflect neutropenia or increased neutrophil apoptosis seen in some cases of severe sepsis; however, this finding warrants further investigation before firm conclusions can be drawn.[23]

In our study, a longer interval from intubation to the development of VAP emerged as a risk factor for recurrent isolation. This suggests that prolonged mechanical ventilation increases the likelihood of colonization, either by the patient’s endogenous flora or by hospital-acquired pathogens, which may subsequently progress to infection.[24] The strong statistical association observed between recurrent isolation and colonization further supports this hypothesis. Biofilm formation on the endotracheal tube can serve as a reservoir for microorganisms, diminishing antibiotic efficacy and predisposing to relapse.[25] Collectively, these findings underscore the importance of airway hygiene, ventilator circuit management, and aspiration strategies, highlighting the need for standardization and oversight of care processes such as aspiration techniques, secretion management, and circuit change intervals.[1,2]

As a diagnostic challenge, only one-third of our patients with VAP had positive blood cultures, and species concordance between TAC and blood culture was as low as 37.5%. This finding indicates that VAP does not invariably result in bacteremia and that the infection may remain largely confined to the lungs. Although this low concordance underscores the diagnostic value of TAC in VAP, it also reminds us that results should be interpreted with caution, given the possibility of contamination.[26] Future studies integrating molecular diagnostic tools, such as rapid multiplex polymerase chain reaction or metagenomic sequencing, may help improve pathogen detection accuracy and guide earlier targeted therapy in neonatal VAP.

Our study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design may introduce missing data and unmeasured confounding, and the single-center setting limits generalizability. Second, neonatal VAP diagnosis is inherently challenging; reliance on clinical and radiographic criteria together with TAC growth may lead to misclassification, and TAC results can be influenced by colonization or contamination because quantitative lower-airway sampling was not routinely available. Third, molecular typing was not performed, precluding assessment of clonal transmission and outbreak dynamics. Finally, although a ventilator care bundle was implemented, formal compliance monitoring data were not available; therefore, we could not evaluate bundle adherence as a determinant of VAP-related outcomes. The modest sample size and limited number of adverse outcomes also reduce the statistical power for multivariable outcome modeling; thus, negative findings in regression analyses should be interpreted cautiously.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we found that neonatal VAP episodes in our unit were predominantly caused by MDR gram-negative organisms and were associated with prolonged NICU hospitalization, while overall antimicrobial susceptibility declined over the study period, underscoring the need for continuous local antibiogram surveillance and regular updates of empirical protocols. Empirical therapy should be guided by local flora and susceptibility patterns and the individual risk profile and should be promptly optimized and de-escalated once culture results become available. Antimicrobial stewardship should prioritize early culture-guided adjustment, judicious use of agents with relatively preserved activity, such as amikacin and colistin, and strict restriction of tigecycline, given its boxed warning and off-label status in neonates, to salvage therapy only when no safer effective alternatives exist. Finally, the observed association between longer intubation duration and recurrent infections supports early weaning strategies and robust infection-prevention measures to mitigate the VAP burden.
