Behavioural responses of Neotropical raptors to drone approaches to nests
Authors: Francisca Helena Aguiar-Silva, Tiago Guimarães Junqueira, Tomás Rivas-Fuenzalida, Santiago Zuluaga, Sebastian Kohn, Juan Sebastián Restrepo-Cardona, Pilar Alexander Blanco-Márquez, Mateo Giraldo-Amaya, Tânia Margarete Sanaiotti, Aureo Banhos, Carlos Augusto Tuyama, Katherine Burgos-Andrade, Daniel Orizano, Fabricio Narváez, Manuel Encabo, Gustavo Diniz Mendes de Carvalho, Karla Aparicio
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We analysed behavioural responses of 27 Neotropical raptor species to 227 drone flights conducted near 119 active nests across eight countries. Reactions occurred in 81% of species and were strongly influenced by ecological traits, breeding stage, and drone flight characteristics. Defensive displays were the most frequent response, whereas attacks and escape behaviours were rare and associated with close approaches, prolonged flights, and overhead approaches. Smaller, more agile species tended to attack, whereas larger eagles usually avoided confrontation. Repeated exposures increased the probability of a reaction, suggesting sensitization rather than habituation. Responses intensified during incubation and brood-rearing, identifying these periods as critical periods for ethical and methodological caution. Our results provide a multi-species framework for assessing drone impacts on Neotropical raptors and define distance, approach, and timing thresholds to minimise disturbance. Incorporating behavioural ecology into flight planning will help ensure that drone-based monitoring remains an effective and non-invasive tool for raptor monitoring and conservation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-47248-5.

Introduction

With technological advances over the last century, several devices, including drones, have been introduced into natural environments for a wide range of purposes1–5. As these devices are integrated into natural settings, wildlife increasingly interacts with these artificial elements6, which often become a frequent component in the habitat. This can directly or indirectly induce behavioural changes and adaptations in species, potentially affecting population dynamics7. Such increasing exposure in recent years has in turn raised ethical, environmental, and security concerns1,8–11.

Drones, or unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs), have become popular and are regularly used in wildlife research worldwide6,12–14. Drones are practical tools with relatively low operating costs15,16. They help increase access to remote sites and reduce the time required to inspect nest contents, assess breeding status, and monitor phenology in cliff- and tree-nesting bird species17–22. However, the use of drones in wildlife studies has also been associated with disturbance, particularly in bird management6,23–27.

Raptors may respond to aerial devices as if they were flying predators or competitors for food17,28,29, triggering stress or defensive behaviours. Such responses can lead to negative consequences when drones are used to monitor breeding sites, including nestling abandonment9,30. In large eagles, falls from nests can occur naturally, as documented for the Harpy Eagle (Harpia harpyja)31; however, disturbances caused by drone approaches can increase the risk of such events, potentially increasing mortality. Other larger eagles, such as the Bald Eagle (Haliaeetus leucocephalus) and the Spanish Imperial Eagle (Aquila adalberti), have been observed damaging eggs or injuring their young when adults abruptly leave the nest in response to perceived threats32,33. Furthermore, direct interactions with aerial devices may cause physical harm to animals25,34 or interfere with their normal activities35,36. These risks highlight the need for careful evaluation of the use of human-made devices in natural environments, which are driving new human-raptor interactions in the airspace (i.e., drone-raptor interactions)8, particularly given the ecological importance of flying wildlife and the growing recognition of aerial habitats as critical components of ecosystems37. Thus, it is essential to identify potential impacts on wildlife to implement best practices that minimise disturbance and reduce bias in behavioural data18,24,38–41.

Several studies have recommended precautionary measures during drone operations, including maintaining safe distances from nests, minimising flight duration, and adopting protocols tailored to species sensitivity and breeding stage for the Osprey (Pandion haliaetus), Turkey Vulture (Cathartes aura), and Griffon Vulture (Gyps fulvus), in North America, Europe, and Asia24,34,38. However, no systematic studies have assessed the behavioural responses of Neotropical raptors to drones, despite their ecological and cultural importance and conservation concerns42,43. To address this gap, this study aims: (i) to advance in our understanding of behavioural responses to drone approaches in Neotropical raptors by systematically evaluating species-specific reactions and examining the influence of ecological traits, drone, and approach characteristics, (ii) from our resulting findings, we hope to produce a guide of recommended drone flight practices for raptor nest monitoring in an effective and safe way for raptors and researchers.

Specifically, we tested the hypothesis that raptors exhibit behavioural responses to drones near active nests, and that the intensity of these responses depends both on species traits such as age class, breeding stage, and body size, and on drone and flight characteristics. We predicted that behavioural responses would be stronger in smaller-size, more territorial species, and more frequent among adults than immatures29,44,45, reflecting nest defence behaviour46. We also expected that closer distances to the nest or the raptor, longer flight durations, higher flight frequency, and larger drone size would increase the probability of active responses. By analysing drone-raptor interactions during breeding periods across a broad Neotropical region, this study provides species-specific empirical evidence and contributes to the development of practices for the ethical and effective use of drones in raptor research and conservation.

Materials and methods

Study system

This study was conducted between 2014 and 2024 at breeding sites of raptor species located in the Neotropical region, including countries of Central (Panama) and South America (Colombia, Ecuador, Venezuela, Brazil, Peru, Chile, and Argentina) (Fig. 1). This geographical region has high species richness and is considered a priority area for raptor research and conservation42,43. The ecosystems encompassed in the study range from lowland humid tropical forests (Darien, Biogeographical Chocó, and Amazon basin) to páramos and tropical montane forests (Andean region), and austral temperate forests (Southern Andes). During drone flights, nests contained adults, eggs, and immatures at various developmental stages, including nestlings, fledglings (defined here as recently fledged individuals that remain in the area immediately surrounding the nest tree), and juveniles aged 8–24 months. In raptors, depending on species-specific developmental rates and ecological factors, this early post-fledging stage typically lasts from a few days in small falcons to several months in large eagles.

Drone flight details

Rotary-wing drones equipped with onboard cameras were flown over the nests of 27 raptor species between 2014 and 2024. Flights were conducted to inspect nest contents, monitor active nests throughout the breeding cycle, measure nest height and nest-tree height, and estimate nestling age. For certain species with multiple known nests, repeated flights (ranging from 2 to 12 per nest) were conducted on different days to monitor the progression of the breeding cycle. Additionally, some flights were conducted to locate access routes to Andean Condor (Vultur gryphus) nests and to count condors and map roosting aggregations, particularly in inaccessible cliff areas. As a secondary objective, we used some drone flights to document behavioural responses to drone approaches.

Drone flights were conducted according to a standardized protocol by skilled researchers, with an observer recording the birds’ responses in the nests and their surroundings. These were conducted at speeds ranging from 2 to 20 m/s, depending on the drone model, which varied in noise levels and size from 119 g (Syma x5sw) to 4.75 kg (Matrice 200) (Supplementary Table S1). All flights included in this study were conducted under suitable weather conditions (i.e., low wind and no precipitation) and during daylight hours, from 6:00 to 17:55. The variation in timing was due to logistical constraints, as accessing some breeding areas requires several hours of travel.

Drone and approach characteristics included drone mass (as specified by the manufacturer), flight height, distance to both the nest and to the raptor, approach type, and flight duration, all recorded by the drone during its approach to the nests. Because the nest types occur at different heights and in environments with differing vertical and structural characteristics, certain flight parameters, such as drone height and approach trajectory, varied accordingly but were consistently recorded. Flight height was the vertical distance (m) from the drone to the ground below its position, representing height above ground level (AGL).

During all flights, photos and videos were captured while the drone followed diagonal, horizontal, vertical (ascending), or overhead (descending) approach trajectories relative to the nest. Recording began at the drone’s take-off and continued until the raptor or nest came into view. The choice of approach trajectory was based on the nest exposure, topography, forest structure, and nest height. To assess the influence of nest exposure on raptor responses to drone approaches, nest sites were categorized into two main types: open (e.g., cliffs, cliff ledges, vegetation platforms on cliffs, and cavities beneath cliff ledges) and forest (e.g., nests on tree platforms or within cavities located in the subcanopy, canopy, or emergent trees).

Following the initiation of each flight, the distance to the nest and to the raptor, the approach trajectory, and the timing of recorded behavioural responses were documented. After completing the objective, the drone flown away from the nest and returned to the pilot. However, if an active behavioural response from the raptor was observed during the approach, the drone’s advance toward the nest was immediately halted, even if the intended flight objective had not yet been met. The total time elapsed between drone take-off and the onset of the behavioural response was then recorded.

Biological and reproductive characteristics of raptors

The parameters measured included biological and reproductive traits, such as body mass and reproductive periodicity, obtained from the published literature47–53, and clutch size, breeding cycle stage, number of adults and immatures present at the nest, and nest position height, which were recorded during field observations.

We analysed data from Neotropical raptors spanning a wide size range, from 61 g in the Tiny Hawk (Microspizias superciliosus)47 to 12 kg in the Andean Condor49(Fig. 2). For most raptor species, female body mass was considered since they typically spend more time in the nest and its surrounding trees during nest-building, incubation, and the early months of parental care for the nestling54,55.

The breeding cycle of raptors was categorised into three stages: (i) pre-laying, including nest building and courtship, (ii) incubation, which begins with the laying of the first egg, depending on the species, and (iii) brood-rearing, defined as the period from hatching until juvenile dispersion, during which parental care is provided56,57. The brood-rearing stage included the nestling, fledgling, and juvenile phases. In this study, the intercycle was defined as the period between juvenile dispersal and the onset of the next breeding season, when adults remained associated with the nest site.

Behavioural responses

A binary classification was employed to distinguish between ‘response’ and ‘no response’ of raptors to drone approaches to nests. The active behavioural responses were further categorised into ‘alert’, ‘defensive’, ‘attack’, and ‘escape’. The alert response represented the raptor reaction characterised by heightened attention and vigilance. Birds exhibited behaviours such as upright posture and standing on the edge of the nest. Additionally, birds engaged in head movements, turning in various directions to survey the surroundings and identify potential threats. The defensive response involved alarm calls, ruffling feathers, opening, and raising wings; indicating a defensive stance against the perceived threat and signalling readiness to react. In instances of an attack response, birds actively pursued the drone, displaying aggressive behaviours. While for escape, the birds flew away from the nest. The response frequency was calculated considering the number of responses per species per number of flights.

The distance between the drone and the raptor in the nest at the onset of a behavioural response was calculated using the drones GPS coordinates, combined with a visual assessment of the raptor’s position. This measure was defined as the response-initiation distance (RID), while the distance between the drone and the raptor when the bird initiated an escape (i.e., left the nest) was defined as the flight-initiation distance (FID)58.

Aggressiveness, agility, and risk

We calculated two indices and a derived risk level to quantify raptor responses (see Supplementary Methods): (i) Aggressiveness Index (AGI) based on the frequency and intensity of defensive and attack behaviours, (ii) Agility Index integrating response latency and body mass, and (iii) Risk level derived from both indices to guide disturbance mitigation thresholds. Each index was classified into low, medium, and high categories using predefined thresholds (see Supplementary Methods).

Statistical analyses

Flights where the birds were not present in the nests, or the surrounding area (n = 49) were excluded from the statistical analysis as they provided no information on raptors’ reactions. To test for differences in the distribution of behavioural response types across breeding stages, we applied a chi-square test of independence. As the assumption of minimum expected frequencies (> 5) was not met, we used a Monte Carlo simulation with 10,000 replicates to estimate the p-value.

We employed Generalised Linear Models (GLM) to investigate raptor behavioural responses to drone approaches. A binary logistic regression assessed whether any response occurred (yes vs. no), whereas a multinomial logistic regression examined which factors influenced the specific type of response (none, alert, defensive, attack, or escape). This dual approach provided both general and detailed insights into raptor-drone interactions. In the multinomial model, ‘none’ was set as the reference category.

The following predictor variables were used: (1) raptor body mass; (2) average reproductive periodicity (annual, biennial, or triennial); (3) breeding stage; (4) number of adults and (5) immatures at the nest; (6) nest height; (7) drone flight height; (8) distance between the drone and the nest; (9) approach trajectory relative to nest (diagonal, horizontal, vertical ascending from below, or overhead descending from above); (10) flight duration; (11) drone size; (12) drone speed; (13) noise levels (decibels; dB); and (14) drone colour (dark or light). To represent drone size, we calculated a Drone Size Index (DSI), as the product of the drone’s diameter (D in cm) squared and its mass (M in kg): DSI = D2 x M. The DSI was standardised using a z-transformation to obtain a Scaled Drone Size Index (Supplementary Methods), which was included as a continuous predictor in the statistical models. This index serves as a proxy for drone size as perceived by the birds, capturing both visual and physical dimensions. Multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF). The overall model effect was evaluated using an analysis of variance (ANOVA) with a chi-squared test to determine which factors affect raptor response. Variables were considered significant predictors if their p-value was < 0.05. Model fit was assessed using the area under the curve (AUC) and the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve for model selection59. Finally, to test whether repeated drone flights led to habituation, we modelled the probability of an active response as a function of flight order using a Generalized Linear Mixed Model (GLMM). All analyses were performed in R60.

Results

Over a 10-year period from 2014 to 2024, we conducted 227 drone flights (40 h) across eight countries in Central and South America on 119 active nests of 27 raptor species (Table 1). Of all flights, 178 (78%; 28 h) involved the presence of birds at the nests (n = 161) or nearby (n = 17). Most flights were concentrated on six species: H. harpyja (n = 64 flights; 36%), Black-and-chestnut Eagle (Spizaetus isidori) (n = 34; 19%), Black-and-white Hawk-Eagle (S. melanoleucus) (n = 32; 18%), V. gryphus (n = 22; 12%), Orange-breasted Falcon (Falco deiroleucus) (n = 14; 8%), and Crested Eagle (Morphnus guianensis) (n = 11; 6%). Drone operations did not cause mid- or long-term impacts on breeding, as 71% of all nests were monitored during subsequent breeding attempts and all of them remained active.

The drone flights were conducted during distinct stages of the breeding cycle of the studied raptors (Table 1), with 20% of flights occurring during pre-laying (Fig. 3A and C), 20% during incubation, 49% during brood-rearing (Fig. 3B, D, E, F), and 11% during the inter-cycle period. Breeding cycles were annual in 20 species (39% of flights), biennial in three (12%), and triennial in four (49%). Regarding offspring, 15 species produced a single chick per cycle, whereas 12 species produced two; the Orange-breasted Falcon (Falco deiroleucus) produced one to three offspring, usually two; the Rufous-tailed Hawk (Buteo ventralis) has been reported to produce one or two offspring per breeding cycle. Response frequencies varied among breeding stages (i.e., defensive behaviours were more frequent during incubation, whereas lack of reaction was more common during pre-laying; Fig. 4). Despite that, the overall response pattern did not differ significantly across stages (χ2 = 11.25; p = 0.18).

Overall behavioural responses

Responses to drone approaches were observed in 81% of the species surveyed, accounting for 37% of individuals (Supplementary Table S3). Excluding cases with no response (n = 64), active reactions comprised defensive displays (58%), alert signals (21%), direct drone attacks (14%), and escape behaviours (7%). Most active responses occurred within the first 5 min of drone flight: defensive and alert signals showed the highest medians (both at 5 min; range: 0.08–8 min and 1–15 min, respectively), whereas attack and escape responses occurred more rapidly (both at 2 min; range: 0.15–7 min and 2–7 min, respectively). Vocalizations occurred in 46% of active responses, with adults contributing to most cases (75%). Among the 101 individuals displaying behavioural reactions, 67% were adults, and these accounted for 71% of the 14 attacks on the drone. In 11 out of 14 attack events, flights were interrupted before contact to avoid injuries to birds and equipment damage, whereas in three cases, drones were damaged following attacks by H. harpyja (n = 2) and Ornate Hawk-Eagle (S. ornatus) (n = 1) (Figure S1).

Behavioural responses varied among the most frequently monitored species, which had the highest number of observed drone flights (Table 1). Buteo ventralis responded in 83% of drone approaches (n = 7), followed by S. melanoleucus (66%; n = 29), H. harpyja (60%; n = 45), S. isidori (57%; n = 27), M. guianensis (57%; n = 9), V. gryphus (45%; n = 13), and F. deiroleucus (33%; n = 12) (Supplementary Table S2, Fig. 5). The Greater Yellow-headed Vulture (Cathartes melambrotus), Yellow-headed Caracara (Daptrius chimachima), Plumbeous Kite (Ictinia plumbea), White-throated Hawk (B. albigula), and Rufous-legged Owl (Strix rufipes) exhibited no reaction to drone approaches, each based on a single flight (n = 1).

We observed variation in raptor responses according to drone approach characteristics and nesting context. The proportion of flights eliciting a response was highest during overhead approaches (73%; n = 19/26), followed by diagonal (63%; n = 17/27) and horizontal approaches (60%; 51/85), and lowest during vertical approaches (43%; n = 10/23). Distances at which raptors initiated behavioural responses to drone approaches varied across reproductive stages and habitat types (forest and open/rocky), ranging from 2 to 300 m (Fig. 6). During brood-rearing, responses occurred at the greatest distances, including attacks at 300 m in forest habitat for S. isidori and defensive responses up to 150 m and 100 m for B. ventralis and Black-chested Buzzard-eagle (Geranoaetus melanoleucus), respectively, in open habitats. In contrast, responses during pre-laying and incubation were generally restricted to shorter distances (< 50 m), with median values below 20 m across both habitats. Alert and defensive behaviours predominated across all stages, whereas attacks and escapes were rare and mostly associated with brood-rearing. In seven escape events involving five species (Supplementary Table S4), the birds left the nest (FID) when the drone was on average 6 m away from it, ranging from 3 m (B. ventralis) to 20 m (S. melanoleucus).

Attacks

Among the recorded attacks, drones were on average 21 m from the bird (range: 3–100 m) and 43 m from the nest (range: 2–300 m). Species involved included Bat Falcon (F. rufigularis), B. ventralis, S. melanoleucus, S. isidori, Black Hawk-Eagle (Spizaetus tyrannus), S. ornatus, H. harpyja, M. superciliosus, and White-tailed Hawk (G. albicaudatus) (Supplementary Table S4). For example, during an inspection of an H. harpyja nest to confirm chick mortality, the drone approached within 2 m of the nest tree; an adult eagle attacked when the drone reached 20 m AGL, carrying it off and releasing it 25 m away at a height of 12 m. A seven-month-old H. harpyja attacked the drone seven minutes after the drone’s take-off, from an unknown perch, when the drone was 5 m from the nest. In two other flights, an S. ornatus adult attacked the drone 8 m above its nest during an egg inspection (Figure S1), and a four-month-old S. tyrannus struck a drone 8 m from the nest, four minutes after take-off, causing it to crash on the forest floor; in these three cases, the birds emerged unexpectedly from the forest canopy and were not detected prior to the flights.

Drivers of behavioural response to drone approaches

The binary logistic model revealed a significant negative effect of the number of adults at the nest on the probability of a behavioural response (OR = 0.40, p = 0.040; Supplementary Table S3). Stage-related effects showed marginal trends, with lower response probabilities during the nestling (OR = 0.11, p = 0.086) and fledgling stages (OR = 0.07, p = 0.054) relative to the pre-laying stage. No other predictors were statistically significant (all p ≥ 0.10). This model achieved a high sensitivity (77%), indicating an ability to correctly identify cases in which a behavioural response occurred. In contrast, specificity was low (31%), reflecting a limited ability to correctly classify non-response cases. The overall accuracy was 71%, and the balanced accuracy indicated modest performance. Given that the model was designed to predict the occurrence of behavioural responses, its high sensitivity suggests it is well suited to detecting positive cases.

The multinomial model (Supplementary Table S4), which assessed specific responses relative to the reference (None), revealed complex patterns. The incubation stage was associated with reduced probabilities of alert responses (OR = 0.63, 95% CI = 0.60–0.66, p < 0.001) and lower probabilities of attack and defensive behaviours (both OR = 0.12, 95% CI = 0.11–0.12, p < 0.001). Pre-laying and nestling stages were associated with higher probabilities of escape behaviour (OR = 275.05 and 3683.30, respectively; both p < 0.001).

The drone approach trajectory was associated with variation in behavioural responses. Overhead approaches were more frequently linked to attack, defensive, and escape behaviours, whereas horizontal and diagonal approaches were more often associated with alert responses. Vertical approaches were associated with a higher of escape responses and fewer alert and defensive behaviours.

Other predictors, including brood size, number of immatures, drone size index, and flight speed, were also associated with behavioural outcomes, although the magnitude and direction of these effects varied across response categories. Some estimates were extremely large (e.g., OR > 10⁸, p < 0.001 for escape, n = 8), likely reflecting sparse data in certain categories and potential quasi-separation. Therefore, these estimates should be interpreted with caution (Supplementary Table S4).

Repeated drone flights did not lead to habituation; instead, the opposite pattern was observed. In a binomial GLMM including nest identity as a random intercept, drone flight order had a significant positive effect on the probability of a behavioural response (OR = 2.29, 95% CI = 1.31–4.91, p = 0.012), indicating that the odds of a behavioural response increased with successive flights.

Discussion

General behavioural responses of raptors to drone approaches

Our results indicate that behavioural responses of Neotropical raptors to drone approaches are shaped by ecological traits, breeding stage, and drone size and approach characteristics. Defensive displays were the most frequent behavioral response, supporting the hypothesis that many raptors perceive drones as aerial threats resembling natural predators29. Drone flights also elicited direct attacks or escape behaviours, indicating stress or agitation, consistent with previous reports of drones disrupting normal behavioural states6,17,23. Conversely, some species showed high tolerance, reacting only when drones approached within 2 m of the nest (e.g., adults and immatures of Variable Hawk G. polyosoma, and adults of Red-throated Caracara Ibycter americanus, H. harpyja, Crowned Eagle Buteogallus coronatus, B. ventralis, and Mountain Caracara Daptrius megalopterus). This tolerance may represent an adaptive strategy to minimise detection by predators or conserve energy61,62.

These patterns indicate that behavioural responses to drones may be context-dependent and vary with reproductive stage. Two attacks were recorded during incubation, involving S. ornatus and G. albicaudatus, the stage that exhibited the highest proportion of active responses (Fig. 4). This stage is particularly critical, as a single disturbance can result in clutch loss, precluding reproductive success. Such sensitivity likely reflects ecological pressures, with interspecific interactions amplifying perceived threats. However, species that developed defensive reactions during the nestling period, such as S. isidori and F. deiroleucus, can also show no reaction during incubation, even when the drone approached to within 5 m of the nest.

Ecological drivers

Active responses can also be interpreted within a trophic context, as intraguild interactions — including competition, kleptoparasitism, and even predation among raptors — strongly influence community structure, territory spacing, and responses to threats63,64. Such ecological pressures may promote heightened vigilance and aggression toward aerial intruders near nests, not only in adults defending offspring but potentially also in immature individuals raised in competitive territories. Mantling behaviour was observed during drone approaches to nests when adults were feeding their chicks, a typical response to avoid kleptoparasitism. In addition, species such as H. harpyja, S. isidori, and B. ventralis are known to prey on smaller raptors65–67, representing cases of intraguild predation63. These interactions may favour heightened vigilance and territorial aggression near nests, as individuals must detect potential competitors or predators when drones approach or hover close to the nest.

In this light, the ecological role of large raptors as superpredators64 may help explain the intensity and nature of their behavioural responses to drone approaches. Such trophic interactions may have driven the evolution of heightened vigilance and territorial aggression, enabling these species to detect and deter potential threats to offspring or nesting areas. In this context, drones may be perceived not merely as novel stimuli but as potential intruders or rival predators, eliciting alert, defensive, or aggressive behaviours. Recognizing the link between trophic position and threat perception is therefore important for understanding interspecific variation in drone responses and for developing species-specific monitoring protocols.

Spatial context also influences responses. In 64% of drone attack events, raptors were not perched on the nest but positioned at a distance from it. Additional attacks occurring away from the nest involved the Savanna Hawk (Buteogallus meridionalis) and Crested Caracara (Caracara plancus), which attacked drones while soaring in thermal currents during landscape-mapping flights, although the proximity of their nests could not be confirmed (TGJ pers. obs.). Together, these observations suggest that aggressive responses are not restricted to immediate nest defence and may also occur during routine aerial activity. Nevertheless, close approaches to the nest can still trigger strong defensive reactions even when adults are not visibly present, underscoring the need for conservative flight planning near active nests. Nest guarding is a typical behaviour among birds, including eagles, and serves to protect nestlings from potential threats, with both parents taking turns to watch the nest from a distance68.

Juveniles and fledgling responses

While defensive responses were generally expected from adults, juveniles also attacked drones (4 out of 14 events). These attacks were recorded across four species (Supplementary Table S2), involving individuals ranging from 4 to 7 months old (S. tyrannus and H. harpyja respectively) to 1-year-old (S. isidori), often in the absence of an adult or when the drone was relatively distant from the nest. Such attacks may represent play behaviour or hunting practice rather than nest defence69. Alternatively, aggressive responses by fledglings and juveniles may reflect behavioural patterns shaped by intra- or interspecific competition within the territory, particularly if individuals were raised in areas where neighbouring raptors frequently interact or attempt to attack nests. In such contexts, heightened aggression toward aerial intruders may develop early as part of territorial or anti-predator responses.

An anecdotal video recording publicly available online (e.g., YouTube: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FpDmh14VQUg) shows an adult Peregrine Falcon (Falco peregrinus) striking a drone with its talons during aerial filming, consistent with predatory or territorial behaviour, although the presence of a nearby nest is unknown. Similarly, an opportunistic report describes an adult S. ornatus flying toward a drone and striking it with its talons outside a nesting context (M. Mazo, pers. comm.; see Supplementary Video 1).

Together, these observations suggest that aggressive interactions with drones are not restricted to nesting contexts. In addition, during the fledgling period, juveniles may also interact with drones while developing hunting skills70, including species of the genus Falco. Overall, these considerations highlight that both adult and immature raptors can exhibit active responses to drones, reinforcing the need to account for age class and behavioural context when evaluating risks associated with drone operations.

Species traits and drone flight characteristics

Our findings indicate that ecological traits, drone approach methods, and flight characteristics influenced the active behavioural responses in 22 (81%) of the 27 species studied. Body mass was statistically significant but had a negligible effect, although smaller-bodied species were more prone to attack. Among drone flight parameters, overhead approaches intensified defensive, attack, and escape responses, whereas vertical approaches tended to suppress these behaviours but still carried a high risk of escape for raptors. In addition, light-coloured drones and higher flight speeds were associated with stronger responses, supporting protocols that favour darker, smaller, and slower drones.

After accounting for reproductive stage, group composition (number of adults and immatures), and flight parameters, body mass remained negatively associated with attack probability: smaller-bodied species showed higher odds of attacking drones, whereas larger species tended to escape. Although the point estimate for body mass in the Attack model suggests quasi-separation and should be interpreted cautiously, the direction and consistency of effects are supported by the opposite pattern in Escape, aligning with previous findings that smaller raptors exhibit more aggressive nest defence28,29. For instance, H. harpyja, one of the heaviest species studied (up to 6 kg52,55), responded to 60% of drone approaches, less frequently than smaller species such as B. ventralis, which displayed more consistent or intense reactions. These results indicate that behavioural responses are not fixed but shaped by a combination of intrinsic factors (e.g. age, breeding stage, and cycles) and extrinsic ones (e.g. nest height).

Species of the genus Falco are known for high territoriality and aggressive nest defence28,29, and in this study, they showed variable responses to drone approaches. Falco deiroleucus remained passive during incubation and early chick-rearing (when only the female was present) but showed vocalizations when the male was nearby. By contrast, a separate report from Guatemala described a pair of F. deiroleucus attacking a drone during brood-rearing (R. Berry, pers. comm.), suggesting that drone use should be avoided during this stage to prevent accidents. In this study, F. rufigularis also responded aggressively as the drone reached nest height, forcing flight termination. Given their exceptional agility, members of the genus Falco are capable of intercepting drones, risking severe injury or death in this process. Nevertheless, our observations indicate that solitary incubating females often remain unresponsive, suggesting that carefully planned, brief, and distant flights by experienced operators may be conducted with reduced risk to assess clutch size in otherwise inaccessible nests.

Distance thresholds and response variability

Most species reacted when drones flew within 50 m of the nest (Fig. 6), although some individuals showed no response even at 2 m. As expected, adults were predominantly responsible for defensive behaviours, reflecting greater investment in nest protection46. Both inter- and intraspecific variation in the RID was evident, ranging from 2 m to over 400 m. For example, S. isidori exhibited alert responses at 2 m (pre-laying) and 5 m (incubation), defensive displays at 50 m (brood-rearing), and even an attack at 300 m (brood-rearing). In H. harpyja, 40% of flights elicited no response, despite similar drone distances that triggered active reactions. Such variation underscores considerable individual-level plasticity.

The broader response range in H. harpyja likely reflects a combination of high nest vigilance and sensitivity to aerial intrusion. In contrast, S. melanoleucus consistently exhibited active responses (Fig. 5), whereas S. isidori reacted across a wider spectrum, possibly reflecting its ecology or a more reactive behavioural profile. Interestingly, V. gryphus, a scavenger not typically associated with nest defense, also exhibited defensive responses, possibly driven by surprise or nest vigilance. Finally, responses from species with limited sample sizes should be interpreted cautiously, although the pattern of reacting only at close distances appears consistent. Overall, these findings caution against assuming uniform behavioural responses among individuals of the same species, as reaction depends on individual state, breeding stage, and flight characteristics.

Flight duration may also influence a behavioural response in juveniles. In one event, a juvenile S. tyrannus attacked a drone after a four-minute flight, despite showing no reaction at shorter exposures. In a subsequent two-minute flight at 20 m, the same individual exhibited defensive behaviour. These contrasting outcomes suggest that prolonged exposure increases the likelihood of a reaction, particularly when birds arrive or return to the nest during drone activity. However, these differences may also reflect the bird’s activity during the drone flight, as the juvenile was not feeding during the attack but was feeding and mantling over prey during the defensive response.

Regarding drone noise, recent findings71 indicate that flyovers and turning maneuvers generate less acoustic disturbance than prolonged hovering. This distinction is particularly relevant for territorial species or individuals caring for offspring, as hovering drones near nests may be perceived as persistent threats. Consistent with this, our results demonstrate more intense behavioural responses during stationary operations near nests. Adjusting drone protocols to minimise hovering and prioritize brief, low-speed lateral flyovers could reduce the likelihood of eliciting defensive or aggressive behaviours, since our results also indicate that higher flight speeds were associated with stronger behavioural responses.

Implications for conservation and drone-based monitoring

This study provides a comprehensive assessment of raptor responses to drone flights across multiple species, breeding stages, and Neotropical countries. Responses were not solely dictated by flight parameters or proximity but were also influenced by breeding strategy, ecological context, and interspecific interactions. Species with triennial breeding cycles may be particularly vulnerable to disturbances, given the lower reproductive output and limited opportunity to replace lost clutches. In our sample, 56% of species produced a single offspring per breeding attempt, increasing the value of each brood and, consequently, the intensity of nest-defensive behaviours observed. Although not all species reacted during incubation, this stage is generally recognised as highly sensitive to disturbance, as prolonged exposure during brooding imposes energetic costs and may lead to nest abandonment57.

Brood size also influenced parental responses: nests with two chicks were less likely to exhibit alert or defensive behaviours and more prone to escape. This contrasts with parental investment theory, which predicts stronger nest-defence motivation when reproductive value is higher29. However, increased parental investment may also promote greater risk sensitivity, favouring avoidance when the potential cost to adults, and future reproduction, outweighs the immediate benefit of defence.

Nest-defence intensity varied with reproductive effort, body size, and nest accessibility. Smaller, agile species tended to attack, whereas larger raptors often relied on passive or evasive strategies. These results support the interpretation that escape can represent an adaptive form of nest protection when parental survival is at risk. Accordingly, flight planning should incorporate brood size as a potential risk factor, avoid drone operations near two-chick nests, or apply stricter buffer distances to reduce stress and minimise potential harm to both birds and equipment.

Our results show no evidence of habituation to repeated drone exposures. Instead, the probability of an active response increased with flight order, indicating sensitization rather than tolerance. This pattern suggests that repeated disturbances may reinforce defensive or aggressive behaviours, as observed in other territorial species under chronic stress conditions24,41. Such responses raise concerns about cumulative energetic costs and the potential for nest abandonment, particularly during sensitive stages. These findings emphasise the importance of limiting repeated flights and incorporating time intervals between operations to reduce stress.

Conversely, aggressive responses were observed in species exposed to human-wildlife conflict, including poaching and nest intrusion72–80. Although not tested in this study, prior negative experiences may sensitize individuals to aerial threats, including drones. Predation remains the main cause of nest failure for birds of prey81,82, and empirical data suggest that birds often perceive drones as flying predators17. Species subject to predation or kleptoparasitism may thus face repeated decisions between remaining stationary or fleeing. Eight species among the 27 studied have previously been reported as victims of predators83–87. The perception of drones as potential predators may stem from these ecological pressures.

Species nesting on open platforms, where prey remains accumulate, may be particularly prone to kleptoparasitism and therefore more likely to interpret drones as potential threats to their food stores88,89. This pattern could explain the defensive behaviour observed in some species. For example, adults of S. isidori vocalized and covered prey when feeding but showed little or no response when not handling prey.

Despite 81% of species exhibiting behavioural responses to drones, no immediate evidence of reproductive failures were recorded, and all monitored nests remained active in subsequent breeding attempts. This suggests that short-term impacts of carefully conducted drone flights may be limited, provided that appropriate precautions are taken. However, the long-term consequences of repeated exposure remain uncertain, particularly for species with low reproductive rates or high sensitivity to disturbance. This concern is amplified by the fact that 20% of the studied species are globally threatened (Table 1), and that up to 50% are regionally listed as Critically Endangered, including F. deiroleucus, M. guianensis, B. coronatus, S. ornatus, S. isidori, H. harpyja, and V. gryphus90–98. Several of these have been exposed to anthropogenic threats for decades73–80, which may exacerbate their vulnerability to additional sources of disturbance.

In addition, individual variation and context-dependent behaviour underscore the need for cautious interpretation of flight protocols. Some individuals may tolerate the presence of drones across multiple flights, while others react aggressively in a single encounter. Furthermore, small sample sizes for certain species limit the ability to draw definitive conclusions about their typical responses. For instance, on a single flight to an active nest of B. albigula, adults were absent and apparently unaware of the drone (Mavic 2 Zoom), which remained near the nest for less than one minute. Given this species’ known aggressiveness, which attacks humans and other raptors near nests99, drone flights near active nests should be strictly avoided.

By identifying species- and context-specific thresholds, drone use can be refined into a non-invasive yet effective conservation tool17,19,20, particularly in remote areas where traditional monitoring is logistically challenging. As drones become increasingly integrated into wildlife research, incorporating behavioural ecology into operational protocols is essential to ensure both data quality and animal welfare.

Nevertheless, our observations confirm that drone-raptor interactions can be unpredictable. Aggressive encounters also occurred outside the nesting context, e.g., with soaring individuals during mapping flights, highlighting the importance of pre-flight assessments of raptor presence both at nests and in surrounding habitats. Based on our results and published recommendations, we refine a protocol tailored to species-specific sensitivities designed to minimise disturbance and enhance both raptor welfare and operator safety during drone-based monitoring (Table 2, Supplementary Table S5).

Recommendations for drone-based monitoring of raptor nests

Prior to a drone flight, raptor activity should be carefully monitored, and the behaviour of both adults and immatures at the nest and in the surrounding area should be documented. These preliminary observations are essential for scheduling flights outside the most sensitive breeding stages and for interpreting species- and context-specific responses. Drone operators should be trained to recognise species-specific behaviours and stress signals to identify early signs of disturbance.

Whenever possible, flights should employ drones equipped with zoom and high-resolution cameras, enabling detailed observations from a safe initial distance of approximately 100 m from the nest. Given that some species can react aggressively even from large distances, this threshold represents a conservative, and practical buffer that minimises the risk of provoking intense behavioural responses. Similar conservative distances have been recommended in previous studies on raptors and other large birds6,41,100,101.

Fixed observation points or pre-programmed flight paths should be preferred to avoid direct overflight of nests, particularly during incubation. The number of flights per nest and per breeding stage should be minimised to reduce cumulative disturbance. During brood-rearing, chicks may tolerate the presence of drones to a greater extent, but repeated exposures can still increase stress or even cause accidental displacement of young birds.

For safe and efficient flights, each flight should involve two trained operators38: a pilot, responsible for controlling the drone; and an observer, responsible for continuously monitoring bird behaviour and alerting the pilot to any signs of hazardous interactions. Flights must be discontinued if raptors exhibit aggression, escape, or any of the response categories described in this study.

Weather conditions should always be assessed prior to operation, and flights should be avoided during high winds and rain, as these can increase flight instability and the risk of incidents. Each flight should be of short, lasting two to four minutes. Smaller, opaque drones, such as the Mavic series, are safer than larger and lighter-coloured models, such as the Phantom series. Although no significant effect of time of day was detected, we suggest exercising caution when considering early-morning flights, as active responses tended to be more frequent during these periods in our observations.

Flight planning should incorporate species’ aggressiveness and agility to anticipate potential risks (Supplementary Table S5). For highly aggressive or critical breeding stages (e.g., incubation), drone overflights should be avoided altogether. Maintaining altitudes of ≥ 100 m AGL is recommended for less aggressive species, and ≥ 150 m AGL for species with high attack probability or pronounced aerial agility. These altitude thresholds are based on evidence that higher flight altitudes reduce the risk of attacks by agile species20,38 and substantially reduce drone noise (in decibels)71, thereby minimising potential acoustic disturbance to nesting raptors.

Recommended flight parameters also vary according to approach type and behavioural risk. Vertical approaches (risk: escape) should maintain greater distances and higher altitudes, with minimal flight duration and speed. Diagonal approaches (risk: alert/defensive responses) require a high and distant profile with short flights durations at low speeds. Horizontal approaches (risk: alert and escape) should be performed at higher altitudes, along peripheral routes, and with short exposure times. Overhead approaches should be avoided during sensitive breeding stages (e.g., incubation) due to their consistently high disturbance potential.

When applied with these precautions, drones can be valuable tools for safe, efficient, and non-invasive monitoring of raptor populations. However, the ethical and scientific justification for each flight should be clearly stated. The potential risks to sensitive individuals, particularly fledglings capable of flight, should always be weighed against the data value of the data to be obtained. Researchers are encouraged to critically evaluate whether aerial monitoring is essential for each research question, especially for threatened raptors whose long breeding cycles amplify the cost of disturbance.

Conclusion

Drones are a valuable tool for raptor monitoring; however, their application requires careful consideration of biological and ecological factors to minimise disturbance and ensure animal welfare. The most important considerations are species-specific behavioural traits, breeding stage, and drone flight characteristics. Although no immediate reproductive failures were observed, the lack of habituation and evidence of sensitization indicate that repeated exposure may increase the probability of adverse behavioural responses. These considerations are particularly important for sensitive species and critical breeding stages, where disturbance may have disproportionate effects.
