Corneal injury associated with scrub typhus following mite exposure in a child in Taiwan: a case report
Authors: Shao-Huan Hung, Jen-Lin Hung, Chi-Ting Horng
Published: 2026

Introduction

Scrub typhus is a mite-borne infectious disease endemic to the Asia–Pacific region, caused by Orientia tsutsugamushi and transmitted by chiggers (larval mites) [2]. The disease commonly presents with systemic manifestations such as fever, rash, headache, myalgia, and eschar formation [3]. Although ocular involvement is considered uncommon, several ophthalmic manifestations have previously been reported, including conjunctivitis, retinitis, optic neuritis, and uveitis, typically occurring during the later stages of systemic infection [4]. These ocular findings are generally regarded as secondary inflammatory or infectious complications associated with systemic disease progression.

In contrast, direct mechanical ocular injury associated with mite exposure at the initial stage of contact has rarely been described. To our knowledge, few reports have documented concurrent corneal trauma and clinically suspected scrub typhus immediately following mite exposure. This case report describes a unique pediatric presentation of corneal trauma with concurrent systemic findings suggestive of scrub typhus, highlighting the importance of recognizing atypical manifestations in endemic areas such as Taiwan [2].

Case report

A previously healthy 9-year-old Taiwanese boy presented with acute eye pain and systemic symptoms following exposure to mites during a family visit to Alishan Forest, a mountainous area in central Taiwan. While walking through overgrown vegetation, he was struck by airborne debris during a sudden gust of wind and immediately experienced tearing and severe ocular discomfort. Direct mechanical trauma possibly related to mite contact and associated environmental particles was considered the most likely mechanism of ocular injury. Within an hour, he was taken to our outpatient clinic.

Ophthalmic examination revealed a live mite attached to the left lower eyelid margin and ocular surface (Fig. 1). Slit-lamp microscopy demonstrated approximately 15 fine linear corneal epithelial abrasions involving the central cornea, accompanied by diffuse fluorescein staining and stromal edema without anterior chamber reaction (Fig. 2). Findings were suggestive of traumatic keratopathy rather than progressive infectious keratitis. The child’s best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) in the affected eye was reduced to 6/30.

Multiple linear abrasions were observed on the central corneal epithelium after eyelid opening, accompanied by stromal edema, consistent with mechanical injury possibly related to mite contact

Topical levofloxacin, gentamicin ointment, hypertonic saline, and carefully monitored topical corticosteroids were administered after infectious keratitis had been clinically excluded. The combination of topical antibiotics was selected to provide broad-spectrum antimicrobial coverage because of concern regarding ocular surface contamination following outdoor environmental exposure and multiple corneal epithelial defects. Corticosteroids were cautiously used to reduce significant stromal edema and ocular surface inflammation [1]. The patient subsequently achieved complete symptom resolution and restoration of BCVA to 6/6 within five days.

On the same day, another live mite was identified attached to the right lower eyelid margin (Fig. 3). The following day, the child developed systemic symptoms including fever (38.5 °C), headache, myalgia, and a maculopapular rash. A 2.0 cm eschar subsequently formed at the site of the mite bite (Fig. 4). Routine laboratory examinations, including complete blood count and liver function tests, were within normal limits. The diagnosis of scrub typhus was primarily based on clinical presentation and endemic exposure history, because confirmatory serologic or PCR testing for Orientia tsutsugamushi was not performed. Based on clinical suspicion, oral doxycycline was initiated. The child showed rapid improvement and achieved full recovery within 10 days.

Discussion

Scrub typhus is a re-emerging rickettsial infection, with an estimated 1 million cases occurring annually in the Asia–Pacific region [2]. It is caused by Orientia tsutsugamushi and transmitted through chigger bites. These mites typically inhabit moist, grassy environments, particularly in endemic mountainous regions such as Alishan in Taiwan [2]. The disease presents a broad clinical spectrum ranging from mild flu-like symptoms to severe complications, including hepatic failure, myocarditis, and multi-organ dysfunction [3].

Ocular manifestations of scrub typhus are uncommon and generally occur during the post-febrile stage of systemic infection. Previously reported ophthalmic findings include conjunctivitis, retinitis, optic neuritis, and uveitis [4]. These manifestations are typically regarded as secondary inflammatory or infectious complications associated with systemic disease progression. In contrast, the present case primarily involved acute traumatic corneal injury possibly related to direct mite exposure at the initial stage of contact. To our knowledge, few reports have described direct corneal injury associated with mite exposure occurring concurrently with clinically suspected scrub typhus.

The classic triad of scrub typhus—fever, headache, and myalgia—is nonspecific and may mimic other febrile illnesses. Eschar formation is an important diagnostic clue, although it is not universally present [5]. In this case, topical corticosteroids were cautiously administered after clinical exclusion of infectious keratitis in order to reduce significant stromal edema and ocular surface inflammation. The diagnosis of scrub typhus was based on the combination of endemic exposure history, systemic manifestations, eschar formation, and rapid clinical response to doxycycline therapy, rather than mite identification alone. Early clinical recognition and prompt initiation of appropriate treatment may help prevent more severe complications.

Preventive measures, including wearing protective clothing, avoiding direct contact with overgrown vegetation, and careful inspection after outdoor activities, may help reduce the risk of mite exposure in endemic forest regions. The absence of confirmatory serologic or PCR testing, lack of species-level mite identification, and limited long-term follow-up should be acknowledged as limitations of this case report.

Conclusion

This case underscores a rare yet clinically important presentation involving concurrent corneal injury possibly associated with mite exposure and systemic findings suggestive of scrub typhus in a child. It reinforces the need for clinicians to consider mite-related ocular trauma and rickettsial infection in the differential diagnosis of acute eye pain and febrile illness in endemic areas. Early clinical recognition and prompt initiation of appropriate topical and systemic treatment may help improve clinical outcomes and reduce the risk of complications [3, 5].
