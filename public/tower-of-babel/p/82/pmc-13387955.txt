Utility of Bilateral Upper Extremity Arterial Access for Complex Neurovascular Intervention and Diagnostic Cervicocerebral Angiography: A Unique Single-Center Experience
Authors: Mackenzie Castellanos, Jonathan J. Wakim, Gabriel R. Arguelles, Jaeha Kim, Austin J. Borja, Gregory Glauser, Om H. Gandhi, Sami Almasri, Luis O. Tierradentro-Garcia, Linda Bagley, Omar A. Choudhri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveWrist-based access for cervicocerebral angiography and neurovascular interventions is increasingly adopted as an alternative to transfemoral access, offering lower complication rates and faster recovery. In select cases, dual arterial access facilitates navigation of complex anatomy and the use of multiple devices. However, evidence supporting bilateral wrist access remains limited. We report our institutional experience (University of Pennsylvania Health System) with this approach.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed consecutive diagnostic and interventional neurovascular procedures performed using bilateral wrist access across a single university health system (September 2019–July 2025). Demographic, clinical, and procedural data were summarized, with a focus on technical considerations and outcomes.ResultsTwenty procedures were performed, including 7 diagnostic and 13 interventional cases. Bilateral radial access was used in 16 cases, and ulnar access in 4. Median procedure time was 117 min for diagnostic cases and 236 min for interventional cases. The most common indication for bilateral access in diagnostic procedures was to facilitate anatomic reach for vertebral artery catheterization, while a transcirculation approach was the primary indication in interventional cases. Target vessel catheterization was achieved in 19 of 20 cases (95%). No major complications occurred. One case required conversion to femoral access due to unfavorable working angles across the aortic arch. One self-limited radial artery wire microperforation was successfully managed with catheter tamponade and conversion to ulnar access. On systematic review of follow-up imaging, 1 symptomatic radial artery occlusion was identified (1/20; 5%), managed conservatively without functional sequelae; no other access-site complications were observed.ConclusionBilateral wrist access is a safe and technically feasible alternative access strategy in carefully selected cases for cervicocerebral angiography and neurovascular interventions. With appropriate planning, this technique may serve as a selective alternative access strategy for complex procedures requiring dual arterial access, particularly posterior circulation and transcirculation interventions.

Introduction

Cervicocerebral angiography remains the gold standard for diagnostic evaluation of the intracranial and cervical vasculature, while neurovascular intervention serves as the cornerstone for therapeutic management.1,2) Applications include conditions like aneurysms, arteriovenous malformations, and ischemic cerebrovascular disease. Historically, the common femoral artery has been the most widely used access site because of its ease of cannulation and large caliber. However, transfemoral access is associated with well-recognized risks, including retroperitoneal hemorrhage, pseudoaneurysm formation, femoral nerve injuries, and prolonged post-procedure immobility, particularly in obese patients and those with peripheral vascular disease.3)

In recent years, attention has shifted toward vascular access in the upper extremities. Transradial access, long established in interventional cardiology, and transulnar access have both been shown to reduce vascular complications, enable faster recovery, and improve patient satisfaction compared with transfemoral access.4–7) In cardiology, these benefits have enabled widespread adoption of wrist access, transforming practice by shortening hospital stays, lowering healthcare costs, and allowing outpatient procedures.8,9) Their growing adoption in neurointerventional practice has demonstrated that wrist-based techniques can achieve comparable procedural success while minimizing morbidity and cost.2,10–15)

Some neurovascular procedures require dual arterial access. Examples include transcirculation approaches where 1 vessel provides working access and the contralateral vessel provides visualization, balloon occlusion testing that requires simultaneous flow assessment, and cases with tortuous anatomy where ipsilateral catheterization fails. Traditionally, operators have used bilateral femoral access or combined femoral and wrist access for these situations.16,17) The dual wrist-based approach aims to maintain the safety profile of wrist access while providing the anatomic flexibility needed for these procedures.

To our knowledge, while prior studies have described unilateral transradial and transulnar access, bilateral wrist access for angiography and neurovascular intervention has been reported only once in the literature. Luther et al. described 2 patients who underwent posterior circulation interventions via bilateral wrist access, both without complication.18) We aim to describe our institutional experience (University of Pennsylvania Health System) of 20 patients who underwent diagnostic angiography or neurovascular intervention using bilateral wrist access.

Materials and Methods

Study population

We included all patients who underwent diagnostic angiography or neurovascular intervention with bilateral wrist access across a multicenter, single university health system. We used the departmental medical records to retrospectively identify patients from the first implementation of the procedure in September 2019 through July 2025. This study was approved by the University of Pennsylvania Institutional Review Board (Protocol # 844179); a waiver of informed consent was granted.

To contextualize bilateral wrist access within our institutional practice: during the study period, the senior author performed approximately 650 cerebrovascular procedures annually (roughly 450 diagnostic angiograms and 200 neurointerventional cases), while the neurointerventional department performed approximately 1500 unilateral wrist-access cases per year. The institution’s broader wrist-based cerebrovascular experience has been described previously, including a published series of transradial and transulnar intraoperative angiography in 70 consecutive neurosurgical cases with a 98.6% procedural success rate and no angiography-related complications,19) and a published transulnar diagnostic and interventional neuroendovascular series of 161 patients in which angiography-related complications occurred in only 6 cases.20)

Access planning and technique

Bilateral wrist access was planned preoperatively by neurointerventionalists for cases with anticipated anatomic complexity or the need for simultaneous bilateral injections in complex interventions. In other cases, the operative plan allowed for conversion to bilateral access intraoperatively if challenging anatomy or additional device support warranted it. Supplementary Figure 1 outlines our institutional decision-making framework for bilateral wrist access planning and intraoperative conversion.

Each patient was brought into the angiography suite and placed supine on the table. Prior to vascular access, wrists were prepped and draped in a sterile fashion. Ultrasound-guided evaluation of the radial and ulnar arteries was performed to determine vessel patency, measure distal vessel diameters, and select the access site. A minimum distal arterial diameter of approximately 2 mm was required for diagnostic access and approximately 2.5 mm for interventional access; vessels below these thresholds prompted consideration of the proximal radial artery, the ipsilateral ulnar artery, or femoral access. Ulnar access was selected when the ipsilateral radial artery demonstrated 1 or more of the following features, consistent with our institution’s previously published criteria:20) a caliber smaller than the ipsilateral ulnar artery on ultrasound or below the sheath-size threshold, total or partial occlusion, vasospasm, a radial loop, failed cannulation, or prior radial artery use (including bypass-graft harvest, prior instrumentation, or dialysis fistula). Local anesthesia was achieved with lidocaine injected at the selected access sites.

A standard micropuncture kit was used for vascular access. Under ultrasound guidance, the micropuncture needle was advanced into the target vessel and an access sheath was inserted (5 Fr for diagnostic cases, 5–7 Fr for interventional cases). A cocktail of verapamil (5 mg), nitroglycerin (200 mcg), and heparin (2000 units) was administered through each sheath to prevent vasospasm and maintain arterial patency. At the conclusion of each procedure, the sheath was removed and hemostasis was achieved with a radial compression band (TR Band, Terumo, Tokyo, Japan) at each access site, following our institution’s previously described hemostasis protocol.19,21) For ulnar access, the TR Band was inverted and positioned slightly proximal to the arteriotomy for enhanced coverage. The band was inflated per the manufacturer’s standard protocol and then deflated by removing approximately 1 mL of air every 2–3 min until hemostasis was achieved while maintaining distal perfusion.

Data collection and statistical analysis

Patient data were extracted from the electronic health record including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), vascular risk factors (smoking, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes), use of antithrombotic medications, and underlying pathology. Procedural characteristics were recorded; these included indications (diagnostic or interventional), vessels accessed, vessels catheterized, procedure duration, fluoroscopy time, method of blood pressure monitoring, and complications. Major complications were defined as ischemia or nerve injury. Access-site complications were assessed by systematic post hoc review of all available follow-up imaging (including duplex ultrasound, CTA, and subsequent catheter angiography when performed) and electronic health record documentation for each of the 20 patients, with specific attention to radial artery occlusion (RAO), pseudoaneurysm, hematoma, symptomatic hand ischemia, and access-site nerve injury. All continuous variables were reported as medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs). All categorical or binary variables were reported as counts and frequencies.

Results

Patient characteristics

Twenty patients underwent diagnostic angiography or neurovascular intervention using bilateral wrist access. Baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The cohort included 12 women and 8 men, with a median age of 63.5 years (IQR, 54.5–69) and a median BMI of 29.7 kg/m2 (IQR, 27.1–40.9). Vascular risk factors were common, including hypertension (n = 14, 70%), hyperlipidemia (n = 14, 70%), smoking history (n = 11, 55%), and diabetes (n = 5, 25%). Out of 20 patients, 7 underwent diagnostic procedures and 13 underwent interventional procedures (Table 2). Indications for diagnostic angiograms included hemorrhage (n = 2, 28.6%), aneurysm (n = 1, 14.3%), meningioma encasing the basilar artery (n = 1, 14.3%), highly vascularized bony lesion at the C4 vertebral body (n = 1, 14.3%), dural fistula (n = 1, 14.3%), and bow hunter’s syndrome (n = 1, 14.3%) (Supplementary Table 1). The predominant indication for neurovascular intervention was aneurysm (n = 11, 84.6%), followed by stroke thrombectomy (n = 1, 7.7%) and cerebellopontine angle tumor requiring preoperative embolization (n = 1, 7.7%) (Supplementary Table 2).

Procedure characteristics

Bilateral radial access was obtained in 16 patients, and the ulnar artery was used in 4 (2 left wrists, 2 right wrists). The median right and left access artery diameters (radial or ulnar) were 3.0 mm (IQR, 2.5–3) and 2.5 mm (IQR, 2.5–2.7), respectively. The median total procedure duration for diagnostic procedures was 117 min (IQR, 110.5–127.5), with a median fluoroscopy time of 13 min (IQR, 12.0–22.1). For interventional procedures, the median total procedure duration was 236 min (IQR, 170–247), with a median fluoroscopy time of 67 min (IQR, 57–79.7).

Rationale for bilateral access

Six of 7 diagnostic angiograms were planned preoperatively for bilateral wrist access (Supplementary Table 1). The primary rationale was to facilitate anatomic reach to bilateral vasculature, such as the left vertebral artery, which can be difficult to selectively catheterize from right-sided access alone (Supplementary Fig. 1). Additionally, other branches originating from the left subclavian artery—such as the ascending cervical and deep cervical arteries—can also present catheterization challenges that may favor bilateral access. One diagnostic case required unplanned intraoperative conversion due to an aberrant right subclavian artery, which prevented right vertebral artery catheterization from the left radial approach.

Twelve of 13 interventional cases were planned preoperatively for bilateral wrist access (Supplementary Table 2). The most common indications were transcirculation approaches (Figs. 1–3), balloon occlusion testing, and contralateral flow visualization during device deployment. One interventional case required unplanned conversion when a basilar artery thrombus could not be reached from the right vertebral artery due to vessel tortuosity.

Outcomes

Target vessel catheterization was successful in 19 of 20 procedures (95%). The single failure occurred in a patient with an anterior communicating artery aneurysm that required femoral conversion for the interventional component due to unfavorable working angles across the aortic arch (Fig. 4). No major complications, including ischemic events, occurred. One right radial artery wire microperforation was managed with catheter tamponade and immediate conversion to ulnar access, resolving without sequelae. On systematic review of available follow-up imaging and electronic health record data for all 20 patients, 1 patient (1/20; 5%) was found to have a symptomatic RAO at the ipsilateral access site. Symptoms consisted of mild post-procedural ipsilateral wrist discomfort with a diminished radial pulse on clinical examination, without evidence of hand ischemia or motor or sensory deficit; hand perfusion remained intact through compensatory ulnar flow. The occlusion was managed conservatively with supportive care and clinical surveillance, without anticoagulation or surgical intervention, and the patient experienced no functional sequelae on subsequent follow-up. The remaining 19 patients had no evidence of post-procedural radial or ulnar artery occlusion. No pseudoaneurysm, hematoma requiring intervention, symptomatic hand ischemia, or access-site nerve injury was identified in the cohort.

Technical considerations

The right wrist was most often used to access the right vertebral artery and the right and left carotid arteries. The left wrist was most often used for the left vertebral artery, followed by the right carotid arteries (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2).

All diagnostic studies were performed using 5-Fr sheaths with a 0.038-inch Glidewire and 5-French Simmons-2 Glidecath (both Terumo). Interventional cases used 5–7-Fr sheaths depending on device requirements, with larger guide systems introduced via exchange or sheathless technique as needed. Detailed device specifications are listed in Supplementary Table 2. For patient positioning, the operator stood on the patient’s right side throughout each procedure. The right arm was positioned on an arm board in standard fashion. The left arm was flexed at the elbow and positioned across the patient’s torso, allowing both access sites to remain within the operator’s reach from a single position (Fig. 5).

Blood pressure monitoring strategies varied by procedure type (Table 3). In interventional procedures, 12 of 13 (92.3%) used invasive monitoring: 7 via dorsalis pedis arterial line and 5 via transduction through an upper extremity sheath not actively being used. Four of the 7 patients who received dorsalis pedis lines had a diagnosis of diabetes. One interventional case used cuff monitoring alone. Diagnostic procedures primarily used noninvasive cuff monitoring (6 of 7 cases, 85.7%), with 1 using upper extremity sheath transduction.

Discussion

We described the safety and feasibility of bilateral wrist access in 20 adult patients undergoing diagnostic angiography or neurovascular intervention at our institution. This approach was successful in 95% of cases, with no major adverse events observed.

Based on our institutional experience, bilateral wrist access is most applicable in cases necessitating direct bilateral vertebral or carotid visualization, assessment of collateral flow, transcirculation approach, or balloon occlusion testing. It is less suitable when anatomic factors—such as small-caliber distal vessels, radial loops, or unfavorable arch configurations—compromise guide support, making femoral or hybrid access preferable. To improve reproducibility, the following objective triggers informed case selection in our practice: (a) a requirement for simultaneous bilateral vertebral artery catheterization or visualization that cannot be reliably achieved from subclavian injection with brachial cuff inflation; (b) a planned transcirculation approach for a posterior circulation or complex bifurcation lesion; (c) a planned balloon occlusion test requiring contralateral real-time flow assessment; (d) a requirement for simultaneous dual-device manipulation with independent working channels; and (e) intraprocedural failure of ipsilateral wrist engagement of a target vessel, with the contralateral wrist offering a more favorable anatomic route than femoral conversion (Supplementary Fig. 1). Anterior circulation and arch-level cases without these features are generally well served by unilateral wrist or femoral access and do not routinely require bilateral wrist puncture. Bilateral wrist access is thus best positioned as an alternative access strategy rather than a default approach, with final access-site selection individualized to each patient’s anatomy, comorbidities, and procedural objectives.

In practice, this approach leverages the anatomic advantages of both sides: simultaneous bilateral injections facilitated collateral flow assessment through the circle of Willis without catheter repositioning, while independent working channels enabled real-time flow monitoring during transcirculation approaches and balloon occlusion testing.

Regarding access laterality, several considerations apply. Right-sided access is more ergonomic for operators working from the right side of the table, as the puncture site is easily accessible. This approach provides straightforward access to the right and left carotid systems and to the right vertebral artery, although catheterizing the left vertebral artery from the right can be difficult due to its steep and more distal takeoff from the subclavian. When the left vertebral origin cannot be directly engaged from the right radial approach, left subclavian injections with transient brachial cuff inflation provide sufficient anterograde filling in most cases. However, in select situations—particularly those involving posterior circulation pathology such as high-flow arteriovenous malformations or dural arteriovenous fistulas—direct left vertebral artery catheterization may be necessary to achieve stable engagement and complete visualization of the angioarchitecture. This represents one limitation of right-sided access alone. Lastly, because most patients are right-hand dominant, complications in the right upper extremity also carry greater functional consequences.

Left-sided access, conversely, provides a more direct route to the left vertebral artery and access to the right carotid system via the arch while reducing functional risk. However, although left carotid vessel selection is achievable from left radial access during diagnostic angiography, obtaining stable guide catheter support for interventions can be substantially more challenging due to the acute angle required to engage the left common carotid artery. In our cohort, bilateral wrist access provided complementary engagement of both vertebral arteries and carotid systems, allowing complete cerebrovascular visualization without femoral conversion.

Safety and technical feasibility

Bilateral wrist access was successfully obtained in all patients in our cohort, with catheterization of all targeted vessels achieved in 19 of 20 cases (95%). The single failure occurred in a patient with an anterior communicating artery aneurysm that required femoral conversion for the interventional component due to unfavorable working angles across the aortic arch (Fig. 4). Notably, this patient had previously undergone diagnostic angiography without difficulty, underscoring that vessel selection during diagnostic studies does not always predict the stability required for interventional maneuvers. Guide catheter support demands are substantially greater during device deployment, and even anatomies that appear accessible on prior studies may prove unstable once dual microcatheters or stents are introduced. This case highlights the importance of procedural flexibility and readiness to convert when guide support is inadequate.

Our success rate for vessel catheterization in this series is consistent with the technical feasibility of bilateral wrist access for contralateral vessel evaluation, which can be variable with a unilateral approach. For context, prior studies have reported lower success rates for contralateral catheterization using a unilateral approach. For example, Jo et al. achieved catheterization success in only 52.2% of left vertebral artery attempts and 82.6% of left internal carotid artery attempts, compared with 96.1% to 98.6% success for ipsilateral vessels.2)

While bilateral wrist access can facilitate reliable contralateral vessel evaluation, this advantage must be weighed against the additional risks inherent to obtaining 2 arterial access sites. In purely diagnostic cases where bilateral vertebral artery evaluation is required but subclavian injection is inadequate, transfemoral access alone frequently permits successful selective catheterization of both vertebral arteries—typically upwards of 90%—without necessitating dual wrist puncture.22) When these diagnostic goals can be reliably achieved femorally, operators should balance the advantages of avoiding a femoral puncture against the added access-site considerations of cannulating 2 radial or ulnar arteries, incorporating patient anatomy, comorbidities, and overall procedural objectives.

Procedural safety depends on operator familiarity with wrist-specific ergonomics, arch anatomy, and hemodynamic monitoring strategies. In our series, invasive arterial pressure monitoring was used in nearly all interventional procedures, most commonly through a dorsalis pedis arterial line or by transducing the access sheath. Diagnostic cases relied primarily on noninvasive cuff monitoring. Familiarity with these alternative monitoring strategies allows operators to maintain continuous hemodynamic assessment without requiring femoral access. Of note, 4 patients who received dorsalis pedis arterial lines had a diagnosis of diabetes; while no access-site complications occurred in these cases, patients with diabetes may have increased vulnerability to peripheral vascular or wound-healing complications, and this should be considered when selecting alternative monitoring sites.

Access-site safety in our cohort included 1 RAO (1/20; 5%) identified on post hoc review of available follow-up imaging, with no other access-site complications. For institutional context, a prior series from our group evaluating transradial and transulnar intraoperative angiography reported an RAO rate of 3 of 22 re-access attempts (13.6%).19) The 5% rate observed here is consistent with the lower end of this institutional range and with published unilateral transradial RAO estimates,23,24) but should be interpreted in light of the ascertainment limits described in the “Limitations and institutional context” section and the absence of a matched comparator cohort.

Patient experience is an additional consideration when evaluating wrist-based access. Prior work has demonstrated a patient preference for transradial over transfemoral approaches for neuroendovascular procedures, with shorter recovery time and greater comfort at the puncture site cited as the primary drivers of this preference.25) Whether bilateral wrist access preserves these patient-reported advantages relative to femoral access has not been formally evaluated. While the avoidance of femoral puncture and earlier post-procedural mobilization would be expected to carry over, the additional access-site risks and the need for bilateral post-procedural hemostasis may temper these benefits, and prospective patient-reported outcomes assessments are warranted.

Efficiency and learning curve

The median procedure time for diagnostic cases in our series was 117 min, with a median fluoroscopy time of 13 min. These durations are longer than those typically reported for unilateral diagnostic angiography. For instance, Matsumoto et al. reported procedural times ranging from 22 min for single-vessel angiography to 46 min for 4-vessel studies.23) The extended duration in our cohort at least in part reflects the higher procedural complexity of cases selected for bilateral wrist access—such as those requiring direct catheterization of both vertebral arteries.

The median procedure time for interventional cases in our series was 236 min, with a median fluoroscopy time of 67 min. For context, this fluoroscopy time is within the range previously reported for interventional wrist-access cases. For example, 1 retrospective study found a mean fluoroscopy time of 107 min across 37 patients undergoing neurovascular intervention via unilateral wrist access.26) Taken together, these findings suggest that while bilateral wrist access is safe and feasible, it may involve tradeoffs in procedural efficiency for diagnostic angiography, particularly given the more complex clinical scenarios in which it is employed. For interventional cases, procedure and fluoroscopy times in this series fell within the range of previously published wrist-access intervention cohorts; whether bilateral wrist access alters procedural efficiency for interventions relative to unilateral wrist or femoral approaches cannot be determined from this series and warrants direct comparison in future studies.

Limitations and institutional context

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was small, limiting statistical power and generalizability. This represents real-world practice patterns, as bilateral wrist access is uncommon and reserved for specific clinical scenarios requiring dual arterial access. Second, there was heterogeneity in underlying pathologies, procedural indications, and vessels studied, limiting the ability to draw conclusions about specific subgroups. Third, as a retrospective study, data collection was limited to information available in the electronic health record. Fourth, access-site patency was assessed only through available clinical follow-up documentation rather than by protocolized post-procedural duplex ultrasound, which may underestimate the true incidence of RAO, particularly for asymptomatic patients. Fifth, in the absence of a concurrent unilateral wrist or transfemoral comparator cohort, the safety-related conclusions of this study cannot be directly generalized, and any incremental access-site risk of bilateral over unilateral wrist access—including delayed RAO, even when asymptomatic—should be formally evaluated in future comparative work. Lastly, all procedures were performed by a highly experienced neurointerventional team with extensive wrist-based expertise. The safety and feasibility demonstrated here likely reflect both institutional experience and operator proficiency with wrist-based access. Bilateral wrist access should be pursued only in settings where operators are proficient with transradial and transulnar techniques.

Conclusion

Our findings suggest that bilateral wrist access is a safe and technically feasible alternative for cervicocerebral angiography and neurovascular procedures, with potential utility in carefully selected cases that require complex or bilateral vessel evaluation. In this series, combined radial and ulnar approaches permitted bilateral cerebrovascular catheterization without femoral access in the majority of cases, positioning bilateral wrist access as a selective alternative access strategy when dual arterial access is required. Future directions include larger, prospective multicenter studies to validate these findings, careful evaluation of long-term vessel outcomes, and direct comparisons of bilateral wrist access with unilateral wrist and femoral approaches. Such investigations will clarify whether bilateral wrist access should be positioned as a selective alternative access strategy for carefully defined anatomic and procedural scenarios in neurointerventional practice.
