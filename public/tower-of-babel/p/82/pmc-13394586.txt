NTAQ1 Promotes Hepatocellular Carcinoma Growth by Facilitating the Protein Degradation of the Tumor Suppressor PRDM2
Authors: Tomohiko Ikehara, Hajime Otsu, Taro Tobo, Kiyotaka Hosoda, Tadashi Abe, Masahiro Hashimoto, Yusuke Nakano, Katsushi Dairaku, Yuya Ono, Yuki Miyata, Chihiro Matsumoto, Takanari Tatsumi, Satoshi Higuchi, Takashi Ofuchi, Akinori Tsujimoto, Yuki Ando, Qingjiang Hu, Yusuke Yonemura, Akira Shimizu, Takaaki Masuda, Yuji Soejima, Koshi Mimori
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRecent studies have demonstrated the crucial role of the arginine/N‐degron pathway, which mediates protein degradation, in cancer initiation and progression. N‐terminal glutamine amidohydrolase 1 (NTAQ1), an upstream enzyme in this pathway that catalyzes the deamidation of N‐terminal glutamine residues, remains poorly understood in tumor biology. This study aimed to investigate the clinical significance and molecular functions of NTAQ1 in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). Copy number variation analysis using The Cancer Genome Atlas identified NTAQ1 as a potential driver of HCC progression. The biological role of NTAQ1 in HCC was investigated using in vitro experiments involving NTAQ1 overexpression (OE) in HCC cell lines. NTAQ1, located on the amplified chromosome 8q, exhibited elevated expression in the HCC tissues. NTAQ1 was also identified as an independent poor prognostic factor in patients with HCC, and immunohistochemical staining revealed that it primarily localized in the cytoplasm and membranes of HCC cells. NTAQ1 OE promoted HCC colony formation. Through in silico analysis, the tumor suppressor gene PR/SET Domain 2 (PRDM2) was identified to have a strong inverse correlation with NTAQ1 at the protein level. In vitro assays revealed that NTAQ1 OE enhanced PRDM2 degradation. Furthermore, NTAQ1 OE reduced DNA repair and apoptosis through decreased PRDM2 expression. In conclusion, NTAQ1 was identified as a novel driver gene in HCC that accelerates tumor growth by promoting degradation of the PRDM2 protein, thereby impairing DNA repair and suppressing apoptosis. These findings indicate that NTAQ1 could be a valuable target for treating HCC.Keywords: Arg/N‐degron pathway, HCC, NTAQ1, PRDM2, protein degradation

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) ranks as the sixth most prevalent malignant tumor globally and is the third leading cause of death related to cancer [1]. The introduction of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) has expanded effective systemic treatment options for advanced HCC, and their combination with local therapies has improved clinical outcomes. However, overall treatment results remain poor [2]. This limited efficacy largely reflects the heterogeneous etiology and genetic variability of HCC, which complicate the identification of patient groups suitable for targeted therapy [3]. Therefore, elucidating the biological mechanisms underlying HCC progression to identify specific driver‐ and pathway‐dependent genes that could serve as therapeutic targets or biomarkers for HCC represents a critical priority.

Protein degradation plays a pivotal role in maintaining intracellular homeostasis, stress responses, cell cycle regulation, and apoptosis. Disruptions in this regulatory system are strongly linked to various diseases, particularly the onset and advancement of cancer [4, 5]. Among the relevant mechanisms, the ubiquitin–proteasome system (UPS)—which mediates most intracellular protein degradation—and its associated E3 ubiquitin ligases (N‐recognition factors) have been widely reported to directly influence tumor proliferation and treatment resistance by regulating the degradation of tumor suppressor factors such as p53 and p27, as well as cell cycle regulators [6]. Recently, the N‐degron pathway (formerly known as the N‐end rule pathway), which centers on degradation signals (degrons) that drive protein turnover, has been shown to play a crucial role not only in controlling the lifespan and quality of cytoplasmic proteins but also in cancer initiation and progression [7, 8, 9]. This pathway operates through a unique mechanism in which the fate of a protein is determined by its N‐terminal amino acid residue. Abnormalities in ubiquitinating enzymes and specific N‐recognition factors are believed to play critical roles in tumor development and chemotherapy resistance, and their potential application in cancer therapy is being actively investigated [10, 11, 12]. Within the Arg/N‐degron pathway, a major branch of this system, N‐terminal glutamine amidohydrolase 1 (NTAQ1) catalyzes the deamidation of N‐terminal glutamine residues and serves as an essential enzyme for degron formation [13]. However, no studies have examined how abnormal NTAQ1 expression or functional changes influence cancer progression, and its role in tumor biology remains unclear.

Chromosomal amplification is an early and common genomic alteration in various cancers, and its contribution to cancer progression has become increasingly evident. This mechanism likely involves the activation of driver genes located within amplified chromosomes through their elevated expression [14]. Building on these findings, we previously developed a screening method using multiregion genomic analysis [15] and identified multiple driver genes on amplified chromosomes in colorectal and esophageal cancers [16, 17, 18]. In this study, we applied this screening system to HCC and identified the protein degradation–related gene NTAQ1 on chromosome 8q. We aimed to elucidate the clinical significance of NTAQ1 in HCC and define its molecular role in HCC progression through bioinformatics and functional analyses.

Material and Methods

Public Datasets

mRNA expression, DNA copy number, mutation, and clinicopathological data for 356 patients with HCC were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) via UCSC Xena (https://xenabrowser.net/). Expression estimates at the transcript level were derived from RSEM‐normalized counts, transformed using log2(x + 1) (unitless normalized expression). In addition, data on protein expression and clinical pathology for 170 patients with HCC were downloaded from the Clinical Proteomic Tumor Analysis Consortium (CPTAC) via the Proteomic Data Commons (PDC; https://proteomic.datacommons.cancer.gov/pdc/). The normalization methods and z‐score calculation techniques used in analyses with datasets from PDC are described in Appendix S1. Protein expression data for human HCC cell lines were obtained from the proteomics dataset of The Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE) (Gygi Lab CCLE proteomics; https://gygi.hms.harvard.edu/publications/ccle.html). Data for the E3 ligase gene set were obtained from the E3 ligase list provided by the Epithelial Systems Biology Laboratory (ESBL) of the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute (NHLBI) (https://esbl.nhlbi.nih.gov/Databases/KSBP2/Targets/Lists/E3‐ligases/).

Patients With HCC and Collection of Clinical Specimens

Primary HCC specimens were collected from 53 patients with HCC (our cohort) who underwent surgery at Beppu Hospital, Kyushu University School of Medicine, and affiliated hospitals between 2001 and 2004. All patients were histologically diagnosed as HCC and were treated according to the Japanese Society of Hepatology Liver Cancer Treatment Guidelines [19]. Resected tumor tissue, paired non‐tumor tissue, and formalin‐fixed, paraffin‐embedded sections from patients with HCC were collected as previously described [20].

Selection of Candidate Genes

Using the TCGA HCC dataset, candidate genes were identified from 20,530 genes based on the following four criteria: (i) mRNA expression is elevated in tumor tissue compared to that in normal tissue (≥ 2‐fold change and p < 0.05); (ii) elevated mRNA levels are linked to a poor prognosis (p < 0.05); (iii) a positive correlation exists between DNA copy number and mRNA expression level (p < 0.05); and (iv) the gene is located on chromosome 1q or 8q. For further analysis, we focused on genes not previously reported to be associated with HCC.

Pan‐Cancer Analysis

Raw counts and quantile‐normalized mRNA data for all cancer types were obtained from TCGA. mRNA expression in each cancer type was compared with that in their corresponding non‐cancer tissue using the TNM plotter database (https://tnmplot.com/analysis/). The boxplots display these values in arbitrary units. Additionally, the Kaplan–Meier plotter (KM plotter) database (https://kmplot.com/analysis/) was used to assess the correlation between mRNA expression and patient survival in the cancer types described in Appendix S1.

To examine whether pan‐cancer NTAQ1 upregulation could be attributable to chromosome 8q copy‐number gain, we also calculated the frequency of 8q arm‐level gain using Broad GDAC Firehose GISTIC2 arm‐level outputs (broad_values_by_arm), which are continuous arm‐level values, and defined gain as an arm‐level value > 0. We further evaluated the association between NTAQ1 mRNA expression and NTAQ1 gene‐level copy‐number values (GISTIC2) within each TCGA cohort (tumor samples only) using Pearson correlation.

Cell Lines and Cell Culture

293FT cells were purchased from Invitrogen (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Human HCC cell lines HuH7, HLE, HLF, and HepG2 were acquired from the JCRB Cell Bank, and PLC/PRF/5 was sourced from DS Pharma Biomedical. Detailed cell culture procedures are provided in Appendix S1. When required, MG‐132 (MedChemExpress; HY‐13259), doxorubicin (Wako; 046‐21523), and actinomycin D (Wako; 010‐21263) were added to cells at final concentrations of 25 μM, 0.5 μM, and 0.1 μM, respectively.

RNA Extraction and Reverse Transcription–Quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT–qPCR)

Total RNA extraction and RT‐qPCR were performed according to the manufacturer's protocol and as previously described [21]. Details are provided in Appendix S1. Gene expression levels were measured using the oligonucleotide primers described in Table S1a.

Immunohistochemical Staining

Immunohistochemical staining of human HCC tissue specimens was performed as previously described [22]. All sections were counterstained with hematoxylin. The following primary antibody was used: anti‐NTAQ1 (1:100; PAB21749; Abnova). Histological evaluation of tumor specimens was independently performed by an experienced research pathologist at Kyushu University Beppu Hospital.

Protein Extraction and Western Blot Analysis

Protein extraction and western blot analyses were performed as previously described [23]. Blots were visualized using FUSION SOLO S (VILBER). The primary antibodies described in Table S1b were used for western blotting.

Generation of HCC Cells Stably Overexpressing NTAQ1

The lentiviral plasmid vector expressing NTAQ1 (pLV[Exp]‐Puro‐CMV. hNTAQ1 [NM_018024.3]) and its corresponding control vector (pLV[Exp]‐Puro‐CMV > ORF_Stuffer) were purchased from VectorBuilder. Generation of HCC cells that stably overexpress NTAQ1 was performed as previously described [16]. Stable cell lines were established by selection with 2 μg/mL for HLE cells and 1.75 μg/mL puromycin for HLF cells, respectively. Control cells were generated by transfecting cells with the empty control vector containing ORF_Stuffer.

Suppression of Gene Expression via Small Interfering RNA (siRNA)

NTAQ1‐specific siRNA (s30155) and negative control siRNA (Silencer Negative Control No. 1 siRNA; AM4611) were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific. Transfection of these siRNAs into HCC cells was performed using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Thermo Fisher) as previously described [24].

Colony Formation Assay

For NTAQ1 overexpression (OE) experiments and NTAQ1 knockdown experiments, we performed the colony formation assay. Images were acquired as described previously [25]. Details are provided in Appendix S1.

Cycloheximide (CHX) Chase Experiment

HLE and HLF cells were treated with cycloheximide (CHX; Sigma; C7698) at final concentrations of 100 μg/mL and 50 μg/mL, respectively, to inhibit de novo protein synthesis. Cells were harvested at 0, 4, 8, and 12 h post‐treatment. Cell lysates were prepared and subjected to western blot analysis.

Phosphorylated Histone H2AX (γH2AX) Assay

For both the DNA damage group (0.5 μM doxorubicin, 24 h) and the non‐damaged group, immunodetection of γH2AX in NTAQ1 overexpression (OE) HCC cells and empty‐vector control (em) cells was compared using the DNA Damage Detection Kit (Dojindo Laboratories) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Detailed procedures and quantification methods are provided in Appendix S1.

Comet Assay

Similar to the γH2AX assay, DNA damage and non‐damaged groups were prepared, and the comet assay was conducted using the OxiSelect Comet Assay Kit (Cell Biolabs, San Diego, CA, USA) following the manufacturer's instructions. Detailed procedures and quantification methods are provided in Appendix S1.

Apoptosis Assay

HLE and HLF cells were used for the in vitro apoptosis assay, performed according to protocols previously described [26]. Detailed information about the apoptosis assay and data processing is provided in Appendix S1.

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA)

Gene expression profiles of NTAQ1 and PRDM2 were obtained from the TCGA dataset. The relationship between NTAQ1 and PRDM2 expression and previously defined gene sets was examined using GSEA [27]. Gene sets defined by biological criteria were retrieved from the Human Molecular Signatures Database (v2024.1) (https://www.gsea‐msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb).

Statistical Analysis

Details of each statistical analysis using public datasets, along with the quantitative methods and statistical approaches used in the in vitro study, are described in Appendix S1. Unless otherwise indicated, cell‐based experiments were performed in at least three independent biological replicates, each measured in technical triplicates. Statistical analyses were performed using R software (v4.3.0), and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

NTAQ1 May Promote Tumor Progression on Chromosome 8q in HCC

We conducted a bioinformatics analysis using the TCGA dataset to identify novel candidate genes in HCC. As previously reported [28], frequent copy number amplifications were observed on the 1q and 8q chromosomal arms in HCC (Figure 1a). Focusing on genes located on chromosomes 1q and 8q, we identified NTAQ1, a gene associated with protein degradation, by applying the screening criteria outlined in the Methods section (Figure 1b). NTAQ1 mRNA levels in HCC tissues were significantly higher than those in normal tissues in both the TCGA dataset and our HCC cohort (p < 0.001) (Figure 1c). Furthermore, analysis of CPTAC data confirmed that NTAQ1 protein expression was significantly elevated in tumor tissues (p < 0.001) (Figure 1d). In the TCGA dataset, NTAQ1 mRNA expression levels and NTAQ1 DNA copy numbers showed a moderately strong positive correlation (r = 0.65, p < 0.01) (Figure 1e). Additionally, the mutation rate of NTAQ1 in the TCGA dataset was only 0.4% (Figure 1f). Furthermore, immunohistochemical staining revealed that NTAQ1 was more intensely stained in tumor cells than in normal hepatocytes, with predominant localization to the cell membrane and cytoplasm of tumor cells (Figure 1g). These findings suggest that NTAQ1 OE in HCC cells may result from DNA copy number amplification.

Prognostic and Clinicopathological Significance of NTAQ1 in Patients With HCC

We evaluated the prognostic and clinicopathological significance of NTAQ1 mRNA expression in HCC. In the TCGA dataset, patients with elevated NTAQ1 mRNA expression had significantly lower OS than those with low expression (p < 0.01) (Figure 1h). In the CPTAC cohort, although the median cutoff did not reveal a significant difference owing to limited statistical power with only 75 cases, the quartile cutoff showed significantly shorter OS in the high‐expression group (p = 0.03) (Figure 1i). Furthermore, multivariate analysis indicated that high NTAQ1 mRNA expression, together with T factor, was an independent prognostic factor in TCGA cases (hazard ratio = 1.63; 95% CI = 1.13–2.36; p < 0.01) (Table 1). Overall, these findings suggest that amplification of NTAQ1 on chromosome 8q represents a fundamental and dominant event in HCC progression, supporting its role as a candidate driver gene involved in tumor development.

NTAQ1 Is a Potential Therapeutic Target for HCC

To investigate whether NTAQ1 could serve as a promising therapeutic target, we performed a comprehensive analysis using TCGA data by incorporating NTAQ1 into the 323 E3 ligase–associated genes defined by the NHLBI and assessing its therapeutic potential. Notably, among genes with a twofold higher mRNA level in tumors than in normal tissues, and compared to other chemotherapy targets currently in preclinical development (DTX2 [29], RNF214 [30], UHRF2 [31], STUB1 [32], NEDD4 [33], PJA1 [34]), and NTAQ1 was the gene most strongly influenced by copy number amplification (Figure S1a,b). In contrast, in the TCGA dataset, UHRF2, STUB1, and NEDD4 did not show significantly increased mRNA levels in tumor tissues (Figure S1c). Additionally, only PJA1 was significantly associated with HCC survival rate (Figure S1d). These findings suggest that NTAQ1 could represent a promising therapeutic target among broadly defined protein degradation–related genes, similar to other genes with established therapeutic potential.

Exploratory Subtype‐Stratified Analyses for Patient Selection in NTAQ1‐Targeted Strategies

To further explore which HCC subgroup may be preferentially selected for NTAQ1‐directed strategies, we performed exploratory subtype‐stratified analyses in TCGA‐LIHC based on TP53 and CTNNB1 mutation status, two major driver genes in HCC. NTAQ1 mRNA expression did not differ significantly between TP53‐mutant and TP53‐wild‐type tumors (Wilcoxon test, p = 0.94) or between CTNNB1‐mutant and CTNNB1‐wild‐type tumors (Wilcoxon test, p = 0.82) (Figure S2a,b), indicating that mutation status in these canonical pathways alone does not define an NTAQ1‐high transcriptional subgroup.

We next examined the gene‐level copy‐number status of NTAQ1 using thresholded GISTIC2 values. The frequency of NTAQ1 copy‐number gain (GISTIC thresholded value ≥ 1) was not significantly different between TP53‐mutant and TP53‐wild‐type tumors (Fisher's exact test, p = 0.34), whereas it was significantly higher in the CTNNB1‐mutant group than in the CTNNB1‐wild‐type group (p = 1.22 × 10−5) (Figure S2c,d). In contrast, the frequency of NTAQ1 amplification (GISTIC thresholded value ≥ 2) did not differ significantly by either TP53 status (p = 0.88) or CTNNB1 status (p = 0.16) (Figure S2e,f).

Finally, we assessed the prognostic impact of NTAQ1 expression within mutation‐defined subsets. In both the TP53‐mutant and CTNNB1‐mutant subsets, Kaplan–Meier analyses showed no significant difference in overall survival between NTAQ1‐high and NTAQ1‐low groups (log‐rank p = 0.34 and p = 0.91, respectively) (Figure S2g,h).

Taken together, our exploratory TCGA‐LIHC subtype‐stratified analyses suggest that patient enrichment for NTAQ1‐directed strategies may be more practically guided by copy‐number/expression context (e.g., 8q gain and/or high NTAQ1 expression) than by TP53 or CTNNB1 mutation status alone.

NTAQ1 Promotes HCC Cell Proliferation

To investigate the biological role of NTAQ1 in HCC, we first performed GSEA using the TCGA dataset. NTAQ1 mRNA levels were positively correlated with gene sets involved in DNA Repair and the Unfolded Protein Response (UPR) (Figure 2a). RT‐qPCR and western blot analysis confirmed the establishment of HCC cell lines HLE and HLF stably overexpressing NTAQ1 (Figure 2b,c), which were used in subsequent cellular experiments. Colony formation assays showed that NTAQ1 OE significantly increased the number of colonies formed by both HLE and HLF cells (Figure 2d). These findings suggest that NTAQ1 promotes the proliferation of HCC cells.

PRDM2 Inversely Correlates With NTAQ1 Protein Expression

First, given that the biological function of NTAQ1 is broadly characterized as protein degradation, we hypothesized that high NTAQ1 expression contributes to HCC proliferation by promoting the degradation of specific tumor suppressor proteins. Using the CCLE dataset, we searched for tumor suppressor genes whose protein expression level in HCC cell lines was inversely correlated with that of NTAQ1. Analysis of the eight HCC cell lines with available NTAQ1 protein expression data revealed that CHFR, EPB41L3, and PRDM2 were the top three tumor suppressor proteins showing a strong inverse correlation with NTAQ1 (Figure 3a). Among these, PRDM2 was selected as the primary candidate based on two criteria: (i) low protein expression in tumor tissues compared with that in normal tissues and (ii) poorer prognosis in the low expression group (Figure 3b,c; Figure S3a,b).

NTAQ1 OE Promotes PRDM2 Protein Degradation in HCC Cells

To evaluate the biological significance of PRDM2 in HCC, GSEA was conducted, which showed negative enrichment of gene sets related to DNA Repair (Figure 3d), consistent with the GSEA results for NTAQ1 (Figure 2a). Western blot analysis further confirmed decreased PRDM2 protein expression in NTAQ1‐overexpressing HCC cells, demonstrating an inverse relationship (Figure 3e). Because the Arg/N‐degron pathway involving NTAQ1 functions as a UPS‐dependent protein degradation system [35], we examined whether NTAQ1‐mediated PRDM2 degradation depends on the proteasome. In NTAQ1‐overexpressing HCC cells treated with the proteasome inhibitor MG‐132 for 8 h (OE + MG‐132), PRDM2 degradation was reduced compared to that in the control cells (OE + DMSO) (Figure 3f), indicating that the proteasome is required for NTAQ1‐mediated regulation of PRDM2. To evaluate whether NTAQ1 affects PRDM2 protein stability, we monitored PRDM2 degradation using a CHX chase assay. In NTAQ1‐overexpressing HCC cells, PRDM2 degradation occurred significantly faster than in control cells carrying empty vectors, indicating decreased protein stability (Figure 3g). Collectively, these findings suggest that NTAQ1 promotes proteolytic degradation of the tumor suppressor protein PRDM2 in HCC cells.

NTAQ1 OE Impairs DNA Repair in HCC Cells

We examined the downstream pathway affected by the reduction of PRDM2 protein to understand how NTAQ1 promotes HCC cell proliferation. Given that the DNA repair hallmark gene set was positively enriched in the GSEA (Figure 2a), we assessed DNA repair capacity in NTAQ1‐overexpressing HCC cells using γH2AX and comet assays. In the γH2AX assay, both doxorubicin‐treated and untreated cells exhibited significantly higher nuclear γH2AX intensity in NTAQ1‐overexpressing cell lines than that in the empty vector controls (Figure 4a,b). Consistently, the comet assay showed a significant increase in the proportion of DNA in the tail region in NTAQ1‐overexpressing HCC cells under both the DNA damage–induced and untreated conditions (Figure 4c,d). These findings suggest that NTAQ1 OE leads to increased DNA damage accumulation, reflecting impaired DNA repair in HCC cells. To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying this effect, we performed western blot analysis. In NTAQ1‐overexpressing HCC cells, PRDM2 reduction was accompanied by decreased protein levels of BRCA1 and RAD51, key factors involved in the DNA damage response (DDR) and homologous recombination (HR) repair (Figure 4e). These findings are consistent with those of previous studies [36, 37], indicating that PRDM2 promotes BRCA1 accumulation, which is essential for RAD51 activation during HR‐mediated repair of DNA double‐strand breaks. In addition, the GSEA results shown in Figure 2a can be interpreted as compensatory transcriptional upregulation of DDR in response to accumulated DNA damage, rather than efficient repair, in line with our hypothesis. Overall, these findings suggest that NTAQ1 promotes HCC cell proliferation by impairing DNA repair capacity through PRDM2 protein degradation.

NTAQ1 OE Suppresses Apoptosis in HCC Cells

As another potential mechanism through which NTAQ1 promotes HCC cell proliferation, we assessed apoptotic markers based on the GSEA results (Figure 2a) and previous evidence indicating that early UPR supports cell survival [38]. Using the apoptosis assay, we found that NTAQ1 OE significantly reduced both early and late apoptotic cells (Figure 5a–d). Furthermore, western blot analysis of NTAQ1‐overexpressing cells showed decreased p53 and NOXA levels, increased Bcl‐2 expression, and reduced BAX and cleaved caspase‐3 levels, indicating suppression of apoptosis (Figure 5e). These findings are consistent with those of previous studies [39, 40] showing that PRDM2 OE induces apoptosis in meningioma and cervical cancer cells. Therefore, in HCC, NTAQ1 likely contributes to cell proliferation by promoting anti‐apoptosis through the downregulation of PRDM2 expression.

Knockdown of Overexpressed NTAQ1 Decreases Proliferative Capacity

To further validate the NTAQ1‐dependent phenotype, we knocked down NTAQ1 by introducing siRNA targeting NTAQ1 into HCC cells overexpressing NTAQ1. This resulted in decreased NTAQ1 mRNA and protein levels, as accompanied by reduced proliferative capacity (Figure S4a–c). In parallel, knockdown increased the protein levels of PRDM2, BRCA1, and p53, which are considered downstream targets of NTAQ1 (Figure S4b).

NTAQ1 Knockdown Reduces the Tumor‐Promoting Effects of NTAQ1‐Overexpressing HLE Cells In Vivo

To evaluate the in vivo significance of NTAQ1, we conducted a subcutaneous xenograft assay using HLE cells. Stable NTAQ1‐overexpressing HLE cells were transduced with either shNTAQ1 or a scramble control, and empty‐vector HLE cells transduced with the scramble control served as the baseline, creating em + shCtrl, OE + shCtrl, and OE + shNTAQ1 groups. Successful knockdown of NTAQ1 in OE + shNTAQ1 cells was confirmed by western blot prior to implantation (Figure S5a). While tumor growth was generally modest, OE + shCtrl xenografts grew faster than em + shCtrl tumors, but NTAQ1 knockdown in NTAQ1‐overexpressing cells significantly reduced this growth (Figure S5b,c). Immunohistochemical analysis of the xenograft tumors revealed stronger NTAQ1 staining, markedly reduced PRDM2 staining, and increased Ki‐67 positivity in the OE + shCtrl group; these effects were partly reversed in the OE + shNTAQ1 group (Figure S5d,e). Overall, these in vivo results support the tumor‐promoting role of NTAQ1 and show an inverse relationship with PRDM2 expression.

Co‐Immunoprecipitation Supports an Association Between NTAQ1 and PRDM2

To address whether NTAQ1 associates with PRDM2 in cells, we performed co‐immunoprecipitation assays in NTAQ1 OE HLE and HLF cells. Because PRDM2 is markedly reduced in NTAQ1‐OE cells, cells were pretreated with MG‐132 (25 μM) for 8 h before lysis to stabilize PRDM2. PRDM2 immunoprecipitation co‐precipitated NTAQ1, whereas IgG controls did not (Figure S6). These data support an association between NTAQ1 and PRDM2 in HCC cells.

Restoration of PRDM2 in NTAQ1‐Overexpressing Cells Partially Reverses NTAQ1‐Dependent Phenotypes

To clarify the causal role of PRDM2 reduction in the NTAQ1‐dependent phenotype, we restored PRDM2 expression in NTAQ1 OE HLE and HLF cells by transient transfection of a PRDM2 expression plasmid. PRDM2 restoration increased BRCA1 and p53 levels compared with empty vector controls, without altering NTAQ1 expression (Figure S7a). Functionally, PRDM2 restoration significantly reduced colony formation in NTAQ1‐OE cells (Figure S7b,c). Moreover, PRDM2 restoration attenuated the doxorubicin (0.5 μM, 24 h)‐induced increase in nuclear γH2AX intensity (Figure S7d,e), suggesting partial recovery of DNA damage response capacity. Finally, in actinomycin D–induced apoptosis assays (0.1 μM, 24 h), PRDM2 restoration increased apoptotic fractions compared with empty vector controls (Figure S7f,g). Collectively, although these rescue experiments do not exclude additional NTAQ1‐dependent targets, they further strengthen the evidence that PRDM2 is a key functional mediator downstream of NTAQ1.

NTAQ1 Is Highly Expressed Across Various Cancers

We analyzed NTAQ1 mRNA expression across multiple cancer types and corresponding normal tissues using the TCGA dataset. NTAQ1 expression was higher in 15 different cancer types, including HCC, than in the respective non‐cancerous tissues (Figure 6a,b). Moreover, elevated levels of NTAQ1 expression were linked to poorer prognosis in several types of cancer (Figure 6c). These findings suggest that NTAQ1 may act as a common driver gene that promotes tumor growth across multiple cancer types.

To further assess whether elevated NTAQ1 expression in non‐liver cancers could be explained, at least in part, by chromosome 8q copy‐number gain, we analyzed TCGA copy‐number data across 15 tumor types. Chromosome 8q arm‐level gain was observed in multiple cancer types, with frequencies reaching approximately 50% in many cohorts. NTAQ1 copy number correlated positively with NTAQ1 mRNA expression across cohorts, with moderate or stronger correlations (Pearson r ≥ 0.4) in most cohorts (Table S2). These findings support a contribution of 8q copy‐number gain to pan‐cancer NTAQ1 upregulation.

Discussion

In this study, NTAQ1, located within an amplified region on chromosome 8q, was identified as a novel driver gene in HCC, and its molecular mechanisms were elucidated (Figure 7). NTAQ1 was overexpressed in HCC tissues, and elevated levels were strongly associated with a worse prognosis. Moreover, NTAQ1 OE markedly enhanced HCC cell proliferation. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate that NTAQ1 plays a critical role in HCC progression by promoting the protein degradation of the tumor suppressor PRDM2 and by negatively regulating DNA repair and apoptosis, thereby highlighting its potential as a novel tumor‐promoting factor in HCC.

Graphical abstract. Schema indicating how NTAQ1 promotes HCC proliferation. Created with BioRender.

Although considerable knowledge has been gained regarding NTAQ1's role in the Arg/N‐degron pathway and its substrate recognition mechanism, little is known about its function in tumor biology. Our pan‐cancer analysis further suggests that NTAQ1 may act as a common driver gene across multiple cancer types. In this study, we provide a detailed functional characterization of its tumor‐promoting role in HCC. We propose the following working hypothesis: Frequent 8q copy number amplification in HCC leads to elevated NTAQ1 expression, which specifically promotes Arg/N‐degron pathway–dependent degradation of tumor suppressor proteins with N‐terminal glutamine, including neo‐N‐termini. This perspective highlights NTAQ1 as an upstream component of the Arg/N‐degron pathway, in contrast to recent reports on individual terminal E3–substrate pairs such as FBXO22–PTEN [41] and UBR5–Rb [42].

To develop this framework, tumor suppressor genes exhibiting an inverse correlation with NTAQ1 expression were identified through in silico analysis as potential substrates targeted by NTAQ1. This approach enabled us to focus on PRDM2 and validate its functional connection. Although current evidence is insufficient to definitively conclude that PRDM2 is a direct substrate of NTAQ1, our co‐immunoprecipitation data support an association between NTAQ1 and PRDM2, and our PRDM2 rescue experiments clarify the causal contribution of PRDM2 loss to NTAQ1‐driven phenotypes. Given NTAQ1's biological role, further studies, such as N‐terminal and neo–N‐terminal deamidation analysis using mass spectrometry, are warranted.

PRDM2 is a chromatin‐modifying transcriptional regulator containing a PR/SET domain that is often epigenetically silenced via promoter methylation in various cancer types [43]. However, functional evidence linking this silencing to tumor proliferation remains limited. Our findings suggest an alternative mechanism involving post‐translational regulation, specifically UPS‐mediated protein degradation, rather than transcriptional control. We demonstrate that enhanced PRDM2 protein degradation contributes to HCC proliferation by (i) reducing DNA repair capacity and (ii) promoting apoptosis resistance. The former effect (i) is supported by reports showing promotion of HR by PRDM2‐mediated BRCA1 retention [36], whereas the latter (ii) aligns with findings from previous hepatoma studies [44]. Collectively, our findings reveal a novel pathophysiological consequence of reduced PRDM2 expression.

Inhibiting the Arg/N‐degron pathway represents a promising cancer treatment strategy, as it can enhance the stability of tumor suppressor proteins or facilitate the degradation of oncogenic proteins. Active research is underway to develop inhibitors targeting N‐recognition factors and their upstream components [7]. Based on these findings, NTAQ1, a deamidase, emerges as a potential therapeutic target, and the development of small molecules that directly block its active site represents a primary therapeutic objective. However, because this approach lacks selectivity for specific substrates, concerns regarding on‐target toxicity remain. As a more targeted alternative, proteolysis‐targeting chimeras (PROTACs) [45]—some of which have been reported to utilize components of the Arg/N‐degron pathway—may provide a more substrate‐selective approach. In this strategy, a PROTAC is designed to degrade the N‐recognin that targets PRDM2, thereby indirectly stabilizing PRDM2.

This study has some limitations. First, the substrate selectivity of NTAQ1 may be broader than currently demonstrated, and the involvement of additional tumor suppressor proteins in its tumor‐promoting effects cannot be excluded. Addressing this will require additional validation, including comprehensive N‐terminal proteomic analyses. Second, the inverse correlation between NTAQ1 and PRDM2 protein expression was not observed in the clinical cohort (Figure S8), a discrepancy that may reflect tumor heterogeneity and variations in tumor purity.

In conclusion, this study identifies NTAQ1 as a novel driver gene in HCC and highlights its potential as a promising therapeutic target. As future proteomic studies further elucidate the NTAQ1–PRDM2 relationship, NTAQ1 may emerge as a key molecule for elucidating the mechanisms underlying HCC progression.
