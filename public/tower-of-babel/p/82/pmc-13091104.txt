Submandibular gland oncocytoma: Imaging features and histopathological correlation of a rare benign salivary gland tumor

Introduction

Oncocytoma of the submandibular gland is an exceedingly rare benign epithelial tumor, representing a very small fraction of all salivary gland neoplasms. It is composed predominantly of oncocytes—large epithelial cells characterized by abundant eosinophilic granular cytoplasm resulting from mitochondrial accumulation. While oncocytomas most frequently arise in the parotid gland, involvement of the submandibular gland is exceptional, with only a limited number of cases reported in the literature. These tumors typically occur in older adults, most commonly between 50 and 70 years of age, without a clear gender predilection, and usually present as painless, slowly enlarging masses that may clinically mimic other benign salivary gland tumors such as pleomorphic adenoma or Warthin’s tumor [1].

The diagnostic evaluation of submandibular gland oncocytoma relies on a multimodal approach combining clinical assessment, imaging, cytological analysis, and histopathological examination. Fine-needle aspiration cytology plays an important role in the preoperative workup by suggesting an oncocytic lesion through the identification of polygonal cells with abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm and prominent nucleoli. However, cytological findings alone may be insufficient to reliably distinguish benign oncocytoma from malignant oncocytic neoplasms, and definitive diagnosis requires histopathological examination of the excised specimen. Cross-sectional imaging, particularly contrast-enhanced computed tomography, typically demonstrates a well-circumscribed lesion within the submandibular gland and is essential for evaluating tumor extent and its relationship to adjacent structures. Although F-18 FDG PET/CT may demonstrate increased metabolic activity due to the high mitochondrial content of oncocytes, its findings are nonspecific and should be interpreted with caution [2].

Surgical excision of the tumor along with the affected submandibular gland remains the treatment of choice and allows both definitive diagnosis and curative management. Given the benign nature of oncocytoma, radical surgical approaches are generally unnecessary. Recurrence and malignant transformation are exceedingly rare but have been reported in isolated cases, underscoring the importance of accurate diagnosis and appropriate follow-up. Overall, the prognosis of submandibular gland oncocytoma is excellent, with most patients achieving complete recovery following surgical treatment and experiencing minimal postoperative morbidity [3].

Case presentation

A 52-year-old male patient with no significant past medical history presented to our department with a slowly enlarging right submandibular mass that had been evolving over approximately three years (Fig. 1, Fig. 2). The lesion was initially small and progressively increased in size over time. It was painless and not associated with dysphagia, dyspnea, fever, weight loss, or night sweats. There was no history of cervical irradiation, prior head and neck surgery, or regular medication use. The patient reported no tobacco, alcohol, or illicit substance use and had no known drug allergies.

Frontal clinical photograph demonstrating a visible swelling in the right submandibular region without overlying skin changes.

Lateral clinical photograph showing a well-defined right submandibular mass with preserved cervical contour.

On physical examination, a firm, well-circumscribed, non-tender mass measuring approximately 4 cm was palpated in the right submandibular region. The lesion was mobile relative to both the overlying skin and deeper planes, with no cutaneous changes or signs of local inflammation. No cervical lymphadenopathy was detected, and cranial nerve examination was unremarkable.

Cervical ultrasound demonstrated a well-defined, oval-shaped hypoechoic mass measuring 4.6 × 2.3 cm located within the inferior portion of the right submandibular gland (Fig. 3). The lesion exhibited homogeneous internal echotexture with moderate internal vascularity on color Doppler imaging, without posterior acoustic shadowing or surrounding inflammatory changes.

Ultrasound examination of the right submandibular gland demonstrating a well-circumscribed, oval-shaped, hypoechoic lesion measuring approximately 4.6 × 2.3 cm, with homogeneous internal echotexture, consistent with a benign salivary gland tumor.

Contrast-enhanced computed tomography of the neck revealed a well-circumscribed, encapsulated soft-tissue mass with homogeneous attenuation and uniform enhancement (Fig. 4). No areas of necrosis, calcification, or infiltrative margins were identified. There was no evidence of invasion of adjacent muscles, vascular encasement, or involvement of neural structures, supporting a benign etiology. Magnetic resonance imaging was not performed, as ultrasound and CT findings demonstrated typical benign features without suspicion of deep extension or malignant behavior, consistent with published imaging recommendations for benign submandibular gland tumors.

Axial contrast-enhanced computed tomography scan of the neck showing a well-defined, encapsulated soft-tissue mass within the right submandibular gland, exhibiting homogeneous attenuation and uniform enhancement, without evidence of invasion of adjacent structures.

Based on clinical and radiological findings, a benign tumor of the right submandibular gland was suspected. Fine-needle aspiration cytology was performed as part of the preoperative assessment and suggested an oncocytic lesion. Surgical excision of the right submandibular gland was therefore indicated.

The patient underwent complete transcervical excision of the right submandibular gland under general anesthesia. The postoperative course was uneventful. Gross examination of the surgical specimen revealed a well-encapsulated, tan-yellow mass. Histopathological analysis demonstrated a well-defined tumor composed of uniform oncocytic cells arranged in lobules, with abundant eosinophilic granular cytoplasm and round nuclei, without cytological atypia, mitotic activity, or necrosis, confirming the diagnosis of submandibular gland oncocytoma (Fig. 5).

Histopathological section of the excised submandibular gland (hematoxylin and eosin stain) demonstrating a well-encapsulated tumor composed of uniform oncocytic epithelial cells arranged in lobules, with abundant eosinophilic granular cytoplasm and round nuclei, without cytologic atypia or mitotic activity, consistent with oncocytoma.

Discussion

Oncocytic neoplasms of the salivary glands are characterized by the proliferation of oncocytes, epithelial cells with abundant eosinophilic granular cytoplasm resulting from marked mitochondrial hyperplasia. These tumors encompass a spectrum ranging from benign oncocytoma to malignant oncocytic carcinoma. Although oncocytomas most frequently arise in the parotid gland, involvement of the submandibular gland remains exceedingly rare, accounting for less than 1% of all salivary gland tumors. Submandibular gland oncocytomas (SMGOs) typically present as slow-growing, well-circumscribed, non-invasive masses, whereas oncocytic carcinomas exhibit infiltrative growth, cytologic atypia, and metastatic potential. Given this overlap within the oncocytic tumor spectrum, accurate diagnosis requires careful correlation of clinical, radiological, and pathological findings [4].

Salivary gland tumors represent approximately 3% of all head and neck neoplasms, with 8%-10% arising from the submandibular gland. Oncocytic tumors constitute only 0.2%-0.6% of all salivary gland neoplasms, and fewer than 35 cases of SMGO have been reported in the literature. These lesions predominantly affect patients between 50 and 70 years of age and show no consistent sex predilection. In contrast, submandibular oncocytic carcinoma is exceptionally rare, with fewer than 15 well-documented cases worldwide, typically occurring in older individuals and demonstrating more aggressive biological behavior, including nodal or distant metastasis [5].

The pathogenesis of oncocytic tumors is not fully elucidated but is believed to be closely related to mitochondrial hyperplasia and dysfunction within epithelial cells. Proposed contributing factors include aging, oxidative stress, and prior radiation exposure. While most oncocytic carcinomas arise de novo, malignant transformation from a pre-existing oncocytoma has been reported. Histopathological features suggestive of malignancy include infiltrative growth, nuclear pleomorphism, elevated proliferative indices such as Ki-67, and perineural or vascular invasion [6].

Clinically, SMGO usually manifests as a painless, firm, and mobile mass in the submandibular region, without overlying skin changes or cranial nerve deficits. Inflammatory symptoms are uncommon and generally occur only in the setting of secondary infection or compression. Constitutional symptoms such as fever, weight loss, or night sweats are typically absent, which helps distinguish these tumors from more aggressive malignant neoplasms [7]. Routine laboratory investigations are usually non-contributory, emphasizing the importance of imaging and pathological evaluation.

From a radiological standpoint, imaging plays a central role in the diagnostic workup of SMGO. Ultrasound typically reveals a well-defined, hypoechoic mass with homogeneous internal architecture and variable vascularity, but it lacks specificity for assessing local invasion or malignancy. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) is particularly valuable, often demonstrating a well-circumscribed, encapsulated lesion with homogeneous attenuation and uniform enhancement, without evidence of necrosis, calcification, infiltrative margins, or invasion of adjacent structures. These features strongly favor a benign etiology and help differentiate oncocytoma from malignant salivary gland tumors, which more frequently show irregular borders, heterogeneous enhancement, and invasive behavior [8].

Fine-needle aspiration cytology (FNAC) represents a useful preoperative diagnostic tool by suggesting an oncocytic lesion through the identification of large polygonal cells with abundant eosinophilic granular cytoplasm, round nuclei, and prominent nucleoli. However, cytological findings alone are insufficient to reliably distinguish benign oncocytoma from malignant oncocytic neoplasms because of significant cytomorphological overlap. Consequently, FNAC should be regarded as an adjunctive diagnostic modality rather than a definitive one, and histopathological examination of the excised specimen remains mandatory for final diagnosis [9].

Histopathologically, oncocytomas are typically well-encapsulated tumors composed of uniform oncocytic cells arranged in solid nests or lobules, lacking significant cytologic atypia, mitotic activity, or necrosis. Adjacent oncocytic hyperplasia is frequently observed and may represent a reactive or preneoplastic phenomenon. In the present case, immunohistochemical analysis was not performed, as the diagnosis was established based on characteristic hematoxylin–eosin findings. According to the literature, oncocytomas usually express cytokeratins such as CK7, CK8, and CK19, while markers such as p63 and SOX10 are typically negative and may aid in excluding malignant oncocytic neoplasms when diagnostic uncertainty persists [10].

In selected cases, 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/computed tomography may demonstrate increased metabolic activity due to the high mitochondrial content of oncocytes. However, this finding is nonspecific and may mimic malignancy, potentially leading to false-positive interpretations. Therefore, PET/CT should be reserved for cases with suspected metastatic disease or equivocal findings on conventional imaging and interpreted with caution in oncocytic tumors [11].

The differential diagnosis of SMGO includes both benign and malignant oncocytic neoplasms. Warthin’s tumor can be distinguished by its characteristic lymphoid stroma, which is absent in oncocytoma. Pleomorphic adenoma may show oncocytic metaplasia but typically exhibits a biphasic epithelial–stromal architecture. Malignant mimics such as oncocytic mucoepidermoid carcinoma and oncocytic acinic cell carcinoma often express markers like SOX10 and p63, unlike oncocytomas. Metastatic oncocytic tumors, particularly from renal cell or hepatocellular carcinoma, should also be considered in patients with a relevant clinical history. Definitive classification relies on integrated evaluation of clinical presentation, imaging findings, cytology, histology, and immunophenotyping when necessary [12].

Surgical excision via transcervical submandibulectomy remains the treatment of choice, allowing complete tumor removal while preserving adjacent neurovascular structures. Intraoperative frozen section analysis may be helpful in selected cases to confirm benignity and guide surgical decision-making. Radical surgical approaches and adjuvant therapies are generally unnecessary in confirmed benign oncocytoma, whereas wider excision and lymphadenectomy may be considered if malignancy is suspected intraoperatively [13].

Postoperative outcomes are typically favorable, with most patients experiencing an uneventful recovery. Recurrence and malignant transformation of SMGO are exceedingly rare but have been reported in isolated cases. Consequently, long-term clinical follow-up with periodic imaging may be advisable, particularly in patients with large tumors or atypical histopathological features [14].

Overall, the prognosis of submandibular gland oncocytoma is excellent following complete surgical excision. Although rare cases of malignant transformation into oncocytic carcinoma have been documented, these appear to represent exceptional events, with clinical behavior influenced by factors such as proliferative index, nodal involvement, and metastatic spread. Continued long-term surveillance is therefore prudent to ensure early detection of rare adverse outcomes [15].

Conclusion

Submandibular gland oncocytoma is a rare benign epithelial tumor characterized by slow growth and nonspecific clinical presentation. Accurate diagnosis relies on a multidisciplinary approach integrating clinical assessment, radiological evaluation, and definitive histopathological confirmation, as cytological findings alone may be insufficient to exclude malignant oncocytic neoplasms. Surgical excision of the affected submandibular gland remains the treatment of choice and is generally curative. Although recurrence and malignant transformation are exceedingly rare, isolated cases have been reported in the literature, supporting the recommendation for long-term clinical follow-up, particularly in patients with large or atypical lesions.
