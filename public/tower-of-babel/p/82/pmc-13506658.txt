Perspectives of Oral Health Practitioners Working in Rural New South Wales: Culture, Wellbeing and Workforce Sustainability
Authors: Lisa Hai My Do, Jessica Biles, Bradley Christian, Woosung Sohn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundWhile limited research has explored the experiences of this workforce, oral health practitioners in rural areas face distinct cultural and personal challenges that influence workforce sustainability and delivery of care. This study aims to understand the cultural and personal experiences of oral health practitioners working in rural New South Wales and identify factors influencing their cultural capabilities, personal wellbeing and workforce sustainability.MethodsA cross‐sectional survey was conducted among oral health practitioners across three rural Local Health Districts in New South Wales. The survey included items on sociodemographic characteristics, cultural capabilities and awareness, and personal experiences. Data was analysed using descriptive statistics and subgroup comparisons made based on profession (dentists vs. other oral health practitioners) and years of experience (less or more than 10 years).ResultsNineteen participants completed the survey, primarily dentists and oral health therapists, with few dental therapists. Most reported initial cultural awareness training; however, less than half reported ongoing training. Most participants reported personal growth associated with their role. Dentists reported greater access to support systems and training opportunities than other oral health practitioners.ConclusionThere is need for continuous cultural capability training and equitable support systems to strengthen rural oral health workforce sustainability. Despite small participant numbers, this study provides valuable insight into practitioner experiences and identifies opportunities for a culturally safe and wellbeing‐focused workforce in rural NSW.

Introduction

The inequalities in oral health outcomes between metropolitan and rural communities in Australia have been recognised as a significant public health concern [1, 2]. Rural regions often face limited availability of oral health services and persistent workforce shortages—factors that collectively sustain inequalities in oral health outcomes among residents [1, 3]. These inequalities are further compounded by socioeconomic disadvantage, geographical isolation and reduced access to specialist and preventive services [4]. Despite systematic and intersectional barriers, regional communities have demonstrated strength such as community‐led initiatives that have proved lasting change; one of which is the role of regional health professionals [5]. Within these contexts, oral health practitioners play a critical role in maintaining community oral health, often working across broad scopes to bridge gaps in service delivery and continuity of care [6].

Although the broader rural health workforce has been widely studied, much of this research has concentrated on medical and nursing professionals, with comparatively limited attention to oral health practitioners [7, 8]. A growing body of evidence highlights that oral health practitioners in rural regions encounter unique challenges. A nuanced understanding of the factors influencing rural oral health practice, spanning personal and cultural perspectives, is essential to improve community outcomes and inform recruitment, retention and support strategies tailored to this workforce. Prior studies highlight recurring challenges, including professional isolation, limited clinical resources and cultural barriers to care [9, 10]. However, there remain limited insights into how oral health practitioners navigate these realities while balancing personal and cultural dynamics that shape their work in rural New South Wales (NSW) [3, 11].

Beyond access and service models, cultural and personal experiences are central to effective and sustainable oral health care, particularly in rural and remote settings. Clinician cultural capabilities are increasingly recognised as a cornerstone of effective and equitable oral health care delivery, particularly in regions with diverse populations and large Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities [12, 13, 14]. At the same time, personal factors, such as family support, community integration and career satisfaction, play a central role in practitioners' decision to relocate to, remain in or leave rural practices [9, 15, 16]. Positive rural experiences, opportunities for professional growth and meaningful connections with the community can enhance practitioner wellbeing and retention [10, 14, 16]. Addressing these interlinked cultural and personal dimensions can therefore strengthen both workforce sustainability and patient centred care in rural regions.

Despite emerging recognition of these factors, there is limited empirical data examining how oral health practitioners navigate the cultural and personal dynamics of rural practice. This paper reports on cultural and personal aspects of oral health practitioners working across multiple rural Local Health Districts (LHDs) in New South Wales (NSW). The discoveries from this paper are intended to inform workforce planning, enhance practitioner support systems and contribute to culturally safe and sustainable models of rural oral health care.

Methods

Study Design

This study followed the STROBE reporting guidelines for observation research and used a cross‐sectional survey design. Oral health practitioners working in rural NSW Local Health Districts (LHDs) were the target population. In New South Wales, LHDs are responsible for delivery of public oral health services within defined geographic areas. The participating LHDs span inland and coastal communities, covering approximately 125,000 to > 250,000 km2 and serving populations of approximately 200,000–275,000 people per LHD. Considering the population of NSW is approximately 8.6 million, each LHD represents roughly 2.3%–3.2% of the total NSW population [17]. Rurality of LHDs was classified using the Modified Monash Model (MMM) with participating sites located in MM5—Small Rural Towns [18].

Participants

Eligible participants were purposively recruited from three LHDs in NSW and consisted of oral health practitioners directly involved in service delivery (e.g., dentists, oral health therapists, dental therapists, dental hygienists, dental prosthetists). Individuals not involved in direct clinical provision were excluded from the study. Email invitations containing the survey link were disseminated via designated LHD representatives (one senior dentist and one senior oral health therapist, with the latter covering two LHDs due to proximity). Data collection occurred over a two‐week period (17th of September 2024–1st of October 2024), which included recruitment and survey completion.

Survey Instrument and Data Collection

All participants were provided with a digital Participant Information Sheet outlining the study objectives, procedures and potential risks.

The survey was developed by the research team following a scoping review of relevant literature and was piloted with five oral health practitioners for clarity prior to dissemination. It comprised 15 items organised into four sections. Items used multiple choice and five‐point Likert type response formats. This paper reports on the three sections related to culture and personal experiences: (1) social‐demographic characteristics, (2) cultural capability and cultural training opportunities and (3) personal experiences and the impact of rural employment.

Data Analysis

Data were exported from REDCap into Microsoft Excel and analysed using SPSS Version 28. Descriptive statistics were used to summarise participant characteristics and item responses. For subgroup analysis, comparisons were conducted for dentists with other oral health practitioners with < 10 years versus 10 or more year's experience.

Ethical Considerations

Ethics approval was obtained from the Greater Western Human Research Ethics Committee (2024/ETH00501). Participants were provided with a detailed information sheet outlining study aims, procedures and potential risks. Informed consent was obtained electronically prior to commencement; the survey used mandatory consent and skip logic to ensure only consenting participants proceeded. Responses were anonymous and de‐identified using unique study IDs. Data were stored securely on the University of Sydney's licence eNotebook (LabArchives) during analysis, accessible only to the research team, and will be archived into the University's Research Data Store (RDS) for 5 years before secure destruction.

Results

Participants

Of the 93 oral health practitioners across three rural Local Health Districts (LHDs) in NSW, 21 completed the survey, yielding a response rate of 22.6%. Two responses were excluded due to ineligibility, resulting in a final sample of 19 participants. Seven returned questionnaires had missing data in the personal experiences section. These were not excluded from the analysis and the missing data remained.

Majority of participants were dentists (42.1%) or oral health therapists (42.1%) and nearly all participants identified as female (94.7%). Most participants reported having over 10 years of clinical experience (63.2%), and two identified as Aboriginal (10.5%).

Of the 19 participants, 21.1% had < 10 years of both clinical experience and rural residency. A further 15.8% had < 10 years of clinical experience but more than 10 years of rural living, while an equal proportion (15.8%) had more than 10 years of clinical experience but < 10 years of rural residency. Nearly half of participants (47.4%) reported more than 10 years of both clinical experience and rural residency.

Cultural Experiences

Majority of participants (n = 15, 78.9%) indicated that cultural awareness training was provided at the start of their employment however, only 42.1% (n = 8) reported ongoing yearly updated trainings. Of the dentists, most reported that cultural awareness training (an educational training that increases the practitioner's understanding of how social and cultural factors influence the health beliefs and behaviours of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples) [19] was provided at the start of their employment and in contrast, responses from clinicians other than dentists (oral health therapists, dental therapists) were more varied, with some agreeing to the availability of the cultural awareness training (n = 8, 72.7%) but a notable portion disagreeing to such availability (n = 3, 27.2%).

Most participants also reported facing cultural challenges when providing oral health care (n = 12, 63.2%) and despite more than half (n = 11, 57.9%) agreeing that support was available for cultural challenges, 31.6% (n = 6) still reported they felt unequipped to manage such challenges. A mixed response was reported from participants on the ability to adapt and learn in a new geographical setting (e.g., rural region) with 68.4% (n = 13) agreeing while 21.1% (n = 4) disagreed.

Personal Experiences

Some participants (n = 8, 42.1%) found it easy to adapt to a new community and environment, while others (n = 11, 57.9%) expressed difficulty adjusting to life away from family and friends. A comparison between clinicians with more and < 10 years clinical experience revealed that those with less clinical experience adapt more easily to new environments and life away from loved ones (71.4%); those with more than 10 years reported a mixed response, 66.7% agreeing and 33.3% remaining neutral.

While all practitioners acknowledged the availability of social well‐being services (n = 14, 73.7%), a larger proportion of dentists (n = 7, 87.5%) reported their availability as compared to practitioners other than dentists. Participants overall perceived their roles as having contributed positively to their personal growth (n = 15, 78.9%) however, responses from oral health practitioners other than dentists were more varied, with some remaining neutral to this statement (n = 2, 18.2%).

More than half of participants (n = 10, 52.6%) reported not receiving employment or relocation incentives for their current position. This group comprised both dentists (n = 4, 40.1%) and other oral health practitioners (n = 6, 60.0%), with an even distribution across levels of clinical experience (n = 5, 50.0% with more than 10 years clinical experience; n = 5, 50.0% with < 10 years clinical experience).

Discussion

This study provides important insights into the cultural and personal experiences of oral health practitioners working in rural NSW. By examining the workforce profile, access to cultural training and the personal experiences of this workforce, the findings highlight both the strengths and challenges within the current rural oral health system.

Cultural Capabilities and Workforce Preparedness

Most participants report receiving some form of cultural awareness training at the start of their employment, though fewer indicated access to regular, ongoing updates. This aligns with previous studies from broader healthcare research, suggesting that while cultural training is increasingly integrated into health service orientation, it is rarely maintained or evaluated for impact and continuity [12, 14, 20]. The finding that over half of the participants experience cultural challenges indicates a continuing gap between initial training and clinical application. Cultural challenges were defined as barriers arising from differences in cultural values, beliefs and practices that influence health behaviours and interactions between practitioner and patient. Framed by principles of cultural safety and the Social Ecological Model, these challenges may affect communication, trust, health‐seeking behaviours, perception of oral health and engagement with services [21, 22]. For practitioners in rural NSW, where Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander populations represent a significant proportion of the community, cultural safety must extend beyond awareness to include confidence, adaptability and institutional support. According to AHPRA, cultural safety requires health practitioners to have the knowledge, skills and attitudes to provide optimal care by critically reflecting on their own biases and power differentials, acknowledging cultural differences and understanding how their own culture influences their practice [23]. This is a dynamic and non‐linear process that requires reoccurring and multi‐faceted support mechanisms.

Embedding continuous and reflective cultural training across the oral health workforce could help reduce the likelihood of cultural challenge encounters in both rural regions and metropolitan areas [24, 25]. Continuous cultural safety training has been shown to improve healthcare professionals' knowledge, attitudes and communication skills; therefore, enhancing their ability to navigate various cultural challenges [24, 26, 27]. Models of cultural safety in dentistry education, such as those proposed by Forsyth et al. [13], highlight the need for contextually relevant and ongoing engagement rather than one‐off sessions [20]. Furthermore, organisational support systems may enable practitioners to navigate cross‐cultural encounters with confidence—through committed leadership, formal policies, adequate resources and partnerships with local Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities—ensuring that rural oral health services are both clinically effective and culturally safe [14, 20].

While participants reported on the availability of initial and ongoing cultural awareness training as well as encounters with cultural challenges, the findings of this study did not establish a direct relationship between cultural awareness training and recruitment or retention outcomes. Instead, cultural awareness training emerged as an important professional and contextual consideration that may have shaped participants' sense of preparedness and confidence when facing cultural challenges.

A systematic scoping review by McCalman et al. [28] described that effective cultural competence in healthcare is not dependent on the individual practitioner's cultural training, but on the multi‐level interventions spanning management systems, workforce development, service delivery and community engagement. The mixed responses about cultural support being available and the perceived preparedness of participants suggest an opportunity for LHDs to implement these multi‐level interventions to better position rural oral health practitioners in cross‐cultural encounters, strengthen competencies and ensure cultural safety.

Personal Experiences in Rural Practice

Consistent with prior research, this study found that personal adjustment and social integration remain a significant challenge for rural oral health practitioners [7, 15, 16]. Although this is not unique to rural practice, the workforce maldistribution in Australia makes it necessary for some oral health practitioners to relocate to rural and remote regions where there are often more employment opportunities and service demand [9, 29]. More than half of the participants reported difficulty adjusting to life away from family and friends, echoing long‐recognised challenges of geographic and social isolation in rural practice. Yet, despite these personal challenges, most respondents identified positive outcomes such as increased adaptability, professional confidence and personal growth, demonstrating the resilience and commitment characteristic of the rural oral health workforce.

Limited access to relocation incentives was reported in this study as a contributing factor to potential workforce turnover [15]. Consisted with this, more than half of participants in this study reported not receiving employment or relocation incentives, regardless of professional role or level of clinical experience. This finding suggests that gaps in incentive provision may be systemic rather than confined to specific workforce groups. The absence of such supports may diminish the attractiveness of rural positions and contribute to workforce instability, particularly in the context of the broader professional and personal demands associated with rural practice [30, 31]. Like findings in broader rural health literature, financial and career support measures, when combined with community connection and a sense of purpose, have been shown to improve practitioner retention and satisfaction [10, 15, 16]. Addressing these factors requires a wide‐picture approach that balances professional incentives with psychosocial and community‐based supports.

Professional Differences: Dentists and Other Oral Health Practitioners

The comparison between dentists and other oral health practitioners revealed differences in perceived workplace support and exposure of cultural training. While most dentists reported receiving cultural training, responses from other oral health practitioners were more varied. This suggests potential inconsistencies in awareness, delivery and/or communication regarding training opportunities. Given that oral health therapists and dental therapists often work closely with children, families and Aboriginal community members, ensuring equitable access to ongoing cultural education is essential. Strengthening multidisciplinary communications [32], mentorship [33] and shared learning opportunities [34] could also enhance cultural responsiveness and mutual understanding across professional boundaries.

These findings mirror previous rural workforce studies showing that interprofessional collaboration and equitable access to continuing professional development are critical to sustaining morale and reducing professional isolation [35, 36, 37]. Future workforce planning should therefore consider the differing needs and expectations of professional subgroups to ensure a cohesive, well‐supported oral health team in rural regions.

Influence of Experience in Rural Practice

Comparisons based on years of clinical experience revealed that practitioners with over 10 years of experience tended to report higher perceived preparedness in managing cultural challenges. This aligns with the current literature suggesting that time spent in rural settings enhances practitioners' adaptability, confidence and understanding of local community needs [38]. Greater duration in clinical practice, particularly in rural practice, likely facilitates deeper cultural immersion and familiarity with community norms and expectations. Over time, this exposure may enhance practitioners' ability to navigate cultural interactions and develop informal networks and local knowledge that help them navigate system limitations, while newer practitioners may initially find these environments more challenging due to unfamiliarity.

Interestingly, those with < 10 years' experience appeared more comfortable adapting to new environments and life away from family, suggesting generational or lifestyle differences in workforce mobility. Younger or early‐career practitioners may view rural practice as an opportunity for growth and adventure, whereas experienced practitioners may have other responsibilities that affect perceptions of isolation or support [39, 40, 41]. These observations reinforce the importance of tailored retention strategies that address different career stages. For example, early‐career practitioners may benefit most from structured mentorship and other learning opportunities, while mid‐ to late‐career practitioners might value leadership roles, professional recognition or flexible work arrangements that support sustainable engagement over time.

Together, these subgroup insights emphasise that the rural oral health workforce is not homogenous but rather, it comprises practitioners at varied career stages and with differing access to professional supports. Recognising and responding to these differences is critical for workforce sustainability, professional satisfaction and long‐term service continuity in rural communities.

Workforce Sustainability and Policy Implications

The findings of this study underscore the interdependence of cultural and personal factors in sustaining a rural oral health workforce. This study revealed challenges in ongoing cultural training, community integration and limited availability of financial employment and relocation incentives, highlighting the multifaceted nature of workforce retention challenges. Evidence from broader rural health literature suggests that retention is shaped not only by financial incentives, but also by access to professional development, career progression opportunities and the ability to establish meaningful community connections [31]. In nursing and allied health contexts, culturally informed practice and community integration have been shown to enhance practitioners' satisfaction and long‐term retention, reinforcing the importance of culturally responsive workforce strategies [42]. Policies that invest in long‐term professional development, regional mentoring frameworks and family‐inclusive relocation supports could improve both practitioner wellbeing and retention. Furthermore, integrating cultural safety benchmarks into workforce accreditation and service evaluation processes may ensure consistent accountability across LHDs.

Limitations

The small sample size of this study and reliance on self‐reported data may limit generalisability. Response bias, particularly social desirability and recall bias, could have influenced findings despite efforts to ensure anonymity. This study should also be considered for self‐selection bias. Silva et al. explained that although many graduates initially enter rural practice, a substantial proportion return to metropolitan areas within a few years [43]. Consequently, this study likely captures practitioners who have chosen to remain in rural practice and may therefore represent a more resilient or motivated subset of the workforce.

Further research using mixed methods, including in‐depth interviews, could enrich understanding of how these experiences evolve over time and across geographic regions.

Despite the small sample size, the cohort is generally reflective of the LHD's clinical cohort in rural areas.

Implications for Practice and Policy

As an exploratory study, the findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution. The results are not intended to be generalisable to all settings but rather to provide preliminary insights into the experiences of the rural oral health workforce, highlight areas for further investigation and inform the development of targeted policies and interventions.

Building on the findings of this paper, few considerations for practice and policy have emerged. These considerations may improve workforce sustainability, have the potential to further support practitioner wellbeing and, overall, could improve service delivery in rural contexts. Conclusion

This study provides valuable insights into the cultural and personal dimensions shaping the experiences of oral health practitioners in rural NSW. It highlights key inequalities in access to cultural capability training and variations in personal factors influencing rural practice. Practitioners reported inconsistent availability, accessibility and perceived effectiveness of cultural training alongside differing levels of support systems, relocation incentives and personal growth. Differences by professional background and years of clinical experience were evident, with dentists reporting greater access to training and organisational supports than other oral health practitioners.

Addressing these inequalities requires a coordinated approach that invests in accessible, continuous cultural capability development, strengthens social and professional support systems and tailors incentives to practitioners' career stages and needs. Strengthening these areas will be essential to building a resilient, culturally responsive and sustainable rural oral health workforce capable of meeting the needs of communities across rural NSW.

Online informed consent was obtained from each participant, ensuring that they understood their right to withdraw from the study at any time without consequence. The survey component of the study incorporated a skip logic function, which ensured that participants could only proceed with the survey after explicitly providing consent. Once the survey was completed, the participants were given the opportunity to opt‐in to the interview component of the study. If participants agreed to take part in the interview, they were contacted directly by the lead researcher via email to then organise a suitable time for the virtual interview.
