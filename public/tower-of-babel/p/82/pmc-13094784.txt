Deep Ensemble Learning to Detect Retinal Vascular Leakage on Ultrawide-Field Fundus Photographs of Patients With Uveitis
Authors: Jongwoo Kim, Nam V. Nguyen, Matias A. Soifer, Ayman G. Elnahry, Mutaz Y. Al-Nawaflh, Melanie Hébert, H. Nida Sen, Sunil Bellur, Shilpa Kodati
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe purpose of this study was to develop a novel deep learning (DL) algorithm to detect retinal vascular leakage (RVL) on ultra-widefield fundus (UWF) images in patients with posterior segment uveitis.MethodsUltra-widefield fluorescein angiography (UWFFA) and corresponding UWF images of patients, who were evaluated at the uveitis clinic at the National Eye Institute, National Institutes of Health, were collected for this study. UWFFA images were used for the assessment of RVL, and the corresponding UWF images were used to train the algorithms. Several DL algorithms with different backbone architectures were trained and tested, and the ensemble learning (EL) method was adopted to enhance classification accuracy.ResultsA total of 405 eyes were included in the study. Two different datasets were generated, wMildRVL (405 eyes) and woMildRVL (excluding mild RVL eyes). EL based on 3 DL models showed superior performance with an accuracy of 0.7704, a sensitivity of 0.7699, a specificity of 0.7713, and an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.8018 for the dataset wMildRVL, and an accuracy of 0.7900, a sensitivity of 0.7819, a specificity of 0.8000, and an AUC of 0.8344 for the woMildRVL dataset.ConclusionsThe proposed EL model demonstrated the potential in distinguishing those with and without RVL on UWF images from eyes with posterior segment uveitis.Translational RelevanceThis algorithm model can be a potential screening tool to detect the presence of RVL on UWF images, thus determining the need for UWFFA, as this would be especially helpful in resource-limited settings or in patients with known adverse effects to the fluorescein dye.

Introduction

Uveitis refers to a heterogeneous group of intraocular inflammatory diseases accounting for up to 10% to 15% of cases of blindness in the United States.1 Retinal vasculitis, meaning inflammation of the retinal vessels, is a common feature of posterior segment uveitic diseases. Although fundus examination may reveal signs associated with retinal vasculitis, such as sheathing and cuffing of the blood vessels, and perivascular exudates,2–4 the diagnosis and assessment of retinal vasculitis relies primarily on fluorescein angiography.5 The degree of retinal vascular leakage (RVL) on fluorescein angiography can help clinicians assess the severity of the disease and guide appropriate therapy for the patients.5 However, fluorescein angiography is an invasive and time-consuming procedure requiring experienced photographers to capture images. Additionally, adverse effects have also been associated with fluorescein dye used in fluorescein angiography imaging, such as allergic reactions.6 Therefore, an alternative, less invasive method would be beneficial to detect RVL.

Machine learning (ML) is an artificial intelligence (AI) technique that enables computers to learn and detect predictive features from images/information without specified rules. It has been adapted in medical fields and has shown promising results in assisting the interpretation of medical imaging and the diagnosis of diseases from radiographic imaging.7–12 In the field of ophthalmology, research studies have demonstrated the capability of ML in diagnosing several common ocular diseases including diabetic retinopathy,13,14 age-related macular degeneration,15,16 retinal vascular occlusion,17 and glaucoma, among others.18–20 Notably, the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has approved IDx-DR,21 EyeArt,22 and AEYE-DS23 systems for detection and assessment of diabetic retinopathy.

Herein, we propose a novel, noninvasive method for detecting RVL on ultra-widefield fundus (UWF) images using ensemble learning (EL) methods based on deep learning (DL) models.

Materials and Methods

Study Oversight

The study was conducted in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the National Institutes of Health. Patients who were evaluated in the uveitis clinic at the National Eye Institute (NEI), National Institutes of Health and underwent UWF imaging and ultra-widefield fluorescein angiography (UWFFA) imaging were screened for enrollment in this study. Inclusion criteria were patients with active or inactive uveitis in whom UWF and UWFFA imaging were performed and available for analysis. Exclusion criteria included significant chorioretinal lesions, or other retinal diseases such as diabetic retinopathy, retinal vein occlusion, or age-related macular degeneration.

Ultra-WideField Fundus Photograph and Fluorescein Angiography Acquisition and Assessment

Both UWF and UWFFA imaging at the same visit were obtained using the Optos 200Tx (Optos PLC, Dunfermline, UK) ultra-widefield imaging system. Standard fluorescein angiographic techniques and image acquisition consisted of early-phase (15–45 seconds) images of the eye of primary interest, and images of the fellow eye at 45 to 60 seconds. In addition to those standard images, late-phase images were obtained at 5 to 10 minutes for both eyes. Eyes with poor or ungradable images were excluded from the study.

Masked grading of UWFFA was performed independently by a dual-grader system (by authors M.A.S., S.B., A.G.E., and M.Y.A.), and the graders had not participated in the examination of the patients. The UWFFA area was divided into 5 sections (posterior pole and 4 quadrants) for grading purposes, and each section was graded separately and classified as follows: 0 = no leakage, 1 = focal leakage, limited up to 2 focal areas, and 2 = diffuse leakage. A composite score from all sections was calculated and used to determine the RVL severity for each eye. RVL severity was graded as absent RVL (composite score ≤2, and without any diffuse leakage on any section), mild RVL (composite score >2 and ≤5, and with no diffuse leakage on any section), or present RVL (composite score >5 with at least one section showing diffuse leakage). In cases of discrepancy, the senior graders (authors S.B. or S.K., with S.K. adjudicating for images that S.B. had graded) were consulted to determine the final assessment. After completing the UWFFA grading, the corresponding UWF images of each group were used for training and testing the algorithms.

Training Datasets

The dataset consisted of UWF images of 405 eyes. Of these, 235 eyes exhibited RVL, as shown on UWFFA images, and 170 eyes did not exhibit RVL. Among 235 eyes with RVL, 15 eyes had mild RVL, and the remaining eyes were classified as present (more than mild RVL). The corresponding UWF images were exported as Tagged Image File Format (TIFF) files, and the size of the images used in the dataset was 4000 × 4000 × 3 pixels.

Two datasets were used for the training of the algorithm. The first dataset, called wMildRVL dataset, includes all images from the original dataset. The second dataset, called woMildRVL dataset, excludes images with mild RVL. As a result, the wMildRVL dataset comprises 405 images, and the woMildRVL dataset comprises 390 images.

Cropped Ultra-Widefield Color Fundus Image Dataset

The original UWF image contains a black background area, as shown in Figures 1A and 1B. Therefore, we performed preprocessing to crop out the background area from the original images. Convolutional Neural Network Classifier for Training and Testing

Figure 2 illustrates a deep neural network, called convolutional neural networks (CNNs), which was used for detecting RVL on UWF images. The architecture of the CNN is composed of a feature extractor block operable for extracting features from the input UWF and a classification block operable for classifying whether the extracted features from the input UWF images exhibit RVL. Eleven different CNN backbone models were evaluated as feature extractors. Among them, VGG19,24 DenseNet121,25 and DenseNet16925 achieved the best performance.

Proposed deep learning model (convolutional neural network).

The classification block consists of a global average pooling layer, two fully connected dense layers, and an output layer. The second fully connected layer is connected with an output layer, which is operable for assigning the probability of RVL in the input image in the class 1 node and probability of non-RVL in the class 0 node. The size of the first and second dense layers in the classification block was 1 × 1024, and dropout ratios of 0.10, 0.25, or 0.50 were used for the first and second dropout layers to resolve overfitting issues. Pre-trained weights of the ImageNet26 were used as initial weights for the CNN classifiers, and all layers in the CNN classifiers including the layers in the feature extraction block were trained again using training images. All UWF images were resized to 512 × 512 × 3 pixels prior to inputting into the CNN classifiers.

We evaluated multiple input sizes to identify the optimal balance between spatial detail and computational performance. Four resized input dimensions—512 × 512, 768 × 768, 896 × 896, and 1024 × 1024—were tested across 4 CNN models. Among these, the 512 × 512 input size consistently achieved the highest accuracy across all four models (VGG19, ResNet152,27 DenseNet121, and DenseNet169). Therefore, this resolution was selected as the final input size for our experiments. Additional details are provided in Table 1.

Python with TensorFlow Keras28,29 was used to implement the CNN classifiers. To train the CNN classifiers, 2 optimizers were used: a stochastic gradient descent (SGD) with learning rate = 0.0001, decay = 1e–6, momentum = 0.9, and nesterov momentum = True, and Adam with learning rate = 0.00005, β2 = 0.999, ε = 1e–8, and weight decay = 0. Epochs = 50 were used for all training. The hardware configuration used was 2 × Intel Xeon Gold 5218 processors 2.3 GHz, 64 hyper-thread processors, 8 × NVIDIA GeForce RTX 2080 Ti (11 GB memory), and Red Hat Enterprise Linux 7.

We evaluated 11 deep learning classifiers, including VGG19, ResNet152, DenseNet121, DenseNet169, EfficientNetB0, EfficientNetB4, EfficientNetB6, EfficientNetV2-S, ConvNeXt-Tiny, ConvNeXt-Small, and ConvNeXt-Base. These architectures were selected because pretrained ImageNet weights were available and their computational requirements were compatible with our GPU hardware.

Ensemble Learning Model Based on Three CNN Classifiers

In ML, EL methods combine the results of multiple algorithms to achieve a superior and more reliable performance than a single algorithm.30 Among these methods, the Bootstrap Aggregating method, or bagging, allows each model to vote or average with weights to enhance the classification accuracy of the final results. Therefore, we propose an EL model to combine the results of three CNN classifiers to generate a more stable and robust output for identifying UWF that exhibits RVL. Figure 3 shows the architecture of the EL model based on three different CNN classifiers that are arranged in parallel operation. In this architecture, each CNN classifier produced its own feature extraction and then generated its own classification result at a respective output layer to provide an output (class 0 for non-RVL and class 1 for RVL).

Proposed ensemble learning based on three convolutional neural networks.

The respective output results of each of the CNN classifiers were aggregated based on hard voting to determine whether an input UWF truly exhibited features of RVL in the combined aggregated output of the CNN classifiers. Training and Testing of CNN Classifiers

A five-fold cross-validation method was used to evaluate the ability of CNN classifiers and EL model to detect RVL on UWF images. For example, five training and test datasets were generated from the dataset of UWF fundus images, and the CNN classifiers were trained and tested five times to evaluate their performance. In each dataset, 80% of the UWF images were used to train the CNN classifiers to detect RVL on UWF images, whereas 20% of the UWF images were used for testing. The training UWF dataset were augmented 6-fold by flipping each of the UWF along the vertical axis and then rotating the UWF ± 10 degrees.

Results

Table 2 shows the performance of the proposed 11 CNN classifiers with 2 different datasets, the wMildRVL and woMildRVL datasets. With the wMildRVL dataset, the CNN classifier based on DenseNet169 backbone feature extractor achieved the best performance with an accuracy of 0.7358, a sensitivity of 0.7447, and a specificity of 0.7235. The corresponding 95% confidence intervals were 0.7136 to 0.7580 for accuracy, 0.6694 to 0.8199 for sensitivity, and 0.6201 to 0.8269 for specificity. In the case of the woMildRVL dataset, the CNN classifier based on DenseNet121 backbone feature extractor demonstrated the best performance with an accuracy of 0.7542, a sensitivity of 0.7052, and a specificity of 0.8177. The corresponding 95% confidence intervals were 0.7183 to 0.7902 for accuracy, 0.6410 to 0.7693 for sensitivity, and 0.7526 to 0.8828 for specificity. We examined two ensemble strategies—hard voting and soft voting. In soft voting, the predicted class probabilities from the three classifiers are aggregated to produce a final decision, whereas hard voting determines the final output based on the majority class vote. Hard voting demonstrated better performance across our evaluations and was therefore selected as the ensemble method for this study.

The EL model for wMildRVL dataset achieved better performance across all three metrics compared to the individual single classifier (DenseNet169). For the woMildRVL dataset, the EL model showed improved accuracy and sensitivity, but a slightly reduced performance in specificity relative to the single classifier (DenseNet121). However, considering the improvements in accuracy and sensitivity alongside the minor drop in specificity, we can conclude that the EL has a better performance than the DenseNet121. In addition, the EL models for both datasets demonstrated more balanced performance across accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity compared to the individual CNN classifier. Discussion

Fluorescein angiography is an important tool in evaluating and monitoring posterior segment involvement in patients with uveitis, especially in the detection of retinal vasculitis. However, fluorescein angiography is time-consuming and requires experienced photographers to capture high-quality images. Moreover, it is an invasive procedure, and serious adverse effects to the fluorescein dye may occur, such as anaphylaxis.6 Thus, other noninvasive imaging modalities are needed to screen for RVL in patients with uveitis. In this study, we developed a novel AI algorithm to detect RVL on UWF images of eyes with uveitis. Among the three proposed CNN classifiers, the DenseNet169 had the best performance with an accuracy of 0.7358, a sensitivity of 0.7447, and a specificity of 0.7235 in the wMildRVL dataset. Meanwhile, the DenseNet121 had the best performance with an accuracy of 0.7542, a sensitivity of 0.7052, and a specificity 0.8177 with the woMildRVL dataset. As expected, the proposed CNN classifiers demonstrated superior performance with the woMildRVL dataset than with the wMildRVL dataset. The proposed EL models also demonstrated better performance than the CNN classifiers alone, achieving an accuracy of 0.7900, a sensitivity of 0.7819, and a specificity of 0.8006 on the woMildRVL dataset, compared to an accuracy of 0.7704, a sensitivity of 0.7699, and a specificity of 0.7713 on the wMildRVL dataset.

ML is an AI technique that enables the detection of predictive patterns in large datasets that human eyes cannot practically perform, and its use in ophthalmology has increased exponentially in recent years. Multiple studies have attempted to predict sex, age, and cardiovascular risks from color fundus photographs with great success.31–34 In predicting sex from color fundus photographs, the model proposed by Korot et al. achieved an AUC of 0.93, an accuracy of 86.5%, a sensitivity of 88.8%, and a specificity of 83.6% when tested with different images from the same dataset.31 However, the accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity decreased to 78.6%, 83.9%, and 78.2%, respectively, when tested with images from an external dataset.31 Interestingly, the model's performance decreased further when foveal pathology was present, suggesting that the fovea's features are important for the model performance.31 Poplin et al. also reported an AUC of 0.97 in predicting sex from color fundus photographs.33 Additionally, the model also achieved an accuracy of 78% to 79% in predicting age, and an AUC of 0.70 in predicting major cardiac events.33 Our proposed EL model exhibited the AUC of 0.8344 in detecting RVL on UWF images. The AUC of our model is slightly lower than these two models, possibly due to the small sample size and the imaging modality. Although UWF images have a greater field of view than traditional color fundus images, the resolution of UWF is lower as compared with traditional color fundus images. Moreover, the foveal area, which may be important for detecting RVL, is better captured with traditional color fundus images. With UWFs, Nguyen et al. developed several models in detecting retinal diseases, such as age-related macular degeneration, diabetic retinopathy, epiretinal membrane, and retinal vein occlusion with great success. The proposed models exhibited AUC up to 0.9647 on the test set after the training phase (https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4418/14/1/105). Zhao et al. also demonstrated an excellent performance with their models in determining the referral criteria for retinal diseases on UWFs, and their AUC values were 0.9111 and 0.902 with the internal data and external data tests, respectively (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12208325/). Although our model's AUC value was slightly slower compared with these models, it is important to note that UWFs in these models have distinct retinal findings, which are more likely to be detected by the models as compared to RVL in our patients with uveitis. The results of our study suggest that the DL model can detect salient features suggesting RVL on UWF, and further studies with larger datasets and traditional color fundus images may be needed to further refine this model. The potential clinical application of this model would be as a screening tool to detect the presence of RVL on color fundus photographs, thus determining the need for UWFFA as this would be especially helpful in resource-limited settings or in patients with known adverse effects to the fluorescein dye.

Although AI has been used extensively in other eye diseases, its use in uveitis has been largely limited because uveitis is still a relatively rare disease.35 Anterior chamber (AC) cells and vitreous haze are important clinical outcomes in patients with uveitis, and several automated AI models have been developed to assess AC cells and vitreous haze objectively, mainly using optical coherence tomography (OCT). Several research groups have compared the automated analysis of AC cells using anterior segment OCT (AS-OCT) to AC cells grading via clinical examination with a slit-lamp using the SUN classification.36–42 In the report by Sharma et al., clinical AC cells determined by a uveitis specialist were strongly correlated to the number of cells counted by the automated cell counting platform ImageIQ (Cleveland, OH, USA; Spearman correlation of coefficient of r = 0.967, P < 0.0001).41 Similarly, Sorkhabi et al. developed an automated AI-based method to quantify AC cells on AS-OCT, and they also showed a significant correlation with clinical SUN assessment by an experienced ophthalmologist (Spearman P = 0.7077, P < 0.001).42 Several authors have reported using OCT for objective measurement of vitreous haze severity.43–45 Notably, Keane et al. measured vitreous signal intensity, which was then divided by the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) signal intensity to yield the vitreous/RPE intensity ratio.43 This ratio showed a significantly positive correlation with the clinical vitreous haze scores (r = 0.566, P = 0.0001).43 These results suggest that AI can be a helpful tool in objectively measuring clinical outcomes in patients with uveitis.

Several authors have also developed an automated detection of retinal vascular leakage on UWFFA.46–48 In our previous study, a set of 200 UWFFA images was segmented for RVL by a certified grader, and they were used to train a DL algorithm to detect RVL on UWFFA.48 The algorithm was then tested on an additional set of 50 images.48 The results showed a moderate concordance with the ground truth assessed by certified graders (Dice Similarity Coefficient of 0.563) on the tested images.48 Similarly, Ehlers et al. developed an automated system to detect RVL on UWFFA in patients with diabetic retinopathy.46 The results showed a significantly strong correlation with certified graders for detection of RVL (intraclass correlation coefficient [ICC] = 0.70–0.86, P = 7.8 × 10−6 to 1.3 × 10−9). This algorithm was then tested in a patient with posterior uveitis and showed consistent results in monitoring the treatment response to adalimumab.47

AI models have also been developed to detect ocular toxoplasmosis on color fundus photographs. In a previous study by Para et al., they utilized a dataset of 160 color fundus retinal images of eyes with ocular toxoplasmosis for training and evaluation of a screening model from a Paraguayan hospital.49 The authors applied a DL CNN model with a few convolutional layers and compared it to a VGG16 model, a Vanilla CNN model, and a Resnet 18 model.49 Among the tested models, VGG16 showed the best performance with an accuracy of 0.96875, a sensitivity of 1.0, and a specificity of 0.9375.49 Although these models showed better performance than the models in our study,49 it should be noted that the models are more likely to detect the distinctive scar on color fundus photographs in ocular toxoplasmosis than salient changes of RVL on UWF images.

Our study includes several limitations, such as size of the dataset, and selection bias. Although we attempted to increase the dataset with image augmentation, it is still relatively small for training a ML algorithm. Furthermore, there may have been a selection bias toward severe presentations of uveitis because a minority of eyes showed minimal leakage on FA. All patients with abnormal retinal findings were excluded from the study, eliminating the potential detection of these abnormal retinal findings as surrogate markers for RVL. Thus, further steps would include refining the model utilizing training images from patients with concomitant retinal pathology. Additionally, our models considered all leakage equally, regardless of the location of the leakage in the retina. Because leakage involving the macula has likely more clinically meaningful effects on visual acuity than peripheral leakage, including location-based detection of leakage in the future models may help with determining clinical severity. Last, it is unclear how this model would apply to entities such as occlusive retinal vasculitis, and this would need to be incorporated into future work.

Conclusions

We developed a proof-of-concept, novel DL algorithm for detecting RVL on UWF images. This DL algorithm showed modest agreement in identifying RVL compared to ground truth by certified graders on UWFFA. The study showed that AI can be of significant utility in screening and evaluating posterior segment involvement in patients with uveitis, especially when UWFFA is not possible. Further studies with larger sample sizes and incorporation of multimodal imaging are needed to improve the performance of the models.
