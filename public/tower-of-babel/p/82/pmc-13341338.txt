Short‐Term Combined Treatment With Tirzepatide and Metformin for Overweight/Obese Chinese Women With Polycystic Ovary Syndrome: A Prospective, Open‐Label, Randomised Controlled Trial
Authors: Zhuoran Yang, Yuxin Xu, Hu Du, Wenya Liu, Haiyan Chen, Dongfang Liu, Lili Zhang, Cong Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsThis study aimed to assess the effects of low‐dose tirzepatide combined with metformin (COM) versus metformin (MET) monotherapy in overweight/obese women with polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS).Materials and MethodsSixty overweight/obese women with PCOS were randomised to the MET group (1000 mg twice daily [BID]) or the COM group (MET: 1000 mg BID, tirzepatide: 5 mg once weekly [QW]) for 16 weeks. The primary outcome was the change in body weight. Secondary outcomes included changes in anthropometric measures other than body weight, body composition, reproductive hormone levels, metabolic and endocrine parameters, inflammatory markers and menstrual cycle regularity. All outcomes were assessed at baseline and week 16. After week 16, participants were switched to MET monotherapy. Barrier contraception was required for 8 weeks and pregnancy outcomes were subsequently evaluated between weeks 25 and 48.ResultsAfter 16 weeks of treatment, compared with the MET group, the COM group resulted in greater reductions in weight (−1.7 ± 2.5 kg vs. −10.4 ± 3.5 kg; p < 0.001), body mass index (BMI) (−0.68 ± 1.82 vs. −4.12 ± 1.37 kg/m2; p < 0.001), visceral adipose tissue (VAT) (−4.67 ± 9.59 vs. −34.13 ± 15.33 cm2; p < 0.001) and reproductive endocrine‐metabolic parameters. Menstrual cycle recovery and total pregnancy rate were higher in the COM group than in the MET group (p = 0.013 and p = 0.014, respectively).ConclusionsIn overweight/obese women with PCOS, low‐dose tirzepatide combined with MET was associated with greater reductions in body weight and visceral fat, along with improvements in metabolic and reproductive outcomes compared with MET monotherapy. Introduction

Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is one of the most prevalent endocrine and metabolic disorders affecting women of reproductive age, with a global prevalence ranging from 11% to 13% [1]. It is characterised by hyperandrogenism, ovulatory dysfunction and polycystic ovarian morphology (PCOM), making it a leading cause of ovulatory infertility [1]. Women with PCOS often present with insulin resistance and hyperinsulinemia and obesity is a major risk factor that exacerbates their metabolic and reproductive abnormalities. Emerging evidence suggests that insulin resistance, obesity and chronic low‐grade inflammation interact in the pathogenesis and progression of PCOS, collectively contributing to metabolic disturbances, ovulatory dysfunction and reproductive endocrine dysregulation [1, 2].

Lifestyle interventions remain the recommended first‐line treatment strategy for managing PCOS, with increased physical activity and dietary modifications promoting weight loss and mitigating cardiometabolic risk [1]. However, in clinical practise, many overweight/obese women with PCOS fail to maintain moderate‐intensity exercise regimens and structured dietary plans, often experiencing weight regain and a recurrence of metabolic abnormalities. Metformin (MET), an insulin sensitizer, is commonly used in women with PCOS for its ability to improve insulin sensitivity, reduce androgen levels and positively impact ovulation and pregnancy outcomes [3, 4]. However, its clinical efficacy remains limited.

Glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists (GLP‐1 RAs, including exenatide, liraglutide and semaglutide) are increasingly recognised as promising therapeutic options for overweight/obese women with PCOS, given their ability to induce significant weight loss and enhance insulin sensitivity [5, 6, 7, 8]. Moreover, combination therapy with a GLP‐1 RA and MET has shown superior efficacy over MET monotherapy in weight reduction, insulin resistance improvement, menstrual cycle restoration and cardiometabolic risk factor management (e.g., waist circumference [WC], blood pressure, blood glucose and lipid profiles) [6, 9, 10]. Regarding reproductive outcomes, clinical trials suggest that short‐term GLP‐1 RA and MET combination therapy significantly enhances ovarian function in women with PCOS [11, 12]. This approach has been associated with higher rates of successful embryo transfer and overall pregnancy compared to MET monotherapy [12]. The benefits may result from weight loss, alleviation of insulin resistance, improvement in hyperandrogenism and optimisation of the ovarian microenvironment, highlighting the potential advantage of GLP‐1 RAs in both reproductive management and preconception metabolic intervention for women with PCOS. Tirzepatide, a novel dual‐target agonist, activates both the glucose‐dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP) and GLP‐1 receptors, offering greater metabolic benefits for individuals with obesity than traditional GLP‐1 RAs. Considering the limited preconception window and the importance of treatment adherence and tolerability, low‐dose tirzepatide may help overweight/obese women with PCOS achieve weight‐loss targets and improve metabolic homeostasis within a relatively short period, while reducing treatment costs and gastrointestinal side effects.

Although published evidence has confirmed the efficacy and safety of tirzepatide in individuals with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and obesity, direct clinical research on its application in overweight/obese women with PCOS remains limited. Therefore, this study was designed as a prospective, open‐label, randomised controlled trial to evaluate the effects of low‐dose tirzepatide combined with metformin (COM) in overweight/obese women of reproductive age with PCOS. This short‐term intervention trial assessed changes in body weight, body composition, glucose and lipid metabolism, inflammatory markers, reproductive hormone levels and pregnancy outcomes. The study aimed to provide new evidence for metabolism‐directed, individualised treatment strategies for PCOS.

Materials and Methods

Participants

This single‐centre study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (Approval No.: 2024204). Eligible women were recruited from the outpatient clinic of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University. A total of 60 women with PCOS who met both the Rotterdam criteria and the Chinese criteria for overweight or obesity (body mass index [BMI] ≥ 24 kg/m2) were enrolled. The diagnosis of PCOS was based on the 2003 Rotterdam criteria, which require at least two of the following three features: (1) ovulatory dysfunction, (2) clinical and/or biochemical hyperandrogenism and (3) PCOM on ultrasound, while excluding other disorders such as congenital adrenal hyperplasia, Cushing syndrome and androgen‐secreting tumours. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Chinese women of reproductive age (18–40 years) with a BMI ≥ 24 kg/m2; (2) diagnosis of PCOS according to the Rotterdam criteria; (3) inadequate response to lifestyle intervention (diet and exercise) for at least 3 months before enrollment; (4) desire for pregnancy and failure to conceive despite more than 12 months of regular, unprotected intercourse. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) allergy to GLP‐1 RAs or MET; (2) pre‐existing diabetes (type 1 or type 2); (3) personal history of benign or malignant tumours, including thyroid carcinoma; (4) severe cardiovascular, renal or hepatic disease; (5) comorbid gynaecological conditions (e.g., ovarian or uterine disorders) or severe male factor infertility that could significantly contribute to infertility; (6) use of statins, herbal medicines, Chinese traditional medicines or other drugs known or suspected to affect reproductive or metabolic function within 3 months before enrollment or during the study period; (7) inability to comply with follow‐up for personal reasons; (8) current pregnancy or intention to become pregnant during the 16‐week treatment period.

Study Design

This was a prospective, open‐label, randomised controlled trial with two groups. The total study duration was 48 weeks, comprising a 16‐week treatment phase with different medications, followed by an 8‐week barrier contraception period and a subsequent 24‐week follow‐up phase to assess reproductive outcomes. All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment. Before randomisation, baseline assessments were conducted for all participants, including age, height, body weight, WC and hip circumference (HC) and menstrual cycle. Laboratory evaluations included liver and kidney function tests, prolactin (PRL), testosterone (TEST) and human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) levels. Thyroid ultrasound was also performed. These assessments were performed to establish baseline characteristics and to exclude individuals with significantly elevated liver enzymes (> 2.5 times the upper limit of normal), impaired renal function (estimated glomerular filtration rate < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2), hyperprolactinemia, tumours or pregnancy. After this screening process, 60 eligible women with PCOS were enrolled. Participants were randomised in a 1:1 ratio to the MET group or the COM group using a computer‐generated block randomisation schedule. The allocation sequence was generated and maintained by an independent study staff member and was not disclosed until the participant had completed enrollment. Participants in the MET group received oral MET starting at 500 mg once daily (QD). In the absence of intolerance or significant adverse effects, the dose was increased by 500 mg every 3 days until a maintenance dose of 2000 mg daily (1000 mg twice daily [BID]) was reached. The COM group received the same oral MET regimen, plus subcutaneous tirzepatide, which was initiated at 2.5 mg once weekly (QW) for the first 4 weeks, then increased to 5 mg QW as the maintenance dose. At the end of the 16‐week treatment phase, the primary outcome (change in body weight) and secondary outcomes (other anthropometric measures, body composition analysis, reproductive hormone levels, glucose and lipid metabolism, inflammatory markers and menstrual cycle regularity) were assessed. Both groups continued oral MET (1000 mg BID) during the subsequent barrier contraception phase (weeks 17–24) and the conception‐attempt phase (weeks 25–48), during which pregnancy status was monitored. Medication adherence, safety and adverse events were evaluated every 4 weeks throughout the study.

Anthropometric Parameters

At baseline and after 16 weeks of treatment, all participants attended scheduled clinic visits for anthropometric assessments conducted by trained study staff. Body weight and height were measured with participants wearing light clothing and no shoes. BMI was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m2). Women with a BMI of 24 to < 28 kg/m2 were classified as overweight and those with a BMI ≥ 28 kg/m2 were classified as having obesity, based on Chinese criteria [13, 14]. WC was measured at the midpoint between the lower rib margin and iliac crest, while HC was measured at the level of the maximal protrusion of the buttocks. The waist‐to‐hip ratio (WHR) was calculated as WC divided by HC. Systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) were measured using an automatic electronic sphygmomanometer (HEM‐7136, OMRON, Japan).

Body Composition Analysis

Body composition was assessed at baseline and after 16 weeks of treatment using dual‐energy X‐ray absorptiometry (DXA; GE Lunar iDXA, United States).

Biochemical Parameters

At baseline and after 16 weeks of treatment, fasting venous blood samples were collected from all participants between 8:00 and 10:00 a.m., following at least 10 h of fasting. Measurements included lipid profiles oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), oral glucose insulin release test (OGIRT), glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c), sex hormones and high‐sensitivity C‐reactive protein (hs‐CRP). Insulin resistance was assessed using the homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA‐IR), calculated as HOMA‐IR = fasting blood glucose (OGTT‐0h, mmol/L) × fasting insulin (OGIRT‐0h, mU/L)/22.5. Plasma glucose levels were measured using the glucose oxidase method, HbA1c was quantified by high‐performance liquid chromatography and serum insulin levels were determined via chemiluminescent immunoassay. Total cholesterol (TC), high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C), low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C), triglycerides (TG) and hs‐CRP levels were analysed using an automated chemistry analyser.

Serum concentrations of luteinizing hormone (LH), follicle‐stimulating hormone (FSH), TEST, PRL, sex hormone‐binding globulin (SHBG) and dehydroepiandrosterone sulphate (DHEA‐S) were measured by chemiluminescent immunoassay. The free androgen index (FAI) was calculated as FAI (%) = TEST (nmol/L)/SHBG (nmol/L) × 100%.

Anti‐Müllerian hormone (AMH), leptin (LEP) and adiponectin (ADP) were measured using commercially available enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (Elabscience, China; E‐EL‐H6234, E‐EL‐H6017 and E‐EL‐H6122, respectively). Assay sensitivities and analytical ranges were within the manufacturer's specifications, with intra‐ and inter‐assay coefficients of variation (CVs) below 10%.

Menstrual and Pregnancy Outcomes

During the 16‐week treatment period, all participants used barrier contraception and recorded detailed menstrual cycle changes. Menstrual irregularity was defined as oligomenorrhea (fewer than 6 menstrual periods in 12 months) or amenorrhea (absence of menstruation for more than 6 months), with each bleeding episode counted as a separate cycle. Menstrual cycle recovery was defined as the resumption of regular menstrual cycles, with a regular cycle defined as an intermenstrual interval of ≥ 21 days and < 35 days. Pregnancy outcomes were assessed during the subsequent conception‐attempt phase (weeks 25–48). During this phase, participants were advised to have intercourse 2–3 times per week until pregnancy was achieved and intercourse frequency was recorded during the outpatient follow‐up visits. At each 4‐week follow‐up visit, participants underwent hCG testing. If hCG was positive, an ultrasound examination was performed to confirm clinical pregnancy, defined as the visualisation of a gestational sac. Miscarriage was defined as pregnancy loss after confirmation of clinical pregnancy and was ascertained based on serial hCG results, follow‐up ultrasound findings, clinical diagnosis and pregnancy outcome documentation in outpatient medical records. Natural pregnancy was defined as conception occurring without any medical or assisted reproductive intervention, except for ongoing MET therapy. Other pregnancies, including those achieved through ovulation induction or in vitro fertilisation and embryo transfer (IVF‐ET), were recorded separately. Natural pregnancy rate (%) = (number of natural pregnancies/number of participants in the corresponding group) × 100%. Other pregnancy rate (%) = (number of other pregnancies/number of participants in the corresponding group) × 100%. Total pregnancy rate (%) = [(natural pregnancies + other pregnancies)/number of participants in the corresponding group] × 100%.

Sample Size and Statistical Analysis

Sample size estimation was based on the primary outcome of body weight change. Drawing from previously published studies of tirzepatide and MET therapy, a conservative between‐group difference of 4.0 kg was assumed, with a pooled standard deviation of 4.6 kg derived from published body weight change data for MET and tirzepatide [9, 15, 16, 17]. Calculations were performed using PASS 2021 software, assuming a one‐sided alpha of 0.025 and 80% power. After accounting for an anticipated 20% dropout rate, the adjusted required sample size was 28 participants per group. To allow for potential attrition and facilitate study implementation, 30 participants were ultimately enrolled in each group.

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS (version 27.0) and GraphPad Prism (version 10.1.2). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range [IQR]), as appropriate. For ANCOVA analyses, adjusted mean differences with 95% confidence intervals were reported. Normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Within‐group comparisons were made using the paired t‐test or the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test. Between‐group comparisons were performed using the independent‐samples t‐test or the Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate. For comparisons across three groups, one‐way ANOVA was used for normally distributed data with homogeneous variances, whereas Welch's ANOVA was applied for normally distributed data with unequal variances. The Kruskal–Wallis test was used for non‐normally distributed data. If the overall comparison was significant, post hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using Bonferroni, Games‐Howell or Dunn‐Bonferroni tests, as appropriate. Categorical variables are expressed as frequency (percentage) and were compared using the chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. All tests were two‐sided, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Changes in Body Composition

After 16 weeks of treatment, changes in body composition were significantly greater in the COM group than in the MET group (Table 2 and Figure 2). Specifically, the mean reduction in VAT was 4.67 cm2 (SD 9.59) in the MET group compared with 34.13 cm2 (SD 15.33) in the COM group (p < 0.001). Total fat mass decreased by 515.56 g (SD 656.24) in the MET group and by 7494.06 g (SD 2395.91) in the COM group (p < 0.001). After adjustment for baseline total fat mass using ANCOVA, compared with the MET group, the COM group showed a significantly greater reduction in total fat mass, with an adjusted mean difference of −7019 g (95% CI, −8426 to −5611; p < 0.001).

Changes in Metabolic Parameters

After 16 weeks, the COM group showed significantly greater reductions in OGIRT‐0h, HbA1c and HOMA‐IR than the MET group (Table 2). Notably, HOMA‐IR decreased by 0.86 (SD 2.15) in the MET group and by 2.65 (SD 2.18) in the COM group (p = 0.026).

Changes in lipid levels also differed significantly between the two groups (Table 2). Reductions in TG, TC and LDL‐C were greater in the COM group than in the MET group (p = 0.030, p = 0.018 and p = 0.023, respectively).

Changes in Inflammatory Markers

As an inflammatory marker, hs‐CRP showed a greater reduction in the COM group than in the MET group (p = 0.040, Table 2). Additionally, changes in LEP and ADP also differed between the two groups (p = 0.002 and p = 0.038, respectively; Table 2).

Changes in Sex Hormones and AMH

After 16 weeks of treatment, the reductions in DHEA‐S and TEST were significantly greater in the COM group than in the MET group (p = 0.016 and p = 0.010, respectively; Table 2 and Figure S1). Notably, AMH declined by 1.00 ng/mL (SD 2.49) in the MET group and by 3.64 ng/mL (SD 1.93) in the COM group (p = 0.002; Table 2 and Figure S2). No significant between‐group differences were observed for changes in SHBG, E2, PRL, LH, LH/FSH or FAI (Table 2).

Menstrual Cycle Recovery and Reproductive Outcomes

All participants presented with menstrual irregularity at baseline. In the MET group, one participant had a previous live birth, four had experienced one miscarriage and one had experienced two miscarriages. In the COM group, one participant had a previous live birth and four had experienced one miscarriage. After 16 weeks, menstrual cycle recovery was observed in 21.4% (6/28) of participants in the MET group and 53.6% (15/28) of participants in the COM group (p = 0.013; Table 3). Among participants who achieved menstrual cycle recovery, the mean weight loss was 7.89 kg (SD 4.67) and AMH decreased by 3.32 ng/mL (SD 1.96). WC, TEST and HOMA‐IR also showed decreases from baseline (Table S1).

Adverse Events

No serious adverse events or deaths occurred during the study. Mild gastrointestinal adverse events were the most common. Nausea was the most frequent adverse event, occurring in 16.7% (5/30) of participants in the MET group and 23.3% (7/30) in the COM group. Diarrhoea was the second most common event, reported in 13.3% (4/30) of participants in the MET group and 16.7% (5/30) in the COM group. Vomiting occurred in 6.7% (2/30) of participants in both groups. In the MET group, two participants withdrew because of intolerable gastrointestinal side effects. In the COM group, one participant withdrew due to cholecystitis and another due to herpes zoster. Most gastrointestinal adverse events were mild and resolved within 1 month.

Discussion

The recent development of several novel agents for the management of T2DM and obesity has provided new therapeutic perspectives for PCOS. This study further extends the available evidence by evaluating the effects of low‐dose tirzepatide, a dual GIP/GLP‐1 receptor agonist, combined with MET on metabolism, inflammation and reproductive outcomes in overweight/obese women with PCOS. The findings demonstrated that, compared with MET monotherapy, COM treatment significantly reduced body weight, improved fat distribution, ameliorated insulin resistance and hyperandrogenism and restored menstrual cycles in overweight/obese women with PCOS. Additionally, COM treatment showed potential advantages in improving pregnancy outcomes.

Obesity is highly prevalent in women with PCOS, playing a critical role in its pathogenesis. Moreover, obesity directly exacerbates metabolic abnormalities and reproductive complications in these women and also indirectly contributes to these via increased insulin resistance and compensatory hyperinsulinemia [18]. In this study, the COM group was significantly superior to the MET group in terms of weight loss, fat distribution improvement and amelioration of cardiovascular risk factors. Women in the MET group lost an average of 1.7 kg, compared to 10.4 kg in the COM group. In comparison, Angelopoulos et al. reported that low‐dose tirzepatide treatment resulted in a mean weight loss of 8.2 kg after 12 weeks [16]. The difference in weight change may be due to the varying treatment durations and the synergistic effects of tirzepatide combined with MET. Similarly, the COM group was more effective than the MET group in reducing WHR and fat mass based on DXA, with a marked decrease in visceral fat. Notably, the proportion of lean mass loss relative to total weight loss was substantially lower in the COM group than in the MET group, consistent with findings from the SURPASS J‐mono study [19]. Women with PCOS often exhibit abdominal obesity and visceral fat accumulation, which can induce adipocyte dysfunction, leading to hyperandrogenism, chronic low‐grade inflammation, worsening insulin resistance and an increased cardiometabolic burden [20]. Rochira et al. systematically reviewed the effects of tirzepatide in individuals with obesity, demonstrating significant reductions in fat mass and improved visceral fat distribution [21]. Our findings further indicate that COM therapy may be particularly effective in reducing abdominal adiposity and improving fat distribution in women with PCOS. Traditionally, a weight loss of 5% or greater has been considered the threshold for meaningful metabolic benefits [22]. Our results showed that greater weight loss was associated with significant improvements in metabolic outcomes among women with PCOS. COM therapy achieved a greater magnitude of weight reduction, accompanied by multifaceted benefits, including reductions in WC, blood pressure, lipid profiles and insulin levels. These findings align with a post hoc analysis of the SURMOUNT‐1 trial, which supports the potential cardiometabolic benefits of tirzepatide in overweight/obese women with PCOS [23].

Approximately 50%–80% of women with PCOS exhibit insulin resistance and their risk of developing diabetes is 5‐ to 10‐fold higher than that of women without PCOS [1, 24]. Although MET remains a first‐line treatment for T2DM, its capacity to improve glucose metabolism, insulin resistance and compensatory hyperinsulinemia is limited. In this study, COM treatment significantly improved glucose metabolism, insulin resistance and lipid profiles in women with PCOS, whereas MET monotherapy did not yield comparable effects. The improvements observed with COM therapy appeared greater than those reported for other GLP‐1 RA plus MET regimens [6, 9, 10]. Our results are consistent with findings from the SURPASS‐1 trial of tirzepatide in patients with T2DM, suggesting that COM therapy may represent a promising option for achieving more rapid and effective improvements in insulin sensitivity and glucose‐lipid metabolism [25]. The greater benefit observed in the COM group may be attributed to the synergistic effects of GLP‐1 and GIP, which enhance glucose‐dependent insulin secretion, improve insulin sensitivity in adipose tissue and may favourably influence lipid metabolism by slowing gastric emptying and reducing intestinal lipid absorption [26, 27, 28].

Chronic low‐grade inflammation is a key pathological feature of PCOS, often exacerbated by overweight or obesity, which further influences the inflammatory state [29, 30]. The hs‐CRP level serves as a sensitive biomarker in women with PCOS, reflecting both chronic low‐grade inflammation and long‐term cardiovascular risk [29]. Our study demonstrated that COM treatment significantly reduced hs‐CRP levels after 16 weeks, whereas MET monotherapy did not produce a similar effect. This finding aligns with the anti‐inflammatory effects of tirzepatide reported by Masson et al., although the changes observed in the MET group differ from those previously reported by Morin‐Papunen et al. [31, 32]. As suggested by earlier studies, the effects of MET on hs‐CRP levels may only become apparent after a longer treatment duration. Obese women with PCOS typically exhibit a distinct state characterised by elevated LEP and reduced ADP levels, a pattern closely linked with insulin resistance, chronic low‐grade inflammation and metabolic disturbances. LEP, a pro‐inflammatory adipokine, is positively correlated with adipose mass and plays a critical role in immunometabolic crosstalk, whereas ADP, a key anti‐inflammatory adipokine, enhances insulin sensitivity in peripheral tissues and improves the lipid profile in women with PCOS [33, 34]. In this study, COM therapy significantly improved the “high LEP‐low ADP” pathological profile in women with PCOS. This finding is consistent with reports by Frøssing et al. and Elkind‐Hirsch et al. [35, 36]. This bidirectional modulation of the adipokine profile may contribute to alleviating chronic low‐grade inflammation in women with PCOS.

PCOS remains a leading cause of anovulatory infertility, with approximately 50% of affected women also presenting with obesity and insulin resistance [1, 2]. Both factors disrupt the hypothalamic–pituitary–ovarian (HPO) axis, leading to ovulatory dysfunction and adverse reproductive outcomes, potentially creating a self‐reinforcing vicious cycle [2]. Compared to the MET group, the COM group was associated with a greater reduction in TEST levels and a markedly higher rate of menstrual cycle recovery. Further subgroup analysis revealed that greater weight loss was significantly associated with improvements in hyperandrogenism. This finding aligns with previous studies reporting that GLP‐1 RAs confer reproductive endocrine benefits in women with PCOS, a result further supported by existing animal experiments on tirzepatide treatment [5, 9, 12, 37]. The therapeutic effects observed in the COM group may be attributed to greater weight loss, leading to a marked reduction in visceral adiposity, significant improvements in insulin resistance and increased SHBG secretion. These changes, in turn, may modulate hypothalamic signalling pathways involved in central HPO axis regulation, thereby reducing androgen production, lowering TEST levels and ultimately improving menstrual cycle regularity and ovulatory function [5]. Elevated AMH is widely regarded as a reliable marker of PCOM in women with PCOS [38, 39]. According to the 2023 international guidelines, AMH has been established as an alternative marker for PCOM and can be used to assess treatment efficacy in women with PCOS [40]. Relevant animal studies have also suggested that tirzepatide may improve reproductive hormone levels, glucose metabolism and lipid profiles in PCOS mice, while increasing the number of developing follicles and ameliorating ovarian histopathology [37]. In this study, a greater reduction in AMH levels was observed in the COM group than in the MET group. This may be attributed to the substantial weight loss and improved insulin sensitivity with COM therapy, with underlying mechanisms potentially involving direct or indirect modulation of the ovarian microenvironment. Our results further support the potential of tirzepatide to improve reproductive endocrine outcomes in women with PCOS.

The present study also evaluated pregnancy outcomes in overweight/obese women with PCOS treated with COM therapy. The results demonstrated a higher total pregnancy rate in the COM group than in the MET group, mainly driven by a significantly higher natural pregnancy rate. No significant difference was found between the two groups in the other pregnancy rate. This finding is broadly consistent with pregnancy outcomes reported in clinical studies of other GLP‐1 RAs, such as exenatide and semaglutide, further suggesting the potential value of tirzepatide in addressing metabolism‐associated infertility [9, 41]. In line with evidence from animal studies by Yang et al., which indicated that GLP‐1 RAs may promote follicular maturation and restore ovulatory capacity, COM therapy may enhance opportunities for natural conception by improving metabolic status, restoring ovulation and menstrual cycle regularity and enhancing follicular development [42].

This study has several limitations. First, it was a single‐centre trial with a small sample size and short duration, highlighting the need for larger, multicenter studies to confirm the impact of tirzepatide on reproductive outcomes. The study population was restricted to Asian women aged 18–40 years with PCOS and overweight or obesity (BMI ≥ 24 kg/m2), which may limit the generalizability of these findings to other ethnic groups, age ranges or BMI categories. Additionally, the open‐label design may have introduced assessment bias, although the use of objective clinical measures helped mitigate this concern. Furthermore, given the aim of minimising side effects and reducing treatment costs in women planning pregnancy, this study focused primarily on low‐dose tirzepatide in women with PCOS. Future research could explore the effects of higher doses in this population.

Conclusion

This study provides preliminary evidence that, in overweight/obese women with PCOS, low‐dose tirzepatide combined with MET may be more effective than MET monotherapy in reducing body weight, improving visceral fat distribution, alleviating insulin resistance and inflammation and improving reproductive endocrine parameters and menstrual cycle regularity, with generally tolerable short‐term side effects. The combination therapy also showed potential advantages in improving pregnancy outcomes. Further large‐scale, double‐blind, randomised trials of longer duration are warranted to evaluate the effect of GIP/GLP‐1 receptor agonists on total pregnancy rates and assisted reproductive outcomes in women with PCOS.
