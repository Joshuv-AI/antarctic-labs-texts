Analysis and research on multilevel video game addiction characteristics among children and adolescents based on association rules
Authors: Pan Wen, Ziyue Tan, Puzhen Su, Yuhang She, Liping Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Video game addiction (VGA) among children and adolescents is a growing public health concern, yet limited research has explored how addiction manifests across varying levels of severity. This study aimed to examine the multi-level characteristics of VGA by identifying behavioral, emotional, social, and family-related patterns associated with different addiction levels. A total of 1106 students from grades 3 to 12 in Shantou City, China, completed an anonymous questionnaire covering demographics, gaming behaviors, emotional responses, peer relationships, and family involvement. Association Rule Mining (ARM), using the Apriori algorithm, was employed to detect patterns in the data. Statistical tests were used to further validate significant associations and interactions. The analysis revealed distinct patterns for different levels of VGA. Moderate to severe VGA was associated with frequent gaming, emotional reliance on games, and parental restriction. Students with mild VGA also played frequently but showed healthier peer relationships, such as discussing personal problems rather than gaming. Those with no VGA had limited interest in gaming, shorter playtime, and more effective emotional regulation strategies. The findings underscore the importance of emotional regulation, peer influence, and family dynamics in understanding VGA severity. Interventions should emphasize emotional management, promote balanced gaming behaviors, encourage healthy peer interactions, and guide parents toward supportive rather than prohibitive involvement. These insights provide practical strategies for preventing and addressing video game addiction in youth.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55005-x.

Introduction

In recent years, video games have become a significant form of entertainment, particularly among children and adolescents1. While video games offer enjoyment and social interaction, the increasing engagement with them has raised concerns about Video Game Addiction (VGA). VGA encompasses both offline and online gaming addictions, with particular emphasis on internet-based massively multiplayer online role-playing games2. Therefore, VGA includes internet gaming disorder (IGD). The American Psychiatric Association (APA) has recognized excessive video gaming as a formal psychological disorder3. In 2013, IGD was included in the fifth edition of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5) as a condition requiring further study4,5. Additionally, the World Health Organization (WHO) officially classified excessive video gaming as a gaming disorder in the International Classification of Diseases, 11th Revision (ICD-11), underscoring the global health implications of uncontrolled gaming behaviors6. Recent studies estimate that approximately 9.9% of adolescents worldwide are impacted by VGA, suggesting that nearly one in ten teenagers may be struggling with this condition7, with a markedly higher rate in boys (6.8%) than in girls (1.3%)8. In certain Asian countries, the prevalence rate for internet addictive was even higher among male adolescents9.

VGA has far-reaching effects that encompass both physiological harm10–12 and mental-health challenges13. Prior studies have shown that excessive gaming is linked to elevated anxiety, depression, and emotional instability14–16. Adolescents with VGA often experience deteriorating academic performance and weakened interpersonal relationships17,18, with severe cases sometimes resulting in aggressive or self-injurious behaviors19,20. Over time, adolescents with VGA may face an increased risk of impaired psycho-social well-being in adulthood, including reduced psycho-social health, lower self-esteem, diminished social support, and lower overall life satisfaction21, emphasizing the urgency of early identification and intervention.

Previous studies have identified various factors associated with game addiction, including gaming-related factors, individual and familial factors, interpersonal relationships, and personality traits22–24. Furthermore, some studies have also highlighted the significance of the number and type of genres played, as well as the amount of time spent gaming online, as predictors of game addiction25. However, much of the existing research has examined these factors in isolation26, overlooking how they interact or collectively influence gaming addiction across different levels of severity. This limitation restricts the development of tailored prevention and intervention strategies that accurately target distinct VGA profiles. Therefore, a more holistic approach is needed to uncover the complex interplay of internal and external factors on gaming addiction.

To address this gap, this study employs Association Rule Mining (ARM), an robust data mining technique that identifies significant correlations within large datasets without relying on predefined assumptions27. By applying ARM, this study aims to capture the complex interactions among internal and external factors at various levels of VGA severity, thereby providing more multidimensional insights and informing targeted interventions more effectively.

Methods

Participants and procedure

At the study-design stage, the minimum required sample size was estimated using the standard formula for cross-sectional studies:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:n=\frac{{z}_{\alpha\:}^{2}\ast\:pq}{{d}^{2}}$$\end{document}

Previous studies reported considerable variation in the VGA prevalence9. Given this heterogeneity, a hypothesized prevalence of 30% was assumed for sample-size estimation, therefore, p = 0.30, q = 1 − p, d = 0.1p, and Z = 1.96 for a two-sided α of 0.05. The estimated minimum sample size was 897. After inflating this number by 10% to account for potential nonresponse and invalid questionnaires, the target sample size was 986.

This study adopted a stratified cluster approach. Shantou was divided into two areas: urban and suburban, from which four primary school, four middle schools and three high schools were selected separately. In each selected school, one class per target grade was randomly chosen, and all students in the selected classes were invited to participate. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (a) students from the selected schools and (b) students for whom signed consent forms were obtained. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (a) students who were absent from school during the survey procedure, (b) students older than 18 years, and (c) students who did not complete the survey.

Before the study, trained researchers informed every student about the purpose of the survey and assured them that the responses would remain anonymous and confidential. Students were also informed that they could whitdraw at any time withour penalty if they felt uncomfortable. After written informed consent had been obtained, questionnaires were distributed to each student and completed during a 20-min class period. Data collection was conducted from November 2021 to February 2022. A total of 1,106 students completed the survey and met the eligibility criteria for included in the final analysis. This number exceeded the minimum required sample size because the study used a stratified cluster sampling design and all eligible students in the selected classes were invited to participate. Therefore, the larger final sample reflected cluster-based recruitment rather than intentional oversampling of any specific subgroup.

Ethic

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shantou University Medical School (No. SUMC-2022-046). Approval was also obtained from the school’s student affairs office and counsellor. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations, including the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all participants and/or their legal guardians prior to participation in the study.

Measures

Demographic characteristics

The questionnaire collected demographic information including grade level, gender, only-child status, migrant status, living arrangements, type of schooling, academic performance, and substance use (smoking and alcohol consumption). These characteristics are presented in Table 1.

Video gaming components

This study examined both extrinsic and intrinsic factors associated with video gaming addiction. Two primary extrinsic factors were evaluated: family involvement and peer relationships. Family involvement was assessed using 3 items. Peer relationships were evaluated using 10 items. Three intrinsic components were analyzed: gaming behaviors, emotional regulation, and self-control. Gaming behavior was assessed through 7 items. Emotional regulation was measured using 5 items. Self-control was assessed using 2 items. Additional data were collected on gaming preferences, including the gaming platforms and the types of video games. The full questionnaire used in this study is provided as Supplementary material Appendix B.

The Video Game Addiction Scale

The Video Game Addiction Scale (VGAS) is an 18-item self-report instrument that the scales was designed as a combination of the subscription of 14-item Video Game Addition Test (VAT)28, 7-item Gaming Addiction Scale (GAS)29, and 19-item Revised Chinese Internet Addiction Scale (CIAS-R)30. Each item has five answers, never, rarely, sometimes, often, and always, rated as 4 and 0 points, respectively. By the VAT scoring, the addition level is divided into 4 levels. A score of 0–22 implies that there is no symptom of addiction in their video gaming behavior, 23–45 shows they are at risk of getting addicted to video games, 46–67 is an alarm that the child has some levels of addiction, and 68–90 represents gamers who are already addicted to video games. The scale had good internal reliability for this aggregated sample (Cronbach’s α = 0.878, KMO = 0.936). For specific indicators, see the supplementary material. (Supplementary material Appendix A)

Data analysis

Data mining, a statistical approach designed to identify patterns within datasets rather than test predefined hypotheses, is particularly useful for generating and refining new hypotheses. In this study, we employed ARM—a rule-based machine learning technique—to uncover relationships between variables and to identify patterns associated with different levels of VGA in children.

ARM works by first identifying sets of co-occurring dichotomous variables (itemsets) within each participant’ s data. It then uncovers specific associations, or rules, among frequent itemsets. The Apriori algorithm31,32 was used to extract these itemsets, which were considered frequent if they co-occurred in at least a specified proportion of the dataset (the support threshold). From these frequent itemsets, ARM generated rules in the form “If itemset X occurs, then Y also occurs”, where X represents the antecedent attributes and Y, predefined in our analysis, represents different levels of VGA. In addition to overall support, rules rules were also evaluated using confidence, defined as the probability that Y occurs when X is present For example, if X and Y co-occur in 10% of participants, but X is present in 70%, the confidence would be low, indicating that Y occurs only in a small subset of participants with X. All analyses were conducted in Python using the mlxtend library.

Dichotomization of variables

For ARM to be effective, all variables were converted into dichotomous indicators that denote the presence or absence of a particular attribute for each participant. The majority of questionnaire items were originally dichotomous or categorical variables. For example, variables such as gender, family structure, and peer interactions were recorded in binary or multiple-choice format. Discrete variables were categorized into binary indicators for each possible value. For example, the variable “sex” was split into two variables, “female” and “male”, each coded as “True” or “False” based on the participant’s gender. Continuous variables were grouped into discrete intervals; for instance, grade was divided into 3-educational stages to reflect educational milestones and ensure sufficient subgroup sizes for pattern discovery, with each stage represented by a binary variable. Similarly, the items from the VGAS scale were based on a 5-point Likert scale. These variables were grouped into discrete intervals and binarized. These transformations ensured the identification of meaningful itemsets and valid rule generation. (Fig. 1)

Association rules mining

ARM was used to characterize groups of children with moderate to severe VGA, mild VGA, or no VGA. We identified rules containing one or two components that effectively distinguished between these groups. The confidence threshold was set at 0.33, meaning that at least one-third of participants with the single or two antecedent attribute antecedent attribute (X) also reported the consequent VGA (or no VGA). The support was set so that the rule would occur in at least 50% of the children who reported different levels of VGA (or no VGA). Since various levels of VGA occurred in different proportions, support differed based on the outcome with 50% of children with mild VGA equal to 33% of the entire sample (support = 0.33). Because these groups (moderate to severe VGA and no VGA) contained fewer participants, further adjustments made for these group sizes: 50% of children with moderate to severe VGA equal to 23% of the sample (support = 0.23) and 50% of children with no VGA equal to 26% of the sample (support = 0.26).(Fig. 1).

Removing redundant rules

ARM can generate a substantial number of rules, many of which may be redundant (e.g., redundant rules containing the same attributes in a different order, such as “grade + sex → mild VGA” and “sex + grade → mild VGA”). To eliminate redundancy, we used lift, which is the ratio of observed support to expected support under independence, to evaluate the association strength between antecedents and the consequent. Consistent with standard practice33, only rules with a lift > 1 were retained, indicating positive dependence. Additionally, Fisher’s exact test was applied to determine whether certain antecedent attributes occurred disproportionately among children with different levels of VGA, with statistical significance determined (P < 0.05) using the Holm-Bonferroni correction34. (Fig. 1)

Testing multiplicative effects

A multiplicative effect reflects that the combined presence of two attributes has an effect on the odds the student reporting a certain level of VGA (or no VGA) that is greater than simply adding the odds of the two individual attributes. In order to test for multiplicative effects, rules were first limited to those where at least one of the two attributes was also significant, after multiple comparison correction, as a single attribute. Rules with possible multiplicative effects were identified by comparing the odds ratios (OR) between the rule with the single attribute to that of the rule with the two attributes. If the OR for the two-attribute rule exceeded the 95% confidence interval of the corresponding single-attribute OR, further analysis was conducted using logistic regression to confirm significant interactions between the attributes. Multiplicative effects are referred to significant interactions between the two attributes within logistic regression analyses. (Fig. 1)

These included 195 children with no VGA, 740 children with mild VGA, and 171 children with moderate to severe VGA. No significant difference was observed in the ranking of only-child status and academic performance between children with different levels of VGA and those without VGA (χ2 = 5.53, P = 0.063; χ2 = 8.46, P = 0.390, Table 1). Characterization of moderate to severe VGA

Association rules mining: single antecedent attribute

Using single-attribute rules, we identified 26 items, of which 12 were excluded due to a lift < 1. The remaining 14 items had a lift > 1, indicating a notable degree of association. Of these, 9 rules characterized moderate to severe VGA through intrinsic factors, while 5 were associated with extrinsic factors, such as parental attitudes and video game content. Intrinsic factors associated with severe VGA included emotional regulation (e.g., feelings related to playing video games were self-fulfillment and happiness, as captured by Q2 and Q3, and playing for relaxation and entertainment as shown in Q9), and gaming behaviors (e.g., frequent gaming and preference for gaming over other leisure activities, as shown in Q1 and Q8). Additionally, students with moderate to severe VGA were more likely to report parental restrictions, such as parents who prohibited them from play video games (Q21) or who expressed concern about the impact of gaming on their education (Q22). They also showed a preference for multiplayer online games and action games (Q13 and Q28). Overall, these rules suggest that moderate-to-severe VGA is associated with increased gaming behavior, a desire for gratification through gaming, heightened parental restriction, and a preference for online action games (Table 2).

Association rules mining: two antecedent attributes

A total of 134 two-attribute rules were identified, of which 32 demonstrated a fair level of consistency after applying lift > 1, Fisher’s exact test, and Holm-Bonferroni correction. Nearly half of these rules (n = 13) combined internal factors, particularly emotional regulation and gaming behaviors. Another 10 rules combined internal factors with external factors, such as family involvement variables. The most common characteristics of students with moderate to severe VGA involved gaming for self-satisfaction or relaxation (53% of the rules; Q2, Q3, and Q9), regular gaming or preference for gaming (37%; Q1 and Q8), and parental restrictions on gaming (28%; Q21). These findings underscore emotional regulation, gaming behaviors, and family involvement factors as the primary determinants of moderate-to-severe VGA, rather than demographic or family characteristics (Supplementary Table A1).

Testing multiplicative effects

To test possible multiplicative effects, we focused on rules that included at least one attribute individually characterized moderate to severe VGA (Table 2). This resulted in 32 rules, of which 12 had odds ratios surpassing the 95% CI of those from single-attribute rules (Supplementary Table A2). This suggests that these 12 two-attribute rules contributed additional predictive power in identifying children with moderate to severe VGA. We further examined whether the inclusion of a second attribute resulted in a multiplicative increase in the odds of reporting moderate to severe VGA. In ARM, confidence represents the conditional probability of reporting VGA given the antecedent attributes. Thus, multiplicative effects are reflected in changes to both the probability and odds of reporting VGA.

Recognizing that these multiplicative effects might be driven by distinct behavioral and familial influences, we next delve into factor categories of moderate to severe VGA—including gaming behaviors, emotional regulation, and family involvement—that appear to play critical roles in amplifying the risk of moderate to severe VGA.

Q1 (gaming behaviors): Students who usually ‘playing video games’ had 2.6 times greater odds of reporting moderate to severe VGA compared to those who did ‘not’ (β(SE) = 0.96 (0.20), eβ= 2.62, P < 0.001). If these students did not refuse a friend’s invitation to play (Q10), the probability of reporting moderate to severe VGA increased from 67% to 78%, with odds 20 times greater than for those who declined the invitation (Table 3). If children whose parents were ‘not’ allowed them to play games (Q21) further raised the probability to 80%, with odds 19.7 times higher (Table 3). Therefore, the presence of child video gaming behaviors that included both accepting a friend’s invitation to play video games and parental restrictions on their game playing increased the odds of moderate to severe VGA.

Q2 (emotional regulation): students who reported feelings of ‘self-fulfillment’ when playing video games had 2.4 times greater odds of reporting moderate to severe VGA compared to students who reported other feelings(β(SE) = 0.89(0.17), eβ=2.44, P < 0.001). When these students also reported playing for ‘entertainment’(Q9), their probability of reporting moderate to severe VGA increased from 71% to 81%. The odds were 15.34 times greater for students who reported both ‘self-fulfillment’ and ‘entertainment’ compared to those who reported alternative feelings (Table 3). Additionally, regular gaming (Q1) increased the probability of moderate to severe VGA to 98%, with odds 10.28 times higher than those who have other behaviors (Table 3). If students preferred ‘multiplayer online games’(Q13), the probability increased to 73%, and the odds were 10.3 times higher compared to students who preferred ‘single games’ (Table 3). Parental restrictions (Q21) further increased the probability to 83%, with odds 20.4 times greater (Table 3). Overall, this indicates that when combined with self-fulfillment of playing video games, students’ reports of playing video games for entertainment, usually playing video games, loving multiplayer online games, and parental restrictions on their game playing all showed multiplicative increases in the odds of a child reporting moderate to severe VGA.

Q9 (emotional regulation): Students who reported playing video games for ‘entertainment’ had 3.1 times greater odds of reporting moderate to severe VGA than those who reported other reasons for gaming (β(SE) = 1.15 (0.20), eβ= 3.16, P < 0.001). If these students also often discuss video games with friends(Q7), their probability of reporting moderate to severe VGA increased from 67% to 86%, with odds 23.4 times greater than for those who did not engage in such discussions (Table 3). Further, if students did not refuse a friend’s invitation to play (Q10), the probability increased to 78%, with odds 14.3 times greater (Table 3). If parents did ‘not’ allow them to play games(Q21), the probability increased to 69%, with odds 14.3 times greater compared to children whose parents allowed gaming (Table 3). Together, this indicates that when combined with playing video games for entertainment, discussion of video games with friends, accepting friend’s invitation to play video games together, and parental restrictions on their game playing all showed multiplicative increases in the odds of a student reporting moderate to severe VGA.

Q21 (family involvement): Students whose parents did ‘not’ allow them to play games had 2.7 times greater odds of reporting moderate to severe VGA compared to those whose parents did (β(SE) = 0.97 (0.21), eβ= 2.66, P < 0.001). If these students also discussed video games with their friends (Q7), the probability of reporting moderate to severe VGA increased from 58% to 90%, with odds 14.3 times greater (Table 3). Preferences for gaming in their spare time (Q8) increased the probability to 84%., and the odds were 15.8 times greater (Table 3). If students did not refuse a friend’s invitation to play video games (Q10), the probability increased to 79%, and the odds were 32.7 times greater (Table 3). Together, this indicates that when combined with parental restrictions on their game playing, discussion of video games with friends, preferring video games in their spare time, and accepting a friend’s invitation to play video games all showed multiplicative increases in the odds of a student reporting moderate to severe VGA.

Characterization of mild VGA

Of the 7 rules with a single antecedent attribute, all showed fair agreement (Table 4). These rules included 4 related to children’s peer relationships and 3 to gaming behaviors. Specifically, mild VGA was associated with regular video game playing (Q1) and the absence of nail-biting or word repetition symptoms before and after gaming (Q25 and 26). social environment factors included frequently discussing personal problems with friends (Q7), not betting on gaming competitions (Q12), disliking online friendships (Q18), and not being hurt by online friends. After controlling for multiple comparisons, 12 rules remained significant (Table A3). All the rules contain either a game behavior element or a social element. The most common traits for mild VGA students were frequent gaming (42% of rules, Q1 and 8), not making online friends or being harmed by online friends (33% of rules, Q14,18 and 20), and discussing personal problems with friends or rejecting gaming-related gambling (33% of rules, Q7, 11 and 12). Subsequent testing showed only one multiplicative effect: the combination of no nail-biting behavior after gaming (Q26) and gaming < 1 h per week (Q4) increased the probability of developing mild VGA (Table A5).

There were 10 rules showing fair consistency (Table 5). There were 11 rules with multiple antecedents that were significant with fair agreement (Table A6). The most common characteristics for No VGA were not playing or disliking video games (42% of rules, Q1 and 8), and thinking about homework or wasting time during gaming sessions (30% of rules, Q2 and Q3). Having zero online friends (Q15), and no history of adverse experiences (Q30) combined with not typically playing video games (Q1) showed multiplicative increases in No VGA probability. Additionally, combinations involving thinking about homework during gaming (Q2), factors including weekly gaming time of less than one hour (Q4), not preferring video games during leisure(Q8), and not readily playing newly discovered (Q27) also demonstrated a similar multiplicative effect.

Discussion

This study analyzes video game addiction in children and adolescents by exploring factors across different VGA severity levels. While previous research linked academic performance and gender to VGA35–37, our findings did not support these as reliable indicators. Instead, using ARM, the study identified that gaming behaviors, emotional regulation, and social environment (peer relationships and family involvement) are key predictors of moderate to severe VGA. Specifically, gaming experiences related to self-fulfillment, entertainment, and relaxation played a central role associated with more severe VGA, whereas adolescents with mild VGA exhibited more balanced gaming and social behaviors. These results highlight the importance of interventions that focus on modifying gaming habits, enhancing emotional regulation, and improving social support structures.

Gaming behaviors emerged as a critical factor in VGA severity. Notably, frequent gaming (Q1) was the most common characteristic of students with mild VGA (42% of rules), suggesting that even those with mild VGA exhibit significant gaming behaviors. Moreover, students who reported frequent gaming were 2.6 times more likely to report moderate to severe VGA. This finding confirms that increased gaming frequency is a robust predictor of higher VGA severity25,38.

Emotional regulation was also found to be central internal factor. Students with moderate to severe VGA reported relying on gaming for self-satisfaction, entertainment, or relaxation, suggesting they struggle with managing negative emotions or stress. This aligns with previous researches suggesting that using gaming as a primary source of emotional fulfillment can be a marker of problematic gaming behaviors39,40 and problematic internet use41. The interplay between emotional dysregulation and frequent gaming behaviors appears to create a feedback loop that increasing the probability of moderate to severe VGA to 98%, which means that the risk of developing moderate to severe VGA is greatly increased42. Consequently, these findings underscore the need for targeted interventions that enhance emotional regulation skills, particularly among adolescents vulnerable to addictive gaming patterns.

For social environment, peer relationships and parental involvement played significant roles. Students who discussed gaming with friends (Q7) were 3.6 times more likely to report severe VGA, indicating the importance of peer interactions around gaming in VGA severity. this finding aligns with research suggesting that gaming-related peer groups may foster social isolation and deviant behaviors that exacerbate addiction18,26,43, highlighting the importance of fostering healthier peer relationships. Those with mild VGA (33% of rules) or no VGA (30% of rules) tend to discuss personal problems or homework with friends, further emphasizing the need for promoting positive peer interactions.

As for family involvement, students whose parents prohibited gaming were 6 times more likely to report moderate to severe VGA. When combined with gaming invitations from friends or gaming for self-satisfaction, parental restrictions further increased the odds of students reporting severe VGA. This presents an interesting paradox, as previous research has linked parents’ neglectful behavior (such as phubbing) to a higher risk of problematic internet use44. Children with more severe VGA may elicit stricter parental limits. Conversely, rigid prohibitions can heighten adolescents’ anxiety or depressive symptoms, prompting them to conceal their gaming and seek peer support26,45. This finding emphasizes the need for more nuanced parental strategies, which balance monitoring with fostering open communication and setting healthy gaming boundaries46. Parents should be encouraged to guide their children in making informed decisions about gaming while promoting broader social and academic involvement to reduce the risk of VGA. Future longitudinal studies are needed to establish the direction of causality.

Overall, the study’s findings emphasize the importance of developing targeted interventions that address both internal and external factors across different stages of VGA. For adolescents with severe VGA, interventions programs should prioritize improving emotional regulation skills, restructuring family interactions, and systematically managing gaming behavior. Specific strategies include cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT) or mindfulness-based training47,48 aimed at enhancing emotional coping skills, structured family counseling sessions to improve parent-child communication and reduce conflict around gaming, and developing practical behavioral strategies such as clear rules on gaming duration and parental guidance rather than strict prohibitions. Parental involvement should ideally blend supportive guidance with the promotion of autonomy, emphasizing effective communication and balanced restrictions.

For those with mild VGA, fostering positive peer relationships and promoting extracurricular activities may help prevent progression to more severe forms. School-based peer-support groups or extracurricular programs promoting teamwork, face-to-face social interactions, and collaborative problem-solving are recommended. These interventions should also encourage open dialogue among peers about managing gaming behaviors, promoting mutual communication, and preventing escalation toward severe addiction.

For students with no VGA, interventions should primarily focus on reinforcing their existing positive behaviors, including maintaining balanced leisure activities, structured time management, and fostering proactive engagement in academic tasks. School and family-based educational programs can reinforce these healthy attitudes, preventing future potential development of problematic gaming behaviors.

Despite these valuable insights, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this study was conducted exclusively in the Chaoshan region of China, limiting the generalizability of findings. Future research should include diverse cultural and age groups to validate these correlations. Second, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference; longitudinal studies are necessary to clarify temporal relationships. Third, reliance on self-reported data may introduce bias; thus, future studies could incorporate objective measures, such as screen-time tracking, to enhance validity. Moreover, although the study covered critical domains including gaming behaviors, emotional regulation, peer relationships, and family involvement, factors like neuropsychological aspects, socioeconomic status, and parental mental health were not assessed. Including these omitted variables would provide a more comprehensive understanding of video game addiction and is recommended for future research.

Finally, while ARM yielded valuable insights, decisions regarding algorithm choice, variable discretization, and hyperparameter settings may have affected results. Given the novelty of applying ARM to internet addiction, we selected the Apriori algorithm for its established methodology, transparency, and availability in widely-used software (e.g., mlxtend for Python). However, emerging methods such as graph-based techniques or FP-Growth might improve outcomes and merit exploration in future studies49–51. Although most variables were categorical in this study, some continuous variables required discretization to enhance interpretability within the ARM framework, potentially reducing specificity. Future research could maintain ordinal structures to preserve information while ensuring method compatibility. Additionally, further investigation is needed into how these methodological choices impacted the findings.

In conclusion, this study advances our understanding of VGA in children and adolescents by highlighting the critical roles of gaming behaviors, emotional regulation, Peer relationships, and family involvement. These insights lay the groundwork for targeted prevention and intervention strategies aimed at reducing VGA severity, while also calling for further research into the complex social dynamics of gaming and the impact of parental strategies on mitigating VGA.
