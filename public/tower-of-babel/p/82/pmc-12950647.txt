Diverse novel and avian-associated viruses in the ileal viromes of Northern Mockingbird (Mimus polyglottos)
Authors: Brian P Bourke, Sergei V Drovetski, Koray Ergunay, Yvonne-Marie Linton, Gary Voelker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractViruses are the most abundant and diverse organisms on Earth, though only a small portion cause disease. Understanding viral diversity is key to understanding and predicting pathogen emergence and zoonotic spillover. Here, we use meta-transcriptomic sequencing to examine the viral communities in the ileum of 25 Northern Mockingbirds (Mimus polyglottos) from various locations across Texas. We assembled high-quality genomes of 43 viral species (40 species identified to 13 families, one to kingdom, and two to realm), 38 of which were novel. They tentatively represent avian- (n = 3), arthropod- (n = 21), plant- (n = 5) and fungi- (n = 4) associated, or other (n = 10) viruses. The arthropod-associated Dicistroviridae family was the most dominant, comprising known and potentially new species. Of potential epidemiological importance were three novel and avian-associated viruses: members of the families Hepeviridae and Picornaviridae, and a new Matryoshka RNA virus. The Matryoshka RNA virus 8 (MaRNAV-8) is sister to other Matryoshka RNA viruses, and its co-occurrence with haemosporida further supports the nested virus-parasite-vector-vertebrate host relationship of this group of viruses, with potential implications for parasite evolution, fitness and load and vector competence. The Picornaviridae virus is a member of an avian hepatovirus clade, found nested within a clade containing both the mammalian pathogens Hepatovirus A – I and the avian Tremovirus pathogens, suggestive of a newly discovered pathogen of Northern Mockingbird. Although the recovered Hepeviridae virus is of unknown pathology, its family members include the Hepatitis E viruses. With the great diversity and novelty described from ileal viromes, discriminating potential pathogens and commensal microbiota from viruses associated with food items remains challenging. A deeper understanding of virus transmission and the risk of potential zoonosis can be enhanced by tracking viruses through the food web and via inter-specific and predator-prey interactions, particular in areas subject to land-use change, where human-wildlife interactions are increased and the risks from emerging pathogens of veterinary and medical importance are more pronounced.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00705-026-06575-8.

Introduction

The considerable interest in viral pathogens over the past decade has been largely driven by a range of scientific, public health, economic, and geopolitical concerns. The effect of the COVID-19 pandemic [1], increased frequency of zoonoses [2], advances in genomics technology [3, 4] and reframing public health threats as matters of national security [5, 6] have all increased attention on viral pathogen research. Notwithstanding this recent interest, our knowledge of global virus diversity remains scant. The International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses (ICTV) currently recognizes less than 20 thousand virus species [7], but global virus estimates range from 10 million to 1 billion species [8, 9]. It has been estimated that 1.67 million unknown viruses infect birds and mammals with up to half of these having zoonotic potential [10].

The advent of highly pathogenic avian influenza [11] and West Nile virus [12] as major public health crises has drawn particular attention to viruses associated with birds. To date, 315 viral pathogens have been associated with wild birds [13], although giving the extent of viral dark matter [14], this is likely a gross underestimate of the true number. Gut microbiota has an important influence on avian health, welfare and performance [15] and the small intestine in particular harbors a great diversity of replicating viruses, many of which are of high pathogenic importance [16]. Studies have shown that non-invasive cloacal and fecal samples yield highly variable microbial diversity and make poor proxy for the small intestine [17–19]. Revealing and characterizing the rich and hidden viral diversity of the small intestine can be an important step in identifying high risk viral lineages, natural reservoirs and important human-wildlife interfaces for zoonosis.

Recent advances in genome sequencing technology have expanded the capacity to detect, identify, and characterize viral pathogens. Meta-omic (meta-genomic and meta-transcriptomic) sequencing, in particular, has enabled the simultaneous sequencing of the complete nucleic acid content of a sample, thereby providing an unbiased, culture-independent and comprehensive framework for the analysis of viral community structure and diversity. These approaches are now being routinely used to characterize viral communities in a wide range of wild [20–24] and domestic [25–28] bird populations. Studies have shown that host taxonomy [21], habitat [23], ecology [29], geographic origin [30], demography [22, 24] and infection status [29] can play an important role in shaping virome diversity.

The Northern Mockingbird (Mimus polyglottos) is a ubiquitous omnivorous bird found across urban and rural landscapes across much of Mexico, the Caribbean and the United States [31]. The species is perhaps most abundant in Texas, where it is found in a range of urban, heavy agricultural, semi-natural and natural habitats [32] and where the crop, livestock and poultry industry is on national strategic importance [33]. Due to this species’ abundance across such habitats, it can be employed as a highly effective sentinel species for viral surveillance across important human-environment-wildlife interfaces.

Here, we employ meta-transcriptomic sequencing to achieve the first characterization of viral communities from the Northern Mockingbird. This survey will provide a baseline for the virus community and pathogenic lineages of this species, upon which further work can be undertaken to understand the factors shaping virus transmission networks and zoonotic risk in the region.

Methodology

Sampling and nucleic acid extraction

This study was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committees of Texas A&M University (permit 2021-0012), U.S. Geological Survey (USGS) Eastern Ecological Science Center (permit 2021-08P) and was conducted in accordance with the approved protocols and guidelines of the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (permit MB205752) and Texas Parks and Wildlife Department (permit SPR-0209-016). Voucher specimens (extended wings and associated tissues) were deposited at the Biodiversity Research and Teaching Collections, Department of Ecology and Conservation Biology, Texas A&M University, College Station, Texas.

For this study, 25 Northern Mockingbirds were collected using a shotgun from June 5–23, 2021, from four locations in Texas, United States (Table 1). Immediately after a bird was collected, approximately 100 mg of ileal content was expelled directly into a 2 mL screw cap tube pre-filled with 1 mL of the DNA/RNA Shield and a mix of 0.5 mm and 0.1 mm ultra-high-density beads (BashingBeads) (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA, USA). Samples were kept at 4◦C until DNA extraction within a maximum of 45 days after collection. Total RNA was extracted from each ileal sample using the ZymoBIOMICS DNA/RNA Miniprep Kit (Zymo Research), following the manufacturer’s protocol, and its concentration measured on a Qubit 4.0 (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) using the High-Sensitivity RNA Assay Kit (ThermoFisher Scientific). All samples submitted for library preparation contained at least 500 ng of RNA.

Library preparation and sequencing

Samples were first ribosomal RNA depleted using the QIAGEN FastSelect rRNA 5S/16S/23S Kit, following the manufacturer’s protocol (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). The RNA sequencing library was prepared using the NEBNext Ultra II RNA Library Preparation Kit, following the manufacturer’s protocol (NEB, Ipswich, MA, USA). Briefly, enriched RNAs were fragmented for 15 minutes at 94°C. First strand and second strand cDNA were subsequently synthesized. cDNA fragments were end repaired and adenylated at 3’ends, and universal adapters were ligated to cDNA fragments, followed by index addition and library enrichment with limited cycle PCR. Sequencing libraries were validated using the Agilent Tapestation 4200 (Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, CA, USA), and quantified using Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) as well as by quantitative PCR (KAPA Biosystems, Wilmington, MA, USA). All libraries were sequenced simultaneously on an Illumina HiSeq 4000 platform (2 × 150bp; Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Library preparation and sequencing was performed by GeneWiz/Azenta Life Sciences (South Plainfield, NJ, USA). All data generated is available as a U.S. Geological Survey data release [34].

Data analysis

Demultiplexed raw Illumina data were initially trimmed and quality filtered using fastp v0.23.3 [35, 36]. Reads associated with the host genome were then removed from the data by using the most closely related NCBI reference genome, California thrasher, Toxostoma redivivum (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/datasets/genome/GCA_013397375.1) as a reference for KneadData v0.12.0 (https://github.com/biobakery/kneaddata) and a bowtie alignment setting of “--very-sensitive-local”.

De novo assembly (MEGAHIT v1.2.9 [37], Trinity 2.15 [38], and Spades v4.0.0 in --meta, --metaviral and --rnaviral modes [39]), virus screening (DeepMicroClass [40] and geNomad [41]), quality filtering (CheckV [42]) and taxonomic classification (Diamond [43]and Megan [44, 45]) was then performed as in the workflow from Ergunay et al [46] and available at https://github.com/rocherbpb/viral_discovery.

Viral genomes were aligned with close and/or congeneric relatives, where possible as described in the International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses (ICTV) report chapters (https://ictv.global/report), using Muscle implemented in SeaView v5.0.4 [47]. Maximum likelihood phylogenetic analysis was performed in IQ-TREE 2 v2.2.0 [48]. Optimal model selection was performed using the -m MFP option and included only amino acid models for viruses via the -msub viral option. A consensus tree was constructed by combining 500 standard nonparametric bootstrap replicate trees (-b 500). Sequence divergence, unless otherwise stated, was pairwise amino acid p-distance (%), i.e., the proportion of sites with different amino acids in the two sequences, as calculated in MEGA7 v11.0.8 [49]. Coverage for each virus was calculated using CoverM [50] using methods count (number of reads aligned to each genome) and mean (average number of aligned reads overlapping each position on the genome) with settings --min-covered-fraction 0.

To examine potential correlation between virus and parasite infection, reference-guided assembly and 18S screening was undertaken. In the case of the former, reads were mapped to the viral reference genome using Minimap2 v2.28-r1209 [51] set for short-read alignment (--sr). The Sequence Alignment Map (SAM) output was then processed with SAMtools v1.19.2 [52] using, sequentially, the settings sort, index and mpileup. A consensus sequence was then generated using iVAR v1.4.2 [53] using the consensus command and settings -q (min. quality score) = 20, -t (min. frequency threshold) = 0, -c (min. insertion frequency threshold) = 0.8, -m (min. depth) = 10. In the case of 18S screening, ribosomal ribonucleic acid (rRNA) was predicted from the assembly data using Barrnap v0.9 [54], and candidate 18S sequences were classified by Kraken2 [55] using the SILVA 138 Small Subunit Ref NR99 database [56]. Sequences classified as taxa of interest and their child taxa were extracted using the extract_kraken_reads.py script. Sequences were aligned against the NCBI nucleotide (nt) database using blastn, and the alignments were classified in Megan.

Results

A rich diversity of viruses was characterized in meta-transcriptomic data from Northern Mockingbird in Texas (Table 1). The number of pair-ended reads obtained from individual samples ranged from 42,710,418 to 75,304,474 [median 63,332,560]. High-quality genomes were recovered from 43 virus species (Table 2).

Avian-associated viruses

Picornaviridae

This genome, assembled from sample TX21038, was initially classified as belonging to the family Picornaviridae (GenBank Acc: PV740365) by Genomad. The genome was 7390 base pairs (bp) in total length, within which a single polyprotein of length 6,615 bp (2205 amino acids) was identified by Prokka. Taxonomic classification by Diamond-Megan identified the virus’ lowest common ancestor (LCA) to be Myna hepatovirus (GenBank Acc: OK334622; 7,889 bp in length).

Phylogenetic analysis was performed by creating a multiple sequence alignment of the P1 region with members of Genus Hepatovirus, as listed in the ICTV Report on Picornaviridae [57] and including the matches from blastx searches of the NCBI core nucleotide database (Fig. 1; Figure S1). The assembled Picornaviridae virus was found as a sister (BS 100%) to Myna hepatovirus (GenBank Acc: OK334622) characterized from a common myna (Acridotheres tristis) sampled in Australia, and within a strongly supported clade that included Robin hepatovirus 2 (GenBank Acc: OQ986612) characterized from a South Island robin (Petroica australis) sampled in New Zealand. This clade forms a sister relationship with a clade of Crahelivirus and Gruhelivirus (BS 95%). These are found clustered within a strongly supported clade (BS 100%) comprising the Hepatovirus A – I, Tremovirus, Crahelivirus and Gruhelivirus.

Root (□) represents the remaining taxa within the family Picorniviridae (Refer to Figure S1; rooted with the ampivirus outgroup, in accordance with ICTV phylogeny)

The level of divergence found between the polyproteins of the virus identified here and its sister taxon, the Myna hepatovirus (GenBank Acc: OK334622), was 38.70% (based on P1 amino acid sequence), which exceeds the divergence set for Hepatovirus species demarcation by the ICTV (> 30%). The virus contained the catalytic residues (DGDD; mapped 4 of 4) and the conserved polymerase motif A (CGIDLDFRNFDASLS; mapped 15 of 15 residues comprising conserved feature), motif B (MPSGMPCTSLLNSVINQTNTMFVL; mapped 23 of 23 residues comprising conserved feature) and motif C (VKILTYGDDIVIILS; mapped 14 of 14 residues comprising conserved feature) within the conserved domain Hepatovirus RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp) (CDD: cd23215; Hepatovirus_RdRp). A region corresponding to the domain of the Hepatitis A virus viral protein (PF12944: HAV_VP), a family of the viral protein found in Hepatitis A viruses, was also present in the genome.

Hepeviridae

This genome, assembled from sample TX21064 (GenBank Acc: PV740366), was initially classified as belonging to the family Hepeviridae by Genomad. The genome was 7149 base pairs (bp) in total length, within which a single polyprotein of length 6,465 bp (2,155 amino acids) was identified by Prokka. Taxonomic classification by Diamond-Megan identified the virus’ lowest common ancestor (LCA) to be La Herreria hepevirus (GenBank Acc: MW147021; 5,451 bp in length).

Phylogenetic analysis was performed by creating a multiple sequence alignment of the ORF1 polyprotein with members of family Hepeviridae, as listed in the ICTV Report on Hepeviridae [58] and including the matches from blastx searches of the NCBI core nucleotide database (Fig. 2). The level of divergence between the ORF1 of the assembled virus and its nearest neighbor, La Herreria hepevirus (GenBank Acc: MW147021), was 59.10%.

Matryoshka RNA virus

A Matryoshka RNA virus (MaRNAV) genome was de novo assembled from sample TX21075 by Trinity and classified as a “prokaryote virus” by DeepMicroClass. The genome was 2924 base pairs (bp) in total length, within which an RdRp protein of length 2,850 bp (950 amino acids) was identified by Prokka. Taxonomic classification by Diamond-Megan placed this virus among unclassified viruses. However, blastx searches identified the two top hits as MaRNAV 4 (GenBank Acc: BK059438; 3,083 bp in length) and MaRNAV 5 (PQ536674; 2,925 bp in length), which were recent entries not yet present on the Megan mapping database (megan-map-Feb2022.db).

Phylogenetic analysis was performed by creating a multiple sequence alignment of the RdRp region using the top matches from blastx searches of the NCBI core nucleotide database and the closest relatives within Narnaviridae listed in Esperanza et al. [59] (Fig. 3). The Mockingbird MaRNAV formed a sister relationship with a MaRNAV 4 and MaRNAV 5, and these taxa in turn formed a sister clade the remaining MaRNAV taxa (MaRNAV 1–3 and 6) and Plasmodium vivax Narna-like virus 1. The level of divergence between the virus and its nearest neighbor, MaRNAV 5 (GenBank Acc: PQ536674), based on the RdRp amino acid sequence, was 51.50%. It was herein referred to as MaRNAV 8 (GenBank Acc: PV726993).

Extended majority-rule consensus tree of the Matryoshka RNA viruses using the RdRp region. Red circles indicate branches with standard bootstrap support greater than 70%. Tree is mid-point rooted

To test for the signature Matryoshka RNA virus-Haemosporida-vertebrate host relationship, we further examined the samples for MaRNAV 8 genomes using reference-guided assembly and screened for haemosporidia. Reference-guided assembly recovered two further complete MaRNAV 8 genomes in samples TX21074 (GenBank Acc: PV753720) and TX21096 (GenBank Acc: PV753719). Barrnap-kraken2 screening of assemblies found Haemosporida 18S sequences in six of the samples (TX21051, TX21061, TX21070, TX21074, TX21075, TX21096). The BLAST-Megan LCA analysis of these sequences identified the genus Haemoproteus from all six samples, as well as the genus Plasmodium from sample TX21096.

Other viruses

Arthropod-associated viruses

Dicistroviridae

A wide array of likely arthropod-associated viruses was assembled from the data. The viral family that was best represented was that of Dicistroviridae. In total nine viral genomes (GenBank Accs: PV775691 - PV775699) came from this family (Figure S2). Although formal genus and species demarcation has not yet been established by ICTV, six of the nine viruses were identified as novel, based on the degree of divergence exceeding those among viral taxa detailed in ICTV Virus Taxonomy Profile for Dicistroviridae (Table 2; Figure S2) [60]. Two (GenBank Accs: PV775691 and PV775694) were found within a genus Aparavirus clade, while three were found within a genus Triatovirus clade (GenBank Accs: PV775692, PV775698 and PV775699).

Chuviridae

One virus was classified as a member of the family Chuviridae (GenBank Acc: PV765996) and placed within a clade (BS 76%) comprising arthropod-associated Chuviruses. The level of divergence observed for this virus exceeded the ICTV criteria for species demarcation (L protein > 10% divergence; Table 2; Figure S3) [61].

Polycipiviridae

Two viruses were found to be members of Polycipiviridae (Table 2; Figure S4). Formal genus and species demarcation has not yet been established by ICTV [62]. However, the first was classified as Solenopsis invicta virus 2 (GenBank Acc: PV806516) based on the degree of Solenopsis invicta virus 2 intra-specific divergence, while the second was identified as a novel (GenBank Acc: PV806517), based on its divergence exceeding inter-specific divergence within the family.

Iflaviridae

Based on ICTV species demarcation (capsid protein > 10% divergence) [63], two new Iflaviruses were classified from samples TX21063 and TX21067 (Table 2). The first (GenBank Acc: PV806518) was found to be most closely related to Hubei picorna-like virus 42 (GenBank Acc: KX884297; Figure S5), and the second (GenBank Acc: PV806519) was found as a sister to Apis iflavirus 1 (GenBank Acc: MZ822075; Figure S6).

Solinviviridae

A total of three viruses (GenBank Acc: PV806520 - PV806522) were found to belong to the family Solinviviridae (Table 2). Phylogenetic analysis was performed on the ORF1 region of close relatives based on blastx searches of the NCBI core nucleotide database and the members of the “Kelp Fly Virus Related Cluster” defined by Shi et al. [64] (Figure S7). Although formal genus and species demarcation has not yet been established by ICTV, these three viruses were identified as novel, based on their degree of divergence exceeding that among viral taxa detailed in ICTV Virus Taxonomy Profile for Solinviviridae. [65].

Permutotetraviridae

Four assembled viruses (PV806523 - PV806526) were initially identified as members of the family Permutotetraviridae (Table 2). Phylogenetic analysis was performed on the RdRp region of close relatives from blastx searches of the NCBI core nucleotide database and members of the “Permutotetra clade” defined by Shi et al. [64] (Figure S8). One (GenBank Acc: PV806523) was placed as a sister to a clade containing Hubei permutotetra like virus 10 (GenbBank Acc: NC_033245), Beihai permutotetra like virus 2 (GenBank Acc: NC_032122) and Jingmen bat permutotetravirus 2 (GenBank Acc: OQ715818). While formal genus and species demarcation has not yet been established by ICTV, all four were considered novel viruses based on their RdRp amino acid divergences exceeding those between the only two member species for Permutotetraviridae listed in the ICTV Interim Report for the family Permutotetraviridae (Table 2).

Fungi-associated viruses

Mitoviridae

Two viruses were initially classified as members of Mitoviridae. The first virus (GenBank Acc: PV806527) was considered Guiyang mito-like virus 11 (GenBank Acc: OM514574) based on its position in the phylogenetic tree and its divergence from Guiyang mito-like virus 11 of less than 10% (Table 2; Figure S9). The second (GenBank Acc: PV806528) was identified as a new member of a mitovirus clade associated with soil and sediment samples (GenBank Accs: PP172378, PP172181, PP172198 and MN035924).

Narnaviridae

Two viruses (GenBank Accs: PV806529 and PV806530) initially classified as member of the family Narnaviridae. Both were considered at least highly divergent strains, with Narnaviridae sp. (GenBank Acc: PV806529) found as a sister taxon to Hubei narna like virus 5 (GenBank Acc: NC_032821), and Narnaviridae sp. (GenBank Acc: PV806530) in an intermediate and poorly supported position between Sanya narnavirus (GenBank Acc: MZ209877) and Zootermopsis nevadensis narna like virus 1 (GenBank Acc: BK067133) (Figure S10, Figure S11 and Table 2).

Plant-associated viruses

Solemoviridae

A total of five viruses (GenBank Acc: PV806531 - PV806535) belonged to the family Solemoviridae (Figure S2), all of whom were considered novel viruses (Table 2; Figure S12). According to ICTV guidelines on species demarcation in the genus Sobemovirus (> 25% nucleotide divergence) and their position in the phylogenetic tree, one of these was identified as belonging to the genus Sobemovirus (GenBank Acc: PV806531) and the remaining four were placed at the level of Solemoviridae [66].

Additional viruses

Tymoviridae (associated with arthropod or plant hosts): Viruses initially classified as Tymoviridae were then phylogentically analyzed by alignment with close matches from blastx searches of the NCBI core nucleotide database and members listed under Tymoviridae in Dreher et al. [67] (Figure S13). The criteria for resolving species from the genera Tymovirus and Marafivirus (20% at the genome and 10% at the capsid protein) [67] confirmed a total of six viruses (Table 2), one of which was found within a genus Marafivirus clade (Figure S13).

Rhabdoviridae (associated with vertebrate, arthropod or plant hosts): A single virus (GenBank Acc: PV806536) was found to be a member of the family Rhabdoviridae. Phylogenetic analysis of the RdRp region for Rhabdoviridae (Figure S14) included members listed under Rhabdoviridae in the ICTV Report [68] and close blastx matches from the NCBI core nucleotide database. The virus is found within a clade comprising members of the genus Alphapaprhavirus, comprising Lepidopteran rhabdo-related virus (GenBank Acc: MT153443), Pararge aegeria rhabdovirus (GenBank Acc: NC_076147) and Hubei lepidoptera virus 2 (GenBank Acc: NC_032907), and it was considered novel as its divergence exceeded those found between other members of the clade.

Orthornavirae (associated with vertebrate or arthropod hosts): A single virus (GenBank Acc: PV806537) was initially classified as Nodaviridae and its closest relatives identified from blastx searches were Orthornavirae sp (GenBank Acc: OR799257) and Nodaviridae sp. (GenBank Acc: PQ792015). It was it considered a novel member of Orthornavirae due to high divergence with its most closely related blast hit, Orthornavirae sp, (GenBank Acc: OR799257; 75.10% divergence at the non-structural protein; Table 2).

Riboviria: Two remaining virus genomes could only be classified to the level of Riboviria. The first virus (GenBank Acc: PV806538) was initially classified by Daimond-Megan as Hubei virga-like virus 11. However, divergence at the principal coding region between this virus and the top blastx hits was high (> 70%) and all returned either unclassified Riboviria or conflicting taxonomy below the level of Riboviria. The second virus (GenBank Acc: PV806539) was initially classified by Diamond-Megan as unclassified virus. Divergence at the RdRp region between this virus and records from the NCBI protein non-redundant (nr) database was high (> 55%), with all top blastx hits returning unclassified Riboviria.

Discussion

Virome studies make up a small portion of avian microbiome publications, and only a minority of these have to date focused on wild birds [69]. Among wild birds, avian-associated viruses from the families Parvoviridae, Picornaviridae, Circoviridae, Reoviridae, Caliciviridae, Adenoviridae, Picobirnaviridae and Astroviridae have been commonly reported. More recently, invertebrate-associated viruses, such as those from the family Dicistroviridae, were found to be prevalent among many avian species [23], and genera of zoonotic potential, such as Deltacoronavirus, Gammacoronavirus, Hepatovirus and Orthonairovirus are frequently reported [70–73]. To date, however, these studies have focused on cloacal, fecal and pharyngeal samples, and virome studies of the ileum in wild birds has been largely overlooked.

Among the most important findings from analyzing the ileal viromes of 25 Northern Mockingbirds from Texas was the characterizations of three novel avian-associated viruses. The first of these was a member of Picornaviridae. Viruses from this family comprise single-stranded, positive-sense RNA genomes containing a single ORF [74]. The Picornaviridae virus identified in the current study is most closely related to the Myna hepatovirus, previously identified from the tissue of an Indian Myna (Acridotheres tristis), collected in New South Wales, Australia [75], and the Robin hepatovirus, identified from a cloacal swab of a South Island robin (Petroica australis) collected in New Zealand [76]. These three viruses form a clade that is sister to the recently defined genera Crahelivirus and Gruhelivirus [77]. Although these viruses are currently of unknown veterinary importance, they can be considered an “avian hepatovirus clade”, which is sister to the mammal hepatovirus clade, comprising Hepatovirus A – I. The members of this latter clade are transmitted via the fecal-oral route, and cause inflammation of the liver tissue, with Hepatovirus A being associated with human and non-human primates, and Hepatovirus B – I having been detected mainly in wild and domestic mammals [57, 78, 79]. From our phylogenetic analysis, the avian and mammal clades appear in a derived position relative to Tremovirus. Species belonging to the genus Tremovirus cause avian encephalomyelitis, which is an inflammation in the central nervous system of birds and is spread mainly via the fecal-oral route [80]. These viruses have been found to naturally infect a range of domestic or game birds, including domestic chicken (Gallus gallus), common pheasant (Phasianus colchicus), common pigeon (Columba livia), Japanese quail (Coturnix japonica) and domestic turkey (Meleagris gallopavo) [81–85]. Further research of this potential avian origin of hepatovirus could enhance our understanding of the virus transmission pathways. Given the close relationships between the hepatovirus identified in Northern Mockingbird and the avian hepatovirus clade with viruses of known and significant pathogenic potential—such as mammal-associated hepatoviruses and tremoviruses—and the first discovery of this avian hepatovirus clade in the Americas, focusing on characterizing the diversity, distribution, transmission, and pathogenicity of these viruses can support better assessments of emerging veterinary and medical risks.

Another important discovery was of a virus that belonged to the family Hepeviridae. Viruses from this family are linear, single-stranded, positive-sense RNA genomes. The family is highly diverse and includes zoonotic, anthropotropic, and animal-associated Hepatitis E virus (HEV) or HEV-like viruses [86]. The ICTV currently recognizes two subfamilies, five genera and ten species. Members of the subfamily Orthohepevirinae are known to infect mammals and birds, whereas those from the subfamily Parahepevirinae have been reported infecting fish [58]. The virus identified here is most closely related to hepeviruses found in a European Robin (Erithacus rubecula) in Spain [30], a Scutigeridae centipede from China [64], house mouse (Mus musculus) from the United States [87], and song thrush (Turdus philomelos) from New Zealand [88] forming a clade whose members appear to be associated with arthropods or primarily arthropod feeding animals. Within our phylogenetic tree, these are nested within a large clade comprising other viruses described as hepeviruses, and distinct from the clade formed by Orthohepevirinae and Parahepevirinae. According to the ICTV, these unclassified viruses may yet represent members of novel families within the order Hepelivirales, rather than taxa within the family Hepeviridae [58]. Further assessment on the occurrence of the hepevirus identified here can enhance our knowledge of the host-virus relationships.

Matryoshka RNA viruses have recently been identified as pathogens of haemosporidian parasites [89]. The first species identified, MaRNAV-1, was associated with Plasmodium vivax infecting humans in Cambodia and Malaysia and mosquitoes in Colombia and Thailand. A second species identified as MaRNAV-2 was associated with Leucocytozoon parasites in Australian magpie (Gymnorhina tibicen), pied currawong (Strepera graculina) and Australian raven (Corvus coronoides) collected in New South Wales, Australia. The viruses MaRNAV-3 and MaRNAV-4 were subsequently found associated with Leucocytozoon parasites in common redpoll (Acanthis flammea) from Alaska and with Haemoproteus parasites in plumbeous vireo (Vireo plumbeus) from New Mexico, respectively [90]. Two further Matryoshka RNA viruses characterized, MaRNAV-5 and MaRNAV-6, were associated with Haemoproteus lophortyx in California quail (Callipepla californica) from California and with Leucocytozoon californicus in common barn owl (Tyto alba) from California, respectively [59]. The most recent characterized, MaRNAV-7, was from blood taken from haemosporidian-infected common whitethroat (Curruca communis) in Lithuania [91]. Here we characterize another Matryoshka RNA virus for the first time, MaRNAV-8, which appears to be most closely related to viruses MaRNAV-4 and MaRNAV-5. We also found that every sample yielding MaRNAV-8 viral assemblies was also positive for an undetermined Haemoproteus species, which is consistent with the association between Matryoshka RNA virus and haemosporidia found in other studies [59, 89–91]. Although the transmission cycles of Matryoshka RNA viruses remain unknown, it has been suggested that the insect vector is the primary host, and the haemosporidian parasite acts as the virus’ “vector” to reach its subsequent host [59]. However, efforts towards viral culturing, infection and replication in cells of avian, vector and haemosporidian origin could support proper characterization of the transmission cycle of these viruses and the development of effective virus-parasite-vector models.

The remaining genomes can be considered mainly arthropod-, fungi- and plant-associated viruses. Among the arthropod-associated viruses, six families were represented, and 17 of the 21 viruses present appear to be novel. Among the four previously recorded viruses, three were viruses associated with the Red Imported Fire Ant (Solenopsis invicta). Two of these, SIV1 (Dicistroviridae) and SIV2 (Polycipiviridae) have been previously reported infecting S. invicta in the United States [92–94]. The former has also been reported infecting ants (family Formicidae) in China [95], Taiwan and Argentina [96]. The third, SIV9 (Dicistroviridae), however, has to date only been reported from a single case in Formosa, Argentina [97]. Our finding demonstrates a far wider distribution of this virus, and the first case in North America. Given the likely specificity of these viruses to Formicidae species, it appears their detection in Northern Mockingbird ileum reflects Formicidae forming a major part of the bird’s diet. This study highlights prospective viral surveillance sampling of bird gut contents as a means to discover and characterize viruses associated with prey species and viruses of medical and veterinary importance potentially circulating in the wider environment.

Among viruses previously associated with fungi, genomes belonging to two families, Mitoviridae and Narnaviridae, were identified in this study, with potential new strains. Although Mitoviruses were originally considered specific to fungus hosts [98], they have all been found associated with a wide range of plant species [99]. One of the Mitovirus species found here, Guiyang mito-like virus 11, was previously identified from a Japanese Buttercup (Ranunculus japonicus). Previous studies have shown virus cross-transmission between plants and fungi [84], [85] and the true host of Guiyang mito-like virus 11 is not possible to define as yet. Further study could increase knowledge of the virus’ host specificity and its relationship with fungus and plant species.

The assembled Solemoviridae genomes, which are primarily associated with plants, appear to represent new viruses. Members of the family Solemoviridae are known to infect a wide variety of plants including important commercial crops such as rice, potato, sugarcane and beet [66]. Transmission can occur via several different routes such as mechanical wounding, vegetative propagation, insect vectors or abiotically through soil. Symptoms of infection can be from absent to severe, and include chlorosis, phloem necrosis, stunting and sometimes sterility. Although four of the five novel Solemoviridae viruses could only be classified to the level of family, one was classified to the genus Sobemovirus. Symptoms include chlorosis and necrotic lesions, and the main route of transmission is mechanical wounding and vectors including flies (Order: Diptera), beetles (Order: Coleoptera), true bugs (Order: Hemiptera), and thrips (Order: Thysanoptera) [100]. Among the most commercially important Sobemovirus is the rice yellow mottle virus, which has had a devastating impact on rice production in Africa [101, 102]. The presence of these plant-associated viruses in bird ileum can therefore be attributed to several different food sources including fruit, seeds and insect vectors.

Among the remaining viruses, six of the identified Tymoviridae viruses also constitute potential novel viruses. Two could only be classified to the level of family, three belonged to the genus Tymovirus, and one was from the genus Marafivirus. Although tymoviruses are generally transmitted by mechanical means and by beetle (families Chrysomelidae and Curculionidae) vectors, Marafivirus species are phloem-limited and rely on phloem-feeding vectors such as leafhoppers (family Cicadellidae) for transmission [103]. Tymoviruses are now increasingly being associated with mosquito (family Culicidae) vectors from Brazil [104], China [105], Colombia [106] and Mexico [107]. Further research could help identify hosts, reservoirs and potential vectors of this diverse viral group.

Understanding the epidemiology and etiology of potential viral pathogens requires systematic surveillance to quantify prevalence, characterize genetic diversity, and elucidate evolutionary relationships and phylodynamics. A rich and overwhelmingly novel viral community has herein been characterized from the ileum of Northern Mockingbirds, and its members represent viruses and potential pathogens of avian, arthropod, and plant species present in Texas. Given the vagile nature of avian species, such viruses have the potential for translocation and transmission across a variety of landscapes, states and countries. The novelty encountered in these viromes indicates that resolving pathogenic from commensal viruses remains challenging, and the definitive and intermediary viral hosts and transmission routes remain poorly understood. In the absence of effective culturing methods, etiological hypotheses can be inferred by examining associations between pathological outcomes and infection prevalence. This study was confined to Northern Mockingbirds populations from Texas, yet this species is common throughout much of North America. Expanding efforts in pathogen screening and agnostic sequencing holds meaningful potential for uncovering viruses and transmission dynamics across diverse ecological landscapes. Despite such shortcomings, our study provides a baseline for investigations on emergence of pathogens and the risk of potential zoonosis. The viruses characterized here can be targets that are tracked through the food web and inter-specific and predator-prey interactions. Such investigations increase understanding of virus transport in avian species from regions undergoing land-use change, where increased human-wildlife interactions heighten the risks posed by emerging pathogens of both veterinary and medical significance.
