Investigation on clinical risk factors of bladder lesion by machine learning based interpretable model
Authors: Yunxin Wang, Jiachuang Li, Yunfeng Song, Hongguo Wei, Zejun Yan, Shuo Chen, Zhe Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBladder lesion commonly occurs in patients with benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH), and the routine screening of bladder lesion is vital for its timely detection and treatment, in which the risk of bladder lesion progression can be effectively alleviated. However, current clinical methods are inconvenient for routine screening. In this study, we proposed a convenient routine screening method to diagnose bladder lesions based on several clinical risk factors, which can be obtained through non-invasive, easy-to-operate, and low-cost examinations. The contribution of each clinical risk factor was further quantitatively analyzed to understand their impact on diagnostic decision-making. Based on a cohort study of 253 BPH patients with or without bladder lesions, the proposed diagnostic model achieved high accuracy using these clinical risk factors. Bladder compliance, maximum flow rate (Qmax), prostate specific antigen (PSA), and postvoid residual (PVR) were identified as the four most important clinical risk factors. To the best of our knowledge, this is the innovative research to predict bladder lesions based on the risk factors and quantitatively reveal their contributions to diagnostic decision-making. The proposed model has the potential to serve as an effective routine screening tool for bladder lesions in BPH patients, enabling early intervention to prevent lesion progression and improve the quality of life.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-75104-x.Keywords: Bladder lesion, Clinical risk factors, Routine screening, Machine learning, Interpretable modelSubject terms: Biomarkers, Risk factors, Urology

Introduction

Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) is a disease that more frequently occurs in elderly males. It can lead to dysuria and seriously threaten the life quality of BPH patients1,2. At its early stage, lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) are commonly unobvious due to functional compensation3,4. However, with the aggravation of LUTS, BPH patients begin to suffer from dysuria5,6, and long-term dysuria can lead to organic lesion of the bladder, e.g., bladder diverticulum (BD) and bladder trabeculation (BT). It was reported that long-term dysuria suffers from approximately 62.5% risk of developing into bladder lesion7. Following the emergence of bladder lesion, it was reported that about 41% of BPH patients with the bladder lesion experienced acute urinary retention or pseudo-acute renal failure, which can be life-threatening in severe cases8. Moreover, bladder lesion is one of the major causes of bladder tumors, accounting for approximately 10% of all cases, owing to urine stasis with prolonged exposure of potential carcinogens to the mucosal surface9. If bladder lesion can be early detected and treated promptly, the probability of bladder lesion progression can be effectively reduced to further improve the survival rate10,11. Thus, BPH patients are commonly advised to undergo routine screening to prevent disease progression at the early stage12–14.

Although there exist several clinical approaches for diagnosing bladder lesion, they are often assumed to be inconvenient or unsuitable for routine screening. In clinical practice, cystoscopy is the gold standard for diagnosis that can directly image the lesion region with high resolution, however, its invasive characteristic can cause discomfort to the patients and limit its use as a routine screening method15–17. Besides, non-invasive medical imaging modalities such as Computed Tomography(CT), Magnetic Resonance Imaging(MRI), and ultrasonography are common diagnostic tools for bladder lesion. Currently, surveillance of bladder lesion recurrence is primarily accomplished using imaging techniques. However, all of them have their own limitations for routine screening: bladder imaging with CT typically requires contrast agents to achieve sufficient contrast, which may cause adverse effects in some patients18; MRI is cost-prohibitive and not applicable for patients with implanted magnetic materials, such as cardiac prostheses and metallic cardiovascular electronic devices19; ultrasonography is generally considered to have relatively low spatial resolution to effectively image, fine structures and detailed pathological changes, making it challenging to detect small bladder lesions20. Additionally, the diagnostic accuracy of ultrasonography relies on the operator’s experience and skills. Experienced clinicians are better at interpreting images and identifying potential issues, whereas less experienced clinicians may miss important details21. Overall, the current clinical approaches have not yet been well established and may cause unnecessary pain and burden to patients, both in terms of inaccurate initial diagnosis and postoperative review. Therefore, there is an urgent clinical need for cost-effective solution to accurately and non-invasively detect bladder lesions.

Clinical risk factors with rich information about bladder function have also been frequently used in the auxiliary diagnosis of bladder lesion22. As they can be obtained by non-invasive, easy operated, and low-cost examinations, the diagnosis of bladder lesion based on those clinical risk factors is expected to be more appropriate for routine screening23,24. Unfortunately, due to the complex diagnostic information within those clinical risk factors. The quantitative contribution of each clinical risk factor to the diagnosis of bladder lesions remains undefined. Consequently, many urologists find it challenging to quickly and effectively master these factors for clinical diagnosis25. Besides, there is also a lack of a multi-parameter comprehensive diagnostic model for the bladder lesion that considers various clinical risk factors. In recent decades, interpretable machine learning has turned out to be good at discovering intricate relationships between certain diseases and clinical risk factors, which can serve as a powerful tool for better integration and more comprehensive interpretation of multiple sources of diagnostic information26,27. However, there remains a paucity of literature showing the function of interpretable machine learning models in diagnosing bladder lesion and evaluating the importance of those clinical risk factors.

In this study, we aimed to establish an interpretable diagnostic model based on those clinical risk factors for conveniently diagnosing the bladder lesion and quantitatively interpreting the contribution of each clinical risk factor on diagnostic decision-making. The importance of different risk factors

Based on the SVM diagnostic model with optimal parameters, the feature importance (FI) and shapley (SHAP) value of each risk factors were calculated and listed in Table 3. Although the importance of those risk factors was ranked different when using FI score or SHAP value, it was consistent that Qmax, PSA, PVR and bladder compliance were always the top four most important clinical risk factors in bladder lesion diagnosis. By further perform Mann-Whitney U test analysis on continuous variables and Chi-square test analysis on categorical variables, the p-values of those top four clinical risk factors were significantly lower than 0.05. This further indicated that the differences on those top four clinical risk factors were more significant among all clinical risk factors. In conclusion, based on the results of both importance analysis and statistical analysis, Qmax, PSA, PVR and bladder compliance should have more significant differences between bladder lesion group and control group, and they should contribute more on final decision-making of bladder lesion diagnosis.

Discussion

In this study, the contribution of those six clinical risk factors on diagnosing bladder lesion was ranked based on FI, SHAP values and p-values, respectively. The slightly different ranking outcomes are supposed to stem from their diverse computational approaches. More specifically, FI is determined by evaluating the reduction in Gini coefficient or entropy at each split point across all trees in the random forest for each individual clinical risk factor29; The p-value assesses the significance of a single clinical risk factor to the random forest model through hypothesis testing and calculating a t-statistic30,31;SHAP value assesses the importance of each clinical risk factor by calculating its marginal contribution across all possible feature sets32,33, where marginal contribution refers to the change in model prediction after adding this clinical risk factor. Overall, among the above computational approaches of these metrics, only the SHAP value further considers the association among different clinical risk factors. Therefore. we ultimately discussed the top four most important clinical risk factors following the order of SHAP value based importance ranking, i.e., bladder compliance, Qmax, PSA and PVR.

Bladder compliance is found to be the first most contributed clinical risk factor for diagnosing bladder lesion, in which patients with abnormal bladder compliance is more likely to suffer from bladder lesion, especially for decreased bladder compliance. The decreased bladder compliance is mainly due to detrusor fibrosis (i.e., massive collagen fibrils hyperplasia), which is the consequence of long-term bladder outlet obstruction accompanied by a bladder lesion34. Consistent decreased bladder compliance was also observed in BPH patients with bladder lesion in previous studies35,36.

The urodynamic characteristics (i.e., postvoid residual and maximum flow rate) are found to be the second and fourth most contributed clinical risk factors for diagnosing bladder lesion. According to the proposed machine learning based interpretable model, PVR is positively correlated to bladder lesion while Qmax is negatively correlated to bladder lesion, which are consistent with the previously reported findings37. The higher PVR level in patients with bladder lesion can be attributed to the fact that the diverticulum of bladder lesion can be functionalized as a reservoir to store more residual urine. The lower Qmax level in patients with bladder lesion can be attributed to the fact that the diverticulum of bladder lesion can obstruct urine outflow from the bladder.

T-PSA is found to be the third contributed clinical risk factor for bladder lesion diagnosis and is positively correlated to bladder lesion in the proposed diagnostic model. PSA is a kallikrein-like serine protease that is mainly secreted by prostate epithelial cells, which is exclusively present in the prostate tissue and serves as an important biomarker of abnormal prostate function38. Bladder lesion is often accompanied by bladder and/or prostate inflammation, and such inflammation typically causes an increased PSA level39. More specifically, the inflammation can activate extracellular signaling mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway and disrupt the structural integrity of the epithelial basement membrane of prostatic basal cells, leading to an increased PSA secretion and an excessive release of PSA into the blood40. Besides, PSA level can also be affected by long-term prostate hyperplasia41, which is often manifested as gradually increased prostate volume with a larger number of prostate epithelial cells. As PSA is mainly secreted by prostate epithelial cells, the increased number of prostate epithelial cells can eventually lead to increased PSA secretion42. In clinical practice, the specific cause of T-PSA elevation often requires careful evaluation by clinicians, and it often suffers from unsatisfactory sensitivity by only using T-PSA for bladder lesion screening.

Only weak contributions on diagnosing bladder lesion were found in detrusor contractility and IPSS&Qol(International Prostate Symptoms Score & Quality of life). Although bladder lesion can cause structural changes on the outer surface of the bladder wall that may further lead to the dysfunction of bladder contraction and relaxation, it rarely affected nerves located in the inner bladder wall43. The detrusor muscles are primarily controlled by those nerves, thus detrusor contractility is assumed to be not fully affected by the bladder lesion alone44. Besides, previous studies have also shown that the detrusor contractility decreased with BPH progression because the bladder function may be impaired and the detrusor muscle may be remodeled over the long-term course of BPH45. The weak contribution of IPSS&Qol in diagnosing bladder lesion is in line with previous studies46,47, which should be mainly due to its characteristic of strong subjectivity. The patients often fail to accurately grade their discomfort, especially when they suffer from severe urinary symptoms, which is one of the most important evaluation indexes in IPSS&Qol.

Those clinical risk factors are also expected to be more jointly used with liquid biopsy, which can further analyze CTCs, ctDNA, ctRNA, proteins and metabolites. For bladder lesions, the urinary biomarkers are supposed to be the best targets, because urine is constantly in contact with bladder mucosa to bring specific biomolecular information about bladder condition and is advantageous in easy and non-invasive sample collection. The use of urinary biomarkers in clinical practice have been proved to offer a promising alternative to multiple cystoscopies, potentially reducing complications and discomfort while providing a safe and innovative diagnostic method for bladder cancer detection and surveillance48. In addition, it enables serial monitoring of those urinary biomarkers, aiding in tracking progression and tailoring therapy49. Among those urinary biomarkers, some of them have been well-established and approved by the Food and Drug Administration(FDA) and European Medicines Agency(EMA), such as NMP22, BTA STAT & TRAK, UroVysion, ImmunoCyt/uCyt + and certain cytokeratins, but have not been included in international guidelines and are infrequently used in clinical practice50; new urinary biomarkers are also being continuously developed, like Adxbladder, Bladder EpiCheck, Cxbladder, Uromonitor and Xpert, but currently without sufficient empirical evaluation to support its clinical routine usage51. Although those urinary biomarkers are mostly designed for bladder cancer, we believe that they should have great potential in aiding bladder lesion diagnosis, considering the close association between bladder lesion and bladder cancer. The joint use of clinical risk factors and urinary biomarkers can take the advantages of both bladder conditions at organ level and bladder changes at molecular level, thus is expected to significantly improve the accuracy of diagnosis and prognosis of bladder lesion.

Overall, this study had successfully built a bladder lesion diagnostic model which high accuracy and quantitatively revealed the importance of those clinical risk factors on diagnostic decision-making. However, there still existed a huge gap from its clinical translation. In this study, constrained by our available resources, only single center and relatively small number of patients were involved. In addition, only six frequently used clinical risk factors for auxiliary diagnosis of bladder lesion were considered, which may not fully covering all potential risk factors. Future study will be carried out on larger and more diverse cohorts from multiple centers, as well as a broader array of variables including more urinary biomarkers, to further elucidate the relationships and outcomes under investigation. Furthermore, other machine learning or even deep learning methods will be investigated in future study to achieve better diagnostic performance or even prediction of its disease progress, and more comprehensive interpretation of the relationship between clinical risk factors with bladder lesion diagnosis or even its progress can be revealed.

Conclusion

In this study, six diagnostic models for bladder cancer lesions were developed using different machine learning algorithms, in which SVM model demonstrated the optimal diagnostic performance, i.e. precision of 86.83%. Based on SVM diagnostic model, and SHAP analysis was found that bladder compliance, Qmax, PSA and PVR were ranked as the first four most important clinical risk factors for diagnosing bladder lesion, which can further inform clinicians in terms of how each risk factor contributes to bladder lesion diagnosis. Therefore, the proposed method can potentially serve as a promising tool for routine screening of bladder lesion in BPH patients because of its non-invasive, relatively easily operated, and low-cost characteristics, in which timely intervention can be implemented to prevent bladder lesion development.

Materials and methods

The collection of patients’ clinical risk factors

In this study, a total of 253 male patients, who underwent BPH surgery from 2011 to 2021, were enrolled through a non-selective review of patients’ information from the medical information system at the First Affiliated Hospital of China Medical University. Among them, 172 patients with bladder lesion were served as bladder lesion group, and 81 patients without bladder lesion were served as control group. Since this is a retrospective study, the recruited patients have been carefully selected to exclude the patients according to the following exclusion criteria: patients exhibiting insignificant voluntary detrusor contractions during urodynamic evaluation or those who had developed prostate cancer within six months. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of China Medical University (ethics number: [2021]489), and written informed consent was obtained from each patient.

As suggested by urologists and clinical manifestations, certain potential clinical risk factors from each BPH patient were chosen for further investigation, consisting of urodynamic characteristics, T-PSA, bladder condition (i.e., bladder compliance and detrusor contractility) and IPSS&Qol level. Bladder compliance refers to the bladder’s ability of response to pressure changes during filling, reflecting the elasticity and distensibility of the bladder wall52. Low bladder compliance may indicate a stiff bladder wall, common in conditions such as interstitial cystitis and bladder fibrosis, which can affect bladder function53. Hence, bladder compliance is an important parameter for assessing bladder health. PVR is the amount of urine remaining in the bladder after voiding, indicating the completeness of urination54. High PVR can be a sign of various pathological conditions, such as bladder outlet obstruction or neurogenic bladder55. These conditions are often closely linked to bladder dysfunction, making PVR a crucial indicator for diagnosing bladder function disorders. Qmax is the peak urine flow rate during voiding, reflecting the patency of the urethra and the function of the detrusor muscle56. A reduced Qmax is typically associated with lower urinary tract obstruction, such as benign prostatic hyperplasia or urethral stricture57. Monitoring Qmax helps identify potential lower urinary tract obstructions, aiding in the diagnosis of various bladder disorders. PSA is commonly used for prostate disease screening. While primarily a marker for prostate cancer, elevated PSA levels can also impact the urinary tract and indirectly affect bladder function (e.g., in benign prostatic hyperplasia)40. Monitoring PSA levels is important for a comprehensive assessment of the relationship between prostate and bladder functions. Detrusor contractility reflects the bladder muscle’s ability to contract during urination, a key parameter of voiding function58. Abnormal detrusor contractility, such as overactivity (causing urgency incontinence) or weakness (causing difficulty in urination), directly indicates bladder dysfunction59. Evaluating detrusor contractility is essential for diagnosing bladder disorders. IPSS is a standardized questionnaire used to assess the severity of lower urinary tract symptoms, with a QoL (Quality of Life) component evaluating the impact of these symptoms on a patient’s quality of life. The IPSS questionnaire covers frequency, urgency, intermittency, weak stream, straining, and nocturia, providing a comprehensive reflection of a patient’s symptoms and quality of life60. This information is crucial for clinicians to understand the patient’s condition and formulate treatment plans. For those clinical risk factors, urodynamic characteristics were examined following the guidelines of the International Continence Society (ICS) and reassessed by two independent clinicians, in which the PVR was determined by the residual amount of urine after urination through a urinary catheter and the Qmax was recorded by the Laborie urodynamic system61. The T-PSA level was measured by electrochemiluminescence (ECL) examination (Cobas e801 system, Roche, Switzerland) with an analytical sensitivity of 0.006 ng/mL. The bladder compliance and detrusor contractility were accessed by urodynamic testing and electromyography, respectively62,63. Urodynamic testing is a series of measurements to assess the function of the lower urinary tract by evaluating changes in pressure within the bladder and urethra64. It primarily involves bladder filling and voiding tests. A thin catheter is inserted into the urethra to infuse physiological saline into the bladder while simultaneously measuring changes in bladder and abdominal pressures, aiming to assess bladder compliance and detrusor contractility64. In clinical practice, the examination process includes both a bladder filling phase and a voiding phase. By recording changes in bladder pressure, abdominal pressure, and detrusor pressure, physicians can identify bladder dysfunction, such as reduced elasticity of the bladder wall or detrusor weakness, and subsequently formulate appropriate treatment plans65. Electromyography testing evaluates the neuromuscular function and activity of the detrusor and other related muscles by recording their electrical activity66. During the examination, electrode pads or needle electrodes are placed in the rectal or perineal area to monitor changes in electrical activity during bladder filling and voiding67. In clinical practice, electromyography testing is often combined with urodynamic studies to provide a detailed assessment of detrusor nerve conduction and muscle coordination64. This helps physicians diagnose conditions such as neurogenic bladder and bladder outlet obstruction, and it guides the development of personalized treatment strategies. Data processing and analysis

The above clinical risk factors were analyzed in accordance with the following procedure: (1) The data set was first divided into training and test data sets according to the requirements of 5-fold cross-validation. Specifically, the dataset was randomly divided into five non-overlapping subsets, and each time, four of them were used as the training set while the remaining one served as the test set. This process was repeated five times to ensure that each subset was used as a test set once. This method enables a better evaluation of the model’s generalization ability and reduces evaluation fluctuations caused by different dataset partitioning approaches; (2) For each fold of data segmentation, Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique(SMOTE) was only applied on the training data set to create new composite samples for sample balance among different categories, thus the test data set was not affected by SMOTE oversampling and the purity of test process can be maintained; (3) All categories of categorical variables were converted to numerical values using Label Encoding, while continuous variables were standardized by using the mean and standard deviation of corresponding continuous variables in training set to prevent data leakage; (4) By setting potential hyperparameter ranges for Support Vector Machines (SVM), Decision Trees (DT), AdaBoost, Random Forest (RF), K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), and Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) models, cross-validation was conducted to select the best-performing hyperparameter combinations on the validation set. This ensures that each model performs well under different data conditions; (5) Based on the previously identified optimal hyperparameters, the final model was retrained on the entire training dataset. Then, the evaluation metrics for the different diagnostic models, such as Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1 Score, and Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC-ROC), were calculated. Finally, the performance of each model on the test dataset was evaluated and compared with each other; (6) SHAP analysis was further performed to quantitatively interpret the contribution of each clinical risk factor on diagnostic decision-making. All the above data processing and analysis were performed by Python 3.9.7.

Evaluation criteria

The following five evaluation criteria were used to qualitatively assess each classification model: accuracy (ACC), precision, recall rate (Recall), F1 score (F1), and AUC-ROC69–71. The consistency between predicted probabilities of bladder lesions and the observed frequencies was also analyzed and assessed by plotting the average calibration curve of 5-fold cross-validation, in which the detailed procedure was as follows: (1)Trained the model in each fold of cross-validation and output predicted probabilities for the corresponding test data set; (2)Divided the probability range [0, 1] into five intervals and assign each predicted probability value to the corresponding interval; (3)Calculated the average predicted probability and the proportion of bladder cancer samples in each fold’s interval; (4)Summarized the cross-validation results and plot the average calibration curve.
