Effects of helium cold atmospheric plasma (CAP) on HT-29 cancer stem cell spheroid
Authors: Glavizh Adibhesami, Faezeh Vakhshiteh, Roya Ghods, Elmira Gheytanchi, Seyedeh Masoumeh Amiri Lachgorabi, Mahdiyeh Bakhtiyari-Ramezani, Zahra Madjd
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractColorectal cancer (CRC) recurrence and therapeutic resistance are largely attributed to cancer stem cells (CSCs), a subpopulation of tumor cells characterized by self-renewal and differentiation capacities. These cells exhibit enhanced antioxidant defenses that maintain redox homeostasis and contribute to resistance against conventional therapies. Cold atmospheric plasma (CAP), a partially ionized gas that generates reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (RONS), has emerged as a promising strategy to exploit redox vulnerabilities in cancer cells; however, its effects on colorectal CSCs remain poorly defined. In this study, CSC-enriched spheroids derived from HT-29 cells were exposed to a helium-based plasma jet for 30 to 240 s. CAP-induced hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) generation was quantified, cell viability was assessed using the MTT assay, self-renewal capacity was evaluated via sphere-forming efficiency, and the expression of stemness-associated genes (SOX2, OCT4, and NANOG) was analyzed by qPCR. CAP treatment generated H₂O₂ in a time-dependent manner across all tested media, with DMEM/F12 supplemented with 10% FBS exhibiting the highest accumulation. Spheroid viability declined proportionally with increasing exposure duration. Notably, CAP markedly suppressed self-renewal capacity. Control spheroids reached a diameter of 247 μm, whereas 240-second CAP exposure reduced the diameter to 87 μm (p < 0.0001), corresponding to a 65% reduction. At the molecular level, OCT4 expression increased by twofold (p < 0.01), while SOX2 and NANOG expression levels remained unchanged. Overall, CAP limits regrowth of HT-29 CSC-enriched spheroids in a time-dependent manner, consistent with ROS-associated oxidative stress, as reflected by H₂O₂ accumulation. The stemness transcriptional response was not uniform with increased OCT4 expression, but unchanged SOX2 and NANOG levels. These findings warrant further translational investigation, including comprehensive reactive-species profiling. Keywords: Colorectal cancer (CRC), Cancer stem cells (CSCs), Cold atmospheric plasma (CAP), Reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (RONS), HT-29 cellsSubject terms: Cancer, Cell biology, Stem cells

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) remains a major global health problem. It is the third most commonly diagnosed malignancy and the second leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide1. According to GLOBOCAN 2022 estimates, approximately 1.93 million new CRC cases and 904,000 related deaths occurred globally in 2022. Alarmingly, this burden is projected to increase substantially, reaching nearly 3.2 million new cases and 1.6 million deaths per year by 20401,2.

Despite advances in surgical techniques and systemic therapies, treatment failure and recurrence remain common3. Accumulating evidence suggests that tumor heterogeneity plays a critical role in these processes. Among heterogeneous tumor cell populations, cancer stem cells (CSCs) have emerged as key drivers of tumor initiation, progression, and relapse4,5. CSCs are characterized by their capacity for self-renewal and differentiation, as well as their ability to survive under hypoxic conditions, nutrient deprivation, and therapeutic stress. These properties contribute to metastatic dissemination and resistance to conventional chemotherapy and radiotherapy4,6.

In CRC, CSC-like populations are commonly identified by functional tumorigenesis capacity rather than marker expression alone. The gold standard for CSC identification remains tumor-initiating ability demonstrated in limiting-dilution xenograft assays, supported by evidence of self-renewal through serial spheroid formation and passaging7,8. Although marker panels and enrichment tools, such as LGR5, CD44, CD133, and ALDH1 activity, are widely employed, their expression alone does not establish definitive CSC identity9. Pluripotency-associated transcription factors including SOX2, OCT4, and NANOG have been reported in CRC models; however, their relevance is context-dependent and should be interpreted in conjugation with functional assays7,10. CSC-associated programs in CRC often involve developmental signaling pathways such as Wnt/β-catenin and Notch, whereas therapeutic resistance is commonly mediated by enhanced DNA damage repair capacity, redox adaptation, and upregulated drug efflux mechanisms9,11.

One distinctive characteristic of CSCs is their tightly regulated redox homeostasis. While bulk tumor cells often show elevated levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS) as a consequence of oncogenic signaling and metabolic reprogramming, CSCs are capable of maintaining comparatively low intracellular ROS levels12. This is achieved through robust antioxidant systems involving glutathione, superoxide dismutase, catalase, and activation of nuclear factor erythroid 2–related factor 2 (NRF2)-mediated transcriptional programs13,14. This redox adaptability supports CSC survival and stemness maintenance but may also represent an intrinsic vulnerability15. Consequently, therapeutic approaches that overwhelm antioxidant defenses and disrupt redox homeostasis have attracted increasing interest as potential strategies to selectively target CSC populations.

Cold atmospheric plasma (CAP) has recently emerged as a promising approach to exploit redox vulnerabilities in cancer cells16–18. CAP is a partially ionized gas generated at atmospheric pressure and near-ambient temperatures (25–40 °C) that produces a complex mixture of reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (RONS), charged particles, ultraviolet (UV) photons, and transient electric fields19–21. Biomedical CAP devices primarily include dielectric barrier discharge (DBD) systems and atmospheric pressure plasma jets (APPJs), which can be operated in either direct mode, where plasma is applied directly to cells or tissues, or indirect mode using plasma-activated liquids (PALs)22–25.

A growing body of evidence indicates that CAP can exert selective cytotoxic effects against cancer cells26,27. This selectivity is attributed to differences in basal ROS levels and redox homeostasis between malignant and non-malignant cells27–29. CAP-derived reactive species can induce oxidative stress, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction and caspase-dependent apoptosis18,27,30.. Beyond direct cytotoxic effects, CAP can also modulate the tumor microenvironment and antitumor immunity31,32. By exploiting redox vulnerabilities, CAP may be particularly effective against redox-adapted cancer cell subsets, including CSC-enriched populations18,28.

Recent studies have begun to explore the effects of CAP on CSC phenotypes33–35. Reported outcomes include reduced stemness marker expression, impaired self-renewal capacity, decreased spheroid formation, and enhanced sensitivity to conventional therapies13,14. However, contrasting findings indicate that, under certain exposure conditions or in specific cellular contexts, CAP may induce stemness-associated features or stress-adaptive responses, raising concerns regarding its dual effects35–37. These discrepancies highlight the need for comprehensive study and mechanistic investigations of CAP effects in well-defined CSC-enriched CRC models.

In this study, we investigated the effects of a helium atmospheric plasma on CSC-enriched spheroids derived from HT-29 colorectal cancer cells. We quantified CAP-induced hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) production and measured cytotoxicity using the MTT assay. We also evaluated sphere-forming efficiency as a surrogate measure of self-renewal, and analyzed the expression of stemness-associated genes, including SOX2, OCT4, and NANOG. This integrated functional and molecular approach aims to clarify the impact of helium CAP on colorectal CSC-like states.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

CSC enrichment in 3D spheroid culture

CSC-enriched spheroids were generated from HT-29 human colorectal adenocarcinoma cells (Iranian Biological Resource Center, Iran) using a serum-free three-dimensional (3D) culture protocol previously described and characterized for HT-29 spheroid formation. Cells were cultured in DMEM/F12 (Gibco, Germany) supplemented with B27 supplement (2×), 20 ng/mL epidermal growth factor (EGF), 20 ng/mL basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF), and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. This serum-free, growth-factor–enriched medium is widely used to promote the enrichment of colorectal cancer stem-like cells in 3D culture38.

Hanging drop spheroid formation technique

For initial spheroid formation, single-cell suspensions were prepared in serum-free spheroid medium at 1 × 10⁴ cells/mL. Droplets (25 µL, 1 × 10⁴ cells per drop) were dispensed onto the inner surface of inverted 8-cm bacterial-grade dish lids. Lids were inverted over PBS-filled bases to maintain humidity and prevent evaporation. Cells were incubated at 37 °C, 5% CO₂ for 72 h, allowing gravity-driven aggregation and spheroid formation. The hanging-drop approach produces uniform, scaffold-free spheroids, which serve as primary units for subsequent expansion and downstream experiments.

Spheroid collection and expansion

After initial spheroid formation, spheroids were carefully recovered by adding approximately 1,000 µL of fresh serum-free spheroid medium and gently transferring them to sterile falcons. Spheroids were then enzymatically dissociated using 0.25% trypsin–ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), 1 mM for 1 min at 37 °C. The resulting single-cell suspension was reseeded at a density of ~ 2 × 10⁵ cells/mL onto poly (2-hydroxyethyl methacrylate) (poly-HEMA)-coated, non-adhesive 24-well plates to expand CSC-enriched spheroids. This approach allows the expansion of CSC-enriched spheroids. Poly-HEMA coatings (10 mg/mL in 95% ethanol, air-dried overnight) are widely used to prevent cell adhesion and promote spheroid growth in 3D culture38,39.

Cold atmospheric plasma treatment

Plasma jet system specifications

A helium-based atmospheric-pressure plasma jet, developed at the Plasma Technology Development Research Center, was employed for all experiments. The jet operated using an alternating current (AC) high-voltage supply (7.5 kV, 11 kHz) with high-purity helium as the working gas at a constant flow rate of 5 L/min, as previously described40–42. Helium flow and inlet pressure were continuously monitored to ensure a stable plasma discharge.

CAP treatment protocol

CSC-enriched spheroids were cultured in 24-well plates coated with poly-HEMA (1 mL medium per well) for 24 h before treatment to ensure stabilization and initiate sphere formation. The plasma jet nozzle was positioned perpendicular to the liquid surface at a fixed distance of 1 cm43. Spheroids were exposed to helium plasma for 30, 60, 90, 120, 180, or 240 s (s) while maintaining constant voltage (7.5 kV), frequency (11 kHz), and helium flow (5 L/min). Untreated spheroids maintained under identical culture conditions served as controls. Following treatment, spheroids were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h before viability assay and gene expression analyses44,45.

H₂O₂ quantification

H₂O₂ in culture medium was quantified immediately following CAP exposure using the oxidation-reduction colorimetric assay kit (ZellBio GmbH, Ulm, Germany), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Absorbance was measured at 546 nm (540–570 nm) using a microplate reader. Standard curves (0–400 µM H₂O₂) were prepared in the same culture medium, and blank readings were subtracted before calculation. H₂O₂ generation was assessed in three experimental conditions: phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.2), Dulbecco’s modified eagle medium/Ham’s F-12 nutrient mixture (DMEM/F12), and DMEM/F12 supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). Each medium (1 mL in 24-well plates) was exposed to CAP for 30, 60, 90, 120, 180, or 240 s under the same plasma parameters described above, and repeated in triplicate. This approach enabled comparison of H₂O₂ generation across different liquid environments.

Cell viability assay

Single-cell suspensions from dissociated CSC-enriched spheroids, derived from HT-29 spheroid cells, were seeded in triplicate at 2 × 10⁵ cells/well in poly-HEMA–coated 24-well plates containing 1 mL spheroid medium per well. After 24 h of incubation at 37 °C and 5% CO₂, cells were exposed to the helium plasma jet for 30, 60, 90, 120, 180, or 240 s under previously described parameters (7.5 kV, 11 kHz, ~ 5 L/min helium flow, 1 cm nozzle-to-cell distance). Cell viability was assessed 24 h post-treatment using the MTT (3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)−2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide) assay. MTT reagent (5 mg/mL in PBS) was added and incubated according to standard protocols. Formazan crystals were solubilized in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), and absorbance was measured at 570 nm with background subtraction at ~ 640 nm. Values were normalized to the matched untreated control to calculate relative viability (%).

Sphere-forming assay

Single-cell suspensions from CSC-enriched HT-29 cultures were plated in serum-free spheroid medium (DMEM/F12 supplemented with 2× B27, 20 ng/mL EGF, and 20 ng/mL bFGF) on poly-HEMA–coated 24-well plates to prevent adhesion in triplicate. To minimize cell aggregation and promote clonal spheroid formation, cells were seeded at limiting dilution, typically ~ 1,000 cells per well in 1 mL medium in 24-well plates. Following CAP treatment of spheres in single-cell form, cultures were maintained at 37 °C and 5% CO₂, and primary spheroids were evaluated on day 10 after 4 days. Spheroids were monitored on days 4, 6, 8, and 10. On day 10, only well-rounded spheroids with diameters ≥ 40 μm were measured using an inverted microscope (Olympus CKX53)46,47. Spheroid diameter was quantified using ImageJ software (version 1.53k, National Institutes of Health, USA). For each condition, at least 40 individual spheroids from three independent wells were analyzed as shown in Fig. 2.

Quantitative real-time PCR analysis

Total ribonucleic acid (RNA) was extracted from HT-29 spheroids using TRIzol reagent (Sigma, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were assessed by NanoDrop spectrophotometry. Genomic DNA contamination has been eliminated using DNase I treatment. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using the reverse transcription kit (YektaTajhiz, Iran). Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was performed with SYBR Premix Ex Taq II (YektaTajhiz, Iran) using gene-specific primers listed in Table 1. Reactions (20 µL total volume) contained 10 µL SYBR Green master mix, 0.4 µM each primer, 2 µL cDNA template, and nuclease-free water. Thermal cycling conditions were: 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 60 s. Melt-curve analysis confirmed amplicon specificity. Gene-specific primers for stemness-associated transcripts (SOX2, OCT4, and NANOG) were normalized to glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) as the internal control. Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2^−(ΔΔCt) method with GAPDH as the internal control. Melt-curve analysis was performed to confirm the specificity of each amplicon. Each sample was analyzed in technical triplicate, and experiments were repeated with three independent biological replicates.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from at least three independent experiments. For normally distributed data with equal variances, comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test. Two-group comparisons used Student’s t-test. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. All analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 10.4.2 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

Results

H₂O₂ generation in culture medium following CAP exposure

The production of H₂O₂ in culture medium was measured immediately after CAP treatment. Exposure of PBS, DMEM, and DMEM/F12 to CAP for 30–240 s resulted in time-dependent increases in H₂O₂ concentration (Fig. 1). Notably, DMEM/F12 supplemented with 10% FBS showed the highest absolute H₂O₂ levels at most time points, followed by DMEM/F12 and PBS.

(A) Helium plasma jet apparatus; (B) Schematic representation of the plasma jet features used for direct irradiation of CSC-enriched cultures; (C) CAP-generated H₂O₂ levels in all media, including PBS, DMEM/F12, and DMEM/F12 + 10% FBS following 30–240 s exposures.

Cancer stem cells derived from HT-29

HT-29 cells cultured under serum-free, non-adherent conditions spontaneously formed compact, visible spheroids with smooth, well-defined contours. Representative images show a uniform spheroid architecture (Fig. 2). By day 3, spheroids reached almost 150 μm in diameter with well-defined contours. These morphological features indicate that the hanging-drop and low-attachment liquid-overlay techniques successfully generated 3D spheroids.

CAP-induced cytotoxicity in CSC-enriched spheroids

MTT assays demonstrated a progressive, time-dependent reduction in spheroid cells viability following CAP exposure (Fig. 2). Viability remained above 90% after 30 s treatment but declined steadily with longer exposures. Treatment for 240 s resulted in approximately 54% reduction in viability relative to untreated controls (p < 0.001). These data indicate dose-dependent cytotoxicity.

CAP effect on spheroid formation and self-renewal capacity

Single-cell suspensions derived from CAP-treated spheroids displayed significantly reduced sphere-forming capacity compared to untreated controls (Fig. 2). Control cultures formed spheroids with diameters ranging from 150 to 350 μm (mean 247 ± 49 μm) after 10 days, while 240 s CAP exposure resulted in spheroids measuring 40–150 μm (mean 87 ± 34 μm, p < 0.0001). Treated spheroids were markedly smaller and fewer in number, indicating impaired self-renewal potential of CSC-enriched populations.

CAP effects on stemness gene expression

qPCR analysis showed that CAP treatment modulated the expression of stemness-associated genes in CSC-enriched spheroids 24 h post-treatment. Specifically, OCT4 transcript levels increased modestly (2-fold, p < 0.01), while SOX2 and NANOG expression levels remained unchanged compared with untreated controls. (Fig. 2).

Discussion

Colorectal cancer remains a leading cause of cancer mortality worldwide, with treatment failure often attributed to CSCs. Cancer stem cells exhibit self-renewal capacity, differentiation plasticity, and enhanced resistance to conventional chemotherapy and radiation, contributing to tumor recurrence and metastasis. Current treatment modalities, such as surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy, often fail to eradicate this subpopulation, underscoring the need for innovative therapeutic strategies targeting CSCs. Cold plasma has emerged as a promising modality, generating RONS capable of selectively inducing cytotoxicity in malignant cells while sparing normal tissue. In this study, we evaluated the effect of CAP on HT-29–derived CSC-enriched spheroids, focusing on ROS generation, cell viability, self-renewal capacity, and stemness-associated gene expression.

CAP exposure induced time-dependent accumulation of H₂O₂ across all considered media. DMEM/F12 supplemented with 10% FBS exhibited the highest levels, likely due to serum protein interactions facilitating RONS generation, while PBS showed lower but consistent accumulation due to minimal scavenging capacity48,49. DMEM/F12 displayed intermediate kinetics, reflecting moderate scavenging by medium components such as amino acids and vitamins. These observations align with previous reports that medium composition critically influences plasma-mediated RONS delivery and biological outcomes50,51. Pyruvate scavenges H₂O₂, thereby reducing CAP-induced oxidative stress and modulating therapeutic selectivity48, while serum proteins and other antioxidants further buffer oxidants and influence dose–response behavior27,52, as the effective biological dose is shaped by plasma parameters, medium composition, and the cellular microenvironment49,53–56.

Cell viability assay demonstrated a time-dependent reduction, with viability declining to ~ 54% after 240 s of CAP exposure. Interestingly, a transient plateau was observed between 60 and 120 s, possibly reflecting early adaptive antioxidant responses or dose-response variation52,57. CSC-enriched populations often possess elevated antioxidant defenses, which may buffer initial oxidative insults58. However, prolonged exposure (≥ 180 s) overcame this resistance, resulting in substantial cytotoxicity. Previous studies report varied CAP cytotoxicity in cancer models, ranging from 30 to 80% viability reduction depending on treatment parameters. Our findings align with this range. Differences in dose–response steepness likely reflect variations in plasma device configuration, medium composition, and intrinsic redox capacity of the CSC population34,59.

CAP-treated spheroids exhibited reduced number and size, disrupted morphology, and impaired self-renewal, as evidenced by sphere-forming assay, consistent with reports that CAP and plasma-activated media suppress tumorsphere formation and CSC-like properties in breast, ovarian, and glioblastoma models60–62. These findings indicate functional loss of CSC properties, mirroring reports in breast and glioblastoma CSCs or spheroids enriched for stem-like cells61–63. Mechanistically, CAP may induce oxidative DNA damage and mitochondrial dysfunction or perturb key self-renewal pathways, such as Wnt/β-catenin and Notch, consistent with studies demonstrating CAP-induced ROS-dependent DNA lesions, mitochondrial stress, and redox-mediated targeting of CSCs28,64–66.

Importantly, these functional impairments were observed despite non-uniform transcriptional changes in pluripotency markers. OCT4 increased modestly, while SOX2 and NANOG remained unchanged. This pattern may reflect stress‑induced transcriptional responses or the selective survival of stress‑resistant CSC subpopulations. In addition, the observed increase in OCT4 may represent an adaptive response to CAP‑induced oxidative stress, consistent with a transient activation of stemness‑associated programs aimed at short‑term survival even as long‑term self‑renewal capacity is diminished28,35–37,67. At the same time, CAP may impose a selective bottleneck that favors the survival of inherently stress‑resistant CSC subpopulations, such as quiescent or low‑ROS cells, which can maintain or transiently elevate OCT4 expression even as the overall self‑renewing population declines. In this context, prior reports have shown that CAP exposure can suppress spheroid formation while transiently elevating stemness‑related transcripts in stress‑resistant subpopulations68–70. According to previous findings, CAP can exert context-dependent effects on CSCs, including reduction of stemness markers at high doses, while low-dose exposure may paradoxically enhance stemness marker expression and self-renewal in some models28,37,67,71.

Notably, upregulation of OCT4 and other stemness markers under oxidative stress has been observed in other cancer types treated with plasma-activated medium, where elevated OCT4 expression correlated with enhanced spheroid formation and oxidative stress resistance mechanisms35. Similarly, low-dose plasma-treated liquids have been shown to increase SOX2, NANOG, and OCT4 expression in osteosarcoma cells in a dose-dependent manner, coupled with enhanced self-renewal ability and tumorsphere formation, while only high-dose treatments suppressed these pro-stemness features36. Indeed, non-thermal plasma has been shown to activate proliferation and increase expression of OCT4, SOX2, and NANOG in normal adult stem cells while maintaining their stemness properties72. These findings are consistent with emerging evidence that ROS generated by CAP can exert complex, context-dependent effects on CSC phenotypes, including ROS-mediated reductions in stemness marker expression and tumorsphere-forming capacity, as well as, under certain conditions, induction of regenerative or stemness-like features28,37,63,67,71. Mechanistically, these responses may be driven by ROS‑mediated transcriptional reprogramming, particularly through STAT3‑dependent signaling and downstream antioxidant effectors such as GPX1, SOD2, and CAT1, which are known regulators of OCT4 dynamics in colorectal CSCs36,73. ROS‑induced chromatin remodeling under sub‑lethal oxidative stress can also transiently elevate OCT4 expression without conferring sustained self‑renewal capacity37,67. Together, these observations highlight the need for further mechanistic studies in colorectal CSC models. Furthermore, these observations underscore the fact that mRNA expression alone does not necessarily predict CSC functionality and that integration with phenotypic assays, such in vivo tumor-initiating potential, is essential6.

Collectively, our data demonstrate that CAP disrupts both viability and self-renewal capacity of HT-29 spheroids in a dose-dependent manner. However, the observed upregulation of OCT4 despite functional impairment highlights complex cellular responses that require further mechanistic investigation before therapeutic translation. This investigation was conducted using a single colorectal cancer cell line (HT-29); and validating these findings with additional cell lines and patient-derived organoids would strengthen their generalizability. Importantly, this study should be regarded as primarily phenomenological, characterizing the observable biological responses of HT‑29 CSC‑like spheroids to CAP rather than establishing a complete mechanistic pathway. Although the results are consistent with a potential contribution of ROS, the conclusion that CAP suppresses CSC properties through ROS remains indirect. Only extracellular H₂O₂ was quantified, whereas intracellular oxidative stress markers (such as the GSH/GSSG ratio or NRF2 activation) and cell‑death mechanisms were not assessed, leaving the downstream molecular events driving the observed effects unresolved. Future studies should incorporate time-course analyses at both mRNA and protein levels, as well as mechanistic controls using scavengers such as catalase and 2-(4-carboxyphenyl)−4,4,5,5-tetramethylimidazoline-1-oxyl-3-oxide (cPTIO), to delineate the relative contributions of H₂O₂, nitrite, and other RONS to CAP-mediated CSC suppression. Finally, the present analysis focused on a limited panel of stemness markers (OCT4, SOX2, and NANOG) at the transcriptional level. Assessment of protein expression would provide a more comprehensive evaluation of stemness regulation.

Conclusions

Our study demonstrates that CAP induces time-dependent cytotoxicity in HT-29 spheroids, impairs clonogenic capacity, and modulates stemness-associated transcription factors without uniformly suppressing their expression. The observed medium-dependent H₂O₂ kinetics highlight the critical role of plasma–liquid chemistry in shaping delivered RONS doses. Notably, the functional loss of sphere-forming ability despite modest OCT4 upregulation, together with unchanged SOX2 and NANOG expression, underscores the complexity of CSC responses to oxidative stress and the limitations of relying on transcriptional readouts alone. These findings highlight the potential of CAP as a therapeutic strategy targeting CSC-enriched populations. Careful dose engineering, including optimization of exposure time, matrix conditions, and cellular context, may maximize cytotoxic efficacy while limiting adaptive resistance in CSC-enriched colorectal cancer models. Further mechanistic and translational studies are warranted to advance CAP toward clinical application in colorectal cancer.

These findings establish a foundation for investigating the use of CAP as a potential approach targeting CSC populations in colorectal cancer. Optimization of exposure parameters, including treatment duration and medium composition, will be essential for future therapeutic development. Subsequent mechanistic studies integrating functional assays with molecular profiling are warranted to advance understanding of CAP’s potential in colorectal cancer treatment.
