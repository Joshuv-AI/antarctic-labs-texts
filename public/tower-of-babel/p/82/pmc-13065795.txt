Deep learning for fast screening and localization of spinal dural arteriovenous fistulas to enhance clinical workflow
Authors: Fei Zheng, Xuyang Cao, Jianmin Xu, Nana Wang, Futao Zhang, Lingling Zhang, Kewei Liang, Li Yang, Qi Guo, Yali Wang, Ping Yin, Xuedan Feng, Xuzhu Chen, Nan Hong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Manual processing of spinal CTA images for detecting dural arteriovenous fistulas (SDAVF) is laborious and operator-dependent. We developed an automated AI system (SDAVFdoc) that integrates a 3D convolutional neural network with anatomical prior knowledge to both identify SDAVF and localize the fistula site in a multicenter study of 718 patients. The system sequentially segments spinal structures, localizes the fistula region using anatomical priors, and finally classifies SDAVF likelihood via DenseNet within the foramina. The draining vein cluster segmentation model, using a threshold of 42.5, achieved high accuracy in distinguishing SDAVF cases, with F1-scores ranging from 0.932 to 0.960 across test sets. The DenseNet-based fistula detection model showed a high AUC of 0.928-0.954 across test sets. Compared to technicians, the system reduced processing time from 40.75 ± 11.57 min to 1.05 ± 0.29 min (P < 0.001) and clicks from 761.80 ± 202.05 to 9.68 ± 2.12 (P < 0.001), greatly streamlining clinical workflows. This AI-driven approach enables fast, accurate screening and localization of SDAVF.

Introduction

Spinal dural arteriovenous fistulas (SDAVFs) represent the majority of spinal vascular malformations, accounting for approximately 70% of such cases1. Although SDAVFs represent the most prevalent type of spinal vascular malformation, they are frequently underdiagnosed due to a combination of their relative rarity, nonspecific symptoms, diagnostic imaging pitfalls, and limitations in clinical thinking, making their misdiagnosis a systemic issue2–4. Delayed treatment of SDAVFs may result in irreversible damage to spinal nerve function5. Misdiagnosis of this condition can lead to incorrect and potentially harmful treatments1. Both missed and wrong diagnoses place significant financial and psychological burdens on the patient. The accurate localization of the fistula is crucial for both diagnosing and treating these malformations6. Recent advancements in non-invasive vascular imaging techniques have significantly enhanced the diagnosis of SDAVFs7. Modalities such as dynamic magnetic resonance angiography has become standard methods for diagnosing SDAVFs8–10. However, due to the challenges in accurately identifying the fistula site, digital subtraction angiography (DSA) remains the gold standard11.

DSA is considered the gold standard for diagnosing and assessing the detailed angioarchitecture of a SDAVF1,12. Nevertheless, spinal angiography, when compared to cranial neuroendovascular procedures, is associated with considerably higher radiation exposure11. This is not only due to differences in vascular anatomy but also because the imaging process involves larger body areas with greater volume and thickness13. For instance, Luetmer et al. reported an average fluoroscopy time of 38 minutes and a mean contrast agent volume of 219 mL in their series of preoperative spinal angiograms of 22 patients with SDAVFs14. Moreover, spinal vascular malformations are easily missed during DSA, often necessitating repeat angiography. In conclusion, the numerous injections required in spinal angiography can lead to prolonged fluoroscopy times, greater radiation exposure, and the use of additional contrast agents15. This highlights the need for non-invasive diagnostic alternatives that reduce radiation risks and supplement traditional diagnostic methods.

Computed tomography angiography (CTA) has emerged as a widely used, minimally invasive, and cost-effective imaging modality for diagnosing vascular disorders16,17. In the context of SDAVFs, CTA is usually performed prior to DSA to guide the diagnostic process. Typically, CTA is useful in locating the fistula site, thus limiting the need for more invasive angiographic procedures. However, the image reconstruction required for CTA, which depends on the expertise of the CT scan technician, is an operator-dependent, time-consuming, and error-prone task18. The accuracy of the reconstruction is directly influenced by the operator’s experience, and such limitations can be particularly problematic in cases with large patient volumes or during emergency situations19,20. Delays in the reconstruction process can lead to extended patient triage times, treatment delays, and increased burden on post-processing staff. Given the increasing demand for CTA examinations, this challenge is becoming more pronounced, highlighting the urgent need for an automated and efficient method to identify and locate SDAVFs while minimizing errors and alleviating the workload of operators.

Artificial intelligence (AI), encompassing various computational models and algorithms designed to address complex real-world problems, has made significant strides in the field of radiomics and medical imaging21,22. Deep neural networks, particularly convolutional neural networks (CNNs), have emerged as effective tools for tackling the technical challenges of medical imaging23–25. U-Net, a specialized CNN, has shown outstanding performance in biomedical image segmentation26,27. In brief, AI’s advancements in automating medical image post-processing tasks have resulted in substantial reductions in time and effort required for such processes28,29. Despite these advances, however, no intelligent diagnostic or recognition system for whole spinal cord CTA has been developed to date. We hypothesize that two main factors contribute to this: the rarity of cases and the complexity of the task. Firstly, SDAVFs are rare, and most publications on the subject are case reports or literature reviews30–32. China has nearly 3275 top-tier hospitals, but few can perform whole-spinal CTA33. Secondly, the identification of SDAVFs remains particularly challenging. Unlike vascular structures in the head, neck, and other regions, SDAVFs are not only small in size but also technically demanding to process34. This difficulty is clearly illustrated by our dataset, which shows that the draining vein cluster occupies merely 0.00185% of the total imaging area (Supplementary Table 1). To address these challenges, we developed a multi-stage workflow specifically tailored for SDAVF analysis. The development of such a system could significantly streamline the diagnosis and management of SDAVFs.

In this study, we developed the SDAVFdoc system, which can automatically screen for patients with SDAVF and further automatically locate the fistula site in the identified patients. We conducted a thorough quantitative evaluation of SDAVFdoc, assessing the model’s algorithmic performance, the quality of image reconstruction in meeting diagnostic standards, and the efficiency of its clinical application. We believe that AI technology, when integrated into the radiology workflow, can enhance operational efficiency, reduce healthcare costs, and ultimately improve patient outcomes.

Clinical evaluation

SDAVFdoc automatically generates draining vein cluster segmentation images from whole spinal cord CTA scans. To further assess its utility, we evaluated whether the generated image quality meets the diagnostic standards expected by radiologists. For the SDAVFdoc, inter-rater agreement in both the retrospective internal and external test sets was substantial, with Cohen’s kappa values of 0.806 and 0.755, respectively. Among the 64 scans in the retrospective internal test set, the overall image pass rate of SDAVFdoc outputs was 92.2% (59/64) for one evaluator and 95.3% (61/64) for the other. In the external test set comprising 60 scans, the pass rate was 90.0% (54/60) according to one evaluator and 93.3% (56/60) for the second evaluator. Notably, images rated as excellent quality (score of 3) accounted for more than 78.1% (50/64) of the retrospective internal test set and more than 78.3% (47/60) of the external test set. The image quality evaluation showed that 3-point ratings corresponded to large, well-defined draining vein clusters, 2-point ratings indicated irregular draining vein clusters with slightly larger volumes, and 1-point ratings represented smaller, isolated draining vein clusters (Fig. 3). Discussions

SDAVF is a vascular anomaly characterized by an abnormal connection between a radicular artery and a radicular vein1,35. The fistula is usually situated near the spinal nerve root within the dura mater36. The abnormal vascular connection creates a direct conduit for pressurized arterial blood to enter the venous system, leading to venous expansion and congestion. The majority of SDAVFs are solitary lesions arising from the lower thoracic and upper lumbar vertebral segmental arteries37. These anatomical understanding forms the foundation for our experimental design. Additionally, SDAVFs predominantly affect male patients, with diagnosis typically occurring in the fifth or sixth decade of life38,39. These demographic findings are consistent with the results of our own research.

Delayed diagnosis of SDAVFs is common, with these conditions frequently being underdiagnosed or misdiagnosed as other inflammatory, degenerative, or oncologic spinal disorders in approximately 22% of patients37,40,41. Catheter-based spinal DSA is the gold standard for diagnosing spinal dural arteriovenous fistulas (SDAVFs) due to its high resolution38. However, DSA is invasive, technically demanding, and carries risks such as radiation exposure, vascular injury, and contrast-related complications42. Its accuracy heavily depends on operator expertise, and incomplete catheterization can lead to misdiagnosis43. In contrast, CTA offers a less invasive alternative. When CTA successfully localizes a suspected SDAVF, it enables a targeted DSA approach. This focused strategy simplifies the procedure, shortens exam time, and reduces the risk of the procedure. Despite the advantages of CTA, its postprocessing remains time-consuming and requires a high level of expertise. Such difficulties highlight the demand for noninvasive imaging tools that can both accurately identify SDAVFs and precisely determine the fistula location.

The SDAVFdoc system employs a comprehensive workflow: spinal cord segmentation (ResUnet1) and draining vein cluster segmentation (ResUnet2) to identify the presence of SDAVF, followed by vertebral body centroid identification (ResUnet3) and intervertebral foramen recognition (ResUnet4) with a connected-domain algorithm for localization. Ultimately, fistula recognition is determined using the DenseNet model. The hybrid system integrates these models into a fully automated pipeline, processing whole spinal cord CTA images to detect SDAVF and accurately localize their position. Notably, this study addresses a critical gap in the field, as there is currently a lack of intelligent diagnostic systems specifically designed for SDAVF. Our work represents a significant step toward developing an automated solution for this clinically significant yet underserved diagnostic challenge.

Remarkably, we also systematically assessed SDAVFdoc’s clinical utility by evaluating its diagnostic image quality and workflow efficiency enhancement capabilities. For technicians, locating very small blood vessel regions is often time-consuming and heavily reliant on experience. Our method significantly reduces the time required to process each image. Additionally, the tool can accurately pinpoint the location of fistulas, providing complete and comprehensive image information with ease. Therefore, SDAVFdoc has the potential to assist technicians in clinical practice by quickly locating blood vessels (fistulas) in CTA images through an automated process. This innovative workflow for whole-spine CTA patient screening and fistula identification offers numerous advantages, including improved interpretative consistency, time and labor savings, and high sensitivity and specificity. If fully integrated into clinical settings, automated processing of segmented images could simplify the reconstruction process and seamlessly align with the clinical workflow.

Initial attempts to segment draining vein clusters directly from original whole spinal cord CTA images proved unsuccessful, resulting in a Dice coefficient of 0. This failure is likely attributable to the extremely small volumetric representation of the vessels, which led the model to overwhelmingly favor background prediction. To address this, we introduced a preprocessing step in which spinal cord segmentation is performed first, substantially reducing the anatomical search space and improving segmentation feasibility. Quantitative support for this approach, including the area ratios of draining vein clusters and spinal cord relative to the total image, is provided in Supplementary Table 1. Moreover, during the image quality assessment, some images were of poor quality. The main reason for this was the relatively small volume proportion of the draining vein cluster. This further highlights the significance of the proportion of the draining vein cluster within the image area in our experiment.

Although the results of this study are promising, there are several limitations. Firstly, the datasets exclusively comprised cases of SDAVF, omitting other spinal vascular malformations such as extradural or perimedullary arteriovenous fistulas, which restricts the model’s current applicability and necessitates future validation on a more diverse spectrum of pathologies. Secondly, the model’s development was specifically optimized for thoracolumbar (T1-L5) CTA imaging protocols, meaning its performance for fistulas outside this region remains unproven and requires dedicated assessment. Thirdly, our cohort did not include rare but clinically challenging cases of SDAVF with multiple fistulous points, leaving the model’s efficacy for such complex variants unknown. Finally, while the AI system demonstrates high diagnostic accuracy, its decision-making process as a deep learning model lacks inherent interpretability, which could pose a challenge for clinical trust and adoption without further development of explainability features. We acknowledge that these limitations do not invalidate our core findings but clearly delineate the scope for future work to enhance the model’s robustness, versatility, and translational potential.

In conclusion, we have successfully developed a CNN model capable of detecting and localizing SDAVF through a fully automated process using whole-spine CTA images. Compared to existing technologies, it provides a time-efficient and objective approach to optimize the segmentation and identification of whole-spine CTA images, thereby reducing costs and improving efficiency. The model demonstrates high reproducibility and generalizability, making it a potentially valuable tool for the non-invasive characterization of SDAVF. The efficiency and accuracy demonstrated by SDAVFdoc in this study may support its future consideration as a supportive tool in clinical workflows.

Study design

The first stage determines whether the patient has an SDAVF. ResUnet1 is used for spinal cord segmentation, followed by expanding the spinal cord region outward by six voxels to create an expansion zone. Only the expanded region is retained, and the region is set to a soft tissue window. Using ResUnet2, the draining vein cluster, specifically the perimedullary veins, is automatically segmented. The resulting mask is then resized to the original image size. The volume of the perimedullary veins is calculated, and if the volume is below a threshold, the patient is considered not to have an SDAVF. Conversely, if the volume exceeds the threshold, the patient is identified as having an SDAVF.

In the second stage, to narrow down the potential anatomical region of the fistula, a three-class segmentation approach using ResUnet3 is applied to the CT images, where Class 0 represents the background, Class 1 represents spherical regions centered on the vertebral body centroids from T1 to L5, and Class 2 represents the sacral region. A connected component labeling algorithm is used to distinguish the spherical regions centered on the vertebral body centroids from T1 to L5. The z-axis coordinates of each spherical region and the sacral vertebral centroid are obtained. Combining this anatomical prior knowledge, the sacral region is located below the L5 vertebra, and the vertebrae above L5 are L4, L3, L2, L1, extending up to T1. By matching the z-axis coordinates of the spherical regions with the vertebral names, the ZT1-ZL5 values are determined. The highest z-axis coordinate of the draining veins (Zhigh) and the lowest coordinate (Zlow) are identified. Among the ZT1-ZL5 17 values, the smallest value greater than Zhigh and the largest value less than Zlow are used to crop the CT region between ZTn and ZTn + x, which represents the potential anatomical region of the fistula.

In the third stage, for fistula localization, the potential anatomical region of the fistula is divided into smaller blocks based on the coordinate values, ranging from ZTn to ZTn + 1, ZTn + 1 to ZTn + 2, and so on. ResUnet4 is applied to each small block to segment spherical regions centered on the bilateral intervertebral foramina and compute the centroids of these regions. The left-side (Lx, Ly, Lz) and right-side (Rx, Ry, Rz) coordinates of the centroids are obtained. A 32 × 32 × 32 CT region is cropped around the intervertebral foramen. Finally, DenseNet is used to evaluate the probability of the fistula being present in each 32 × 32 × 32 CT region. To further reduce the false positive rate, patients with output probabilities below 0.2 for any potential anatomical region of the fistula are predicted as negative. For patients with probabilities greater than 0.2, in order to enhance the detection rate, the CT region with the highest probability is identified as the region containing the fistula. The ZTn + x − ZTn + x + 1 range, along with the left or right coordinates, is then used to determine the precise location of the fistula within the intervertebral foramen.

Patient data and CTA images acquisition

All whole spinal cord CTA scans were performed as part of routine clinical care. The scanning range extended from the upper edge of the eye socket to the end of the coccygeal vertebra, with a total of 1198–1205 slices along the z-axis. The inclusion and exclusion criteria were consistent across all three participating centers. Patients with suspected SDAVF underwent CTA. This cohort comprised 616 patients from Center 1 and 102 patients from Centers 2 and 3. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients who underwent spinal cord CTA examination; (2) patients with complete imaging and clinical data; (3) the presence or absence of SDAVF was definitively confirmed using DSA or surgical findings. The exclusion criteria included: (1) incomplete imaging data; (2) poor image quality; (3) patients with recurrence of SDAVF after surgery or those requiring repeated follow-up or monitoring.

Two blinded, experienced radiologists independently assessed images in the retrospective sets using a 3-point scale to evaluate diagnostic suitability. The prospective set was designed primarily to evaluate model performance under real-world clinical conditions. It also incorporated assessments of workflow efficiency, including time savings and the number of post-processing operations required.

Radiologist’s annotations

Given the substantial dataset, manual segmentation was conducted on scans from 83 patients diagnosed with SDAVF, with the remaining cases processed using automatic segmentation. A randomly selected subset of 83 SDAVF-positive patients from the primary cohort served as the annotated dataset. The annotation process was performed by radiologists with a standardized protocol using ITK-SNAP 3.8.0 (http://www.itksnap.org): for ResUnet1, spinal cord labels were generated through direct manual segmentation on whole-spine CTA images; for ResUnet2, draining vein cluster annotations were created within an anatomically constrained region derived from the spinal cord mask dilated by 6 voxels; while for both ResUnet3 and ResUnet4, reference labels consisted of standardized spherical regions centered on vertebral body centroids and bilateral intervertebral foramina centroids, respectively, as identified by radiological experts. Algorithm-generated annotations were reviewed and corrected by radiologists.

To minimize human errors, we adopted a hierarchical annotation method for all scanned images in the database. Initially, the dataset was pre-annotated using ITK-SNAP, where the spinal cord, draining vein cluster (for SDAVF-positive patients), the center of the vertebral body region, and the bilateral intervertebral foramina were marked with different masks. Subsequently, two technicians with more than two years of post-processing experience independently corrected the pre-annotated images to generate the initial annotation results. Afterward, two radiologists with over five years of experience rechecked the accuracy of the initial annotations and made corrections when necessary to ensure the precision of the annotations. In cases where the opinions of the two radiologists differed, a senior arbitration expert, a radiologist with over ten years of experience, made the final decision. The average time required for pre-annotating each case was approximately 47 ± 17 minutes, with the time for annotating the draining vein cluster being 38 ± 14 minutes.

SDAVFdoc model development

The SDAVFdoc model is an automated hybrid system that employs five CNNs, including four segmentation models based on ResUnet and one classification model based on DenseNet, for segmenting and recognizing spinal structures and the draining vein cluster. It is specifically designed to detect and localize SDAVFs in whole spinal cord CTA images.

The ResUnet-based segmentation models were developed as follows. Initially, we resampled the voxel size of all images to (0.5, 0.5, 0.625). Given the large size of the images, it was not feasible to input them in their entirety into the segmentation network. Therefore, we adopted a patch-based processing approach. The patch sizes were as follows: [160, 160, 320] for the spinal cord, [80, 80, 700] for the draining vein cluster, [144, 144, 400] for the vertebral bodies, and [120, 120, 80] for the intervertebral foramina. Since draining vein clusters are more prominent under soft tissue window settings, the images were adjusted to soft tissue window mode to enhance feature visibility. To improve dataset diversity and the model’s robustness, we applied random flipping to both the images and their corresponding labels. Specifically, a 50% probability of flipping was applied along each of the three spatial dimensions (x, y, and z). This method enabled the model to learn variations in image orientation and generalize better across different spatial transformations.

The DenseNet-based classification model was trained and evaluated as follows. The primary cohort was randomly partitioned into training and validation subsets at a 7:3 ratio. Model evaluation was performed using two independent test cohorts: a retrospective dataset and a prospectively collected test set. To enhance the model’s robustness and generalization ability, we implemented several data augmentation techniques on the training images. These techniques included random rotation, random flipping, and random scaling. For random rotation, the images were rotated within a range of [−π/12, π/12] with a 50% probability, simulating variations in image orientation to help the model adapt to different angles. Random flipping was applied along each of the three spatial dimensions (x, y, and z) with a 50% probability, enabling the model to learn spatial invariance. Additionally, random scaling was performed within a range of 90% to 100% of the original size, with a 50% probability, allowing the model to handle variations in image size and improving its robustness to scale changes.

The ResUnet and DenseNet models together form a hybrid system called SDAVFdoc, which features a fully automated workflow that ensures seamless data transfer between components. In this study, the Adam optimizer (with a learning rate of 1e-4) was used to train the ResUNet/DenseNet model and optimize its parameters. To control the learning rate more effectively, we employed the WarmupCosineSchedule learning rate scheduler. This scheduler initiated three warm-up steps at the beginning of training, gradually increasing the learning rate from 0.1 times the initial value to the set base value of 1e-4. Afterward, it used a cosine annealing strategy to progressively decrease the learning rate until the end of training. This scheduling method facilitated smooth convergence during training and improved the overall performance of the model. The model was trained for 1,000 epochs using ResUnet and for 100 epochs using DenseNet. All computational experiments were conducted using Python 3.8 with PyTorch 1.9.0 framework, deployed on a Linux-based workstation equipped with an NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPU (48 GB GDDR6 memory)

Together, these models form a hybrid system, SDAVFdoc, with a fully automated pipeline that ensures seamless data transfer between components. The input data comprises integrated, continuous three-dimensional images of whole-spine CTA. The model’s output provides a classification of whether the patient has an SDAVF, along with the specific location of the fistula (e.g., at the right intervertebral foramen of L3).

Clinical evaluation

The clinical evaluation of the SDAVFdoc system encompassed both image quality assessments and an analysis of clinical efficiency.

The automated SDAVFdoc is designed to provide an efficient, quality-assured, and unattended image recognition and diagnostic process. With the reconstruction of draining vein clusters directly impacting the subsequent fistula localization, accurate segmentation of these draining vein clusters is critical for proper diagnosis, and the segmented images are evaluated by two experienced radiologists (with more than 8 years of experience) to determine whether they meet clinical diagnostic standards.

For the image quality assessment, a retrospective internal test set and an external test set were utilized to calculate the image pass rate, with subjective evaluation scores assigned as follows: a score of 3 represents excellent image quality, with complete vascular delineation, no artifacts, and easy diagnosability; a score of 2 indicates good image quality, where the vascular delineation is relatively complete but some vessels remain unclear, with minimal artifacts, though still sufficient for diagnosis; and a score of 1 denotes poor image quality, where most vessels are unclear, severe artifacts are present, and diagnosis is difficult. Images achieving a minimum score of 2 were considered to fulfill diagnostic quality standards and were consequently classified as passing. In the event of disagreement between the two evaluators, a consensus was achieved through joint reevaluation.

The clinical efficiency of SDAVFdoc was evaluated between November 2024 and January 2025, focusing on two main aspects: time consumption (work duration) and number of clicks (labor intensity). Specifically, during the technician’s operation, another author (CXY) recorded the time and tracked the number of clicks in real-time by manually drawing tally marks, which were summed up afterwards. A prospective cohort of 28 patients (15 with negative SDAVF and 13 with positive SDAVF) was included in the study, and the time spent throughout the entire post-processing workflow, including the reconstruction of draining vein clusters and identification of potential fistula sites by two technicians, as well as the number of clicks involved, was recorded. Similarly, the time and number of clicks required for SDAVFdoc to analyze the same 28 patients were documented. The average results from the two technicians were compared with those of SDAVFdoc, allowing for an assessment of the model’s impact on clinical efficiency, particularly in terms of reducing time consumption and labor intensity, thereby improving the overall diagnostic workflow.

Statistical analysis

Comparative analyses of clinical characteristics among study groups were performed using standardized statistical approaches. Categorical variables were assessed using Pearson’s chi-square test. Normally distributed continuous variables were compared using independent two-sample t-tests or paired t-tests, as appropriate. All statistical tests were conducted as two-sided, with statistical significance defined by a p-value of less than 0.05. The analysis was performed using SPSS 26 (SPSS Inc., Armonk, NY). For model performance evaluation, we implemented a comprehensive assessment framework incorporating multiple metrics: (1) standard classification metrics, including accuracy, precision, recall, specificity, F1-score, and the area under the ROC curve (AUC), to evaluate discriminatory capability; (2) confusion matrices for detailed accuracy quantification; and (3) Dice coefficient44 as a specialized metric for spatial overlap accuracy in segmentation tasks. This multi-dimensional evaluation strategy ensured robust assessment of both classification and segmentation performance.
