Bmpr2 Loss in the Vascular Endothelium Enhances Neoangiogenesis and Growth of Lung Metastatic Lesions in a Mouse Model of Breast Cancer
Authors: Jordan A Harry, Sofia I Skebo, Devon V Cole, Vincent Dinh, M Martin VandenBroek, Abby K Lee, Natasha Camacho, Hollie N Piepjohn, Victoria Hoskin, Kimberly J Laverty, Yan Gao, Douglas Quilty, Peter A Greer, Mark L Ormiston
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBone morphogenetic protein‐9 (BMP9) has been implicated as a regulator of metastasis and tumor angiogenesis, with contrasting studies demonstrating both pro‐ and antiangiogenic roles for BMP9 across different cancer cell lines and animal models. However, these works have yet to define the contribution of the type‐II BMP receptor (BMPR‐II) to these processes, or assess whether the effects of BMP9 are mediated via actions on the endothelium, the tumor, or its microenvironment. Here, we demonstrate that the heterozygous (Bmpr2 EC+/− ) or homozygous (Bmpr2 EC−/− ) deletion of BMPR‐II in the pulmonary endothelium is associated with increased overall burden and vascularization of metastases in the lungs of mice subjected to the EO771 orthotopic engraftment model of metastatic breast cancer. These increases, relative to Bmpr2 EC+/+ littermates, were observed despite equivalent primary mammary tumor growth across mice of all genotypes. In vitro, secreted factors or extracellular matrix components from BMPR‐II‐silenced human pulmonary arterial endothelial cells (HPAECs) did not alter EO771 proliferation relative to controls. However, endothelial BMPR‐II depletion did eliminate the capacity of BMP9 to suppress both HPAEC migration to VEGF165 and EO771 transmigration across an HPAEC monolayer. In a tail vein injection model, the short‐term establishment of EO771 cell metastatic lesions was equivalent in the lungs of female Bmpr2 EC+/− and Bmpr2 EC−/− mice, relative to Bmpr2 EC+/+ controls, suggesting that the enhanced lung tumor burden observed in orthotopically implanted mice with endothelial Bmpr2 deletion is likely a consequence of enhanced tumor vascularization, rather than altered lung retention and engraftment. Our findings identify an important role for endothelial BMPR‐II signaling in regulating the vascularization of metastatic lesions in the lungs.Keywords: angiogenesis, bone morphogenetic protein 9 (BMP9), bone morphogenetic protein type II receptor (BMPR‐II), breast cancer, metastasis, neovascularization, vascular endothelial growth factor

Introduction

Angiogenesis is central to the growth and metastatic potential of solid tumors. In breast cancer, excessive or dysregulated vascularization not only fuels primary tumor growth via the supply of oxygen and nutrients, but also facilitates cancer cell entry into the circulation through the tortuous and permeable tumor vasculature [1, 2, 3]. Angiogenesis is also key to promoting the growth of breast cancer metastases in other tissues, including the lungs, brain, liver, and bone, contributing to significantly reduced survival rates for individuals with advanced metastatic disease [4, 5, 6, 7, 8]. While antiangiogenic therapies targeting traditional mediators like vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) initially demonstrated promise in controlling tumor growth [9, 10, 11, 12, 13], their clinical application has been limited by poor efficacy and side effects, including venous thromboembolism, hypertension, and hemorrhaging [14, 15, 16]. Additionally, reducing the blood supply to tumors limits the ability of chemotherapies, injected radiotherapies and immune cells to infiltrate these tissues and favors the selection of more aggressive cancer cells [17].

More recently, therapeutic approaches targeting tumor angiogenesis have shifted towards the goal of vascular normalization, with the aim of providing a quiescent, but functional vasculature that limits invasion and metastasis, while also facilitating the delivery of intravenous therapeutics [18, 19, 20]. Bone morphogenetic protein (BMP) signaling has emerged as a promising target for such strategies. BMPs are ligands of the transforming growth factor‐β superfamily that signal through complexes comprised of type‐I and type‐II receptors. In the endothelium, the type‐I receptor, Alk1, pairs with either the BMP type‐II receptor (BMPR‐II) or the activin type‐II receptors, ActR‐IIa or ActR‐IIb, to form receptor complexes for circulating BMP9, BMP10, or heterodimers of these two ligands [21, 22]. Downstream of receptor binding, canonical BMP signaling involves the phosphorylation and nuclear translocation of the transcriptional mediators, Smad1, Smad5 and Smad9, which induce the expression of target genes, including many genes involved in endothelial proliferation and angiogenesis.

Studies examining the functional effects of endothelial BMP signaling have identified BMP9 as a circulating vascular quiescence factor that suppresses angiogenesis and promotes endothelial monolayer integrity [23, 24, 25]. Deletion of the gene encoding BMP9, but not BMP10, is associated with enhanced mammary tumor growth, vascularization, and lung metastasis in the syngeneic EO771 orthotopic mouse engraftment model of metastatic breast cancer [26]. Similarly, BMP9 overexpression and recombinant BMP9 therapy reduce tumor growth in mouse models of lung cancer and glioblastoma, respectively [20, 27].

While promising, these studies are limited by uncertainty regarding the degree to which the effects of BMP9 are attributable to its actions on the endothelium, versus any direct effect of BMP9 on the tumor, or modification of the tumor microenvironment. In the pulmonary endothelium, loss of BMPR‐II or BMP9 has been shown to enhance angiogenesis via VEGF‐mediated pathways [28, 29]. The vascular stabilizing capacity of BMP9 is also highly dependent on type‐II receptor utilization. BMP9 has been shown to suppress angiogenesis when its type‐II receptor, BMPR‐II, is present, but promote endothelial proliferation and angiogenesis when BMPR‐II is lost [30]. This suggests that differential effects may arise as a consequence of signaling through alternative type‐II receptors, such as ActR‐IIa/b. Proangiogenic effects of BMP9 have also been reported in models of hepatic, ovarian, and bladder cancer [28, 31, 32, 33]. However, the specific role of BMPR‐II or other type‐II receptors in these models has yet to be explored.

The current study addresses these outstanding questions by exploring the specific impact of BMPR‐II loss in the endothelium on the establishment, neovascularization and growth of breast cancer metastases in the lung. We demonstrate a critical role for BMPR‐II in tumor vascularization in the lungs of female mice, but no measurable impact of endothelial BMPR‐II loss on the frequency of pulmonary metastatic engraftment. Together, these findings provide insights that can help to guide future BMP‐targeted cancer therapies.

Methods

Experimental Animals

All animal protocols were approved by the Queen's University Animal Care Committee (Kingston, Ontario). Animal welfare was monitored and logged in accordance with the Canadian Council on Animal Care. Animals were monitored daily for primary tumor size (pre‐ and post‐resection), weight loss and other signs of pain or distress. Endothelial conditional Bmpr2 knockout mice were created as detailed previously and were maintained on a C57BL/6 J background. Cre recombinase was expressed in the endothelium of the lungs, brain, and retina using the L1‐Cre construct [34], which is driven by a transgenic segment of the Alk1 promoter. All experimental animals were heterozygous for the Ai6 reporter construct, which enables ZsGreen fluorescent protein expression in Cre+ cells. Animals also possessed either one (Bmpr2 EC+/− ), two (Bmpr2 EC−/− ) or no (Bmpr2 EC+/+ ) copies of a floxed Bmpr2 allele, allowing for the selective deletion of Bmpr2 in Cre+ZsGreen+ endothelial cells.

To assess ZsGreen expression in the mammary endothelium of naïve mice, animals were infused i.v. with 20 µg of α‐CD31‐AF647 antibody (clone: MEC13.3; Biolegend, San Diego, CA) 10 min before sacrifice. Upon sacrifice, the systemic circulation was perfused with 1:50 heparin‐saline, followed by 2% paraformaldehyde (PFA). Tissues were fixed overnight in 2% PFA before imaging for CD31 and ZsGreen.

Orthotopic EO771 Model

EO771 cells, lentivirally transduced to express red fluorescent protein (EO771‐RFP), were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) media with 1.0 × 104 units/mL Penicillin, 1.0 × 104 µg/mL Streptomycin and 25 µg/mL Amphotericin B (Anti‐Anti; Gibco, Grand Island, NY) and 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS) (Wisent, St‐Bruno, Quebec). 5.0 × 104 cells/mouse were suspended in 20 μL of a 50:50 Matrigel (Sigma‐Aldrich, Oakville, ON): phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) mixture and injected into the fourth mammary fat pad of 8–12 week‐old female Bmpr2 EC+/+ , Bmpr2 EC+/− or Bmpr2 EC−/− littermates. Tumor size was monitored through palpation every second day, starting 10 days after implantation. Tumor volume was calculated using an assessment of mean diameter and was based on an assumption of spherical tumor growth. Once primary tumors reached a volume of 500 mm3, mice were anesthetized and tumors were resected and weighed as part of a recovery surgery. A subset of primary tumors were fixed overnight in 4% PFA (Sigma Aldrich, St Louis, MO) and transferred to PBS for 24 h, followed by sequential overnight incubations at 4°C in 15% and 30% sucrose (Fisher Scientific), before embedding in Optimum Cutting Temperature (OCT) compound (Fisher Healthcare, Houston, TX). For the analysis of ZsGreen expression in the mammary tumor vasculature, non‐serial 10 µm sections were imaged for RFP, ZsGreen and DAPI nuclear stain (Biolegend, San Diego, CA) using a Zeiss, AXIO Imager M1.

One additional recovery tumor resection surgery was performed if primary tumor regrowth was detected. Animals were excluded from the study if more than one additional regrowth occurred. No bias was observed in the genotypes of mice excluded from the study due to excessive mammary tumor regrowth. Four weeks after primary tumor resection, animals were sacrificed using 50 mg/kg sodium pentobarbital. The heart and the lungs were flushed with PBS containing 20 units/mL heparin (Sigma Aldrich), perfused with PFA, and inflated via the trachea with a 1:1 mixture of OCT and PBS. After inflation, the lungs, liver, and brain were excised from the mouse, fixed overnight in 4% PFA and embedded in OCT as detailed above.

Before fixation, lung tumor burden was quantified in explanted lungs as a measure of whole‐lung RFP fluorescence using a Lumina LT Series III In Vivo Imaging System (IVIS; Perkin Elmer, Waltham, MA). RFP radiant efficiency was quantified as a measure of emitted light divided by excitation light P/sµW/cm2, where P/s is a measure of photons (P) per second (s) and µW is a measure of microwatts. Ventral and dorsal lung images were quantified and radiant efficiency for each mouse was reported as the mean of the two values. The threshold for measurable RFP fluorescence over baseline was set at two standard deviations above the mean RFP radiant efficiency for naïve lungs.

Quantification of Small Metastases in the Liver and Brain

For the quantification of small liver and brain metastases in the orthotopic model, non‐serial 10 µm sections, separated by 100 µm of tissue for brain and 50 µm for liver, were stained with DAPI before imaging of RFP fluorescence. Six (brain cortex) or eight (liver) nonoverlapping fields of view (FOV) were collected from each tissue. The total number of individual metastatic events per image were counted and averaged for a mean number of metastases per FOV.

Vascular Analysis

For the orthotopic model, large lung metastases (≥ 500 µm in diameter) were identified. Non‐serial 10 µm sections, separated by 100 µm of tissue, were cut through tumor‐containing regions and stained with DAPI before epifluorescent imaging using a Zeiss, AXIO Imager M1. Both the tumor center and border regions (2/3 tumor, 1/3 surrounding lung tissue) were imaged for ZsGreen and RFP. To quantify vascular density, images of the ZsGreen channel were randomly assigned a numerical identifier, uploaded to FIJI and threshold values established using the over/under setting to obtain a black‐and‐white image of the vascular network. To quantify vascular density at tumor borders, the tumor border was traced before establishing threshold values. Vascular density was quantified on thresholded images as a percentage of ZsGreen+ pixels per image. For vascular network analysis, the thresholded images were analyzed using the ImageJ Angiogenesis Analyzer plugin in ImageJ (developed at the US National Institutes of Health and available at http://rsb.info.nih.gov/nih-image/), using the settings in Supporting Information S1: Table S1. Network composition was determined for each tumor as a measure of total segment length, branching interval, and percent isolated segments. Lung sections were also stained for DLL4 using a 1:400 dilution of goat‐α‐DLL4 antibody (AF1389; Bio‐Techne, Minneapolis, MN) and a 1:500 dilution of donkey‐α‐goat AF647‐conjugated secondary (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA). Lung sections were imaged for DLL4, ZsGreen and a DAPI nuclear stain.

Endothelial Cell Migration Assay

Human pulmonary arterial endothelial cells (HPAEC) cells were cultured in Endothelial Cell Growth Medium‐2 (EGM‐2) media (Lonza, Basel, Switzerland) and transfected with siRNAs targeting BMPR2 or a nontargeting control (Horizon Discovery, Cambridge, UK). SiRNA silencing and validation of knockdown efficiency by qPCR and immunoblotting were conducted as described previously using the primers detailed in Supporting Information S1: Table S2 and the antibodies detailed in Supporting Information S1: Table S3. After 24 h, cells from each siRNA group were pretreated with 1 ng/mL BMP9 in EGM‐2 media or a media control for an additional 24 h before resuspension in Endothelial Basal Medium (EBM‐2) (Lonza) supplemented with Antibiotic/Antimycotic (Anti‐Anti) and 0.5% FBS, with or without 1 ng/mL BMP9 to match the pretreatment condition. 1.0 × 105 cells/well in 500 μL were added to the upper compartment of a 24−well, 8 µm pore transwell insert, pre‐coated for 16 h with 100 µg/mL collagen (both Corning Inc., Corning, NY) in PBS. The bottom compartment of each transwell contained 1 mL of EBM‐2 supplemented with Anti‐Anti, 0.5% FBS, and 25 ng/mL VEGF165 (Peprotech, Rocky Hill, NJ). After a 4−h incubation, the transwell inserts were removed and the upper surface was swabbed to remove any unmigrated cells before staining with Camco Quikk Stain II (Cambridge Diagnostic Products, Fort Lauderdale, FL). Once dry, the membranes were excised, mounted onto glass slides with DPX (Sigma‐Aldrich) and imaged using a Zeiss, AXIO Imager M1 microscope. The number of cells per FOV was calculated from a total of four images per insert and averaged for the total number of migrated cells.

Assessment of EO771 BMP Responsiveness

EO771 cells were plated at a density of 5.3 × 104 cells/cm2 on 6 cm plates and allowed to grow overnight. Cells were then starved in DMEM supplemented with 0.1% FBS and Anti‐Anti for 4 h before stimulation with 10 ng/mL of either BMP4, BMP6 or BMP9 (all Peprotech), or specified concentrations of BMP9 for 2 h before protein collection. Cells were washed with PBS before lysis in RIPA buffer (50 mM Tris‐HCl, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% SDS, 1% Igepal‐CA‐630, 10 mM NaF, 0.5% Sodium Deoxycholate and 2 mM Sodium Orthovanadate; all Sigma Aldrich), supplemented with EDTA‐free protease inhibitor cocktail (cOmplete Mini, Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany). For each sample, 20 µg of protein was loaded into a 10% SDS‐PAGE gel, transferred to PVDF (Millipore, Burlington, MA), and probed for BMPR‐II, phosphorylated Smad1/5/9, total Smad1 or α‐tubulin using the antibodies detailed in Supporting Information S1: Table S3. Smad1/5/9, total Smad1 and α‐tubulin, blots were imaged with ECL Prime (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL), densitometry was performed using Bio‐Rad Image Lab software and Smad protein levels were normalized to α‐tubulin as a loading control. For BMPR‐II protein, abundance was normalized to a total protein loading control using LiCor Revert 700 staining and destaining before primary antibody incubation. Blots were then imaged using a LiCor Odyssey imaging system. The profiling of BMP receptor expression was performed by qPCR as described previously using the primers detailed in Supporting Information S1: Table S2.

EO771 Proliferation

For assessment of proliferative responses to BMP9, EO771 cells were plated in a 96−well plate at a density of 5.3 × 104 cells/cm2 and incubated for 72 h before stimulation with BMP9 at a range of concentrations. Cells were cultured for an additional for 24 h before an 8‐h incubation with Bromodeoxyuridine (BrdU) labeling solution and assessment of BrdU incorporation using an ELISA BrdU Colourimetric kit (Roche, Basel, Switzerland), as detailed in the manufacturer's instructions. As a secondary validation, cells were plated at a density of 1.0 × 104 cells per well in 24‐well tissue culture plates and incubated in DMEM + 10% FBS overnight before the addition of 10 ng/mL BMP9. DMEM media containing 10% FBS and Anti‐Anti, with or without BMP9, was replenished every 48 h after the initiation of BMP9 treatment. Cell counts were performed from days 0 to 4 to determine cellular proliferation.

To determine the impact of HPAEC‐derived secreted factors and matrix components on EO771 proliferation, HPAECs were plated in 6‐well plates at a density of 3.0 × 105 cells per well and cultured for 24 h. HPAECs were then transfected with siRNAs targeting BMPR2 or a nontargeting control and were re‐plated 24 h later in new 6‐well plates to ensure there no contribution to either the secretome or ECM from wild‐type HPAECs before knock down. Cells were cultured for 4 days, after which media was harvested and plates were decellularized. EO771 cells were then seeded in 6‐well dishes at a density of 5.0 × 105 cells per well in plates containing conditioned ECM and fresh EGM2 media, or in new plates containing conditioned media. Cell counts were collected every 24 h using Type‐M cassettes on a Moxi Z cell counter (Orflo, El Cajon, CA).

EO771 Invasion Assay

HPAECs were transfected with siRNAs targeting BMPR2 or a nontargeting control 24 h before plating on collagen‐coated, 24‐well, 8 µm pore transwell inserts at a density of 2.0 × 105 cells per insert. Cells were cultured in EGM‐2 for 32 h before treatment with 1 ng/mL of BMP9 in EGM‐2 or control media into the upper compartment of the transwell. After 16 h, the media in both the upper and lower compartments was aspirated and 4.0 × 105 CFSE (Biolegend) labeled EO771 cells, suspended in 0.5 mL EBM‐2 containing Anti‐Anti and 10% FBS, were added to the upper compartment of the transwell insert, with or without 1 ng/mL BMP9 to match the pretreatment condition. The bottom compartment of the transwell was filled with 1 mL of equivalent media containing 100 ng/mL of recombinant human Chemokine Ligand 8 (CCL8) (Peprotech). After a 16‐h incubation, the upper component of the transwell inserts were swabbed to remove unmigrated cells before fixation with 4% PFA at room temperature for 20 min. The inserts were washed twice with PBS and left to air dry before mounting with Mowiol containing 1.5 µg/mL DAPI as a nuclear stain. The number of migrated CFSE+ EO771 cells per FOV was quantified by epifluorescence across a total of four images per insert and reported as an average.

EO771 Tail Vein Engraftment Model

For short‐term engraftment studies using the tail vein injection model, female Bmpr2 EC+/+, Bmpr2 EC+/− , and Bmpr2 EC−/− mice, aged 8–12 weeks, received a lateral tail vein injection of 1.0 × 106 EO771 cells labeled with CellBrite Orange (Biotium, Fremont, CA), as per manufacturer's instructions. Mice were sacrificed 72 h after injection and lung tissue was flushed, excised, and fixed using the endpoint procedure detailed for the orthotopic model above. For the quantification of lung metastases, non‐serial 10 µm sections, separated by 100 µm of tissue, were stained with DAPI before epifluorescent imaging. The total number of metastatic events was quantified across eight lung tissue sections and averaged for a mean number of metastatic events per animal.

Statistical Analysis

Student's t‐tests were used for comparisons between two groups. Multiple comparisons were assessed by one‐way ANOVA, followed by the appropriate post‐hoc test for significance, as specified in the figure legends. Normal distribution was assumed for all statistical analyses, with the exception of the comparison of mice with or without detectable lung tumors in the orthotopic EO771 model. For this case, a Chi‐squared test was used to compare the proportion of mice with detectable tumors in each genotype. All data are reported as mean +/– standard error of mean (SEM).

Results

Endothelial Bmpr2 Loss Enhances Lung Tumor Burden in the EO771 Orthotopic Model

To determine the precise contribution of endothelial BMPR‐II signaling to breast cancer metastasis in the lung, mice bearing a heterozygous (Bmpr2 EC+/−) or homozygous (Bmpr2 EC−/−) deletion of Bmpr2 in their pulmonary endothelium were subjected to the EO771 orthotopic model, alongside unfloxed littermate controls (Bmpr2 EC+/+). Primary mammary tumors were resected from the mammary fat pad once they reached a size of 500 mm3. Following primary tumor resection, mice were maintained for an additional 28 days before sacrifice, allowing time for the seeding and growth of spontaneous lung metastases (Figure 1A). Cre expression in this model was under the control of a transgenic segment of the Alk1 promoter, limiting Bmpr2 deletion to the endothelium of specific vascular beds, including the lungs, brain and retina [30]. While some degree of Cre‐driven ZsGreen reporter expression was also detectable in the mammary endothelium (Supporting Information S1: Figure 1A,B), this did not impact the rate of primary mammary tumor growth, which was equivalent across all genotypes (Figure 1B, Supporting Information S1: Figure 1C). Primary tumor masses were also equivalent across Bmpr2 EC+/+, Bmpr2 EC+/− and Bmpr2 EC−/− mice at explant (Figure 1C), indicating that any difference in lung tumor abundance between genotypes would not be a consequence of altered mammary tumor burden.

Assessment of metastasis in the lung by whole‐lung RFP fluorescence (Figure 1D) identified no differences in the proportions of mice from each genotype that developed detectable lung metastasis (Figure 1E). However, when comparing the subset of mice from each group with detectable lung metastasis, Bmpr2 EC+/− and Bmpr2 EC−/− mice exhibited a significantly higher lung metastatic burden when compared to their Bmpr2 EC+/+ counterparts. The liver and brains of all mice were also assessed for metastasis. Unlike the large metastatic lesions observed in the lungs, metastasis in liver and brain were limited to small lesions consisting of ~20 cancer cells or fewer (Supporting Information S1: Figure 2A–D). While these small metastatic events were detectable in all mice examined, no significant differences in the number of discrete metastatic lesions were observed between Bmpr2 genotypes in livers or brains.

Endothelial Bmpr2 Loss Enhances the Vascularization of Lung Metastases

The observation of increased lung metastatic burden with endothelial Bmpr2 loss suggests an impact of this deletion on the establishment of metastatic lesions, vascularization to support their growth, EO771 proliferation, or some combination of these factors. Native ZsGreen expression in the Cre+ pulmonary endothelium was used to assess vascular density and network structure at both the periphery and the center of large (≥ 500 μm diameter) lung metastases (Figure 2A). While vascularization at the tumor border was unchanged between genotypes (Figure 2B), vascular density was significantly elevated in the center of tumors from Bmpr2 EC−/− mice relative to those from Bmpr2 EC+/+ controls (Figure 2C). Tumors from Bmpr2 EC+/− and Bmpr2 EC−/− mice exhibited a more developed vascular network, as quantified by elevated total vessel length, and increased branching relative to metastases from Bmpr2 EC+/+ littermates (Figure 2D–F). An accompanying reduction in the percent of isolated endothelial segments was observed in Bmpr2 EC+/− and Bmpr2 EC−/− mice, indicating greater endothelial connectivity in these networks (Figure 2G). Regions of active vascular sprouting contained endothelial cells that stained positive for the tip cell marker DLL4 (Figure 2H), indicating neo‐angiogenesis as the likely driver of tumor vascularization across mice of all genotypes.

EO771 Proliferation Is not Influenced by BMP9 or Endothelial BMPR‐II Loss

While our in vivo and in vitro findings identify a role for endothelial BMPR‐II signaling in regulating the vascularization and growth of EO771 metastases in the lung, these results do not rule out any direct effects of BMP9 signaling on EO771 cell proliferation, or a role for the BMPR‐II‐deficient pulmonary endothelium in promoting metastatic growth through effects on other cells in the metastatic niche. In keeping with previous reports, treatment of EO771 cells with BMP ligands, including BMP4, BMP6 and BMP9, induced canonical signaling, as measured by increased Smad1/5/9 phosphorylation (Figure 4A). When examining BMP9 responsiveness across a range of doses, pronounced Smad1/5/9 phosphorylation was only observed at concentrations ≥ 5 ng/mL (Figure 4B), well above the 300‐500 pg/mL found in the circulation [35]. Effects at this concentration have been linked to BMP9 signaling via Alk2 as an alternative type I receptor to Alk1 [25]. BMP receptor expression profiling aligned with these findings, identifying BMPR‐II (Bmpr2) as the most abundantly expressed type‐II receptor in EO771 cells, as well as high levels of Alk2 (Acvr1) and Alk3 (Bmpr1A) gene expression relative to Alk1 (Acvrl1) (Figure 4C). This type‐I receptor profile contrasted with naïve mouse lung tissue, where Alk1 was the dominant type‐I receptor (Figure 4D). Elevated Alk1 expression is likely a reflection of the high abundance of endothelial cells in the lung and is supportive of a central role for BMP9 and BMP10 in regulating pulmonary vascular structure and stability.

Despite the induction of canonical signaling in EO771 cells at elevated BMP9 concentrations, assessment of BrdU incorporation across a range of BMP9 concentrations indicated no impact of BMP9 on EO771 proliferation (Figure 4E). Long‐term EO771 proliferation was similarly unaffected by prolonged treatment with 10 ng/mL BMP9 (Figure 4F). Moreover, culturing EO771 cells in the presence of conditioned media or decellularized matrix from BMPR‐II‐silenced HPAECs had no impact on growth rate when compared to secreted products from nontargeting siRNA HPAEC controls (Figure 4G, H). Together, these findings indicate that endothelial BMPR‐II loss likely impacts the establishment or growth of lung metastatic lesions via direct effects on the vasculature, and not via the actions of BMP9 or endothelial‐derived factors on EO771 cells.

Endothelial BMPR‐II Loss Does Not Enhance EO771 Lung Engraftment

Beyond the potential effects on tumor angiogenesis, the enhanced lung metastatic burden observed in Bmpr2 EC+/− and Bmpr2 EC−/− mice may also be attributable to a pulmonary endothelium that is more permissive to the trans‐endothelial migration and metastatic seeding of circulating EO771 cells. This process was first assessed in vitro using a modified Boyden chamber‐based endothelium transmigration assay, consisting of EO771 cells seeded onto HPAEC monolayers, with or without siRNA‐mediated BMPR‐II depletion. As with the HPAEC migration studies (Figure 3), endothelial BMPR‐II depletion alone had no impact on the transmigration of EO771 cells across the endothelial monolayer in response to a CCL8 chemotactic stimulus (Figure 5A, B). However, BMPR‐II loss did eliminate the capacity of BMP9 to enhance monolayer integrity and block this EO771 transmigration. Despite this enhancement of in vitro transmigration, in vivo assessment of EO771 lung engraftment in female mice using a tail vein injection model (Figure 5C) demonstrated no impact of homozygous or heterozygous endothelial Bmpr2 deletion on the number of engraftment events at 72 h postinjection, relative to Bmpr2 EC+/+ controls (Figure 5D).

Discussion

This study provides insights into the critical role for endothelial BMPR‐II as a regulator of metastatic breast cancer neovascularization and growth in the lung. The work builds on a previous report showing similar findings in BMP9‐deficient, Gdf2 knockout mice subjected to the orthotopic EO771 model [26]. However, while the previous study focused on the contribution of BMP9 to the establishment, neovascularization, growth and metastatic potential of the primary mammary tumor, our selection of a conditional mouse model lacking Bmpr2 in the pulmonary endothelium enabled a clear delineation of the importance of endothelial BMPR‐II‐mediated signaling to the engraftment and vascularization of secondary metastases in the lung, independent of any direct actions of BMP9 on other host cell types, or the tumor itself. Beyond circumventing the embryonic lethality observed with global homozygous Bmpr2 deletion [36], the lower frequency of Cre expression in the mammary endothelium allowed for the use of primary tumors as intra‐animal controls, confirming that the enhanced lung tumor burden in Bmpr2 EC+/− and Bmpr2 EC−/− mice was not a consequence of an increased primary tumor load in these animals.

The observation of increased lung metastatic burden and metastasis‐associated angiogenesis in Bmpr2 EC+/− and Bmpr2 EC−/− mice was supported by in vitro findings demonstrating an impact of BMPR‐II loss on the capacity of BMP9 to inhibit the migration of human pulmonary endothelial cells. In particular, the impact of BMPR‐II loss on endothelial migration to a VEGF gradient aligns well with the spatially restricted nature of enhanced vascularization in the center of lung metastases from Bmpr2 EC+/− and Bmpr2 EC−/− mice, but not the border region. This finding raises the possibility of a role for differential oxygen levels on the regulation of angiogenesis by BMPR‐II‐mediated signaling. Importantly neither endothelial migration nor monolayer integrity was impacted by BMPR‐II loss in the absence of BMP9. Instead, BMPR‐II depletion eliminated the capacity of BMP9 to promote a stable and quiescent endothelium. These results are in line with previous findings demonstrating a role for BMP9 as a circulating vascular stability factor that blocks VEGF and fibroblast growth factor‐induced angiogenesis, promotes monolayer integrity, and prevents the transmigration of leukocytes from the circulation into the lungs [37]. Our results expand on this existing work by clarifying the importance of BMPR‐II to the stability of the pulmonary endothelium in the context of metastatic seeding and neovascularization.

It is noteworthy that the proportion of mice exhibiting some degree of measurable lung tumor burden was not significantly impacted by pulmonary endothelial Bmpr2 loss, and that the mean lung metastasis rate observed in our study aligned well with the 52% rate that has been reported previously for this model [38]. The result indicates that the tissue‐specific deletion of endothelial Bmpr2 in the lung did not measurably impact the vasculature of the primary mammary tumor, or its capacity to support metastasis to other organs. Despite our in vitro observations of enhanced EO771 transmigration through a BMPR‐II‐deficient HPAEC monolayer, these results did not translate over to the orthotopic or short‐term tail vein injection models, which showed no effect of endothelial Bmpr2 loss on the frequency of EO771 cell engraftment in the lungs of Bmpr2 EC+/− or Bmpr2 EC−/− mice, relative to Bmpr2 EC+/+ controls. Importantly, the number of metastatic events in the liver and brain of mice subjected to the orthotopic model were also not significantly altered across all three Bmpr2 EC genotypes, despite Cre‐mediated endothelial Bmpr2 deletion in the brains of Bmpr2 EC+/− and Bmpr2 EC−/− animals. However, the variability in the frequency of these small metastatic events, particularly in the brain, suggest that additional studies may be necessary to definitively assess the impact of Bmpr2 loss on metastasis to organs other than the lung.

When taken together, the results of this study clearly demonstrate an important role for endothelial BMPR‐II signaling in controlling the neovascularization and growth of breast cancer metastases in the lung. However, these findings are limited by uncertainty regarding the specific downstream signaling events and molecular pathways that mediate these effects. Previous work has attributed the angoiproliferative activity of BMP9 in the lung to the prolonged activation of canonical signaling and downstream canonical targets, such as Id1, when BMPR‐II is lost [30]. We have yet to confirm this prolonged activation in our in vivo models, nor have we ruled out a role for noncanonical signaling via established pathways like Notch, Wnt or p38 MAPK in these processes. BMPR‐II signaling can also induce crosstalk with conventional angiogenic mediators like VEGFA and VEGFC [28, 39]. This crosstalk can vary between vascular beds in different organs. In hepatocellular carcinoma, BMP9 signaling has been shown to promote HIF1α activation and VEGFA induced angiogenesis [28]. In contrast, recent studies in the pulmonary endothelium have instead shown that loss of BMPR‐II or BMP9 induces proangiogenic responses via disrupted Notch signaling and elevated Alk1 receptor expression, respectively [29, 40]. Future work examining the differential activation of these targets in vivo will help to provide mechanistic insights that can fuel novel therapeutic interventions that capitalize on the vascular stabilizing effects of BMPR‐II.
