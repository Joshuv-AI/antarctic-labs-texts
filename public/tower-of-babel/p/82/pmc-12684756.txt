Toward a nurse-oriented management framework for premature ovarian insufficiency: Integration of guidelines and consensus recommendations
Authors: Li Jiang, Qianjun Xia, Min Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesTo assess the methodological quality of recent clinical practice guidelines (CPGs) and consensus statements on premature ovarian insufficiency (POI) to formulate a nurse-oriented management framework, thus promoting nurses’ adherence and advancing evidence-based nursing practice.MethodsA systematic search was conducted to identify CPGs and consensus statements on POI published in English or Chinese between 2019 and 2024. The methodological quality of included CPGs was independently assessed by two authors using the Appraisal of Guidelines for Research and Evaluation II (AGREE II) instrument. Similarly, the quality of consensus statements was evaluated using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Checklist for Text and Opinion papers. Recommendations from high-quality publications were extracted and synthesized into a preliminary management framework. This framework was specifically tailored to align with the perspective and clinical context of nursing practice. The preliminary framework was subsequently refined through an expert consultation process to ensure its validity and practicality.ResultsFour CPGs and two consensus statements, all rated as “high quality”, were included in the framework. Concordance between the authors ranged from substantial to near-perfect agreement (0.79–1.0). In developing the framework, recommendations from the CPGs were identified and consolidated into three categories: management of high-risk POI populations, management of POI patients, and management of patients with POI-related complications.ConclusionsThe included CPGs and consensus statements concerning POI were all recommended for use in clinical practice. Using existing evidence, we developed a nurse-oriented management framework to bolster nurses’ adherence to the guidelines and foster evidence-based nursing practices. Further research is needed to provide evidence-based health care in this field.

Introduction

Premature ovarian insufficiency (POI) is a clinical condition characterized by loss of ovarian function, indicated by irregular menstrual cycles, amenorrhea, or oligomenorrhea in women under 40 years of age, and lasting for at least four months [1]. The condition is confirmed with two serum follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) measurements taken at least one month apart, each exceeding 25 U/L [2]. POI is relatively common, affecting 1 % of women by the age of 40 and 0.1 % of women by the age of 30 [3]. Women diagnosed with POI may face a range of physical and emotional challenges that can significantly affect their lives. These include diminished fertility or complete infertility; increased risk of cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, osteoporosis, cognitive decline, and depression; and an increased overall mortality rate [[4], [5], [6], [7]].

Nurses play a crucial role in supporting women with POI [8,9]. They are instrumental in presenting succinct, evidence-based information to improve patients’ understanding of their condition and to illuminate the treatment options available to them [10,11]. The psychological toll of POI is profound, and thus it is important for nurses to provide support as patients navigate the emotional turbulence and daily stress associated with their condition. Given the intricate nature of POI, a collaborative, multidisciplinary strategy is often necessary, with nurses working alongside specialists from endocrinology, reproductive medicine, and mental health to form a cohesive care team. Nurses often assume the essential role of coordinators, ensuring that patients receive comprehensive and coordinated medical care [12,13]. Considering POI’s chronicity, nurses also assume patient monitoring and management tasks, which include tracking the disease course and evaluating the effectiveness of treatments.

Clinical practice guidelines (CPGs) and expert-led consensus statements offer robust recommendations for optimizing patient care. They are developed through rigorous evidence synthesis: CPGs rely on systematic reviews and formal evidence grading, whereas consensus statements emerge from structured, multidisciplinary panel deliberation—often employed when high-level evidence is limited. Many healthcare organizations and research entities have invested considerable resources in crafting and regularly updating POI-specific CPGs and consensus statements. These documents provide a comprehensive set of recommendations spanning diagnostic protocols and treatment strategies to addressing chronic health effects and offering psychological support [14]. Despite their comprehensive nature, these CPGs and consensus statements are often tailored primarily for physicians and may lack the specific, actionable guidance necessary for nursing professionals. The nursing-related components within them are not consistently organized in a manner that facilitates easy application by nurses in their clinical practice. There is a need for guidance that is more attuned to the unique roles and responsibilities of nurses, ensuring that the recommendations are accessible in a nursing context.

This study assessed existing CPGs and consensus statements for POI to extract recommendations pertinent to nursing practice, then refined the findings into a comprehensive framework. The resulting framework is intended to deepen nurses’ comprehension of POI and enhance their ability to diagnose and treat the condition. Insights gained from a thorough evaluation of the strengths and limitations of current CPGs or consensus statements might contribute to the future development of nursing research and the formulation of appropriate guidelines, thereby increasing the standards of nursing practice.

Methods

Problem establishment

This study employed the PIPOST framework [15]. The letters in the framework name stand for the following: P for the “population” of women who currently have POI or are at risk of developing it; I for “intervention,” pertaining to nursing care strategies for POI; P for “professional,” specifically nurses in this study; O for “outcome,” including the prevalence of POI, menstrual cycle regularity, FSH levels, and the occurrence of complications associated with POI; S for “setting,” which includes hospitals, community centers, and homes; and T for “type of evidence,” which, in this context, are CPGs and expert consensus statements, which are documents designed to support health professionals in clinical decision-making and patient management [16].

Literature search

A thorough search was conducted across various electronic databases, including PubMed, Medline, EBSCO, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), Wanfang Data, and CQVIP. In addition, guideline repositories such as the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE), the Registered Nurses Association of Ontario (RNAO), the Trip Medical Database (Trip), and the Guidelines International Network (GIN) were explored. The search utilized a combination of subject terms and keywords, as well as incorporated a review of citations. The search period was from January 2019 to December 2024. The search queries and strategies in each database are illustrated in Appendix A (Table S1).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The CPGs and consensus statements included in the study met the following criteria: 1) the latest versions; 2) written in either English or Chinese; 3) development processes articulated and evidence-based; 4) included details such as title, development group, content, and references; and 5) encompassed nursing-oriented guidance for the management of POI, including strategies for high-risk groups, patient care, and managing POI-related complications.

Excluded from the study were repeated publications of identical documents, preliminary drafts or summaries, and directly translated versions.

Literature screening

The retrieved records were integrated into the NoteExpress software to identify and eliminate duplicate entries. Two authors conducted an initial independent screening of the literature on the basis of titles, abstracts, and keywords. Then, they engaged in a thorough review of the full texts. For each CPG and consensus statement that qualified, the authors independently extracted a comprehensive set of data, including the title, publication year, geographical region, issuing organization, version of the guideline, and its scope. Discrepancies were resolved by discussion or were judged by the third author.

Literature evaluation

Clinical practice guidelines

Two authors evaluated the methodological rigor of the selected CPGs via the Appraisal of Guidelines for Research and Evaluation II (AGREE II) tool [17]. This tool is a comprehensive 23-item checklist structured across six domains: Scope and Purpose, Stakeholder Involvement, Rigor of Development, Clarity of Presentation, Applicability, and Editorial Independence. Items are rated on a Likert scale from 1, indicating “strongly disagree,” to 7, signifying “strongly agree.” The scaled percentage score calculation formula was as follows: (obtained score − minimum possible score)/(maximum possible score − minimum possible score) × 100 %, where the obtained score was the sum of the appraisers’ scores for each item.

Drawing on contextual insights and a comprehensive literature review [16], the authors delineated a three-tiered system for evaluating and recommending CPGs. Those attaining over 60 % in five or six domains were deemed high quality, those scoring above 60 % in three or four domains were considered average quality, and those with scores above 60 % in two or fewer domains were classified as low quality. Only the recommendations from high-quality CPGs were incorporated.

Consensus statements

The methodological quality of consensus statements was appraised using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist for Text and Opinion Papers [18,19]. This six-item instrument evaluates whether 1) the source of the opinion is clearly identified, 2) the source possesses recognized expertise in the field, 3) the interests of the target population are the primary focus, 4) the stated position is derived from an analytical process and underpinned by logical reasoning, 5) existing literature is appropriately referenced, and 6) any divergence from the literature is logically defended. Each item is rated as “Yes,” “No,” “Unclear,” or “Not applicable,” culminating in a judgement of “Include,” “Exclude,” or “Seek further information.” All included consensus statements were rated as high quality. Therefore, recommendations were extracted solely from these statements.

Inter-rater reliability

The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was the key measure of consensus among the raters via the AGREE II or JBI instrument. Two-way random-effects models with absolute agreement were applied. Following a discussion to reach a consensus on how to apply the criteria, the authors independently evaluated the items [16]. SPSS version 17.0 was used for the ICC analysis.

Recommendation summary and framework development

Two authors (M.Yang & L. Jiang) extracted and summarized the recommendations, adhering to specific criteria: 1) overlapping or complementing recommendations across different sources were consolidated into a single recommendation; 2) in cases of conflicting advice, precedence was given to the most reputable source backed by the most substantial evidence quality; and 3) recommendations involving overlapping responsibilities were rephrased from a nursing perspective when the original action was beyond a nurse’s primary authority. Following this, the third author (Q. Xia) reviewed all recommendations for clinical applicability.

For example, multiple CPGs and consensus statements recommend, “Unless contraindicated, hormone replacement therapy (HRT) should be continued at least until the physiological menopause age.” However, the age range for menopause varies from 50 to 60 across the guidelines. The framework removed specific age ranges from the guidelines to resolve this inconsistency. As another example, two CPGs highlight POI-related complications that span multiple systems. We consolidated these complications and categorized them based on systems (e.g., skeletal, cardiovascular); symptom types (e.g., psychological, sexual); and specific health areas (e.g., reproductive, metabolic). In addition, the framework does not recommend an optimal HRT regimen. There are two reasons: prescribing HRT regimens is beyond the scope of nursing responsibilities, and current research on optimal HRT protocols is limited, leading to clinical variability in administration methods, dosages, and compound types. Instead, the framework underscores the key role of nurses in HRT, particularly medication education and long-term follow-up.

The synthesized recommendations were used to develop an initial nursing framework. This preliminary version was peer-reviewed by six nursing experts specializing in gynecological care. Feedback was gathered via a survey with four structured questions that assessed the experts’ agreement with the draft and willingness to adopt it, along with an open-ended question for suggestions on improvement. The authors then refined the draft based on the experts’ insights to create the final framework. The final version was returned to the experts for review and endorsement. Detailed information about the experts and the review questions can be found in Appendix A (Table S2 and S3).

The expert review process adopted a hybrid Delphi method, which included sequential written consultations and structured discussion phases. In the asynchronous independent review stage, six panelists received encrypted evaluation packages. The packages included 1) an annotated draft framework, 2) a structured questionnaire with five core assessment items, 3) summaries of clinical guideline evidence, and 4) instructions for submitting anonymous feedback within 72 h. We compiled an inventory of conflicting feedback, identifying different viewpoints through quantitative discrepancy analysis (items with inter-rater score differences of ≥2 points in preliminary ratings were considered divergent). Then, we convened a synchronous 40-min video conference with the expert panel to resolve differences. We used the following protocol for the moderated conference: 1) evidence-based presentation of disputed items (10 min); 2) real-time anonymous voting (10 min); 3) open debate on unresolved issues (10 min); and 4) formal consensus confirmation through iterative feedback refinement (10 min). This two-phase approach ensured systematic reconciliation of expert judgments while maintaining methodological rigor and procedural transparency.

Results

Literature search results

Our initial database search retrieved 1,333 records, with 432 duplicates automatically filtered out by NoteExpress. Following an initial review of titles and abstracts, 809 records were excluded because they were not relevant. We then scrutinized the full texts of 102 records that seemed to align with our criteria. Among these, 96 were excluded for multiple reasons, such as irrelevance (N = 74), being protocol drafts or CPG summaries (N = 8), and being duplicates, direct translations, or outdated versions (N = 12). Characteristics of CPGs and consensus statements

Of the six documents, four were published specifically for women with POI [14,[21], [22], [23]], one provided general recommendations on the diagnosis and intervention of menopause [20], and one focused on hormone therapy for menopausal women [24]. Countries that developed the documents included the United Kingdom (N = 2) [14,24], China (N = 2) [21,22], Germany and Austria (N = 1) [20], and a collaboration of multiple countries (N = 1) [23]. The CPGs and consensus statements were produced by medical societies and a government agency.

The summary of clinical practice guidelines and consensus statements

There are three primary categories and their subtopics of nursing recommendations for POI: 1) Management of high-risk POI populations, covering risk factors and nursing suggestions for suspected POI patients; 2) Management of confirmed POI patients, including HRT, nonhormonal treatments, psychological care, and healthy lifestyle practices; 3) Management of POI-related complications, focusing on potential complications and their long-term monitoring and management.

Appendix A (Table S4) summarizes specific recommendations from the CPGs and consensus statements we analyzed. Notably, two documents [21,22] offered recommendations across all three categories, two [14,23] covered two categories, and two [20,24] focused solely on the management of POI patients.

Framework development

Following the systematic synthesis of recommendations and integration of expert feedback, we developed a specialized framework to optimize nursing clinical care protocols for POI. In this development phase, we modified or eliminated elements that were beyond the scope of nursing responsibilities, thereby enhancing the framework’s practical applicability.

The iterative expert review process yielded strong consensus on the framework’s value. The initial peer review by six specialists confirmed the framework’s face validity and clinical relevance. Subsequently, the hybrid Delphi method produced a high level of quantitative agreement, as detailed in Appendix A (Table S5). Finally, the structured video conference successfully resolved all remaining divergent items, leading to the formal consensus that validated the finalized framework (Fig. 2). Discussion

This study systematically searched for CPGs and consensus statements pertaining to the nursing care of POI patients and evaluated their quality via the AGREE II and JBI instruments. In addition, the authors devised a nurse-oriented management framework to help nurses assess and educate POI patients and explain treatment options.

Although the included CPGs and consensus statements were of relatively high quality, there still remains room for improvement. The lowest score was for applicability (53.1 %), like the findings of some previous studies [25,26]. This indicates that many CPGs lack practical suggestions for implementing the guidelines. In particular, item 20 of the AGREE II tool required that the CPGs consider the potential resource implications (e.g., specialized staff, new equipment, expensive drug treatment) of applying the recommendations. However, most CPGs do not discuss these implications. We identified a need for the experts who formulate CPGs to anticipate obstacles and provide practical solutions for implementing guidelines at the individual or organizational level in different environments [27].

Our framework identified key risk factors linked to POI, including chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and infectious etiology. The selection of these factors was initially informed by a clinical expert consensus [21], and their relevance was subsequently strengthened by a retrospective analysis of patient cohorts, which also aligned with findings from existing epidemiological studies [28,29]. Nurses play a critical role in helping patients proactively identify and manage these risks. For example, in cases where exposure to chemotherapy—a high-risk factor for ovarian damage—is unavoidable, nurses should implement targeted interventions as outlined in the framework: 1) provide pre-chemotherapy counseling on fertility preservation options (e.g., oocyte cryopreservation), 2) monitor patients for early signs of ovarian insufficiency (e.g., menstrual irregularities, elevated FSH levels), and 3) coordinate with oncologists and reproductive specialists to integrate protective strategies. This structured approach is designed to ensure timely risk mitigation and align with the framework’s emphasis on evidence-based, interdisciplinary management to improve clinical outcomes.

Because POI can have multiple causes and its pathogenesis is not well understood, challenges exist in developing targeted therapies. HRT is an established cornerstone of POI management, as robust evidence has demonstrated its efficacy in alleviating menopausal symptoms and reducing osteoporosis-related morbidity [30]. To optimize long-term health outcomes, current guidelines recommend continuing HRT until patients reach the median age of natural menopause. Nurses must emphasize comprehensive patient education regarding the benefits of HRT, including cardiovascular risk reduction through endothelial function improvement, osteoprotective effects via bone mineral density preservation, maintenance of secondary sexual characteristics, enhancement of genitourinary health, and support for achieving peak bone mass in peripubertal cases. However, while HRT remains the first-line treatment for POI patients under 40 years without contraindications, its clinical application requires rigorous risk-benefit evaluation in breast cancer survivors and other high-risk populations. Specifically, HRT may elevate oncological risks through estrogen receptor-mediated pathways [31]. Thus, nurses can play a pivotal role in educating patients and helping them make decisions based on individual cancer risk, pharmacogenomic considerations, and nonhormonal therapeutic alternatives. To ensure scientifically sound and safe therapeutic decisions, nurses could provide evidence-based clarification of therapeutic misconceptions, interactive risk communication using decision aids, and longitudinal monitoring of treatment adherence and potential complications.

Despite a scarcity of research and a lack of consensus on the benefits of nonhormonal treatments for POI patients, alternative therapies such as auricular point sticking [32] might be valuable in easing estrogen deficiency symptoms for those unable to undergo HRT. Assessing the suitability of a particular treatment for a patient is not the responsibility of a nurse. Nurses, however, play a crucial role in ensuring that patients follow medical advice and maintain ongoing follow-up, akin to the approach taken with HRT.

Previous studies have shown that women with POI are more likely to experience depressive symptoms than women of similar age without POI [33,34]. Consequently, psychological care to mitigate anxiety and depression has been strongly recommended in clinical guidelines despite limited evidence quality. Nurses are central to this endeavor. As healthcare professionals who frequently interact with patients, they are uniquely equipped to observe and assess psychological needs with timeliness and acuity. Through education and communication, nurses can improve patients’ understanding of their condition, which in turn can alleviate apprehension and uncertainty. Moreover, nurses are adept at marshaling resources for psychological support and intervention, including but not limited to counseling, support groups, and pharmacological therapies. Through these approaches, nurses can not only ameliorate the psychological health of patients but also foster their active engagement and self-management, thereby increasing their self-efficacy and resilience [35,36].

Lifestyle factors such as obesity, smoking, alcohol consumption, and unbalanced nutrition are known contributors to accelerated ovarian follicular depletion and premature menopause [37,38]. Despite limited evidence quality, lifestyle modification remains strongly recommended in clinical guidelines due to its cost-effectiveness and safety profile. Our framework prioritizes lifestyle modification through structured nurse-led interventions tailored to outpatient settings. For example, nurses monitor bone mineral density in POI patients via dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry scheduling and collaborate with radiologists to interpret results. They educate patients on self-monitoring tools (e.g., home blood pressure cuffs, digital scales) and use standardized risk-assessment templates to track trends in weight and cardiovascular markers during follow-up visits. To manage complications such as osteoporosis, nurses implement evidence-based protocols, including calcium/vitamin D supplementation, counseling, and referral to physical therapists for strength training, while coordinating with endocrinologists for pharmacological interventions if needed. This proactive, protocol-driven approach ensures continuity of care and early complication mitigation, aligning with the framework’s emphasis on interdisciplinary collaboration and patient empowerment.

POI is associated with many potential complications; however, there is a scarcity of detailed descriptions regarding characteristics such as incidence rate, severity, and impact scope in patients with POI. In addition, there is a frequent discrepancy between complications and the recommendations provided, resulting in a lack of standardized protocols for their management. Consequently, the content of our research framework on this topic is somewhat preliminary and awaits further refinement and supplementation by future studies. In response to these gaps, Kiriakova et al. and a previous study from our research team have explored POI guidelines, particularly those focused on potential bone health issues and cardiovascular diseases in POI patients, and have proposed a series of targeted management strategies [26,39]. However, the implementation of these strategies necessitates further clinical validation and patient education to ensure their effectiveness and patient adherence. In this context, nurses play a vital role in regularly monitoring changes in patients’ conditions, engaging in relevant research, and providing ongoing care and support to ensure long-term health management for patients with POI.

Limitations

First, limiting the literature review’s scope to guidelines written in English or Chinese may have affected the generalizability of results and introduced sampling bias. Second, while the AGREE II tool effectively evaluated the methodological quality of the guidelines, it did not assess content or reporting quality, which could have influenced our findings. Third, although two researchers independently assessed the methodological quality, subjective bias in the evaluation cannot be entirely ruled out. However, extensive discussions among all authors were held to address any discrepancies, leading to a strong inter-rater agreement that enhanced the reliability of our conclusions.

Clinical implication

This nurse-oriented management framework empowers nursing professionals by providing a clear protocol for the management of POI. It facilitates a structured approach to patient education, psychosocial support, symptom management, and long-term follow-up, thereby strengthening the nursing role in gynecologic care and potentially improving health outcomes and quality of life for women with this condition.

Conclusion

After conducting an extensive literature search, evaluating literature, and summarizing recommendations, we crafted a nurse-oriented management framework with a defined execution plan for promoting evidence-based nursing practices and enhancing nurses’ compliance. The overall methodological quality of CPGs was mostly high to moderate, and all were recommended for clinical practice. However, our findings also highlight the need to improve the practical applicability of current guidelines for nurses. The identified lack of stakeholder involvement, particularly from the nursing community, suggests that future guidelines should prioritize more inclusive development processes to ensure they meet the needs of all relevant parties. In the future, the integration of this nurse-oriented management framework into routine nursing practice is recommended to standardize and improve care for women with POI. Furthermore, high-caliber guidelines should be complemented by extensive, multicenter clinical studies to provide more robust and compelling data for the clinical management of POI.
