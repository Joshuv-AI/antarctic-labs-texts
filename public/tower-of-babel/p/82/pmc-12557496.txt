Iron limitation differentially affects viral replication in key marine microbes
Authors: Charmaine C. M. Yung, Rachel L. Kelly, Kathryn M. Kauffman, Brady R. Cunningham, Amy E. Zimmerman, Alexandra Z. Worden, Seth G. John
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Viral lysis accounts for much of microbial mortality in the ocean, and iron (Fe) is a critical micronutrient that can limit phytoplankton growth. However, interactions between Fe nutrition of microbes, including both heterotrophic bacteria and phytoplankton, and viral lysis are not well known. Here, we present viral infection dynamics under Fe-limited and Fe-replete conditions for isolates of three types of marine picoplankton, the photosynthetic picoeukaryote Ostreococcus, the cyanobacterium Synechococcus, and the heterotrophic bacterium Vibrio. Iron limitation of Ostreococcus resulted in slowed growth and reduced viral burst sizes; this is similar to prior results from studies of larger eukaryotic phytoplankton, where reduced viral replication under Fe limitation is attributed to the viral reliance on host metabolism and replication machinery. Fe limitation of one Vibrio impacted dynamics of its virus similarly, lengthening the latent period before infected cells burst to release new viruses, and reducing the number of infective particles released upon lysis. In contrast, for another Vibrio isolate, Fe limitation had no discernible effect on replication of its virus. Furthermore, dynamics of three cyanophages that infect the same Synechococcus isolate were not affected by Fe limitation of the host, either in terms of latent period or burst size. The results show that some marine viruses, particularly cyanophages, can replicate efficiently even when host growth is compromised. These findings have implications for marine ecology and carbon cycling in Fe-limited regions of the global ocean.

Phytoplankton and heterotrophic bacteria play crucial roles in ocean ecosystems, with phytoplankton serving as the base of marine food chains and sequestering carbon, while heterotrophs drive nutrient cycling and remineralization. Abundance of these microbes is controlled “bottom–up” by nutrient availability and “top–down” by predation and viral infection (1). Because viruses rely on host metabolism and machinery for replication (2–4), top–down and bottom–up processes can be intertwined. For example, viral replication has been shown to be compromised in nitrogen or phosphate-limited phytoplankton cultures (5, 6). Iron is an important micronutrient for marine microbes, limiting productivity in ~30% of the ocean (7, 8). Iron is necessary for various metabolic processes including photosynthesis, respiratory electron transport, and DNA replication. Iron limitation of host phytoplankton has been associated with decreased viral infectivity for a few eukaryotes in culture and nature (9, 10). Iron limitation may therefore affect ocean microbes (including unicellular eukaryotes) directly by compromising their metabolism and indirectly by compromising the ability of infecting viruses to replicate using host machinery (11, 12).

Here, we examine viral infection dynamics in microbes that co-occur in an ocean region known to undergo iron (Fe) limitation (8). Experiments were performed under conditions of Fe sufficiency and Fe limitation in isolates of Ostreococcus, a photosynthetic picoeukaryote that is highly abundant in mesotrophic environments, Synechococcus, a ubiquitous marine cyanobacterium, and Vibrio, heterotrophic bacteria often found in coastal environments. These host isolates represent taxa with different metabolic strategies for resource acquisition (13). However, interactions between host Fe limitation and viral infection dynamics remain unknown for these broadly distributed microbes.

Results

We tested Fe-replete and Fe-limited cultures of microbes we found to co-occur (Dataset S1) in the periodically Fe-limited eastern North Pacific Ocean (ENP) (8). For all experiments, Fe-replete and -deplete conditions were, respectively, characterized by Fe concentrations at which additional Fe did not lead to increased growth rate or where growth rates decreased. The prasinophyte Ostreococcus lucimarinus was infected with prasinoviruses OLV1 and OLV7, isolated from the ENP using the same strain as studied herein (CCE9901, hereafter Ostreococcus) (14). Reduced iron availability resulted in 43% lower Ostreococcus growth rates after acclimation relative to the replete treatment (Table 1). Viruses were added to Fe-limited and Fe-replete cultures at initial multiplicities of infection (MOI) near 0.9 and 0.4 for OLV1 and OLV7 experiments, respectively. Viral infection occurred, exhibiting increased free viral particles after a latent period (Fig. 1), and a corresponding decrease in the concentration of living cells. The dynamics of viral infection differed significantly in Fe-replete versus Fe-limited treatments. Specifically, the lytic cycle progressed faster in Fe-replete cultures, as evidenced by a shorter latent period, increased burst size, a larger increase in viral particle abundances, and a lower abundance of living cells at experiment termination.

Experiments were also performed with Vibrio splendidus strain 10N.222.46.C2 (aka strain V36, hereafter referred to as V36) and another species, Vibrio sp. 10N.261.52.C3 (aka strain V40, hereafter V40). V36 was infected with a podovirus, and V40 with a myovirus (Fig. 1 and Table 1). With an MOI of 0.1, and dilution of Vibrio cells after the initial infection step, these ‘one-step’ experiments tracked a single cycle of infection, replication, and subsequent cell lysis. Iron limitation reduced growth rates by 35 and 13% for V36 and V40, respectively, compared to Fe-replete cultures. The latent period of the podovirus for V36 was not affected by Fe limitation and was minimally decreased under low-Fe conditions for the myovirus infecting V40. Burst size was not different at the two Fe concentrations tested for V36 (18 ± 3 in Fe-replete media and 17 ± 1 in low-Fe media), despite host growth reduction at the lower Fe concentration. In V40, burst size decreased significantly from 31 ± 6 to 12 ± 2 under Fe-replete and Fe-limited conditions, respectively.

Finally, experiments were performed for Synechococcus sp. WH7803 (hereafter Synechococcus) with cyanophages Syn9, S-PM2, and S-SM1, using one-step methodology and 0.1 MOI. Fe-limited cultures showed growth rate reductions between 45% and 55% relative to Fe-replete cultures. Each cyanophage had a distinct latent period and their burst sizes differed (Table 1). When each cyanophage encountered the host under low-Fe versus high-Fe growth, the latent period length did not differ significantly. Burst sizes were also similar for each cyanophage under the two host-Fe conditions tested.

Discussion

Viral replication is expected to be compromised when host metabolism is slowed (1–3), such as under iron-limiting conditions. The reduction in virus replication efficiency under Fe limitation observed for Ostreococcus (Table 1) corresponds with results from the larger prasinophyte Micromonas and the more distant, much larger phytoplankton Phaeocystis and Chaetoceros (9, 10). This result is consistent with the tendency for viral replication to slow down when host metabolism is impaired by inefficient enzyme function. For example, Fe deficiencies in humans can reduce viral replication in hosts (15). Here, this occurred for one Vibrio—indicating marine bacteriophages can be similarly restricted. However, our work demonstrates that for other picoplankton, in this case isolates of the cyanobacterium Synechococcus and another Vibrio, host Fe limitation does not impact latent period or burst size (Table 1).

These results indicate that some viruses can replicate efficiently despite compromised host metabolism, in a manner seemingly independent of host-growth rate. The mechanisms by which low Fe limits host growth are diverse, because Fe is involved in many essential metabolic processes. Yet, diminished growth rate is an unambiguous sign of compromised cell metabolism, and it is therefore remarkable that host Fe limitation does not hinder virion production compared to cells that have sufficient Fe and higher growth rates.

Viral lysis is a major force of marine microbial mortality, and thus, our findings may have important ramifications for ocean ecology and carbon cycling. Studies of marine viruses as a collective have indicated that ocean Fe concentrations alter infection dynamics (16–18), but without delineating opposing responses by different host–virus pairs demonstrated here. Moreover, our results from Synechococcus sp. WH7803 and paired cyanophage, and trends reported for cyanobacterial infection in the low-Fe sub-Antarctic South Pacific Ocean (19), suggest similar patterns. The possibility that some viruses are capable of replicating efficiently within Fe-limited hosts, while others are not, has broad implications for microbial turnover and how we model host–virus interactions across ocean nutrient regimes.

Materials and Methods

Microbes were cultured in their preferred media, with bioavailable Fe concentrations buffered by EDTA (Table 1). Viruses were propagated in Fe-limited cultures to minimize Fe carryover. Similar methods were used for each host–virus pair when possible, though unavoidable differences in how species are cultivated, and how viruses can be propagated and enumerated, led to methodological variations. Iron concentrations were chosen to span a narrow range between Fe-replete conditions (concentrations above which additional Fe did cause increased growth rate) and Fe-limited conditions, characterized by significantly lower growth rates compared to Fe-replete conditions.
