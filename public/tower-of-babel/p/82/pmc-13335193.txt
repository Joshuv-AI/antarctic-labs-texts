Biomass – derived N-doped carbon-supported CuMnO2 for efficient bisphenol A degradation via visible light – activated peroxymonosulfate: synergistic radical and non-radical pathways
Authors: Nguyen Tien Hoang, Nguyen Quynh Anh Ngo, Thi Thao Minh, Dinh Nhi Bui, Hien Y Hoang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bisphenol A (BPA) is an endocrine-disrupting contaminant frequently detected in natural and engineered waters. Visible-light-driven peroxymonosulfate (PMS) activation offers a sustainable and efficient route for BPA abatement. Herein, a novel biomass-derived heterogeneous catalyst, crednerite CuMnO2 anchored on N-doped rice-straw-lignin-derived carbon (RS-LC(N)), was successfully developed for BPA degradation via visible-light/PMS activation. The catalyst exhibited outstanding kinetics at neutral pH, achieving a pseudo-first-order rate constant (kobs) of 0.1989 min−1 under optimized conditions (10 mg L−1 BPA, 1.0 mM PMS, and a catalyst dose of 1.0 g L−1). Radical scavenging experiments revealed a dominant radical pathway (SO4˙−/˙OH), accompanied by a complementary non-radical contribution involving singlet oxygen (1O2) and interfacial electron-transfer mechanisms (ETM). LC-MS/MS (liquid chromatography – tandem mass spectrometry) analysis identified BPA transformation products consistent with two major degradation routes, including radical-mediated hydroxylation/ring cleavage and non-radical oxidation via1O2/ETM, ultimately leading to mineralization. Structural characterization indicated that increasing CuMnO2 loading moderately reduced the specific surface area while maintaining favorable mass transfer and adsorption characteristics at neutral pH. The catalyst exhibited good tolerance toward complex water matrices, with decreasing kobs observed from deionized water to natural waters, treated wastewater, and brackish water, in agreement with increasing ionic strength and dissolved organic matter. Excellent stability was demonstrated over five consecutive cycles, with negligible morphological or phase changes and minimal activity loss. Metal leaching remained at the low 10−2% level and further decreased with repeated use, highlighting the environmental robustness of the catalyst.

Introduction

BPA is a ubiquitous endocrine-disrupting contaminant widely detected in wastewater, surface water, and even drinking water, originating mainly from polycarbonate/epoxy production and consumer product lifecycles. Health concerns have intensified following the European Food Safety Authority lowering the tolerable daily intake (TDI) to 0.2 ng kg−1 day−1. Recent surveys further confirm the occurrence of BPA and related compounds across multiple environmental matrices, reinforcing the urgency of effective and sustainable treatment technologies.1–3 Advances in BPA sensing and environmental analytics also reveal persistent exposure at trace levels, implying that treatment systems must operate efficiently under realistic, low-concentration and matrix-rich conditions.4–6

Conventional advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) based on hydroxyl radicals (˙OH), such as UV/H2O2 and ozonation, have been extensively studied but are restricted by high energy demand and narrow optimal pH ranges. In contrast, peroxymonosulfate-based AOPs (PMS-AOPs) stand out because PMS activation can proceed via both radical (SO4˙−/˙OH) and non-radical pathways, such as singlet oxygen (1O2) and electron-transfer mechanisms (ETM), depending on catalyst properties and reaction conditions.7–9

Among metal oxides, Cu–Mn mixed oxides, particularly crednerite CuMnO2, have demonstrated high PMS activation efficiency toward phenolic and bisphenolic compounds, with many systems showing dominant 1O2 and clear ETM contributions.10,11 The synergistic redox cycling of Cu2+/Cu+ and Mn4+/Mn3+, together with oxygen vacancies and exposed crystal facets, strongly influences PMS activation efficiency and mechanistic pathways.12 In addition, CuMnO2 is a narrow-bandgap p-type semiconductor responsive to visible light, enabling photo-assisted PMS activation via photogenerated electron transfer, an aspect often overlooked when only heterojunction effects are considered.13

Biomass-derived carbon supports are essential for translating mechanistic insights into environmentally and economically viable systems. Lignin, an abundant aromatic polymer from agricultural residues, can be converted into hierarchically porous carbon with high π-electron density and readily N-doped to enhance conductivity, hydrophilicity, and metal anchoring.14 Green organosolv processing combined with chemical activation (KOH/KHCO3) enables the fabrication of rice-straw-lignin-derived carbons, with KHCO3 shown to be particularly effective for producing N-doped carbons from rice straw.15–17 On the application side, increasing evidence suggests that N-doped carbon promotes PMS activation via non-radical pathways, especially 1O2/ETM, by facilitating interfacial electron transfer through graphitic-N sites.18,19 Recent trends further highlight hybridization of lignin-derived carbons with visible-light-active semiconductors to exploit photogenerated charge carriers and non-radical PMS activation.20,21

In practical water treatment, PMS-based systems must tolerate complex water matrices. Bicarbonate and carbonate ions (HCO3−/CO32−) can scavenge radicals to form less reactive carbonate radicals (CO3˙−), while chloride ions (Cl−) may either favor non-radical pathways or induce reactive chlorine or bromine species, depending on conditions.22 Notably, PMS has been reported to effectively degrade BPA via non-radical pathways even under high chloride concentrations, demonstrating resistance to salinity.23 Reliable mechanistic elucidation therefore requires multimodal evidence, including selective quenchers, EPR spectroscopy, and complementary photophysical and electrochemical analyses, to avoid exaggerated assignment of 1O2 and to clarify the role of ETM.9,24,25

On this basis, we propose a biomass-based heterogeneous system by anchoring CuMnO2 onto RS-LC(N) to activate PMS under visible light at near-neutral pH for efficient BPA degradation. The catalyst is designed to enrich BPA within hierarchical pores, provide multivalent Cu/Mn centers and oxygen vacancies to regulate SO4˙−/˙OH and 1O2/electron-transfer pathways, and enhance PMS activation through photogenerated electron harvesting. This study aims to quantitatively elucidate radical versus non-radical contributions under realistic water matrices, optimize CuMnO2 loading and N-doping levels, minimize Cu/Mn leaching, and systematically evaluate BPA degradation pathways and byproduct safety, thereby advancing practical PMS-based treatment strategies for BPA-contaminated waters.

Materials and methods

Preparation of N-doped rice-straw-lignin carbon (RS-LC(N))

Rice straw lignin (RS-lignin), obtained as described previously, was used as the carbon precursor. Briefly, chopped rice straw was mildly demineralized using dilute HCl (0.1–1.0 M) at 60 °C for 1–2 h (solid-to-liquid ratio ≈ 1 : 10) to remove alkali and alkaline earth metals and silica, thereby improving the thermal behavior of the precursor.26 The solid was washed to near-neutral pH, dried at 60 °C overnight, ground, and sieved (<200 mesh).

Pre-carbonization was conducted in a tubular furnace under N2 flow (300 mL min−1) by heating at 5 °C min−1 to 500 °C and holding for 60 min, yielding RS-char. Chemical activation was performed using KHCO3 as a green activating agent. KHCO3 was dissolved in a minimal amount of water and thoroughly mixed with RS-char at a weight ratio of 3 : 1 (KHCO3 : RS-char). After drying at 80 °C, the mixture was heated under N2 (300 mL min−1) at 5 °C min−1 to 820 °C (effective range 800–850 °C) and held for 60 min. The activated carbon was washed sequentially with hot deionized (DI) water and dilute HCl (0.1 M) to remove residual inorganic salts, followed by repeated DI water washing until the conductivity of the filtrate was below 5 µS cm−1. The obtained RS-LC was dried at 80 °C for 12 h.

Nitrogen doping was performed via an ex situ thermal treatment using urea and melamine as N precursors. RS-LC was mixed with a urea/melamine mixture (1 : 1 wt), with a total (urea + melamine)-to-carbon ratio of 8 : 1. A small amount of EtOH/H2O (1 : 1, ∼10 mL g−1 carbon) was added to ensure homogeneous mixing. After drying at 80 °C, the mixture was thermally treated under N2 (300 mL min−1) by heating to 350 °C at 3 °C min−1 (30 min hold) to anchor nitrogen species, followed by heating to 850 °C at 5 °C min−1 and holding for 60 min. The resulting material was gently washed with EtOH/DI water, dried at 80 °C, ground, and sieved (<200 mesh) to obtain RS-LC(N). The selected N precursors and thermal window (800–900 °C) favor pyridinic- and graphitic-N species, consistent with previous studies on lignin-derived N-doped carbons.27,28

Anchoring of CuMnO2 on RS-LC(N)

CuMnO2 nanoparticles were anchored onto RS-LC(N) via a low-temperature hydrothermal deposition-crystallization route to obtain a well-dispersed crednerite phase strongly coupled with the carbon support. Typically, RS-LC(N) (200–500 mg) was dispersed in 100–200 mL of DI water or an EtOH/H2O (1 : 1) mixture and ultrasonicated for 20–30 min to ensure uniform wetting and exposure of N-doped sites and surface defects, which serve as anchoring centers for metal oxides.29

Aqueous precursor solutions of Cu(NO3)2·3H2O and Mn(NO3)2·4H2O (0.05–0.10 M, Cu : Mn molar ratio = 1 : 1) were slowly added to the RS-LC(N) suspension under continuous stirring. The pH was adjusted to approximately 12 using NaOH (1–2 M), and the mixture was stirred for 30 min to promote uniform precipitation of Cu/Mn hydroxide intermediates onto the carbon surface. The suspension was then transferred to a Teflon-lined autoclave and hydrothermally treated at 80–120 °C for 12–24 h.

This synthesis protocol follows reported pathways for forming nanosized crednerite CuMnO2via coupled redox transformation of Cu2+/Mn2+ to Cu+/Mn3+ under alkaline hydrothermal conditions, yielding well-crystallized CuMnO2 at relatively low temperatures.30,31 After cooling to room temperature, the solid was collected by filtration, washed repeatedly with DI water and ethanol to remove residual alkali and nitrate ions, and dried at 60–80 °C overnight.

To enhance crystallinity and interfacial bonding between CuMnO2 and the carbon support, the hydrothermal product was further annealed at 350–380 °C for 2 h under N2 with a heating rate of 2 °C min−1. The final CuMnO2 loading was typically controlled within 5–15 wt% (based on RS-LC(N)) by adjusting precursor concentration and carbon mass, and was quantified by ICP-OES. The resulting composites were denoted as RS-LC(N)/CuMnO2-x, where x represents the weight percentage of CuMnO2. A schematic of the synthesis procedure is provided in Fig. S1. For comparison or scale-up considerations, microwave-assisted hydrothermal synthesis can also be employed as an alternative route.32

Material characterization

X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were collected on a Bruker D8 Advance diffractometer (Cu Kα, λ = 1.5406 Å, 40 kV) over 2θ = 10–80° with a step size of 0.02–0.03° and a scan rate of 1–3° min−1. Samples were dried (60–80 °C), finely ground, and evenly spread on a flat holder. For clarity, raw data were background-subtracted and mildly smoothed (Savitzky–Golay) without altering peak positions or relative intensities. Phase identification was performed by comparison with standard reference cards.

Raman spectra were recorded using a Horiba LabRAM HR Evolution system (1800 lines mm−1 grating; ∼1.0 cm−1 resolution) with a 532 nm laser at low power (<1–2 mW) to avoid thermal effects. Spectra were collected in the ranges of 100–800 cm−1 (metal – oxygen vibrations) and 1200–1700 cm−1 (carbon D/G bands), averaging ≥3 scans per sample. Polynomial baseline correction was applied prior to calculating the ID/IG ratio from the D (∼1350 cm−1) and G (∼1580 cm−1) bands.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was conducted on a Thermo Scientific K-Alpha spectrometer (base pressure < 1.0 × 10−9 mbar, spot size ∼400 µm). Survey spectra were acquired at 100 eV pass energy, while high-resolution spectra (C 1s, N 1s, O 1s, Cu 2p, Mn 2p) were recorded at 20–40 eV. Binding energies were calibrated to C 1s = 284.8 eV; Shirley background subtraction and pseudo-Voigt fitting were applied, and elemental compositions were quantified using Scofield sensitivity factors. For stability evaluation, post-reaction samples were thoroughly washed, dried, and analyzed under identical conditions.

UV-visible diffuse reflectance spectra (UV-vis DRS) were obtained using a Shimadzu UV-2600 spectrophotometer equipped with an integrating sphere (BaSO4/Spectralon reference) over 200–800 nm. Absorption properties were analyzed using the Kubelka–Munk function F(R).

Morphology and elemental distribution were examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, JEOL JSM-IT200) coupled with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX). Samples were Pt-coated (∼5 nm) and imaged at an accelerating voltage of 10 kV and a working distance of 8–10 mm.

Copper and manganese contents were quantified by ICP-OES (Agilent 5110) using external calibration, with detection limits of ∼2 µg L−1 for Cu and ∼3 µg L−1 for Mn. For loading determination, solids were digested in aqua regia (HCl : HNO3 = 3 : 1, v/v) at 95 °C for 3 h. In cycling tests, reaction filtrates (0.22 µm) were acidified with 1% HNO3 prior to ICP-OES analysis to calculate metal leaching percentages.

Photocatalytic degradation of BPA

Visible-light-driven PMS activation (photo-PMS) experiments were conducted in a 300 mL water-jacketed glass reactor (working volume 250 mL) under continuous stirring (600 rpm) at 25 ± 2 °C. A visible LED source (420 ± 10 nm) was positioned 5–7 cm above the solution surface, providing an irradiance of 20 mW cm−2, as measured by a calibrated radiometer (Thorlabs PM100D with S120VC sensor), corresponding to a photon flux of ∼702 µmol m−2 s−1. BPA stock solution (1 g L−1) was prepared in methanol (MeOH) and diluted to the desired concentration. PMS (Oxone®, KHSO5-based) was used as the oxidant.

Unless otherwise stated, experiments were performed under standard conditions: [BPA]0 = 10 mg L−1, pH 7.0, [PMS] = 1.0 mM, catalyst loading = 1.0 g L−1, and visible-light irradiation (420 ± 10 nm). Prior to illumination, the catalyst was dispersed in the BPA solution and stirred in the dark for 30 min to establish adsorption–desorption equilibrium. The reaction was initiated by PMS addition and light irradiation (t = 0). Aliquots (1–2 mL) were withdrawn at set intervals and immediately quenched either by MeOH (10% v/v) or excess Na2S2O3, followed by filtration (0.22 µm) when necessary.33 BPA degradation kinetics were analyzed using a pseudo-first-order model, ln(C/C0) versus time, to obtain the apparent rate constant (kobs). Control experiments included PMS + light (no catalyst), catalyst + light (no PMS), and catalyst + PMS in the dark.

BPA concentrations were quantified by HPLC-UV (C18 column, 30 °C, 1.0 mL min−1) using an ACN/H2O (60/40, v/v) mobile phase containing 0.1% formic acid, with detection at 276–278 nm. Calibration curves were established over 0.05–20 mg L−1. For enhanced sensitivity and matrix-rich samples, LC-MS/MS (ESI, negative mode, MRM) was employed to quantify BPA and identify transformation products. Mineralization was assessed by TOC and DOC measurements following APHA Method 5310.

Reactive species were identified using radical quenching experiments in combination with electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy under optimal conditions. MeOH (1 M), tert-butanol (0.2 M), p-benzoquinone (1 mM), and furfuryl alcohol (FFA) (10 mM) were used to probe SO4˙−/˙OH, ˙OH, O2˙−, and 1O2, respectively. EPR spectra were recorded on a Bruker EMXplus (X-band) spectrometer using DMPO (0.1 M) for ˙OH/SO4˙− and TEMP (0.05 M) for 1O2 trapping after 5 min of illumination. Potential artefacts associated with PMS-spin trap interactions were mitigated by cross-validation with FFA consumption and kinetic analysis. PMS utilization was monitored by iodometric titration, measuring I3− absorbance at 352 nm.

Matrix effects were evaluated using real water samples (tap, river, lake, and WWTP effluent), which were filtered (0.45 µm) and stored at 4 °C prior to use. Synthetic brackish water was prepared by adding NaCl to achieve a salinity of 5.84 g L−1. Key physicochemical parameters are summarized in Table S3.

Results and discussion

Characteristics of materials

The structural, chemical, and optical properties of RS-LC, RS-LC(N), pristine CuMnO2, and RS-LC(N)/CuMnO2 composites were systematically characterized by XRD, Raman spectroscopy, XPS, UV-Vis DRS, and N2 adsorption–desorption analyses, yielding mutually consistent results.

XRD patterns (Fig. 1A) confirm that both pristine CuMnO2 and the composite catalysts exhibit the characteristic reflections of monoclinic crednerite CuMnO2 (space group C2/m), in agreement with the standard reference (JCPDS/PDF, No. 50-0860).34 This is consistent with previous reports showing that CuMnO2 stabilizes in the crednerite phase under inert atmospheres at elevated temperatures (∼900 °C).35 Comparison with CuMnO2 synthesized via ion-exchange and sol–gel/solid-state routes reveals identical peak positions and the well-known spinel-crednerite phase transition behavior under oxidizing/reducing conditions, confirming the phase purity of the present materials.13 In the composites, increasing CuMnO2 loading leads to a proportional increase in oxide peak intensity, while the broad carbon background remains visible, indicating the coexistence of both phases, as reported for CuMnO2/rGO systems.36

(A) XRD patterns, (B) Raman spectra, (C) XPS survey spectra, (D) high-resolution C 1s XPS spectra, (E) high-resolution O 1s XPS spectra, (F) UV-vis diffuse reflectance spectra.

Raman spectra (Fig. 1B) display the typical D (∼1350 cm−1) and G (∼1580 cm−1) bands of carbon in the 1200–1700 cm−1 region. The ID/IG ratio increases from 1.203 (RS-LC) to 1.285 (RS-LC(N)) and further to 1.352–1.562 with increasing CuMnO2 loading, indicating higher defect density and reduced sp2 domain size, consistent with the Ferrari-Robertson model for disordered carbon.37 In addition, distinct metal–oxygen vibration bands appear in the 400–700 cm−1 region only for CuMnO2-containing samples, matching reported Raman features of crednerite CuMnO2.38 Similar ID/IG enhancement upon oxide-carbon coupling has been observed in CuMnO2-rGO and MnO2-rGO systems, supporting the role of interfacial defects in catalytic enhancement.39

XPS analysis (Fig. 1C–D and S2A–C) confirms the presence of C, N, O, Cu, and Mn species. The N 1 s region consists of pyridinic N (∼398–399 eV), pyrrolic/graphitic N (∼399.5–400.5 eV), and oxidized N (∼401–402 eV), consistent with established assignments for N-doped carbons.40–42 The Cu 2p3/2 peak at ∼932.8–934 eV with shake-up satellites (940–945 eV) indicates mixed Cu+/Cu2+ states,43 while Mn 2p3/2 signals at ∼641.8–642.7 eV with multiplet splitting at ∼645–647 eV confirm the coexistence of Mn3+/Mn4+ species.43,44 O 1s spectra comprise lattice oxygen (OL, ∼529.8–530.2 eV), surface hydroxyl/defect oxygen (∼531–532.5 eV), and a weaker adsorbed oxygen component (∼533 eV) (Fig. 1E), following recent recommendations for catalytic oxides.45 The relative increase in O lattice (OL) at higher CuMnO2 loadings suggests improved oxide crystallinity, whereas enhanced defect-related oxygen at low-to-medium loadings implies the generation of catalytically active surface sites.

UV-vis DRS spectra (Fig. 1F) reveal a pronounced enhancement in visible-light absorption after incorporation of CuMnO2 into the carbon matrix. At higher loadings, a slight bathochromic shift of the absorption edge is observed, which can be attributed to defect-induced sub-bandgap states and electronic interactions at the oxide-carbon interface. Bandgap estimation from multiphase UV-vis DRS must be interpreted with caution, as Tauc analysis is highly sensitive to the fitting range and assumed transition type. Accordingly, DRS is employed here solely as qualitative evidence of optical absorption behavior rather than for quantitative bandgap determination.46 This observation is consistent with prior reports identifying bulk CuMnO2 as a p-type semiconductor with an absorption edge extending into the near-visible region. Upon hybridization with carbon, a more pronounced exponential absorption tail emerges, which can be attributed to defect-related electronic states introduced by oxide-carbon interactions.47

Based on the combined structural, surface, and optical analyses, N2 adsorption–desorption measurements at 77 K (Fig. S2A and Table S1) reveal a gradual decrease in specific surface area (SSA) with increasing CuMnO2 loading, consistent with a micropore-blocking mechanism. During impregnation and crystallization, oxide species preferentially occupy carbon micropores while largely preserving the mesoporous network, thereby maintaining bulk mass transport. A gradual reduction in SSA is observed, decreasing from above 1200 m2 g−1 for the parent carbon materials to below 800 m2 g−1 as the CuMnO2 loading increases. Correspondingly, the total pore volume declines from ∼0.88 to ∼0.63 cm3 g−1, mainly due to a substantial reduction in micropore volume (∼0.53 to ∼0.30 cm3 g−1), while the mesopore volume remains nearly constant (≈0.33–0.35 cm3 g−1). The average pore diameter shifts slightly from 2.8 to 3.4 nm, in agreement with the enhanced oxide crystallinity (XRD), increased defect density (ID/IG, Raman), and higher lattice oxygen contribution associated with Cu2+/Mn4+ species (XPS).

Such SSA reduction with increasing oxide loading is widely reported for activated carbon-metal oxide systems, where micropore loss is compensated by the formation of new interfacial active sites that enhance catalytic functionality. The baseline SSA values of RS-LC and RS-LC(N) are also consistent with biomass- or lignin-derived activated carbons activated by K2CO3/KOH (typically ∼1200–2200 m2 g−1), supporting the reliability of the carbon framework in this study.48 Compared with solid-state or high-temperature synthesis routes for mixed-metal oxides, the present hydrothermal anchoring strategy sacrifices a limited degree of bulk crystallinity but achieves superior oxide dispersion and stronger oxide-carbon interfacial coupling, which is more favorable for PMS activation under neutral pH conditions.49,50

Photocatalytic efficiency of BPA degradation

BPA showed negligible degradation under light-only or PMS-only conditions (C/C0 ≈ 0.9–1.0 after 60 min; Fig. 2A), indicating that direct photolysis at 420 nm is insignificant and PMS cannot be efficiently self-activated under visible light without a catalytic interface, consistent with previous PMS/Vis reports.51 The light/PMS system without catalyst led to only marginal improvement, attributable to weak PMS photoexcitation, but remained far inferior to heterogeneous photo-PMS systems where interfacial electron transfer is enabled.52,53

Introducing RS-LC or RS-LC(N) markedly enhanced BPA removal under light/PMS system (Fig. 2A). RS-LC promotes BPA pre-enrichment via its micro-mesoporous structure, while N-doping further accelerates PMS activation by providing defect-associated electron-transfer centers. This behavior is consistent with prior studies showing that N-doped carbon materials activate PMS through electron-transfer mechanisms (ETM) and/or 1O2 pathways under neutral conditions, imparting higher tolerance to inorganic ions compared with radical-dominated systems.9,54

Anchoring CuMnO2 onto RS-LC(N) further boosts photocatalytic performance. As shown in Fig. 2B, RS-LC(N)/CuMnO2 exhibits steep kinetic profiles under light/PMS, with kobs increasing with CuMnO2 loading up to 10–12.5 wt% and declining at higher loadings. This volcano-type behavior reflects a balance between increasing availability of redox-active Cu2+/Cu+ and Mn4+/Mn3+ centers at low-moderate loadings and adverse effects at excessive loadings, including pore blockage, light shielding, and enhanced charge recombination. Similar trends have been reported for CuMnO2/PMS and other transition-metal oxide systems, in which PMS activation efficiency is strongly governed by oxide dispersion and surface accessibility.10,55 Although Co- or multimetal-based catalysts can deliver higher rate constants (k ≈ 0.3–3.5 min−1), their practical application is often constrained by metal leaching and toxicity concerns. In this context, RS-LC(N)/CuMnO2 offers a well-balanced performance, achieving efficient BPA degradation while maintaining improved environmental safety.56,57

Parametric studies under optimized conditions reveal that kobs peaks at near-neutral pH, increases with PMS concentration and catalyst dosage before reaching saturation, and decreases with increasing initial BPA concentration (Fig. 2C). The observed pH dependence is governed by PMS speciation (pKa ≈ 9.4). At neutral pH, HSO5− is the predominant species and can be efficiently activated to generate SO4˙−. Under acidic conditions, PMS tends to undergo hydrolysis, while alkaline environments favor the formation of the less reactive SO52− species and carbonate-derived radicals, both of which suppress overall degradation kinetics.58,59 Increasing PMS concentration enhances radical generation up to the millimolar level, beyond which self-scavenging reactions limit further rate improvement, a phenomenon widely observed in PMS-AOPs (Fig. 2C).60,61 Similarly, catalyst-dose saturation arises from light scattering, shielding, and particle aggregation at high loadings, consistent with general principles of heterogeneous photocatalysis (Fig. 2C).62,63 The decrease in kobs at higher BPA concentrations reflects active-site saturation, inner-filter effects, and competition with intermediates, consistent with reports on PMS-driven degradation of BPA and related pollutants (Fig. 2C).64,65

Overall, the obtained kobs value (0.1989 min−1 at pH 7.0 under visible light) is comparable to many reported Cu-based and N-doped carbon/PMS systems under neutral conditions.66–68 Unlike some Co- or multimetal-based catalysts, RS-LC(N)/CuMnO2 combines efficient BPA degradation with low metal leaching, good cycling stability, and effective performance in realistic water matrices. The use of a biomass-derived N-doped carbon support further enhances the environmental sustainability and practical applicability of the present system.

Reactions involved in the catalytic system

The enhanced visible-light/PMS activity is attributed to the synergistic interaction between photoactive CuMnO2 and conductive N-doped carbon. Under visible-light irradiation, CuMnO2 likely serves as the primary photoresponsive component, generating photogenerated electrons that promote PMS reduction and reactive oxygen species generation. Meanwhile, the N-doped carbon matrix facilitates interfacial electron transport through defect-associated pyridinic/graphitic-N sites and promotes surface-confined PMS activation pathways. The strong oxide-carbon interfacial coupling therefore enhances charge utilization efficiency and contributes to both radical and secondary non-radical oxidation processes.

To further clarify the role of photogenerated charge carriers in the visible-light-assisted PMS activation process, transient photocurrent (Fig. 4A), electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) (Fig. 4B), and photoluminescence (PL) analyses (Fig. 4C) were performed. RS-LC(N)/CuMnO2-10 exhibited the highest photocurrent response, the smallest Nyquist arc radius, and markedly suppressed photoluminescence intensity compared with pristine CuMnO2 and RS-LC(N), indicating more efficient charge separation and faster interfacial electron transfer arising from the oxide-carbon heterointerface.13 After PMS addition, the photocurrent response further increased while photoluminescence intensity decreased, confirming that PMS acts as an effective electron acceptor that consumes photogenerated electrons and accelerates ROS generation.68 These results strongly support the proposed synergistic mechanism involving CuMnO2 photoexcitation coupled with carbon-mediated electron transfer during PMS activation.13

Quenching experiments (Fig. 2D) provide clearer semi-quantitative evidence for the coexistence of radical and non-radical pathways during BPA degradation. TBA, a preferential ˙OH scavenger, reduced kobs by ∼35%, indicating a substantial contribution from ˙OH. In comparison, MeOH, which efficiently quenches both SO4˙− and ˙OH, caused nearly 60% inhibition, confirming the important participation of SO4˙− beyond the hydroxyl-radical pathway. These results suggest that BPA degradation is governed predominantly by radical-mediated oxidation involving the combined action of SO4˙− and ˙OH. p-Benzoquinone (p-BQ) caused moderate inhibition, suggesting a minor contribution from O2˙−, whereas FFA produced only limited inhibition (∼15%), indicating that 1O2 acts as a secondary non-radical oxidizing species rather than the primary pathway. A slight inhibition by EDTA is attributed to partial complexation of surface Cu/Mn sites, which partially disrupts the redox-assisted PMS activation cycle. Moreover, the enhanced activity under illumination supports a photo-assisted PMS activation mechanism involving photogenerated electron transfer at the oxide-carbon interface. These observations are consistent with EPR and kinetic analyses, confirming the coexistence of dominant radical and secondary non-radical pathways in the RS-LC(N)/CuMnO2/PMS/Vis system.33,60,69

In the radical channel, multivalent Cu and Mn species in CuMnO2 (Cu2+/Cu+ and Mn4+/Mn3+) facilitate PMS activation through interfacial redox cycling, generating SO4˙− and subsequently ˙OH via hydrolysis or exchange reactions (eqn (13)–(19), Table 1). Such Cu–Mn synergistic activation has been widely reported to be more efficient and stable than monometallic oxides in phenol/bisphenol degradation systems.11,67

The non-radical pathway proceeds through two complementary routes. First, oxygen vacancies at the CuMnO2-carbon interface promote PMS and O2 adsorption and activation (eqn (20)–(22), Table 1), enabling energy-transfer-driven 1O2 formation without radical chain propagation. Second, N-doped carbon (notably pyridinic and graphitic N) establishes an efficient ETM bridge between adsorbed BPA and PMS, allowing surface-confined oxidation without free-radical diffusion into the bulk solution (eqn (11)–(12), (25) and (26), Table 1).70 Such mechanisms are well documented for Cu–Mn/PMS and N-doped carbon/PMS systems, particularly for electron-rich substrates under neutral conditions.11,55

EPR spin-trapping and kinetic monitoring further corroborate this mechanistic framework. RS-LC(N)/CuMnO2 exhibited the strongest ROS signals, the fastest PMS consumption, and the highest H2O2 formation among all samples (Fig. 3A and B), consistent with efficient O2 reduction and PMS activation (eqn (5)–(8) and (23)–(28), Table 1). Clear DMPO-˙OH and DMPO-SO4˙− signals were observed and intensified with increasing CuMnO2 loading, whereas TEMP-1O2 signals were detectable but comparatively weaker (Fig. 3C–E). Time-resolved EPR revealed rapid ˙OH formation at early stages, followed by gradual increases in SO4˙− and O2˙−, while 1O2 remained moderate (Fig. 3F and S3), in agreement with the quenching results (MeOH > TBA; minor effect of FFA). These results indicate that SO4˙−/˙OH radicals dominate BPA degradation under the present neutral pH and illuminated conditions, with 1O2 and ETM providing secondary contributions. This trend aligns with recent reviews showing that metal-containing photo-PMS systems are typically radical-dominated, whereas metal-free carbon systems favor non-radical pathways, although both may coexist depending on pH, electrolyte composition, defect density, and light exposure.9

The complete photocatalytic PMS activation and BPA oxidation network is schematically summarized in Table 1 as follows: PMS speciation and photoexcitation (eqn (1)–(4)), O2 reduction and indirect ˙OH formation (eqn (5)–(8)), photoinduced PMS activation on carbon surfaces (eqn (9)–(12)), Cu/Mn redox cycling (eqn (13)–(19)), oxygen-vacancy-assisted activation steps (eqn (20)–(22)), ROS interconversion and 1O2 generation (eqn ((23)–(28)), BPA oxidation and mineralization pathways (eqn (29)–(40)), and catalyst regeneration via ETM processes (eqn (41)–(43)).

LC-MS/MS analysis (Table S2 and Fig. S4) revealed the rapid disappearance of BPA (m/z 227, RT 8.7 min), accompanied by the formation of oxygenated intermediates and open-ring acids, indicative of a typical oxidation-cleavage-mineralization sequence. The elution order, from small organic acids to phenolic intermediates and finally BPA, followed the increasing polarity of the oxidation products and was consistent with recent LC – MS/MS studies on BPA degradation via AOPs.71

At the early stage, mono-hydroxy-BPA (m/z 243) and di-hydroxy-BPA (m/z 259) appeared transiently, reaching maxima before declining, consistent with hydroxylation induced by ˙OH/SO4˙– and/or 1O2 in heterogeneous PMS systems. Similar behavior has been reported for Cu–Mn-based PMS catalysts operating at neutral pH, where hydroxylated BPA derivatives dominate within the first 10-20 min.72 Concurrently, C–C bond cleavage between the aromatic rings was evidenced by the detection of p-isopropenylphenol (m/z 133, RT 7.2 min) and 4-hydroxyacetophenone (m/z 135, RT 5.2 min). These compounds are recognized fingerprints of ipso-substitution and selective dealkylation pathways associated with 1O2/electron-transfer mechanisms, as confirmed by DFT calculations and LC-MS analyses in BPA oxidation and other oxidative systems (e.g., ferrate and photochemical processes).73,74

Quinone-related intermediates, including p-benzoquinone (m/z 107) and hydroquinone/catechol (m/z 109), were detected at very short retention times (≈2.0–2.4 min), exhibiting a reversible redox relationship (HQ ⇄ BQ). Such quinone/di-phenol species are widely reported as key transitional intermediates preceding aromatic ring opening in BPA degradation across photocatalytic and Fenton-like systems.75,76 Subsequently, open-ring carboxylic acids: maleic/fumaric (m/z 115; RT 1.6–1.8 min) and oxalic acid (m/z 89; RT 1.1 min), accumulated and became dominant after 45–60 min, consistent with the general AOPs pathway in which C4/C2 acids represent the final intermediates prior to complete mineralization to CO2 and H2O.71,77,78

Overall, the LC-MS/MS results support a coherent degradation pathway over RS-LC(N)/CuMnO2 (Fig. 5): BPA undergoes initial hydroxylation to mono/di–OH–BPA via ˙OH/SO4˙−, parallel dealkylation to p-isopropenylphenol and 4-hydroxyacetophenone via1O2/ETM, followed by conversion of phenolic species to quinones/di-phenols, ring opening to maleic/fumaric and oxalic acids, and eventual mineralization. Compared with many PMS-based catalysts, the distinctive advantage of RS-LC(N)/CuMnO2 lies in its ability to simultaneously promote radical pathways (Cu2+/Cu+ and Mn4+/Mn3+) and non-radical pathways (1O2/ETM on N-doped carbon), thereby accelerating aromatic ring opening and enhancing deep mineralization. The identified intermediates are fully consistent with the proposed radical-initiated oxidation steps (eqn (29)–(35)) and subsequent ring-opening and mineralization reactions (eqn (36)–(40)).

TOC analysis (Fig. 4D) revealed a substantial decrease in total organic carbon during the reaction process, confirming that BPA degradation proceeded beyond transformation of the parent compound toward deep oxidation and partial mineralization. The continuous TOC reduction, together with the gradual disappearance of oxygenated intermediates identified by LC-MS/MS, supports the proposed pathway involving hydroxylation, quinone formation, aromatic ring opening, and conversion into small carboxylic acids prior to final mineralization to CO2 and H2O. These results provide direct evidence that the RS-LC(N)/CuMnO2/PMS/Vis system promotes not only rapid BPA degradation but also efficient mineralization of organic carbon species.

Influence of water matrix

Under standard conditions (BPA 10 mg L−1, pH 7.0, PMS 1.0 mM, RS-LC(N)/CuMnO2 1.0 g L−1, 25 ± 2 °C, 420 ± 10 nm), BPA degradation was strongly affected by the water matrix (Fig. 6, S5 and Table S3). The apparent rate constant followed the order: DI water > river > tap > lake > WWTP effluent > brackish water, corresponding to increasing ionic strength, turbidity, and DOC (Fig. 6A and B). In DI water, kobs reached ∼0.20 min−1, whereas natural and real waters showed progressively lower values. This trend is consistent with PMS-based AOPs, where background constituents attenuate light penetration, scavenge reactive oxygen species (ROS), and compress the electrical double layer (EDL), collectively suppressing heterogeneous reaction rates.79

(A and B) Kinetic curves and rate constants of photocatalytic degradation of BPA using RS-LC(N)/CuMnO2-10 in different real aqueous samples and (C and D) in the presence of different aqueous matrix parameters.

Among anions (Fig. 6C and D), HCO3− exhibited the strongest inhibitory effect, as carbonate species efficiently scavenge ˙OH and SO4˙− to form less reactive CO3˙−, while also interfering with ROS cycling. Although bicarbonate has been reported to promote PMS activation in certain Cu-based systems via enhanced Cu redox cycling, under the present conditions (CuMnO2 on N-doped carbon, neutral pH, visible light), radical scavenging dominated, leading to net inhibition.80,81 Cl− caused a slight to moderate decrease in kobs, attributed to the conversion of ˙OH/SO4˙− into reactive chlorine species (RCS), which are generally less effective under visible light. This explains the discrepancy with reports showing Cl− promotion under UV-C or high chloride conditions.23,82 NO3− had only a minor effect, while SO42− was essentially inert. In contrast, PO43− significantly suppressed activity by competing for Cu/Mn active sites and scavenging radicals, despite reports of phosphate-assisted PMS activation at higher concentrations or different pH regimes.83,84

Divalent cations (Ca2+, Mg2+) moderately reduced kobs, mainly due to EDL compression and changes in surface microenvironments via ion bridging or interactions with DOM. A similar, weakly negative trend was observed with increasing ionic strength (NaClO4), consistent with classical EDL theory.85,86 Natural organic matter (DOC ≈ 5 mg L−1) markedly inhibited degradation through light shielding, competitive adsorption, and ROS quenching, in line with previous PMS-AOP studies.87 In contrast, dissolved oxygen acted as a promoter: increasing DO enhanced kobs by facilitating O2˙−/HO2˙ formation, H2O2 generation, and 1O2 production, thereby coupling radical and non-radical pathways.88,89 Turbidity (50 NTU) caused a pronounced decrease in activity due to light scattering and absorption, which shortened the effective optical path length.79

Overall, the results indicate that (i) dissolved oxygen is a key accelerator; (ii) HCO3−, PO43−, DOC, and turbidity are the dominant inhibitors; (iii) Cl− plays a dual role but acts mainly as an inhibitor under visible light; (iv) EDL compression explains much of the activity loss in real waters; and (v) the catalyst maintains high performance even in complex matrices, supporting its potential for practical applications, although further optimization is needed for high-salinity or NOM-rich waters.

Catalyst stability and metal leaching

After five consecutive cycles, RS-LC(N)/CuMnO2-10 exhibited excellent structural stability and catalytic durability. The XRD patterns (Fig. 7A) showed that the characteristic CuMnO2 reflections were well preserved after cycling, indicating that the crednerite/delafossite phase remained intact without phase transformation or amorphization, consistent with the intrinsic stability of layered CuMnO2. SEM images before and after reuse (Fig. 7C and D) revealed no obvious pore collapse or severe agglomeration, confirming the preservation of the hierarchical morphology essential for surface area and PMS contact.

Kinetic degradation curves and corresponding rate constants (E), and metal leaching profiles (F) during the photocatalytic degradation of BPA over RS-LC(N)/CuMnO2-10 for five consecutive cycles.

XPS analysis (Fig. 7B and Table S4) provides mechanistic insight into this stability. In the C 1s spectra, the sp2 CC component decreased markedly (from 56.73 to 24.20%), whereas oxygen-containing groups increased (C–O: from 15.89 to 27.90%; O–CO: from 10.38 to 27.03%), indicating mild surface oxidation of RS-LC(N). These newly formed hydrophilic and coordinating functionalities facilitate anchoring of metal/oxy-hydroxide species, thereby suppressing metal migration. The N 1s spectra showed nearly unchanged distributions of pyridinic, pyrrolic, and graphitic N, indicating the persistence of stable M–Nx anchoring sites that are well known to immobilize metal centers on carbon supports.

In the O 1s region, both lattice oxygen and –OH/defect-related oxygen increased, whereas adsorbed oxygen decreased, implying an aged but catalytically favorable surface enriched in active oxygen species and defects that promote PMS activation. The evolution of Cu and Mn states further supports sustained redox activity. For Cu 2p, the intensified shake-up satellites and the increased Cu+ shoulder indicate a surface enriched in Cu(ii) with an operative Cu(ii)/Cu(i) redox buffer. For Mn 2p, the pronounced increase in satellite/multiplet features, together with changes in the main components, is consistent with Mn(iii)/Mn(iv) cycling under PMS activation conditions, in line with established Cu–Mn cooperative redox mechanisms.

These results suggest that RS-LC(N)/CuMnO2 maintains a dynamically stabilized redox-active surface during repeated PMS activation. Although extended cycling may gradually alter the relative distribution of Cu/Mn oxidation states and surface oxygen species because of continuous surface oxidation and reconstruction, the preserved crystallinity, stable morphology, and ultralow metal leaching collectively indicate excellent long-term structural stability. Notably, the increase in oxygen-containing surface groups after cycling may further stabilize Cu/Mn active sites through stronger interfacial coordination with the N-doped carbon matrix, thereby mitigating metal migration and sustaining PMS activation activity.

This structural robustness translated directly into catalytic performance (Fig. 7E): the BPA degradation profiles (C/C0vs. time) across five cycles were nearly overlapping, with only a slight decrease in kobs (from 0.20 to 0.14 min−1 in the fifth cycle), indicating good reusability and sustained catalytic activity under repeated PMS activation conditions. These surface reconstructions correlate well with the extremely low metal leaching observed (Fig. 7F). Over five cycles, Cu and Mn leaching decreased from 0.072% to 0.028% and from 0.054% to 0.023%, respectively, remaining at the 10−2 wt% level and even declining with reuse. Such values are comparable to or lower than those reported for many PMS catalysts, where metal dissolution is often a major limitation.90 The slight activity decrease, together with the declining leaching trend, suggests a self-stabilizing behavior arising from (i) increased oxygen-containing groups on RS-LC(N) that immobilize metal species, (ii) robust N-centered M–Nx coordination, and (iii) a defect-rich Cu–Mn oxide surface that sustains redox cycling without significant metal loss.

Overall, the combined evidence from phase-stable XRD, morphology-preserving SEM, XPS indicating an actively stabilized surface, consistently high BPA degradation kinetics, and ultralow metal leaching demonstrates that RS-LC(N)/CuMnO2-10 is a highly reusable and durable PMS catalyst. This performance aligns well with current understanding of Cu–Mn synergistic PMS activation and highlights the critical role of the N-doped carbon matrix in stabilizing metal centers, outperforming many previously reported pure metal oxide systems in both activity retention and cycling stability.91,92

Conclusion

This study demonstrates a sustainable and efficient strategy for BPA removal by integrating a biomass-derived porous carbon scaffold with a visible-light-responsive mixed oxide. Rice-straw lignin was converted into an N-doped hierarchical carbon support to anchor crednerite CuMnO2, yielding a Co-free photo-PMS system that operates effectively at circumneutral pH. Among the tested materials, RS-LC(N)/CuMnO2-10 achieves the optimal balance between accessible redox sites and preserved porosity, delivering rapid BPA degradation with a pseudo-first-order rate constant of 0.1989 min−1 at pH 7.0 (1.0 mM PMS, 1.0 g L−1 catalyst). The high activity under visible light highlights the importance of interfacial coupling between CuMnO2 and N-doped lignin carbon, which enriches BPA via adsorption, maintains efficient pore transport, and facilitates charge transfer to PMS and the Cu/Mn redox couple.

Mechanistically, BPA oxidation follows a hybrid pathway dominated by radical chemistry. Photo-assisted Cu/Mn redox cycling activates PMS to generate SO4˙− and ˙OH as the primary oxidants, while a complementary non-radical route involving singlet oxygen and surface electron-transfer mediation on N-doped carbon contributes in parallel. This dual-pathway framework accounts for both the high activity at neutral pH and the robustness of the system in complex water matrices. LC-MS/MS analysis confirms sequential BPA hydroxylation, oxidation to quinone-type intermediates, aromatic ring opening, and conversion to short-chain carboxylic acids prior to mineralization.

From an application perspective, the catalyst exhibits strong durability. After five consecutive cycles, only a minor decline in activity is observed, and metal leaching remains very low and decreases with reuse (Cu: ∼0.072% to ∼0.028%; Mn: ∼0.054% to ∼0.023%), indicating effective stabilization of CuMnO2 by the lignin-derived carbon matrix and a self-conditioning surface during operation. Moreover, meaningful BPA removal is retained in tap, river, lake, wastewater effluent, and brackish waters, despite inevitable kinetic moderation by salinity, dissolved organic matter, and turbidity.

Overall, this work shows that lignin-derived N-doped porous carbon functions as an active partner rather than an inert support, enhancing PMS activation through adsorption enrichment, defect-assisted electron shuttling, and strong oxide anchoring. The resulting Co-free RS-LC(N)/CuMnO2 photo-PMS platform offers a green, scalable approach for visible-light-driven, neutral-pH treatment of BPA and related phenolic contaminants, representing a significant step toward practical biomass-engineered persulfate-based water purification.
