A Novel CRISPR/Cas9-mediated Mouse Model of Colon Carcinogenesis
Authors: Hajime Kashima, Anthony Fischer, Daniel A Veronese-Paniagua, Vered A Gazit, Changqing Ma, Yan Yan, Marc S Levin, Blair B Madison, Deborah C Rubin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground & AimsHuman sporadic colorectal cancer (CRC) results from a multistep pathway with sequential acquisition of specific genetic mutations in the colorectal epithelium. Modeling CRC in vivo is critical for understanding the tumor microenvironment. To accurately recapitulate human CRC pathogenesis, mouse models must include these multi-step genetic abnormalities. The aim of this study was to generate a sporadic CRC model that more closely mimics this multi-step process and to use this model to study the role of a novel Let7 target PLAGL2 in CRC pathogenesis.MethodsWe generated a CRISPR/Cas9 somatic mutagenesis mouse model that is inducible and multiplexed for simultaneous inactivation of multiple genes involved in CRC pathogenesis. We used both a doxycycline-inducible transcriptional activator and a doxycycline-inactivated transcriptional repressor to achieve tight, non-leaky expression of the Cas9 nickase. This mouse has transgenic expression of multiple guide RNAs to induce sporadic inactivation in the gut epithelium of 4 tumor suppressor genes commonly mutated in CRC, Apc, Pten, Smad4, and Trp53. These were crossed to Vil-LCL-PLAGL2 mice, which have Cre-inducible overexpression of PLAGL2 in the gut epithelium.ResultsThese mice exhibited random somatic mutations in all 4 targeted tumor suppressor genes, resulting in multiple adenomas and adenocarcinomas in the small bowel and colon. Crosses with Vil-LCL-PLAGL2 mice demonstrated that gut-specific PLAGL2 overexpression increased colon tumor growth.ConclusionsThis conditional model represents a new CRISPR/Cas9-mediated mouse model of colorectal carcinogenesis. These mice can be used to investigate the role of novel, previously uncharacterized genes in CRC, in the context of multiple commonly mutated tumor suppressor genes and thus more closely mimic human CRC pathogenesis.Keywords: Colorectal Neoplasia, CRISPR/Cas9, Mouse Models, PLAGL2

Summary

We generated a CRISPR/Cas9 somatic mutagenesis mouse model. This novel model enables us to investigate multistep tumorigenesis and early colon cancer pathogenesis by inducing mutations in 4 tumor suppressor genes, Apc, Pten, Smad4, and Trp53.

Human sporadic colorectal cancer (CRC) results from a multistep pathway characterized by the sequential acquisition of specific genetic mutations in the colorectal epithelium, most commonly including APC, KRAS, SMAD4, PIK3CA, and TP53. The classic adenoma to carcinoma sequence involves mutations in the Adenomatous Polyposis Coli (APC) gene, which is a gate-keeper tumor suppressor that is mutated in most human CRCs. APC mutation initiates tumorigenesis in both small and large intestine1 but is not sufficient to generate invasive carcinoma. Rather, the accumulation of mutations in multiple genes leads to advanced and metastatic cancer.2 Modeling human colon cancer in vivo using mouse models is critical for understanding the complexity of the tumor microenvironment, and for mouse models to accurately recapitulate human CRC pathogenesis, they must include such multi-step genetic abnormalities.

Following the report of the Apcmin mouse with spontaneous Apc mutation resulting in adenoma formation,3,4 multiple additional mouse models have been reported with various mutations generated by conditional gene knockout, among other methods.5 Technical issues and the complexity of breeding have until recently limited the number of mutations that could be generated per mouse.3 However, this changed with the introduction of CRISPR/Cas9 techniques, which facilitate simultaneous targeting of multiple genes.6,7 Here, we have generated a unique CRISPR somatic mutagenesis model that is both inducible and multiplexed for targeting multiple genes simultaneously. By transgenic expression of multiple guide RNAs (gRNAs),8 we achieved random mutations (insertions/deletions) in the endogenous canonical tumor suppressors Apc, Pten, Smad4, and Trp53, resulting in frameshifts and null alleles. Through brief Cas9 expression, we sporadically introduce an array of mutations in these genes, allowing us to recapitulate the natural history of CRC in a model that more accurately mimics the progressive stages of CRC. We show that these mice develop adenomas and invasive adenocarcinomas at 3 months of age.

Moreover, we used this model to further explore the role of the Let7 miRNA target PLAGL2 in colorectal carcinogenesis, by crossing these mice to transgenic mice, which inducibly overexpress PLAGL2 in the intestinal epithelium. Let7 miRNAs repress epithelial tumorigenesis and are depleted in most CRCs.9, 10, 11 Let7 miRNAs also directly repress PLAGL2 gene expression.12 PLAGL2 is a zinc finger transcription factor expressed at high levels in the intestine during ontogeny,13 and which is absent/minimally expressed in normal mouse and human intestine and colon but is upregulated in adenomas and CRC.14, 15, 16 We previously showed that PLAGL2 promotes gut stem cell identity in part via direct transcriptional activation of ASCL2.12 Others have also shown oncogenic effects in leukemia17,18 and glioma.19 However, the precise mechanisms by which PLAGL2 contributes to the pathogenesis of CRC are not known.20, 21, 22, 23 Our prior observation that ASCL2 is a target of PLAGL2, induced by PLAGL2 overexpression in normal epithelium and in colon cancer cells, suggests that PLAGL2 may play a role in early colon cancer pathogenesis by driving a stem cell signature, which, in turn, predicts CRC relapse.24 The absence of PLAGL2 expression in adult mice13 and in normal human small intestine and colon provide further support for its potential as a novel, specific target for cancer therapy.

Here, we crossed the icCas9N;PPAS mice which, conditionally generate random somatic mutations in Apc, Pten, Smad4, and Trp53 via the high-fidelity Cas9 nickase, to mice with PLAGL2 overexpression in the gut epithelium, and show that this increases colon tumor burden, with a significant increase in colon tumor growth and colon tumor multiplicity. This conditional model using CRISPR/Cas9-mediated mutation of multiple tumor suppressor genes more closely mimics human CRC pathogenesis. These mice can be used to investigate the role of novel, previously uncharacterized genes in CRC.

Results

icCas9N;PPAS Mice Develop Intestinal Adenomas and Adenocarcinomas Following Doxycycline Administration

We achieved inducible Cas9 expression in icCas9N mice (Figure 1A) using the mouse Vil1 (villin) promoter to drive both a doxycycline (dox)-dependent activator (rtTAM2) and a dox-inactivated repressor (tTSKid). By using both a dox-inducible transcriptional activator and a dox-inactivated transcriptional repressor,25 we were able to obtain tight, non-leaky expression of the Cas9 nickase as assessed in an in vitro SRIRACCHA reporter assay (Figure 1B).26 HEK 293T cells were transfected with pCMV-rtTA or pCMV-rtTA-ires-tTS plasmids, and co-transfected with the insulator-flanked TRE3G-Cas9D10Aires-CreERT2 construct, then treated with dox. In the absence of dox, leaky CRISPR activity was detected in cells that contained the CMV-rtTA construct only, but not in cells co-transfected with the CMV-rtTA-ires-tTS plasmid, which co-express the dox-inactivatable repressor.

We generated the PPAS transgenic mouse, which constitutively expresses U6 promoter driven expression of gRNA pairs that target protospacer sequence targets in mouse Pten, Trp53, Apc, and Smad426 (Figure 1C and Table 1). We bred the inducible icCas9N mice to the PPAS mice (Figure 1D) and drove mutagenesis with dox chow treatment. We found that icCas9N;PPAS mice develop adenomas and adenocarcinomas, but only in mice fed with dox (Figure 1E). As expected, adenomas and adenocarcinomas were not found in the intestines or colons of untreated icCas9N;PPAS mice.

We employed 2 strategies to minimize off-target mutations, including inducible expression, which limits Cas9 nuclease activity, and the use of the Cas9D10A paired-nickase, which decreases off-target cutting 50- to 1500-fold.27 To assess the specificity of mutagenesis, 5 intestinal tumors and 5 normal adjacent intestinal tissues from icCas9N;PPAS mice (n = 4) were analyzed. We sequenced 4 colon tumors and 1 jejunal tumor as well as 4 adjacent normal colon samples and 1 adjacent normal jejunal sample. A representative example is shown in Figure 1F. We found that tumors contained mutations in Apc, Pten, Smad4, and Trp53, but no mutations were found in normal adjacent colon or small bowel tissue from dox-fed mice (Figure 1F).

icCas9N;PPAS Mouse Tumor Location, Histology, and Mutation Analysis

In initial experiments, we fed dox chow to icCas9N;PPAS mice for 2 weeks (n = 7), and analyzed the incidence, location, histology, and mutation profiles of intestinal tumors. We observed that 4 of 7 mice developed intestinal tumors in small bowel and/or colon (Figure 2A) with no lesions found in normal tissue (Figure 2B). Two developed small bowel tumors only, 1 had a single colon tumor, and 1 had multiple small intestinal and colonic tumors. Colon tumors from individual icCas9N;PPAS mice showed varied histological patterns (Figure 2C–D), including adenomas and invasive adenocarcinoma. Illumina sequencing revealed that genomic DNA from normal adjacent colon tissue in icCas9N;PPAS mice was almost completely without mutation, similar to what we observed from sequencing wild type (WT) colon genomic DNA (Figure 2B, E). Tumors from the same mouse showed different mutational patterns and mutation frequencies in the 4 tumor suppressor genes. For example tumor #1 (Figure 2C) showed 38.2% non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) in Pten, 51.4% in Smad4, and 62.6% in Trp53 (Figure 2F). Tumor #2 (Figure 2D) had 81.3% in Apc, 59.0% in Trp53, 0.5% in Pten, and 0.2% in Smad4 (Figure 2G). Immunohistochemical analyses to detect β-catenin or SMAD4 showed moderate expression in normal adjacent colon tissue (Figure 2H, K); however, tumor #1 showed reduced SMAD4 protein expression (Figure 2L), and β-catenin expression was similar to normal adjacent colon tissue (Figure 2I). On the other hand, tumor #2 showed moderate expression of SMAD4 (Figure 2M) similar to normal adjacent colon tissue, yet more intense nuclear staining for β-catenin (Figure 2J). Because stromal and neuronal tissues are not targeted with this genetic model relying on the epithelial-specific expression driven by the Vil1 promoter, tissue samples (which contain non-epithelial cells) are expected to have non-mutated genomic DNA at each of these 4 targeted genes.

Increased Plagl2 and Stem Cell/Cell Proliferation Marker Gene Expression in icCas9N;PPAS Intestinal Tumors

We extracted total RNA from icCas9N;PPAS colon tumors and normal adjacent colon tissues and performed quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) analyses to quantify expression of Plagl2 and its target genes. Plagl2 mRNA expression was significantly increased in colon tumors compared with normal colon tissue (P = .0014), consistent with observations in human CRC.14, 15, 16 Expression of Ascl2, a gut stem cell marker and PLAGL2 target gene,12 was significantly increased in icCas9N;PPAS colon tumors compared with icCas9N;PPAS normal colon (P = .0023) (Figure 3C), as was expression of Lgr5, an ASCL2 target gene and marker of the rapidly proliferating crypt base columnar stem cell (P = .0326), also consistent with observations in human CRC. Finally, mRNA expression of the proliferative cell marker Ki67 was also increased (P = .0079) in icCas9N;PPAS colon tumors compared with normal colon.

We next examined the DNA sequence of the 4 targeted tumor suppressor genes including Apc, Pten, Smad4, and Trp53 in colon tumors. Each tumor showed differences in allele patterns and in the frequency of indel mutations. We calculated the frequency of NHEJ events in the 4 tumor suppressor genes from icCas9N;PPAS-colon tumors (n = 18) (Figure 3D). Each tumor exhibited a unique mutation pattern. For example, some tumors had a high frequency of Apc mutations, whereas others showed no mutation in Apc yet had a high frequency of Smad4 and Pten mutations.

Tumorigenesis in icCas9N;PPAS and icCas9N;PPAS;Vil-LCL-PLAGL2 Mice

The precise role of PLAGL2 in colon cancer pathogenesis is still unclear, despite compelling evidence for a pro-tumorigenesis effect. To explore the interaction of PLAGL2 with mutations in Apc, Pten, Smad4, and Trp53, 4 tumor suppressor genes that are commonly mutated in human colon cancer, and a subset of which have known interactions with Let7 micro RNAs (miRNAs),28, 29, 30 we crossed icCas9N;PPAS with Vil-LCL-PLAGL2 mice and determined effects of PLAGL2 overexpression on intestinal tumorigenesis (Figure 4G). icCas9N;PPAS mice (n = 20) and icCas9;PPAS;Vil-LCL-PLAGL2 mice (n = 18) were fed dox chow for 3 weeks and also received TAM (2 injections of 250 mg/kg on day 49 and day 51) as per our previous experiments. Gene expression in tumors and normal colon were examined at 16 weeks of age (schema, Figure 4H).

We first examined gene expression in normal adjacent colon to evaluate the effect of PLAGL2 overexpression in normal intestine. Previous experiments suggested that PLAGL2 drives an intestinal stem cell signature in gut epithelium,12 via increased ASCL2 expression. As expected, expression of the PLAGL2 target gene Ascl2, was significantly increased (P = .03) (Figure 4I). However, additional gut stem cell marker genes including Lgr5, Sox9, and Olfm4 showed no change in expression (Figure 4I). In addition, there was no change in expression of various markers of gut epithelial differentiation, including Fabp2 and lysozyme (Lyz1). We also examined the DNA sequence of the 4 targeted tumor suppressor genes including Apc, Pten, Smad4, and Trp53 in colon tumors from icCas9N;PPAS;VIL-LCL-PLAGL2 mice, and calculated the frequency of NHEJ events in the 4 tumor suppressor genes (n = 15) (Figure 4J). Similar to the icCas9N;PPAS mice, each tumor exhibited a unique mutation pattern.

To address the hypothesis that PLAGL2 plays a role in early colon cancer pathogenesis and to begin to elucidate the mechanisms for PLAGL2-induced increased tumor growth, we performed qRT-PCR analyses to quantify mRNA expression of PLAGL2 targets as well as other stem cell markers in icCas9N;PPAS;Vil-LCL-PLAGL2 vs icCas9N;PPAS intestinal tumors. icCas9N;PPAS;Vil-LCL-PLAGL2 colon tumors had significantly increased Tnfrsf19 mRNA expression (P = .0017), and a trend towards increased Ascl2 (P = .07) and Lgr5 (P = .06) mRNA expression compared with icCas9N;PPAS colon tumors (Figure 5J).

We compared mutation patterns in icCas9N;PPAS and;icCas9N;PPAS;Vil-LCL-PLAGL2 colon and small bowel tumors. The average frequency of Smad4 mutation was increased in icCas9N;PPAS;Vil-LCL-PLAGL2 colon tumors (P < .05). There were no significant differences in the frequency of Pten, Apc or Trp53 mutations.

Discussion

Here, we report a novel mouse model of CRC that combines dox-inducible Cas9 nickase expression and constitutive expression of guide RNAs to induce somatic mutations in 4 genes commonly mutated in human colon cancer, including Apc, Pten, Trp53, and Smad4.1,31,32 The PPAS;icCas9N mice develop adenomas and invasive adenocarcinomas throughout the small bowel and colon. In contrast to the Apcmin mouse, both adenomas and adenocarcinomas were commonly observed in the PPAS;icCas9N colon. Also, sequence analysis revealed that each tumor (among and within mice) has unique mutation patterns and mutation frequencies (Figure 2, Figure 3). We have shown that this model can recapitulate mutational events in a temporally regulated manner and can also be used to elucidate the mechanisms by which novel genes such as PLAGL2 play a role in colon cancer pathogenesis. Finally, we showed that PLAGL2 overexpression increases colon tumor size and results in a trend towards an increase in colon tumor numbers.

We chose to induce somatic mutations in Pten, Trp53, and Smad4, as well as Apc, because all have been shown to be frequently mutated in human colon cancer and play a role in its pathogenesis31,33 because of key gatekeeper functions regulating canonical oncogenic pathways. For example, germline PTEN mutations result in hamartomatous polyp syndromes. PTEN inactivation by hypermethylation has been observed in microsatellite instability (MSI)-high sporadic CRC,34 and mutations are also found in 9% of CRCs.35 PTEN suppresses the activity of PI3 kinase and its downstream effectors, AKT and mTORC1, which are known to drive cancer development and progression. Mutations in PTEN disrupt this critical suppressor activity36 with homozygous deletion of Pten in the mouse germline, leading to embryonic lethality.37 SMAD4, a member of the transforming growth factor (TGF)-ß superfamily, is frequently mutated in sporadic CRC.19,38,39 Loss of SMAD4 expression correlates with poor prognosis in CRC, including invasive adenocarcinoma, lymph node, and systemic metastasis.40, 41, 42, 43 TP53, one of the most commonly mutated tumor suppressor genes in human cancers,44 is activated during DNA damage and is responsible for cell cycle arrest, senescence, apoptosis, and cell removal and repair.45, 46, 47 These 4 tumor suppressor genes are ideal targets because indel mutations induced by Cas9D10A via specific gRNAs in this model trigger loss of function.

In addition, Let-7 miRNAs also have been shown to interact with each of the associated signal pathways including Wnt, TP53, Bmp/TGF-β, and PI3-kinase pathways. For example, TP53 affects Let-7 function by modulating Let7 miRNA transcription and processing.28,29 PTEN inactivation leads to upregulation of PLAGL2 in polyps from PTEN-deficient mice.30

A relatively low number of tumors was generated in this model after 2 weeks of dox treatment. One of the trade-offs of using a dox-inactivated repressor (tTS) system is the lower maximal induction possible with this system, because tTS inactivation is unlikely to reach 100%, Also, a small subset of icCas9/PPAS mice did not develop intestinal tumors despite the administration of dox chow. It is possible that somatic gene mutation did occur but in rare circumstances simply did not progress to tumor formation, or mutations did not occur in stem cells. However, this is unlikely because we did not detect mutations in the 4 target genes in normal intestine or colon. It is also possible that mutations were induced but that DNA repair occurred or that abnormal cells were eliminated after the dox treatment period. This does not appear to affect the usefulness of our model because we observed tumor formation in the vast majority of dox-treated mice.

The number of tumors per mouse increased in mice treated with dox for 3 weeks compared with mice that received dox for 2 weeks, suggesting that the longer treatment period increases the likelihood of mutagenesis, perhaps through increased exposure of target gene sequences to Cas9D10A and gRNA RNPs, or perhaps through increased exposure in stem cells. Tumors occurred in all segments of the intestine, from duodenum to distal colon, and there was no significant difference in their distribution among mice. We were unable to determine whether individual tumor cells have heterozygous or homozygous mutations in the various target genes because we performed bulk genomic analysis of DNA isolated from the entire tumor. Single cell genotyping would be required for such an assessment.

Intestinal overexpression of PLAGL2 in the TAM inducible Vil-LCL-Plagl2-mouse increased mRNA expression of the known PLAGL2 target Ascl2 in normal intestine as per Figure 4I, consistent with our previous studies, which showed that PLAGL2 promotes stem cell identity, at least in part through direct transcriptional activation of ASCL2.12 ASCL2 is expressed in gut stem cells and is required for intestinal epithelial stem cell specification and maintenance, via interaction with TCF4 and β-catenin to transactivate Lgr5 and Sox9.48 Increased Ascl2 expression is consistent with a possible role for PLAGL2 in maintaining stem cell identity in normal epithelium; however, Ascl2 mRNA expression was not further increased in icCas9N;PPAS;Vil-LCL-PLAGL2 compared with icCas9N;PPAS tumors.

PLAGL2 overexpression in the intestines of Vil-LCL-PLAGL2 mice without mutation in Apc, Trp53, Pten, and Smad4, did not induce small bowel or colon tumors, suggesting that PLAGL2 overexpression alone may be insufficient to initiate adenoma formation or adenocarcinoma, at least in the timeframe examined.

However, PLAGL2 overexpression in the icCas9N;PPAS intestine resulted in increased colon tumor number and size. This was accompanied by an increase in the rate of Smad4 mutation as well as increased Tnfrsf19 expression in colon tumors. TNFRSF19 has been recognized as a colon cancer stem cell marker, highly expressed in PROM1/CD133, CD44, and LGR5 enriched human colon cancer stem cells; TNFRSF19 has been shown to be expressed at much higher levels in colon cancer stem cells compared with all other colon cancer cell types.49 Our observation that Tnfrsf19 expression is significantly increased in PLAGL2-overexpressing tumors suggests increased tumor stemness, consistent with our previous observations of an increased stem cell signature in normal epithelium as noted above and previously.12 Also, others have shown an interaction between TNFRSF19 and TGFβ signaling in nasopharyngeal cancer. Increased TNFRSF19 expression inhibits TGFβ signaling by interacting with TGFβ receptor type 1; TNFRSF19 binds to and inhibits the kinase domain of TGFβR1, blocking Smad2/3 association and signal transduction, thus promoting tumorigenesis.50 Mutations in Smad4 were increased in PLAGL2 overexpressing tumors, providing additional support for TGFβ inhibition as at least part of the mechanism for increased tumor growth from PLAGL2 overexpression. If TNFRSF19 plays a role downstream of PLAGL2 in driving tumorigenesis in our model, perhaps tumor growth/formation is fueled by dual inhibition of TGFβ signaling; mutations in Smad4 and concomitant inhibition of TGFβR1 may supercharge the blockade of TGFβ signaling and promote the likelihood of tumor formation. PLAGL2 overexpression did not affect small bowel tumor number, and we observed a small (∼2 mm2) decrease in tumor size. In contrast to colon tumors, TNFRSF19 expression and SMAD4 mutation rates were unchanged in small bowel tumors. Future experiments will focus on further analysis of these signaling pathways in icCas9N;PPAS;Vil-LCL-PLAGL2 mice, and we will explore the mechanisms for their differential regulation in PLAGL2 overexpressing small bowel vs colon tumors.

The robust induction of endogenous mouse Plagl2 in tumors may underlie the observation that PLAGL2 over-expression did not greatly fuel tumor incidence or progression (Figure 3C). Because Let-7 reduction is very prevalent in colon cancer, in addition to other cancers, upregulation of PLAGL2 may also occur in the course of canonical tumorigenesis via the inactivation of the key tumor suppressors targeted here. PLAGL2 loss-of-function studies via inactivation of the mouse Plagl2 gene, in the context of the icCas9N;PPAS model, will help elucidate such a role in this mouse model.

In summary, we have developed a novel mouse model of colon cancer using drug-inducible CRISPR/Cas technology, which facilitates the targeting of multiple tumor suppressor/other genes with roles in colorectal carcinogenesis, thus more closely recapitulating human CRC pathogenesis. Our studies of PLAGL2 have demonstrated that this mouse model may be used to investigate the role of novel genes in human colon cancer in the setting of mutations in multiple genes of interest, providing a targeted approach and an environment that more effectively recapitulates the molecular events in human small bowel and colon tumorigenesis.

Methods

Mice

Mouse research was performed according to the ARRIVE guidelines. We generated 3 novel mouse models including: (1) gut-specific Cas9 expressing transgenic mice (icCas9); (2) mice that constitutively drive guide (g) RNA expression (PPAS); and (3) mice with gut-specific overexpression of PLAGL2 (Vil-LCL-PLAGL2). DNA constructs for pro-nuclear injection were generated by standard molecular cloning techniques, including linearization and pronuclear injection, to achieve random integration. Male and female mice were included in all studies. All mice were maintained on the C57BL/6 background.

All mice were generated with the support of the Mouse Genetics Core of the Digestive Diseases Research Core Center, Washington University School of Medicine, and the Genome Engineering and Stem Cell Center, Washington University School of Medicine.

icCas9N mice

To drive inducible, intestine specific expression of Cas9, we generated mice that use the gut-specific villin promoter to drive a dox-dependent activator (rtTAM2) and a dox-inactivated repressor (tTSKid). The presence of both the dox-dependent activator and inactivated repressor elements produced tight, non-leaky Cas9 expression (Figure 1B). A Cre-ERT2 cassette was included so that this mouse could be used for future studies of additional candidate genes targeted in other tissue specific knockout mouse lines.

PPAS mice

We generated transgenic mice that use an RNA polymerase III (Pol III) U6 promoter to drive expression of 8 gRNA pairs, with each pair individually targeting the Apc, Pten, Smad4, and Trp53 tumor suppressors.

Vil-LCL-PLAG2 mice

The gut-specific villin promoter was linked to a loxP flanked CreERT2 cassette followed by HA-tagged Plagl2, generating a self-activating, all-in-one Vil-LCL-Plagl2 mouse. Treatment with TAM results in inducible, small bowel and colon-specific HA-tagged Plagl2 overexpression.

Breeding and Genotyping

icCas9N mice were bred to PPAS mice to generate icCas9N;PPAS mice. icCas9N;PPAS mice were bred to Vil-LCL-PLAGL2 mice to generate icCas9N;PPAS;Vil-LCL-PLAGL2 mice, Mice were genotyped at postnatal day (d) 6 (TransnetYX) (primers are in Table 4).

Doxycycline and Tamoxifen Administration

icCas9N:PPAS mice were fed standard mouse chow containing dox (Envigo, 6000 Doxycycline TD.01533) for 2 weeks (w) or 3 w, beginning at age 6 w. Body weights were measured 2 times per week after dox. The mice fed dox chow for 2 w were sacrificed at ages 12 w to 39 w to determine the optimal time to detect tumorigenesis. Based on these results, mice were then fed dox chow for 3 w and were sacrificed at 16 w. Three w of dox ingestion resulted in robust small bowel and colon tumorigenesis. For experiments comparing tumorigenesis in icCas9N;PPAS and icCas9N;PPAS;Vil-LCL-PLAGL2 mice, dox chow was fed for 3 w beginning at age 6 w. icCas9N;PPAS (control) and icCas9N;PPAS;Vil-LCL-PLAGL2 mice were also administered TAM via intraperitoneal injection (250 mg/kg/day administered on day 49 and day 51; Cayman). TAM was diluted with peanut oil and adjusted to 25 mg/mL concentration. Mice were sacrificed at 16 w.

Tumor Examination and Tissue Collection

After euthanizing the mice via isoflurane inhalation (Isothesia, Patterson Veterinary), whole intestines were removed and rinsed with cold phosphate buffered saline (PBS). Intestines were cut longitudinally and observed under the dissecting microscope. Tumor numbers were counted, and each tumor was visualized and recorded by digital imaging for quantitation of tumor size. Tumors were removed from normal adjacent intestine, divided and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, and frozen in liquid nitrogen for DNA, RNA, and protein extraction. Normal adjacent duodenum, jejunum, ileum, cecum, proximal colon, mid colon, and distal colon were also fixed and frozen for histologic analysis and DNA, RNA, and protein extraction.

Histology and Immunohistochemistry/Immunofluorescence

Intestines were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde overnight at 40 C and washed in PBS prior to dehydration and paraffin embedding. Sections (5–6μ) were prepared from paraffin blocks for histologic analysis. Sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin for routine histology.

For immunohistochemical and immunofluorescence analyses, antigen retrieval was performed with Diva Decloaker (BIOcare Medical) in a pressure cooker for 30 minutes. Sections were washed 3 times in ddH2O for 5 minutes each and incubated in 3% hydrogen peroxide (30% hydrogen peroxide [HX0635-3 Millipore] diluted with ddH2O) for 15 minutes at room temperature. Sections were washed twice in ddH2O for 5 minutes each and in PBS-T once for 5 minutes. SuperBlock (PBS) Blocking Buffer was used according to the manufacturer's protocol (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Antibodies include β-catenin rabbit monoclonal antibody (mAb) (Cell Signaling Technology, D10A8, 1:200), Smad4 rabbit polyclonal antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, #9515, 1:200), and anti HA-tag rabbit mAb (Cell Signaling Technology, C29F4, 1:200)

Sections were incubated in a humidified chamber with primary antibody diluted in Da Vinci Green Buffer (Biocare Medical) overnight at 4 °C. Sections were then washed 3 times in PBS-T for 5 minutes each. Rabbit-HRP polymer (Biocare Medical) was added and incubated in a humidified chamber for 30 minutes at room temperature, and sections were washed 3 times in PBS-T for 5 minutes each. Diaminobenzidine (DAB; Betazid DAB, Biocare Medical) was used for colorimetric detection. Sections were counterstained with hematoxylin.

For immunofluorescence analyses, secondary antibodies were applied in blocking buffer for 1 hour at room temperature and sections counterstained with DAPI. Secondary antibody for immunofluorescence was AlexaFluoro 568 (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Histologic Analysis of Tumors and Criteria for Identification of Adenoma and Adenocarcinoma

Histologic criteria for the identification of invasive adenocarcinoma and grading of dysplasia in adenomas in mice were based on criteria recommended by the World Health Organization (WHO) for human adenomas and adenocarcinomas.51 Briefly, adenoma of small intestine and/or colorectum is a benign, premalignant neoplasm composed of dysplastic epithelium. Adenoma, by definition, has low-grade dysplasia in epithelium in which dysplastic epithelial cells have elongated, hyperchromatic nuclei with homogeneous, smudged chromatin and abundant mitotic activity. Nuclei are relatively uniform in size, showing pseudostratification, and sitting on the basement membrane without loss of polarity. In adenoma with high-grade dysplasia, the dysplastic epithelial cells have enlarged, hyperchromatic nuclei with marked nuclear pleomorphism, stratification, and loss of polarity. Adenoma with high-grade dysplasia also demonstrates complex cribriform architecture and/or near-solid growth of dysplastic epithelial cells. Invasive adenocarcinoma of small intestine and/or colorectum is a malignant epithelial tumor composed of tumor cells and tumor glands with glandular or mucinous differentiation and demonstrated features of destruction of non-neoplastic/normal structure (ie, invasion).

DNA and RNA Extraction From Mouse Intestines

Intestinal tissues or tumors were placed into prefilled bead tube kits (Benchmark Scientific, D1033-28) and homogenized in DNA/RNA extracting solution (Qiagen, AllPrep DNA/RNA Mini kit) with 1% 2-mercaptoethanol after removal from liquid nitrogen. Tissues were homogenized with BeadBug homogenizer (BenchMark Scientific) for 30 seconds at 4000 rpm. Tubes were centrifuged for 3 minutes at 13000 rpm and supernatants transferred to additional 2 mL tube. DNA and RNA extraction were performed according to Qiagen AllPrep DNA/RNA Mini kit instruction manual. Nucleotide concentrations were determined by Nanodrop2000 (Thermo Scientific).

Reverse Transcription and qRT-PCR

cDNA was prepared from total RNA (1 μg) using SuperScript IV Reverse Transcriptase (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat # 18090010) with Oligo(dT)20 Primer (Invitrogen, #18418020). qRT-PCR was performed using Bullseye Real Time qPCR Master Mix (Midsci, Cat# BEQPCR-S) by CFX Connect Real-Time PCR Detection System (Biorad). Primers are listed in Table 5. PCR was performed in triplicate. Median cycle number was used for analysis. 2-ΔΔCT was calculated, and 18s rRNA or GAPDH was used as a loading control (housekeeping gene).

Statistical Analysis

The results are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean. All graphs are plotted as dot plots to show each data point. The Welch t-test was used to compare the means in 2 groups.

For analysis of tumor burden following 2 or 3 w of dox treatment, the minimum, maximum, and median number of tumors and the interquartile range were calculated, and we employed a non-parametric Wilcoxon 2-sample test to compare the number of tumors between 2 and 3 w in small intestine, colon, and the entire intestine combined.

Tumor number in in icCas9N;PPAS vs. icCas9N;PPAS;Vil-LCL-PLAGL2 mice was compared using a non-parametric Wilcoxon 2-sample t-test. Tumor size (area) in in icCas9N;PPAS vs. icCas9N;PPAS;Vil-LCL-PLAGL2 mice was compared by mixed effect regression analysis.

Mutation rate in Apc, Pten, Smad4 and Trp53 was compared in icCas9N;PPAS vs icCas9N;PPAS;Vil-LCL-PLAGL2 mice. These data were not normally distributed, so we used nonparametric Wilcoxon rank sums test to compare mutation rates in the 2 groups of mice. Statistical significance was determined as P < .05. Symbols for P values are as follows: ∗P < .05, †P < .01, and ‡ P < .005. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS.
