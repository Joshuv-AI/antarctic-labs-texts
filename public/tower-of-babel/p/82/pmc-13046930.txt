Perspectives and Real-World Clinical Practice of Portuguese Dermatologists on Biologic Dose Spacing in Psoriasis: Results from a Nationwide Survey
Authors: César Ferreira, Tiago Torres
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionBiologic therapies have transformed the management of moderate-to-severe psoriasis but are associated with long-term treatment burden and substantial healthcare costs. Dose spacing, defined as extending dosing intervals in patients with controlled disease, has emerged as a potential optimization strategy. However, data on real-world implementation and clinician perspectives remain limited.MethodsWe conducted a national, cross-sectional survey among Portuguese dermatologists experienced in prescribing biologic therapies for psoriasis. An anonymous, web-based questionnaire assessed clinicians’ perspectives and real-world practices regarding biologic dose spacing, including eligibility criteria, preferred biologic classes, implementation strategies, outcomes after loss of disease control, and limiting clinical factors.ResultsSeventy-five dermatologists completed the survey (response rate 48.4%). All respondents considered dose spacing feasible. The most frequently cited eligibility criteria were absolute Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI) ≤ 1, body surface area (BSA) ≤ 1%, and a 90% improvement in PASI (PASI 90). Interleukin-23 (IL-23) inhibitors were perceived as the most suitable class for dose spacing (93.3%). In routine practice, dose spacing was applied frequently by 30.7% of respondents and occasionally by 48.0%. Most clinicians (69.3%) required more than 12 months of sustained disease control before initiating dose spacing, predominantly using progressive extension of dosing intervals (96.0%). IL-23 inhibitors were the biologics most frequently dose-spaced in current practice. Following loss of disease control, 86.7% reported successful recapture of response after reintroduction of standard dosing. The main factors limiting dose spacing were a history of difficult-to-control psoriasis (77.3%) and concomitant psoriatic arthritis (72.0%).ConclusionBiologic dose spacing is already integrated into clinical practice in Portugal. Further prospective studies are needed to establish standardized criteria and guide safe implementation.Keywords: Biologic therapy, Dose spacing, Drug administration schedule, Questionnaires, Psoriasis, Real-world evidence

Introduction

Psoriasis is a chronic, immune-mediated inflammatory disease with a substantial impact on quality of life and frequent association with several comorbidities [1]. Advances in the understanding of its pathogenesis, particularly the role of tumour necrosis factor (TNF), interleukin-17 (IL-17), and interleukin-23 (IL-23), have driven the development of highly effective biologic therapies, which have transformed the management of moderate-to-severe psoriasis. However, this therapeutic progress has been accompanied by a significant and sustained economic impact on healthcare systems [1, 2].

Biologic agents currently approved for psoriasis include TNF inhibitors (adalimumab, infliximab, certolizumab, and etanercept), interleukin-12/23 (IL-12/23) inhibitors (ustekinumab), IL-17 inhibitors (secukinumab, ixekizumab, brodalumab and bimekizumab), and IL-23 inhibitors (guselkumab, risankizumab, and tildrakizumab) [3, 4].

With the growing availability and long-term use of biologics, the concept of dose spacing has emerged as a pragmatic strategy aimed at optimizing biologic therapy. This approach involves extending the interval between administrations in patients with well-controlled disease, aiming to reduce costs and treatment burden, minimize adverse effects, and improve treatment adherence [5, 6].

Recent studies have shown that, in appropriately selected patients, dose spacing can maintain clinical control without compromising efficacy, particularly with IL-17 and IL-23 inhibitors [5–7].

Thus, dose spacing of biologic therapies represents a promising and increasingly patient-centered strategy, aligned with the pursuit of more sustainable treatment approaches.

However, gaps remain regarding the standardization of eligibility criteria and implementation strategies, underscoring the need for further research and specific guidance [6, 8].

Consequently, real-world data exploring current clinical attitudes and practices are essential to inform future research, support consensus development, and guide the rational integration of dose spacing into personalized psoriasis management.

The aim of this study was to systematically characterize real-world clinical attitudes and practices regarding dose spacing in psoriasis treatment, focusing on prevailing trends, perceived benefits and risks, and factors influencing decision-making among Portuguese dermatologists.

Materials and Methods

A national, cross-sectional survey was conducted among dermatologists practicing in Portugal with experience in prescribing biologic therapies for psoriasis. The survey was specifically developed for this study by the authors and was informed by previously published surveys and real-world studies addressing biologic dose tapering and spacing in psoriasis. The study aimed to explore clinicians’ perspectives and real-world clinical practices regarding biologic dose spacing in patients with psoriasis.

Data were collected using a secure, web-based questionnaire platform, ensuring structured data capture and anonymous participation. Responses were anonymized through automatically generated unique identifiers, with no collection of personally identifiable information.

In Portugal, ethical approval by a Research Ethics Committee is legally required for studies within the scope of clinical research, as defined by Law No. 21/2014 of 16 April (Lei da Investigação Clínica). This law applies to interventional and non-interventional clinical studies conducted in human participants or involving individual health data. The present study consisted of an anonymous, voluntary, web-based survey of healthcare professionals and did not involve patients, clinical interventions, biological samples, or the collection of individual health data. As such, it does not meet the legal definition of a clinical study under Law No. 21/2014 and therefore does not require prior approval. The study was conducted in accordance with general ethical principles and the Declaration of Helsinki.

Therapeutic dose spacing was defined as the “extension of the interval between biologic administrations.” The questionnaire did not capture exact interval durations in weeks or months, as extension strategies varied according to biologic class, baseline dosing schedule, and individual patient characteristics. The survey instrument consisted of a structured, anonymous, web-based questionnaire specifically developed for this study, with an estimated completion time of < 5 min. The questionnaire comprised 19 items organized into four predefined sections. The first section included five items addressing participant demographics and clinical practice characteristics, including age, sex, and practice setting (public, private, or mixed). Respondents were also asked to self-assess their level of experience with biologic therapy using a 0–5 Likert scale and to report whether they had access to a dedicated psoriasis clinic within their practice.

The second section (4 questions) explored clinicians’ perspectives beyond routine practice was also explored by assessing the perceived feasibility of implementing dose spacing, the clinical outcome measures and treatment targets used to support this decision, the biologic classes in which dose spacing would be considered (TNF, IL-17, IL-12/23, and IL-23 inhibitors), and the pharmacological characteristics perceived to favour dose spacing within specific classes.

The third section comprised eight items focusing on real-world implementation and clinical decision-making. Respondents were asked to report the criteria they most commonly use to define controlled psoriasis, the frequency with which they apply dose spacing in their own practice, the duration of sustained disease control they typically require before initiating this strategy, and the practical approaches adopted for its implementation. Respondents were also asked to indicate the biologic agents to which they had applied dose spacing at any point in their clinical practice, as well as those they currently space most frequently. Additional items explored management strategies following loss of disease control after dose spacing and the clinical factors considered contraindications to this approach.

Finally, the questionnaire assessed clinicians' overall perceptions of the adequacy of the existing scientific evidence on biologic dose spacing and their views on the role of this strategy in contemporary psoriasis management. This final section included two items.

Question formats included multiple-choice, single-best-answer, and Likert-scale responses. All questionnaire items were mandatory, and predefined response options were used to ensure consistency of responses across respondents.

All analyses were performed using International Business Machines, Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (IBM SPSS) Statistics for Windows, version 31.0.1.0. Survey responses were analyzed using descriptive statistics and are presented as absolute numbers and corresponding percentages. All questionnaire items were mandatory; where applicable, multiple responses were permitted for individual questions.

Results

A total of 155 Portuguese dermatologists with experience in prescribing biologic therapies for psoriasis were invited to participate. Seventy-five completed questionnaires were received and included in the analysis, corresponding to a response rate of 48.4%. The median age of respondents was 45.0 years, and 53.3% (n = 40) were female. Most respondents reported mixed public-private clinical practice, 64.0% (n = 48), whereas 28.0% (n = 21) worked exclusively in the private setting and 8.0% (n = 6) exclusively within the Portuguese National Health Service. Regarding experience with biologic therapy, 89.3% (n = 67) of dermatologists rated themselves at levels 4–5 on a 0 to 5 scale, whereas 10.7% (n = 8) reported levels 0–3. Additionally, 42.7% (n = 32) stated that they had access to a dedicated psoriasis clinic.

Clinicians’ Perspectives Regarding Biologic Dose Spacing in Patients with Psoriasis

All respondents (n = 75) considered biologic dose spacing a feasible strategy for patients with well-controlled psoriasis. When asked which criteria could support eligibility for dose spacing, clinicians most frequently identified Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI) ≤ 1 58.7% (n = 44), body surface area (BSA) ≤ 1% 56.0% (n = 42), and a 90% improvement in PASI (PASI 90) 56.0% (n = 42). Less frequently selected criteria included Dermatology Life Quality Index (DLQI) ≤ 3 37.3% (n = 28), Physician Global Assessment (PGA) ≤ 1 34.7% (n = 26), and PASI ≤ 3 30.7% (n = 23) (Fig. 1).

When asked about therapeutic classes perceived as most suitable for dose spacing, IL-23 inhibitors were the most frequently selected at 93.3% (n = 70), followed by IL-17 inhibitors at 72.0% (n = 54) and IL-12/23 inhibitors at 57.3% (n = 43). TNF inhibitors were the least frequently chosen class for this strategy at 38.7% (n = 29). The factors perceived to favour dose spacing were sustained drug efficacy at 77.3% (n = 58), mechanism of action at 49.3% (n = 37), dosing schedule at 46.7% (n = 35), risk of immunogenicity at 37.3% (n = 28), and safety profile at 28.0% (n = 21) (Table 1).

Real-World Clinical Practices Regarding Biologic Dose Spacing in Patients with Psoriasis

In routine clinical practice, dose spacing was variably adopted: 48.0% (n = 36) of respondents reported using it occasionally, 30.7% (n = 23) frequently, and 16.0% (n = 12) rarely, while 5.3% (n = 4) indicated that they never apply this strategy.

When stratified by experience with biologics, respondents with higher experience levels (4–5) usually reported adopting dose spacing frequently or occasionally 80.6% (n = 54), rarely 13.4% (n = 9), and never 6.0% (n = 4); in contrast, among respondents with lower experience levels (≤ 3), frequent and rare uses were equally reported (37.5%, n = 3), followed by occasional use (25.0%, n = 2).

Regarding the duration of disease control required before initiating dose spacing, most commonly defined as PASI ≤ 1 and BSA ≤ 1%; 69.3% (n = 52) of respondents reported initiating dose spacing only after > 12 months of sustained disease control, whereas 21.3% (n = 16) reported waiting between 6 and 12 months. Only 4.0% (n = 3) reported initiating dose spacing after < 6 months of disease control, and no respondent indicated initiating dose spacing immediately after achieving disease control (Table 2).

The preferred strategy for implementing dose spacing was progressive extension of the dosing interval, selected by 96.0% (n = 72). Treatment discontinuation with on-demand reinitiation was selected by 12.0% (n = 9), while discontinuation with reintroduction after relapse was reported by 5.3% (n = 4).

When asked to identify all biologic agents for which they had ever applied dose spacing, regardless of frequency, IL-23 inhibitors were most frequently reported, including risankizumab 68.0% (n = 51) and guselkumab 53.3% (n = 40). Among TNF inhibitors, adalimumab had been spaced by 50.7% (n = 38) of respondents, whereas etanercept 16.0% (n = 12) and infliximab 5.3% (n = 4) were less frequently spaced. Ustekinumab was reported as having been spaced by 49.3% (n = 37). Among IL-17 inhibitors, secukinumab and ixekizumab had been spaced at least once by 33.3% (n = 25) of respondents, while brodalumab was reported by 22.7% (n = 17) (Fig. 2).

Comparison of biologics ever dose-spaced and those currently most frequently dose-spaced in real-world clinical practice. Results are presented as the proportion of respondents reporting dose spacing for each biologic

When respondents were asked which biologic they currently space most frequently, IL-23 inhibitors again predominated, with risankizumab reported by 57.3% (n = 43) and guselkumab by 49.3% (n = 37). Adalimumab was the third most frequently spaced agent 32.0% (n = 24), followed by ustekinumab 21.3% (n = 16). IL-17 inhibitors were less frequently reported in current spacing practice, including brodalumab 13.3% (n = 10), secukinumab 12.0% (n = 9), and ixekizumab 10.7% (n = 8) (Fig. 2).

Following loss of disease control after dose spacing (not defined by a prespecified numerical threshold but referred to the clinician’s judgement of loss of adequate disease control after dose spacing), 86.7% (n = 65) of respondents reported successful recapture of therapeutic response after returning to the standard dosing regimen. In 14.7% (n = 11) of cases, the same biologic was not reintroduced, and 5.3% (n = 4) reported loss of efficacy upon reinitiation (Table 2).

The most commonly reported factors limiting the implementation of dose spacing were a previous history of difficult-to-control psoriasis (77.3%, n = 58) and the presence of psoriatic arthritis (72.0%, n = 54) (Table 3).

Clinical factors limiting the implementation of biologic dose spacing in routine practice

Regarding the perceived strength of the available scientific evidence supporting biologic dose spacing, 60.0% (n = 45) considered it insufficient to formally guide this practice. However, 58.7% (n = 44) considered that this strategy provides meaningful benefits and warrants further study, 40.0% (n = 30) reported that it may be useful when applied with caution, and 1.3% (n = 1) considered dose spacing exclusively in exceptional circumstances.

Discussion

The present analysis shows that Portuguese dermatologists universally recognize therapeutic dose spacing of biologics as a viable strategy in patients with controlled psoriasis, aligning with international trends aimed at optimizing treatment, reducing costs, and minimizing adverse effects. Nearly half of Portuguese dermatologists reported occasionally adopting dose spacing, and about one-third reported doing so frequently, indicating that this approach is already partially integrated into psoriasis management in Portugal. When stratified by experience with use of biologic therapy, most dermatologists with greater familiarity with biologics reported adopting dose spacing either occasionally or frequently, indicating that most experienced clinicians integrate this strategy into routine practice, whereas less experienced clinicians showed a more heterogeneous distribution of practices. This pattern may reflect a more structured and cautious implementation of dose-spacing strategies among more experienced practitioners [3].

The most commonly used criteria to define eligibility for dose spacing/disease control—PASI ≤ 1, BSA ≤ 1%, and PASI 90—reflect a preference for stringent markers of true clinical disease control when considering dose spacing. In contrast, quality-of-life measures such as DLQI ≤ 3 were less frequently used, possibly reflecting the recognition that patient-reported outcomes may underestimate ongoing disease activity in individuals who have adapted to their disease burden despite persistent clinical activity.

Comparison between biologics that have ever been dose-spaced and those currently most frequently dose-spaced (see Fig. 2 for details) reveals a clear refinement in real-world clinical practice, allowing visualization of how dose-spacing practices have evolved from exploratory use across multiple biologic classes to a more selective and evidence-driven approach.

Although dose spacing has been attempted across all biologic classes, current strategies are predominantly focused on IL-23 inhibitors, reflecting a shift away from exploratory approaches toward a more selective, confidence-driven application. In contrast, TNF inhibitors and the IL-12/23 inhibitor ustekinumab show a marked reduction in use from historical to current levels, indicating progressive deprioritization of sustained dose-spacing strategies. A similar decline is observed among IL-17 inhibitors, supporting the view that this class is not considered a primary target for dose spacing in routine practice.

The preference for IL-23 inhibitors as the biologic class most suitable for dose spacing is supported by accumulating evidence demonstrating high durability of response, sustained disease control after treatment withdrawal, convenient dosing schedules, and favourable safety profiles. In addition, emerging data suggest that IL-23 blockade may affect tissue-resident memory T cells—key drivers of psoriasis recurrence—further supporting the suitability of this class compared with TNF and IL-17 inhibitors. Moreover, clinical trial data, such as those from the GUIDE study, demonstrate that extending the dosing interval of guselkumab to 16 weeks in “super-responders” maintains disease control without increased adverse events [9].

These findings are further reinforced by real-world evidence showing that a substantial proportion of patients treated with risankizumab or guselkumab can successfully extend dosing intervals beyond the approved label while maintaining sustained clinical remission, supporting the feasibility of dose spacing within this therapeutic class [7, 9–11].

IL-17 inhibitors and IL-12/23 inhibitors were regarded as intermediate options, whereas TNF inhibitors were ranked lowest, reflecting their greater interindividual variability in response, lower long-term durability, and higher immunogenicity, as described in the literature [12–16].

Compared with an earlier international survey from 2020, the reduced role attributed to TNF inhibitors in our cohort underscores an evolution in clinical practice, likely driven by the widespread adoption of newer biologic classes and accumulated real-world experience [8].

The near-universal preference for progressive extension of the dosing interval (96.0%) suggests that clinicians favour gradual, adaptive strategies rather than more abrupt approaches such as treatment discontinuation. Taken together, these data indicate that dose spacing has moved beyond a conceptual notion and is increasingly implemented as a pragmatic, cautiously applied strategy in routine clinical practice.

Importantly, when relapse occurred after spacing, most clinicians reported successful recapture of therapeutic response upon return to the standard regimen (86.7%). This finding supports the perceived safety of the strategy, although a minority did observe loss of efficacy after re-initiation, underscoring the need for careful patient selection.

The presence of psoriatic arthritis and a history of difficult-to-control disease were the main factors discouraging dose spacing. These findings are consistent with existing literature, which suggests greater clinical instability in these subgroups, for whom fixed-dosing regimens may be preferable [7, 10]. It should be noted that the questionnaire did not formally differentiate between mild, well-controlled psoriatic arthritis and active articular disease. Therefore, the reported reluctance to implement dose spacing in the presence of psoriatic arthritis likely reflects concern regarding patients with clinically active or unstable joint involvement, in whom treatment de-escalation may carry a higher risk of relapse or irreversible joint damage.

Importantly, this underscores that dose spacing should not be applied universally and highlights patient selection as a critical determinant of success, identifying specific clinical phenotypes in which dose spacing may be inappropriate or require heightened caution.

Finally, the perception that the current scientific evidence is insufficient to formally guide dose spacing—reported by 60.0% of respondents—highlights the need for prospective, comparative, and controlled studies capable of establishing standardized eligibility criteria and clear clinical algorithms. Nonetheless, 58.7% of respondents acknowledged clear potential benefits, suggesting that clinicians recognize the value of this strategy despite current limitations in the evidence base. The view that spacing “may be useful but should be applied with caution” (40.0%) accurately reflects the current state of knowledge: an emerging and promising practice that remains insufficiently anchored by normative guidance.

Dose spacing carries broader clinical implications, including potential reductions in treatment burden, increased patient adherence, and health-related quality of life. These aspects are particularly relevant in chronic, lifelong conditions such as psoriasis, where sustained treatment intensity may not always be necessary once stable disease control is achieved.

Beyond disease control, dose spacing has broader clinical implications, including a reduction in treatment burden, which may translate into improved adherence and enhanced health-related quality of life. These considerations are particularly relevant in a chronic, lifelong condition such as psoriasis, where sustained full-intensity treatment may not be necessary once stable disease control has been achieved. At a health system level, dose spacing should also be viewed within the context of healthcare sustainability. The increasing reliance on high-cost biologic therapies places growing pressure on publicly funded healthcare systems. In this context, clinically appropriate dose optimization—guided by careful patient selection and close monitoring—may represent a responsible balance between individual patient benefit and the sustainable use of healthcare resources.

In summary, from a practical standpoint, these findings support the view that dose spacing should not be considered a universal strategy, but rather a selective, patient-specific intervention [17]. Stringent objective disease control, absence of psoriatic arthritis, and biologics with durable pharmacodynamic profiles, particularly IL-23 inhibitors, appear to represent the best conditions for its consideration. Conversely, patients with unstable disease or significant extracutaneous involvement may require maintenance of standard dosing regimens.

Limitations

The interpretation of real-world attitudes and practices regarding dose spacing must consider the inherent limitations of survey-based research. While such studies provide valuable insight into contemporary clinical decision-making, they reflect clinicians’ perceptions and self-reported practices rather than objective treatment outcomes. The 48.4% response rate is comparable to that reported in a previously published international survey. Notably, although the proportional response rate is similar, the present study includes a higher absolute number of respondents, strengthening the robustness of the observations. Other important limitations include the cross-sectional design of the study and the potential for recall and reporting bias. In addition, the survey does not capture objective clinical outcomes or long-term patient-level data, limiting conclusions regarding effectiveness and safety. Finally, although the observed attitudes may be broadly applicable to countries with comparable healthcare systems and access to biologic therapies, the findings primarily reflect current clinical practice in Portugal and should be interpreted within this national context.

Conclusion

Overall, this study demonstrates that therapeutic dose spacing is already part of clinical practice in Portugal, although it is applied heterogeneously. The predominant use of IL-23 inhibitors and the preference for gradual dose interval extension suggest a progressive integration of this strategy into personalized management of patients with psoriasis.

Future prospective and pragmatic clinical trials are needed to define standardized, evidence-based dose-spacing algorithms that balance sustained disease control with long-term safety and healthcare sustainability.

These findings provide a timely overview of evolving real-world clinical behavior and may support future consensus initiatives, guideline development, and health policy discussions aimed at balancing therapeutic efficacy, safety, and sustainability.
