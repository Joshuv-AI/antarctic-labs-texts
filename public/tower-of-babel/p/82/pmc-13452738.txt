Radiographic Osteoarthritis and Patient-Reported Outcomes One Year Following Tibial Plateau Fracture Surgery
Authors: Leonardo Moreno-Degante, Bernardo Pettet-Ruiz, Jorge Ponce-de-Léon-Dominguez, Ernesto Torres-Casado, Paolo Chiquini-Ramírez, Hector-M Reyes-García, Mauricio Zarate-de-la-Torre, Alberto González-Díaz, Paulina Herrera-Novoa, Ernesto Roldan-Valadez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction: This study aimed to characterize early clinical, functional, and radiographic outcomes after surgical treatment of tibial plateau fractures and to identify independent predictors of moderate-to-severe post-traumatic knee osteoarthritis (Kellgren-Lawrence (KL) grades 3-4) at 12 months, with emphasis on the relative contribution of fracture morphology and radiographic osteoarthritis severity. Materials and methods: We conducted a retrospective analysis of prospectively collected clinical data from consecutive adults who underwent open reduction and internal fixation of tibial plateau fractures at a tertiary trauma center between March 2017 and June 2025, with twelve-month follow-up completed by mid-2026. Fractures were classified using the Schatzker system. At 12 months, patients underwent standardized clinical and imaging follow-up, including Rasmussen clinical and radiologic scores, KL grading, the Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (KOOS), and the 36-Item Short Form Health Survey (SF-36) physical and social functioning domains. Group comparisons between KL grades 0-2 and 3-4 were conducted using non-parametric tests. Multivariable logistic regression identified predictors of KL grades 3-4, and multivariable linear regression explored correlates of KOOS and SF-36 physical functioning. Results: Of 109 eligible patients, 106 (median age 43 years; 57.5% male) completed the 12-month evaluation. High-energy patterns predominated, with Schatzker types V-VI accounting for 51.8% of fractures. Median KOOS was 77; median SF-36 physical and social functioning scores were 80 and 88.5, respectively. While 80.2% of knees had KL grades 0-2, 19.8% already exhibited grades 3-4 and showed lower KOOS (median 63 vs 79) and lower SF-36 physical functioning (median 70 vs 82). In multivariable models, older age and female sex independently predicted KL grades 3-4, whereas fracture complexity did not. Moderate-to-severe osteoarthritis was the only variable independently associated with worse knee-specific and general physical function, corresponding to mean reductions of approximately 13 points in KOOS and 15 points in SF-36 physical functioning. No variable independently predicted Rasmussen clinical category. Conclusions: Most patients achieved satisfactory short-term outcomes after tibial plateau fracture surgery, yet nearly one-fifth exhibited moderate-to-severe radiographic osteoarthritis at the latest follow-up with clinically meaningful functional decrements. The Schatzker classification did not independently predict these outcomes, whereas early KL grades 3-4 emerged as the strongest correlate of functional impairment. Systematic integration of radiographic osteoarthritis grading with validated patient-reported outcome measures is essential for risk stratification and longitudinal follow-up planning.

Introduction

The tibiofemoral joint must simultaneously provide stability and a wide range of motion to support weight-bearing, gait, and rotational activities. This stability arises from the interplay between osseous morphology, articular cartilage, menisci, static ligamentous restraints, and dynamic muscular control [1-4]. Variations in tibial plateau geometry, such as slope and concavity, influence load distribution and ligament strain and have been linked to altered knee biomechanics and injury risk [3-6].

Tibial plateau fractures disrupt this finely tuned system and endanger both joint congruence and soft-tissue stabilizers. They account for approximately 1-2% of all fractures and constitute a heterogeneous group of injuries that range from low-energy split-depression patterns in osteoporotic bone to high-energy bicondylar fractures with severe comminution and soft-tissue compromise [7-9]. Population-based data from a CT-validated series reported an annual incidence of 10.3 per 100,000 inhabitants, with a predominance of high-energy injuries among younger men and an increasing incidence of low-energy fragility fractures among older women [7]. Motor-vehicle collisions and high-velocity trauma are especially associated with complex bicondylar and posteromedial fracture patterns [10-13].

Beyond the bony injury, tibial plateau fractures frequently involve the menisci, cruciate and collateral ligaments, and the posterolateral corner. Radiographic parameters such as condylar widening and articular depression are associated with a higher probability of associated soft-tissue damage, highlighting the limitations of plain radiographs in fully characterizing the injury [14,15]. Cross-sectional imaging refines this assessment: CT improves depiction of fracture morphology and guides preoperative planning, whereas MRI better identifies meniscal and ligamentous injuries that may alter the surgical strategy [15,16].

Historically, the Schatzker classification has been the most widely used system, grouping tibial plateau fractures into six types according to fracture line orientation and degree of depression [17,18]. Although intuitive and clinically useful, conventional two-dimensional classifications incompletely describe the complexity of many fracture patterns, particularly in the posterior quadrants and metaphyseal-diaphyseal regions [19,20]. CT-based schemes such as the three-column concept and four-quadrant surface mapping address these limitations by incorporating multiplanar morphology and posterior column involvement, thereby improving communication with surgeons and supporting more individualized fixation strategies [19-26].

Surgical management has evolved from traction and limited fixation to stable anatomical reduction with minimally invasive plate osteosynthesis and dedicated posteromedial, posterolateral, and posterior approaches [20,27-33]. Biomechanical studies continue to refine fixation strategies, particularly for posteromedial shear fragments and multiplanar patterns, emphasizing the need for column-based stabilization to restore condylar support and prevent secondary collapse [12,13,34]. Despite these advances, residual step-off, malalignment, and unrecognized soft-tissue injuries can compromise long-term joint health.

Clinical and radiological outcomes after operative treatment of tibial plateau fractures vary widely. Several cohorts have shown that many patients achieve acceptable functional scores, whereas others progress to stiffness, persistent pain, or post-traumatic osteoarthritis (PTOA) despite apparently satisfactory reductions [35-40]. Large registry-based analyses demonstrated an increased long-term risk of total knee arthroplasty compared with the general population, underscoring the potential for early degenerative change after these injuries [41,42]. More recently, retrospective series have highlighted a mismatch between radiological consolidation and patient-reported pain or function, suggesting that conventional radiographic endpoints may underestimate the true burden of disease [43,44].

PTOA after tibial plateau fracture is thought to arise from a combination of acute cartilage damage, residual joint incongruity, altered load transmission, and patient-specific susceptibility. Epidemiologic studies of post-traumatic hip and knee osteoarthritis have identified age, obesity, high-energy trauma, and baseline joint damage as relevant risk factors, but the relative contribution of fracture morphology and early surgical reconstruction remains incompletely defined [45,46]. At the same time, emerging work in quantitative imaging and AI aims to provide more reproducible grading of knee osteoarthritis severity and progression, potentially allowing finer characterization of early structural changes after trauma [47].

Given this complexity, patient-reported outcome measures are indispensable for capturing the clinical impact of tibial plateau fractures beyond what radiographs can reveal. The Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (KOOS) has demonstrated validity, responsiveness, and interpretability across a spectrum of knee injuries and early osteoarthritis, making it a suitable tool for evaluating symptoms, function in daily living, sport/recreation, and knee-related quality of life [48,49]. Generic instruments such as the 36-Item Short Form Health Survey (SF-36) complement disease-specific scales by summarizing global health-related quality of life, and the total SF-36 score has been proposed as a single, psychometrically sound measure in diverse populations [50,51]. However, prospective data that simultaneously integrate CT-based fracture severity, radiographic knee osteoarthritis grading, and validated patient-reported outcomes following tibial plateau fracture remain scarce, particularly in Latin American settings.

In this context, our study aims to evaluate clinical, functional, and radiological outcomes one year after surgical treatment of tibial plateau fractures in a tertiary Mexican referral center. Specifically, we seek to determine whether baseline fracture magnitude and morphology, as assessed by the Schatzker system with adjunctive CT when available, are associated with early PTOA and with patient-reported knee function and health-related quality of life. We hypothesize that greater initial articular damage and more complex column involvement will correlate with worse KOOS and SF-36 scores and with more pronounced radiographic degenerative changes at one year.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This study was a retrospective analysis of a prospectively maintained clinical cohort of consecutive adult patients who underwent surgical treatment for tibial plateau fractures at the Department of Traumatology of the Instituto Nacional de Rehabilitación, Mexico City, Mexico. The recruitment period extended from March 2017 to June 2025. The 12-month follow-up for the most recently enrolled participants was completed by mid-2026, and the analytical database was subsequently locked. Study conduct and reporting followed the recommendations of the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement.

Participants

Eligible patients were adults aged 18 years or older with a diagnosis of tibial plateau fracture confirmed on plain radiographs, skeletal maturity, and an indication for surgical management according to the attending trauma surgeon. Additional inclusion criteria were the absence of prior surgical treatment of the affected knee and an anticipated clinical and radiological follow-up of at least 12 months after surgery.

Exclusion criteria comprised conservative (nonoperative) management; peri-implant fractures or loosening of osteosynthesis material; prior osteomyelitis or active infection at the planned surgical site; associated fractures of the upper or lower limbs that could interfere with functional assessment; comorbidities such as poliomyelitis sequelae or other neurological diseases affecting gait; a documented history of ligamentous or meniscal injury in the affected knee; and loss to follow-up before the 12-month evaluation.

A total of 109 surgically treated tibial plateau fractures were identified during the study period. Three patients did not complete the 12-month clinical and radiographic evaluation or had missing key variables and were excluded. The final analytic cohort therefore comprised 106 patients with complete 12-month clinical, functional, and radiographic data. No imputation was performed; all analyses were conducted on complete cases.

Sample size

The minimum required sample size was calculated assuming a 24% prevalence of PTOA at 12 months, a 95% CI, and a tolerable absolute sampling error of 10 percentage points. Using the formula \begin{document} n = \frac{Z^2 p q}{e^2} \end{document}, where Z = 1.96, p = 0.24, q = 0.76, and e = 0.10, the estimated minimum sample size was 70 patients. Allowing for potential loss to follow-up, the planned recruitment range was 70-90 patients. The final analytic sample of 106 patients exceeded this minimum requirement.

Data sources and variables

Data were obtained from the institutional electronic medical record through the Hospital Automated Information System (Sistema Automatizado de Información Hospitalaria, SAIH). Demographic variables included age, sex, date of injury, and date of hospital admission. Perioperative variables comprised date of surgery, side of the operated knee, hospital length of stay, time to unassisted walking, comorbidities, and fracture classification.

The main exposure variable was fracture morphology, as defined by the Schatzker classification [17]. The Schatzker type assigned to each patient was corroborated by preoperative anteroposterior and lateral knee radiographs, admission and operative notes, and, when available, preoperative CT. For some analyses, fracture patterns were additionally grouped as low-complexity (types I-IV) vs high-complexity (types V-VI).

Secondary variables included fracture evolution time (interval between trauma and surgery), timing of weight-bearing, and radiographic and clinical measures of joint status at 12 months. Radiographic osteoarthritis severity was assessed using the Rasmussen radiological score [52] and the Kellgren-Lawrence (KL) classification [53]. Clinical and functional outcomes were evaluated with the KOOS [40], the Rasmussen clinical score [52], and the SF-36 [50]. Comorbidities were recorded individually and subsequently dichotomized for analysis as the presence or absence of at least one chronic systemic condition.

Imaging acquisition and fracture classification

All patients underwent standard anteroposterior and lateral knee radiographs acquired using equipment from Siemens (Erlangen, Bavaria, Germany) or Del Medical (Bloomingdale, IL, USA). Digital images were processed using a Carestream Health 2014 system (Carestream Health, Rochester, NY, USA). When available, preoperative CT was performed using a 64-slice helical scanner (LightSpeed VCT (GE HealthCare, Chicago, IL, USA)). Axial, sagittal, and coronal reconstructions, as well as three-dimensional reconstructions, were reviewed to characterize fracture morphology.

Fractures were classified according to Schatzker using plain radiographs and, when present, CT scans, considering involvement of the lateral, medial, or both plateaus; the presence of split, depressed, or combined patterns; and whether articular involvement was partial or complete [17].

Surgical management and postoperative care

All patients were operated on by orthopedic trauma surgeons following standard principles of open reduction and internal fixation. Surgical approach, type of fixation, and use of bone grafts or substitutes were selected according to fracture pattern, soft-tissue status, and surgeon preference. Perioperative care included prophylactic antibiotics, thromboprophylaxis, and early mobilization in accordance with institutional protocols. The timing of weight-bearing progression was individualized based on fracture stability and radiographic consolidation and was recorded as a study variable; the observed mean time to full weight-bearing was 12 weeks.

Clinical and functional follow-up

At the most recent standardized follow-up visit (median 14 months; range 12-48 months), patients attended a clinical evaluation. The clinical and functional status of the operated knee was evaluated using the KOOS questionnaire, which assesses pain, symptoms, activities of daily living, sport and recreational function, and knee-related quality of life [40]. The Rasmussen clinical score was also applied to rate pain, walking capacity, knee extension, range of motion, and stability [52].

Health-related quality of life was measured with the SF-36 questionnaire at the same 12-month visit [50]. The instrument provides scores for eight domains (physical functioning, role limitations due to physical problems, bodily pain, general health, vitality, social functioning, role limitations due to emotional problems, and mental health); for this study, the physical functioning and social functioning domains were analyzed as continuous outcomes.

Radiographic evaluation

Standard anteroposterior and lateral knee radiographs were obtained at 12 months postoperatively. Radiological outcomes were assessed using the Rasmussen radiological score, which evaluates articular depression, tibial plateau width, coronal alignment (varus/valgus), and the presence of osteoarthritic changes, and categorizes results as excellent, good, fair, or poor based on the total score [52].

Additionally, the KL classification was applied to grade post-traumatic knee osteoarthritis on a five-point scale: grade 0 (normal), grade I (doubtful), grade II (mild), grade III (moderate), and grade IV (severe) [53]. For multivariable analyses, KL grades were also dichotomized into absent/doubtful/mild osteoarthritis (grades 0-2) vs moderate/severe osteoarthritis (grades 3-4).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables (age, follow-up time, length of hospital stay, time to unassisted walking, global KOOS, and SF-36 physical and social functioning scores) were summarized as medians and IQR after checking distributional assumptions using the Shapiro-Wilk test and inspecting histograms. Because KOOS and SF-36 scores, as well as most perioperative continuous variables, showed non-normal distributions, non-parametric methods were applied. Categorical variables (sex, operated side, comorbidities, Schatzker type, KL grade, and Rasmussen clinical and radiologic categories) were expressed as absolute frequencies and percentages.

Fracture patterns were classified according to the Schatzker system and recorded as types I-VI. KL grades were analyzed both as an ordinal variable (0-4) and as two categories: absent, doubtful, or mild osteoarthritis (grades 0-2) vs moderate-to-severe osteoarthritis (grades 3-4). Between-group comparisons of continuous outcomes across two categories (e.g., KL 0-2 vs 3-4) were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test. In contrast, comparisons across more than two categories (e.g., Schatzker I-VI or Rasmussen categories) used the Kruskal-Wallis test with post hoc inspection of median and IQR patterns.

Associations between ordinal or continuous variables (e.g., age, KL grade, Rasmussen clinical and radiologic categories, KOOS, and SF-36 scores) were examined using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. Correlation coefficients were interpreted in terms of magnitude (small, moderate, or strong) and direction.

To identify independent predictors of structural and functional outcome at 12 months, several multivariable models were fitted. First, a logistic regression model was constructed with KL grades 3-4 vs 0-2 as the dependent variable and age, sex, fracture complexity (Schatzker V-VI vs I-IV), and presence of at least one comorbidity as candidate predictors. Results were expressed as OR with 95% CI.

Second, multiple linear regression models were built with the KOOS global score (defined as the arithmetic mean of the five subscale scores: pain, symptoms, activities of daily living, sport/recreation, and quality of life; each scored 0-100, with higher values indicating a better outcome) and the SF-36 physical functioning score as dependent variables. Independent variables included KL group (0-2 vs 3-4), age, sex, fracture complexity, and comorbidity status. Regression coefficients (β) with 95% CI were reported. Model assumptions were assessed by inspection of residual plots.

Third, an ordinal logistic regression model with a logit link was used to explore correlates of the Rasmussen clinical category (excellent, good, fair, and poor), including KL group, age, fracture complexity, and sex as predictors. Proportional ORs with 95% CI were reported, and the proportional odds assumption was evaluated.

All tests were two-sided, and a p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data management and descriptive analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics version 27.0 (IBM Corp. Released 2020. IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 27.0. Armonk, NY: IBM Corp.); multivariable models were additionally fitted using R software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Research and Ethics Committee of Instituto Nacional de Rehabilitación Luis Guillermo Ibarra Ibarra (approval number: 18/21 AE-2025-1, approval date: April 7, 2025). The investigation was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Because all procedures were part of standard clinical care and the data were analyzed in anonymized form, the protocol for this retrospective research analysis was classified as involving no more than minimal risk. All patients provided written informed consent for the use of their clinical and imaging data for research purposes.

Fracture morphology according to the Schatzker classification was dominated by high-energy patterns. Types V and VI accounted for 24/106 (22.6%) and 31/106 (29.2%) fractures, respectively, followed by type II in 30/106 (28.3%), type III in 10/106 (9.4%), type IV in 9/106 (8.5%), and type I in 2/106 (1.9%). Thus, bicondylar and metaphyseal-diaphyseal dissociation patterns (types V-VI) represented more than half of the surgically treated cases (Table 2).

At 12 months, Rasmussen clinical scores were excellent in eight patients (7.5%), good in 66 (62.3%), fair in 28 (26.4%), and poor in four (3.8%). Rasmussen radiologic scores were excellent in 10 patients (9.4%), good in 23 (21.7%), fair in 44 (41.5%), and poor in 29 (27.4%) (Table 2).

Functional and health-related quality of life outcomes

At the 12-month evaluation, median global KOOS was 77 (IQR 67.5-83.8; range, 28-98), indicating overall good but not fully normal knee-specific function. Median SF-36 physical functioning score was 80 (IQR 70-87; range, 6-100), and median SF-36 social functioning score was 88.5 (IQR 71.5-99.8; range, 13-100) (Table 3).

Relationship between fracture pattern and outcomes

There was no significant association between fracture morphology and patient-reported outcomes at 12 months. KOOS, SF-36 physical functioning, and SF-36 social functioning scores did not differ across Schatzker types I-VI (Kruskal-Wallis tests, all p > 0.10). Similarly, when fracture patterns were dichotomized into low-complexity (types I-IV) and high-complexity (types V-VI), there were no statistically significant differences in KOOS or SF-36 scores between groups (Mann-Whitney U tests; all p > 0.10). High-energy patterns were overrepresented among patients with fair or poor Rasmussen clinical and radiologic categories, but these trends did not translate into significant differences in patient-reported function (Figure 3).

Relationship between KL grade and outcomes

When KL grade was analyzed as an ordinal variable (0-4), higher grades correlated moderately and inversely with KOOS (Spearman ρ = −0.32; p = 0.0009) and SF-36 physical functioning scores (ρ = −0.31; p = 0.0014), whereas the correlation with SF-36 social functioning was weaker and did not reach statistical significance (ρ = −0.17; p = 0.089).

Grouped analysis showed a clear gradient in functional outcomes according to radiographic osteoarthritis severity. Patients with KL grades 0-2 had a median KOOS of 79 (IQR 74-86), whereas those with grades 3-4 had a median of 63 (IQR 54-78; Mann-Whitney U test, p = 0.0009). Median SF-36 physical functioning score decreased from 82 (IQR 75-88) in the grades 0-2 group to 70 (IQR 45-80) in the grades 3-4 group (p = 0.0047). SF-36 social functioning scores were numerically lower in patients with grades 3-4 (median 65; IQR 63-98) than in those with grades 0-2 (median 90; IQR 80-100), but the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.073) (Table 4, Figure 3).

Age and other correlates

Age showed a strong positive correlation with KL grade (Spearman ρ = 0.51; p = 3.2×10⁻⁸), indicating that older patients were more likely to develop moderate-to-severe radiographic osteoarthritis at 12 months. The direct correlation between age and KOOS was small but statistically significant (ρ = −0.21; p = 0.028), whereas its correlation with SF-36 physical functioning was weaker and borderline non-significant (ρ = −0.18; p = 0.058).

Rasmussen clinical category was modestly but significantly associated with SF-36 social functioning (ρ = −0.25; p = 0.0085), with patients in fair or poor clinical categories reporting lower social participation. Associations between Rasmussen categories and KOOS or SF-36 physical functioning were smaller and did not reach conventional significance thresholds.

Multivariable analyses

Multivariable models were fitted to identify independent predictors of radiographic and functional outcomes at 12 months. In a logistic regression model including age, sex, fracture complexity (Schatzker V-VI vs I-IV), and the presence of at least one comorbidity, both age and sex remained independently associated with moderate-to-severe radiographic osteoarthritis. Each additional year of age increased the odds of KL grades 3-4 by 5.5% (OR 1.06 per year; 95% CI 1.01-1.10; p = 0.014), corresponding to an OR of 1.72 per decade (95% CI 1.12-2.63). Female sex showed a borderline association with higher odds of grades 3-4 (OR 3.72; 95% CI 1.00-13.81; p = 0.049); however, the CI included the null value, and the wide range suggests imprecise estimation in this sample. Fracture complexity (OR 2.38; 95% CI 0.75-7.54; p = 0.14) and the presence of comorbidities (OR 0.96; 95% CI 0.26-3.48; p = 0.95) were not statistically significant predictors. The model demonstrated acceptable discrimination, with an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.81. Given the 21 outcome events and four predictor variables, the EPV ratio was 5.25, below the conventional threshold of 10. However, simulation studies have demonstrated that this rule can be relaxed when other model performance indicators are satisfactory [54]. The area under the curve of 0.81 and the biological plausibility of the significant predictors (age, female sex) support the stability of these estimates. However, they should be interpreted with caution (Table 5).

Age, sex, fracture complexity, and comorbidities did not show significant independent effects in the adjusted model (all p > 0.10). The model explained 17.6% of the variance in KOOS (adjusted R² 0.14) (Table 6).

When SF-36 physical functioning was considered as an outcome, KL grades 3-4 again emerged as the only variable independently associated with poorer physical health-related quality of life. Patients with grades 3-4 had, on average, a 14.7-point lower SF-36 physical functioning score than those with grades 0-2 (β = −14.73; 95% CI −24.52 to −4.94; p = 0.004), whereas age, sex, fracture complexity, and comorbidity were not significantly associated with the outcome (all p ≥ 0.53). This model accounted for 10.5% of the variance (adjusted R² = 0.06) (Table 6).

Finally, an ordinal logistic regression model with the Rasmussen clinical category as the dependent variable identified no independent predictors. Neither KL group (proportional OR 0.98; 95% CI 0.54-4.64; p = 0.40), age (proportional OR 0.98 per year; 95% CI 0.95-1.01; p = 0.28), fracture complexity (proportional OR 0.94; 95% CI 0.43-2.09; p = 0.89), nor sex (proportional OR 0.75; 95% CI 0.29-1.96; p = 0.56) was associated with a shift toward a worse Rasmussen category.

Together, these analyses indicate that structural osteoarthritis, rather than fracture pattern, is the strongest correlate of 12-month knee-specific and physical health-related quality of life following tibial plateau fracture surgery. At the same time, categorical clinical grading appears less sensitive to these factors.

Discussion

In this single-center cohort of 106 surgically treated tibial plateau fractures, most patients achieved good short-term clinical and functional recovery at a median of 14 months, yet a non-trivial proportion already showed structural degeneration and meaningful residual symptoms. Median KOOS global score was 77, and median SF-36 physical and social functioning scores were 80 and 88.5, respectively, ranges typically interpreted as good outcomes. However, almost one in five knees (19.8%) had KL grades 3-4 at one year, and this subgroup showed substantially worse knee-specific and general physical function than patients with grades 0-2 (Tables 2-4, Figure 3). These findings illustrate the complex and often nonlinear relationship among articular reconstruction, radiographic healing, and the patient’s lived experience after a tibial plateau fracture.

Comparison with previous functional outcome studies

Our KOOS and SF-36 scores confirm that tibial plateau fractures are not benign injuries, even when treated in a specialized tertiary center. Recent midterm series similarly describe persistent pain, reduced sports participation, and work limitations despite apparently satisfactory absolute scores and technically adequate reductions. Neidlein et al. reported that both simple (Schatzker I-III) and complex (Schatzker IV-VI) fractures achieved acceptable mid-term functional outcomes. Still, many patients overestimated the speed and completeness of their recovery and continued to report limitations several years after injury [55]. In our cohort, fracture type alone was not associated with KOOS or SF-36 outcomes, and high-energy Schatzker V-VI patterns did not independently predict moderate-to-severe radiographic osteoarthritis after accounting for age, sex, and comorbidities (Table 2, Table 3, Table 5).

Long-term studies reinforce the chronic impact of these injuries. Stevens and colleagues, in a classic series of operatively treated tibial plateau fractures, found that at a mean of 7.6 years, many patients still had pain and functional limitations despite apparently satisfactory radiographs [35]. Honkonen likewise documented secondary degenerative arthritis and incomplete functional recovery in a substantial proportion of patients at long-term follow-up, with particularly poor outcomes when alignment and meniscal status were compromised [56]. More recent cohorts, including those by Mattiassich et al. and others, have shown that symptoms and radiographic osteoarthritis may progress between mid-term and very long-term follow-up, even in cases initially rated as satisfactory [57-59]. Against this backdrop, our one-year results should be interpreted as an early step on a trajectory that, for some patients, culminates in clinically significant PTOA and reduced quality of life.

Early PTOA and its functional impact

A key result of this study is the strong association between early radiographic osteoarthritis and patient-reported outcomes. At 12 months, knees with KL grades 3-4 had markedly lower KOOS global and SF-36 physical functioning scores than knees with grades 0-2, with median differences of approximately 16 and 12 points, respectively (Table 4, Figure 3). In multivariable linear regression, KL grades 3-4 remained independently associated with a 13.3-point reduction in KOOS and a 14.7-point reduction in SF-36 physical functioning after adjustment for age, sex, fracture complexity, and comorbidity (Table 6). These effect sizes exceed commonly accepted thresholds for clinically relevant change, underscoring the impact of structural degeneration on everyday function.

Our KL distribution, with nearly one-fifth of knees already in grades 3-4 at 12 months, is lower than the long-term prevalence reported in older cohorts but directionally similar. Honkonen found radiographic degenerative changes in about 44% of patients at approximately eight years [56], and Mattiassich et al. reported progression of symptoms and osteoarthritis over more than two decades [57]. Contemporary series confirm that proximal tibial fractures carry a substantial risk of radiographic osteoarthritis and functional deterioration at mid-term follow-up, particularly in higher-energy patterns and older patients [58,59]. In our logistic model, age and sex were the main independent predictors of KL grades 3-4; each additional decade of age increased the odds of moderate-to-severe osteoarthritis by roughly 70%, and female patients had higher odds than male patients (Table 5). In contrast, fracture complexity and comorbidity status were not significant predictors once these factors were considered.

Taken together, these observations are consistent with emerging concepts of PTOA as a multifactorial process in which injury pattern, surgical reconstruction, pre-existing joint vulnerability, and systemic risk factors (such as age and sex) interact to drive structural and symptomatic progression [41,45,60-62]. The presence of KL grades 3-4 as early as 1 year in our cohort may serve as an early marker of a less favorable trajectory, identifying patients at increased risk of accelerated osteoarthritis and eventual total knee arthroplasty [41,60].

Radiographic-clinical relationships and the role of scoring systems

Radiographs remain central for evaluating step-off, condylar widening, and alignment after tibial plateau fracture, but their correlation with patient-perceived function is imperfect. In our series, both KL grade and Rasmussen radiologic score correlated with KOOS and SF-36 physical functioning, but correlation coefficients were in the small-to-moderate range. Furthermore, our ordinal logistic regression model did not identify the KL group, age, fracture complexity, or sex as independent correlates of the Rasmussen clinical category once all covariates were entered simultaneously.

These findings echo those of Bormann et al., who described a mismatch between radiological consolidation and functional outcome in tibial plateau fractures: some patients showed acceptable radiographs but relevant symptoms, while others demonstrated radiographic osteoarthritis with surprisingly preserved function [41,43]. Rasmussen clinical and radiologic scores remain simple, pragmatic tools. In our cohort, worse clinical categories were more frequent among patients with lower KOOS and SF-36 social functioning scores, suggesting that clinical grading still captures important dimensions of pain, gait, and stability. However, the modest correlations and the lack of strong independent predictors for Rasmussen categories highlight that structural, clinical, and functional dimensions only partially overlap; no single metric fully captures the patient’s perspective.

Patient-reported outcome measures: KOOS and SF-36

The combined use of KOOS and SF-36 allowed a nuanced appraisal of knee-specific symptoms and broader health-related quality of life. KOOS has been widely validated in anterior cruciate ligament injury and knee osteoarthritis, and its use is increasing in post-traumatic contexts. Gandek and co-workers demonstrated that KOOS subscales show good validity and responsiveness across a wide range of knee disorders, supporting their use as a primary functional endpoint in studies of tibial plateau fractures and early osteoarthritis [48,63].

In our cohort, the KOOS global score clearly discriminated between KL 0-2 and KL 3-4 groups and remained independently associated with radiographic osteoarthritis severity in multivariable modeling (Table 4, Table 6, Figure 2). The SF-36 physical functioning domain complemented KOOS by capturing broader limitations in activity and work capacity, whereas the social functioning domain reflected the extent to which knee symptoms affected participation in social roles. Long-term series of tibial plateau fractures have similarly shown that, even when radiographs and surgeon-rated scores suggest technically successful reconstruction, a subset of patients continues to experience clinically meaningful deficits in daily life [35,56,57].

Imaging, AI, and the perspective of medical imaging

From an imaging-focused standpoint, our results intersect with evolving work in CT-based characterization, osteoarthritis grading, and AI. Classical two-dimensional classifications, such as the Schatzker classification, are simple and widely used but fail to fully capture posterior and multi-column involvement. CT-based schemes, including the three-column concept by Luo and the four-quadrant mapping proposed by Chang, better capture the three-dimensional morphology of tibial plateau fractures and are now integral to preoperative planning in complex patterns [19,21,23,24,64-66]. Our distribution of fracture types and the frequent use of approaches tailored to posteromedial or posterolateral fragments are consistent with this CT-driven paradigm [28,30,32,67,68].

For PTOA, standard weight-bearing radiographs graded by KL remain the reference standard. Still, several deep learning models now achieve radiologist-level performance in automated KL grading and prediction of osteoarthritis progression [69]. Segura and colleagues recently proposed an AI-based knee osteoarthritis classification system that combines automated feature extraction with clinical labels, illustrating how imaging and machine learning may be integrated to stratify disease severity in large cohorts [47]. Our dataset, which couples manually graded KL scores with validated KOOS and SF-36 measures at a defined post-injury time point, exemplifies the type of curated cohort that could be used to develop or externally validate AI tools specifically aimed at post-traumatic knee osteoarthritis after tibial plateau fracture.

In future work, combining CT-derived morphologic descriptors (such as column involvement, posterior slope, and metaphyseal comminution) with automated radiographic grading and longitudinal patient-reported outcomes could yield multimodal models that predict which patients are most likely to develop symptomatic osteoarthritis or require total knee arthroplasty. Such a direction is highly aligned with the translational imaging focus of medical imaging and would move the field beyond purely descriptive series toward prediction-guided care.

Clinical implications

Our findings have several practical implications. First, clinicians should anticipate that most patients will regain independent ambulation and reasonably good knee function by one year. Still, a significant minority will continue to experience pain, activity limitations, or reduced quality of life (Tables 2-4, Figures 1-2). This is particularly relevant for older patients and for women, who in our cohort were more likely to exhibit moderate-to-severe radiographic osteoarthritis at 12 months, independent of fracture pattern and comorbidities (Table 5). These observations are in line with broader osteoarthritis literature showing age and female sex as dominant risk factors for structural degeneration and symptomatic disease [45,61].

Second, the presence of KL grades 3-4 at one year clearly identifies a high-risk subgroup with substantially worse KOOS and SF-36 physical functioning scores (Tables 4-6, Figure 2). For these patients, intensified non-operative management, structured rehabilitation, weight optimization, and targeted pain strategies, together with closer imaging and clinical follow-up, may be warranted. Given the long-term data showing increased risk of degenerative change and total knee arthroplasty after tibial plateau fractures [35,41,56,60], early identification and proactive management of this subgroup could mitigate future disability.

Third, the absence of a strong association between Schatzker type and patient-reported outcomes in our series suggests that treatment algorithms should focus less on fracture-type labels and more on three-dimensional morphology, soft-tissue status, and patient-specific factors. Modern column-based and quadrant-based classification systems, in combination with CT and, when indicated, MRI, may be more informative for surgical planning and prognosis than traditional two-dimensional schemes alone [14,17,21,24,26,28,70,71].

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. We analyzed a prospectively defined cohort treated according to consistent surgical principles at a high-volume tertiary center and used validated patient-reported outcomes alongside established clinical and radiographic scores. Outcomes were assessed at a relatively homogeneous follow-up point, and data analysis combined non-parametric methods, correlation coefficients, and multivariable modeling, providing a granular view of structural and functional relationships (Tables 1-6, Figures 1-2). The inclusion of both radiographic and patient-reported endpoints is particularly relevant given the documented mismatch between radiographs and symptoms [41,43,62].

Limitations should also be acknowledged. Although the analytic sample of 106 patients exceeds the original sample size calculation, it remains modest and limits the precision of subgroup analyses, especially within individual Schatzker types. Our approximately 14-month follow-up is sufficient to detect early PTOA but not to capture the full evolution toward advanced osteoarthritis or conversion to total knee arthroplasty, as reported in longer-term series [35,56,57]. Radiographic assessment relied on plain radiographs; we did not incorporate systematic CT-based quantification of step-off, slope alteration, or rotational deformities, nor did we systematically evaluate concomitant meniscal or ligamentous injuries, which are known to influence prognosis [14,15,71]. Finally, although losses to follow-up were low, selection bias remains possible if patients with worse outcomes were less likely to attend the one-year visit. Prospective studies incorporating pre-injury or very early post-trauma baseline assessments would provide a more precise estimation of recovery trajectories and the true functional burden attributable to tibial plateau fracture. In particular, the logistic regression model for predictors of moderate-to-severe osteoarthritis had an EPV ratio of 5.25 (21 events, four predictors), below the traditional recommendation of 10. Therefore, the results of this multivariable analysis should be considered exploratory until validated in larger cohorts. Additionally, the variable follow-up duration (range: 12-48 months) introduces heterogeneity in the timing of outcome assessment; future studies should standardize follow-up intervals.

Future directions

Future research should extend follow-up in this cohort to five and 10 years to determine how early KL and Rasmussen scores relate to structural progression, functional decline, and arthroplasty risk. Larger multicenter datasets will be necessary to develop robust prognostic models that incorporate clinical variables, three-dimensional imaging metrics, and patient-reported outcomes. Within the scope of medical imaging, there is a particular opportunity to explore AI tools for automated KL grading, CT-based morphologic analysis, and integration of imaging-derived features with trajectories of KOOS and SF-36 over time [47,67]. Such tools could support risk-stratified follow-up and more timely joint-preserving interventions, with the ultimate aim of delaying or preventing progression to disabling PTOA after tibial plateau fracture.

Conclusions

In this retrospectively analyzed cohort of 106 surgically treated tibial plateau fractures, most patients achieved good clinical, functional, and radiographic outcomes at approximately 14 months. Nevertheless, nearly one-fifth of knees already exhibited moderate-to-severe radiographic osteoarthritis, and this subgroup showed clinically meaningful reductions in KOOS global and SF-36 physical functioning scores. Fracture morphology, according to the Schatzker classification, was not an independent predictor of either radiographic osteoarthritis or patient-reported outcomes after adjusting for age and sex. In contrast, early KL grades 3-4 emerged as the strongest independent correlate of worse knee-specific and general physical function.

These findings underscore that structural degeneration, rather than fracture type, is most strongly associated with early post-traumatic impairment after tibial plateau fracture surgery. Systematic integration of radiographic osteoarthritis grading with validated patient-reported outcome measures is therefore essential in follow-up, both to identify high-risk patients and to guide targeted rehabilitation and non-operative management. Future work combining three-dimensional imaging descriptors, longitudinal functional data, and AI-based image analysis may enable more precise risk stratification and personalized care pathways to delay or prevent progression to disabling PTOA and the need for knee arthroplasty.
