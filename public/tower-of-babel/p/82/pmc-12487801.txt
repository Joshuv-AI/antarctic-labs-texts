Autologous Platelet-Rich Plasma Injections for Treatment of Dry Eyes: A Pilot Study
Authors: Anchal Tripathi, Avinash Mishra, Atul Bhirud, Mohini Agrawal, Vidhu Dhar Dangwal, Lav Pathak
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Purpose:To study was to study the efficacy of autologous platelet-rich plasma injections (PRPi) in the treatment of dry eyes.Methods:This prospective, interventional study was conducted at a tertiary eye care center, India. One hundred and sixty eyes of 80 patients with newly diagnosed dry eyes were included. One eye received PRPi (cases = 80 eyes), and the other eye of the patient received a lubricating drop (control = 80 eyes). Cases who did not improve at 1-month follow-up (FU) were reinjected with PRP. The outcomes were measured on the basis of ocular surface disease index (OSDI), Schirmer’s test (ST), noninvasive tear breakup time (NIBUT), tear meniscus height (TMH), and ocular surface staining (OSS) score done at baseline and 1-month, 2-month, 3-month, and 6-month FU.Results:Sixty cases showed a significant improvement in all the outcome measures at 1 month (P < 0.001), when compared to controls; whereas, 20 cases had to undergo repeat injections. On FU at 2 months, 16 cases (out of those 20 cases) showed a significant improvement (P < 0.05), while 4 cases still did not improve clinically at 3 months. All the outcome parameters (ST, NIBUT, TMH, and OSS and OSDI scores) maintained a significant improvement at 6-month FU, including those four cases (P < 0.001). All the patients were highly satisfied with the intervention.Conclusion:PRPi, as a monotherapy given on pro-re-nata basis, is a simple and efficacious modality of treatment in dry eyes, verified by improvement in subjective and objective tear film parameters.

INTRODUCTION

Dry eye disease (DED) is a common ocular surface disorder characterized by tear film instability, hyperosmolarity, inflammation, and neurosensory dysfunction.123 A major cause of DED is aqueous tear deficiency, often resulting from lacrimal gland dysfunction due to acinar atrophy, fibrosis, duct obstruction, or lymphocytic infiltration with proinflammatory cytokines.4 Current treatments primarily provide symptomatic relief through tear substitution and anti-inflammatory therapies, but they do not fully address the underlying causes of the disease.123 Autologous blood derivatives, like platelet-rich plasma (PRP), are emerging as promising treatments in regenerative medicine, including ophthalmology, due to their high concentration of growth factors and anti-inflammatory cytokines that promote tissue repair and reduce inflammation.56789101112 While PRP has shown benefits in treating corneal ulcers and persistent epithelial defects, its potential for treating DED remains less explored. PRP injections (PRPi) into the lacrimal gland may offer several advantages over traditional therapies, such as consistent delivery of growth factors directly to the affected area, reducing patient reliance on frequent topical applications, and ensuring better compliance.1112 Previous studies, such as those by Avila et al., have demonstrated symptomatic improvement in patients with severe DED treated with PRPi, but these studies were limited by small sample sizes and lacked robust control groups.1314 More recent research, including a 2021 interventional study and a 2022 retrospective study, has provided additional evidence supporting the efficacy of PRPi in severe DED.15 However, these studies still had limitations, including small sample sizes and variability in study design. This study aims to address these gaps by evaluating the effectiveness of PRPi administered on a pro-re-nata (PRN) basis compared to standard artificial tears, potentially offering a newer therapeutic option for managing dry eyes. Our study aims to investigate the effectiveness of PRPi targeting the lacrimal gland for stimulating its function and improving dry eye symptoms.

METHODS

This was a hospital-based prospective interventional comparative study conducted from January 2023 to June 2023 at a tertiary eye care center, India. It was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (Reference number: 01/03/MAR/MHJRC/2023) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was taken from all the study participants.

Patients aged ≥18 years were selected through subjective questionnaire using ocular surface disease index (OSDI) tool, inbuilt in Sirius (Costruzione Strumenti Oftalmici, Italy). The patients were categorized into having mild (13–22), moderate (23–32), and severe (33–100) dry eyes based on this score.16

Patients underwent complete ophthalmic examination in the form of best-corrected visual acuity and anterior segment examination by slit-lamp biomicroscopy.

Schirmer’s test-I (ST) was performed in all the eyes, using Whitmann’s Schirmer strips, and a reading of ≤15 mm in 5 min was taken as cutoff for dry eyes.1617 Similarly, other tests such as noninvasive tear film breakup time (NIBUT ≤10 s) through Sirius (Costruzione Strumenti Oftalmici, Italy), tear meniscus height (TMH < 0.25 mm) through Sirius (Costruzione Strumenti Oftalmici, Italy), and corneal staining with rose bengal dye (ocular surface staining [OSS] score >3 by van Bijsterveld index) were conducted for assessing DED.1617 Qualitative meibomian gland evaluation was also done through Sirius (Costruzione Strumenti Oftalmici, Italy).

Patients with ocular surface diseases other than dry eyes, infectious or allergic ocular disease, who were unwilling, uncooperative, or unable to give consent, pregnant women, regular contact lens wearers and patients who underwent ocular treatment for any other eye disease as well as those who had undergone previous refractive eye surgeries, those using oral supplements like omega-3 fatty acids, having meibomian gland dysfunction, on topical drugs causing dry eyes as side effect like antiglaucoma drugs, immunosuppressants or drugs with preservatives, were excluded from the study. Patients with systemic diseases like diabetes mellitus that might alter the results were also excluded from the study.

Ten mL of patient’s own venular blood was extracted into a 3.8% sodium citrate (Beckton Dickinson) vial. This total blood was centrifuged via soft spin at 2000 rpm for 10 min. The supernatant layer obtained from this centrifuge was again centrifuged via hard spin at 3000 rpm for 10 min. The lower one-third of this centrifuge constitutes PRP. Prior to use, PRP was activated by adding 10% calcium chloride at a ratio of 1 PRP: 0.05 CaCl2. The PRP was used immediately after preparation, and hence, no storage or handling measures were required.

One eye of the patient received PRPi, that is study eye, based on computer-generated randomization, while the contralateral eye received dry eye treatment as per conventional treatment protocol, depending on the severity of the disease.12316

One mL PRP was injected, through the skin, over the area of the lacrimal gland, that is, lateral one third of the orbital rim, up to 4–5 mm deep, superotemporally, toward the lacrimal fossa of the frontal bone, abutting the lacrimal gland area [Figure 1a]. The site was cleaned with 5% Betadine before injecting, and PRP was injected under strict aseptic precautions, using an insulin syringe. The length of the needle in insulin syringe is approximately 4–5 mm, which provides a good access to the lacrimal gland area [Figure 1b]. No local anesthesia was used prior to the injection.

(a) Method of injecting platelet-rich plasma over the lacrimal gland area, (b) length of the insulin syringe needle is approximately 4 mm

The procedure was done by a single ophthalmologist. Patients were clearly instructed not to put any artificial tear eye drops in the PRP injected eye. Patients were asked to maintain a diary for daily treatment, and adherence to the prescribed treatment was monitored through these diaries. Patients did not undergo any additional procedures that could alter the lacrimal volume (lacrimal plugs or lacrimal occlusion) or received any drug like topical steroids. The study also ensured that the ophthalmologist who performed PRPi was not the one who examined (ST, TMH, NIBUT, and OSS) or questioned the patient for OSDI score.

All the demographic data of the patients as well as the aforementioned dry eye parameters were tabulated and analyzed.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed by SPSS version 25. Wilcoxon rank sum test and Fisher’s exact test were used to compare parameters between cases and controls at baseline and FUs. Pearson’s Chi-squared test was used to analyze significance of change in all parameters at FUs. Friedman test was used to analyze P value for change in parameters over time. Overall P value for comparison of change in parameters over time between the two groups was calculated using generalized estimating equations method. Confidence interval was kept at 95%, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

A total of 160 eyes of 80 patients were enrolled (80 cases/study eyes and 80 controls). The mean age at presentation was 59.45 ± 13.18 years. There were 52 (65%) females and 28 (35%) males. Of 80 patients, 2.5% (n = 2) of patients had mild, 31% (n = 25) had moderate, and 66% (n = 53) had severe dry eyes. The etiology of dry eye in all our patients was aqueous deficiency.

The baseline parameters of OSDI score and tests for dry eyes in cases and controls were similar with P > 0.9 as depicted in Table 1. The mean OSDI at baseline, in our study, was 37.96 ± 10.73, which improved to 10.06 ± 5.41 after 6 months, in patients receiving PRPi (P < 0.001). The injection was well tolerated by all the cases and there were no serious adverse effects.

DISCUSSION

As we know that host cells are more suitable for the regeneration of the existing tissue rather than cells which are engineered by in vitro methods. The use of human blood is a key element in regenerative medicine and platelet-derived preparations, rich in growth factors, which is now being increasingly used in ophthalmology and medicine.5678 For instance, autologous serum (AS) eye drops are being used for treating dry eyes. Similarly, PRP is also being used in several ocular surface disorders such as corneal ulcers and persistent epithelial defects.678

PRP is already being used in dermatology for hair growth and skin rejuvenation, as well as in other branches of medicine such as orthopedics (for osteoarthritis) and sports medicine.6781112 PRP is one of the autologous blood derivatives containing a high concentration of platelets. It has an anti-apoptotic effect and contains several growth factors and anti-inflammatory cytokines that play an active role in fighting the inflammatory cascade in dry eyes.678910 Thus, PRP in the form of injection (PRPi) may prove to be of great utility in managing dry eyes. AS primarily mimics the natural tear film with lower concentrations of growth factors, providing lubrication and mild regenerative benefits for dry eye.678910 In contrast, PRPi have a higher concentration of platelets and growth factors, offering more potent anti-inflammatory and tissue-regenerative properties for deeper ocular surface healing.678910 Furthermore, route of administration of PRPi provides pharmacokinetic superiority over AS.678910

Our study demonstrated significant improvements across all objective and subjective parameters of dry eye evaluation, which aligns with previous research findings. Notably, the increase in ST and TMH suggests a boost in tear production, likely due to the anti-inflammatory properties of PRP and its ability to stimulate damaged acinar cells.4818 Enhanced NIBUT indicates improved tear film stability, potentially due to increased mucin secretion from the lacrimal gland or conjunctival goblet cells.45678910 In addition, improvements in OSS scores reflect better ocular surface health and rehabilitation, likely due to the anti-apoptotic and antifibrotic effects of the growth factors present in PRP.45678910 The OSDI, a key subjective measure, also showed a significant improvement, reflecting enhanced patient comfort and satisfaction with the treatment.456 Notably, many patients expressed a preference for PRPi over daily eye drops, indicating better compliance and ease of use. This is particularly relevant in the management of DED, where frequent dosing and poor adherence to topical treatments pose significant challenges. Unlike conventional treatments, PRPi provides a sustained effect without relying on patient compliance, ensuring consistent therapeutic outcomes.

Our results are consistent with earlier studies, such as those conducted by Avila et al. in 2014 and 2019, which demonstrated symptomatic improvement in patients with severe dry eyes receiving PRPi into the lacrimal gland.1314 However, our study is distinct in several ways. First, we employed a larger sample size and provided a more comprehensive analysis of ocular surface changes compared to these earlier investigations, which were limited by their smaller populations and narrower focus. A 2021 interventional study reported statistically significant improvements in all dry eye test parameters except TBUT, which contrasts with our findings of improvements across all measured outcomes, including TBUT.15 This discrepancy could be due to differences in patient populations, study designs, or treatment regimens. The prospective design of our study and systematic use of a reinjection criterion, based on clinical assessment, may have contributed to more robust and consistent results. Furthermore, a recent retrospective study in 2022 involving 28 eyes with Sjogren’s syndrome also supported PRPi as an effective treatment for severe dry eyes.19 However, unlike our study, this research used a fixed monthly injection schedule without specific reinjection criteria, and both study groups received lubricant eye drops, potentially confounding the results. In contrast, our study avoided the use of concomitant topical treatments, allowing for a clearer evaluation of PRPi’s direct effects on the lacrimal gland and ocular surface health.

A key innovation in our study was the development of a specific criterion for reinjection, which was based on clinical parameters rather than a fixed schedule. PRPi was repeated only when indicated, avoiding unnecessary interventions and minimizing patient discomfort. Out of 80 eyes, only 20 required a reinjection, and no further treatments were needed, with all patients showing sustained improvement at the 6-month FU. This PRN-based approach reduces the invasiveness of treatment and ensures that only those patients who truly need additional therapy receive it, optimizing both efficacy and safety.

We observed only two minor adverse reactions: one patient experienced transient periorbital edema that resolved spontaneously and another experienced vasovagal syncope immediately after the injection, which was easily managed. These findings suggest that PRPi is generally safe and well tolerated, with a favorable side effect profile compared to other invasive treatments.

Despite these promising findings, our study has several limitations. First, while our sample size was larger than those of some earlier studies, it remains relatively small and was conducted at a single center, which may limit the generalizability of the results. In addition, FU period was limited to 6 months, which may not capture the long-term durability of the effects of PRPi. Furthermore, we did not include histopathological examinations or advanced imaging studies to definitively confirm the mechanism of lacrimal gland function recovery. Future research should involve multicenter studies with larger and more diverse populations to validate our findings. Long-term FU is also necessary to determine the duration of PRPi’s effects and its potential role in the sustained management of DED. In addition, studies incorporating histopathological and imaging evaluations could provide more insight into the cellular and structural changes occurring in the lacrimal gland post-PRPi, further elucidating its therapeutic mechanism.

In conclusion, in today’s digital era, where the prevalence of DED is rapidly increasing, particularly due to prolonged screen use, managing this condition effectively and conveniently is more important than ever. Traditional therapies often require frequent dosing, which can be burdensome for patients and may result in poor adherence and suboptimal outcomes. Our PRN regimen of PRPi offers a novel therapeutic approach that not only alleviates symptoms but also targets the underlying pathology, potentially providing a more definitive treatment option for patients with aqueous tear deficiency. Given the promising results, PRPi could emerge as a groundbreaking treatment for dry eyes, particularly in cases where conventional therapies are inadequate.
