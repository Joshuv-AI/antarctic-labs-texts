A cross-sectional study unveiling the global impact and future projections through 2060 of osteoarthritis
Authors: Xuefeng Luo, Xiangru Shen, Runxing Kang, Wei Huang, Zhenglin Zhu, Junyi Liao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractOsteoarthritis is a leading degenerative disease worldwide that significantly impairs physical function and quality of life. With an ageing population, the epidemiological burden of osteoarthritis is expected to intensify, making systematic analysis and trend prediction essential for refining treatment and prevention strategies. Data from the 2021 Global Burden of Disease database was employed to analyse trends in the incidence, prevalence, and disability-adjusted life years of osteoarthritis from 1990 to 2021. This research spans global, regional, and national perspectives, aiming to guide future health interventions. By 2021, the number of patients affected by osteoarthritis had reached 607 million, with predictions estimating a rise to 765 million by 2060. There is a positive correlation between Social Development Index and osteoarthritis burden, illustrating that higher incidence and prevalence rates are associated with greater economic development. East Asia currently leads with the highest count of osteoarthritis cases, whereas Oceania has the fewest. Osteoarthritis incidence is climbing across all adult age groups, with women consistently exhibiting higher rates than men at every age. High body mass Index stands as the most significant risk factor for the condition. The total number of osteoarthritis cases is expected to keep rising, underscoring the ongoing challenge in managing this pervasive disease. Osteoarthritis persists as a substantial global health challenge, exhibiting a continuous upward trend from 1990 to 2021. This study provides critical insights for public health policy-making and resource allocation, supporting future interventions and disease control efforts.

Introduction

Osteoarthritis, one of the most debilitating diseases worldwide, significantly impacts patients by causing pain, disability, and considerable socio-economic costs [1, 2]. With ageing populations, it is anticipated that by 2050, the global count of osteoarthritis sufferers could near one billion, posing significant challenges for disease management and placing considerable pressure on healthcare systems worldwide [3].

Previous research indicates that osteoarthritis ranks as the twelfth leading cause of years lived with disability worldwide, it further shortens patients’ lifespans by restricting their physical mobility [4]. Meanwhile, osteoarthritis lacks any treatment options for remission, with arthroplasty remaining the only definitive surgical intervention [5]. Therefore, identifying temporal, age-related, geographical, and location-specific trends in the burden of osteoarthritis, as well as quantifying the contribution of various risk factors, is critical for developing effective prevention strategies and allocating treatment resources. The Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study offers a comprehensive framework that enables a nuanced assessment of the impacts of osteoarthritis across different geographical regions and time periods [6–8]. Notably, the 2021 GBD study has documented substantial alterations in the global disease burden in the wake of the 2020 COVID-19 outbreak [6]. Yet, the precise ramifications of these alterations for osteoarthritis are still shrouded in uncertainty.

Therefore, this study was meticulously designed to address the following research questions: (i) How do geographical location, social development index (SDI), age, and gender influence the global burden of osteoarthritis? (ii) What is the contribution of key risk factors such as BMI to osteoarthritis disability-adjusted life years (DALYs)? (iii) How will the burden of osteoarthritis evolve by 2060? By meticulously addressing these questions, we aim to provide actionable insights for policymakers to tailor prevention and resource allocation strategies.

Methods

Data source and disease definition

Approved by local ethics committees and adhering to STROBE guidelines [9], this study utilized GBD 2021 data (1990–2021) from the Global Health Data Exchange (http://ghdx.healthdata.org/gbd-results-tool) [7]. The 2021 GBD study provided the foundational data into the burden of 369 diseases and injuries in 204 countries and territories from 1990 to 2021 [6, 7]. In this study, the data on the case of incidence, prevalence, and DALYs in osteoarthritis was collected, as well as their corresponding rates at the global, regional, and national levels. Osteoarthritis cases were identified using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision (ICD-10) codes, encompassing M15–M19. This study was conducted using secondary data from this collaborative project, without direct interaction with any of the participants.

SDI analysis

The GBD database encompasses 21 regions and 204 countries, categorized by SDI quintiles. The SDI is a composite measure of per capita income, total fertility rate (age < 25 years), and average educational attainment (age ≥ 15 years), reflecting a country’s overall social and economic development [10]. SDI categories—low, low-middle, middle, high-middle, and high—were used to compare the disease burden across varying levels of socioeconomic development [11]. The ‘dplyr’ and ‘ggplot2’ packages in R (version 4.4.1) were employed for data manipulation and visualization.

Global and regional burden analysis

To analyse the global distribution and regional differences in the burden of osteoarthritis, we generated global maps and regional comparative analyses. The data were aggregated by geographical regions as defined by the GBD study, and maps were created using R (version 4.4.1) with the ‘ggplot2’ and ‘sf’ packages to visualize the distribution of disease burden.

Prediction of osteoarthritis to 2060

To predict the future burden of osteoarthritis, we utilized the Bayesian Age-Period-Cohort (BAPC) model, implemented in R (version 4.4.1) with the ‘Integrated Nested Laplace Approximation (INLA)’ and ‘BAPC’ packages. This sophisticated model predicts incidence and prevalence trends up to 2060, integrating age, period, and cohort effects to offer a nuanced understanding of disease progression [12]. By examining these dimensions, the BAPC model provides essential insights into the potential evolution of osteoarthritis, shaped by demographic shifts, historical health trajectories, and anticipated changes in risk factors and medical interventions.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses and data visualizations were performed using R (version 4.4.1) and JD_GBDR. We commenced by extracting osteoarthritis incidence data across various years from the GBD database, subsequently organizing and cleansing this data in R (version 4.4.1) to ensure its integrity and consistency. Descriptive statistics were generated for all key variables, and results were expressed as means with 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs). For trend analyses, P-values < .05 were considered statistically significant. For the analysis of the all-age population, the age-standardized rate (ASR) was used per 100 000 population, including age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR), age-standardized prevalence rate (ASPR), and age-standardized DALYs rate. To guarantee precise execution of these analyses, the ‘broom’ and ‘dplyr’ packages were installed and loaded. These tools were instrumental in efficiently extracting and organizing regression results, facilitating the accurate calculation and interpretation of the EAPC.

Across the five SDI regions, there is a consistent pattern of steady growth in the number of individuals affected by osteoarthritis (Fig. S1). In 2021, regions with a high SDI bore the most substantial disease burden, exhibiting the highest rates of incidence, prevalence, and DALYs at 931.334, 7897.269, and 283.130 per 100 000 population (Table 1; Tables S1 and S2). However, these regions also displayed the smallest EAPCs for these indicators at 0.209, 0.242, and 0.269, suggesting a trend towards stabilization in growth rates (Table 1; Tables S1 and S2).

Conversely, low SDI regions reported the lowest rates of incidence, prevalence, and DALYs, each marked at 251.031, 5605.580, and 190.931 per 100 000 population (Table 1; Tables S1 and S2), positioning them as the areas with the least disease burden.

The middle SDI regions, however, demonstrated the most rapid increase in disease metrics, drawing significant concern. From 1990 to 2021, the cases changes of incidence, prevalence, and DALYs in these areas were 168.443%, 195.937%, and 200.086% (Table 1; Tables S1 and S2). Additionally, these regions recorded the highest EAPCs for ASIR, ASPR, and ASR of DALYs, at 0.479, 0.517, and 0.570 (Table 1; Tables S1 and S2), indicating accelerating growth trends that demand close monitoring.

Trends in disease burden correlated with SDI

As clearly depicted in Fig. S2, during the period from 1990 to 2021, there was a discernible positive correlation between the SDI and the burden of osteoarthritis across 21 regions. There was a marked positive relationship between SDI and the ASIR (Fig. S2A, r = 0.78, P < 0.001), suggesting that osteoarthritis incidence tends to be higher in regions with greater economic development. Similarly, both the ASPR and the ASR of DALYs showed positive correlations with SDI (Fig. S2B; r = 0.84, P < 0.001; Fig. S2C, r = 0.8479, P < 0.001).

When examining the SDI distribution of 204 countries in 2021, positive correlations were also apparent between SDI and osteoarthritis-related metrics, such as ASIR, ASPR, and ASR of DALYs (Fig. S2D, r = 0.6396, P < 0.001; Fig. S2E, r = 0.6687, P < 0.001; Fig. S2F, r = 0.7029, P < 0.001). This underscores the association between higher socio-economic status and increased incidence, prevalence, and disease burden of osteoarthritis.

Distribution of disease burden across 204 countries

The geographical distribution of disease burden, as depicted in Fig. 1, reveals that osteoarthritis imposes a relatively lighter burden in low-income countries, particularly in Africa and Southeast Asia. Conversely, higher ASIR, ASPR, and ASR of DALYs are observed in the Americas, Australia, and Europe (Fig. 1A–C).

The trends in the EAPC for ASIR, ASPR, and ASR of DALYs across these 204 countries are clearly visualized in Fig. 1D–F. It is noteworthy that in regions with heavier disease burdens such as the Americas, Australia, and Europe, the growth trends in osteoarthritis prevalence are not as pronounced. On the other hand, in several developing nations within Africa and Asia, there is a significant and noticeable upward trend in osteoarthritis, highlighting a need for increased focus and intervention.

Composition of osteoarthritis burden

Osteoarthritis predominantly includes knee osteoarthritis, hip osteoarthritis, hand osteoarthritis, and other osteoarthritis. Both the analysis of ASIR, ASPR, and ASR of DALYs, as well as comparisons between 1990 and 2021, indicate that the proportional distribution of these osteoarthritis categories has remained stable (Fig. S3A–C). Across 21 geographical regions, most areas report that knee osteoarthritis accounts for more than 50% of cases, while hip osteoarthritis is the least prevalent, consistently comprising less than 10% of cases.

When analysing the composition of disease burden from the 2021 ASR of DALYs, it is observed that in certain regions, the proportion of knee osteoarthritis falls below 50% (Fig. S3C). These regions include Sub-Saharan Africa (49.6%), high-income North America (49.5%), Central Europe (42.1%), Eastern Europe (38.6%), and Central Asia (34.8%). Furthermore, in regions such as Eastern Europe and Central Asia, the prevalence of knee osteoarthritis is lower than that of hand osteoarthritis, with proportions of 38.6% versus 47% and 34.8% versus 50%, respectively (Fig. S3C).

Sex and age dynamics in osteoarthritis

In 21 global regions, the ASIR, ASPR, and ASR of DALYs have consistently been higher among women than men in both 1990 and 2021, underscoring the gender disparities in osteoarthritis prevalence (Fig. 2A–F).

From 1990 to 2021, the incidence of osteoarthritis has risen in all age groups, with the most notable increase (34.44%) observed in the 20–54 year age group (Fig. S6). However, the incidence levels remained relatively stable in the 65–74 year age group from 2005 to 2010. In both 1990 and 2021, the peak incidence of osteoarthritis was recorded in individuals aged 55–59, at 16.35% and 18.0% respectively (Fig. S6).

The latest data from 2021 indicates that females in every age group have higher rates and numbers in incidence, prevalence, and DALYs compared to males (Fig. 3A–F). When analysing incidence by age, there is a yearly increase in cases up to the age bracket of 50–54; after this age, the incidence numbers progressively decline (Fig. 3A and D). In terms of incidence rates: the highest rates are reached between ages 55–59, followed by a gradual decline, with a subsequent rebound in the 90–94 age group (Fig. 3A and D). Regarding prevalence: there is an annual rise in the number of cases up to the age of 55–69; thereafter, the prevalence declines, possibly due to mortality factors (Fig. 3B and E). The prevalence rates increase steadily with age among the existing patient population (Fig. 3B and E). Patterns in DALYs rates and cases mirror those observed in prevalence trends (Fig. 3C and F).

Risk factor

Globally, high body mass index (BMI) is the primary contributor to the DALYs associated with osteoarthritis. In 2021, the influence of high BMI on osteoarthritis varied across different SDI groups. On a global scale, 20.73% of osteoarthritis DALYs were attributable to high BMI (Fig. S4A). In regions with a high SDI, this figure climbed to 23.62%, compared to only 14.02% in regions with a low SDI (Fig. S4A). From 1990 to 2021, there was a consistent increase in the proportion of osteoarthritis DALYs attributed to high BMI across most SDI regions (Fig. S4B). Notably, in the high SDI regions, there was a discernible annual decrease between 2000 and 2009 (Fig. S4B).

Moreover, high BMI remains the primary risk factor for both knee and hip osteoarthritis. In high SDI regions—where the disease burden is most acute—high BMI accounted for 38.88% of knee osteoarthritis DALYs (Fig. S4C) and 40.76% of hip osteoarthritis DALYs (Fig. S4E). In contrast, in low SDI regions—which experience the lowest disease burden—high BMI contributed 21.88% and 21.59% to knee and hip osteoarthritis DALYs (Fig. S4C and E). Over the period from 1990 to 2021, regardless of the specific type of osteoarthritis, the contribution of high BMI to disease burden markedly increased across all SDI regions (Fig. S4D and F).

Prediction to 2060

The future predictions to 2060 from the GBD study for osteoarthritis were presented in Figs S5, S7, and S8. Globally, the number of osteoarthritis is expected to continue increasing. By 2060, it is projected that there will be 765 million individuals (95% UI: 659–871, Fig. S5) affected by osteoarthritis, including 432 million (95% UI: 361–503, Fig. S5) with knee osteoarthritis, 270 million (95% UI: 215–325, Fig. S5) with hand osteoarthritis, 48.4 million (95% UI: 39.6–57.2, Fig. S5) with hip osteoarthritis, and 70 million (95% UI: 62.3–77.6, Fig. S5) with other types of osteoarthritis.

As illustrated in Fig. S7, the global ASIR for osteoarthritis is expected to peak in 2042 at 563.27 per 100 000 (95% UI: 532.11–594.44), followed by a gradual decline, with a projected ASIR of 552.43 per 100 000 (95% UI: 428.15–676.70) by 2060. This suggests that the rate of new osteoarthritis cases may decrease slowly over time.

By 2060, the ASR for osteoarthritis DALYs is estimated to reach 277.62 per 100 000 (95% UI: 238.43–316.80, Fig. S8). Specifically, the ASR for knee osteoarthritis DALYs is expected to be 142.64 per 100 000 (95% UI: 117.75–167.52, Fig. S5), hand osteoarthritis at 87.92 per 100 000 (95% UI: 69.38–106.46, Fig. S5), hip osteoarthritis at 142.64 per 100 000 (95% UI: 69.38–106.46, Fig. S5), and other osteoarthritis types at 142.64 per 100 000 (95% UI: 117.75–167.52, Fig. S5).

Discussion

For the past few years, osteoarthritis has emerged as the most common chronic joint disease, affecting most individuals over the age of 65 [13]. In 2020, there were approximately 600 million individuals suffering from osteoarthritis worldwide, accounting for 8% of the global population [14]. Due to its escalating medical and social costs, osteoarthritis has become a significant public health issue [15]. A comprehensive assessment of epidemiological patterns of osteoarthritis may aid policymakers and clinicians in devising suitable prevention and management strategies. Our findings demonstrate a dramatic escalation in the global incidence of osteoarthritis from 1990 to 2021, with the number of cases increasing nearly threefold. This pronounced rise aligns with earlier research, highlighting a persistent upward trend in osteoarthritis prevalence across the globe [3].

Despite the stability of the global ASPR, the total number of osteoarthritis cases and the raw prevalence rate have surged significantly from 1990 to 2021, which mirrors the demographic trends of a growing and aging global population [16, 17]. These factors have driven the expansion of the osteoarthritis burden over the past 30 years and account for our projected decline in the ASIR after 2042 (Fig. S7). In 1990, the ASPR of osteoarthritis was predominantly higher in regions like high-income Asia Pacific and high-income North America, while it was notably lower in Southeast Asia and Eastern Sub-Saharan Africa—a trend that continued through to 2021. This skew is reflected in the higher ASPRs observed in high SDI regions and the lower rates in low SDI regions [18]. The elevated prevalence of osteoarthritis in high-income regions can be explained through multiple dimensions. Primarily, these areas benefit from superior healthcare infrastructure and living standards, which extend life expectancy and accentuate the demographics of an ageing population; these factors naturally increase the incidence and diagnostic rates of osteoarthritis. On the other hand, low-income regions, constrained by scarce medical resources, often witness a significant underdiagnosis of the condition, obscuring the true extent of its prevalence [11, 18, 19]. Moreover, high-income regions often experience elevated obesity rates, where increased BMI significantly intensifies strain on critical joints such as the knees and hips, exacerbating the prevalence of osteoarthritis [20, 21]. Furthermore, in high-income regions, there is a tendency for higher engagement in sedentary occupations such as office work, which often leads to reduced physical activity and diminished muscle strength [22, 23]. Additionally, specific genetic predispositions that increase susceptibility to osteoarthritis may be more prevalent among certain populations in high-income countries, contributing to higher incidences of the condition [24]. Therefore, high-income regions should prioritize the development of early diagnosis standards and treatment management strategies, while lower-income countries, grappling with rapid population growth and acute shortages of medical resources, require enhanced medical assistance and comprehensive medical guidelines.

Our analysis revealed that the ASR for osteoarthritis incidence, prevalence, and DALYs saw the fastest increase between 2000 and 2005, followed by a gradual decline after 2005. Between 2014 and 2021, the ASR remained relatively stable with minimal changes (Figs S5, S7, and S8). This trend may be attributed to the global recognition of the obesity issue in 2005, which prompted the implementation of relevant countermeasures [25]. From an age-centric viewpoint, our analysis indicates that the risk of developing osteoarthritis increases with age, displaying a unimodal distribution with a peak incidence in the 55–59 age group, a trend supported by another research [26]. Nonetheless, it is imperative to address the needs of all age groups, implementing tailored preventive, management, and therapeutic interventions for individuals across the lifespan.

Furthermore, our study reveals that women bear a significantly higher burden of osteoarthritis compared to men, and several factors may account for this disparity. Firstly, the decline in oestrogen levels post-menopause may impact cartilage health in women, rendering joint cartilage more vulnerable to degeneration and consequently elevating the risk of developing osteoarthritis [27]. Secondly, various studies suggest that inherent differences in muscle strength and joint stability between genders may influence susceptibility to osteoarthritis. Specifically, women generally have wider pelvises than men, a structural difference that can result in uneven force distribution across the knee joints, thereby altering joint biomechanics and increasing the risk of osteoarthritis [28–30]. Additionally, variations in obesity patterns also contribute significantly. Women tend to accumulate fat in the buttocks and thighs, which can increase the load on the joints of the lower limbs, particularly stressing the knee and hip joints [31, 32]. Finally, occupational demands also play a significant role in the increased prevalence of osteoarthritis among women, who are more often engaged in tasks requiring repetitive joint movements, such as household chores. This continuous physical strain can significantly raise the risk of developing osteoarthritis. Notably, a study observed a higher prevalence of knee osteoarthritis among Japanese women compared to American women, highlighting the influence of such occupational stressors [33].

In this research and earlier associated studies, high BMI stands as the sole risk factor for osteoarthritis with a quantitatively determined attributable burden in GBD assessments [3]. However, since it represents just around 20% of the risk, it is imperative that future research develops attribution metrics for additional potential risk factors, such as elevated bone mineral density (BMD) [34], Metabolic Syndrome (MetS) [35], smoking and previous knee injury [36]. Moreover, the translation of these risk factors into viable prevention and treatment strategies remains a subject of vigorous debate. Particularly controversial is the role of smoking in osteoarthritis, which continues to spark significant scholarly debate. Although some epidemiological studies suggest a negative correlation between smoking and the risk of knee osteoarthritis, this association could potentially be influenced by selection bias [37, 38]. Despite conflicting evidence, some studies continue to consider smoking a significant risk factor for the development of osteoarthritis [39, 40]. Additionally, risk factors for osteoarthritis vary by gender. A study exploring gender-specific risk factors found that older age, a BMI of 27 or higher, and a lower education level were associated with a higher risk of osteoarthritis in men. Conversely, the presence of bilateral thumb osteoarthritis was linked to a significantly higher risk in women [32]. Looking ahead, there is a pressing need to innovate and implement treatment and prevention strategies specifically tailored to these identified risk factors, aiming to significantly alleviate the burden of osteoarthritis.

Although this study offers a comprehensive analysis of the global burden and epidemiological trends of osteoarthritis, it still exhibits certain limitations. Firstly, this analysis is dependent on health data from a diverse spectrum of countries and regions, with notable variations in quality and completeness that may introduce significant biases into the findings due to inconsistent data collection practices. Secondly, GBD data typically emphasizes long-term trends, inherently introducing a delay that may overlook rapid fluctuations in disease burden, particularly in environments where socio-economic conditions are rapidly evolving. Lastly, the predictions of future disease burden are based on current trends and assumptions, which could be significantly altered by unforeseen socioeconomic developments and advancements in medical technology. Thus, researchers and policymakers must take these factors into account to assess and tackle the global challenge posed by osteoarthritis more precisely.

Conclusions

In summary, osteoarthritis continues to escalate as a significant public health issue, with global cases rising steadily from 1990 and projected to surge to 765 million by 2060. High-SDI regions bear the heaviest disease burden, while rapidly increasing rates in middle-SDI regions demand urgent preventive measures. Key findings: (i) Knee osteoarthritis dominates cases, with significantly higher prevalence among women; (ii) High BMI is the primary risk factor driving DALYs. Recommended strategies: (i) Policy interventions: high-SDI regions focus on weight management and early diagnosis; middle-SDI regions boost healthcare infrastructure. (ii) Research priorities: investigate other risks and gender-tailored strategies; (iii) Global collaboration: create registries to track trends, assess interventions, and ensure equitable resource distribution. Multidimensional collaboration is essential to develop personalized healthcare frameworks addressing diverse population needs.
