Electrocardiographic Risk Stratification in Critically Ill Cardiac Patients
Authors: Katherine C. Wu, Richard T. Carrick
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

The cardiac intensive care unit (CICU) is one of the most complex and difficult medical settings in which to provide care. Not only are admitted patients among the sickest in the hospital, with heavy burdens of comorbidities and high acute risk for serious major adverse cardiovascular events including death, but the volume of clinical data that are available and must be monitored for each patient is enormous.1 Patients undergo repeated batteries of testing, including blood work, electrocardiography (ECG), and imaging, often multiple times daily. In addition, a host of biophysical signals are captured from continuous physiologic monitoring from telemetry, plethysmography and vascular pressure transducers as well as from cardiac and respiratory mechanical support devices. Synthesizing all of these data in a meaningful way that allows care teams to intervene before an individual patient’s clinical deterioration can be an overwhelming task for even the most experienced CICU physicians. Recently, advanced computational techniques such as deep learning (DL) have emerged as powerful tools to leverage, automate, and standardize the interpretation of physiologic data. ECGs are particularly attractive and amenable to this approach, given their low cost, wide-availability, and the known electrophysiologic manifestations of myriad cardiovascular diseases.2, 3, 4

Toward this purpose, in this issue of JACC: Advances, Harmon et al5 present their work using an ECG-DL model previously designed to identify hyperkalemia with new application toward prediction of mortality in CICU patients. Here, ECGs from a large single-center cohort of 11,324 CICU patients with both 12-lead ECG and laboratory measurement of serum or plasma potassium at the time of CICU admission were used to generate continuous ECG-DL model predictions of potassium ≥5.0 mEq/L. These continuous hyperkalemia predictions were then used as the input for logistic regression assessment of the primary outcome of in-hospital mortality and Cox regression assessment of 30-day and 1-year mortality as secondary outcomes. Notably, the ECG-DL hyperkalemia model’s diagnostic discrimination was substantially lower than presented in the original model derivation (C-statistic of 0.71 vs 0.85-0.88), in part explainable by the authors’ decision to use a lower threshold to define hyperkalemia (≥5.0 vs ≥5.5 mEq/L) along with differences in this CICU cohort compared to the broader cohort of all outpatient/inpatients in which the model was originally developed. After adjusting for age and indices of comorbidity and disease severity, the authors found that ECG-DL hyperkalemia predictions were associated with in-hospital (OR: 1.06 per 0.1 increase in ECG-DL hyperkalemia prediction; 95% CI: 1.03-1.10), 30-day (OR: 1.03 per 0.1 increase; 95% CI: 1.01-1.06), and 1-year mortality (OR: 1.03 per 0.1 increase; 95% CI: 1.01-1.05). However, after further adjustment for laboratory-based potassium levels, the association was attenuated for in-hospital mortality (OR: 1.04 per 0.1 increase in ECG-DL hyperkalemia prediction; 95% CI: 1.00-1.08) and lost independent prognostic significance for 30-day and 1-year mortality. Additionally, the authors highlight in their discussion that patients with “false positive” hyperkalemia predictions (eg those with normal potassium laboratory values, but elevated ECG-DL prediction, accounting for >25% of the total cohort) were also at increased risk of in-hospital mortality.

Although this work is hypothesis generating and requires validation in external cohorts and comparison to DL-ECG models explicitly derived to predict CICU mortality, a number of interesting observations arise. While true that the model offers limited incremental information regarding longer-term outcomes such as 30-day and 1-year mortality (where ECG-DL output was not an independent predictor), the authors’ findings suggest that ECG-DL model predictions of hyperkalemia may provide information about a patient’s in-hospital prognosis extending beyond the presence of hyperkalemia. As described above, even patients with normal potassium levels but high-risk ECG-DL model predictions (“false positives”) were at increased likelihood of death before discharge. Two, otherwise identical, patients with the same age, comorbidities, disease severity, and potassium levels but a difference in ECG-DL score of 0.5 would be expected to have a >20% difference in their likelihoods of in-hospital mortality based on these results. Interestingly, the ECG-DL model also appeared to stratify patients into graded risk tiers relative to the “true negative” category (concordant ECG-DL and laboratory exclusion of hyperkalemia), whereby risk for subsequent mortality was greatest for “true positives” (concordant ECG-DL and laboratory detection of hyperkalemia) but was also elevated in both “false negatives” (those with hyperkalemia not detected by ECG-DL) for which risk was greater than that for “false positives” (incorrect ECG-DL prediction of hyperkalemia). These risk tiers appeared to recapitulate significant differences in patient comorbidities and disease severity using a combination of ECG-DL prediction and laboratory testing of hyperkalemia, findings that parallel that of a recent study of Asian patients presenting to the emergency room.6

What could be the potential utility of the proposed ECG-DL in CICU patients? Despite the ECG-DL models’ original, intended use for predicting hyperkalemia, ECG is unlikely to replace gold standard laboratory measurement of potassium levels, particularly in the CICU setting. However, given that ECG is readily accessible and quick to perform in the CICU, automated risk assessments derived from a single ECG (when used in conjunction with simultaneous potassium laboratory testing) could offer significant advantages over existing CICU risk scores (eg, APACHE II [Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation] and SOFA [Sequential Organ Failure Assessment]) that are much more complex to derive and often require 24 hours of multidimensional data collection.7 Elevated ECG-DL predictions in those without true hyperkalemia (“false positives”) may result from the incidental detection of other clinically significant, potentially life-threatening pathology with electrophysiologic manifestations that overlap with those related to hyperkalemia. For example, severe acidemia, ischemia, and new conduction abnormalities such as left bundle branch block may similarly result in QRS prolongation, PR interval delay or heart block, ST-segment deviations, and peaking of T-waves, even in the absence of overt hyperkalemia. Conversely, in patients with true hyperkalemia not flagged by the ECG-DL model, low-risk predictions may reflect an absence of significant electrophysiologic manifestations, and thereby attenuated risk, despite the elevated laboratory value. For example, dialysis patients in whom potassium levels are chronically elevated may not develop significant ECG manifestations or risk of arrhythmia until much higher levels of serum potassium than a patient with new, acute hyperkalemia. Alternatively, there could be unappreciated interactions between potassium levels and effects on the electrophysiologic properties of the myocardium, perhaps in the setting of subclinical myocardial disease, as postulated by the authors, that impact outcome. Interpretation is somewhat limited in this study by the lack of information regarding the time interval between ECG performance and collection of laboratory-based potassium levels. For example, it is possible that incorrect ECG-DL predictions may reflect either rapidly corrected hyperkalemic states (eg, the postcardiac arrest or diabetic ketoacidosis patient) or acutely developed hyperkalemia (eg, the acute renal failure or precardiac arrest patient) depending on which test was performed first and the duration of delay prior to performing the second. Unfortunately, minimal assessment of these “false positive” and “false negative” patients was included in the present manuscript and saliency analysis for interpretability of the ECG-DL predictions was not performed, which highlights limitations of the black box nature of some machine learning approaches. Future study using more readily explainable models may help clarify the electrophysiologic basis for these interesting results.8,9

This study from Harmon et al5 further highlights the challenges of developing novel DL tools for clinical use. Not only are there enormous barriers to clinical implementation of these advanced computational models, but CICU cardiologists must already contend with an overwhelming number of automated alerts and noisy data when caring for their patients. Deployment of novel prediction tools in the health care setting should be predicated on rigorous proof that they improve some aspect of patient care for which they were designed and may require prospective, randomized trials. Alternatively, there could be rationale for use for administrative (eg to inform or confirm case mix and guide hospital resource allocation) and/or research purposes (eg to more uniformly define baseline risk from which to compare impact of interventions). There thus remains significant promise, yet to be realized, for DL-ECG models to add value beyond that of existing clinical tools in the CICU.
