Laparoscopy versus open surgery in truncal vagotomy with gastrojejunostomy for peptic pyloric stenosis: a retrospective study of 151 patients
Authors: Souhaib Atri, Mahdi Hammami, Amine Sebai, Ahmed ben Mahmoud, Nadia Rabhi, Dhouha Cherif, Houcine Maghrebi, Amine Makni, Anis Haddad, Montassar Kacem
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPeptic ulcer disease continues to be a major health concern. Surgical intervention is usually reserved for complications, such as pyloric stenosis, leading to gastric outlet obstruction. We compared the outcomes of laparoscopic and open surgery.Materials and methodsThis is a retrospective study comparing Laparoscopic Truncal vagotomy and Gastrojejunostomy with open procedure. We examined the medical records of 151 patients treated for peptic pyloric stenosis at La Rabta Hospital in Tunis, Tunisia, from January 2000 to December 2018. The study focused on benign gastric outlet obstruction due to peptic ulcer disease, with patient progress monitored for over 24 months.ResultsA hundred and fifty-one patients treated for peptic ulcer stenosis by gastojejunostomy and truncal vagotomy were included in the study, featuring 52 cases of LTVGJ 99 cases ofOTVGJ. The male-to-female ratio was 5.2, with a mean age of 48 years for both groups. Perioperative outcomes revealed that OTVGJ had a shorter mean operative time (95 min) compared to LTVGJ (115 min), while the median hospital stay was longer for OTVGJ (7.9 days) versus LTVGJ (5.5 days). Both groups exhibited similar rates of delayed gastric emptying and postoperative gastrointestinal symptoms, with no cases of leakage or operative mortality. Diabetes was the only significant risk factor for delayed gastric emptying.ConclusionLaparoscopic truncal vagotomy and gastrojejunostomy for gastric outlet obstruction secondary to peptic ulcer disease is a safe and effective modality, offers low morbidity and satisfactory clinical status on long term follow up.Keywords: Peptic ulcer, Pyloric stenosis, Truncal vagotomy, Gastrojejunostomy, Open surgery, Laparoscopy

Introduction

Over the past three decades, advancements in medical and endoscopic treatments have redefined the approach to managing peptic ulcer disease (PUD). Nevertheless, complications arising from PUD, such as bleeding, perforation, and gastric outlet obstruction (GOO) remain a prominent health concern. Gastrointestinal outlet obstruction is the more precise term for “pyloric obstruction” or “stenosis” as the mechanical compression or blockage rather occurs in the distal stomach (pyloric, antrum, or duodenum) than the pylorus itself [1]. The pathophysiology of GOO in peptic ulcer stenosis is predominantly characterized by chronic inflammation and ulceration in the pyloric region. Obstruction typically occurs in the duodenal bulb, often as a result of scarred duodenal ulcers, or proximally, where the diagnosis of carcinoma is most probable [2].

Along with the development of Minimal Access Surgery (MAS), the laparoscopic approach has emerged as a promising alternative to conventional open surgery for treating peptic ulcer stenosis [3]. The introduction of MAS has enabled traditional procedures to be fully carried out through laparoscopic methods. In managing peptic pyloric stenosis, standard surgical approaches include truncal vagotomy and gastrojejunostomy (TV and GJ) [4]. The aim of surgical treatment is not only to alleviate the mechanical obstruction but also to address the underlying ulcerative disease effectively. This study aims to inform evidence-based management regarding the potential advantages of laparoscopic surgery for peptic ulcer stenosis in achieving better patient outcomes.

Material and methods

A comprehensive retrospective analysis was conducted, involving the medical records of patients treated for peptic pyloric stenosis at the Department of Surgery, La Rabta Hospital, Tunis, Tunisia, from January 2000 to December 2018. A total of 151 patients underwent surgical intervention for benign gastric outlet obstruction due to peptic ulcer disease.

The diagnosis of peptic pyloric stenosis was confirmed through upper gastrointestinal series, computed tomography, or upper endoscopy. Various data were collected, including age, gender, admission laboratory values, preoperative preparations, surgical approach, additional procedures, operating time, length of postoperative hospital stay, both intraoperative and postoperative complications, incidence and duration of postoperative ileus, as well as the duration until a semisolid diet could be resumed.

Early complications were defined as those occurring within 30 days following the surgery. Follow-up information was obtained by conducting patient interviews at the outpatient ward and reviewing medical charts. Statistical analysis was done using the Statistical Package for Social Science (SPSS) version 25.0 for Windows (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Inter-group comparisons were performed using Student's t-test for continuous variables and a two-tailed chi-square test for discrete variables. The accepted level of statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Operative procedure

The operative procedure for LTVGJ (laparoscopic total vagotomy with gastrojejunostomy) involved the following steps. Initially, two 10-mm trocars and one or two 5-mm trocars were used for the procedure. A diagnostic laparoscopy was performed to confirm the lesion. Bilateral truncal vagotomy was performed first. The liver was retracted upwardly, and stomach traction was applied by pulling caudally to expose the esophagogastric junction area. The lesser omentum near the junction was opened, and the pharyngoesophageal ligament was dissected medially. The branches of the anterior vagus were identified on the anterior surface of the esophagus, lifted, and resected. The posterior vagus, located posterior to the esophagus, was divided similarly. The resected tissues were sent for pathological confirmation.

For the gastrojejunostomy, a window was created by dividing the gastrocolic ligament along the greater curvature of the stomach. The posterior surface of the stomach was visualized, and the proximal jejunum, approximately 20 cm distal to the ligament of Treitz, was pulled up and hitched to the posterior surface of the stomach using intracorporeal sutures. The anastomosis was performed using a linear stapler, with one stapler placed in each organ. After inspecting the staple lines for luminal patency and bleeding, the common enterotomy hole was closed with laparoscopic intracorporeal continuous sutures. The trocar sites were closed, and the subcutis and cutis were restored with skin staples.

The operative procedure for OTVGJ (open total vagotomy with gastrojejunostomy) followed a similar process but with some differences. A midline incision was made from the xiphoid process above the umbilicus. Dissections were typically performed using electrocautery. Unlike the laparoscopic approach, all branches of the vagus were ligated after division. The gastrojejunostomy was performed using a handsewn technique with running sutures.

Results

A total of 151 patients, who were surgically treated for gastric outlet obstruction due to peptic ulcers, were included in the study. There were 52 cases of LTVGJ and 99 cases of OTVGJ. The sex ratio of male/female was 5.2 (127 male vs 24 female). Patients in the LTVGJ and OTVGJ groups were of similar mean age (48 years) with a maximum frequency between 40 and 65 years. There was no significant difference in the age and sex of the 2 groups. Preoperative comorbidities according to the American Society of Anesthesiology class and the stage of the diseases were also similar. In preoperative assessment, 146 patients (96.7 %) presented with vomiting, 118 patients (78.1 %) had abdominal pain. There was no significant difference in nutrition deficit in both groups (anemia, hypoalbuminemia, hypokalemia, hypochloremia…) (Table 1). The OTVGJ had a higher percentage of gastric distension (53.5 % vs 25 %). In all patients, electrolyte imbalance was corrected and the stomach was aspirated.

Regarding complications, only 3 patients suffered general complications: respiratory infection (aspiration pneumonias) treated with antibiotics. However, results were similar between the two groups regarding delayed gastric emptying (10.1 % in the OTVGJ group vs. 7.7 % in the LTVGJ group, P = 0.62). there were no leakages nor operative mortality in either group.

The two groups had no significant difference in the postoperative gastrointestinal symptoms (VISIK classification) (Annex A).

A univariate analysis was conducted to determine a possible correlation with postoperative gastroparesis. Diabetes was the only significant patient characteristic that was related to development of postoperative gastroparesis (P = 0.04).

Discussion

The management of gastroduodenal ulcers has evolved following the introduction of proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) and the eradication treatment for Helicobacter Pylori [5]. Surgical procedures are, currently, almost exclusively reserved for peptic ulcer complications such as hemorrhaging ulcers [6], perforation, and gastric outlet obstruction (GOO) [7]. Although older studies demonstrated a stable or even an increase in the number of patients admitted with one of these complications [8], a recent study published in 2020 demonstrated that the rate of admissions for all peptic ulcer complications has been decreasing in the United States [9]. To date, surgery for peptic ulcer stenosis remains an important treatment modality either as initial therapy or following unsuccessful pyloric dilation.

Laparoscopic surgery is a well-established alternative to open surgery across disciplines. While the extent of its impact may vary depending on the specific procedure, the overall benefits of laparoscopy, including reduced postoperative pain, improved cosmetic outcomes, shorter hospital stays, and faster recovery, are widely acknowledged in literature [[10], [11], [12]].

Conventional therapeutic interventions for gastric outlet obstruction (GOO) resulting from peptic ulcer disease, which remains unresponsive to conservative treatment and endoscopic dilation, mainly involve surgical options including both open and laparoscopic procedures, such as vagotomy and pyloroplasty [13].

In our study, all patients were treated with truncal vagotomy (TV) and gastrojejunostomy (GJ) either laparoscopic or open surgery. The mean age in both groups was 48 years [40–65] and males were more affected in the total population (Sex Ratio: 5.2). Our observations are similar to other studies where the majority of patients presenting with GOO secondary to PUD are men in their fifth and sixth decades [14,15]. The clinical preoperative presentation in our study population was also comparable to other studies with non-bilious vomiting being the most common symptom followed by abdominal pain [7,16]. In a recent retrospective study comparing LTVGJ with open surgery, Bhuiyan et al. also reported the occurrence of loss of appetite in 80 % of their population study [7].

The mean operative time of LTVGJ in our study (115 min) is comparable to previous studies as shown in Table 3. However, the reported mean operative time was longer than that in the OTVGJ group which is in sharp contrast to what is reported by Kim et al. [14] in their retrospective study including 21 patients where it was shorter in the LTVGJ group as opposed to the OTVGJ group. This disparity could be due to surgeons' experiences in laparoscopy. Another reason could be the fact that the gastrojejunostomy, when done laparoscopically, was performed with a stapler, but when done in open surgery, a handsewn technique was involved. The mean operative time for laparoscopic TV & GJ in previous older studies (published between 1990 and 2010) ranged from 114 to 220 min [[17], [18], [19]]. Rangarajan et al. reported the longest median operative time for laparoscopic TV & GJ (220 min). Nevertheless, when analyzing our data, we found that this duration in early years was superior to 110 min. Interestingly, the mean operative time became less than 100 min and was comparable to the open group in the past five years. These findings highlight the fact that with increasing laparoscopic expertise, operating time is significantly reduced.

In our study, the median for postoperative days to bowel movement restoration and tolerance to liquid diet in the LTVGJ group is 3.4, being shorter than the OTVGJ group (4.9). The same observations were reported by Kim et al. in their comparative study (3.4 for LTVGJ vs 5.7 for OTVGJ [14]. These findings were further corroborated by Bhuiyan et al. [7]. In the same study, the authors reported the occurrence of delayed gastric emptying in 26 % of patients treated laparoscopically. Our study revealed a remarkably smaller percentage of patients (7.7 %), which was also less than the OTVGJ group.

Furthermore, our study showed that the postoperative hospital stay was shorter for patients who were treated laparoscopically (5.5 vs 7.9). Our observations are consistent with previous studies [7,14].

Although our study was done retrospectively, we found clear benefits of LTVGJ in all aspects of postoperative outcomes compared with OTVGJ. Our study suggests that the laparoscopic approach for truncal vagotomy and gastrojejunostomy is at least as safe and effective as open truncal vagotomy and gastrojejunostomy in terms of shorter hospital stay.

A thorough and holistic evaluation of each surgical patient's unique circumstances is essential in providing patient-centered care and optimizing long-term outcomes in the management of complicated peptic ulcer disease (PUD).

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. First, it was conducted at a single center (La Rabta Hospital), which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other populations or healthcare settings. Second, the retrospective design introduces potential selection bias and relies on existing medical records, which may be incomplete or lack detailed patient-reported outcomes. Third, despite including 151 patients, the study may be underpowered to detect differences in rare but important complications such as anastomotic leakage, venous thromboembolism (VTE), or mortality. Finally, the study did not account for variations in surgical expertise, and differences in the surgeons' experience and technique may have influenced the perioperative outcomes.

Conclusion

The incidence of complicated peptic ulcer disease, precisely stenosis, has declined in recent years due to significant advancements in medical therapy. Nonetheless, minimally invasive surgery continues to play a crucial role in managing gastric outlet obstruction and the underlying peptic disease. Laparoscopic truncal vagotomy with gastrojejunostomy (LTVGJ) represents a viable and safe option, offering reduced operative time and shorter hospital stays as opposed to open surgery. It can be performed with minimal morbidity and better early postoperative outcomes. However, meticulous attention to anatomical identification and skillful handling of the stapler are critical for success. Further clinical experience and long-term outcome data are necessary before this approach can be recommended as a standard surgical practice.
