The extracellular matrix as a dynamic regulator of brain function and plasticity
Authors: Chuang Ge, Maral Tajerian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe brain extracellular matrix (ECM) is a dynamic and complex scaffold that not only provides structural support but also plays a key role in regulating synaptic plasticity, axonal regeneration, and cell signaling throughout development and into adulthood. This review focuses on the major components of the brain ECM—including structural proteins (e.g., collagen, elastin), adhesion glycoproteins (e.g., laminin, fibronectin, tenascin), and proteoglycans (e.g., aggrecan, brevican), with an emphasis on their region-specific expression patterns and dynamic changes during development and disease. We explore the role of the ECM in neurodegenerative diseases, psychiatric disorders, and chronic pain, and examine its interactions with glial cells (particularly astrocytes and microglia), which actively remodel the ECM through secretion of matrix-degrading enzymes such as matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs). This review also explores the unique challenges of studying the brain ECM, including its specialized composition and the technical limitations of existing methods. We discuss how recent advances in imaging, mass spectrometry, and omics technologies may provide new insights into ECM dynamics. Finally, we consider future directions, highlighting the potential of AI-based modeling and multi-omics integration to reveal the role of the ECM in brain function and dysfunction and inform a novel therapeutic strategy for neurological and psychiatric diseases.

Introduction

The extracellular matrix (ECM) of the brain remains one of the most understudied components of the central nervous system. Although individual aspects of brain ECM biology have been reviewed—including perineuronal nets in synaptic plasticity and specific ECM molecules in individual disease contexts—an integrative framework connecting ECM mechanisms across neurodegenerative, psychiatric, and pain-related disorders is lacking. This review addresses this gap by synthesizing current evidence on ECM composition, glial-mediated remodeling, and disease-specific ECM alterations, while evaluating how recent technological advances in spatial omics, mass spectrometry, and high-resolution imaging are reshaping the field. The ECM is a complex three-dimensional scaffold that surrounds cells and tissues, accounting for approximately 40% of the volume during fetal development and 20% of the human adult brain (Dityatev et al., 2010a; Syková and Nicholson, 2008). The ECM is composed of a heterogeneous mixture of proteins and carbohydrates organized into distinct structural and signaling assemblies. Fibrillar proteins such as collagens and elastin provide tensile strength and structural integrity (Soles et al., 2023); while laminins form self-assembling polymeric networks that constitute the core architecture of basement membranes (Yurchenco, 2011). Additional matricellular glycoproteins; including fibronectins and tenascins; modulate cell–matrix interactions and ECM remodeling (Murphy-Ullrich and Sage, 2014). These protein networks are interwoven with proteoglycans (PGs) such as aggrecan and brevican; as well as glycosaminoglycans (GAGs) including chondroitin sulfate (CS) and hyaluronic acid (HA); which together regulate molecular diffusion; growth factor availability; and mechanical properties of the matrix (Alonge et al., 2019). Cellular interactions with the ECM are mediated primarily through dedicated ECM receptors; including integrins; discoidin domain receptors (DDRs); syndecans; and CD44; which transduce biochemical and mechanical cues into intracellular signaling pathways (Dityatev and Schachner, 2003; Karamanos et al., 2021). While neural cell adhesion molecules (NCAMs) play important roles in neuron–neuron and glia–neuron interactions, they are not the principal receptors mediating ECM adhesion (Maness and Schachner, 2007). Through coordinated receptor engagement; the ECM regulates tissue homeostasis; synaptic plasticity; and blood–brain barrier integrity (Dityatev and Schachner, 2003; Dityatev and Schachner, 2006). Spatially, the brain ECM is organized into several distinct architectures, including the diffuse interstitial matrix, perineuronal nets (PNNs), the vascular basement membrane, perisynaptic ECM, perinodal ECM, and axonal coats—each exhibiting distinct molecular composition and functional specialization (Dityatev and Schachner, 2006). PNNs enwrap primarily the soma and proximal dendrites of specific neuronal subtypes; perisynaptic ECM; enriched in brevican; surrounds individual synaptic contacts and modulates neurotransmitter diffusion; perinodal ECM occupies the nodes of Ranvier; where it serves as an ion-diffusion barrier critical for saltatory conduction; and axonal coats form thin ECM sheaths around presynaptic terminals (Fawcett et al., 2019; Frischknecht and Seidenbecher, 2012). Continuous ECM remodeling by astrocytes, microglia, neurons, oligodendrocyte-lineage cells, and vascular-associated cells establishes a dynamic structural and signaling network that integrates cellular activity with tissue-level organization.

The ECM does not merely transmit signals, it stores and filters information (Ferrer-Ferrer and Dityatev, 2018). Through mechanisms such as sulfation patterning; proteolytic remodeling; and activity-dependent assembly; the ECM encodes past neural activity; developmental history; and environmental exposures in a persistent physical form (Burnside and Bradbury, 2014; Sorg et al., 2016). In this sense, the ECM operates as a slow-changing structural layer that complements fast synaptic transmission and transient intracellular signaling. This property may be a mechanism by which experiences can produce durable changes in neural circuitry and behavioral output.

This reconceptualization challenges the notion of cellular autonomy. Neuronal and glial phenotypes are not solely determined by genetic programs or intrinsic signaling pathways but are constrained and specified by the surrounding matrix (Hynes, 2009). Identical cells embedded in distinct ECM environments exhibit fundamentally different functional properties; highlighting the ECM as a contextual regulator of cellular identity (Rozario and DeSimone, 2010). Based on accumulating evidence, we propose that the ECM may represent a distinct regulatory layer of brain organization that operates alongside — and interacts with — genetic programs, cellular processes, and neural circuit dynamics.

ECM remodeling constitutes a mode of intercellular communication together with chemical neurotransmission and electrical signaling (Dityatev et al., 2010a). Through glial-mediated synthesis and degradation; mechanical tuning; and receptor engagement; the ECM orchestrates coordinated changes across neurons; astrocytes; microglia; and endothelial cells. This distributed control positions the ECM as a central organizer of critical periods; synaptic stabilization; and long-term circuit commitment (Pizzorusso et al., 2002). Consequently; alterations in ECM structure actively organize disease states by stabilizing maladaptive network configurations (Fawcett et al., 2022).

In this review, we present emerging evidence demonstrating how the brain ECM shapes neural function in health and disease. We focus on its molecular composition, dynamic remodeling, and interactions with glial and vascular systems, and examine its roles in neurodegenerative diseases, psychiatric disorders, and chronic pain. Finally, we discuss technological advances and conceptual frameworks that are redefining ECM biology and highlighting ECM as a key component of brain function.

Foundations, composition, and dynamic remodeling of the brain extracellular matrix

Historical overview

The formal study of the brain ECM dates to the 19th century. In 1865, Deiters’ posthumously published work provided among the earliest detailed depictions of individual nerve cells, in which he distinguished dendritic processes from the single axon; although his illustrations showed neurons in their tissue context, he did not conceptually identify or characterize the non-cellular space surrounding them (Deiters and Schultze, 1865). Gierke introduced the term “Grundsubstanz;” describing it as a transparent; homogeneous; supportive substance; laying one of the earliest conceptual foundations for what we now interpret as a non-cellular component of brain tissue (Gierke and Theil, 1885). Camillo Golgi first drew attention to dense reticular structures surrounding neuronal cell bodies; providing an initial description in 1893 and a more detailed account in 1898. These structures; later termed perineuronal nets (PNNs); were described as continuous envelopes enwrapping neuronal somata and extending along proximal dendrites (Golgi, 1885; Celio et al., 1998; Golgi, 1893). Ramón y Cajal contested Golgi’s interpretation of perineuronal nets, dismissing them as likely precipitation artifacts caused by the coagulation of extracellular fluids during fixation (Ramón y Cajal, 1909). While Cajal’s critique was eventually shown to be incorrect regarding PNN authenticity; his reference to extracellular fluid nonetheless drew attention — albeit inadvertently — to the existence of a non-cellular component in brain tissue. In 1903; Nissl proposed the term “interzelluläres Grau (intercellular gray matter)”; arguing that it expanded as brain complexity increased; which corresponded to neuropil; but strengthened the concept that “the brain has intercellular substance” (Nissl, 1903). Following Cajal’s influential dismissal of perineuronal nets as fixation artifacts; the study of these structures was largely abandoned by mainstream neuroscience from the 1920s onward. Although a small number of researchers; including Besta and Belloni; continued to document PNNs in both normal and pathological mammalian brains; the field was dominated by the neuron doctrine; with the prevailing focus on neuronal connectivity rather than the extracellular environment (Celio, 1999).

In the 1950s to 1960s, histochemical techniques such as periodic acid–Schiff (PAS) staining led to a breakthrough in neurobiology by visually confirming the presence of glycosaminoglycans (GAGs, initially known as mucopolysaccharides) in brain tissue. These studies identified components such as chondroitin sulfate (Feigin, 1980) and hyaluronic acid (Arteta, 1956) and highlighted their heterogeneous distribution across brain regions and changes associated with aging. At the same time; the rapid development of electron microscopy paradoxically contributed to the denial of ECM presence in the brain; due in part to artifacts introduced by sample preparation methods. Aldehyde-based fixation protocols induced tissue dehydration and shrinkage; resulting in measurements that showed intercellular spaces as narrow as 15–20 nm; occupying less than 5% of tissue volume. Subsequent studies using alternative techniques demonstrated that ECM volume estimates are highly sensitive to conventional processing; and more realistic measurements placed brain ECM volume closer to 20% (González-Aguilar, 1969; Van Harreveld et al., 1965). As the ECM was starting to be viewed as more than just “empty space”, more effort was made to quantify it. For example, Charles Nicholson quantified the ECS volume fraction to 15–20% through tetramethylammonium ion (TMA+) diffusion method and dye dilution experiments, establishing the function of ECS as an ion buffer and signal diffusion medium and highlighting the heterogeneous and dynamic nature of the ECS as a function of cell volume, ECM composition, and neural activity (Nicholson and Phillips, 1981).

During the 1980s and 1990s, advances in molecular biology catalyzed a major turning point in brain ECM research. Researchers cloned and characterized a central nervous system-specific family of hyaluronan-binding proteoglycans (lecticans/hyalectans, including aggrecan, brevican, neurocan, and versican), along with their associated link proteins (such as HAPLN1 and HAPLN2) (Rauch et al., 1992; Yamada et al., 1994; Ruoslahti, 1996; Hirakawa et al., 2000; Yamaguchi, 2000). The identification of these core constituents defined the brain’s unique biochemical microenvironment, establishing at the molecular level that the brain ECM is not a mere extension of peripheral connective tissue, but rather a highly specialized macromolecular network intimately coupled to neural function. At the end of the 20th century, biochemical separation techniques (such as guanidine hydrochloride extraction) helped identify the core components of the brain ECM (Rauch, 2004); Concurrently; PNNs were formally highlighted as a separate entity—a specialized ECM structure distinct from the diffuse neuropil matrix that envelops neuronal cell bodies (Celio and Blumcke, 1994). Finally, continuous advances in high-resolution imaging, such as confocal microscopy, enabled the three-dimensional reconstruction of the ECM, revealing its dynamic spatial assembly (Fig. 1).

Extracellular matrix composition

The mechanical backbone of the ECM is primarily formed by structural proteins such as collagen and elastin, with type IV collagen enriched in vascular basement membranes (Gould et al., 2005) and type VI collagen prominent in interstitial matrices (Cescon et al., 2016; Gregorio et al., 2018). Other isoforms, including types I, III, and V, are less abundant within the brain parenchyma (constituting <5%) but are concentrated in the meninges and the adventitia of large-caliber blood vessels (Wareham et al., 2024). These elements confer tensile strength and elasticity, supporting tissue integrity and vascular compliance.

ECM glycoproteins, including laminins (LN-511, LN-111), fibronectin (FN), and the tenascin family (TnC, TnR), function as multi-domain integrators of cell adhesion, migration, and receptor-mediated signaling. Laminins are key components of the vascular basement membrane and maintain blood-brain barrier (BBB) integrity via integrin-mediated anchoring, while also promoting axonal growth (Motallebnejad and Azarin, 2020; Halder et al., 2023; Hayashi et al., 2021), indicating that distinct laminin isoforms contribute to neural development, synaptic stability, and BBB homeostasis through integrin-mediated signaling pathways. Fibronectin, through its RGD motifs, regulates cell adhesion and migration during development primarily via integrin receptors such as α5β1 and αv-containing integrins (Takahashi et al., 2007; Marchetti et al., 2010; Stettler and Galileo, 2004). Tenascin-C (TnC) interacts with the integrin receptor α8β1 to promote neuronal adhesion and neurite outgrowth, as demonstrated in embryonic neurons (Varnum-Finney et al., 1995). In contrast; Tenascin-R (TnR) plays a key structural role in the perineuronal nets (PNNs) of the mature brain; where it supports the assembly and stabilization of aggrecan-rich ECM; thereby maintaining inhibitory circuit integrity (Morawski et al., 2014).

Proteoglycans and their sulfated glycosaminoglycan (GAG) side chains confer functional specificity to ECM domains. Anchored by the massive, non-sulfated hyaluronan (HA) backbone via link proteins, the lectican family defines the microarchitecture of the brain ECM, while HA itself regulates extracellular volume and diffusion (Melrose, 2023; Arranz et al., 2014), through its highly hydrophilic nature and interactions with cell surface receptors like CD44. Aggrecan is central to PNNs, and its highly sulfated chondroitin sulfate (CS) chains form a dense electrostatic and steric barrier. Importantly, specific sulfation motifs within these CS chains mediate the sequestration of signaling molecules like OTX2 (Miyata and Kitagawa, 2016; Grødem et al., 2025; Beurdeley et al., 2012), thereby stabilizing inhibitory synapses and constraining excessive plasticity. Other lecticans exhibit distinct spatial and functional profiles: versican V2 is prominently expressed in white matter and is essential for the structure of the node of Ranvier and saltatory conduction. In V2-deficient mice, the formation of the extracellular matrix surrounding the nodes is largely impaired, with concomitant loss of tenascin-R and phosphacan from the perinodal space, providing strong evidence that versican V2 organizes the nodal ECM assembly in vivo (Dours-Zimmermann et al., 2009). Furthermore; brevican specifically regulates perisynaptic microenvironments and synaptic plasticity; while neurocan strongly influences developmental neurogenesis and injury responses. Heparan sulfate proteoglycans (HSPGs) such as perlecan and GPI-anchored glypicans; through their fine-tuned sulfation patterns; bind and present growth factors (e.g.; FGF2; BMP4); regulating neurogenesis and synapse maturation (Kerever et al., 2014; Hagihara et al., 2000). In addition, perlecan is a key structural component of the vascular basement membrane, where it cooperates with laminins and type IV collagen to stabilize the extracellular matrix architecture and support blood–brain barrier integrity (Nakamura et al., 2019).

Extracellular matrix remodeling

Within the central nervous system, the extracellular matrix is not a static scaffold but a highly dynamic environment subject to continuous remodeling. This dynamic regulation—involving the synthesis, structural reorganization, and degradation of matrix components—is orchestrated through interactions among neurons, astrocytes, microglia, and endothelial cells (Lau et al., 2013; Tewari and Conant, 2024; Dityatev et al., 2010b). Neuronal dendrites and axons traverse the ECM, interacting with glial processes to form tripartite synapses (De Luca et al., 2020; Letourneau et al., 1994). Disruption of key extracellular matrix components can drive pathological states by uncoupling neuron–glia interactions, leading to impaired synaptic maturation, defective perineuronal net formation, and network dysfunction (Geissler et al., 2013). Understanding these molecular mechanisms offers crucial insights into the regulation of brain function, synaptic plasticity, and pathology.

The structural integrity and functional state of the ECM are primarily governed by a balance between synthesis and proteolytic degradation. Astrocytes act as the primary source of ECM synthesis, secreting core components such as hyaluronan (HA), chondroitin sulfate proteoglycans (CSPGs), and tenascin-C to maintain the perisynaptic microenvironment and form glial scars that border lesions and support tissue repair following CNS injury (Melrose, 2024; Faissner et al., 2010; Anderson et al., 2016). Conversely, ECM degradation is heavily dependent on specific proteases, notably matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and the ADAMTS (a disintegrin and metalloproteinase with thrombospondin motifs) family. Microglia, as the resident immune sentinels, are a major source of these degradative enzymes (Kreutzberg, 1996; Picard et al., 2021). Under physiological conditions, localized release of MMPs (particularly MMP-2 and MMP-9) and ADAMTS proteoglycanases (such as ADAMTS-4 and -5) by both microglia and astrocytes facilitates the targeted breakdown of CSPGs and the disruption of perineuronal nets (PNNs), thereby modulating synaptic stability and network dynamics (Soles et al., 2023; Kelly et al., 2015; Gottschall and Howell, 2015). Single-cell transcriptomic analyses of the brain further indicate that MMP expression is highly context-dependent and distributed across specific neural, glial, and vascular cell populations within the CNS (Zeisel et al., 2018; Vanlandewijck et al., 2018).

In pathological states, such as epilepsy, neuroinflammation, or chronic pain, aberrant activation of transcription factors like HIF-1 and NF-κB strongly upregulates the expression of MMPs (e.g., MMP-9, MMP-2, MMP-3) and ADAMTS. This cascade leads to excessive PNN degradation, maladaptive neuronal excitability, and pathological pain signaling (Yang et al., 2022; Dubey et al., 2017; Rempe et al., 2016). This proteolytic activity is tightly counterregulated by tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases (TIMPs), which neutralize MMPs to prevent unconstrained matrix destruction (Baker et al., 2002). Furthermore; the turnover of the massive HA backbone is regulated by specific hyaluronidases (such as HYALs and TMEM2); ensuring the coordinated remodeling of the entire macromolecular network (Sherman et al., 2015; Tobisawa et al., 2021).

Recent work has expanded our understanding of ECM turnover beyond extracellular proteolysis, identifying receptor-mediated endocytosis as a complementary mechanism. Dankovich and colleagues demonstrated that neurons actively internalize specific PNN components, such as Tenascin-R, via endocytic uptake and subsequently recycle them back to the cell surface (Dankovich et al., 2021). This finding reveals that the neuronal role extends beyond being passive residents embedded within the matrix or localized producers of core PNN components (such as aggrecan and link proteins) (Carulli et al., 2006; Giamanco et al., 2010). Instead, neurons contribute to a highly localized, activity-dependent membrane resurfacing process that allows for rapid ECM reorganization independent of global enzymatic degradation.

Beyond local synaptic effects, ECM dynamics are closely linked to neurovascular function and blood-brain barrier (BBB) integrity. Within the neurovascular unit, astrocyte endfeet form an intimate interface with the vascular basement membrane, where they actively regulate perivascular ECM composition to maintain BBB stability and neurovascular coupling (Abbott et al., 2006). Brain endothelial cells interact with basement membrane ECM via integrins; particularly β1-integrin; to regulate tight junction proteins (claudin-5; ZO-1); thus maintaining barrier homeostasis (Osada et al., 2011; Edwards and Bix, 2019). GPR126 and GPR124–RECK–WNT7A/B complexes orchestrate integrin and β-catenin signaling to guide angiogenesis and BBB patterning during development and repair (Kakogiannos et al., 2024; Anderson et al., 2011; Cho et al., 2017; Vanhollebeke et al., 2015). Under ischemic or inflammatory conditions, MMP-mediated ECM degradation increases BBB permeability, facilitating immune cell infiltration, while subsequent ECM reconstruction contributes to barrier repair (Zhang et al., 2021a; Paul et al., 1998). Consistent with these mechanisms, recent work on the high-inflammation subtype of schizophrenia has identified pronounced remodeling of both ECM and the vasculature within the subventricular zone (SEZ). Transcriptomic analyses revealed increased expression of several collagens (e.g., COL1A1, COL3A1, COL4A1) along with ECM-stabilizing molecules such as TIMP1, FN1, and TGFB2, suggesting a fibrosis-like thickening of the SEZ niche and potential disruption of its neurogenic milieu (North et al., 2024).

In summary, brain ECM remodeling reflects a coordinated balance of protease activity, transcription factor-driven signaling, and receptor-mediated recycling. As new effectors continue to be identified—including the cell-surface hyaluronidase TMEM2 and the molecular machinery underlying endocytic ECM clearance—there is growing opportunity to develop selective therapeutic approaches that move beyond broad-spectrum protease inhibition toward pathway-specific modulation of maladaptive plasticity (Fig. 2).

ECM in disease pathogenesis

The brain ECM plays a key role in the pathogenesis of various diseases by regulating cell behavior, tissue integrity, and signaling pathways. As ECM research has primarily focused on cancer, with numerous reviews examining its impact on tumorigenesis and metastasis, this review will not discuss cancer-related aspects. Instead, we will focus on the involvement of ECM in three key disease categories: neurodegenerative disorders, psychiatric disorders, and pain. The following section will explore how ECM alterations influence disease progression and provide potential therapeutic insights into the unique mechanisms underlying each disease.

ECM in neurodegenerative diseases

Dysregulation of the brain ECM is implicated in several neurodegenerative disorders, including Alzheimer’s disease (AD), Parkinson’s disease (PD), and multiple sclerosis (MS), with disease-specific mechanisms and consequences. While ECM remodeling is a common feature across these diseases, the specific mechanisms and consequences vary significantly depending on the disorder.

In Alzheimer’s disease (AD), structural remodeling of the brain ECM actively contributes to disease progression through defined molecular interactions. Heparan sulfate proteoglycans (HSPGs), key components of both interstitial ECM and vascular basement membranes, bind β-amyloid (Aβ), facilitating its aggregation and extracellular retention (Shimizu et al., 2009). More recently; a study using a murine model of amyloidosis shows that cerebellar ECM proteoglycans (e.g.; brevican) actively bind amyloid-β; forming ECM–Aβ complexes that alter its aggregation behavior. These complexes are preferentially taken up by microglia; suggesting the ECM facilitates amyloid clearance and helps prevent plaque formation in this region (Cangalaya et al., 2026). More broadly, regional differences in ECM composition, particularly in proteoglycan abundance and structure, may shape how amyloid-β is sequestered and cleared, thereby contributing to brain region–specific vulnerability or resistance to plaque formation.

In addition to Aβ sequestration, aberrant ECM signaling extends to tau pathology; for instance, integrin engagement drives PI3K-Akt signaling cascades that promote filamentous tau-induced proinflammatory astrogliosis (Wang et al., 2023). PNNs; which preferentially stabilize parvalbumin-positive (PV+) interneurons and inhibitory circuit function; show significant alterations in AD brains. While multiple studies have reported reduced PNN labeling using Wisteria floribunda agglutinin (WFA) lectin; recent evidence demonstrates that chondroitin sulfate sulfation patterns are altered in AD; with shifts in the ratio of 4-sulfated to 6-sulfated CS chains (Scarlett et al., 2022; Logsdon et al., 2022). These sulfation changes may reduce WFA binding affinity, suggesting that observed decreases in WFA-labeled PNNs may partly reflect altered PNN composition rather than outright structural loss. Regardless of whether the primary change is compositional or quantitative, these PNN alterations are associated with synaptic destabilization, impaired inhibitory control, and cognitive decline. At the vascular interface, thickening of the basement membrane and enzymatic disruption of its heparan sulfate components collectively impair perivascular and glymphatic clearance of Aβ, further exacerbating amyloid accumulation (Thomsen et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2021b). Therefore, the brain ECM constitutes an important regulatory link in AD pathology by modulating Aβ dynamics, tau-mediated inflammation, synaptic integrity, and vascular function. It is noteworthy that, as our knowledge of the ECM expands, we may revise our markers for pathology in AD. For example, a recent study has identified CRES (Cystatin-Related Epididymal Spermatogenic), a protein previously thought to function in reproductive tissues, as a novel component of the brain ECM, where it exists in part as a functional amyloid and may contribute to ECM structure and brain function (Gomez et al., 2026). This finding challenges the idea that amyloid is synonymous with pathology and suggests that amyloid structures can be bona fide physiological ECM elements. More broadly, it increases the molecular diversity of “known” ECM components and indicates that ECM structure may rely partly on self-assembling protein aggregates.

In Parkinson’s disease (PD), ECM dysregulation is characterized by abnormal enrichment of fibrillar ECM components, including type I and IV collagens and fibronectin, particularly in cortical regions. Proteomic and histological analyses link this ECM accumulation to astrocyte activation and reduced expression of synaptic proteins, suggesting a mechanistic connection between ECM remodeling, neuroinflammation, and synaptic dysfunction (Downs et al., 2022). Increased ECM deposition is predicted to elevate local matrix stiffness; thereby altering integrin-mediated mechanotransduction in neurons and glial cells and impairing synaptic plasticity (Franze, 2013). Consistently; transcriptomic analyses of dopaminergic neurons derived from PD patients reveal downregulation of ECM-related pathways; including fibronectin–integrin signaling axes; accompanied by reduced firing frequency and abnormal glutamate transport (Stern et al., 2022).

In multiple sclerosis (MS), ECM remodeling generates a fibrotic and mechanically restrictive microenvironment that promotes inflammation while limiting repair. Lesion regions exhibit marked upregulation of collagens (types I, III, and V), proteoglycans such as biglycan and decorin, and hyaluronan (HA), forming dense ECM structures that enhance immune cell infiltration and inhibit remyelination (Mohan et al., 2010). Accumulated high-molecular-weight HA specifically suppresses oligodendrocyte progenitor cell maturation through TLR2-dependent signaling; providing a direct molecular mechanism linking ECM composition to remyelination failure (Sloane et al., 2010). Altered expression of ECM glycoproteins; including tenascin-R/C and matricellular proteins such as SPARC and SPARCL1; further modulates cell adhesion; migration; and inflammatory signaling (Stephenson et al., 2024). Mechanical measurements in chronic demyelination models reveal increased tissue stiffness associated with excessive ECM deposition; reinforcing the concept that ECM mechanics act as a physical and biochemical barrier to regeneration (Urbanski et al., 2019).

These findings underscore the disease-specific roles of ECM: in AD, compositional shifts and impaired clearance mechanisms affect Aβ/tau dynamics and synaptic integrity; in PD, fibrotic ECM accumulation is linked to altered mechanotransduction and synaptic dysfunction; and in MS, a mechanically stiff, HA-enriched matrix modulates immune responses and actively inhibits repair mechanisms (Table 1).

ECM in psychiatric disorders

ECM alterations are increasingly recognized as critical contributors to the pathophysiology of psychiatric disorders, where changes in PNN density and matrisome gene expression (Pantazopoulos et al., 2021) disrupt the balance between synaptic stability and plasticity. In schizophrenia; this imbalance manifests primarily as a consistent reduction in PNN density across multiple brain regions; a pathology intimately linked to the dysfunction of parvalbumin-positive (PV+) inhibitory interneurons. Key postmortem studies identified significant PNN decreases in the entorhinal cortex and amygdala (Pantazopoulos et al., 2010); and prefrontal cortex (Mauney et al., 2013); often accompanied by aggrecan and chondroitin-6-sulfate abnormalities (Pantazopoulos et al., 2015). These postmortem investigations also uncovered marked CSPG-related glial abnormalities: in the amygdala and entorhinal cortex of schizophrenia subjects; CSPG-labeled (WFA-positive) glial cells were strikingly increased in the absence of astrogliosis; with concurrent upregulation of multiple CSPG mRNAs (including versican; neurocan; and brevican) in the basal amygdala (Pantazopoulos et al., 2010). Subsequent analyses revealed that aggrecan-immunoreactive glial cells and chondroitin-6-sulfate glial clusters were conversely decreased in the same region (Pantazopoulos et al., 2015); indicating that CSPG abnormalities differentially affect distinct glial cell populations and suggesting that aberrant glial CSPG metabolism may directly contribute to PNN destabilization. These deficits extend to the thalamic reticular nucleus; where they coincide with oxidative stress and parvalbumin neuron loss (Steullet et al., 2018). Beyond these region-specific histochemical findings; transcriptomic profiling across cortical and subcortical brain regions has revealed widespread dysregulation of matrisome gene expression in schizophrenia; affecting key ECM functional categories including CSPGs; ECM glycoproteins; cross-linking enzymes; and ECM regulators (Pantazopoulos et al., 2021). These data indicate that ECM pathology in schizophrenia is not confined to PNN alterations in discrete brain areas but constitutes a brain-wide disruption of matrisome organization.

Recent convergent models suggest that PNN degradation acts as a mechanistic bridge between NMDA receptor hypofunction and impaired cortical network synchrony (Hosseini et al., 2025). Specifically; the loss of PNNs—which normally protect PV+ neurons and close critical periods of plasticity—may prevent the proper stabilization of inhibitory circuits during neurodevelopment; as evidenced by transcriptome alterations in PV+ neurons. Although PNN reductions and NMDA receptor dysfunction have been documented in separate patient samples; they converge on a model in which PNN degradation may amplify the impact of glutamatergic hypofunction on PV+ interneuron maturation and synaptic integration; positioning PNN pathology as a mechanistic link among glutamatergic dysfunction; dopaminergic dysregulation; and impaired cortical network synchrony in schizophrenia (Enwright III et al., 2018). Furthermore; disrupted Reelin signaling—demonstrated in both Reln-deficient mice and human cohorts—and the downregulation of ECM-synapse interaction molecules (e.g.; L1CAM; NPTXR) further compromise network stability and promote maladaptive plasticity (Zhu et al., 2025; Tavitian et al., 2025; Unzueta-Larrinaga et al., 2026; Valderrama-Mantilla et al., 2025).

In contrast to the widespread deficits seen in schizophrenia, ECM remodeling in depression and certain autism spectrum disorder (ASD) models often involves a paradoxical “over-stabilization” of neural circuits. In depression, chronic stress induces a region-specific increase in PNN density within the hippocampus and amygdala, restricting circuit plasticity in a manner that correlates with cognitive impairment and the persistent encoding of trauma (Blanco and Conant, 2021; Smail et al., 2023; Koskinen et al., 2020). This “biological scarring” is particularly evident in individuals with a history of childhood maltreatment, who demonstrate elevated PNN density in the ventromedial prefrontal cortex (Tanti et al., 2022). However; the remodeling process is complex and context-dependent; for instance; postmortem evidence reveals reduced hippocampal PNNs in individuals with major depressive disorder; an effect that is further modulated by comorbid substance use disorder (Valeri et al., 2024). Similarly; in ASD models such as Shank3B or Engrailed-2 (En2) knockout mice; increased PNN density across the cerebellum; hippocampus; and somatosensory cortex is associated with social deficits and impaired sensory discrimination (Liu et al., 2023; Mattioni et al., 2024). This regional PNN upregulation is further supported by postmortem human studies identifying alterations in the cerebellum and striatum (Brandenburg and Blatt, 2022); as well as molecular profiling of hippocampal tissue from children with ASD; which reveals significant early-developmental alterations in ECM composition (Rexrode et al., 2024).

The role of ECM proteolysis in these conditions is bidirectional, depending on the basal state of the matrix. While ASD models like Shank3B exhibit PNN accumulation, the Fmr1 knockout model of Fragile X syndrome displays pathologically elevated MMP-9 activity that leads to PNN deficits—particularly in the auditory cortex. In this context, pharmacological or genetic inhibition of MMP-9 is required to restore normal PNN formation and normalize sensory processing (Pirbhoy et al., 2020; Wen et al., 2018). This requirement for active ECM proteolysis is mirrored in affective disorders, where depression is associated with elevated peripheral TIMP4 and MMP3 (Ghelfi et al., 2025). Importantly; this peripheral signature reflects the central necessity of MMP activity for antidepressant efficacy. For instance; the antidepressant venlafaxine provides therapeutic benefit by stimulating MMP-9-dependent PNN proteolysis to restore excitatory/inhibitory balance (Alaiyed et al., 2019; Alaiyed et al., 2020), while the sustained effects of treatments like ketamine similarly require intact PNN structures for network restabilization (Donegan and Lodge, 2017; Alaiyed and Conant, 2019). Thus, MMP-9 acts as a key regulator of neural plasticity; its therapeutic modulation requires a precise understanding of whether the underlying pathology involves matrix deficiency or excessive stabilization. Apart from these enzymatic changes, prefrontal astrocytes further exacerbate stress-induced synaptic dysfunction by upregulating SPARC and various collagens (e.g., COL5A1), leading to physical enlargement of the synaptic cleft (Zhang et al., 2024). Finally; RNA-sequencing has revealed a sex-dependent dysregulation of ECM organization in ASD; with collagen genes consistently upregulated in males; potentially contributing to the higher incidence of the disorder in this population (Sobhani and InanlooRahatloo, 2024).

Ultimately, the transition from animal models to human clinical applications remains a significant challenge, necessitating a more nuanced understanding of region-specific ECM dynamics across the lifespan. While animal models provide invaluable mechanistic insights—such as the divergent roles of MMP-9 across different ASD genotypes—their translation is limited by species differences in ECM composition. Future research must evaluate ECM-targeted therapies as a means to restore neural plasticity, moving beyond broad-spectrum modulation to target specific effectors like the cell-surface hyaluronidase TMEM2 or receptor-mediated endocytic clearance (Table 2).

ECM in Psychiatric Disorders.

ECM in pain

Pain is a complex sensory and emotional experience, with chronic pain—encompassing both neuropathic and inflammatory modalities—driven by sustained maladaptive plasticity and central sensitization (Woolf, 2011). While the precise spatiotemporal dynamics of ECM remodeling in pain remain relatively underexplored compared to neurodegenerative conditions; recent studies reveal that the matrisome acts as a critical; bidirectional rheostat along the nociceptive neuraxis. This is illustrated by the opposing functions of perineuronal nets (PNNs) at different anatomical levels. In the spinal dorsal horn; microglia-mediated degradation of PNNs surrounding projection neurons removes critical protective and inhibitory barriers; thereby exposing neurons to excitotoxicity; elevating glutamate signaling; and driving the initial sensitization of pain circuits (Tansley et al., 2022). Conversely; within higher-order processing centers such as the somatosensory cortex; the development of chronic pain is associated with a progressive build-up of PNNs. This increased matrix density reinforces and stabilizes excitatory synaptic transmission; effectively “hardwiring” the perception of chronic pain (Mascio et al., 2022). Together, these distinct mechanisms resolve a central paradox in the field: pathological pain is not simply a state of global matrix degradation, but rather a misaligned structural plasticity where spinal matrix loss initiates hypersensitivity, while cortical matrix accumulation locks it in.

Apart from primary sensory processing, ECM alterations also contribute to the affective and cognitive comorbidities frequently associated with chronic pain. In mouse models, the hippocampal ECM undergoes structural and biochemical degradation following injury, characterized by disrupted collagen and laminin microarchitecture and decreased tissue rigidity (Tajerian et al., 2018). This loss of structural integrity; which compromises cellular support; is driven by elevated levels of matrix metalloproteinase 8 (MMP8); targeted modulation of MMP8 can alleviate both pain hypersensitivity and its associated cognitive dysfunction (Tajerian et al., 2018). Similarly; specific matrisome components dynamically control behavioral outcomes in emotion-regulating hubs like the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC). For instance; the knockdown of laminin β1 (LAMB1) in the ACC exacerbates both neuropathic pain and anxiodepressive-like behaviors via disrupted integrin β1–mediated intracellular signaling (Li et al., 2021). These findings highlight a direct molecular overlap between chronic pain and psychiatric pathologies, establishing the ECM as a bridge between nociception and affective states.

Finally, converging evidence from human genetics and preclinical therapeutics underscores the systemic and central importance of the ECM in pain vulnerability. Genetic pathway analyses have linked specific single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in structural matrisome genes, such as COL1A1, to an increased risk of developing inflammatory and neuropathic pain, suggesting a fundamental genetic predisposition to ECM dysregulation (Parisien et al., 2019). Building on these findings; therapeutic strategies are moving toward targeted modulation of the ECM–neuroimmune interface. For example; targeted pharmacological inhibition has revealed distinct temporal windows for ECM proteolysis: early MMP-9 activity initiates pain by driving microglial activation via inflammatory cytokine cleavage; whereas late MMP-2 expression maintains neuropathic pain through astrocyte signaling (Kawasaki et al., 2008). These mechanisms position the neural and spinal ECM as dynamic regulators of nociceptive transmission and neuroinflammation, offering new therapeutic avenues that target the structural basis of chronic pain rather than its neurochemical symptoms alone (Table 3).

Integrative framework: three axes of ECM pathology

Drawing together the ECM mechanisms discussed across neurodegenerative (Section 2.1), psychiatric (Section 2.2), and pain (Section 2.3) pathologies, we propose three interdependent pathogenic axes: (1) the PNN–interneuron–plasticity axis, where alterations in perineuronal nets (PNNs) drive imbalances in excitation–inhibition dynamics; (2) the glial–ECM–inflammation axis, in which astrocyte and microglial remodeling of the ECM orchestrates chronic neuroimmune states; and (3) the ECM–vascular–BBB axis, where matrix remodeling disrupts endothelial signaling and blood-brain barrier integrity.

These axes interact synergistically, shaping the cellular microenvironment, modulating disease progression, and determining therapeutic responsiveness. Recognizing the ECM as a multi-dimensional disease modulator, rather than a passive background, suggests it may serve as a direct therapeutic target (Fig. 3).

Challenges in studying the ECM

The brain ECM presents significant challenges for research due to its molecular complexity and regional heterogeneity. Unlike the ECM in other tissues, the brain matrix is enriched with lecticans, tenascins (e.g., tenascin-R), and highly sulfated glycosaminoglycans such as chondroitin sulfate E (CS-E) (Long and Huttner, 2019; Deepa et al., 2006). These chemical features confer distinct charge densities and topographical properties that critically regulate ionic homeostasis and synaptic plasticity. However, this complexity severely limits the applicability of conventional proteomic and extraction approaches. Moreover, in vitro models employing purely purified ECM proteins often fail to recapitulate the molecular diversity of the native brain microenvironment. Recent studies using multi-electrode arrays demonstrate that utilizing tissue-specific, brain-derived ECM—rather than isolated proteins—is essential for accelerating and accurately modeling neuronal network formation (Lam et al., 2019). The intricate interactions between these complex matrices and neuronal receptors are crucial for synaptic stability but remain difficult to reproduce under artificial culture conditions (Fawcett et al., 2019).

Beyond compositional analysis, real-time visualization of the ECM in vivo remains technically challenging. Conventional fluorescence labeling requires fixation, which inherently disrupts dynamic ECM remodeling. While recent breakthroughs utilizing glycan-binding fluorophores have finally enabled live imaging of ECM dynamics (Fiore et al., 2025); deep-tissue visualization remains constrained by inherent light scattering and signal attenuation within the dense neuropil (Wang et al., 2018). Capturing the spatiotemporal evolution of the ECM is critical; as its molecular composition changes dynamically. For instance; during early postnatal maturation; the ECM transitions from a hyaluronan-rich provisional environment to a CSPG-dominant matrix; a shift necessary for closing critical periods and driving circuit refinement (Ruoslahti, 1996). At the mechanistic level; these accumulated CSPGs inhibit axon growth and restrict synaptic plasticity through a highly complex interactome; while classical pathways involve protein tyrosine phosphatases (PTPσ and LAR) (Shen et al., 2009); significant structural inhibition is also mediated by Nogo receptors; specifically NgR1 and NgR3 (Dickendesher et al., 2012).

Translating this structural and biochemical knowledge into therapeutic manipulation poses the major challenge. Matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) inhibitors, for instance, show theoretical promise in limiting pathological ECM degradation in neurodegenerative conditions; however, they carry significant off-target risks due to broad catalytic cross-reactivity, potentially paralyzing physiological ECM turnover (Vandenbroucke and Libert, 2014). Similarly; tissue-engineered scaffolds derived from decellularized ECM hold potential for reconstructing neural lesions; yet obstacles regarding vascularization; immunogenicity; and lineage-specific differentiation must be overcome (Somasekhar and Griffiths, 2023). To achieve precise molecular targeting; ECM-targeted nanodelivery systems offer a novel approach for localized modulation. Nevertheless; the dense brain ECM itself often acts as a physical barrier; limiting nanoparticle diffusion and cellular uptake in a tissue-specific manner; necessitating advanced surface modifications (e.g.; PEGylation) to optimize mobility (Cahn et al., 2024; Tomasetti et al., 2016). Addressing these challenges will require interdisciplinary approaches combining advanced optical microscopy, biomaterials engineering, and systems neuroscience.

Technological innovations in ECM research

In recent years, technological innovations have substantially advanced ECM research, overcoming longstanding barriers of matrix insolubility and deep-tissue visualization. Advanced imaging modalities, high-resolution mass spectrometry, and spatial omics not only reveal the precise molecular composition and dynamic architecture of the brain ECM but also provide new perspectives for disease modeling and regenerative medicine (Table 4).

Technological Innovations in ECM Research.

Advanced imaging

Advanced optical and magnetic resonance imaging techniques have greatly enhanced our ability to visualize the spatiotemporal dynamics of the ECM, enabling observation of ECM dynamics in real time. A major historical limitation has been the reliance on tissue fixation, which inherently disrupts matrix remodeling. However, recent breakthroughs have circumvented this; for instance, the development of Rhobo6—a glycan-binding fluorophore—now enables wash-free, live imaging of the highly glycosylated ECM. Applicable across diverse models from in vitro matrices to live tumor microenvironments, this technology captures dynamic ECM alterations during development and disease without the artifacts of traditional labeling (Fiore et al., 2025). At the macroscopic and systems level; dynamic contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (DCE-MRI) utilizing gadolinium agents is effective for tracking interstitial fluid (ISF) dynamics and quantifying ECM permeability changes; such as those seen in post-stroke vasogenic edema. When coupled with artificial intelligence algorithms for segmenting abnormal matrix regions; this approach improves diagnostic specificity (Talkington et al., 2025). At the microscopic structural level; combining two-photon microscopy with collagen hybrid peptide (CHP) probes—which specifically target the exposed sites of denatured collagen—enables highly sensitive in vivo visualization of cerebral vascular basement membrane damage (Bennink et al., 2018). Furthermore; nonlinear optical techniques; such as multiphoton microscopy and second harmonic generation (SHG); label-free imaging of the three-dimensional organization of fibrillar collagens (Aghigh et al., 2023; James and Campagnola, 2021). To map the matrix at the nanoscale, super-resolution methods like stimulated emission depletion (STED) and structured illumination microscopy (SIM) are increasingly deployed. These technologies can visualize the nanoscale spatial relationship between the ECM (such as perineuronal nets) and neural cells, revealing how matrix structures influence synaptic plasticity and tripartite neuroimmune interactions (Dzyubenko et al., 2023). Together with recent breakthroughs in prolonged time-lapse live imaging—which have recently revealed the dynamic; bidirectional remodeling of the perisynaptic ECM during brain development and critical period closure (Nakajo et al., 2025).

Mass spectrometry

If advanced imaging resolves the architecture of the ECM, mass spectrometry reveals its molecular composition in detail. Historically plagued by the low solubility and complex post-translational modifications of matrix proteins, ECM proteomics has advanced rapidly. By utilizing high-resolution MS combined with optimized sample preparation methods—such as chaotropic agent extraction and hydroxylamine digestion—researchers recently generated a comprehensive ECM protein atlas across 25 mouse organs. This study revealed that, despite a shared core matrisome, protein abundance varies up to 4000-fold between tissues, forming highly distinct, organ-specific matrix signatures (McCabe et al., 2023). Quantitative MS techniques; particularly data-independent acquisition (DIA) and isobaric labeling; have outperformed traditional data-dependent methods in dynamic range and reproducibility; allowing for the precise; multiplexed measurement of ECM proteins (Krasny et al., 2018). Recent analytical breakthroughs utilizing advanced DIA modalities; such as diaPASEF; have further improved the analysis of highly cross-linked and insoluble matrix compartments (Bayne et al., 2024). The integration of LC-DIA-MS/MS with on-slide tissue digestion has pushed proteomics into spatial dimensions. This approach has achieved detailed spatial depth in profiling both ECM proteins and glycosylation patterns—such as critical shifts in chondroitin sulfate sulfation—directly within human Alzheimer’s disease brain tissue (Nigro et al., 2026). Complementing this; mass spectrometry imaging (MSI) techniques like MALDI-IMS enable the direct topographical mapping of ECM proteins across tissue sections; revealing disease-specific spatial vulnerabilities (Angel et al., 2018). Beyond protein backbones; specialized MS approaches utilizing enzymatic sugar release have successfully sequenced the structural diversity of glycosaminoglycans (e.g.; chondroitin sulfate disaccharides); illuminating their precise roles in cell signaling (Alonge et al., 2019). Together, these matrisomic advances have substantially expanded the known catalog of ECM components and their post-translational modifications.

Multi-omics integration and systems biology

Moving beyond isolated molecular analyses, the advent of multi-omics has provided the systems-level perspective for ECM research, allowing for the integration of proteomics, glycomics, transcriptomics, and interactomics. At the foundational level, large-scale proteomics initiatives have established comprehensive ECM catalogs. Platforms such as MatrisomeDB 2.0 have mapped the matrix composition across diverse tissues and pathologies, providing a bioinformatic resource for identifying brain-specific matrisome signatures (Shao et al., 2023; Naba, 2023). Given that the brain ECM is exceptionally rich in complex sugars, targeted glycomics has become essential. Utilizing liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) combined with advanced bioinformatics, researchers can now quantitatively profile the highly specific sulfation codes of glycosaminoglycans—such as chondroitin sulfate disaccharides in human and rodent brain tissue (Alonge et al., 2019). This precise structural characterization is a critical prerequisite for decoding how these complex glycan networks dynamically regulate growth factor binding and neural cell adhesion. Simultaneously; transcriptomic profiling provides a real-time snapshot of matrix synthesis. In neurodegenerative contexts; such as amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS); transcriptomics has successfully identified specific ECM components as key pathogenic drivers (Rossi et al., 2025). While transcriptomics maps the synthetic capacity of cells, integrating these datasets with corresponding proteomic profiles is becoming increasingly essential to elucidate the complex post-transcriptional regulatory mechanisms that ultimately govern matrix remodeling.

To understand how the matrix physically communicates with cells, interactomics systematically maps the physical connections between ECM proteins and cell surface receptors. By defining multi-protein adhesion complexes (e.g., the consensus ‘adhesome’) and their associated intracellular signaling networks, researchers can decipher the coordinated role of the ECM in instructing cellular behavior (Horton et al., 2015). Finally; the emerging field of degradomics offers new mechanistic insights into dynamic ECM turnover by systematically characterizing protease substrates and their cleavage products (neoepitopes). While this powerful analytical approach has been predominantly applied to peripheral pathologies; such as mapping protease information flow in osteoarthritic cartilage (Bhutada et al., 2025), its application to brain ECM research could help identify biomarkers of neuroinflammation and synaptic matrix degradation.

These innovations have substantially advanced ECM analysis and our understanding of the neural ECM from qualitative observations to quantitative, multidimensional models. By integrating advanced imaging, spatial mass spectrometry, and multi-omics frameworks, the field can now begin to construct detailed molecular maps of the brain ECM, with implications for therapeutic development and regenerative medicine in the central nervous system.

Future directions

Future research on the brain extracellular matrix (ECM) will need to advance across multiple fronts—from molecular-level characterization of ECM heterogeneity to improved therapeutic targeting, to next-generation in vivo imaging and systems-level analysis—in order to fully leverage the ECM as a diagnostic and therapeutic platform in neurological and psychiatric disease.

A critical priority is to decipher the “glycan sulfation code” that underlies PNN functional diversity. Chondroitin sulfate (CS) disaccharides exist in multiple sulfation isoforms (CS-A, CS-C, CS-D, CS-E), and recent studies have demonstrated that CS sulfation patterns vary significantly across brain regions (Belliveau et al., 2024); influence the binding of signaling molecules such as semaphorin 3 A and Otx2 (Beurdeley et al., 2012; Dick et al., 2013), and can confound histochemical detection methods such as WFA labeling (Härtig et al., 2022). Understanding how disease states alter the sulfation landscape of PNNs—as has been suggested in Alzheimer’s disease (Logsdon et al., 2022) and schizophrenia (Pantazopoulos et al., 2015)—may reveal compositional changes that are currently being misinterpreted as simple gains or losses of PNNs (Scarlett et al., 2022). Developing region- and disease-specific sulfation profiles through glycosaminoglycan mass spectrometry and lectin-based multiplex labeling will be essential for accurate characterization of ECM changes across disorders.

A second key direction is the refinement of ECM-targeted therapeutic strategies. Chondroitinase ABC (ChABC), the most widely studied ECM-modifying enzyme, degrades CSPGs and can restore neuroplasticity in preclinical models of spinal cord injury (Bradbury et al., 2002); but remains limited by immunogenicity; blood-brain barrier (BBB) penetration; and its nonselective destruction of the matrix. MMP inhibitors have entered early clinical trials but are hampered by off-target effects stemming from broad-spectrum inhibition (Vandenbroucke and Libert, 2014); moreover; evidence that specific MMPs such as MMP9 are dynamically regulated in disease-specific contexts—for example; elevated MMP9 expression in the brains of depressed subjects and in response to SNRI treatment (Alaiyed et al., 2020)—underscores the need for temporally and molecularly precise monitoring of MMP activity rather than blanket inhibition. Future approaches should aim for greater specificity: sulfation-selective modulation of PNN components; engineered enzymes or gene therapy vectors with cell-type-specific promoters; and selective targeting of individual ECM molecules rather than wholesale matrix degradation. LOX modulators that address tissue stiffness and inflammation also warrant further investigation; particularly in fibrotic neuroinflammatory conditions such as multiple sclerosis (Mohan et al., 2010; Stephenson et al., 2024). Additionally, small molecules and antibodies designed to modulate specific PNN components offer a promising avenue for treating pain and cognitive impairment (Fawcett et al., 2019), where precise tuning of synaptic plasticity—rather than gross PNN removal—is the therapeutic goal.

Third, multi-omics integration and advanced spatial resolution techniques will be essential for mapping the “hardwired” versus dynamic regulatory mechanisms of the ECM. Integrating genomic, proteomic, glycomic, and metabolomic data can enable systematic characterization of the molecular networks and signaling interactions of the ECM (Naba, 2023). Spatial transcriptomics and imaging mass spectrometry have already been applied to construct three-dimensional molecular maps of the ECM in tumor and neural tissues (Angel et al., 2018). Performing similar analyses in healthy and diseased brains across species would enable the generation of comprehensive ECM atlases; comparable to existing single-cell and connectomic atlases; that could be correlated with functional magnetic resonance imaging connectivity readouts to identify mechanisms of brain plasticity and regional vulnerability to disease. Such atlases should aim to resolve not only PNNs but also other specialized ECM sub-structures—including perisynaptic matrices; perinodal ECM; and axonal coats—at the nanoscale; as these compartments likely exert distinct regulatory functions that remain poorly characterized. These efforts should be complemented by live imaging approaches—including glycan-binding fluorophores (Fiore et al., 2025); second harmonic generation microscopy (Aghigh et al., 2023), and advanced confocal methods—that can capture real-time ECM dynamics in vivo, a capability that remains technically challenging but is essential for understanding how the ECM is remodeled during disease onset and progression.

Fourth, ECM research will benefit from the development of improved preclinical model systems and biomarkers to bridge the translational gap. Patient-derived matrix organoids and organ-on-a-chip technologies can recreate pathological ECM environments in vitro, enabling the screening of ECM-modulating compounds in human-relevant contexts (Cho et al., 2021). Decellularized brain ECM scaffolds from different neuroanatomical regions have shown potential for neural repair applications; supporting region-specific neural differentiation and survival (Simsa et al., 2021). On the biomarker front; circulating ECM fragments (such as collagen IV/VI cleavage peptides); MMP activity profiles; and dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI indicators (Ktrans; Ve) have been explored to monitor ECM permeability and interstitial fluid dynamics. Expanding the biomarker toolkit to include CS sulfation signatures (Alonge et al., 2019); PNN-derived degradation products (Alonge et al., 2019), and circulating ECM proteomics will be important for disease stratification, treatment monitoring, and pharmacodynamic assessment.

Finally, computational and AI-driven approaches can serve as integrative tools to synthesize the increasingly complex datasets emerging from ECM research. Machine learning models can be applied to multi-omics and imaging data to predict ECM-related signaling patterns, identify imaging biomarkers, and aid in the design of biomimetic ECM materials and enzyme inhibitors (Lupori et al., 2023; Paveliev et al., 2024). However, these computational tools should be viewed as complementary rather than a replacement for—the fundamental biological and chemical investigations outlined above. The greatest advances will likely come from interdisciplinary efforts that combine glycobiology, advanced imaging, translational neuroscience, and computational modeling to achieve a truly systems-level understanding of how the brain ECM regulates neural function in health and disease.

Conclusions

The growing body of evidence reviewed here obliges us to fundamentally reconceptualize brain organization. The extracellular matrix is neither a passive structural scaffold nor a secondary modifier of cellular function; it is a central, organizing component of neural systems that dictates how cells behave, how circuits stabilize, and how experiences become biologically embedded.

Across neurological, psychiatric, and pain-related disorders, aberrant ECM remodeling emerges as a unifying pathological principle. Alterations in perineuronal nets, the interstitial matrix, and vascular basement membranes disrupt the excitation/inhibition balance, neuroimmune signaling, and blood–brain barrier integrity. Because these structural changes often persist long after the initiating insult has been resolved, they effectively lock neural circuits into maladaptive states. This persistence suggests that ECM remodeling acts not just as a downstream marker of disease, but also a primary stabilizer of pathological “attractor states”—a conceptual framework deeply rooted in computational models of psychiatric disorders (Loh et al., 2007)—that constrain clinical recovery.

As conceptualized in our integrative framework, the ECM exerts these disease-modifying effects through three interdependent axes: (1) the PNN–interneuron–plasticity axis, which governs critical period closure and circuit flexibility; (2) the glial–ECM–inflammation axis, through which astrocytes and microglia coordinate neuroimmune and structural responses; and (3) the ECM–vascular–BBB axis, which links matrix remodeling to cerebrovascular function and metabolic support. Together, these axes illustrate how dynamic ECM remodeling integrates cellular, circuit-level, and systemic insults into a coherent pathological continuum.

The brain ECM cannot be reduced to a mere byproduct of cellular or genetic activity. Unlike transient intracellular signaling or rapid synaptic firing, the matrix provides a persistent, physical memory of past neural events that bidirectionally constrains future circuit plasticity. In this capacity, it functions as an autonomous regulatory layer that contextualizes how genetic and cellular programs are ultimately expressed within the brain.

Ultimately, through the ECM biology, the historical divide between the reticular and neuron doctrines becomes less categorical. Neural integration arises not only from discrete synaptic signaling between individual cells but also through a continuous, highly instructive extracellular matrix that physically and biochemically coordinates activity across the entire central nervous system.
