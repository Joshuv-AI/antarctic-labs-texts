90% minimum effective concentration of ropivacaine for ultrasound-guided supra-inguinal fascia Iliaca compartment block for postoperative analgesia following total hip arthroplasty: a dose-finding study
Authors: Yanping Zhang, Qin Zhang, Bo Guo, Liang Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe ultrasound-guided supra-inguinal fascia iliaca compartment block (S-FICB) is a widely utilized regional anesthesia technique for postoperative pain management following total hip arthroplasty. Current evidence suggests that effective analgesia with S-FICB necessitates the administration of relatively high volumes of local anesthetic, which may consequently elevate the risk of local anesthetic toxicity and contribute to postoperative lower limb motor block.MethodsFifty-four patients with total hip arthroplasty were enrolled. Ultrasound-guided S-FICB was applied, the volume of the local anesthetic ropivacaine was set to 30 ml. Using the biased-coin design (BCD) sequential allocation method. The initial concentration of 30 ml ropivacaine was 0.25% and the interval concentration at 0.025%. Positive postoperative analgesia following S-FICB was defined by the simultaneous fulfillment of the following criteria: (1) The S-FICB block was successful. (2) The Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) scores maintained < 4 at 6, 12, 24 h postoperatively. (3) The effective compression times of Patient-Controlled Intravenous Analgesia (PCIA) < 5 within 24 h postoperatively. Failure to meet any one of these criteria was classified as negative postoperative analgesia. If the previous patient had a positive postoperative analgesia, the next patient was randomized to a lower concentration (defined as the previous concentration with a decrement of 0.025%), with an 11% probability (b = 0.11), or it would be administered the same concentration, with an 89% probability (1-b = 0.89). If a negative postoperative analgesia occurred, the next patient received a higher concentration (defined as the previous concentration with a increment of 0.025%). The study was terminated when 45 positive postoperative analgesia were achieved.ResultsForty-five patients (83%) were positive postoperative analgesia. The 90% minimum effective concentration (MEC90) of 30 ml ropivacaine for ultrasound guided S-FICB for postoperative analgesia following total hip arthroplasty was 0.286 (95% CI, 0.242 ~ 0.338) and the 95% minimum effective concentration (MEC95) was 0.314 (95% CI, 0.241 ~ 0.410).ConclusionThe MEC90 and MEC95 of 30 ml ropivacaine for ultrasound guided S-FICB for postoperative analgesia were 0.286% and 0.314%, respectively.Trial registrationThe trial was registered at the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (registration number: ChiCTR2400092413, registration date: 2024 November 15).Keywords: Supra-inguinal fascia iliaca compartment block, Total hip arthroplasty, Ropivacaine, The biased-coin design (BCD) sequential allocation method, Effective concentration

Introduction

Total hip arthroplasty (THA) is currently an effective treatment for avascular necrosis of the femoral head, femoral head fractures, osteoarthritis, and related pathologies [1].However, this procedure is highly traumatic and the postoperative pain is severe, with 15.9% of patients experiencing unacceptable pain [2]. The Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) protocol aims to reduce surgical stress responses and promote early recovery of patients through multidisciplinary intervention, and postoperative pain management being a critical component for its successful implementation. Multimodal analgesia (MMA) combines the application of pharmacological agents with different mechanisms or different analgesic techniques to minimize the side effects of various analgesics and achieve optimal pain control [3]. Within the MMA strategies, regional block techniques have emerged as an important component due to its demonstrated analgesic efficacy and fewer side effects [4].

Hebbard et al. [5] described ultrasound-guided S-FICB in 2011, which can not only achieve effective and balanced diffusion of local anesthetic (LA) in the fascia iliaca compartment (FIC), but also improve the success of femoral nerve (FN), lateral femoral cutaneous nerve (LFCN) and obturator nerve (ON) block. S-FICB effectively alleviates the pain of patients with hip fractures and hip arthroplasty, which is widely used in clinical practice and has become important part of MMA after THA [6, 7].

To optimize adequate diffusion of LA in the FIC, ultrasound-guided S-FICB conventionally utilizes a higher injectate volume [8, 9]. Koc et al. [10] demonstrated that administering 50 mL of bupivacaine for S-FICB achieved comparable postoperative analgesic efficacy between lower and higher concentrations. This result holds significant clinical implications, particularly for THA patients, who are predominantly elderly. The administration of low-concentration LA not only provides effective analgesia but also reduces the risk of systemic LA toxicity and related complications [11]. Determining the concentration-effect relationship is critically important to identify the minimum effective concentration (MEC) of LA that achieves satisfactory nerve block analgesia while maintaining an acceptable safety profile.

Therefore, in this study, the MEC90 of 30 ml ropivacaine for ultrasound-guided S-FICB for postoperative analgesia following THA was measured by the BCD sequential allocation method, which provides a reference for clinical application of LA dosage.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This prospective study was conducted at Chongqing Traditional Chinese Medicine Hospital from November 2024 to January 2025 with approved by the Institutional Review Board of Chongqing Traditional Chinese Medicine Hospital, Chongqing, China (approval number: ky-66, approval date:16/11/2023). Letters of informed consent were obtained from all patients. This study was conducted in full compliance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Trial registration was performed via the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (registration number: ChiCTR2400092413, registration date: 15/11/2024).

The study examined patients aged 18 to 80, categorized as physical status I–III according to the American Society of Anesthesiologist (ASA) and undergoing THA for the first time. Of these patients, we excluded those with (1) Body mass index (BMI) < 18 or BMI > 35, (2) Allergic to related drugs, (3) Severe heart, lung, liver and kidney disease, (4) Peripheral neuropathy, (5) Patients with chronic pain treatment, (6) Alcohol or drug addicts, (7) Combined with psychiatric diseases or dementia who unable to cooperate, (8) Pregnant women, (9) Patients with contraindications to nerve block puncture. In addition, patients with serious complications, the surgical duration > 2 h, and the request of patients to terminate the trial were excluded from the analysis.

Study protocol

Upon arrival in the operating room, electrocardiography, pulse oximetry and invasive arterial blood pressure monitoring were routinely performed. All blocks were performed by an experienced anesthesiologist with ultrasound-guided S-FICB. The S-FICB was performed using a sterile technique with the patient in a supine position. After skin disinfection and draping, the high-frequency (12–3 MHz) linear ultrasound transducer (Mindray TE9S; Shenzhen, China) was placed parallel to the inguinal ligament along the inguinal crease to identify the femoral artery. The transducer was then rotated to align with the vertebral axis and subsequently moved laterally until the anterior superior iliac spine (ASIS) was visualized. With the caudal end of the transducer positioned over the ASIS and the cranial end directed toward the umbilicus. The iliac muscle (IM) and fascia iliaca (FI) were identified. On this view, the deep circumflex iliac artery (DCIA) can be visualized, which is a significant anatomical landmark. The caudal end of the transducer was slightly rotated toward the foot side to avoid the ASIS, thereby facilitating needle insertion. Under the guidance of ultrasound, a 22G nerve block needle was introduced at the transducer caudal edge. In-plane approach technique was used, the FI was penetrated and hydro dissected, separating the FI from the IM. The predetermined concentration of 30 ml ropivacaine was injected (Fig. 1). The block effect was evaluated 30 min after the completion of S-FICB, the FN sensory block was evaluated in the anterior part of the distal thigh and the LFCN was evaluated in the lateral thigh.

After the evaluation of the blocking effect was completed, general anesthesia was induced by midazolam (0.05 mg/kg), propofol (1 ~ 2 mg/kg), and sufentanil (0.3-0.5ug/kg), and laryngeal mask airway (LMA) was inserted. Dexamethasone 8 mg antiemetics was given immediately after induction. Anesthesia was maintained by inhalation of sevoflurane 0.5 ~ 0.8 minimal alveolar concentration (MAC), continuous infusion of 2 ~ 8 mg/kg/h propofol and 6 ~ 10 ug/kg/h remifentanil maintain Bispectral index (BIS) at 40 ~ 60. 30 min before the end of surgery, 40 mg of parecoxib was administered intravenously.

All surgical procedures were performed by a consistent team of experienced orthopedic surgeons. Postoperatively, patients were transferred to the post-anesthesia care unit (PACU) for recovery. The LMA was removed upon meeting standardized extubation criteria, including return of protective airway reflexes, adequate spontaneous ventilation (respiratory rate > 10 breaths/min, SpO₂ >92% on room air), and hemodynamic stability. Immediately following LMA removal, PCIA was initiated and maintained for 48 h postoperatively. The analgesic drugs were Sufentanil 1.5ug/kg, butorphanol 0.04 mg/kg and diluted to 100 ml with normal saline at a load dose of 2 m1. The background dose was 2 ml/h, the single compression dose was 0.5 ml, and the locking time was 15 min. If the VAS score was ≧ 5, intravenous tramadol 50 mg should be administered as rescue analgesia.

During the follow-up period, only the researchers responsible for randomization and drug preparation achieved the positive or negative postoperative analgesia status of the patients. Positive postoperative analgesia following S-FICB was defined by the simultaneous fulfillment of the following criteria: (1) The S-FICB block was successful. (2) The VAS scores maintained < 4 at 6, 12, 24 h postoperatively. (3) The effective compression times of PCIA < 5 within 24 h postoperatively. Failure to meet any one of these criteria was classified as negative postoperative analgesia. The concentration of ropivacaine in all patients was determined according to the BCD sequential allocation method. According to the previous study and pre-experiments [12–14], the concentration of ropivacaine was set to be 0.25% when the first patient in this study received S-FICB, and the interval concentration at 0.025%. If the previous patient had a positive postoperative analgesia, the concentration of ropivacaine for the next patient will be randomly assigned by BCD (Randomization Rule for BCD: After tossing a coin, the probability of head is given by b= (100 - Γ)/Γ, and the setting of the Γ is according to the desired effective percentage. For example, to target MEC90 (minimum effective concentration at the 90th percentile), Γ = 90, then the next patient has a probability of b ≈ 11% to decrease by 0.025% and a probability of (1 - b) ≈ 89% to remain unchanged) [15]. If a negative reaction occurred, the next patient received a higher concentration (defined as the previous concentration with a increment of 0.025%). The trial was terminated upon attainment of 45 positive postoperative analgesia.

Outcome measurements

The primary outcome of this study was positive or negative postoperative analgesia with S-FICB, as this was used to determine the ropivacaine concentration in subsequent patient. The pinprick grade in the FN and LFCN innervation area was evaluated to determine whether S-FICB was successful. No change in pain was grade 0, pain reduction was grade 1, and pain complete disappearance was grade 2. 30 min after S-FICB, if the sensory blockade in the anterior part of the distal thigh and lateral thigh both reached grade 2, the block was considered successful; otherwise, it was considered failed. The VAS scores (0 for painless and 10 for severe pain) at rest and activity (passive hip flexion) at 6, 12, and 24 h postoperatively was recorded, and the effective compression times of PCIA were also recorded within 24 h postoperatively.

Secondary outcome included lower limb motor block, the number of required rescue analgesia and block-related complications (hematoma, infection, vascular puncture, nerve injury, local anesthetic toxicity, etc.). The Bromage score was used to assess lower limb motor block: 0 (full foot and knee bend); 1 (only knee move); 2 (only foot move); 3 (no foot or knee move). Bromage score 2–3 was divided into “lower limb motor block”.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 27.0 software package and GraphPad Prism 10.1.2 were used for statistical analysis. The measurement data were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (x ± SD) or median (interquartile range). The count data were expressed as the number of cases or a percentage. Probit regression analysis was used to calculate the MEC90 and MEC95 and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) of 30 ml ropivacaine for postoperative analgesia by S-FICB.

Discussion

In this study, the MEC90 and MEC95 of 30 ml ropivacaine for ultrasound guided S-FICB for postoperative analgesia were 0.286% and 0.314%, respectively. Our study indicated that higher concentrations of ropivacaine in S-FICB associated with an increased incidence of lower limb motor block. To our knowledge, this is the first MCE study of ropivacaine for S-FICB postoperative analgesia.

Postoperative pain management is a critical component of Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) protocols for total hip arthroplasty (THA). Effective analgesia not only significantly alleviates pain and facilitates early ambulation but also reduces postoperative complications and improves patient satisfaction [16]. The hip receives its sensory innervation primarily from the anterior lumbar plexus (FN, ON) and the posterior sacral plexus (including the superior gluteal nerve and sciatic nerve), with a predominant concentration of nociceptors localized in the anterior articular region [17]. A single injection of S-FICB effectively blocks the FN, LFCN, and OA, providing comprehensive anterior hip analgesia. Compared to conventional lumbar plexus blocks (LPB), S-FICB presents several clinically relevant advantages, including simplified supine positioning, enhanced procedural feasibility, and a potentially improved safety profile, thereby establishing it as a viable alternative to the anterior LPB approach [5, 18]. A prospective, randomized, double-blind clinical study compared LPB and S-FICB in postoperative analgesia after hip and femur surgery in children, and concluded that the analgesic efficacy of the two block methods was consistent, and the operation time of S-FICB was shorter due to its supine positioning and limited needle trajectory [19]. Similarly, Bravo et al. [20] reported that no significant differences were found in morphine requirement and pain control between LPB and S-FICB for THA. Currently, ultrasound-guided S-FICB is widely used for postoperative analgesia after hip arthroplasty.

Chen et al. [13] reported that S-FICB provides superior acute pain relief compared to conventional FICB in elderly patients with hip fractures. Therefore, the study investigated the analgesic efficacy of different ropivacaine concentrations for S-FICB during the first 24 h following THA. To comprehensively evaluate postoperative analgesia, we employed two objective measures: (1) VAS scores, which directly quantify pain intensity. (2) the effective compression times of PCIA, which reflect analgesic requirements. These complementary metrics collectively provide a robust assessment of S-FICB effectiveness. The study established the following criteria for positive S-FICB analgesia: (1) The S-FICB block was successful. (2) The VAS scores maintained < 4 at 6, 12, 24 h postoperatively. (3) The effective compression times of PCIA < 5 within 24 h postoperatively. Patients meeting all three criteria were classified as S-FICB positive postoperative analgesia, while failure to meet any single criterion resulted in classification as S-FICB negative postoperative analgesia. The conditions for determining the analgesic effect of S-FICB in the study thus emerged and formed the basis for our primary outcome analysis.

Applying the same ultrasound approach, the successful blockade of S-FICB was defined as the presence of sensory blockade in both the FN and the LFCN territory, previous studies have reported different effective volumes. Using Dixon’s up-and-down method, Yamada et al. [12] determined that the 95% effective volume (EV95) of 0.25% ropivacaine for ultrasound-guided S-FICB was 26.99 mL, while Zhang et al. [21] utilized the BCD sequential method and established the EV95 of 0.33% ropivacaine to be 34.06 ml. These studies primarily focused on determining the effective dose required for successful S-FICB within 30 min. In contrast, this study was conducted under the MMA protocol and specifically investigated the MEC90 and MEC95 of ropivacaine for 24 h postoperative analgesia following S-FICB, which were 0.286% and 0.314%, respectively, thereby providing clinically significant and practical value.

The S-FICB is a type of fascial plane block that employs a high-volume LA injection to achieve simultaneous blockade of the FN, LFCN, and ON. A cadaveric dose-finding study determined that for ultrasound-guided S-FICB, the 90% Minimum effective volume (MEV90) required for simultaneous staining of the FN, LFCN, and ON was 62.5ml [22]. In a human cadaveric pilot study [23], a computed tomography (CT scan) -guided pressor/depressor sequence was found 40 mL might be sufficient for three-nerve blockade. Koc et al. [10] first demonstrated that when performing S-FICB with 50 mL bupivacaine, a lower concentration of 0.2% provided equivalent analgesic efficacy compared to 0.25%. It can be logically hypothesized that reducing LA concentration might decrease the incidence of motor blockade in fascial plane block. Genc et al. [24] confirmed this hypothesis, however, when using 40 mL of 0.2% bupivacaine for S-FICB, a high incidence of lower limb motor blockade was observed at both 6 and 24 h postoperatively. The investigators attributed this finding to the relatively large injectate volume (40 mL) used in their protocol. Patients undergoing THA are predominantly elderly and frequently exhibit reduced tolerance to LA. Higher LA volumes may increase the risk of lower limb motor blockade, which can impede early postoperative rehabilitation. Multiple clinical studies have demonstrated that 30 mL of LA for S-FICB provides effective postoperative analgesia in hip surgery [25–27]. Therefore, this study standardized the ropivacaine volume at 30 ml.

However, the ON blockade efficacy of S-FICB and its assessment remain controversial. A radiological cadaver investigation showed that the ON could not be effectively blocked neither by supra-inguinal needle insertion with five centimeters of hydrodissection-mediated needle advancement (S-FICB-H) nor any other technique targeting the FIC with 40 ml injectate, leading the authors to conclude that ON blockade may require separate targeting rather than relying on fascial plane blocks [28]. Lee et al. [29] also demonstrated that 40 ml of 0.25% ropivacaine for S-FICB cannot achieve effective blockade of the ON. Clinical studies evaluating ON blockade during S-FICB have traditionally relied on assessing the change of skin sensation in the inner thigh [18, 30]. However, previous study found that only 20% of patients had a small part of skin sensation block in the inner thigh after ON block, and even 57% of patients had no distribution of the ON, it also recommended that the strength of thigh adductor muscle be used to evaluate the blocking effect of ON [31]. FN block during S-FICB also reduces the strength of thigh adductor muscle, which may interfere with the evaluation of ON block effect. Besides, previous scholars proposed that an ON block cannot relieve postoperative pain after THA [32].

In view of the above, the successful blockade of S-FICB in this study was determined based on the disappearance of pinprick pain in the FN and the LFCN innervation region, rather than relying on ON blockade, similar to the approach adopted by Genc et al. [24]. In their study, the success rate of S-FICB was 87% with 40 mL of 0.225% bupivacaine and 100% with 0.25% bupivacaine. In comparison, this study showed success rates of 75% with 30 mL of 0.225% ropivacaine and 94% with 0.25% ropivacaine. The observed differences may be attributed not only to the type and volume of LA used, but also to differences in anesthetic protocol and the assessment time point. Specifically, the evaluation of S-FICB efficacy in the former study was performed at 6 h postoperatively, when residual effects of spinal anesthesia might still persist, potentially confounding the assessment of blockade efficacy.

Motor blockade remains one of the principal limitations of lower extremity nerve blocks. Clinical evidence indicates that even following single-shot adductor canal blocks, increasing LA volumes may lead to quadriceps muscle weakness [33]. The mechanism of lower limb motor blockade during S-FICB involves the spread of LA within the fascia iliaca compartment, leading to FN blockade. In posterolateral-approached THA, Carella et al. [34] demonstrated that S-FICB offers positive results, including orthostatic intolerance symptoms and functional walking performance at day 1 and day 2. Furthermore, their study reported significant reductions in both 48 h morphine consumption and opioid-related adverse effects in the S-FICB group.

As a medium and long-acting local anesthetic widely used in peripheral nerve blocks, ropivacaine exhibits concentration-dependent sensory-motor dissociation [35], with lower concentrations producing less pronounced motor blockade. Previous research demonstrated that reducing ropivacaine concentration during FN blockade decreases the incidence of quadriceps weakness [36]. This study confirmed the observation, showing an overall 27% incidence of lower limb motor blockade at 6 h postoperatively, with decreasing frequency at lower concentrations. Notably, among the 15 patients exhibiting motor blockage at 6 h, two maintained persistent blockade at 24 h follow-up, both receiving higher concentrations (0.3% and 0.325%) exceeding the MEC90 (0.286%) threshold. These results suggested that 30 ml of MEC90 ropivacaine used for postoperative analgesia after S-FICB has a relatively minimal effect on lower limb motor block in patients undergoing THA. This favorable profile supports key ERAS objectives, including early ambulation, functional recovery, prevention of thromboembolic complications.

There are also some limitations in this study. First, some previous studies have recommended that single-shot S-FICB should utilize a LA volume of 40 ml or more [8, 9], this study employed a fixed dose of 30 ml of ropivacaine to optimize the balance between analgesic efficacy and patient safety. Consequently, the MEC for larger ropivacaine volumes was not investigated. Second, motor block assessment was performed using the Bromage score, however, more objective and unbiased data technology such as dynamometry can be used to measure the strength of the quadriceps muscle. Third, the study enrolled patients aged 18 ~ 80 years, and did not specifically examine the potential influence of age on the MEC90 of ropivacaine for S-FICB.

Conclusion

The MEC90 and MEC95 of 30 ml ropivacaine for ultrasound guided S-FICB for postoperative analgesia following total hip arthroplasty were 0.286% and 0.314%, respectively. The MEC90 offers effective analgesia while minimizing lower limb motor blockade, thereby facilitating ERAS protocols. These findings provides crucial references for precision anesthesia practice and hold significant clinical implications. While this study confirms the efficacy of 30 mL ropivacaine for S-FICB, further RCT studies are also needed to verify the efficacy and safety of 0.286% ropivacaine for postoperative analgesia after THA.
