Edentulism and incident hip fracture among middle-aged and older adults in China: evidence from the CHARLS
Authors: Xin Chen, Qianyi Wang, Zhibai Zhao, Zheng Cheng, Qianglin Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHip fractures are a major cause of disability and mortality in aging populations, yet risk prediction based solely on traditional clinical factors remains limited. Edentulism is prevalent in later life and may reflect broader systemic vulnerability through pathways involving impaired nutrition, functional decline, and frailty. However, longitudinal evidence linking edentulism to hip fracture risk remains limited. This study examined the association between edentulism and incident hip fracture and explored whether frailty might represent a potential pathway.MethodsData were obtained from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study, a nationally representative cohort of Chinese adults. A total of 7,385 community-dwelling participants aged ≥ 45 years without prior hip fracture at baseline (2011) were followed through 2020. Baseline edentulism was self-reported, and incident hip fracture was defined as the first report during follow-up. Associations were estimated using multivariable Cox proportional hazards models. Frailty, assessed using a frailty index, was evaluated in exploratory mediation analyses as a potential pathway. Subgroup and sensitivity analyses, including temporally ordered analyses of frailty, were conducted to assess robustness.ResultsOver up to 9 years of follow-up, 313 participants (4.24%) experienced a first hip fracture. In fully adjusted models, edentulism was associated with a higher risk of incident hip fracture (hazard ratio 1.55, 95% CI: 1.08–2.20, p = 0.016). Kaplan-Meier analyses showed a significantly higher cumulative incidence of hip fracture among edentulous individuals (log-rank p < 0.001). Exploratory mediation analyses were consistent with frailty as a potential pathway underlying the observed association, with an estimated indirect effect corresponding to 9.2% of the total association. The findings were robust across subgroup and sensitivity analyses.ConclusionsEdentulism was associated with an elevated risk of hip fracture among middle-aged and older adults in China. Exploratory analyses were consistent with frailty as a potential pathway underlying this association, although causality cannot be established. Dentition status may inform targeted strategies for identifying individuals at heightened hip fracture risk, guiding interventions such as frailty assessment, nutritional support, fall prevention, and integrated oral health management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27856-6.Keywords: Edentulism, Hip fracture, Frailty, Mediation analysis, CHARLS

Introduction

Hip fractures represent a major and growing public health challenge worldwide, contributing substantially to disability, loss of independence, and mortality in older adults [1]. In 2019, an estimated 14.2 million hip fractures occurred globally, including nearly 2.0 million cases in China [2]. The burden of hip fracture is expected to continue rising, largely driven by population aging and the increasing prevalence of osteoporosis. From the age of 50 years, the lifetime risk of hip fracture has been estimated at approximately 17% in women and 6% in men [3]. Beyond their profound clinical consequences, hip fractures also impose substantial economic costs related to hospitalization, rehabilitation, and long-term care [4]. Although several established risk factors, such as advanced age, low bone mineral density, and falls, have been well documented, these factors explain only part of the variability in fracture risk [5], highlighting the need to identify additional markers of fracture vulnerability.

Oral health, particularly tooth loss and edentulism, has increasingly been recognized as an important component of overall health and a potential indicator of systemic vulnerability. Edentulism is common in later life, with an estimated prevalence of 16.4% among Chinese adults aged ≥ 45 years in 2018 [6]. A growing body of epidemiological evidence suggests that tooth loss is associated with a range of adverse conditions, including cognitive decline, depressive symptoms, and increased mortality [7, 8]. Emerging studies have also linked poor oral status to skeletal outcomes, showing that fewer natural teeth and impaired occlusal support are associated with lower bone mineral density, falls, and fracture risk [9–12]. Several mechanisms may underlie these associations. Tooth loss can reduce masticatory efficiency and alter food choice, thereby contributing to insufficient intake of protein, calcium, and vitamin D, which in turn may accelerate muscle weakness, physical decline, and skeletal fragility [13]. In addition, loss of occlusal support may impair proprioceptive input, postural stability, and balance control, thereby increasing susceptibility to falls and fractures [14, 15]. Together, these nutritional and functional consequences suggest that edentulism may increase vulnerability to hip fracture through both direct effects on falls and broader deterioration in physical resilience.

Frailty is a multidimensional geriatric syndrome characterized by reduced physiological reserve and increased vulnerability to stressors [16]. It is commonly quantified using the frailty index (FI), which reflects the accumulation of age-related health deficits. Importantly, many consequences of poor oral health, including malnutrition, sarcopenia, reduced muscle strength, impaired balance, functional decline, and the broader burden of comorbidity and psychological distress, overlap closely with core components of frailty. Under this framework, edentulism may contribute to the accumulation of health deficits and accelerate the development of frailty, which in turn is a well-established predictor of falls, fragility fractures, disability, and mortality [16, 17]. Accordingly, frailty may represent a biologically plausible intermediate pathway through which edentulism increases vulnerability to hip fracture.

However, important knowledge gaps remain. Existing studies have largely focused on cross-sectional associations between tooth loss and bone mineral density, falls, or fracture-related outcomes, rather than prospectively ascertained incident hip fracture [9–11]. Moreover, although poor oral health has been hypothesized to influence fracture risk through nutritional compromise, impaired balance, and physical decline, these pathways have not been adequately examined in longitudinal studies [12]. In particular, the mediating factors in the association between edentulism and hip fracture has rarely been evaluated. Evidence from nationally representative cohorts of middle-aged and older Chinese adults also remains limited. These gaps are especially relevant in China, where rapid population aging, together with the high and increasing prevalence of diabetes and periodontal disease, may contribute to a growing burden of both tooth loss and osteoporotic fractures [18]. Because edentulism is largely irreversible once established, identifying potentially modifiable intermediate factors, such as frailty, is particularly important for hip fracture risk stratification and prevention.

The China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) is an ongoing nationally representative cohort of middle-aged and older Chinese adults (aged ≥ 45 years) with repeated assessments of health status, functional capacity, and health outcomes [19]. Leveraging its longitudinal design, the present study aimed to evaluate the longitudinal association between edentulism and incident hip fracture and to explore the potential mediating role of frailty.

Methods

Study design and population

This study used data from the CHARLS, with the baseline survey conducted in 2011 and follow-up waves in 2013, 2015, 2018, and 2020. CHARLS employs a multistage, stratified probability sampling design covering 150 counties and 450 communities/villages across 28 provinces [19]. Data are collected through standardized household interviews, physical measurements, and biomarker assessments in subsets of participants.

Participants who completed the baseline interview in 2011 were considered for inclusion. We excluded individuals who (1) were younger than 45 years; (2) reported a history of hip fracture at baseline; (3) had missing information on dentition status; or (4) lacked follow-up information on hip fracture ascertainment; (5) had implausible values (e.g., body mass index [BMI] < 10 or > 60 kg/m²); or (6) had missing data on covariates (e.g., heart disease, stroke, hypertension, diabetes/high blood glucose, arthritis, fall history, memory-related disease). The final analytic sample selection is shown in Fig. 1.

Flowchart of participant selection

CHARLS was approved by the Biomedical Ethics Committee of Peking University (IRB00001052-11015), and all participants provided written informed consent (https://charls.pku.edu.cn/).

Assessment of dentition status

Edentulism was determined by the self-reported question: “Have you lost all your teeth?” Participants responding “yes” were classified as edentulous, and those responding “no” as non-edentulous.

Assessment of incident hip fracture

At each follow-up wave, participants were asked: “Have you fractured your hip since the last interview?” According to the survey instructions, interviewers were trained to provide respondents with clarification regarding the meaning and anatomical location of the “hip bone” when asking the question. Incident hip fracture was defined as the newly reported hip fracture during follow-up. Participants were followed from baseline until the first occurrence of a hip fracture, loss to follow-up, death, or the end of follow-up (2020), whichever occurred first. Because hip fracture was ascertained at discrete follow-up waves, event time was assigned to the interview year of the wave in which hip fracture was first reported, with follow-up time calculated from baseline.

Covariates

Baseline covariates were prespecified based on prior literature, biological plausibility, and availability in CHARLS [12, 20–22]. Sociodemographic variables included age (years), sex (male/female), educational attainment (middle school or below, high school/vocational school, college or above), residence (urban/rural), health insurance coverage (yes/no, based on any reported public or private medical insurance). Lifestyle factors comprised smoking status (never/former/current; ever smoking defined as ≥ 100 cigarettes in the lifetime), alcohol consumption (never/rarely, < once per month, ≥once per month), and sleep duration (hours/day, self-reported total of nighttime sleep and daytime naps). Clinical factors included BMI (kg/m²), fall history (yes/no, assessed by “Have you fallen down in the last two years?”), and self-reported physician diagnoses (each yes/no) of heart disease, stroke, hypertension, diabetes/high blood glucose, arthritis, and memory-related disease (e.g., Alzheimer’s disease, cerebral atrophy, and Parkinson’s disease). Medication burden was assessed as the number of medication categories used for chronic conditions during the past two years. This count was calculated by summing self-reported medication use for major chronic conditions, including hypertension, diabetes, heart disease, stroke, arthritis/rheumatism, chronic lung disease, liver disease, kidney disease, digestive disease, dyslipidemia, cancer, memory-related disease, and emotional, nervous, or psychiatric problems. The resulting variable was treated as a continuous covariate.

Cognitive function was assessed using a composite score ranging from 0 to 21, with higher scores indicating better cognitive performance [23]. Consistent with prior CHARLS studies [23, 24], the score was derived from multiple domains, including orientation and attention assessed using selected items from Telephone Interview of Cognitive Status (0–5 points), episodic memory assessed by immediate and delayed word recall (0–10 points), executive function assessed by serial subtraction (0–5 points), and visuospatial ability assessed by figure drawing (0–1 point).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are summarized as medians (interquartile ranges, IQR) and categorical variables as counts (percentages). Baseline characteristics were compared between participants with and without incident hip fracture using the Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables and the χ² test for categorical variables.

Kaplan-Meier curves were used to estimate cumulative incidence of hip fracture by baseline edentulism status, with differences assessed using the log-rank test. Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) for the association between edentulism and incident hip fracture. The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using Schoenfeld residuals (global test p = 0.120, Supplementary Fig. 1). Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors, with all values < 2. We fitted sequential models with increasing covariate adjustment: Model 1: unadjusted; Model 2: adjusted for age, sex, education, residence, and health insurance; Model 3: Model 2 plus smoking, alcohol consumption, and sleep duration; Model 4: Model 3 plus BMI, fall history, cognitive function, and medication burden; Model 5: Model 4 plus heart disease, stroke, hypertension, diabetes/high blood glucose, arthritis, and memory-related disease.

Subgroup analysis

Prespecified subgroup analyses were conducted by age (< 65/≥65 years), sex, BMI (< 24, 24-27.9, ≥ 28 kg/m2), smoking status, alcohol consumption, hypertension, heart disease, diabetes, arthritis, stroke, and cognition function. Cognitive impairment was defined as a cognitive score one standard deviation below the mean of the study population [24]. Effect modification was assessed by including multiplicative interaction terms between edentulism and each subgroup variable. Subgroup models were adjusted for covariates in Model 5, excluding the stratification variable.

Exploratory mediation analysis

To explore whether frailty may partially mediate the association between edentulism and incident hip fracture, we performed a mediation analysis using the FI as the mediator, with covariate adjustment consistent with Model 5. Mediation effects were estimated using a simulation-based approach with 1,000 bootstrap resamples to obtain 95% CI [25]. The proportion mediated was calculated as the estimated indirect effect divided by the total effect.

Frailty was operationalized using a 32-item FI at baseline based on the deficit accumulation approach, incorporating deficits across activities of daily living and instrumental activities of daily living, physical function limitations, chronic conditions, psychological health indicators, and self-rated health (Supplementary Table 1) [26]. Each deficit was coded as 0 or 1, and the FI was calculated as the proportion of deficits present, with higher values indicating greater frailty. Because edentulism and FI were measured at the same baseline wave, mediation estimates from the primary analysis were interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than causal. Temporally ordered mediation was further evaluated in sensitivity analyses.

In addition to the primary mediation analysis using the FI, we conducted exploratory mediation analyses using indicators of phenotypic frailty and physical function, including BMI, handgrip strength, gait speed, and five-time chair-stand test (CS-5). Gait speed was assessed over a 2.5-meter walking course at usual pace. The CS-5 measured the time required to complete five repeated sit-to-stand movements without using the arms, with longer duration indicating poorer lower limb function [27]. Handgrip strength was measured using a dynamometer, with the average of the maximum values from both hands used for analysis [27].

Sensitivity analyses

We conducted several sensitivity analyses to assess robustness: (1) logistic regression as an alternative modeling approach; (2) calculation of E-values to evaluate the potential impact of unmeasured confounding; (3) excluding participants whose dentition status changed during follow-up. Because dentition information was unavailable in 2020, we restricted to individuals with consistent dentition status in 2011 and 2018 and examined incident hip fractures during 2013–2018; and (4) to strengthen temporal ordering for the exploratory mediation analyses, we recalculated FI and CS-5 using 2013 data and re-evaluated indirect effects for hip fractures occurring during 2015–2018 within the restricted cohort. These analyses were intended to provide supportive evidence regarding potential pathways, rather than to establish causality.

All analyses were performed using R (version 4.4.2). Statistical tests were two-sided, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Among 17,708 participants interviewed at baseline, 7,385 were included in the analytic cohort after applying prespecified exclusion criteria (Fig. 1). Over up to 9 years of follow-up (2011–2020), 313 participants (4.24%) experienced an incident hip fracture, with a median time to fracture of 4 years (Table 1). Compared with participants without hip fracture, those who developed hip fracture were older, had lower BMI, and were more likely to have lower educational attainment, rural residence, and lower cognitive function (all p ≤ 0.01). They were also more likely to have edentulism, heart disease, stroke, arthritis, memory-related disease, medication burden, and a history of falls (all p < 0.05).

Edentulism and incident hip fracture

Kaplan-Meier curves indicated a higher cumulative incidence of hip fracture among participants with baseline edentulism than among those without (log-rank p < 0.001), with increasing separation over time (Fig. 2). Over up to 9 years of follow-up, the cumulative incidence was 8.84% (95% CI: 6.22%-11.38%) in the edentulous group and 3.93% (95% CI: 3.47%-4.39%) in the non-edentulous group (absolute difference 4.91%), corresponding to approximately one additional hip fracture per 20 edentulous individuals over 9 years of follow-up.

Kaplan-Meier curve of incident hip fracture by edentulism status

In Cox models, baseline edentulism was associated with a higher hazard of incident hip fracture (crude HR 2.30, 95% CI: 1.66–3.20, p < 0.001) (Table 2). The association persisted after full adjustment for sociodemographic factors, lifestyle behaviors, fall history, BMI, cognitive function, medication burden, and comorbidities (Model 5: HR 1.55, 95% CI: 1.08–2.20, p = 0.016). Effect estimates attenuated with covariate adjustment, suggesting partial confounding by sociodemographic and health-related factors. However, even with additional adjustment for baseline FI, the association between edentulism and incident hip fracture remained materially unchanged (HR: 1.51, 95% CI: 1.06–2.15, p = 0.023).

Exploratory mediation analyses

In exploratory mediation analyses, the findings were suggestive of partial mediation by FI, with an estimated indirect effect corresponding to 9.17% (95% CI: 2.71%-47.39%, p = 0.022) of the observed association (Fig. 3). Additional exploratory mediation analyses using indicators related to phenotypic frailty and physical function, including handgrip strength, CS-5, gait speed, and BMI, were performed to explore more specific pathways linking edentulism to hip fracture (Supplementary Fig. 2). Among these indicators, only CS-5 showed a statistically significant indirect effect (estimated proportion: 3.77%, 95% CI: 0.24%-13.63%, p = 0.034), whereas no significant indirect effect was observed for handgrip strength, gait speed, or BMI. These findings should be interpreted cautiously, as edentulism, FI, and CS-5 were measured at the same baseline wave and therefore the primary mediation analyses do not establish causality.

Subgroup and sensitivity analyses

In subgroup analyses, the association between edentulism and incident hip fracture was generally consistent across prespecified subgroups (Fig. 4). Statistically significant associations were observed among females (HR: 1.86, 95% CI: 1.16–2.99, p = 0.010), participants aged < 65 years (HR: 1.89, 95% CI: 1.09–3.27, p = 0.023), never smokers (HR: 1.88, 95% CI: 1.20–2.92, p = 0.006), and never drinkers (HR: 1.81, 95% CI: 1.21–2.71, p = 0.004). Significant associations were also observed among participants without hypertension (HR: 1.58, 95% CI: 1.05–2.38, p = 0.029), without stroke (HR: 1.57, 95% CI: 1.09–2.25, p = 0.015), without diabetes (HR: 1.57, 95% CI: 1.09–2.25, p = 0.016), without arthritis (HR: 1.67, 95% CI: 1.06–2.62, p = 0.027), without heart disease (HR: 1.60, 95% CI: 1.10–2.34, p = 0.015), with sleep duration ≥ 7 h (HR: 1.64, 95% CI: 1.01–2.65, p = 0.043), with BMI ≤ 24 kg/m² (HR: 1.62, 95% CI: 1.08–2.45, p = 0.020), and without cognitive impairment (HR: 1.58, 95% CI: 1.01–2.47, p = 0.048). However, none of the interaction tests were statistically significant (all p for interaction > 0.05), indicating no clear evidence of effect modification across subgroups.

Findings were robust in sensitivity analyses. Logistic regression yielded estimates consistent in direction and magnitude with the primary time-to-event models (Supplementary Table 2). The E-value for the fully adjusted estimate (Model 5) was 2.46, and the E-value for the lower confidence limit was 1.39, indicating that an unmeasured confounder would need to be strongly associated with both edentulism and hip fracture to fully explain the observed association. Restricting analyses to participants with stable dentition status between 2011 and 2018 produced similar associations for hip fractures occurring during 2013–2018 (Supplementary Table 3). In analyses strengthening temporal ordering for exploratory mediation analyses (FI and CS-5 measured in 2013; hip fractures ascertained during 2015–2018), the findings remained consistent with a potential role of frailty as a pathway (estimated proportion: 5.41%, 95% CI: 0.39%-25.70%, p = 0.046) (Supplementary Fig. 3), although these analyses still do not establish causality. However, when CS-5 was reevaluated under the same temporally ordered framework, no significant indirect effect was observed.

Discussion

In this nationally representative cohort of Chinese adults aged ≥ 45 years, baseline edentulism was associated with a higher hazard of incident hip fracture over up to 9 years of follow-up. The association persisted after adjustment for sociodemographic factors, lifestyle behaviors, BMI, and major comorbidities, and remained robust in multiple sensitivity analyses. Collectively, these findings suggest that complete tooth loss may serve as a pragmatic marker of elevated fracture vulnerability in midlife and older adulthood. Exploratory analyses were consistent with frailty as a potential pathway underlying a modest proportion of this association.

Our findings align with prior observational evidence linking oral health to skeletal outcomes [28, 29]. Early work by Astrom et al. reported that hip fracture risk increased with the degree of tooth loss in Swedish dental patients [30]. Subsequent studies in South Korea, the United States, and Japan reported similar associations, including potential threshold effects, such as markedly elevated risk among individuals with ≥ 15 missing teeth [3, 9, 31]. More recently, large-scale cohort and administrative-linked studies have clarified these relationships [3, 22]. Notably, the Oral Health Screening to Assess Keys of Aging study, linking dental checkups with claims among Japanese adults aged ≥ 75 years, found that lower tooth count was associated with surgically treated hip fractures, with women appearing disproportionately affected [3]. Women are at higher risk of fragility fractures due to postmenopausal estrogen deficiency, smaller skeletal structure, and lower bending strength [32]. In our cohort, stratified analyses showed directionally consistent associations in both sexes. However, the interaction test was not statistically significant, potentially reflecting limited power due to low fracture counts or shared mechanistic pathways, such as frailty, influencing risk in both men and women. Nevertheless, the higher absolute risk observed in women aligns with established biological and skeletal differences and emphasizes the importance of considering sex-specific vulnerability in fracture prevention strategies [32].

The present study extends the literature in several ways. First, it provides the first nationally representative longitudinal evidence from China demonstrating a progressively higher cumulative incidence of hip fracture among adults with edentulism. Sensitivity analyses restricted to individuals with stable dentition further reduce the likelihood that short-term fluctuations account for these associations. Second, exploratory analyses were consistent with frailty as a potential pathway underlying part of the observed association, supporting the hypothesis that edentulism may reflect broader physiological vulnerability beyond oral health alone [33]. The potential pathway is consistent with a behavioral-functional interpretation in which edentulism contributes to deconditioning, thereby accelerating deficit accumulation, while frailty increases hip fractures through reduced muscle strength mass, strength impaired postural control, higher fall risk. Specifically, edentulism may impair mastication and dietary quality, contributing to undernutrition, sarcopenia, and reduced musculoskeletal resilience, which over time may increase frailty, fall risk, and fracture susceptibility [34]. Beyond nutritional pathways, tooth loss may also affect physical and psychosocial functioning: reduced confidence in eating and social interactions, depressive symptoms, and lower participation in daily activities may contribute to declines in muscle strength, balance, and physiological reserve [35, 36]. Emerging evidence also indicates that occlusal function and periodontal mechanoreceptors influence postural stability and neuromuscular coordination, such that edentulous individuals may have compromised head and trunk control [37], further elevating fall risk. In our exploratory mediation analyses, the FI showed an estimated indirect effect corresponding to 9.2% of the observed association, consistent with a potential but incomplete pathway. To further explore which components of frailty might contribute to this pathway, we additionally examined several indicators related to phenotypic frailty, nutritional status, and physical function, including BMI, handgrip strength, gait speed, and CS-5. These exploratory analyses did not reveal consistent indirect effects. Although CS-5 showed a statistically significant indirect effect in the baseline analysis, this finding was not robust in temporally ordered analyses, and no significant indirect effect was observed for handgrip strength, gait speed, or BMI. Taken together, these findings suggest that the association between edentulism and hip fracture is unlikely to be explained by a single isolated physiological pathway. Rather, it may reflect a broader state of accumulated vulnerability captured by the FI, while individual components such as lower limb function, muscle strength, mobility, or nutritional status may contribute only partially. The modest proportion associated with FI also suggests that additional mechanisms may be involved, including chronic systemic inflammation, comorbidity burden, and unmeasured skeletal factors such as reduced bone mineral density, impaired bone microarchitecture, or altered remodeling dynamics [38]. Accordingly, the relationship between edentulism and hip fracture risk is likely multifactorial, with frailty representing one potential, but not exclusive, pathway.

From a clinical and public health perspective, these findings suggest that edentulism may serve as a readily identifiable marker of increased hip fracture vulnerability. Tooth loss is common, easily ascertainable in routine care, and reflects the cumulative burden of oral and systemic health decline. Incorporating dentition status into geriatric risk assessment could help identify individuals who may benefit from comprehensive evaluations of frailty, fall risk, and fracture prevention [39]. The exploratory findings regarding frailty as a potential pathway highlight the potential value of integrated, multidisciplinary strategies addressing oral health, nutrition, and physical function simultaneously. Such approaches may include preventive dental care and prosthodontic rehabilitation where appropriate [40], nutritional optimization with adequate protein and micronutrients [41], psychosocial support, and structured exercise targeting strength, balance, and gait stability [42]. Opportunistic screening of edentulous patients, particularly those with additional risk factors, may facilitate earlier intervention and coordinated care. Given the observational design, these implications should be interpreted as supporting risk stratification and hypothesis generation rather than direct evidence that dental interventions alone prevent fractures.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, incident hip fractures were self-reported without medical record verification, raising the possibility of outcome misclassification. Although CHARLS interviewers were trained to explain the anatomical location of the hip when asking the question, some participants may still have had difficulty distinguishing hip fractures from other femoral or pelvic fractures. Second, despite extensive covariate adjustment, residual confounding may persist from unmeasured factors, including bone mineral density, vitamin D status, osteoporosis, a direct measure of health literacy, and detailed nutritional profiles [43]. Although we adjusted for medication burden using the number of medication categories used for chronic conditions, detailed information on specific medication classes associated with falls was not available. Therefore, residual confounding related to fall-risk medications cannot be excluded. Third, the study population consisted of community-dwelling Chinese adults aged ≥ 45 years, which may limit generalizability to institutionalized populations, younger adults, or regions with different healthcare systems and oral health profiles. Fourth, edentulism was assessed by self-report, and CHARLS does not provide detailed oral health information, such as the number of remaining teeth, periodontal status, denture use, or objective measures of masticatory performance. This may have introduced exposure misclassification and prevented a more detailed evaluation of oral health severity. In particular, the lack of denture-use information limited our ability to further elucidate the pathways linking edentulism to hip fracture, including whether prosthodontic rehabilitation may mitigate risk through improved nutrition, balance, or physical function. Prior evidence suggests that denture use may mitigate fall risk among older adults with substantial tooth loss [40]. Given that approximately 60% of edentulous adults aged > 60 years in China reportedly use dentures [44], the observed association may be underestimated. In addition, we were unable to examine potential dose-response relationships between the number of remaining teeth and incident hip fracture. Future studies incorporating detailed clinical oral assessments and validated fracture outcomes are needed to clarify these mechanisms.

Conclusions

Edentulism was associated with an elevated risk of hip fracture among middle-aged and older adults in China. Exploratory analyses were consistent with frailty as a potential pathway underlying this association, although causality cannot be established. Dentition status may inform targeted strategies for identifying individuals at heightened hip fracture risk, guiding interventions such as frailty assessment, nutritional support, fall prevention, and integrated oral health management.
