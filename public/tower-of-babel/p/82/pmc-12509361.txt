Indigenous knowledge systems and malaria prevention in Zimbabwe: a market basket analysis
Authors: Byron Chapoterera, Anesu Marume, Nicholas Midzi, Keshena Naidoo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMalaria remains a major public health challenge in Zimbabwe, reporting the highest prevalence in Manicaland Province, where indigenous health practices and religious beliefs influence healthcare-seeking behaviours. Understanding the interplay between biomedical and traditional approaches is essential for designing culturally sensitive malaria control strategies. The study explored malaria prevention practices and the role of indigenous knowledge systems, including traditional and spiritual health practices, among adults in Manicaland Province, Zimbabwe.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 480 adults aged 18 years and above, recruited through stratified random sampling. Data were collected using structured questionnaires administered in face-to-face interviews. Variables included sociodemographic characteristics, recent malaria experiences, use of indigenous and biomedical malaria prevention methods, and visits to health facilities, traditional healers, or church prophets. Descriptive statistics were computed, and association rule mining (ARM) using the Apriori algorithm in R was employed to identify frequent co-occurring behaviours and health-seeking patterns.ResultsParticipants were predominantly female (69.0%) and within the 25–44 age range (57.5%). A majority reported employment (60.0%) and low household income (47.9%). In the previous three months, 36.7% of respondents and 54.0% of families had experienced malaria. Primary sources of indigenous knowledge included older people (27.7%), informal discussions (7.7%), and observations (5.6%). Association rule mining revealed strong linkages between visits to church prophets and avoidance of traditional healers, as well as notable patterns involving use of cow dung, health facility visits, and indigenous practices. For instance, individuals who visited prophets were highly likely not to have visited traditional healers (support = 0.66, confidence = 0.91, lift = 1.08).ConclusionHealth-seeking behaviour for malaria in Manicaland Province is influenced by complex interactions between spiritual, traditional, and biomedical systems. Indigenous knowledge plays a significant role in malaria prevention and treatment. Public health programmes should integrate culturally informed strategies that respect local belief systems while promoting effective malaria control interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12936-025-05543-y.

Background

Malaria remains a persistent global health challenge, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), where the disease is endemic and continues to cause high morbidity and mortality [1]. In 2023, an estimated 263 million malaria cases were reported globally, an increase of nearly 11 million cases compared to 2022 [2, 3]. While there was a gradual decline in malaria incidence globally between 2015 and 2022, the incidence and mortality rates remain well above the targets set by the Sustainable Development Goals [4]. Traditional knowledge systems and practices shape health-seeking behaviours related to malaria, and in some instances, pose challenges to the adoption of biomedical prevention and treatment strategies.

Malaria prevention and control strategies being conducted in Zimbabwe are largely focused on vector control, which includes indoor residual spraying, long-lasting insecticidal nets (LLINs) [5, 6]. Additional strategies involve case management, prophylaxis, especially for vulnerable populations such as pregnant women and visitors to malaria-endemic areas, social and behaviour change, disease and vector surveillance [7, 8]. Indigenous Knowledge Systems (IKS) refer to the local knowledge, beliefs, practices, and skills that are developed and sustained by indigenous and local communities over generations, often through direct interaction with their environment [9, 10]. These systems are deeply rooted in cultural traditions, languages, and worldviews and represent an underexplored yet potentially valuable resource [11]. Communities in malaria-prone regions utilise traditional healing practices, locally grown medicinal plants, and community-based approaches to prevent and treat malaria [12]. A growing body of evidence suggests that sociocultural and environmental factors play a critical role in influencing malaria-related behaviours. Although widely practised across SSA, these indigenous approaches are often poorly documented, and their interaction with formal health systems remains insufficiently understood [13]. IKS are of significant importance in the fight against malaria as primary healthcare systems are struggling to adapt to changes in malaria transmission patterns linked to climate change [14]. The formal health systems remains insufficiently understood [13].

Extreme weather events and global warming associated with climate change have altered rainfall patterns, temperature, and humidity, which in turn affect mosquito breeding sites and malaria transmission cycles. Recent data from 2022 show a concerning resurgence, with the highest number of malaria cases recorded since 2004. Predictive models that account for environmental factors and health interventions suggest that, in the absence of intensified efforts, annual malaria cases could increase by 25% to 30% by the 2050s. Existing health systems, which are already dealing with a critical shortage of trained healthcare workers, are unable to address the threat of increased malaria transmission, especially in malaria-prone regions [4]. Global initiatives such as Roll Back Malaria and increased international funding have helped to reduce health inequities and improve malaria outcomes. However, the vision of malaria eradication remains elusive [15]. In addition to climate change, other challenges such as insecticide and drug resistance, human migration between high and low malaria transmission areas, and the genetic diversity of malaria parasites continue to undermine control efforts [16]. However, health policy makers and health providers have paid little attention to the role of IKS in malaria prevention and treatment.

Common traditional malaria control methods include the use of herbal remedies, burning of specific plants or dung to repel mosquitoes, steaming as a fever remedy, and spiritual healing rituals. While many of these practices have not undergone rigorous scientific validation, they continue to form the backbone of community-based malaria management in many regions and remain an integral part of community health-seeking behaviour [17]. In South Africa, for instance, communities in malaria-endemic regions use indigenous rainfall forecasting to anticipate the onset of malaria season, underscoring the value of IKS in early warning systems [18]. Integrating such traditional forecasting models with modern technologies has been proposed as a promising strategy to enhance community resilience to disease outbreaks and anticipate extreme weather events due to climate change [14]. Furthermore, environmental factors such as proximity to rivers and housing structure require culturally appropriate health education and targeted structural improvements, like the installation of eave gauzes [19].

The effectiveness of IKS is also affected by climate change and should be considered in strategies to mitigate the effects of climate change on communities [20]. For example, changes in rainfall and growing seasons may reduce the availability of medicinal plants traditionally used to manage malaria, thereby limiting access to culturally relevant and locally trusted forms of care [21]. Such disruptions to IKS not only threaten health-seeking behaviours but also undermine community resilience in malaria-endemic regions.

In addition to these biomedical and programmatic barriers, environmental changes, particularly those driven by climate change, pose a growing threat to malaria control and elimination strategies [22]. Climate variability alters rainfall patterns, temperature, and humidity, which in turn affect mosquito breeding sites and malaria transmission cycles [20]. These environmental shifts also impact IKS, which many communities rely on for malaria prevention and treatment [20]. For example, changes in rainfall and growing seasons may reduce the availability of medicinal plants traditionally used to manage malaria, thereby limiting access to culturally relevant and locally trusted forms of care [23, 24]. Such disruptions to IKS not only threaten health-seeking behaviours, but also undermine community resilience in malaria-endemic regions.

Despite the widespread use of IKS, most malaria programmes ignore the traditional practices and beliefs of the local communities. This can result in low acceptance of national health campaigns and poor engagement with allopathic health systems [25]. It is crucial for health policy makers and health providers to understand how communities manage malaria at the grassroots level and whether traditional practices complement or compete with biomedical services [26]. This study investigates the use of indigenous knowledge in malaria prevention and management and examines how these practices coexist with, or diverge from, biomedical health services. The aim is to identify community-based strategies that can inform the design of integrated and culturally responsive malaria control programmes.

Methods

Study design

A cross-sectional descriptive study was conducted to explore malaria prevention practices and the role of indigenous knowledge systems among adults residing in Manicaland Province, Zimbabwe. The study aimed to examine participants’ health-seeking behaviours and the use of traditional and indigenous methods for malaria prevention and treatment.

Study setting

A community-based household survey was conducted in Manicaland Province, Eastern Zimbabwe. Manicaland Province reports the highest burden of malaria in Zimbabwe and is also known for a high proportion of religious objectors to conventional health services. This unique epidemiological and cultural context provided an appropriate setting to investigate the interplay between formal healthcare systems and traditional knowledge systems.

Study population and sampling

A total of 480 individuals aged 18 years and above were recruited using stratified random sampling to ensure representation across age, gender, and household income categories. Eligible participants were those who had resided in the community for at least six months and were willing to provide informed consent. The sampling frame was a village household register listing all households in the selected villages. Households were selected through simple random sampling using computer-generated numbers. In each selected household, an adult who was present and knowledgeable about malaria prevention and treatment practices was interviewed.

Data collection

Three trained research assistants were engaged to conduct data collection. A structured questionnaire was administered through face-to-face interviews. The tool collected data on sociodemographic variables, malaria episodes experienced by the respondent and household members in the preceding three months, healthcare-seeking behaviour (e.g., visits to health facilities, traditional healers, and church prophets), and malaria prevention practices, including the use of herbs, repellents, and other culturally embedded methods.

In addition to closed-ended questions, respondents were probed using open-ended questions to describe the types of indigenous remedies used, how they were prepared, and their perceived effectiveness. The questionnaire was developed in English, translated into local Shona language, and back translated into English for content validation. It was pretested in a demographically similar area within Manicaland Province, not included in the study sample. Feedback from the pre-test was used to refine the tool for clarity and cultural sensitivity.

Variables

Key variables included age, gender, employment status, household size, income level, and recent experiences with malaria. The questionnaire also assessed specific malaria prevention and treatment behaviours. Respondents were asked to report whether they had visited a health facility, consulted a traditional healer, or sought assistance from a church prophet or spiritual healer in the past three months for any health-related issue. Participants were further asked to identify any herbs, plants, or repellents they had used, providing local names, which were later translated into their scientific equivalents.

Ethical considerations

Ethical clearance was sought and granted by the University of KwaZulu-Natal Biomedical Research Ethics Committee (BREC/00005514/2023) and the Medical Research Council of Zimbabwe (MRCZ/A/3168). Additional permissions were obtained from the Permanent Secretary of Health and local provincial and district authorities in Manicaland. Participants were asked to sign an informed consent from before being interviewed. Confidentiality, privacy, and the right to withdraw at any point were upheld throughout the study.

Data management

Data were cleaned and validated for completeness. There were no omissions noted in key analytical variables. Indigenous plant and herb names were translated into scientific nomenclature to ensure consistency. Binary variables were generated for key behaviours of interest, such as “visited a health facility,” “consulted a traditional healer,” “used cow dung,” and “visited a church prophet” to prepare the dataset for association rule mining. These variables were then transformed into a transactional format.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to analyse the data on sociodemographic characteristics of participants. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were summarised using means and standard deviations.

Association Rule Mining (ARM) was applied (using the arules package in RStudio) to link participant practices and consultation with health providers in order to determine common patterns. The Apriori algorithm was employed to extract frequent item sets and generate rules, with minimum thresholds set for support, confidence, and lift to ensure that only meaningful associations were retained. Each association rule represents a unique and non-redundant combination of behaviours or practices that tend to co-occur. The top 20 most frequent rules were selected for interpretation. Results were visualised using network plots to display the strength and connectivity of relationships among health-seeking and prevention behaviours.

Results

Nearly half (47.9%) were classified as living in low-income households, while 31.9% and 20.2% were categorised as high- and middle-income households, respectively. Most participants reported that their sources of indigenous knowledge related to health and malaria prevention were derived from older people (27.7%), informal discussions (7.7%), and observations (5.6%). Fewer participants cited herbalist-led teachings (4.4%), religion (2.5%), cultural festivals (2.1%), peer teachings (1.5%), parents (0.8%), and books (0.4%) as sources.

Community-based and informal community malaria prevention and control remedies

Respondents identified various plant-based and organic materials used for malaria prevention and treatment. The most commonly reported remedy was cucumber magnolia tree (Magnolia acuminata), used by 24% (n = 116) of participants. Other notable remedies included horsemint (Monarda punctata) (17%, n = 81), Wing pod (Xeroderris stuhlmanni) (15%, n = 69), fever tea (Lippia javanica) and cow dung, each used by 11% (n = 50) of respondents, and resurrection plant (Myrothamnus flabellifolius), used by 7.6% (n = 36). Less frequently cited methods included sagewood (Lippia guarri) (7.2%, n = 34), elephant dung (3.8%, n = 18), and combined remedies, such as cucumber magnolia and horsemint (3.2%, n = 25), and cow dung mixed with herbs and horsemint (0.2%, n = 1) (Table 2). Variations of this approach include the use of horsemint, in which cow dung is burned and horsemint is hung in the same area for added protection. Other famous cures include cucumber magnolia tree, which is used alone by steeping fresh leaves in boiling water for steam inhalation, and elephant dung, which is dried and burned, with the smoke used to repel mosquitos in the same way that cow dung is. Additional medicinal or curative techniques were also documented. Sagewood (Lippia guarri) root is cooked to make a drinking infusion that is said to alleviate malaria symptoms. Wing pod (Xeroderris stuhlmanni) is made by grinding the bark and combining it with water for oral use, which has both medicinal and maybe preventative properties.

Resurrection plant (Myrothamnus flabellifolius), another well-regarded traditional remedy, involves soaking the stem in water until it turns green, after which the liquid is consumed. Finally, fever tea (L. javanica) leaves are boiled, and the resultant decoction is taken orally, aligning with known antimalarial uses of this plant.

Association rules linking traditional, religious, and formal health-seeking behaviours

A total of 190 unique association rules were extracted. Various relationships were identified with the use of cow dung in most of the rules; use of herbs, vegetation, and tree products was not within the top 20. These patterns point to a high degree of healthcare pluralism in the community, with individuals frequently combining modern, traditional, and spiritual interventions in complex, but structured ways.

The most frequently occurring rule indicated that individuals who visited a prophet were highly likely not to have visited a healer (support = 0.66; confidence = 0.91; lift = 1.08; count = 317). Conversely, not visiting a healer strongly predicted a visit to a prophet (confidence = 0.78). Formal healthcare use also showed strong associations: individuals who used a health facility were less likely to have visited a traditional healer (support = 0.46; confidence = 0.75). Additionally, among those who did not use health facilities, 98% also reported not visiting a healer, highlighting a cluster of individuals disengaged from formal and traditional biomedical care pathways. Intriguingly, the use of cow dung in health-seeking behaviour showed moderate prevalence and was frequently associated with both visits to prophets and formal health facility use, suggesting a complex layering of beliefs and practices.

Discussion

This study explored Indigenous Knowledge Systems (IKS) regarding malaria prevention and treatment in Manicaland Province, Zimbabwe, and plurality in health-seeking practices. The findings offer important insights into how communities integrate formal healthcare services with traditional and religious-based practices. The study brings in a novel approach to understand IKS and how communities decide which appropriate care to choose.

The results confirm the plurality of malaria-related health-seeking pathways, revealing distinct trajectories such as the use of herbs alongside visits to traditional or spiritual healers, either concurrently with or before accessing biomedical care. These patterns are consistent with recent findings across sub-Saharan Africa, where ethnomedical beliefs, structural barriers, and community norms strongly influence care-seeking behaviour [27]. Some community practices, such as the use of herbs or smoke from dung, may have potential value and, with appropriate testing and validation, could be integrated into health education as supportive measures, while emphasizing the importance of timely care-seeking and recognition of symptom escalation [28–30]. Training trusted spiritual or religious leaders to identify danger signs and refer individuals to formal health facilities could further promote early engagement with biomedical services by leveraging established social trust [31, 32]. Although such strategies may fall outside conventional biomedical models, context-aware programming that embraces pluralistic care pathways and bridges indigenous and biomedical knowledge systems can enhance malaria treatment outcomes and support timely, culturally appropriate care-seeking behaviours.

A key finding is that the use of indigenous or community-based malaria prevention strategies, such as herbal remedies, the use of smoke from cow dung, and other local repellents, does not appear to discourage individuals from accessing biomedical services. Instead, many respondents reported simultaneously utilising both formal and informal sources of care. This suggests a complementary, rather than substitutive, relationship between indigenous and biomedical approaches in most households. This aligns with previous research indicating that traditional knowledge systems are often integrated with formal healthcare practices, enhancing community engagement in malaria control efforts [33, 34].

However, our association rule mining (ARM) results indicate a nuanced exception when consideration included spiritual and religious factors. Malaria preventive behaviours showed a difference when considering the religious system and the spiritual healer system. Individuals who reported visiting church prophets or religious healers were significantly less likely to have visited formal health facilities in the preceding three months. This pattern aligns with existing evidence that in some religious communities, especially those that emphasize faith healing, the use of biomedical care may be discouraged or perceived as a demonstration of spiritual weakness [35–37]. Notably, Manicaland Province reports the highest proportion of religious objectors to health services in Zimbabwe, and this social reality may shape community norms around healthcare utilization [38, 39].

Despite this trend among religious followers, most respondents did not frame indigenous practices as a replacement for medical care. Instead, traditional knowledge was often positioned as part of a broader ecosystem of health management relying on knowledge passed down from elders, peer discussions, and community observations. This resonates with the Afrocentric metatheories of knowledge, where traditional healing systems and health philosophies are grounded in lived experiences and spiritual interconnectedness [40, 41]. The retention of such intergenerational systems reflects their epistemological strength and resilience.

Our findings further reveal specific indigenous remedies widely used in the study area. Remedies, such as cucumber magnolia were mostly seen as a form of treatment [13]. The medicinal properties of these remedies may require additional study. This mirrors findings from a study on mosquito behaviour following introduction of cows [42]. The burning of cow dung, elephant dung, and plant leaves produced smoke believed to repel mosquitoes [13]. Though empirical validation remains limited, several studies hypothesize that smoke from organic sources can alter mosquito behaviour by masking human scent or creating a physical barrier [43].

Remedies such as elephant dung and L. javanica were associated with either mosquito prevention or symptom relief. Studies have shown that L. javanica, have insect-repellent and antimicrobial properties [44]. However, other remedies lack scientific corroboration or present potential health risks, especially if used as a substitute for medical treatment despite prior episodes of malaria. This highlights the need for rigorous scientific evaluation of traditional remedies to assess safety, efficacy, and potential integration into national malaria control strategies [44, 45]. There is an additional need for further scientific evaluation of a majority of these community-based indigenous remedies to determine their safety and efficacy [46].

The coexistence of indigenous and biomedical care reflects broader health-seeking trends among marginalised populations in sub-Saharan Africa. Our study parallels findings from other SSA malarious regions, whereby people also utilise herbal treatments, steaming, and dung smoke for malaria management due to limited access to formal healthcare [46, 47]. These communities often cite structural barriers, distance, affordability, and inconsistent service delivery as reasons for resorting to indigenous remedies first. The parallels underscore the importance of developing malaria control interventions that integrate indigenous knowledge and address healthcare access inequities [46].

The use of remedies such as cucumber magnolia, horsemint, and cow dung, as observed in the study, reveals a complex matrix of cultural continuity, health beliefs, and practical adaptation in malaria prevention [48]. While well-known botanical treatments, like cucumber magnolia and horsemint have attracted global research interest, local remedies still face challenges in standardisation, dosage accuracy, and clinical validation [49]. Nonetheless, collaborations between biomedical researchers and traditional healers can enhance mutual learning, improve malaria control, and foster respect for these indigenous knowledge systems [50]. Documenting and integrating such age-informed Indigenous knowledge into public health strategies can improve early warning systems and strengthen community resilience.

Strength and limitations

A key strength of this study lies in its application of market basket analysis to cross-sectional data to quantify co-occurring health behaviours in a context where both indigenous and biomedical practices are present. While not traditionally applied in indigenous knowledge systems research, this approach offers a novel way to detect widespread patterns of practice at the population level. It serves as a complementary method to qualitative inquiry by providing a structured framework for identifying dominant and emerging behavioural clusters that warrant further exploration.

However, the use of market basket analysis is inherently limited by its reliance on cross-sectional data. As such, it does not capture the underlying cultural meanings, motivations, or contextual factors that often shape indigenous knowledge systems. Qualitative methods, such as in-depth interviews, focus groups, or ethnographic studies, are better suited to explore these dimensions. Future research incorporating such approaches is recommended to deepen the understanding of the behavioural patterns identified in this study.

While the study explored indigenous and spiritual care-seeking behaviours in depth, there was limited capture of the various biomedical care pathways from the respondent narratives. This limited our ability to comprehensively describe the full spectrum of malaria care-seeking trajectories. Future studies should integrate questions that allow for a richer understanding of how individuals navigate between formal and informal systems during malaria episodes.

The reliance on self-reported data may introduce recall or social desirability bias, particularly in sensitive domains such as health-seeking behaviours and religious practices. While the study used stratified sampling to enhance representativeness, findings may not be generalizable to all regions of Zimbabwe or urban populations with different socio-cultural dynamics. Lastly, association rule mining identifies patterns but does not infer causality; further inferential analysis would be required to validate the strength and directionality of observed relationships.

Conclusion

The findings of this study underscore the diverse strategies employed by communities to manage and control malaria, many of which are rooted in indigenous knowledge systems. Notably, these practices do not function as substitutes for formal health services but rather operate alongside them as complementary approaches. However, a potential policy concern, though not directly examined in this study, is the risk of delayed presentation to formal health facilities, as individuals may initially rely on traditional or community-based remedies. This delay could have implications for malaria outcomes and warrants attention in the design of health promotion and behaviour change interventions. Policy frameworks should therefore aim to integrate culturally accepted community practices within broader malaria control strategies, while simultaneously strengthening community engagement in timely care-seeking behaviours.

Recommendations

Future studies should explore the motivations and beliefs underlying religious objections to biomedical care, particularly in high-burden provinces like Manicaland. Qualitative approaches, such as in-depth interviews or focus group discussions, could provide a richer understanding of how individuals make decisions about malaria prevention and treatment within spiritual and cultural frameworks. Additionally, operational research that tests interventions co-developed with traditional and religious leaders could shed light on scalable models for integrated malaria control in similar contexts.

From a policy perspective, public health strategies must move beyond binary divisions between traditional and biomedical care. Health policies that ignore the complex and pluralistic nature of care-seeking in malaria-endemic regions risk being misaligned with community realities. To be effective, malaria control efforts must adopt culturally sensitive health communication strategies and formally recognize the role of community-based actors, including traditional leaders, herbalists, and religious figures.

At the implementation level, collaborative models that incorporate indigenous knowledge and practices, while providing clear, evidence-based guidance on effective malaria prevention and treatment, may foster trust in the health system and improve uptake of services. Training and sensitizing healthcare providers to respectfully engage with local belief systems can further reduce stigma, promote respectful dialogue, and encourage timely care-seeking behaviour.
