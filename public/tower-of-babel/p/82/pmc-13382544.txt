Commentary on “Skeletal Muscle Index to Red Cell Distribution Width Ratio: A Novel Prognostic Indicator for Patients with Stage I-III Colorectal Cancer”
Authors: Yiheng Yu, Daxiong Zeng
Published: 2026

We have read the recently published article by Choi and Kang with great interest [1]. In this excellent study, the authors focused on a novel prognostic marker based on the skeletal muscle index (SMI) and red cell distribution width (RDW) for patients with colorectal cancer (CRC). They retrospectively analyzed the characteristics of 585 patients with stage I-III CRC who underwent surgery. The results indicated that the ratio of SMI to RDW was a novel and significant predictor of overall survival in patients with stages I-III CRC, demonstrating greater prognostic power than either SMI or RDW alone. Although the conclusion is interesting, we have some points to emphasize regarding this study.

Firstly, there are some confounding disorders that should be considered to avoid affecting the results of RDW. RDW is a transitional parameter of variation in the size of circulating red blood cells and is frequently used as a marker for the differential diagnosis of anemia. Recently, it has been proven to be associated with poor prognosis in tumors, inflammatory diseases, and cardiovascular diseases [2,3]. Nevertheless, plenty of conditions have been proven to elevate RDW levels, such as diabetes mellitus, hypertension, hematological diseases, renal or hepatic dysfunction [4,5]. Therefore, these diseases in the subjects should be excluded or adjusted.

Secondly, a proportion of subjects received perioperative transfusion (ranging from 7.5% to 14.4% in different groups), which might significantly affect RDW levels. Moreover, nutritional status and anemia both impact RDW levels. Some drugs (erythropoietin, iron supplements, multivitamins, and antibiotics) and measurement details (anticoagulants, elapsed time between blood collection and RDW measurement) might also affect RDW levels or reference ranges [4,5]. Characterizing this information would help demonstrate the prognostic role of RDW in CRC patients.

In conclusion, we believe that the prognostic role of SMI and RDW in stage I-III CRC patients remains unclear unless the above factors affecting RDW levels are excluded or adjusted. A larger-scale prospective study might be more helpful and convincing.
