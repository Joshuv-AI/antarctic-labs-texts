Feeding and growth of the new mixotrophic dinoflagellate Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 and the prey spectrum extension of its sister species Gymnodinium smaydae
Authors: Ji Hyun You, Hae Jin Jeong, Sang Ah Park, Haneum Kwon, Se Hee Eom, Minji Kwon, Nam Seon Kang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mixotrophic dinoflagellates play critical roles in marine food webs and biogeochemical cycles. To investigate the feeding of the newly described mixotrophic dinoflagellate Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405, its feeding mechanism and edible prey species were explored. Its growth and ingestion rates on the optimal prey Heterocapsa rotundata as a function of prey concentration were also measured. To compare the feeding behaviors of Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 and its sister species Gymnodinium smaydae, 11 algal species not previously tested for feeding by G. smaydae were examined. Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 fed on prey using a peduncle, like G. smaydae. Among 28 algal prey species offered, Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 ingested dinoflagellates but did not feed on the prymnesiophyte, cryptophyte, chlorophyte, raphidophyte, or diatom. Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 was able to feed on 15 of 21 dinoflagellate species, including Alexandrium fraterculus, Alexandrium minutum, Alexandrium tamarense, Amphidinium carterae, Lingulaulax polyedra, and four Prorocentrum species that G. smaydae does not feed on. The number of edible prey species for Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 is greater than that for G. smaydae. While the autotrophic growth rate of Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 was − 0.08 d− 1, the maximum growth and ingestion rates on H. rotundata were 0.46 d− 1 and 0.74 ng C predator− 1 d− 1, respectively, which are considerably lower than those of G. smaydae on the same prey. Therefore, Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 may trade off the kinds of its edible prey species with growth and ingestion rates. These results provide a basis for better understanding the ecological roles of mixotrophic dinoflagellates in marine ecosystems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00248-026-02739-w.

Introduction

Phototrophic dinoflagellates are ubiquitous and a major component of marine ecosystems [1–7]. Over the past three decades, many phototrophic dinoflagellates, formerly known as autotrophs, have been reclassified to mixotrophs [8–16]. Interests in mixotrophic dinoflagellates have increased because they play diverse ecological roles as primary producers, prey, and predators in marine ecosystems [2, 4, 17–20]. Furthermore, approximately 90% of the dinoflagellates causing global red tides are mixotrophs [21]. Therefore, it is necessary to determine whether a phototrophic dinoflagellate also possesses mixotrophic capability.

Mixotrophic dinoflagellates can ingest diverse prey items, including bacteria, small flagellates, dinoflagellates, and ciliates [12, 14–15, 22–28]. Feeding on diverse prey species is known to be an important strategy of mixotrophic dinoflagellates that cause global harmful algal blooms (HABs) [21]. Therefore, determining their edible prey species is a critical step in understanding their ecology and evolution. However, mixotrophic ability has been examined for fewer than 10% of ca. 1,200 reported phototrophic dinoflagellates [8–14, 16, 26–27, 29–31], and growth or ingestion rates are known for only ca. 50 mixotrophic species [8–13, 15–16, 22–23, 25, 29–30, 32–33]. Therefore, when a new phototrophic dinoflagellate is discovered, its mixotrophic ability should be examined. If it is mixotrophic, the types of edible prey species and the specific prey species supporting its optimal growth and ingestion rates then need to be investigated to better understand its ecophysiological characteristics.

A dinoflagellate isolated from coastal waters of Tongyeong, Korea, in May 2024 was identified as a species of the genus Gymnodinium, related to Gymnodinium smaydae, one of the fastest-growing mixotrophic dinoflagellates [26, 34]. G. smaydae has a relatively narrow range of edible prey (5 of 19 tested prey species) but exhibits a high mixotrophic growth rate (2.23 d− 1) on its known optimal prey [26]. To better understand ecological and evolutionary traits of these two species, the edible prey spectrum and growth and ingestion rates of the new species ought to be compared with those of G. smaydae. Additionally, more algal species should be tested to determine whether G. smaydae can feed on other prey species and how this affects its mixotrophic growth rate.

In this study, a phylogenetic tree and brief morphological descriptions were provided to confirm the generic affiliation of the isolated strain. In addition, its feeding mechanism and edible prey species range were explored using 28 microalgal species. Growth and ingestion rates of Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 feeding on the optimal prey Heterocapsa rotundata were determined as a function of prey concentration. To compare the feeding characteristics of Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 with those of its sister species G. smaydae, the ingestion of 11 prey species by G. smaydae was also tested. These results provide a basis for understanding the ecological roles of this new mixotrophic dinoflagellate in marine ecosystems.

Materials and Methods

Preparation of Experimental Organisms

A cell of Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 was isolated from plankton samples collected off Tongyeong, Korea, in May 2024 (water temperature, 21.8 ℃; salinity, 31.6). When each of several potential prey species such as Amphidinium carterae, H. rotundata, Isochrysis galbana, and Prorocentrum cordatum was added to aliquots of the plankton samples, cell proliferation of Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 was markedly observed when H. rotundata cells were added. Therefore, a clonal culture was established from these cells using two successive single-cell isolations and was subsequently maintained with H. rotundata HRSH1201 added as prey at a final concentration of approximately 30,000 cells mL− 1. As cell density increased, cultures were transferred sequentially to 50-, 250-, and 800-mL culture flasks (Corning Inc., New York, NY, USA) containing fresh H. rotundata cells in 0.2-µm filtered sea water (FSW) and maintained through weekly transfers in 800-mL flasks at 20 ℃ under 20 µmol photons m− 2 s− 1 from a light-emitting diode (LED) lamp with a 14:10 h Light: Dark (L: D) cycle.

A culture of G. smaydae GSSH1005, originally isolated from Shiwha Bay, Korea, in May 2010 [34], was also used. A dense culture (ca. 20,000 cells mL− 1) of G. smaydae was transferred weekly to a 250-mL flask containing 0.2-µm FSW, and H. rotundata HRSH1201 was added as prey at a final concentration of ca. 70,000 cells mL− 1 under the same conditions described above.

The strains of potential microalgal prey species were maintained under 20–50 µmol photons m− 2 s− 1 from an LED lamp with a 14:10 h L: D cycle at 20 ℃ (Table 1). However, the strain of Margalefidinium polykrikoides used in this study required slightly higher irradiance (50–100 µmol photons m− 2 s− 1) with a 24:0 h L: D cycle, as it did not grow under lower irradiance with a dark period [13]. All cultures of potential microalgal prey species, except for the diatom Skeletonema dohrnii, were transferred once every two to four weeks to 250-mL flasks containing f/2 − Si or L1 − Si medium [35–36]; f/2 + Si medium was used for S. dohrnii.

Morphology of Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405

The general morphology of living cells of Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 was examined using an inverted microscope (Axiovert 200 M; Carl Zeiss, Göttingen, Germany) at 1,000× magnification. Image analysis software (ZEN3.0 Pro; Carl Zeiss) in combination with a digital camera attached to the inverted microscope was used to measure the length and width of living cells of Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 (n = 30).

For field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM) analysis, approximately 10-mL Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 cultures (ca. 5,000 cells mL− 1) was fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide (OsO4; Electron Microscopy Sciences, Hatfield, PA, USA) for 10 min and collected onto 3-µm polycarbonate (PC) membrane filters (Whatman Nuclepore Track-Etched Membranes; Whatman, Kent, UK). The filters were rinsed with 50% FSW diluted with distilled water and then three times with distilled water to remove residual salts. The membranes with the attached cells were dehydrated in a graded ethanol series (10, 30, 50, 70, 90, and 100%), followed by two additional changes in absolute ethanol. After critical-point drying using an automated dryer (EM CPD300; Leica, Wetzlar, Germany), the filters were mounted onto aluminium stubs (Electron Microscopy Sciences) using copper conductive double-sided tape (Ted Pella, Redding, CA, USA), sputter-coated with gold (MC1000; Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan), and observed for cell surface morphology with a high-resolution FE-SEM (Sigma 500/VP; Carl Zeiss, Göttingen, Germany).

For transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis, Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 cultures were transferred into 10-mL test tubes and fixed with 2.5% (v/v; final concentration) glutaraldehyde for 1.5 h. The fixed cells were then transferred to 10-mL centrifuge tubes and concentrated by centrifugation at 1,610 × g for 10 min using a centrifuge (VS-5500; Vision, Bucheon, Korea). The resulting pellet was resuspended in 0.2 M sodium cacodylate buffer (pH 7.4) and washed once. After washing, the pellet was post-fixed for 90 min with 1% (w/v) OsO4 prepared in deionized water and subsequently embedded in agar. Dehydration was performed through a graded ethanol series (50, 60, 70, 80, 90, and 100%), followed by two additional changes in absolute ethanol. The dehydrated pellet was then embedded in Spurr’s resin (Electron Microscopy Sciences) and sectioned using an ultramicrotome (EM UC7; Leica). Finally, ultrathin sections were stained with 3% (w/v) aqueous uranyl acetate followed by lead citrate and examined using a Sigma 500/VP TEM (Carl Zeiss).

DNA Sequencing and Phylogenetic Analysis

Ten cells of Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 were transferred into each 0.2-mL polymerase chain reaction (PCR) tube containing sterile distilled water. PCR amplification of small subunit (SSU), large subunit (LSU), and internal transcribed spacers (ITS) rDNA regions was performed using the primer sets listed in Table S1 and standard reaction mixtures (BioFACT Co., Ltd., Daejeon, Korea). All PCRs were run on an AllInOneCycler (Bioneer, Daejeon, Korea) following the thermal cycling conditions described in You et al. [6]. PCR products were purified and sequenced on an ABI 3730XL DNA Analyzer (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA), and the sequences were assembled and edited in ContigExpress (Infomax, Frederick, MD, USA). Pairwise sequence differences between Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 and other Gymnodinium species were calculated from BLAST alignments based on the proportion of mismatches and gaps relative to alignment length.

LSU rDNA sequences of Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 and related dinoflagellates obtained from GenBank were aligned using MEGA v12 [37] to reconstruct the phylogenetic tree. The LSU rDNA phylogeny was inferred using Bayesian analysis in MrBayes v3.1 under a GTR + G+I model [38] and maximum likelihood analysis in RAxML 7.0.3 under the GTRGAMMA model [39]. Using MEGA v12, patristic distances between Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 and other Gymnodinium species were calculated based on the LSU rDNA phylogenetic tree. For Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405, each distance was then normalized by dividing it by the patristic distance between Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 and the most distant taxon in the tree (e.g., an outgroup species).

Design of the experiments conducted in this study

For experiment 1, a dense stock culture (ca. 4,000–5,000 cells mL− 1) of Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 growing mixotrophically on H. rotundata under the same conditions described above was transferred to an 800-mL culture flask containing 0.2-µm FSW. For experiment 2, a dense stock culture (ca. 10,000 cells mL− 1) of G. smaydae growing mixotrophically on H. rotundata under the same conditions was transferred to a 270-mL culture flask containing 0.2-µm FSW. A 1-mL aliquot was taken from each culture flask, and prey cells were counted using a Sedgewick-Rafter counting chamber (SRC) every two days under a light microscope until they were no longer detectable in the water. Concurrently, Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 or G. smaydae cells were counted using the same method to determine predator cell concentration.

In experiments 1 and 2, the initial concentrations of Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405, G. smaydae, and each target algal species were established using an autopipette to dispense predetermined volumes of cultures with known cell densities into six-well plates (Table 2). The experimental concentrations were selected to achieve comparable carbon-based levels among treatments whenever possible. For each prey species, one experimental well (predator + prey), one prey control well (prey only), and one predator control well (predator only) were prepared, and the plates were incubated on a shelf under a 14:10 h L: D cycle (20 µmol photons m− 2 s− 1) at 20 ℃. After 1 and 24 h of incubation, 10–40 cells of the predator species in each experimental well were observed under a dissecting microscope at 20–63× magnification to determine whether the predator was able to feed on each target prey species. Feeding was inferred from sustained peduncle insertion into prey cells lasting more than 2 min and was further supported by observations of prey content transfer or a reduction in prey cell size in some cases.

When feeding by predator species was confirmed, a subset of predator cells was gently transferred from the experimental wells to confocal dishes (SPL100350, SPL Life Sciences Co., Ltd., Pocheon, Korea) for photographic documentation using a digital camera attached to inverted microscopes (Axiovert 200 M, Carl Zeiss; CX21, Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) at magnifications of 200–1,000×.

Growth and Ingestion Rates

Experiment 3 was designed to determine the growth and ingestion rates of Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 (Table 2). The growth and ingestion rates of Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 feeding on its optimal prey, H. rotundata, were measured as a function of prey concentration. In the preliminary test, Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 showed positive growth when fed on H. rotundata, Heterocapsa niei, P. cordatum and A. carterae among the provided algal prey species. It grew better when fed on H. rotundata than when fed on H. niei, P. cordatum and A. carterae; therefore, H. rotundata was selected as the optimal prey species for Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405.

For experiment 3, a dense stock culture (ca. 4,000 cells mL− 1) of Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 growing mixotrophically on H. rotundata under a 14:10 h L: D cycle at 20 µmol photons m− 2 s− 1 was transferred into an 800-mL culture flask containing 0.2-µm FSW. Prey elimination was monitored and predator cell concentrations were determined in the same manner as described for experiments 1 and 2. Two days after the prey cells became undetectable, the culture was used in this experiment.

The initial concentrations of Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 and H. rotundata in triplicate 40-mL experimental (Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 cells + H. rotundata), triplicate prey-control (H. rotundata only), and triplicate predator-control flasks (Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 only) were established as shown in Table 2. To standardize water conditions across treatments, filtered water from the predator or prey cultures was added to the corresponding control flasks only, following established protocols [6, 13, 23, 26, 29–30]. Five milliliters of f/2 − Si medium were added to each flask, which was then filled to capacity with freshly prepared FSW and capped. Prior to subsampling, the condition of Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 and its prey was examined under a dissecting microscope. To determine the actual predator and prey densities (cells mL− 1) at the beginning of the experiment and after 4 d of incubation (Table 2), 5-mL aliquots were removed from each flask and fixed with 5% Lugol’s solution, and all or more than 200 cells of Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 and H. rotundata in two 1-mL SRCs were counted. After sampling at 0 d, the flasks were refilled to capacity with FSW, capped, and incubated on shelves for 4 d under the same conditions described above. The dilution of the cultures associated with refilling the flasks at 0 d was taken into account when calculating growth and ingestion rates.

The specific growth rate of Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405, µ (d− 1), was determined using the equation:

[26], and that of Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 was estimated from its mean cell volume (1,610 µm3, calculated as the volume of a sphere based on the equivalent spherical diameter [ESD]) according to Menden-Deuer and Lessard [42]. Incubation times for ingestion rate measurements were identical to those used for growth rate estimations.

Swimming Speed

A culture (ca. 3,000 cells mL− 1) of Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 growing mixotrophically on H. rotundata was transferred to a 250-mL culture flask. When prey cells were no longer detectable, an aliquot from this flask was transferred to a 40-mL culture flask and allowed to acclimate for 30 min to avoid interference from water flow. The video camera was focused on a field corresponding to a single circle in the culture flask under a dissecting microscope at 20 ℃, and the swimming behavior of Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 cells was recorded at a magnification of ca. 20× using a video recording system (SRD-1673DN; Samsung Techwin, Seongnam, Korea) with a CCD camera (Sony DXC-C33, Sony co., Tokyo, Japan). The average and maximum swimming speeds were analyzed for all swimming cells observed between 10 and 40 min. The average swimming speed was calculated from the linear displacement of cells in 1 s during single-frame playback (n = 30).

Statistical Analysis

To examine the pairwise relationships among the maximum ingestion rates, maximum mixotrophic growth rates, and cell sizes of peduncle-feeding mixotrophic dinoflagellates, correlation analyses were performed. The normality of all variables was tested using the Shapiro –Wilk test, and none followed a normal distribution. Therefore, Spearman’s rank correlation analyses were conducted. In addition, a simple linear regression analysis was performed only for the relationship that was statistically significant in the Spearman correlation tests to visualize the trend. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS ver. 25 (IBM-SPSS Inc., Armonk, NY, USA), with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

General Morphology and Genetic Characterization of Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405

Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 has a conical epicone with a rounded apex that is smaller than the ellipsoid hypocone (Figs. 1a–e). This dinoflagellate has a horseshoe-shaped apical groove, one of the three diagnostic characters of the genus Gymnodinium (Figs. 1a, e). The apical groove runs anticlockwise and is displaced to the right side of the cell (Fig. 1e). A sharp, elongated ventral ridge is located above the right side of the cingulum (Fig. 1b). The length and width of living cells of Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405, after satiation with prey followed by starvation for approximately two weeks, were 12.2–19.7 μm (16.5 ± 0.36 μm) and 8.7–16.2 μm (12.2 ± 0.32 μm), respectively (n = 30). Thin sections observed by TEM showed that Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 has a nuclear chamber and the nuclear fibrous connective, two of the three diagnostic characters of the genus Gymnodinium (Figs. 1f–j).

The LSU rDNA sequence (GenBank Accession No. PX568887) of Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 was 1,445 bp long. The LSU rDNA sequence of Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 differed from that of G. smaydae by 8.86% (52 of 587 aligned positions) and an unidentified Gymnodinium sp. isolated from the radiolarian Spongotrochus glacialis by 11.96% (162 of 1,355 aligned positions), as determined from the NCBI BLAST pairwise alignments that included both mismatches and gap positions. The LSU rDNA sequence of Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 also differed by 8.46–17.71% (48–198 bp) from those of species belonging to the same major clade in the LSU rDNA phylogenetic tree (Fig. S1). The range of LSU rDNA sequence divergence among Gymnodinium species has been reported as 9.1–17.5% (51–165 bp) [34]. Therefore, Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 represents a previously unknown species that is genetically distinct from G. smaydae based on LSU rDNA sequence comparisons.

In the phylogenetic tree based on LSU rDNA sequences, Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 clustered within the large Gymnodinium sensu stricto clade and formed a basal branch in a small subclade containing G. smaydae and an unidentified Gymnodinium sp. isolated from S. glacialis (Fig. S1). Within this large Gymnodinium sensu stricto clade, Gymnodinium species were resolved into three main subclades, except for Gymnodinium impatiense and Gymnodinium corii, which were placed outside the Gymnodinium sensu stricto clade (Fig. S1). The freshwater species G. impatiense did not cluster with any Gymnodinium species, consistent with a previous study reporting that this taxon does not form a stable clade with any described Gymnodinium species [43]. G. corii clustered with Biecheleriopsis and Ansanella species, forming a distinct clade separate from the Gymnodinium sensu stricto clade; within this clade, it grouped more closely with Biecheleriopsis adriatica, consistent with a previous study suggesting that this strain is likely a misidentified clone of B. adriatica [44].

The average (± SE) and maximum swimming speeds of Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 were 235.3 ± 11.5 and 400.0 μm s− 1, respectively (n = 30).

Discussion

The results of the present study indicate that Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 is a new mixotrophic dinoflagellate capable of feeding on diverse microalgal prey and employing a peduncle-based feeding strategy. The discovery of this new peduncle-feeding mixotrophic dinoflagellate is important because the number of known peduncle-feeding mixotrophic dinoflagellates is lower than that of engulfment-feeding mixotrophic dinoflagellates [11–12, 25, 26, 29–30, 33, 45]. In addition, the present study shows that Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 requires feeding for growth, suggesting that this species is an obligate mixotroph.

Trophic Mode and Feeding Mechanism

So far, only about 3% of the approximately 3,500 formally described dinoflagellates in the taxonomic database [46] have been identified as mixotrophic [2, 8–17, 25–26, 29, 31–33, 45, 47–52]. Although many Gymnodinium species are known only as autotrophs, new mixotrophic species are being described [2, 9, 26, 31]. Among 274 Gymnodinium species currently listed in the taxonomic database [46], only about 2% have been identified as mixotrophic; only Gymnodinium aureolum, Gymnodinium catenatum, Gymnodinium corollarium, Gymnodinium impudicum, and G. smaydae have been reported as mixotrophic species [2, 9, 26, 31]. Therefore, Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 represents the sixth Gymnodinium species for which mixotrophy has been reported.

Mixotrophic dinoflagellates are known to feed on prey using two main mechanisms: direct engulfment and peduncle feeding [2, 9, 11–12, 14–15, 25–26, 29–30, 33, 45, 47, 49]. Across dinoflagellate genera, approximately 30 peduncle-feeding mixotrophic species have been reported. Of these, only two Gymnodinium species, G. aureolum and G. smaydae, are known peduncle feeders [2, 26]. The mixotrophic G. catenatum and G. impudicum are engulfment feeders, whereas the feeding mechanism of G. corollarium has not yet been identified [9, 31]. Therefore, Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 is the third reported peduncle-feeding mixotrophic species in this genus. Feeding mechanisms of mixotrophic dinoflagellates can affect the upper limit of edible prey size as well as optimal prey species [11, 21]. Although peduncle-feeding mixotrophic dinoflagellates usually feed on prey that are smaller than themselves, some can feed on prey that are larger than their own cell size [This study, 21, 23, 26]. Therefore, different feeding mechanisms may allow mixotrophic Gymnodinium species to exploit a range of prey sizes and types, thereby occupying distinct ecological niches in marine ecosystems.

The Spectrum of Edible Prey Species

Among the mixotrophic dinoflagellates, Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 can feed on one of the largest numbers of algal prey species (15 species); only three species–Karlodinium armiger (24 species), Karlodinium veneficum (17 species), and Takayama helix (16 species)–are known to feed on more prey species [13–14, 48, 53–55]. Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 has an advantage over other mixotrophic dinoflagellates due to the wide range of edible prey species (15) [This study]. Furthermore, as in K. armiger, and K. veneficum [14, 48, 53–55], the use of a peduncle to feed on algal prey could help explain the large number of edible prey species.

Among the mixotrophic Gymnodinium species, G. aureolum, G. catenatum, G. impudicum, G. smaydae, and Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 have been tested to determine which algal prey species they can feed on [2, 9, 26]. The number of edible algal prey species of Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 is much greater than that of the other tested mixotrophic Gymnodinium species, which ingest 6–8 species [2, 9, 26]. The feeding mechanism of Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 is likely to facilitate the ingestion of a wide variety of algal species, as observed in other peduncle-feeding dinoflagellates such as K. armiger and K. veneficum [14, 48, 53–55]. However, it is difficult to generalize among Gymnodinium species that peduncle-feeding mixotrophic species have higher numbers of edible algal prey species than engulfment-feeding species, because the numbers of edible algal prey species of the peduncle-feeding G. aureolum and G. smaydae are similar to those of the engulfment-feeding G. catenatum and G. impudicum. To test this hypothesis, further studies on the mixotrophic ability and edible algal prey species of Gymnodinium species are required.

The prey spectrum of Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 differs substantially from that of other mixotrophic dinoflagellates (Fig. 5) [8–9, 16, 27, 32, 47, 50–52, 56–60]. The upper size limit of edible prey species of Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 (38 μm in ESD) is greater than that of other mixotrophic Gymnodinium species, including G. smaydae, G. catenatum, G. aureolum, and G. impudicum, whose reported upper prey size limits range from 12 to 23 μm. This difference likely reflects the ability of Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 to feed on relatively large prey species, such as Alexandrium tamarense and Lingulaulax polyedra (32.6–38.2 in ESD), which are not ingested by the other Gymnodinium species (Fig. 5) [2, 9, 26]. Indeed, Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 is the first reported peduncle-feeding mixotrophic dinoflagellate that can feed on A. tamarense and L. polyedra (Fig. 5). A. tamarense and L. polyedra are known to cause HABs in the coastal waters of many countries [21, 61–64]. The strains of A. tamarense and L. polyedra used in this study produce paralytic shellfish toxins and yessotoxins, respectively [65–66]. The ability of Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 to ingest these toxin-producing prey species is consistent with potential interactions with toxic HAB species in natural environments. Prior to the present study, only a few engulfment-feeding mixotrophic dinoflagellates (e.g., Ak. sanguinea, Fragilidinium cf. mexicanum, L. polyedra, T. helix, and Takayama tasmanica) had been reported to feed on A. tamarense or L. polyedra [9, 13, 16, 32]. Therefore, the present study adds Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 to the list of predators capable of feeding on these large toxic dinoflagellates.

Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 can feed on the dinoflagellates Scrippsiella acuminata and Prorocentrum micans (Fig. 5). Prior to the present study, there had been no reported peduncle-feeding mixotrophic dinoflagellate species that were able to feed on both prey species (Fig. 5). In previous studies, feeding on S. acuminata or P. micans had been reported only for a small number of mixotrophic dinoflagellates, such as G. smaydae, K. armiger and the engulfment-feeding F. cf. mexicanum [9, 13–14, 26, 32]. Because S. acuminata and P. micans are widely distributed and occasionally cause HABs [3, 21, 67], the ability of Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 to feed on these common HAB species may enhance its survival and competitive potential in many coastal environments.

The lower size limit of the edible algal prey species of Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 (10 μm) is larger than that of other mixotrophic Gymnodinium species (e.g., G. smaydae, G. catenatum, G. aureolum, and G. impudicum), which can feed on the small prey I. galbana (5 μm) [2, 9, 26]. This difference may be because Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 cannot feed on I. galbana, whereas the other Gymnodinium species can. Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 also did not ingest T. amphioxeia and R. salina. Under careful microscopic observation, Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 did not attempt to attack I. galbana, T. amphioxeia, or R. salina at all. Therefore, Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 may not physically or chemically detect these small microalgae.

Among mixotrophic Gymnodinium species, Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 can feed only on dinoflagellate species. Its sister species, G. smaydae, feeds primarily on dinoflagellates but can also ingest I. galbana, although this occurs rarely [26]. In contrast, G. aureolum, G. catenatum, and G. impudicum can feed on prey species from different classes, including the cryptophyte, raphidophyte, and dinoflagellate [2, 9]. However, the mixotrophic growth and ingestion rates of G. aureolum are lower than those of G. smaydae and Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 (see the next subsection for details). Therefore, G. aureolum may have evolved to trade off growth and ingestion rates for broader taxonomic prey diversity. The mixotrophic growth and ingestion rates of G. catenatum and G. impudicum have not yet been reported and thus should be determined.

Ingestion Rate

Among the 42 mixotrophic dinoflagellates, the maximum ingestion rates, as measured using their respective optimal prey species, range from 0.01 to 23.17 ng C predator− 1 d− 1 (Table S2). Within this range, Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 has the 18th highest maximum ingestion rate. Furthermore, among peduncle-feeding mixotrophic dinoflagellates, the maximum ingestion rate of Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 on H. rotundata (0.74 ng C predator− 1 d− 1) is lower than those of B. resplendens, three Dinophysis species, G. smaydae, and K. armiger when fed their respective optimal prey species (1.30–1.85 ng C predator− 1 d− 1), but higher than those of nine other species (0.04–0.49 ng C predator− 1 d− 1) (Table S2; Fig. 6a). The maximum ingestion rate of Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 on H. rotundata is similar to those of Dinophysis infundibulum and Pfiesteria piscicida when fed their optimal prey species (0.72–0.73 ng C predator− 1 d− 1). The maximum ingestion rates of the peduncle-feeding mixotrophic dinoflagellates are not significantly correlated with their cell sizes (Spearman’s rho test, p > 0.1; Fig. 6a). In general, ingestion rates of mixotrophic dinoflagellates are affected by relative sizes, capture, and/or handling time [27, 32, 68]. Therefore, factors other than the sizes of the peduncle-feeding mixotrophic dinoflagellates may affect their maximum ingestion rates. For example, when H. rotundata is offered as the same prey species, the maximum ingestion rate of Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 is higher than that of any other peduncle-feeding mixotrophic dinoflagellates except for G. smaydae (Table S3; Fig. 6d). The maximum ingestion rate of Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 is half that of G. smaydae, which has a smaller size (10.5 μm in ESD) than Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 (14.4 μm). The maximum swimming speed of Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 (400 μm s− 1) is comparable to that of H. rotundata (360 μm s− 1), whereas the maximum swimming speed of G. smaydae (707 μm s− 1) is considerably higher than that of H. rotundata [16, 26]. Therefore, Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 may have more difficulty capturing small, fast prey cells than G. smaydae, which may be partially responsible for the lower ingestion rate of Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405.

Growth Rate

Among the 46 mixotrophic dinoflagellates, the maximum growth rates, as measured using their respective optimal prey species, range from 0.03 to 2.23 d− 1 (Table S2). Within this range, Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 has the 23rd highest maximum mixotrophic growth rate (Table S2). Furthermore, among peduncle-feeding mixotrophic dinoflagellates, the maximum mixotrophic growth rate of Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 on H. rotundata is higher than those of Cryptoperidiniopsis sp. (0.23 d− 1), Dinophysis acuta (0.17 d− 1), G. aureolum (0.17 d− 1), and Thecadinium kofoidii (0.13d− 1) when fed their respective optimal prey species. However, it is lower than those of 10 other species, which range from 0.72 to 2.23 d− 1 (Table S2; Fig. 6b). The maximum mixotrophic growth rate of Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 on H. rotundata is similar to those of five other species when fed their optimal prey species (0.43–0.50 d− 1). The maximum mixotrophic growth rates of peduncle-feeding mixotrophic dinoflagellates are significantly negatively affected by predator size (Spearman’s rho test, p < 0.05; Fig. 6b). However, their maximum mixotrophic growth rates are not significantly related to the maximum ingestion rates of the predators (Spearman’s rho test, p > 0.1; Fig. 6c). In general, the growth rates of mixotrophic dinoflagellates are affected by both ingestion and photosynthesis [6, 13, 48, 69]. The phagotrophic growth rate (0.54 d− 1) of Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 on H. rotundata, that is, its mixotrophic growth rate minus its autotrophic growth rate, is higher than that of K. armiger on H. rotundata (0.49 d− 1), although the maximum mixotrophic growth rate of Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 on H. rotundata (0.46 d− 1) is lower than that of K. armiger on H. rotundata (0.59 d− 1) (Table S3; Figs. 6e, f). Furthermore, the growth rates of mixotrophic dinoflagellates are also affected by the nutritional quality of prey species, swimming activity, and gross growth efficiency (GGE) [27, 29, 52]. Thus, these factors may account for the lack of a significant correlation between the maximum mixotrophic growth rates and the maximum ingestion rates of the predators.

Among the mixotrophic Gymnodinium species, the maximum mixotrophic growth rates of Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 and G. smaydae are higher than that of G. aureolum (0.17 d− 1) (Table S2). There is a significant negative relationship between the maximum mixotrophic growth rates and cell sizes of peduncle-feeding mixotrophic dinoflagellates (Spearman’s rho test, p < 0.05; Fig. 6b). The cell sizes of Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 and G. smaydae are smaller than that of G. aureolum (19.4 μm). Furthermore, the maximum ingestion rates of Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 and G. smaydae (0.74–1.59 ng C predator− 1 d− 1) are also higher than that of G. aureolum (0.06 ng C predator− 1 d− 1) (Table S2). Therefore, the combination of the smaller sizes and higher maximum ingestion rates of Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 and G. smaydae may be partially responsible for their higher maximum mixotrophic growth rates.

The predation contribution to total growth rate (PredCTGR) [21] of Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405, G. smaydae, and G. aureolum differed considerably among these species. The autotrophic growth rates of Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 and G. smaydae (− 0.08 to 0.01 d− 1) are lower than that of G. aureolum (0.12 d− 1) [2, 26]. Consequently, the PredCTGR values of Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 and G. smaydae exceed 95%, whereas that of G. aureolum is only 29%. Therefore, feeding may be a primary strategy of Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 and G. smaydae for the growth but a supplementary strategy of G. aureolum. In natural environments, Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 and G. smaydae can be abundant when their prey is also abundant, but G. aureolum can be abundant even under prey-depleted conditions. G. aureolum blooms have been widely reported despite its lower growth rate, whereas G. smaydae blooms are rarely observed despite its higher growth rate [21], leading to the hypothesis that Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 may also be rarely observed in blooms due to its similarity to G. smaydae.

Among the peduncle-feeding mixotrophic dinoflagellates that cannot grow without prey, the GGE of Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 on H. rotundata (21%) is lower than that of the mixotrophic dinoflagellates Paragymnodinium shiwhaense on A. carterae (49%), G. smaydae on H. rotundata (62%), and Shimiella gracilenta on T. amphioxeia (508%) [26, 29, 33]. However, the GGE of Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 on H. rotundata is similar to that of the heterotrophic dinoflagellates Oxyrrhis marina on B. adriatica (18%) and Effrenium voratum (20–26%), Gyrodinium jinhaense on D. salina (12–21%), and Protoperidinium bipes on Skeletonema costatum (20%) [70–73]. Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 may have low GGE because it grows only when it feeds on prey, like the heterotrophic dinoflagellates.

Feeding Behaviors of Mixotrophic Dinoflagellates as a Potential Proxy for Differentiating Sister Species

The difference in the LSU rDNA sequences between Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 and its sister species G. smaydae is 8.9%. Furthermore, based on the LSU rDNA phylogenetic tree, the normalized patristic distance between Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 and G. smaydae was 0.29, which is relatively small compared with the distances observed between Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 and other Gymnodinium species (0.26–0.70). However, the differences in the number of edible prey species, maximum ingestion rate, and maximum mixotrophic growth rate between these two species are 27, 73, and 132%, respectively. Therefore, the differences in feeding-related properties between Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 and G. smaydae are not fully reflected by their LSU rDNA divergence, suggesting that feeding-related traits, together with rDNA divergence, may serve as useful proxies for differentiating the two species. Similarly, the difference in the number of edible prey species between T. helix and its closely related species T. tasmanica, 24.1%, is much greater than that in their LSU rDNA sequences, 1.5% [13, 16]. The difference in the number of edible prey species between K. armiger and K. veneficum, 25.0%, is greater than that in their LSU rDNA sequences, 4.8% [14, 48, 53–55, 74]. Furthermore, the difference in the number of edible prey species between P. cordatum and P. donghaiense, 6.3%, is greater than that in their LSU rDNA sequences, 2.9% [9]. However, to use the feeding behaviors of mixotrophic dinoflagellates as a proxy for differentiating closely related species, the feeding behaviors of more species in other genera should be explored.

Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 can feed on many prey species (15 species) but has a relatively low growth rate (0.46 d− 1), whereas G. smaydae can feed on fewer species (8 species) but exhibit a higher growth rate (2.23 d− 1). Therefore, Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 may trade off growth and ingestion rates against the number of edible prey species. This species may have an advantage in surviving under photosynthetically unfavorable conditions by feeding on its edible prey, including common dinoflagellate species such as A. tamarense, L. polyedra, and Prorocentrum spp., which often cause blooms.

This study demonstrates that the newly described small (14.4 μm in ESD) dinoflagellate Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 is an obligate mixotroph that relies on a peduncle-based feeding mechanism to ingest a wide range of dinoflagellate prey. Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 cannot grow autotrophically, but it shows clear prey concentration-dependent growth and ingestion responses on the optimal prey H. rotundata, indicating that it can function as an important mixotrophic grazer in marine food webs despite its relatively low maximum mixotrophic growth and ingestion rates compared to its sister species G. smaydae. Although Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 and G. smaydae form a well-supported sister pair on the LSU rDNA phylogeny with only a modest sequence difference (8.9%), the differences in the number of edible prey species, maximum ingestion rate, and maximum mixotrophic growth rate between the two species are 27 to 132%. These results indicate that feeding-related traits can diverge substantially even between closely related mixotrophic dinoflagellates, suggesting that such traits may serve as a useful ecological proxy to differentiate sister species alongside traditional genetic markers.

Several small dinoflagellates around 10–15 μm in size have recently been revealed to be mixotrophic [3, 6, 9, 12, 22–23, 26–27, 29–30, 33–34, 48–49, 51–55, 75], and Gymnodinium sp. GSTY2405 and G. smaydae add to this group of functionally important taxa. In such morphologically similar species, differences in prey spectra and mixotrophic performance may help structure local plankton communities and influence the occurrence of blooms when common dinoflagellate prey are abundant. Further work examining the feeding characteristics of additional small mixotrophic dinoflagellates will help clarify how general the observed patterns are and refine our understanding of their ecological roles.
