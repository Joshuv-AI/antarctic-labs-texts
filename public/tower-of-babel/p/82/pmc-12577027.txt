Bioelectrical impedance analysis for evaluation of adverse cardiovascular events in patients with acute coronary syndromes: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Hanchi Xu, Lin Wang, Yinghui Wang, Bingbing Ran, Manli Wang, Lu Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) is a noninvasive method for assessing body fluid composition and may offer prognostic value in patients with acute coronary syndrome (ACS).MaterialsIn this longitudinal cohort study, we enrolled 467 patients with ACS to investigate the association between BIA-derived parameters and 1-year major adverse cardiac events (MACE), including all-cause mortality, reinfarction, or heart failure (HF) requiring rehospitalization. BIA focused on measuring the electrodynamic properties of intracellular and extracellular fluids.ResultsPatients who developed MACE had significantly higher impedance (Z, calculated as the vector sum of resistance [R] and reactance [Xc]) compared to those without MACE. A Z value of 417.5 was identified as the optimal cutoff for predicting MACE risk (log-rank test, p < 0.001). Using this cutoff, the sensitivity and specificity for predicting MACE were 24.8% and 91.5%, respectively, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.60. Elevated Z remained an independent predictor of MACE after adjustment for baseline confounders in both univariate (HR 2.93, 95% CI 1.98–4.35, p < 0.001) and multivariate cox regression analyses (HR 3.95, 95% CI 2.55–6.10, p < 0.001). Incorporating BIA with conventional measures such as LVEF significantly enhanced prognostic accuracy (p < 0.05). Moreover, the Z threshold allowed for refined risk stratification.ConclusionIn ACS patients with elevated troponin, Z showed potential prognostic value for MACE. Combining BIA with LVEF improved prognostic discrimination, suggesting Z may complement LVEF for more comprehensive risk stratification.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12245-025-01020-0.Keywords: Bioelectrical impedance analysis, Acute coronary syndromes, Major adverse cardiac event, Troponin, Left ventricular ejection fraction

Introduction

Acute coronary syndrome (ACS) comprises unstable coronary artery diseases, ranging from unstable angina to myocardial infarction with wall motion. It serves as an umbrella term for diseases involving sudden blockage of blood flow to the heart muscle [1]. ACS frequently manifests as the initial clinical presentation of coronary vascular disease, with most suspected ACS patients potentially progressing to acute myocardial infarction (AMI) [2].

Studies show that 5–10% of heart attack survivors die within 1 year, and nearly 30% need to be readmitted to the hospital [3]. Even with significant progress in diagnosing and treating ACS in recent decades [1], several factors are expected to increase the burden of ACS, including population growth, rising obesity rates, an aging population, and high prevalence of cardiovascular risk factors like unhealthy lifestyles and existing comorbidities [4], posing new challenges for managing prognosis [5]. Invasive coronary angiography, considered the gold standard for diagnosing ACS and guiding subsequent treatment, is limited by its specialized nature and high cost, restricting its widespread use [6]. In this context, refining a new set of noninvasive prognostic indicators with clinical value could facilitate further risk stratification of the disease, ultimately improving prognosis.

Malnutrition and related body composition changes are associated with poorer nutritional status and adverse health outcomes in various cardiovascular diseases (CVDs), including AMI, as per the European Society of Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism (ESPEN). Hence, monitoring and assessing nutritional status becomes crucial in relation to CVD risk [7]. Emerging evidence suggests that subtle changes in body water composition may occur during ACS development, potentially serving as an early indicator of poor prognosis for future ACS events [8]. Therefore, using relevant technologies to assess body composition, we can identify patients at high risk for adverse outcomes early. This early identification allows for more accurate risk stratification and personalized therapeutic strategies, ultimately improving prognosis [8, 9]. Several indirect methods, such bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA), dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry, computed tomography, and magnetic resonance imaging, can be employed for this purpose [10]. BIA stands out as a reliable, non-invasive, economically feasible option with high reproducibility, requiring minimal training compared with other techniques like dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry, hydrostatic weighing, skinfold measurements, or computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging [11–14].

BIA is a non-invasive method that evaluates body fluid status and body composition by measuring the impedance (Z) of the body to a weak alternating electrical current. Impedance Z consists of resistance (R) and reactance (Xc), which reflect the conductivity of body fluids and the functional status of cell membranes, respectively. Derived parameters such as the extracellular water to total body water ratio (ECW/TBW) can indicate fluid retention, while the phase angle reflects cellular health. These bioelectrical parameters also provide insights into overall obesity and fat distribution, aiding in the assessment of an individual’s physical and nutritional status [9].

Although extensive literature has explored the application of BIA in predicting cardiovascular events, the specific association between BIA-derived parameters and major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) in patients with ACS has not been fully investigated. Notably, patients with ACS often first present to the emergency department, where their condition may rapidly evolve and clinical heterogeneity is high. Emergency physicians are frequently required to make initial risk assessments before cardiology specialists become involved. Therefore, the use of BIA—a rapid, non-invasive, and bedside tool—at this early stage may facilitate early risk stratification and support clinical decision-making.

Accordingly, the aim of this study was to evaluate the prognostic value of BIA for predicting MACE in ACS patients with elevated troponin, and to explore its clinical utility in acute-phase risk assessment.

Methods

Study design and population

In this longitudinal, prognostic cohort study, from March 2022 and September 2023, we followed 518 adult patients admitted to the Department of Cardiovascular Medicine at the First Hospital of Jilin University. The study enrolled patients who met the following criteria: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) all patients satisfied the diagnostic criteria for ACS as outlined in the 2023 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines for the management of ACS [6]. Patients were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) presence of other severe underlying diseases or significant complications; (2) incomplete clinical data; (3) inability to adhere to regular dual antiplatelet therapy and statin treatment; (4) a documented history of heart failure (HF) or evidence of significant volume overload (e.g., marked peripheral edema or pulmonary rales involving more than 50% of the lung fields); (5) current use of diuretics; (6) undergoing renal replacement therapy (e.g., hemodialysis or peritoneal dialysis); and (7) presence of an implanted cardiac pacemaker or defibrillator. Out of the initial population, 467 patients underwent BIA measurements and had their peak cardiac troponin (cTn) levels monitored during hospitalization, and these patients were ultimately included in the final analysis. Six patients were lost to follow-up due to personal reasons (Fig. 1). Given that BIA measurements are susceptible to interference from fluid status—particularly in patients with ACS accompanied by volume overload—we applied strict exclusion criteria to eliminate individuals with evident signs of congestion in order to minimize potential bias in the BIA results.

Study flowchart. cTnI Cardiac troponin I

To investigate the relationship between BIA parameters and varying levels of cTn, patients were initially stratified into AMI and non-AMI ACS groups. The diagnosis of AMI was established based on clinical symptoms, electrocardiographic changes, and elevated troponin levels, in accordance with the ESC guidelines for ACS management. The non-AMI group comprised ACS patients who did not meet the diagnostic criteria for AMI but were considered at high risk for coronary artery disease with troponin levels < 1. This stratification aimed to further explore the potential clinical utility of BIA parameters and other baseline characteristics across different degrees of myocardial injury as reflected by troponin levels. Subsequently, to further investigate the prognostic value of BIA parameters in cardiovascular outcomes, the study population was reclassified into MACE and non-MACE groups according to the occurrence of MACE during follow-up, aiming to evaluate the potential role of BIA in predicting cardiovascular events.

Ethics statements

All participants provided written informed consent for their participation in the study, which was reviewed and approved by the Bioethics Committee of The First Hospital of Jilin University, Jilin, China. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

BIA measurement methods and principles

BIA operates under the principle of viewing the human body as a conductive cylinder. Since the electrical properties of human cells, fluids, and cell membranes differ, the Z generated by an electric current passing through the body varies. BIA measures the R and Xc of different currents using external circuits [15, 16]. R primarily reflects the electrical properties of intra- and extracellular fluids, while Xc relates to the capacitive properties of cell membranes. The equation expresses this relationship [17]. Here, R signifies the body’s resistance to alternating current, and Xc measures the charge stored in cell membranes. Using single or multiple current frequencies allows detection of conductive (fluids) and non-conductive (bone, fat) tissue components [15, 18]. This study utilized multi-electrode BIA technology. All measurements were conducted at a single site under standardized conditions of humidity (60%) and temperature (24 °C) between 9:00 AM and 2:00 PM. The same researcher operated the device (Inbody 770c, Korea) to ensure consistency. The equipment employs multi-frequency currents and a multi-electrode configuration to deliver precise body composition data. Specifically, BIA measurements partition the body into the upper limbs, lower limbs, and trunk, using various configurations such as hand-to-foot, foot-to-foot, and hand-to-hand (Fig. 2). All measurements were performed after the patients rested in the supine position for five minutes to ensure data accuracy and reliability.

Clinical endpoints and outcomes

Within one year of an ACS event, MACE were defined as all-cause mortality, recurrent MI—occurring more than 24 h after admission in patients initially diagnosed with ST-segment Elevation Myocardial Infarction (STEMI) or non-ST-segment Elevation Myocardial Infarction (NSTEMI)—or rehospitalization or outpatient visits due to worsening symptoms of acute HF. In cases where multiple MACE occurred in a single patient, events were prioritized in the following order: mortality > recurrent MI > HF. Notably, each patient was assigned only one MACE in the analysis to avoid duplication.

Data analysis

Categorical data are expressed as absolute numbers and percentages. For continuous variables, normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Variables with a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while those with a non-normal distribution were reported as median and interquartile range (IQR). Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was employed to evaluate correlations. Comparisons of continuous data sets were performed using the non-parametric Mann–Whitney U test. The optimal dichotomization cut-off value for BIA was determined using Youden’s Index. Additionally, patients with a cardiac troponin I (cTnI) level of ≥ 1µmol/L were categorized as high-risk according to established clinical practice guidelines. The Kaplan–Meier method was utilized to analyze the incidence of clinical endpoints, and log-rank tests were used to compare differences between groups. Cox proportional hazards models calculated univariate hazard ratios (HRs) for evaluating MACE and mortality. Variables with a p-value of less than 0.05 were included in multivariable regression analyses. A stepwise approach was applied in the multivariate models, using p-value thresholds of 0.05 for inclusion and 0.1 for exclusion to determine variable retention or removal.

Result

Baseline characteristics

A total of 467 patients underwent BIA within 24 h of the acute index event, with comprehensive measurements recorded. Among them, 370 were diagnosed with AMI, including 281 cases of STEMI and 89 cases of NSTEMI. An additional 97 patients with unclassified ACS subtypes were grouped under the general ACS category (Fig. 1). Six patients were excluded from further analysis because they could not be followed up. Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of the final study population. Patients with AMI were more likely to be male (79.0%) than those with ACS (67.4%). Additionally, the two groups differed in traditional cardiovascular risk factors like smoking and alcohol consumption. AMI patients had more muscle mass and lean body weight, as well as higher TBW, Intracellular Water (ICW), and ECW. However, the ECW/TCW ratio、BIA and the performance of percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) values did not differ significantly between the groups (Table 2).

MACE vs. no MACE outcomes in the study population

Among the 461 patients who completed the 1-year follow-up analysis, 133 experienced MACE (death: n = 18, reinfarction: n = 23, HF: n = 92). Patients who experienced MACE had significantly higher median brain natriuretic peptide (BNP) and troponin levels than those who did not experience MACE. The median BNP value in the MACE group was 86 pg/mL (IQR: 25.00, 208.00 pg/mL) compared with 37.5 pg/mL (IQR: 14.53, 96.20 pg/mL) in the non-MACE group. The median troponin level was higher (3.82 ng/mL; IQR: 0.16, 16.60 ng/mL) among patients who experienced MACE than among those who did not (1.06 ng/mL; IQR: 0.06, 9.22 ng/mL), as well as a lower ejection fraction (EF), a measure of heart function (51.31% ± 7.61% vs. 59.19% ± 5.45%, respectively) (Table 3).

Prognostic value of BIA

Patients with higher Z scores were significantly more likely to experience a MACE in both univariate (HR 2.93, 95% CI 1.98–4.35, p < 0.001) and multivariate (HR 3.95, 95% CI 2.55–6.10, p < 0.001) Cox regression analyses adjusted for baseline data (Table 4). Combining Z with a traditional heart function measure (left ventricular EF [LVEF]) led to even more accurate predictions of MACE risk (p < 0.01 for integrated discrimination improvement and p = 0.015 for continuous net reclassification index [NRI]).

We further analyzed the data after dividing the study population into subgroups based on the value of cTnI: ACS (cTnI < 1µmol/L) and AMI (cTnI ≥ 1µmol/L). In both subgroups, Z scores were significantly associated with MACE risk in the regression analysis (ACS group: HR 1.01, 95% CI 1.002–1.017, p = 0.014; AMI group: HR 1.008, 95% CI 1.005–1.011, p < 0.001). Next, we examined the association of Z with each specific clinical outcome (death, HF, and readmission) within the entire cohort. Z was significantly associated only with HF (HR 1.006, 95% CI 1.002–1.009, p = 0.001) but not with death (HR 0.997, 95% CI 0.989–1.005, p = 0.472) or readmission (HR 1.002, 95% CI 0.996–1.008, p = 0.491). To further explore the prognostic value of BIA parameters in HF patients with different LVEF categories, we classified patients who experienced a decline in EF during hospitalization into three groups according to current HF guidelines: HF with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF, LVEF ≤ 40%), HF with mildly reduced ejection fraction (HFmrEF, LVEF 41–49%), and HF with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF, LVEF ≥ 50%). The mean Z values were 370.73, 349.91, and 366.83 for the HFrEF, HFmrEF, and HFpEF groups, respectively, with no statistically significant differences observed among the groups (P > 0.05).

Both the optimal Z cutoff value, determined by the Youden Index, and further assessment of patients with elevated troponin (≥ 1µmol/L) allowed for additional risk stratification beyond that achieved with troponin alone (Fig. 3).

Discussion

This study evaluated the prognostic value of BIA in patients with elevated troponin levels. Our findings confirm that BIA has prognostic significance in this population, enabling further risk stratification beyond what troponin levels alone can provide, to optimize clinical management.

Traditionally, BIA has been used primarily in non-CVD populations. Limited research exists on its utility in diseases without severe nutritional decline or specific clinical conditions like HF risk factors and cardiovascular events. Early identification of individuals at risk for CVD is crucial for effective preventive care [19]. BIA offers insights into the body’s bioelectrical parameters, reflecting the efficiency of biochemical and energetic processes, potentially serving as a prognostic factor for CVD [7, 20]. Previous meta-analyses support this observation [21]. Additionally, prior studies have reported associations between body water distribution, as measured by BIA, and CVD incidence in various populations, including hemodialysis patients, those with chronic illnesses (like cancer and ischemic heart disease), and even healthy populations [7]. Our results demonstrate a strong correlation between BIA and MACE, highlighting its potential for predicting adverse events in patients with elevated troponin. Furthermore, they indirectly suggest that elevated BIA indicates an increased risk of CVD.

LVEF is a key hemodynamic factor impacting prognosis in patients with AMI. Studies have shown that reduced LVEF accurately predicts future adverse events in these patients [22, 23]. Our findings indicate that Z was significantly associated with MACE and retained its independent prognostic value after adjustment for LVEF and other potential confounders. Additionally, BIA provides an estimate of phase angle (PhA), previously linked to various diseases including CVD [7, 21, 24, 25]. Lower PhA values have been associated with a higher risk of cardiovascular events, particularly in women [7]. Therefore, BIA is not intended to replace LVEF but can serve as a complementary tool, providing additional information for comprehensive risk stratification when used in combination with LVEF. In this study, we further identified an optimal cutoff value of 417.5 for Z, which may aid in identifying patients at high risk of MACE and holds potential for integration with conventional markers such as LVEF in clinical practice to optimize individualized follow-up and intervention strategies.

In this study, although Z was significantly associated with MACE in both ACS and AMI patients, the hazard ratios were close to 1, indicating limited predictive power. This may be due to the heterogeneity of MACE components, as the role of Z varies across different outcomes. Subgroup analysis further revealed a stronger association with HF, but no significant correlation with mortality or readmission. These findings suggest that BIA may be more suitable for assessing the risk of specific cardiovascular events rather than serving as a single marker for comprehensive identification of high-risk patients. In this study, although differences in Z values were observed among HF patients with different LVEF classifications, these differences were not statistically significant. This may be related to compensatory fluid redistribution during the early phase of HF following acute coronary events. Patients with HFrEF, who have more severe cardiac dysfunction, tend to exhibit greater fluid retention and consequently higher impedance values. Due to the limited sample size in the subgroups, these findings require validation in larger cohorts. Overall, the combination of BIA parameters with LVEF may provide a more accurate assessment of prognosis in patients with ACS.

BIA is a convenient, user-friendly, cost-effective, and noninvasive tool for assessing body composition [26]. BIA utilizes electrical impedance to estimate various physiological parameters such as hydration status, somatic cell quality, and cell integrity [24]. This marker serves as a valuable prognostic tool in various clinical settings, including CVD, potentially by reflecting malnutrition [25]. From a BIA biology perspective, elevated troponin not only impacts cardiomyocyte function and heart structure, but also alters body component proportions, justifying the link between BIA and troponin elevation and enabling prognosis assessment and risk stratification. Volume overload is a key pathophysiological basis for the various symptoms and signs observed in HF patients. The gold standard for assessing volume status is the measurement of pulmonary capillary wedge pressure via catheterization [27], but its invasive nature limits its widespread clinical application. An elevated BIA fluid index, such as ECW/TBW, is an important non-invasive indicator of increased volume load and fluid retention [28]. In left HF, fluid imbalance typically manifests as pulmonary congestion, whereas in right HF, peripheral edema is more common. These abnormalities can be accurately detected through BIA. Furthermore, compared to traditional weight monitoring methods, BIA not only enables the dynamic identification of the causes of weight changes (such as increases due to fluid retention or decreases caused by changes in body composition) but also provides more precise quantitative data. This aids in the early detection of fluid imbalance, accurate assessment of volume status, and optimization of volume management strategies. This study excluded patients with a documented history of HF or evident signs of congestion—such as marked peripheral edema and pulmonary rales involving more than 50% of the lung fields—to minimize potential interference of fluid retention on BIA measurements. However, it should be noted that even in ACS patients without overt congestion, subclinical fluid abnormalities may still affect the accuracy of BIA parameters and introduce bias into prognostic assessments. As highlighted by Scicchitano et al. in their review, fluid status in patients with CVDs is a critical factor influencing both BIA parameters and their prognostic value. This impact is not only due to direct alterations in impedance values but may also result from mechanisms such as systemic inflammation, neurohormonal activation, and endothelial dysfunction [29]. Therefore, future studies may benefit from incorporating markers of congestion—such as pulmonary B-lines detected by ultrasound—to adjust BIA data and enhance their prognostic accuracy and reliability in clinical practice.

Interestingly, despite no significant pre-assessment differences in ICW and ECW between the ACS and AMI groups, Z values did not differ significantly. This suggests that cardiomyocyte injury rapidly alters the body composition ratio of intracellular and extracellular water, while other structures like muscle and fat have not yet undergone compensatory changes. While established risk factors like LVEF, troponin [30, 31], and electrocardiographic [32] variability are valuable for predicting AMI prognosis, they may have limitations such as low sensitivity, delayed response, or a requirement for long-term monitoring. In contrast, the readily available clinical risk factors identified in this study can be easily incorporated into a multistep risk stratification process, allowing for earlier identification of high-risk patients. This further emphasizes BIA’s superior sensitivity over other judgmental parameters. Docherty et al. reported broader post-MI risk factors in large clinical trials two decades ago [33], including age >70 years, heart rate ≥ 70 beats/min, smoking history, LVEF ≤ 30%, HF, and lack of reperfusion at the index MI. Compared with these, our study population is more innovative and relevant as it focuses specifically on patients with elevated troponin levels.

Although BIA cannot replace traditional prognostic tools such as echocardiography, cardiac MRI, or troponin, it offers a noninvasive, low-cost, and easily accessible method for assessing myocardial injury. When combined with echocardiography, BIA may enhance the prediction of cardiovascular complications. In clinical practice—particularly in the emergency setting before a full cardiology evaluation—BIA can assist in early risk stratification and help identify patients at high risk for MACE, thereby providing valuable prognostic information to guide clinical decision-making.

Cardiac ultrasound and myocardial nuclear magnetic resonance rely on operator experience and image acquisition accuracy. BIA, as objectively acquired data, avoids these limitations and can be a specific and highly sensitive parameter for predicting prognosis in patients with elevated troponin (a diagnostic and prognostic marker). Recent advancements in artificial intelligence suggest that machine learning algorithms can automatically classify and risk-stratify CVDs based on raw cardiac ultrasound data, with potential for even higher accuracy than cardiologists [34]. Future studies could integrate BIA data into cardiac ultrasound measurements to develop new AI-powered risk prediction models. Although the Thrombolysis In Myocardial Infarction (TIMI) and Global Registry of Acute Coronary Events (GRACE) scores are widely used for assessing the risk of ACS patients [35, 36], they primarily rely on traditional clinical variables such as age, heart rate, blood pressure, and electrocardiographic changes. In contrast, BIA provides additional prognostic information by assessing body composition, including fat mass, lean body mass, and hydration status. These body composition parameters are closely associated with ACS prognosis and can reveal risk factors not captured by traditional scoring systems. Unlike traditional scores, BIA enables dynamic monitoring of physiological changes, especially during the acute phase, and is well-suited for clinical applications in various settings due to its non-invasive and convenient nature. Furthermore, BIA measurements contribute to personalized treatment management, offering continuous prognostic assessment, particularly during long-term follow-up.

CTn is a critical marker for diagnosing acute CVDs. Several studies have shown that peak troponin levels are closely associated with the occurrence of cardiovascular events and play a significant role in clinical risk assessment [37, 38]. Therefore, although this study primarily focuses on BIA as a predictive tool, the role of cTn in assessing patient risk, particularly in ACS patients, should not be overlooked.

This study has certain limitations. Although previous studies have emphasized that Low-density Lipoprotein Cholesterol (LDL-C) levels and their dynamic changes are closely associated with cardiovascular risk, no significant differences in terminal LDL-C levels or LDL-C changes were observed between the MACE and non-MACE groups in our study (p >0.05). This result may be attributed to poor follow-up adherence and a low rate of lipid retesting after discharge, which could have led to an underestimation of the potential effects. Due to data source constraints, only one MACE event was recorded per patient during the follow-up period. This approach helps ensure the independence of events and avoids potential bias from repeated counting in risk modeling, and it is consistent with common analytical strategies for composite cardiovascular endpoints reported in previous studies [39, 40]. However, this method may underestimate the incidence of non-fatal events, such as recurrent myocardial infarction and HF, particularly in subgroups with higher mortality rates. In this study, according to the predefined event-recording criteria, no cases of reinfarction occurring after HF were observed. Future studies with larger sample sizes and more comprehensive follow-up data could consider conducting recurrent event analyses to more comprehensively assess the cumulative risk and total burden of MACE.

Conclusion

BIA may serve as a useful adjunct for predicting future MACE in patients with elevated troponin levels. At the 1-year follow-up, BIA was significantly associated with an increased risk of MACE. BIA provided significant incremental prognostic value beyond conventional LVEF assessment, and may assist clinicians in identifying high-risk patients when used alongside standard measures. Further validation in larger, multicenter cohorts is warranted before routine clinical adoption.
