A methodology for developing dermatological datasets: lessons from retrospective data collection for AI-based applications
Authors: Alma Pedro, Pamela Romero, Soledad Vidaurre, Ana M Cabanas, Atsuko Galaz, Leonel Hidalgo, Karina Carrasco, José Gerardo Tamez-Peña, Ricardo Díaz-Domínguez, Cristian Navarrete-Dechent, Domingo Mery
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe integration of artificial intelligence into dermatological research has underscored the need for robust and well-structured dermatological datasets. However, these datasets vary widely in their development processes, and there is currently no standard methodology to create such datasets. This work identifies three pressing needs for the building of dermatological datasets focus on skin tumor classification: the need for multimodal datasets, the definition of minimum metadata requirements, and the inclusion of underrepresented populations to address the scarcity of health data.MethodsWe propose a practical methodology to create dermatological datasets from clinical records, incorporating both images and patient metadata. The process consists of four key stages: getting the institutional review board approval and analysis of clinical information sources, data recording and structuring, processing of clinical data and images, and quality assessment. This methodology was derived from hands-on experience in building two datasets from Chilean and Mexican populations, respectively.ResultsThe methodology allows the creation of well-structured datasets by simplifying data organization and enabling replication. Each step includes practical guidance for dealing with typical challenges, such as image metadata categorization and technical validation by dermatologists and computer scientists.ConclusionOur contribution offers a reproducible, scalable, and interdisciplinary framework for creating dermatological datasets, especially useful for countries initiating dataset creation. In addition to the methodological proposal, we highlight common pitfalls and offer recommendations to mitigate them.Keywords: Dataset methodology, Skin cancer, Clinical metadata, Dermatology

Background

In recent years, artificial intelligence (AI) algorithms have demonstrated considerable potential in the medical domain [1]. Dermatology, in particular, has emerged as a leading area of application due to the visual nature of skin diseases and the growing availability of clinical data imaging [2, 3]. Among the most widely adopted algorithms are deep neural networks, which have shown notable performance in tasks such as skin cancer classification and lesion detection [4–8]. According to GLOBOCAN, skin cancer remains a major global health concern, with both melanoma skin cancer (MSC) and non-melanoma skin cancer (NMSC) showing rising incidence and mortality rates [9]. It has been estimated that one person dies of skin cancer every four minutes [9, 10]. Europe, Asia, Australia, and New Zealand are the regions with the highest incidence and mortality of MSC, while NMSC cases are more frequent in North America, Asia, Australia, and New Zealand [11]. Currently, skin cancer affect approximately 1 in every 5 000 individuals worldwide [12]. Dermatologists consider it a public health concern, and some even classify it as an epidemic due to the increasing incidence rates. The burden of disease is disproportionately high in regions with robust diagnostic infrastructure and reporting systems, leading to concentrated research efforts in these geographic areas [11]. These environments feature well-curated dermatological datasets, which are essential for training and validating AI models, and are predominantly derived from high-income countries [5, 7]. In contrast, low- and middle-income countries often face limitations in the development of dermatological datasets due to barriers such as limited access to dermatological care, insufficient digital infrastructure, and lack of standardized protocols [6]. This geographic imbalance restricts the generalizability of AI models and may exacerbate health inequities by excluding underrepresented populations [5, 7].

Although publicly available dermatological data sets have improved access to clinical and dermoscopic images, they often lack comprehensive metadata, and many reflect demographic biases, particularly in the representation of skin tone [10]. Most of these datasets originate from high-income settings, which reinforces structural disparities and contributes to algorithmic bias [13, 14]. This lack of inclusivity is a key factor in what is increasingly referred to as ‘health data poverty’, a challenge especially pertinent in Latin America [15]. Wen et al. highlight the urgent need for standardized metadata protocols, citing inconsistencies between datasets and a general lack of methodological transparency in dataset construction [5]. These limitations hinder reproducibility and challenge cross-cohort comparisons.

One of the efforts to standardize dermatological data comes from the international standard for medical images, DICOM (Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine). The adoption of this standard in the dermatological field has occurred in metadata encoding, image repositories, and electronic medical records. While some work has begun to implement the DICOM standard [16] successfully, it is important to highlight that its scope is limited because it has been implemented in the context of dermoscopy images. This highlights that implementing DICOM in the dermatological field is still a complex task, especially in clinical images, because these are obtained through cameras on mobile devices. This type of acquisition lacks standardization and therefore generates variations in image quality [17]. It is projected that the future adoption of the DICOM standard will allow for the easy use of AI, especially to improve interoperability issues, that is, to allow communication and integration between digital platforms and applications [18]. However, addressing ethical, regulatory, medical-legal, and labor issues is necessary to integrate the DICOM standard and AI into dermatology successfully.

The International Skin Imaging Collaboration (ISIC) remains the most prominent source of publicly accessible dermatological data [19]. However, it lacks a standardized methodology for dataset construction and has faced criticisms for issues such as duplicate cases within training and test sets [10]. These methodological gaps underscore the need for a structured, reproducible workflow that ensures data quality, diversity, and integrity, especially when such datasets underpin a diagnostic tool meant for global deployment [20]. Despite an increase in published efforts to share dataset development pipelines, few studies present clearly defined, step-by-step methodologies [15, 16, 21–24]. Notable exceptions include PAD-UFES-20, focused on the Brazilian population, which documents a process starting from clinical consultation to histopathological confirmation and secure data storage for cases involving suspected neoplasms. Metadata and clinical images are stored on a secure web server, and a final quality review is carried out for each sample [21].

The first dermatological dataset from Argentina reports metadata and images from 623 patients, describing the main phases of development: data collection, selection, preprocessing, and technical validation [15]. BCN20000 dataset from Spain similarly describe structured pipelines that include clinical data acquisition, preprocessing, and validation, comprises lesions from complex anatomical areas, including the nails and mucosa. Its creation involved several stages: image acquisition, image type separation, automated patient identification, linking images with clinical records, data registration, and technical validation [22]. Other examples include the SLICE-3D dataset [23], which integrates clinical snipetts taken from 3D total-body photography, and the DERM12345 [24], notable for its hierarchical classification system and inclusion of diverse skin types. The SIIM-ISIC 2020 Challenge dataset [16] outlines a seven-step protocol addressing image curation, duplicate detection, metadata registration, and validation. These examples illustrate the heterogeneity of methodologies used in dataset development (see Table 1) [15, 16, 21–24]. Although many include clinical oversight, workflows vary widely in terms of image acquisition, metadata documentation, and validation. The role of dermatologists is consistently emphasized across studies [15], but clinical environments often lack the infrastructure, training, or technical capacity to support robust dataset development [25]. Emerging smaller-scale datasets have helped address the information gap in certain countries across the Americas [15, 21]. These contributions are valuable for other countries in the region, beginning to engage in dermatological research.

This study aims to contribute a structured, empirically grounded methodology for dermatological dataset development. Moreover, increasing worldwide datasets to improve artificial intelligence applications, and diminishing skin cancer diagnosis disparities among different populations. Our approach is informed by two real-world case studies, one conducted in Chile (Table 2) and the other in Mexico (Table 3), and is designed to address three critical gaps observed in current public datasets: (1) The need for multimodal datasets that integrate both images and associated metadata to more accurately reflect clinical practice and support deep learning applications. Most existing public datasets are limited to image data only; (2) The absence of standardized metadata protocols, including clear guidelines on which clinical variables should be recorded, how they should be defined, and what criteria guide their inclusion; (3) the limited availability of dermatological datasets from underrepresented regions such as Central and South America, which hinders the development of inclusive, generalizable AI tools [26]. Current classification systems rely predominantly on data from Europe, North America, and Oceania [5, 18], leading to globally imbalanced data representation [27]. In response to these challenges, we present a practical methodology co-developed by experts in dermatology and computer science. Our proposed framework simplifies each step of dataset construction. In addition to the methodological framework, we identify challenges arising during real-world clinical implementation and propose mitigation strategies. The rest of the article is organized as follows: Methods section presents the proposed methodology. Results section summarizes the two case studies in which we applied the proposed methodology. Developing dermatological datasets: challenges and recommendations section shows the main challenges in creating dermatological datasets and describes practical recommendations for developing retrospective and prospective datasets. Analysis based on previous efforts to create datasets section provides an analysis based on key aspects of previous initiatives for creating dermatological datasets. Limitations section describes the limitations of the proposed methodology. Finally, Conclusion section presents the conclusions.

Methods

The methodology proposed for the development of dermatological data sets consists of four sequential stages (see Fig. 1). Each stage was applied to two modalities of information: clinical images and associated metadata. The final output was a structured, AI-ready dataset comprising de-identified images and corresponding clinical data.

Methodology for constructing a dermatological dataset from historical records. The methodology consists of four stages applied to images and associated metadata

Stage 1: get the institutional review board and analysis of clinical information sources

The first step was obtaining approval from the institutional review board, an essential requirement for legal and ethical use of data. After approval, we defined the lesion types included in the dataset. Subsequently, we analyzed the source and structure of clinical data. Records existed in physical and digital formats and typically included patient consultations, clinical notes, and images of dermatological lesions. We assessed completeness, legibility, and content of each record, focusing on three core elements: patient history, lesion characteristics, and diagnostic information [28].

In this stage, diagnostic confirmation was required. We reviewed each case based on predefined inclusion criteria: (1) a complete clinical record; and (2) diagnostic confirmation through histopathology, clinical follow-up, or expert consensus [8]. We also verified whether the data was collected under a formal technical validation protocol. Verification included documenting the original research objective for which the experts generated the data, and we identified the number and qualifications of the experts involved. The information about the number of experts involved and their roles in the diagnostic process ensures methodological transparency. We observed that clinical data and images were stored on different platforms: medical notes on institutional servers or physical files, and images on mobile devices. This separation posed challenges when linking the data. A practical solution was to match the timestamps of the medical record entries with the date and time of image acquisition.

Stage 2: data recording and structuring

To ensure privacy, all data were de-identified and stored in Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA)-complaint protected servers. A unique alphanumeric case code was assigned to each patient record, accessible only by the responsible researcher. In our implementation, a five-digit numeric format was used (e.g., case 00001). The second step was to determine the variables and the database structure. We record the cases in a tabular XLS file in the third step. The XLS file contains three sheets of information: the metadata (Fig. 2a), the description of the variables (Fig. 2b), and the coordinates of the images (Fig. 2c).

Metadata file structure. a File to record the metadata according to each clinical case. b File to describe the variables recorded in the metadata file. c File to record the identified coordinates of the images (image obtained from thispersondoesnotexist.com [29])

Metadata sheet

We named the columns according to the defined variables, and the rows correspond to each medical case. The number of variables depended on the information available in the medical records. We ordered the reported common categories as follows: identifier of the person recording the information, case identifier, diagnosis, demographic variables, variables describing the lesion, clinical history variables, and other variables (e.g., exposure to UV radiation, exposure to arsenic, family history, etc.).

Coordinate sheet

We used the sheet to organize image coordinates. Registration began with defining a format for the image name, which made it possible to differentiate the modality obtained. For example, an initial letter indicates the modality (e.g., C=clinical or D=dermoscopic), five numbers to indicate the case number, and a hyphen to separate the lesion number expressed in two digits. Thus, name C00001_01 indicates a clinical image, case number 00001, and lesion number 01. We stored all images in JPG format and organized them into folders based on case number.

After assigning the names to the images, the next step was determining their coordinates. These coordinates are necessary in dermoscopic and clinical images because they precisely delimit the region of the lesion. Dermatologists determined the coordinates using the Make Sense application [30] and recorded them in the database. The file has the following structure: image identifier, diagnosis, x-coordinates, y-coordinates, x-resolution, y-resolution, and image type.

Stage 3: processing of clinical data and images

The processing stage was applied to enhance image characteristics and metadata. We eliminated low-quality images, such as those that are blurry or have low contrast, which do not allow the identification of the content. Subsequently, the images were cropped based on the identified coordinates. The coordinates allowed a central cutout of the lesion. The next step was to anonymize the clinical images to remove personally identifiable elements, including facial features, tattoos, and visible clothing. Processing the metadata file began with identifying the variables that needed to be categorized because they had multiple values. We then defined a scale to categorize them, and attached their definitions to the variable description sheet. Once categorized, the following process was to code them. This coding allowed them to be processed by artificial intelligence algorithms.

Stage 4: quality assessment

The assessment reviewed the consistency between the image information and the metadata file. This stage involved rigorous validation of the dataset to ensure internal consistency. The following steps were derived: We examined images for duplicates, mislabeling, and incorrect formatting. Also, we corrected discrepancies in image names or class labels. For metadata review: Each record was audited to detect records with missing data in variables to complete them, eliminate duplicate cases, and correct records labeled in erroneous classes, correct file formatting errors; in the final step, when we delete an image, we also delete its corresponding metadata entry to maintain consistency. The fundamental part of the quality assessment was the thorough manual visual inspection to review the images and metadata file. In this visual inspection, the intervention of computer engineers and the expertise of dermatologists were necessary to identify errors in the database. The engineers resolved formatting errors to facilitate the use of the data in developing AI algorithms. The medical specialists corrected errors in the clinical information and improved aspects for using the data in future clinical research. Both profiles enriched the development of the database.

Developing dermatological datasets: challenges and recommendations

The development of high-quality dermatological databases involves overcoming several significant challenges arising from clinical practice constraints, technical barriers, and the intrinsic complexity of medical data. Drawing from our experience in Chile and Mexico as well as existing literature [5, 18, 31], we identify these principal issues and propose context-specific recommendations.

Complexity and Fragmentation in Data Storage. In clinical practice, especially in public health systems, dermatologists manage high patient volumes with limited time for structured documentation. As a result, information is often stored in physical records or fragmented across incompatible systems. Efforts to make records accessible through digitization frequently rely on unstructured scanned or image-based formats, which lack standardized semantics and are unsuitable for automated processing. Extracting usable data from these sources often requires error-prone OCR or NLP methods.Recommendation: Prioritize structured, machine-readable data collection at the point of care. Implement digital workflows that capture clinical and imaging data in standardized, interoperable formats. Avoid reliance on scanned forms or unstructured text; design data pipelines and forms specifically for algorithmic compatibility.

Implement digital workflows that capture clinical and imaging data in standardized, interoperable formats. Avoid reliance on scanned forms or unstructured text; design data pipelines and forms specifically for algorithmic compatibility.

Incomplete or Inconsistent Clinical Records. Ensuring consistency across records is a central challenge in database construction. Many datasets contain missing metadata, undocumented diagnoses, or incompatible variable formats [5, 31, 32]. These inconsistencies reduce the quality and reliability of the dataset, especially for machine learning applications. Errors can also occur when images and clinical records lack clear linkage, particularly when identifiers or timestamps are inconsistently recorded.Recommendation: Define essential variables prospectively, enforce strict inclusion criteria, and implement unique, consistent identifiers for both patient cases and images. Use regular auditing and reconciliation procedures to maintain robust links between records and images. Quality assurance protocols should involve both clinicians and data scientists to proactively flag and address errors or omissions.

Presence of External Artifacts and Privacy Risks. Macroscopic images often contain external artifacts unrelated to the lesion—such as anatomical structures (e.g., hands), ink markings, paper pointers, sutures, clothing, or measurement scales—that can interfere with interpretation or model training. Shadows and reflections caused by lighting may further distort lesion appearance [33]. Additionally, images may inadvertently include identifiable features such as faces, tattoos, or clothing patterns, raising significant privacy concerns, particularly in open-access datasets.Recommendation: Avoid capturing non-lesional elements (see Fig. 5), including background textures, ink, sutures, fingers, rulers, or clothing. Apply preprocessing techniques (e.g., coordinate-based cropping and artifact labeling) to remove or annotate artifacts. Ensure all images are anonymized, eliminating personally identifying information before dataset release. Exclude any cases where anonymization is not feasible, especially for open-access datasets.

Complexity in Variable Design and Interpretation. Translating nuanced clinical concepts into standardized, algorithm-ready variables is a key challenge in dermatological datasets. Metadata must be structured into meaningful columns, yet many clinical features are complex, high-dimensional, or difficult to categorize. Although some variables may be binary (e.g., presence/absence), others require carefully designed categorical schemes. Selecting relevant variables and defining them appropriately requires deep dermatological expertise.Recommendation: Assemble a multidisciplinary team, including dermatologists and computer scientists, to guide the selection and structuring of variables. Ensure expert validation to preserve clinical relevance and technical usability. Develop a comprehensive data dictionary that defines each variable and includes categorical schemes, especially for high-dimensional features. This documentation is essential to support external reuse and improve interpretability for non-clinical researchers.

Implementation scenarios: retrospective versus prospective approaches

While the methodological principles outlined in Table 4 apply universally, their practical implementation differs substantially depending on whether datasets are developed from existing clinical archives or through new data collection initiatives. Understanding these distinctions enables research teams to allocate resources appropriately and establish realistic timelines for dataset completion.

Retrospective data collection presents the advantage of immediate data availability but introduces unique complexities stemming from the heterogeneous nature of historical clinical records. Legacy datasets often reflect the documentation practices and technological constraints of their original collection period, resulting in fragmented information stored across multiple systems, inconsistent terminology, and variable image quality standards.

In our Chilean case study, for example, clinical notes existed in electronic format while images were stored separately on mobile devices, requiring careful timestamp matching to establish proper linkages. The Mexican data set, collected for nearly a decade, showed an evolution in diagnostic criteria and imaging protocols that required extensive harmonization efforts. These characteristics require intensive curation workflows, including the development of inclusion criteria that balance data completeness against sample size considerations. Research teams must also navigate complex ethical frameworks when repurposing clinical data for research, often requiring extensive de-identification procedures that can limit the depth of available metadata.

In contrast, prospective data collection offers the opportunity to establish standardized protocols from the inception of the project, fundamentally altering resource allocation and timeline considerations. Teams can pre-define essential clinical variables, establish consistent imaging protocols, and seamlessly integrate data collection into clinical workflows. Prospective datasets should be designed with machine readability in mind from the outset, using structured formats and interoperable standards to ensure compatibility with downstream analytical tools. This approach typically requires longer initial planning phases, but significantly reduces subsequent processing time. The structured nature of prospectively collected data minimizes the extensive quality assessment procedures required for retrospective datasets, though it demands ongoing monitoring to ensure protocol adherence throughout the collection period. Furthermore, prospective studies enable real-time quality assurance, allowing immediate correction of inconsistencies rather than post-hoc remediation efforts.

The choice between retrospective and prospective approaches ultimately depends on research objectives, available resources, and institutional capacity. Retrospective datasets excel in providing immediate access to diverse clinical presentations and can capture rare conditions that might require extended collection periods in prospective studies. However, they require substantial curation expertise and may limit the scope of clinical variables available for analysis. Prospective approaches offer superior data quality and consistency but require significant upfront investment in protocol development and may face challenges in recruiting sufficient cases within reasonable timeframes.

Many successful dataset development initiatives combine both approaches, using retrospective data to establish baseline collections while implementing prospective protocols to ensure ongoing data quality and completeness.

Analysis based on previous efforts to create datasets

Our methodological proposal considers key aspects of previous efforts to create dermatological datasets (see Fig. 6). These include the modality of information in the datasets, metadata reporting, diversity and representation, the integration of multidisciplinary teams, and ethical approval issues [5, 15, 18, 21, 24]. It is important to highlight that, although no standardized methodology exists [5, 23], previous research efforts demonstrate essential steps in dataset creation. Our methodological proposal considers four basic steps identified as common and essential in the reviewed works.

One of the recurring problems in AI methods is the lack of diversity in datasets [18]. Databases predominantly contain information on white skin, and only a small proportion include images of darker skin. This lack of diversity affects the performance of deep learning models and clearly highlights the information biases currently existing in dermatological databases. The proposed methodology can contribute to developing datasets to mitigate these representation-related biases. In addition, our proposal considers the creation of multimodal databases by integrating image information and patient metadata. Recent research shows that the most successful AI models integrate multimodal information [32, 34, 35]. Multimodal systems achieve a closer approximation to dermatological clinical practice, as experts consider a comprehensive patient assessment when diagnosing. Databases that integrate metadata information enable experimentation with variables related to skin cancer [36]. Determining the metadata that contributes most to skin cancer classification will be a guideline for establishing a minimum of metadata recording in a database for future research.

Limitations

While our methodology was designed to be broadly applicable to dermatological dataset creation, the validation case studies presented in this work focused specifically on skin tumors and oncological dermatology. The Chilean dataset included seven lesion categories (actinic keratosis, basal cell carcinoma, cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma, melanoma, nevus, seborrheic keratosis, and others), while the Mexican dataset focused exclusively on basal cell carcinoma. This tumor-focused validation was driven primarily by data availability and the presence of confirmed histopathological diagnoses in our retrospective clinical records.

While the four-stage framework addresses general challenges in clinical data management, its application to other dermatological domains, such as inflammatory, infectious, or genetic conditions, would require disease-specific adaptations. These may include alternative diagnostic criteria, additional metadata (e.g., severity scores, microbiological tests), and longitudinal or multimodal data capture. Further empirical validation across diverse dermatological conditions is needed to confirm the methodology’s utility beyond skin tumor applications and to identify condition-specific adaptations required. Future research should validate this framework across inflammatory diseases, infectious conditions, and rare genetic skin disorders to establish broader applicability and refine implementation guidelines for different dermatological subspecialties.

Conclusion

This article addressed the pressing need to define basic guidelines for the creation of dermatological datasets, given the lack of standardized development procedures. We proposed a practical, four-stage methodology designed to support the construction of high-quality dermatological image databases. The framework emphasizes a balance between clinical relevance and computational utility and is validated through collaboration between experts in dermatology and computer science. Its simplicity is intended to support clinicians—even in remote or resource-limited settings—in organizing medical data efficiently. A key requirement for applying this methodology is access to historical dermatological records, a condition met by many institutions with existing repositories of clinical information. This reliance on retrospective data is advantageous, yet the methodology is also adaptable to prospective data collection by defining acquisition protocols from the outset.

The two case studies presented illustrate how the methodology performs under contrasting data acquisition environments. In Chile, the dataset was constructed from structured hospital records and supported by institutional infrastructure, while in Mexico, the process relied on localized efforts with more limited documentation. The main difference between the cases lies in the scope and detail of the collected metadata. This disparity exposes a central limitation: when datasets are derived from historical records, the variables available for modeling are limited to what was originally documented. In such cases, it is not possible to introduce new metadata retrospectively, restricting the flexibility needed for model training. Furthermore, the methodology was applied only to neoplastic skin conditions; adaptation and validation for other dermatological diagnoses, such as inflammatory or infectious dermatoses, remain future work.

Additional challenges arise from the clinical environment itself, where constraints such as limited staff, regulatory compliance, and ethics approvals can impact the scope and pace of dataset development. Despite these challenges, the methodology offers a replicable and structured approach for building datasets suitable for training AI diagnostic algorithms. While initially designed for dermatology, this framework is flexible enough to be adapted to other medical imaging fields involving both visual data and structured metadata.
