Prognostic nutritional index as a prognostic marker in metastatic esophageal squamous-cell carcinoma treated with immune checkpoint inhibitor
Authors: K Yoshino, M Tamba, H Osumi, S Fukuoka, M Ogura, S Udagawa, K Shimozaki, T Wakatsuki, E Shinozaki, K Yamaguchi, K Chin, A Ooki
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe prognostic nutritional index (PNI) is an inflammation- and nutrition-based indicator that serves as a prognostic factor for various cancer types. This study aimed to evaluate the association between PNI and survival in patients with esophageal squamous-cell carcinoma (ESCC) receiving immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI)-based therapies.Materials and methodsThis single-center retrospective study included two cohorts: 109 patients treated with nivolumab monotherapy as second-line or later therapy and 92 patients receiving first-line ICI-based treatments (ICI plus chemotherapy or nivolumab plus ipilimumab).ResultsIn the nivolumab monotherapy cohort, higher PNI (PNI ≥ 40.5) was linked to longer overall survival (OS) compared with lower PNI (16.2 versus 5.5 months, P = 0.001). In the first-line cohort, 92 patients received ICI plus chemotherapy (n = 60) or nivolumab plus ipilimumab (n = 32). Higher PNI was linked to better OS in both the ICI plus chemotherapy (21.2 versus 7.7 months, P = 0.0008) and the nivolumab plus ipilimumab (not reached versus 10.2 months, P = 0.02) cohorts. Multivariate analysis identified PNI status as an independent prognostic factor in both cohorts. Dynamic changes in PNI (delta PNI ≥ 1.25) 1 month after treatment were linked to better progression-free survival in patients with lower PNI receiving nivolumab monotherapy or nivolumab plus ipilimumab but not in those receiving first-line ICI plus chemotherapy.ConclusionsPNI and its dynamic changes may serve as useful indicators of prognosis in patients with ESCC receiving ICI-based therapies.Key words: esophageal squamous-cell carcinoma, prognostic nutritional index, immune checkpoint inhibitors, nivolumab, ipilimumab

Introduction

Esophageal cancer is the seventh most common malignancy and the sixth leading cause of cancer-related death, with esophageal squamous-cell carcinoma (ESCC) comprising ∼85% of cases worldwide.1,2 ESCC is especially prevalent in Asia, accounting for over 80% of global cases. It is a highly aggressive cancer with poor prognosis, particularly in metastatic or recurrent stages. Cytotoxic agents have long been the standard for treating unresectable advanced or recurrent ESCC, though their effectiveness is limited by toxicity and low response rates.

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have transformed treatment for various cancers, including ESCC. Nivolumab, an anti-programmed cell death protein 1 (anti-PD-1) antibody, significantly improved overall survival (OS) in previously treated ESCC, as shown in the phase III ATTRACTION-3 trial.3 In first-line settings, anti-PD-1 antibodies such as nivolumab and pembrolizumab, combined with chemotherapy (ICI plus chemotherapy), have shown survival benefits in phase III trials for patients with ESCC.4,5 The CheckMate 648 trial demonstrated that nivolumab plus ipilimumab, an anti-cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein-4 antibody, prolonged OS compared with chemotherapy alone. Consequently, ICI plus chemotherapy and nivolumab plus ipilimumab are now standard first-line treatments.6, 7, 8 Despite these advances, objective response rates (ORRs) to ICI monotherapy and combinations remain modest (10%-20%).9 Current immunotherapy biomarkers in ESCC, such as programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression and tumor mutational burden, require tumor tissue and molecular analyses. While PD-L1 expression is currently used to guide patient selection for anti-PD-1 therapy, its predictive utility is challenged by tumor and immune heterogeneity and the uncertain benefit of ICIs with lower PD-L1 expression cut-offs.10, 11, 12, 13 Thus, there is a critical need for non-invasive, cost-effective biomarkers to identify patients likely to benefit from ICI therapy.

Systemic inflammatory biomarkers are increasingly recognized as prognostic indicators in various cancers.14 Circulating lymphocyte counts can reflect the body’s immune and inflammatory status.15 The prognostic nutritional index (PNI), which combines serum albumin levels and lymphocyte counts, offers insight into a patient’s nutritional and immune condition. Initially developed to predict post-operative complications in gastrointestinal surgery,16 PNI has shown prognostic value across multiple cancers.17, 18, 19 In ESCC, studies have linked PNI to treatment outcomes with ICI plus chemotherapy20,21 or ICI monotherapy.22,23 However, its association with outcomes in patients with ESCC treated with nivolumab plus ipilimumab remains unexplored. Most existing studies also focus on baseline PNI, with limited evaluation of dynamic PNI changes during treatment.24, 25, 26, 27

This study evaluated the clinical utility of baseline PNI and dynamic PNI changes [delta PNI (dPNI)] as biomarkers for prognosis in two cohorts: one receiving nivolumab monotherapy as second- or later-line therapy and the other receiving either ICI plus chemotherapy or nivolumab plus ipilimumab as first-line treatment.

Materials and methods

Study design

This retrospective, single-institution study was conducted at the Cancer Institute Hospital of the Japanese Foundation for Cancer Research. Eligible patients were aged ≥18 years, with histologically confirmed ESCC and unresectable advanced or recurrent disease. All had an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status (PS) of 0-2. Patients receiving nivolumab monotherapy as second- or later-line therapy or first-line ICI-based therapies were included. Those treated with nivolumab monotherapy between April 2019 and April 2023 or with first-line ICI-based therapy from January 2022 to July 2024 were enrolled. Exclusion criteria included nivolumab as adjuvant chemotherapy, other advanced cancers, or missing laboratory data within 4 weeks of treatment initiation.

This study involved human participants and was approved by the Certified Review Board of the Cancer Institute Hospital of the Japanese Foundation for Cancer Research [Institutional Review Board (IRB)]. The study was approved by the IRB of the Japanese Foundation for Cancer Research (registration number: 2023-GB-159) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was not required owing to the opt-out procedure described on the hospital website.

Treatment

This retrospective study included patients who had initiated ICI-based treatments in clinical practice. In the nivolumab monotherapy cohort, patients received 240 mg every 2 weeks or 480 mg every 4 weeks. In the nivolumab plus ipilimumab group, nivolumab was given at 240 mg every 2 weeks or 360 mg every 3 weeks, combined with intravenous ipilimumab at 1 mg/kg every 6 weeks. Among the first-line ICI-based treatment cohort, in the ICI plus chemotherapy group, patients received intravenous pembrolizumab at 200 mg every 3 weeks or 400 mg every 6 weeks or nivolumab at 240 mg every 2 weeks or 480 mg every 4 weeks with fluorouracil and cisplatin. Chemotherapy included intravenous cisplatin at 80 mg/m2 every 3-4 weeks and continuous fluorouracil at 800 mg/m2/day on days 1-5 of each 3- to 4-week cycle. These treatment regimens were determined by the treating physicians in accordance with clinical guidelines and were continued until disease progression, unacceptable adverse events (AEs), or patient withdrawal. ICI-based therapy was considered the first-line treatment for residual or recurrent disease after chemoradiotherapy.

Assessment

Patients were included in the study if their total lymphocyte count and serum albumin level were available within 4 weeks before treatment initiation. PNI was calculated as 10 × serum albumin (g/dl) + 0.005 × total lymphocyte count (/mm3). Patient characteristics, AEs, and clinical outcomes were extracted from electronic medical records. Clinicopathological data included age, sex, metastatic sites, number of metastatic organs, primary resection, and history of esophagectomy or radiotherapy. Hematological and non-hematological toxicities during ICI-based therapy were assessed using the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for AEs (version 5.0). Treatment response was evaluated per RECIST version 1.1 in patients with at least one measurable lesion. ORR was defined as the proportion of patients with complete or partial response (CR or PR). Disease control rate (DCR) was defined as the proportion of patients achieving CR, PR, or stable disease. PD-L1 expression was assessed using the Dako 28-8 pharmDx assay (manufactured by Agilent Technologies, Carpinteria, CA), and it was approved as a complementary diagnostic. Tumor proportion score (TPS) was calculated as the percentage of viable tumor cells showing partial or complete PD-L1 membrane staining relative to all viable tumor cells.

Statistical analysis

This study evaluated the correlation between PNI and OS, progression-free survival (PFS), duration of response (DOR), ORR, and safety. It also assessed the association between dPNI at 1 month after treatment, and OS, PFS, DOR, ORR, and safety. Cut-off values for PNI and dPNI were determined using time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves in the nivolumab monotherapy cohort, with prediction time set to median OS, to distinguish high versus low PNI. Median follow-up was defined as the time from treatment initiation to the last follow-up. OS was the time from treatment start to death or last follow-up. Patients who were alive at the end of the follow-up period or were lost to follow-up were censored at the date of last confirmed survival. PFS was the time from treatment start to disease progression or death. DOR was defined as the period from first objective response (CR or PR) to progression or death. Survival curves were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and correlations were tested using the log-rank test. Statistical significance was set at two-sided P < 0.05. Cox proportional hazards analysis included variables with P < 0.05 from univariate analysis in the multivariate model, providing adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Summary statistics were reported as frequencies and proportions for categorical variables and medians with ranges for continuous variables. Fisher’s exact test was used for categorical comparisons. Analyses were carried out using EZR version 1.55 (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University, Saitama-shi, Saitama, Japan), based on R and R Commander.28

A time-dependent ROC curve was used to identify the optimal PNI cut-off for stratifying OS. A threshold of 40.5 was selected to separate high (PNI ≥ 40.5) and low (PNI < 40.5) PNI groups (Supplementary Figure S2A, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2025.100222). Of 109 patients, 56 (51%) had higher PNI. Compared with the low PNI group, the high PNI group had significantly more ECOG PS 0 and fewer ECOG PS 2 patients (Table 1), with no other notable differences. Median PFS was 4.6 months (95% CI 2.4-5.8 months) in the high PNI group, significantly longer than 1.9 months (95% CI 1.3-2.8 months, P = 0.03) in the low PNI group (Figure 1A). Similarly, median OS was longer in the high PNI group at 16.2 months (95% CI 9.8-22.4 months) versus 5.5 months (95% CI 2.9-7.9 months, P = 0.001) in the low PNI group (Figure 1B). Among 104 patients with measurable lesions (52 in each group), ORR was similar: 31% in the high PNI group and 27% in the low PNI group (Supplementary Table S1A, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2025.100222). The high PNI group had a higher DCR (62% versus 35%). Conversely, more patients in the low PNI group showed PD at initial evaluation (54% versus 39%). Median DOR was longer in the high PNI group at 15.5 months [95% CI 4.2 months-not reached (NR)] compared with 5.8 months (95% CI 3.0-8.8 months, P = 0.02; Figure 1C). In multivariable Cox analysis, PNI status was independently associated with PFS (HR 0.64, 95% CI 0.42-0.97, P = 0.035; Supplementary Table S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2025.100222) and OS (HR 0.57, 95% CI 0.35-0.94, P = 0.0028; Table 2).

Next, we evaluated the association between dynamic PNI changes after treatment in the low PNI group and clinical outcomes. dPNI was defined as the change in PNI 1 month after treatment, with the cut-off determined using time-dependent ROC analysis, as with baseline PNI. A threshold of 1.25 was selected (Supplementary Figures S2B and S3, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2025.100222). Patients who experienced disease progression or death within 1 month after the initiation of treatment, as well as those who did not have a follow-up laboratory assessment of serum albumin and lymphocyte count at the 1-month time point, were excluded from the analysis. Among low PNI patients, 18 with dPNI ≥ 1.25 had better PFS (median 5.5 months, 95% CI 1.9-9.6 months) and OS (median NR, 95% CI 5.1 months-NR) than 27 patients with dPNI < 1.25 (PFS 1.9 months, 95% CI 0.9-2.4 months, P = 0.005; OS 5.5 months, 95% CI 2.2-7.3 months, P = 0.002; Figure 2A and B). Improvement in PFS and OS in low PNI patients with dPNI ≥ 1.25 were comparable to high PNI patients, with 1-year OS rates of 60% versus 56% (Figures 1 and 2). The PD rate at initial evaluation was 39% in low PNI patients with dPNI ≥ 1.25, the same as in the high PNI group. In contrast, PD occurred in 67% of low PNI patients with dPNI < 1.25.

First-line ICI-based treatment cohort

Baseline characteristics of the 92 patients are shown in Table 3. Median age was 66 years (range 42-88 years), and 28% had prior esophagectomy. ECOG PS was 0 in 59%, 1 in 35%, and 2 in 6%. Sixty patients (65%) received ICI plus chemotherapy, and 32 (35%) received nivolumab plus ipilimumab. PD-L1 TPS was assessed in 55 patients: 35 (64%) had TPS < 1% and 20 (36%) had TPS ≥ 1%. At a median follow-up of 12.6 months, median PFS and OS in the ICI plus chemotherapy cohort were 7.0 months (95% CI 3.9-8.1 months) and 12.8 months (95% CI 8.8-21.2 months), respectively (Supplementary Figure S4, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2025.100222). In the nivolumab plus ipilimumab group, median PFS and OS were 3.5 months (95% CI 2.2-4.9 months) and 13.5 months (95% CI 7.2 months-NR), respectively.

A time-dependent ROC curve was used to identify the optimal PNI cut-off for stratifying OS. A threshold of 41.0 was selected to separate high (PNI ≥ 41.0) and low (PNI < 41.0) PNI groups (Supplementary Figure S2C, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2025.100222). Similarly, dPNI was detected with a cut-off of −3.00 (Supplementary Figure S2D, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2025.100222). Higher PNI was observed in 37 (62%) of 60 patients in the ICI plus chemotherapy group and in 19 (59%) of 32 patients in the nivolumab plus ipilimumab group (Supplementary Figure S5, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2025.100222). No significant differences in ECOG PS or PD-L1 TPS were found between high and low PNI groups in either treatment arm (Table 3). In the ICI plus chemotherapy group, the high PNI group showed a trend toward longer PFS than the low PNI group [median 7.9 months (95% CI 4.2-9.5 months) versus 3.9 months (95% CI 2.7-8.1 months), P = 0.09; Figure 3A], though not statistically significant. Median OS was significantly longer in the high PNI group [NR (95% CI 11.4 months-NR) versus 7.7 months (95% CI 5.1-11.2 months), P = 0.0004; Figure 3B]. In the nivolumab plus ipilimumab group, high PNI was associated with significantly longer PFS [median 4.4 months (95% CI 2.4 months-NR) versus 1.3 months (95% CI 0.5-4.7 months), P = 0.009] and OS [median NR (95% CI 16.9 months-NR) versus 7.2 months (95% CI 1.1-13.5 months), P = 0.004; Figure 3C and D]. Among 80 patients with measurable lesions (60 ICI plus chemotherapy, 20 nivolumab plus ipilimumab), the PD rate was over twice as high in the low PNI group across both cohorts (Supplementary Table S1C and E, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2025.100222). DOR tended to be longer in the high PNI group in both cohorts (Supplementary Figure S6, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2025.100222). PNI was independently associated with PFS (HR 0.60, 95% CI 0.37-0.97, P = 0.04; Supplementary Table S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2025.100222) and OS (HR 0.33, 95% CI 0.18-0.61, P = 0.0004; Table 2). Neither treatment regimen nor PD-L1 TPS significantly affected PFS or OS.

In the nivolumab plus ipilimumab group, patients with dPNI ≥ −3.00 had significantly better PFS than those with dPNI < −3.00 [median 3.6 months (95% CI 0.9 months-NR) versus 0.9 month (95% CI 0.5-5.1 months), P = 0.02; Figure 4C], but OS did not differ [13.1 months (95% CI 3.4 months-NR) versus 1.8 months (95% CI 1.0 months-NR), P = 0.77; Figure 4D]. The PD rate at initial evaluation in low PNI patients with dPNI ≥ −3 improved by 20%, matching the high PNI group (Supplementary Table S1F, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2025.100222). In contrast, PD occurred in 100% of low PNI patients with dPNI < −3.

Safety

Treatment-related AEs with potential immunological causes are summarized in Supplementary Table S3, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2025.100222. In the nivolumab monotherapy cohort, 17% experienced AEs of any grade. Pneumonia was the most common AE in both high and low PNI groups. Grade ≥ 3 AEs occurred in 9% of high PNI patients. In the first-line ICI-based treatment cohort, immune-related AEs were more frequent in the nivolumab plus ipilimumab group than in the ICI plus chemotherapy group across both PNI status. Grade ≥ 3 AEs occurred in 25% with nivolumab plus ipilimumab and in 8% with ICI plus chemotherapy. AE frequency was not affected by PNI status.

Discontinuation rates owing to immune-related AEs did not significantly differ by PNI group (Supplementary Table S4, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2025.100222). In the nivolumab monotherapy cohort, 15 (29%) of 52 low PNI patients who discontinued treatment received subsequent chemotherapy versus 29 (57%) of 51 in the high PNI group (Supplementary Table S5, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2025.100222). In the first-line ICI-based treatment cohort, fewer patients in the low PNI group initiated subsequent treatment than in the high PNI group (40% versus 62% for ICI plus chemotherapy, 46% versus 83% for nivolumab plus ipilimumab).

Discussion

This study evaluated the clinical relevance of PNI, an inflammation- and nutrition-based marker, and its dynamics by assessing associations with clinicopathological features, prognosis, treatment outcomes, subsequent treatment induction, and safety in two ESCC cohorts: 109 patients receiving nivolumab monotherapy as second- or later-line therapy and 92 patients receiving first-line ICI-based treatment, including ICI plus chemotherapy or nivolumab plus ipilimumab.

The prognostic role of PNI in ICI-based ESCC therapy, particularly with nivolumab plus ipilimumab, remains unclear.20, 21, 22, 23 In multivariate analysis, PNI was independently associated with OS in both treatment cohorts, while treatment regimen and PD-L1 status were not. PNI’s impact likely reflects both nutritional and immune status—critical factors in tumor progression and ICI efficacy.29,30 Other inflammatory or nutritional indices such as neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio,8,26, 27, 28 modified Glasgow prognostic score,22,23,31 and lung immune prognostic index32 were also examined (Supplementary Table S6, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2025.100222), but only PNI remained significant in multivariate analysis. Although previous studies used standard ROC analysis to determine PNI cut-offs (range 35.9-52.4),8,26, 27, 28 this study employed time-dependent ROC incorporating survival data.33 The derived cut-offs align with past findings, supporting its reproducibility. PNI may, therefore, be a useful prognostic marker in patients with ESCC receiving ICI-based therapy.

In the nivolumab monotherapy cohort, median PFS, OS, and DOR were 2.5, 9.5, and 8.8 months, respectively—comparable to the ATTRACTION-3 trial (1.7, 10.9, and 6.9 months).3 Thus, ICIs benefit only a subset of patients with ESCC.9 In this study, high PNI was associated with longer PFS (4.6 months), more durable response (DOR 15.5 months), and improved OS (16.2 months), exceeding ATTRACTION-3 outcomes. These findings suggest that high baseline PNI may identify patients who are more likely to benefit from nivolumab. In contrast, low PNI patients had poor outcomes: median PFS of 1.9 months, OS of 5.5 months, and a PD rate of 54%. These results align with prior studies linking low PNI to poor prognosis. Low PNI patients also had fewer ECOG PS 0 and more ECOG PS 2. A decline in the general condition likely hindered therapy continuation—only 29% received subsequent chemotherapy versus 57% with high PNI. Notably, dPNI ≥ 1.25 during treatment was linked to a better prognosis, even in low PNI patients, with PFS, OS, and PD rates (39%) comparable to high PNI cases (Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2025.100222). These results highlight PNI’s utility and its dynamic monitoring as a simple, non-invasive tool for estimating prognosis and informing decisions. For low PNI patients without dPNI ≥ 1.25 at 1 month after treatment, early care planning and goals-of-care discussions may be warranted.

As first-line ICI-based therapies are now the standard for ESCC,6, 7, 8 nivolumab monotherapy as second- or later-line treatment is expected to decline in clinical use. Therefore, PNI was evaluated in a first-line cohort to assess the applicability of findings from the monotherapy cohort. In both ICI plus chemotherapy and nivolumab plus ipilimumab groups, high PNI was associated with longer PFS, OS, and more durable responses than low PNI. Notably, low PNI patients had a high PD rate (62%) at initial evaluation with nivolumab plus ipilimumab. These findings support PNI’s utility in first-line ICI-based therapies. However, dPNI’s clinical value varied by treatment. In the ICI plus chemotherapy group, dPNI failed to stratify PFS in low PNI patients. Chemotherapy often causes lymphopenia and hypoalbuminemia—effects that may dilute dPNI values compared with chemotherapy-free regimens. In contrast, in the nivolumab plus ipilimumab group, low PNI patients with dPNI ≥ −3.00 showed PFS and PD rates similar to high PNI patients, consistent with nivolumab monotherapy results. Still, dPNI was not associated with OS. Most first-line patients had ECOG PS 0 regardless of PNI, and those with dPNI < −3.00 had higher subsequent treatment rates than those with dPNI ≥ −3.00. This may explain the lack of OS difference by dPNI in low PNI patients, underscoring the importance of close monitoring and early imaging to initiate timely therapy.

Certain limitations of this study should be noted. Firstly, this was a single-center retrospective study at a cancer-specialized hospital in Japan, which may have introduced selection bias and limited generalizability. The retrospective and exploratory design also restricted result interpretation. Regarding safety, the incidence of AEs was relatively low, and mild AEs may have been underestimated. However, grade ≥ 3 AEs were not associated with PNI, suggesting that ICI-based therapies may be safely administered even in patients with low PNI. Secondly, although PNI and its dynamics were assessed, the mechanisms underlying its influence on immunotherapy outcomes remain unclear. Further studies are needed to clarify the biological basis, particularly systemic inflammation and immune function. Thirdly, the small sample size limited the statistical power. Fourthly, substantial PD-L1 data were missing in the nivolumab monotherapy group. However, ATTRACTION-3 exploratory analysis found that PD-L1 status was not a strict predictor of nivolumab efficacy. Accordingly, nivolumab is approved in Japan for second- or later-line use regardless of PD-L1 status. These limitations highlight the need for prospective validation studies. Despite this, our findings provide meaningful insight into PNI’s utility in ICI-based therapies.

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that PNI is a significant prognostic factor in patients with ESCC treated with ICI-based therapies. PNI and its dynamics may serve as simple and clinically informative tools for prognostication and aid in optimizing the management of patients with ESCC.
