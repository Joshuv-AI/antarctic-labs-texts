Spatial assessment of Water, Sanitation, and Hygiene (WASH) infrastructure, groundwater quality, and their association with childhood diarrhoeal risk in India
Authors: Aaina Murziya, Niharika Tripathi, Praveen Kumar Pathak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDiarrhoea has become a leading cause of mortality among young children, especially in low- and middle-income countries. Despite significant developments in Water, Sanitation, and Hygiene (WASH) in recent years, regional disparities persist. This research aims to examine the spatial heterogeneities in the prevalence of diarrhoea across districts in India and explore how unimproved WASH facilities and untreated groundwater quality contribute to diarrhoeal diseases using data from the fifth round of the National Family Health Survey [1] and Central Ground Water Board (CGWB), Government of India, for the corresponding period.MethodsThe spatial clustering of diarrhoea was examined using univariate local indicator of spatial association (LISA) and bivariate LISA maps, and Moran’s I statistics. Finally, Ordinary Least Squares Regression (OLS), Spatial Lag Model (SLM), and Spatial Error Model (SEM) were fitted to examine the associations between WASH, groundwater quality, and the risk of diarrhoea. Indeed, spatial regression models revealed significant spatial autocorrelation (SEM λ = 0.560, p < 0.001; SLM ρ = 0.522) and better-fitting models than OLS. The lack of improved sanitation and hygiene was significantly associated with an increased risk of diarrhoea, even after adjusting for spatial autocorrelation.ResultsThe study revealed that more than 75% of children under five years in India reside in households with unimproved WASH facilities. About 7.3% of children under five suffered diarrhoea, with relatively higher prevalence among households lacking improved sanitation and hygiene facilities. Furthermore, about 25% of Indian districts had groundwater quality unsuitable for drinking (highlighting spatial variation across districts/states). LISA maps and Moran’s I (0.274, p < 0.01) identified spatial clustering of diarrhoea, with hotspots predominantly located in the eastern and central parts of India.ConclusionsThe findings from our study confirmed that the spatial pattern of diarrhoea was associated with groundwater contamination, particularly in areas with elevated nitrate and total dissolved solids (TDS). Therefore, policymakers are encouraged to make concerted efforts to improve access to quality WASH facilities and enhance groundwater quality to reduce the burden of diarrhoea among children, particularly in India's high-cluster districts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28441-7.

Introduction

Childhood diarrhoea remains one of the most persistent public health challenges globally and continues to be a leading cause of morbidity and mortality among children under five years of age. Despite substantial progress in reducing child mortality, diarrhoeal diseases still account for nearly 9% of under-five deaths worldwide, with the burden disproportionately concentrated in low- and middle-income countries, particularly in South Asia and sub-Saharan Africa [2–4]. In India, diarrhoea continues to contribute significantly to child mortality, reflecting persistent structural inequalities in access to safe water, sanitation, and healthcare [5].

A substantial body of literature identifies inadequate water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) conditions as primary drivers of diarrhoeal diseases [6, 7]. However, emerging evidence suggests that improvements in WASH infrastructure alone do not consistently translate into reductions in diarrhoeal burden, particularly when implemented in isolation [8, 9]. For instance, access to “improved” water sources does not necessarily ensure microbiological safety, as contamination may occur during collection, storage, or distribution [6, 7]. Similarly, sanitation interventions without sustained behavioural change often fail to interrupt faecal–oral transmission pathways [10]. These findings highlight that diarrhoeal risk is shaped by complex interactions between infrastructural, environmental, and behavioural factors rather than by individual WASH components alone, consistent with child survival frameworks [11].

Within this broader context, groundwater quality represents a critical yet underexplored dimension of diarrhoeal risk, particularly in countries like India where a large proportion of the rural population depends on groundwater for drinking purposes [12, 13]. Indian aquifers are increasingly affected by both geogenic and anthropogenic contaminants, including nitrates, fluoride, arsenic, and microbial pathogens [14, 15]. Exposure to contaminated groundwater has been associated with gastrointestinal illness and diarrhoeal morbidity among children [16, 17]. However, most large-scale epidemiological studies have either overlooked groundwater quality or treated water access as a homogeneous indicator, thereby underestimating environmental exposure risks.

Importantly, groundwater contamination interacts closely with WASH conditions. Poor sanitation practices, such as open defecation and inadequate waste management, can lead to faecal seepage into shallow aquifers, thereby contaminating drinking water sources [18, 19]. At the household level, unsafe water storage, inadequate hygiene practices, and delays in water collection further mediate exposure pathways [20]. These interactions suggest the need for a more integrated conceptualisation of environmental and household-level determinants of diarrhoeal risk.

This study adopts a structured conceptual framework that distinguishes between household-level WASH conditions and environmental groundwater quality as related but analytically distinct determinants of childhood diarrhoea. WASH variables, including access to drinking water, sanitation facilities, and hygiene practices, represent proximate exposures operating at the household level. In contrast, groundwater quality is treated as an underlying environmental exposure that influences the safety of drinking water sources [11, 19, 21, 22].

Figure 2 presents the conceptual framework guiding this study. Groundwater quality indicators and WASH factors are conceptualised as the primary exposure domains associated with childhood diarrhoeal prevalence. Groundwater quality reflects potential exposure to unsafe drinking water, while WASH factors represent household conditions related to water access, sanitation facilities, and hygiene practices that influence the transmission of pathogens responsible for diarrhoeal diseases. Childhood diarrhoea is considered the outcome variable. Demographic, socioeconomic, and behavioural factors are included as covariates, as they may influence both exposure conditions and diarrhoeal outcomes. The framework illustrates the hypothesised pathways linking groundwater quality and WASH factors to childhood diarrhoeal prevalence while accounting for relevant covariates [23].

Within this framework, groundwater quality is not conceptualised as a component of WASH infrastructure but as a complementary environmental determinant that interacts with WASH conditions to shape diarrhoeal risk. This distinction allows for a more nuanced understanding of how environmental contamination and household-level practices jointly influence child health outcomes.

Another limitation in existing research is the insufficient attention to spatial heterogeneity in diarrhoeal prevalence. Evidence suggests that health outcomes are geographically clustered due to shared environmental, infrastructural, and socio-economic characteristics [24, 25]. While some studies in India have examined district-level variations using spatial techniques [25, 26], they have largely focused on WASH indicators without incorporating groundwater quality or fully capturing localised spatial relationships.

Against this backdrop, the present study addresses these gaps by integrating groundwater quality, WASH indicators, and socio-demographic factors within a unified analytical framework. Using district-level data aligned with the National Family Health Survey (NFHS-5) period and multi-seasonal groundwater data from the Central Ground Water Board [27], the study applies both conventional regression and spatial analytical techniques, including Local Indicators of Spatial Association (LISA), spatial regression models, to examine the determinants and spatial distribution of childhood diarrhoea in India.

Data and methods

The data for this study were obtained from the National Family Health Survey (NFHS-5), conducted between 2019 and 2021. The survey follows a multistage stratified sampling design to produce nationally representative estimates across states, districts, and urban–rural areas. In rural areas, villages or Primary Sampling Units (PSUs) were selected using Probability Proportional to Size (PPS), followed by systematic sampling of households. In urban areas, a three-stage design was adopted involving the selection of wards (PPS), Census Enumeration Blocks (CEBs), and households [28–30]. The survey provides detailed household-level information on WASH indicators, including sources of drinking water, sanitation facilities, and hygiene practices. To complement the NFHS-5 data, groundwater quality data were obtained from the Central Ground Water Board (CGWB), Ministry of Jal Shakti, Government of India for the period 2019–2021 [27]. The CGWB dataset comprises observations from 29,941 monitoring locations across India, collected across multiple seasons (pre-monsoon, monsoon, and post-monsoon). These observations include key physicochemical parameters such as pH, nitrate, fluoride, total dissolved solids (TDS), and electrical conductivity [27].

The CGWB dataset represents one of the most extensive nationally representative groundwater quality monitoring systems in India, covering multiple hydrogeological regions and districts across the country. Groundwater quality parameters as included in this study comprise pH, nitrate, fluoride, total dissolved solids (TDS), electrical conductivity (EC), calcium, magnesium, sulphate, and chloride. Among these, certain parameters, particularly nitrate and TDS, are more directly associated with water contamination and potential health risks, including gastrointestinal illness [16]. Other indicators, such as calcium and magnesium, are not directly linked to diarrhoeal infection but were included to capture the broader hydrochemical profile of groundwater and underlying geogenic and anthropogenic processes affecting water quality. Similarly, fluoride and arsenic were interpreted primarily as indicators of groundwater quality deterioration and environmental contamination rather than direct causes of diarrhoeal disease [15, 31]. In the analysis and interpretation, greater emphasis was placed on parameters with established relevance to drinking water contamination and environmental health risks, while the role of other chemical indicators was interpreted cautiously. To ensure temporal comparability with NFHS-5, groundwater observations were first pooled across seasons and years (2019–2021). Subsequently, all available monitoring points within each district were aggregated to generate district-level estimates. Specifically, for each groundwater parameter, a simple arithmetic mean was calculated by averaging values across all sampling locations, seasons, and years within a given district. No weighting scheme was applied due to the absence of information on sampling intensity or aquifer representation across locations.

The resulting district-level groundwater indicators were then merged with NFHS-5 data using district identifiers. District boundary maps/shapefiles used for spatial aggregation and visualisation were based on the [32] Administrative Atlas (censusindia.gov.in). Although NFHS-5 cluster-level geospatial data are available with geographic displacement limits, the present study adopted a district-level ecological linkage approach to maintain consistency in the analytical design and unit of analysis between NFHS-5 and CGWB datasets. This approach ensures consistency with the unit of analysis used in the study and aligns with district-level policy planning in India.

It is important to note that groundwater quality can exhibit substantial intra-district variability, particularly for contaminants such as nitrate and fluoride. While the use of district-level averages provides a standardized and policy-relevant measure of exposure, it may mask localized heterogeneity within districts. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as reflecting average district-level exposure rather than individual-level groundwater conditions. Another limitation of the study is the absence of microbiological groundwater quality indicators in the CGWB dataset. Consequently, the analysis relied on physicochemical parameters as proxy indicators of groundwater contamination and drinking water quality conditions.

NFHS-5 data were available for 707 districts; however, groundwater quality data were available for only 614 districts. Districts from states and union territories such as Sikkim, Manipur, Arunachal Pradesh, Mizoram, Nagaland, Ladakh, and Lakshadweep were excluded due to the absence of groundwater data. The final analytical sample was therefore restricted to districts with complete information on both NFHS-5 indicators and groundwater quality.

Groundwater safety was assessed as a key variable using a composite classification based on contamination levels, following the approach suggested by [33, 34]. The groundwater quality index was derived from multiple physicochemical parameters, including pH, electrical conductivity (EC), chloride (Cl⁻), sulphate (SO₄2⁻), nitrate (NO₃⁻), total hardness (TH), calcium (Ca2⁺), magnesium (Mg2⁺), and fluoride (F⁻). Each parameter was evaluated against permissible limits defined by the Bureau of Indian Standards (BIS), and a composite groundwater quality score was computed at the district level. Based on this score, groundwater quality was categorised into five levels: excellent (0–25), good (25–50), poor (50–75), very poor (75–100), and unfit for consumption (> 100).

For example, a district exhibiting elevated nitrate (> 45 mg/L), high total dissolved solids or electrical conductivity, and excessive hardness levels would receive a higher composite score and be classified as “poor” or “very poor,” indicating deteriorating water quality. In contrast, districts where most parameters remain within BIS permissible limits would fall under the “excellent” or “good” categories.

Analytical strategy

The data that were used in this study were analysed in Stata version 17.0. Descriptive statistics and cross-tabulations were used to present the pattern of diarrhoea across selected demographic, socioeconomic, WASH facilities, and groundwater quality parameters in India. Furthermore, the Chi-square (χ2) test was used to examine the statistically significant differences in the burden of diarrhoea across selected explanatory variables, including WASH indicators, groundwater quality, socio-demographic characteristics, child care-related habits, behavioural variables and sanitation in the environment, a was employed [38, 39]. We used a multiple linear regression model to examine the association between WASH facilities, groundwater quality, and demographic and socioeconomic characteristics, and the risk of diarrhoea among children in India. The study considered a p-value less than 0.05 to be statistically significant and used a 95% CI to assess the accuracy of the estimates [40].

Spatial statistics

To examine the spatial distribution of childhood diarrhoea across Indian districts, both bivariate and multivariate regression analyses were used, with the spatial aspect of the data analysed using GeoDa software [41]. The study applied univariate and bivariate Moran’s I statistics, along with LISA, to capture spatial clustering patterns of diarrhoea among Indian districts. These methods assess the extent to which values in one district are similar to those in neighbouring areas, helping to identify spatial clusters and outliers. Moran’s I was used to quantify the intensity of spatial clustering, with Z-scores indicating the strength of this clustering at varying spatial distances [42]. A higher absolute Z-score signifies more pronounced clustering, particularly as geographic distance increases. LISA statistics enabled the identification of areas with higher concentrations of childhood diarrhoea, particularly associated with inadequate WASH conditions and poor groundwater quality [43, 44]. The LISA analysis provided a localised view of clustering by comparing each district’s value to its neighbours and calculating the statistical significance (p-values) for each observation. LISA cluster maps were developed to visualise spatial patterns and highlight areas of statistically significant clustering. These included: High-High clusters (hot spots), where similar districts surround districts with high diarrhoea prevalence; Low-Low clusters (cold spots), indicating areas of low prevalence with neighbouring districts also showing low rates; High-low outliers, where high-prevalence districts are adjacent to low-prevalence areas; Low–high outliers, where high-prevalence neighbours border low-prevalence districts and non-significant areas where no clear spatial pattern was detected. While univariate Moran’s I measured the spatial dependence of diarrhoea prevalence alone, bivariate Moran’s I assessed the relationship between diarrhoea and district-level characteristics such as water, sanitation, and hygiene indicators. Although both statistics help detect spatial structure in the data, their reliability may decline in the presence of uneven or irregular spatial clustering. Moran’s I statistic varies from −1 to + 1. The value of + 1 denotes strong positive spatial autocorrelation, indicating similar values are clustered together, while a value of −1 suggests strong negative spatial autocorrelation, where neighbouring areas have contrasting values. In contrast, a negative index suggests that neighbouring units are more likely to have differing characteristics. To further examine the underlying factors linked to the occurrence of diarrhoea among children under five in India, several spatial regression models were employed. Among them, the Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) method served as a baseline approach. The Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) model was used as an initial baseline approach to examine the association between childhood diarrhoea prevalence and explanatory variables, including WASH indicators, groundwater quality, and socio-demographic factors. OLS estimates the average (global) relationship between the dependent and independent variables under the assumption that observations are independent and that residuals are randomly distributed [45, 46].

In this study, OLS was primarily employed to identify general patterns of association and to assess whether spatial dependence exists in the data. Diagnostic tests based on OLS residuals, such as tests for spatial autocorrelation, were used to determine whether spatial regression models (e.g., Spatial Lag Model and Spatial Error Model) were more appropriate. The presence of spatial dependence would indicate that neighbouring districts influence each other, violating OLS assumptions and requiring spatial modelling. The basic equation for OLS is as follows:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{Y} = \upalpha + \beta \mathrm{X} + \upvarepsilon$$\end{document}

Y is the dependent variable, X is the matrix of the independent variables, which, since we are estimating coefficients, are unknown, β is the vector of linear coefficients to be estimated, α is an intercept, and ϵ is the error (generally assumed to be normally distributed with mean 0, but constant variance). The Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) procedure assumes of independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) observations. Nevertheless, falsehoods often arise in spatial datasets due to spatial dependence, as it is plausible that the values of adjacent units have an influence. Therefore, spatial autocorrelation is directly considered by means of spatial econometric models, such as the Spatial Lag Model (SLM) or the Spatial Error Model (SEM). Particularly, the Spatial Lag Model (SLM) takes into consideration spatial dependence through a spatially lagged dependent variable, i.e., the results remain relevant to the outcome produced in a certain place within neighbourhood areas. The model is able to capture spillovers in space and, therefore, would be applicable in explaining interdependencies in a spatial phenomenon, such as the business outcomes of public health. This model includes a spatially lagged dependent variable (WY) as a regressor in the regression equation to account for spatial dependence.\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${Y}_{i}=\delta \sum_{j\ne \mathrm{i}}{W}_{ij}{Y}_{j}+\beta {X}_{j}+{\varepsilon}_{j}$$\end{document}where Yi is the diarrhoea prevalence for the ith district, δ denotes the coefficient of spatial autoregressive, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${W}_{ij}$$\end{document} is the spatial weight matrix that defines the connectivity between district i and j, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${Y}_{j}$$\end{document} denotes the prevalence of diarrhoea of j th district, βj is the vector of regression coefficients \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${X}_{j}$$\end{document} is the matrix of predictor variables, and εj is the residual.

In addition, while the SLM accounts for spatial dependence in the outcome variable by incorporating a spatially lagged term, the SEM addresses spatial autocorrelation in the error term, indicating that unobserved factors affecting the dependent variable are spatially correlated [47, 48].The basic form of the Spatial Error Model (SEM) is:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${Y}_{i}=\beta {X}_{j}+\lambda \sum_{j\ne \mathrm{i}}{W}_{ij}{Y}_{j}{\varepsilon}_{j}+\varepsilon$$\end{document}

Here, Yi is the prevalence of diarrhoea in ith district, the coefficient of spatial autoregressive is denoted as λ, W ij is the spatial weight matrix that determines the connectedness between district i and j, the prevalence of diarrhoea of jth district is referred to as Yj, the regression coefficient vector is defined as βj, the predictor variables matrix as Xj, and residual is represented as εj.

However, the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) is widely applied for model comparison. Lower AIC values suggest a better fit while penalising model complexity. Suppose the SEM has lower AIC values than the SLM in the study. In that case, it indicates that spatial dependence is better explained by unobserved spatially correlated errors rather than direct spatial spillover effects in the dependent variables.

Results

Descriptive results

The descriptive analysis (Supplementary Table A1 and Figures A1- A4) indicates that approximately 7.3% of children under five years of age experienced diarrhoea in India. The WASH facilities indicators showed that 88.7% of households in the study had access to improved drinking water, although only one-fifth had improved sanitation, with more than three-fourths still relying on unimproved sanitation. Regarding hygiene, 74% of respondents did not follow proper practices, while only one-fourth of the sampled population maintained good hygiene.

From the three models, it was confirmed that significant spatial dependence in the prevalence of under-five child diarrhoea across districts in India, as revealed by the significant Lambda (λ = 0.560, p < 0.001) in the SEM model and the substantial Rho (0.522) in the SLM model. The spatial models provide a more suitable fit than the OLS model, as contemplated in the lower Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) values (3774 in spatial models vs. 3909 in OLS) and higher adjusted R2 values (SLM = 0.364, SEM = 0.368, compared to 0.156 in OLS). Among the key predictors, unimproved sanitation reveals a strong and significant positive association with child diarrhoea across all models (OLS: β = 0.021, SLM: β = 0.016, SEM: β = 0.022, p < 0.001). Additionally, unimproved hygiene is significantly associated with increased diarrhoea prevalence in all models, although with a smaller effect size (OLS: β = 0.003, SLM: β = 0.002, SEM: β = 0.013, p < 0.001). From the socioeconomic and demographic factors, it was confirmed that households with a higher percentage of Schedule Tribes (ST) population were negatively associated with diarrhoea prevalence (OLS: β = −0.004, SLM: β = −0.002, SEM: β = −0.011, p < 0.001). Additionally, lack of mass media exposure is strongly linked to higher diarrhoea prevalence in all models (OLS: β = 0.075, SLM: β = 0.047, SEM: β = 0.051, p < 0.001). Accessibility to water also appears to be a critical factor, as districts where households take more than 30 min to fetch water reveal a marginally significant positive association with diarrhoea prevalence (OLS: β = 0.082, SEM: β = 0.087, p = 0.062).

Discussion

The present study examines spatial heterogeneities in childhood diarrhoea and its association with WASH conditions and groundwater quality, while accounting for key demographic, socioeconomic, and contextual covariates, across 707 districts in 28 states and 8 union territories of India. In addition to WASH indicators, groundwater quality variables were included to capture the broader environmental context influencing drinking water safety and associated exposure pathways. The LISA maps and Moran's I (0.274, p < 0.01) revealed strong spatial clustering of diarrhoea prevalence, particularly in the eastern and northeastern regions of India, which have historically had poor WASH infrastructure, low literacy rates, and vulnerability to flooding [49–51]. The high–high clusters observed in Bihar, Odisha, West Bengal, and Assam are consistent with previous studies that report lower access to improved sanitation, lower maternal education levels, and higher burdens of child morbidity in these regions [19, 25]. OLS and spatial regression revealed significant associations between diarrhoea and access to hygiene (β = −0.41), nitrate levels, and maternal education. However, the presence of residual spatial autocorrelation suggests that spatial heterogeneity remains partially unexplained; however, this may also reflect the influence of unobserved or omitted variables, such as environmental, behavioural, or healthcare access factors, indicating the need for both more localised modelling approaches and the inclusion of additional relevant predictors. Further, children in rural areas (AOR = 1.039) and those with no media exposure had higher odds of diarrhoea, highlighting gaps in information dissemination and behaviour change communication. Media exposure has been positively linked with knowledge of hygiene practices and prompt healthcare-seeking [52, 53].

The spatial analysis offers critical insights that go beyond what conventional regression captures. The Global Moran's I statistic (0.274, p < 0.01) revealed significant spatial autocorrelation, confirming that diarrhoeal prevalence is not randomly distributed but spatially clustered. This confirms the presence of regional hotspots and underscores the need for targeted interventions. LISA further identified high-high clusters, primarily concentrated in the eastern and northeastern states, including West Bengal, Bihar, Jharkhand, Odisha, Assam, and parts of Uttar Pradesh. While earlier studies [25, 54], have identified spatial disparities in WASH conditions and related health outcomes, they did not explicitly examine childhood diarrhoeal disease in conjunction with groundwater quality. The present study extends this literature by integrating these dimensions using updated NFHS-5 data and advanced spatial analytical approaches. Spatial clustering in these regions can be attributed to a combination of factors: poor drainage, frequent waterlogging, limited access to piped water and sanitation infrastructure, high population density, and socioeconomic deprivation [50, 51]. These districts often have a high concentration of marginalised communities (e.g., SC/ST), lower female literacy, and limited public health outreach [55, 56]. The clustering likely reflects underlying disparities in infrastructure and broader structural inequalities. The application of Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regression with spatial diagnostics confirmed the influence of hygiene access (β = −0.41), maternal education, and groundwater quality on diarrhoeal prevalence,however, the presence of significant residual spatial autocorrelation indicated model misspecification or spatial heterogeneity.

Moreover, groundwater contamination, particularly elevated nitrate and total dissolved solids (TDS), showed spatial correspondence with diarrhoeal clusters. This complements WASH-related determinants by incorporating hydro-geochemical dimensions of drinking water quality. This relationship may operate through multiple pathways: high nitrate levels can indicate contamination from agricultural runoff or sewage, increasing the likelihood of faecal pathogen presence, while elevated TDS may reflect broader water quality deterioration that can influence taste and perceived acceptability, thereby affecting household storage practices and conditions conducive to microbial contamination [16, 31]. Districts in northern Gujarat, Uttar Pradesh, and Bihar had the highest nitrate loads and correspondingly elevated diarrhoea prevalence, reinforcing the environmental dimension of the diarrhoeal burden. In some central districts, improved water sources were still positively associated with diarrhoea prevalence. This pattern may reflect issues related to water quality beyond source classification, such as contamination during distribution or household storage, as documented in previous studies [8, 20], which highlight that improved water sources do not necessarily guarantee microbiological safety.

Additionally, the spatial heterogeneity in the association of media exposure and household wealth with diarrhoea suggests that behavioural and awareness-related factors may vary in importance across regions. For instance, districts in southern India showed greater reductions in diarrhoea prevalence with higher media exposure, indicating more effective dissemination of hygiene-related messages in those areas, as suggested by Biran et al. [53] and Bhattacharya & Singh [52]. Spatial analysis not only validated the findings of multivariate models but also highlighted the uneven geography of risk, emphasising the necessity of region-specific interventions. These findings support the call by Fotheringham et al. [57] and Diez Roux [24] for the adoption of spatial thinking in public health research, especially for diseases with environmental and infrastructural determinants.

It is important to note that the findings of this study should be interpreted as associational rather than causal. The cross-sectional nature of NFHS-5 data limits the ability to establish temporal relationships between exposure and outcome variables. Additionally, the use of district-level aggregated data introduces an ecological dimension, where observed associations may not necessarily reflect individual-level relationships. Therefore, while the study identifies significant links between WASH conditions, groundwater quality, and childhood diarrhoea, these should not be interpreted as causal effects. Longitudinal and experimental studies would be required to establish causality more definitively. The district-level linkage between NFHS-5 and CGWB data may introduce exposure misclassification, as groundwater quality can vary substantially within districts and NFHS cluster locations are spatially displaced for confidentiality. This ecological aggregation may also lead to ecological fallacy, where area-level associations do not necessarily reflect individual-level relationships.

Policy recommendation

The findings of this study suggest the importance of strengthening integrated WASH strategies in alignment with existing national programmes such as Jal Jeevan Mission (JJM) [58] (https://jaljeevanmission.gov.in), Swachh Bharat Mission (SBM) [59] (https://swachhbharatmission.gov.in), National Rural Health Mission [60] (https://nhm.gov.in), and POSHAN Abhiyaan [61] (https://poshanabhiyaan.gov.in).

Given the observed associations between WASH conditions, groundwater quality, and childhood diarrhoea, these programmes may benefit from more coordinated and context-specific implementation. For instance, JJM could consider incorporating water safety planning and periodic groundwater quality monitoring, particularly in regions where groundwater contamination is more prevalent. Similarly, SBM initiatives may further emphasize behaviour change communication and hygiene promotion, especially in regions identified with higher diarrhoeal burden.

In addition, strengthening maternal health awareness through NRHM and related platforms may help address behavioural and knowledge-related factors associated with child health outcomes. Targeted approaches focusing on socioeconomically disadvantaged and geographically vulnerable populations, including tribal and minority-dominated areas, may also be important, given the observed disparities. These recommendations are intended to complement existing efforts by highlighting areas where integrated and region-specific strategies may enhance programme effectiveness.

Conclusion

This study emphasises that the substantial spatial patterning of childhood diarrhoea burden in India is concerning and largely driven by a complex interplay of WASH access, groundwater safety, household characteristics, and demographic composition across diverse regions. Although there is robust evidence of the protective benefits of improved hygiene, there is clear evidence that the stand-alone benefit of interventions to increase access to water or sanitation is small. Meaningful, integrated WASH interventions may therefore be required. Moreover, the spatial analysis shows that local settings play a major role in determining disease patterns, underscoring the relevance of localised interventions. Policymakers are likely to focus on high-risk regions identified through spatial clustering and increase spending on water infrastructure, maternal education, and hygiene promotional campaigns for the population. The research also emphasises the need to closely monitor and control groundwater quality, particularly in the most affected districts, to safeguard child health and promote the achievement of Sustainable Development Goal 6, which aims for clean water and sanitation.
