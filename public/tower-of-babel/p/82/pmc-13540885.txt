Exploring the use of machine and deep learning in genome-wide association studies: a comprehensive review
Authors: Salvatore D’Antona, Mawada Elmagboul Abdalla Abakar, Daniele Ramazzotti, Marco Antoniotti, Alex Graudenzi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The advent of high-throughput sequencing technologies has generated increasingly large and complex genomic datasets, necessitating analytical approaches capable of capturing high-dimensional and potentially nonlinear genetic interactions. This situation has significantly impacted the entire field of Genome-Wide Association Study (GWAS), whose primary goal is the identification of genomic traits and variants that are statistically associated with the risk of a disease. However, traditional GWAS methods may show reduced performance when applied to highly polygenic and nonlinear genetic architectures. Computational strategies from Artificial Intelligence (AI) and, in particular, from machine- and deep-learning may provide a powerful tool to overcome such limitations, especially by capturing nonlinear interactions and complex hidden regularities in large-scale data, which traditional GWAS approaches might overlook. To date, only a few approaches have been introduced and systematically assessed. In this review, we describe the main characteristics and limitations of standard statistical approaches for GWAS, the main uses of AI methods in computational genomics, and recent attempts to leverage AI strategies in GWAS. Particular attention will be devoted to key issues, such as the interpretability of methods and results, and the curse of dimensionality. More specifically, the review presents 30 methods designed to leverage AI in GWAS, as well as presenting a comprehensive set of evaluation metrics for their performance, also providing references to the most frequently used databases, and biobanks. Overall, this work may serve as a starting point for both dry- and wet-lab researchers, aiming to extract deeper insights from genomic data by moving beyond traditional linear additive assumptions, and leveraging large-scale datasets through AI-driven approaches.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13040-026-00584-8.

Introduction

Recent advances in genotyping, and DNA analysis have revealed that the expression of most phenotypes cannot be attributed to a single gene or mutation, but rather to the cumulative effect of numerous genes, and variants [1]. Consequently, researchers have initiated an in-depth examination of the entire human genome to identify associations between multiple variants, and complex phenotypes through GWASs [1]. Historically, these studies have focused on Single Nucleotide Polymorphisms (SNPs), as these variants play a key role in the inheritance, and development of complex traits, and diseases, such as type II diabetes, and schizophrenia [2, 3]. The primary aim of GWAS is to identify SNPs associated with disease susceptibility within populations, thereby shedding light on familial risks [4].

Numerous reviews address the interface between Machine Learning (ML) and Deep Learning (DL), on the one hand, and GWAS, on the other. Some of these reviews summarize commonly used methods, emphasize a set of specialized tools, or provide a methodological overview of a range of approaches in a particular domain [5]. For instance, they have a historical perspective on ML in genetic epidemiology, describe software tools such as Combi, GenNet, or GMSTool, and evaluate the general pros and cons of ML in human genetics [5]. Other reviews focus on plant GWAS, comparing statistical approaches, Bayesian methods, and ML techniques, primarily in terms of computational efficiency, statistical power, and single-locus versus multi-locus approaches [6]. Although these reviews are useful, sometimes they do not provide a comprehensive, multidimensional comparison of AI approaches in terms of architecture, interpretability, integration of prior biological information, and feature selection approaches. Our narrative review, on the other hand, summarizes a range of AI approaches applied to GWAS studies, with a particular focus on interpretability. These include interpretable methods, post-hoc interpretability tools, representation learning approaches, and performance optimization techniques. This review thus provides a timely, multidimensional and comprehensive overview of AI approaches in GWAS studies, highlighting their opportunities and limitations.

During the first wave of GWASs, researchers typically tested one SNP at a time using univariate regression [7]. More recent approaches have employed Linear Mixed Models (LMMs) with random effects [8–11], which, despite their advancements, have struggled to distinguish true causal SNPs from neighboring variants along the DNA sequence because of their interdependence with the phenotype. This challenge, known as Linkage Disequilibrium (LD), occurs when physically close variants are inherited together rather than independently, complicating the identification of true causal variants [11]. To address this issue, fine-mapping techniques have been developed to identify causal SNPs post-hoc [12]. However, as genomic data continue to expand through high-throughput sequencing technologies, researchers face the challenge of analyzing increasingly large, and complex genomic regions, focusing not only on SNPs, but also on other types of variants, such as insertions, deletions, and structural variants (the size of which can range from a few base pairs to millions). These have been shown to have their own roles in influencing phenotypic traits, adding further complexity to the interpretation of GWAS results [13].

For instance, GWASs uncovered SNPs in Crohn’s disease linked to genes, such as ATG16L1, and IRGM, allowing reassessment of the role of autophagy in inflammation [14, 15].

GWASs' findings have several clinical implications, aiding early disease diagnosis, personalized treatment, and drug development [16–19]. Examples of early detection include the nonsynonymous variant in the CFH gene, rs1061170, which explains 50% of the genetic risk for Age-Related Macular Degeneration (AMD) within the study population [18]. A multi-locus analysis of AMD susceptibility loci showed that 99% of individuals carrying the highest-risk genotypes, including CFH variants, had AMD, and that 85% of these cases were in advanced stages [18]. An example of a drug development case is the statin target HMGCR, which was previously identified through a GWAS in association with serum cholesterol levels [20]. Interestingly, this knowledge extends beyond common diseases, and reveals differences in variability among different ethnic groups, and when analyzing rare variants [21].

While traditional genetic methods like linkage analysis have been highly successful in identifying the causal genes for Mendelian diseases [22, 23], the investigation of complex, polygenic diseases has necessitated the development of GWAS [3]. Translating findings to complex diseases often requires large-scale genomic data. While early GWAS successes, such as the discovery of the role of the complement factor H (CFH) gene in age-related macular degeneration, were achieved with modest sample sizes and clear clinical diagnoses [16], the polygenic nature of most complex traits means that progressively larger cohorts are needed to uncover variants with smaller effect sizes [3, 18]. In the modern genomics era, this often involves leveraging vast biobanks with genomic data linked to comprehensive electronic health records [24], alongside robust statistical methods to automate the analysis [25]. In addition, the possible stratification of the population within the cohort under examination, and the difficulty of identifying variants with small effect sizes are additional limitations of this type of study [26]. Although translational genomics, and precision medicine promise to improve health care by linking laboratory, and GWAS discoveries to clinical practices, due to the aforementioned reasons, the results obtained from GWASs are not yet suitable in most clinical settings [27].

One possible solution to analyze the output data obtained from GWAS (i.e., summary statistics) is through the use of AI that includes supervised ML methods, which are algorithms trained on labeled data to make predictions on unseen examples. DL, a subset of ML, instead leverages architectures based on Deep Neural Networks (DNNs) to model complex patterns and relationships in data. Such approaches have shown promise for the analysis of large, and complex datasets [28, 29]. For instance, ML models provide a powerful framework for feature selection, aiming to identify crucial variants (i.e., biosignatures) and discard irrelevant ones. However, the utility of this approach, its ‘effectiveness’, must be carefully defined. In the high-dimensional context of GWAS, effectiveness cannot be measured by predictive accuracy (e.g., sensitivity and precision) alone, but must also encompass the stability of the selected features [30, 31]. Feature selection is notoriously difficult when the number of features far exceeds the number of samples, and many ML methods can exhibit high variability, selecting different feature subsets in response to small changes in the training data. This instability can lead to a high false-positive rate and hinder biological interpretation, particularly if models are poorly trained or overfit [32]. The primary advantage of ML-based feature selection over traditional univariate methods is its inherent ability to model and detect complex, non-linear interactions between features (i.e., epistasis), which may have little to no individual effect [32]. It is this capacity to capture a portion of the ‘missing heritability’ that makes the approach compelling, despite the associated challenges. Previous studies on Alzheimer’s disease have shown promising results in the identification of significant biosignatures, using this type of approach [33, 34]. When the goal is to identify complex SNP–SNP interactions (epistasis), ML-based methods can serve as computationally tractable heuristic approaches. In this specific context, they are vastly more efficient than performing an exhaustive search of all possible multi-SNP combinations, a task that is computationally infeasible for genome-wide data [35]. However, it is crucial to note that these ML strategies are considerably less computationally efficient than standard, non-interactive filtering methods such as p-value thresholding or LD clumping, which remain the fastest approaches for initial dimensionality reduction [36]. Furthermore, by modeling the joint effect of multiple SNPs, ML methods can potentially mitigate false positives arising from complex LD patterns that confound single-marker tests. However, the use of AI has known limitations, including the lack of transparency in its decision-making processes (the “black box” problem), the requirement of large sample sizes to achieve statistical power, and the need for researchers to thoroughly understand the capabilities, and constraints of different algorithms [25, 37].

In this review, we provide a comprehensive overview of state-of-the-art AI methods applied to GWAS. For AI/ML/DL models we consider both paradigms: (i) direct application to individual-level genotypes as the primary association method (replacing conventional single-variant tests) and (ii) using these methods on pre-calculated GWAS summary statistics or together with standard GWAS for tasks such as fine-grained mapping, risk prediction or interaction detection (complementing conventional approaches). Specifically, Sect. 2 presents the standard GWAS workflow, and the limitations that researchers frequently face. Section 3 reports the latest AI models employed in GWASs, showing their effectiveness, and Sect. 4 discusses the general characteristics, and limitations of standard AI methods (contextualizing them with respect to the cited studies). Section 5 describes the most commonly used databases, repositories, and biobanks used in GWASs, as well as the most frequent metrics used to evaluate GWAS performance. Finally, Sect. 6 summarizes our review, and discusses possible future applications.

In summary, GWASs aim to identify the biological mechanisms underlying various diseases. If successful, the results can pave the way for experimental hypothesis generation through the identification of loci often associated with genes’ function [18]. Summary of main limitations, and challenges in GWAS

Complexity of genetic architecture

The role and detection of epistasis (gene-gene interactions) in complex traits present another major challenge for GWAS. While biologically plausible, the general importance of epistasis for human complex traits is debated [60]; for many traits, such as adult height, the vast majority of genetic variance appears to be additive [61]. The difficulty in detecting epistasis stems not only from the immense computational burden and statistical power required to test millions of interactions, but also from other critical factors. These include the precision of phenotype definition, low statistical power to detect interactions involving rare variants (i.e., low MAF), and the possibility that genotyped SNPs do not adequately tag the true causal variants [60]. While studies in model organisms have been instrumental in demonstrating the biological mechanisms of epistasis, extrapolating its population-level importance to humans is challenging [62]. The genetic architecture of laboratory strains is often shaped by artificial selection and inbreeding, which can dramatically alter allele frequencies compared to natural human populations. Because the statistical signature of epistasis is highly dependent on the frequencies of the interacting alleles, its apparent importance in model organisms may not directly reflect its role in human complex traits [62]. Therefore, while these studies confirm the biological reality of gene-gene interactions, detecting their contribution in large, outbred human populations remains a distinct and formidable challenge. Recent methodological advances, such as Bayesian methods, and AI algorithms, can improve epistasis detection, however larger sample sizes, and computing power are essential [41].

Multiple hypothesis testing

One of the GWASs’ obstacles is related to multiple hypothesis testing (i.e., the need to test the associations between millions of SNPs, and the phenotype under examination). For WGS-based studies, a more stringent threshold, often cited as P \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ < 2.5 \times 10^{-8}$$\end{document}, is required to maintain a family-wise error rate of 5% for single-variant tests in European populations [63]. For more complex analytical strategies that combine single-variant tests with window-based analyses of rare variants, even more stringent thresholds in the range of P \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ < 1 \times 10^{-8}$$\end{document} may be necessary, depending on the specific tests and windowing strategies employed [64]. One effective way to address this challenge is to augment the cohort size under investigation [65]. However, assembling large cohorts, especially for intricate or costly phenotypes, may not always be feasible [65].

Other approaches reduce the number of tests using gene- or pathway-centric association analyses, or by narrowing the focus to specific genomic regions or biologically significant factors [43, 44].

Alternatively, Bayesian statistical methods provide a probabilistic approach to GWAS that can help solve the multiple test problem without the need for genome-wide significance thresholds. Bayesian statistical methods estimate the probability that a particular variant is associated with the phenotype, given the observed data. For instance, fine-mapping methods such as FINEMAP [66] can be used to examine localized regions of the genome defined by LD blocks. These methods can estimate the probabilities that individual variants within localized regions are associated with the phenotype by examining alternative configurations of causal SNPs. In this approach, the posterior probabilities estimated for individual variants will be distributed over the total number of variants in the localized region, effectively accounting for the large number of variants considered.

At a broader scale, genome-wide Bayesian variable selection models, such as Bayesian sparse linear mixed models [67], extend this principle to the entire genome. These statistical methods have the advantage of providing a direct estimate of the evidence of association, while implicitly controlling for the large number of variants examined in the genome. Bayesian statistical methods can also be employed to incorporate prior knowledge about the study results by examining associations with genes or biological pathways [45]. However, the application of Bayesian statistical methods can be limited by the computational intensity of the method and the potential for overfitting the results [45].

Non-disclosure of causal variants

A key difficulty for researchers is that, although GWAS identifies genomic regions linked to specific phenotypes, pinpointing the exact causal variants often remains elusive [46]. This is mainly because of the fact that many genetic variants within a region tend to be in LD, thus making it difficult to discern the actual causal variants [46]. Another aspect that complicates the interpretation of the results is that most SNPs are located in the non-coding regions of the genome [47]. Additional methods are needed to identify causal variants, and their target genes, such as resequencing in different populations, fine mapping, functional analyses, and evolutionary genetic studies [48–52, 68, 69].

Population stratification

The heterogeneity of the population in the examined cohort can introduce false associations or obscure true signals [53]. In practice, two main strategies are widely used to mitigate the effects of population stratification in GWAS. The first consists of including ancestry-informative covariates derived from PCA of the genotype matrix, which capture the main axes of population structure [70]. The second is genomic control, which adjusts association statistics using an inflation factor to correct for residual population structure [71]. In addition to these approaches, other strategies have been proposed, including family-based validation and consistency checks across different ethnic groups [54, 55].

Finally, the immediate clinical utility of many GWAS findings, and their applicability remain debated [72]. Even for diseases, such as type 1 diabetes mellitus, where GWAS-discovered loci explain the majority of heritability, population-level genetic screening is impractical because of the high number of false positives [72]. Screening for rare variants with strong effect sizes is likely to lead to viable treatments, and the development of reliable diagnostic tools. On the other hand, Polygenic Risk Scores (PRS) compile the effects of thousands to millions of genetic variants, each contributing a small amount, to estimate an individual’s genetic likelihood of developing a disease, and their usefulness is debated [73]. PRSs are computed by assigning weights to specific alleles based on their effect sizes, as identified through GWAS, and aggregating these contributions into a single score. These scores show promise for certain diseases, such as cancer, and coronary artery disease, as they assist in risk stratification, and facilitate personalized risk management approaches [74, 75]. However, translating genetic risk knowledge into improved long-term clinical practice remains challenging.

Unexplained heritability of complex traits

Another limitation of GWAS is its inability to account for all genetic contributions to complex traits, a concept often discussed in terms of broad-sense versus narrow-sense heritability. Broad-sense heritability represents the proportion of phenotypic variance due to all sources of genetic variation, including additive, dominant, and epistatic effects. In contrast, narrow-sense heritability captures only the proportion attributable to additive genetic effects [76]. Standard GWAS methods, which estimate the summed effects of individual SNPs, primarily capture additive genetic variance. Therefore, the heritability estimated from GWAS, often referred to as SNP-based heritability, is an estimate of narrow-sense heritability [77]. A significant challenge, often termed the ‘missing heritability’ problem, has arisen because the narrow-sense heritability explained by early GWAS was substantially lower than the broader heritability estimates from family and twin studies [56]. Adding to this complexity, heritability is not a fixed biological constant, but rather a population-specific measure that estimates the proportion of phenotypic variation for a trait that can be attributed to genetic variation among individuals within that specific population at a specific time [78]. Its value is inherently contingent on the distribution of both genetic and environmental factors in the population under study. For instance, in a more environmentally uniform population, where environmental variance is reduced, the heritability of a trait may appear higher simply because genetic differences account for a larger proportion of total phenotypic variance [79]. Turning to the sources of the missing heritability gap itself, it is now understood to arise from several factors. A primary contributor is the vast number of rare variants (MAF < 1%), whose effects were not captured by the common-variant genotyping arrays used in early GWAS [80]. In support of this, large-scale whole-genome sequencing studies have shown that a substantial fraction of heritability for traits such as height is indeed explained by these rare variants [80]. In addition to this biological source,other contributing factors include the large number of common variants with effects too small to detect without enormous sample sizes, and the complex influence of gene-environment interactions [57, 58]. Finally, beyond these biological contributors, a portion of the missing heritability gap also reflects a methodological artifact; even the heritability attributable to common variants has been systematically underestimated by widely used statistical methods, such as LD Score Regression, this method assumes an equal contribution of all SNPs to heritability and thereby misattributes true genetic signal as confounding bias, leading to deflated estimates of SNP-based heritability [81]. This is particularly important for diseases significantly influenced by environmental factors, where different subsets of genes may be involved depending on the specific risk environment, such as lung cancer, which is influenced by factors, such as pollutants or smoking [82]. In addition, heritability estimates may be inflated due to shared environmental effects, particularly in classic twin studies [59]. Nevertheless, the results of GWASs are expected to lead to an increased understanding of heritability with advances in sample sizes, methodologies, and study designs.

Applications of AI in GWAS: current efforts, and findings

The development of high-throughput sequencing technologies has generated vast and complex genomic datasets; however, the limitations of standard analytical methods, as discussed in Sect. 2, have created a clear need for more powerful computational strategies.

ML, DL, and Statistical Learning (SL) are computational techniques that offer a compelling paradigm shift for several fundamental reasons. SL (e.g., penalized regression, Bayesian methods) refers to approaches based on probabilistic modeling and explicit parameter estimation, often prioritizing inference and interpretability. In contrast, ML and DL methods are primarily data-driven and optimized for predictive performance, with DL also enabling the learning of hierarchical representations of complex genomic models. Usually in genomic data, AI models are inherently suited to capture complex, non-linear interactions that underlie epistasis, a major component of the genetic architecture of complex traits that is largely invisible to standard single-variant association tests [83]. In addition, AI provides a powerful framework for multi-omics data integration. By simultaneously analyzing data from the genome, transcriptomic, proteomic, and other molecular layers, DL models can identify systems-level patterns and build a more holistic understanding of disease pathology that transcends genomics alone [84, 85]. Finally, AI models offer the unique ability to embed prior biological knowledge directly into their architecture. By structuring Neural Networks (NNs) to reflect known biological pathways or gene-regulatory networks, these ‘biologically informed’ models can be guided towards more plausible and interpretable solutions, moving from pure statistical association to mechanistic insight [86, 87]. It is this combination of capabilities in detecting non-additive effects, integrating heterogeneous data, and leveraging biological knowledge that provides the primary motivation for applying AI to GWAS.

The search terms “GWAS,” “machine learning,” and “deep learning” were used in combination within the database to identify articles published from January 2019 to December 2024. The inclusion criteria were studies that utilized AI approaches to analyze GWAS data, including SNP arrays or summary data, for prediction, feature selection, or imputation tasks. The key inclusion criterion for this review was the assessment of studies based on their approaches to interpretability, which is a major theme of the review. Furthermore, preprints and review articles were generally excluded from the search results. However, in some cases, we also included relevant methods that had not been published but were highly cited or were emerging trends in their respective fields.

In this study, interpretability is considered to have three main aspects: (i) transparency of the model architecture, (ii) ability to quantify feature contributions, and (iii) presence of post-hoc explanations. These aspects enable a more systematic evaluation of models than relying solely on qualitative methods. It has also been recognized that there is a spectrum of interpretability and that not all models are equal in terms of difficulty of interpretation.

The following are the operational definitions of interpretability used in this study.High interpretability: models that show explicit and directly accessible relationships between features and predictions.Medium interpretability: models that do not have inherent interpretability, but can provide feature importance using intrinsic mechanisms or post-hoc explanation techniques.Low interpretability: models in which feature contributions are indirect, highly nonlinear, or dependent on the latent space, such that explanations rely primarily on approximations.

Finally, to improve conceptual clarity, we distinguish three categories of computational approaches used in GWAS. SL encompasses classical regression and Bayesian methods, widely used in statistical genetics, characterized by explicit model assumptions and interpretable parameters such as effect sizes or posterior distributions. ML methods extend this framework by enabling flexible pattern recognition and predictive modeling without rigid parametric assumptions. DL, a subset of ML, further captures complex, hierarchical, and nonlinear relationships through multilayered neural architectures. This distinction is important, as several widely used GWAS methods (e.g., LDpred, LASSO-based models) are not inherently AI-driven, despite being used in conjunction with AI pipelines.

Directly interpretable models

These models are inherently interpretable by design. They provide clear insights into how input features (i.e., SNPs) contribute to the output. Examples include linear models, decision trees, and models that output feature importance or effect size.

Models that are directly interpretable hold a unique place in the field of computational genomics, where interpretability and transparency, in addition to predictive ability, are emphasized. A major unifying aspect of the works discussed in Supplementary Material 2 is the design of models that allow for the identification of specific genetic variants or groups of variants, such as genes and pathways, that are linked with the predictions made by the model. Models are built not only to make accurate predictions but also to generate outputs that are inherently interpretable.

The key feature of these models is the explicit estimation of the contribution of individual SNPs to phenotypic variation. With SL methods such as shrinkage, Bayesian ridge regression [89], and the combination of Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator LASSO and ridge regression [89, 106], denoted by the Elastic Net method, it is possible to overcome multicollinearity and overfitting problems, thereby estimating the effect size of SNPs on phenotypic traits. Ensemble methods, such as bagging-based decision trees [88] and gradient boosting classifiers [88] also allow for the estimation of the effect size of SNPs on phenotypic traits), which maintain interpretability through feature importance measures. Thus, SNPs can be ranked based on their predictive importance for the phenotype. Other examples include hybrid methods such as GASVeM, which combines Genetic Algorithms (GA) and Support Vector Machines (SVM) [90], or Just Add Data (JADBio-Gen), which uses Epilogi for feature selection [25, 107]. These methods maintain transparency since they output ranked sets of SNPs or statistically equivalent signatures with clear predictive relevance.

A second common theme is the use of biological knowledge to inform the structure of models and improve their interpretability. GenNet [91] uses hierarchical relationships from SNPs to genes and pathways. Stepwise Learning of Epistatic Mechanisms (SLEM) uses multilevel information from molecules and cells, limited by prior knowledge on SNPs associated with schizophrenia [94]. Other methods, such as the risk predictor for Alzheimer’s disease using GNN-AD [92], use LD between SNPs to uncover epistatic effects. This allows for the visualization of complex genotype-to-phenotype relationships. In summary, prior knowledge in the form of biological networks, pathway information, or constrained topology is used to reduce the complexity of model learning.

Managing scarcity and uncertainty is another crucial aspect. The regularization method, Bayesian shrinkage, and penalties focus only on a specific subset of predictive variants, thereby reducing noise and overfitting and providing interpretable results. The posterior distribution, effect size coefficients, and feature importance provide quantitative measures of certainty for each SNP, thus increasing reproducibility and generalizability across datasets [89, 91, 94].

Another quality that makes directly interpretable models unique is the balance between predictive performance and interpretability. Although some flexibility is lost, ensemble methods, cross-validation, hyperparameter optimization, and multi-cohort validation provide robust predictive performance. However, the most important feature is that interpretability is an integral component, in contrast to ML models, where it is an add-on feature. The outputs, such as selected SNPs, network weights, and feature contributions, are inherently interpretable, thus providing insights into genotype-phenotype relationships.

In conclusion, direct interpretable models, as seen in previous studies, share some core features. These features include the explicit mapping of predictors to outcomes, the integration of prior biological knowledge, management of sparsity and uncertainty, and the balance between interpretability and predictive performance [25, 88–92, 94, 106, 107]. These features enable the model to predict phenotypes or disease risk with accuracy and provide insights into the mechanisms, thereby bridging the gap between statistical learning and biological understanding.

Practical implications for genomic studies

From a technical perspective, directly interpretable models remain one of the most reliable approaches for performing genomic association studies. Their reliance on direct effect estimation, regularization, and feature selection makes them scalable to high-dimensional SNP data while maintaining statistical interpretability. Moreover, their results, such as effect magnitude, ranked SNP importance, and identified genetic signatures, can be easily incorporated into downstream biological validation and clinical interpretation. Therefore, interpretable linear models and feature selection-based approaches remain among the most suitable strategies for studies seeking to discover candidate variants, develop polygenic risk models, and inform clinical decision-making pipelines.

Post-hoc interpretability methods

These methods are applied after a model is trained to interpret its decisions. They are often used for “black box” models. Examples include SHAP, Layer-wise Relevance Propagation (LRP), Permutation Feature Importance (PFI), Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (GRAD-CAM).

A second category of methods in computational genomics focuses on post-hoc interpretability. In this category, predictive models with strong predictive ability, including complex ML and DL architectures, are first trained and then analyzed to extract meaningful biological insights. These methods are distinct from those based on inherently interpretable models, in which interpretability is integral to the predictive model. Across the literature in Supplementary Material 2, a recurring theme is the use of interpretability tools for linking predictive models with strong predictive ability to biological interpretability. A key characteristic of this category is the use of powerful predictive models, including complex ML and DL architectures such as deep neural networks, multilayer perceptron networks, convolutional networks, and gradient boosting frameworks. These powerful predictive models can model complex SNP interactions and nonlinear relationships. For instance, in using DNNs, complex genotype-phenotype relationships are modeled using multiple transformations [95, 96]. In another instance, convolutional or residual NNs are used for hierarchical representation of genomic variation by transforming SNP data into high-dimensional representations for use in DL [99]. These powerful predictive models are often accompanied by modern optimization algorithms for minimizing overfitting, including adaptive learning rates, dropout regularization, and L1/L2 regularization.

Considering the complexity of these models, interpretability is incorporated through the use of explanation techniques that provide importance scores for features of the input data or specify genomic regions that affect the model output. Several studies have used feature attribution techniques to measure the importance of each SNP in the model output. For example, LRP propagates the relevance of the model output backward through the layers of the network to identify the features of the input that are most relevant to the classification output [95]. Other techniques use the model structure to calculate the importance of the output of individual SNPs, such as connection weight analysis for a Multilayer perceptron (MLP) [96], and feature perturbation techniques, such as permutation feature importance, where the performance of the model is measured when individual SNPs are randomly permuted [98]. Visualization techniques are also used, especially for deep NNs, where techniques such as GRAD-CAM are used to identify genomic regions that are responsible for the classification of phenotypes.

A further recurring theme across this series of investigations is the integration of ML predictions with statistical validation frameworks. This is particularly relevant because feature attribution methods often produce a multiplicity of candidate variants. Several methods exist for combining feature attribution methods with statistical filtering or multiple testing correction. For example, relevance scores or connection weights may be evaluated against statistical thresholds, Mahalanobis distance outlier detection, or false discovery rate correction to distinguish meaningful genomic signals from noise [96]. This integration of ML with statistical validation is indicative of a larger effort to ensure that ML-derived results are consistent with established best practices in GWAS.

These models often incorporate dimensionality reduction and representation learning to make genomic data more tractable for complex modeling. Techniques such as PCA or feature filtering might be used to extract relevant genetic components prior to training DL models [97]. In other cases, SNP matrices may be converted into alternative representations, such as images or graphs to facilitate the use of advanced neural architectures [99]. These transformations significantly improve the predictive ability, however they also increase the necessity for post-hoc interpretability methods that are able to effectively back-map representation space into biological space.

Finally, this series of investigations demonstrates a larger methodological trend in genomic ML: the disconnection between predictive modeling and interpretation. Rather than using architectural constraints to ensure transparency, researchers are increasingly using powerful predictive models and applying feature attribution methods to those results. This allows for the exploitation of the predictive advantages afforded by DL architectures while still providing results that are interpretable and able to inform biological hypotheses, such as identifying candidate disease variants or candidate variants in regions associated with variation in phenotype [95, 98, 99].

In conclusion, post-hoc interpretability approaches in genomics can be distinguished by the following common characteristics: the use of powerful predictive models that can learn complex relationships between genotypes and phenotypes, the use of feature attribution and visualization to interpret the predictions of the models, the use of statistical validation to enhance robustness, and the use of representation learning to cope with the high dimensionality of genomic data [95–99]. In summary, explainable AI approaches can convert complex ML models into tools that not only have predictive capabilities for phenotypes but also have the potential to derive meaningful insights from genomic data.

Practical implications for genomic studies

These methods provide a highly significant compromise between predictive accuracy and biological interpretability. When combined with more sophisticated ML or DL models, they have the potential to model complex nonlinear relationships between genetic variants, while still providing interpretable relevance scores for SNPs or genomic regions. However, their robustness is highly dependent on the stability of feature attribution techniques and the use of statistical validation techniques. Therefore, these methods are promising in the area of discovery in large-scale genomic datasets; however, validation is required before model predictions can be used to establish clinical biomarkers.

Representation learning models

These models learn representations (or features) from data that are useful for downstream tasks. They often involve unsupervised or self-supervised learning. Examples include autoencoders and other DL models that learn latent representations.

Representation learning models represent a substantial paradigm shift in the use of artificial intelligence for genomics. Instead of focusing on feature engineering or developing direct SNP-level interpretability, representation learning models aim to develop compact and informative representations of high-dimensional genomic data that capture underlying latent biological structures. A clear trend has emerged across the studies summarized in Supplementary Material 2: the use of deep neural networks to reduce large genotype matrices or phenotype datasets to lower-dimensional embeddings that capture meaningful relationships between variants, genes, and phenotypes. These representations can then be used to capture complex nonlinear relationships that are challenging to model using traditional GWAS methods.

A key feature of representation learning models is the use of deep neural networks tailored to extract hierarchical features from genomic data. Fully connected neural networks are often used to model nonlinear genotype-phenotype relationships by successively transforming SNP inputs into higher-level feature abstractions [88]. Similarly, Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) are used to leverage spatial correlations between neighboring variants by applying filters to SNP sequences, thus enabling the identification of local genomic features, such as haplotype blocks or allele interactions [88, 89]. These networks are particularly useful for identifying regional genetic signals, as convolutional filters can be used to learn hierarchical representations of variant interactions without the need for explicit biological annotation.

Another major theme is the application of autoencoder-based representation learning for dimensionality reduction and the extraction of latent structure in genomic or phenotypic data. Autoencoders involve compressing high-dimensional input data into a low-dimensional latent space, followed by reconstructing the input data, thereby forcing the network to learn low-dimensional representations of complex relationships. For example, autoencoder architectures have been used in the context of phenotype imputation, where the latent representation of the data captures correlations among phenotypes and allows for the reconstruction of missing values in large-scale biobank datasets [100]. Similarly, stacked autoencoders can be used to learn low-dimensional representations of SNPs that capture nonlinear relationships before applying the learned weights to downstream predictive models [103].

Representation learning models often rely on sequence- and graph-based architectures to represent long-range dependencies among genomic variants. Recurrent neural networks, such as Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) and Gated Recurrent Units (GRU), process SNPs as ordered sequences and learn long-range dependencies among loci, thus allowing the identification of epistatic interactions that could potentially extend over large genomic distances [88]. Similarly, convolutional and recurrent neural networks can be combined with biologically informed architectures that aggregate SNPs into gene-level representations before modeling interactions across the genome [102]. These models allow representation learning models to infer polygenic architectures that arise from distributed networks of interacting variants rather than from individual SNP associations.

Another important aspect of these models is their ability to integrate diverse biological and environmental information into learned representations. Some models include demographic, socioeconomic, or technical variables together with genetic variables, thus enabling the learning of joint representations of genetic and environmental risk factors [102]. Others include hierarchical genomic information, such as chromosomal organization, linkage disequilibrium, or functional annotations, into neural architectures to guide representation learning towards biologically meaningful patterns [92]. By including such contextual information into the learned latent space, these models aim to capture the multifactorial nature of complex diseases.

Although representation learning models have great predictive power, they are generally associated with low intrinsic interpretability, because the learned latent variables do not have direct biological meaning. While some studies aim to analyze intermediate layers or latent embeddings to provide insights into biological mechanisms, the connection between individual SNPs and model predictions is often indirect and requires further analysis. Thus, representation learning models are often followed by downstream analyses, such as association testing, feature attribution analysis, or statistical filtering, to provide insights into biologically meaningful representations.

Studies included in Supplementary Material 2 have shown that representation learning models use DNNs to uncover hidden patterns in high-dimensional genomic data [88, 89, 92, 100, 102, 103]. In contrast, the combination of dimensionality reduction, nonlinear feature extraction, and multiple data sources provides a solid foundation for modeling complex genotype-phenotype associations. However, the reliance on learned latent representations reflects the difficulty in achieving a balance between predictive accuracy and biological interpretability in modern genomic ML.

Practical implications for genomic studies

Representation learning models are of great interest in the context of large-scale genomic data, given their ability to efficiently handle high-dimensional genotype matrices and learn a compact latent representation of complex biological structures. However, the indirect association between learned latent factors and individual genetic variants limits their interpretability and clinical relevance. Therefore, representation learning models are currently most useful as preprocessing or feature learning algorithms that can facilitate downstream predictive or association analyses.

Performance-optimized models

These models are primarily designed for high predictive performance, often at the cost of interpretability. They may be complex and “black box” in nature, such as a DNN, without specific interpretability components.

A final set of methods listed in Supplementary Material 2 can be generally classified as performance-optimized models, in which the primary model design goal is to achieve maximal predictive performance rather than interpretability or biological representation. Throughout these studies, ML and DL approaches have been set up to effectively capture complex genotype-phenotype associations by exploiting flexible model architectures, ensemble learning techniques, and large-scale genomic data. Unlike interpretable or representation-driven models, these models are primarily focused on predictive performance and often cast genomic prediction as a high-dimensional pattern recognition problem.

The primary theme running through these methods is the use of highly flexible model architectures that can effectively model nonlinear relationships and high-order interactions among SNPs. Multilayer perceptrons and deep neural networks are typically used to approximate complex functions that associate genomic variants with phenotypic traits, thus allowing for the modeling of nonlinear effects without explicitly specifying interactions [89]. In some cases, the architecture itself is optimized using evolutionary algorithms or other automated search methods, which helps to identify network architectures that are well-suited to capture patterns associated with traits in high-dimensional genomic data [89]. The importance of architectural flexibility underscores the understanding that complex traits are shaped by polygenic factors and nonlinear interactions that are difficult to model using standard statistical approaches.

Another recurrent aspect is the inclusion of diverse genomic and functional data in predictive models. Several approaches go beyond simple genotype data by including GWAS summary statistics, LD data, functional annotations, or other types of genomic signals. For instance, DL models can integrate multiple types of genomic data, such as LD scores and functional annotations, to predict the probability that a variant is associated with a disease or trait [104]. These approaches seek to leverage the power of multiple layers of genomic information to improve predictions and better nominate candidate loci that may not meet traditional genome-wide significance thresholds.

Performance optimization is achieved using ensemble and hybrid learning methods that combine the best practices of different modeling paradigms. Instead of using a single predictive model, some studies have used different types of neural networks or combined ML and DL models using a stacked ensemble method. These models combine different pattern recognition abilities, such as feedforward neural networks that identify nonlinear interactions between features and recurrent networks that identify dependencies between SNPs over time, to improve the accuracy of complex trait prediction [88]. By combining the predictions of multiple models, ensemble methods can reduce variance and improve generalization performance on unseen data.

Another common aspect of these models is the optimization of model parameters and training procedures to achieve the best possible predictive performance. Hyperparameter optimization using cross-validation, genetic algorithms, or other automated search algorithms is commonly used to find the optimal training procedure [89]. Moreover, these models commonly use sophisticated training techniques, such as transfer learning, large-scale pretraining, and regularization methods, to improve generalization performance when applied to new, independent datasets or related phenotypes [104]. These techniques reflect the increasing availability of large genomic datasets and the need to effectively use these datasets for predictive modeling.

Despite their high predictive accuracy, performance-optimized models are often difficult to interpret because many of them use complex neural network architectures or ensemble methods that function as “black boxes.” While some models provide outputs such as variant association probabilities or polygenic risk scores, the underlying relationships between individual SNPs and predictions are often difficult to directly identify [89, 104]. Therefore, these models are typically used as secondary tools in conjunction with traditional GWAS or interpretable ML models, where their high predictive accuracy can help identify candidate loci or guide risk stratification.

In conclusion, the studies mentioned above are part of a larger trend in the application of predictive modeling in precision medicine. Performance-optimized models are commonly used in tasks such as disease risk prediction, trait prediction, and treatment response modeling, where optimization of predictive accuracy is of great importance. For example, ML classifiers trained on sets of SNPs can predict adverse drug reactions or other clinically relevant outcomes, even when only a small set of variants is known [105]. At the same time, models that scale GWAS summary statistics using LD information seek to improve polygenic risk prediction and to improve the predictive value in genetic studies [89].

Together, performance-optimized models in genomic analysis emphasize the use of predictive accuracy through flexible modeling architectures, the combination of multiple genomic variables, ensemble learning methods, and full hyperparameter optimization [88, 89, 104, 105]. Although such models often involve a trade-off between interpretability and accuracy, they represent a major step forward in the development of predictive genomics and related fields, such as disease risk prediction and personalized medicine.

Practical implications for genomic studies

The models discussed above are advantageous when the predictive performance of the model is the primary goal, as seen in disease risk prediction and phenotype classification. The ability of these models to learn complex nonlinear relationships between genetic variants makes them suitable for large-scale datasets and high-dimensional genomic features. However, their lack of interpretability and the difficulty in attributing predictions to individual SNPs make it difficult to apply them directly to biological discovery. Therefore, these models are most useful when combined with an interpretable approach or a traditional GWAS that can interpret and validate their predictions.

From the perspective of implementation, the above approaches show significant variation. In terms of robustness, which refers to the ability to generalize to independent datasets, Directly Interpretable Models and Post-Hoc Methods with statistical validation are probably currently dominant, as they both incorporate uncertainty estimation and noise reduction. In terms of scalability to biobank-scale datasets (such as those with more than 500,000 participants), Performance-Optimized Models and Representation Learning models could be superior because of their ability to leverage GPU-accelerated mini-batch training, whereas standard linear models are constrained by memory. For a clinical application, a hybrid strategy appears: Post-Hoc Interpretability Methods on Performance-Optimized models are currently the most promising. This approach provides the high level of accuracy required for risk stratification, while also providing interpretable feature weightings that are critical for regulatory approval and clinician acceptance, thus closing the gap between model interpretability and clinical utility.

Limitations of AI methods for computational genomics

Confounders

Determining whether an unmeasured or confounding variable (i.e., a confounder) creates or masks associations with an outcome is one of the most difficult challenges in data analysis. This happens because the confounders can distort the relationship between the characteristics, and the outcome, but are often not included in the model because they cannot be measured or considered not significant. While confounders may lead to inflated prediction accuracy by allowing the model to exploit spurious associations rather than true causal effects, this apparent accuracy is misleading. Such inflation usually results in poor generalization performance, especially when applied in genomics with subtle signals or to new populations where the confounding factor’s distribution differs or is absent. Consequently, models affected by confounding often fail external validation and produce misleading interpretations of the relationships between genetic characteristics and outcomes [108].

Confounding factors in GWAS occur when the genetic structure of a population differs between cases, and controls. For instance, an ML model trained in a previous study to predict autism spectrum disorder using genotyping data initially appeared to be successful, but further analysis showed that the results were more indicative of shared ancestry than autism spectrum disorder [109–111]. Confounders can also occur during data collection or in coexpression networks used to predict gene functions or disease associations, where batch effects can introduce spurious correlations [112]. Likewise, electronic health record data can be confounded by factors, such as socioeconomic status, which affects the accuracy of the results [113]. Cross-validation does not protect against confounding effects, which is evident when the model performs poorly in new contexts. To prevent these problems, experimental designs should ideally be randomized with respect to potential confounders. When this is not possible, as is common in observational studies, confounding must be addressed analytically. For the specific and pervasive issue of population stratification in GWAS, the standard statistical approach is to use PCA. By calculating the principal components of the genetic data, researchers can capture the major axes of ancestral variation and include them as covariates in the regression model, thereby adjusting for their confounding effects [114]. For other known confounders, such as age, sex, or experimental batch, the standard method is to similarly include them as explicit covariates in the statistical model. However, when using complex models like deep neural networks, simply adding a confounder as an input feature is insufficient and can be misleading. If the confounder is processed through the same hidden layers as the primary predictors (i.e., the genetic variants), the model may learn to predict the outcome based on the confounder’s signal alone, rather than learning the confounder-adjusted effects of the genetic data. The correct architectural approach is to ensure that confounders are used for adjustment, not prediction. This can be achieved by designing a network where the confounder inputs have a separate path that is integrated with the main predictive pathway only at or near the final output layer. This forces the model to learn a representation of the genetic data that is independent of the confounder’s influence [115, 116]. More advanced techniques, such as adversarial deconfounding, take this a step further by adding a secondary objective to the model: to simultaneously predict the primary outcome while being unable to predict the confounding variable from its own internal representations, thus explicitly removing the confounding signal [117]. Care must always be taken, however, as incorrectly adjusting for a variable that is a collider, a variable influenced by both the genetic exposure and the outcome, can paradoxically introduce bias rather than remove it [118]. Conditioning on a collider by including it as a covariate can create a spurious association between the variables that influence it, even if no direct causal relationship exists. This unintended bias can distort the model’s results, and lead to incorrect conclusions [118].

Vulnerable models

Among the models examined in this paper (see Section Applications of AI in GWAS: Current Efforts, and Findings) that could be most affected by confounding factors are: DeepRisk, Graph-DNN Late-Onset Alzheimer’s Disease (LOAD) Classifier, and ResNet. DeepRisk is vulnerable because it uses recognized confounding factors (demographics) openly in the model, resulting in a high probability that the model will retain these less complex, non-genetic signals. The Graph-DNN LOAD Classifier model is vulnerable because it uses a latent representation that is more vulnerable to the influence of the most common confounding factor in genetics: population structure. ResNet as well could be affected by confounders, since it does not explicitly deploy strategies to address them in genotype–phenotype prediction [97, 99, 102].

Curse of dimensionality

The problem known as the “curse of dimensionality” is one of the most pronounced limitations of DL when applied to genetic datasets [119]. Genomic data exhibit a pronounced imbalance between a large number of variables (i.e., genetic variants), and a much smaller number of samples (i.e., participants in the cohort) [120]. The genomic domain is considered a big data domain in terms of data volume. This makes the training of DL models difficult, despite the fact that some repositories provide access to public genomic datasets. It is necessary to perform significant preprocessing, and harmonization of these datasets to properly train the model.

Vulnerable models

This challenge affects all models used in GWAS and beyond, as they often use hundreds or even millions of features. However, following we list those models that we believe to be particularly vulnerable to this problem, based on their architecture and data management: Deep-COMBI, DeepGenomeScan, Hybrid ANN-LSTM Stacking Model, ResNet, and Sequence Modeling NN. For each of these models, default regularization, and dropout methods are often insufficient to handle extremely high features-to-samples ratios in genomics. Without intensive preliminary selection of features to be included in the model or architectures specifically designed to handle extreme sparsity, these models are likely to be particularly vulnerable to the curse of dimensionality [88, 95, 96, 99].

Distributional differences

Distributional differences are common in GWAS data that may have differences in the distribution related to allele frequencies, for instance, due to ancestry. Accounting for distributional differences remains an open research topic. Several batch correction methods are commonly used, such as quantile normalization, empirical Bayes adjustment for variables measured with ComBat19, surrogate variable analysis to estimate, and correct unknown noise sources [121], and canonical correlation analysis, which allows the identification of common patterns between lots [122, 123]. It is worth noting that employing such strategies can inadvertently cause information loss between the training, and testing parts in cross-validation. More elaborate approaches, starting with domain adaptation, and transfer learning [124], include unsupervised feature transformations, and supervised learning of robust feature representations [125, 126]. An additional approach involves adversarial learning, a collection of techniques aimed at misleading models through the provision of misleading inputs. In particular, a model trained to predict the dataset from which each example originates can be used to generate penalties for the primary prediction task [127–129].

Vulnerable models

Among the methods reviewed in this study, these are most likely to be affected by this limitation: Bayesian Linear Model with Gaussian Priors, Combined LASSO and Ridge Regression Model, DEEP*HLA, DeepRisk, GA-Optimized MLP Trait Predictor, GenNet, GNN-AD Risk Model, LDpred Polygenic Effect Estimator, ResNet, and Spatial Pattern Recognition Network because they rely on population-specific genetic patterns (allele frequencies, LD, haplotypes). Differences between the training, and testing cohorts can reduce accuracy, bias predictions, and compromise interpretability [89, 91, 92, 99, 101, 102].

Hyperparameter tuning

Finally, the ML/DL model tuning is a key phase during the training step. In this phase, the initial results are examined to provide direction to the tuning process so that the parameters conform to the research questions and datasets. For instance, the learning rate, batch size, momentum, and weight decay are some of the crucial hyperparameters in DL architectures that require fine-tuning [130]. However any ML/DL model is characterized by a broad range of heterogeneous parameters to be optimized. If these hyperparameters are not properly tuned, problems, such as underfitting (i.e., it fails to capture the underlying patterns in the data) or overfitting (i.e., it becomes overfitted to the training data such that it will not work properly on new data not yet seen) may arise [131]. It is important to emphasize that hyperparameter optimization should only be performed on the training folds during cross-validation, using a rigorous analytical pipeline that prevents information leakage. Therefore, the relationship between hyperparameter optimization and information leakage is a well-established and critically important aspect of genomic AI. In genomics, researchers must balance these hyperparameters for model performance optimization, while avoiding the aforementioned issues. Only in this way it is possible to obtain a correct model. Many algorithmic strategies currently exist for hyperparameter tuning, such as Bayesian optimization, grid search, random search, evolutionary algorithms, and gradient-based optimization methods [132–136].

Vulnerable models

The following models are likely to be the most affected by this limitation: Bayesian Linear Model with Gaussian Priors, Combined LASSO and Ridge Regression Model, DEEP*HLA, DeepCOMBI, DeepRisk, GA-Optimized MLP Trait Predictor, GNN-AD Risk Model, LASSO-AD Risk Predictor, LDpred Polygenic Effect Estimator, PCA-Boruta Hybrid Model, Stacked Autoencoder-Association Rule Mining Analysis SAERMA, and Spatial Pattern Recognition Network, because these models employ complex architectural elements (e.g., CNNs, LSTMs, GNNs, Multitask Learning, Autoencoders) that rely on special hyperparameters. Finding the optimal configuration for these elements in the context of genomic data is non-trivial, and is computationally expensive, and consequently, they are highly susceptible to performance loss in the absence of extensive, and intensive resource tuning [89, 92, 93, 95, 101–103].

Information leakage

Occurs when the testing dataset inadvertently affects the training dataset, thereby leading to dependencies between examples, and overestimating the performance evaluation of the model, ultimately falsely suggesting new biological relationships [108]. Leakage can result from changes that collectively account for multiple examples, including standardization or evaluation of key aspects, or from supervised feature selection performed outside of cross-validation [108]. One solution is provided by ML toolkits, which help reduce losses by ensuring that transformations are learned only from the training set, and then applied independently to both training, and testing sets [108].

Vulnerable models

The following models can be vulnerable to information leakage because of their structure: AutoComplete, DeepRisk, GNN-AD Risk Model, LASSO-AD Risk Predictor, Modular DNN, and PCA-Boruta Hybrid Model. This is because they contain key dependencies in the data processing, and model training pipelines. Vulnerability does not necessarily lie in the intrinsic structure of the model itself, but rather in the organization of the analytical pipeline. Specifically, any stage in which data are transformed, such as feature selection, imputation, normalization, or graph construction, must be strictly limited to the training set before model adaptation. Failure to apply rigorous, and leak-proof cross-validation at each stage of the pipeline can result in the unintended use of test set information, leading to overestimated performance, unreproducible results, and biased conclusions about genomic associations [92, 93, 100, 102].

Interpretability

In DL and some complex ML models, the limitation of interpretability has been a constant concern, particularly in high-risk applications such as healthcare [137]. However, interpretability is not considered to be a dichotomous property, but rather a multidimensional one. For example, some models such as decision trees, rule-based models, and even penalized regression methods such as LASSO fall into the high interpretability category, as they allow the contribution of individual predictors to be quantified directly. In some cases, these models can be interpreted even more easily than traditional regression models, particularly if sparsity is applied [138].

In contrast, DL models such as DNNs, with multiple layers and thousands of parameters, are typically categorized as low interpretability models, or “black boxes”, because it is not easy to trace how these models arrive at their decisions [139]. To address these issues, recent advances in eXplainable AI (XAI) have developed post-hoc methods that aim to provide insight into how these models arrive at their decisions without compromising predictive performance, shifting them from low to medium interpretability. There are two widely used methods: SHAP [140] and Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations (LIME) [141]. SHAP, based on cooperative game theory, provides an importance value for each feature in making a prediction, based on Shapley values [140]. This can be particularly useful for quantifying individual contributions. LIME, on the other hand, provides explanations for individual predictions by approximating how these models arrive at those predictions [141].

Therefore, it can be argued that although DL models such as DNNs have been described as “black boxes” in terms of limited interpretability, recent advances in XAI have shown that these models could be made more interpretable, particularly in certain cases. For example, in some cases, these models can even provide information that goes beyond traditional measures such as effect sizes that can be provided in classical statistical models. For example, while logistic regression can provide an odds ratio representing an average effect, SHAP can even provide information about nonlinear effects and individual variation in feature contributions [140].

Vulnerable models

The models most likely to be affected by this limitation are: AutoComplete, DeepGWAS, DeepRisk, Dense Feedforward Network, Graph-DNN LOAD Classifier, GWAS-ML VIPN Predictor, Hybrid ANN-LSTM Stacking Model, JADBio, Modular DNN, PFI-based ML Comparison, SAERMA, 2023, Spatial Feature Extraction Network, and Spatial Pattern Recognition Network. Indeed, these models use complex architectures, with non-transparent internal representations. The incorporation of latent features, and multiple hierarchical levels obscures the explicit correspondence between input variants (e.g., SNPs), and predicted outcomes. Predefined feature importance methods provide, at best, approximate, and partial interpretability [88, 89, 92, 97, 98, 100, 102–105].

Sample dependency

Independent and Identically Distributed (IID) [110] is a major concern in the use of AI with genomic data arising in ML models and k-fold cross-validation methods. Genetic dependence is a common phenomenon in genetic studies and is generally overlooked. This can cause distortions in results and overestimate the performance of ML models [142–144].

Biological systems have inherent structures that cause dependence at various levels. For instance, LD causes statistical dependence among genomic locations, so these need special treatment in the analysis of GWASs [145]. Besides these biological dependencies, one of the most common situations where the IID assumption may be violated in ML models of genomic studies arises from the pooling of case-control datasets from various studies or cohorts into a single dataset. These datasets may be independent within each cohort or study. However, pooling these datasets may cause dependence due to various factors associated with each cohort or study. These factors may include genotyping platforms used in the studies, population ascertainment methods, phenotype definitions, or even laboratory procedures [146]. Pooling these datasets and treating them as IID and using a single ML model may cause the model to overfit cohort-specific biases rather than biological signals. These biases may cause overestimation of the model’s performance and may not perform well in independent validation datasets. These problems may be further complicated in k-fold cross-validation methods, where the samples are divided randomly without considering the cohort of origin. Models that explicitly account for covariance, such as mixed-effects models, can mitigate these dependencies. However, these models are computationally complex, and may not be widely adopted in ML approaches [10, 145, 147–149].

Vulnerable models

Among the reviewed works, GNN-AD Risk Model, LDpred Polygenic Effect Estimator, and ResNet could be the most affected by this limitation, because their methodology directly reflects the interdependencies between genetic data (LD, and kinship), therefore, they are prone to overfitting, and overestimation of performance, unless due attention is paid to dependency management or validation, and verification of results from independent datasets [89, 92, 99].

Unbalanced data

A supervised learning task is considered balanced when the examples are evenly distributed across outcome values. Real datasets are rarely perfectly balanced, which can bias the model training, and evaluation [150]. Such an imbalance can underlie models that over-learning the majority class, and under-learning the minority class, which might be particularly critical when the minority class is of primary interest, such as in disease detection. Imbalance is common in genomics, and requires researchers to use statistical methods to ensure the validity of the few positive examples found. For instance, conservative significance thresholds in alignment queries, GWAS projects, and motif scanning are essential for controlling false positives due to the low number of true positives compared to the size of the genome. Problems involving non-coding variants, such as predicting chromatin states, gene expression, and disease status, are particularly imbalanced [151]. Various strategies have been used to address class imbalance, including oversampling the minority class, undersampling the majority class, and weighting examples [152]. These methods emphasize the minority class but exhibit trade-offs: oversampling increases computation, undersampling discards data, and weighting requires optimal weight determination. Tools, such as the imbalanced-learn package for scikit-learn, and caret in R, help implement these strategies [153–155]. Although these methods often reduce the accuracy for the majority class, they increase it for the minority class, which is desirable. Balancing should be performed within the training fold to ensure that the model is evaluated against the expected class distribution. Rebalancing can uncalibrate the estimator, thereby affecting the predicted probabilities, but not necessarily the rankings, which can be corrected by post-processing. Performance metrics are affected differently by imbalances. Accuracy can be misleading; Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic, and Area Under the Precision-Recall Curve (AUROC, AUPRC) are alternatives. AUROC is influenced by false positives due to a high number of negatives, whereas AUPR is not, and is preferred for imbalanced problems. No metric is universally the best, therefore careful evaluation is necessary. Class imbalance also affects regression tasks, where varying the output distributions can bias the evaluation. Evaluating each output individually or in similar groups can mitigate this bias.

Vulnerable models

Models that may be vulnerable to imbalanced data include: DeepRisk, Dense Feedforward Network, Gradient Boosting Classifier, GWAS-ML VIPN Predictor, Hybrid ANN-LSTM Stacking Model, PFI-based ML Comparison, SAERMA, as they are sensitive to class distribution biases, and, without careful application of balancing strategies, their performance is likely to be overestimated or unreliable, particularly in detecting rare but clinically significant events [88, 98, 102, 103, 105]. While moderate levels of class imbalance may be addressed using standard procedures that involve class reweighting and resampling, commonly implemented within modern ML tools, severe imbalances, especially in the presence of rare disease classes, may still lead to biased training and testing of the predictive model [156]. Under these conditions, the predictive accuracy of the model may be artificially inflated unless careful attention is given to the choice of testing metrics [156].

Resources

GWAS is a broad field with many facets, and data sources. In this regard, in this section, we report some of the most important, and commonly used biobanks, and repositories that can be consulted. The main role of biobanks is to offer access to individual genotypic and phenotypic data collected from well-phenotyped cohorts. It helps in custom analyses of raw genomic and phenotypic data. On the other hand, repositories offer aggregated and disseminated GWAS summary statistics. These statistics include results of SNP-trait association studies conducted in previously published research studies. These resources help to perform meta-analyses without requiring individual-level data. Although some biobanks offer aggregated derived statistics, their main role is the collection of individual genomic and phenotypic data.

Main biobanks and repositories currently available and maintained

GWAS summary statistics are increasingly being made available to the public through dedicated repositories and publications associated with large biobank projects, which are accessible to researchers and allow for efficient and systematic consultation. Data were collected from cohorts of hundreds of thousands of genotyped individuals, and combined with questionnaires, laboratory measurements (e.g., collection of biological samples, such as blood, and saliva), and/or electronic health records of the participants. Often, participants are re-contacted over the years to assess the evolution of their health status, and thus obtain more accurate data [24].

Below we briefly describe 14 biobanks from nine different countries that provide large-scale individual-level genomic datasets, followed by examples of repositories that host GWAS summary statistics.

One of the most comprehensive databases in Europe is the UKB, which currently (as of January 3, 2025) includes data from 488,377 individuals from the United Kingdom, with an age at recruitment between 37, and 73 years (54% females) (https://www.nealelab.is/uk-biobank). The database contains data on 4,024 phenotypes, related to quantitative traits, anthropometric traits, blood cell traits, metabolites, cognitive traits, and brain imaging traits. In addition, UKB participants have been assessed for a wide range of phenotypes, including diet, work history, and health status [24, 172].

Although GWASs are historically Eurocentric (with 86.3% representation of the total number of studies up to 2019), efforts have been made in recent years to investigate individuals with ethnically diverse ancestry, and to identify differences in terms of genetic architecture between different populations [173]. Nevertheless, these studies are far from achieving fair representation of the global population. This is evidenced by the fact that to date, only 5.9% of GWAS datasets include East Asian participants [174]. In this regard, models trained on one population can be adapted to another using AI techniques, such as transfer learning, and domain adaptation, which show promise for reducing population biases. Inclusive data sharing practices, and the moral creation, and application of AI models are essential to the efficacy, and equity of such strategies.

In this respect, projects, such as BioBank Japan (BBJ) can be credited [160] as one of the largest Asian biobanks, with 199,982 participants (53.1% males, with an average age of 62.7 years, and 61.5 years for females, https://biobankjp.org/#gsc.tab=0). The project has conducted several genomic surveys, and identified genetic variations related to the susceptibility to 85 diseases (including malignant tumors, brain, respiratory, cardiovascular, metabolic, and pediatric diseases), as well as drug responses. Information was collected through DNA, and serum samples from participants, and surveys related to habits, such as smoking, diet, and alcohol consumption, body measurements, including height, weight, systolic, and diastolic blood pressure, and past health history [160].

African populations are also significantly underrepresented in GWAS, accounting for only approximately 1.1% of the population [174]. Moreover, despite the considerable genetic, and linguistic diversity within African populations, more than 90% of the ethnolinguistic groups lack genetic data. The lack of large-scale genomic studies in Africa is the result of many limitations, such as the lack of adequate research funding, and infrastructure from African governments. To overcome these limitations, the H3Africa database was created (http://www.h3africa.org), which consists of a cohort of 118,065 participants. The traits analyzed are related to kidney disease, rheumatic heart disease, AIDS, tuberculosis, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, stroke, cancer, and noncommunicable diseases [163].

The All of Us initiative aims to comprehensively map the genetic basis of complex diseases by enrolling 1 million individuals across the United States [38, 39]. To date, the program has provided genomic data from 245,388 participants, including a significant proportion of historically underrepresented communities [38, 39]. More than 1 billion genetic variants with a possible role in the occurrence of complex phenotypes have been identified, including many previously unreported SNPs. Researchers can access the data through the All of Us Researcher Workbench, with an average access time of approximately 29 hours [38, 39].

In addition to biobanks that provide individual-level genomic and phenotypic information, several repositories have been developed to collect and distribute GWAS summary statistics from published research. Among these, the GWAS Catalog is arguably one of the most comprehensive repositories [169]. As of July 2022, the GWAS Catalog contained approximately 400,000 SNP-trait associations, identified across more than 45,000 separate GWAS, in approximately 6,000 publications [169]. The data included a wide range of information, such as metadata on the publication, study design, samples, and traits investigated. The catalog includes sequence-based GWAS data, rare variant association tests, and copy number variation analysis. In addition, the data were reported in a standardized manner to facilitate comparisons between different studies [169].

While the summary statistics of the GWAS tend to be publicly accessible through the repositories, access to the individual genotypic and phenotypic data within the biobank tends to require an application and, in some cases, may even incur an access fee.

Taking into account the range of analytical methods applied in the field of AI-assisted GWAS, the following paragraph will focus on the most used and relevant set of evaluation metrics. These are grouped into three categories, each of which directly relates to the main challenges discussed in the current review.

Classification performance

The classification performance of AI models can be measured in a variety of ways, although the most commonly applied metric is AUROC, as it evaluates the performance of the model across all possible classification thresholds, resulting in a threshold-independent evaluation of the separation between the classes of case and control samples [175]. However, considering the fact that class imbalance is a significant challenge in many genomic datasets, in which the prevalence of disease is low, AUPRC has been found to be a more informative evaluation metric, as it specifically evaluates the performance of the minority class [176]. Similarly, the F1-score, or the harmonic mean of precision and recall, has been found to be of increasing importance, as it provides a balanced evaluation metric, even in the presence of class imbalance, in which the distribution of positive and negative classes may differ significantly [175].

Statistical reliability

GWAS are inherently subject to statistical problems, including multiple hypothesis testing and the need for accurate estimation of effect size. The FDR, typically controlled using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure, remains essential for managing the false positive rate among numerous associations, a critical consideration given the millions of tests performed in a typical GWAS [177]. In addition to this, the OR provides an interpretable measure of effect size for case-control studies, quantifying the strength of the association between genetic variants and disease status [178]. Together, these metrics address the limitations of multiple testing and the identification of causal variants, described above [178].

Interpretability

As discussed in Sect. 4, the “black box” nature of many of these models represents a notable challenge to the clinical adoption of these models. As a partial solution to these challenges, feature importance methods have emerged as a prominent tool to link the predictions of the models to individual genetic variants. The LRP and PFI metrics offer quantitative estimates of the contribution of individual features to the predictions of the models [179–181]. These methods offer a partial explanation of the predictions of the models, which represents a notable advancement towards reconciling the performance of the models with the underlying biology [179–181].

Validation strategies

In addition to the metrics discussed above, robust validation strategies offer a vital tool to ensure the generalization of the models. As discussed above, nested cross-validation represents a vital tool to ensure avoidance of overfitting, which represents a notable challenge to the performance of these models [179–181]. Group cross-validation represents another tool to account for correlations between the data, which might otherwise be ignored using conventional cross-validation [179–181].

The metrics and validation strategies described above represent the most commonly used tools in the reviewed manuscripts. These offer a direct solution to challenges related to classification accuracy, statistical rigor, and interpretability, which are key challenges in the field of AI-driven GWAS. For readers interested in the technical details of the metrics described above, Supplementary Material 4 provides a comprehensive table of all the metrics described above, including formulas, definitions, and terminology.

Conclusions

The application of AI to GWAS may overcome some of the key limitations of traditional analytical methods. Importantly, the development of these approaches could allow for the analysis of complex genetic interactions, and clarification of the genetic architecture of complex diseases. In this respect, genomic data are rapidly reaching sufficient quantities to allow the training of new, and more efficient AI algorithms, thus providing fertile ground for progress in the field. Despite the notable examples presented above, current AI methods are not yet capable of capturing genetic interactions entirety, nor do they consider how environmental factors affect gene expression. Furthermore, the interpretability of these models is insufficient for their application in precision medicine. Beyond these methodological hurdles, a more fundamental gap exists between the development of AI-GWAS models and their actual clinical utility. This review finds that while precision medicine is often the stated goal, the field has yet to produce significant evidence of meaningful translational impact. This shortfall can be attributed to two critical, sequential failures in the translational pipeline: a lack of rigorous external validation and a subsequent lack of demonstrated clinical utility [182, 183]. First, the vast majority of novel AI-GWAS models are assessed only on internal data, which provides no guarantee of the model’s generalization. External validation, the process of testing a model’s performance on entirely independent datasets, preferably from different populations, ancestries, and clinical settings, is the absolute minimum requirement for assessing its real-world robustness [184, 185]. Without it, a model’s high performance is likely attributable to overfitting, rendering it useless for any population outside of the one it was trained on. Second, even for the few models that have undergone external validation, there is a scarcity of evidence for true clinical utility. Clinical utility is not merely predictive accuracy; it is the demonstrated ability of a model to prospectively improve patient outcomes, change clinical decision-making for the better, or provide a net benefit to the healthcare system [186]. Proving this requires resource-intensive prospective studies and, ideally, randomized controlled trials, which remain exceedingly rare in the field [187]. Consequently, while AI-GWAS continues to excel at generating novel predictive models and potential biomarkers, it has not yet crossed the critical threshold from academic exercise to clinically actionable tool. Future studies will need to address these limitations, and they will likely explore many directions, such as the following:

Federated learning: Allowing decentralized model training within institutions without sharing raw data, federated AI offers a solution to privacy-preserving GWAS on heterogeneous, and globally distributed datasets [188].

Combining multi-omics data: Combining genomic, transcriptomic, epigenomic, proteomic, and environmental data can significantly enhance the capacity of AI models to identify causal disease mechanisms [189].

AI in Real-Time Clinical Contexts: Embedding AI models within Electronic Health Records (EHRs) can facilitate real-time risk calculation, and personalized treatment planning, thus advancing the field into truly AI-based precision medicine [190].

Overall, these research directions will require not only methodological creativity but also sustained efforts at ethical data sharing, standardization, and interdisciplinary research focused on demonstrating clinical validity and utility.
