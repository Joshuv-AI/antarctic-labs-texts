Viral infections: The Biothermodynamics Point of View
Authors: Fleming Martínez, William E. Acree Jr
Published: 2026

More than 100 years ago, the British physicist Lord Ernest Rutherford (1871-1937) quoted that science could be divided in two categories, namely, “physics” and “stamp collecting”. However, surely, he would have been totally surprised to see the great transformation of biology from simple “stamp collection” into the modern biochemistry and biophysics, along the 20th century. In this context, several years before Rutherford, the English chemist Sir Edward Frankland (1825-1899) expressed his opinion as “I am convinced that the future progress of chemistry as an exact science depend very much upon the alliance with mathematics” ( 1 ). Otherwise, the French mathematician Henri Poincare (1854-1912) pointed out that knowledge turns into science as it is described mathematically ( 2 ). Of course, Frankland and Poincare were correct as demonstrated by the recent developments in mathematical chemistry and biology, in particular in the field of biophysical chemistry ( 3 ).

Regarding the mathematization of biology a clear example is the development of theoretical and applied biothermodynamics, i.e. the thermodynamic descrip-tion of all living beings ( 4 , 5 ). Thus, the applications and significance of biothermodynamics imply a wide range of items across different fields, that includes medicine, bioengineering, and ecology.For instance, i) drug design and development, ii) bioengineering and synthetic biology, iii) ecology and ecosystem dynamics, iv), biomedical research, and v) understanding biological systems ( 5 ).

Biological systems include the behavior of microorganisms varying from virus to bacteria and even to protozoa. In turn, owing the role of some of these biological agents in infectious diseases that have been affecting humanity from the beginning of history, the application of biothermodynamic principles to understand some viral infections is a successful goal of modern science ( 6 ).

Some studies about thermodynamic description of viruses involved the analysis of capsid assembly and viral evolution ( 7 , 8 ), as well as several other investigations as summarized earlier ( 6 ). The same review article reported the evolution of this subject from the beginning of classical thermodynamics in the 18th and 19th centuries through biothermodynamics of viruses in the 1950s, to the modern concept of virothermodynamics in the last decade ( 6 , 9 ).

The practical usefulness of modern virothermodynamics is based on the Gibbs energy associated to viralus replication, which in turn is calculated by means of the Hess’s law appli,ed involving taking into account the proportion of every element present in the principal biomacromolecules, mainly, carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, sulfur and phosphorous. In this way, as the more negative the Gibbs energy, the is more favorable the viralus replication also isbecomes ( 10 , 11 ). For successfulness of this new tool, the exact elementary composition of virus is required. Process favorability has been used for the understanding of the virus infection propagation as well as to identify the body tissues and/or organs most affected following infection.

Specifically, the following virus and viral diseases have been studied: SARS, MERS and SARS-CoV-2 (COVID-19) and some variants like Omicron BA.2.86 Pirola, EG.5.1, XBB.1.16 Arcturus, CH.1.1 and BN.1 ( 11 - 19 ), Monkeypox and Vaccinia viruses ( 20 ), West Nile virus ( 21 ), Coxsackieviruses A and B ( 22 ), Dengue virus ( 23 ), Rotavirus A ( 24 ), norovirus ( 25 ), Chikungunya virus ( 26 ), and Yellow fever virus ( 27 ).

It is important to note that owing the increasing risk of the apparition of new local or pandemic viral infections in modern society the global efforts of biophysicochemical science to understand and even stop them will be one of our more important duties as academicians.

Furthermore, owing the worldwide increasing in cancer cases, our educated guess is that one or more research groups will study viruses that lead to cancer in the not too distant future as expected from a recent review article about oncogenic viral pathways as drivers of viral cancers ( 28 ). As these authors mention in the respective Abstract: “Henceforth owing to viral and host complexities in viral carcinogenesis, a singular mechanistic pathway remains yet to be established; hence there is an urgent need to integrate concepts from system biology, cancer microenvironment, evolutionary perspective, and thermodynamics to understand the role of viruses as drivers of cancer” ( 28 ).
