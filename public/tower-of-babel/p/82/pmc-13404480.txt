What factors were associated with human papillomavirus vaccine hesitancy among parents of adolescent girls? A mixed-methods study in Southwestern Nigeria
Authors: Abdul-Afeez Abimbola Adedeji, Segun Bello
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionVaccine hesitancy contributes to the dampened public enthusiasm for vaccination in several countries, including Nigeria. Human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccine acceptance is influenced by various factors; studies have examined some of these factors in sub-Saharan Africa, including Nigeria. This study aimed to examine HPV vaccine hesitancy and associated factors among parents of adolescent girls in Ibadan North East Local Government Area based on the WHO 3C model.MethodsA cross-sectional mixed-methods study was conducted, comprising four Focus Group Discussion sessions with parents and semistructured, interviewer-administered questionnaires, using a multistage sampling procedure to select 402 parents. Quantitative data were analysed using descriptive statistics, χ2 tests and logistic regression analysis with IBM SPSS V.22. Qualitative data were thematically analysed using ATLAS.ti V.25.ResultsThe mean age of parents was 43.7±7.4 years, 66.9% were women, 50.7% identified as Christians and 95.0% belonged to the Yoruba ethnic group. Respondents were knowledgeable about HPV infection (51.5%) and HPV vaccine (64.7%); 61.4% had a positive attitude towards HPV vaccination. About 27.4% were hesitant to receive the HPV vaccine. The perceived necessity of vaccine (adjusted OR (AOR)=4.61, CI 1.14 to 18.72), trust in the efficacy of vaccines (OR=3.54, CI 1.09 to 11.51) and HPV vaccine safety (AOR=8.46, CI 2.73 to 26.18) had a higher influence on HPV vaccine hesitancy, while availability of the vaccine (AOR=0.23, CI 0.11 to 0.50) had a lesser influence.ConclusionsThere was a significant level of HPV vaccine hesitancy among parents of adolescent girls, and associating factors included limited knowledge about HPV and its vaccine, as well as concerns about vaccine safety, vaccine necessity and vaccine availability. There is a need for targeted public health interventions that contribute to the prevention of HPV-related diseases.Keywords: Human Papillomavirus Viruses, Vaccination, Adolescent

Introduction

Human papillomavirus (HPV) is the most common sexually transmitted viral infection, which leads to over 90% of cases of cervical cancer in women.1 HPV infection, usually asymptomatic, can also cause a range of conditions in men and women, including other types of anogenital cancers (vagina, vulva, anus, penis), genital warts and head and neck cancers.1,2 In Nigeria, cervical cancer is the second most common cancer in women, contributing an estimated 12 075 new cases and 7968 deaths annually to the global burden.2 Strategies recommended by WHO have been adopted to reduce the burden of cervical cancer caused by HPV, including 90% of girls being fully vaccinated with HPV vaccines by age 15, 70% of women being screened with a high-performance test by 35 years of age and again by 45 years of age and 90% of women identified with cervical cancer receiving treatment.3

Vaccination against HPV is the most effective public health intervention for preventing persistent infection with high-risk HPV types, cervical cancer and other HPV-related cancers.3 There are currently six HPV vaccines prequalified by the WHO; Cervarix, Cecolin, Gardasil 4, Walrivax, Cervavax and Gardasil 9. The bivalent HPV vaccines contain virus-like particles to protect against high-risk HPV types 16 and 18; the quadrivalent vaccine against HPV types 6, 11, 16, 18 and the nonavalent vaccine against high-risk HPV types 6, 11, 16, 18, 31, 33, 45, 52 and 58.2 It is recommended for all girls aged 9–14 years as a two-dose schedule (6 months apart) before they become sexually active.4

As of 2023, 133 countries have introduced the HPV vaccine globally and 24 countries in Africa, including Nigeria.5 The introduction of HPV vaccination was considered suboptimal in many countries, including countries in Africa, with the HPV vaccination coverage by age 15 estimated at 22% in 24 (~51%) of the 47 WHO Africa Member States.5 HPV vaccination coverage in Nigeria for women was estimated at 48.56% as of July 2023.5 A study in Ibadan6 reported a low uptake of the HPV vaccine among adolescent girls between 15 and 17 years, 83.1% of the respondents had not received an HPV vaccine before. In 2023, the Federal Government of Nigeria introduced the HPV vaccine into the routine immunisation (RI) schedule and integrated it with other vaccines during supplementary vaccination activities.7 A phased approach was adopted: the first phase was conducted in 15 states, including the Federal Capital Territory in the last quarter of 2023, and the other 21 states, including Oyo state, in May 2024.7,8 This mass vaccination campaign was aimed at vaccinating about 7.7 million girls aged 9–14 years by the end of 2024 and about 16 million eligible girls by 2025.7 According to the Oyo State Primary HealthCare board, the Multi-Age Cohort campaign in Oyo state was concluded with a total coverage of 98%, vaccinating girls aged 9–14 years.8

The WHO’s definition of vaccine hesitancy refers to the delay in acceptance or refusal of safe vaccines despite the availability of vaccination services.9 Vaccine hesitancy has, over the years, become a huge challenge to vaccine uptake by end-users, making it a public health challenge.10 Current data reveal a worrying trend where eligible populations either delay or refuse the HPV vaccine, driven by factors such as lack of confidence in vaccine safety and efficacy, misinformation and inadequate healthcare recommendations.11,12 Parental consent and acceptance are essential before administration because the targeted population is regarded as ‘legal minors’ in Nigeria.13 Studies have shown a high level of hesitancy in HPV vaccine uptake both from parents and eligible girls in Ethiopia and the USA.11,14

Similar studies done in China, India and Jordan showed that there are disparities between the willingness to vaccinate and the actual vaccination behaviour which are often determined by the level of knowledge and perception about the disease and vaccine.15 The increasing rates of vaccine hesitancy pose a threat to global health and the targets under Sustainable Development Goal 3 on good health and well-being. A better understanding of the determinants of vaccine hesitancy is pivotal to designing effective policies to boost immunisation rates.16 The SAGE working group on vaccine hesitancy proposed the 3Cs model to comprehensively analyse determinants of vaccine hesitancy from three domains, which include complacency, convenience and confidence.17 This model has been adopted in studies to analyse COVID-19 and influenza vaccine hesitancy.17,18 Previous studies done in Oyo6 and Lagos13 have focused on parental intention, willingness, awareness and health worker recommendation. This study investigated the prevalence and associated determinants of HPV vaccine hesitancy among parents of adolescent girls in Ibadan North East Local Government Area (LGA), Oyo State, Nigeria, thus providing evidence-based insights to inform public health strategies.

Methods

Study setting

The study was conducted between 19 December 2024 and 23 January 2025, in Ibadan North East LGA, one of the most populous LGAs in Oyo State, with a land area of about 19.9 km2. Ibadan North East LGA borders with Egbeda, Ona Ara, Ibadan North, Lagelu, Akinyele and Ibadan South East LGAs. The area has 12 administrative wards, which are mainly urban residential areas and the commercial hub of Ibadan.19

Ibadan North East LGA hosts about 50 healthcare facilities accessible to the residents20 for their healthcare needs. Healthcare facilities, such as primary and secondary health facilities, are strategically located in various communities. They offer basic medical care, immunisation, maternal and child health services, health education and comprehensive medical services, playing a crucial role in preventive and curative healthcare respectively.21

Prior to the introduction of the HPV vaccine to the Nigerian RI schedule, the subsidised HPV vaccine was available and could be procured from selected healthcare facilities across Nigeria.22 The high cost of the vaccine at the time made it inaccessible for the average Nigerian family, resulting in few adolescents being vaccinated.23 After the routinisation of the HPV vaccine in 2024, there was still a low awareness and acceptance of the HPV vaccine among adolescents and adults in Oyo state.24,25

Study design

The study was a cross-sectional study using a convergent parallel mixed-methods approach comprising Focus Group Discussions (FGDs) and a survey using semistructured interviewer-administered questionnaires.

Study population

The study was conducted among parents (mothers or fathers) of adolescent girls in Ibadan North East LGA, Oyo State.

Participants’ selection

Participants included in the study were parents of adolescent girls aged 10–19 years residing within the study area for more than a year and gave written consent to participate in the study.

A multistage sampling procedure was used to identify the respondents. Eight out of the 12 administrative wards were randomly selected for the study using balloting. These wards included Agugu, Alafara, Basorun, Oke Adu, Ode Aje, Oje, Aperin and Ojagbo. A total of 40 settlements were then sampled, with five settlements selected from each of the eight selected wards in the LGA using the master list of all settlements and simple random sampling using balloting. Due to the population density in the study area, houses were systematically selected with an interval of two houses from the start point and sampled from each settlement to achieve adequate spatial coverage across households. The start point in each settlement was a known landmark in the settlement. Finally, an eligible parent (male or female) was randomly selected in each selected house, irrespective of the number of eligible parents in each house. Houses were skipped in the event that the house had no eligible parent or the parent was absent during the time of the interview.

Participants for the FGD were recruited from those who participated in the quantitative data collection. Out of the eight preselected administrative wards for questionnaire administration, four were randomly selected for the FGDs. These wards included Agugu, Basorun, Ode Aje and Oje.

The patient and public statement

The Local Health Authority in Ibadan North East was involved in the conduct of the study, especially during the FGDs. The results of this study will be shared with the Department of Health Education in the authority.

Procedure for data collection

The quantitative and qualitative data were collected between 19 December 2024 and 23 January 2025. It conducted four FGDs with the parents of adolescent girls27,28 in the study area. All FGDs were conducted primarily in English; the native language, Yoruba, was used based on the participants’ request. The FGDs followed a guide developed to cover the following key topics: HPV vaccination knowledge, confidence, complacency, convenience and ideas for improving uptake (online supplemental file 2). The sessions took a minimum duration of 40 min, with each session having between 5 and 6 participants who fulfilled the inclusion criteria. The discussions during the sessions were audiorecorded on getting an informed consent from participants. Key notes were captured by a trained note taker present during the sessions.

A semistructured interviewer-administered questionnaire was adapted from a validated instrument,15 having four sections to determine HPV vaccine hesitancy, identify determinants by adopting the ‘3Cs’ model of vaccine hesitancy and assess the knowledge, attitude and belief about the HPV vaccine, and sociodemographic characteristics of parents (online supplemental file 1). The section on attitude had 12 questions concerning the ‘3Cs’ domains to measure the parents’ attitudes. Four questions on complacency, as well as three questions on their convenience and five questions on their confidence in HPV vaccination. The knowledge section had 10 questions on the knowledge about HPV infection and the HPV vaccine to examine parents’ cognition. The sociodemographic section collected parents’ information on age, gender, ethnicity, religion, occupation, marital status, education level, number of adolescent girls and family type. The question that measured vaccine hesitancy was answered with one of the following responses: ‘acceptance without hesitance’, ‘hesitance’ and ‘refusal’. The tool was pretested before being used for this study. This tool consistently measured the intended construct; the Cronbach’s alpha result showed good reliability (α=0.73).

Measurement of variables

Dependent variable: vaccine hesitancy (hesitance/refusal vs acceptance).

Independent variable: sociodemographic characteristics, knowledge and attitude of the parents towards HPV infection and vaccination based on the 3Cs (complacency, confidence and convenience).

The knowledge of parents on HPV and the HPV vaccine was measured on a three-point scale of ‘Yes’, ‘I don’t know’ and ‘No’, while the scores of 3, 2 and 1 were assigned for correct, no idea and incorrect responses, respectively. Attitudes and beliefs of parents on the HPV vaccine were measured on a 5-point scale of strongly agree, agree, undecided, disagree and strongly disagree. Scores of 5, 4, 3, 2 and 1 were assigned for positive statements and the reverse for negative statements. The threshold of 15 points represented the midpoint of the obtainable knowledge score range and was used as the predetermined criterion for distinguishing between good and poor knowledge. A similar assessment was adopted for the attitude categorisation. The data from the FGD session were discussed under four themes, including level of HPV vaccine hesitancy, knowledge and sources of information, factors associated with HPV vaccine hesitancy and ways of improvement.

Data analysis and management

The qualitative data were analysed thematically using AtlasTi V.25 for data management. All data collected in Yoruba were translated into English before analysis. All recordings were transcribed, harmonised and properly verified with notes taken during the FGD session. The thematic analysis process included: transcribing the recordings, collating the responses, generating initial codes from the research questions, attaching codes to significant quotes, examining the codes and looking for patterns and connections to cluster similar codes and create candidate themes, developing themes and writing the synthesis of each theme and attaching illustrative quotes. The mixed-methods integration approach was employed, whereby concordance between quantitative and qualitative findings was examined to derive inferential interpretations addressing the research questions.

Quantitative data collected were analysed using SPSS V.22. The knowledge of parents about HPV and the HPV vaccine was categorised into good or poor. The knowledge scale had a minimum obtainable score of 1 and a maximum obtainable score of 20. The good knowledge was categorised based on scores above or equal to 15 points. The poor knowledge was categorised based on scores below 15 points. The responses on attitude were computed and categorised into positive or negative attitudes. The attitude scale ranged from 16 to 46. The positive attitude was categorised based on scores above 35 points. The negative attitude was categorised based on scores below 35 points. Responses on HPV vaccine hesitancy were analysed into ‘Hesitance’ or ‘Acceptance’ with hesitance encompassing both hesitance and refusal. There were no missing data as the tool used was interviewer-administered. Descriptive statistics using frequencies, percentages, mean and SD were used to present the distribution of the sociodemographic data, level of knowledge, HPV vaccine hesitancy and level of attitude of parents based on the 3C domains. χ2 test and logistic regression were used to identify factors influencing HPV vaccine hesitancy by adjusting for confounders such as sociodemographic data (eg, educational qualification, occupation, number of girls, family type), level of knowledge and attitude at a 95% CI (α=0.05 level of significance). Multicollinearity diagnostics showed that all independent variables had VIF (Variance Inflation Factor) values below 5 and tolerance values above 0.2, indicating no evidence of multicollinearity in the model. The goodness-of-fit was assessed with the Hosmer-Lemeshow test, which was not statistically significant (p>0.05), indicating good fit.

Knowledge of parents about HPV infection and vaccine

Overall, 51.5% of the respondents were knowledgeable about HPV infection, and 64.7% knew about the HPV vaccine. More than half, 247 (58.2%), of the respondents believed HPV infection is sexually transmitted. About 195 (48.5%) knew that HPV can be prevented by the use of a condom, 187 (46.5%) believed HPV can also be prevented by having only one sexual partner. Only 135 (33.6%) knew HPV does not affect women only, and 260 (64.7%) thought HPV infection can lead to cervical cancer. About three-quarters, 304 (75.7%), of the respondents had heard of the HPV vaccine and 272 (67.7%) knew the vaccine prevents HPV infection. About 235 (58.5%) of the respondents knew the HPV vaccine prevents cervical cancer, 241 (60%) did not know the vaccine also prevents genital warts and 309 (76.9%) knew that the HPV vaccine is available in Nigeria. Attitude and beliefs of parents about HPV and the vaccine

Above two-thirds, 289 (71.9%) agreed that HPV infection can lead to serious health complications, 234 (58.2%) of the respondents agreed that everyone is at risk of HPV infection, 231 (57.5%) disagreed that HPV vaccination will promote promiscuous behaviours, 290 (72.1%) agreed that HPV vaccination is necessary and effective for preventing HPV infection, 288 (71.6%) agreed that the HPV vaccine is highly effective, 295 (73.4%) agreed that the HPV vaccine is safe for their child, 316 (78.6%) agreed that the government provides safe vaccines, 343 (85.3%) agreed that the vaccine-related advice provided by medical staff is reliable, 192 (47.8%) agreed to have heard negative information about the vaccine. About 162 (40.3%) disagreed that the cost prevents them from vaccinating against HPV, 246 (61.2%) disagreed that time/distance prevents them from vaccinating against HPV and 203 (50.5%) did not know if the HPV vaccine is available at the nearest health facility to them (see online supplemental figure 1).

Association between 3Cs and HPV vaccine hesitancy of parents

Online supplemental table 1 shows the association between the WHO 3Cs model of vaccine hesitancy and the level of HPV vaccine hesitancy. Factors influencing HPV vaccine hesitancy among parents

The results of the logistic regression that adjusted for confounders to identify influencing factors of HPV vaccine hesitancy based on the 3Cs domains are shown in table 3. The confounders were included from the statistically significant factors associated with HPV vaccine hesitancy (see table 2). The findings suggested that parents who were in an extended family (adjusted OR (AOR)=2.38, 95% CI 1.20 to 4.73; p=0.01) were more likely to be hesitant than those in a nuclear family type. Parents who disagreed with the necessity of HPV vaccination (AOR=4.61, 95% CI 1.14 to 18.72; p=0.03) were more likely to be hesitant than those who perceived HPV vaccination as a necessity. Parents who perceived low efficacy in the HPV vaccination were more likely to be hesitant towards vaccination (AOR=3.54, 95% CI 1.09 to 11.51; p=0.04). Parents who perceived that the HPV vaccine was unsafe were more likely to be hesitant towards HPV vaccination (AOR=8.46, 95% CI 2.73 to 26.18; p=0.001). Parents who were not aware or certain about the availability of the HPV vaccine at the nearest health facility (AOR=0.23, 95% CI 0.11 to 0.49; p=0.001) were less likely to be hesitant than those who were aware of the availability.

Qualitative results

Four FGDs were conducted with 22 parents of adolescent girls. The majority of the participants (54.5%) were women. Participants had a mean age of 46.1±6.9. 14 (63.6%) participants were traders, and 13 (59.1%) were Muslims. All individuals included in the discussion were both those who were hesitant and those who had accepted. The following themes were explored.

Theme I: level of HPV vaccine hesitancy

Participants highlighted the acceptance of the HPV vaccine for their children when offered for free. The findings revealed that more than half 12 (54.5%) of the parents were hesitant to vaccinate their children. These decisions were influenced by factors which were later identified over the course of the discussion. Here was their comment:

Like I said earlier, I don’t know much about the vaccine, so I will make my findings first before accepting. (FGD 1 – R1)

Parents mentioned that there had been negative information spreading about the vaccine, especially on social media.

I heard it through social media (Facebook and WhatsApp), and the information I heard was that it is not a good vaccine and it is a new vaccine that is intended to harm the children. (FGD 3 – R2)

Findings showed that the parents who were hesitant required the right information for them to decide to accept the HPV vaccine. Seven (31.82%) parents had accepted the vaccine without hesitation.

Yes, I will accept it because the vaccine is very effective. (FGD 2 – R2)

Yes, I will accept it, as I said before, I accepted that my child should be vaccinated during the campaign. (FGD 3 – R4)

Theme II: knowledge and source of information

The vast majority of parents acknowledged that they do not know enough about the vaccine. 13 (59.1%) of the participants were not well informed about the HPV vaccine. Many did not find out about the importance of the vaccine and what it protects children against. A parent heard the vaccine was a weapon targeted at children.

I have never heard of this vaccine before; the only thing I heard was that there was a new vaccine that will be weaponized against our children. (FGD 3 – R3)

Findings revealed that some of the parents knew about the HPV vaccine but did not have detailed knowledge of the vaccine. Nine (40.9%) of the participants had good but insufficient knowledge about the HPV vaccine.

Yes, I have heard about the vaccine. I think it is a new vaccine for me. However, when I learned about the vaccine last year (2024), it was said to be an existing vaccine for preventing cervical cancer. (FGD 2 – R5)

Theme III: factors associated with HPV vaccine hesitancy

The potential risks highlighted included the parents’ beliefs and opinions, lack of knowledge and information about the vaccine, questions about long-term vaccine safety, trust in health workers, perceived vaccine benefits, vaccine availability, affordability and distance barrier.

Findings revealed that having sufficient knowledge of HPV infection and the vaccine contributed to acceptance. Parents emphasised clear explanation of the importance of the vaccine to them is essential to guide their decision. Educating parents on the importance of the vaccine may result in improving the acceptance of the vaccine.

If I am aware that the vaccine is good for my children, I will take my child to the hospital for vaccination. (FGD 1 – R4)

I got my children vaccinated because the health workers explained the importance of the vaccine to me. (FGD 1 –R2)

The safety of the vaccine was one of the concerns of the parents. There were parents who do not accept the injectable vaccine due to safety concerns and past experiences.

I will accept only after I know it is safe for my child. (FGD 3 – R5)

No, I don't accept injectable vaccines. (FGD 4 –R5)

Recommendations from health workers were also mentioned to be a contributing factor to vaccine acceptance. The parents believed that health workers have more in-depth and verified knowledge about vaccinations, and due to the relationship built between health workers and parents, it helps parents to decide on their children’s vaccinations.

The advice I get from healthcare workers helps me to decide on my children’s immunization. (FGD 1 – R5)

Recommendations from health workers often influence my consent for vaccination. (FGD 2 – R1)

The lack of understanding of the benefits of the vaccine contributed in a lot of parental hesitance to accepting the HPV vaccine. The parents who understood the benefits of getting their children vaccinated against HPV infection mentioned that they never hesitated to accept the vaccine.

I can accept only after I have verified the importance and benefits. (FGD 2 – R6)

Yes, I have heard about it. It is used for preventing cervical cancer. (FGD 4 – R1)

Free immunisation services in some health facilities were found encouraging, as the cost of immunisation was suggested as an influencing factor for the uptake of vaccines.

Many times, my husband has played a huge role in that aspect and also friends, healthcare workers and the government, especially for making healthcare free for children. It is encouraging. (FGD 1 – R3)

Travel time/distance and availability of vaccine were indicated as part of the factors that influence the uptake of vaccines among parents.

There is a healthcare facility close by, but I do not know if the vaccine is available. (FGD 2 – R6)

Theme IV: ways for improvements

Parents suggested strategies which included creating awareness, strengthening the relationship between healthcare workers and stakeholders, inclusion into supplementary immunisation activities and the retraining of healthcare workers.

Almost all parents recommended more awareness of the benefits of the HPV vaccine and its availability at various health facilities. Findings stated there were rumours and misinformation about the HPV vaccination, which the awareness creation will help control.

More awareness is needed to spread the information on the importance and availability of the vaccine. (FGD 3 – R2

Parents suggested that a strong relationship between the healthcare workers and the community members would build trust and enable parents to make decisions based on the recommendations of the health workers.

I think the primary healthcare workers should have a good relationship with the local attendants because many of us prioritize them and trust them. If they can link with them, it can help with the immunization. (FGD 3 – R5)

Health workers should collaborate with local healers and local birth attendants to educate them and also help to reach people who do not go to health facilities. (FGD 4 – R2

Some parents also suggested that the vaccination should be included in other immunisation activities such as outreaches, immunisation campaigns and so on.

They can include in the regular immunization activities in the community and create more awareness. (FGD 1 – R4)

The study findings revealed that the parents had experienced unpleasant attitudes from healthcare workers, and there were also encounters where healthcare workers were unable to communicate the importance and benefit of the vaccine to guide the decision to vaccinate their children.

There should be a retention for health workers in order to give a clear explanation to parents on the importance of the vaccine. (FGD 4 – R3)

Discussion

This study investigated and revealed a gap in the acceptance of the HPV vaccine by parents, level of knowledge about HPV infection and vaccination, attitude and beliefs about the vaccine and the associated factors of the hesitancy. This study contributes to existing literature by addressing a critical gap, specifically by quantifying the level of HPV vaccine hesitancy and identifying the factors associated with the observed hesitancy. The study revealed that a significant number of parents were hesitant to vaccinate their daughters against HPV. The findings from the FGDs also revealed a similar level of hesitance, and the decision was influenced by factors which were later identified. These findings align with a study in northern Nigeria where about one-third of parents would choose not to vaccinate their children against the HPV even if the vaccine was free or subsidised.20 The consistency of these findings suggests that HPV vaccine hesitancy may be driven by common underlying factors that transcend regional and sociocultural differences, pointing to broader systemic, informational or belief-related influences. The observed hesitancy and refusal rates in this study suggest the need for targeted, culturally sensitive educational programmes by community health workers to address concerns and misconceptions among parents. This finding also correlates with the level of vaccine hesitancy among parents in high-income countries, with 33.3% in Italy,29 23% in the USA14 and low-income countries like Ethiopia, with 39.02% HPV vaccine hesitancy among school girls.11

Although the study findings showed that parents had an overall good knowledge of HPV infection and vaccination, there were existing disparities. The discussion with parents revealed that the vast majority acknowledged they do not know enough about the vaccine. This disparity in knowledge levels is consistent with findings from a study in Ogun State, Nigeria, where awareness campaigns were instrumental in improving knowledge about the HPV vaccine among parents and caregivers30 compared with the low awareness in Ibadan, Oyo State.31 Studies carried out in different parts of Nigeria have reported both poor31,32 and good13 parents’ knowledge on HPV infection and HPV vaccination with the nationwide awareness. The suggested association between knowledge levels and vaccine acceptance highlights the importance of educational initiatives in promoting vaccine uptake.30

The study findings showed that overall, three in five parents had a positive attitude towards HPV vaccination, while others had a negative attitude. The findings suggested that the majority of the parents understood and had a positive attitude towards the risk of HPV infection and the benefits of the HPV vaccine, trust the effectiveness and safety of the vaccine. Parents highlighted that their attitude was shaped by their previous knowledge about the vaccine. The presence of negative attitudes aligns with evidence from a study in sub-Saharan Africa suggesting that systemic barriers, including concerns about vaccine safety, lack of trust in healthcare providers and cultural beliefs, influence HPV vaccination.33 This reinforces the critical role of awareness and health education in promoting vaccine acceptance through the engagement of community leaders.

The study findings suggested several associated factors with HPV vaccine hesitancy, including ethnicity, religion, education level, occupation, family type, attitude and knowledge about HPV infection and vaccination. Limited access to accurate information, combined with cultural beliefs and misconceptions, may have contributed to their hesitance based on the findings from the FGD. This suggests that targeted engagement with religious and community leaders could be an effective strategy in reducing hesitancy.22 Parents with poor knowledge about HPV and its vaccine demonstrated higher hesitancy, similar to the study done in Ilorin,34 emphasising the importance of awareness campaigns and targeted educational interventions. Addressing misconceptions through well-structured health education programmes, community outreach and social media campaigns by the Local Primary Healthcare Board in collaboration with community-based organisations could help improve HPV vaccine acceptance. The study conducted in Lagos state recommended health education programmes focused on increasing awareness of the availability and knowledge of the HPV vaccine.35 A study in Nigeria highlighted that trust in government and health authorities significantly impacts attitudes towards vaccination. Mistrust can lead to negative perceptions and reduced vaccine uptake.36 In this study, findings indicated that the hesitancy rate was attributed to concerns about vaccine safety and efficacy, underscoring the need for transparent communication from health authorities to the public. The finding also suggested that acceptance by some parents was influenced by the recommendations from healthcare workers and the school authority. This correlates with a study done in Ibadan37 that reported that healthcare providers were trusted information sources for parents to get recommendations on vaccination. This also emphasises the need for regular capacity-building activities for healthcare providers at the facility level to provide evidence-based information to build parents’ confidence in the vaccine.

Findings showed that parents did not have a clear understanding of the risk of HPV infection and the benefits of vaccination. Barriers such as distance to healthcare facilities and vaccine costs can deter vaccination. In Nigeria, community engagement strategies have been employed to inform people about the importance of vaccines and address accessibility issues.38 This study revealed that parents were not aware of the availability of the vaccine despite it being free. Non-significant findings in this study should not be interpreted as evidence of the absence of an association, but rather as an indication that larger and more adequately powered studies are required.

Limitations of the study

The cross-sectional design limited the ability to establish causality between identified factors and vaccine hesitancy. Second, the reliance on self-reported data may introduce social desirability bias, with parents potentially under-reporting hesitancy or refusal due to perceived societal expectations. Additionally, the study was conducted in a specific region, which may limit the generalisability of the findings to other areas and nationally. The statistical analysis did not account for the clustered nature of the sampling design, which might have led to underestimation of SEs and overstatement of statistical significance. The relatively small sample size may have limited statistical power, and as such, these results should be interpreted cautiously. Future research should consider longitudinal designs, incorporate diverse populations and methods such as multilevel modelling or robust SE adjustment to improve accuracy and extend these findings.

Conclusion

This study examined the current level of HPV vaccine hesitancy among parents of adolescent girls in Ibadan North East LGA, revealing a significant level of HPV vaccine hesitancy. Knowledge of HPV infection and the vaccine was significantly low among parents of adolescent girls. Based on the WHO 3C model of vaccine hesitancy, the study findings suggested that parents were concerned about the safety of the vaccine and the effectiveness of the vaccine; a significant proportion were unaware of the availability of the vaccine. These concerns, alongside the low level of knowledge about the virus and vaccines, were possibly associated factors of HPV vaccine hesitancy. These findings underscore the need for targeted public health interventions to bridge knowledge gaps, enhance vaccine confidence, improve accessibility and promote HPV vaccination coverage, ultimately contributing to the prevention of HPV-related diseases.
