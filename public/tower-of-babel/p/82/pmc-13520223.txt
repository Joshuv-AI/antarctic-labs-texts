Previous-day physical restraint duration as a bedside signal for following-day delirium: a retrospective patient-day analysis in a mixed ICU
Authors: Aki Sasaki, Tomoki Kuribara, Takeshi Unoki, Junpei Haruna, Hiroomi Tatsumi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDelirium is common in the intensive care unit and associated with adverse outcomes. However, bedside indicators that flag patients at high risk for delirium the following day are lacking. Although physical restraint has been linked to delirium, many prior studies relied on cross-sectional, same-day assessments that cannot exclude reverse causation. This study aimed to evaluate whether cumulative previous-day physical restraint duration was a bedside signal for following-day delirium.MethodsWe conducted a single-center retrospective observational cohort study using patient-days (00:00–24:00 h) as the unit of analysis, with the exposure and covariates derived from more granular, time-stamped nursing records aggregated into calendar-day units. The exposure was cumulative physical restraint duration on the previous day, reported per 8-h increase. The outcome was incident delirium on the following day, defined as an Intensive Care Delirium Screening Checklist (ICDSC) score ≥ 4. The primary analysis was restricted to patient-days without previous-day delirium (ICDSC 0–3). We fitted a generalized linear mixed model with a random intercept for each patient. The restraint duration was modeled using a natural cubic spline (degrees of freedom = 3).ResultsOf 1,482 patient-days from 281 patients, 263 patient-days were excluded due to coma. The primary analysis included 729 patient-days from 247 patients without previous-day delirium (115 incident delirium events, 15.8%). The association between restraint duration and following-day incident delirium was non-linear (likelihood ratio test, P < 0.001). Using no restraint as the reference, the odds ratios were 2.43 (95% confidence interval [CI], 1.64–3.61) at 8 h, 5.16 (95% CI 3.39–7.85) at 16 h, and 10.01 (95% CI 6.31–15.88) at 24 h. Sensitivity analyses using complete-case analysis, last observation carried forward, best-case/worst-case extreme scenarios, and adjustment for previous-day mental status yielded consistent results.ConclusionsIn this single-center cohort, longer restraint duration on the previous day was associated with a higher risk of incident delirium the following day and may serve as a bedside-observable signal rather than evidence of a causal effect. Residual confounding may remain; prospective observational studies are needed to confirm this signal before its clinical utility is evaluated.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40560-026-00910-6.

Background

Delirium is a major manifestation of acute brain dysfunction in critically ill patients and is frequently observed in the intensive care unit (ICU) [1, 2]. ICU delirium is associated with prolonged mechanical ventilation [2, 3], increased ICU and hospital length of stay, and higher mortality rates [3–5]. Longer durations of delirium also correlate with long-term cognitive impairment after ICU discharge. Identifying patients at high short-term risk—particularly for the following day—is clinically important but requires reliable bedside indicators. Early risk stratification enables medical teams to adjust observation frequency and target non-pharmacological preventive interventions to the patients most likely to benefit.

Physical restraint is sometimes used in the ICU to address safety concerns such as self-extubation or falls [6, 7]. Although physical restraint has been associated with delirium [8, 9], these associations come largely from cross-sectional, same-day assessments, which cannot exclude reverse causation—that is, restraint being applied in response to delirium or its early manifestations rather than preceding it. It therefore remains unclear whether restraint documented on one day is followed by delirium on the next, and thus whether it could serve as a bedside signal for identifying patients at risk.

To address this, we used routinely recorded nursing data in a patient-day analysis that aligns previous-day restraint with following-day incident delirium. Rather than estimating a causal effect, we modeled restraint as a continuous cumulative duration to characterize the dose–response relationship, and evaluated whether previous-day restraint duration could serve as an observational, bedside-observable indicator of following-day delirium. This continuous, temporally aligned approach captures dose–response information that binary, same-day analyses cannot.

Methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective observational cohort study was conducted in a mixed ICU. We adopted a patient-day framework, aligning previous-day (Day D − 1) exposures with following-day (Day D) outcomes to preserve temporal ordering (Fig. 1). We used electronic medical records and nursing documentation for patients admitted to a six-bed general (medical–surgical) ICU affiliated with Sapporo Medical University Hospital, a 924-bed teaching institution, between January 1, 2020, and August 31, 2022. The unit of analysis was the patient-day (00:00–24:00 h), defined by calendar day. Although the unit of analysis was the patient-day, the exposure and covariates were derived from more granular, time-stamped nursing records within each calendar day. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Graduate School of Nursing, Sapporo City University (2022–11-18), and was conducted with permission from Sapporo Medical University Hospital (342–177). The study information was publicly posted, and eligible patients were provided with the opportunity to opt out. This study was reported in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) statement [10].Fig. 1Exposure–outcome time alignment using a sliding-window structure. Participants

The inclusion criteria were adults aged ≥ 18 years who stayed in the ICU for at least 3 nights. A stay of at least 3 nights was defined as including 3 overnight periods crossing midnight during the ICU stay (i.e., when the patient was in the ICU at 00:00 on at least three occasions). This criterion was set to enable the stable construction of a patient-day data structure where the exposure (previous day) and outcome (following day) were aligned by calendar day, and to reduce information loss due to short ICU stays. The exclusion criteria were patients with dementia documented in their medical records and those transferred to our ICU after staying in another ICU for more than 24 h.

Exposure

The primary exposure was the cumulative duration of physical restraint on the previous day, aligned with the delirium outcome on the following day so that exposure preceded outcome in the analytic dataset. Physical restraint was defined as restricting a patient’s free movement using devices such as restraining belts, mittens, or limb restraints with the aim of limiting patient movement; movement restriction due to medical devices without the use of such restraint equipment was not included. Restraint duration was calculated as the cumulative time (in minutes) within each ICU day (00:00–24:00) based on the start and end times documented in nursing records and was converted to 8-h (480-min) units to facilitate clinical interpretability.

Outcome

The primary outcome was incident delirium on the following day (Day D). Trained nurses assessed delirium every 8 h using the Intensive Care Delirium Screening Checklist (ICDSC) [11]. Delirium was defined as an ICDSC score ≥ 4 [11]. To account for intraday fluctuations, the highest ICDSC score within the day was used as the representative value. Patient-days corresponding to ICU admission and discharge were excluded because the frequency of ICDSC assessments on these days differed from that on other ICU days. Following the ICDSC assessment procedure, patient-days recorded as comatose were considered unassessable and were excluded from the analysis. In contrast, patient-days with no recorded ICDSC were treated as missing; the outcome was not imputed, and these patient-days were excluded at the model estimation stage (see Handling of missing data).

Data sources and collected variables

We extracted data from electronic medical records and ICU nursing documentation and constructed a day-level (patient-day) analytic dataset (00:00–24:00).

Because the design required both a previous-day exposure (Day D − 1) and a following-day outcome (Day D), and the inclusion criterion required at least 3 nights in the ICU, the first patient-day eligible to contribute exposure data corresponded to ICU Day 2 (with the outcome assessed on ICU Day 3). Data were organized as consecutive patient-day pairs; patient-days without a defined previous day (e.g., ICU admission day) or without a corresponding outcome day (e.g., ICU discharge day) were excluded (Fig. 1). Patient-level variables included age and sex. Day-level variables included ICU day (calendar day since ICU admission), modified Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (mSOFA) score (calculated by excluding the neurological component from the original SOFA score to minimize potential multicollinearity, given that delirium was used as the outcome), use of mechanical ventilation, use of dexmedetomidine, and doses of midazolam, propofol, and fentanyl. Admission type was categorized as medical, elective surgery, or emergency surgery, consistent with the Japanese Intensive Care Patient Database (JIPAD) classification [12].

Statistical analyses

Continuous variables are presented as median (interquartile range [IQR]) and categorical variables as number (%). The distribution of physical restraint duration was examined using a histogram.

The primary analysis was restricted to patient-days on which the patient was free of delirium on the previous day (ICDSC 0–3), with incident (new-onset) delirium on the following day as the outcome. We fitted a generalized linear mixed model (GLMM) with a logit link and a random intercept for each patient to account for within-patient correlation. The model was adjusted for pre-specified covariates: admission type, ICU day, age [13, 14], sex [15, 16], mSOFA score, mechanical ventilation [17, 18], dexmedetomidine use [16, 18], midazolam dose [13, 18], fentanyl dose [19, 20], and propofol dose [16, 18]. These doses were expressed as weight-adjusted daily values (mg/kg/day for midazolam and propofol, and μg/kg/day for fentanyl). All time-varying covariates were defined using values from the previous day to maintain temporal alignment with the exposure. In this model, the previous-day restraint duration (per 8-h increase) was modeled using a natural cubic spline with 3 degrees of freedom (df), selected based on sample size considerations, as this provides sufficient flexibility to capture non-linearity while limiting overfitting [21]. The curves presented in the figures represent the direct output of the spline model without any additional graphical smoothing. We report adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) at selected restraint durations (0, 8, 16, and 24 h), together with a curve of predicted probabilities from the spline model. The overall association was assessed using a likelihood ratio test comparing the full model with a reduced model excluding the spline terms for restraint duration, together with the Akaike information criterion (AIC).

As a descriptive analysis, we calculated the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) to describe the apparent discrimination of the primary model. Because this study was designed to characterize the dose–response association rather than to develop a clinical prediction tool, the AUC is presented descriptively, and formal model validation was not undertaken. Statistical significance was defined as two-sided P < 0.05.

All analyses were performed using R version 4.4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). GLMMs were fitted using lme4 (version 1.1-35.5) with the BOBYQA optimizer (maximum 200,000 function evaluations). Multiple imputation was performed using mice (version 3.16.0). Natural cubic splines were generated using the splines package (base R). The apparent AUC was calculated using pROC (version 1.18.5). Figures were created using ggplot2 (version 4.0.1).

Handling of missing data

We pre-specified that patient-days with missing outcome data would be excluded from the model estimation without imputation because imputing the binary delirium outcome would require additional assumptions and could increase the risk of outcome misclassification. Missing variables, if present, were imputed using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) [22]. We generated 10 imputed datasets (m = 10) with 20 iterations. We assumed missing at random for the imputation model; however, missing not at random mechanisms could not be excluded.

Sample size

As the sample size was determined by the available data, we additionally estimated the minimum detectable effect for an 8-h increase in restraint duration, accounting for within-patient correlations; details are provided in Additional file 1.

Sensitivity analysis

To assess the robustness of the primary findings, we pre-specified three sensitivity analyses. First, we performed a complete-case analysis to evaluate the potential impact of missing data. Second, we conducted an extreme-case sensitivity analysis in which all patient-days with missing delirium assessments were assumed to be non-delirium (best case) or delirium (worst case). In this analysis, restraint duration was modeled as a linear term (per 8-h increase) rather than a natural cubic spline, because the objective was to confirm the direction of the association under extreme missing-data assumptions, not to characterize the dose–response shape. Third, we performed the sensitivity analysis using the last observation carried forward method, in which missing delirium assessments were replaced with the most recent available value within the same patient, reflecting the clinical expectation that delirium status tends to persist across consecutive days. In addition, we performed a post hoc sensitivity analysis to examine whether the association was independent of ICDSC-detectable prodromal status on the previous (exposure) day. Because the exposure was lagged by one day, the previous-day ICDSC was used to index the patient's clinical state at the time restraint was applied. We re-fitted the primary GLMM (natural cubic spline, df = 3; multiple imputation; identical covariates and random-intercept structure) with the addition of previous-day mental status (normal [ICDSC 0] versus subsyndromal [ICDSC 1–3]) as a covariate. We additionally stratified the analytic sample by previous-day mental status. Because the normal stratum was sparse, covariate-adjusted models were not feasible; restraint duration was modeled as a linear term (per 8-h increase) using unadjusted Firth penalized-likelihood logistic regression. For comparability, the subsyndromal stratum was also estimated without further covariate adjustment, using a random-intercept generalized linear mixed model with restraint duration as the sole fixed effect. A restraint-by-stratum interaction was tested.

Distribution of physical restraint duration and missing data

The distribution of physical restraint duration is shown in Additional file 2. Missing data for the primary analysis population are summarized in Additional file 3. Among the 780 patient-days that satisfied the previous-day non-delirium criterion (ICDSC 0–3) and were therefore eligible for the incident delirium analysis, the outcome (following-day delirium) had the highest proportion of missing values (51 patient-days, 6.5%), followed by restraint duration (33, 4.2%). All other covariates were complete. Missing covariates, including restraint duration, were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations, whereas patient-days with a missing outcome were excluded at the model estimation stage; the primary analysis therefore included 729 patient-days (780 − 51).

Primary analysis

The primary results are presented in Table 2 and Fig. 3. The association between previous-day physical restraint duration and following-day incident delirium was significant (likelihood ratio test, P < 0.001; ΔAIC = 43.8 favoring the model with restraint), demonstrating a non-linear dose–response relationship. Using no restraint (0 h) as the reference, the odds ratios at selected durations were 2.43 (95% CI 1.64–3.61) at 8 h, 5.16 (95% CI 3.39–7.85) at 16 h, and 10.01 (95% CI 6.31–15.88) at 24 h. The predicted probability of following-day delirium increased with longer restraint duration on the previous day, with a steeper rise at higher exposure levels. Sensitivity analysis

Additional file 4 presents the complete-case analysis as a sensitivity analysis (702 patient-days from 243 patients, 111 events). The association between restraint duration and following-day incident delirium remained significant (likelihood ratio test, P < 0.001) and was consistent with the results of the primary analysis. Using no restraint (0 h) as the reference, the odds ratios at selected durations were 2.33 (95% CI 1.26–4.30) at 8 h, 4.80 (95% CI 2.30–10.02) at 16 h, and 9.13 (95% CI 3.63–22.95) at 24 h.

In the extreme-case sensitivity analysis for missing outcome data (n = 51 patient-days with missing assessments, 6.5%), the positive association between the previous-day restraint duration and following-day incident delirium was preserved under both extreme assumptions. Restraint duration was modeled as a linear term (per 8-h increase) in this analysis, as the objective was to confirm the direction of the association rather than to characterize the dose–response shape. The odds ratio per 8-h increase was 2.08 (95% CI 2.07–2.10) when all missing outcomes were assumed to be non-delirium (best case), and 1.77 (95% CI 1.45–2.16) when all were assumed to be delirium (worst case). The direction of the association was unchanged under either scenario (Additional file 5).

In the last observation carried forward analysis (726 patient-days from 246 patients, 115 events), the results were consistent with the primary analysis. Using no restraint (0 h) as the reference, the odds ratios were 2.45 (95% CI 1.35–4.45) at 8 h, 5.17 (95% CI 2.74–9.76) at 16 h, and 9.93 (95% CI 4.89–20.16) at 24 h (Additional file 6).

In a post hoc sensitivity analysis additionally adjusting for previous-day mental status, the dose–response association was essentially unchanged from the primary analysis: using no restraint as the reference, the adjusted odds ratios were 2.28 (95% CI 1.41–3.69) at 8 h, 4.78 (95% CI 2.86–7.99) at 16 h, and 9.47 (95% CI 5.38–16.66) at 24 h (Additional file 7). When the analytic sample was stratified by previous-day mental status, the association persisted among patient-days with no ICDSC-detectable prodromal features (normal stratum, ICDSC 0; 14 events in 146 patient-days), with an unadjusted odds ratio per 8-h increase of 2.67 (95% CI 1.39–5.13; Firth penalized likelihood). A comparable unadjusted estimate was observed in the subsyndromal stratum (ICDSC 1–3; 101 events in 583 patient-days; 2.19 [95% CI 1.88–2.56]), and the restraint-by-stratum interaction was not significant (likelihood ratio test, P = 0.38). Because the stratified models omit the covariates included in the primary analysis, these estimates are not directly comparable to the primary odds ratios and are presented to assess whether the exposure–outcome association is present within each mental-status stratum.

Discussion

While many prior studies have measured physical restraint and delirium concurrently on the same day, leaving the temporal ordering between the two ambiguous, this patient-day analysis aligned exposure (previous-day cumulative restraint duration) and outcome (following-day incident delirium) on separate calendar days and quantified their dose–response relationship. Longer cumulative restraint duration on the previous day was associated with a substantially higher predicted probability of delirium on the following day, with risk rising more sharply at longer exposure levels. These findings suggest that previous-day restraint duration can help identify patients at higher risk of delirium on the following day, drawing on information already captured in routine ICU documentation.

Previous studies have reported associations between physical restraint and delirium, but many have relied on cross-sectional same-day assessments [9, 17, 23, 24], leaving the temporal direction unclear. McPherson et al. addressed this gap: in a cardiovascular ICU cohort, they examined whether the presence of restraints or immobilizing devices on the previous day was associated with delirium on the following day, after adjusting for previous-day mental status (i.e., delirium status on the day preceding the outcome assessment) [15]. Their analysis aimed to identify modifiable risk factors and treated previous-day mental status as a confounder to be adjusted for. The present study had a different aim. Rather than estimating a causal effect, we sought to evaluate whether previous-day cumulative restraint duration could function as a bedside signal for following-day delirium. Reflecting these aims, we made several methodological choices that build on the earlier work.

A bedside signal is most useful when delirium is not already present, so we restricted the primary analysis to patient-days without previous-day delirium and focused on the emergence of new-onset cases. We also defined the exposure narrowly. Movement restrictions related to therapeutic devices—such as femoral arterial catheters, intra-aortic balloon pumps, or ventricular assist devices—were excluded because they may primarily reflect treatment requirements and illness severity rather than physical restraint as documented in nursing records. Including such device-related restrictions could obscure the interpretation of previous-day restraint duration as a bedside signal. We focused on restraint time as documented in nursing records to evaluate a routinely available indicator that reflects patient behavior and bedside care processes in daily ICU practice. Restraint was quantified as a continuous cumulative duration over the calendar day and modeled with a natural cubic spline, allowing flexible estimation of the dose–response shape without imposing binary or categorical assumptions. Finally, the cohort was a general mixed ICU, rather than a specialty-specific population. Modeling restraint as a continuous variable revealed a pattern that a binary classification would not have captured: a modest increase at shorter durations and a much steeper one once restraint extended across much of the preceding day. A recent multicenter randomized trial compared restrictive and liberal wrist-strap restraint strategies in mechanically ventilated ICU patients and found no significant difference in delirium- or coma-free days [25]. This finding underscores that restraint exposure is unlikely to be a primary modifiable target for delirium prevention, and supports the present framing of previous-day cumulative restraint duration as a bedside-observable signal rather than a causal factor.

The shape of the curve influences how this signal is interpreted across its range. At lower durations, the association with following-day delirium is present but modest. Brief restraint episodes are often initiated for procedural safety or in response to transient behavioral changes, and may be discontinued once the immediate concern resolves [6]. Such episodes appear to provide relatively little information about following-day delirium. In contrast, at longer durations, the predicted probability rises more steeply. Once restraint has persisted across most of the preceding day, the association with following-day delirium is substantially stronger.

These findings have potential clinical implications, although prospective studies are needed to confirm them. The shape of the dose–response curve indicates that predicted risk increases sharply at longer restraint durations. Therefore, patients restrained for most of the preceding day may warrant heightened attention for delirium prevention. As cumulative restraint duration aggregates information across an entire 24-h period, it may capture risk that point-in-time bedside observation alone may not readily detect; therefore, reviewing the previous day's full restraint record may complement current bedside assessment.

In settings with well-structured documentation, previous-day restraint duration may be obtainable from existing nursing records and may help identify higher-risk patient-days for preventive attention. Reviewing the total restraint time from the previous day during shift handovers or multidisciplinary rounds could help identify patients at high risk of delirium on the following day. Sustained restraint on the preceding day may also prompt review of delirium prevention measures, including orientation support, sleep hygiene, early mobilization where feasible, and reassessment of sedation and analgesia. In this approach, restraint duration is used to identify higher-risk patient-days for preventive attention, not as a modifiable target. However, the recording formats and data quality for restraint use vary across institutions, and some facilities may not be capable of routinely capturing restraint durations [26, 27]. Therefore, implementing this approach will likely require standardized documentation and systems that enable reliable data extraction.

This study has several strengths. First, we built an explicit temporal alignment between exposure and outcome into the study design, which reduces the interpretational challenges common to cross-sectional analyses. Second, restraint duration was treated as a continuous exposure and modeled using a natural cubic spline, allowing us to present the shape of the association without relying on arbitrary categorization. Third, the GLMM accounted for within-patient correlations, which is appropriate for repeated patient-day measurements. Fourth, multiple imputation and multiple sensitivity analyses, both pre-specified and post hoc, confirmed that the direction of the association was robust.

However, this study also has several limitations. First, the study was conducted at a single center, and generalizability may be limited; findings may not extend to settings with different restraint practices or delirium assessment systems. Second, because physical restraint was initiated and continued based on clinical judgment, residual confounding is likely. Unmeasured factors, such as agitation severity, depth of sedation, frequency of unsafe behaviors, and prodromal delirium symptoms, could influence both restraint duration and following-day delirium. Important patient-level factors, such as alcohol use disorder and pre-existing psychiatric conditions, were not systematically available in this study. However, these conditions are known risk factors for delirium and may also increase agitation and the need for physical restraint, thereby acting as potential confounders. To assess whether confounding by ICDSC-detectable prodromal features on the previous day could account for this association, we additionally adjusted for previous-day mental status and performed stratified analyses in a post hoc sensitivity analysis. The dose–response association was essentially unchanged and persisted among patient-days with no ICDSC-detectable prodromal features, suggesting that confounding by ICDSC-detectable prodrome alone does not explain the association. By lagging exposure to the previous day and excluding previous-day delirium, this design also reduces reverse causation relative to same-day analyses. Nevertheless, ICDSC 0 indicates the absence of ICDSC-detectable features on that day, not the absence of prodrome; subthreshold behaviors that do not meet ICDSC item thresholds may escape detection, so confounding by such undetected prodrome cannot be excluded. Thus, restraint duration may partly function as a proxy for these factors, and the observed association should be interpreted as a bedside signal rather than as an effect of restraint itself. Third, although patients with diagnosed dementia were excluded, unrecognized or undocumented cognitive impairment (e.g., mild cognitive impairment) may have been present, potentially acting as a confounder. This exclusion may have reduced the inclusion of a high-risk population for delirium and may limit the generalizability of our findings to patients with pre-existing dementia. Fourth, hypoactive delirium may have been under-recognized in routine clinical assessments, potentially leading to outcome misclassification. Because hypoactive delirium is less likely to prompt physical restraint, such misclassification could bias the observed association between previous-day restraint duration and following-day delirium in either direction. Fifth, because we required an ICU stay of at least three nights, short stays and early deaths were less likely to be included, which may introduce selection bias and further limit generalizability. Sixth, ICU discharge days were excluded from the analytic dataset because the frequency of ICDSC assessments differed on those days; consequently, delirium that developed after ICU discharge could not be captured, and the present analysis cannot speak to post-ICU delirium occurrence. Seventh, missing outcome data on 51 patient-days (6.5%) could have introduced bias if missingness was related to delirium status, although the direction of the association was preserved under extreme-case assumptions. Eighth, we did not undertake formal model validation (e.g., internal/external validation, calibration assessment), because the study was designed to characterize the dose–response association of cumulative restraint duration with following-day delirium rather than to develop a clinical prediction tool. The apparent AUC is therefore reported as a descriptive measure of model fit, and formal validation will be an essential next step if this signal is to be developed into a prediction instrument.

Conclusions

The study findings suggest that, in this single-center cohort, longer previous-day physical restraint duration was associated with a higher risk of delirium on the following day, following a non-linear dose–response pattern with pronounced risk elevation at longer exposure levels. Because restraint practices and their association with delirium may be shaped by facility culture, staffing levels, and local sedation–analgesia protocols, these factors should be considered when interpreting these findings. The use of restraint is closely tied to the patients’ clinical course and clinicians’ decisions; therefore, some residual confounding is likely, and the association should be interpreted as a bedside signal observable in routine documentation, not as a causal effect of restraint alone. Prospective multicenter observational studies are warranted to confirm this signal across diverse ICU settings. Subsequent implementation studies could then evaluate whether incorporating previous-day restraint duration into routine risk assessment supports delirium prevention in clinical practice.
