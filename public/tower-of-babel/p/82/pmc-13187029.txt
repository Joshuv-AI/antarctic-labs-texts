Osteoblast-derived CAR3 synergizing with collagen and bone sialoprotein enhances bone formation
Authors: Xiaoxin Ma, Qing He, Chen Ye, Jinglun Zhang, Xinyi Zhou, Yuxuan Zhang, Tian Gan, Zhe Li, Fangfang Song, Yufeng Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Osteoblasts orchestrate the infiltration and crystallization of mineral precursors within collagen fibrils. Certain osteoblast-secreted mineralization-inducing proteins further stimulate bone formation. In this study, scRNA-seq analysis of murine skull and long bone revealed a striking expression pattern of carbonic anhydrase III (Car3) in osteoblasts. We uncovered a pivotal role for CAR3 in osteoblast lineage cells, revealing its critical function in skeletal development and homeostasis. Conditional ablation of Car3 in Prx1-lineage cells resulted in osteopenia and markedly impaired osteoblast activity, underscoring its functional role. Mechanistically, the primary transcription factor RUNX2 directly regulated Car3 expression, mediating its spatiotemporal expression during development. Notably, CAR3 promoted collagen intrafibrillar mineralization by forming a ternary complex with COL1A1 and bone sialoprotein (BSP), thereby facilitating mineral deposition. Furthermore, CAR3-functionalized scaffolds significantly improved bone repair and regeneration by promoting both matrix mineralization and recruitment of Prx1-lineage cells. These findings establish CAR3 as a critical coordinator of osteoblast differentiation and collagen interfibrillar mineralization, positioning it as a central mediator for maintaining skeletal integrity and enabling regeneration.

Introduction

The development and homeostasis of the skeletal system are governed by dynamic interactions among mesenchymal stem/progenitor cells, osteoblasts (OBs), osteocytes, chondrocytes, vascular cells, and osteoclasts (OCs).1,2 Biomineralization, a hierarchically regulated process orchestrated by osteoblasts, involves collagen assembly, amorphous calcium phosphate (ACP) phase transformation, and precise control by non-collagenous proteins (NCPs).3 NCPs exert critical regulatory functions in this process. For instance, bone sialoprotein (BSP) facilitates hydroxyapatite (HAp) nucleation through its RGD (Arg-Gly-Asp) motif,4 while osteocalcin (OCN) directs crystal orientation via calcium ion chelation.5 Additionally, chondroitin sulfate (CS) stabilizes amorphous calcium phosphate (ACP) phases through its anionic properties, mitigating supersaturation-driven interfacial mismatches to ensure spatially ordered crystal alignment.6 Disruptions in these processes—such as abnormal collagen fibril assembly due to COL1A1/A2 mutations, excessive hydroxylation from P3H1/CRTAP complex defects, or dysregulated mineral deposition kinetics caused by IFITM5 variants—lead to developmental bone deformities and osteogenesis imperfecta (OI).7,8 These findings underscore the necessity to elucidate the genetic mechanisms of osteoblast differentiation, which are pivotal for maintaining skeletal integrity and function.

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has revolutionized the dissection of skeletal cell heterogeneity and regulatory networks during development and disease.9 In our study, scRNA-seq analysis of murine skull and long bone development revealed a striking expression pattern of Car3 in osteoblasts,10,11 particularly within mature osteoblast clusters marked by Bglap and Col1a1. CAR3, a zinc metalloenzyme regulating intracellular pH via CO₂ hydration, is well-characterized in skeletal muscle for modulating mitochondrial ATP synthesis and lipid metabolism.12–14 However, its spatiotemporal expression dynamics and mechanistic contributions to skeletal development remain unexplored.

Given our data demonstrating a progressive increase in CAR3 expression during osteoblast differentiation and bone development, we sought to elucidate its role in bone homeostasis. To this end, we conditionally deleted Car3 in mesenchyme-derived osteoprogenitor cells using the Prx1-Cre model. This manipulation revealed a critical function of CAR3 in postnatal bone mass accrual. Collectively, our findings uncover a previously unrecognized role for Car3 in regulating progenitor cell lineage commitment and collagen mineralization by modulating the infiltration and crystallization of ACP within collagen fibrils.

Results

Mineralization dynamics and CAR3 expression during embryonic bone development

To investigate marker genes in osteoblast differentiation, we reanalyzed scRNA-seq data (GSE174716) from E17.5 murine cranial tissues using a standardized workflow. Uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) dimensional reduction identified eight transcriptionally distinct clusters (Fig. S1A). These were annotated as meningeal cells, osteodermal progenitors, chondroblasts, preosteoblasts, dermal cells, hypodermal cells, mature osteoblasts, and chondrocytes based on lineage-specific markers (Fig. S1A, B). The pathway enrichment analysis (KEGG) revealed significant enrichment of “ossification” and “osteoblast differentiation” in preosteoblasts and osteoblasts (Fig. S1B). Notably, Car3 ranked among the top 10 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in osteoblast clusters (Fig. S1B), though its mechanistic role in bone homeostasis remains uncharacterized. Monocle 3-based pseudotime ordering rooted at osteodermal progenitors revealed bifurcation into two lineages: Fate 1 (osteoblasts) and Fate 2 (osteoprogenitor cells s) (Fig. 1a). Car3 expression was dynamically upregulated post-branching in the osteogenic trajectory (Fig. 1a, b). Von Kossa staining revealed macroscopic mineralization onset at E15.5, evidenced by black-stained calcium phosphate deposits (Fig. 1c). In contrast, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) detected ultrastructural evidence of collagen fibril alignment (monodisperse fibrils, ~25–30 nm diameter) and localized electron-dense calcium phosphate deposits as early as E14.5 (Fig. 1d), suggesting mineralization initiation precedes macroscopic detection. These findings delineate a critical temporal window (E14.5–E15.5) for osteoblast-mediated mineralization. Immunofluorescence across E13.5–E16.5 demonstrated spatiotemporal correlation between CAR3 protein levels and mineralization progression (Fig. 1e), mirroring the spatial expansion of calcified matrix observed via Von Kossa. Co-localization analysis confirmed CAR3 enrichment in Prx1+ stromal cells (Fig. S2). Western blot results consistently showed that the level of CAR3 progressively increased during development, paralleling SP7 and RUNX2 expression (Fig. S3).

Tissue clearing and immunofluorescence of E15.5 embryos revealed CAR3 expression was not restricted to craniofacial bones but also prominent in long bones (femur, tibia) and axial skeletal elements (vertebrae, ribs) (Fig. S4). Fate mapping using Prx1-Cre; Rosa-tdTomato mice confirmed CAR3 expression was restricted to Prx1-lineage cells in E15.5 hindlimbs (Fig. S5). tdTomato-labeled cells included osteoblasts, osteocytes, and stromal progenitors, recapitulating the previously reported Prx1-Cre targeting patter.15 The Prx1-lineage-restricted expression pattern of CAR3 provides strong mechanistic justification for employing a Prx1-driven conditional knockout strategy to investigate its role in osteogenesis. Collectively, these findings establish CAR3 as a regulator of embryonic bone development, with spatial expression dynamics mirroring key skeletal morphogenetic events.

Car3 is highly expressed in osteoblast-lineage cells in postnatal limb bone osteoblast

To further reveal the expression of Car3 in postnatal bone formation, sc-RNA sequencing on 5-month-old mouse femurs were analyzed (Fig. 2a, GSE221936, Fig. S6). It revealed that Car3 was highly expressed in osteoblast-lineaging cells, which is associated with the expression progression of Sp7 and Dmp1 (Fig. 2b). Furthermore, Car3 exhibited highly concordant expression pattern with osteogenesis-related markers such as Col1a1 and Col1a2 (Fig. 2b, S6), suggesting Car3 may occupy a key position in the regulatory network of bone development. We employed 8-week-old Prx1-Cre; tdTomato reporter mice. It showed co-localization of CAR3 (green fluorescence) with Prx1-lineage cells (tdTomato+) in femur sections (Fig. 2c). Additionally, in 8-week-old mice subjected to sequential intraperitoneal injections of calcein and alizarin red S (5-day interval), the results of immunofluorescence staining revealed the location of CAR3 paralleling the calcein-alizarin-labeled bone matrix, indicating CAR3 as a marker of nascent bone formation (Fig. 2d). Immunofluorescence analysis of femurs from aged mice (12-month-old) identified CAR3 localization within osteoblasts and adipocytes (Fig. 2d). Finally, in 8-week-old mouse femurs, CAR3 co-localized with COL1A1 in regions of active bone formation. However, in aged mice, CAR3 notably localized to adipocytes (Fig. 2e), suggesting potential role of CAR3 on adipogenesis. Oil Red O staining demonstrated a significantly higher number of adipocytes in CAR3-knockout BMSCs compared to wild-type group (Fig. S7). qPCR analysis of adipogenic markers further confirmed enhanced adipogenic differentiation potential in CAR3-deficient mesenchymal stem cells, implying the suppression of CAR3 on adipogenic differentiation. Car3 is a transcriptional target of RUNX2

To elucidate the transcriptional regulatory mechanism governing CAR3’s osteoblast-specific expression, we focused on RUNX2, which is the master switch of osteogenic differentiation.16 Co-expression analysis revealed that CAR3 shares spatial expression patterns with RUNX2 and SP7 in femoral osteoblasts (Fig. 3a, S8A). Immunofluorescence assays demonstrated markedly reduced CAR3 levels in the trabecular bone of Runx2 + /- mice compared to wild-type littermates (Fig. 3b), suggesting RUNX2-dependent regulation of CAR3. To validate this, we analyzed RUNX2 ChIP-seq data from mouse embryonic osteoblast precursors, which identified a strong binding peak at the Car3 promoter during osteogenesis (Fig. 3c, GSE54013). Notably, this binding intensity diminished during advanced mineralization stages (Fig. 3c), implying stage-specific regulatory dynamics. Functional validation via RUNX2 overexpression in BMSCs significantly upregulated Car3 transcription (Fig. 3d), further supporting its direct regulation. Intriguingly, despite high co-localization of SP7 and CAR3 in bone marrow (Fig. S8A), SP7 overexpression failed to activate Car3 expression (Fig. S8B), excluding SP7 as an upstream transcriptional regulator of CAR3. CAR3 plays a critical role in osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs

Super-resolution immunofluorescence microscopy revealed a dynamic spatiotemporal expression pattern of CAR3 during BMSC mineralization. At day 7 of mineralization, CAR3 exhibited pronounced upregulation compared to day 0 (Fig. 4a), aligning with its association with mature osteoblasts, as demonstrated in scRNA-sequencing of bone marrow niches (Fig. 2a, b). Western blot and qPCR analyses further validated this temporal correlation, showing that CAR3 expression paralleled the induction of osteogenic markers, including osteopontin (OPN), osterix (OSX), and interferon-induced transmembrane protein 5 (IFITM5) (Fig. S9A, B). A pivotal finding was the enrichment of CAR3 in extracellular vesicles. Western blot of exosomal fractions (Fig. 4b) and colocalization studies using the exosomal marker PKH26 (Fig. 4c) confirmed CAR3’s presence in these extracellular vesicles. Immunoelectron microscopy with colloidal gold labeling localized CAR3 not only to secreted exosomes but also to extracellular collagen fibers in calvarial bone (Fig. 4d, S10). Matrix vesicles (MVs), a specialized subtype of extracellular vesicles critical for hydroxyapatite nucleation, are known to concentrate mineralization regulators such as alkaline phosphatase (ALP) and annexin A5 (ANXA5).17,18 This supports CAR3’s potential role in MVs-mediated collagen mineralization. Notably, mitochondrial enrichment of CAR3 was observed during cranial bone development. Subcellular fractionation combined with western blot and co-localization using Mitotracker at day 14 revealed elevated mitochondrial CAR3 levels compared to baseline (Fig. S11). To investigate the necessity and sufficiency of CAR3 in osteogenic mineralization, we employed CAR3-knockout BMSCs and lentiviral overexpression (OE-CAR3) systems in BMSCs. Alizarin Red S quantification demonstrated a reduction in calcium deposition in CAR3-deficient cells, whereas OE-CAR3 enhanced mineralization versus controls (Fig. 4e–h). Western blot analysis of osteogenic markers corroborated these findings, with CAR3-knockout cells showing diminished protein levels (Fig. 4g) and OE cells exhibiting accelerated osteogenic progression (Fig. 4h). These results align with prior evidence that CAR3 is critical for osteogenic differentiation.

CAR3 deletion reduces bone mass by suppressing osteoblast activity

We generated Prx1+ mesenchymal stem cell-specific Car3 knockout mice and performed skeletal whole-mount staining on newborn pups at postnatal day 1. The results showed no significant differences in skull size or long bone length between the two groups, indicating that Car3 ablation in MSCs does not impair skeletal development during early postnatal stages (Fig. S12A, B). Immunofluorescence confirmed efficient CAR3 knockout in bone marrow cells of Prx1-Cre; Car3fl/fl mice, while residual CAR3 signals in muscles validated tissue specificity, as muscle is not a primary target of Prx1-Cre-driven recombination (Fig. 5a). Micro-CT analysis of 8-week-old femurs revealed significant reductions in bone volume/total volume (BV/TV), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), and trabecular number (Tb.N), along with increased trabecular separation (Tb.Sp) in the Prx1- Cre; Car3fl/fl mice compared to Car3fl/fl mice (Fig. 5b, c). Cortical bone analysis further demonstrated decreased Ct.Th (cortical thickness), Ct.B.Ar (cortical bone area), and Ct.T.Ar (total cortical area) (Fig. 5d), collectively indicating that Car3 knockout significantly compromised femoral bone mass in adult mice. Safranin O/Fast Green and tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) staining showed no alterations in growth plate morphology or osteoclast differentiation (Fig. S12C). To further quantify osteoblast activity, dynamic histomorphometry using sequential calcein and alizarin red labeling revealed a marked decrease in the mineralization apposition rate (MAR) within the distal femoral cortical bone of Prx1- Cre; Car3fl/fl mice (Fig. 5e). CAR3 regulates collagen intrafibrillar mineralization via binding to COL1A1 and recruit BSP

Previous findings demonstrated that CAR3 expression correlates with mineralization initiation and exhibits extracellular secretion capacity. In a three-dimensional docking model simulating intact collagen fibrils (containing α2 chains), CAR3 exhibited a multi-site cooperative binding pattern, with residues such as Arg91 and Asn62 forming hydrogen bonds with hydroxyproline residues (such as Hyp24, Hyp27) in collagen fibrils (Fig S13). To elucidate the relationship between CAR3 and Collagen, immunofluorescence staining of type I collagen co-incubated with CAR3 protein and calcium phosphate solution confirmed CAR3-mediated biomimetic mineralization on collagen fibrils (Fig. 6a). Co-IP experiments further revealed COL1A1-CAR3 complex formation during late-stage BMSC mineralization (Fig. 6b). To replicate the collagen mineralization in vitro, we employed the polymer-induced liquid-precursor (PILP) process. This mechanism involves ACP precursors stabilized by polyelectrolytes, such as polyaspartic acid (PAsp), poly (acrylic acid) (PAA), and poly(allylamine hydrochloride) (PAH), infiltrating collagen gap zones and transforming into HAp crystals within intrafibrillar compartments.19,20 TEM analysis revealed that reconstituted type I fibrillar collagen displayed the characteristic 67-nm periodic banding, confirming proper staggered assembly of procollagen molecules into fibrillar structures (Fig. 6c). Although TEM showed no ACP stabilization by CAR3 alone (Fig. S14), HRTEM demonstrated oriented nanocrystals within intrafibrillar compartments in PAA-containing groups, with SAED patterns showing arc-shaped diffraction along collagen’s longitudinal axis (Fig. 6c). Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping revealed homogeneous intrafibrillar distribution of Ca and P signals (Fig. 6d, S15). CAR3-functionalized collagen scaffolds enhance bone defect healing

Based on the critical role of CAR3-collagen interactions in establishing mineralization process, this study developed a recombinant CAR3-functionalized collagen scaffold to address bone defects. In 8-week-old C57BL/6 mice with bilateral 3-mm calvarial defects, CAR3-modified collagen membranes were implanted into the right-side defects, while unmodified collagen membranes served as left-side controls. After 8 weeks, micro-CT analysis revealed a significantly increase in new bone formation area and a significantly higher bone volume fraction in the CAR3-functionalized scaffold group compared to the unmodified scaffold controls (Fig. 8a). Von Kossa staining revealed that CAR3-functionalized scaffolds induced higher calcium phosphate nucleation density within the collagenous osteoconductive matrix at the 8-week of implantation (Fig. 8b), suggesting enhanced osteointegration. Furthermore, the CAR3-functionalized scaffolds exhibited enhanced recruitment of Prx1-lineage cells, marked by tdTomato expression following Cre-mediated recombination, in the calvarial defect model (Fig. 8c, d), using Prx1-Cre; Rosa26-ZsGreen-tdTomato mice. Discussion

The biomineralization process represents a sophisticated interplay between organic matrices and inorganic mineral phases, orchestrated primarily by osteoblasts.18 Our study revealed that CAR3 is closely associated with osteogenic differentiation and maturation. To conditionally delete Car3 from all osteoblast lineage cells, we utilized Prx1-Cre, which is active in BMSCs and differentiated lineage cells. Prx1-Cre; Car3fl/fl mice exhibited impaired osteogenesis at the adult stage but not during the perinatal period. The CAR3-functionalized scaffold effectively enhanced bone regeneration. These data suggest that CAR3 is critical for maintaining and regenerating the adult skeleton. Mechanically, CAR3 promotes collagen fibril mineralization by binding to collagen and recruiting BSP. These results significantly advance our understanding of bone formation mechanisms by identifying CAR3 as a key molecular bridge connecting the organic scaffold with mineral deposition.

Our study establishes that CAR3 exhibits a lineage-specific expression pattern in osteoblasts, implicating its potential role in osteogenic differentiation. scRNA-sequencing confirmed CAR3 as a marker of terminally differentiated osteoblasts. This expression pattern raises critical questions about its transcriptional regulation within the osteoblast lineage hierarchy. RUNX2, the master transcriptional regulator of osteoblast commitment, operates through a well-characterized cascade: it directly activates SP7, and together, these factors synergize with canonical Wnt signaling to drive osteoprogenitor differentiation while suppressing chondrogenesis.21 SP7, in turn, regulates terminal osteogenic markers via canonical GC-box motifs (consensus sequence: 5’-(G/T)GGGCGG-3’).22 Based on this paradigm, we hypothesized that CAR3 expression might be governed by RUNX2 or SP7. Sequence analysis of the promoter analysis of CAR3 revealed no conserved GC-box elements, suggesting regulatory divergence from the canonical SP7 axis. CHIP-seq analysis demonstrated a potential RUNX2 target. This finding underscores RUNX2 as the dominant upstream regulator of CAR3 in osteoblasts, potentially through direct binding to its promoter. This regulatory divergence may reflect lineage-specific adaptations. While RUNX2 and SP7 typically co-regulate osteoblast maturation genes, CAR3 appears to bypass SP7-dependent mechanisms. Notably, RUNX2’s ability to independently regulate CAR3 aligns with its broader role in coordinating both early and late osteogenic programs. This study positions CAR3 as a RUNX2-driven marker of osteoblast maturation, operating outside the canonical SP7/GC-box axis.

Carbonic anhydrase (CA), an evolutionarily ancient enzyme, plays a pivotal role in biomineralization across diverse calcifying organisms.23 Phylogenetic analyses reveal that α-CA isoforms were independently recruited into calcium carbonate (CaCO₃) mineralization processes in metazoans, serving roles such as supplying HCO₃⁻, nucleating minerals, or acting as structural protein.24 In marine calcifiers like corals, mollusks, and echinoderms, CA facilitates the conversion of metabolic CO₂ into HCO₃⁻ or CO₃²⁻ ions, which are critical for shell and skeleton formation.23,25,26 This mechanism highlights a conserved carbon concentration strategy in marine systems, where CA optimizes the use of metabolic CO₂ for sustainable biomineralization. In the contrast, human bone tissue primarily consists of an orderly arrangement of inorganic calcium phosphate and organic components such as collagen fibers.27 While CAR3 is expressed in osteoblasts, its role in bone formation likely differs from marine calcification. CAR3 may regulate local pH or ion gradients, but its catalytic activity is notably lower than other CA isoforms.25 Therefore, CAR3’s promotion of mammalian bone tissue formation may involve a different mechanism.

Our findings demonstrate that CAR3 is secreted into the extracellular matrix, prompting an investigation into its potential role in collagen mineralization. Type I collagen, the primary organic component of the bone matrix, provides a topological template for mineral deposition.28,29 Unlike the stratified mineralization observed in nacreous layers of shells, the confined architecture of collagen fibrils poses challenges for biomimetic mineralization.3,30,31 Studies suggest that during natural bone formation, osteoblast-secreted NCPs orchestrate intrafibrillar crystallization of inorganic minerals by spatiotemporally controlling nucleation, growth, and orientation.32 Our study reveals that CAR3 stably interacts with assembled collagen fibrils. While CAR3 dynamically stabilizes collagen fibers through multi-site binding, it lacks standalone activity to initiate hierarchical mineralization. Notably, CAR3 cannot stabilize ACP without cofactors, underscoring the necessity of synergistic partners for functional activation. Using STRING analysis of Mass spectrometry data, we identified BSP as a critical collaborator of CAR3. BSP, a core member of the SIBLING family, facilitates HA alignment along collagen fibrils via phosphorylated domains that coordinate calcium ions within collagen gap regions.33 Molecular docking confirmed stable CAR3-BSP complex formation at two interaction interfaces, suggesting cooperative regulation of intrafibrillar crystal orientation. CAR3-modified collagen fibrils exhibited significantly enhanced BSP recruitment compared to controls, supporting this mechanistic model.

Using transgenic tools, we found CAR3 deficiency reduces trabecular and cortical bone mass in adult mice, while no significant skeletal defects were observed during the perinatal stage. This stage-specific phenotype may stem from compensatory mechanisms involving crosstalk between osteoprogenitors, osteoblast, and mesenchymal stromal cells during skeletogenesis. To effectively capture CAR3’s role in this osteogenic lineage, we employed the Prx1-Cre driver, which depletes CAR3 not only in committed osteoblasts but also earlier in mesenchymal stromal and osteoprogenitor populations. This broader targeting was essential, as CAR3 expression occurs downstream of PRX1 but is not directly regulated by the osteoblast-specific transcription factor SP7. Thus, using Prx1-Cre allowed us to evaluate the impact of CAR3 loss on both early lineage commitment and later bone homeostasis, providing a more comprehensive understanding of its function within the skeletal system.

Based on degradation characteristics, materials applied for bone repair are broadly categorized into two main classes: bio-inert materials and biodegradable materials.34 While bio-inert materials, such as metallic and ceramic scaffolds, are clinically established, their permanence and potential for stress-shielding are limitations.35–38 Consequently, biodegradable materials that provide temporary structural support and degrade in harmony with new tissue formation have become a research priority. Our developed CAR3-functionalized collagen scaffold advances this paradigm by integrating active biological instruction into a biodegradable platform. This system utilizes absorbable collagen membranes as a bioactive carrier. The fibrillar architecture of the collagen not only provides a biomimetic mechanical microenvironment that replicates native bone matrix but also facilitates stable immobilization of the recombinant CAR3 protein, likely through hydrogen bonding and other non-covalent interactions with surface amino acid residues. This functionalization transforms the scaffold from a passive structural template into an active signaling platform. The CAR3-loaded bio-membrane acquires potent regulatory capacity, enabling it to actively recruit Prx1-lineage osteochondroprogenitors to the defect site and directly promote collagen intrafibrillar mineralization, thereby enhancing the quality and efficiency of bone regeneration.

In conclusion, we demonstrated a previously unknown and profound role of Car3 in osteoblast lineage cells. Conditionally deletion of Car3 using Prx1-Cre exhibit osteopenia and compromised osteoblast activity. Mechanistically, RUNX2, the master transcription factor governing osteoblast differentiation, directly regulates Car3 expression, establishing its spatiotemporal expression pattern during skeletal development. Intriguingly, our findings revealed that CAR3 protein facilitates collagen intrafibrillar mineralization by forming a ternary complex: it binds to COL1A1 fibrils and recruits BSP. Collectively, these data unveil a novel mechanism by which CAR3 coordinates osteoblast differentiation and collagen mineralization, positioning collagen-directed mineralization as a central driver of skeletal homeostasis and regeneration. However, this study is subject to notable limitations. First, the specific structural domains of CAR3 responsible for mediating fibril binding remain uncharacterized. Furthermore, the molecular mechanisms underlying CAR3-dependent spatiotemporal recruitment of BSP to collagen fibrils remain unresolved, including the biochemical basis for its preferential targeting to collagen hole zones where early mineralization occurs.

Method and materials

Mice

All mice experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Wuhan University (the approval number of the laboratory: MLIC2022003). Conditional null alleles of Car3 (Car3fl/fl) mice, Prx1 Cre mice, Runx2-KO mice were purchased from Cyagen. Prx1 Cre; Car3fl/fl mice were produced by using the Prx1Cre and Car3fl/fl transgenes. To generate embryos, female mice (dams) were examined for vaginal plugs the morning after mating. The noon of the day when the plug was observed was regarded as embryonic day 0.5 (E0.5). The dual-fluorescent reporter mice (Rosa26-ZsGreen-tdTomato) from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd. carry the genotype *Rosa26-CAG-Rox-ZsGreen-Stop-Rox-tdTomato-polyA.

Skeletal staining

The optimized dual skeletal staining protocol was developed based on established methodologies with technical modifications. Experimental procedures were conducted as follows: Neonatal pups (P1) underwent cryoanesthesia in sealed bags containing hydrated ice. Specimens received thermal pretreatment via immersion in 60 °C water bath prior to microsurgical removal of ocular tissues, integumentary system, and visceral organs. Subsequent skeletal preparation involved sequential fixation in 95% ethanol for 12 h followed by lipid removal in anhydrous acetone for 24 h. Cartilage-specific staining was achieved using an improved formulation (0.03% Alcian Blue in a mixture of 80% ethanol and 20% glacial acetic acid) with overnight incubation, followed by 0.005% Alizarin Red solution immersion for 4 h. Specimens were then treated with 1% KOH for 12-24 h to facilitate clearing. Processed specimens were preserved in a clearing solution containing 50% glycerol and 50% 1% KOH until achieving full transparency.

Exosome isolation

Extracellular vesicles were isolated from cell culture supernatant using differential ultracentrifugation. Briefly, conditioned medium was collected after 48 h of cell culture and centrifuged at 300 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove floating cells, followed by centrifugation at 2 000 × g for 20 min to eliminate dead cells and large debris. The supernatant was then filtered through a 0.22 µm pore-size polyethersulfone (PES) membrane to remove residual particulates. To concentrate extracellular vesicles, the clarified supernatant was ultracentrifuged at 100 000 × g for 70 min at 4 °C using a Type 70 Ti fixed-angle rotor (Beckman Coulter, USA). The pellet was washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and subjected to a second ultracentrifugation step under identical conditions to remove contaminating proteins. The final exosome-enriched pellet was resuspended in 100–200 µL of PBS and stored at −80 °C until further use.

Mitochondrial isolation

The mitochondrial isolation protocol was developed based on established methodologies. Collect BMSCs and centrifuge at 500 × g for 3 min (4 °C), then carefully aspirate the supernatant. Resuspend the pellet in PBS and repeat centrifugation (500 × g, 3 min, 4 °C) for additional washing. For cranial tissue, it needed to be minced first. Following this, aspirate PBS and resuspend the pellet in 2 mL of pre-chilled homogenization buffer supplemented with 20 μL of 100 mmol/L PMSF. Incubate the suspension on ice for 2 min. Homogenize cells sufficiently using a Dounce tissue grinder, then add 200 μL of equilibrium buffer directly to the grinder. Transfer the homogenate to a new tube and centrifuge at 1 200 × g for 3 min (4 °C). Collect the supernatant and repeat this low-speed centrifugation until no sediment is visible to ensure complete removal of intact cells. Transfer the clarified supernatant to a 1.5 mL tube and centrifuge at 15 000 × g for 2 min (4 °C). Discard the supernatant, resuspend the pellet in 500 μL of buffer, and repeat high-speed centrifugation (15 000 × g, 2 min, 4 °C) and finally get the crude mitochondrial fraction.

Immunofluorescence staining

Cultured cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 15 min at room temperature (RT), and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100/PBS for 10 min, and blocked in 5% normal serum (species-matched to secondary antibodie) supplemented with 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 1 h at RT. Primary antibodies diluted in antibody dilution buffer (1% BSA, 0.1% Tween-20/PBS) were applied overnight at 4 °C. After three washes with PBS containing 0.05% Tween-20 (PBST), species-specific Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:500; Invitrogen) were incubated for 1 h at RT protected from light. Nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; 1 μg/mL) for 5 min.

For tissue sections, all specimens were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for overnight at RT. Antigen retrieval was performed prior to staining by incubating slides in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 95 °C for 20 min, and specimens were finally mounted using ProLong™ Diamond Antifade Mountant (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and cured for 24 h at RT.

Exosomes were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for overnight at RT. PKH26 was utilized for exosome labeling. And primary antibody and Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibody were used to show the location of CAR3 in the exosomes.

Imaging was conducted using a Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscope equipped with ×63 oil immersion objectives. Z-stack acquisitions (0.5 μm intervals) were processed using Leica LAS X software (v3.7). Quantitative analysis of fluorescence intensity and colocalization was performed with ImageJ/Fiji (v2.3.0).

The primary antibodies used in this study was listed below:

anti-RUNX2 (ab236639, Abcam, USA), anti-SP7 (ab209484, Abcam, USA), anti-CAR3 (15197-1-AP, Proteintech, China), anti-COL1A1 (ab138492, Abcam, USA).

Intrafibrillar collagen mineralization

The PAA - ACP solution was prepared by mixing equal volumes of CaCl₂ and Na₂HPO₄ solutions in the presence of PAA (final concentrations: Ca²⁺ at 4.5 mmol/L; HPO₄²⁻ at 2.1 mmol/L; PAA at 50 μg/mL in TBS solution). 20 μL of type I collagen stock solution sourced from rat tails (with a concentration of 5 mg/mL, provided by Coring Biotech, China) was combined with 500 μL of assembling solution (containing 50 mmol/L glycine and 300 mmol/L KCl, adjusted to pH 9.3). 2.5 μL collagen solution was carefully dropped onto each 200-mesh nickel grid. The grids were then placed in a humid chamber maintained at 37 °C for 12 h. After that, the collagen fibrils on the grids were cross -linked using a 0.05% glutaraldehyde solution for 1 h at 37 °C. Next, they were thoroughly washed with deionized water and left to air-dry at room temperature. The grids were then subjected to mineralization processes. They were incubated with the PAA-ACP solutions at 37 °C for 4 h and 12 h, respectively. Once the incubation was complete, the grids were rinsed with deionized water, air-dried, and then analyzed using a Transmission Electron Microscope (TEM, HT7700, Hitachi, Japan) and a High-Resolution Transmission Electron Microscope (HRTEM, JEM - F200, JEOL, Japan) equipped with Selected Area Electron Diffraction (SAED) and Energy-Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDS) analysis.

Immunoelectron microscopy

The samples (tissues or cell pellets) were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 mol/L phosphate buffer at 4 °C for 4 h, followed by graded ethanol dehydration and resin embedding. Ultrathin sections (100 nm) were collected on coated grids and subjected to antigen retrieval when necessary, before blocking with 5% BSA or 10% normal serum in PBS. Primary antibodies were incubated overnight at 4 °C, followed by gold-conjugated secondary antibodies (colloidal gold) for 2 h at room temperature. After post-fixation with 2.5% glutaraldehyde, sections were negatively stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, then examined by transmission electron microscopy (80 kV) with imaging at 10 000×-100 000× magnification.

Micro-computed tomography (Micro-CT)

The femurs were scanned using a Bruker micro-CT system (Skyscan 1276, Belgium). Each sample was scanned layer-by-layer (scanning resolution: 4.8 μm, voltage: 80 kV) to acquire raw images. Three-dimensional reconstructions were generated using CTVox and CTAn software, and parameters including BV/TV (BV/TV), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), trabecular number (Tb.N), trabecular separation (Tb.Sp), Ct.Th (cortical thickness), Ct.B.Ar (cortical bone area), and Ct.T.Ar (total cortical area) were analyzed.

Histological analysis

The crania and femurs were decalcified in a commercial ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) solution (Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China) at room temperature for 3 months, followed by dehydration in a graded ethanol series, paraffin embedding, and sectioning into 5 μm-thick slices. Hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining, VON KOSSA staining, Safranin-O, and Tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) staining were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China).

In vitro osteogenic differentiation

Bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) and cavarial suture-derived stem cells (Lee et al. 2020) were cultured in Osteogenic medium, containing 10 mmol/L β-glycerophosphate, 50 μg/mL vitamin C, and 1 nmol/L dexamethasone, for osteogenic differentiation for 7 and 14 days.

After 7 and 14 days of culture, alkaline phosphatase (ALP) staining and ALP activity assays were performed using a BCIP/NBT Chromogenic Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, China) and an ALP Detection Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, China), respectively, following the manufacturers’ protocols. qRT-PCR and Western blotting were employed to evaluate gene and protein expression levels, respectively. The expression levels of osteogenesis-related genes (Ifitm5, Runx2 and Bglap) and Car3 were determined via quantitative reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (qPCR). To analyze target protein levels in BMSCs, Western blotting (detecting RUNX2, IFITM5, OPN, OCN, and SP7) was conducted after 14 days of culture.

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Takara, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were determined using a Nanodrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). cDNA was synthesized using a PrimeScript™ RT Reagent Kit (Takara, Japan). qRT-PCR was performed using ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, China). The relative expression levels of target genes were quantified using the 2⁻ΔΔCt method, with glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (Gapdh) as the internal reference. All experiments were performed in triplicate. Western blot analysis

Cells were lysed with radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Roche, Switzerland). Total protein concentration was quantified using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, China). Equal amounts of protein were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk for 2 h, incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, and then with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence detection system (Millipore, USA). Relative protein expression levels were analyzed using ImageJ software. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Molecular docking analysis

Three-dimensional structures of proteins CAR3 (AF-P16015-F1-model_v4) and BSP (AF-Q61711-F1-model_v4) were downloaded from the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database (https://alphafold.ebi.ac.uk/). The three-dimensional structure of collagen (PDB entry code 3B0S) was obtained from the RCSB PDB database (https://www.rcsb.org/). Molecular docking between CAR3 and BSP was performed using ClusPro 2.0 (https://cluspro.bu.edu/). The protein-protein docking method in HDOCK (http://hdock.phys.hust.edu.cn/) was employed to predict the binding mode of CAR3 with collagen. Visualization was conducted using PyMOL.

Immunofluorescence assays of CAR3-BSP-Ca Complex

A 75 µg/mL solution of rat-tail type I collagen in an assembly buffer (50 mmol/L glycine, 300 mmol/L KCl, pH 9.3) was prepared. This solution was applied to 14-mm aminated glass coverslips and incubated at 37 °C for 12 hours to allow fibril polymerization. The formed matrices were then lightly crosslinked with 0.05% (v/v) glutaraldehyde for 1 h at 37 °C, followed by air-drying. The dried collagen-coated coverslips were incubated with 0.1 mg/mL recombinant CAR3 protein for 2 h at room temperature. Subsequently, they were transferred to a mineralization solution containing a polyacrylic acid-stabilized amorphous calcium phosphate (PAA-ACP) precursor. This solution, with final concentrations of 4.5 mmol/L Ca²⁺, 2.1 mmol/L HPO₄²⁻, and 50 µg/mL PAA (PAA, 225 kD) in TBS, was incubated with the samples overnight at 37 °C. The samples were then processed for immunofluorescence. They were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the anti-CAR3 antibody (1:1 000 dilution). After washing, species-specific Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:500; Invitrogen) were applied for 1 h at room temperature, protected from light. The staining procedure was then repeated sequentially using the anti-BSP antibody. Finally, to label newly deposited calcium phosphate, the samples were incubated with 2 mg/mL calcein at 37 °C for 4 h before imaging.

Co‑immunoprecipitation of COL1A 1 and CAR3

BMSCs were harvested after 7 days of osteogenic induction and lysed. The lysate was centrifuged at 12 000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. A 30 μl aliquot of the supernatant was reserved as the input sample. The remaining was subjected to overnight incubation at 4 °C with either a specific anti-COL1A1 antibody (ab260043, Abcam) at a dilution ratio of 1:30 or non-specific immunoglobulin G (IgG; ab172730, Abcam) at a dilution ratio of 1:1 000. Pre‑equilibrated Protein A/G agarose beads were then added, and incubated for an additional 2 h at 4 °C to capture the antigen‑antibody complexes. The beads were then pelleted by centrifugation at 3 000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C, washed gently 3–5 times with ice‑cold PBS. Finally, 30 μL of 2×SDS‑PAGE loading buffer was added to the washed beads, and the sample was heated at 95 °C for 5 min to denature and dissociate the proteins. Following centrifugation, the supernatant was collected and subjected to Western blot analysis, using anti‑CAR3 and anti‑COL1A1 antibodies for detection.

In vivo bone defect model

All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with protocols approved by the Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee of Hubei Provincial Center for Disease Control and Prevention. Following anesthesia and surgical exposure of the skull, two standardized bone defects (3 mm in diameter) were created in each animal using a saline-cooled dental trephine bur. The CAR3-functionalized scaffolds were prepared by Type I collagen-based membrane’s immersing in 0.1 mg/mL recombinant CAR3 at 37°C for 2 h before use. The control group received no treatment, leaving the defects unfilled, whereas the other groups had their defects fully covered with CAR3-functionalized scaffolds. After careful suturing, all animals received a three-day course of penicillin. Postoperative evaluation at 8 weeks involved euthanasia via anesthetic overdose, after which the cranial samples were collected and preserved in 4% paraformaldehyde.

Statistical analysis

Results were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and analyzed using one-way ANOVA with post hoc Tukey’s test between the means of three or more groups. Student t-test was used to determine whether or not there was a statistically significant difference between the means of two groups. The statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism software.
