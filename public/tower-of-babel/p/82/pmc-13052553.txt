Oxygen therapy for children with an oxygen saturation of 90–93% in Malawi: the NoGoLO2 pilot randomised controlled trial
Authors: Kristen S Joseph, Tisungane Mvalo, Nicole Shilkofski, Tim Colbourn, Holly Schuh, Marieke M van der Zalm, Stephen Howie, Carina King, Eric D McCollum
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveOxygen is a cornerstone treatment for pneumonia with hypoxaemia. WHO guidelines recommend oxygen therapy for children with a peripheral arterial haemoglobin oxygen saturation (SpO2) <90%. Children in low- and middle-income countries with an SpO2 of 90–93% are at increased risk of mortality and may benefit from oxygen therapy. We aimed to determine the feasibility of a randomised controlled trial comparing standard of care (no oxygen), low-flow nasal cannula (LFNC) and high-flow nasal cannula (HFNC) among children with pneumonia and an SpO2 of 90–93%.DesignPilot, open-label, three-armed randomised control trial (NCT06176664).SettingSalima District Hospital, Malawi.PatientsChildren 1–59 months old with WHO-defined pneumonia and an SpO2 of 90–93%.InterventionsStandard care (no oxygen), LFNC or HFNC.Main outcome measuresThe primary outcome was feasibility defined as an average of ≤2 protocol deviations per participant. Secondary outcomes included consent refusal, participant attrition and treatment failure within 14 days. Treatment failure was defined as an SpO2 <90% for standard care participants or <94% in the LFNC and HFNC arms despite maximum therapy as described, any new severe respiratory distress (grunting, very severe chest indrawing) or any new WHO-defined emergency sign.ResultsOf the 29 eligible patients, 21 (72%) were enrolled and randomised. There was an average of 0.8 (16/21) deviations per participant. Seven (43.8%) protocol violations were related to study intervention protocols and nine (56.3%) to medication availability and routine care. No participants withdrew from the study. Three of 21 participants (14.3%) had treatment failure. Introduction

In 2021, 5.1 million children died worldwide, with over 50% of deaths in African low- and middle-income countries (LMICs).1,3 Pneumonia remains a leading cause of childhood mortality, accounting for almost 800 000 deaths in 2021.3 Hypoxaemia (low arterial blood oxygen concentrations) is a key marker of pneumonia severity and mortality risk,4,6 and it is prevalent among children with pneumonia in LMICs, with an estimated 31% affected.7 8 The WHO defines hypoxaemia as a peripheral arterial haemoglobin oxygen saturation (SpO₂) <90% and recommends hospitalisation and oxygen therapy.9 Emerging evidence suggests that an SpO₂ of 90–93% is common and incurs elevated mortality risks among children in LMICs.48 10,15 Observational data from Malawi indicates children with an SpO2 of 90–93% treated with oxygen had better survival than those referred at a lower SpO₂, highlighting the potential benefit of oxygen.10 However, WHO guidelines do not recommend oxygen for children with an SpO₂ of 90–93% unless emergency signs are also present.

A randomised controlled trial (RCT) is needed to determine the optimal management for children with an SpO2 of 90–93% in LMICs. Evidence from high-income countries supports permissive hypoxia strategies targeting SpO2 of 88–92% among multiple paediatric patient populations including bronchiolitis and mechanically ventilated patients.16,19 However, optimal thresholds and treatment strategies may differ in LMICs with high burdens of anaemia and malnutrition and without advanced respiratory support options. High-flow nasal cannula (HFNC) with oxygen has shown promise in improving ventilation and work of breathing.20,22 Additionally, as it has the ability to blend air and oxygen from compressed oxygen or an oxygen concentrator, it may be oxygen sparing, a key issue in resource-constrained settings where oxygen is scarce. However, the effectiveness of HFNC in African LMICs remains an important unanswered question.23 24 To address this evidence gap, we designed an open-label, three-arm RCT comparing standard care without oxygen to low-flow nasal cannula (LFNC) and HFNC oxygen therapy among children with pneumonia and an SpO₂ of 90–93%. As RCT implementation in LMICs poses unique challenges, we conducted a pilot RCT with the primary objective to assess protocol fidelity.25 Secondary objectives included evaluating consent refusal rates, participant attrition, intervention safety and treatment failure.

Methods and materials

Study design

The NoGoLO₂ study was an open, three-arm RCT comparing LFNC and HFNC oxygen to standard care without oxygen therapy for children with an SpO₂ of 90–93% in Malawi. This pilot feasibility study was conducted at Salima District Hospital (SDH) in Salima, Malawi from April to June 2024.

Study population

Children 1–59 months old with WHO-defined pneumonia and an SpO2 of 90–93% were eligible. WHO-defined pneumonia was defined as cough or difficulty in breathing with either fast breathing (≥60 breaths/min in 1 month olds, ≥50 breaths/min in 2–11 months old, ≥40 breaths/min in 1–5 years old), chest indrawing or ≥1 WHO general danger sign(s) (vomiting everything, history of acute convulsions, lethargy, inability to eat or drink).26 Exclusion criteria included gestational age <34 weeks, congenital heart disease, sickle cell disease, congenital anomalies, previous participation or a WHO-defined emergency sign. Emergency signs included: (1) obstructed breathing; (2) apnoea; (3) grunting; (4) severe respiratory distress ≥2 signs (head nodding, nasal flaring, tracheal tugging) and ≤38.3°C axillary temperature or ≥3 signs and >38.3°C axillary temperature; (5) uncompensated shock (capillary refill time >3 s, tachycardia and weak pulse, or low or unmeasurable systolic blood pressure); (6) Blantyre coma score ≤4; (7) active convulsions; or (8) diarrhoea with severe dehydration (altered mental status, sunken eyes, very slow skin pinch return). Children with an emergency sign and an SpO2 of <94% received oxygen therapy per WHO guidelines. They could be re-evaluated and enrolled within 24 hours if the sign resolved.

Study site and staffing

SDH is a 250-bed government facility that hospitalises ∼7000 children annually. The highest level of paediatric respiratory support is LFNC from oxygen concentrators. One general physician, four non-physician clinical officers and 10 nurses staff the SDH paediatric ward. The SDH research infrastructure is supported by the University of North Carolina Project Malawi. Additional dedicated research staff included one research nurse and one paediatrician who were trained according to study protocols and equipment use. The study paediatrician and nurse screened and enrolled participants and provided study care, including two times per day examinations, vital sign monitoring every 4 hours and weaning of all participants from LFNC and HFNC oxygen support. SDH staff were sensitised to the project prior to initiation and nurses were trained to monitor participants overnight with vital sign checks every 4 hours but were restricted from any participant oxygen or HFNC weaning. Vital signs were logged overnight and reviewed during a handoff procedure each morning. Additionally, study procedures were reviewed during an evening handoff daily. The study paediatrician was available by phone at all times to address any participant issues identified by SDH nursing staff, including overnight, and assistance with oxygen escalation as needed.

Screening and randomisation

Children were screened for eligibility at both the outpatient clinic and inpatient paediatric ward of SDH by study staff, which included a clinical examination for signs of pneumonia and pulse oximetry measurement (Masimo Rad-G) of 90–93% while breathing in room air and using a paediatric-sized clip probe. All patients presenting to the paediatric ward were screened for eligibility. If the patient was receiving oxygen prior to enrolment, oxygen was weaned to room air and the patient was monitored for a 5-minute wash-out period to determine oxygen saturation. Within 24 hours of presentation to SDH, caregivers of eligible children provided written informed consent in the local language to participate. Participants were randomised 1:1:1 using a computer-generated randomisation sequence with block permutations of seven patients to one of three study arms: standard care, LFNC or HFNC oxygen.

Trial interventions

The trial had two interventions: LFNC and HFNC oxygen. Participants in the standard care arm were observed in the hospital without oxygen as per WHO recommendation.26 WHO recommends children with pneumonia and without any general danger signs to be treated with oral antibiotics at home. For participant safety and to maintain consistency in participant care between the trial arms, standard care participants were hospitalised for at least one night. They met treatment failure criteria if they subsequently had an SpO2 of <90%.

Participants in the LFNC arm received oxygen at 0.5–2 L/min (LPM) from a 10 LPM capacity oxygen concentrator by simple nasal cannula with flow adjusted to maintain an SpO₂ of ≥94%. HFNC arm participants received 2 L/kg/min oxygen flow via the HFNC device (Airvo 3, Fisher & Paykel) using a single-use nasal cannula interface (Optiflow Junior, Fisher & Paykel) and tubing, and a 10-LPM capacity oxygen concentrator to warm, humidify and blend air and oxygen to a specific fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2) concentration. For all HFNC participants, the FiO2 was titrated to maintain an SpO2 of ≥94%. The HFNC device included multiple nasal cannula sizes, a battery backup system for uninterrupted use during power outages and an automated system for disinfecting the device between participants.

After 12 hours participants in the LFNC and HFNC arms were systematically weaned from support by study staff following a protocol. LFNC participants aged 1–2 months were initiated on 0.5 LPM and weaned directly to room air, while 2–59 months old were initiated on 2 LPM, weaned to 1 LPM after 12 hours and then transitioned to room air after an additional 4 hours. HFNC participants were weaned on the same time schedule as LFNC participants from 2 L/kg/min to 1 L/kg/min followed by room air. In both groups, children were reassessed 1 hour after each wean. In all study arms, those with worsening respiratory distress or hypoxaemia had nasopharyngeal suctioning and received nebulised salbutamol. If respiratory distress was unresolved, oxygen support was re-escalated, and weaning was reattempted after 4 hours. All participants were monitored for at least 12 hours in room air before discharge. LFNC and HFNC participants met treatment failure criteria and exited the trial protocol if they developed severe respiratory distress or an SpO2 of <94% despite maximum therapy (LFNC participants 1–2 months old: 0.5 LPM, 2–59 months old 2 LPM; HFNC participants 2 L/kg/min and 100% FiO2). Participants with treatment failure were escalated at the discretion of the treating physician in collaboration with the physicians at SDH, including referral to a tertiary hospital when appropriate.

Participants in all three study arms received intravenous antibiotics while hospitalised and oral antibiotics at discharge to complete at least 5 days (d) of treatment, per WHO pneumonia treatment guidelines. The study relied on hospital medication stocks, but when stock outs became frequent, a dedicated medication supply was procured to augment and support local supplies.

Participants were contacted by phone to ascertain their 14-day outcome starting from day of enrolment with a 2-day window period through day 16. If participants were unreached by day 16, then a home visit was conducted within the next 3 days. All participants without phone access had one home visit between day 14 and day 16.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was trial protocol feasibility, defined as an average of ≤2 protocol violations of any kind per participant. Protocol deviations were classified as intervention-related if they were violations in the oxygen intervention including initiation, assessments or weaning. Deviations were classified as routine care protocol deviations if related to antibiotic or non-oxygen supportive care. Secondary outcomes included the proportion of eligible children consented and enrolled, participant attrition and the proportion of participants with follow-up by day 14, as well as the characteristics and completeness of potential endpoints for a larger trial, including hospital length of stay (LOS), mortality and treatment failure within 14 d of intake. Treatment failure was defined as an SpO2 of <90% for standard care participants or <94% in the LFNC and HFNC arms despite maximum therapy as described, any new severe respiratory distress (grunting, very severe chest indrawing), ongoing oxygen use at day 14 or any new WHO-defined emergency sign.

Sample size and statistical analysis

A sample size of 21 children provided 80% power to estimate, with 8% precision, that at least 90% of participants would have two or less protocol violations. Analyses were intention to treat. Continuous variables with a normal distribution were summarised as mean (SD) and with a non-parametric distribution as median (IQR). Categorical variables were summarised as number (percentage). We used the χ² test of independence to compare categorical outcomes between the three study arms and one-way analysis of variance for between-arm comparisons of continuous outcomes. An ⍺ level of 0.025 was set after adjusting for multiple hypothesis testing by Bonferroni correction.

Patient and public involvement

Members of the public were involved in the design, approval and oversight of the trial protocol, providing valuable input to enhance its relevance and ethical conduct.

Across all study arms, there were 16 protocol violations among 21 participants for a mean of 0.8 violations per participant (table 2). Three participants (14.3%) had two protocol violations, and no children had >2. Intervention-related protocol violations accounted for seven events (43.8%), including three delayed clinical assessments and four incorrect oxygen weaning decisions. Three incorrect oxygen weans occurred when monitored by SDH nurses. Despite deviations in the weaning protocol, all three children tolerated the oxygen weans without clinical consequences. Two delayed clinical assessments were related to caregivers temporarily leaving the ward with their child, with reassessments completed after returning. Routine care protocol violations occurred in nine cases (56.3%), all of which were from first-line medication stock outs, including eight first-line antibiotic stock outs and one salbutamol nebuliser stock out. In all cases, alternative treatment regimens were used.

Discussion

In preparation for a larger trial, this pilot RCT demonstrates the feasibility of implementing a randomised protocol of LFNC and HFNC oxygen and standard care without oxygen for children with an SpO2 of 90–93% in a low-resource hospital in Malawi. Study procedures, including screening, enrolment, randomisation, intervention delivery and follow-up, were all successfully executed. Importantly, all participants completed the study. Overall, protocol adherence was high, and the nature and frequency of protocol deviations primarily reflected operational challenges rather than participant safety concerns. These findings support the feasibility of conducting a similarly designed, larger RCT powered for a treatment failure outcome.

Caregiver hesitancy, particularly related to oxygen therapy, emerged as an enrolment challenge, as 28% of 29 eligible participants declined consent. Among those who provided a reason, fear of oxygen was a common theme. This finding aligns with previous LMIC studies showing many caregivers perceive oxygen as causally related to death rather than life-saving.27 28 Misconceptions and fear surrounding oxygen have been documented across diverse settings, including Malawi, and shown to influence care-seeking and treatment acceptance.27,29 However, this fear is modifiable as survival and quality of care become more common.30 As future studies and implementation efforts expand oxygen access, especially with newer modalities like HFNC, community sensitisation and caregiver engagement will be essential to build trust to improve oxygen uptake and will be essential for a successful NoGoLO2 trial. Additionally, study enrolment may improve over the course of a multiyear trial as communities become more familiar with oxygen therapy.

Protocol violations in this pilot RCT were commonly attributed to two key operational challenges: medication availability and 24-hour research staff coverage. Stock-outs of first-line antibiotics necessitated substitutions with alternative regimens, reflecting systemic supply constraints that are common across many LMICs. Additionally, the pilot trial was staffed during daytime hours by dedicated research personnel, with overnight care provided by hospital staff trained to monitor and notify an on-call study paediatrician. This arrangement led to several protocol violations from delayed clinical assessments and incorrect oxygen weaning. While SDH staff were trained not to wean participants, some participants were still inadvertently weaned. In the formal NoGoLO2, we plan to address these limitations through trial medications and 24-hour in-hospital coverage by a dedicated research team, as the intention of the study is to provide proof of efficacy of interventions. However, we recognise this will reduce generalisability of results to routine care where these variations in quality are more likely to occur.

Trial follow-up for 14-day outcomes was successfully achieved for all participants, with most reached by phone. Despite widespread mobile phone access in Malawi, several caregivers lacked phones. For these participants, study staff conducted in-person home visits—the first time our team implemented patient tracing in this study area. While successful, home visits were resource intensive, highlighting the substantial logistical and staffing resources required for follow-up in a larger trial. To improve participant monitoring in the early postdischarge period, we plan to explore the feasibility of wearable pulse oximeters to track participant SpO2 levels between hospital discharge and follow-up. This approach, informed by the pilot trial, aims to balance the rigour of outcome ascertainment with the operational demands of community-based follow-up in rural Malawi.

It is unknown whether children with an SpO2 of 90–93% require oxygen or if close monitoring alone may sufficiently ensure clinical safety and recovery. In this pilot RCT, all participants were hospitalised and monitored to optimise safety and standardise clinical oversight. Should a larger trial fail to demonstrate superiority of either oxygen approach over hospitalisation without oxygen, the role of hospital versus home monitoring without oxygen therapy will warrant further investigation as a next step. In the trial’s current design, we have conservatively assumed that hospital monitoring, even without oxygen, is necessary to safeguard trial participant safety.

This study has several limitations. First, the study was conducted at a single district hospital with an established research infrastructure, which may not be representative of care in other LMICs. Second, caregiver hesitancy around oxygen therapy contributed to consent refusal, which could introduce selection bias or narrow the external validity of the trial by restricting the types of participants who take part. This underscores the need for targeted community engagement during the full trial and in routine healthcare systems.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this pilot RCT demonstrates a multiarm oxygen protocol for hospitalised children with an SpO2 of 90–93% can be feasibly implemented in Malawi. High protocol adherence, successful intervention delivery and complete follow-up across all arms support the implementation of the full NoGoLO₂ trial, which will evaluate the comparative effectiveness of oxygen strategies for Malawian children with an SpO2 of 90–93%.
