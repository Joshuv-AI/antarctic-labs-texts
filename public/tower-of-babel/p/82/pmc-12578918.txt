Crustal constraints on the surface expression of mantle upwelling in a back-arc passive margin setting
Authors: Jeong-Yeon Hwang, Sung-Joon Chang, Yu Jin Sohn, Kwang-Hee Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPassive margins offer valuable insights into the interplay between tectonic processes and surface elevation, as topographic signals are often preserved under long-term tectonic quiescence. Gangwon Province, located in the mid-eastern Korean Peninsula, exemplifies such a setting, shaped by back-arc opening of the East Sea (Sea of Japan). Here, we present Vp/Vs ratios, residual topography, and high-resolution S-wave crustal velocity models from joint inversion of teleseismic receiver functions and Rayleigh-wave dispersion curves. Our results reveal low S-wave velocities and high Vp/Vs ratios throughout the crust beneath the coastal region, suggesting shallow-reaching thermal effects from mantle upwelling. In contrast, west of the Taebaek Mountain Range, low velocities are confined to the lower crust, and Vp/Vs ratios are relatively low, consistent with thick, felsic crust inhibiting upward thermal propagation. These findings demonstrate how crustal properties modulate the surface and thermal expressions of mantle upwelling, likely caused by edge-driven convection in passive margin settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-22114-y.Keywords: Receiver function, Crustal structure, Passive margin, Mantle upwelling, Edge-driven convectionSubject terms: Natural hazards, Ocean sciences, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

Continental topography is a first-order expression of Earth’s geodynamic processes. While largely governed by isostatic responses to variations in crustal thickness and density, it is also shaped by dynamic support from mantle convection1–3. Passive continental margins, due to their tectonic quiescence, preserve topographic signals produced by past tectonic and mantle processes, and thus provide key constraints on the mechanisms that drive long-term surface evolution4,5.

Gangwon Province, in the mid-eastern part of the Korean Peninsula, exhibits prominent topographic contrasts across the NNW–SSE trending Taebaek Mountain Range (TMR), including a steep coastal escarpment (Fig. 1). This region was formed by back-arc opening of the East Sea (Sea of Japan) during the early Neogene6,7, and associated basaltic formations are distributed in northeastern Gangwon Province8 (Fig. 1). These features suggest that the region retains a strong imprint of its tectonic history, making it an ideal natural laboratory to examine how crustal structure and mantle dynamics shape continental topography in passive margin settings.

Understanding whether present-day topography is isostatically compensated or influenced by deeper mantle processes requires accurate constraints on crustal structure. Crustal thickness and density estimates are critical to distinguish isostatic from dynamic topographic components and to avoid misinterpretation of geodynamic signals9,10. Receiver function analysis is an effective tool for probing 1D crustal structure in low-seismicity regions like the Korean Peninsula, using P-to-S conversions from teleseismic waveforms11. However, its lateral resolution depends strongly on the spatial density of seismic stations. Previous receiver function studies in the Korean Peninsula12–16 were limited by sparse and uneven station coverage, reducing their ability to resolve lateral crustal variations or identify localized features. The recent deployment of dense seismic arrays, particularly following the 2016 Gyeongju and 2017 Pohang earthquakes (Fig. S1), now enables improved imaging of crustal structure in previously underrepresented areas such as Gangwon Province.

In this study, we leverage 96 seismic stations and approximately five years of recordings to present a high-resolution crustal S-wave velocity model for Gangwon Province, derived from joint inversion of teleseismic receiver functions and Rayleigh-wave dispersion curves. The advantage of this approach is that it reduces the trade-offs between depth and velocity inherent in using receiver functions alone by adopting average velocities inferred from surface waves. By integrating additional constraints such as Vp/Vs ratios from H– stacking, residual topography, Bouguer gravity anomalies, and surface heat flow measurements, we investigate how variations in crustal structure influence the topographic and thermal signatures of mantle upwelling in a passive margin setting.

Results

Distributions of Moho depth and residual topography

We first examined Moho depth variations across Gangwon Province to evaluate whether they conform to Airy isostasy. Moho depths derived from our joint inversion of receiver functions and Rayleigh-wave dispersion curves range from 23.9 to 35.7 km, with an average depth of 31.1 km (Fig. 2). The spatial distribution shows a shallow crust beneath the coastal region, an abrupt deepening across the TMR, and the greatest depths in the southern part of Gangwon Province. This result is consistent with our H– stacking estimates, which yield Moho depths ranging from 23.1 to 36.0 km with uncertainties of ± 0.9–4.7 km (Fig. S2). Importantly, both methods reveal similar spatial patterns, with a standard deviation of about ± 0.8 km, which are generally consistent with previous receiver function studies12–14,16.

Because the lateral resolution of receiver function imaging is largely controlled by inter-station spacing, the recently deployed dense array used in this study, with an average spacing of 12.6 km, provides significantly improved lateral resolution compared to previous works. The enhanced lateral resolution of this study allows us to identify a notable discrepancy between Moho depth and surface topography beneath the TMR (Figs. 2d, S3). Residual topography reaches values up to 1200 m along the eastern part of Gangwon Province. In contrast, relatively small residuals are found west of the TMR, with weakly negative residual topography in localized areas, including the region of the deepest Moho depth. These observations suggest that Airy isostasy explains most of the topographic variation in Gangwon Province, while positive departures from isostatic balance occur along the eastern part, pointing to additional dynamic or compositional contributions to surface elevation.

Spatial variations in S-wave velocities and Vp/Vs ratios

In Gangwon Province, Vp/Vs ratios range from 1.68 to 1.85, with an average of 1.75 (Fig. 3i). Higher Vp/Vs ratios ( 1.77) are observed at most coastal stations, whereas lower Vp/Vs ratios are more common inland. These results are consistent with previous studies13,17. When considered together, the Vp/Vs ratios and S-wave velocity structure reveal a distinct contrast between coastal and inland regions (Fig. 3). The coastal region, marked by high Vp/Vs ratios, exhibits relatively low S-wave velocities (3.3–3.7 km/s), forming an elongated NNW-SSE trending anomaly along the coastline (L1) that extends continuously to the lower crust (~ 24 km depth). In contrast, the inland region is characterized by lower Vp/Vs ratios, and relatively low S-wave velocities (3.7–4.0 km/s) forming a broader anomaly (L2), confined to the lower crust (~ 30 km depth). Notably, 1D velocity profiles from stations within L2 show velocity inversions below ~ 20 km depth extending down to the Moho (Fig. S4).

To assess model uncertainties, we performed multiple genetic algorithm (GA) inversions with different random seeds and constructed an ensemble of acceptable models. The standard deviation of S-wave velocities across the ensemble was then evaluated at each depth (Fig. S5). Overall, the uncertainties are small (± 0.2 km/s) throughout most depths, but slightly higher values are observed in the shallow crust (< 10 km). This increase is likely due to the limited sensitivity of the Rayleigh-wave phase velocity range employed in the inversions. Nevertheless, the magnitude of these variations remains modest, supporting the robustness of the principal structural features identified in this study.

Discussion

Non-isostatic uplift in Gangwon Province

Moho depth estimates from the joint inversion of receiver functions and Rayleigh-wave dispersion curves in Gangwon Province show relatively shallow crust beneath the coastal region and abrupt thickening across the TMR. However, this variation in crustal thickness does not fully align with surface topography, particularly beneath the TMR, as indicated by positive residual topography (Fig. 2c). Such discrepancies, combined with negative Bouguer gravity anomaly (Fig. 2a), are widely recognized as evidence of non-isostatic support10,18,19, and are commonly interpreted as surface expressions of sub-lithospheric processes, including dynamic support from mantle flow1,20.

The eastern margin of the Korean Peninsula underwent significant lithospheric thinning during the Neogene, driven by slab rollback of the Pacific plate, followed by extensional deformation, thermal erosion, and hydrous weakening of the overriding Eurasian plate6,7,21. These processes likely promoted passive mantle upwelling beneath the thinned lithosphere, leading to decompression melting and the initiation of rifting22,23. The resulting upwelling may have generated localized convection cells, enhancing edge-driven convection, a mechanism that becomes more effective in regions with strong lateral variations in lithospheric thickness24, such as the continental margin along the eastern Korean Peninsula (Fig. 4a). Supporting this interpretation, Song et al.21 identified pronounced low-velocity anomalies at 60–120 km depth beneath the eastern coastal region, which spatially correspond to the areas of positive residual topography in our study. These anomalies likely reflect the presence of partial melts associated with localized mantle upwelling. Additionally, helium-isotope signatures from fault-related springs along the eastern Korean Peninsula spatially align with the areas of thinned lithosphere21 and positive residual topography, suggesting that the observed volatiles originate from asthenospheric upwelling25.

Similar patterns, where elevated topography is maintained not solely by crustal thickness but by dynamic mantle support, have been reported in other passive margin settings26–28. The spatial correlation between positive residual topography, negative Bouguer gravity anomaly, and crustal thinning in eastern Gangwon Province supports the interpretation that present-day elevation is influenced by active mantle dynamics in addition to isostatic effects.

Lateral variability in crustal response to mantle dynamics

Although mantle dynamics appear to influence the eastern part of Gangwon Province, the crustal properties exhibit clear lateral variations. Notably, coastal and inland regions differ not only in Moho depth but also in crustal S-wave velocity structure and average Vp/Vs ratios.

The coastal zone, referred to here as the L1 area, is characterized by relatively low S-wave velocities and high Vp/Vs ratios extending into the lower crust (Fig. 3). These features closely align with geophysical signatures typically associated with mantle upwelling. This process may supply mafic material to the crust and enhance heat flux, leading to thermal weakening and reduced rigidity along the coastline29, thereby lowering S-wave velocities and increasing Vp/Vs ratios. This interpretation is supported by the presence of Neogene basaltic formations in northeastern Gangwon Province (Fig. 1c), suggesting that partial melts may have ascended to near-surface levels.

In contrast, the L2 area in the inland region, encompassing the TMR, exhibits relatively low Vp/Vs ratios and S-wave velocities. These low velocities are laterally extensive but vertically confined to the lower crust (Fig. 3). Several 1D velocity profiles in the L2 area show velocity inversions below ~ 20 km depth extending to the Moho (Fig. S4), suggesting that mantle-derived thermal effects may be largely confined to the lower crust. Despite evidence for non-isostatic uplift west of the TMR, similar to that observed in the coastal region, upward thermal propagation appears limited.

One plausible explanation for the suppression of thermal influence in the inland region relates to the crustal composition and thickness. During the Jurassic, Gangwon Province underwent the Daebo orogeny, which led to the widespread emplacement of granitoid plutons extending from the surface down to the lower crust (~ 20 km), particularly within the Gyeonggi Massif30–32 (Fig. 1c). These granitoid intrusions, characterized by their felsic composition, significantly reduced the average density of the crust by partially replacing the typically mafic lower crust33. Simultaneously, the extensive and thick felsic intrusions may have acted as a thermal buffer, impeding the upward transport of heat and mantle-derived material due to their strong viscosity contrast with underlying upwelling mantle34. Supporting this interpretation, S-wave velocities at 4 km depth show a general negative correlation with surface heat flow, whereas the relationship becomes largely decoupled at 30 km depth (Figs. 3a, S6). This pattern may imply that thermal anomalies in the lower crust are insulated from the surface by the intervening granitoid plutons, although alternative explanations cannot be ruled out. To more rigorously test this hypothesis, targeted thermal modeling is recommended in future work.

Taken together, our results suggest the following scenario for Cenozoic tectonic evolution in Gangwon Province (Fig. 4). Localized mantle upwelling, likely caused by edge-driven convection at the continental margin, facilitated the ascent of thermal anomalies to near-surface levels along the coast, where the crust is relatively thin. In contrast, the inland region, underlain by thick, felsic crust, appears to have inhibited vertical thermal propagation. These findings highlight the potential role of crustal properties in modulating both the surface and thermal expression of mantle upwelling, revealing rapid spatial variability even within a geographically limited region in passive continental margin settings.

Methods

We analyzed waveform data from 96 seismic stations, including 24 broadband and 41 accelerometer stations operated by the Korea Meteorological Administration (KMA), 7 broadband and 4 accelerometer stations operated by the Korea Institute of Geoscience and Mineral Resources (KIGAM), and 20 temporary broadband stations deployed by Pusan National University (PNU) in and around Gangwon Province (Fig. S7). These dense seismic arrays have an average inter-station spacing of 12.6 km, which is significantly smaller than the previous study of 25.8 km16, thereby providing higher spatial resolution. These stations recorded 369 teleseismic events (Mw ≥ 5.8) that occurred between March 18, 2019, and May 31, 2024, with epicentral distances of 30°–90° (Fig. S8a). After the mean and trend removal, we applied a 0.01–2.0 Hz bandpass filter and selected waveforms with a signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) ≥ 3.0. Horizontal components were corrected for sensor misorientation using Rayleigh-wave polarization analysis39.

Receiver functions were computed using Seispy40, with a water-level parameter of 0.01 and a Gaussian filter width of 2.511,12. We excluded receiver functions exhibiting no clear P-to-S conversion, shifted P-wave arrivals, or strong energy on the transverse component. Moho depths and Vp/Vs ratios were estimated using H– stacking41, assuming an average crustal P-wave velocity of 6.3 km/s and applying weighting factors of 0.7, 0.2, and 0.1, for Ps, PpPs, and PpSs + PsPs phases, respectively13.

To mitigate trade-offs between crustal thickness and seismic velocity, we performed a joint inversion of receiver functions and Rayleigh-wave dispersion curves using a micro-genetic algorithm (micro-GA), which accelerates convergence by replacing all but the best-fit model in each generation14,42. Rayleigh-wave phase velocity maps and their checkerboard tests (Fig. S9) were adopted from Park et al.43, who applied Helmholtz tomography to incorporate amplitude information and account for finite-frequency effects44. These maps cover periods from 10 to 25 s at 5 s intervals with an effective lateral resolution of approximately 0.16° × 0.16° and a grid spacing of 0.03° × 0.03°. Dispersion data were extracted at the nearest grid point to each station for use in the inversion. In our inversion, these dispersion data serve as constraints, such that whenever the difference between the observed Rayleigh-wave phase velocities and the synthetic phase velocities predicted from the trial velocity models at a given period becomes significant, a penalty is added to the fitness function of micro-GA12,14.

To minimize azimuthal and epicentral bias in receiver function stacking, we selected 147 teleseismic events with similar epicentral distances and categorized them into three back-azimuth groups (Fig. S8b). The ranges of epicentral distances and back-azimuths were approximately 43°–50° and 198°–229° (Group 1), 37°–45° and 175°–194° (Group 2), and 45°–50° and 145°–159° (Group 3), respectively. Considering that the vertical resolution of receiver function analysis is on the order of 1–2 km, given a corner frequency of ~ 1 Hz associated with Gaussian filter width of 2.5, we constructed S-wave velocity profiles for each group using 23 layers, with 1 km intervals to 6 km depth and 2 km intervals from 6 to 40 km depth. Groups with fewer than five receiver functions or profiles with unreasonably high velocities ( 4.0 km/s above 6 km depth) were excluded. For stations with fewer than 15 receiver functions, stacking was performed without back-azimuth grouping. We repeated the inversion five times per group with different random seeds in micro-GA, and obtained the final S-wave velocity profile for each station by averaging 5 to 15 best-fit profiles, depending on the number of usable groups (Fig. S10).

To assess the contribution of mantle dynamics to the uplift of Gangwon Province, we computed residual topography as the difference between observed and isostatic topography following the Airy compensation framework45. The isostatic topography was derived from our final 23-layer S-wave velocity profiles, where Moho depth was defined as the layer at which S-wave velocity exceeds 4.0 km/s with the highest velocity gradient. For each layer above the Moho, the density was estimated by converting S-wave velocities to P-wave velocities using station-specific Vp/Vs ratios and applying the empirical relation of Brocher46. The layer thickness-weighted average of these densities provided a mean crustal density for each station (Fig. S11), which was then compared to a reference crustal column defined at isostatic equilibrium45. In this study, we did not apply any additional corrections for sediments or the buoyant effects of Jurassic granitoid plutons in the study area, as both features are inherently accounted for in our velocity model. The shallowest layers in our models exhibit S-wave velocities of ~ 1.7–2.36 km/s at ~ 1 km depth, which we interpret as representing the sedimentary cover. Jurassic granitoid plutons in the lower crust are also partially represented in the S-wave velocity models as localized zones of relatively low velocities at ~ 30 km depth (Fig. 3g).
