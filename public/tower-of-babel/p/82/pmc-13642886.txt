Knowledge, attitudes, and practices towards mental health amongst adults in Jigawa State, northwest Nigeria
Authors: Nuruddeen Muhammad, Fawaz Babandi, Umar Also, Usman Muhammad Ibrahim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMental health disorders including depression, anxiety, and substance use disorders affect an estimated 150 million people across Africa, yet services remain grossly under-resourced and largely inaccessible. In many settings, including Nigeria, limited awareness, stigmatizing attitudes, and suboptimal mental health practices further compound the burden. This study assessed knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) toward mental health among adults in northwest Nigeria.MethodsWe conducted a community-based cross-sectional study in Jigawa State, northwest Nigeria, guided by the classical Knowledge–Attitude–Practice (KAP) framework. A total of 398 adults were recruited using a multistage sampling technique. Data were collected through a combination of online surveys and interviewer-administered questionnaires. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS version 22.0, with significance set at p ≤ 0.05.ResultsParticipants had a mean age of 34.5 ± 8.2 years (range: 18–65), with 83.9% aged 25–45 years. Although 70.9% demonstrated adequate mental health knowledge, attitudes and practices were considerably poorer. Less than half expressed willingness to interact comfortably with individuals living with mental illness (43.2%), believed they could lead productive lives (44.2%), or had ever sought professional help for mental or emotional concerns (43.7%). Only 34.7% rejected the notion that individuals with mental illness are inherently dangerous, and just 20.4% would permit their children to interact with someone experiencing mental illness. Overall, 48.5% exhibited positive attitudes, and 46.7% reported satisfactory practices. Female sex independently predicted both knowledge and attitudes. Females were twice as likely as males to have adequate knowledge (aOR = 2.0; 95% CI: 1.1–3.6) and nearly twice as likely to demonstrate positive attitudes (aOR = 1.9; 95% CI: 1.1–3.5). Prior hospitalization and known family history of mental illness were significantly associated with more favorable attitudes. Residence in the southwest senatorial zone independently predicted satisfactory practices (aOR = 0.4; 95% CI: 0.2–0.9), while absence of prior mental illness was associated with lower odds of appropriate practices (aOR = 0.4; 95% CI: 0.2–0.9). Knowledge scores demonstrated moderate positive correlations with both attitudes (r = 0.4, p < 0.001) and practices (r = 0.4, p < 0.001).ConclusionDespite relatively adequate levels of knowledge, attitudes and practices toward mental health remain suboptimal among adults in northwest Nigeria. Consistent with the classical KAP framework, knowledge was positively associated with both attitudes and practices. Strengthening community-level mental health literacy, reducing stigma, and expanding accessible services are urgently needed to translate knowledge gains into improved behavioral outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s44192-026-00536-y.

Introduction

Globally, approximately one in four individuals will experience a mental or neurological disorder during their lifetime [1, 2]. The World Health Organization (WHO) reports that over 1 billion people worldwide currently live with a mental health condition, with anxiety and depression being the most prevalent [3]. Mental disorders rank among the leading contributors to long-term disability and account for a significant portion of lost healthy life years [4]. WHO estimates indicate that mental health conditions are responsible for roughly one-sixth of all years lived with disability (YLDs) globally [4], and people with severe mental illnesses often have a life expectancy 10–20 years shorter than the general population [4].

In 2021, there were an estimated 727 000 suicides worldwide, making suicide a major cause of death among young people [5]. Depression and anxiety alone are estimated to reduce global productivity by around US $1 trillion annually [6]. Despite this burden, median government expenditure on mental health in many countries remains low, at approximately 2% of total health budgets [6]. There is a global median of just 13 mental health professionals per 100 000 people, reflecting persistent workforce shortages and slow progress in implementing community-based mental health services [7].

To address these challenges, WHO Member States have committed to the Comprehensive Mental Health Action Plan 2013–2030, which seeks to strengthen governance and community-based care, enhance prevention and promotion strategies, and improve data collection and research on mental health [8].Despite ongoing global efforts to improve mental health, substantial gaps persist in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where mental disorders impose a significant burden yet receive limited attention from health systems and policymakers. Structural challenges, including scarce resources, inadequate investment, and a wide treatment gap, impede effective care delivery [9]. Critical barriers to developing mental health services in these settings include insufficient financing, a limited trained workforce, difficulties integrating mental health into primary care, and low prioritization in national health agendas [10].

The shortage of mental health professionals in LMICs is severe, leaving populations far below the threshold needed to meet basic mental health service requirements [11]. Community-based psychosocial interventions for conditions such as substance use disorders remain grossly inadequate; at least 75% of individuals with mental or substance use disorders receive no treatment, with structural, policy, and cultural factors limiting intervention scale-up [12]. Persistent stigma further exacerbates these challenges, resulting in delayed help-seeking, restricted access to care, human rights violations, and poor clinical outcomes [13].

Primary care providers face additional hurdles, including insufficient training, limited resources, and disruptions from crises such as pandemics, all of which compromise the diagnosis and management of mental disorders at the community level [14]. Community-based interventions, where available, are often insufficiently integrated into local infrastructures and fail to address critical barriers such as transportation, resource limitations, and community engagement [15]. Compounding these issues, mental health practitioners encounter widespread ethical and professional challenges, including restricted patient autonomy, resource constraints, and inadequate access to information, reflecting deeper systemic problems within service delivery [16].

In Nigeria, similar to other low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), efforts to integrate mental health into primary care through the Mental Health Gap Action Programme (mhGAP) pilot have revealed substantial challenges, including insufficient training, supervision deficits, and structural limitations. Despite these obstacles, evidence indicates that successful integration is possible with adequate planning and support [17]. This is particularly important given the rising incidence of mental health disorders in the country [18, 19], compounded by persistent stigma, limited availability of local services, and the need for patients to travel long distances to access care. Consequently, fewer than one-fifth of individuals with mental disorders receive treatment, and retention in care remains extremely low [20].

The country faces a profound shortage of mental health professionals, with roughly 250 psychiatrists serving a population exceeding 200 million [21]. Factors such as stigma, inadequate infrastructure, and limited research exposure further constrain psychiatric practice, highlighting a substantial treatment gap [21]. Mental health continues to be a neglected public health priority in Nigeria, with significant deficits in policy, financing, workforce capacity, research, and integration of services into primary care, despite increasing demand [22].

These nationwide challenges are similarly reflected in our study area, where mental health services are limited to a single state-owned facility, the availability of trained personnel to provide basic mental healthcare is inadequate, and both funding and policy implementation remain insufficient. The limited prioritization of mental health services and infrastructure may further hinder the delivery of mental health services, including mental health education, health promotion activities, community mobilization, and mass media campaigns. The availability of such interventions could improve community knowledge, which may in turn foster positive attitudes and appropriate mental health practices. Conversely, deficiencies in these services may contribute to poor knowledge, negative attitudes, and inadequate mental health practices among community members [23, 24].

A study conducted in Nigeria on knowledge, attitudes, and practices toward mental health showed that awareness of mental disorders was high, with 95.5% of respondents having heard about mental illness and 31.0% reporting a relationship with someone affected by a mental disorder. Despite this high level of awareness, nearly half of the participants demonstrated poor knowledge of mental disorders, although the majority exhibited positive attitudes. Furthermore, having a relative with a mental disorder was significantly associated with better knowledge levels [23].

Similarly another study revealed that, misconceptions regarding the causes of mental illness were common, while negative perceptions toward individuals with mental disorders were widespread. Up to 96.5% of respondents considered mentally ill persons dangerous because of perceived violent behavior. Social intolerance was also prominent, as 82.7% reported being unwilling to engage in conversations with individuals with mental illness, and only 16.9% would consider marrying someone with a mental disorder. However, few socio-demographic factors were found to significantly predict poor knowledge and negative attitudes [24].

Based on this context, we hypothesized that adult community members in Jigawa State possess limited mental health knowledge, negative attitudes, and poor practices, and that knowledge is positively associated with both attitudes and practices. Accordingly, this study aimed to assess the level of knowledge, attitudes, and practices toward mental health among adults in Jigawa State, northwest Nigeria, with the objective of informing policymakers and stakeholders to develop actionable strategies for strengthening mental health services.

Methods

Study design and population

This study utilized a descriptive cross-sectional design to assess adult residents across the three senatorial districts of Jigawa State, employing both household surveys and an online questionnaire [25, 26]. The study employed a balanced mixed-mode data collection approach combining online self-administered and interviewer-administered surveys to enhance coverage, inclusiveness, response rates, representativeness, and data quality. While the online method improved accessibility, convenience, and rapid data collection among digitally literate participants, the interviewer-administered approach enabled participation of individuals with limited internet access or lower literacy levels. Equal allocation across both modes ensured comparability of responses, minimized selection bias, and allowed assessment of potential mode effects [25, 26].

Residents were defined as individuals belonging to a household within the selected community who had lived there for at least six months. Eligibility for participation was limited to adults aged 18 years and above. Individuals excluded from the study included temporary residents, visitors to the selected communities, critically ill persons, permanent residents who were unavailable during the period of data collection, and those unable to participate in the online survey because they lacked access to an Android device or internet connectivity.

Study area

Jigawa State, in northwestern Nigeria, has an estimated population of approximately 8 million as of 2024, based on projections from the 2006 census [27]. The state’s healthcare infrastructure includes over 700 primary health centers staffed by primary healthcare workers who follow the national Standing Order for diagnosis, treatment, and referral, alongside 18 secondary healthcare facilities managed by state and federal authorities. The healthcare workforce comprises 182 medical doctors, 1,827 nurses, and more than 3,000 Community Health Extension Workers (CHEWs) [25]. Mental health services are provided primarily through a state psychiatric hospital, with additional specialized care accessible at federal tertiary institutions within the state [27].

Sampling technique

A minimum sample size of 405 participants was determined and equally apportioned between two recruitment modalities: online respondents (n = 203) and participants interviewed face-to-face (n = 203) similar to an approach utilized by Mauz et al. [29]. For the interviewer-administered component, a multistage sampling design was implemented. In the first stage, one Local Government Area (LGA) was randomly selected from each of the three senatorial districts of Jigawa State: Gumel (northwest senatorial zone), Hadejia (northeast senatorial zone) and Dutse (southwest senatorial zone) using simple balloting. In the second stage, one rural (Gumel: Dan ama, Hadejia: Yankoli, Dutse: Kudai) and one urban political ward (Gumel: Dantanoma,, Hadejia: Atafi, Dutse: Limawa) were randomly chosen within each selected LGA. In the third stage, one settlement (Gumel: Dantanoma and Dan Ama, Hadejia: Atafi central, and Aguyaka, Dutse: Limawa and Kudai) were randomly selected from each selected ward.

Within each selected settlement, households were enumerated and assigned unique identification numbers to establish a sampling frame. The required sample size was proportionately allocated according to the number of eligible residents in each settlement. Systematic sampling was subsequently applied: the sampling interval was calculated by dividing the total number of households by the allocated sample size. After randomly selecting the first household, subsequent households were identified by adding the sampling interval until the target number was reached. In each selected household, one eligible adult was chosen through simple random selection from a comprehensive list of household members.

For the online survey component (n = 203), LGAs and community-based WhatsApp groups across the three senatorial districts were identified. The WhatsApp groups were identified through community leaders in the selected LGAs and the state ministry of health, including the Jigawa State Career Mentoring Group, which comprises registered eligible respondents across the senatorial zones and is coordinated by group administrators from the Ministry of Health, as well as the Gumel Majalissarmu Group and the Hadejia and Dutse community groups representing Hadejia and Dutse LGAs, respectively. Sample allocation to each district was proportionate to its projected population size: (Hadejia: 180,249, Dutse: 434,063, Gumel: 180,249). Group administrators were contacted and provided with a Google Forms link to the survey, with periodic reminders issued to enhance participation. Responses were included consecutively and monitored across districts until the proportionately allocated sample size for each senatorial zone was attained.

Classical Knowledge–Attitude–Practice (KAP) framework

The Knowledge–Attitude–Practice (KAP) framework is a public health model that explains the relationship between what people know, how they feel, and how they behave. It posits that knowledge influences attitudes, which in turn shape practices. We utilized these three domains separately to identify gaps between knowledge, beliefs, and behaviors targeted towards informed and evidence based mental health interventions in Nigeria. Though, the model acknowledges that this relationship is not always linear, as social, cultural, and environmental factors may influence outcomes [30]. It would provide a concrete baseline that would guide policy makers and other relevant stakeholders.

Procedure for data collection

Data were collected using a combination of a modified interviewer-administered instrument and a self-administered questionnaire adapted from previously validated tools [18–23, 27, 28]. The questionnaires were administered through an electronic data collection platform using Google Forms, with responses from both data collection modes automatically captured in real time.

The questionnaire comprised four sections: Section I captured participants’ socio-demographic characteristics; Section II assessed mental health knowledge; Section III evaluated attitudes toward mental health; and Section IV examined mental health–related practices.

To ensure cultural relevance and linguistic suitability, the questionnaire was independently translated into Hausa by two professionals from the Department of Linguistics, Bayero University Kano. Reliability testing was conducted through a pretest involving 40 questionnaires administered in a geographically separate LGA outside the main study area. The instrument demonstrated good internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha coefficients of 0.85 for knowledge items, 0.78 for practice items, and 0.84 for attitude items, yielding an overall average reliability score of 0.82.

Six trained healthcare workers served as research assistants and received comprehensive instruction on study objectives, community entry procedures, ethical considerations, and advocacy. For the online component, WhatsApp group administrators facilitated survey dissemination and issued reminders every six hours to optimize response rates. Participants were encouraged to carefully review and answer each item, and administrators were available to provide clarification when needed.

Consistency was ensured through standardized questionnaire administration, ongoing supervision, and cross-checking for completeness, while fidelity was maintained via structured questionnaires, uniform training, and adherence to study protocols during household interviews. These measures enhanced the study’s internal validity by ensuring that observed differences in household knowledge, attitudes, and practices reflected true variation rather than methodological bias, while also supporting the reliability and reproducibility of findings.

Data management

Field obtained through Microsoft Excel were cleaned and appropriately analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The normality test was conducted for the continuous variables using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Normally distributed numerical data were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while skewed numerical data were summarized using median with interquartile range (IQR). The categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages.

All questionnaires with completion time less than half the median duration were excluded from analysis. Other parameters considered for exclusion and or non-response that could potentially bias our findings were: Repeated contradictory answers showing a gap in understanding the content, responses with 20% or more unanswered items, logically inconsistent responses. Similarly, responses suspected of being altered and multiple submissions from respondents were excluded [31].

We used a scoring system for the outcome variables [31]. Fifteen questions each were used to assess knowledge, attitudes, and practices respectively. Knowledge items were scored 1 for all correct responses and 0 for incorrect or “Don’t know” answers, while attitude questions were rated on a 5-point Likert scale (5 = strongly agree to 1 = strongly disagree), and a reverse scoring system was employed for all the negatively worded questions. Similarly, practice questions were scored 1 for each correct practices and 0 for incorrect practices [31].

The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test showed that knowledge, attitude, and practice scores were normally distributed. The mean was used to categorize the outcomes variables: “Above-average scores” indicated positive attitudes, adequate knowledge, or satisfactory practices, whereas “below-average” indicated unsatisfactory practices, inadequate knowledge, negative attitudes.

The outcome variables were knowledge (inadequate vs. adequate), attitude (negative vs. positive), and practices (unsatisfactory vs. satisfactory), while socio-demographic and other characteristics served as independent variables. Binary logistic regression was used to identify independent determinants of mental health knowledge, attitude, and practice, with statistical significance set at P ≤ 5%.

Ethical approval

Ethical clearance for this study was obtained from the Health Research Ethics Committee of the Jigawa State Ministry of Health (Approval No. JGHREC/2024/0076) on 24 September 2024. The research was conducted in strict adherence to the ethical principles set forth in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Of the 405 questionnaires distributed, 398 satisfied the eligibility criteria and were included in the final analysis, yielding a response rate of 98.3%.

Socio-demographic and other characteristics

Participants’ ages ranged from 18 to 65 years, with a mean (± SD) of 34.5 ± 8.2 years. The majority (83.9%) were within the 25–45-year age bracket. Slightly over half (51.0%) reported living in households comprising 5–10 members.

Monthly income varied widely, ranging from ₦0 to ₦19,223,818, with a median income of ₦258,500 (interquartile range: ₦30,000–₦231,250). Approximately two-thirds (63.6%) reported earning ≥₦80,000 per month. Nearly all respondents (96.7%) had attained tertiary education as their highest level of educational qualification. Most participants (89.2%) identified as Hausa, and 22.1% were self-employed. A substantial proportion (70.4%) resided in urban areas, while 61.8% were married. More than half (54.0%) reported a prior history of hospitalization. About one-quarter (22.6%) were from the North-East senatorial zone of the state. The majority (93.7%) lived with other family members. A previous history of mental health challenges was reported by 12.1% of respondents, and 5.8% indicated a family history of mental illness (Table 1).

Parameters used to assess attitudes towards mental health

Fewer than half of respondents (43.2%) expressed willingness to feel comfortable interacting with individuals living with mental illness, and 44.2% believed that people with mental illness are capable of leading productive lives. Only 34.7% agreed that individuals with mental illness are not inherently dangerous. Moreover, less than one-quarter (20.4%) indicated that they would permit their children to interact or play with someone experiencing mental illness. In contrast, a substantial majority (72.1%) endorsed the view that mental health problems should be openly discussed within the community. Paradoxically, nearly two-thirds (63.1%) also agreed that mental illness ought to be concealed from the community (Table 3).

Descriptive statistics of knowledge, attitudes and practices

Knowledge scores ranged from 4 to 15, with a mean ± SD of 13.0 ± 2.0. Overall, 70.9% of respondents demonstrated adequate knowledge of mental health, whereas 29.1% had inadequate knowledge. Attitude scores varied between 2 and 15, with a mean ± SD of 10.1 ± 2.6. Fewer than half (48.5%) exhibited positive attitudes toward mental health, while a slightly higher proportion (51.5%) demonstrated negative attitudes. Practice scores also ranged from 2 to 15, yielding a mean ± SD of 10.1 ± 2.4. Less than half of respondents (46.7%) reported satisfactory mental health practices, compared with 53.3% who had unsatisfactory practices. Knowledge scores showed a statistically significant, moderate positive correlation with both attitude (r = 0.4, p < 0.001) and practice scores (r = 0.4, p < 0.001) (Table 5).

Factors associated with knowledge of mental health

Female sex emerged as an independent predictor of adequate mental health knowledge among adults. A significantly greater proportion of respondents aged over 45 years (85.7%, p = 0.01), widowed individuals (100%, p = 0.01), those whose highest educational attainment was Quranic education (100%, p = 0.001), and retirees from the civil service (100%, p < 0.001) demonstrated adequate knowledge of mental health.

Multivariable analysis indicated that male respondents had lower odds of possessing adequate mental health knowledge. Female adults were approximately twice as likely as their male counterparts to have adequate knowledge (aOR = 2.0; 95% CI: 1.1–3.6) (Table 6). Furthermore, a significantly higher proportion of non-indigenes (83.3%, p = 0.05), individuals residing with friends (87.5%, p = 0.05), those with health insurance coverage (80.5%, p = 0.001), and respondents without a prior history of mental health conditions (72.8%, p = 0.04) demonstrated adequate knowledge of mental health (Supplementary Table 1).

Discussion

Community knowledge, attitudes, and practices related to mental health in Nigeria have generally remained limited, with numerous studies highlighting significant gaps in mental health literacy and persistent negative perceptions. For instance, one community survey reported widespread misconceptions regarding the causes of mental illness and high levels of fear or stigma toward affected individuals. In that study, a majority of respondents perceived people with mental illness as dangerous and were hesitant to engage with them socially, reflecting entrenched misbeliefs and intolerant attitudes [24].

Overall, evidence across various Nigerian settings indicates that, despite increasing awareness of mental health issues, substantial knowledge deficits and stigmatizing attitudes continue to exist. These limitations can hinder help-seeking behavior, compromise appropriate practices, and reduce effective community engagement with mental health services, highlighting the importance of targeted educational interventions to improve mental health literacy and reduce stigma [23].

In our study, 70.9% of respondents demonstrated above-average knowledge, 48.5% exhibited positive attitudes, and 46.5% reported satisfactory practices. These findings exceed those reported in a previous Nigerian study, where approximately half of respondents (51.2%) had poor knowledge of mental disorders, though the majority (90.0%) held positive attitudes [23], as well as findings from southwest Nigeria [24]. In contrast, studies from Ethiopia reported lower levels of KAP: one study found only 44.0% of participants had adequate mental health knowledge, 93.4% demonstrated unfavorable attitudes, and 75.2% reported good practices [32], while another reported that nearly 47.1% had inadequate knowledge and 48.3% held unfavorable attitudes toward mental health problems [33].

The comparatively better results in our study may reflect heightened attention to mental health in recent years, possibly driven by more widespread awareness campaigns and communication efforts, which have contributed to improvements in knowledge, attitudes, and practices relative to previous studies in the region. These findings not only address existing gaps in baseline evidence on knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding mental health, but also demonstrate that adults in the study area possessed relatively higher levels of knowledge compared with their attitudes and practices toward mental health. This suggests that although efforts to improve knowledge remain important, greater emphasis should be placed on interventions that promote the translation of knowledge into positive attitudes and improved mental health practices.

Female respondents in this study demonstrated significantly higher levels of mental health knowledge compared with their male counterparts. This finding is consistent with evidence from China that examined gender differences in health literacy and reported superior mental health literacy among women [34]. Similar patterns have been documented in other studies, which consistently show that men tend to have lower levels of mental health knowledge than women [35–37].

Several plausible explanations may account for this disparity. Women often exhibit stronger health-seeking behaviors and greater engagement with health information resources. In many contexts, they also assume primary caregiving and household health management roles, including responsibility for children’s wellbeing. These roles may increase their exposure to health information through peer networks, healthcare encounters, and self-directed learning, thereby enhancing their mental health knowledge. Our findings highlight the need for gender-sensitive and targeted interventions within the study area to improve mental health knowledge among boys and men, who are also at greater risk of mental health disorders, particularly those related to substance use. Consequently, the study provides important guidance for prioritizing mental health education and health promotion campaigns.

In contrast to our findings, which indicate that older age was associated with better mental health knowledge, most existing literature reports higher mental health literacy among younger individuals [38–40]. Younger age groups are often more adept at recognizing mental disorders and accessing mental health information. Nevertheless, some studies have shown that older adults may exhibit lower levels of stigmatizing attitudes toward mental health conditions [38]. The association observed in our study may be explained by the greater health consciousness typically seen among older individuals. With increasing age, people may become more attentive to their overall wellbeing and more receptive to health information disseminated through community programs, healthcare encounters, and media platforms. In contrast, younger individuals may prioritize other social or economic concerns, potentially placing less emphasis on mental health education.

Our findings indicate that retired civil servants exhibit higher levels of mental health knowledge. While comparative evidence specifically examining the impact of occupational or retirement status on mental health literacy is limited, existing literature highlights an increased prevalence of mental health conditions, particularly anxiety and depression, among older adults [41–44]. These conditions are often linked to factors such as reduced income, comorbid medical illnesses including hypertension and diabetes, bereavement, and social isolation. The consequent increased interaction with healthcare services, including hospital visits and counseling, likely provides retired individuals with greater exposure to mental health education, which may partly explain their enhanced knowledge in this domain.

Our findings indicate that women exhibited more positive attitudes toward mental health than their male counterparts. This aligns with national survey data showing that men are less likely than women to endorse favorable attitudes toward seeking professional help for mental health concerns, suggesting comparatively better help-seeking attitudes among females [45]. Consistently, previous research has reported that women are more open-minded and supportive of the community integration of individuals with mental illness, reflecting more favorable attitudes toward mental health acceptance [46]. Likewise, systematic review evidence indicates that women are generally more willing to recommend professional help and hold more optimistic beliefs regarding treatment outcomes compared to men, further supporting superior attitudes in females [47]. These observations are consistent with our classical KAP framework, which posits that knowledge shapes attitudes; in our study, females also demonstrated higher mental health knowledge. Furthermore, we observed a statistically significant positive correlation between mental health knowledge and attitudes, reinforcing the link between understanding and perception.

Our findings indicate that a prior history of hospitalization is associated with more positive attitudes toward mental health. Researches specifically examining this factor are limited; most existing studies have focused on healthcare workers’ attitudes toward patients admitted with mental illness [48–50] or on patients currently receiving mental healthcare [51–53]. Although these studies were not exclusively centered on mental health services, they highlight the potential role of exposure to counseling, general health information, or hospital-based interactions in shaping perceptions. This suggests that general hospital clinics and inpatient wards could serve as effective platforms for mental health education, potentially enhancing attitudes and reducing stigma in broader patient populations.

In our study, a family history of mental illness and a household consisting of 5 to 10 members were associated with more positive attitudes toward mental health. This finding aligns with research conducted in northern Nigeria [54], as well as studies from other regions across multiple continents [55–57]. Exposure to mental illness within the family may enhance understanding and reduce stigma, likely through desensitization and greater familiarity. Family history can also influence help-seeking behaviors and perceptions of mental health services, with relatives of affected individuals often demonstrating increased tolerance and empathy compared with those without such experience. While not all familial interactions are supportive, they nonetheless play a significant role in shaping individual perceptions of mental illness and engagement with care [57]. Although most research on smokers has focused on attitudes toward smoking rather than mental health, our study found that non-smokers exhibited more positive attitudes toward mental health. This may reflect greater overall health consciousness among non-smokers compared with those who smoke.

Our study revealed that respondents from the northwest senatorial zone demonstrated more satisfactory mental health practices. This is likely attributable to the presence of the state’s only government-owned mental health facility within this zone, providing greater availability and easier access to services compared with other regions. Similar trends have been reported in previous studies, highlighting that improved availability, accessibility, and the removal of barriers enhance engagement with mental health practices [58–63]. This observation is further supported by our finding that respondents with health insurance exhibited better mental health practices, suggesting that sustained access without financial obstacles promotes adherence to mental health and broader healthcare interventions.

Conclusion

Consistent with our hypothesis, this study revealed a moderate level of knowledge, alongside suboptimal attitudes and practices toward mental health. Females demonstrated higher knowledge and more positive attitudes, aligning with the classical KAP framework. Positive attitudes were also significantly associated with prior hospitalization, family history of mental illness, and non-smoking status. In contrast, better mental health practices were observed among respondents with a documented history of mental illness, those residing in areas with available mental health services, and individuals with health insurance, highlighting the critical role of both geographic and financial access in shaping mental health practices. Furthermore, as predicted by the KAP framework, knowledge was positively and significantly correlated with both attitudes and practices. These findings underscore the urgent need for targeted government and stakeholder interventions to expand availability and ensure universal access to mental health services, complemented by sustained health education and counseling programs.

Study limitations

This study had some limitations. The cross-sectional design limited the ability to establish causal relationships, although the findings provide a basis for future analytical studies. The study was further limited by the inclusion of only one LGA from each of the three senatorial zones in the state, which may have affected the representativeness and generalizability of the findings. However, this limitation was partly minimized through the selection of LGAs across all senatorial zones and the inclusion of both rural and urban areas.

In addition, the online survey may have included participants who were not originally from the study area; however, non-indigenous respondents who had lived and worked in the state for a considerable period were included because they access health information and healthcare services within the state. Additionally, a dual data collection approach was employed, combining online surveys with interviewer-administered questionnaires to capture participants who may have been missed during household visits, particularly men and individuals working away from their primary residences.

The use of online surveys may systematically exclude individuals with lower socioeconomic status, rural populations, older adults, and those with lower levels of education, thereby limiting the representativeness of the sample. In addition, online surveys are prone to self-selection bias, which may further affect the generalizability of the findings. To address these limitations, interviewer-administered questionnaires were also employed, and the combination of both approaches potentially helped to minimize these biases.

Similarly the study was limited by studying one LGA from each of the three senatorial zones of the state, however the rural and urban selection provided good representation across the state.
