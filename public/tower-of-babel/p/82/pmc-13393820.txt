Vaccination with an HSV-1 virus containing CD-137L reduces herpetic corneal disease when used both prophylactically and therapeutically
Authors: Patrick M. Stuart, Ziao-Tang Yin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Ocular disease caused by HSV-1 is a leading cause of infectious blindness. The major underlying cause for this disease is the activation of a strong inflammatory response initiated by viral infection.•Development of an effective vaccine to this disease has been the aim of many and varied investigations. Most reports have described successful prevention when administered prior to infection.•However, very few have shown efficacy when applied to latently infected animals.•We present data indicating a vaccine construct that displays effectiveness when used both prophylactically as well as therapeutically.

Introduction

Corneal infection with herpes simplex virus 1 is initiated by the infection of cells at the site of primary infection. For the cornea, which is typically the corneal epithelium. Once the virus proliferates there, it spreads to underlying tissues of the cornea and this is followed by infection of nerves which are found in the cornea and the subsequent retrograde transport of virus to the trigeminal ganglia (Tuli and Sonal, 2009; Preston, 2000; Summers et al., 2001). Once there, the virus replicates in neurons with some of these newly formed viruses returning to the periphery (Summers et al., 2001). In most cases, particularly in animal models, within a week following infection infectious virus typically can no longer be detected in the cornea as the virus establishes a latent infection of the neurons in the TG (Preston, 2000; Summers et al., 2001; Feldman et al., 2002; Cunningham et al., 2006; Knipe and Cliffe, 2008). During latency, the viral genome is present, but few signs of infectious virions are detected in these infected neurons, but they do express viral RNA, with latency associated transcripts being one of the most abundant RNA species. Furthermore, the viral mRNA found in the latently infected neurons will lead to the production of some viral proteins (Cunningham et al., 2006; Knipe and Cliffe, 2008). This latent infection can be interrupted by various forms of stimuli which can include, but are not limited to such things as fever, menses, sunlight (UV), irradiation, stress, and trauma, some of these stimuli are immunosuppressive and others are non-immune in nature (Smith and Robinson, 2002; Umene and Sakaoke, 1999; Liang et al., 2009; Shimeld et al., 1989; Shimeld et al., 1990). Following reactivation, new infectious viruses are produced in the neurons of the trigeminal ganglia and the virus travels via anterograde axonal transport back to the epithelial surface. Once there the virus replicates and re-stimulates the host immune responses which were generated during primary infection. The result of these preexisting immune responses is the primary mechanism leading to recurrent herpetic stromal keratitis (r-HSK) (Farooq and Shukla, 2012; Azher et al., 2017; Rowe et al., 2013; Keadle et al., 2002).

Currently treatment of HSK involves anti-viral therapy with acyclovir, or its derivatives, in addition to topical steroids to control the inflammatory response (Rowe et al., 2013; Keadle et al., 2002). However, since there exist acyclovir-resistant HSV-1 isolates (Burrel et al., 2013), this suggests that new means of targeting this virus are warranted. To that end, new drugs are being tested for their ability to treat this virus (Bernier and Morrison, 2018; Gazquez Casals et al., 2023). However, a more effective means of treating this infection would be to develop a vaccine that is effective in preventing pHSK, and more importantly, preventing rHSK. While most vaccines which have been developed are effective in treating pHSK in animal models when given prior to infection (Ghiasi et al., 1995; Heilingenhaus et al., 1995; Inoue et al., 1990, 22, Nesburn et al., 1994) very few have resulted in successful treatment of rHSK when used therapeutically to prevent recurrent HSK in latently infected individuals (Nesburn et al., 1994; Richards et al., 2003; Royer et al., 2016; Royer et al., 2017; Petro et al., 2015; Petro et al., 2016; Gmyrek et al., 2022; Gmyrek et al., 2022). We and others have shown that vaccination with certain HSV-1 components limits both viral pathogenesis and prevents primary and recurrent corneal disease only when administered prior to infection (Ghiasi et al., 1995; Heilingenhaus et al., 1995; Inoue et al., 1990, 22, Nesburn et al., 1994, Daheshia et al., 1997). If administered following infection these vaccines are mostly ineffective in preventing recurrent disease (Daheshia et al., 1997). Thus, it is difficult to develop effective vaccines for both primary and recurrent infections of HSV. The development of a therapeutic vaccine would be of clinical significance since most patients who present with HSK are latently infected (Summers et al., 2001; Feldman et al., 2002; Cunningham et al., 2006; Knipe and Cliffe, 2008; Smith and Robinson, 2002; Umene and Sakaoke, 1999). This was illustrated by a clinical trial in which the vaccine was only effective in women who had never been infected with herpes virus and ineffective in those who had a history of such infections (Farooq and Shukla, 2012). That said, we reported that vaccination with a vhs-defective mutant of HSV-1 (25.26) significantly reduced the rate of reactivation (Keadle et al., 2002; Walker et al., 1998) and reduced disease and at the same time observed significantly elevated anti-HSV-1 titers (Keadle et al., 1997; Keadle et al., 2002). Similar results were also seen in studies by Richards, et al. who nasally vaccinated latently infected mice with a mixture of HSV-1 glycoproteins and recombinant E.coli heat-labile enterotoxin B subunit as an adjuvant (Walker and Leib, 1998). Royer, et al. (Richards et al., 2003; Royer et al., 2016) using an ICP0 deleted and attenuated virus (HSV-1 0ΔNLS), which resulted in increased antibody responses also demonstrated significant protection from developing pHSK from a subsequent corneal challenge with wild-type HSV-1. Similarly, Petro, et al. (Royer et al., 2017) reported that a glycoprotein D deleted viral vaccine also protected mice from pHSK when administered prophylactically and again protection was due to a strong antibody response. Which in this case was one that mediated FcγR-effector antibodies involved in ADCC (Petro et al., 2015). Thus, it appears that vaccination leading to strong antibody responses are effective in preventing pHSK (Keadle et al., 1997; Richards et al., 2003; Royer et al., 2016; Royer et al., 2017; Petro et al., 2015; Petro et al., 2016). Gmyrek, et al. went on to report that their vaccine, in addition to augmenting antibody responses also enhanced the levels of CD8+ T cell (Gmyrek et al., 2022). Since HSK is primarily an inflammatory disease, a change of immune response from one that is dominantly inflammatory to one that is mediated by neutralizing antibodies and HSV-1-specific CD8+ T cells would be significant. Our target in these studies have focused on therapeutic administration of vaccine into latently infected mice (Keadle et al., 1997; Keadle et al., 2002). In this paradigm, mice are vaccinated following the establishment of a latent HSV-1 trigeminal ganglia infection in a similar fashion to what we have done in the past (Keadle et al., 1997; Keadle et al., 2002). In the current study we decided to create an HSV-1 vaccine that expresses the CD137L costimulatory gene (Gmyrek et al., 2022). Our rationale was that studies investigating this molecule have demonstrated that it preferentially enhances CD8+ T cell responses (Yin et al., 2022; Shuford et al., 1997; Lee et al., 2003; Robertson et al., 2008; Lee et al., 2014) and has been, on occasion, seen to also augment B cell function as well (Welten et al., 2015; Lin et al., 2009). When we investigated the role of CD137L in a murine model of rHSK, we demonstrated that mice which do not express CD137L displayed significantly increased rHSK when compared to wild-type mice (Gmyrek et al., 2022). Results from the current study investigating the efficacy of vaccination with HSV-1 transgenically expressing CD137L indicate that such vaccinated mice had significantly reduced primary and recurrent HSK in mice.

MATERIALS AND methods

Mice

Investigations with mice conformed to the Association for Research in Vision and Ophthalmology (ARVO) Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research. C57BL/6 (B6) were purchased from NCI. NIH inbred strain of mice was originally obtained from Harlan OLAC (Oxford, England) and have been bred in the animal facility for over 15 years under the supervision of the staff in Comparative Medicine.

Infection of mice

For all experiments described, we used age matched male and female mice from the aforementioned strains that were 6–10 weeks old. For primary disease we infected scarified corneas of mice with 2 × 105 pfu of the McKrae strain of HSV-1 [44]. This dose of virus is used, because greater doses will significantly increase mortality in the infected mice. Eye swabs were taken from these mice for 7 to 10 days following infection to monitor the persistence of viral shedding. For recurrent disease, mice were infected on the scarified cornea with 106 PFU HSV-1 McKrae strain as previously described [44]. Note: We use a higher dose of viruses to establish latency as we have used this dose successfully for over 30 years. Each mouse received an intraperitoneal (IP) injection of 0.5 ml pooled human serum (Sigma Chemicals, St. Louis MO; ED50 for virus neutralization = 1:1600) concurrent with infection. Administration of human anti-HSV antibodies at the time of ocular infection has been shown to protect mice from death and corneal disease during primary infection, while allowing for the establishment of latency and subsequent reactivation of virus after corneal UV-B exposure (Shimeld et al., 1989; Shimeld et al., 1990). These human antibodies are undetectable at the time of UV-B irradiation, 5 weeks after primary infection. To confirm infection, only mice displaying infectious virus obtained from eye swabs taken three days post-infection was used for subsequent reactivation (West et al., 2014).

UV-B irradiation and virus reactivation

Mice were reactivated from latency as previously described (Zhang et al., 2010). Briefly, the eyes of all latently infected mice were examined for corneal opacity prior to UV-B irradiation, and only animals with clear corneas were used, and mice with residual corneal disease from the primary infection are removed from the study. At least 5 weeks after primary infection, the eyes of latently infected and control mock-infected mice were exposed to 250 mJ/cm2 of UV-B light using a TM20 Chromato-Vu transilluminator (UVP, Inc., San Gabriel, CA), which emits UV-B at a peak wavelength of 302 nm. Irradiated mice were swabbed with sterile cotton applicators from day 0 to day 7, unless otherwise indicated. The swab material was cultured on Vero cells, as described above, to detect recurrent virus shedding from the cornea. Reactivation was defined as the finding of any HSV-positive eye swab on any day after UV-B exposure, with day 0 swabs serving as a control [44].

Clinical evaluation

On the designated days after viral infection or UV-B reactivation, a masked observer, who is unaware of the experimental groups, examined the mouse eyes through a binocular dissecting microscope to score clinical disease. Stromal opacification was rated on a scale of from 0 to 4, where 0 indicates clear stroma, 1 indicates mild stromal opacification, 2 indicates moderate opacity with discernible iris features, 3 indicates dense opacity with the loss of defined iris detail except pupil margins, and 4 indicates total opacity with no posterior view. Corneal neovascularization was evaluated as described previously (Gmyrek et al., 2022) using a scale of from 0 to 8, where each of the four quadrants of the eye is evaluated for the density of vessels that have grown into them. We also measured the corneal blink reflex as a means of determining corneal nerve damage. This was accomplished by loosely holding the mouse and touching the cornea with the blunt tips of a surgical forceps without touching the eyelashes and whiskers. The cornea was divided into 5 areas (4 quadrants and center area), loss of blink reflex referred to the inability of the mouse to blink when an area was touched and was recorded as 0. Positive blink reflex referred to the ability to blink when an area of the cornea was touched and was recorded as 1. The total score of the 5 areas would be the final score of corneal blink reflex for a mouse. A score of 0 indicated a complete loss of corneal sensation such that the mouse failed to blink when any area of the cornea was touched. A score of 5 indicated retention of some degree of sensation such that the mouse blinked when any area of the cornea was touched (Miller et al., 1996). Note: Uninfected, UV-B irradiated control mice were used as a baseline.

Vaccination of mice

Since we wanted to reduce the risk that the vaccine construct would latently infect trigeminal ganglia, we decided to do our vaccinations into the right hip of the mice. This was accomplished by injecting 2 × 105 pfu of the HSV-1-CD137L virus or uninoculated media intramuscularly into the right hip of mice. We decided on this dose for vaccination as this was the suggested dose to use for these studies (Dr. Daniel Carr, University of Oklahoma personal communication). This vaccination was followed up 2 weeks later with a booster shot of the same vaccine construct. For pHSK, mice were vaccinated as described and 2 weeks following the second dose of vaccine, the mice were infected with 5 × 105 wild-type HSV-1 McKrae strain. For rHSK in latently infected mice, vaccination was done 3 weeks following primary infection. These mice were then UV-B reactivated 2 weeks after the second vaccine dose. In these studies, we employed both male and female mice ages 6 to 10 weeks at the time of their inclusion in the study. As stated earlier, any animal that did not shed virus during initial infection were excluded. Likewise, for any animal that demonstrated corneal disease during initial infection to establish viral latency was also excluded. These criteria are used to ensure that for primary infection, there is evidence of viral infection and that no disease was seen in those animals which were to be latently infected.

PCR for HSV-1 latent genomes in trigeminal ganglia

Detection of HSV-1-CD137L genomes in the trigeminal ganglia of infected mice were performed as previously described (Gmyrek et al., 2022). Trigeminal ganglia were removed from vaccinated mice, and genomic DNA was extracted using the DNeasy blood and tissue kit from QIAGEN (Cat #69,506). This DNA was then subjected to pcr analysis using the primers that confirmed the presence of CD137L in our HSV-1 construct as previously described (Gmyrek et al., 2022). These primers were chosen because they distinguish between the human CD137L construct and the endogenous murine CD137L (Gmyrek et al., 2022). Those primers were: (forward), 5′-AAA ACC TCC CAC ATC TCC CC-3′; (reverse), 5′-CTT GTG AAA CCC GAC AAC CC-3′. Adipsin gene was used as internal reference as follows, mouse adipsin forward primer was 5′AGTGTGCGGGGATGCAGT3′; reverse primer was 5′ACGCGAGAGCCCCAGGTA3′. (Primers were made by Integrated DNA Technologies, Coralville, IA.) and subjected to 30 rounds of amplification. The resultant pcr products were run on a gel using the adipsin gene as control.

Analysis of anti-HSV-1 antibody titers

Serum was collected from mice 4 weeks after prophylactic vaccination or 2 weeks after therapeutic vaccination and examined for HSV-specific antibody content as previously described (Keadle et al., 1997). Briefly, for ELISA, serial fourfold dilutions of mouse serum were incubated for 2 h in duplicate wells of a 96-well plate coated with purified HSV-1 glycoprotein. Biotinylated goat anti-mouse immunoglobulin G (IgG) was subsequently used in a colorimetric assay to determine specific IgG amounts based on comparison to a standard curve generated as previously described (Keadle et al., 1997).

Statistics

All statistical analyses were performed with the aid of Sigma Stat for Windows, version 2.0 (Jandel, Corte Madera, CA). The log rank test was used to compare disease scores. Student’s unpaired t-tests were used to compare virus titer data, genome copies, LAT+ cells and antibody titers.

RESULTS

In a previous report we compared HSV-1-CD137L virus to wild-type virus and demonstrated that while the initial infection with these viruses in the presence of antibodies to HSV-1 resulted in similar corneal titers and similar levels of viral DNA in the infected trigeminal ganglia, the disease following UV-B irradiation was remarkably dissimilar. Following reactivation, mice infected with wild type virus displayed significant corneal disease, while those infected with HSV-1– 137 L virus did not show much, if any corneal disease following reactivation (Gmyrek et al., 2022). As a follow-up to that publication, we also did separate studies to determine the blink response when a different strain, NIH inbred mice, were infected with these two strains of mice and as seen in Fig. 1 the blink response for HSV-1-CD137L latently mice was not diminished following UV-B reactivation when compared to those mice latently infected with the parental HSV-1 McKrae strain. This observation led us to believe that diseases associated with the infection of HSV-1– 137 L virus would display a significantly reduced disease profile following UV-B reactivation regardless of if it were B6 or NIH mice. When mice were vaccinated with HSV-1-CD137L virus by inter-muscular injection and then infected in the cornea with HSV-1, resultant corneal disease was prevented (Fig. 2). Likewise, when vaccinated prior to infection of the cornea with HSV-1 along with anti-HSV-1 antibody to establish latency and were then reactivated 6 weeks later, corneal disease was significantly reduced when compared to mice that were sham vaccinated (data not shown). It was clear that prophylactic vaccination of mice with HSV-1-CD137L significantly reduced corneal disease when mice were subsequently infected with the parental strain of HSV-1. Consequently, these observations encouraged us to examine whether therapeutic vaccination of latently infected mice would be able to significantly reduce recurrent HSK following UV-B reactivation. Being able to significantly reduce rHSK in these mice would set this vaccine apart from several other reported vaccine constructs and place it amidst those few showing some degree of efficacy when used in a therapeutic paradigm (Keadle et al., 1997; Keadle et al., 2002). Results of the experiment indicated that vaccination of latently infected mice with an intra-muscular administration of HSV-1-CD137L virus was able to significantly reduce or in some cases virtually prevent rHSK. Thus, for all the measures that we use to evaluate corneal disease: corneal opacity, corneal neovascularization, and blink responsiveness all demonstrated responses indistinguishable from control uninfected mice. We did perform some preliminary studies to determine anti-HSV-1 antibody responses. Serum samples were taken from both control and vaccinated mice at Day 10 (8 mice in each group). The antibody titers were control mice: 2.55 ± 0.4 log 10; vaccinated mice: 3.7 ± 0.3 log 10. When subjected to student’s t-Test, these differences were significant (p < 0.05). However, since the sample size was small, further studies need to be performed to confirm these results.

It should also be noted that one concern that we had was that vaccination with HSV-1-CD137L might result in co-infection of trigeminal ganglia was also evaluated by PCR analysis of trigeminal ganglia to determine if DNA from this virus could be detected there. We had intentionally performed our vaccination protocol to limit the possibility of that taking place. Had that been the case, then one could posit that there would be the potential for recombination of the vaccine virus with the HSV-1 virus already present in trigeminal ganglia. Consequently, we isolated trigeminal ganglia from vaccinated and latently infected mice and tested the DNA isolated from these samples for the presence of DNA derived from HSV-1-CD137L. Results indicated that while wild type HSV-1 was present in these trigeminal ganglia samples, we could not detect any DNA from HSV-1-CD137L viruses (data not shown).

Discussion

Our publication indicating that infection with HSV-1 viruses containing the CD137L gene did not result in significant primary or recurrent forms of HSK in mice (Gmyrek et al., 2022) encouraged us to examine whether vaccination with this virus either before infection (prophylactic vaccination) or following establishment of viral latency (therapeutic vaccination) would significantly reduce corneal disease when used in these two separate paradigms. We were not at all surprised that vaccination prior to infection resulted in a virtual lack of primary HSK. This observation is consistent with quite a few reports over the past several decades (Ghiasi et al., 1995; Heilingenhaus et al., 1995; Inoue et al., 1990, 22, Nesburn et al., 1994, Daheshia et al., 1997, Richards et al., 2003, Royer et al., 2016, Royer et al., 2017, Petro et al., 2015, Petro et al., 2016, Gmyrek et al., 2022). It seems that prevention of primary disease using many different constructs will result in significant if not complete elimination of corneal disease. That said, those vaccines showing significant reduction in corneal disease when applied therapeutically to latently infected mice rarely result in reproducible changes in recurrent HSK (Keadle et al., 1997; Keadle et al., 2002; Walker and Leib, 1998). However, several reports have demonstrated that vaccination can significantly alter viral replication and shedding of virus (Yun et al., 2020; Nesburn et al., 1994). Our observation that vaccination with HSV-1-CD137L can reduce corneal disease in latently infected mice can now be added to those vaccine constructs that can be successfully used in a therapeutic model of this disease.

We believe that we have demonstrated that expressing CD137L in a recombinant HSV-1 virus has significant value both in preventing primary infection and significantly reducing recurrent disease in latently infection mice is very important and potentially useful in the development of a vaccine to treat individuals suffering from herpetic eye disease. We hope that future studies will be performed to more fully characterize the cells (T cells, B cells, dendritic cells, and natural killer cells [57,58]) as well as factors that are boosted because of using this vaccine. This will be critical to the understanding of the mechanism(s) which underlie the successful use of this vaccine therapeutically. In particular, these studies should characterize the B cell (antibody responses), T cell (primarily the generation of CD8 expressing T cells that have the ability to target virally infected cells) and possible NK and NKT cell responses (which are known to directly participate in anti-viral responses). A concerted examination of these responses will provide direct evidence as to the underlying mechanisms responsible for the efficacy of this vaccine. It should be pointed out, that it may very well be the case that some, if not all, of these responses will be implicated.

It has been postulated that using a replication competent virus as a vaccine construct has significant drawbacks as such vaccine viruses could recombine with the virus that is latently infecting the individual. The concern here is that this recombination could result in a more pathogenic strain of HSV-1. We disagree with this premise as attenuated viruses that do replicate have been used successfully in many other viral infections. We also contend that the likelihood of this virus recombining with a preexisting latent HSV-1 is unlikely as the vaccine will not be administered to a geographic region that is innervated by the trigeminal ganglia. Our prediction has been supported by several publications. These include the recent publication by Wang, et al. demonstrating that the most efficient means of vaccinating against HSV-2 was using a replication competent virus is another example of using competent virus vaccines can sometime prove to be highly effective. It also suggests that subunit and replication incompetent viruses may not provide the best protection (Wang et al., 2022). It is interesting that a similar argument was made in an earlier report by Wang, et al. that employed a vaccine wherein they created an HSV-1 strain in which the UL-29 gene was partially deleted but was still replication competent (Nesburn et al., 1994). An earlier report by Davido, et al. demonstrates similar results when using an attenuated strain of HSV-1 that had targeted mutations in the UL39 gene which encodes infected cell protein 6 (ICP6) (Wang et al., 2026). The reason for these observations may be that the replication competent virus may be superior in activating a strong innate immune response as competent viruses not only will activate pattern recognition molecules in infected cells, they their infection, which will lead to cell death would be a stronger signal for the production of cellular danger associated protein that are also important in activating the innate immune response (Davido et al., 2018; Leib et al., 1999).

In contrast, there are several mRNA based vaccines against HSV-2 that express either gC, gD and gE (Ishikawa et al., 2009) as well as a mRNA vaccine expressing gB2, gC2, gD2, and gE2 (Egan et al., 2020) have demonstrated protection against primary infection and to some extent reduce latency in infected dorsal root ganglia (Cho et al., 2025). This reduction in viral latency when used prophylactically could result in reduced corneal disease upon reactivation of the latent infection, though this aspect of these vaccines was not rigorously investigated.

In summary, we present data indicating that vaccination with HSV-1-CD137L virus results in protection from both pHSK as well as rHSK. It is the ability of this vaccine to protect from the more common form of corneal disease (rHSK) this is particularly notable as this allows one to use this vaccine therapeutically with confidence.
