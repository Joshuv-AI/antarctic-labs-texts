Adaptive gyration mechanism in fan worm gill filaments for enhanced filter-feeding efficiency
Authors: Xinxin Chen, Wei Jiang, Jiangkun Wei, Zheng Chen, Zhao Pan, Hamed Rajabi, Jianing Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Fan worm gill filaments passively gyrate via V-shaped section and unbalanced torsion.•Passive gyration increases particle capture efficiency by 141% on average.•Gill filaments achieve efficient filtration, showing physical intelligence.

Introduction

The intricate interplay between living organisms and their fluid environments has long fascinated scientists, with recent advances in fluid dynamics shedding light on a wide array of biological phenomena [[1], [2], [3]]. Underwater suspension feeders, a group of organisms that extract particles from water, exemplify this intricate relationship [4,5]. Their filter apparatuses, often composed of bristled arrays or mucous nets, serve as sieves or paddles to manage particle capture and feeding currents [4,6,7]. The effectiveness of these feeding structures is fundamentally influenced by their interaction with surrounding fluid flows, enabling suspension feeders to maximize particle encounter rates, maintain suspended food, and efficiently clear depleted water [8,9].

In various suspension feeders, the food-catching surfaces are typically located on the leeward side of the supporting structure [10,11]. The relatively low current velocity, microturbulence, and eddying, which occur leeward, could facilitate particle capture [12,13]. When currents vary, benthic mobile animals, such as hydroids [14] and crinoids [[15], [16], [17], [18]], actively reorient and maintain their food-catching surfaces at leeward locations by adjusting their posture. Filter-feeding fish, such as herring (Chordata: Actinopterygii) [19,20], swim almost constantly, forcing water and suspended food into their mouths while actively using gill rakers to protrude from the gill arches into the pharyngeal cavity [21]. In contrast, some sessile suspension feeders are unable to actively reorient their filter apparatuses to adapt to changing water flow. Under such conditions, efficient feeding can become highly challenging, particularly due to the nature of the water flow.

Recent advances in soft, biohybrid, and reconfigurable robotic systems have demonstrated adaptive behaviors in complex fluidic and biological environments by leveraging material compliance, structural morphology, and externally mediated reconfiguration [[22], [23], [24], [25]]. A broad range of studies have shown that adaptive functionality can be achieved through soft architectures combined with external fields, programmable actuation, or assisted repositioning, enabling robots or devices to maintain functionality under dynamic loading, constrained spaces, or changing environmental conditions [26,27]. Collectively, these works highlight a dominant paradigm in which adaptation is achieved through active control strategies or externally imposed stimuli, even when implemented within mechanically compliant systems [26,27]. In parallel, a growing body of biological and bio-inspired studies points to an alternative adaptive pathway, particularly in aquatic environments, where functional reconfiguration arises passively from fluid-structure interactions [28]. In many aquatic organisms, compliant appendages undergo flow-induced bending, reorientation, or shape transformation, allowing effective interaction with surrounding flows without centralized control, sensing, or active actuation [[29], [30], [31], [32], [33]]. Such observations suggest that, alongside actively controlled soft robotic systems, passive mechanical adaptation mediated by geometry and material compliance constitutes a distinct and biologically prevalent adaptive strategy, offering complementary design insights for soft and compliant structures operating in complex and dynamic fluidic conditions.

Fan worms (Annelida: Sabellidae), or “feather duster” worms, are among the most diverse and ubiquitous suspension sessile feeders, characterized by their striking crown composed of gill filaments for filter feeding [31,34,35]. Despite their immobile nature and absence of musculature in their feeding structures, fan worms thrive across diverse marine habitats, from intertidal zones to abyssal depths, thanks to their remarkable feeding efficiency and environmental adaptability [36,37]. While they are frequently considered as invasive species, they have successfully colonized a wide range of marine habitats, from intertidal to abyssal depths worldwide (Fig. S1) [38], which may be ascribed to their high filter-feeding efficiency, allowing them to adapt to and colonize multifarious marine environments [[39], [40], [41]]. An extreme case demonstrates that some species of fan worms can live for over 300 years, showcasing their remarkable ability to endure and thrive across diverse conditions [42]. However, the mechanism underlying their adaptability, particularly in response to dynamic fluid conditions, remains largely unexplored.

In this combined experimental and theoretical study, we explored the structural and functional flexibility of filter-feeding in fan worms under random flows. We built scenarios of varying water flows to examine their adaptability by combining computational fluid dynamics, a discrete phase model, and robotic design to gain insights into the adaptive strategies of fan worms. This research may not only seek to uncover a self-adaptive filter-feeding mechanism in fan worms, but also have potential implications for the design of novel underwater particle collection systems.

Materials and methods

Animal husbandry

Ten fan worms (Annelida: Sabellidae) were housed in an aquarium (600 mm × 500 mm × 350 mm) with a complete reef ecosystem and raised at Sun Yat-Sen University, Shenzhen, China (23°N, 114°E). We added sea salt (sodium chloride) to the purified water to prepare the seawater, keeping the mass ratio at 0.025 and the temperature at 25 °C. An illuminator (120 W, 12,000 K) was employed to simulate sunlight irradiation. We confirm that these locations/activities did not require specific permission, and the study did not involve endangered or protected species.

High-speed imaging

We randomly selected a fan worm from the aquarium and gently placed it horizontally in the aquarium filled with seawater with a pair of tweezers. The fan worm was fixed through the proximal part of the tube with a plastic buckle. This buckle adhered to the vertical wall of the tank. A high-speed camera (Phantom, VEO 340L, USA) equipped with a micro-lens (Canon, cx33, Japan) and a light source were used to film the dynamics of the gill filaments of the fan worm. A frame rate of 1000 fps was used to film the fan worm from its gill filaments to the tube base along the long axis of the tube. We employed an aquarium pump to generate horizontal flows toward the gill filaments to observe the motion of the gill filaments. We processed the high-speed images via the Phantom Camera Control 3.3 software (Vision Research, Wayne, USA) and Tracker 4.9.1 software (Copyright (c) Douglas Brown, USA).

Scanning electron microscopy

We prepared four fresh gill filaments of the fan worm to observe the microstructures. The gill filaments were soaked in 2.5% glutaraldehyde for 3 h at 25 °C. Subsequently, they were cleaned with 0.1 mol L−1 phosphate buffer (pH = 7) and dehydrated with 70–100% ethanol (concentration increment: 10%). Next, the samples were processed with tert‑butyl alcohol for solvent replacement, oven-dried for 10 min, and coated with gold-palladium for 60 s. Finally, the samples were observed using a scanning electron microscope (SEM) (FEI Quanta 200, Czech Republic) in the high-vacuum mode at 15 kV.

Histology of gill filaments

The gill filaments of a fan worm were prepared by sectioning and staining techniques [43,44]. As a preliminary procedure, the fresh gill filament samples were fixed in Bouins’ fixative, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned using a pathological microtome (JB-P5, Wuhan Junjie Electronics Co., Ltd.) at 3 evenly distributed positions along the long axis of the gill filament with a distance interval of 5 mm. Tissue sections were dewaxed in xylene and rehydrated in an ethanol series. According to the standard scheme, the gill filament sections of the fan worm were stained with the hematoxylin/eosin staining method [45]. The sections were examined using an optical microscope (Nikon eclipse E100, Nikon, Japan) and the NIKIN DS-U3 imaging system.

Confocal laser scanning microscopy imaging

We placed the fresh supporting axis of the filament in a test Petri dish with glycerol (99%, Macklin, China). The presence and the distribution of the material of these samples were visualized under a confocal laser scanning microscope (TCS-SP5, Leica, Germany) equipped with four solid-state lasers (wavelengths: 405, 488, 561, and 633 nm). Blue, green, and red lights correspond to the autofluorescence of soft materials, less-sclerotized materials, and highly-sclerotized materials, respectively [46].

Atomic force microscopy

We prepared four fresh supporting axes of gill filaments dissected from a fan worm. We examined them in the water phase by atomic force microscopy (AFM) at a temperature of 21 °C to prevent the onset of desiccation and to characterize Young’s modulus of the supporting axis of the gill filament (Fig. S2). We performed nanoindentations with AFM using a scanning probe microscope (SPM) (Dimension Fast scan Bio, Bruker, USA), where Probes PFQNM-SMPKIT-12 M (Bruker, USA) for liquid phase examination were used. We set the indentation depth of 50nm, only about 0.025% of the thickness of the supporting axis of the filament, to avoid any potential influence of the underneath substrate on Young’s modulus. NanoScope Analysis software 2.0 (Bruker, USA) was then used for data visualization and processing.

Measurement of torque on gill filaments

We 3D printed (Form 3, Formlabs, USA) a gill filament-inspired device in a 20X scaled-up size composed of columnar support (9.00mm×6.00mm×21.00mm) and two arrays of pinnules (length = 25.19mm, diameter = 0.82mm). The dimensions of the device are shown in Fig. S3. A syringe needle (length = 16.00mm; diameter = 0.45mm) goes through the columnar support and is glued vertically on the bottom of the tank (500mm×500mm×30mm) filled with seawater (salinity is 0.025). We weaved a net by the cotton thread and connected the pinnule tip to a force sensor 30 mm above the water surface (Fig. S3). We used a water pump (DC40H-24110, ZKSJ, China) to jet the water flow around the 3D print. We recorded the stretching force in the thread Fd (MCK-Z-1, ZWJG, China) using a force gauge (LSB200, FUTEK, USA) connecting the thread. We defined the angle between the cotton thread and the water surface as β, and the length of the pinnules as lp. Therefore, the fluidic force exerted on the pinnule can be calculated as FW−C=Fd/(cosβ)2, and the initial torque of the gill filament can be described as T=FW−Clp=Fdlp/(cosβ)2. We could alter the incidence angle θ to quantify the relationship between twisting torque and the incidence angle.

Fluid mechanics simulation

We used fluid mechanics simulations to examine the flow behavior around the gill filaments (Fig. S4). Based on the geometry of gill filaments measured through SEM and anatomy images, the gill filament models were constructed by computer-aided design software SolidWorks (V. 2019; Dassault Systèmes, France) and then imported to the Fluent module of ANSYS® Academic Research Mechanical, Release 2021 R1, the finite element software package for simulation. The global fluid domain was set within a cuboid region with a length of 66mm, a width of 30mm, and a height of 2mm, and was discretized into a non-uniform mesh of tetrahedral elements (Fig. S5). We applied a velocity inlet condition and a pressure outlet condition at the front and rear boundaries of the computational domain, respectively. On other boundaries, we employ symmetrical boundary conditions. The seawater was considered an incompressible liquid with a density of 1024 kg/m3 and a kinematic viscosity of 1.047 × 10–6 m2/s at 20 °C [47]. We carried out 3D standard k-ε simulations of models with seven different inlet velocities (U=0.10, 0.20, 0.30, 0.40, 0.50, 0.60 and 0.70m/s), respectively, following the flow speed range of the watershed where the fan worms were previously collected, reportedly about 33.60cm/s [48]. Our study on the grid convergence of the simulation model can be found in supplementary material (Fig. S6).

Results

Gyration in the gill filament

Fan worms capture and feed on planktic micro-particles through a specialized filter-feeding system of flexible gill filaments (Fig. 1A). During filter feeding, a fan worm extends approximately 50 feather-like gill filaments out of a calcareous tube, radiating evenly to directions marked by the position angle α(0≤α<360deg) (Fig. 1B). The elongated gill filaments, which have an average slenderness ratio of ζ=67.82±10.94 (n=120 gill filament samples), bear two longitudinal rows of ciliated pinnules, with ∼300 pinnules in total on each filament. The gill filaments are essentially cylinders with feathery feeding tentacles in quiescent conditions. We gradually increased the water speed towards the fan worm from 0m/s to 0.37m/s along the x axis (Fig. 1B). Upon that, the gill filaments tended to gyrate and eventually aligned in the direction of the water flow (Fig. 1B and Movie S1). Fig. 1C,D show schematics of the gyration of gill filaments viewed from both the top and side, respectively. Although the gill filaments at varying position angles gyrated at different angles to conform to the flow, most gill filaments could remain relatively straight when impacted by the flow (lower image of Fig. 1B). Here, the orientation angle of the gill filament θ was defined as the angle between the axis of symmetry and the water flow (Fig. 1E). We found that θ started from the initial orientation angles of 21.30deg to 154.20deg, and all gill filaments converged to θ=0deg at which they finally aligned the food groove opening toward the x axis (Fig. 1E). For instance, by comparing two strands of gill filaments located at α=25.80deg and α=158.70deg, respectively, we found that the first one gyrated towards the final orientation within 1.00s, a duration of only 40% the other, which lasted 2.50s. We showed the duration of the gyration of the gill filament with respect to the initial orientation angle θ0 for complying with the water flow (Fig. 1F). The adjustment time T was defined as the time that the gill filament requires to align with the direction of water from its initial orientation. A fitting T=0.01θ0+0.68(R2=0.94) suggested that the adjustment time showed an ascending linear trend when the initial orientation angle increases. Measurements from multiple independent specimens show that, although some variation in adjustment time exists, the reorientation consistently occurs within a comparable timescale (Fig. S7), demonstrating good repeatability and dynamic robustness of the adaptive gyration behavior. Adaptive gyration of gill filaments occurred after the impingement of water flow; however, the mechanism of flexibility for gyration and the potential advantages of the adaptive kinematics of gill filaments remain unclear.

Mechanism of flow-driven gyration

The structural and material properties of gill filaments might be crucial for the adaptive gyration. We first carefully examined the morphology of the gill filaments (Fig. 2A). Gill filaments are about 20 mm long and are covered by two rows of pinnules. Each cilia-coated pinnule is 1.23 mm in length and 0.04 mm in diameter. The distance between the adjacent pinnule bases is 0.09 mm (Fig. 2B). At the base of the gill filament, these pinnules give rise to a pair of parallel ridges, namely basal folds, which run along the filaments for 3−4 mm (Fig. S8 and Table S1). We cut a strand of gill filament evenly into four segments and observed the cross-sections to examine the effect of their internal structure on their flexibility using sectioning and staining techniques (Fig. 2C,D, and E). Each gill filament consisted of a porous central core, in which two narrow ridges were set so that the surface of the support was divided into a dorsally long arc and a ventrally short arc. The core of the filament consists of a specialized supporting axis of large vacuolated cells surrounded by a connective tissue sheath on which muscles actuate to open and close the branchial crown. From the cross-sectional view of the gill filament, the porous core was composed of Nc=12±4 vacuolated cells (n=10 section samples, Fig. 2F). Utilizing a confocal laser microscope, we found that the supporting axis of the gill filament had no observable material gradient in either the axial (Fig. 2G) or circumferential (Fig. 2H) directions. When excited by a stable solid-state laser with various wavelengths, each part of the gill filament emitted light of distinct colors. Specifically, the supporting axis of the pinnule was composed of a highly sclerotized material and a less sclerotized material, emitting red and green lights, respectively. Conversely, the supporting axis of the filament showed blue light, indicating that it was composed of a soft material. Furthermore, nanoindentation measurements show that the average Young’s modulus of the supporting axis of the gill filament was E¯=7.92MPa (n=3 gill filament samples) and remained consistent across different samples, which is lower than the filtration appendages of most filter-feeders, including crinoid (Actiniaria) [49], African shrimp (Atya gabonensis) [50], baleen bristles (Eubalaena australis) [51], and sponges (Phylum Porifera) [52]. To evaluate the effects of external torque on the adaptation of gill filaments, a 20X-magnified gill filament-inspired device was 3D printed and placed in seawater under a flow velocity of 0.74 m/s, the applicability of which was demonstrated in supporting information. The fluid-driven torque on artificial gill filaments under different initial orientation angles θ0 was then measured (Figs. 2J,S3). We altered the orientation angle θ0 with an increment of 20deg, and recorded the torques acting on the gill filament under each orientation. The torque exhibited a trend of initially increasing in reverse and then decreasing in reverse. For θ0=0deg and 180deg, the torque was equal to zero, making both of these orientations at equilibrium positions. However, we applied any disturbance angle δθ0, at these two equilibrium positions and observed that the torque δTp, induced by this perturbation at θ0=0deg has the opposite sign to δθ0, indicating that the torque acted to reduce the disturbance angle. In contrast, the sign of the torque δTp was consistent with δθ0 at θ0=180deg, suggesting that the torque would drive an increase in the disturbance angle. Consequently, the stability of θ0=0deg was higher than that of θ0=180deg, which further proved in supplementary material. The relationship between the torque and the orientation angle follows the equation Tp=−2.33×10−3sin(1.75×10−2θ0)(R2=0.92) (Fig. 2J).

Postmortem examination revealed that the porous structure of gill filaments may also significantly impact their twisting locomotion. The porosity of the gill filaments was defined as η=A/S, in which A was the area of the porous core, and S was the outer area of the cross-section of the supporting axis. We measured A=9.79×10−3mm2 and S=1.86×10−2mm2, arriving at a porosity of η=52.63%±8.91%. We created models with a range of porosities to evaluate the effect of the porosity on the twisting of the gill filament (Fig. 2K). We performed a torque simulation with a torsion angle of 180 deg for the model with a wall thickness of 2×10−3 mm, which corresponds to the thinnest wall thickness observed in the filament. The applied torque in simulation was measured as Tt=2.67×10−9N·m, under the rotation angle δ of the supporting axis of the filament increased monotonically with an ascending porosity η (Fig. S10 and Table S2). When the porosities η varied from 0% to 70%, the rotation angle δ ramped slowly. However, for porosities greater than 70%, the rotation angle δ increased steeply. Geometric analysis of the filaments showed that their porosity, η=52.63%±8.91% (n=9), falls within a range that allows for significant torsion. This suggests that the compliance of the gill filaments to varying flow conditions is likely facilitated by both the external torque induced by fluid flow and the internal structural characteristics of the filaments, including their porous nature.

Benefits of gyration for gill filaments

The adaptive gyration of gill filaments could have considerable implications for the nutrient acquisition and overall survival of fan worms. Leveraging the measured geometry of gill filaments, we employed projection micro stereolithography (PµSL) 3D printing technology (NanoArch S240D, BMF Precision, China) to create two models of robotic gill filaments (RoboGFs), representing the pre-gyration and post-gyration configurations of the biological gill filaments, while maintaining the same geometrical size as the fan worm (Figs. 3A,S11). The as-fabricated RoboGFs can be further installed onto a motor-driven device serving as a filtering robot for collecting suspended microparticles underwater (Fig. 3B). Filtering robots equipped with different RoboGFs were then individually placed in customized channels designed for testing particle capture capabilities (Fig. 3C). Shown in Fig. 3D, at varying velocities of 0.5, 1.0, 1.5 and 2.0 cm/s, filtering robots equipped with gyrated RoboGFs demonstrated a significant enhancement in the particle capture compared with those equipped with non-gyrated RoboGFs. Across all tested flow velocities, gyration of the gill filaments resulted in an average increase of 141% in the number of captured particles. The enhancement was statistically significant for each velocity, as confirmed by paired t-tests (p < 0.01, Fig. 3D), demonstrating that adaptive gyration robustly improves particle capture performance over a wide range of flow conditions. In order to further investigate the particle capture capabilities of gill filaments at different orientation angles, flow rates, and particle sizes, we conducted an extended study through fluid simulation. By employing suspended particles with an averaged diameter of 5μm, the number of particles captured by the gill filaments demonstrated an approximately decreasing trend with respect to the increased orientation angle for a certain flow velocity (Fig. 3E). Notably, the gill filament exhibited the highest particle capture capacity when the orientation angle was 0deg(Fig. 3E). In addition, with an increase in flow velocity, the number of captured particles increased under a fixed orientation angle (Fig. 3E). We then turned to map the particle capture capacity by particle size and orientation angle. As shown in Fig. 3F, when the flow velocity remained constant at 0.3m/s, the capture capacity of a gill filament for particles in a certain size becomes weakened and then enhanced with respect to an increasing orientation angle. Furthermore, the particle capture capacity of the gill filament gradually deteriorated with an increase in particle size under the same orientation angle (Fig. 3F). Merz reported the living Eudistylia vancouveri (Annelida: Sabellidae) performed the highest capture rate between 3 and 6μm [48], which matches well with our both theoretical and simulated prediction; namely, the gill filaments captured the greatest number of particles with a combination of the orientation angle θ=0deg and the particle size dp=3μm. In such a bio-inspired filtering system, we should consider its stability in the flow with a changed orientation angle. Here, we took θ=0deg and θ=180deg as an example, under which conditions the best particle capture performance can be achieved (Fig. 3E,F). Fig. 3G,H showed a noticeable decrease in shear stress when applying θ=0deg and θ=180deg, respectively, particularly at the base of the feather-like pinnules. By adapting to the direction of water flow, the filaments can be flexibly gyrated to better align with the current, enhancing their particle capture capacity and optimizing the collection of micro-particles. At the same time, this dynamic gyration helps distribute the mechanical stress more evenly across the filaments, minimizing the risk of damage and improving the overall efficiency of the filter-feeding process.

Fluid dynamics for gyrated gill filaments

To investigate the mechanism behind the flexible directional enhancement of gill filaments for particle capture, we combined computational fluid dynamics simulations and flow visualization techniques to examine the flow dynamics around the gill filaments at different orientation angles. In the case where the water flow is directed toward the gill filament at θ=0deg, the pinnules on both sides of the filament form a shallow V-shaped groove towards incoming flow (Fig. 4A). This configuration causes a slight deceleration of the flow within a small range of the filaments, after which the flow circumvents the filaments. Notably, on the leeward side of the gill filaments, the streamlines undergo sharp distortions and form localized low-speed zones, creating a small-scale vortex pair (Fig. 4B), under which condition particles will move along the closed streamlines and exhibit a stagnant behavior in the vortex and thus be captured by the food groove on the gill filaments, as proved in supplementary material. This arrangement allows for efficient capture of particles while maintaining a relatively smooth flow pattern around the filaments. In contrast, when the gill filament was gyrated such that its symmetry axis was perpendicular to the water flow at θ=90deg (Fig. 4C), the pinnules on the upstream side significantly decelerated the incoming flow, causing the projections of the pinnules on both sides to overlap on the yoz plane, potentially reducing the effective filtering area, when the flow line is distorted but no vortex is observably formed (Fig. 4D). When the orientation angle θ=180deg, the pinnules on both sides form a shallow V-shaped groove with their apices facing away from the incoming flow (Fig. 4E). The flow experiences a broader deceleration range due to the influence of the pinnules, and then it passes through the pinnules on both sides. Additionally, at θ=180deg, a localized low-speed vortex zone emerges on their leeward side, with the intensity of the vortex zone exceeding θ=0deg (Fig. 4F). However, at this stage, the food groove of the gill filaments is oriented towards the incoming flow, which means that the stagnation effect induced by the vortex on the particles does not have a beneficial effect on particle capture. Furthermore, the water flow undergoes momentary acceleration as it passes through the gaps between the pinnules, potentially enhancing the structural filtering speed and enabling processed water to traverse the structure more rapidly (Fig. 4G,H and I). Although gill filaments with different orientation angles have varying effects on the surrounding water flow, an orientation angle of θ=0deg exhibits higher flow velocity on the windward side of the pinnules (Fig. 4). Additionally, in aquatic environments, direct interception is recognized as the primary mechanism for particle capture. The ability of an aquatic collector to capture particles can be assessed by examining the flow patterns around it and analyzing the distance between the limiting streamlines of particles, based on a specific particle size. The gill filament exhibits the largest interception area when the orientation angle θ is either 0deg or 180deg. To better understand this, we conducted an examination of the streamlines surrounding the gill filaments under these two conditions. Our findings show that when the initial orientation angle θ of the gill filament is set to 0 deg, the incoming flow closely adheres to the surface of the filament, wrapping around it at an approximate angle of 65deg (Fig. S13A and S13B). The distance between limiting streamlines of 5-μm particle is measured to be approximately 8.29μm (Fig. 4J). The pinnules cause the streamlines to converge toward the surface of the filament when flowing around the root of the pinnules, which potentially enhances the possibility for secondary particle filtration (Fig. S13A,S13B). In contrast, when the initial orientation angle θ=180deg, the wrapping angle of the incoming flow around the filament’s surface reduces to only 44∘, and the distance between the limiting streamlines of 5-μm particle is reduced by 66%. Fig. S13D,S13E demonstrate that the existence of pinnules further decreases the wrapping angle of the incoming flow around the filament by approximately 31%, and the distance between the limiting streamlines of 5-μm particle by 61%. In both cases, the wrapping angle of the incoming flow around the pinnule is about 69deg. However, there is a notable difference in the distance between the limiting streamlines of the 5 µm particle (Fig. S13C,S13D). According to our particle capture model in the supplementary material, its correctness has been confirmed through comparison with simulation results (Fig. 4K). The filtration efficiency of both filament and pinnule can be expressed as ηDI=2h/D, where 2h is the minimum distance between the limiting streamlines upstream of the collector and D is the diameter of the collector.

The variation in orientation angles primarily impacts the wrapping angle and the distance between limiting streamlines upstream of the gill filament, which significantly influences filtration efficiency. Consequently, the adaptive gyration of gill filaments optimizes filtration area, reduces the deceleration of incoming flow due to the structure, and increases the distance between limiting streamlines, resulting in enhanced particle capture capability from multiple perspectives.

Discussion

Food availability plays a crucial role in the survival of suspension feeders and significantly impacts the morphology and behavior of benthic suspension feeders [54,55]. Suspension feeders actively or passively trap suspended food from the exogenous flow around them [12,[56], [57], [58], [59]], and water flow determines the delivery rate of particles to them [60,61]. The water flow is highly random in the ocean, which may enhance the probability of food particles encountering the filtrating apparatuses, but could also pose a challenge to the ability of the suspension feeders to manipulate the filters to adapt. The feeding apparatus of the sessile fan worm consists of approximately 50 feather-like gill filaments, with each filament decorated with around 300 ciliated pinnules used for filter-feeding, the system of which will be arduous if active control is adopted. We found in our study that the specialized gill filaments of the fan worm are able to gyrate to adapt to the water flow in a passive way by transducing the unbalanced torsion formed on the ciliated pinnules to the inner support structure, which is sufficiently flexible for adaptive gyrating but rigid enough for upright support. Such passive adaptation is achieved by the complex combination of the well-shaped exterior morphology and the porous interior structure with fine-tuned material properties, presenting a physical/mechanical intelligence that can greatly reduce the control complexity when facing the unpredictable water flow in the ocean environment [62,63].

The probability of particles captured by the gill filament is primarily affected by the velocity of the surrounding flow, with higher speeds typically transporting a greater number of potential particle food in a given time [50]. The filament can only be stably balanced when the tip of the V-shaped cross-section formed by the pinnules faces the incoming flow, in which orientation the surrounding water would flow faster than in other states, and the resulting vortex pair facilitates the retention of particles near the food groove of the filament, thereby increasing the probability of food particles encountering the filter-feeding structures. Consequently, this represents the optimal orientation for efficient filter-feeding. In aquatic ecosystems, suspension feeders employ a variety of strategies to intercept particle food, including size-based sieving and adhesive capture mechanisms [47,64]. Given that fan worms consume food particles mainly ranging between 3–6 µm [48] and the distance between their two pinnules is approximately 90 µm, it is improbable that fan worms rely solely on structural size constraints for filtration. In fact, the pinnules of fan worms are coated with a layer of self-secreted mucus [65], enabling them to adhere to particles through viscous force. In the context, the adaptive gyration of gill filaments may contribute to increasing the contact area with incoming water flow, consequently enhancing the filtration efficiency. Additionally, the adaptive gyration can not only mitigate the stress experienced by the pinnules, but also prevent the delicate food groove from the damage of high-speed particles, hence prolonging the life-span of the functional structure (Fig. 3G,H). The fluid experimental conditions used by the research institute are still idealized to some extent and have not fully covered the complex flow characteristics that may exist in natural aquatic environments. Future research can further introduce controlled turbulence, multi-scale disturbances, or compliant surface materials with environmental response characteristics to systematically evaluate the robustness and applicability of this passive adaptation mechanism under more realistic flow conditions. However, the fine machining and precise assembly of soft material components at the micrometer scale remain challenging due to current manufacturing and processing constraints. Therefore, future research should explore novel fabrication techniques or optimize existing methods to enable the development of biomimetic flexible surface structures, facilitating a more comprehensive investigation of the effects of biological surface properties on fluid-structure interactions.

Nowadays, microplastic pollution has emerged as a global concern, with its pervasive presence confirmed in nearly all environmental settings [66,67]. Detecting and monitoring micro contaminants in situ is hence of great significance for identifying and mitigating potential ecological and human health risks in the environment. However, the traditional devices for detecting microscale contaminants in situ under sea level face limitations due to the traditional submarine vehicles and robots that are generally encased in pressure-resistant metallic vessels, which are bulky and need to endure fatal risk from unpredictable fracture [68]. Recent progress in soft underwater robots inspired by marine creatures has shown great potential in machines dealing with actuation, sensing and pressure-resistance under sea, which could validate the bionic solution performing in an aquatic environment [69]. Fan worms, with the adaptive gill filaments, exhibit remarkable efficiency in capturing and retaining suspended particles sized 6 µm and below, we found in our study that filtering robot equipped with gyrated RoboGFs achieved an average increase of 141% in particle capture, which is able to shed lights on the future development of detecting and monitoring devices in situ in the ever-changing acquire environment.

Conclusion

Fan worm gill filaments embody a passive adaptive strategy that integrates external morphology, porous internal support, and material compliance to maintain efficient filter-feeding in variable flows. This flow-induced gyration not only promotes particle capture by stabilizing a favorable hydrodynamic orientation, but may also reduce structural stress and protect delicate feeding components under changing flow conditions. More broadly, these findings reveal how suspension feeders can achieve high functional performance with minimal control complexity, offering a biologically grounded design principle for adaptive filtration and particle-capture systems in dynamic aquatic environments.
