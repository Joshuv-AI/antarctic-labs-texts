Acute subdural hematoma (ASDH) is a worrisome condition, of which the mortality still remains high.1 The majority of traumatic ASDHs originate from bridging veins that are torn into the dural border cell layer, or from cortical contusion with blood suffusion or cortical artery injury.2 Associated brain contusion tends to be associated with a worse prognosis.3 Diagnosis is made by nonenhanced brain CT imaging, which typically shows a crescent-shaped hyperdense extra-axial collection. In cases of life-threatening rise in intracranial pressure, surgery can be indicated to alleviate intracranial pressure through a craniotomy with or without bone flap replacement.1

We report a case of traumatic ASDH secondary to laceration of the middle meningeal artery (MMA) by a skull base fracture spanning through the foramen spinosum (FS). We discuss relevant radiological and surgical anatomy as well as surgical implications.

Illustrative Case

A 76-year-old male, taking apixaban for atrial fibrillation, was admitted to our level I trauma center in the backdrop of a ladder fall (2.5 m high). The ambulance rescue service reported a patient with an initial Glasgow Coma Scale (GSC) score of 15, whose neurological status worsened on the way to the hospital, reaching a GCS score of 9. At arrival, he was intubated and his pupils were equal and reactive to light. Transcranial Doppler ultrasound showed signs of intracranial hypertension (ICH) (pulsatility index 1.99). Brain CT imaging showed an intracranial extra-axial acute right frontotemporal hematoma extending toward the middle fossa with a threatening mass effect (Fig. 1). This hematoma was lens-shaped and extended across the cranial vault beyond sutures, which suggested an epidural or subdural hematoma (Fig. 1). After receiving prothrombin complex concentrate,4 the patient was sent to the operating room for an emergency evacuation of his hematoma by the on-call neurosurgical team. The patient was positioned supine, his head was rotated 90° to the opposite side, and a 3-pin Mayfield head clamp was used. A right frontotemporal approach was performed. After the craniotomy, no epidural bleeding was encountered. On opening the dura mater, clotted blood was found in the subdural space. After evacuation of the hematoma, the temporal skull base (which was easily accessible because of the mass effect the hematoma had on the parenchyma) (Fig. 2) was inspected. The FS was accessed intradurally, and an arterial hemorrhage from the MMA was seen. A linear basal skull fracture had lacerated both the dura and the MMA. Pulsatile bleeding originating from the FS was halted by applying Horsley wax (Fig. 2). Hemostasis was obtained, the surgical field was irrigated with saline, and the dura mater was closed in a watertight fashion. After the surgery, a second look at the imaging showed a nondisplaced temporal skull fracture extending toward the FS (Fig. 1). FIG. 2.Intraoperative view after ASDH evacuation. The patient was monitored in the intensive care unit and extubated on postoperative day 3. He had a GCS score of 12 (E3M6V3), diplopia due to right oculomotor nerve palsy, and semantic paraphasia. Except for a pulmonary infection that improved with antibiotics, his postoperative course was uneventful. The patient’s neurological status gradually improved over the following month, reaching a GCS score of 15 with neither phasic nor motor impairment. At 3 months postoperatively, the patient was neurologically intact.

Discussion

Observations

ASDH is a worrisome condition and a leading cause of death in the young adult population.5 Depending on severity, surgery can be indicated to alleviate ICH.1,6 Patients may require hematoma evacuation through a craniotomy with bone flap replacement, a hinge craniotomy, or a decompressive craniectomy.1 When surgery is warranted, timing is a key prognostic factor.7,8 Surgery is optimally performed within 4 hours of the trauma or 2 hours from clinical worsening.7,8 This surgical procedure is routinely performed by residents or attendees who are building their experience on these cases.9 The combination of a potentially less experienced surgeon and the intrinsic urgency warrant extra attention to pitfalls. Here, we present a case of ASDH secondary to the proximal laceration of the MMA at the FS and address its educational value. To the best of our knowledge, an ASDH secondary to a skull base fracture encompassing the FS has not been mentioned in the literature.

Imaging assessment allows us to make an accurate diagnosis and avoid pitfalls.10 In our case, it was challenging to sort out whether the hematoma was epidural or subdural, as it shared imaging features of both. It was predominantly anterior and inferior on the right side and did not extend in a crescent-like fashion along the cranial vault, but was rather lens-shaped. However, it went across the sutures. A careful second-look examination of the CT scan obtained after the surgical procedure showed an hyperacute hematoma at the basal skull close to the FS, with an adjacent balloon-shaped acute hematoma, extending upward (Fig. 1). This atypical appearance was a hint that something unusual was happening. Bone window images showed a temporal bone fracture passing through the ipsilateral FS, suggesting a potential lesion of the MMA. This was only appreciated after confirming the laceration during surgery. Epidural hematoma secondary to a fracture passing through the FS11,12 and epidural hematoma secondary to MMA pseudoaneurysm rupture13 have been reported in the literature. However, ASDH in this context has not been. Linear nondisplaced basilar cranial fractures following high-energy cranial trauma must be accurately assessed as they can offer critical insight into the etiology or lead to critical complications.14–16 The older age of the patient, associated with increased dural adhesion to the skull, probably contributed to the intradural bleeding of the MMA rather than formation of an epidural hematoma. It is worth mentioning that clinical features (secondary and rapidly progressive neurological worsening), in addition to the imaging features, brought up the possibility of an arterial origin in this case.

In this case, the important imaging feature was involvement of the FS. As stated by Krayenbühl et al., “an improved knowledge about the FS may not only help for middle fossa approaches but also in trauma surgery when the foramen has to be explored in order to get proper hemostasis.”17 The FS is an easily identifiable landmark in middle cranial fossa microsurgical practice. Despite many variations, it is classically described as bilateral, anteromedial to the mandibular fossa, and posterolateral to the foramen ovale.17 The zygomatic root of the temporal bone can serve as an external landmark for locating the FS, which lies approximately 25 mm beneath it.18 The “point O” described by Liu and Yi in 2020 can also help in accessing the FS.19 This landmark is defined as the junction point of the temporosphenoid suture and the infratemporal ridge. The FS transmits the MMA, the middle meningeal vein, and a recurrent branch of the mandibular nerve (spinous nerve).17 The prospect of laceration of the MMA at the FS impacts surgical planning. Here the key lies in the surgical exposure, which must both prevent brain swelling and allow access to the FS to perform hemostasis. In a recently published “how I do it” article on ASDH, an incision tailored to access the middle cranial fossa was proposed.17 Also, it is recommended to systematically inspect the middle cranial fossa after blood clot evacuation. In our case, after evacuation of the hematoma, the already atrophic brain had shifted enough to clearly see the temporal skull base. If this is not the case, a rongeur can be used to remove the temporal bone flush with the skull base to carefully inspect the middle fossa floor and FS. In cases of ASDH secondary to a vascular lesion such as a ruptured aneurysm10 or as our case, we recommend using a Mayfield head clamp rather than a circular pad (as used by some teams) to permit adequate rotation of the patient if needed.20

Lessons

We share our experience in dealing with an ASDH secondary to laceration of the MMA at the FS. ASDHs impose a systematic and rigorous assessment of the imaging, which we must search for clues of the associated etiology. Rapid deterioration is frequently seen in patients taking direct oral anticoagulants, and we can add to the literature that an arterial bleeding coming from the MMA at the FS should be born in mind. We further emphasize the need for a surgical exposure that allows management of brain swelling and access to the FS. A large enough craniotomy should performed, with the bone flap left out as needed, to allow inspection of the FS. The latter is easily done when the brain has shifted due to mass effect; otherwise, it can to be achieved by removing the temporal bone until it is flush with the middle fossa floor. Rapid surgical intervention and obtaining hemostasis at the level of the FS resulted in good patient recovery.
