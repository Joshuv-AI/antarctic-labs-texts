Comparison of Weight‐Based Versus External Ear‐Size‐Based Technique for Selection of Laryngeal Mask in Pediatric Surgical Patients: A Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Muhammad Asghar Ali, Bushra Salim, Muhammad Saad Yousuf, Samie Asghar, Saad Ebrahim Siddiqui, Ayesha Shoaib, Hameed Ullah, Qamar Ul Hoda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsThe Laryngeal Mask (LM) has grown in appreciation for use in the pediatric population. The manufacturer recommends that the size of the LM to be used in children should be based on actual body weight. This method may not be appropriate in emergencies, where the weight of the patient is unknown, resulting in the correlation between the weight and size of the LM being impossible. Weight‐based recommendations may also not be suitable as the development of the child's oropharyngeal cavity is related to height and age rather than weight. To overcome these concerns, various alternatives to weight‐based techniques have been tried. One of them is the external ear size for LM size selection. This study aimed to evaluate the two methods by assessing the proper placement and adequacy of ventilation.MethodsA total of 137 children, aged between 6 months and 18 years, undergoing surgery, under general anesthesia with muscle relaxation and positive pressure ventilation, were included. After obtaining approval from the ethical review committee, written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The children were randomly assigned to one of two groups: the weight‐based group (Group W) and the external ear‐size‐based group (Group E), after fulfilling the study's inclusion criteria. Both groups were compared for the accurate placement of the LM.ResultsDemographic data were comparable in both groups. The leak was audible in 44.1% in Group W versus 11.6% in Group E. Leak fraction (> 15%) was significantly higher in Group W, 44.1% as compared to Group E, 11.6%. As a result, reinsertion with a one size larger LM was more in Group W. Mucosal injury and laryngospasm were also more pronounced in Group W.ConclusionExternal ear size‐based selection of LM size is an effective and more accurate method than weight‐based selection.

Introduction

The Laryngeal Mask (LM) has grown in appreciation for use in the pediatric population [1]. LMs for pediatric patients are available in different sizes [2]. The manufacturer typically recommends selecting the appropriate size based on the child's actual body weight (ABW) [3]. However, this weight‐based approach has several limitations: (a) the weight ranges corresponding to each LM size are often broad [3], (b) this method may not be appropriate for overweight or underweight children, as it is based on ABW rather than ideal body weight [3, 4], (c) in emergency settings, where the patient's weight may be unknown, determining the appropriate LM size using this method is impractical [5], and (d) weight‐based sizing may be inaccurate, as the growth and development of a child's oropharyngeal cavity are more closely related to age and height than weight [6]. These factors can result in improper size selection, which may lead to complications such as airway trauma or injury from an oversized LM or airway obstruction and inadequate ventilation from an undersized device [7]. Additionally, Devitt et al. reported that leak fractions varied between 13% and 27% depending on peak airway pressures, further highlighting the importance of proper LM sizing [8].

To address these concerns, several researchers have explored alternative methods to the weight‐based approach for selecting LM size in both adult and pediatric patients, that is, tongue width, thyromental distance, the width of three fingers (ring, middle, and index fingers), and external ear size for LM size selection [9, 10, 11, 12, 13]. The literature highlights variations in external ear dimensions among different ethnic populations [14]. However, our review did not identify any local studies or data from Pakistan involving pediatric patients where external ear size was evaluated or used as a reference for LM size selection. Therefore, we employed an external ear size–based method for selecting LM size in pediatric surgical patients at our tertiary care hospital in Pakistan.

Therefore, the objective of the study was to evaluate whether external ear size is a noninferior alternative to body weight for selecting the appropriate LM size in pediatric surgical patients. The primary outcome was to evaluate the two methods by assessing the proper placement and adequacy of ventilation. Secondary outcomes were to determine the leak fraction and incidence of mucosal injury in the two groups.

Materials and Methods

This study was a prospective, randomized, double‐blind, controlled noninferiority trial registered with ClinicalTrials (NCT03931122) and conducted over 18 months at the main operating rooms of a tertiary care hospital in Pakistan. After approval from the departmental research committee (Department of Anaesthesiology, The Aga Khan University) and the ethical review committee of the Aga Khan University (ERC # 2022‐0246‐21104), 137 children were included in this study. A computer‐generated randomization technique was used to assign patients to either a weight (W) or ear‐size (E) group. All children of either gender, aged between 6 months and 18 years, with an American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classification of I or II, undergoing elective surgical procedures lasting up to 2 h, in whom an LM was indicated as an airway device, were included. Children with external ear deformities, a history of external ear surgery, oropharyngeal lesions, or a recent upper respiratory tract infection (URTI) within the past 2 weeks, those requiring rapid sequence induction and intubation, or if there was refusal from the patient or parent, were excluded. Informed consent was obtained from the parents of children meeting the inclusion and exclusion criteria, and assent was obtained from children above 7 years of age. One of the investigators recorded each patient's weight and measured their external ear dimensions (maximum vertical and horizontal). On the day of surgery, one of the investigators (not the primary anesthetist) determined the LM size based on the assigned group. After writing the LM size in the proforma, it was handed over to the primary anesthetist (having experienced a minimum of 50 LM insertions), who was blinded to the patient's group allocation. The primary anesthetist used the LM (Ambu® AuraOnce™) size as per the proforma.

The anesthesia machine (Avance CS2‐GE) was tested for leaks before use. Upon arrival in the operating room, standard monitoring was applied to all patients, for example, electrocardiography, noninvasive blood pressure measurement, pulse oximetry, capnography, fraction of inspired oxygen, and volatile agent analyzer. Following inhalational or intravenous induction, cis‐atracurium 0.04 mg/kg was given [15]. Bag‐mask ventilation was done for 3 min; after that, the selected LM was placed using the technique recommended by the manufacturer (pencil insertion technique and/or slight lateral rotation) [2]. After insertion, the pilot balloon was used to inflate the LM cuff at a pressure of 60 cm H2O using a manometer (Control‐Inflator “Pocket” VBM Medizintechnik, Sulz a. N., Germany). Proper placement of LM was determined by assessing chest expansion on positive pressure ventilation, the absence of audible leaks, and a square waveform on the capnograph. Following this, orophyrangeal leak pressure (OLP) was assessed by turning the APL valve to 30 cm H2O and maintaining the fresh gas flow at 3 L/min; the steady state circuit pressure was recorded as OLP, following which the APL valve was opened completely. At this point, the leak fraction was determined by setting the ventilator to deliver a Tidal Volume TV of 8 mL/kg (ABW) on volume‐controlled mode at a frequency of 20 breaths/min with an inspiration‐to‐expiration ratio of 1:2. The difference between the inspired and expired TV was recorded and the leak fraction was calculated by dividing it by the inspired tidal volume. If there was no leak or the leak was less than 15%, the patient continued to be ventilated at this TV. If there was a leak of more than 15%, the TV was reduced to 7 mL/kg, and if the leak fraction was still more than 15%, the TV was further reduced to 6 mL/kg. If at this TV (6 mL/kg) leak fraction was still more than 15%, the LM was changed to one size larger, and the same sequence was repeated. In the case, at TV 6 mL/kg with one size larger LM, the leak fraction was still more than 15%, LM was replaced by ETT, and the case was declared as “failure of LM for controlled ventilation.” In patients who were successfully ventilated with the LM, their respiratory rate was modified to keep end‐tidal CO2 levels within the range of 35–45 mmHg throughout the surgical procedure.

At the end of the surgery, the residual neuromuscular blockade was reversed with neostigmine methylsulfate 0.01 mg/kg and glycopyrrolate 0.01 mg/kg [16], and the child's LM was removed in a deep plane of anesthesia after the restoration of adequate ventilation. After the removal of the LM, the oral cavity was inspected for blood staining to rule out any mucosal injury. The patient was transferred to the recovery room and was discharged after meeting the standards for discharge from the recovery room.

The sample size was estimated using PASS 11 (power and sample size software), based on the data from Park et al. [17], who reported inspiratory and expiratory tidal volumes and leak fractions in pediatric patients. We specifically used the size 2 LM subgroup, as this was most representative of our expected study cohort (children aged 1–6 years, 10–20 kg; in our study, the median LM size was 2.0). At the time of trial design, exact data for our planned primary outcome (ventilation adequacy defined by leak fraction > 15%) were unavailable; therefore, we pragmatically used the most closely related published data, consistent with clinical trial methodology that allows the use of surrogate endpoints when identical outcomes are lacking [18, 19, 20]. Park et al. [17] reported a mean leak fraction of 11.4% with SD 7.6 for the size 2 group. Using this SD and a noninferiority margin of 5% points, the required sample size was 37 per group (74 total) to achieve 80% power at a one‐sided α of 0.025. Our final recruitment of 137 participants (68 and 69 per group) exceeded this requirement, providing > 95% power for the primary comparison.

Data analysis was conducted using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS‐19, Chicago, IL). In both Groups W and E, the frequency and percentage were computed for categorical variables like gender, ASA status, comorbid, LM size selected, proper placement of LM, adequacy of ventilation, number of cases with device failure/intubation, and blood‐stained secretions on the device after removal. For quantitative variables such as age, weight, height, BMI, ear dimensions, leak fraction, and oropharyngeal leak pressure, the mean and standard deviation were computed. An independent two‐sample t‐test was employed to compare the mean tidal volume difference between the two groups. A p‐value of ≤ 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant, and a 95% confidence interval was used to consider noninferiority.

Results

This randomized controlled trial included a total of 137 children. Although the calculated sample size was 136, one additional child was included. This participant was initially suspected to have an external ear deformity, but subsequent review confirmed eligibility, resulting in a final sample of 137 participants.

Discussion

Our study evaluated a new method for LM size selection based on ear pinna measurements. To date, only a few studies have assessed this method [21, 22]. The findings of this study suggest that the ear‐size‐based method is an effective approach for selecting the appropriate LM size in pediatric patients. Additionally, our findings showed that gender‐based adjustments were not required, as ear size is primarily associated with age and, to a lesser extent, with physical build [21].

In Group W, one participant experienced failed ventilation, necessitating LM removal and endotracheal intubation. Among the same group, 13 patients had blood‐tinged secretions upon LM removal, and 7 developed laryngospasm. This was likely due to the higher incidence of reinsertion in Group W compared to Group E. These findings suggest that an external ear‐size‐based approach can provide a more tailored fit, minimizing the risks associated with suboptimal LM sizing.

The rate of successful insertion with adequate ventilation observed in our study aligns with previously reported LM insertion outcomes [12, 21]. Zahoor et al. [12] compared weight‐based and ear‐based techniques in 210 children, concluding that the ear‐size‐based method serves as a reliable approach for selecting the appropriate LM size. Jha et al. [21] also concluded that the auricle method is a feasible approach to weight‐based calculations. Devitt et al. [8] reported that leak fraction varied between 13% and 27% depending on peak airway pressures, while Mishra et al. [22] found comparable peak airway pressures (13.23 ± 2.63 vs. 12.49 ± 3.32 as compared to 12.98 ± 2.87 vs. 12.10 ± 2.98) in both groups, consistent with our findings. A key observation in our study was that the ear‐size‐based technique resulted in a higher first‐attempt success rate compared to the weight‐based approach, 8.7% (n = 6) of patients in Group E were managed with a larger size LM, as compared to 39.7% (n = 27) in Group W. This suggests that anatomical markers may provide a more precise estimation of the appropriate LM size, reducing the need for reinsertion or adjustments.

Our study has a few limitations. It did not include pediatric cases younger than 6 months, a population in which supraglottic device placement is particularly crucial. Additionally, we did not categorize patients based on weight percentiles. As a prospective exploratory trial with a convenience sample of 137 patients, further multicenter trials are needed to validate these findings. Furthermore, while we did not identify any major pitfalls of this method, it was only tested in healthy children, and its applicability to those with congenital anomalies (anatomical anomalies) remains uncertain. Another limitation relates to our sample size estimation. Our initial calculation simplified the variability reported in Park et al., whereas the true standard deviations for inspiratory and expiratory tidal volumes were larger (41 and 31 mL). On repeating the calculation using Park's leak fraction data (size 2 group: 11.4% ± 7.6), we confirmed that our recruited sample (n = 137) was more than sufficient to detect noninferiority with high power. Finally, while previous authors [e.g., 12, 21] have used agreement‐based methods for evaluating airway sizing, our study was designed as a noninferiority RCT and therefore employed a between‐groups comparison. We acknowledge that agreement approaches represent a complementary analytic strategy in this area.

The strength of our study lies in demonstrating that the method based on ear size achieved a higher success rate in LM size selection compared to the weight‐referenced method. This method is simple, eliminates the need to memorize tables or formulas, and can be easily integrated into routine clinical practice. It may be especially useful for emergency doctors, ambulance staff, and emergency medical technicians, who have limited time to refer to weight‐based charts during pediatric airway management. Furthermore, this method can be extremely useful in difficult airway algorithms. While some clinicians may require training to adopt this technique, its advantages include improved success rates, fewer airway‐related complications, and enhanced patient safety, making it a valuable alternative to traditional methods.

The ear‐based technique shows potential as an alternative approach for LM size selection in pediatric patients, given the close correlation between airway and pinna growth [12]. It is easy to understand and implement, removing the necessity of memorizing weight‐based tables.
