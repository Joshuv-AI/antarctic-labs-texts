Neuralgic amyotrophy presentation of acute intermittent porphyria: A case report
Authors: Julian Theuriet, Mathieu Gerfaud‐Valentin, Cécile‐Audrey Durel, Laurent Gouya, Antoine Pegat
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and AimsPorphyrias are inherited metabolic disorders caused by mutations in genes encoding enzymes involved in the heme biosynthetic pathway, leading to the accumulation of heme precursors. Acute hepatic porphyrias (AHP), including acute intermittent porphyria (AIP), can present with predominant peripheral neurological manifestations, often leading to a misdiagnosis as Guillain‐Barré syndrome.MethodsWe report a case of AIP initially presenting as a peripheral neuropathy mimicking Parsonage‐Turner syndrome (neuralgic amyotrophy, NA). Clinical and electrophysiological evaluations were conducted, including nerve conduction studies and needle electromyography (EMG).ResultsA 41‐year‐old woman presented with burning pain and electric shock‐like sensations in the shoulders and trunk, alongside asymmetrical motor weakness in the upper limbs affecting arm abduction and finger extension. Electrophysiological evaluation revealed involvement of the superior trunk of the brachial plexus and the posterior interosseous nerve. Initially diagnosed with NA, she showed significant improvement in proximal strength over nine months but relapsed at fourteen months with severe finger extension weakness. Concurrent severe abdominal pain with constipation led to the identification of elevated urinary porphobilinogen (PBG) and delta‐aminolevulinic acid (ALA) levels, confirming AHP and specifically AIP via genetic and biochemical testing. The patient received hemin and givosiran infusions, resulting in decreased ALA levels, improvement of motor weakness, and no further attacks.InterpretationThis case underscores the need to consider AIP in the differential diagnosis of acute neuropathies like NA, especially when accompanied by abdominal pain and severe constipation. Early recognition and appropriate testing for PBG and ALA can prevent misdiagnosis and enable targeted treatment.

BACKGROUND AND AIMS

Porphyrias are inherited metabolic disorders caused by mutations in genes encoding for enzymes implicated in the heme biosynthetic pathway. 1 , 2 They result mainly in the accumulation of heme precursors, and the nature of the symptoms depends on the physicochemical properties of the precursors that accumulate. 1 , 2 They are classified as either hepatic or erythropoietic porphyrias depending on the tissue that produces the toxic precursors. The acute hepatic porphyrias (AHP), which include acute intermittent porphyria (AIP), hereditary coproporphyria (HCP), variegate porphyria (VP), and delta‐aminolevulinic acid dehydratase (ALAD) deficiency porphyria (ADP), can present with predominant neurological manifestations. 1 , 2 The presence of associated abdominal pain, central manifestations, and autonomic dysfunction can help the clinician to reorient the diagnosis to porphyria. 3 The peripheral nervous system can be involved, with the motor nerves preferentially affected, and these patients can be misdiagnosed as Guillain‐Barré syndromes. 1 , 2 Here, the case of a 41‐year‐old woman, whose initial manifestation of AIP was a peripheral neuropathy mimicking Parsonage‐Turner syndrome, also known as neuralgic amyotrophy (NA), is reported. The latter consists in an acute inflammatory neuropathy that usually affects the upper limbs and is responsible for a multifocal painful paresis. 3 , 4 The patient gave her informed consent to the use of her data for research purposes, and the study was carried out in accordance with the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki.

CASE REPORT

A 41‐year‐old woman was admitted to the emergency room for asthenia, weight loss, headache, tinnitus, dyspnea persisting for several weeks, along with odynophagia for one week, which had been treated with antibiotics, notably metronidazole. Her medical history was notable for fibromyalgia, which had necessitated morphine use for 5 years but had been stopped for several months, and antiphospholipid antibody syndrome, for which she was on a vitamin K antagonist. She reported cannabis use. A blood‐cell count revealed severe anemia with a hemoglobin level of 4.3 g/dL. She was transfused with four units of packed red blood cells, which improved the hemoglobin level. Chest, abdomen, and pelvis scan and gastroscopy were normal. A gynecological evaluation showed metrorrhagia without any detectable lesions. During her two‐week hospitalization, she developed diffuse pain, particularly severe in the abdomen, which was attributed to cannabis withdrawal syndrome. One month later, she was readmitted to our hospital due to upper extremity pain, which quickly progressed to motor weakness. She reported worsening diffuse pain, primarily affecting her shoulders and trunk, characterized by severe shock‐like and burning sensations, present both at rest and during sleep, without improvement under tramadol. Clinical examination found motor weakness affecting arm abduction (3/5 on the modified Research Council (MRC) scale on the right arm, 5/5 on the left), shoulder external rotation (3/5 on both sides), and finger extension (3/5 on both sides). Dysesthesia was noted on the lateral sides of both shoulders. Deep tendon reflexes were normal. Nerve conduction study (NCS) found slightly decreased sensory nerve action potential (SNAP) amplitudes in the territory of the superior trunk of the brachial plexus (radial nerve, median nerve to the thumb, and musculocutaneous nerve). Needle electromyography (EMG) showed fibrillations at rest in muscles innervated by the C5 and C6 roots (deltoid, infraspinatus, and brachioradialis muscles) on both sides and in the posterior interosseous nerves (finger extensor muscles). In these muscles, recruitment during contraction was reduced. NCS and EMG in lower limbs were unremarkable. Sympathetic skin response (SSR) and electrochemical skin conductance (ESC) using Sudoscan (Impeto Medical, France) were abnormal in both feet. At that time, neuralgic amyotrophy (NA) involving the superior trunk of the brachial plexus and the posterior interosseous nerve was diagnosed, associated with a possible small fiber neuropathy (SFN). She reported an improvement of her symptoms during the following months. At nine months after the onset of symptoms, a full recovery of proximal strength was noted, with a partial improvement (4/5) in finger extension. The EMG also showed improvement, with no more fibrillations at rest and the appearance of high amplitude and enlarged motor unit action potentials during contraction, suggestive of chronic reinnervation. Fourteen months after the disease onset, she experienced an acute relapse characterized by worsening motor weakness in the left hand, accompanied by abdominal burning pain and severe constipation lasting for two weeks. Visual hallucinations were described for two days when an opioid treatment was started. On examination two months after this relapse, she exhibited severe weakness (2/5) in left finger extension. NCS and needle EMG confirmed further deterioration of the posterior interosseous nerve function, with the reappearance of fibrillations in the finger extensor muscles. Given these atypical symptoms for NA, her urine—noticeably orange in color—was tested for porphobilinogen (PBG) and delta‐aminolevulinic acid (ALA). PBG and ALA were elevated at 34 nmol/mol creatine (N < 3) and 97.4 nmol/mol creatine (N < 1), respectively, leading to a diagnosis of neurovisceral crisis of AHP. The biochemical profile was suggestive of AIP, confirmed by genetic testing, which found a heterozygous pathogenic variant in the HMBS gene (c.591_592delinsG, p.(Trp198Glyfs*57)). She received a hemin treatment for four days (at 3 mg/kg) to treat the ongoing acute crisis and monthly infusions of givosiran (at 2.5 mg/kg) to avoid recurrence of the symptoms. The ALA level decreased <10 nmol/mol creatine, and givosiran was stopped after two infusions. Five months after the last givosiran infusion, the ALA level remained low, and no further attacks occurred.

INTERPRETATION

Here, the case of a patient diagnosed with an AIP, who presented with a phenotype corresponding to NA, is presented. An NA/Parsonage‐Turner syndrome was initially considered because the patient met three of the five classical clinical criteria: i‐“acute onset of very severe, analgesic‐resistant unilateral, or bilateral shoulder and/or upper arm pain”, ii‐“pain often worse at night and also severe when arm is at rest”, iii‐“multifocal peripheral nervous system symptoms and signs that can be bilateral but asymmetric”. 3 , 4 Moreover, her motor weakness involved classical NA hot spots represented by the superior trunk of the brachial plexus and the posterior interosseous nerve. 3 , 4 She was treated for an infection a few weeks before the neurological symptoms, as observed in almost half of the patients with NA. 3 , 4 Relapses are possible in NA, with an average frequency of around 5 years. 4 The correct diagnosis of AIP was made following an early relapse associated with severe abdominal pain and constipation.

Peripheral neuropathy is a classical manifestation of AIP, but never described in the literature with the term NA or Parsonage‐Turner. The neuropathy associated with AIP is typically described as a motor‐predominant axonal neuropathy, which preferentially affects the proximal muscles of the upper extremities. 1 , 2 Symptoms may frequently progress to the lower extremities. The weakness typically develops rapidly within a few weeks and diffuse areflexia is commonly observed. 1 , 2 In the most severe cases, this can lead to tetraparesis and respiratory insufficiency requiring intensive care unit admission. 1 , 2 Cranial nerves can also be affected, especially the VIIth, and Xth nerves. 1 , 2 More rarely, focal motor neuropathy can be found, especially affecting the radial or fibular nerves, resulting in wrist/finger or foot drop, respectively. 1 An autonomic neuropathy often accompanies or precedes motor neuropathy, with symptoms such as tachycardia, hypertension, excessive sweating, temperature dysregulation, dysuria, and severe constipation with pseudo‐obstruction. 1 , 2

All these features can be observed in Guillain‐Barré Syndrome, an acute dysimmune neuropathy. Thus, AIP is frequently misdiagnosed as Guillain‐Barré Syndrome, especially in an axonal form named acute motor axonal neuropathy (AMAN). 1 Other differential diagnoses include chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyradiculoneuropathy (CIDP), heavy metal toxicity, and motor neuron diseases. 2 However, NA is not mentioned in the literature as a possible phenotype or differential diagnosis of AIP. The case presented herein suggests that NA should be added to the list of differential diagnoses for acute porphyrias. We cannot exclude the possibility that AIP and NA were two independent events triggered by a common factor, such as the infection occurring a few weeks before the neurological symptoms. Future case reports will be crucial for further investigating of this issue. However, the two neurological attacks were associated with severe constipation and abdominal pain, along with SFN, symptoms not associated with NA.

Even though the diagnosis of acute porphyria neuropathy is challenging, some clinical features can help. First, abdominal pain precedes neuropathy in most cases. 1 , 2 SFN responsible for neuropathic pain can also be present, classically involving the trunk and the proximal part of limbs in a “bathing suit” distribution. 5 The patient described here had burning pain in this territory, and electrophysiological tests were in favor of an SFN. She also reported chronic pain, as frequently described in patients with AIP, 5 leading in the present case to the misdiagnosis of fibromyalgia, which likely misguided the clinicians during the diagnostic process. Additionally, psychiatric symptoms such as anxiety and insomnia may occur. 1 , 2 Finally, the central nervous system can be affected, with headaches, epilepsy, hallucinations, vasogenic edema linked to posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome, and syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) with hyponatremia. 1 , 2 Exacerbating factors such as medications or infections are also frequently found, although nonspecific, as was the use of metronidazole and tramadol in this patient.

In conclusion, clinicians should consider the diagnosis of AIP and use qualitative spot urine testing for PBG and ALA levels when encountering a patient presenting with an NA phenotype associated with abdominal pain, severe constipation, neuropsychiatric symptoms, or small fiber involvement, especially if relapses occur.
