Differences in opioid use and overdose among younger and older justice-impacted adults
Authors: Meghan M. O’Neil, Wayne Kepner, Garland Gerber, Thomas Wojciekowski, Debra Pinals
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundJustice-impacted persons aged 40 and up with substance use disorders (SUD) demonstrate increased health risks and health disparities relative to general population peers. Persons with SUD are less likely to age out of criminal behavior, appearing on criminal dockets, in jails, prisons, and under community supervision throughout the life course, with greater community-level cost burdens as they age. Justice system involvement presents health risks that compound with SUD to undermine well-being, which is amplified as people age and experience age-related health decline. Propensity for premature mortality from overdose is startlingly high for this population, highlighting demand for targeted policies to better meet the needs of this vulnerable group. To better understand justice-impacted older adults in treatment for SUD and inform policy, we examined opioid use outcomes among 357 low-income justice-impacted adults in SUD treatment in the Midwest, USA, including a natural oversampling of Black and American Indian or Alaska Native (AIAN) persons. We explored patterns among persons in their 40s, 50s, and 60s, relative to those under 40, conceptualizing life-course risk factors and using logistic regression to assess overdose, opioid use, and opioid agonist medication use.ResultsSignificant differences in opioid use by age were observed, with older persons less likely to report opioid prescription misuse or illicit opioid use. Differences were not significant once controlling for user preferences, race/ethnicity, gender, family, childhood, and life course experiences. Overdose history was also significantly less likely for the 40 and older SUD patient, though this was no longer significant when controlling for demographic covariates. Opioid agonist medication use did not significantly differ by age.ConclusionsJustice-involved patients aged 40 and up in SUD treatment were less likely to have experienced overdose or report opioid use, relative to their younger peers, but this variation dissipated when considering demographic, family and/or life course factors. Targeted treatment services for gender and racial minorities may be beneficial for patients 40 and up. We identify preference for one substance, versus two, as protective against overdose and opioid use among older persons who use drugs.

Introduction

Twenty-twenty three and 2024 marked a decrease in overdose deaths involving opioids (CDC, 2025), yet, the overdose crisis continues to impact the United States with extraordinary consequences including premature mortality and large tolls across many sectors including the criminal justice system, the economy, and public health. In the morass of related issues, aging and older people who have experienced overdose and/or misuse of opioids have received less attention, however (Han et al., 2023). The White House Office of National Drug Control Policy recently released new priorities which may vary in their implementation and policy initiatives by age of the target population, with older adults benefiting from tailored supports that consider their specific needs (White House, 2025). These new priorities include to (1) reduce the number of overdose fatalities, with a focus on fentanyl and, (2) to provide treatment that leads to long-term recovery. Providing treatment that leads to long-term recovery among older justice-involved persons may be especially attractive to states wrangling with how best to allocate budgets given the high costs associated with housing older persons in the justice system who may present with complex and expensive medical needs.

While the population of persons who use illicit drugs is aging, the literature surrounding older people who use drugs—defined as those 40 and older—is under-theorized (Zolopa et al., 2022). This knowledge gap may be due to the high risk of premature mortality among persons with opioid use disorder (OUD) (mean age = 30) that suggests persons with OUD may not reach older ages because they have already experienced death (Darke et al., 2006). Indeed, persons who use opioids may be between six and thirty times higher to experience premature mortality relative to non-opioid users of the same gender and age (Kuerbis, 2020; Huhn et al., 2018). The vast majority of all drug related deaths are due to opioids (Darke et al., 2006; Gossop et al., 2002; Strang et al., 2008). With the adulteration of non-opioids with other substances becoming more common, overdose deaths from opioids can occur even among persons who are not aware they are using opioids, such as cocaine users (Di Trana et al., 2022).

Limited data are available on older people who have SUD, especially among high-risk subpopulations who use opioids, such as those who are experiencing homelessness. Problematic opioid use in older adults includes prescription and illicit opioids (Dufort & Sayman, 2021). According to the CDC, the prevalence of drug overdose was highest for adults ages 35–44 (62.00 per 100,000) however, the largest percent increase in the prevalence of drug overdose deaths was found among those ages 65 and older (Spencer et al., 2024). Overdoses involving synthetic opioids and stimulants have increased in recent years (Mattson et al., 2021). The age-adjusted prevalence of drug overdose deaths per 100,000 nearly quadrupled between 2002 and 2022 (8.2–32.6) (Spencer et al., 2024).

The overdose crisis has impacted Americans of all ages and backgrounds; however, access to data has traditionally been limited for hard-to-reach vulnerable populations, especially economically marginalized and racial minority groups. Mistrust towards authority seated in historical racial discrimination has been found to limit participation in formal research among marginalized racial groups including American Indian Alaskan Native (AIAN) and Black persons (Scharff et al., 2010). Older adults who have histories of justice system involvement, e.g., history of arrest, incarceration, or government supervision, are at particular risk for premature mortality and co-morbidities relative to their peers who have not experienced lifetime criminal justice involvement (Han et al., 2021, 2022). Health equity concerns are paramount among this population with pronounced increases in recent years of substance use-related premature mortality among AIAN and Black persons (Kariisa et al., 2022; Spencer et al., 2024) who also are overrepresented in the US criminal justice system (Nielsen & Silverman, 2019; Ross & Gould, 2015). Native Americans are incarcerated at alarming rates, estimated to be 40 times the national average (Lamphere & Hassett, 2022). Black persons are also incarcerated at rates disproportionate to their share of the population (Kamalu et al., 2010).

There is a need for age specific research related to SUDs to inform policy and clinical practice. Such research has the potential to improve treatment outcomes and quality of life for adults with SUD as they age. Research has found that adverse childhood experiences are associated with history of overdose among patients in outpatient addiction treatment (Asheh et al., 2023). ACEs tend to be higher among younger persons and people of color, but little is known how this may bear upon overdose and treatment experiences. There are gaps in the literature surrounding ACEs and older adults’ experiences with overdose, warranting further investigation.

Overdose

During the past 20 years, there has been an alarming rate of opioid related fatalities among older adults (Humphreys & Shover, 2023). From 2002 to 2021, there was a significant increase in opioid overdose among those 65 plus, at a rate growing faster than any other age group (Spencer et al., 2024). The highest rates of overdose in older adults were among Non-Hispanic Black American adults (Friedman & Shover, 2023).

Opioid use among older adults intersects with aging-related physiological changes, increased prevalence and severity of chronic conditions, high numbers of prescription medications and changing patterns of substance use; older adults are therefore particularly vulnerable to the harmful health consequences of drug use. (Carew, Cominsky, 2018; Kepner et al., 2023; Rosen et al., 2011). Additionally, opioid metabolism in older adults differs significantly from younger populations due to age-related physiological changes, affecting both pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics (Prostran et al., 2016). These factors contribute to a more unpredictable response to opioids in older individuals.

SUD and intravenous drug use increase incidence of chronic disease, mental health, and treatment needs. Among those over the average age of adult overdose death (41.7) (Jones et al., 2024), there may be protective factors among older persons—40 and up—who use drugs who have not experienced death. For these reasons, research is warranted to learn more about people over 40 who present to treatment centers with SUD.

Current research suggests a lack of age-specific treatment strategies tailored for older adults with OUD (Jones et al., 2023a, b). Older people who use drugs are more likely to be parents relative to their younger peers, in which their drug use may undermine not only their own health and well-being but that of one or more children—making even more important the role of successful treatment. Current treatment efforts may not address the unique social, medical, familial, and psychological conditions facing older adults. This gap underscores the need for targeted research to better understand people with OUD over 40 (Han et al., 2015).

Between 2021 and 2022, those 65 and older experienced the largest percent increase in the rate of drug overdose deaths from 2021 to 2022, at 10.0% (from 12.0 to 13.2) but held the lowest rate of overdose per 100,000 relative to younger age cohorts (Spencer et al., 2024). Increases in overdose from synthetic opioids, such as fentanyl were identified between 2013 and 2022 (Spencer et al., 2024). Overdoses from heroin and natural and semisynthetic opioids, which includes drugs such as morphine, oxycodone, and hydrocodone decreased in recent years between 2021 and 2022 (Spencer et al., 2024). In 2021 and 2022, rates of overdose were highest among AIAN persons (Spencer et al., 2024). AIAN persons experienced the largest increase in overdose deaths between 2021 and 22, with the rate per 100,000 increasing 15% from 56.6 to 65.2 (Spencer et al., 2024).

Medication for opioid use disorders

Overall, there have been rising trends in OUD prevalence among middle aged adults as indicated by a significant increase in OUD treatment admissions among those aged 50–60 (Badrakalimuthu et al., 2012; Huhn et al., 2018). In a nationally representative sample, it was reported that 61.7% of persons with OUD who utilize Medication for Opioid Use Disorders (MOUD), including methadone, buprenorphine, or extended-release naltrexone, are 35 years of age or older (Jones et al., 2023a, b). More research is needed to identify the patterns surrounding older persons who use opioid agonist medications and whether they are significantly different in their use of agonist medications from younger persons who use drugs. This would ideally entail identification of risk factors that differentiate opioid use risk among the older and younger cohorts.

SUD treatment – older adults

There are higher rates of comorbid physical and mental illness among older adults in SUD treatment (Badrakalimuthu et al., 2012; Han et al., 2021). There are differences in treatment adherence and medication types between older and younger cohorts. Given the more common comorbidities for older persons, SUD treatment could practice and policy can benefit from a better understanding of patterns and risk factors.

Older adults may experience reduced metabolism and increased sensitivity to opioids which means they are often more likely to be prescribed lower doses or long-acting medications such as methadone (Dufort & Samaan, 2021). It may also mean more difficulty tapering off opioid based medications and side effects that need to be managed. Age-based differences in tolerance levels and health risks also influence the choice and dosage adjustments for MOUDs. The intersection of ageism and MOUD-related stigma also serves as a unique barrier to treatment adherence for older adults. (Han et al., 2024; Wyman et al., 2018; Kepner et al., 2022; Conner & Rosen, 2008).

Insurance for substance use treatment often differs between younger and older individuals, with younger people typically having fewer chronic health issues and potentially accessing coverage through parental plans, while older adults may face more complex coverage needs due to comorbid health conditions and reliance on Medicare or supplemental insurance (Mojtabai & Zivin, 2018).

The transition period from adult to older adulthood (40–60 years) involves evolving treatment needs and challenges with older adults. This period often requires increasingly tailored interventions (e.g. multidisciplinary approach, geriatric principles of care) that address the evolving physical, psychological, and social aspects of both middle-aged and older populations. With perceived physical health tending to decline as people enter these older years and issues of chronic pain become more prevalent (Fayaz et al., 2016; Hopman et al., 2009), there should be additional concerns that overprescription of opioid painkillers among this population may contribute to increased risk for progression to OUD and overdose.

Criminal justice system – older adults

The criminal justice system is facing an aging crisis that mirrors the aging of the baby boomer population in the United States (Rikard & Rosenberg, 2007; Williams et al., 2012). A myriad of healthcare issues follow this trend, including mental health issues along with OUD (Maschi et al., 2012; Rikard & Rosenberg, 2007). Community-based middle-aged and older adults involved in the criminal justice system are at high risk for experiencing co-occurring medical multimorbidity, mental illness, and SUD (Tverborgvik et al., 2023). In one study looking at incarceration history, HIV and chronic pain, distant incarceration and drug-related incarceration history was associated with opioid use disorder and opioid misuse (Lichtiger et al., 2024). Interventions that address all three social and medical risk factors are needed for this population (Han et al., 2021).

Racial disparities in the criminal justice system also exist that interlace, and can exacerbate, social determinants of health. This means that the aging population in the criminal justice system is disproportionately minority; this includes a distinct overrepresentation of at least two high-risk populations experiencing recent increases in harm from the overdose crisis—Black and AIAN persons (Nielsen & Silverman, 2019; Ross & Gould, 2015). There are also notable racial disparities in health outcomes in the United States, making the aging of the criminal justice system an issue that is further complicated by the high prevalence of many physical and behavioral health issues among this population (Binswanger et al., 2012; Golembeski & Fullilove, 2005; Iguchi et al., 2005; Kovera, 2019; Sugie & Turney, 2017). There are also racial disparities in drug arrests that occurred throughout the opioid crisis (Donnelly et al., 2022). These trends in criminal justice system outcomes mirror the racial disparities in the pace of opioid use issues observed in Black populations in the United States in recent years, as research has indicated that Black Americans have begun to outpace their White counterparts in opioid overdose death rates during this time (Furr-Holden et al., 2021).

Individuals are at elevated risk for overdose following criminal justice system involvement. One study found risk of overdose death is 40 times higher in the first two weeks following incarceration compared to the general population (Ranapurwala et al., 2018). Moreover, duration of incarceration was associated with greater odds of overdose post-release (Lim et al., 2012). A range of risk and protective factors for overdose following criminal justice system involvement have also been identified, including homelessness, older age (associated with increased likelihood), and having children. Furthermore, studies have noted racial/ethnic disparities in overdose risk, with some minoritized groups experiencing differential rates compared to Non-Hispanic White individuals. (Flam-Ross et al., 2022; Krawczyk et al., 2020.)

The current study

The present study uses a novel sample of 357 civilian, non-institutionalized1 adult patients with arrest histories in clinical treatment for SUD in the Midwest, USA between 2021 and 2023 to identify significant relationships between age (under 40 versus 40 and above) and the following health outcomes, specifically (1) overdose; (2) use of opioids; (3) use of opioid agonist medications. We hypothesize that patients 40 years of age and above were significantly different from their under 40 adult peers in terms of history of overdose, opioid use (illicit or misuse) history and prior use of opioid agonist medications. All patients in the study either do not have medical insurance or receive Medicaid and the sample includes a natural oversampling2 of Black and AIAN persons. The sample is derived from a larger participant pool (n = 401) in which we select only those who self-report a history of arrest. The primary objective of this study is to examine how age influences substance use patterns among justice-impacted individuals with SUD.

Methods

Patient data were collected in person over a two-year period at substance use treatment centers through self-report Qualtrics life course history surveys with the assistance of trained research staff. Institutional Review Board approval was obtained at the PIs institution. Patients were selected from a community-based behavioral health agency that manages patients who are uninsured or receiving Medicaid in the county. Inclusion criteria included: active receipt of substance use treatment services, over 18 years of age, uninsured or Medicaid recipient, positive for arrest history. In addition to inclusion criteria of having been arrested, we included covariates for count of misdemeanor and felony charges (which defined the extent and severity of arrest history). Opioid use (lifetime) was positive if the participant reported primary or secondary substance of choice equaled opioids, or if other substances used reported opioid use. The questions asked if the participant had a primary or secondary substance of choice equal to: “Synthetic opioids, e.g., (Oxycontin, Fentanyl, Vicodin)”, Heroin, or Other (fill in blank other opioid). Participants were also asked if they had used (lifetime) opioid agonist medications such as Methadone, or Buprenorphine: Suboxone, Subutex, and if their use was with a prescription, higher or more dosage than prescribed, or used without a prescription.

Life-course history surveys we administered contained scientifically validated measures such as the Kessler-6 (Kessler et al., 2003) and the Adverse Childhood Experiences (ACEs), questions adapted from the Boston Reentry Study, as well as new questions developed in coordination with persons who use drugs and treatment center staff. The Kessler-6 instrument assessed various aspects of mental health using an ordinal scale to ask about past 30-day experience with various symptoms (e.g., During the past 30 days, about how often did you feel … nervous). The ACEs survey asked participants about their childhood experiences with stressors and trauma (e.g., Before you turned 18 years old… Did a parent or other adult in the household often … Push, grab, slap, or throw something at you? or ever hit you so hard that you had marks or were injured? ), providing a count score ranging from 0 to 10 of the number of these stressors experienced prior to age 18. Additional survey questions were developed with insights drawn from focus groups with people who use drugs, SUD based clinicians, recovery support specialists, and administrators and judges working with sobriety, drug or criminal courts. Participants received a $30 gift card for completing the survey. This study was approved by the PI’s home institution (IRB name/number) and holds an NIH Certificate ofConfidentiality.

Logistic regression was utilized to measure age (40 and older) as the independent variable predicting (1) lifetime overdose (0 = No; 1 = Yes); (2) lifetime use of opioids; (3) lifetime use of opioid agonists relative to their under 40 peers. Coefficients were loaded into seven models consisting of the following (1) base model independent and dependent only; (2) demographics (Gender: Male = 0, 1 = Female; race/ethnicity: White, Black, Hispanic, AIAN, Other Non-White); (3) family/childhood/life course (ACEs, mother/father problematic alcohol use history: 0 = No, 1 = Yes; mother/father problematic drug use history: 0 = No; 1 = Yes, age at first drug use); (4) health (Kessler-6; self-rated health, lifetime suicidality/attempted suicide 0 = No,1 = Yes); (5) socioeconomic status (annual income during year prior to treatment, educational attainment, divorced: 0 = No, 1 = Yes; ever homeless: 0 = No, 1 = Yes); (6) criminal justice ever incarcerated: 0 = No, 1 = Yes; count of lifetime misdemeanor convictions, count of lifetime felony convictions; (7) SUD behaviors – full model (multiple treatment episodes: 0 = No, 1 = Yes; reported having both a primary and secondary drug of choice: 0 = No, 1 = Yes).

Models were estimated in a stepwise manner, with each successive variable cluster listed above included at each additional step. This was done to identify where potential mediation and/or suppression effects might be apparent at the variable cluster level. While age differences in outcomes are of primary concern (Model 1), these additional models allowed for identification of areas by which age differences could be explained. Multiple imputation was used to manage missing data, with missing data for participants regressed on measures with completely valid data for all participants (ACE scores, number of misdemeanors, polysubstance use scores, Kessler scores). A total of 5 imputations were estimated and pooled for each measure with missing data, as all but one of the measures had less than 5% missing data.

Results

Over half of the sample of patients in SUD treatment reported lifetime opioid use, however, among patients 40 and up, opioid use was less common (48% relative to 63%) relative to patients under 40 years of age as depicted in Fig. 1.

Descriptive statistics for our sample are described in Table. 1.

Most patients were male and the average age of the patient group was 41. Participant age ranged from 20 to 67 years old and the median age is 39.

Patients, both under and over 40, were economically disadvantaged with average incomes ranging from $10,001–29,999 for the full year prior to entering treatment3. Over 70% of participants reported a history of homelessness.

Among those 40 and above, the most frequently reported primary substance used was alcohol, then cocaine/crack cocaine, then opioids, which accounted for 92% of the reported primary substances among this age bracket. This was relative to alcohol, then opioids, and finally cocaine/crack cocaine among those under 40 years of age reporting primary substance of choice and these three selections accounted for 81% of all primary substance preferences among this younger age bracket. As such, older persons who use drugs preferred a smaller range of primary substances, with fewer reporting benzodiazepines, crystal methamphetamine, hallucinogens, marijuana or other drugs compared to younger persons who use drugs. Age related variation in primary substance preference was notable with 55% of those 40 and above indicating primary preference for alcohol relative to 42% of those under 40 years of age reporting primary preference for alcohol.

Among secondary drug of choice approximately 11% of those reporting secondary drug use selected opioids as their secondary substance of choice for those under 40 and those over 40. No Black or AIAN persons reported opioids as their secondary substance of choice, so the variation by age cohort was due to variation in drug preferences by race/ethnicity. Notably, people aged 40 and above were more likely to report no secondary substance of choice, 26% relative to 12% of those under 40. Among those reporting a secondary substance of choice, the most frequent drug among older persons was marijuana and among those under 40, cocaine/crack cocaine.

More than half of the sample reported having experienced a drug or alcohol overdose, with 59% of those under 40, and 44% of those over 40 reporting overdose history. Over half of the sample reported having a prior suicide attempt or having prior suicidality, but like overdose, history of suicidality/suicide attempt was more prevalent among younger patients (64% vs. 44%). A score of 13 on the Kessler-6 indicates the patient is likely to be experiencing severe mental illness and the mean score for the sample was 12.27 with little variation by age reported. Measures of disadvantage were prevalent with a 71.59% rate of prior homelessness, 65% rate of incarceration (though 100% reported arrest history), over 80% report preference towards at least two drugs, and nearly 90% of the sample has experienced multiple treatment admissions. The mean ACE score was 4.11 with younger patients reporting higher average ACEs (4.49 vs. 3.68) relative to their 40 and up peers. Younger patients were more likely to self-identify as White Non-Hispanic, 72%, and less likely to self-identify as Black, 13%, as depicted in Fig. 2, relative to patients 40 and up, 60% of whom identified as White Non-Hispanic and over 31% identified as Black, as depicted in Fig. 3. Younger and older patients were just as likely (4%) to self-identify as AIAN.

The combination of very high rates of prior homelessness, and population metrics of SUD diagnosis, justice system history, and economic disadvantage make the sample especially valuable given how transient this population can be, sentiment of mistrust towards authority and thereby potential reluctance to participate in research, and general underreporting in traditional administrative datasets.

Discussion

In a sample of adult, noninstitutionalized4 persons with criminal justice system histories in treatment for SUD, we find that persons 40 years of age and older are significantly less likely to report history of overdose (drug or alcohol) and opioid use (heroin, fentanyl, morphine, oxycodone, synthetic opioids-other) relative to their younger peers, though this significant relationship dissipates with inclusion of race/ethnicity and gender, and, age, family, childhood, and life course experiences, respectively. History of overdose and opioid use then does not vary substantively between those aged 40 and up relative to their younger peers when accounting for demographic differences among treatment center patients, however, among older persons existing co-morbidities and health risks could still culminate in greater or unforeseen adverse outcomes for this highly vulnerable aging population, especially when taking into account justice system and incarceration histories which are known to result in diminished health compared to peers who have not been arrested throughout their lifetime. Pain conditions are common among older persons (Reid et al., 2015), which can present risk for addiction stemming from prescription misuse, especially among persons reporting alcohol use disorder or illicit drug use (Chang, 2018). However, we do not find evidence that older persons are more likely to misuse opioids when evaluating prescription misuse as well as illicit use.

Persons 40 years and older were more likely to favor just one substance relative to their under 40 peers, a potential protective factor in their longevity as adults who use drugs. We note, however, that it is possible that due to high risk of lethality among persons who misuse opioids, persons 40 and older may have lost their peers who did engage in opioid misuse to premature mortality. This could contribute to the greater share of older persons who report alcohol as their primary relative to those under 40. This pattern has clinical ramifications for treatment providers seeking to best serve the health and well-being of their patients. Service providers may benefit from carefully considering the comorbidities of older persons who may present with cirrhosis—advanced scarring of the liver—and other medical concerns more prominent among the aged.

Black patients comprised 21.55% of all participants, relative to population estimates of 12.3% Black for this Midwestern county (U.S. Census, 2022). The proportion of patients identifying as AIAN or Black mirror similar trends in the criminal justice system, whereas AIAN and Black persons are substantively overrepresented. The larger portions of AIAN and Black patients relative to their representation in the greater community could also be a manifestation of the social determinants of health, in which these groups tend to experience higher levels of poverty, receive lower quality public services, e.g., inferior public schools and emergency services, and limited access to human capital such as parental professional networks and gains from maternal education. These social determinants of health undermine well-being and contribute to health disparities between Non-Hispanic White persons and AIAN and Black persons. While AIAN and Black patients represented 17% of all patients under 40 years of age, they comprised 35% of all patients 40 years and older. As such, recovery services focused on aging persons in treatment should carefully consider any specific needs or resources to best serve aging persons of color. Resources aimed at long-term recovery may include, for example, the active recruitment and retention of peer-recovery support personnel from communities of color.

Treatment services were spread somewhat evenly by age, with the mean age of SUD treatment patients at 41 years old. Younger and older patients were most likely to report following one of the three substances as their primary substance of choice: alcohol, cocaine/crack cocaine, or opioids. Approximately 40% of older as well as younger patients had preferences towards opioids or cocaine/crack cocaine collectively. While the most commonly reported primary drug of choice was alcohol among younger and older cohorts, younger patients were more likely to report primary preference towards opioids compared to those 40 and older who were more likely to report cocaine/crack cocaine as their preferred substance of choice. Of note, a recent review of 79 studies worldwide reported cocaine to be the most likely adulterated drug which suggests that aging persons who use drugs may still face risk of opioid overdose even if their primary substance of choice is cocaine relative to opioids (Di Trana et al., 2022).

When it came to secondary substance preference, older adults were just as likely to report “opioids” relative to their younger peers, but this variation was limited to those who identified as Non-Hispanic White. Zero Black or AIAN persons selected opioids (natural or synthetic) as their secondary drug of choice, so we are observing an age cohort effect only among White persons. Given the high rates of premature mortality among Black and AIAN persons due to drug and alcohol related causes (Kepner et al., 2023; Friedman & Shover, 2023) we may be observing only the patients who were engaged in lower risk drug use behaviors. A potential protective factor reported among older patients was the higher likelihood of reporting no secondary substance of choice, with 88% of those under 40 reporting a preference for at least two substances and 73% of those 40 and up reporting the same5. Polysubstance use was a significant covariate in Table 3 when estimating opioid use by age. Given the risks associated with polysubstance use, it is telling that older patients were less likely to report preferences towards more than one substance—perhaps a small secret to their longevity as a person who uses drugs but exceeds the expected life expectancy of someone who uses drugs.

While the sheer number of patients reporting opioid agonist medications use was higher as a portion of older patients, their agonist medication use was not significantly different relative to their younger peers. Substance use treatment and harm reduction programs can improve the effectiveness of interventions by tailoring their strategies to address the specific substance of choice among older adults. Educating older adults who use cocaine on the risk of adulteration with opioids and the dangers surrounding opioid use may be an effective harm reduction strategy for older adults who may be opioid naive. It will also be important for prescribers to be aware that misuse of prescription opioids can also set up this potential for an SUD and therefore must be monitored and addressed when needed. Policy makers can allocate resources more efficiently by developing substance specific programs for older adults. Finally, stigma-related campaigns for older adults should consider substance-specific prevention and education campaigns.

Differences by race/ethnicity were observed but differences in opioid use and overdose between AIAN and Non-Hispanic White persons were not significant when regressing opioid use by age and gender. Rather, Non-Hispanic Black patients were significantly less likely to report opioid use or overdose relative to Non-Hispanic White patients. This could tell a story not just of trends, but potentially be picking up social determinants of health such as differences in treatment engagement by race/ethnicity or inequities in overdose mortality, e.g., people of color are more likely to experience death when overdosing and therefore would not be a participant in the study. Notably, while Black persons comprised 13% of patients under 40 years of age, they constituted more than twice that percentage of persons 40 and up (31%) which suggests that culturally specific resources could be quite relevant among aging persons in treatment for SUD. Moreover, the lower portion of under 40 patients among this subpopulation presents an avenue for targeting resources to younger persons of color that may provide earlier intervention for people facing SUD in ways that may be limited. These findings suggest there is opportunity to target earlier interventions to decrease drug related mortality among older adults by engaging them at earlier ages.

Our findings suggest that the apparent protective effect of older age against overdose is not due to age itself, but rather reflects differences in other characteristics between these age groups. While modal self-reported health between younger and older patients was the same, younger patients more often presented with a history of suicidality/suicide attempt(s) and had on average, higher ACE scores. Our findings suggest younger patients may be in more precarious health in some ways, e.g., history of suicidality, with higher risk drug use behaviors, which could explain attrition via premature mortality for these younger patients as they age. More research is warranted to better understand substance use trends among older persons especially in the context of rising trends in overdose among marginalized people of color and shifting opioid use patterns which contribute to premature mortality.

Limitations

The present study is limited by self-reported data which may be subject to recall bias or social desirability effects; these limitations are more pronounced regarding sensitive topics like substance use and criminal history. Geographic limitations and the focus on uninsured or Medicaid-eligible individuals restrict the generalizability of findings. Survivor bias limits the interpretation of age-related findings. Our sample considered arrest history as a proxy for criminal justice system history, while also controlling for incarceration history, and count of misdemeanors and felonies, but this doesn’t capture the broad sectors of the justice system such as government supervision and may overstake justice involvement for those arrested but not charged6. Another limitation of this study pertained to the use of lifetime outcome measures. Use of measures with more discrete observation periods (e.g., past year opioid use) would have provided greater utility, but unfortunately, these data were not available. Additionally, some variables were dichotomous, which may have limited the nuance of certain findings. Persons 40 and older were less likely to report polysubstance use behaviors in descriptive analyses, but polysubstance use was not the focus of our analysis. Polysubstance use served as a control variable which limits our ability to robustly report on this metric. Future research is warranted to examine more closely the descriptive findings surrounding reduced likelihood of polysubstance use among older treatment center patients. Finally, while we oversampled Black and AIAN individuals, their resulting proportions in the sample still limit the depth of analyses for these key populations.

Conclusion

In a sample of non-institutionalized, justice-impacted persons who were uninsured or receiving Medicaid in treatment for SUD in the Midwest, USA, we find significant differences in opioid use by age, with older persons—those 40 and up—less likely to report opioid use. Differences did not remain, however, once taking into consideration user preferences and controlling for race/ethnicity, family, childhood, and life course experiences. Overdose history was also significantly less likely for the 40 and older SUD patient, though this was no longer significant when controlling for patient race/ethnicity. Our results suggest that to better serve patients in treatment for SUD, targeted services and outreach to communities of color and other marginalized persons who use drugs, such as the unhoused—70% of our sample reported history of homelessness—take into account the needs of individual communities to ensure treatment services are reaching those who may be underserved by currently available resources and thereby experiencing overdose mortality when utilizing opioids or other drugs. Finally, as a protective harm-reduction strategy observed in our results, single drug preference, and avoidance of secondary or polysubstance use could act as a harm reduction strategy to increase longevity among persons who use drugs.
