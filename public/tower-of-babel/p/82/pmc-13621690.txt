Bacillus-based probiotic supplementation reshapes rumen bacterial and fungal communities and enhances carbohydrate-degrading functional capacity in weaned yaks
Authors: Yining Xie, Yangji Cidan, Fengbo Sun, Cuomu Renqing, Zhuoma Cisang, Dengyun Wang, Duoji Cideng, Wangdui Basang, Yanbin Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study evaluated the effects of dietary supplementation with Bacillus-based probiotics on growth performance, nutrient digestibility, rumen fermentation, and microbial functional capacity in weaned yaks. Twenty animals were randomly assigned to a basal diet (control group, CON) or the same diet supplemented with Bacillus subtilis and Bacillus licheniformis (probiotic group, PRO) for 90 days. Probiotic supplementation increased average daily gain (P < 0.05) and tended to increase dry matter intake (P = 0.059). In addition, neutral detergent fibre and acid detergent fibre digestibility were improved (P < 0.05), suggesting improved degradation of structural carbohydrates. Rumen fermentation was altered, with increased concentrations of butyrate and isovalerate and reduced ammonia nitrogen, suggesting improved fermentation efficiency and nitrogen metabolism. Microbial analysis showed that probiotics reshaped both bacterial and fungal community structures without affecting α-diversity, indicating selective modulation of key microbial taxa. Notably, the relative abundance of carbohydrate-degrading genera, including Xylanibacter, was increased. Metagenomic analysis further demonstrated changes in microbial functional capacity, as evidenced by increased abundance of carbohydrate-active enzymes and genes associated with cellulose, hemicellulose, chitin, lignin, and starch degradation. Introduction

Rumen development and microbial fermentation are fundamental determinants of nutrient utilization, growth performance and health in young ruminants. In herbivorous animals, the rumen microbiota converts structural carbohydrates from forages and concentrates into volatile fatty acids (VFAs), which supply up to 70% of the metabolizable energy required for maintenance and production. Therefore, efficient rumen fermentation is essential for maximizing feed efficiency and animal productivity. The establishment of a stable and functionally coordinated rumen microbial community during early life is particularly critical, as disturbances in microbial composition or activity may impair fiber degradation, reduce energy supply and ultimately limit growth performance [1, 2].

The rumen microbiome consists of bacteria, fungi, archaea and protozoa that interact synergistically to degrade complex plant polysaccharides. Among these groups, bacteria and anaerobic fungi play dominant roles in the decomposition of cellulose and hemicellulose. Anaerobic fungi physically penetrate plant tissues and disrupt lignocellulosic structures, facilitating bacterial colonization and enzymatic hydrolysis. Bacteria subsequently ferment released sugars into VFAs, microbial protein and other metabolites that directly support host metabolism [3–5]. Consequently, the digestive efficiency of the rumen depends not only on microbial diversity but also on the functional coordination and ecological complementarity among different microbial guilds. Understanding how nutritional interventions regulate both microbial composition and functional capacity is therefore of practical importance for improving animal performance.

Feeding management is widely recognized as one of the primary drivers shaping rumen microbial ecology. In weaned ruminants, concentrate feeding increases the supply of readily fermentable carbohydrates and can enhance growth potential and production efficiency. However, rapid changes in substrate availability may also alter rumen fermentation patterns, reduce microbial stability and increase the risk of digestive disorders. High-concentrate diets have been shown to modify microbial community structure, alter carbohydrate degradation pathways and influence fermentation end products in cattle, sheep and goats [6–8]. These effects are particularly pronounced in young animals, whose rumen microbiota are still immature and highly sensitive to dietary changes. During this developmental stage, microbial imbalance may impair digestion efficiency and subsequently reduce growth performance. Therefore, effective nutritional strategies to stabilize microbial functional organization under concentrate-feeding conditions are urgently needed.

Probiotics have been increasingly used as functional feed additives to enhance nutrient utilization, gut health and rumen fermentation efficiency in ruminants. Among them, spore-forming Bacillus species, such as Bacillus subtilis and Bacillus licheniformis, have attracted particular attention due to their high environmental tolerance, strong enzyme production capacity and antimicrobial activity. Supplementation with these probiotics has been reported to enhance feed intake, nutrient digestibility, antioxidant status and growth performance in various livestock species [9, 10]. In addition, probiotics may promote rumen fermentation by stimulating fibrolytic activity and increasing VFA production [11, 12]. Despite these promising results, most previous studies have focused primarily on animal performance or bacterial community changes, whereas their effects on cross-kingdom microbial interactions and carbohydrate-degrading functional systems remain poorly understood, particularly under controlled concentrate-feeding conditions.

This issue is highly relevant for yaks (Bos grunniens), the predominant livestock species on the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau. Yaks are exposed to harsh environmental conditions, including chronic hypoxia, low temperatures and seasonal forage shortages, which frequently restrict nutrient intake and growth [13]. To support early development, concentrate supplementation is often introduced during the weaning period. However, the abrupt transition from milk to solid feed combined with concentrate feeding may disturb the immature rumen microbiota and reduce fermentation stability [14, 15]. Developing effective dietary strategies to enhance rumen digestion and microbial ecological resilience is therefore essential for improving the productivity of weaned yaks under plateau production systems.

Carbohydrate-active enzymes (CAZymes) provide a direct functional indicator of the rumen microbiome’s capacity to degrade complex polysaccharides. The abundance and diversity of CAZyme families reflect the potential for cellulose, hemicellulose and other carbohydrate breakdown. Integrating CAZyme profiling with bacterial and fungal community analyses offers a microbial functional ecology perspective for evaluating how dietary interventions regulate rumen metabolism beyond simple taxonomic shifts [16, 17]. Nevertheless, whether probiotic supplementation can modulate rumen bacteria–fungi communities and associated carbohydrate-degrading functions under concentrate-feeding conditions has not been systematically investigated in yaks.

Therefore, the present study evaluated the effects of dietary probiotic supplementation on growth performance, rumen fermentation characteristics and microbial functional capacity in weaned yaks under a concentrate-feeding system. By integrating 16 S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing, internal transcribed spacer (ITS) amplicon sequencing, and shotgun metagenomics, together with CAZyme functional annotation and measurements of fermentation parameters and digestive enzyme activities, we aimed to clarify how probiotics regulate rumen microbial communities and carbohydrate-degrading functions and to determine their potential benefits for improving nutrient utilization and growth performance in plateau yaks. We hypothesized that Bacillus-based probiotic supplementation would enhance rumen fermentation efficiency by improving microbial functional coordination and increasing carbohydrate-degrading capacity under the same dietary conditions.

Materials and methods

Experiment design and animal management

The experiment was conducted from June to October 2024 on the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau (approximately 3,335 m above sea level; 29°45′–30°08′ N, 90°51′–91°28′ E). A total of twenty healthy one-year-old male yaks with similar body condition were obtained from the experimental farm of the Tibet Academy of Agricultural and Animal Husbandry Sciences and had an average initial body weight of 55.73 ± 1.58 kg. Animals were stratified by initial body weight and randomly assigned to two treatment groups (CON and PRO; n = 10 per group). Each yak was housed in an individual pen and received its assigned diet separately throughout the experimental period. Accordingly, the individual yak was considered the experimental unit for all measurements and statistical analyses. The CON group was housed indoors and fed a basal concentrate-based diet, whereas the PRO group received the same diet supplemented daily with a commercial Bacillus-based probiotic product, BOVACILLUS™/BioPlus® 2B (Novonesis, Copenhagen, Denmark), at a total dose of 1.18 × 10¹⁰ CFU/animal/day. The probiotic product contained Bacillus subtilis DSM 5750 and Bacillus licheniformis DSM 5749. The product was formulated with calcium carbonate as the carrier and kieselgur as an anticaking agent. The probiotic preparation was thoroughly mixed with the concentrate immediately before feeding to ensure uniform intake and was stored at 4 °C before use. The viable count, strain information, and carrier composition were based on the manufacturer’s product information and publicly available assessments of the corresponding Bacillus-based probiotic product, and independent verification of viable cell counts before administration was not performed. The experimental period lasted 90 days. All animals were housed indoors and fed twice daily at 09:00 and 16:00. The probiotic preparation was thoroughly mixed with the concentrate to ensure uniform intake. All animals had free access to fresh drinking water throughout the experiment. Feed intake, behavior, and fecal conditions were monitored daily, and no clinical signs of disease were observed. Body weight was recorded at the beginning and end of the trial before the morning feeding.

Feed and fecal sample collection and analysis

During days 85–90 of the experiment (5 consecutive days), individual dry matter intake (DMI) was determined based on the difference between the amount of total mixed ration (TMR) offered and refused, expressed on a dry matter (DM) basis as average daily intake per animal. Fresh TMR samples were collected daily at 08:00, 15:00, and 22:00 and combined at a ratio of 4:3:3 to form a representative daily sample. After five days, samples were pooled and subsampled (~ 250 g) using the quartering method. Refused feed was collected and weighed daily at 23:00. Feed samples were dried at 65 °C for 12 h, ground (Cyclotec 1093, FOSS, Denmark), passed through a 1 mm sieve, and stored at room temperature. Fecal samples were collected directly from the rectum immediately after defecation at 8 h intervals (08:30, 16:30, and 00:30) over the same 5-day period. Approximately 50 g was collected per sampling, resulting in ~ 750 g per animal. Samples were stored at − 20 °C until analysis. Feces were thawed, dried at 65 °C for 72 h, ground, and sieved (1 mm) prior to analysis.

The DM content was determined according to AOAC (2016; method 930.15). Crude protein (CP; N × 6.25) was measured using the Kjeldahl method [18]. Ether extract (EE) was determined according to AOAC (2016; method 920.39). Neutral detergent fiber (NDF) and acid detergent fiber (ADF) were analyzed, following previous studies [19]) using an Ankom 220 fiber analyzer. Ash content was determined by combustion at 550 °C for 8 h (AOAC, 2016; method 942.05). Apparent total tract digestibility was estimated using acid-insoluble ash (AIA) as an internal marker, following previous studies [20].

Blood and rumen fluid sample collection

On the last day of the experiment, blood and rumen fluid samples were collected sequentially before the morning feeding. Approximately 10 mL of blood was obtained from the jugular vein of each yak using vacuum tubes. Samples were collected either without anticoagulant for serum separation or with sodium heparin as an anticoagulant for plasma preparation. Blood samples for serum were allowed to clot at room temperature for 1 h, whereas anticoagulated samples were processed directly. All tubes were centrifuged at 3,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C to obtain serum and plasma. The separated serum and plasma were aliquoted into microcentrifuge tubes and stored at − 80 °C until subsequent biochemical and antioxidant analyses.

Subsequently, rumen fluid was collected using an oral stomach tube connected to a vacuum pump. The pH of fresh rumen fluid was measured immediately using a portable pH meter. To minimize saliva contamination, the first approximately 30 mL of rumen fluid was discarded. The remaining rumen fluid was filtered through four layers of sterile gauze to remove feed particles. Subsequently, about 30 mL of filtered rumen fluid was collected from each animal and divided into sterile polypropylene tubes. Samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, transported to the laboratory on dry ice, and stored at − 80 °C until analysis.

Serum antioxidant and blood biochemical analysis

Serum antioxidant and biochemical parameters were determined using commercial colorimetric assay kits according to the manufacturers’ protocols. Antioxidant indices included glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px, kit no. JN24284X), total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC, kit no. JN24358X), malondialdehyde (MDA, kit no. JN24318X), and total superoxide dismutase (T-SOD, kit no. JN24918X). Biochemical indices included total protein (TP, kit no. JN24507X), albumin (ALB, kit no. JN24503X), globulin (GLO, kit no. JN65499X), blood urea nitrogen (BUN, kit no. JN24213X), alanine aminotransferase (ALT, kit no. JN24365X), aspartate aminotransferase (AST, kit no. JN24366X), triglycerides (TG, kit no. JN24770X), total cholesterol (TC, kit no. JN24772X), and glucose (GLU, kit no. JN24560). These biochemical parameters were analyzed using a Cobas 6000 automated biochemical analyzer (Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany). Kits were obtained from Shanghai Jining Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China), and all measurements were performed in duplicate for each sample.

Rumen fermentation parameters and enzyme activities

Rumen fluid pH was measured immediately after collection using a portable digital pH meter. Samples were filtered through four layers of sterile gauze to remove feed particles. Individual VFAs, including acetate, propionate, butyrate, isobutyrate, valerate, isovalerate, hexanoate, and isohexanoate, were quantified by gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) [21, 22]. Briefly, 100 µL of rumen fluid was mixed with 500 µL potassium hydrogen sulfate solution (500 g/L) and 2-ethylbutyric acid (8.5 mM) as an internal standard, followed by extraction with 1 mL dimethyl carbonate. After vortexing and centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 ℃, the upper organic phase was transferred to GC vials for analysis. GC–MS determination was performed using an Agilent 5977B GC–MS system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) equipped with an HP-FFAP capillary column. VFA concentrations were calculated based on external standard calibration curves. Ammonia nitrogen (NH₃–N) was measured by UV–visible spectrophotometry (UV-2450; Shimadzu, Tokyo, Japan) with absorbance measured at 620 nm [23]. In addition, the activities of neutral protease (kit no. JN24383X), lipase (kit no. JN24761X), α-amylase (kit no. JN24263X), cellulase (kit no. JN24574X), and lysozyme (kit no. JN62598X) in rumen supernatant were measured using commercial colorimetric enzyme activity kits (Shanghai Jining Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Rumen microbial community and metagenomic analyses

DNA extraction

Total microbial genomic DNA was extracted from 20 rumen fluid samples (n = 10 per treatment) using the Mag-Bind® Soil DNA Kit (Omega Bio-tek, Norcross, GA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions [24]. DNA integrity was verified by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, and DNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Wilmington, DE, USA). Extracted DNA was stored at − 80 °C until subsequent sequencing analyses.

16 S rRNA gene sequencing and analysis

The bacterial community was characterized by amplification of the V3–V4 region of the 16 S rRNA gene using the primer pair 338 F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′). PCR products were purified, quantified, pooled at equimolar concentrations, and sequenced on an Illumina MiSeq platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) using paired-end 2 × 300 bp chemistry. Raw sequencing reads were quality-filtered using fastp (v0.19.6) to remove adapter sequences, low-quality bases (quality score < 20), reads shorter than 50 bp, and reads containing ambiguous nucleotides. Paired-end reads were merged using FLASH (v1.2.11). Quality filtering, chimera removal, and clustering into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) at 97% sequence similarity were performed using the VSEARCH plugin in QIIME2 (v2020.2). Taxonomic assignment was conducted against the SILVA 138 database. Alpha diversity indices (Ace, Chao1, Shannon and Sobs) were calculated to evaluate community richness and diversity. Beta diversity was assessed using Bray–Curtis dissimilarity matrices and visualized by principal coordinate analysis (PCoA). Differences in bacterial community structures among treatment groups were tested using analysis of similarities (ANOSIM).

ITS sequencing and analysis

The fungal community was profiled by amplification of the internal transcribed spacer (ITS) region using the primer pair ITS1F (5′-CTTGGTCATTTAGAGGAAGTAA-3′) and ITS2R (5′-GCTGCGTTCTTCATCGATGC-3′). PCR products were purified, quantified, pooled, and sequenced on the same Illumina MiSeq platform. Sequence processing, chimera removal, OTU clustering, and diversity analyses followed the same procedures as described for 16 S rRNA gene sequencing. Taxonomic assignment was conducted using the UNITE 9.0 database with a confidence threshold of 0.7.

Shotgun metagenomic sequencing and analysis

Shotgun metagenomic libraries were constructed using the NEXTflex® Rapid DNA-Seq Kit (Bioo Scientific, Austin, TX, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) to generate 150-bp paired-end reads. Raw reads were quality-filtered using fastp (v0.23.0) to remove adapter sequences, low-quality bases, reads containing ambiguous nucleotides, and short sequences. Host-derived reads were removed by alignment against the yak reference genome from Ensembl Release 104 using BWA (v0.7.17) [25]. The resulting host-filtered clean reads were retained for downstream metagenomic assembly, gene prediction, and functional annotation [26].

Gene prediction and non-redundant gene catalog construction

High-quality clean reads were assembled de novo using MEGAHIT (v1.2.9). Contigs longer than 300 bp were subjected to open reading frame prediction using Prodigal (v2.6.3) in metagenomic mode [27]. Predicted genes with nucleotide lengths ≥ 100 bp were retained and translated into amino acid sequences. To construct a non-redundant gene catalog, predicted genes were clustered using CD-HIT (v4.6.1) with 95% sequence identity and 90% coverage thresholds. Clean reads were subsequently mapped back to the catalog using SOAPaligner (soap2.21release), and gene abundance was normalized as reads per kilobase per million mapped reads (RPKM) to enable quantitative comparisons among samples [28].

CAZyme annotation and functional profiling

Protein sequences from the non-redundant gene catalog were annotated against the CAZy database (v12) using DIAMOND BLASTP (v2.0.13) for functional assignment. Identified carbohydrate-active enzymes (CAZymes) were classified into glycoside hydrolases (GHs), glycosyltransferases (GTs), polysaccharide lyases (PLs), carbohydrate esterases (CEs), carbohydrate-binding modules (CBMs), and auxiliary activities (AAs). Relative abundances of CAZyme families were calculated based on RPKM-normalized values and compared between treatment groups using heatmaps and multivariate analyses. Furthermore, CAZymes were grouped according to substrate specificity, including cellulose, hemicellulose, pectin, chitin, lignin, peptidoglycan, starch, starch/cellulose, cellulose/chitin, cellulose/hemicellulose, and cellulose/hemicellulose/chitin, to characterize carbohydrate-degrading functional capacities of the rumen microbiome under concentrate-feeding conditions with or without probiotic supplementation [29, 30]. Taxonomic assignment of CAZyme-contributing species was performed by aligning CAZyme-encoding genes against the NR database (20241007) using DIAMOND BLASTP, and the taxonomic source of each gene was assigned based on the best-hit result. Species-level contributions to substrate-specific CAZyme categories were calculated according to the RPKM-normalized abundance of CAZyme-encoding genes assigned to each taxon.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as means ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Differences between the two groups were analyzed using an independent-samples t-test. For microbiome, CAZyme family, functional category, and correlation analyses involving multiple comparisons, P-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg correction to control the false discovery rate. Statistical significance was declared at P < 0.05, and 0.05 ≤ P < 0.10 was considered a trend. For high-dimensional microbiome, CAZyme, and correlation analyses, results were interpreted cautiously when they were not retained after Benjamini–Hochberg correction. All figures were prepared using GraphPad Prism (version 9.0; GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA).

Rumen fermentation parameters

Dietary supplementation with Bacillus-based probiotics increased the concentrations of butyrate and isovalerate in rumen fluid (P < 0.05; Table 5). A tendency toward increased propionate concentration (P = 0.065) and decreased NH₃–N concentration (P = 0.083) was also observed in the PRO group compared with the CON group. In addition, the molar proportion of isovalerate was increased (P < 0.05), whereas the proportion of valerate tended to decrease (P = 0.100). No significant differences were detected in pH, acetate concentration, total VFA, or acetate-to-propionate ratio between the two groups.

Rumen enzymatic activities

Dietary supplementation with the Bacillus-based probiotic increased ruminal neutral protease, lipase, and cellulase activities compared with the CON group (P < 0.05; Table 6). Amylase activity tended to be higher in the PRO group (P = 0.086), whereas lysozyme activity did not differ significantly between the two groups.

16 S rRNA sequencing of rumen microbiota

Dietary supplementation with the Bacillus-based probiotic altered the rumen bacterial community structure. Alpha diversity analysis based on the Ace, Chao1, and Shannon indices showed that probiotic supplementation had no significant effect on bacterial richness or diversity in the rumen of weaned yaks (Fig. 1A–C). However, PCoA revealed a clear separation between the CON and PRO groups, indicating that probiotic supplementation reshaped the rumen bacterial community structure (Fig. 1D). At the phylum level, Bacillota was the dominant bacterial phylum (Fig. 1E). At the genus level, Xylanibacter was the predominant genus (Fig. 1F). Differential abundance analysis further showed that probiotic supplementation increased the relative abundances of Xylanibacter and norank_o_WCHB1-41_d, while decreasing the relative abundances of Saccharofermentans, [Eubacterium]_coprostanoligenes_group, NK4A214 group, and Butyrivibrio (Fig. S1A).

ITS sequencing of rumen fungal microbiota

Dietary supplementation with the Bacillus-based probiotic altered the rumen fungal community structure. Alpha diversity analysis based on the Ace, Chao1, and Shannon indices showed that probiotic supplementation had no significant effect on fungal richness or diversity in the rumen of weaned yaks (Fig. 2A–C). However, PCoA revealed a clear separation between the CON and PRO groups, indicating that probiotic supplementation reshaped the rumen fungal community structure (Fig. 2D). At the phylum level, Ascomycota was the dominant fungal phylum (Fig. 2E). At the genus level, Thelebolus was the predominant genus (Fig. 2F). Differential abundance analysis further showed that probiotic supplementation significantly increased the relative abundances of Thelebolus, Naganishia, and Sporormiella, while decreasing the relative abundance of Hyaloraphidiales_gen_Incertae_sedis (Fig. S1B).

Diversity of carbohydrate-active enzymes

After quality control and host sequence removal, a total of 280.78 Gb of clean metagenomic data were retained from the 20 rumen samples, with an average of 14.04 Gb per sample. The clean reads were assembled into 1,453,697–2,217,342 contigs per sample, with assembly sizes ranging from 850.46 Mb to 1.23 Gb. The N50 values ranged from 480 to 597 bp. Functional annotation identified six major classes of CAZymes, including glycoside hydrolases (GHs), glycosyltransferases (GTs), carbohydrate esterases (CEs), carbohydrate-binding modules (CBMs), auxiliary activities (AAs), and polysaccharide lyases (PLs). A total of 694 CAZyme families were identified across the metagenomic dataset. The top 50 most abundant CAZyme families were compared at the family level, showing distinct differences in their relative abundances between the CON and PRO groups (Fig. 3A). Several CAZyme families involved in the degradation of complex plant polysaccharides, including cellulose, hemicellulose, chitin, lignin, and starch, exhibited higher relative abundances in the PRO group. Specifically, GH106, GH28, GH95, GH29, GH115, GH92, GH53, and CE1 were enriched in the PRO group. In addition, GH20 and CE19, which are associated with chitin degradation, were also more abundant in the PRO group. In contrast, GH163, GH13_9, and CE4 showed relatively higher abundances in the CON group. Functional categorization further demonstrated that CAZyme genes involved in cellulose, chitin, lignin, and starch degradation were significantly enriched in the PRO group, whereas those involved in peptidoglycan degradation were significantly reduced (Fig. 3B–H).

Species–function contribution analysis

Species–function contribution analysis revealed that bacteria were the primary contributors to carbohydrate degradation, with Prevotella dominating multiple substrate degradation pathways (Fig. 6). These percentages represent the relative contribution of CAZyme-encoding genes assigned to each taxon rather than the total taxonomic abundance of that genus. Specifically, the relative contribution of Prevotella-derived CAZyme genes to cellulose degradation increased from 10.09% in the CON group to 31.65% in the PRO group. Similar trends were observed for chitin (5.89% vs. 21.40%), lignin (11.78% vs. 35.75%), and peptidoglycan degradation (10.18% vs. 29.03%). For starch degradation, Ruminococcus was the main contributor in the CON group (9.98%), whereas Prevotella became dominant in the PRO group (27.85%).

Discussion

This study evaluated the effects of dietary Bacillus-based probiotics on growth performance, nutrient digestibility, rumen fermentation, serum physiological status, rumen bacterial and fungal communities, and carbohydrate degradation potential in weaned yaks under identical concentrate feeding conditions. Probiotic supplementation increased ADG, improved NDF and ADF digestibility, enhanced ruminal neutral protease, lipase, and cellulase activities, and elevated butyrate and isovalerate concentrations. Moreover, probiotics reshaped rumen bacterial and fungal community structures without significantly altering α-diversity. Metagenomic analysis further showed enhanced carbohydrate degradation potential, particularly for cellulose, chitin, lignin, and starch degradation. These results suggest that Bacillus-based probiotics may improve growth performance by enhancing rumen fermentation efficiency and microbial carbohydrate-degrading capacity.

Improved growth performance is a direct indicator of probiotic efficacy in young ruminants. In this study, the PRO group showed higher ADG and a tendency toward increased DMI, suggesting that Bacillus-based probiotics promoted growth not only by stimulating feed intake but also by improving nutrient utilization. Previous studies have shown that probiotics can improve ADG and feed efficiency in ruminants [31]. The increased NDF and ADF digestibility in the PRO group further supports this interpretation. Fiber utilization is particularly important for yak production because plateau forage resources fluctuate markedly in quality and availability. During the post-weaning stage, the rumen microbiota remains under development and is sensitive to dietary and environmental changes. Therefore, improved fiber digestibility may increase fermentable substrate availability and energy supply, thereby supporting growth. Consistently, previous studies reported that Bacillus supplementation, including Bacillus subtilis and Bacillus licheniformis, improved fiber and dry matter digestibility and feed utilization efficiency, likely through enhanced microbial activity and enzymatic degradation of dietary substrates [32, 33]. Bacillus probiotics may also promote growth by modulating rumen fermentation patterns and microbial composition [34].

The improvement in nutrient digestibility was accompanied by enhanced ruminal digestive enzyme activities. Probiotic supplementation increased neutral protease, lipase, and cellulase activities, with a tendency toward higher amylase activity. Bacillus subtilis and Bacillus licheniformis can produce extracellular enzymes, including proteases, cellulases, amylases, and lipases [35]. These enzymes have the potential to contribute to substrate degradation if they are produced under ruminal conditions. However, the present study did not directly determine Bacillus germination or enzyme secretion within the rumen. Therefore, the observed increases in cellulase and other enzyme activities may reflect both potential direct effects of probiotic-derived enzymes and indirect effects mediated through changes in the indigenous rumen microbiota [36]. The increase in cellulase activity was consistent with improved NDF and ADF digestibility, indicating enhanced structural carbohydrate degradation, in line with previous findings [37, 38]. Increased neutral protease and lipase activities also suggest enhanced protein degradation and altered lipid hydrolysis. Overall, these changes indicate that Bacillus-based probiotics improved the rumen digestive environment and promoted a more active microbial fermentation system [39].

Rumen fermentation parameters further supported the positive effects of probiotic supplementation on rumen function. The PRO group showed increased butyrate and isovalerate concentrations, a tendency toward higher propionate, and reduced NH₃–N concentration. Butyrate is an important energy source for rumen epithelial cells and plays key roles in epithelial development, barrier function, and energy metabolism [40, 41]. Thus, increased butyrate may reflect enhanced fermentation activity and contribute to rumen development in weaned yaks. Previous studies have also shown that butyrate promotes rumen papillae development and nutrient absorption [42, 43]. Isovalerate, a branched-chain VFA derived mainly from amino acid fermentation, can act as a growth factor for certain fiber-degrading microbes; therefore, its increase is generally associated with enhanced protein degradation and cellulolytic microbial activity [44, 45]. The decrease in ruminal NH₃–N, consistent with lower serum BUN, suggests improved nitrogen utilization through enhanced microbial assimilation or reduced ammonia accumulation, which agrees with reports that direct-fed microbials improve rumen ecology and nutrient utilization [46]. Notably, ruminal pH, total VFA concentration, and acetate-to-propionate ratio were not significantly affected, indicating targeted modulation of specific fermentation products rather than broad disruption of overall fermentation patterns [47].

Changes in serum antioxidant and biochemical parameters indicated improved physiological status in weaned yaks. The PRO group had higher T-AOC, lower MDA, and a tendency toward higher T-SOD activity, suggesting enhanced antioxidant defense and reduced lipid peroxidation. Similar effects of Bacillus licheniformis or Bacillus subtilis on SOD, T-AOC, and MDA have been reported previously [48, 49]. Weaning, dietary transition, and plateau environments may induce oxidative stress in young yaks; therefore, enhanced antioxidant capacity may help maintain physiological stability and support growth. In addition, increased serum TP and GLO concentrations suggest improved protein metabolic status and potentially enhanced humoral immune function, consistent with previous findings in small ruminants [50, 51]. The decrease in serum BUN, together with reduced ruminal NH₃–N, further supports the view that probiotic supplementation improved nitrogen utilization efficiency.

Sequencing results showed that probiotic supplementation reshaped the rumen bacterial community without significantly affecting bacterial α-diversity, indicating that probiotics mainly altered specific taxa rather than overall richness or diversity. At the genus level, Xylanibacter and norank_o_WCHB1-41_d were enriched in the PRO group, whereas Saccharofermentans, [Eubacterium]_coprostanoligenes group, NK4A214 group, and Butyrivibrio were reduced. Xylanibacter is phylogenetically related to Prevotella and is commonly associated with xylan and hemicellulose degradation, suggesting that its enrichment may contribute to structural carbohydrate utilization [52, 53]. Because rumen microorganisms ferment dietary carbohydrates into VFAs that serve as major energy sources for ruminants [54], the positive correlations of Xylanibacter and norank_o_WCHB1-41_d with butyrate, isovalerate, and digestive enzyme activities further suggest their potential involvement in enhanced fermentation and nutrient degradation. Conversely, several reduced genera were negatively correlated with fermentation or enzyme parameters, indicating that probiotics shifted the bacterial community toward a composition more favorable for carbohydrate utilization and VFA production.

Probiotic supplementation also altered the rumen fungal community. Although fungal α-diversity was unchanged, PCoA showed clear separation between CON and PRO groups. At the genus level, Thelebolus, Naganishia, and Sporormiella were enriched, whereas Hyaloraphidiales_gen_Incertae_sedis was reduced in the PRO group. However, some detected fungal genera are not typical anaerobic rumen fungi, and their functions remain insufficiently characterized; therefore, their ecological roles should be interpreted cautiously. Moreover, some of these fungal taxa may represent transient microorganisms originating from feed or the housing environment rather than active rumen residents. Rumen fungi contribute to fiber degradation by physically disrupting plant cell walls and interacting with bacteria during lignocellulose degradation [55, 56]. In this study, Thelebolus and Sporormiella were positively correlated with butyrate and cellulase activity, suggesting their potential involvement in improved fiber degradation and fermentation. Therefore, the observed correlations between these fungi and fermentation parameters should be interpreted with caution and should not be taken as direct evidence of causal functional roles. Nevertheless, the bacterial and fungal results together indicate that Bacillus-based probiotics were associated with differences in bacterial and fungal community composition, which may be related to enhanced rumen functional capacity.

Metagenomic CAZyme analysis further suggested an enhanced carbohydrate-degrading potential in the rumen microbiome. A total of 694 CAZyme families were identified, and the CAZyme profiles differed between CON and PRO groups. Several GH and CE families associated with plant polysaccharide degradation were enriched in the PRO group, indicating greater genetic potential for complex carbohydrate degradation. Functional classification further showed higher gene abundance related to cellulose, chitin, lignin, and starch degradation. CAZyme families, such as GHs and CEs, are key functional units for microbial degradation of complex carbohydrates, including cellulose and hemicellulose [57, 58]. These changes were consistent with increased cellulase activity and improved NDF and ADF digestibility, suggesting that enhanced carbohydrate-degrading potential was associated with improved digestive performance. The reduced abundance of peptidoglycan degradation-related genes may reflect altered microbial functional allocation, although this requires further investigation.

Species–function contribution analysis identified the relative contribution of Prevotella-derived CAZyme genes as a major contributor to multiple carbohydrate degradation pathways in the PRO group. After probiotic supplementation, the relative contribution of Prevotella-derived CAZyme genes to cellulose, chitin, lignin, and peptidoglycan degradation increased markedly. For starch degradation, the dominant contributor shifted from Ruminococcus-derived CAZyme genes in the CON group to Prevotella-derived CAZyme genes in the PRO group. Prevotella is one of the most abundant and functionally versatile rumen bacterial genera and can degrade starch, proteins, and hemicellulose [59, 60]. Notably, Xylanibacter, which was enriched in the PRO group, is phylogenetically related to Prevotella and has similar potential for polysaccharide degradation [61]. Therefore, although functional contribution analysis mainly attributed carbohydrate degradation to Prevotella, the enrichment of Xylanibacter may also contribute through functional redundancy or synergistic interactions. The enhanced relative contributions of Prevotella-derived CAZyme genes and related taxa-derived CAZyme genes suggest improved substrate utilization flexibility and optimized functional allocation for carbohydrate degradation, which may partly explain the increased fermentation products, improved fiber digestibility, and enhanced growth performance in the PRO group.

Overall, dietary supplementation with Bacillus subtilis and Bacillus licheniformis improved growth performance in weaned yaks by modulating rumen fermentation, digestive enzyme activity, microbial community structure, and carbohydrate degradation function. Under identical dietary conditions, probiotics promoted a rumen microbial ecosystem with enhanced fermentation efficiency and substrate utilization capacity, thereby improving fiber digestibility, beneficial VFA production, antioxidant status, and ultimately ADG. These findings provide mechanistic evidence from the perspectives of rumen fermentation, microbiota, and functional potential, supporting Bacillus-based probiotics as a nutritional strategy for plateau yak production. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. Rumen fluid samples were collected only once before the morning feeding on the final experimental day; therefore, rumen fermentation parameters, microbial community composition, and metagenomic functional profiles represent endpoint conditions rather than longitudinal dynamics throughout the feeding period. In addition, dry matter intake and nutrient digestibility were determined during the final digestibility trial (days 85–90), and thus may not reflect temporal changes across the entire 90-day experiment. Furthermore, because baseline rumen microbial samples were not collected before probiotic supplementation, the observed differences in microbial community composition represent endpoint comparisons between the CON and PRO groups. Therefore, the present study cannot completely distinguish probiotic-associated effects from the natural maturation of the rumen microbiota during the post-weaning period. Future studies using multi-dose designs, longitudinal sampling throughout the feeding period, functional gene expression analysis, enzyme activity assays, and in vitro fermentation will help clarify the active microbial pathways involved in carbohydrate degradation and provide a more precise basis for nutritional interventions in plateau environments with fluctuating forage resources and environmental stress.

Conclusion

In conclusion, dietary supplementation with Bacillus-based probiotics (Bacillus subtilis and Bacillus licheniformis) improved growth performance and nutrient utilization in weaned yaks under a concentrate-feeding system. Probiotic supplementation enhanced rumen fermentation by increasing butyrate and isovalerate concentrations, improving digestive enzyme activities, and promoting more efficient nitrogen utilization. Furthermore, probiotics reshaped both bacterial and fungal community structures without altering α-diversity, indicating targeted modulation of key microbial taxa rather than global diversity shifts. Notably, the enrichment of carbohydrate-degrading genera such as Xylanibacter and the increased contribution of Prevotella to multiple degradation pathways suggest enhanced microbial functional coordination. Metagenomic analysis further demonstrated that probiotic supplementation increased the abundance of carbohydrate-active enzymes and genes associated with cellulose, chitin, lignin, and starch degradation, indicating improved capacity for complex carbohydrate utilization. Collectively, these findings suggest that Bacillus-based probiotics promote a more functionally efficient rumen ecosystem by enhancing microbial interactions and carbohydrate-degrading potential, thereby improving fermentation efficiency and supporting growth performance in weaned yaks under plateau production conditions.
