Spectral-distortion-suppressed deep learning for fiber sensor photoacoustic microscopy
Authors: Liying Zhu, Xiaoxuan Zhong, Xuanhao Zhang, Huan Cheng, Long Jin, Yizhi Liang, Lidai Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractFiber laser sensors offer significant advantages for photoacoustic microscopy (PAM), including compact size, electromagnetic immunity, and suitability for fast scanning systems. However, its signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) may rapidly degrade when the field of view (FOV) is enlarged. This compromised SNR adversely affects the accuracy of blood oxygen saturation (sO2) derived from noisy photoacoustic signals. To address this problem, a two-stage deep learning framework for fiber laser sensor-based PAM is proposed. The first stage reduces the 3D data to 2D image and suppresses the noises. The second stage integrates the dual-wavelengths images and suppresses the spectral distortion, so that the accuracy of sO2 can be preserved. The network performance is validated using imaging datasets acquired with a conventional high-SNR photoacoustic microscopy system. Results demonstrate that this approach does not only denoise images acquired with the unfocused fiber laser sensor, but also maintains high fidelity in sO2 calculation, addressing a key challenge in fast functional PAM.Keywords: Photoacoustic microscopy, Deep learning, Fiber sensor, Image denoising

Introduction

Photoacoustic (PA) microscopy is an emerging biomedical imaging technology that captures light-induced ultrasound waves to visualize optical contrast [1], [2], [3], [4]. Piezoelectric transducers are commonly used in PA imaging to detect ultrasound signals. However, these sensors typically require tight focusing to achieve high sensitivity, which limits the imaging field of view (FOV) and the imaging speed. In practice, the limited acoustic focal region often restricts the detection range to tens to hundreds of microns, which is insufficient for many applications. Many photoacoustic microscopy (PAM) systems scan both the acoustic and optical foci to achieve large FOV and high sensitivity, leading to slow scanning speed. To achieve large FOV and high imaging speed in one system, researchers have explored new sensing technologies, detection mechanisms and scanning strategies [5], [6], [7], [8], [9]. The integration of new optical ultrasonic sensors with optical scanning represents a potential solution, as they have demonstrated the capability for fast imaging over a larger FOV [10]. However, their detection sensitivity is usually limited, requiring high excitation pulse energy to achieve enough signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) [5]. Another type of fiber-optic sensor, namely fiber laser sensor, can provide improved sensitivity and oxygen-saturation imaging within a 1.2 × 1.2-mm2 FOV [11], but its sensitivity diminishes when the FOV is further enlarged, leading to poor SNR and inaccurate oxygen saturation (sO2) quantification. Achieving accurate sO2 imaging in large FOV, e.g., sub-centimeter scale, with a fiber laser sensor necessitates effective SNR enhancement.

Besides new sensing technologies, deep-learning methods, such as image denoising [12], [13], [14], [15], undersampled images reconstruction [16], [17], [18], [19], resolution improving [20], [21], [22], and motion correction [23], [24], [25], have been explored to further enhance PA imaging. For image denoising in PAM, Zhao et al. [14] proposed a multitask residual dense network (MT-RDN) that can achieve image denoising, super-resolution, and vascular enhancement simultaneously. Liu et al. [13]. proposed a unified photoacoustic microscopy image reconstruction network (UPAMNet) for both PAM image super-resolution and denoising. The UPAMNet shows superior performance in different PAM datasets. He et al. [12] proposed a deep learning method to remove noise from PAM images based on generative adversarial network, and the results show that the proposed method is effective in noise removal and artifacts suppression. While these denoising methods have demonstrated significant efficacy in enhancing SNR, they exhibit two limitations: (1) Their reliance on processed 2D image data inherently restricts access to richer underlying information that could enable superior microvascular extraction and denoising; and (2) Their efficacy and robustness have not been adequately validated for functional PAM imaging tasks, such as quantitative sO2 mapping, where preserving the linear relationship between optical absorption and signal intensity is critical. Consequently, their applicability to comprehensive multi-wavelength functional imaging studies remains uncertain.

Here, we present a two-stage neural network framework that uses 4D spectral PAM volumetric data to jointly address the noise suppression and sO2 distortion problems in fiber-laser-sensor PAM. The framework includes two sub-networks: a dimensionality reduction and denoising network, and a wavelength fusion network.

(1)The Dimensionality Reduction and Denoising Network (DRD-Net): Departing from traditional projections methods (e.g., maximum intensity projection) that are highly sensitive to noise spikes, DRD-Net employs supervised learning to achieve adaptive 3D-to-2D projection and retain more complete information, which provides a significantly more informative and less noisy 2D input for the subsequent stage. The resulting high-fidelity, information-rich 2D representations preserve significantly more microvascular detail than conventional projections, directly addressing the information bottleneck of existing 2D-input methods, yet remain computationally efficient for subsequent processing.

We evaluated the two-stage neural network on in vivo data and compared it with other conventional and deep-learning methods. Our method has comparable noise-suppression performance with other popular methods and much improved accuracy in sO2 measurement. We further demonstrate our method on monitoring hypercapnia-induced cerebral hemodynamic changes in murine models. Results show that the new method does not only achieve 0.08-Hz C-scan speed over a 5 × 8-mm2 FOV, but also achieve superior SNR and accuracy in sO2 imaging.

Methods

Two-stage noise and distortion suppressed deep learning network

Fig. 1. illustrates the flowchart of the proposed two-stage network, comprising DRD-Net and WF-Net. In stage one, the DRD-Net reduces the dimensionality of the volumetric PAM data from 3D to 2D and suppresses random noises in the data. Because the fiber laser sensor has non-uniform sensitivity across the entire FOV, DRD-Net also incorporates a sensitivity calibration scheme to address this problem. In stage two, the 2D images at different optical wavelengths, i.e., 532 nm and 558 nm, are sent into WF-Net to correct the spectral distortions induced in stage one. We generate a ratio map between the two wavelengths as auxiliary guidance for the sub-network. The outputs of the dual-wavelength images were used to calculate the sO2 map with minimal noise and spectral distortion.

The flowchart of the proposed two-stage network pipeline. DRD-Net, Dimensionality Reduction and Denoising Network; WF-Net, Wavelength Fusion Network.

Dimensionality reduction and denoising network

The architecture of DRD-Net is illustrated in Fig. 2. Regarding the dimensionality reduction design, we employ a similar architecture that proposed in [26]. Dimensionality reduction is performed through projection learning modules (PLM), which employ unidirectional pooling layers to implement discriminative features selection while simultaneously achieving dimension compression. In DRD-Net, the PLM is composed of two 3D convolutional layers with 3 × 3 × 3 kernels followed by a unidirectional max-pooling layer. Each traversal through PLM induces an expansion of feature channels and progressive compression along the projection axis. Through sequential processing across four cascaded PLMs, the original PAM volumetric data ultimately collapsed into a plane, as annotated in Fig. 2 (PLM is denoted by the symbol ‘P’ in Fig. 2).

The architecture of dimensionality reduction and denoising network.

To compensate for the non-uniformity in detection sensitivity, a 2D sensitivity map is used to provide additional information. We acquired an image of a piece of uniform black tape across the entire scanning field and use its reciprocal as the sensitivity map. The necessity of this map stems from the fiber laser sensor's intrinsic non-uniform sensitivity across the FOV. This variation leads to a spatially dependent SNR. For functional imaging like sO₂ quantification, which relies on precise signal amplitude ratios, this non-uniformity introduces spatially varying errors, making reliable measurement across the tissue challenging. Therefore, integrating the sensitivity map is essential for accurate functional imaging.

We propose an intensity compensation strategy to apply the sensitivity map to the 2D image, so that the signal magnitudes can be normalized by the local detection sensitivity. The U-Net structure [27] is used to combine the features extracted from the sensitivity map and the PLMs. The encoding pathway employs sequential downsampling stages, each comprising of two 3 × 3 2D convolutional layers followed by 2 × 2 max-pooling. Correspondingly, the decoding pathway utilizes upsampling stages featuring 3 × 3 transposed convolutions for spatial resolution recovery and channel halving. Attention gate structures are adopted to incorporate sensitivity information in the decoding pathway (denoted as symbol ‘A’ in Fig. 2) [28]. These gates dynamically modulate the flow of features based on both the encoded structural content and the spatially varying sensitivity context. By assigning higher weights to regions enriched by the sensitivity map, particularly those with originally weaker signals, the network is encouraged to excavate latent vascular features within low SNR domains. This design helps ensure that even in low sensitivity areas, important structural details are preserved and accurately reconstructed. Final reconstruction is achieved through a 1 × 1 2D convolution layer, yielding enhanced projection images. The network learns to extract and reconstruct the meaningful features of the input, discarding noise through its hierarchical processing and skip connections. This strategy ultimately enables the network to generate structurally rich representations for final reconstruction and suppresses random noises.

Wavelength fusion network

The architecture of WF-Net is illustrated in Fig. 3. This module comprises two identical cascaded fusion blocks. Each block is constructed with paired U-Net branches. For each wavelength channel, e.g., 532 nm, the corresponding U-Net receives tripartite inputs: (1) an element-wise product between the 2D image at this wavelength and its pixel-by-pixel ratio map, (2) the 2D image at this wavelength, and (3) the 2D image from the other wavelength. The ratio map of the 532 nm channel is defined as R532 = I532/ I558, where I532, or 558 denotes the pixel values of maximum intensity projected 2D Gaussian‑filtered image at the designated wavelength. The ratio map of the 558 nm channel R558 is the reciprocal of R532. These ratio maps serve as a cross-spectral feature that enhances the network’s ability to distinguish arteriovenous structures. The tripartite inputs are fused at the input level by concatenating them along the channel dimension.

The architecture of wavelength fusion network.

Through ablation studies, we empirically find that sequentially applying the fusion blocks twice yields much better performance than using it once. Further increasing the number of fusion blocks will escalate the network's parameters, consequently imposing heightened demands on the volume of training data required for effective model optimization. To balance the architectural complexity and data efficiency, we choose a dual fusion block configuration.

Experiments

Dataset collection

To train the network, we acquired paired images with both a fiber laser sensor and a high-sensitivity confocal OR-PAM probe. As shown in Fig. 4a, two laser pulses at 532-nm and 558-nm wavelengths sequentially illuminate the specimen through the confocal OR-PAM probe. Both laser pulses were repeated at 4 kHz, and the time delay between the paired laser pulses is 1 μs. The pulse energy was 150 nJ for each wavelength. Detailed laser system and confocal OR-PAM system specifications are provided in our prior work [29]. The fiber laser sensor was placed between the specimen and the confocal imaging probe with a 1.5-mm distance from the specimen. The photoacoustic waves were recorded by both the confocal imaging probe and the fiber laser sensor [30]. The confocal imaging probe uses a focused ultrasonic transducer and thus provides high-sensitivity data. The fiber laser sensor acquires relatively low sensitivity data, typically reduced by an order of magnitude. The confocal imaging probe, together with the optical excitation beam, was mechanically scanned across the FOV, so that we can acquire paired and co-registered 3D images for network training.

we acquired 16 pairs of dual-wavelength 3D photoacoustic images of the mouse brain (1280 × 1280 A-lines, 3.2 × 3.2 mm² scanning area) and partition them into 1236 paired training samples for each optical wavelength (256 × 256 pixels, 128-pixel stride, excluding non-informative regions). The validation data comprised 5 additional image pairs segmented with a 256-pixel stride, yielding 85 paired samples, while 8 independent images were similarly processed to create a test set of 136 paired samples. To train the DRD-Net, we only need paired datasets from one optical wavelength, thus the total training dataset number was 2472 for stage one training. The second stage training dataset number was 1236.

After training, we applied the network to enhance the image of fiber laser sensor PAM. Fig. 4b shows the optical scanning fiber laser sensor PAM system. To enlarge the FOV, the fiber laser sensor was positioned 4 mm above the sample. The dual-wavelength pulsed laser operated at 200 kHz with 150-nJ pulse energy for each wavelength. The B-scan rate was 160 Hz, and the C-scan rate was 0.08 Hz for scanning a 5 × 8 mm2 area.

Animal preparation

Eight-week-old C57 mice were used in experiments. Anesthesia was induced through inhalation of 1.5 % isoflurane. To maintain body temperature, a 37°C heating pad was placed beneath the animals throughout the procedures. We prepared a cranial window on the skull following the protocol described in prior work [11]. During the hypercapnia experiments, we replaced the inhalation air with 50:50 air/CO2 mixture. Laser excitation energy was maintained within the safe tissue exposure limit regulated by the American National Standards Institute (ANSI) guidelines. All the operations related to animals were approved by the animal ethical committee of the City University of Hong Kong.

Network training

To train the two-stage network, we first train the DRD-Net until convergence, then freeze its parameters and subsequently train the WF-Net. Adam [31] is employed to optimize the networks with 1 × 10−4 learning rate. The network is trained on the Pytorch-GPU environment and implemented in NVIDIA RTX A6000. The DRD-Net and WF-Net were trained for approximately 27 h and 10 h, on single-wavelength (2472 patches) and dual-wavelength (1236 samples) datasets, respectively. These dataset sizes are sufficient for model convergence and robust performance, as evidenced by the high metrics on the independent test set. The total training time is considered reasonable given the complexity of the 3D-to-2D reconstruction task. It is important to note that after training, the inference is computationally efficient, allowing for rapid processing of new data.

Results and discussions

Validation on paired PAM data

To validate the performance, traditional denoising methods including Gaussian filtering and BM4D [33], along with recent deep learning-based approaches such as UPAMNet [13] and IPN [26] were employed for side-by-side comparison. IPN is a deep learning framework designed for 3D-to-2D segmentation tasks in medical imaging. It was included as a relevant baseline due to its relevance to our task of reconstructing high-quality 2D images from 3D PAM data. UPAMNet is a unified network specifically designed for PAM image reconstruction, capable of both super-resolution and denoising. It was chosen as a relevant method dedicated to enhancing PAM images. To mitigate edge artifacts, all deep-learning-based approaches adopted an overlap strategy with average blending. The original data was divided into 256 × 256 patches using a stride of 128 in the scanning directions. During reconstruction, overlapping pixels were averaged to generate the final reconstructed images.

Quantitative results are summarized in Table 1. WF-Net achieves the lowest MSE (0.0053), the highest SSIM (0.7327), and the highest PSNR (23.131 dB), outperforming all other methods. Compared to the MIP method, WF-Net reduces the MSE by more than 55 % and increases SSIM and PSNR by approximately 74 % and 3.55 dB, respectively. When compared to the deep learning method like IPN, WF-Net still shows a clear advantage, with a 23.2 % lower MSE, a 16.8 % higher SSIM, and a 0.942 dB higher PSNR.

From the intensity images, we can see that the IPN method shows obvious non-uniform sensitivity in the entire FOV, i.e., the left side intensity is obviously higher than the right side. Our method incorporates a sensitivity map in the DRD-Net, which serves as the spatial prior for the network to adjust the signal amplitude accordingly. Our two-stage DRD-Net and WF-Net thus achieved superior vascular recovery from low-SNR data and maintained uniform sensitivity in a large FOV.

In terms of functional imaging, we evaluated sO2 accuracy across these methods. Because it is challenging to find the true sO2 in microvessels in vivo, we use the high-sensitivity results acquired by confocal PAM as ground truth for comparison purpose. As presented in Table 1, although deep learning-based approaches demonstrate reasonable capability in reconstructing vascular structures, this does not necessarily ensure the accuracy of sO2. Erroneous computational outcomes often occur due to the highly nonlinear nature of neural network models, as evidenced by the poor sO2 MSE and PSNR performance of deep learning methods like IPN (MSE: 0.0484, PSNR: 13.283 dB), and UPAMNet (MSE: 0.0697, PSNR: 11.805 dB). Non-deep-learning methods like Gaussian Filtering (MSE: 0.0380, PSNR: 14.686 dB) and BM4D (MSE: 0.0428, PSNR: 13.797 dB) also give significant errors in sO2 results. To solve this problem, WF-Net utilizes the signal amplitude ratio between the two optical wavelengths to constrain network outputs and maintain sO2 accuracy. Our two-stage network achieves a low MSE of 0.0159 and a significantly higher PSNR of 18.175 dB. Our method reduces sO2 MSE by 58.2 % compared to the next best method (Gaussian: 0.0380). Furthermore, WF-Net's sO2 SSIM of 0.7681 significantly outperforms all others, confirming its superior ability to preserve both structural and sO2 integrity. To reduce the impact of background noise on sO2 quantification, images were masked using ground truth segmentations before the above quantitative analysis.

We further evaluate the denoising efficacy and sO2 accuracy at different SNRs. The SNR is defined as the ratio of the mean signal intensity to the standard deviation of background noise, expressed in decibels (dB). We acquired murine cerebral images under three laser energy levels (50 nJ, 150 nJ, 200 nJ) and show them in Fig. 6. The SNR values of the raw and processed images are labelled on each intensity image for quantitative comparison. At the lowest energy level (50 nJ, Fig. 6a), the MIP images exhibit extremely low SNR, resulting in poor visualization of vascular structures and almost no discernible sO2 information. UPAMNet achieves slightly SNR improvement over the raw MIP images but it shows much lower performance compared to WF-Net in preserving fine vascular details. In contrast, both IPN and our method (WF-Net) significantly enhance the visibility of vessels. Notably, the sO2 maps reconstructed by WF-Net closely resemble the ground truth, demonstrating superior functional preservation even under severe noise conditions. As the laser energy increases to 150 nJ (Fig. 6b) and 200 nJ (Fig. 6c), the SNR of the raw data improves, and WF-Net consistently achieves higher SNR and more accurate sO2 distributions compared to both the raw MIP and results of other deep learning methods. These results demonstrate that our algorithm effectively extracts vascular structures while maintaining sO2 close to the ground truth. Even under low-fluence conditions characterized by severe signal degradation, our model consistently outperforms the original MIP data and UPAMNet results in preserving both structural and functional fidelity, highlighting its potential for laser energy limited imaging scenarios. Furthermore, this robust performance persists under higher excitation energies.

In vivo fast functional imaging with enhanced fiber laser sensor PAM

A key advantage of fiber laser sensors in PAM systems is their capacity for fast optical scanning. Enhanced by our network, the SNR can be significantly improved without compromising the sO2 accuracy. We conducted in vivo experiments to investigate how acute hypercapnia affects cerebral vessels. The dual-wavelength pulsed laser (200 kHz, 150-nJ pulse energy) provided a B-scan rate of 160 Hz and a C-scan rate of 0.08 Hz for scanning a 5 × 8-mm² area. The skull of the region of interest (ROI) was thinned using a surgical dental drill without penetrating through to the underlying brain tissue.

First, we acquired baseline images under normocapnic condition. Then, acute hypercapnia was induced via letting the mice inhaling high CO₂ gas (50 %:50 % air/CO2 mixture) for 37.5 s, triggering rapid cerebral sO₂ reduction. Last, inhalation gas was switched back to normal air for recovery. Representative images of the relative hemoglobin concentration (CHb) in baseline, hypercapnia, and recovery are presented in Fig. 7a-c. Compared to the MIP and other deep learning methods, WF-Net provides visually clearer and more continuous vascular structures across all conditions. The vessels reconstructed by WF-Net exhibit higher contrast and better background suppression, making even small vessels more discernible. To quantitatively analyze the vascular response, we selected some segments of main arteries and veins (indicated by yellow and blue arrows in Fig. 7a). The change of average CHb in the vessels are presented in Fig. 7d and Fig. 7e. In arterial vessels (Fig. 7d), all methods exhibited a characteristic response: an initial slight increase (1–2 %), followed by a decrease to a trough (-5 % to −7 % at ∼75–100 s), and finally a rebound to peak values post-CO₂ inhalation (7–14 % at ∼175–180 s). In venous vessels (Fig. 7e), the response patterns differed. WF-Net exhibited the intermediate amplitude changes, with an initial peak of ∼2 % at 25–30 s, a trough of −6 % at 125–150 s, and a final peak of ∼1 % at 200–210 s. In contrast, IPN showed higher initial peaks (3–4 %) and shallower troughs (-4 % to −5 %), while UPAMNet and MIP displayed intermediate behavior. Furthermore, for spatial analysis, the white lines in Fig. 7a indicate the paths spanning 620 μm along which the CHb profiles were extracted. Fig. 7f compares the CHb distribution along the vessel path for the four methods. Physiologically, the CHb along one segment of vessel is expected to change smoothly without abrupt fluctuations. Quantitative statistical analysis of the spatial distribution revealed that WF-Net achieved the lowest standard deviation (σ=0.0248) and coefficient of variation (CV=0.1088), indicating superior spatial consistency compared to IPN (σ=0.1187, CV=0.2925), UPAMNet (σ=0.0484, CV=0.1613), and MIP (σ=0.0426, CV=0.2092). Fig. 7f demonstrates these differences visually: WF-Net maintained the smoothest profile, while IPN exhibited pronounced fluctuations with a peak-to-valley difference of 0.54. UPAMNet showed lower fluctuations but still irregular variations. The statistical analysis demonstrated the superior ability of WF-Net to suppress noise and accurately quantify the vessel signal.

Average sO₂ values in the trunk arterys and veins are plotted in Fig. 8c, revealing acute hypercapnia patterns: raw data analysis showed venous sO₂ decreasing from 73 % to 57 % versus arterial reduction from 93 % to 75 %, while the WF-Net reconstruction demonstrated venous decline from 71 % to 60 % compared to arterial decrease from 95 % to 75 %. Because the trunk vessels have relatively high SNR, original data can show reasonable sO2 results. The sO₂ dynamics calculated from these two methods exhibited similar trends, but our method has much less random fluctuations. Following CO₂ inhalation cessation, both arterial and venous sO₂ gradually returned to baseline. This results demonstrate our method can effectively suppress noises and minimize distortions in sO2 measurement. However, UPAMNet showed significant prediction errors during the recovery phase (140–225 s), particularly in vein measurements where it exhibited an unrealistic overestimation of sO₂ levels, reaching approximately 84–85 % compared to the more physiologically plausible 70–73 % range shown by other methods. Additionally, the trends of sO₂ changes derived from IPN-predicted images were less pronounced during acute hypercapnia, with discontinuous sO₂ representation in the predicted vascular structures.

We further demonstrate the performance of our method in low-SNR regions marked with yellow/purple boxes in Fig. 8a. The original sO2 values in the two selected regions are too noisy. Moreover, as shown in Fig. 8d,e, the average value of the original sO2 in region 1 does not exhibit significant changes hypercapnia. In comparison, WF-Net does not only dramatically reduce the noises in the sO2 of the small vessels, but also reveals detectable sO₂ dynamics that agrees with the trend in the trunk vessels. In Region 2, both the results from the original data and WF-Net predictions show similar sO₂ drop in hypercapnia. In the recovery stage, our method shows the averaged sO2 value in the small vessels gradually approach to the baseline level, which agrees well with the trunk vessel results and known physiological responses [11]. However, UPAMNet exhibits significant prediction errors during the recovery phase, particularly showing unrealistic overestimation of sO2 levels in small vessels compared to the physiologically plausible ranges demonstrated by WF-Net and other methods. IPN shows moderate performance with acceptable recovery patterns, while MIP demonstrates a more pronounced decline in sO2 during the recovery phase, potentially underestimating the physiological recovery, which shows that the high noise limits the sO2 accuracy in in vivo functional imaging.

Similar to the CHb profiles, the sO2 distribution along the vessel path, as shown in Fig. 8f, provides additional evidence of WF-Net's ability in noise suppression. The WF-Net curve exhibits a smooth and physiologically realistic sO2 profile, maintaining values between approximately 70 % and 85 % throughout the vessel length with only gradual fluctuations. This is consistent with the expected behavior of sO2 in blood vessels, where changes should be gradual rather than abrupt. In contrast, the IPN method shows dramatic and unrealistic fluctuations, with sudden drops to near 0 % sO2 between 150 and 250 µm, and sharp peaks reaching close to 100 %. The MIP curve displays high noise with rapid oscillations across the entire 0–100 % range, clearly demonstrating the method's susceptibility to background noise and poor SNR. These results clearly shows the advancement of our method in accurate sO2 quantification.

Conclusion

The trade-off between sensitivity and FOV in fiber laser sensor has long hindered the optimal performance of fast functional optical-resolution PAM. Deep learning is an effective approach to denoise the PAM data. However, the high nonlinear operation often leads to distorted sO2 results. Here, we developed a two-stage deep learning network featuring two key innovations: (1) The DRD-Net overcomes the information loss inherent in traditional fixed projections (e.g., MIP), enabling high-fidelity, structure-preserving 2D representations to be extracted from low-SNR 3D volumetric data, which effectively resolving the sensitivity limitation of prior 2D methods; and (2) The use of ratio between spectral data in WF-Net to constrain network outputs, ensuring accurate sO2 quantification. This framework enables adaptive signal optimization that simultaneously enhances sensitivity and preserves sO2 accuracy. Validation on in vivo datasets confirms the superior performance.

The proposed framework, while optimized for fiber-laser-sensor PAM, exhibits significant potential for broader application. The adaptive projection learning of the DRD-Net represents a generic approach for extracting high-fidelity 2D representations from noisy volumetric data, applicable to other imaging systems employing 3D acquisition with various transducer types. Likewise, the physics-informed constraint in WF-Net is directly relevant to any multi-wavelength quantitative biomedical imaging technique. It should be noted that certain components of our current implementation are tailored to the fiber laser sensor. For instance, the sensitivity compensation module in DRD-Net addresses the specific non-uniformity of this sensor. Consequently, applying this framework to alternative transducers would necessitate calibrating for their respective characteristics, which is a promising direction for future research.
