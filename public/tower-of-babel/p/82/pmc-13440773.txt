Case of diagnosis and management of primary intradural extramedullary Ewing’s sarcoma
Authors: Kannah Elangovan, Karpaka Vinayakam Gopalakrishnan, Sathish Anand V.S., Vidhyaalakshmi Sundararaajan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Primary intradural extramedullary Ewing’s sarcoma (IEES) is an exceptionally rare, aggressive central nervous system malignancy. It poses distinct diagnostic and therapeutic challenges compared with osseous Ewing’s sarcoma.Case Description: A 45-year-old female presented with 6 weeks of progressive bilateral lower limb pain. The magnetic resonance imaging revealed a T1-hypointense, T2-hyperintense intradural extramedullary mass from L3–S1 (~2 × 0.5 × 1 cm). Through an L3–L5 laminectomy, the tumor was fully excised under neurophysiological monitoring. Although the tumor extended radiologically to the S1 level, it was accessed through the L3 -L5 laminectomy after durotomy, which provided adequate exposure for dissection and complete excision of the extending intradural lesion. The histopathology demonstrated sheets of small round cells, while the immunohistochemistry showed CD99 and FLI-1 positivity. The patient received adjuvant vincristine, doxorubicin, cyclophosphamide (VAC)/ifosfamide/etoposide chemotherapy, and intensity-modulated radiation therapy. At 12-month follow-up, there was no evidence of recurrence.Conclusion: IEES demands a high index of clinical suspicion and aggressive multimodal treatment. For this patient, surgical decompression and chemotherapy have left the patient disease-free 1 year postoperatively. However, the patient will continue to require careful surveillance for recurrent disease. Further studies are warranted to develop standardized management protocols.

INTRODUCTION

Primary intradural extramedullary Ewing’s sarcoma (IEES) is an extraordinarily rare clinical entity; we were only able to identify 54 cases in the literature involving the central nervous system with a frequency of 1/million/year.[7,2] It is mostly found in young adults (median age 30.2 years; 63% male) and typically occurs in the lumbar spine. IEES presents insidiously with pain in 70%, motor deficits in 50%, and sphincter disturbance in 27% of cases, frequently mimicking schwannoma, meningioma, or ependymoma.[4,7,10] Notably, the prognosis is poor, with a median overall survival of just 14 months; 1- and 5-year overall survival rates are ~80% and 27%, respectively.[1,5,6] Histopathological confirmation with CD99/FLI-1 immunostaining and molecular EWSR1 fusion testing is required to establish the diagnosis.[3,8] Molecular confirmation of an EWSR1 mutation is considered the gold standard for confirming Ewing sarcoma, particularly in uncommon anatomical locations. Unfortunately, EWSR1 molecular testing was not available at our institution because of resource limitations and was not feasible for this patient. Therefore, the diagnosis was established based on the characteristic histopathological features together with a supportive immunohistochemical profile (CD99 and FLI-1 positivity, along with the exclusion of other small round cell tumors by the remaining IHC panel). Here, a 45-year-old female was diagnosed with L3-S1 intradural/extramedullary IEES diagnosed on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and routinely excised through a L3-L5 laminectomy.

CASE DESCRIPTION

A 45-year-old female presented with a 6-week history of progressive bilateral lower limb pain worsened by activity. Her neurological examination was normal. The contrast MRI demonstrated a well-defined, nodular, intradural extramedullary mass (2 × 0.5 × 1 cm) extending from L3 to S1. It was hypointense on T1, hyperintense on T2, and uniformly enhanced [Figures 1 and 2]. With C-arm fluoroscopic guidance and continuous intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring (IONM), an L3–L5 laminectomy and midline durotomy were performed [Figure 3a]. Although the tumor was soft, highly vascular, and adherent to the L3–S1 nerve roots, a complete macroscopic excision was achieved without any significant IONM changes [Figure 3b]. The patient was discharged neurologically intact on postoperative day 2.

Histopathology and immunohistochemistry

The histopathology and genetic diagnosis of primary IEES were confirmed. The macroscopic specimen measured 2.0 × 0.5 × 1.0 cm. The histopathology showed sheets of small round cells with fine chromatin, scant cytoplasm, perivascular pseudorosettes, mild pleomorphism, mitotic figures, and focal hemorrhage [Figure 4a]. The immunohistochemistry (IHC) was positive for CD99, FLI-1, vimentin, and INI-1 and negative for glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), epithelial membrane antigen (EMA) , leukocyte common antigen (LCA) synaptophysin, desmin, myogenin, smooth muscle actin (SMA), CD34, and Myeloperoxidase (MPO)[Figure 4b-d].

The tumor cells exhibited fine nuclear chromatin and minimal eosinophilic cytoplasm with mitotic figures and areas of recent hemorrhage. (×400 magnification). Immunohistochemical staining demonstrated positive expression for (b) CD99, (c) vimentin, and (d) FLI-1. (Scale bar: 100 micrometer for all four images).

Chemotherapy

The patient completed four cycles of vincristine, doxorubicin, cyclophosphamide (VAC) alternating with two cycles of ifosfamide/etoposide (IE), followed by adjuvant intensity-odulated radiation therapy with curative intent. Eleven additional alternating chemotherapy cycles are planned. At 12-month follow-up, there is no recurrence or neurological deficit.

DISCUSSION

IEES is a rare entity and often poses diagnostic ambiguity. As MRI findings are nonspecific, definitive diagnosis requires histopathological confirmation including IHC markers (i.e., CD99, FLI-1, and genetic markers, particularly EWSR1 fusion) [Table 1].[7,8] In this case, molecular confirmation via EWSR1 fusion testing was precluded by resource constraints. Diagnosis therefore rested on histomorphological features supported by a corroborative IHC panel (CD99 and FLI-1 positivity) alongside exclusion of morphological mimics. Although CD99 and FLI-1 co-expression is strongly suggestive of Ewing’s sarcoma, it lacks absolute specificity, and the lack of molecular confirmation represents a limitation of this report, a limitation shared by other reported cases from similarly resource-constrained settings. Complete surgical resection is the primary determinant of outcome, though high vascularity and nerve root adherence frequently impede radical excision.[9] Adjuvant VAC/IE chemotherapy and radiotherapy are universally advocated; however, survival benefit remains limited owing to aggressive tumor biology and propensity for leptomeningeal dissemination, with recurrence observed in 34% of cases [Table 1].[5,6] The 12-month recurrence-free status in our patient is encouraging, but long-term surveillance remains mandatory given the high rate of documented late relapses. Although a multidisciplinary, individualized approach is the current standard of care, prospective registries and collaborative studies are needed to better define optimal future therapeutic sequencing for IEES.[10]

CONCLUSION

Primary IEES is a rare and aggressive malignancy that necessitates a comprehensive treatment approach involving surgical intervention, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy. Given its poorer prognosis compared to traditional Ewing’s sarcoma, it is critical to identify future improved innovative therapies and enhanced treatment methodologies.
