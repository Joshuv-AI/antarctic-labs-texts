Asparaginase-Based Treatment Modifications and Their Effect on Pediatric Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia Outcomes: A Single-Center Experience
Authors: Fatma Burçin Kurtipek, Dilek Kaçar, Turan Bayhan, Ayça Koca Yozgat, Özlem Arman Bilir, Namık Yaşar Özbek, Hüsniye Neşe Yaralı
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveAsparaginase is essential in acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) therapy, but toxicity frequently necessitates formulation switching or discontinuation. This study evaluates real-world patterns of asparaginase use and the frequency and causes of formulation switching, and it assesses the impact of formulation switching and incomplete dosing on survival outcomes.Materials and MethodsWe conducted a retrospective single-center study of 313 children with ALL treated according to Berlin-Frankfurt-Munster-based protocols between January 2009 and January 2024. Three asparaginase preparations were used: native Escherichia coli L-asparaginase, pegylated E. coli asparaginase, and Erwinia chrysanthemi asparaginase. We evaluated formulation changes or discontinuation, their causes, and their impact on event-free survival (EFS), overall survival (OS), and cumulative incidence of relapse (CIR). To avoid reverse causation, patients unable to complete asparaginase because of an early event (induction death or refractory disease) were analyzed separately. The remaining patients were grouped as having received standard complete treatment, drug shortage or toxicity-related formulation switch with preserved dose, or drug shortage or toxicity-related discontinuation with incomplete dose.ResultsThe median age of the patients was 6.9 years, 58.1% were male, and 86.6% had B-cell ALL while 13.4% had T-cell ALL. Asparaginase formulation change occurred for 78 patients (24.9%), increasing across risk groups (standard: 4.0%, intermediate: 15.4%, high: 42.9%), most often due to hypersensitivity (64 patients, 20.4%). After a median follow-up of 5.2 years, 5-year OS and EFS for the whole cohort were 87.4% and 81.6%, respectively. Among 302 evaluable patients, 5-year EFS was 83.1% in the standard complete treatment group, 93.8% in the formulation-switch group, and 69.1% in the incomplete treatment group (p=0.027 for three-group comparison); the corresponding 5-year CIR rates were 13.3%, 2.2%, and 15.9%. Formulation switch with preserved dose was not associated with inferior EFS (adjusted hazard ratio [HR]: 0.23, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.07-0.77), and discontinuation showed a numerically lower EFS that was not statistically significant (HR: 1.03, 95% CI: 0.36-2.91).ConclusionFormulation switching with preserved total cumulative exposure did not compromise outcomes, supporting the safety of substituting alternative preparations to maintain asparaginase exposure. Incomplete treatment showed a nonsignificant trend toward inferior EFS that warrants confirmation in larger cohorts. Ensuring access to alternative asparaginase formulations is critical, particularly where drug supply is constrained.

Introduction

Acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) is the most common pediatric malignancy, accounting for approximately 75% of pediatric leukemias [1]. Since its introduction in the 1960s, asparaginase has remained a cornerstone of ALL therapy [2]. By catalyzing the hydrolysis of circulating asparagine to aspartic acid and ammonia, asparaginase effectively depletes plasma asparagine, leading to the selective death of leukemic cells while sparing normal tissues with intact asparagine synthetic capacity [3].

Multiple asparaginase preparations are currently used in clinical practice, each with distinct pharmacokinetic and immunogenic profiles. Native Escherichia coli L-asparaginase exhibits high enzymatic activity but has a relatively short half-life of 1.2-1.4 days and significant immunogenicity. Polyethylene glycol (PEG)-conjugated E. coli asparaginase (PEG-asparaginase) offers an extended half-life of approximately 5-7 days and reduced immunogenicity, allowing for less frequent dosing. For patients who develop hypersensitivity reactions to E. coli-derived preparations, Erwinia chrysanthemi derivate asparaginase (Erwinase) serves as a non-cross-reactive alternative, though it requires more frequent administration due to its shorter half-life of 0.65 days [4].

Despite its established efficacy, asparaginase therapy is associated with a considerable burden of treatment-related toxicities that often necessitate formulation modification or discontinuation [5]. Hypersensitivity reactions, the most common immunological adverse events, occur more frequently with native formulations [6]. Furthermore, asparaginase can lead to serious and potentially fatal complications, including pancreatitis, hepatotoxicity, thrombotic events, and metabolic disturbances such as hyperglycemia and hypertriglyceridemia [7].In the context of evolving formulations and ongoing availability challenges, evaluating real-world utilization and clinical outcomes remains essential. However, data on patients requiring multiple formulation switches or toxicity-driven treatment modifications are limited [8, 9, 10].

In this single-center retrospective study, we evaluated real-world patterns of asparaginase use and the frequency and causes of formulation switching and assessed the impact of formulation switching and incomplete dosing on survival outcomes. We distinguished these modifications from incomplete exposure resulting from early treatment failure, aiming to avoid reverse causation and to offer guidance for optimal management strategies in pediatric ALL.

Materials and Methods

Patients were treated at a single center: Ankara Pediatric Hematology Oncology Training and Research Hospital until 2019, and thereafter at Ankara Bilkent City Hospital, to which the department was relocated. The clinical team, treatment protocols, and patient records remained continuous throughout the study period. The study was approved by the Ankara Bilkent City Hospital Institutional Ethics Committee (approval no: TABED 1/1547/2025, date: 13.08.2025). Patients aged 0-18 years with newly diagnosed ALL between January 2009 and January 2024, treated according to Berlin-Frankfurt-Munster (BFM)-based protocols, were included. Those initially treated at other centers or with secondary leukemia were excluded.

Treatment Protocol

BFM-based protocols apply risk-stratified treatment based on age, white blood cell count at diagnosis, minimal residual disease (MRD) status, and molecular/cytogenetic characteristics, accordingly dividing patients into standard-risk, intermediate-risk, and high-risk groups. During induction (Protocol IA), native E. coli L-asparaginase was administered at 5000 U/m2 intramuscularly on days 12-33 (eight doses). High-risk patients with T-cell ALL who proceeded to the augmented Protocol IB arm received an additional 12 doses of native E. coli L-asparaginase at 5000 U/m2/day on days 50, 52, 54, 57, 59, 61, 78, 80, 82, 85, 87, and 89, for a cumulative total of 20 doses. During re-induction (Protocol II), L-asparaginase was given at 10,000 U/m2 intravenously on days 8, 11, 15, and 18 (four doses). For high-risk patients, intensive consolidation consisted of treatment blocks, each including a single intravenous dose of native E. coli L-asparaginase at 25,000 U/m2 on day 6; patients proceeding to hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) received three high-risk blocks before transplantation, whereas non-transplant high-risk patients received all six blocks.

In cases of a hypersensitivity reaction to native E. coli L-asparaginase leading to discontinuation, alternative formulations were used according to protocol guidelines and availability. Pegylated E. coli asparaginase (pegaspargase; Oncaspar®, Servier Pharmaceuticals LLC, Boston, MA, USA) was administered at 2500 U/m2 (maximum: 3750 U), given once or twice (2-week interval) depending on the timing of the adverse event. One dose of pegaspargase at 2500 U/m2 serves as a substitute for four doses of unmodified E. coli L-asparaginase. Erwinia-derived asparaginase (Erwinase®, Porton Biopharma Limited, Salisbury, UK) was primarily reserved for patients with hypersensitivity to both of the E. coli-derived formulations and was given intramuscularly at 10,000 U/m2 on alternate days according to protocol-defined dose equivalence. In cases of native E. coli L-asparaginase shortages, whichever alternative preparation was commercially available was utilized.

Definitions

A complete asparaginase course was defined as administration of all protocol-specified doses, with permitted formulation substitutions that preserved the cumulative planned dose. A presenting white blood cell count of ≥50x109/L was used to define high presenting leukocytosis, consistent with the National Cancer Institute/Rome high-risk threshold. Central nervous system (CNS) status at diagnosis was classified according to standard criteria as CNS1 (no blasts in cerebrospinal fluid), CNS2 (<5 leukocytes/µL with blasts present), or CNS3 (≥5 leukocytes/µL with blasts, or clinical CNS involvement such as cranial nerve palsy or an intracranial mass); CNS2 and CNS3 were grouped as CNS-positive disease for analysis. To assess the prognostic impact of asparaginase modification while avoiding reverse causation, patients were classified into four mutually exclusive groups: 1) standard complete treatment, including patients who had completed all protocol doses with the originally prescribed preparation; 2) drug shortage or toxicity-related formulation switch with preserved dose, including patients who had switched from native E. coli L-asparaginase to pegylated E. coli or Erwinia asparaginase while maintaining the planned cumulative dose; 3) drug shortage or toxicity-related incomplete treatment; and 4) incomplete exposure due to an early event that precluded the completion of asparaginase, such as induction death or refractory disease. The fourth group was analyzed separately as these patients’ incomplete treatment was a consequence rather than a cause of the adverse outcome; including them in comparative survival analyses would have introduced reverse causation and immortal-time bias.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are reported as median (range) and categorical variables as frequency (%). Event-free survival (EFS) was defined as the time from diagnosis to the first event (relapse, death from any cause, or secondary malignancy), censored at last follow-up, while overall survival (OS) was the time from diagnosis to death from any cause. The cumulative incidence of relapse (CIR) was estimated treating non-relapse death as a competing risk. Patients undergoing HSCT were not censored at transplant but were followed through transplant for relapse and death; thus, survival reflects the full post-diagnosis course. Survival was estimated by Kaplan-Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. A multivariable Cox proportional hazards model for EFS was constructed including asparaginase exposure group, immunophenotype, risk group, age (≥10 vs. <10 years), sex, presenting white blood cell count (≥50 vs. <50x109/L), and CNS involvement (CNS2/3 vs. CNS1). BFM-based risk groups were used in the multivariable model as a composite prognostic adjustment. The multivariable model was fitted on 300 of 302 evaluable patients with complete covariate data; two patients with missing CNS status were excluded by listwise deletion. All tests were two-sided and p<0.05 was considered significant. Analyses were performed using Python (lifelines package; Python Software Foundation, Wilmington, DE, USA) and R version 4.0 (R Foundation, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Patient Characteristics

A total of 313 children with ALL treated according to BFM-based protocols between 2009 and 2024 were included in this study. The median age at diagnosis was 6.9 years (range: 0.5-17.8), and 90 patients (28.8%) were 10 years of age or older. There was a male predominance (182 boys [58.1%] vs. 131 girls [41.9%]). B-cell precursor ALL was the predominant immunophenotype (271 patients, 86.6%), while 42 patients (13.4%) had T-cell ALL. According to BFM-based stratification, 25 patients (8.0%) were categorized as being of standard risk, 169 (54.0%) intermediate risk, and 119 (38.0%) high risk. A presenting white blood cell count of ≥50x109/L was recorded for 61 patients (19.5%) and CNS involvement (CNS2 or CNS3) for 41 patients (13.1%). HSCT was performed for 47 patients (15.0%). Asparaginase Exposure and Formulation Modifications

All patients received asparaginase as part of the BFM backbone, with native E. coli L-asparaginase as the first-line product for 310 patients (99.0%). Sixty-one patients (19.5%) received pegylated E. coli asparaginase and 31 (9.9%) received Erwinia chrysanthemi asparaginase as a substitute. Overall, formulation change occurred for 78 patients (24.9%), and its frequency increased markedly across risk groups (standard: 4.0%, intermediate: 15.4%, high: 42.9%; Table 2). The principal indication for formulation change was hypersensitivity, recorded in 64 cases (anaphylaxis in 49); less frequent reasons included infection (n=4), hepatotoxicity (n=3), and thrombosis, hyperammonemia, or low asparaginase activity (n=1 each). Reflecting real-world clinical challenges, seven patients faced issues in accessing an alternative formulation. Ultimately, the alternative was obtained for four of these patients, allowing them to receive their planned cumulative doses. In contrast, the drugs could not be obtained for the remaining three patients, resulting in a reduced cumulative dose.

Survival Outcomes

After a median follow-up of 5.2 years (range: 0.1-14.4), the estimated 5-year OS and EFS for the entire cohort were 87.4% and 81.6%, respectively (10-year OS: 84.8%; 10-year EFS: 75.7%), consistent with previously reported BFM results [7].

Eleven patients (3.5%) who could not complete asparaginase because of an early event (induction death or refractory disease) were analyzed separately. The remaining patients were classified as having undergone standard complete treatment (n=220), formulation switch with preserved dose due to toxicity or drug shortage (n=62), or dose reduction due to toxicity or drug shortage (n=20). Among the 302 evaluable patients, 5-year EFS was 83.1% in the standard group, 93.8% in the formulation-switch group, and 69.1% in the incomplete treatment group (log-rank p=0.027 for three-group comparison). Corresponding 5-year OS estimates were 90.0%, 93.7%, and 85.0% (p=0.086), and the 5-year CIR rates with non-relapse death as a competing risk were 13.3%, 2.2%, and 15.9%, respectively. In pairwise comparison, the formulation-switch group differed significantly from the standard complete group for EFS (p=0.036), whereas the incomplete treatment group did not (p=0.17). Overall, 259 patients (82.7%) remained in continuous remission, 33 (10.5%) relapsed, and 39 (12.5%) died; treatment outcomes according to exposure group are presented in Table 3. Notably, for 17 of the 20 patients in the incomplete treatment group, the missed doses were limited to a single phase of the protocol, and so most had still received the majority of their planned asparaginase rather than experiencing an extensive reduction. Kaplan-Meier curves are shown in Figure 1.

Multivariate Analysis

In a multivariable Cox proportional hazards model for EFS (300 of 302 evaluable patients with complete covariate data; 2 excluded for missing CNS status) adjusted for immunophenotype, risk group, age, sex, presenting white blood cell count, and CNS involvement, formulation switch with preserved cumulative dose was the only variable independently associated with EFS, showing a significantly lower event risk (hazard ratio [HR]: 0.23, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.07-0.77; p=0.017). Incomplete treatment was not significantly associated with EFS (HR: 1.03, 95% CI: 0.36-2.91; p=0.96). The remaining covariates showed only nonsignificant trends, including age of ≥10 years (HR: 1.86, p=0.054), high-risk group (HR: 1.81, p=0.083), white blood cell count of ≥50x109/L (HR: 1.70, p=0.15), and CNS involvement (HR: 1.31, p=0.53). The independent association of formulation switch with superior EFS should be interpreted with caution and is addressed in more detail in the following section. Results are summarized in Table 4.

Discussion

In this single-center real-world study of 313 children treated according to BFM-based protocols, we examined how asparaginase modifications affect outcome, distinguishing formulation switching from true dose reduction. Our central finding is that switching to an alternative preparation while preserving the planned cumulative dose was not associated with inferior survival. On the contrary, these patients exhibited favorable outcomes, although this likely reflects selection effects rather than a true protective effect. In contrast, drug shortage or toxicity-related incomplete treatment resulted in a numerically lower EFS that did not reach statistical significance. Formulation change was common (24.9%) and increased markedly across risk groups (from 4.0% in standard-risk to 42.9% in high-risk patients), most often driven by hypersensitivity, consistent with previously reported rates for native E. coli asparaginase in BFM-based protocols [11]. Representing the institutional continuity of one of the largest pediatric leukemia centers in Turkey, this cohort provides the most comprehensive, single-center real-world data reflecting local asparaginase tolerance patterns and treatment modifications within the framework of national BFM-based protocols.

A key methodological point distinguishes our analysis from crude comparisons of relapse proportions. Patients often fail to complete asparaginase therapy due to early adverse events. These events include induction death or refractory disease. Including these patients in comparative survival analyses conflates cause and consequence. This introduces immortal-time bias and inflates the apparent association between incomplete exposure and poor outcome. We therefore analyzed these 11 patients separately. In multivariable analysis adjusted for risk group, white blood cell count, CNS involvement, and other prognostic factors, formulation switch with preserved cumulative dose was the only independent predictor of EFS, whereas dose reduction was not significantly associated with outcome.

Our findings align closely with the broader literature once exposure is properly characterized. The Children’s Oncology Group analysis [12] and the NOPHO ALL2008 truncation study [13] both demonstrated that suboptimal asparaginase exposure increases relapse risk, particularly in non-high-risk patients. Importantly, the NOPHO study observed that early discontinuation was associated with inferior outcome, especially when E. coli asparaginase was not replaced by an alternative formulation [13]. These data support our findings that maintaining exposure through formulation switching preserves therapeutic efficacy. Consistent with this, the Dana-Farber Cancer Institute consortium experience [14] demonstrated that children switched to Erwinia asparaginase after E. coli hypersensitivity achieved outcomes comparable to those without allergy, provided that adequate exposure was maintained. The post hoc analysis of CALGB 10403 [15] indicated that early PEG-asparaginase discontinuation negatively impacted outcomes for standard-risk patients (5-year OS of 66% vs. 80%) but had no significant impact in cases of high-risk disease (for EFS, p=0.32). Likewise, in our cohort the effect of dose reduction did not reach significance, consistent with the limited statistical power afforded by a small number of truncated patients. The Japanese JACLS ALL-02 study [16] reached similar conclusions, reinforcing that the antileukemic benefit of sustained asparagine depletion cannot be fully replaced by other agents. Using a survival-based approach, Dos Santos et al. [17] likewise identified the number of administered L-asparaginase doses as a prognostic factor in childhood ALL, underscoring that cumulative exposure rather than the occurrence of a formulation change per se is the key determinant of outcome.

A particularly relevant aspect of our study concerns real-world constraints on asparaginase availability. In several cases, the intended alternative preparation could not be administered due to limited supply of Erwinia or pegylated asparaginase, occasionally resulting in treatment delays or dose omissions. This issue, although infrequently emphasized in cooperative-group trials conducted in well-resourced settings, is highly pertinent to centers facing intermittent drug shortages. Our data suggest that, where alternative formulations are accessible, the switching of preparations is an effective strategy to maintain asparaginase exposure and protect outcome. Conversely, supply interruptions that force genuine dose reduction may carry prognostic consequences. Ensuring reliable access to all three asparaginase preparations should therefore be regarded as a quality-of-care priority.

These observations also underscore the importance of structured toxicity management. Expert frameworks, including Society of Hematologic Oncology state-of-the-art reviews on asparaginase toxicity [18, 19], a European Society for Medical Oncology-Cancer Horizons roundtable on asparaginase toxicity management [20], and Delphi consensus definitions of severe asparaginase-associated toxicities [21] emphasize that hypersensitivity and silent inactivation should prompt a switch to a non-cross-reactive preparation rather than permanent discontinuation and that therapeutic drug monitoring of serum asparaginase activity is the optimal means of confirming adequate exposure [22]. The absence of serum asparaginase activity and anti-asparaginase antibody measurement in our cohort is a limitation in this regard, since clinically complete dosing does not guarantee adequate enzymatic exposure. Silent inactivation may have led to the underestimation of truly suboptimal exposure in some patients.

Study Limitations

Several additional limitations of this study should be acknowledged. Most importantly, the favorable outcome of the formulation-switch group must not be interpreted as a causal or protective biological effect. Although this association remained statistically significant after multivariable adjustment, it is most plausibly explained by survivorship and immortal-time bias, time-dependent treatment allocation, and selection bias inherent to retrospective analyses: a patient must survive and remain on treatment long enough to develop hypersensitivity and switch formulations, which enriches the group with patients who avoided early events. Although patients with induction death or refractory disease were excluded to minimize bias, residual confounding cannot be entirely ruled out. Readers should therefore not infer that formulation switching itself improves prognosis; rather, our data indicate that maintaining cumulative asparaginase exposure through alternative preparations does not appear to compromise patient outcomes. The retrospective design also carries the possibility of incomplete data capture and unmeasured confounding.

Although diagnosis dates were ultimately available for the entire cohort, the exact date of relapse could not be retrieved for a small number of relapsed patients, who therefore did not contribute to relapse-timing analyses; given their small number and distribution across groups, this is unlikely to have materially biased the comparisons. The dose-reduction group was small (n=20) and most of these patients missed only a limited number of doses; the study is therefore likely underpowered to detect a clinically meaningful adverse effect of dose reduction, and the nonsignificant findings for this group should be interpreted with corresponding caution. The single-center design may also limit generalizability. Risk-group adjustment was aligned with BFM-based stratification, which provides a comprehensive prognostic classification incorporating clinical, hematological, and molecular features, and we additionally adjusted for presenting white blood cell count and CNS involvement. Nevertheless, MRD was not included as an independent covariate, as standardized and validated MRD quantification was not uniformly available throughout the study period; detailed cytogenetic and molecular risk features were likewise incompletely captured. These unmeasured factors represent a source of residual confounding.

Conclusion

In a real-world pediatric ALL cohort, toxicity-related asparaginase formulation switching with preserved cumulative dose did not compromise survival, whereas dose reduction showed a numerically lower but nonsignificant EFS. These findings support the safety of substituting alternative preparations to maintain asparaginase exposure when toxicity arises and highlight the critical importance of ensuring access to alternative formulations, particularly where drug supply is constrained. Larger multicenter studies incorporating therapeutic drug monitoring are needed to define the prognostic threshold of asparaginase exposure and to confirm whether dose reduction independently affects outcome.
