Long term management of refractory angina due to cardiac syndrome X with peripheral nerve field stimulation (PNfS)
Authors: Andrea Perera, Teo Goroszeniuk, Keyoumars Ashkan
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

In 2011 we reported a case of refractory angina secondary to cardiac syndrome X due to Epstein-Barr Virus managed successfully with peripheral nerve field stimulation (PNfS); a novel technique in the treatment of refractory angina [1,2]. This technique is based on the invention of subcutaneous target stimulation, (current terminology “peripheral nerve field stimulation") published in RAPM in 2006 [3]. We have followed-up the patient now for over 10 years and wish to demonstrate the long-term efficacy of the technique previously reported.

Cardiac syndrome X is described by typical cardiac chest pain in the context of a normal coronary angiogram thought to be secondary to microvascular insufficiency. The authors wish to highlight this case as after 13 years of follow-up the PNfS was still efficacious, with diminished efficacy reported by the patient following implanatable pulse generated (IPG) expiration with resumed effect upon replacement.

Refractory angina is defined as stable angina over 3 months in duration due to reversible myocardial ischemia, in the presence of coronary artery disease which cannot be managed with optimal medical therapy or revascularisation therapy (angioplasty or bypass surgery) [4]. A variety of neuromodulation techniques have been trialled for the relief of refractory angina from non-invasive methods such as transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS) to invasive methods such as spinal cord stimulation (SCS) [5]. However, SCS implantation may not be suitable for all patients and exposes patients to risks of spinal intervention (albeit small), a more complex and longer procedure. PNfS refers to a modality of neuromodulation aimed at targeting non-dermatomal peripheral pain receptors associated with an area of pain [[1], [2], [3],6]. The proposed mechanism behind PNfS in the management of angina is based on recognised Gate theory of Melzack and Wall proposed in 1965 which underpins much of neuromodulation. The theory proposes a “gate” at the spinal dorsal horn by which activation of large fibre non-nocieceptive sensory activation closes the “gate” for nociceptive stimuli [7]. It is known that transient PNfS has the ability to elicit sustained analgesic effects over 60 days; suggesting that there is more behind the analgesic effects of PNfS gate Theory alone [8]. Modern imaging techniques such as fMRI have revealed alterations in the response of dorsal column stimulation following PNfS; suggesting that cortical neuroplasticity within the somatosensory cortex may play a significant role in the analgesic effects of PNfS [8]. Other mechanisms that have been reported are changes in the levels of endogenous opioids produced; beta-endorphins, enkephalins and dynorphin all increase [9]. A decrease in levels of sympathetic catecholamines in response to PNfS for angina has been documented and this is also associated with reduced levels of angina due to decreased sympathetic tone and cardiac work load [9]. While much of the work relates to angina, it is hypothesised that the same mechanisms would be applicable to cardiac syndrome X. PNfS typically involves placing an electrode in the subcutaneous tissue at the centre of the perceived pain with the aim of providing a wider area of coverage [[1], [2], [3],6]. PNfS activates the large fibre sensory nerves across the distribution of perceived pain rather than a specific peripheral nerve or the central nervous system as in spinal cord stimulation therefore in theory offering a wider distribution of coverage. PNfS may offer an attractive alternative to SCS for refractory angina in selected patients.

The case describes a 59-year-old female presenting with refractory angina due to cardiac syndrome X due to Epstein-Barr-pericarditis. Her pain was largely central chest pain which radiated into the mandible and both arms. She showed exercise inducible ischemia on exercise tolerance testing and was on maximal medical management. She underwent an insertion of a SCS which was only partially effective targeting right sided chest and back pain only. Despite only a partial effect from her SCS when she fell and fractured her electrode there was a significant rebound in her pain which could not be managed medically. Therefore, we elected at this stage to re-insert her SCS which resulted in an improvement in her right sided chest and back pain; but her pain remained only partially treated. Given this we opted to proceed with a trial of PNfS.

Placement selection of the PNfS was determined by a standardised protocol patients underwent during work up described here. All patients underwent clinical psychologist assessment followed by a 2-week program of pain management strategies and counselling as a standard of care. We then trialled standard external non-invasive test stimulation to confirm the area of pain described by the patient; this involved using an external nerve mapping probe (Neuro-Trace, HDC Corporation) over the areas where pain was experienced. The probe was placed over the skin in this region and stimulation was started at 2Hz and increased until paraesthesia was achieved. This allowed the epicentre of the pain to be isolated. Following this the patient underwent a temporary trial with percutaneous electrodes at the site identified by external neuromodulation. The temporary leads were left in situ for 1 week and if the patient reported an improvement in pain of >50 % we proceeded to permanent implantation. In this case her pain indeed improved by over 50%.

Therefore she subsequently underwent insertion of leads subcutaneously as shown in Fig. 1. At this time a Synergy IPG meant that not all the leads could be used at once and this did not provide satisfactory pain relief, so these leads were removed. A further trial with two further octrodes was undertaken with good effect by using a second IPG; RF renew system (Advanced Neuromodulation Systems Inc; octrodes and IPG). Her angina reduced in frequency and duration from 75 minutes to 15 minutes a day with an 80 % reduction in her pain. However, the two IPG systems were causing some pocket discomfort. At this time, it was possible to integrate the SCS and the PfNS to a single EON Mini system with two dual extension leads. She reported good analgesia from stimulation without the discomfort of two IPG devices. She went on to have two IPG changes (most recently to a St Jude Prodigy IPG); self-reporting each time the IPG expiration due to lack of efficacy most recently over 13 years after initial implantation.
