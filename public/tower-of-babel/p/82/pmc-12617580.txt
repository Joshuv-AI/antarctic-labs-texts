Pattern of care in locally advanced non-small cell lung cancer: A tertiary care experience from India
Authors: Aswin Ravi, Sushmita Pathy, Supriya Mallick, Prabhat S. Malik, Sunil Kumar, Rajeev Kumar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Introduction and Objective:Heterogeneity in locally advanced (LA) Non-Small Cell Lung Cancer (NSCLC) group necessitates a multi-disciplinary approach to its management. Surgery is the standard of care treatment if resectable; otherwise, concurrent chemo-radiation (CCRT) is preferred. This audit aims to analyse various treatment strategies in real-world settings and their resultant outcomes.Materials and Methods:Medical records of patients presented to the lung cancer clinic at our centre from January 2014 to December 2018 were retrieved. Cases with stage III NSCLC were included in this retrospective analysis. Demographic and clinical data, treatments offered with associated outcomes and side effects were analysed. The progression-free survival (PFS), and overall survival (OS) were computed using Kaplan-Meier survival curves. Cox proportional hazard model (univariate and multivariate) was used to assess factors affecting PFS and OS.Results:A total of 396 patients of LA NSCLC were eligible for descriptive demography; 53% of them were >60 years of age and male-to-female ratio was 4:1. The proportion of squamous cell carcinoma and adenocarcinoma were 55.8% and 31.3%, respectively. Survival analysis was limited to 310 patients who received any form of cancer treatment. Following multi-disciplinary discussions, the intent of treatment was decided to be curative in 240 of them. Upfront surgical resection was performed in 28 patients (11.6%) with 22 receiving adjuvant chemotherapy. Pre-operative chemotherapy followed by surgery was offered to 20 patients (8.3%). The majority of patients taken for definitive chemo-radiation (CTRT) were treated by induction chemotherapy followed by concurrent CTRT (46 patients = 19.2%). Sequential CTRT (SCRT) was opted for 30 patients (12.5%). Concurrent CTRT (CCRT) was received by only 12 patients (5%). Neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NACT-including preoperative and induction chemotherapy before definitive radiotherapy or CTRT) was administered to 200 patients (83.3%). Of these, 104 patients (43.3%) of NACT were not amenable to curative local treatment later on due to poor performance status (PS), comorbidities, poor cardiopulmonary reserve or logistic issues. The median follow-up was 30.7 months. Survival was assessed by intention to treat analysis. The median PFS and median OS of the curative intent group were 11.9 months (95%CI = 8.99-14.81), and 30.7 months (95%CI = 24.42-36.98), respectively. Upfront resectable patients had longer survival when compared to NACT followed by surgery. Survival outcomes for NACT followed by CCRT were superior to CCRT or SCRT. Both median OS and PFS were found to be superior with surgery plus adjuvant therapy in comparison to definitive CTRT. Long-term survival outcomes were also better with surgical resection. No significant differences in survival outcomes were observed between CCRT and SCRT approaches, likely due to the limited number of patients in the CCRT group. Overall adherence to treatment protocols was 56.7% and adherence to chemotherapy protocols was 50.4%. PS and reception of intended treatment were significant prognostic factors in multivariate analysis for survival. The median PFS and OS of the patients treated with palliative intent were 6.5 months (95% CI: 4.2-12.7), and 9.1 months (95% CI: 3.1-15.7), respectively.Conclusion:Whenever feasible, curative intent treatment should be offered to fit LA-NSCLC patients. Although stage-wise comparison was not performed, it was seen that survival is longer when tumour is resectable. Homogenous treatment protocols and well-equipped cancer facilities must be in place to avoid deviations from standard recommendations.

INTRODUCTION

Lung cancer is the most common cancer reported globally and the foremost contributor to cancer-related mortality. The incidence is 2.48 million and mortality is 1.82 million according to global figures. India sees 81,748 new cases, and the mortality is 75,031 annually.[1] Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounts for approximately 85% of all new lung cancer cases.[2] Smoking accounts for 85-90% of its incidence.[3] The most prevalent type is squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) among smokers and adenocarcinoma among non-smokers.[4] Extensive imaging and invasive workup are imperative in its diagnosis and staging.[56] The treatment options, prognosis and long-term outcomes differ significantly based on the stage at presentation. Inherent aggressive biology, ignorance of symptoms at the early stages, lack of routine screening and social barriers often result in a delayed diagnosis of lung cancer at advanced stages. The 5-year survival for localised disease is 61.2%, regional disease is 33.5% and metastatic disease is only 7%.[7] Approximately 29% of patients present with stage III or locally advanced (LA) stage.[8] LA NSCLC is highly heterogeneous in which tumour size can vary from T1-T4 and nodal involvement can vary from N0-3.[9] The intent of treatment for LA-NSCLC is curative. However, many patients are not suitable for curative treatment due to age, extensive disease at presentation, poor performance status (PS) and multiple co-morbidities. Comprehensive geriatric assessment (CGA) tools will be useful for assessing cognitive function, functional capacity, nutritional status and co-morbidities in the elderly.[10]

Patients with resectable stage III disease can be managed with surgery along with neoadjuvant or adjuvant therapy. In cases of positive surgical margins, post-operative radiation therapy (RT) is recommended.[1112] In completely resected stage III NSCLC with EGFR mutation who received previous adjuvant chemotherapy or are ineligible for platinum-based chemotherapy, osimertinib targeted therapy is recommended.[13] In unresectable cases, definitive chemo-radiotherapy (CTRT) is the widely practised approach. However, the progression-free survival (PFS) remains 6-8 months.[1415161718] Concurrent chemo-radiation (CCRT) is the standard of care as it improves OS compared to sequential chemo-radiotherapy (SCRT-induction chemotherapy is given before radiotherapy alone) or radiotherapy (RT) alone by nearly 4-4.5% at 5 years, but at the cost of higher rates of oesophagitis.[19] Nevertheless, many patients in real-world settings are not suitable for this approach and receive either SCRT or RT alone. Durvalumab, which targets programmed death receptor ligand-1 (PDL-1) in tumour cells, improves PFS and OS with manageable toxicity in patients with non-progressive disease after definitive CTRT. It counteracts the immune evasion when used for consolidation in unresectable tumours.[20] In low-and middle-income countries (LMICs), only few patients can afford Durvalumab. This retrospective scientific audit is intended to provide information on the clinical stage at presentation, the types of treatments offered, and treatment outcomes of locally advanced NSCLC treated at our centre.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and data collection

This retrospective study was conducted in accordance with declaration of Helsinki and good clinical practices guidelines. Ethical approval was sought from the Institute‘s Ethics Committee (IECPG-343/22.07.2020). Medical records of all LA-NSCLC patients treated at our centre from January 2014 to December 2018 were retrieved. All of them were eligible for demographic analysis. Patients lacking complete treatment details, without histopathological proof of malignancy, small cell lung cancer (SCLC), early-stage NSCLC, advanced stage NSCLC, sarcoma, carcinoid, bronchial tumours, mesothelioma, thymoma and metastasis from other primary tumours were excluded. Patients who participated in clinical trials were not excluded. Figure 1 summarises different diagnoses encountered and the selection of patients for analysis. Studying the pattern of care, and treatment-related outcomes were primary objectives of the study. Sociodemographic and clinical characteristics and assessment of treatment compliance were the secondary objectives.

Patient selection for descriptive and survival analyses

Study outcomes and statistical analysis

American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) 8th edition was employed for staging.[9] Descriptive statistics were used to summarise demographic details, disease characteristics and treatment modalities. The date of diagnosis was defined as the date of reporting of the biopsy. Different treatments offered represents the pattern of care. Patients with favourable performance status, limited comorbidities and adequate cardiopulmonary reserve received curative intent treatment. Surgical resection with adjuvant therapy and definitive CTRT were the treatment options in this group. In the rest of the patients, palliative treatment in the form of short courses of RT or chemotherapy was offered. Response was evaluated based on Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) version 1.1.[21] PFS was calculated from the date of initiation of treatment to date of first disease progression, relapse, or death. OS was calculated from the date of diagnosis to the date of death from any cause. Median survival estimates were evaluated descriptively using the Kaplan-Meier (KM) survival curves with the two-sided 95% confidence interval (CI). Data concerning disease progression and mortality were non-recordable for a fraction of patients. They were censored at time of outcome analysis based on last available data in medical records.

Baseline characteristics were compared among different treatment groups. Mann-Whitney test and student t-test were used for univariate analysis. An unadjusted hazard ratio (HR) with 95% CI was completed to quantify the strength of the relationship between these characteristics and survival outcomes (PFS and OS). All baseline characteristics found statistically significant in univariate analysis (P < .05) were subsequently subjected to multivariate stepwise Cox regression analysis to determine independent predictors of survival outcomes. STATA 15.0 was used for statistical analysis.

RESULTS

Demographic profile and clinical characteristics

Out of 3715 patients who were registered in the lung cancer clinic, 396 LA-NSCLC patients were eligible for demographic analysis. The elderly population (age >60 years) constituted 53% (210 patients). There was a preponderance of males (4:1). Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) PS was ≥2 in 162 patients (40%) and 335 patients (85%) were smokers. A total of 143 patients (36.1%) reported one or more co-morbidities. Cough (78.3%), shortness of breath (57.3%) and chest pain (54.4%) were the most common symptoms. The number of patients who underwent positron emission tomography-computed tomography (PET-CT) and endobronchial ultrasound were 263 and 74, respectively. SCC was the commonest histology (221 patients-55.8%) followed by adenocarcinoma (124 patients-31.3%). Other histologies such as large cell carcinoma and neuroendocrine differentiation constituted 1.7% and the rest belonged to not otherwise specified group (44 NSCLC, NOS patients-11.1%). Molecular testing for EGFR, ALK, ROS-1 and PDL-1 was carried out in 39, 39, 13, and 4 patients, respectively. Among them, the mutation was detected in 9, 4, none and 2 patients, respectively. Nodal involvement was characterised as follows; N0 in 15.9%, N1 in 5.1%, N2 in 54% and N3 in 25%. Stage IIIB (200 patients-50.5%) and IIIA (115 patients-29%) constituted the most, rest (81 patients-20.5%) belonged to stage IIIC. The demographic profile and clinical data are summarised in Table 1. After excluding 86 patients who did not receive any form of cancer treatments, 310 patients were included in the outcome analysis. After multi-disciplinary discussion, 240 (77.4%) patients were eligible for curative intent treatment, and the remaining 70 patients (22.6%) were treated with palliative intent [Figure 1].

The curative intent treatment group

Patients included in this group are depicted in Table 2. Upon initial evaluation at multi-disciplinary clinic, they were suitable to receive local treatment. All resectable stage IIIA NSCLC cases were taken for upfront surgical excision (28 patients = 11.6%). Out of the resected patients, 22 received adjuvant chemotherapy for 3-4 cycles. Pre-operative chemotherapy was given for tumour down-staging in 20 patients (8.3%). Lobectomy was the preferred surgical approach (33 patients), followed by pneumonectomy (13 patients) and sleeve lobectomy (2 patients).

The chemotherapy regimen in neoadjuvant, adjuvant and concurrent settings were platinum doublets. In non-squamous histologies, pemetrexed was combined with platinum. Only 118 patients planned for chemotherapy could complete the scheduled number of chemotherapy cycles (50.4%). The term neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NACT) in this study describes the usage of chemotherapy before surgery (pre-operative), RT or CCRT (induction). Reassessment was usually carried out after 3-4 cycles. Among the patients receiving NACT, 104 patients (43.3% of the curative group) remained unsuitable for local treatment and received chemotherapy alone. The cause was attributed to early progression (66 patients) and poor PS or cardiopulmonary reserve (38 patients) Dose reduction was necessary for 2.5% of patients due to poor tolerance.

The radical RT dose was 60 Grays (Gy), and it was delivered by Image Guided-Intensity Modulated Radiotherapy (IG-IMRT). None of the patients received pre-operative RT. Three patients had positive margins post-surgery, but they could not be started on post-operative RT due to poor cardiopulmonary reserve, or logistic constraints. The majority of patients taken for definitive CTRT were treated by induction chemotherapy followed by CCRT (46 patients = 19.2% of the curative group). SCRT was opted for 30 patients (12.5%). CCRT was received by only 12 patients (5%) [Table 2]. Only two patients received consolidation durvalumab for non-progressive disease after CTRT.

The median follow-up was 30.7 months. Survival outcomes were evaluated using an intention-to-treat analysis. The median PFS of the curative intent group 11.9 months (95%CI = 8.99-14.81), and median OS was 30.7 months (95%CI = 24.42-36.98). Median PFS was 35.8 months (95% CI = 0-85.71), and OS did not reach in the cohort which underwent upfront surgery. When pre-operative chemotherapy was given, the median PFS was 25.2 months (95% CI = 21.76-28.64) and the median OS was 51.3 months (95% CI = 23.88-78.72). There was no significant difference in either PFS or OS between upfront resected and cases who underwent surgery after pre-operative chemotherapy [Figure 2]. Local failure (74 patients = 30.8%) was the predominant pattern of progression. This includes 36 patients whose disease progressed loco-regionally (15%). Distant metastases were diagnosed in 52 patients (21.7%).

NACT-> Surgery + adjuvant treatment vs Surgery + adjuvant treatment

The median PFS of patients who received definitive CTRT was 14.3 months (95% CI = 11.22-17.39), and median OS was 34.6 months (95% CI = 25.32-43.88). The median PFS of patients in the induction chemotherapy followed by CCRT group was 16.5 months (95% CI = 12.7-20.29) and median OS was 49.5 months (95% CI = 17.77-81.23). The median PFS and OS associated with CCRT were 6.9 months (95% CI = 1.31-12.49), and 35.7 months (95% CI = 9.41-61.99), respectively. The median PFS and OS associated with SCRT were 12.9 months (95% CI = 9.89-15.91), and 31.4 months (95% CI = 13.72-49), respectively. The PFS achieved with induction chemotherapy followed by CCRT was higher when compared to CCRT and SCRT (HR = 0.46; 95% CI = 0.21-0.99; P = 0.05) [Figure 3]. The PFS was significantly lower in definitive CTRT group of patients when compared to surgical resection group (HR = 1.81; 95% CI = 1.02-3.19; P = 0.04). A trend for OS detriment was also seen in the KM survival curve. However, it was not statistically significant (HR = 1.89, 95%CI = 0.94-3.83, P = 0.08) [Figure 4].

Univariate analysis for PFS identified male gender, ECOG PS ≥2, smoking, co-morbidities, serum albumin and non-reception of treatment as poor prognostic markers. Yet, PS (HR = 4.62, 95%CI = 2.45-8.72, P ≤ 0.001) and non-reception of treatment (HR = 0.31, 95%CI = 0.22-0.44, P ≤ 0.001) were the significant factors in subsequent multivariate analysis. Palliative intent treatment group

This group of patients was treated by either thoracic RT or systemic therapy. The median PFS of the palliative intent population was 6.5 months (95% CI = 4.2-12.7 IQR), and the median OS was 9.1 months (95% CI = 3.1-15.7).

DISCUSSION

The present study provides insights into different treatment approaches offered in real-world settings and the resultant outcomes in LA-NSCLC. Its prevalence in the elderly population (age > 60 years) and men correlates with international data.[1] Smoking remains the single most important risk factor.[3] Among LA-NSCLC, SCC and adenocarcinoma constituted 55.81% and 31.31%, respectively. In a previous study from our institute, 29% of patients presented in locally advanced stages and adenocarcinoma was the most common histology.[8] ECOG PS was ≥2 in 40% patients in this study. Among the frail individuals with poor PS, multiple co-morbidities make the treatment decisions challenging.

Curative treatment was intended for approximately 80% of patients however 22.6% ended up receiving palliative intent treatment only due to either progressive disease, deterioration in PS or other logistics. The other possible reasons for decreased utilisation of curative intent treatment include inappropriate use of molecularly targeted agents in frail individuals and physician or patient preferences.[22] Relatively higher proportion of patients received palliative intent treatment in this study compared to our previous institutional data.[23] In our study, three-fourths of patients receiving curative-intent treatment underwent NACT. It is primarily due to poor performance status, limited cardiopulmonary reserve, or logistical constraints for local treatments. Adherence to curative treatment was only 56.7%. Just over half the number of patients who were planned for chemotherapy could complete the scheduled number of cycles. As per a previous institutional review, 41.8% of patients received more than three cycles of neoadjuvant therapy.[23]

All upfront resectable tumours were subjected to surgical resection with adjuvant treatment. An improvement in resection rates, pathological complete response rates and median OS with pre-operative chemotherapy were demonstrated in many phase II trials. They highlighted median OS benefit ranging from 3 to 18 months without significant PFS advantage.[2224252627] However, in this study, we did not observe any significant survival benefit with NACT as compared to those operated upfront. However, it is noteworthy that chemotherapy (NACT) was mostly given for borderline resectable disease, or when the patient is not completely fit for the surgical treatment initially. In the era of immunotherapy, this observation should be challenged with neoadjuvant immunotherapy. Nevertheless, the affordability of such expensive drugs is a big challenge in LMICs.

Only one-third of the curative group could receive definitive CTRT and only 5% received CCRT (initially planned for 9.6% of curative). Older patients with poor PS, poor cardiopulmonary reserve and co-morbidities may not tolerate CCRT and SCRT is generally preferred for them.[28] Long waiting periods for initiation may be another possible explanation for fewer patients receiving CCRT as many had progressive disease at the time of RT planning. Such patients were unsuitable for curative radiation. As per previous phase II and III studies, the median OS in patients receiving CCRT ranged from 15 to 29 months, 14 months in SCRT and 9-12 months in patients receiving RT alone.[2228293031] The corresponding median OS results of the current study are in alignment with these figures. Although not statistically significant, a trend towards improvement of median PFS and OS was in the induction chemotherapy followed by CCRT group when compared to SCRT group. This can be attributed to the fact that PS, cardiopulmonary reserve and response to chemotherapy were better in the former group.

Patients who underwent surgical excision achieved significantly higher PFS than definitive CTRT group. Without a stage-stratified estimate, an accurate interpretation of such results is not possible. Nevertheless, similar improved results with surgical excision were also seen in one of the recent multi-centric retrospective analyses.[32] The 4- and 5-year OS are 20% in our study. This is comparable to existing studies quoting 4-year OS of 22%- and 5-year OS ranging between 15.7 and 44%.[33343536]

The radiation dose received in the definitive setting was 60 Gy in 30 fractions. Roy et al.[37] evaluated the efficacy of accelerated hypo-fractionated radiation with concomitant cisplatin-based chemotherapy (AHFx-RT-CT) after NACT in LA-SCC of the lung. After a median follow-up of 15 months, AHFx-RT-CT showed improved response rate, survival, QOL and favourable toxicity when compared to conventional fractionated RT arm. Despite lower biological effective doses, improved outcomes can be related to cisplatin as a radio-sensitiser. The superiority of platinum-based doublet is well established, and in our institute, all patients received the same.[38] In the Pacific trial, 5-year OS and PFS rates were significantly better in the durvalumab immunotherapy arm at the cost of higher grade 3 and 4 events adverse events.[20] Affordability and the study period (2014–2018), which was before or during the approval of durvalumab, could be the reasons why only two patients received it in this study.

ECOG PS ≥2 and non-reception of intended treatment were significantly poor prognostic factors for PFS in the multivariate analysis. Non-reception of intended treatment was the only significant factor associated with inferior OS. This suggests the need of timely initiation of loco-regional treatment and adherence to radical treatment strategies.

The poor outcomes associated with palliative intent treatment underscore the importance of considering curative intent treatment whenever feasible. But at the same time, compliance to the treatment is important. The tolerability of curative treatment is a major issue in unfit patients. Data on the side effects of various treatment modalities were insufficiently documented and, therefore, not presented in this paper.

Even though advanced treatment equipment, techniques and expertise are available in India, most are confined either to large academic institutions or private corporate setups. A large number of referrals to higher academic centres might lead to long waiting periods resulting in significant delays in the initiation of treatment whereas the cost of treatment and financial burden may be a limitation in private centres.[39] Various international guidelines are available for practical implementation; however, due to resource constraints, deviations from them are common in LMICs. It is desirable to issue country-specific guidelines. In LMICs, higher out-of-pocket expenditure has been a barrier to treatment protocol adherence. The current survival estimates suggest that there is a huge need for improvements in cancer treatment in LMICs.

The inclusion of a relatively small number of patients and inherent biases of a retrospective study are downsides of the study. The lack of complete demographic, clinical, treatment, outcome and side effect documentation in patient case record files was a major limitation. Moreover, significant heterogeneity made comparisons between different treatment groups difficult. Role of preoperative immunotherapy is rapidly evolving, but none of the patients in this study received the same. If a sufficient number of patients had received durvalumab immunotherapy, survival outcome of the definitive CTRT could have been better.

CONCLUSIONS

Heterogeneity exists in the population of LA-NSCLC and treatment decisions should be made after multi-disciplinary discussions. Nevertheless, the outcomes are still poor if intended aggressive approach is not received by curative groups of patients. Improving adherence to treatment protocols remains a key area of focus. Homogenous treatment protocols and well-equipped cancer facilities are still an unmet need in LMICs.
