Intracellular iron homeostasis-regulated epigenetic reprogramming contributes to −7/del(7q) leukemia
Authors: Jianan Zheng, Yiman Peng, Xintong Deng, Lanxin Zhang, Zhongwang Wang, Xuelan Chen, He Li, Yanxin Li, Qi Zhang, Liuxian Zheng, Ziyi Qi, Lan Wang, Min Lu, Wenguo Cui, Li Wang, Yu Wu, Weili Zhao, Ting Niu, Chong Chen, Yu Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chromosome copy number variations are poorly understood drivers of human malignancies. −7/del(7q) is common in acute myeloid leukemia, confers a poor prognosis, and is thought to harbor several tumor suppressors. Previously, we identified the histone methyltransferase KMT2C as a tumor suppressor in this region. Here, through a differentiation CRISPR screen in hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells, we find that the mitochondrial iron transporter ABCB8 is essential for their differentiation. ABCB8 deficiency accelerates leukemogenesis in vivo and disrupts iron homeostasis, reducing cytoplasmic iron availability and impairing iron-dependent enzymes, including the histone demethylase KDM6A. Consequently, ABCB8 loss elevates H3K27me3 levels, repressing differentiation genes in an iron- and KDM6A-dependent manner. Notably, ABCB8 and KMT2C, neighboring genes on 7q, cooperatively regulate H3K27me3 to suppress leukemogenesis. Our findings reveal ABCB8 as a tumor suppressor in −7/del(7q) acute myeloid leukemia and uncover an epigenetic collaboration between neighboring tumor suppressors, driven by iron-mediated chromatin remodeling.

Introduction

Somatic copy number alterations (CNAs) are a hallmark of cancer1,2 and occur as frequently as single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) in cancers. Recent work shows that over 70% of cancers harbor CNAs, with some lacking detectable driver SNVs altogether3. Unlike SNVs, which typically cause loss- or gain-of-function mutations in individual genes, CNAs often involve the amplification or deletion of larger chromosomal segments or even entire alleles. Examples include chromosome 17p deletions, monosomy 7, and 7q deletions3–6. These alterations can lead to the co-deletion or co-amplification of hundreds of genes. Cancers with such chromosomal CNAs are frequently associated with aggressive progression, drug resistance, and poor clinical outcomes7,8. For instance, acute myeloid leukemia (AML) with loss of chromosome 7 or del(7q) is classified as high-risk with an adverse prognosis8,9. Therefore, elucidating the molecular mechanisms by which CNAs drive tumor initiation and progression is crucial for advancing cancer research and therapy.

Studies from multiple groups have demonstrated that frequently deleted chromosomal regions are enriched in tumor suppressor genes (TSGs)7,10–12. For example, several TSGs, including EIF5A, PHF23, and ALOX15B, were recently identified on chromosome 17p, one of the most commonly deleted regions in human cancers and home to the well-known tumor suppressor gene TP537,13–15. These studies not only highlight the complexity of the mechanisms by which chromosome 17p deletions promote tumor formation but also uncover tumor suppressive networks in cancers, such as the polyamine-hypusine axis, the PHF23-SIN3-HDAC epigenetic regulatory pathway, and arachidonic acid metabolism reprogramming.

Interestingly, TSGs located in these deleted chromosomal regions often exhibit rare mutations in cancers. Additionally, some TSGs, such as KMT2C on chromosome 7q36, drive tumorigenesis only when their mRNA levels are reduced by half, suggesting that the remaining wild-type allele is essential for tumor cell growth16. Given that hundreds of genes undergo copy number changes with chromosomal deletions, their collective impact on cancer biology remains underexplored. Further research into these mechanisms is critical for advancing our understanding of cancer progression and identifying potential therapeutic targets.

Here, we report the identification of ABCB8 as a tumor suppressor gene on chromosome 7q36. Loss of ABCB8 inhibits the differentiation of hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs) and promotes AML development in mouse models, similar to KMT2C chromosome 7 loss or 7q deletions, which are frequently observed in myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS) and AML and are often associated with poor prognosis17–19. ABCB8 encodes an ATP-binding cassette subfamily B member 8, which is involved in mitochondrial iron transport. Mechanistically, the tumor-suppressive function of ABCB8 is linked to epigenetic reprogramming, likely through its impact on the iron-binding histone demethylase KDM6A. This results in increased trimethylation of lysine 27 on histone H3 (H3K27me3), a key epigenetic mark. Our findings uncover a critical iron-epigenetic regulatory network mediated by the ABCB8-KDM6A axis, highlighting its essential role in cancer biology.

Results

Abcb8 loss prevents HSPCs differentiation

By analyzing the samples from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA)-LAML20 samples, we found that 67 genes, in addition to KMT2C, are highly frequent co-deletions. Among them, 55 are conserved in the mouse genome. Considering that heterozygous deletion of KMT2C or chromosome 7q leads to a differentiation block in hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs)16,21, we evaluated the individual impact of these 55 genes on HSPCs differentiation. This was measured by flow cytometry analysis of the differentiation markers Gr-1 and CD11b in c-Kit+ HSPCs isolated from murine bone marrow (Fig. 1A, Supplementary Data 1). As shown in Fig. 1B, C, two sgRNAs targeting Abcb8 were found to induce a dramatic decrease in the percentage of CD11b+ or Gr-1+ differentiated cells, compared to sgScr controls (Fig. 1B, C, Supplementary Fig. 1A). A similar differentiation blockade in HSPCs was observed with Abcb8 shRNA (Fig. 1D, Supplementary Fig. 1B), supporting that Abcb8 plays a role in hematopoiesis. We further analyzed the transcriptomics of mouse HSPCs (c-Kit+) with Abcb8 or control shRNA. Compared to the control shRen group, genes involved in various differentiation pathways like lymphocyte differentiation, regulation of leukocyte differentiation, and regulation of hematopoiesis were significantly decreased in shAbcb8 HSPCs (Fig. 1E). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) also showed that WP_HEMATOPOIETIC_STEM_CELL_DIFFERENTIATION and KEGG_HEMATOPOIETIC_CELL_LINEAGE gene sets were significantly enriched in control shRen group (NES = −2.05 and −2.10; p.adj < 0.01 and <0.01, respectively; Fig. 1F). Interestingly, from scRNA-seq analysis of bone marrow from 25 healthy human donors22, we found that ABCB8 is mainly expressed in HSPCs and progenitor cells (GMP and MEP), with mRNA levels dramatically decreasing in mature myeloid cells (Fig. 1G, H, Supplementary Fig. 1C). Also, CytoTRACE23, a computational method to predict undifferentiation states of cells according to their scRNA-seq data, was applied to analyze the correlation between ABCB8 expression and cell differentiation. The result shows that ABCB8 is positively correlated with CytoTRACE scores along the HSPC-GMP-monocyte differentiation axis (Fig. 1I). Moreover, we sorted mouse LSK (Lin−Sca-1+c-Kit+), CMP (Lin−Sca-1−c-Kit+CD34+CD16/32−), GMP (Lin−Sca-1−c-Kit+CD34+CD16/32+), MEP (Lin−Sca-1−c-Kit+CD34−CD16/32−), macrophage (CD11b+), and granulocyte (Gr-1+) cells (Fig. 1J) out of bone marrow, and measured the Abcb8 expression by RT-qPCR. As Fig. 1J shows, Abcb8 mRNA levels are much higher in progenitor cells (LSK, CMP, GMP and MEP) than in more mature cells (CD11b+ or Gr-1+ cells). Together, these results suggest a role for Abcb8 in the differentiation of hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells into myeloid cells.

To explore the hematopoietic phenotypes of Abcb8 deficiency in vivo, we performed a competitive bone marrow transplantation assay. As Fig. 1K shows, CD45.1 wild-type c-Kit+ HSPCs were transduced with GFP-linked shRNAs (shAbcb8 #1, #2 or control shRen), followed by transplantation into irradiated CD45.2 recipients. Peripheral blood analysis from recipient mice showed that the percentage of GFP+ cells in the shAbcb8 CD45.1 population was progressively reduced compared to that in the shRen cohort at 6 and 8 weeks after transplantation (Fig. 1L). Of note, myeloid cells (CD11b+ or Gr-1+) in GFP+ cells were decreased in the shAbcb8 group at 6 and 8 weeks post-transplantation (Supplementary Fig. 1D).

We harvested bone marrow from recipient mice at 8 weeks post-transplantation. Although GFP⁺ cell numbers in shAbcb8 groups were reduced in bone marrow (Supplementary Fig. 1E), the percentage of LSK (Lin−Sca-1⁺c-Kit⁺) stem/progenitor cells within the GFP⁺ population was increased (Fig. 1M). Further flow cytometry analysis of LSK subpopulations showed increased proportions of short-term hematopoietic stem cells (ST-HSC, Lin−Sca-1+c-Kit+CD48+CD150+) and long-term hematopoietic stem cells (LT-HSC, Lin−Sca-1+c-Kit+CD48−CD150+) while decreased proportions of multipotent progenitor cells (MPP, Lin-Sca-1+c-Kit+CD48+CD150−) in the shAbcb8 group compared to the control shRen ones (Fig. 1N). These results indicate a myeloid differentiation block induced by Abcb8 KD alone, suggesting that it may have the potential to develop MDS-like features.

Abcb8 deficiency drives leukemia formation

To investigate the role of ABCB8 in leukemogenesis, we first analyzed ABCB8 status in leukemia patients. Stratifying the TCGA-LAML patient cohort by the change in the copy number of ABCB8 demonstrated a shorter overall survival in patients whose leukemias carried ABCB8 deletions (Fig. 2A). Leukemia cells with ABCB8 deletions have a significant reduction of the ABCB8 mRNA level compared to those with intact ABCB8 (Fig. 2B). Given that ABCB8 is often co-deleted with other genes like KMT2C in chromosome 7q deletions, we analyzed the impact of ABCB8 expression level on survival by using the maximally selected rank statistics (maxstat) method, as implemented in the survminer package24. The results showed that low ABCB8 expression level is associated with a poor prognosis in multiple cohorts, including the Beat AML25, GSE14617326 and GSE3764227 (Supplementary Fig. 2A–E). Of note, the expressions of other genes close to ABCB8, like KMT2C or EZH2, are not decreased in the ABCB8 low cohorts, indicating that lower ABCB8 expression may be a poor prognosis indicator (Supplementary Fig. 2C, E). Consistent with our previous results that ABCB8 loss induces HSPCs differentiation block, analysis of the transcriptomics from the Beat AML cohort showed that lower leukemia ABCB8 expression had significantly reduced enrichment of genes involved in several differentiation-related pathways, like hematopoietic cell lineage and myeloid differentiation pathway (Fig. 2C). Gene set enrichment analysis has similar results. The POSITIVE_REGULATION_OF_STEM_CELL_DIFFERENTIATION and STEM_CELL_DIFFERENTIATION gene sets were significantly negatively enriched in ABCB8low AML samples (NES = −1.69 and −1.62; p.adj < 0.05 and = 0.01, respectively; Fig. 2D).

We further investigated whether Abcb8 loss promoted leukemogenesis with a mouse transplantation-based leukemia model. Given the co-occurrence of ABCB8 deletions with TP53 and NF1 alterations in TCGA-LAML (Supplementary Fig. 2F), we introduced shAbcb8 or control shRen, combined with shNf1, into Trp53−/− mouse bone marrow-derived c-Kit+ HSPCs, followed by transplantation into sub-lethally irradiated wild-type B6 recipient mice (Fig. 2E). Recipient mice were monitored regularly. After 10 weeks post-transplantation, mice in the shAbcb8 cohort displayed significantly increased peripheral white blood cell (WBC) counts compared to the control shRen cohort, along with variably decreased hemoglobin (Hb) and platelet (PLT) levels, indicative of leukemic expansion and suppression of normal hematopoiesis (Supplementary Fig. 2G). Consequently, the shAbcb8 cohort had a significantly shorter AML-free survival (median onset 71 days for shAbcb8 #1, 83 days for shAbcb8 #2), while only two of nine mice in the shRen cohort developed AML (Fig. 2F). The other shRen mice developed myeloproliferative neoplasm, as shown by more mature myeloid cells instead of blast cells in peripheral blood smear (Fig. 2G). Flow cytometry revealed that GFP-linked shAbcb8 was enriched in leukemia cells, suggesting a selective advantage for Abcb8-deficient cells during leukemia progression. All recipients with Abcb8 knockdown developed AML, partially expressed c-Kit and CD11b/Gr-1 (Fig. 2H). Compared to the shRen cohort, recipient mice transplanted with shAbcb8 developed aggressive acute myeloid leukemia as shown by shAbcb8+ blasts enriched in PB (Fig. 2G), severe leukemia cell infiltration into spleen and liver (Supplementary Fig. 2H), and the ability to generate AML in second transplanted wild-type recipient mice (Fig. 2I). The Abcb8 mRNA levels in the harvested leukemia cells were reduced (Fig. 2J), and ABCB8 protein levels were decreased by approximately half (Fig. 2K). Similarly, genes downregulated in 7q deleted human AMLs compared to those without 7q deletions were significantly negatively enriched in shRen HSPCs relative to shAbcb8 HSPCs (NES = −1.50, p-value < 0.01) (Supplementary Fig. 2I).

We also investigated the ABCB8 impact on leukemia genesis with CRISPR/Cas9 (Supplementary Fig. 2J). Consistently, Abcb8 sgRNAs, together with shNf1; Trp53−/−, promoted leukemia formation in recipient mice (median onset: 102 days for sgAbcb8 #1); 114 days for sgAbcb8 #2) while only one of six control sgScr recipient mice developed leukemia during the observation period (Fig. 2L). Similar to the shAbcb8 cohort, recipient mice transplanted with sgAbcb8 HSPCs developed AML, as shown by WBC, red blood cell (RBC), CD11b+/Gr-1+; c-Kit+ (Fig. 2M, Supplementary Fig. 2K), blasts in blood smear (Fig. 2N), leukemia infiltration in spleen, liver, and bone marrow (Supplementary Fig. 2L). Similarly, compared to the sgScr cohort, mice receiving sgAbcb8-transduced tumor cells exhibited more aggressive leukemia, indicated by accelerated leukemogenesis upon secondary transplantation (Supplementary Fig. 2M). Moreover, we examined the Abcb8 mutation status in harvested bone marrow from the sgAbcb8 AML cohort. As shown in Fig. 2O, 17 out of 20 individually picked sgAbcb8 bone marrow cells exhibited loss-of-function mutations in Abcb8. Of note, 75% of the mutated cells were heterozygous, with the other Abcb8 allele remaining wild-type, supporting a haploinsufficient tumor suppressor effect of Abcb8.

ABCB8 loss leads to nuclear iron deficiency, contributing to HSPCs differentiation

ABCB8 belongs to the ABC transporter family. It is located on the mitochondrial membrane, in charge of pumping mitochondrial iron and Fe-S out to the cytoplasm. It has been reported that Abcb8 loss led to the accumulation of mitochondrial ferrous ions (Fe2+) in cardiac tissue28. We observed the accumulation of iron in mitochondria in sgAbcb8-infected murine myeloid progenitor 32D cell line, as detected by a mitochondria-specific fluorescent dye FerroGreen (Supplementary Fig. 3A). Similar results were observed in the human HL-60 cell line carrying sgABCB8 (Supplementary Fig. 3B, C). Of note, the nuclear Fe2+ level was reduced in HL-60 cells infected with sgABCB8 when another iron dye, FerroOrange, was used (Fig. 3A–C). These results were confirmed with the ferrozine assay29,30, measuring non-heme iron level in isolated mitochondrial and nuclear proteins (Fig. 3D, Supplementary Fig. 3D). Following ABCB8 knockout, mitochondrial ferrous iron (Fe2+) levels were significantly increased, while nuclear ferrous iron was markedly decreased (Fig. 3E). Given Fe2+ is an important cofactor for DNA and histone demethylases31,32, we proposed that ABCB8 loss may cause epigenetic reprogramming that contributes to Abcb8 deletion-driven HSPCs differentiation block and transformation. To investigate it, we first treated HSPCs carrying shAbcb8 or shRen with ferrous ammonium sulfate (FAS) to increase the Fe2+ level. Flow cytometry analysis showed that although iron supplementary treatment has little effect on freshly isolated murine c-Kit+ HSPCs with control shRen, it significantly rescued the differentiation block induced by shAbcb8 compared to control shRen, as shown by reduced stemness marker c-Kit+% and increased mature cell surface marker Gr-1+%. This phenotype can be inhibited when the ferrous ion chelator deferiprone (DFP) is added together with FAS (Fig. 3F). Similar results were observed in leukemia cells. We assessed the impact of shAbcb8 on H3K27me3 using the CUT&Tag sequencing in sorted c-Kit+CD11b−Gr-1− HSPCs. We then explored the role of KDM6A in ABCB8-regulating the HSPC differentiation. We examined KDM6A’s impact on the ABCB8-overexpressed cells, which can promote shAbcb8 AML cell differentiation (Fig. 5E, Supplementary Fig. 5B). In this assay, we found that the effect of ABCB8 in driving cell differentiation was dramatically abolished by sgKdm6a, as shown by increased c-Kit+% and reduced Gr-1+% from flow cytometry analysis (Fig. 5E), and fewer blasts from cytospins images (Fig. 5F). To address whether the demethylase activity of KDM6A is required for Abcb8’s tumor-suppressive function, we cloned the wild-type (WT) KDM6A and a catalytically inactive, enzyme-dead (ED) KDM6A mutant, and investigated their impact on shAbcb8 leukemia (Supplementary Fig. 5C, D). shAbcb8 leukemia cells developed 2nd leukemia quickly in recipient mice, while exogenous KDM6A(WT) but not KDM6A(ED) expression significantly inhibited shAbcb8 leukemia progression in recipients, as indicated by dramatically reduced GFP+% leukemia cells and blasts in the peripheral blood, and thus extended recipients’ survival, KDM6A (ED) exhibited only a transient inhibitory effect at 2 weeks without survival benefit (Fig. 5G-–I). This indicates that KDM6A-mediated tumor suppression primarily depends on its catalytic activity. Altogether, these results support that ABCB8 promotes myeloid differentiation through Fe²⁺-dependent KDM6A demethylation.

Given that the role of ABCB8 in tumor biology is mainly through KDM6A, we have screened an epigenetic drug library individually to assess its impact on inhibiting shAbcb8 leukemia cell growth. Among these, multiple EZH2 inhibitors, including tazemetostat, EI1, etc, exhibited dramatic repression on the shAbcb8-driven AML cell viability compared to wildtype murine c-Kit+ HSPC controls (Fig. 5J, Supplementary Fig. 5E). More tazemetostat-treated, fewer shAbcb8 AML cells survived, fewer blasts were observed in cytospin images, and higher differentiation markers CD11b and Gr-1 were expressed (Fig. 5K–M). The trimethylation and acetylation of H3K27 were correspondingly altered with tazemetostat treatment (Fig. 5N). Molecularly, RNA-seq analysis of tazemetostat-treated shAbcb8 AML cells confirmed upregulation of myeloid differentiation pathways and associated genes (Fig. 5O, Supplementary Fig. 5F). Corroborating this, GSEA demonstrated that tazemetostat treatment not only upregulated the downregulated genes in shAbcb8 c-Kit+ HSPCs (NES = 1.33, p.adj < 0.01; Fig. 5P), but also FAS upregulated gene expression signature and KDM6A targets (NES = 1.34, p.adj < 0.01; NES = 1.37, p.adj < 0.01; Fig. 5P). Thus, our work identifies ABCB8 as a critical factor in leukemia maintenance and suggests EZH2 as a potential therapeutic target for −7/del(7q) AMLs.

The co-deletion of ABCB8 and KMT2C cooperatively drives leukemia formation

ABCB8 is on chromosome 7q36, where another tumor suppressor gene KMT2C identified in leukemia is located. In most cases, ABCB8 is co-deleted with KMT2C in -7/del(7q) MDS or AML samples (Supplementary Fig. 6A). Furthermore, we have demonstrated that ABCB8 may work through KDM6A, which is in the same COMPASS-like complex with KMT2C39. Thus, we investigated whether there is any synergistic effect of ABCB8 and KMT2C deletions on tumorigenesis (Fig. 6A). As Fig. 6B shown, compared to shKmt2c only, co-knockdown of Abcb8 and Kmt2c promoted leukemia formation in recipient mice with significantly shorter tumor onset (median onset 89 days for shAbcb8 #1; median onset 87.5 days for shAbcb8 #2; median onset 131 days for control shRen). Consistently, shAbcb8; shKmt2c cohort mice developed higher WBC, lower RBC, Hb and PLT counts, compared to control shRen; shKmt2c cohort (Supplementary Fig. 6B). Blood smears results and H&E stainings exhibited aggressive leukemia cells (blast) in the peripheral blood and infiltrated into the spleen, liver, and bone marrow (Supplementary Fig. 6C). The mRNA level of Abcb8 was reduced correspondingly (Supplementary Fig. 6D). shAbcb8; shKmt2c vs shRen; shKmt2c cells were dramatically increased in the harvested bone marrow compared to that in pre-injection, indicating that the Abcb8- and Kmt2c-deficient cells were more malignant than Kmt2c-loss only cells (Fig. 6C). Given that Abcb8 or Kmt2c loss alone could block HSPCs differentiation, we investigated the impact of the co-repression of Abcb8 and Kmt2c in HSPCs stemness and differentiation, and found that shAbcb8; shKmt2c synergistically increased c-Kit% (Fig. 6D). Agreeing with this, we found that ABCB8 and KMT2C cooperatively regulate gene expression, especially for genes involved in differentiation-related pathways like myeloid cell differentiation, by transcriptomic analysis of murine c-Kit+ HSPCs transduced with shRen, shAbcb8, shKmt2c, or shAbcb8; shKmt2c (Fig. 6E–G, Supplementary Data 3). Some genes are mainly regulated by Abcb8, and some genes are mainly under the control of Kmt2c, but many other genes are downregulated by both Abcb8 and Kmt2c loss. Consistently, we observed synergistic effects on the H3K27me3 signal of these genes regulated by both Abcb8 and Kmt2c from murine c-Kit+ HSPCs CUT&Tag data (Fig. 6H). We evaluated the dependence of human -7/del(7q) AML cells on ABCB8. We found that human AML cell lines with chromosome 7 deletions (UoCM1 and SCU-1) were sensitive to exogenous ABCB8 expression, while control cell lines (HL-60 and U937) were largely unaffected (Fig. 7E, F). Consistent with this, FAS supplementation reduced the viability of -7/del(7q) AML cells (Fig. 7G).

Having previously shown that Abcb8-deficient leukemia cells are sensitive to the EZH2 inhibitor tazemetostat, we next examined the effect of tazemetostat on −7/del(7q) AMLs. In a UoCM1 cell line-derived xenograft (CDX) model, tazemetostat treatment reduced tumor burden, as indicated by significantly lower spleen weight and a reduced percentage of human CD45+ leukemia cells in the bone marrow (Fig. 7H). Blood smear analysis also exhibited more differentiated cells in the tazemetostat-treated cohort (Fig. 7I). Notably, we collected the fresh peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from AML patients (West China Hospital) and treated them with tazemetostat in vitro for 48 h. PBMCs from −7/del(7q) AML patients showed significantly lower viability compared to those from other AML subtypes (Fig. 7J).

In summary, we identified ABCB8 as a tumor suppressor gene on chromosome 7. Its loss inhibits HSPC differentiation and promotes acute myeloid leukemia formation, likely via the Fe-KDM6A axis, and acts cooperatively with another chromosome 7 tumor suppressor gene, KMT2C (Fig. 7K).

Discussion

Chromosome copy number alterations are a hallmark of cancer, which are often associated with poor prognosis. Complete or partial chromosome 7 loss, generally containing hundreds to thousands of genes, has long been recognized as a poor-prognosis biomarker for myeloid malignancies. However, only a few of the chromosome 7 genes, like KMT2C16, SAMD9/SAMD9L42, CUX143, etc, have been demonstrated to play a role in promoting the process of tumor initiation and progression. Here, our studies identified multiple tumor suppressor candidate genes. Among them, we revealed that loss of ABCB8, located on 7q36.1, could drive an MDS-like syndrome by itself or AML combined with TP53 and NF1 loss in mice. We further illustrated that ABCB8, a transporter participating in the output of mitochondrial iron, affects the differentiation of hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells into myeloid cells, likely through regulating iron-containing histone methylase KDM6A. These results provide a mechanism of chromosome 7 loss in tumor biology, in which the mitochondrial iron transporter contributes to epigenetic regulation, HSPCs differentiation and cell transformation.

In this study, we illustrated a cellular pathway through which a mitochondrial iron transporter, ABCB8, regulates the epigenetic modification, histone H3 lysine 27 tri-methylation. As we know, the dysfunction of mitochondrial metabolic enzymes IDH1/2 affects DNA methylation levels through TET family proteins. IDH1/2 mutants produce a new metabolite, 2-hydroxyglutarate, which can bind to and inhibit the enzymatic activity of DNA demethylase TET. Here, we reported another way that mitochondrial function plays a role in regulating histone methylation through Fe2+ homeostasis and KDM6A, using Fe2+ as a cofactor to accomplish the histone demethylase activity. ABCB8 deficiency, causing the defect of iron output from mitochondria, reduced the nuclear iron level, affected histone demethylase function, and related histone H3K27 methylation level. Our findings would expand the understanding of the molecular mechanisms by which mitochondria function to influence epigenetic regulation.

Interestingly, we found the cooperation of ABCB8 and KMT2C, both of which are located on chromosome 7q36. In the frequently deleted chromosomal fragments, it has been reported that multiple tumor suppressor genes are enriched in this region. Moreover, the cooperation among these TSGs was identified before. In most cases, this cooperation is generally cooperative among different cellular pathways, like epigenetic factors PHF23 with transcription factor p53. It is more likely that genes co-deleted with the chromosome deletions promote tumorigenesis through multiple cellular pathways. However, our finding is that the co-deletion of ABCB8 and KMT2C in -7/del(7q) AML affects the same COMPASS-like complex, which may fine-tune this complex function on regulating transcriptomics and have a synergic effect on driving tumor formation.

Methods

Ethics statement

For studies involving human AML samples described below, ethics approval was provided by the Ethical Research Committee on Biomedical Research, West China Hospital of Sichuan University (No.HX-2019-(114)). All participants provided written informed consent prior to participating in the study.

For studies involving mice described below, all animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of West China Hospital. Because this study employed leukemia models in which solid tumor volume is not applicable, a specific maximal tumor size/burden was not defined in the approved protocol. Animals were monitored daily for humane endpoints predefined by the ethics committee (e.g., >20% body weight loss, hind‑limb paralysis, moribund state). All animals were euthanized by cervical dislocation after anesthesia when reaching the experimental endpoint, without exceeding the maximal tumor burden as defined by the ethics committee.

Human specimens

AML samples were obtained from West China Hospital of Sichuan University, with recruitment conducted between 2021 and 2022, including 2 with −7/del(7q), and 3 with intact 7q. Chromosome 7 status was confirmed by karyotype analysis. Following transport at 4 °C, bone marrow samples were processed to isolate tumor cells from Bone Marrow Mononuclear Cells (BMMCs) using Ficoll-Paque PLUS density gradient centrifugation. All patients were pathologically diagnosed with AML by pathologists at West China Hospital. As the information on sex or gender was not relevant in this study, no related analysis was carried out.

Mice

Male and female C57BL/6 mice (6-8 weeks old, 20–25 g) were obtained from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd. (Nanjing, China). Both male and female mice were used, as this study did not consider sex as a biological variable. NOD.Cg-Prkdcscid Il2rgtm1/Bcgen (NSG) mice were obtained from Beijing HuaFukang Biological Technology Co., Ltd. Bone marrow cells were freshly isolated from femurs and tibias of male 6 to 8-week-old donor mice, and c-Kit+ HSPCs were sorted using c-Kit (CD117) Microbeads (Miltenyi Biotec Cat# 130-091-224, RRID: AB_2753213) and a QuadroMACS separator (Miltenyi Biotec, South San Francisco, CA). For secondary transplant experiments, 1 × 106 bone marrow leukemia cells were injected into 4.5 Gy irradiated C57BL/6 recipient mice.

Stained images were acquired using a panoramic MIDI slide scanner (3DHISTECH). For immunofluorescence analysis, first, the cells were stained with the respective live-cell probes. Specifically, FerroGreen (mitochondrial Fe2+) (Dojindo, #M489) and FerroOrange (cellular Fe2+) (Dojindo, #F374) were used. To spatially profile Fe2+ reduction (Fig. 3A, B), we used ImageJ to extract pixel-by-pixel fluorescence along a linear track ROI traversing the cell. Next, to quantify nuclear Fe2+ MFI (Fig. 3C), DAPI-stained nuclei were defined as ROIs. All antibodies used in this study are listed in Supplementary Data 6.

In vitro drug response assays

Primary leukemia cells were isolated from shAbcb8 tumor-bearing mice and cultured in BCM medium [45% DMEM, 45% Iscove’s modified Dulbecco’s medium, 10% FBS, 2 mmol/L glutamine, 50 μM β-mercaptoethanol, penicillin (100 U/mL)/streptomycin (0.1 mg/mL)] and were plated in 96-well plates with a cell density of 10,000 cells per well. Nuclear and active mitochondrial extraction

Mitochondrial protein lysates were extracted using the following protocol. Generation of acetyllysine antibodies and affinity enrichment of acetylated peptides44. Briefly, cells were harvested and homogenized on ice in mitochondrial isolation buffer (10 mM KCl, 1.5 mM MgCl₂, 10 mM Tris-Cl, pH 7.4) supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche, Germany). The homogenate was then centrifuged at 1200 g for 10 min at 4 °C to collect the precipitate (unbroken cells and nuclei) for nuclear extraction. The resulting supernatant was subjected to a second centrifugation at 4000 g for 20 min at 4 °C to eliminate lysosomes and the endoplasmic reticulum. The mitochondrial pellet was subsequently washed with the isolation buffer and centrifuged at 10,000 g for 15 min at 4 °C. The collected mitochondrial precipitate was used for subsequent experiments. The precipitate (unbroken cells and nuclei) was collected and further lysed in mitochondrial isolation buffer on ice for 15 min. The homogenate was then centrifuged at 1200 g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove the supernatant. The resulting nuclear precipitate was washed twice in isolation buffer and used for subsequent experiments.

Mitochondrial and nuclear iron assay

First, complete mitochondria and nuclei were extracted using the methods mentioned above. Non-heme iron was measured using the following method44. In brief, equal amounts of mitochondrial proteins were mixed with protein precipitation solution (1:1 10% trichloroacetic acid plus 1 mol/L HCl) followed by heating for 1 h at 95 °C to release iron. After centrifugation at 4 °C and 16,000 g for 10 min, the supernatant was mixed with an equal volume of chromogen solution (1.5 M sodium acetate, 0.5 mM ferrozine, 0.1% (v/v) thioglycolic acid). Absorbance at 562 nm was measured on a multi-mode microplate reader (BioTek Synergy™ H4).

Histone H3 modification multiplex assay

Total histone proteins were extracted from murine AML cells infected with either shRen or shAbcb8 using the EpiQuikTM Total Histone Extraction Kit (EpiGentek, Cat# OP-0006-100) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. To profile histone H3 modifications, approximately 100 ng of the extracted histone proteins per well was analyzed using the EpiQuikTM Histone H3 Modification Multiplex Assay Kit (Colorimetric; EpiGentek, Cat# P-3100), strictly following the manufacturer’s instructions.

RNA-seq analysis

After library construction, RNA sequencing was performed by an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 sequencing platform with 150 bp paired-end sequencing. The RNA-seq reads were aligned to the mouse reference genome (mm10) by STAR_2.7.10b. Transcript abundances, significance levels, and differentially expressed genes of mouse cells and patient tissue cells were generated and identified by DESeq2 (v.1.26.0) (RRID: SCR_015687). To minimize potential batch effects, expression matrices were adjusted using the removeBatchEffect function from the limma R package prior to principal component analysis (PCA). The significantly differentially expressed genes in each group were used to perform Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analyses with the R package clusterProfiler (v. 3.14.3) (RRID: SCR_016884). GSEA (RRID: SCR_003199) was utilized to identify the significantly enriched pathways by default parameters. Preranked function was performed to clarify the overall transcriptome similarity between samples. Pheatmap (v.1.0.12) (RRID: SCR_016418) was used to visualize differentially expressed genes with z-scores.

scRNA-seq analysis

For scRNA-seq of human bone marrow, we acquired the data from the GEO database under the accession number GSE12044622. The output of the cellranger and count matrix were read using the Read10X function from the Seurat package (v. 4.3.0) (RRID: SCR_016341). Then the Seurat pipeline was implemented for downstream analyses. Genes expressed in fewer than three cells were not considered. Cells that had mitochondrial ratios lower than 10%, expressed at least 200 but not more than 6000 genes and gene total counts above 200 were retained for analysis. To integrate all the single cells from different samples for unsupervised clustering, the R package Harmony (v. 0.1.1) (RRID: SCR_022206) was used to reduce the batch effect. After data normalization, the ‘vst’ mode of the “FindVariableFeatures” function was performed for the top 2500 highly variable genes detection. UMAP was performed for cell dimensionality reduction. R package CytoTRACE (v. 0.3.3) (RRID: SCR_022828) was used to estimate cell differentiation potentials. The differentiation trajectory of myeloid cells was constructed using the R package Slingshot. The boxplots display ABCB8 expression levels, calculated as pseudo-bulk averages across cell types.

CUT&Tag analysis

H3K27me3 CUT&Tag assays were performed using the NovoNGS CUT&Tag 2.0 High-Sensitivity kit (Novoprotein Scientific, N259-YH01-01A). For samples without spike-in controls, libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform with 150-bp paired-end reads. Reads were quality controlled using fastp (RRID: SCR_016962) and aligned to the mouse genome (mm10) using Bowtie2 (RRID: SCR_016368). SAMtools (RRID: SCR_002105) was used to convert SAM files into sorted BAM files. Normalized bigWig files were generated using DeepTools (RRID: SCR_016366) with “--normalizeUsing BPM --binSize 50”. Data normalization and differential peak analysis were performed using DESeq2 (RRID: SCR_015687).

For samples containing E. coli λ sequences as spike-in controls, libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq X Plus platform with 150-bp paired-end reads. Reads were quality controlled using fastp and aligned to the mouse genome (mm10) and the E. coli λ spike-in sequence using Bowtie2. Sorted BAM files were generated with SAMtools. A scaling factor for each sample was calculated as 10,000 divided by the number of fragments mapped to the E. coli genome. Normalized bigWig files were generated using DeepTools with “--normalizeUsing None --binSize 50 --scaleFactor <scaling factor>”. IgG samples were normalized without scaling (“--normalizeUsing None --binSize 50”). For differential peak analysis, 1/scaling factor was provided as the size factor to DESeq2 to standardize counts across samples.

For all H3K27me3 data, peaks were called using MACS3 with “--broad --broad-cutoff 0.1 -q 0.05 -f BAMPE”. Peak annotation was performed using ChIPseeker with tssRegion set to (−3000, 3000). Broad peaks were converted into GRangesList objects, and consensus counts were generated. Visualization of the average CUT&Tag profiles was performed using DeepTools and IGV (RRID: SCR_011793). For GSEA of H3K27me3 changes, each gene was represented by the peak with the highest mean signal within its promoter. Genes were then ranked by a metric calculated as the cube of the log2FC multiplied by the mean signal to prioritize genes with both large changes and high signal. Enrichment analysis was performed using the R package clusterProfiler with default parameters and GO Biological Process gene sets.

To analyze the synergistic effect of ABCB8 and KMT2C on H3K27me3 levels, we first extracted H3K27me3 peaks corresponding to genes showing a synergistic effect at the RNA level. For each gene, the average H3K27me3 signal across all peaks was calculated, followed by row-wise z-score normalization. The normalized values were then used to compute the average H3K27me3 level for each group.

Clinical correlation analysis

Genetic alteration data were obtained from TCGA-LAML via cBioPortal (https://www.cbioportal.org/)20. Survival analyses for TCGA-LAML20, Beat AML25, GSE14617326 and GSE3764227 cohorts were performed using the survminer R package (RRID: SCR_021094). In the TCGA-LAML cohort, patients were classified into 7q-intact and 7q-deletion groups based on the CNV status of 7q genes. BeatAML cohort data were downloaded from https://biodev.github.io/BeatAML2/. For the Beat AML25, GSE14617326 and GSE3764227 cohorts, patients were divided into ABCB8-high and ABCB8-low groups based on ABCB8 expression, and the optimal cut-point was determined using the maximally selected rank statistics. For the GSE14617326 cohort, only primary AML samples were included in the analysis. The correlation between ABCB8 expression and KDM6A binding signature in the BeatAML cohort was analyzed by Seurat. The default “NormalizeData” method was used to normalize the data.

The Bulk RNA-Seq and H3K27me3 CUT&Tag data of AML patients were acquired from the GEO database under the accession number GSE15213640. Raw RNA-seq counts were normalized using DESeq2, and the resulting gene expression levels are provided in Supplementary Data 5. Based on these normalized values, we selected three samples with the highest and three with the lowest ABCB8 expression among patients with both H3K27me3 and RNA-seq data (the two intermediate samples showed no significant expression differences and were therefore excluded from downstream analysis). For CUT&Tag analysis, we used the peak and normalized bigWig files provided by the authors. A consensus peak set was generated from the six selected samples (three ABCB8-high and three ABCB8-low) by merging individual peak sets using the reduce function in the GenomicRanges package. To compare H3K27me3 signals between the two groups, we averaged the bigwig files of individual samples within each group using the deepTools bigwigAverage function, generating one representative bigwig file per group. The H3K27me3 signal intensity over the consensus peak regions was then quantified using deepTools computeMatrix and visualized with plotHeatmap.

The Leucegene Project Bulk RNA-Seq dataset of AML patients was obtained from GSE67040. The KDM6A ChIP-Seq data of mouse HSPCs were acquired from the GEO database under the accession number GSE8648834. Those peak files were downloaded and annotated using the “annotatePeak” function of ChIPseeker (RRID: SCR_021322). The KDM6A ChIP-Seq data of human OCI-AML2 cells were acquired from the GEO database under the accession number GSE21427441. Raw sequencing data were processed identically to the CUT&Tag analysis pipeline, with MACS3 peak calling performed using parameters “-g hs -q 0.05 -f BAMPE”.

Statistics & reproducibility

No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. For in vitro studies, 3–4 independent experiments were performed. For in vivo studies, the sample size per group ranged from 4–11, based on the number of animals that developed tumors, including both male and female mice, which have been shown to be sufficient to detect physiologically relevant differences while maintaining experimental feasibility. All key experiments were repeated independently at least twice with similar results, and sample sizes for each experiment are clearly indicated in the figure legends. No data were excluded from the analyses. Unless otherwise stated, samples were randomly assigned to groups prior to the experiments. Investigators were blinded to group allocation during animal transplantation and outcome assessment. No sex-specific differences were observed. Unless otherwise noted, n represents the number of biological replicates. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (v5.01, RRID: SCR_002798) and PASW Statistics 18 software. Two-tailed Student’s t-tests were used for comparisons between two groups, while one-way or two-way ANOVA was utilized for single-variable or two-variable comparisons among multiple groups, respectively. Additional statistical methods, including the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, Wald test, hypergeometric distribution, and log-rank test, were applied as appropriate for specific datasets. Exact P values are provided in the Source Data file, and statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. Error bars are presented as SD or SEM, as specified in the figure legends.
