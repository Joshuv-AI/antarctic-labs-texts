Unconditional de novo generation and functional validation of synthetic promoters with a diffusion model
Authors: Chen Lin, Zhengwen Zhu, Xinglong Wang, Yonghua Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGenerative neural networks enable de novo design of synthetic genetic elements. Here, we present DiPro, an unconditional diffusion model that learns E. coli promoter features to generate novel sequences without conditional input. DiPro directly generates 50-bp promoter sequences from Gaussian noise using a time-conditioned Transformer denoiser, without auxiliary discriminators or recognizers. DiPro accurately recapitulates natural promoter statistics, including sequence motifs, k-mer spectra, conserved −35/−10 spacing (16–18 bp). Over 90% of generated sequences were classified as real promoters by an independent recognizer, indicating high structural fidelity. To design stationary-phase–specific promoters, generation was guided by inpainting randomly masked regions (three non-overlapping 6-mers, 18 bp) within sigma38-associated (σ38) seed sequences, yielding 5000 candidates while preserving global promoter architecture. Bayesian classification resolved these sequences into three distinct clusters with divergent −35/−10 composition and motif conservation. Experimentally, 81.6% of tested promoters exhibited stationary-phase activity, with the strongest variant reaching 79.6% of the activity of the constitutive promoter J23119. Promoters displayed distinct activation timing, reflecting heterogeneous stress responses. As a proof-of-concept, selected promoters were used to drive a lysis protein, achieving phase-specific and tunable cell lysis that matched promoter activation timing, with early-phase promoters reducing culture density to an optical density at 600 nm of approximately 1.5–2.0. Together, these results establish DiPro as a generative framework for creating functional bacterial promoters with programmable temporal dynamics, offering a new tool for synthetic biology and metabolic engineering.Keywords: Diffusion model, Deep learning, Stationary phase promoter, Metabolic engineering, Cell lysis

Introduction

Promoters are essential cis-regulatory DNA sequences that control the transcription rate of downstream genes and thereby determine protein expression levels in living cells [1]. The activity of promoters directly influences both protein abundance and secretion efficiency, making them critical targets for optimizing recombinant protein production and metabolic flux in microorganisms [2,3]. Despite extensive efforts to engineer strong constitutive promoters, it remains challenging to design promoters with activities surpassing those of the strongest natural counterparts or time-dependent samples that regulates cell behavior at specific period [4,5]. In Escherichia coli, growth-phase–dependent promoters exhibit dynamic transcriptional activity that varies across exponential and stationary phases, enabling precise temporal regulation of gene expression [6,7]. These promoters are controlled by cellular physiological states and global regulators such as σ70, ensuring coordinated responses to environmental and metabolic changes [8]. Understanding and engineering such phase-specific promoters is crucial for optimizing dynamic gene expression in synthetic and metabolic circuits [9]. Their rational design can improve time-dependent protein production, stress adaptation, and lifespan control in engineered microbial systems [10].

Bioinformatics tools have long been used to dissect promoter sequence–function relationships and to guide de novo design by identifying conserved features such as the −35 and −10 motifs and their optimal spacing [11]. These motif-centric and statistical approaches together with synthetic-library screens have clarified key determinants of promoter strength, for example, the TTTACG/−35 and TATAAT/−10 combination and an ∼17-bp spacer [12], but they struggle to capture the higher-order, nonlinear interactions among sequence elements that ultimately govern activity, leaving accurate strength prediction challenging [13]. By contrast, deep-learning (DL) methods can learn complex, context-dependent sequence features directly from large datasets and have shown strong performance in promoter strength prediction and sequence generation. For example, generative artificial intelligence (AI) models, such as Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs), Variational Autoencoders, and diffusion models, have recently been applied to de novo promoter design by learning sequence features from native promoters [2,14]. These models enable controllable generation of promoters with desired transcriptional strength by integrating predictive networks that evaluate sequence functionality [10,[15], [16], [17]].

In E. coli, stationary-phase promoters play a crucial role in adapting cells to nutrient limitation, oxidative stress, and other environmental challenges encountered when growth ceases [18,19]. These promoters are typically activated by the alternative sigma factor σS (RpoS) [20], which directs RNA polymerase to transcribe genes involved in stress resistance, osmoprotection, DNA repair, and metabolic reprogramming. Through this regulation, stationary-phase promoters ensure cell survival and maintenance under unfavorable conditions [21]. Designing and engineering such promoters is important for both fundamental and applied synthetic biology [22]. From a biological perspective, they enable precise control of gene expression during late growth phases, providing insight into bacterial regulatory networks [23]. From an engineering standpoint, synthetic stationary-phase promoters can be exploited to achieve time-delayed or growth-dependent expression of target genes, minimizing metabolic burden during exponential growth and activating production pathways or cell lysis at optimal times [24]. Thus, rational design of stationary-phase promoters offers a powerful strategy for dynamic control in metabolic engineering, biosensing, and programmed cell lifespan regulation. Yet despite substantial promise, de novo construction of phase-specific promoters with programmable temporal dynamics remains a formidable, unresolved challenge. This difficulty stems from the highly nonlinear and context-dependent sequence–activity relationship of σ38-recognized promoters, the lack of predictable rules for higher-order regulatory grammar, the strong influence of intracellular physiological states on promoter output, and the inherent complexity of engineering tunable, growth phase–locked activation dynamics with conventional design principles.

In this study, we aim to establish an unbiased and novel generative framework for designing functional Escherichia coli promoters with programmable stationary-phase specificity. The primary objectives are to develop an unconditional diffusion-based generative model that can faithfully capture native promoter architectural rules without relying on supervised recognizers or discriminators. We also incorporate a masked inpainting strategy to guide the generation of sigma-38-dependent stationary-phase promoter candidates. We employ Bayesian clustering to classify generated sequences into functionally distinct subgroups for rational candidate prioritization. We experimentally validate the temporal activity and functional utility of synthetic promoters in a programmable cell lysis system. By achieving these goals, we seek to address current bottlenecks in phase-specific promoter design and provide a generalized and scalable tool for dynamic gene regulation in synthetic biology and metabolic engineering.

Materials and methods

Strains and plasmids

E. coli BL21(DE3) was used for plasmid construction and recombinant protein expression. The expression cassette carrying the synthetic promoters, native stationary-phase promoters (PkatE and Pbola), green fluorescent protein (GFP) [25] or CoIM were synthesized by GenScript (Nanjing, China) and cloned into the pET-22b via restriction enzyme cutting sites of BglII and BlpI, all the synthetic promoters and the gene encoding the expression cassette was provided in Table S1.

DNA manipulation

To construct the pET-22b plasmid carrying different synthetic promoters, the pET-22b/GFP or pET-22b/CoIM plasmid was used as a template for PCR amplification, with primers listed in Table S2. The PCR products were ligated using homologous recombination with the One Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). To modify promoters for the induction of CoIM expression, the pET-22b/CMP-E3/araC-CoIM plasmid was used as a template for PCR, and synthetic double-stranded promoters carrying homologous arms (synthesized by GenScript as Minigene products) were ligated using the One Step Cloning Kit.

Cultivation

The plasmids were chemically transformed into E. coli to generate engineered strains [26]. Single colonies were inoculated into Luria–Bertani (LB) medium containing appropriate antibiotics (kanamycin 50 μg/mL or ampicillin 100 μg/mL, depending on the plasmid) and incubated overnight at 37 °C with shaking at 220 rpm. Seed cultures were subsequently inoculated into Terrific Broth (TB) supplemented with antibiotics and grown at 37 °C with shaking at 220 rpm until the optical density at 600 nm (OD600) reached the desired level. Cultivation was performed in 250 mL Erlenmeyer flasks, with each flask containing 50 mL of TB. For protein induction, isopropyl-β-d-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) was added to a final concentration of 0.1 mM, or arabinose was added to a final concentration of 10 μM. After induction, the cultivation temperature was reduced to 20 °C.

Dataset preparation

We used a curated collection of promoter sequences derived from multiple public databases covering diverse Escherichia coli strains. The full set includes 11,886 non-redundant records after strict filtering. We removed duplicated entries and discarded sequences with high similarity beyond a unified threshold to guarantee sequence independence and eliminate risks of data leakage. Each record contains a 50-base-pair region directly upstream of the transcription start site [27]. The dataset includes promoters regulated by dominant sigma factors including sigma 70 and sigma 38, covering both constitutive and growth-phase-dependent regulatory patterns. Independent training and test partitions were constructed with completely separated sequence sets to avoid information leakage during model evaluation. Generalization was enhanced by DNA-aware augmentation, including reverse complements and low-rate single-nucleotide substitutions that mimic sequencing noise. Sequences were tokenized with SentencePiece to yield discrete inputs for the diffusion language model.

Model architecture

DiPro is a diffusion language model (DLM) built on a Transformer [28] backbone comprising an embedding layer, a time-conditioned encoder, and a diffusion module, as shown in Fig. 1. Tokens are embedded in a 512-dimensional space and normalized with LayerNorm. The encoder stacks 12 layers with 8-head self-attention and GELU feed-forward blocks at fourfold width, using rotary position embeddings (RoPE) for positional encoding. Model training

We minimized a composite objective comprising mean-squared error in embedding space (Ldiffusion = ‖ fθ(Xt, t) - X0 ‖2) and token-level cross-entropy with label smoothing. Optimization used AdamW with warmup–decay scheduling, mixed-precision training and gradient-norm clipping; an exponential moving average (EMA) of parameters was maintained for robust inference. Periodic sampling monitored convergence and sample quality.

Evaluation of generated sequence quality

We conducted a multi-level quantitative comparison between generated promoters and the training set to assess sequence quality and functional fidelity. First, we used information-theoretic sequence logos derived from per-position base frequencies and Shannon entropy, with I = 2 - H and H = -∑ pi log2 pi, to visualize conservation at key regulatory elements such as the −35 and −10 boxes. This approach quantifies positional specificity and provides direct evidence for the preservation of core regulatory motifs.

At a finer resolution, we analyzed frequency distributions of k-mers for k from 2 to 6 to evaluate the fidelity of short motifs, and we quantified agreement with the training distribution using the coefficient of determination R2. In addition, we compared global composition metrics, including GC and AT content and per-base proportions, as well as basic length statistics, to verify that the overall composition of generated promoters is consistent with that of natural promoters.

Bayesian promoter classification based on a Gaussian mixture model

To functionally group and triage generated promoters, we constructed a Bayesian classifier based on a full-covariance Gaussian mixture model (GMM) [30]. Each promoter sequence was represented by an 87-dimensional feature vector comprising multi-scale k-mer frequency profiles and basic composition descriptors. All features were standardized by Z-score normalization and the dimensionality was reduced to 10 via principal component analysis (PCA), retaining 53.0% of the total variance while removing redundant and noisy dimensions. In this reduced feature space, the optimal number of clusters was determined by fitting GMMs with K ranging from 2 to 10 and selecting the value that minimized the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). This procedure identified K = 3 as the optimal cluster number (Fig. S1). The final GMM was trained with full covariance matrices via expectation–maximization with 100 random initializations and a maximum of 1000 iterations, and returned posterior cluster probabilities P(ck|x). Cluster quality was quantified by three complementary metrics. The Silhouette Score was 0.090, the Calinski–Harabasz Index was 568.8, and the Davies–Bouldin Index was 2.56. Because the GMM is a soft-clustering method that models overlapping, elliptical distributions rather than hard boundaries, moderate Silhouette values are expected and reflect the probabilistic nature of the assignments rather than poor separation. Maximum-a-posteriori labels and posterior magnitudes enabled confidence-aware selection of candidates, and cluster-level summaries of GC content revealed compositional preferences that guide downstream sampling.

Evaluation of baseline generative models

Three representative deep generative models for bacterial promoter design were selected as benchmark baselines. Baseline models were chosen based on wide adoption in the field, public accessibility of trained models and source code, and consistent focus on de novo promoter generation. All baseline models were implemented with default parameters recommended in their original publications to ensure fair comparison. Identical evaluation workflows including sequence motif analysis, k-mer spectrum quantification, and regulatory element spacing statistics were applied to both DiPro and baseline generated sequences for direct and unbiased benchmarking.

Results

Establishing DiPro to unconditional generate de novo promoters

We introduce DiPro, an unconditional diffusion framework for de novo generation of non-natural promoters. The model starts from Gaussian noise and, through a time-conditioned Transformer denoiser, iteratively generates 50-bp promoter sequences without relying on an auxiliary discriminator or a separate recognizer (Fig. 1). DiPro was benchmarked against three representative deep generative models for promoter design, including generative adversarial networks, variational autoencoders, and early diffusion-based generative tools. The performance was assessed by comparing conserved motifs, spatial distributions of key motifs, and the −35 to −10 spacing statistics (Fig. 2A). To select the training epochs with the best performance, we visualized the sequence-logo comparisons of sequences generated during training and predicted the real promoter portion (RPP, defined as the fraction of generated sequences classified as authentic promoters by PromoR) in the generated data using PromoR [31], a tool previously shown to achieve high accuracy in classifying real and artificial promoters (Fig. 1C and D).

We observed that the generated data stabilized after 600 epochs, with an RPP exceeding 90% and the recapitulation of conservation at the −35 and −10 regions (Fig. 2B–S1). The classical elements, TT at −35 and TATAAT at −10, were aligned in position and shape with those of natural promoters. Analysis of k-mer spectra demonstrates high agreement between generated and natural sets from 2-mer to 6-mer, with a mild decline as k increases. At 6-mer resolution, point-wise regression attains a R2 = 0.891, while correctly highlighting key fragments such as TATAAT (Fig. 2C–S2).

In the spatial domain, the positional distribution of key motifs relative to the TSS matches that of the natural set, with AT-rich content around −10 and conserved elements around −35 showing aligned peak positions and amplitudes (Fig. 2D). The inter-element spacing between −35 and −10 forms a sharp peak at 16 to 18 bp, with the DiPro curve closely overlapping the natural distribution, indicating accurate capture of σ70 promoter structural constraints (Fig. 2E); baseline methods exhibit peak shifts or broadening. Collectively, these results demonstrate that DiPro reproduces natural promoter features in an unconditional setting and achieves superior fidelity compared to baselines at compositional, local-motif, and global organizational scales.

Generating stationary-phase promoters using DiPro

To design novel promoters with desired functions, we employed a masking strategy by providing a partially masked sequence for the model to generate the missing area. First, stationary-phase promoters were collected and analyzed according to their strength. Stationary-phase promoters regulated by σ38, also known as RpoS [32], exhibit pronounced AT-rich extended −10 features [33], weaker −35 dependence, and −35/−10 spacing concentrated at 16–18 bp, with distinctive composition between −10 and the TSS. σ38-annotated samples were included during training, and stationary-phase–targeted sequences were generated by inpainting: for each high-expression σ38 seed, multiple non-overlapping 6-mer windows were masked and completed by the diffusion model, preserving global spacing while reshaping local content. This strategy yielded 5000 candidates. Each candidate was encoded as an 87-dimensional feature vector and the dimensionality was reduced to 10 via PCA. Bayesian clustering using a full-covariance GMM was then applied in this reduced space. The number of clusters was objectively determined by scanning K from 2 to 10 and selecting the value that minimized the BIC, which identified K = 3 as the optimal model (Fig. S3). The resulting GMM partitioned the candidates into three compositionally distinct groups with calibrated posterior probabilities (Fig. 3A).

The left panel of Fig. 3A displays the sample distribution projected onto the first two principal components, which explain 12.0% and 8.4% of the total variance, respectively. Three clusters are differentiated by color and occupy distinguishable regions of the feature space. Because the GMM assigns probabilistic memberships rather than hard boundaries, partial overlap between clusters in the two-dimensional projection is expected. The classification confidence heatmap in the right panel reveals that the majority of samples exhibit high posterior confidence, and only 287 out of 5000 total candidates represent borderline samples with low classification confidence, with a mean maximum posterior probability of 0.805, confirming the reliability of the Bayesian clustering outcome. Sequences in Cluster 0 display AT-rich characteristics typical of σ38 promoters in the −10 region (Fig. 3B), whereas the conservation signal in the −35 region is relatively weak. In contrast, sequences in Cluster 1 contain a prominent specific motif in the −35 region, such as a consecutive G/C-rich segment, and their −10 region composition differs markedly from that of Cluster 0. Conversely, sequences in Cluster 2 exhibit moderate features, with conservation signals in both the −35 and −10 regions intermediate between those of the former two clusters. Sequence logo analysis further indicated distinct sequence features among the three clusters, suggesting potential functional divergence.

Characterizing the activity of generated stationary phase promoters

The transcriptional activity of the generated stationary-phase promoters was evaluated using GFP as a reporter [34]. Fluorescence intensity was monitored at key time points (10, 15, 20, 30, 40, 45, 50, 55 and 60 h, consistent with Fig. 5) throughout a 60 h fermentation period to fully capture the temporal transcriptional activity and stationary-phase-specific activation pattern (Fig. 4). To ensure representativeness and structural diversity for reliable experimental validation, 60 promoter candidates were strictly selected from 5000 generated sequences based on four quantitative criteria: (1) balanced sampling across clusters with 20 candidates selected from each of Cluster 0, Cluster 1, and Cluster 2; (2) high-confidence filtering with a GMM posterior classification probability of no less than 0.7; (3) preference for candidates harboring conserved −35/−10 core motifs and canonical 16–18 bp spacer length; (4) removal of sequences with over 90% identity to eliminate redundant sequences and maximize structural diversity. Promoter sequences were randomly selected from each computationally defined cluster for experimental validation (Table S1). To more specifically and rigorously evaluate the stationary-phase activity of synthetic promoters, two well-characterized σ38-regulated native stationary-phase promoters, PkatE and Pbola, were included as stationary-phase positive controls, in addition to the strong constitutive promoter J23119 (as a general strength benchmark). Our results demonstrated that 49 out of the 60 tested promoters (81.6%) exhibited significantly higher activity than the negative control. However, all synthetic promoters showed lower strength than the strong constitutive promoter J23119 (Fig. 4), which is one of the most active constitutive promoters [35] and was used as a general positive control for promoter strength. Among the generated promoters, the most active one, P11, reached 79.6% of the total activity of J23119, and more importantly, P11 exhibited superior stationary-phase activity compared with native counterparts, reaching 128% and 115% of the activity of PkatE and Pbola, respectively. Most of the functional synthetic promoters also showed comparable or slightly higher stationary-phase activity than PkatE and Pbola, which further validates the rational design of our synthetic promoters. The results in Fig. 4 confirmed that all native and synthetic stationary-phase promoters exhibited negligible activity during the exponential growth phase (10 h) and were specifically activated after 20 h, whereas the constitutive promoter J23119 maintained consistent high activity across all growth phases. All data were presented as mean ± SD (n = 3) with relative error ≤5%. This successful generation of functional sequences confirms that the masked-region redesign strategy of DiPro can effectively produce active stationary-phase promoters. The high success rate and superior activity of partial synthetic promoters highlight the model's practical utility for de novo promoter design.

Characterizing time-dependent activity of the generated stationary-phase promoters

Next, we characterized the time-dependent activity of the generated promoters by tracking the OD600 and fluorescent intensity of the top 20 most active designs. To eliminate ambiguity and enable quantitative classification, we strictly defined four growth phases based on fermentation time: (1) Exponential growth phase: 0–20 h (rapid OD600 increase, negligible stationary-phase promoter activity); (2) Early stationary phase: 20–30 h (OD600 stabilization, initial promoter activation); (3) Middle stationary phase: 30–50 h (peak promoter activity); (4) Late stationary phase: 50–60 h (sustained activity under nutrient limitation). Promoters were categorized by the first time point of significant fluorescence elevation relative to the exponential phase. The time-dependent activation profiles of the native stationary-phase promoters PkatE and Pbola were also monitored as references, and both exhibited typical mid-stationary-phase activation characteristics, which were consistent with the main activation trend of our synthetic promoters. We found that 14 promoters were active before the 20 h time point, when OD600 stabilized, indicating they are not strictly stationary-phase promoters (Fig. 5). Furthermore, even among stationary-phase promoters, we observed variation in their timing of activation, likely due to sensing different cellular stresses. Based on the above quantitative time criteria, we divided the stationary phase into three periods: early, middle, and late. We found that 20% and 60% of these promoters were activated in the early and middle phases, respectively, and displayed higher overall induction activity than those activated late. This may be attributed to the relatively abundant nutrient availability during the early to middle stationary phase, whereas promoters induced later are likely constrained by nutrient depletion and deteriorating environmental conditions.

An analysis of the cluster origins of the top 20 most active promoters revealed that 12 originated from Cluster 0. Notably, the most active temporally induced promoters across all three phases all belonged to Cluster 0, suggesting that the sequence features of Cluster 0 may be associated with high inducible activity. Promoters active in both late-phase and early-phase were also identified. Among these top 20 promoters, five came from Cluster 2, all of which were active during the early and middle stationary phase, respectively. Only two highly active promoters originated from Cluster 1, both of which were early-phase promoters. The unique G/C-enriched −35 specific motif in Cluster 1 likely reduces its binding compatibility with σ38. This structural feature thereby directly contributes to the observed lower proportion of highly active samples and restricted expression timing. These results demonstrate that the designed promoters can function effectively as stationary-phase promoters and exhibit considerable diversity based on their distinct induction timing.

Induction of cell lysis using stationary phase promoters, a case study

As a proof-of-concept for the functional application of our designed promoters, we implemented a programmed cell lysis system using stationary-phase-specific promoters to control the expression of a lethal protein. To rigorously validate lysis efficiency, the negative control without the CoIM cassette and the strong constitutive promoter J23119 (added in Fig. 6B) were included as controls. We selected three of the strongest generated promoters, each demonstrating activation during the early (P05), middle (P14), and late (P11) stationary phase, respectively. These promoters were used to drive the expression of the cell lysis protein CoIM (Fig. 6A), which was chosen for its previously reported high efficacy in degrading E. coli cell walls even under high-density cultivation conditions [36]. The performance of this system was evaluated by monitoring the growth of engineered strains over a 60 h fermentation period. As shown in Fig. 6B, the negative control strain, which lacked the CoIM expression cassette, exhibited robust growth, reaching a final OD600 of approximately 8. The constitutive promoter J23119 mediated complete cell lysis and served as the positive control for maximal lysis activity. In stark contrast, strains carrying the CoIM gene under the control of the synthetic promoters showed markedly impaired growth, confirming the functional triggering of cell lysis.

Schematic of the cell lysis experiment.

(A) Lysis process of E. coli expressing the ColM protein. (B) Changes in OD600 during the expression of the ColM cell lysis protein induced by time-dependent stationary-phase promoters.

The early-stationary-phase promoter P05 (the strongest among the three) mediated the most potent lysis effect. The culture expressing CoIM from P05 reached a maximum OD600 of only 3.4 and subsequently sustained a low density between 1.5 and 2.0 for the remainder of the cultivation, indicating effective and sustained cell death. The two other promoters, P14 and P11, mediated a less severe but still significant growth restriction. The OD600 in these cultures plateaued at higher levels, with P14 maintaining a range of 4.2–4.8 and P11 maintaining 4.5–5.1. The incomplete lysis mediated by middle- and late-stationary-phase promoters (P14, P11) was attributed to their weaker transcriptional activity relative to the early-stationary-phase promoter P05 and the constitutive promoter J23119, which is fully consistent with the activity data presented in Fig. 4. This gradation in lysis efficiency correlates with both the intrinsic strength and the timing of activation of the respective promoters. Overall, this case study successfully demonstrates that the DiPro-generated stationary-phase promoters are not only transcriptionally active but also capable of executing complex biological function.

Discussion

This study developed a stable diffusion-based model, DiPro, for the de novo generation of promoters. Initial analyses demonstrated that the designed promoters accurately recapitulated key features of the natural training set, as evidenced by sequence logos, k-mer spectra, and functional motif distributions [37]. We then employed DiPro to design stationary-phase promoters by inpainting randomly masked regions within seed sequences. Subsequently, we developed a Bayesian-based machine learning model to cluster the generated promoters. Representative samples from each cluster were experimentally characterized, revealing that 81.6% exhibited induction activity. Furthermore, we found that these stationary-phase promoters were activated during three distinct stages (early, middle, and late). Leveraging this temporal precision, we used selected promoters to drive expression of the cell lysis protein CoIM, thereby regulating cell death. Notably, promoter P05 triggered cell lysis with high efficiency. This work establishes a novel methodological framework and validates its practical utility.

Generative networks are now well-established for promoter design. Pioneering GAN-based models demonstrated the feasibility of designing functional E. coli promoters [15], and when combined with a predictive network, enabled the design of promoters with tunable activity levels. Subsequently, GAN-based frameworks were adapted for designing eukaryotic promoters [16], enabling applications in Saccharomyces cerevisiae with potential extension to filamentous fungi and microalgae [38]. In more recent work, diffusion models have been applied. However, early implementations relied on a separate promoter recognition model to guide generation [39]. This limitation has been addressed by later approaches. To further improve the efficiency of promoter discovery and reduce experimental screening burden, we have integrated a promoter activity prediction module into the DiPro workflow, which is publicly available in our GitHub repository: https://github.com/wangxinglong1990/DiPro. This module supports the prediction of promoter authenticity, strength classification, and transcriptional activity estimation, and can be directly used to prioritize candidates generated by DiPro before experimental validation. In the present study, our stable diffusion-based model generates promoter sequences that mimic natural statistics without relying on an auxiliary recognition model. As an unconditional generative model, DiPro learns high-order sequence grammar and long-range contextual features beyond canonical −35 and −10 motifs, consistent with recent reports that functional promoters can retain activity without strict consensus binding sites. Our experimental observation that numerous generated sequences lacking perfect canonical motifs still exhibit robust stationary-phase activity further supports that DiPro captures hidden non-canonical regulatory determinants. We employed a Bayesian GMM clustering strategy to stratify generated candidates into functionally distinct subgroups, which achieved efficient screening and a high 81.6% functional success rate. Moreover, we introduced a novel Bayesian classification method to categorize promoters and facilitate the selection of candidates from distinct clusters. This integrated generative–predictive framework enables explicit interpretation of non-canonical regulatory elements and fully exploits the capacity of diffusion models to discover unknown regulatory patterns beyond known sequence signatures. Our study demonstrates that this combined approach can be effectively utilized for functional promoter design.

Regulating lysis protein expression using stationary-phase promoters offers a promising approach to enhance biosynthetic processes. For example, Jaishankar and Srivastava developed a stationary-phase promoter library via random mutagenesis and identified a promoter with 16-fold increased activity compared to its wild-type counterpart, enabling high-level gene expression [19]. Similarly, Beshay et al. utilized stationary-phase promoters to control the expression of the kil gene, enhancing the secretion of recombinant proteins in E. coli [40]. Selecting appropriate stationary-phase promoters is crucial for controlling cell lysis, as E. coli can recover cell density even after lysis [41]. This necessitates dynamic control of cell lysis to maintain sufficient cell density for intracellular recombinant protein production. We found that complete cell lysis hindered recovery, and prolonged low cell density was detrimental to protein accumulation. This study we found the stationary phase promoters with activity at different period, and we utilized these promoters successfully controlled the cell death.

This study revealed that both the design method and the resulting synthetic promoters can be utilized to control cell lifespan, highlighting the importance of developing diverse promoters for precise lifespan regulation in metabolic engineering. Notably, DiPro goes beyond recapitulating canonical −35 and −10 motifs and captures hidden regulatory features that support promoter activity even in the absence of strict consensus sequences, consistent with recent findings that functional promoters can rely on non-canonical regulatory grammar. While this study provides a proof of concept for designing stationary-phase promoters, further development will focus on interpreting non-canonical regulatory determinants and integrating activity prediction to fully unlock the capacity of diffusion models for de novo promoter discovery. Further experimental validation will also be performed to generate more diverse promoters and expand the toolbox for dynamic gene regulation in metabolic engineering.
