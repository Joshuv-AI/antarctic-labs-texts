Rapid changes of mRNA expressions of cardiac ion channels affected by Torsadogenic drugs influence susceptibility of rat hearts to arrhythmias induced by Beta‐Adrenergic stimulation
Authors: Katarina Hadova, Jana Kmecova, Katarina Ochodnicka‐Mackovicova, Eva Kralova, Gabriel Doka, Lenka Bies Pivackova, Peter Vavrinec, Tatiana Stankovicova, Peter Krenek, Jan Klimas
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractDrug‐induced long QT syndrome (LQTS) and Torsades de Pointes (TdP) are serious concerns in drug development. Although rats are a useful scientific tool, their hearts, unlike larger species, usually do not respond to torsadogenic drugs. Consequently, their resistance to drug‐induced arrhythmias is poorly understood. Here, we challenged rats with rapid delayed rectifier current (Ikr)‐inhibiting antibiotic clarithromycin (CLA), loop diuretic furosemide (FUR) or their combination (CLA + FUR), and examined functional and molecular abnormalities after stimulation with isoproterenol. Clarithromycin and furosemide were administered orally at 12‐h intervals for 7 days. To evaluate electrical instability, electrocardiography (ECG) was recorded either in vivo or ex vivo using the Langendorff‐perfused heart method under basal conditions and subsequently under beta‐adrenergic stimulation. Gene expression was measured using real‐time quantitative PCR in left ventricular tissue. Indeed, FUR and CLA + FUR rats exhibited hypokalemia. CLA and CLA + FUR treatment resulted in drug‐induced LQTS and even an episode of TdP in one CLA + FUR rat. The combined treatment dysregulated gene expression of several ion channels subunits, including KCNQ1, calcium channels and Na+/K + ‐ATPase subunits, while both monotherapies had no impact. The rat with recorded TdP exhibited differences in the expression of ion channel genes compared to the rest of rats within the CLA + FUR group. The ECG changes were not detected in isolated perfused hearts. Hence, we report rapid orchestration of ion channel reprogramming of hearts with QT prolongation induced by simultaneous administration of clarithromycin and furosemide in rats, which may account for their ability to avoid arrhythmias triggered by beta‐adrenergic stimulation.Keywords: clarithromycin, furosemide, LQTS, Torsades de pointes

INTRODUCTION

Torsades de pointes (TdP) is a life‐threatening 1 polymorphic ventricular tachycardia most frequently occurring in patients with long QT interval syndrome (LQTS). LQTS is associated with delayed ventricular repolarization 2 and is caused by either increased depolarizing Na+ and Ca2+ currents or decreased repolarizing K+ currents. 3 Morphologically, TdP is characterized by a twisting of the QRS complex, and it can both terminate spontaneously and occasionally degenerate into ventricular fibrillation and sudden cardiac death. 4

Certain evidence indicates that some drugs trigger acquired LQTS with subsequent risk of TdP. 5 , 6 Drug‐induced LQTS is determined by an interaction of the drug with cardiac ion channel subunits, with the rapid delayed rectifier current (I Kr) being the most commonly affected. 4 Importantly, drug‐induced TdP arises only in the presence of more than one risk factor, such as heart disease, metabolic and electrolyte abnormalities, and channelopathies. 7 , 8 Drug‐induced LQTS and TdP thus have become a reason for intensive non‐clinical and clinical proarrhythmic cardiac safety assessments during drug development. 7

Small rodents are the predominant species in basic and preclinical research due to their low cost, short life cycle, and easy genetic manipulation. However, rats are not appropriate surrogates for evaluating the torsadogenic potential of drugs. 9 , 10 In contrast to larger species, rat heart does not respond to torsadogenic drugs, 11 and this peculiar resistance to drug‐induced arrhythmias is poorly understood. Consequently, reports of in vivo recording of TdP in rat 12 , 13 are scarce. However, despite the marked differences in electrophysiology, fundamental mechanisms can potentially be identified in rodents, though validation in larger animals is still required. 14

Clarithromycin is a highly prescribed antibiotic worldwide, and its importance and therapeutic benefits were also demonstrated during the COVID‐19 pandemic. 15 However, as with some other macrolide antibiotics, 16 there is a certain risk of QT prolongation associated with clarithromycin use due to the blockade of the Kv11.1 (human ether‐a‐go‐go‐related gene hERG). 17 Also, furosemide belongs to the QT‐prolonging substances with a conditional risk of TdP, mainly due to drug–drug interactions. 18 , 19 Studies have shown both a potassium‐dependent 20 and potassium‐independent 21 association between the use of furosemide and QT interval prolongation. Nevertheless, the use of clarithromycin in hypokalemia is an explicit contraindication featured in the summary of product characteristics (SmPC). 22

Here, we aimed to evaluate cardiac electric stability during beta‐adrenergic stimulation in rats after co‐administration of clarithromycin and furosemide. We also sought to investigate acquired abnormalities in expression patterns of ion channels subunits participating in the action potential of rat heart. We hypothesized the presence of abnormalities on ECG or rhythm, especially QT interval prolongation, in this challenging environment, together with gene expression changes of ion channels subunits induced by aberrations in the electrophysiology of the rat heart. However, due to above‐mentioned peculiar resistance of the rodent heart to arrhythmias, we did not expect to observe life‐threating arrhythmias, such as TdP even after beta‐adrenergic stimulation.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals and treatment

In the study, male 16–18‐week‐old Wistar rats (Department of Toxicology and Laboratory Animal breeding, Dobra Voda, Slovak Republic) were used. The rats were kept under standard conditions and received food and water ad libitum. At the end of treatment period, the rats were housed for 24 h in metabolic cages to monitor the extent of diuresis. All experimental procedures involving the use of experimental animals were approved by the State Veterinary and Food Administration of the Slovak Republic and by the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Pharmacy, Comenius University Bratislava, Slovak Republic. The investigation conforms to the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals: Eighth Edition (2010) published by the US Committee for the Update of the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals; to the National Research Council and to the EU adopted Directive 2010/63/EU of the European Parliament and of the Council on the protection of animals used for experimental and other scientific purposes.

Treatment

Animals were randomized into four groups and treated with clarithromycin (Abbvie S.r.L, Italy; CLA, 100 mg/kg p.o), furosemide (Teva Operations Poland Sp. z.o.o., Poland; FUR, 100 mg/kg p.o.), or a combination of both (CLA + FUR) for 7 days at 12‐h intervals. Controls (CON) received vehicle. The clarithromycin and furosemide were suspended in a methylcellulose solution. All drugs were administered orally by gavage using a bulb‐tipped gastric gavage needle. At the end of treatment, a 12‐lead ECG (SEIVA EKG PRAKTIK) was recorded 1–2 h after the last administration of drugs, as the drugs reached the maximum plasma concentration at this time. 23 , 24

In vivo electrocardiography

The rats (280–320 g of weight) underwent electrocardiographic (ECG) measurements at basal conditions as well as during beta‐adrenergic stimulation with isoproterenol (Sigma‐Aldrich). The rats were anesthetized with tribromoethanol (Sigma‐Aldrich; 15 mL/kg, intraperitoneally), as this agent keeps the heart rate stable. 25 Standard 12‐lead electrocardiograms were recorded using needle electrodes placed subcutaneously. The heart rate was evaluated as an ECG cycle length (RR duration). The duration of QT was determined from the onset of the QRS complex to the end of the T wave (i.e., in the crossing of the isoelectric line) in either of the simultaneously recorded leads. Six consecutive beats were evaluated, and the arithmetic means of RR and QT were obtained. The duration of QT was corrected as QTc (in ms) = QT/(RR/150)1/2. 26

In vivo beta‐adrenergic stimulation

For experiments with the cumulative infusion of isoproterenol, a one‐lead ECG was measured using needle electrodes. Lead II was evaluated and the dose response curve for RR and QT was analyzed. 26 A cumulative dose of isoproterenol (0, 5, 10, 15, 20, 30, and 45 ng/min) was applied via vena jugularis under anesthesia.

Isolated heart electrocardiography

Additional ex vivo experiments were performed to discriminate between the direct effects of the drugs on QT (QTc) duration and the effects of electrolytes imbalance following furosemide application. The setting of the experiment was the same as that for the in vivo experiment. The rats were randomized into CON (n = 6), CLA (n = 6), FUR (n = 5), and CLA + FUR (n = 7) groups and treated as described above (section 2.2). As described previously, 27 the animals were pre‐heparinized (500 IU, i.p.). The hearts were excised under anesthesia (Thiopental 45 mg/kg, 5% solution, i.p. VUAB Pharma a.s.; Czech Republic), quickly cannulated and retrogradely perfused under constant pressure mode (90–100 cm H2O) according to the Langendorff method with a filtered Krebs–Henseleit solution containing NaCl 118.00 mmol/L, KCl 4.70 mmol/L, MgSO4.7H2O 1.66 mmol/L, KH2PO4 1.18 mmol/L, CaCl2.H2O 2.00 mmol/L, NaHCO3 25.00 mmol/L, and glucose 11.00 mmol/L (all chemicals from Centralchem, Slovakia) saturated with 95% O2 and 5% CO2 (pH 7.4 ± 0.05, 37°C). Bipolar ECG was recorded from spontaneously beating hearts using two platinum electrodes placed in the muscle of the free left ventricular wall. The first electrode was positioned into the muscle of the upper anterior wall and the second beneath the posterior wall. The ECG measurements were carried out at basal conditions or under stimulation by isoproterenol (10−7 M). Electrocardiograms were recorded in a 30‐minute stabilization period by a Powerlab 8/30. LV (AD Instruments) and analyzed using Chart 5 software for Windows (AD Instruments). Non‐sinus beats were excluded, and 10 consecutive sinus beats per recording were analyzed and averaged.

Determination of arrhythmias

All recordings were analyzed, and arrhythmias were determined according to The Lambeth Conventions (II): Guidelines for the study of animal and human ventricular and supraventricular arrhythmias. 11

Plasma potassium analysis

Heparinized blood samples were collected from the v. cava inferior. Plasma was obtained by gentle centrifugation (4000 rpm) at 4°C for 15 min. Electrolytes concentration was measured routinely by means of a commercially available diagnostic kit and using the ADVIA 1800® Clinical Chemistry System (Siemens AG). We used indirect ion‐selective electrode (ISE) methods to measure the concentrations of potassium.

RNA isolation and RT‐qPCR

Left ventricular tissue samples as sections through the tissue in the middle of free wall from rats were quickly frozen in liquid nitrogen and homogenized. Total RNA was isolated with TRI Reagent® (Sigma‐ Aldrich) according to the manufacturer's protocol. To prove the quality of the isolated RNA, electrophoresis in 2% of agarose gel (Agarose, Sigma‐ Aldrich) was carried out and subsequently reverse transcription was performed on intact RNA samples (High capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit with RNAse inhibitors; Applied Biosystems). Expressions of selected cardiac genes (see Table 1) were evaluated by quantitative PCR (RT‐qPCR) using the SYBR™ Select Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific) on a StepOnePlus™ Real‐Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific), as described previously. 28 , 29 , 30 The primers for qPCR were designed by Primer‐BLAST. 31 For the specific primer sequences, see Table 1. The relative expressions were calculated using efficiency correction, 32 with beta‐2‐microglobulin (B2M) and beta actin (ACTB) used to calculate the normalization factor according to Vandesompele. 33 The relative expressions with Cq > 35 were considered to be beyond detection limit and therefore not detectable.

RESULTS

Additive effect of furosemide on clarithromycin‐induced cardiac repolarization in vivo

Both clarithromycin as well as furosemide affected cardiac repolarization in vivo. While the effect of furosemide was only moderate, the effect of clarithromycin was considerably more pronounced, as exhibited by a prolonged QT (and QTc). We also observed an additive effect of furosemide on the clarithromycin‐induced increase in QT (and QTc) duration (Table 2, Figure 1A). However, the increased duration of QTc was differentially modulated by isoproterenol infusion between groups. While isoproterenol practically normalized QTc in the CLA group, this indicator of repolarization remained substantially elevated in the CLA + FUR group at rising isoproterenol concentrations (Figures 1B and 2). Moreover, we recorded dysrhythmias, including a period of Torsades de Pointes‐like polymorphic ventricular tachycardia in one rat, from the CLA + FUR group (further described as CLA + FURTdP) in the presence of isoproterenol infusion (Figure 3). However, we did not find any other anomalies in this particular rat (see Table 2). Importantly, no other rat manifested any irregularity during the whole ECG measurement; thus, the CLA + FURTdP rat was excluded from the statistical analysis because of its apparent clinical peculiarity.

Reduction of isoproterenol‐triggered arrhythmias in CLA + FUR ex vivo

Controversially, ex vivo experiments unveiled findings opposite to the prolonged QT interval observed in vivo. While furosemide and clarithromycin monotherapies caused no or only minimal aberrations in the recorded electrocardiograms, their combination surprisingly reduced QTc duration at basal condition and even considerably reduced the incidence of isoproterenol‐provoked dysrhythmias (Figure 4).

Combined clarithromycin and furosemide therapy results in cardiac molecular reprogramming with complex changes in the expression of ion channels

To better understand the ECG findings on the molecular level, we analyzed the expressions of several genes related to ion currents (Table 3). First, we observed a substantial elevation of mRNA expression of the KCNQ1 gene (also known as Kv7.1; KVLQT1) in CLA + FUR hearts but only a tendency toward elevation of Kcnh2, the gene encoding the target channel of clarithromycin inhibition (i.e., KCNH2, also known as Kv11.1; hERG). Expression of the remaining potassium channels subunits and HCN channels was not changed in CLA + FUR. Additionally, we observed universal enhancement in a panel of genes encoding Ca2+‐regulating proteins but a stable expression of the gene encoding the voltage‐gated sodium channel in CLA + FUR.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we detected drug‐induced LQTS in rats treated with a combination of clarithromycin and furosemide together with beta‐adrenergic stimulation. More notably, we also recorded TdP ventricular tachyarrhythmia in a single rat. This observation is surprising because the hearts of small animals like rats are known to resist the effects of torsadogenic drugs, especially I Kr inhibitors, on an ECG or rhythm. 11 One possible explanation of this resistance is the different ion currents controlling repolarization in rat compared to those in humans (the primary repolarization current in adult rats is I to, not I Kr 39 ). Moreover, ventricular arrhythmias in rat hearts tend to self‐terminate. 11 Owing to the small ventricular mass of the rat heart, it is not possible to maintain the re‐entrant circuits which give rise to TdP, 40 because re‐entry depends on tissue size. 10 Because of the lack of effect of torsadogenic drugs on the heart rhythm, rodents are even not recommended for non‐clinical assessment of the effect of pharmaceuticals on ventricular repolarization and proarrhythmic risk in vivo, unlike larger species, such as dog, monkey, swine, rabbit, ferret, and guinea pig. 39 These species are more prone to develop arrhythmia, including TdP, therefore, they are reported in TdP research more frequently (e.g., 41 , 42 , 43 , 44 , 45 , 46 ) Moreover, QT prolongation and TdP in these species have been shown to be associated with dysregulation of the mRNA of ion channel subunits. 47 Our noteworthy recording of TdP in rat suggests a disrupted resistance of rat heart to torsadogenic substrate and emphasizes the risk of co‐administration of clarithromycin and furosemide.

Both clarithromycin and furosemide belong to drugs independently associated with prolongation of QTc in everyday practice. 48 The major mechanism believed to be involved in clarithromycin‐induced QT prolongation is blockage of the I Kr. 49 Drug‐induced interference with I Kr is the most common cause of acquired LQTS/TdP. 50 However, rat heart weakly expresses the human form of the ion channel subunit controlling I Kr (Kv11.1). There is only one variant – rat ERG encoded by Kcnh2 expressed in the rat heart. 28 There is also evidence that I Kr blockers do not affect this current in rats 51 and that they do not affect the action potential in small rodents. 52 Nevertheless, our results are in accordance with studies reporting prolonged QTc in rat after combined therapy as well as clarithromycin alone. 49

Taking into account drug‐induced TdP, it occurs only in the presence of more than one risk factor. 7 According to the AHA/ACC/HRS guideline, one of the precipitating factors of TdP episodes is exposure to hypokalemia induced by diuretics. 53 Therefore, the use of furosemide inducing hypokalemia is contraindicated with simultaneous administration of clarithromycin due to the risk of arrhythmia. We confirmed the indirect effect of furosemide in combined treatment using isolated rat hearts. This enables the confounding effects of other organs, systemic circulation and circulating neurohormonal factors to be avoided. 54 Of note, our results point out the significance of furosemide presence in circulation in vivo, as we did not find QT prolongation or increased incidence of arrhythmias ex vivo. Thus, furosemide might provide an indirect drug interaction with clarithromycin possibly due to hypokalemia 49 leading to LQTS and TdP.

To reach increased cardiac demands, we applied isoproterenol, inducing beta‐adrenergic stimulation. It is well known that beta‐adrenergic stimulation with isoproterenol is used to suppress recurrent TdP in the refractory cases, since it shortens the QT interval and effective refractory period by increasing the heart rate. 2 This is congruent with our data from clarithromycin‐treated rats. However, CLA + FUR animals were additionally challenged by K+ deprivation caused by furosemide, further decreasing the repolarization reserve 55 necessary for orderly and rapid repolarization. 56 Disrupted repolarization reserve might therefore have prevented QT normalization in the CLA + FUR animals. Moreover, hypokalemia can provoke electrical instability during concomitant beta‐adrenergic stimulation, 57 which could have manifested as TdP in the CLA + FURTdP rat. Thus, beta‐adrenergic stimulation significantly affected the combined therapy in our experimental model, mimicking acquired LQTS.

Changed electrical vulnerability of cardiac tissue might result from a distinct gene profile. 58 The augmentation of the expression of genes encoding cardiac muscle myosin heavy chain (MYH6 and MYH7) associated with possible cardiac injury in CLA + FUR proves that the drug combination impacted the heart itself. 59 Therefore, rapid gene reprogramming might be the molecular basis of the electrical adaptability of the rat heart to a challenging environment manifested by complex alterations in ion channel expression patterns. Marked upregulation of the gene expression of calcium regulatory proteins, sodium/potassium‐transporting ATPase subunits and KCNQ1 seems to be a key player in preserving cardiac electrical stability in the CLA + FUR rats during stressful conditions. This notion is supported by results from the CLA + FURTdP rat, which exhibited a different expression trend of genes for the ion channels compared to the rest of the CLA + FUR animals. The CLA + FURTdP rat failed to upregulate the above‐mention genes and consequently developed TdP. A major contributor to stable repolarization, or large repolarization reserve, is the normal function of the rapid and slow delayed rectifier currents I Kr and I Ks. Bearing in mind I Kr blockade by clarithromycin, it is more likely that the reduced repolarization reserve with the further added stress of, for example, an I Kr blocking drug or a subtle genetic defect, is sufficient to precipitate TdP. 56 Therefore, the CLA + FURTdP rat failed to adapt to the increased requirements and exhausted the repolarization reserve, with consequent TdP. Together, our findings suggest orchestration changes in rat heart to ion channels genes participating in the adaptation to a challenging environment caused by clarithromycin and furosemide administration.

Some limitations of the present study need to be noted. There remains some debate surrounding the inhibition of I Kr by clarithromycin in rats, since some studies have reported no effect of I Kr blockers in rat. 51 On the other hand, the delayed rectifier potassium current (I K) inhibition by macrolide antibiotic erythromycin was confirmed in rat ventricular myocytes. 60 We did not perform functional measurement of the I Kr current, so we cannot report I Kr inhibition by clarithromycin. We also could not evaluate the function of the other ion channels, as we did not measure the ion currents. Our measurement of gene expression showed dysregulation of newly synthetized channels and a change in the demands of rat heart after treatment. However, altered expression also reasonably affects the current density, 61 but this is not the rule. 62 We assessed mRNA expression to show the changes to gene regulation, however, real protein expression does not necessarily have to describe the same trend. The appearance of TdP was observed in only one rat. Certainly, it is impossible to estimate the real occurrence of TdP in the whole population of Wistar rats, similarly as it is difficult to give an accurate number of the incidence of TdP in humans because cases of TdP are often not recognized or not reported. 63 Although our observation of TdP in a single rat is unique, for better clarification of the molecular background and statistics, a higher number of rats with TdP would confirm our results.

CONCLUSION

Conclusively, concomitant administration of clarithromycin and furosemide in rats resulted in QT prolongation during beta‐adrenergic stimulation. The occurrence of TdP in only one rat was in agreement with well‐known resistance of small rodents to torsadogenic drugs, and a measurement of TdP in rat is therefore rather surprising. We suggest that the arrhythmia resistance might be the result of the molecular adaptation of rat hearts to the challenging environment associated with observed deregulated ion channel genes, including KCNQ1, calcium channels, and adenosine triphosphatase subunits. This notion is supported by the unique data from the TdP rat, which failed to reprogram gene expression in response to the medication and consequently developed TdP. Further studies are necessary to clarify all molecular changes and ion current function.
