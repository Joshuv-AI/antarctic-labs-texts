Nursing care challenges and overcoming strategies among stroke patients
Authors: Priyanka P. Nayak, Sasmita Das, Mamata Swain
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Stroke remains a leading cause of disability and mortality globally. Nurses play a pivotal role in post-stroke recovery. This study aimed to assess the nursing care challenges and overcoming strategies related to varying levels of activity impairment among stroke patients.MATERIALS AND METHODS:A cross-sectional study was conducted in 2024, India, involving 100 nurses from the neurology ward and High Dependency Unit (HDU) at IMS and SUM Hospital, Bhubaneswar. Participants were selected using convenience sampling, and data were collected through interviews. The Barthel Index (BI) was used to assess stroke patients’ activity impairment. Nursing challenges were measured using the Nursing Care Challenges Assessment Scale (NCCAS), and strategies were evaluated with the Nursing Strategies for Stroke Care Scale (NSSCS). Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 22 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), applying descriptive statistics, Pearson correlation, and ANOVA, with a significance level of P < 0.05.RESULTS:A strong negative correlation (r = -0.827, P < 0.01) was found between nursing care challenges and the BI score, indicating that greater activity impairment was associated with increased nursing difficulties. Additionally, a strong positive correlation (r = 0.850, P < 0.01) was observed between nursing challenges and strategies used, suggesting that as challenges intensified, more advanced strategies were implemented.CONCLUSION:The severity of activity impairment in stroke patients significantly increases nursing care challenges, highlighting the need for tailored, evidence-based strategies. Strengthening nurse training and resource allocation is essential to optimize stroke care outcomes.

Introduction

Stroke remains a major global health burden and is the second leading cause of death and disability worldwide.[12] Among neurological disorders, it ranks fourth in contributing to Disability-Adjusted Life Years (DALYs).[3] The impact of stroke differs by population due to variations in prevalence, incidence, and case fatality rates.[4] In India, a systematic review covering 1960–2018 revealed that prevalence rates ranged from 44.54 to 150 per 100,000 overall, with higher variability in urban areas (45–487 per 100,000) compared to rural regions (55–388.4 per 100,000).[5] The incidence also showed wide variation: urban areas reported 33–123 per 100,000, while rural areas averaged 123.57 per 100,000. The 30-day case fatality rate ranged from 41.08% to 42.06% in urban settings and from 18% to 46.3% in rural populations.[6] Post-stroke impairments such as spasticity, hemiplegia, dysphagia, aphasia, visual deficits, mood disturbances, and cognitive dysfunction are common.[789] These limitations severely affect daily activities including mobility, feeding, bathing, and communication, thus increasing dependency on caregivers.[1011] Functional impairments not only reduce patient autonomy but also add significant burden to families, underscoring the need for robust rehabilitation strategies and caregiver support systems.[12]

Nurses play a central role in stroke recovery, providing physical care, psychological support, rehabilitation assistance, and patient monitoring.[13141516] One of the most critical components of this care is fall prevention. Stroke survivors have a heightened fall risk, which may result in injuries, longer hospital stays, and poorer recovery outcomes.[1718] Among the most disabling stroke outcomes is hemiparesis, partial paralysis or weakness on one side of the body, which impairs motor functioning and complicates activities of daily living.[19] To address this, nurses must manage a range of care challenges, such as feeding difficulties, pressure ulcer prevention, thrombosis management, and cognitive care.[20] The severity of these challenges often correlates with the patient’s level of activity impairment, which is commonly measured using the Barthel Index (BI), a validated tool for assessing independence in daily activities.[21]

Emerging research underscores the necessity for structured nursing strategies to support patient rehabilitation effectively. For instance, a review by Aqueveque et al. (2020)[22] explored novel rehabilitation technologies and emphasized the ongoing difficulty of motor recovery and the need for tailored interventions. Other studies have highlighted stroke survivors’ experiences in rehabilitation settings, pointing to psychological, environmental, and behavioral contributors to risks like falls, and advocating for preventive strategies aligned with individual patient needs.[23] Despite technological and therapeutic advances, gaps remain in understanding the specific challenges nurses encounter when caring for stroke patients and the strategies they employ to overcome these challenges.[2223] Much of the existing literature centers on patient outcomes, often overlooking the complexity of nursing roles in facilitating these outcomes. Addressing this gap is crucial—not only to enhance the quality of nursing care but also to inform policy development and improve multidisciplinary stroke rehabilitation models.

This study aimed to assess the nursing care challenges faced by nurses in managing stroke patients with varying levels of activity impairment, as measured by the BI. It also aimed to identify the specific strategies used by nurses to overcome these challenges, with the goal of informing evidence-based, patient-centered stroke care practices that enhance functional recovery and support nursing effectiveness.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

This quantitative, cross-sectional, correlational study was conducted at the Institute of Medical Science and SUM Hospital, Bhubaneswar, Odisha, India. The study spanned one year, from January 20, 2022, to January 20, 2023, and focused on understanding the relationship between stroke patients’ activity impairment levels and the nursing care challenges and strategies adopted by nurses. Data were collected from nurses working in the neurology ward and High Dependency Unit (HDU), where stroke rehabilitation and monitoring are routinely undertaken.

Study participants and sampling

Nurses were eligible to participate in the study if they were currently employed in the neurology ward or HDU of the selected tertiary care hospital, were directly involved in the care of stroke patients during the data collection period, and had a minimum of six months of clinical experience specifically in stroke care. Additional inclusion criteria included as provision of written informed consent. Nurses were excluded if they had less than six months of stroke care experience, were on leave or unavailable during data collection, held purely administrative or supervisory roles without direct patient care responsibilities, or had prior involvement in the development or pilot testing of the study tools to avoid potential bias.

The sampling technique used was purposive sampling. A total of 100 nurses participated. The sample size was calculated based on an expected proportion of stroke-related patient falls reported by nurses, which was estimated at 36% from the study by Bjartmarz et al.[24] Using the formula:

n = Z² × p(1−p)/d²

Where Z = 1.96 (95% confidence interval), p = 0.36, d = 0.09 (9% margin of error), the required sample size was approximately 98. To compensate for non-responses, the final sample was set at 100.

Data collection tools and techniques

Demographic tool

The demographic data in this study were collected using a structured Sociodemographic and Professional Profile Tool, specifically designed to capture relevant background variables of the participating nurses. This tool included items related to age, gender, educational qualification, years of clinical experience, ward posting, work shift pattern, previous stroke care training, type of residence, monthly income, and marital status. The tool was developed through a comprehensive literature review and expert validation to ensure content relevance and clarity. Its purpose was to identify potential associations between nurses’ backgrounds and the challenges they encounter in stroke care.

Barthel Index (BI)

The BI is a validated tool used to assess a stroke patient’s ability to perform activities of daily living (ADLs) independently. It includes 10 functional domains: feeding, bathing, grooming, dressing, bowel and bladder control, toilet use, transfers, mobility, and stair climbing. Each domain is scored based on the level of assistance needed, with total scores ranging from 0 (total dependency) to 100 (full independence). In this study, scores were categorized into four levels of impairment: very severe (0–20), severe (21–60), moderate (61–90), and mild (91–100), in line with existing literature.[2425] Lower scores indicated a higher degree of dependency, suggesting increased care needs, while higher scores reflected greater independence. The BI has been extensively validated across rehabilitation and acute care settings with high reliability (Cronbach’s α ranging from 0.89–0.95) and is widely used in stroke outcome studies.[2627]

Nursing Care Challenges Assessment Scale (NCCAS)

The Nursing Care Challenges Assessment Scale (NCCAS) is a self-developed instrument designed for this study to assess nurses’ perceived difficulty in managing stroke care challenges. The tool comprises six core domains: fall prevention, feeding difficulties, pressure ulcer management, cognitive support, emotional distress, and communication barriers. Each item is scored using a 4-point Likert scale: 0 = No difficulty, 1 = Mild difficulty, 2 = Moderate difficulty, and 3 = Highly difficult. The total score is calculated by summing the domain scores, with higher totals indicating greater perceived difficulty and care burden. For interpretive purposes, scores were classified into three categories: mild challenges (<40% of total score), moderate challenges (40–60%), and severe challenges (>60%). Higher scores reflect more complex care environments, often associated with patients having higher dependency and multidimensional needs. Psychometric testing showed strong reliability and validity. The instrument demonstrated high internal consistency in a pilot test involving 50 nurses (Cronbach’s α = 0.85). Content validity was confirmed through expert panel review (CVI = 0.88; CVR = 0.82), while exploratory factor analysis supported its construct validity. The domain structure and rating system align with prior validated frameworks used in neurology and stroke care contexts. Comparable structured tools have been used in previous research, including the works of Tarvonen-Schröder et al. and Bjartmarz et al., which aimed to systematically quantify nursing workload and task-specific burden in neurological and rehabilitative care settings.[2829]

Nursing Strategies for Stroke Care Scale (NSSCS)

This structured questionnaire was developed to assess the frequency with which nurses employ specific strategies to address stroke-related care challenges. The NSSCS evaluates how frequently nurses implement specific care strategies to overcome stroke-related challenges. Items are grouped under five domains: fall prevention, feeding support, pressure ulcer prevention, cognitive stimulation, and stress/anxiety reduction. Each is rated on a 4-point Likert scale (0 = Never used, 1 = Sometimes, 2 = Often, 3 = Very often).

The total strategy score reflects how actively and consistently the nurse applies recommended practices. Higher scores indicate greater use of evidence-based strategies, suggesting high engagement and proactive care. Lower scores reflect infrequent or limited strategy use, potentially pointing to gaps in training or workload constraints. The scores were interpreted as: Low strategy use: <40% of total score, Moderate use: 40–60%, High strategy use: >60%.

The tool showed good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.80), supported by strong content validity (CVI = 0.87, CVR = 0.84), and construct validity via factor analysis. This approach aligns with models in nursing care quality evaluation, and studies like El Sayed et al. (2022) and Aqueveque et al. (2020) have emphasized similar scoring approaches to guide care planning and staff training.[3031]

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee of the Institute of Medical Sciences and SUM Hospital, Bhubaneswar (IEC Regd. No.: ECR/627/Inst/OR/2014/RR-20; Letter No.: Ref.no./IEC/IMS.SH/SOA/2021/231). The study adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki[32] and the Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR) National Ethical Guidelines.[33] Written informed consent was obtained from all participants after providing detailed information about the study objectives, voluntary nature of participation, and confidentiality measures. No financial or material incentives were provided. Participants were assured that they could withdraw from the study at any time without any consequences. All collected data were anonymized and stored securely to maintain privacy and confidentiality.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 22 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics—frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations—were used to summarize demographic data and scale scores. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Results indicated that the data for nursing care challenge scores (W = 0.912, P = 0.002), strategy usage scores (W = 0.924, P = 0.004), and activity impairment scores (W = 0.937, P = 0.006) deviated from normal distribution. Despite this, parametric tests such as ANOVA and multiple linear regression were applied, as the sample size (N = 100) was sufficiently large to satisfy the assumptions of the Central Limit Theorem, which allows the use of parametric methods for moderately non-normal data in large samples. ANOVA was used to examine group differences in nursing challenges and strategy usage based on levels of activity impairment. Multiple linear regression was performed to assess the predictive relationship between activity impairment and both nursing care challenges and strategies used. Prior to regression, assumptions of linearity, multicollinearity, and homoscedasticity were tested and found to be satisfactory. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Correlation and regression analysis

The relationship between patient functional status, nursing care challenges, and the strategies employed was analyzed using Pearson’s correlation and linear regression techniques. As depicted in Figure 2, a strong and statistically significant negative correlation was found between the BI scores (reflecting Activities of Daily Living, or ADL) and nursing care challenge scores (r = –0.827, P < 0.01). This indicates that as a patient’s functional independence decreases, the complexity and frequency of nursing care challenges increase substantially.

Scatter plot showing correlation between nursing care challenges and patient activity impairment (Barthel Index score)

In contrast, Figure 3 shows a strong positive correlation between nursing care challenge scores and the extent of strategies implemented by nurses (r = 0.850, P < 0.01). This suggests that increased care complexity is associated with the adoption of more frequent and intensive nursing strategies. These findings highlight that nurses adapt their care approach proportionally to the level of patient dependency and care burden.

Scatter plot showing correlation between nursing care challenges and strategies employed

Table 5 presents the results of a linear regression analysis examining the extent to which activity impairment predicts nursing care challenges among stroke patients. The findings reveal a statistically significant negative relationship between activity impairment and nursing care challenges, with a regression coefficient of β = -0.162, t = -14.564, R² =0.684, and P < 0.001. This indicates that as patients’ functional independence decreases, the burden of nursing care increases considerably. The model explains approximately 68.4% of the variance in nursing care challenges, suggesting that activity impairment is a strong and independent predictor. In practical terms, patients with lower BI scores—indicating higher dependency—require more complex, intensive, and frequent nursing interventions. These results reinforce the importance of early assessment of functional status and personalized care planning in stroke rehabilitation settings.

Discussion

Sociodemographic factors and challenge perception

The present study identified no statistically significant association between nurses’ sociodemographic variables and their perceived level of nursing care challenges [Table 1]. This indicates a consistent experience of difficulty across different age groups, genders, educational backgrounds, and levels of clinical experience. Such uniformity reinforces the understanding that the challenges inherent in stroke care are systemic and task-specific rather than person-specific. This finding highlights the need for institutional strategies that support all nurses, irrespective of their background, thereby promoting equity in professional development and care delivery support systems.[34]

Severity of challenges in stroke care

The study revealed that a substantial number of nurses encountered moderate to severe challenges in caring for stroke patients, particularly related to workload pressure, time constraints, communication barriers, and limited specialized training [Table 2]. These results are consistent with the findings of Saramago et al.,[35] who noted similar stressors among stroke nurses due to the complex and urgent nature of care provision. Communication with patients suffering from aphasia or cognitive impairments and educating their families were particularly challenging, echoing the recurrent theme in literature that effective communication is central to quality stroke care.[35] The reported gaps in knowledge further emphasize the demand for structured, ongoing educational interventions, as supported by Dehghan et al.[34]

Patient impairment and nursing challenges

The severity of patient impairment was found to be significantly associated with the magnitude of nursing care challenges [Table 3]. As patient dependency increased, nurses faced proportionally greater burdens in areas such as feeding, repositioning, and preventing complications. These findings align with those of Joshi et al.,[36] who documented heightened caregiver stress in response to increased patient disability. Our correlation and regression analyses further reinforce this relationship, indicating that patient impairment is the most critical predictor of nursing difficulty. The absence of moderation by sociodemographic factors underscores the universal impact of patient acuity on care challenges. These findings advocate for the implementation of acuity-based staffing models, early rehabilitation protocols, and equitable distribution of high-dependency cases to optimize nursing effectiveness.

Coping strategies and overcoming techniques

Despite the intensity of care demands, nurses demonstrated proactive coping mechanisms, including reliance on peer support, practical problem-solving, and continuous learning [Table 4]. Peer collaboration was found to be an essential strategy for mitigating stress.[35] Nurses in this study frequently reorganized care routines and engaged in informal learning to enhance their care capacity. Similar patterns have been observed by Rohisha et al.,[37] who noted that access to accurate information and peer interaction significantly reduced psychological burden among frontline healthcare workers. While these adaptive strategies are commendable, the dependence on personal initiatives indicates an institutional gap. The absence of structured psychological support, such as debriefing sessions or resilience workshops, may place undue emotional strain on staff. This calls for a more systematized approach to staff wellness and professional resilience-building.

Insights from correlation and regression analyses

Statistical analyses revealed that patient functional status had a strong inverse correlation with nursing care challenges, while coping strategies exhibited a positive correlation [Figures 2 and 3]. These findings suggest a dynamic response pattern, where increased complexity of care demands is met with adaptive behavior by nurses. Regression analysis [Table 5] confirmed that patient impairment was the dominant predictor, explaining 68.4% of the variance in nursing challenge scores. Coping strategies contributed modestly to reducing perceived burden, indicating that while self-initiated approaches are beneficial, they are not sufficient alone. These findings align with those of Babapour et al.,[38] who identified job-related stress as a significant determinant of care quality and nurse well-being, emphasizing the necessity of combining personal strategies with institutional interventions.

Implications for practice and policy

The universal nature of the challenges observed in this study demands a comprehensive institutional response. It is critical for hospital administrators to address workload distribution, particularly in units with high-dependency patients. Ensuring optimal nurse-to-patient ratios and assigning support staff for physically demanding tasks can significantly alleviate burden. Structured interdisciplinary communication practices such as multidisciplinary rounds and targeted communication training can further enhance care coordination and reduce miscommunication-related stress. Additionally, formal family education sessions can reduce nurse-patient-family friction, promoting shared responsibility in care.

A key finding of this study was the urgent need for enhanced clinical training. Stroke care is highly specialized, and nurses require periodic, up-to-date instruction on stroke assessment, rehabilitation techniques, and complication prevention. Dehghan et al.[34] demonstrated that structured workshops significantly enhance emergency care competencies, a model that could be replicated in stroke units. Allocating protected time for education without increasing clinical workload would improve both participation and outcomes.

From a policy standpoint, the development of mentoring programs, wellness initiatives, and resilience training is recommended. Emotional strain, often unaddressed in nursing, can be mitigated through counseling, peer support networks, and psychological safety protocols. Noorian et al.[39] emphasized that multifaceted institutional support significantly enhances caregiver capacity and morale. Nurse managers should routinely evaluate workplace stress and invite staff feedback to develop responsive, context-specific support mechanisms.

Strengths and limitations

This study offers meaningful contributions to the understanding of how varying levels of stroke-related activity impairment influence nursing care challenges. Its use of a validated assessment framework, combined with a substantial sample size (N = 100), enhances the internal validity and supports the generalizability of the findings within similar clinical contexts. The integration of quantitative analysis with tool-specific validation strengthens the methodological rigor, allowing for nuanced insights into domain-specific challenges such as fall prevention, feeding, and pressure ulcer management. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. The single-center design may limit the external validity, as organizational structures, staffing patterns, and care protocols may differ across institutions. The reliance on self-reported data introduces the possibility of recall or social desirability bias, which could influence the accuracy of reported challenges and coping strategies. The cross-sectional nature of the study further restricts causal inferences, preventing conclusions about the directionality of observed relationships. Moreover, the exclusion of patient perspectives and clinical outcome measures narrows the scope of interpretation, limiting a holistic understanding of stroke care dynamics. Despite these constraints, the study provides a solid empirical foundation for future longitudinal and multicenter research. It underscores the need for integrated, patient-centered approaches and reinforces the importance of equipping nurses with the resources and training required to manage the evolving demands of stroke rehabilitation.

Conclusion

This study highlights the significant challenges nurses face in providing care to stroke patients, particularly as patient impairment increases. Tasks such as fall prevention, feeding assistance, and pressure ulcer management become more demanding with greater levels of disability. Importantly, these challenges are not influenced by nurse demographics, indicating that systemic and patient-related factors are the primary drivers of nursing strain. Despite these challenges, nurses employ adaptive strategies—including peer support, time management, and ongoing learning—to maintain care quality. Correlation and regression analyses confirm that while patient acuity is the strongest predictor of care burden, effective coping mechanisms can partially alleviate perceived strain. To improve outcomes for both patients and providers, healthcare institutions should align stroke care protocols with global benchmarks, such as the WHO’s Rehabilitation 2030 initiative. Recommendations include improving nurse-patient ratios, prioritizing stroke-specific training, integrating early rehabilitation practices, and providing psychosocial support. Strengthening infrastructure and workforce capacity in neurology and high-dependency units is essential. Overall, investing in nursing support systems and evidence-based policies will enhance care quality, reduce provider burnout, and ensure that stroke patients receive optimal, recovery-focused care.

Recommendations

To strengthen stroke care, regular competency-based training should be integrated into nursing education. Quarterly workshops, simulation drills, and interdisciplinary case reviews are recommended to enhance clinical preparedness and teamwork. Mentorship programs pairing junior nurses with experienced staff should be established to support skill development and emotional resilience. A stroke care resource toolkit including quick-reference guides and communication aids should be developed to assist in addressing common clinical challenges. Brief daily huddles are advised to foster peer support and rapid problem-solving. Nursing assignments should reflect patient acuity, with severely impaired patients receiving lower nurse-to-patient ratios or additional support. Access to rehabilitation services and basic rehab training for nurses should be ensured. Routine assessments of nurse workload and stress using surveys or focus groups are recommended to guide timely interventions. Overall, structured training, acuity-based staffing, resource support, and regular monitoring are essential to improve nurse performance and patient outcomes in stroke care.
