Double trouble: Hybrid management of inappropriate sinus tachycardia and sinus bradycardia in the era of cardioneuromodulation

Introduction

Tachycardia-bradycardia syndrome is typically diagnosed in patients who present with symptomatic sinus node dysfunction in combination with other supraventricular tachyarrhythmias. In this article, we describe tachycardia-bradycardia syndrome caused exclusively by dysfunction of the sinoatrial node (SAN). We propose a new nomenclature for this phenomenon: “tachy-brady SAN syndrome.” We also explore potential management strategies, particularly in the context of emerging techniques such as cardioneuroablation (CNA)1, 2, 3 and SAN hybrid sinus node sparing (SNS) ablation.4, 5, 6

Case report

A 31-year-old fit woman was referred to a neurocardiology clinic for electrophysiological and autonomic evaluation because of the co-occurrence of symptomatic inappropriate sinus tachycardia (IST) and intermittent episodes of severe symptomatic sinus bradycardia (SB). The bradycardia episodes occurred ∼8 times per year and were exclusively associated with infections, even mild ones.

The patient had been working as a farmer for over 5 years, engaging in high-intensity physical activity daily. Interdisciplinary consultations and examinations ruled out both physiological and pathological causes of sinus tachycardia. The patient’s echocardiography revealed normal cardiac function. An exercise stress test showed persistent sinus tachycardia before and throughout the test, with very good exercise capacity (up to 16 metabolic equivalent tasks) and only mild dizziness at peak exertion.

Thirty-day Holter electrocardiographic (ECG) monitoring showed a mean daily sinus heart rate of 95 beats/min, with documented episodes of severe nonparoxysmal sinus tachycardia episodes reaching 186 beats/min, accompanied by palpitations, presyncope, and dizziness (Figure 1). In contrast, each infection was associated with intermittent, severe resting SB episodes (<30–35 beats/min), leading to presyncope, dizziness, and profound fatigue. A cardiovascular autonomic test using Finapres NOVA (Finapres Medical Systems) with beat-to-beat blood pressure monitoring showed normal responses for all the following: Valsalva maneuver, deep breathing test, carotid sinus massage, active stand test and tilt test. The only abnormal finding was IST, without features of postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome (POTS) or other dysautonomic features. An atropine challenge test (0.02 mg/kg intravenous) confirmed sinus tachycardia, with the heart rate increasing from 100 to 140 beats/min within 10 minutes. The Malmö POTS symptom score (MAPS) yielded a score of 47. The patient also reported exertion-related symptoms, including muscle weakness, sudden loss of arm strength, and syncope. These findings were consistent with cardiovascular autonomic dysfunction (CVAD) and symptomatic SB, limiting pharmacologic treatment options.

Several management strategies were discussed with the patient:1.Pacemaker implantation combined with ivabradine and bisoprolol, with subsequent SAN modification or ablation, if necessary. The potential risk of lead damage because of intense physical activity was highlighted.2.CNA, acknowledging the risk of worsening IST, with the option of initiating pharmacologic therapy before second-stage hybrid SNS ablation.3.Direct hybrid SNS ablation, with confirmation of SAN parasympathetic denervation via extracardiac vagal nerve stimulation, followed by a 4-week cardiac rehabilitation program.

After shared decision making, the patient chose the second option. She was referred for an electrophysiology study (EPS), performed both with and without general anesthesia. At baseline, the corrected sinus node recovery time (cSNRT) was normal (410 ms). Under general anesthesia, a repeat EPS revealed an abnormal cSNRT (550 ms), and her resting sinus rhythm slowed to 50 beats/min. Right-sided extracardiac vagal nerve stimulation via the right jugular vein induced 15-second asystole and 17-second complete atrioventricular block (Figure 2). Six ganglionated plexus sites were mapped and ablated: left superior, left inferior, left superior paraseptal, left inferior paraseptal, right superior paraseptal, and right inferior paraseptal (180–240 seconds per site, 40 W, 18 mL/min irrigation) (Figure 2). The sinus rate increased from 50 to 85 beats/min, and the cSNRT normalized to 300 ms. A subsequent 2-mg intravenous atropine challenge produced no further increase in sinus rate or change in the Wenckebach point within 10 minutes. The patient was discharged on dabigatran (150 mg twice daily) and ivabradine (5 mg twice daily). Because of previous adverse effects from β-blockers, including severe episodes of bradycardia during minor infections, the patient declined β-blocker monotherapy. During the first 6 months, IST symptoms persisted despite increasing ivabradine to 10 mg twice daily. There was no improvement in symptom control during intense physical activity, with smartwatch recordings showing sinus rhythm reaching up to 180 beats/min and episodes of presyncope only. After another mild infection, combination therapy with ivabradine (5 mg twice daily) and bisoprolol (2.5 mg once daily) was initiated. This led to a marked improvement in symptoms, with MAPS decreasing from 47 to 24—a value comparable to healthy individuals. Seven-day Holter ECG monitoring showed satisfactory sinus rhythm control, with a mean heart rate of 77 beats/min and a maximum of 140 beats/min. After this improvement and shared decision making, the patient was no longer considered for hybrid SNS ablation and was approved to continue combined treatment with ivabradine and bisoprolol, along with the use of birth control pills.

Discussion

This case highlights a complex diagnostic and therapeutic challenge, requiring careful consideration of multiple clinical management strategies aligned with current guidelines7, 8, 9 and recent advancements in interventional neurocardiology, particularly CNA1, 2, 3 and hybrid SNS ablation.4, 5, 6

The patient exhibited 2 distinct manifestations of sinus node dysfunction: persistent IST,10, 11, 12 occurring daily, and intermittent episodes of severe SB.

In this case, tachycardia-bradycardia syndrome was attributed solely to the dysfunction of the SAN. The persistent tachycardia was punctuated by episodes of symptomatic bradycardia, constituting what we refer to as “tachy-brady SAN syndrome.” This pattern significantly limited pharmacologic treatment options. After shared decision making, an invasive approach was pursued, including an EPS, CNA, and hybrid SNS ablation. Before the availability of these techniques, treatment would have relied on pacemaker implantation for intermittent SB (class IC/IB),8,9 combined with intensive pharmacologic therapy for IST (class IIaB/IIaC).7

Importantly, CNA has been associated with the potential onset or worsening of IST or POTS. For example, in a cohort of patients treated for vasovagal syncope, up to 28% required pharmacologic treatment for symptomatic sinus tachycardia during 12-month follow-up after CNA and 7% still required it after 12 months.1 In our case, CNA did not exacerbate IST symptoms. Instead, it served as a therapeutic bridge, facilitating the initiation of pharmacologic treatment for severe IST.

Patients with CVAD often present with a variety of overlapping phenotypes, including IST, POTS, vasovagal syncope (with cardioinhibitory, mixed, or vasovagal mechanisms), orthostatic hypotension and hypertension, inadequate resting SB, and vagally mediated bradyarrhythmias. These complex presentations, particularly in the context of post–coronavirus disease syndrome, are increasingly diagnosed and managed.13 Effective management of complex CVAD requires a comprehensive diagnostic approach, including prolonged ECG monitoring, cardiovascular autonomic test, and home-based ECG monitoring with symptom correlation. Dedicated tools, such as MAPS, offer practical and reliable ways to evaluate disease progression, validate symptom severity, and monitor treatment effectiveness over time.14 Thanks to the global adoption of CNA and hybrid SNS ablation in recent years, shared decision making has become more achievable, especially in younger, active patients with complex CVAD.15 As a result, clinicians can now explore a broader range of management options beyond traditional guideline-based approaches. These include class IB recommendations for pacemaker implantation in the treatment of symptomatic, documented SB and class IIaB/IIaC recommendations for high-dose pharmacologic therapy (eg, ivabradine and/or bisoprolol) in the treatment of IST.

Although the treatment strategy described in this case led to a favorable outcome, it should not be considered universally applicable. Caution is warranted because of the potential for IST exacerbation after CNA. Nevertheless, this case illustrates a successful management approach for patients with coexisting IST, POTS, and vagally mediated bradycardia. Notably, it is uncommon to encounter young and physically active individuals with both symptomatic IST and intermittent or episodic vagally mediated SB. The management of such cases is often complex and requires a personalized evaluation. Advances in cardioneuromodulation, ablation techniques, telemonitoring, pharmacotherapy, and pacemaker strategies have enabled the development of individualized hybrid treatment pathways. The integration of shared decision making with both invasive and noninvasive approaches remains the gold standard for delivering personalized preventive care.
