High Endothelial Venules in Small Cell Lung Cancer: Prognostic Subtypes and Therapeutic Implications for Immunoradiotherapy
Authors: Jiatian Fang, Zilian Wang, Hui Wang, Jianxin Xue, Kai Kang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSmall cell lung cancer (SCLC) is a highly aggressive neuroendocrine malignancy with an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, and current immunotherapy provides limited benefit. This highlights the need to identify microenvironmental features enabling effective immune responses. High endothelial venules (HEVs) are critical for lymphocyte recruitment and antitumor immunity, yet their roles in SCLC remain poorly understood. To investigate HEV‐related features among SCLC patients, five bulk RNA‐seq datasets comprising 583 tumor samples were integrated. HEV‐related signatures stratified SCLC patients into two subtypes, C1 and C2, associated with HEV‐high and HEV‐low states, respectively. C1 showed a more immune‐active microenvironment and superior prognosis. Consistently, using single‐cell RNA‐seq data and validating the findings by immunofluorescence staining in an independent cohort of 80 patients with SCLC, we confirmed that the presence of HEV structures was associated with a favorable prognosis. We further established a prognostic model and identified five genes, PDCD1, CXCL9, ITK, ITGAL, and SH2D1A, as key favorable prognostic factors. A nomogram incorporating age, tumor stage, and the prognostic model was also developed and exhibited satisfactory performance. Additionally, we explored the translational potential of promoting HEV formation as a therapeutic strategy in SCLC. In murine SCLC models, radiotherapy combined with immunotherapy promoted HEV formation and enhanced antitumor efficacy. HEVs appeared to serve as a critical link underlying the therapeutic synergy between radiotherapy and immunotherapy. Collectively, these findings highlight the biological and clinical relevance of HEVs in SCLC and suggest that combination strategies aimed at promoting HEV formation may help overcome the limited efficacy of immunotherapy.

Introduction

Small cell lung cancer (SCLC), representing approximately 15% of all lung cancer cases, is an aggressive neuroendocrine (NE) carcinoma characterized by rapid growth, early metastasis, and frequent therapeutic resistance, leading to poor long‐term outcomes [1, 2]. SCLC is typically classified into limited‐stage (LS) and extensive‐stage (ES) disease. Concurrent chemoradiotherapy represents the standard of care for LS‐SCLC, while chemotherapy combined with immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) serves as the first‐line regimen for ES‐SCLC [3]. Importantly, SCLC is associated with a profoundly immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME), which limits effective antitumor immune responses and contributes to the modest benefit of ICB [4]. As a result, only a subset of patients derive durable benefit from immunotherapy [5]. These challenges underscore the need to better define the immunobiological heterogeneity of SCLC, identify robust predictive biomarkers, and develop strategies to improve immunotherapeutic efficacy.

High endothelial venules (HEVs) are specialized blood vessels that mediate lymphocyte trafficking to lymph nodes and other secondary lymphoid organs [6]. A lack of sufficient intratumoral CD8+ T lymphocytes is a key obstacle to effective cancer immunotherapy; however, HEVs can form within tumors and serve as major sites for lymphocyte entry at baseline and following treatment with anti‐programmed death protein 1 (PD‐1)/anti‐programmed death‐ligand 1 (PD‐L1) immunotherapy [7]. The presence of HEVs has been associated with favorable patient prognosis and improved responses to ICB [8, 9].

Despite the established role of HEVs in enhancing lymphocyte trafficking and antitumor immunity in several cancers such as breast cancer and glioblastoma [10], their relevance in the TME of SCLC remains largely unexplored. Therefore, comprehensive characterization of HEVs in SCLC is essential to elucidate disease heterogeneity [11] and support precise therapeutic stratification. In this study, we classified SCLC into HEV‐high and HEV‐low subtypes based on bulk RNA‐sequencing data. By integrating these findings with single‐cell RNA‐sequencing (scRNA‐seq) data and the immunofluorescence staining of tumor sections from SCLC patients, we characterized the clinical and prognostic significance of HEVs in SCLC. Furthermore, murine models were used to investigate the induction of HEVs by immunoradiotherapy and to define their role in mediating treatment efficacy, thereby supporting HEV formation as a potentially translatable mechanism underlying immunotherapy sensitization.

Materials and Methods

Data Source and Preprocessing

Comprehensive multi‐dimensional datasets, including transcriptome sequencing data, matched clinical data, and somatic mutation calls, were systematically compiled from five independent cohorts reported in the published literature. The expression profile contains 583 tumor samples, as follows: 271 from Nabet et al. [12], 107 from Liu et al. [13], 81 from George et al. [14], 79 from Jiang et al. [15], and 45 from Wang et al. [16], as well as 146 normal lung tissue samples: 107 from Liu et al. [13], 7 from Jiang et al. [15], and 32 from Wang et al. [16]. A total of 555 matched clinical annotations were filtered to exclude samples lacking available overall survival (OS) data and pathological stage information, with 521 cases remaining. Somatic mutation calls were collected from Liu et al. [13] and George et al. [14].

The expression profile was converted into transcripts per million (TPM) format and subjected to log2(TPM + 1) transformation for normalization. Then batch effects across the five cohorts were eliminated with the R package Surrogate Variable Analysis (SVA) (v3.58.0). The expression data of tumor samples were randomly split into a training set (n = 408) and a validation set (n = 175) at a 7:3 ratio, with paired clinical data available for 367 and 154 samples, respectively. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed to evaluate the effectiveness of batch effect removal. A total of 39 HEV‐related signatures were curated from previously published studies [17, 18] (Table S1). We also leveraged scRNA‐seq datasets from previous studies to explore the link between the abundance of HEV and survival outcomes [19, 20]. The flowchart of this study is displayed in Figure 1A.

HEV‐Based Subtyping

Subtypes were identified by de novo non‐negative matrix factorization (NMF) [21] based on the expression profiles of HEV‐related genes with the R package NMF (v0.28). To determine the optimal cluster number, cophenetic correlation coefficients were computed across an incremental range of cluster k values, and the k value corresponding to the steepest decline in cophenetic correlation was designated as the optimal clustering solution. Gene set variation analysis (GSVA) was employed to verify subtype classification based on the expression patterns of HEV‐related genes using the R package GSVA (v2.2.0). The subtype classification was correlated with established SCLC molecular subtypes with the clinical annotations from Nabet et al. [12], which comprised the largest cohort (n = 194) in our training set.

Differential Expression Analysis and Functional Enrichment Analysis

Differential expressed gene (DEG) analysis was performed using the R package limma (v3.66.0). DEGs between tumor and normal tissues were filtered with an absolute value of log2 fold change (|log2FC|) > 2 and an adjusted p value < 0.05, while DEGs between distinct HEV‐based subtypes were screened with a |log2FC| > 1 and an adjusted p value < 0.05.

Functional enrichment analyses were performed with the R package clusterProfiler (v4.16.0), including Gene Ontology (GO) over‐representation analysis (ORA) and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) with the HALLMARK gene sets retrieved from the molecular signature database (MSigDB) using the R package msigdbr (v25.1.1). All gene identifiers were standardized using the R package org.Hs.eg.db (v3.21.0).

Immune Infiltration and Immunotherapy Response Prediction

TME analysis was conducted using the R package ESTIMATE (v1.0.13) to calculate stromal, immune, and ESTIMATE scores for each sample [22]. In parallel, the CIBERSORT algorithm was applied to estimate the relative proportions of 22 immune cell types in the TME with 500 permutations [23]. The ICB therapy response of each sample was evaluated based on tumor immune dysfunction and exclusion (TIDE) score with the Python package TIDE (v1.3.8) [24, 25].

Somatic Mutation Burden Analysis

The R package maftools (v2.26.0) [26] was employed to process the mutation data and calculate tumor mutation burden (TMB), the number of non‐synonymous mutations per mega‐base.

Prognostic Model Construction and Validation

Based on the subtype‐specific DEGs, univariable Cox regression analysis was performed to screen for prognosis‐related genes (adjusted p < 0.05), followed by multivariable Cox regression analysis to further identify independent prognostic genes (p < 0.05). For each candidate gene, samples were stratified by median expression level, with prognostic relevance evaluated via Kaplan–Meier (K–M) survival curves and log‐rank tests (p < 0.05) with the R package survminer (v0.5.1).

The R package Mime1 (v0.0.0.9000) [27] was utilized to integrate 10 machine learning (ML) algorithms‐Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO), Ridge regression, Elastic net, Step Cox regression, survival support vector machine, CoxBoost, supervised principal components, partial least squares regression for Cox models, Random Survival Forest, and gradient boosting machine (GBM)‐and 101 algorithm combinations for the construction of a robust survival risk prediction framework. Harrell's concordance index (C‐index) was computed, and the model with the highest C‐index in the validation set was designated as the optimal model. The prognostic value of identified candidate genes was evaluated across pan‐cancer types using the Cancer Immunology Data Engine (CIDE) platform [28]. Risk scores were calculated using the optimal model, and patients were stratified as high−/low‐risk groups by the median risk score. The prognostic performance for 1‐, 2‐, and 3‐year OS was evaluated via time‐dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves with the R package timeROC (v0.4) and K–M survival curves in training and validation cohorts.

Construction of the Nomogram

Univariable (p < 0.1) and multivariable (p < 0.05) Cox regression analyses identified independent prognostic factors from clinical characteristics and optimal model‐derived risk scores. A nomogram was constructed to predict 1‐, 2‐, and 3‐years OS, and its predictive performance was evaluated using ROC curves, decision curve analysis (DCA), calibration, and K–M survival curves.

Single‐Cell RNA Sequencing Analysis

ScRNA‐seq data and corresponding clinical information from patients with SCLC were obtained from a previously published dataset [19]. Based on the original cell‐type annotations provided in that study, we selected 18 patient samples in which endothelial cells (ECs) were identified for downstream analyses. Sixteen of these patients had matching survival information that could be used for subsequent survival analysis.

Murine scRNA‐seq data were derived from our previously published dataset [20]. Tumor samples were collected on Day 9 after treatment initiation from mice subjected to different therapeutic regimens. Each treatment group included two biological replicates, resulting in a total of eight tumor samples. ECs were extracted according to the original cell‐type annotations reported in the source publication.

All downstream analyses of ECs from both human and mouse datasets were conducted using the R package Seurat (v4.3.0). Raw expression matrices were processed following the standard Seurat workflow. Briefly, gene expression data were normalized, highly variable genes were identified, and the data were scaled while regressing out mitochondrial gene expression and ribosomal gene expression ratios.

PCA was performed on the scaled expression matrix of highly variable genes. To correct for batch effects across samples, the R package harmony (v0.1.0) was applied to the PCA embeddings. Based on inspection of the elbow plot, the top 20 principal components were retained for subsequent analyses. Cell clustering was then performed using the FindNeighbors and FindClusters functions in Seurat, which construct a shared nearest neighbor graph followed by community detection using the Louvain algorithm.

Following initial clustering, cell clusters were annotated based on the expression of established canonical marker genes. DEG analysis was conducted using the R package Model‐based Analysis of Single‐cell Transcriptomics (MAST) (v1.20.0). For each endothelial subpopulation, DEGs were defined as genes exhibiting a log2FC greater than 0.5 relative to all other clusters and an adjusted p value < 0.05. ORA based on the identified DEGs was performed using the R package clusterProfiler (v4.16.0).

Mice and Tumor Models

Six‐ to eight‐week‐old male C57BL/6J mice were purchased from Gempharmatech Co. Ltd. (Jiangsu, China). Mice were housed and bred under specific pathogen‐free conditions at Sichuan University with sterilized food, bedding, and water. Mice were maintained at a controlled temperature of 23°C ± 2°C and a relative humidity of 30%–70% under a 12‐h light/12‐h dark cycle (lights on from 06:00 to 18:00).

Murine SCLC models were constructed as previously described [20, 29]. Briefly, C57BL/6J mice were subcutaneously inoculated in the right hind limb with 1 × 105 PRM‐SCLC tumor cells, which were kindly provided by Prof. Chong Chen. The tumor volumes were measured with calipers using the formula volume = (a × b2)/2, in which a and b are the largest and smallest perpendicular diameters, respectively. Once tumors reached an approximate volume of 100 mm3, mice were randomly allocated into different treatment groups.

Prior to irradiation, mice were anesthetized and positioned in a custom‐designed lead shielding device to ensure that only the tumor area was exposed to radiation. Irradiation was performed using an RS‐2000 X‐ray irradiator (Rad Source Technologies, Alpharetta, GA, USA) operated at 160 kV and 25 mA with a dose rate of 1.836 Gy/min, using 0.3 mm copper filtration and a source‐to‐surface distance of 30 cm. From the day of treatment initiation, anti‐PD‐1 antibody (InVivoMAb, clone RMP1‐14) was administered intraperitoneally at a dose of 200 μg per mouse every 3 days. To block HEVs in vivo, an anti‐peripheral node addressin (PNAd) antibody (BioLegend, clone MECA‐79) was administered intraperitoneally at 100 μg per mouse on days 1, 4, and 7 after treatment initiation. Mice were euthanized when tumor volume exceeded 2000 mm3. Survival was calculated from the first day of treatment until death.

Multiplex Immunofluorescence

A tissue microarray (TMA) containing tumor specimens from 80 patients with SCLC was obtained from Superbiotek (Shanghai, China). For murine experiments, tumor tissues from each treatment group were harvested on Day 9 after treatment initiation, fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for at least 48 h, and subsequently processed into formalin‐fixed paraffin‐embedded (FFPE) blocks. FFPE samples were sectioned at a thickness of 4 μm and subjected to multiplex immunofluorescence staining. The following primary antibodies were used: anti‐PNAd (Biolegend, 120,802, 1:50), anti‐CD31 (Abcam, 182,981, 1:500), anti‐CD31 (Maixin, MAB‐0720, prediluted), anti‐CD3 (CST, 85061, 1:200), anti‐CD8 (CST, 98941, 1:100), anti‐PD‐1 (CST, 84651, 1:100), and anti‐T cell factor 1 (TCF1) (CST, 2203, 1:100).

Slides were processed on the staining module for de‐paraffinization, epitope retrieval, and endogenous peroxidase quenching. Multiplex staining was performed in sequential rounds, each consisting of nonspecific site blocking (Discovery Inhibitor and Discovery Goat Ig Block, Ventana), incubation with unlabeled primary antibodies, detection with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)‐conjugated secondary antibodies (Discovery OmniMap anti‐rabbit, anti‐goat, and anti‐mouse; Ventana), and labeling with Opal reactive fluorophores, which covalently tag the primary epitope. Each staining round was followed by a heat‐induced denaturation step to remove both primary and secondary antibodies before the next cycle. Nuclear counterstaining was performed with spectral 4′,6‐Diamidino‐2‐phenylindole (DAPI) (Akoya). Images were acquired using the Vectra Polaris Automated Quantitative Pathology Imaging System (Akoya). Quantification of HEV structures and vascular areas was conducted using QuPath (v0.4.4). A classifier was trained by manual annotation of representative positive and negative regions and subsequently applied to all sections for automated quantitative analysis.

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were performed using R (v4.5.1), Python (v3.10.9), and GraphPad Prism (v9.5.0). Continuous variables were compared via Wilcoxon rank‐sum tests, and categorical variables via Pearson chi‐square or Fisher's exact tests. The p values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction. Tumor growth curves were analyzed using two‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey's multiple‐comparison test for comparisons involving more than two treatment groups or Sidak's multiple‐comparison test for two‐group comparisons at individual time points. Survival differences were assessed with log‐rank tests. All statistical tests were two‐tailed, with statistical significance defined as p < 0.05.

Results

Differential Expression Analysis Between Tumor and Normal Tissues

Five independent cohorts were integrated, and batch effects were effectively eliminated (Figure S1A–C). PCA was then conducted to visualize differences in gene expression patterns between tumor and normal samples (Figure 1B). Across SCLC tumor and normal tissues, a total of 536 DEGs were identified, with 319 genes upregulated and 217 genes downregulated in tumor tissues. Notably, tumor tissues exhibited elevated expression of achaete‐scute homolog 1 (ASCL1) and neuronal differentiation 1 (NEUROD1), two genes closely associated with the NE phenotype of SCLC (Figure 1C).

Subsequently, GSEA revealed that cell cycle‐related pathways, including E2F targets and G2/M checkpoint, were significantly upregulated, whereas immune‐related pathways, such as inflammatory response and interferon gamma response, were significantly downregulated (Figure 1D). ORA was further performed to characterize the biological processes associated with SCLC tumors, demonstrating that upregulated genes in SCLC tumors were primarily enriched in cell cycle‐related pathways (Figure 1E), while downregulated genes were mainly associated with immune response, inflammation, and antigen presentation (Figure 1F). These comparisons illustrated that SCLC exhibited an enhanced proliferative potential and an immunosuppressive TME.

HEV‐Based Subtype Characterization

To explore the role of HEV in SCLC, we focused on HEV‐related signatures as the basis for subsequent analyses and investigated their protein–protein interactions (Table S1 and Figure S2A). NMF was performed on transcriptomic data from 408 patient samples in the training set to identify SCLC classification based on HEV‐related genes. Cophenetic correlation coefficients identified k = 2 as the optimal clustering number, corresponding to a clear clustering pattern in the consensus matrix (Figure S2B,C).

Consequently, the samples were divided into two subtypes: C1 (n = 153) and C2 (n = 255) (Figure S2D). Comparative analysis revealed distinct expression patterns of HEV‐related genes between the two subtypes, with subtype C1 showing elevated expression of genes involved in lymphocyte homing, including C‐C motif chemokine ligand 19 (CCL19), C‐C motif chemokine ligand 21 (CCL21), and intercellular adhesion molecule 1 (ICAM1) (Figure 2A). Correspondingly, subtype C1 also exhibited a significantly higher GSVA score for the HEV‐related signatures compared to C2 (Figure 2B). Accordingly, we defined C1 as the HEV‐high subtype and C2 as the HEV‐low subtype. We then evaluated the association between NMF‐derived subtypes and clinical outcomes. K–M survival analysis demonstrated that patients in C1 had significantly longer OS than those in C2, indicating that the two subtypes of SCLC correlated with distinct clinical outcomes (Figure 2C). We additionally explored the association between our NMF‐derived HEV subtypes and the major SCLC molecular subtypes [30] (Figure 2D). Specifically, the C2 subtype, characterized by a lower HEV abundance, was predominantly associated with the NE SCLC‐A (ASCL1) and SCLC‐N (NEUROD1) subtypes. In contrast, the C1 subtype with a higher HEV abundance contained a greater proportion of non‐NE SCLC‐I (Inflamed) and SCLC‐P (POU2F3) subtypes.

To further elucidate the biological differences between C1 and C2 subtypes, we investigated their pathway enrichment and immune landscape. A total of 590 DEGs were identified between C1 and C2, with 176 downregulated and 414 upregulated in C1 (Figure 2E). Notably, immune‐related genes including programmed cell death 1 (PDCD1), ICAM1, and C‐X‐C motif chemokine ligand 9 (CXCL9) exhibited significantly higher expression levels in C1. ORA consistently revealed that these upregulated genes were primarily enriched in immune‐related pathways (Figure 2F). Additionally, we performed GSEA, which demonstrated C1 was enriched in immune activation signatures but depleted in cell proliferation and cell cycle‐related pathways (Figure 2G), indicating that C1 was characterized by active immune status while C2 displayed enhanced proliferative activity. CIBERSORT analysis revealed that the C1 subtype exhibited a higher infiltration of CD4+ activated memory T cells, M1 macrophages, and activated natural killer (NK) cells, whereas C2 was characterized by elevated abundances of M2 macrophages and resting NK cells (Figure 2H). ESTIMATE analysis further validated the distinct TME profiles between C1 and C2, demonstrating significant enrichment of immune cell infiltration in C1 (Figure S3A–C). We next explored the somatic mutation landscape of these two subtypes (Figure S3D,E) and calculated their TMB (Figure S3F,G). No significant differences in mutation patterns were observed between the two subtypes, implying that mutational burden is not a primary determinant of the classification between C1 and C2. Collectively, SCLC was stratified into two subtypes as follows: C1 exhibited a high abundance of HEVs and an active immune phenotype, whereas C2 showed low HEV abundance and an immunosuppressive phenotype.

HEV Abundance Is Associated With Favorable Prognosis in SCLC Patients

Analysis of scRNA‐seq data from tumor samples of patients with SCLC enabled the identification of multiple vascular endothelial subtypes, including HEV‐like cells, as well as a distinct lymphatic endothelial population (Figure 3A). Each endothelial subtype exhibited a distinct transcriptional profile in subtype‐specific marker genes, with venules marked by high expression of ACKR1 [31]. Among these venules, those with high expression of canonical HEV‐associated genes such as SELE, SELP [7], and ICAM1 were identified as HEV‐like cells (Figure 3B), which is consistent with the endothelial subtype classification established earlier based on RNA‐seq. These expression patterns further supported the robustness and biological relevance of the previous subtype annotation.

Functional enrichment analysis revealed that, compared with other endothelial subsets, HEV‐like cells displayed significant upregulation of pathways related to immune cell adhesion and T cell activation, highlighting their specialized role as entry portals for immune cell trafficking into the TME (Figure 3C). To evaluate the clinical relevance of HEV structures, 16 SCLC patients were stratified into two groups according to the proportion of HEV‐like cells among total vascular ECs. Patients with a higher proportion of HEV‐like cells exhibited significantly improved survival outcomes compared with those with a lower proportion (Figure 3D).

To further validate these findings, multiplex immunofluorescence staining was performed on a TMA comprising tumor samples from 80 patients with SCLC. Colocalized expression of CD31 and PNAd, a hallmark glycoprotein complex that is particularly prominent in HEVs, confirmed the presence of HEV structures [8]. Notably, dense infiltration of CD3+ T cells was observed in close proximity to HEV structures, suggesting active lymphocyte recruitment into the TME via HEVs (Figure 3E). Patients were further stratified based on the presence or absence of HEVs in tumor tissues. Despite their relatively low prevalence, HEV‐positive patients exhibited significantly superior survival outcomes compared with HEV‐negative patients (Figure 3F). Collectively, these results provided convergent transcriptomic and histopathological evidence that HEV abundance was strongly associated with improved prognosis in patients with SCLC.

Construction of a Risk Prediction Model

Based on the close association of HEVs with SCLC prognosis and our prior subtyping classification, we further explored pivotal genes regulating the prognostic value of HEVs and established a predictive model. To initially identify key genes correlated with the survival outcomes of SCLC patients, 590 DEGs identified between subgroups with distinct HEV‐abundance were subjected to a systematic feature selection pipeline (Figure 4A). Briefly, univariable Cox regression analysis initially pinpointed 161 genes with significant prognostic relevance (Figure S4A). Subsequent multivariable Cox regression narrowed this set down to 24 candidates (Figure S4B), which were further validated via the log‐rank test, yielding 20 survival‐associated genes (Table S2). Based on these 20 preselected genes, we developed 101 combinations of ML survival modeling pipelines on the training cohort (n = 367). Among these, LASSO regression plus GBM (LASSO + GBM) outperformed other models (Table S3). It achieved a C‐index of 0.66 in the training cohort and 0.63 in the validation cohort (Table S3), demonstrating acceptable robustness and predictive efficacy. Ultimately, the optimized model identified five core prognostic variables: PDCD1, CXCL9, ITK, ITGAL, and SH2D1A. We additionally quantified each gene's selection frequency across diverse ML models to validate their relative importance, confirming that these five genes were frequently recovered by different models (Figure 4B). The optimal model was then applied to compute the risk score for each sample, based on which patients were stratified into high‐ and low‐risk groups. All five core genes were highly expressed in the low‐risk group, and samples with lower risk scores had better survival outcomes (Figure 4C). We also validated the prognostic value of the identified genes across multiple cancer types using the CIDE platform, consistently confirming their protective effects in the majority of cancers (Figure S5A–E). In our study, the K–M curves further demonstrated a longer OS in the low‐risk group compared with the high‐risk group in both training and validation cohorts (Figure 4D,E). Time‐dependent ROC curves demonstrated that these five genes were reliable predictors of survival, with the area under the curve (AUC) values of approximately 0.7 for 1‐, 2‐, and 3‐year survival prediction (Figure S6A,B). We additionally verified that the low‐risk group correlated with elevated HEV abundance (Figure 4F), and further assessed ICB response in distinct risk groups using TIDE scores, which were significantly lower in the low‐risk group identified by the GBM model (Figure 4G) [24]. Collectively, the model‐derived risk score reflected HEV abundance and enabled the prediction of patient survival and response to immunotherapy.

Subsequently, we integrated the GBM risk score with clinicopathological characteristics to establish a clinically applicable nomogram. Univariable and multivariable Cox regression analyses were performed to identify independent prognostic factors, revealing that age (≥ 65 vs. < 65), tumor stage (ES vs. LS), and the GBM risk score were significantly associated with OS (Figure 5A,B). Notably, the clinical characteristics had no significant correlation with HEV abundance (Figure S7A–C). Building on these findings, we incorporated the three identified variables to construct a nomogram for individualized survival prediction (Figure 5C). We then performed a comprehensive evaluation of the nomogram in both training and validation cohorts. Specifically, the nomogram assigned a unique risk score to each sample and stratified patients into high‐ and low‐risk groups. The log‐rank test confirmed that patients in the high‐risk group exhibited a markedly shorter OS than those in the low‐risk group (Figure 5D). For 1‐, 2‐, and 3‐year OS prediction, the nomogram achieved AUC values mostly exceeding 0.8 (Figure 5E, Figure. S8A–D) and exhibited a C‐index of 0.712 in both training and validation sets. Additionally, DCA and calibration curves collectively confirmed the robust and reliable predictive performance of the nomogram (Figure S8E–L). Taken together, our nomogram, which integrated the GBM prognostic risk score based on bulk RNA‐seq alongside key clinical variables, exhibited robust predictive performance.

Radiotherapy Combined With Immunotherapy Represents a Promising Strategy Through Induction of HEVs

HEVs are specialized vascular structures that facilitate lymphocyte trafficking into the TME and support effective antitumor immune responses. According to our multiplex immunofluorescence analyses, HEVs were detected in only approximately 20% of tumor samples from SCLC patients, and the C1 subtype characterized by higher HEV abundance was less prevalent than the C2 subtype with lower HEV abundance. Together, these findings indicate that inadequate HEV structures may restrict immune cell infiltration in SCLC and partly contribute to its limited responsiveness to immunotherapy. This raises the possibility that combination strategies may help overcome this barrier and improve therapeutic efficacy.

In murine SCLC models, combined radiotherapy (15 Gy delivered in 5 fractions) and immunotherapy significantly suppressed tumor growth and prolonged OS compared with either modality alone (Figure 6A–C). To explore the underlying mechanisms, scRNA‐seq was performed on tumor samples collected on Day 9 after treatment initiation, with two biological replicates analyzed per treatment group. ECs were resolved into five distinct subpopulations (Figure 6D). Among these subsets, HEV‐like cells were characterized by high expression of addressins such as Icam1 and chemokines including Cxcl10, whereas the Junb + ECs subset exhibited elevated expression of stress‐response genes such as Junb and Hspa1a (Figure 6E, Figure S9). Notably, the proportion of Junb + ECs was markedly increased following radiotherapy, consistent with a radiation‐induced stress response. In contrast, the HEV‐like subset was selectively expanded following combined radiotherapy and immunotherapy, suggesting that the combination treatment induces a key endothelial phenotypic transition toward HEV differentiation (Figure 6F).

These findings were further validated by immunofluorescence staining, which revealed the presence of HEV structures with a characteristic cuboidal morphology in tumors treated with the combination therapy (Figure 6G). Quantitative analysis confirmed that the proportion of HEVs among total ECs was significantly higher in the combination treatment group compared with all other groups (Figure 6H). Moreover, TCF1+ PD‐1+ CD8+ T cells were frequently observed in the vicinity of HEV structures, consistent with a stem‐like T cell phenotype associated with durable immunotherapy responses [20, 32] (Figure 6I).

To directly assess the functional contribution of HEVs to treatment efficacy, HEV structures were blocked in vivo using a HEV‐targeting antibody. Strikingly, HEV blockade significantly reduced the antitumor efficacy of the combined radiotherapy and immunotherapy regimen (Figure 6J–L). Together, these results demonstrate that combined radiotherapy and immunotherapy exert antitumor effects, at least in part, by promoting EC reprogramming toward HEVs within the TME and provide a rationale for the development of HEV‐centered translational therapeutic strategies in SCLC.

Discussion

SCLC is a highly aggressive malignancy with poor survival outcomes and limited therapeutic benefit from immunotherapy. A profoundly immunosuppressive TME, together with the rapid development of treatment resistance [33], remains a major obstacle to durable disease control in SCLC. Although HEVs are increasingly recognized as key vascular structures that promote lymphocyte trafficking and antitumor immunity [34], their relevance in SCLC has remained poorly understood. In this context, using bulk RNA‐seq, scRNA‐seq data, and immunofluorescence analyses, distinct HEV‐related subtypes of SCLC were identified and the prognostic and immunological significance of HEVs was systematically characterized. The murine SCLC models further suggest that treatment‐induced HEV remodeling may represent a mechanistically and translationally relevant avenue for enhancing the efficacy of immunotherapy.

In this study, we stratified SCLC into two subtypes, C1 and C2, associated with HEV‐high and HEV‐low states, respectively. C1 displayed a more robust immune activation status with significant enrichment of immune‐related pathways, which was aligned with the previous proposition that HEVs promote antitumor immunity by enabling lymphocytes to enter the tumor [8]. Consistent with the observations in other tumor types, such as breast cancer [35], a high density of HEVs in SCLC was also significantly associated with better prognosis. Moreover, previous studies supported the utility of HEVs in predicting cancer patients' survival [7]. Collectively, even though HEVs are scarce in SCLC, their contribution to patient prognosis remains significant.

Based on the subtyping classification, we constructed a GBM model to predict SCLC patients' risk using five genes as follows: PDCD1, CXCL9, ITK, ITGAL, and SH2D1A; high‐risk scores correlated with poorer prognosis and all five genes acted as protective factors. PDCD1, which encodes the immune checkpoint PD‐1, is a well‐established therapeutic target in multiple cancers [36, 37, 38]. As a specific marker expressed on activated CD4+ and CD8+ T cells [39], high PDCD1 expression may reflect increased T‐cell infiltration and immune engagement within the TME. Similarly, ITK, a key mediator in T cell receptor signaling, promotes effector T cell development [40] and thus enhances antitumor immunity. CXCL9 is a chemokine involved in T cell recruitment. It has been shown to enhance ICB response and restrict cancer growth [41]. ITGAL serves as a receptor for ICAM‐1 [42], which is a marker expressed on HEVs [43]. Its expression correlates with favorable prognosis and immunotherapy sensitivity in pan‐cancer [44, 45]. While SH2D1A has been reported to drive proliferation and invasion in ovarian [46] and liver cancers [47], elevated SH2D1A expression correlates with a favorable clinical prognosis across most cancer types, consistent with our findings in SCLC. Importantly, integrating the GBM model with clinical features further improves its prognostic performance in SCLC and may facilitate optimized clinical treatment strategies.

We observed that 15 Gy/5 fractions of radiotherapy combined with immunotherapy could promote the formation of HEVs in immunocompetent mice. Compared with radiotherapy or immunotherapy alone, this combined regimen significantly suppressed tumor growth and prolonged survival. Consistently, the MATCH clinical trial demonstrated that 15 Gy/5 fractions of radiotherapy, when combined with ICB, elicits robust and favorable antitumor effects [48]. One key mechanism by which radiotherapy synergizes with immunotherapy is the enhancement of T cell‐mediated antitumor immunity [49]. As HEVs mediate the entry and trafficking of T cells into tumor tissues, we propose that radiotherapy combined with ICB promotes the formation of HEVs, which in turn facilitates immune cell infiltration into the tumor and sustains effective antitumor immune responses. Previous studies have validated the efficacy of alternative strategies to induce HEV formation, including systemic activation of the lymphotoxin β receptor [9, 50, 51] and Fc‐optimized anti‐cytotoxic T‐lymphocyte‐associated protein 4 (CTLA‐4) antibodies [52]. These HEV‐targeted interventions can either exert therapeutic benefits as monotherapy to improve patient prognosis or synergistically enhance the efficacy of multiple immunotherapies [7]. Given the favorable prognostic value and low abundance of HEVs in SCLC, combining HEV‐inducing therapeutic strategies with radioimmunotherapy may improve treatment efficacy, a notion that deserves further exploration.

Nonetheless, our study has several limitations. Our risk model was only evaluated internally due to limited and imbalanced datasets, which may contribute to the insufficient predictive robustness. Furthermore, while we have preliminarily delineated the role of HEVs in SCLC, the precise regulatory pathways and underlying molecular mechanisms remain largely unexplored. The mechanism by which radiotherapy combined with immunotherapy promotes HEV formation also remains to be fully elucidated. Additionally, further investigations are required to elucidate the biological roles of candidate genes identified in this study in regulating the prognosis of SCLC. Their context‐dependent and tumor‐specific functions across different cancer types also remain to be fully explored.

In summary, this study stratified SCLC into HEV‐high and HEV‐low subtypes, showed that HEV abundance was associated with favorable prognosis, established a risk stratification model integrating HEV features with clinical characteristics, and further demonstrated that radiotherapy combined with immunotherapy promotes HEV formation and improves therapeutic efficacy, thereby highlighting the translational potential of therapeutic strategies that enhance HEV formation.
