Statin prescription rates for prevention of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease in adults 40–75 years old with type 1 diabetes
Authors: Michelle Diane Lundholm
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesAdults with type 1 diabetes (T1D) are at markedly elevated risk of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD). Guidelines recommend statin use for ASCVD prevention in diabetes between the ages of 40 and 75 years. This study aimed to evaluate statin prescribing rates for primary and secondary prevention of ASCVD in this age range with T1D and to identify disparities and barriers to optimal statin use.Study design, setting, and participantsA retrospective cross-sectional study of 266 adults with T1D aged 40–75 years was conducted at an integrated health system between 2020 and 2024. Demographic features, statin prescribing patterns, low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol levels and use of additional lipid-lowering agents were extracted from medical records. Barriers to prescribing were identified via endocrine physician documentation.ResultsAmong 266 adults with T1D aged 40–75 years, only 43.2% (95% CI 0.37 to 0.49) were prescribed guideline-recommended statin and 39.3% of those with a history of ASCVD received a high-intensity statin. Overall, 47.7% (95% CI 0.42 to 0.54) of patients achieved the latest LDL cholesterol targets, and 53.0% (95% CI 0.47 to 0.59) if using pre-2023 targets. Deferral to another healthcare professional (23.3%), statin intolerance (15.8%), and clinical inertia (9.0%) were the most common barriers to therapy. In multivariable analyses, female sex was independently associated with lower odds of receiving guideline-recommended statin therapy (aOR 0.45, 95% CI 0.24 to 0.85, p=0.015) and lower odds of achieving LDL targets (OR 0.43, 95% CI 0.28 to 0.64, p=0.046), while ASCVD history was associated with higher odds of statin use (aOR 2.75, 95% CI 1.34 to 5.57, p=0.005). Very few patients received adjunctive lipid-lowering agents (ezetimibe 4.1%, PCSK9 inhibitor 0.4%, none on bempedoic acid).ConclusionsNotable gaps exist in statin prescribing and LDL goal attainment among adults with T1D, particularly women. Efforts to enhance care coordination, promote healthcare professional education and expand the use of adjunctive lipid-lowering therapies may help improve cardiovascular prevention in this high-risk population.

Introduction

Adults with type 1 diabetes (T1D) are at a significantly increased risk of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD), with rates comparable to, and in many reports exceeding, those seen in type 2 diabetes (T2D).13 A large report from the UK General Practice Research Database found men with T1D had approximately twofold to fourfold increased risk and women with T1D had a fivefold to tenfold increased risk of cardiovascular events compared with individuals without diabetes.4 Another large study from the Swedish national Diabetes Register found patients with T1D had at least threefold increased risk of cardiovascular disease, but potentially as high as 16-fold increased risk in patients who were diagnosed with T1D before the age of 10 years.5 ASCVD is not only highly prevalent but also the leading cause of morbidity and mortality in individuals with diabetes, accounting for a substantial proportion of adverse health outcomes and premature deaths in this population.158

The effectiveness of statin therapy in reducing ASCVD events and all-cause mortality among patients with diabetes is well-established.9 Statins decrease the risk of major cardiovascular events and death regardless of baseline cholesterol levels, making them the cornerstone of lipid management.10 While studies are disproportionately conducted in the T2D population, similar benefits are seen in T1D.1113 Randomised trial data specific to T1D have historically been limited, so much of the evidence base has been extrapolated from T2D; however, a recent target trial emulation by Blais et al demonstrated that statins used for primary prevention in adults with T1D were associated with reductions in major cardiovascular events without new safety concerns, providing T1D-specific support for guideline-directed statin use.14

Current professional guidelines reflect this evidence (table 1). Since 2019, the American Diabetes Association (ADA) has recommended that all adults with diabetes aged 40–75 years receive at least moderate-intensity statin therapy for primary prevention of ASCVD, escalating to high-intensity statin for those with enhanced risk or established ASCVD.15 16 The American College of Cardiology and American Heart Association (ACC/AHA) similarly advise statin therapy for adults aged 40–75 with diabetes, regardless of estimated 10-year ASCVD risk, with high-intensity statins favoured for those at high cardiovascular risk and for secondary prevention.17 18 In this age group, the consensus is clear regarding the benefit of statin therapy. While the data are largely from studies on T2D, the guidelines include statements that similar statin treatment approaches should be used for people with T1D.

Despite these recommendations, gaps remain in the implementation of guideline-directed statin therapy for adults with diabetes. The aim of this study is to evaluate real-world statin prescription rates for both primary and secondary prevention of ASCVD among adults aged 40–75 years with T1D. Additionally, we identify disparities and barriers that hinder optimal statin use and risk reduction in this high-risk group.

Methods

This is a retrospective cross-sectional study spanning a large multicentre integrated health system anchored by a quaternary-care academic main campus, with more than a dozen community-based outpatient clinics across northeastern Ohio. This study was reviewed by the Cleveland Clinic Institutional Review Board (IRB) and granted exempt status, with a waiver of informed consent because the research posed no more than minimal risk to participants. The study population consisted of adults with T1D between the ages of 40–75 years at latest follow-up, who were seen by an endocrinologist in endocrinology clinics across northeastern Ohio between 2015 and 2018 and had at least one subsequent visit between 2020 and 2024. This criterion selected patients with an ongoing longitudinal relationship with an endocrinologist.

Patients were excluded if they were not on insulin therapy or if they had T2D, monogenic diabetes, or if they were pregnant within the study period. This cohort is a subset of the population reported in prior studies.19 20 There were 4259 adult patients seen in endocrinology clinic between 2015 and 2018 with at least one ICD-coded diagnosis of T1D in their chart. After applying strict T1D diagnosis criteria, 625 patients had confirmed T1D, of whom 346 were 40–75 years old at their most recent clinic visit and 266 returned for at least one visit between 2020 and 2024 (figure 1). Using the electronic medical record, charts were reviewed for demographic data, cholesterol labs, and prescriptions as of December 2024. Medication reconciliation was performed at each visit and included prescriptions from outside of our health system. Endocrinologist notes were reviewed for documented barriers to statin prescription. The primary outcome was guideline-recommended statin prescription rates. The secondary outcome was attainment of low-density lipoprotein (LDL) target. An a priori sample size calculation was not performed because the study includes all available eligible cases within the predefined study period.

Definitions

Atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) was defined as a diagnosis or history of coronary heart disease (myocardial infarction, stable or unstable angina, coronary artery stenosis, or history of coronary revascularisation), cerebrovascular disease (ischaemic stroke, transient ischaemic, or carotid artery stenosis) or peripheral arterial disease.

For the present study, guideline-recommended statin use was defined in accordance with the ADA and the ACC/AHA as moderate-intensity or high-intensity statin for primary prevention and high-intensity statin for secondary prevention, regardless of LDL level.15 18 Moderate-intensity statin was defined as atorvastatin 10–20 mg, rosuvastatin 5–10 mg, simvastatin 20–40 mg, pravastatin 40–80 mg, lovastatin 40–80 mg, fluvastatin 80 mg, or pitavastatin 2–4 mg daily. High-intensity statin was defined as atorvastatin 40–80 mg or rosuvastatin 20–40 mg daily.

For measurement of the secondary outcome, LDL target was defined as <100 mg/dL (<2.6 mmol/L) for primary prevention and <55 mg/dL (<1.4 mmol/L) for secondary prevention from the ADA Standards of Care.15 Prior to 2023, the ADA’s LDL targets were defined as <100 mg/dL (<2.6 mmol/L) for primary prevention and <70 mg/dL (<1.8 mmol/L) for secondary prevention.21 We reported attainment of both the latest and the pre-2023 LDL targets because this guidance change occurred during the study period.

Statin intolerance was defined as the inability to tolerate a statin at the intended intensity due to adverse effects or drug interactions that were associated with a patient’s previous use of one or more statin doses or formulations, resulting in use of a lower dose than guidelines recommend, or discontinuation of statin therapy altogether. In contrast, statin refusal was defined as a patient’s unwillingness to try statin therapy for any reason (including patient preference, perceived risk, medication concerns, cost and access, lack of education, cognitive or psychological factors or cultural or social factors).15 Clinical inertia was defined as the lack of initiation or intensification of statin therapy despite the patient not meeting guideline-recommended statin intensity, without documented contraindication, intolerance or patient refusal, and without evidence of a shared decision-making discussion regarding deviation from guidelines. Cases in which statin management was explicitly deferred to another healthcare professional (such as a primary care physician or other specialist) were classified separately.

Statistical methods

Number (N) and percentage were reported for categorical variables, mean and SD were reported for normally distributed continuous variables, and median and IQR were reported for non-normally distributed continuous variables. 95% CI was reported for cohort-wide primary and secondary outcome proportions. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used as a test for normality. Pearson χ2 tests and Fisher exact tests were used for categorical variable comparison, and Student’s t-test was used for normally distributed continuous variables. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used for continuous, non-normally distributed variable comparison. Multivariable analysis was performed using logistic regression; candidate covariates for the model were selected a priori based on clinical relevance, or p<0.20 on univariable analysis. Variables that were not of specific clinical interest did not demonstrate significant association with the outcome and did not meaningfully alter the coefficients of other predictors and were iteratively excluded from the final model. ORs were reported with their 95% CIs. A significance level of 0.05 was assumed for all tests. R statistical software (version 4.5.2) was used for statistical analysis.22

Patient and public involvement

There was no patient or public involvement in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research study.

Results

Of the included 266 patients, 55.3% (n=147) were female, 80.8% (n=215) identified as white, and 21.1% (n=56) had a history of ASCVD. The median age was 52.9 years, IQR (45.3, 61.8), HbA1c was 7.6%, IQR (6.7, 8.6), and BMI was 27.5 kg/m2, IQR (23.9, 31.5). These and additional demographic details are summarised in table 2.

Looking at LDL cholesterol level, 19.6% (n=11) of patients with prior ASCVD had an LDL less than 55 mg/dL. Of those without prior ASCVD, 55.2% (n=116) had LDL under 100 mg/dL. Overall, 47.7% (n=127, 95% CI 0.42 to 0.54) of patients were at their guideline-directed LDL target by latest guidance. Using pre-2023 LDL guidelines, 53.0% (n=141, 95% CI 0.47 to 0.59) patients were at LDL target.

The median LDL for patient on guideline-recommended statin therapy was 80 mg/dL, IQR (63, 103) vs 95.5 mg/dL, IQR (72, 120) if not on statin therapy (p=0.003). Patients were more likely to be at their LDL target if they were on guideline-recommended statin compared with lower intensity or no statin (56.5%, n=65 vs 41.1%, n=62, p=0.012).

Of the 142 patients who were prescribed a statin, 39.4% (n=56) were prescribed by an endocrinologist and 60.4% (n=86) were prescribed by another healthcare professional, such as primary care or another specialist. Patients who received their statin from an endocrinologist were more often on the guideline-recommended statin intensity (91.1%, n=51, vs 74.4%, n=64, p=0.013) and were also more likely to reach their LDL target (71.4%, n=40 vs 33.8%, n=24, p<0.001).

Barriers and disparities to prescribing statins

From endocrinology physician notes, the main reason for statin under-treatment was deferring to another healthcare professional in 23.3% (n=62), followed by statin intolerance in 15.8% (n=42), clinical inertia in 9.0% (n=24), and patient refusal in 6.4% (n=17) (table 4). Other reasons in 2.6% (n=7) of patients were lack of cholesterol panel results or lab or prescription issues. Comparing to those without ASCVD, patients with ASCVD were less likely to have their statin care deferred (12.5% vs 26.2%, p=0.031) and more likely to report statin intolerance (32.1% vs 11.0%, p<0.001). Causes of statin intolerance were statin-associated muscle spasms in n=15, transaminitis in n=7, other (nausea, rhabdomyolysis, drug interaction or hives) in n=5 and were not specified in the remaining n=15.

Patients who were not on guideline-recommended statin due to clinical inertia were more likely to already have an LDL as latest target than patients who had any other barrier (66.7%, n=16, vs 36.2%, n=46, p=0.005). When using the less strict pre-2023 target, 70.8% (n=17) of cases of clinical inertia already attained their LDL target.

Women were less likely to receive guideline-recommended statin than men (36.7%, n=54, vs 51.3%, n=61, p=0.017), despite a numerically similar prevalence of ASCVD between women and men with T1D (21.8%, n=32 vs. 20.2%, n=24, p=0.750). Women were also less likely to have LDL at the latest target (42.2%, n=62, vs 56.3%, n=67, p=0.022). Endocrinologists were more likely to defer statin prescription to other healthcare professionals for women than men (28.6%, n=42, vs 16.8%, n=20, p=0.017). There were also numerically higher rates of statin intolerance in women than men that were not statistically significant (18.4% n=27, vs 11.7%, n=14, p=0.138). Women over 55 years old (by which age the vast majority of women are post-menopausal) did not have significantly higher rates of guideline statin prescription compared with women younger than 55 years (39.0%, n=23, vs 35.2%, n=31, p=0.643).

Non-white individuals had a higher rate of ASCVD than white individuals (35.3%, n=18, vs 17.7%, n=38, p=0.006). There was numerically greater guideline-recommended statin use in non-white patients though this was not statistically significant (54.9%, n=28, vs 40.5%, n=87, p=0.061). Non-white patients and white patients had similar observed rates of LDL target attainment (52.9%, n=27, vs 46.5%, n=100, p=0.409).

Patients over 55 years of age had a significantly higher rate of ASCVD (30.8%, n=36, vs 13.4%, n=20, p=0.002) than patients under 55 years old. There was numerically more guideline-recommended statin use (47.9%, n=56, vs 39.6%, n=59, p=0.177) and fewer patients at LDL target (41.9%, n=49, vs 52.3%, n=78, p=0.090) for patients over 55 years old without a statistically significant difference. There was no difference between the median age of a patient on a guideline-recommended statin (54.0 years, IQR (46.7, 63.7)) and the median age of a patient that was not on a guideline-recommended statin (51.9 years, IQR (45.2, 61.0), p=0.132).

In a multivariable logistic regression model including sex, race, age and history of ASCVD, female sex remained independently associated with lower odds of receiving guideline-recommended statin intensity compared with males (adjusted OR (aOR) 0.45, 95% CI 0.24 to 0.85, p=0.015). ASCVD history was also associated with higher odds of statin use (aOR 2.75, 95% CI 1.34 to 5.57, p=0.005). Race and age were not independently associated with guideline-recommended statin prescribing in this model, and inclusion of additional covariates (BMI, smoking, blood pressure and LDL values) did not materially change the effect estimates.

In a logistic regression analysis, women had about 57% lower odds of being at LDL target than men (OR 0.43, 95% CI 0.28 to 0.64, p=0.046). No other variables were independently associated with meeting the LDL target, and adjusted and unadjusted effect size by sex was roughly equivalent.

Other LDL-lowering therapies

Other LDL-lowering agents were very rarely used in the studied T1D patient population: ezetimibe was prescribed in 4.1% (n=11) of all patients, the majority for primary prevention (n=8 of 11), PCSK9 inhibitor (alirocumab) was used in 0.4% (n=1), for secondary prevention, and no patients (n=0, 0%) were on bempedoic acid, despite the high prevalence of statin barriers and need for further LDL-lowering treatments. Of 59 patients with statin intolerance or refusal, only 6.8% (n=4) were on an alternative LDL-lowering therapy.

Discussion

This study identifies important gaps in the management of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease risk among adults with T1D. Despite guidelines supporting statin therapy in this high-risk group, fewer than half of the patients between 40 and 75 years old who followed longitudinally with an endocrinologist at an integrated health system were prescribed a guideline-recommended intensity of statin, regardless of whether the statin was indicated for primary or secondary ASCVD prevention. There was particularly low uptake in primary prevention, as over half of eligible T1D patients without a history of ASCVD were not on any statin. Even among those at greatest risk due to a history of ASCVD, less than 40% were on high-intensity statin as recommended.

Selecting patients with T1D who maintained longitudinal relationships with their endocrinologist offered advantages for evaluating real-world statin prescription rates. This approach facilitated inclusion of individuals with ongoing speciality care, allowing for a more accurate assessment of prescribing practices and reducing the confounding effects of care fragmentation or loss to follow-up. Importantly, trust and rapport develop over time between patients and healthcare professionals, which can facilitate more open discussions about preventive therapies and adherence to guideline-based care. In contrast, patients seen only once or infrequently may not have the same opportunity to address all aspects of diabetes care and complication management, including cardiovascular risk reduction. We suspect that statin prescribing rates may have been even lower among patients who did not have an established longitudinal relationship with their endocrinologist. The requirement for stable speciality follow-up, combined with a strict age window and rigorous T1D definition, likely selected a more intensively managed group than broader registry-based cohorts, which should be considered when comparing absolute treatment rates.

Prior studies have reported wide variability in statin use among individuals with T1D, reflecting differences in study populations and guideline frameworks. Two large European studies conducted between 2020 and 2022, which relied on the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) and European Atherosclerosis Society (EAS) guidelines, reported statin utilisation rates of 14.7%–19.3% in patients with T1D, lower than those observed in the present study.23 24 This difference is largely attributable to age distribution, as more than half of patients in those cohorts were younger than 40 years of age, a group in whom the benefits of statin therapy are less well established. Similarly, a study from the Netherlands found that lipid-lowering therapy was prescribed in 22%–46% of individuals with T1D who met treatment criteria under 2018 Dutch, 2018 ADA and 2014 National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) guidelines.25 The present study demonstrates comparable patterns using more recent ADA guidance. Collectively, these findings underscore that under-implementation of guideline-recommended statin therapy represents a persistent challenge across healthcare systems.

As a secondary outcome, attainment of LDL cholesterol targets was also suboptimal. Under half of the included patients achieved the LDL thresholds set out in the latest guidance. Less than 20% of patients with ASCVD had an LDL of <55 mg/dL. Even by the pre-2023 guidelines, which were less strict for secondary prevention, still only about half of patients attained the recommended LDL target.

The modest difference in median LDL between those on guideline-directed intensity and those that were not (approximately 15 mg/dL) may reflect a prescribing bias, wherein patients with lower baseline LDL were less likely to be initiated on guideline-recommended statin therapy. This confounding factor is more indicative of current prescribing patterns than the true pharmacologic effectiveness of statins. Additionally, the LDL difference may be lessened by the impact of statin non-adherence.26 27 Similar patterns have been noted in other real-world cohorts, where lower apparent LDL ‘gaps’ between treated and untreated groups largely reflect selective prescribing and imperfect adherence rather than the biologic effect of statins.28

Our findings further suggest that, among those treated, statin therapy alone was insufficient for the majority; less than 60% of patients on appropriate statin dosing attained their LDL target. This indicates an ongoing need for statin adherence and adjunctive LDL-lowering therapies, yet these agents were rarely used despite evidence of efficacy in T1D.29 30 Only 4% of our cohort were on ezetimibe, and almost no patients received PCSK9 inhibitors or bempedoic acid, despite frequent statin non-acceptance. Cost and insurance accessibility for some agents may contribute, as well as limited familiarity with newer agents among nonspecialists or patient reluctance for injectable therapies in the case of PCSK9 inhibitor therapy. The reported low utilisation rates of non-statin therapies align with contemporary European data, where ezetimibe and PCSK9 inhibitors were also prescribed in only a small minority of adults with T1D despite very low LDL goal attainment, suggesting that underuse of adjunctive LDL-lowering agents is a broadly observed issue rather than one unique to this health system.24

Healthcare professional-related barriers also contributed to suboptimal statin prescribing. Endocrinologists performed better than other healthcare professionals in following guidelines (which originated from endocrinology and cardiology organisations) when they did prescribe statins, and they were more likely to attain target LDL levels. However, statin management was frequently deferred to other healthcare professionals. In many cases, endocrinology notes referenced primary care, internal medicine or cardiology as the prescriber for lipid management, with endocrinology visits prioritising glycaemic control, technology and complication screening. Even in the context of longitudinal endocrine follow-up, competing visit priorities, limited time and perceptions that another healthcare professional is better positioned to initiate or titrate statins likely contributed to the observed deferral. This deference was the most commonly documented barrier to statin prescribing, highlighting persistent challenges in care coordination and unclear ownership of cardiovascular risk management in this patient population. These findings underscore the importance of clear inter-professional communication and defined roles in preventive cardiovascular care for patients with T1D.31

One factor potentially contributing to lower rates of statin prescription outside of endocrinology is the reliance on alternative organisational guidelines, such as the U.S. Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) guidance for primary ASCVD prevention.32 The USPSTF recommendations, released in 2016 and updated in 2022, advocate for the use of 10 year cardiovascular disease risk calculations—specifically the ACC/AHA Pooled Cohort Equation—to guide statin initiation for primary prevention.33 However, this approach is problematic in the T1D population, as widely used cardiovascular disease risk calculators (including the Pooled Cohort Equations, Framingham Risk Score, QRISK3 and UKPDS Risk Engine) are not validated for T1D and underestimate ASCVD risk in this population.34 35 As a result, reliance on these tools may contribute to under-recognition of risk and subsequent statin undertreatment in T1D.

Another healthcare professional-related barrier to statin prescribing is clinical inertia. In our study, the majority of patients who were not on a guideline-recommended statin due to clinical inertia were already at their LDL goal. This finding suggests that clinical inertia may be driven, in part, by the perception that statin therapy is unnecessary when the LDL is at target. Importantly, current guidelines emphasise statin initiation in T1D for this age group, irrespective of baseline LDL level, because of statin’s additional benefits, which include improvement of other lipid parameters, reduction of inflammation and stabilisation of atherosclerotic plaque.11 15 17 36 This disconnect between healthcare professional perceptions and evidence represents an opportunity for targeted educational interventions to address this care gap.

Patient-level barriers also played a role, with statin intolerance and refusal accounting for a significant proportion of undertreatment. The rates of statin intolerance were higher for patients with a history of ASCVD, which is unsurprising given the greater number and higher intensity of statin prescriptions used in this population. Other LDL-lowering therapies were markedly underutilised, as fewer than 10% of patients with statin intolerance or statin refusal were prescribed ezetimibe, PCSK9 inhibitor or bempedoic acid. Greater consideration for alternative LDL-lowering options may help address residual risk, particularly in this population.

Disparities in statin prescribing were observed, particularly with respect to sex. Women with T1D were significantly less likely than men to receive guideline-recommended statins or achieve LDL targets, despite an equal prevalence of ASCVD between the sexes. In a multivariable model adjusted for age, ASCVD and race, female sex remained independently associated with lower odds of receiving guideline-recommended statin intensity and lower odds of attaining LDL targets in logistic regression, underscoring that these sex disparities persisted after accounting for clinical covariates. These findings are shared with a recent study of a large cohort of European adults that found sex inequalities in statin prescribing between men and women.23 Among other things, this disparity has been previously attributed to lower statin acceptance among women, healthcare professional bias and underestimation of ASCVD risk in women.15 37 38 Reproductive considerations may also contribute, as statin therapy was previously contraindicated in pregnancy for concerns of teratogenic effects. However, large studies have not found evidence of teratogenicity for which the U.S. Food and Drug Administration removed the warning in 2021, allowing for more individualised risk-benefit assessments.153943 If there were healthcare professional apprehension about initiating statins during potential childbearing years, our results indicated that this hesitancy persisted beyond menopause as women over 55 years of age did not have higher prescription rates than women below 55, and the association for lower statin use in women was not affected by age in multivariable analysis.

Our study supports a trend towards lower statin acceptance in women compared with men, but our findings suggest that deferred ownership of statin care for women is also a relevant factor. This may be related to a greater number of healthcare professionals (ie, primary care and obstetric and gynaecology healthcare professionals) who coordinate women’s health maintenance, further complicating ownership of guideline-based statin initiation and monitoring.

By comparison, age and race did not emerge as significant determinants of guideline-directed statin use in this sample, despite higher ASCVD prevalence in non-white and older patients, limited by the study’s sample size and demographic composition.

A limitation of this study is that some clinical factors which influence individual ASCVD risk assessments, such as duration of diabetes, family history of premature ASCVD or other comorbidities, were not captured. This is particularly relevant in primary prevention because ADA guidelines state that patients who are considered high-risk for ASCVD by clinical factors should be on high-intensity statin therapy with a stricter LDL goal of <70 mg/dL.15 Thus, incorporation of these factors may have revealed even lower rates of guideline-recommended statin utilisation and LDL goal attainment. This would further support the conclusion that more attention is needed for ASCVD prevention in patients with T1D. Additionally, the retrospective design and reliance on physician documentation may underestimate the prevalence of some barriers, such as undocumented patient refusal or insurance challenges with non-statin therapies. While we reported on statin prescribing rates, statin non-adherence (defined as statin medication prescribed but not reliably taken) is also a significant barrier that was not assessed in this study. This analysis benefited from inclusion of a large integrated health system with both a quaternary-care main campus and community-based clinics, allowing for representation across diverse clinical settings. Nonetheless, generalisability may be limited as this reflected a modest final sample size within a single regional health system, and care may vary in other healthcare contexts.

Despite these limitations, our findings present persistent opportunities to improve preventive cardiometabolic care for adults with T1D. Efforts to clarify healthcare professional ownership, enhance team-based communication and integrate clinical decision support, such as EMR-based best practice alerts, may help close the care gap. Moreover, more robust consideration and use of adjunctive LDL-lowering agents is needed, particularly for statin-intolerant or refractory patients. Special attention should be paid to ensuring equitable treatment for women and other groups at risk of under-treatment. When viewed alongside contemporary data from Europe, our results suggest that underuse of statins and poor LDL goal attainment are more widespread in T1D.2325 By focusing on patients between 40 and 75 years in longitudinal endocrine care, this study reveals actionable opportunities to enhance prescribing practices and care coordination.

Taken together, our results highlight an ongoing gap between guideline recommendations and current practice, potentially contributing to elevated ASCVD risk in adults with T1D. Strategies to promote healthcare professional education, improve care coordination and integrate multidisciplinary approaches are needed to advance cardiovascular prevention in this population. Future studies are needed to evaluate the impact of such interventions on adherence to guidelines and long-term cardiovascular outcomes in T1D.
