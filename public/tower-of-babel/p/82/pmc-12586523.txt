Structural insights into natural compound inhibitors of the human metapneumovirus nucleocapsid protein via molecular dynamics and free energy landscape analyses
Authors: Maha M Alawi, Raidan M Alyazidi, Leena H Bajrai, Hattan S Gattan, Thamir A Alandijany, Ibrahim A Al-Zahrani, Vivek Dhar Dwivedi, Esam I Azhar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHuman Metapneumovirus (HMPV) is a major contributor to acute respiratory tract infections, particularly affecting children, the elderly, and immunocompromised individuals. Despite its global prevalence, no specific antiviral treatments or vaccines are available, highlighting the urgent need for effective therapeutic interventions. This study utilized a comprehensive computational drug discovery approach to identify potential inhibitors targeting the highly conserved nucleocapsid (N) protein of HMPV, a crucial component in viral replication and transcription. A virtual screening of 1,227 natural compounds from the NP-lib database was performed, identifying MOLPORT-001-742-110, MOLPORT-001-812-855, and MOLPORT-001-740-100 as the top candidates based on their docking scores and binding energies. The initial results were validated through re-docking, molecular interaction analysis, and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. MOLPORT-001-742-110 demonstrated the highest stability with minimal deviations in Root Mean Square Deviation (RMSD) and Root Mean Square Fluctuation (RMSF) analyses, as well as a well-defined low-energy conformation in the Free Energy Landscape (FEL). Key hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions were retained, reinforcing its strong binding affinity. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and superimposition studies further supported the stability and adaptability of these compounds within the binding site. Comparative analyses with the control compound confirmed the superior inhibitory potential of the selected ligands, particularly MOLPORT-001-742-110. This study underscores the utility of computational approaches in identifying natural product-based inhibitors and provides a foundation for experimental validation and development of antiviral therapies against HMPV.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-22428-x.Keywords: Human metapneumovirus, Nucleocapsid protein, Natural inhibitors, Molecular dynamics simulation, Antiviral drug discoverySubject terms: Drug discovery, Virtual screening

Introduction

Human metapneumovirus was first identified in the Netherlands in 2001 and is considered one of the leading causes of acute respiratory tract infections among people of all ages worldwide, particularly among young children, the elderly, and immunocompromised patients. HMPV belongs to the family Pneumoviridae and has been classified into two major genetic lineages, A and B, further divided into sub lineages A1, A2, B1, and B2. The virus circulates globally, with seasonal peaks in winter and spring1–3. Over the years, HMPV has gained significant attention due to specific notable outbreaks. A tremendous surge in the number of cases of infection with HMPV was noticed from November 2022 to March 2023, with a positivity rate of 9.6% among tested patients. The outbreak was marked by the beginning of two novel lineages, A2.2.1 and A2.2.2, which highlighted the evolution of the virus4. More recently, in late 2024, China reported an increase in cases of HMPV, especially for children under the age of 14, but according to health officials. However, this has raised public concern for them, the increase still falls within that typical seasonal pattern and presents no new pandemic threats whatsoever5–7. Infection with HMPV is spread by direct contact with infected persons, through contact with contaminated surfaces, and via respiratory droplets. Clinical manifestations of HMPV infection range from mild upper respiratory symptoms, including cough, fever, and nasal congestion, to severe conditions such as bronchiolitis and pneumonia, particularly among vulnerable populations8. There is no specific antiviral treatment or vaccine against HMPV despite its prevalence. Management mainly focuses on supportive care; thus, there is a need for effective therapeutic strategies9. The HMPV N protein is considered an essential protein in viral replication, encapsidating the viral RNA genome and interacting with other viral components to enhance replication and transcription10. Because of this important function and its high conservation among different HMPV strains, the N protein is a very attractive target for anti-HMPV drug development. Interference with N protein function might disturb the virus life cycle and, consequently, reduce infection1. Natural products have been among the densest sources within drug development, thanks to the great variety of bioactive molecules with possible therapeutic use11. Recent progress in some of these in silico computational approaches has employed virtual screening and molecular dynamics simulations that facilitate the identification and optimization of natural product-derived inhibitors against different viral targets12. The mentioned in silico methods include a screening strategy through the efficient screening of large natural compound libraries, predictions of binding affinity, and assessing the stability of potential inhibitor-target complexes13. The integrated in silico drug discovery approach was employed in this study to identify potential inhibitors of the HMPV nucleocapsid protein from a database of natural compounds.

Although a few previous studies have explored natural compounds against the HMPV N protein, these works primarily focused on docking-based screening or limited simulation analyses. In contrast, our study employs a comprehensive computational pipeline, combining virtual screening, molecular dynamics (MD), free energy landscape (FEL) mapping, binding free energy calculations (MM-GBSA), residue-wise energy decomposition, DCCM, and SASA analysis. This approach enables a deeper understanding of the structural stabilization, conformational adaptability, and residue-specific contributions involved in ligand binding to the HMPV N protein.

Methodology

Protein Preparation and binding site prediction

The structure of the Nucleocapsid protein of Human Metapneumovirus has been retrieved from the protein data bank using its ID: 5FVD14,15. The structure was further refined and pre-processed in UCSF Chimera by removing water molecules, ions, and all other non-protein components16. This showed the integrity of the structures, and the missing hydrogen atoms were added to complete proper bonding and stabilize the structures. The protonation states of the amino acid residues were then corrected at physiological pH with the help of the Chimera tool, “Dock Prep.” The binding site of the N protein was predicted using the CASTp web tool, a server for computing accessible surface areas and pocket regions in proteins17. The binding site was identified as a collection of residues, and the centroid of the binding pocket was used to define the centre grid box for the docking studies. The size of the grid box utilised for the docking studies 20Å and the supplied X coordinate was 18.09, for Y coordinate 12.54 and for Z coordinates − 2. 26.

Virtual screening and ADME analysis

The virtual screening was performed on the MTiOpenScreen web server18. The NP-lib database was used as an input library, one of the extensive natural product compound collections available, downloaded from the NP-lib website19–21. NP-lib library from the MTiOpenScreen server, which contains approximately 2,500 curated natural product compounds optimized for high-throughput virtual screening. This library is designed to include drug-like natural compounds with structural diversity, favorable bioavailability, and known biological activity, making it well-suited for identifying novel antiviral candidates targeting the HMPV N protein. Prior research has demonstrated the effectiveness of natural products in targeting viral proteins, especially those involved in RNA binding. Furthermore, the MTiOpenScreen platform ensures a high-quality ligand set with drug-like properties, making it a rational and efficient choice for identifying potential inhibitors of the HMPV nucleocapsid protein.

The compounds were prepared by converting them into the required format and energy minimizing before screening. The binding site for the N protein was defined to ensure that the grid was centred appropriately based on the residues predicted by CASTp. Library compounds were screened for their target site binding based on the predicted docking energies. Three top-scoring compounds were selected based on their docking scores and binding interactions. Along with these, a known HMPV nucleocapsid protein inhibitor was selected as a reference compound for further comparative analysis. Further, the pharmacokinetic properties of the selected compounds were also studied using the SwissADME webserver22.

Re-docking and comparative analysis

To confirm the robustness of the results of the initial screening, re-docking of the three short-listed compounds to the N protein was considered essential using the same binding site parameters. Re-docking was done using the Autodock vina software using the same grip of virtual screening to confirm reproducibility and exactitude of the predicted binding pose23,24. Analogous docking for the reference compound was carried out to have a comparative platform. All hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic interactions, and van der Waals contacts for each of these complexes were analyzed, both visually and with the scoring functions.

To evaluate the binding efficiency of the docked compounds relative to their size, Ligand Efficiency (LE) values were calculated. LE was determined using the formula:

LE = -∆G/ NHA​.

where ΔG represents the docking binding affinity (in kcal/mol) and NHA is the number of heavy (non-hydrogen) atoms in the ligand molecule. The number of heavy atoms was determined from the molecular formula using SwissADME25.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation

MD Simulation was performed for selected compounds and reference compounds in complex with the N protein through the use of the Free academic Desmond software of Schrödinger26,27. Simulations were run in explicit water using the TIP3P model of water. The complex was placed in a cubic simulation box, and periodic boundary conditions were applied to maintain a 10 Å buffer distance from the edges28. Sodium and chloride ions were added to neutralize the system, while a 0.15 M NaCl concentration was maintained to mimic the physiological strength. The OPLS_2005 force field was used during the simulation, while long-range electrostatic interactions were calculated by the PME method. Energy minimization was first conducted using the steepest descent algorithm for 5000 steps on the system to remove steric clashes and for stability before the simulation29. Following minimisation, the system was equilibrated under an NVT and NPT ensemble at 300 K and 1 atm pressure30,31. The production MD simulation was run for 500 ns under NPT ensemble, with a 2 fs time step, and the snapshots of the system were saved every 10 ps for trajectory analysis.

Post-MD trajectory analysis was performed using the Simulation Interaction Diagram and Simulation Event Analysis tools in Desmond. RMSD on the protein backbones and ligand involved, and the RMSFs on the C-atom involving protein backbones and ligand were analyzed post-running to assess behavior on system stability and thereby binding. Further analysis of the dynamics of the binding pocket was done to observe key interactions retained during the trajectory. Positional deviations of the initial docked pose from the final bound pose were calculated. For the stability and compactness, further analysis of Solvent Accessible Surface Area (SASA) and Protein–ligand interaction maps analysis was included.

Principal component analysis and free energy landscape

Initially, the Desmond MD trajectories were converted into DCD files using the VMD program, and the principal component analysis (PCA) and Dynamic Cross-Correlation Matrix (DCCM) were performed to explore the conformational space sampled by the N protein-ligand complexes. Using the bio3D package in R, a covariance matrix of atomic fluctuations was generated from the trajectory32,33. The first two PCs generated, PC1 and PC2, were generated to represent the major transformational changes, while the calculated free energy landscape was computed in the Geo-measures plugin based on PC1-PC2 choice as reaction coordinate to locate minimum energy states of the system: Minima were considered as the metastable conformation from the FEL plot34,35.

Superimposition analysis

UCSF Chimera was used to superimpose the minimum energy structures from FEL analysis on their initial docked poses16. Such superimposition helped in viewing the structural deviations and conformational adjustments upon binding. The RMSD values between the initial and final poses upon binding were computed to quantify such changes upon binding, providing insight into the stability and adaptability of the proteins in binding compounds within the N protein binding site.

MM-GBSA binding free energy and energy decomposition analysis

The binding free energy (ΔG) of the docked protein–ligand complexes was estimated using the Molecular Mechanics Generalized Born Surface Area (MM-GBSA) method implemented in the Prime module of Schrödinger Suite36. To gain insights into the energetic contributions of individual residues to ligand binding, per-residue energy decomposition analysis was performed using custom Python matplotlib.

Results

Virtual screening analysis and ADME analysis

The virtual screening of natural products comprising 1,227 compounds to identify candidates inhibiting the nucleocapsid protein of human metapneumovirus. The binding site of nucleocapsid protein was defined based on the residues predicted by CASTp. Screening results were evaluated based on the calculated docking energies, returning compounds with binding energies ranging from − 11.3 kcal/mol to -1.2 kcal/mol. Among them, the top three compounds, MOLPORT-001-742-110, MOLPORT-001-812-855, and MOLPORT-001-740-100, show higher docking scores − 11.3 kcal/mol. -10.9 kcal/mol, and − 10.5 kcal/mol, respectively (Supplementary File 1). The scores represent better interactions with the target protein. Additionally, Gamma-Fagarine (PubChem CID: 107936) was considered as the reference compound, has been included for comparison. These top-ranked compounds, along with the reference, were short-listed for re-docking and molecular interaction analysis.

Further, the SwissADME-based in silico profiling was performed to evaluate the pharmacokinetic properties, drug-likeness, and potential toxicity of the lead compounds compared to the reference ligand (Gamma-Fagarine). All compounds showed no PAINS or Brenk structural alerts, indicating the absence of common pan-assay interference motifs or high-risk toxicophores. MOLPORT-001-812-855 and MOLPORT-001-740-100 demonstrated high gastrointestinal absorption, while MOLPORT-001-742-110 showed low absorption, potentially due to its higher molecular weight (540.47 g/mol) and topological polar surface area (TPSA) of 177.89 Å², exceeding the optimal threshold for passive permeability. Only MOLPORT-001-740-100 and Gamma-Fagarine were predicted to cross the blood–brain barrier. None of the leads were identified as P-gp substrates, and most showed minimal CYP450 inhibition liabilities, although MOLPORT-001-742-110 was predicted to inhibit CYP2C9 and the reference ligand inhibited multiple CYP isoforms. Lipinski’s rule violations ranged from 0 to 2, with bioavailability scores between 0.17 and 0.55, suggesting favorable oral bioavailability for MOLPORT-001-812-855, MOLPORT-001-740-100, and Gamma-Fagarine. Water solubility predictions indicated moderate to poor solubility for the leads, consistent with their higher hydrophobicity (consensus Log P ranging from 2.99 to 5.66). Overall, MOLPORT-001-740-100 presented the most balanced ADMET profile, combining high absorption, moderate solubility, low CYP inhibition, and good bioavailability, supporting its candidacy for further development Table 1.

Re-docking and molecular interaction analysis

Re-docking was performed for all top three compounds along with the reference to ensure their binding pose and interactions. The re-docking energies of the HMPV N protein when complexes with, MOLPORT-001-742-110, MOLPORT-001-812-855, and MOLPORT-001-740-100 were − 11.1 kcal/mol, -10.8 kcal/mol and − 10.5 kcal/mol respectively, whereas the re-docking score of the reference compound was − 6.3 kcal/mol. The energies of the re-docking showed minor changes, which may indicate the reproducibility of the binding affinities as predicted37. To assess the size-adjusted binding performance of the selected ligands, ligand efficiency (LE) was calculated. MOLPORT-001-742-110, with a binding affinity of − 11.1 kcal/mol and 40 heavy atoms, exhibited an LE of 0.28 kcal/mol per heavy atom. MOLPORT-001-812-855 showed a slightly higher LE of 0.33 kcal/mol, with a docking score of − 10.8 kcal/mol and 33 heavy atoms. MOLPORT-001-740-100 demonstrated an LE of 0.30 kcal/mol based on a binding energy of − 10.5 kcal/mol and 35 heavy atoms. The reference compound, Gamma-Fagarine, displayed the LE of 0.37 kcal/mol.

Table 1 shows all the molecular interactions of the listed compounds within the binding site of nucleocapsid protein. herein, the HMPV N-MOLPORT-001-742-110 complex showed good binding with hydrogen bond interactions with Ser174, Thr183, Gln250, Thr257, Arg260, and Glu374 and with significant hydrophobic interactions with residues Leu167, Ile176, Val178, Ala254, Trp261, Ile334, Ile335, and Ala378. Besides, π-π stacking interactions with Arg186 and Trp261 enhanced further stability. In the HMPV N-MOLPORT-001-812-855 complex a distinctive interaction profile is realized where Met337 takes part in hydrogen bonding, while Leu167, Ile176, Val178, Ala254, Trp261, Ile334, Ile335, and Met337 take part in extensive hydrophobic interactions. For HMPV N-MOLPORT-001-740-100 complex the hydrogen bonding interaction was with Ser174 along with substantial hydrophobic contact contribution by Ile176, Val178, Ala254, Trp261, and Met337. In the reference complex, only a few interactions were observed, considering that hydrophobic contacts were solely attained by Ile176 and Ala254, which basically justifies why this compound has much lower binding affinity compared to the top three candidates. These analyses confirmed the superior binding capabilities of the shortlisted compounds, especially in forming hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions, which are very important for protein-ligand stability. Variations in interaction profiles underlined the structural diversity and adaptability of the compounds to stabilize the protein-binding site (Fig. 1 and Table 2).

Molecular dynamics simulation

The RMSD was analysed to investigate the stability of protein-ligand complexes throughout the 500 ns molecular dynamics simulation. In the RMSD profiles, clear trends in compound stabilities could be perceived. The most stable trajectory results from HMPV N-MOLPORT-001-742-110, where protein RMSD fluctuations range from 2 to 4 Å and ligand RMSD below 4 Å High stability in binding with minor displacement. In the complex, HMPV N-MOLPORT-001-812-855, protein RMSD henceforth stabilized at around 3 Å while the ligand RMSD fluctuates between 4 and 6 Å, thereby reflecting that the ligand has undergone minor adjustments in its position within the binding pocket. However, in the HMPV N-MOLPORT-001-740-100 complex it was seen that there was a sudden rise in the RMSD after 400 ns to around ~ 4 Å for the protein RMSD and beyond 8 Å after 450 ns for the ligand RMSD, showing evidence of loss of stable interactions in the last phase of the simulation. Indeed, the reference complex represented the highest deviations, as in such cases, RMSD of a protein reached more than 4 Å and of a ligand, more than 25 Å. For better comparison, the apo protein was simulated for 100 ns and it revealed an initial phase during the first ~ 20 ns, after which the system displayed fluctuations ranging between 3.5 and 4.5 Å. This indicates that while the protein it exhibited a higher degree of conformational mobility in the absence of a bound ligand (Supplementary Fig. 1). In this view, weak binding stability could be suggested, as well as the significant changes in the conformation. So, regarding the abovementioned issues, it may be concluded that the compound MOLPORT-001-742-110 was the most stable one and further strengthened its potency as the best inhibitor (Fig. 2).

RMSF analysis has been performed to describe the flexibility of amino acid residues after the binding process of ligands. In all protein-ligand complexes, Protein RMSF of flexible loop regions are moderate, with marked peaks around residues 50, 100, and 300, corresponding to dynamic regions involved in ligand accommodation. RMSF analysis of apo protein demonstrated that most residues exhibited fluctuations below 2 Å, indicating overall rigidity in the apo state (Supplementary Fig. 2) In this case, for MOLPORT-001-742-110, the lowest fluctuations (~ 2–3 Å) characterized the highest stability of interactions in a binding site and very minor perturbations of the protein structure. Likewise, HMPV N-MOLPORT-001-812-855, the protein experienced high fluctuations (~ 4–7 Å) indicating the flexibility of some residual regions. For the HMPV N-MOLPORT-001-740-100 complex, the protein demonstrated slightly higher values (~ 3–4 Å) with pronounced flexibility of some loop regions, thus pointing to moderate binding stability. This corresponds to huge fluctuations higher than 6 Å for the reference compound, which reflects weak binding and massive conformational changes. The result further established that MOLPORT-001-742-110 had more stable interactions with the target protein (Fig. 3).

Protein RMSF plot analysis of the HMPV N protein complexed with the natural compounds (a) MOLPORT-001-742-110 (b) MOLPORT-001-812-855 (c) MOLPORT-001-740-100 and reference compound (d) Gamma-Fagarine (Reference).

RMSF analysis was also done to observe atomic fluctuations for the selected compounds during the simulation. Hence, the calculated values of RMSF showed that both the compounds, MOLPORT-001-742-110 and MOLPORT-001-812-855, were in a state of relatively low atomic fluctuation at ~ 1–2 Å, which confirmed the stability of the conformation of the ligands inside the binding pocket. The MOLPORT-001-740-100 showed somewhat higher fluctuations of ~ 2–3 Å, indicating minor instability. For the reference compound, the highest RMSF values of ~ 12–14 Å were recorded, hence proving its unstable binding mode and major conformational changes during the course of the simulation. These results point to the conclusion that MOLPORT-001-742-110 and MOLPORT-001-812-855 have more stable binding, while MOLPORT-001-740-100 had moderate stability, and the reference compound had the weakest interaction profile (Fig. 4). The results of the molecular dynamics simulation gave an all-rounded assessment of the binding stability of the selected inhibitors. Consistently, MOLPORT-001-742-110 showed higher stability with less deviation of RMSD, lower fluctuations in protein RMSF, and strong ligand stability that reassures its strong potential to be a lead inhibitor. MOLPORT-001-812-855 exhibited regular stability; however, for MOLPORT-001-740-100, the stability decreased after the middle stage of the simulation. The reference compound had the weakest binding profile among all the studied compounds, including high conformational changes and instability. It is concluded that MOLPORT-001-742-110 has the highest potential to be further validated experimentally for inhibiting HMPV nucleocapsid protein.

Comparison of molecular interactions between Docking and MD simulation last pose

A comparison of the molecular interactions from the docking results and the final MD simulation pose will provide an overview of how key ligand-protein interactions are maintained with time. It lost some minor interactions in comparison to docking: interaction with Gln250 and Glu374 were present in docking, while hydrogen bonding interactions with Ser174, Thr182, Thr257, and Arg260 are still preserved in the MOLPORT-001-742-110 complex. Most hydrophobic interactions, except those of Val178 and Ile176, which became more limited, are preserved quite well in magnitude. In particular, it preserved the interactions with Leu167, Ile176, Ala254, Trp261, Ile334, Met337, Ala378, and Leu383; this already may serve as evidence of good hydrophobic stabilization of the ligand within the binding pocket. MOLPORT-001-812-855 showed a significant loss of hydrogen bonding, with no hydrogen bonds present in the final pose, from previous indications of docking with Met337. The hydrophobic interactions with Ile176, Val178, Ala254, Trp261, Ile334, Ile335, and Ala378 remained, suggesting that while the ligand lost hydrogen bonding, it maintained strong van der Waals interactions to stabilize binding. MOLPORT-001-740-100 also comprised the loss of hydrogen bonding with respect to the docking, which resulted in initially having an interaction with Ser174. The following hydrophobic interactions remained consistent, as expressed with Ile176, Val178, Ala254, Trp261, Ile334, Ile335, and Ala378; this ligand appeared to remain in a relatively favourable binding conformation without major disruptions. This compound showed the least activity from both docking and MD simulations during the control experiment. Hydrophobic interactions, including those involving limited areas-just Ile271, Leu273, and Leu282 residues in a final pose orientation-have shown only some regions. Their smaller profile for interactions underlines the low stability on their interaction, considering the other best hits.

Overall, MOLPORT-001-742-110 results showed higher retention of key interactions, with hydrogen bonds maintained and hydrophobic contact, hence further underpinning stability in a strong binding pocket. On the other hand, both MOLPORT-001-812-855 and MOLPORT-001-740-100 had lost certain hydrogen bonds and, with more dependency on hydrophobic interactions, while the control compound was weakest in regard to its stability in binding. Although fewer hydrogen bonds were observed in MOLPORT-001-742-110 complex, the presence of polar interactions contributed to ligand stability within the binding pocket. These findings therefore validated MOLPORT-001-742-110 as the most promising inhibitor (Fig. 5; Table 3).

Solvent accessible surface area (SASA) analysis

The per-residue SASA analysis provided insights into the degree of solvent exposure of the apo HMPV N protein compared to its ligand-bound states. Among the complexes, MOLPORT-001-740-100 displayed the most significant SASA reduction (~ 90–150 Å²), reflecting extensive protein–ligand contact and reduced solvent exposure around the binding site. MOLPORT-001-742-110 and MOLPORT-001-812-855 also exhibited reduced SASA values (~ 100–200 Å²), consistent with stable and well-packed binding conformations. In contrast, the Gamma-Fagarine complex retained relatively higher SASA (~ 100 to 175 Å²), indicating that the ligand induced less pronounced structural compaction and solvent shielding. These results indicate that ligand binding generally promotes structural compaction and reduces solvent exposure, with MOLPORT-001-740-100 inducing the greatest conformational tightening of the HMPV N protein (Fig. 6). The apo protein exhibited the highest solvent exposure, with an average SASA of ~ 150 Å²(Supplementary Fig. 3).

Protein-ligand interaction mapping

The interaction fraction analysis of the protein-ligand complex over 500 ns MD simulation showed that in MOLPORT-001-742-110 complex residues including Ser174, Thr182, Thr257 and Arg257 contributed in hydrogen bond formation for more than 80% of simulation time, while Arg186 contributed to hydrophobic bond (85% of interaction fraction) and water bridge (30% of interaction fraction). In the MOLPORT-001-812-855 complex Ser174, Glu374, and Glu375 residues participated in water bridge formation for more than 50% of 500 ns time frame. Similarly in the complex MOLPORT-001-740-100 Thr257, Arg260, and Gln279 contributed in the major water bridge formation for 30%, 50% and 20% of interaction fraction. This analysis showed that hydrogen bonds and water bridge are major components for maintaining the stability of the complex. (Fig. 7).

Principal component analysis (PCA)

Principal component analysis was carried out to investigate the conformational space sampled by protein-ligand complexes during MD simulation. The first three principal components in the PCs plot, the so-called PC1, PC2, and PC3, describe the distribution of conformation states, while variance contributions have been analysed through the distribution of eigenvalues. In the PCA for MOLPORT-001-742-110 presents only one well-defined conformational cluster, reflecting only small deviations within this simulation. That is to say, data points in this biplot are fairly localized, reflecting the fact that this complex exhibits stable dynamical behaviour within these data. With this eigenvalue distribution, one can thus infer that PC1 describes ∼28.4% and PC2 around 17.7% of the total variance-the main changes are indeed contained within those few components with higher values of variance. The data in the case of MOLPORT-001-812-855, presents a slightly broader conformational space, while the data points are more dispersed. This gives evidence for greater flexibility in protein-ligand interactions: PC1 accounts for 27.3% of the variance, while 17.9% comes from PC2 with moderate fluctuation in the complex. Besides, the complex MOLPORT-001-740-100 presents a wider spread of points, indicating huge conformational variations. These PCA projections mean that there would be more residual movement within the binding site, since PC1 contributes as high as 31.2% and PC2 adds up to 19.5%. This is expected to mean less stability in the binding interaction, which could explain the increased RMSD values during the simulation. The reference compound had the most dispersed conformational distribution among the compounds, with great structural fluctuations. PC1 contributes 36.2%, reflecting large-scale conformational transitions. The wide dispersion of points reflects poor stability in binding and highly dynamic interaction with the protein (Fig. 8).

Dynamic Cross-Correlation matrix (DCCM) analysis

To investigate the impact of ligand binding on the internal motion and allosteric communication of the HMPV N protein, a dynamic cross-correlation matrix (DCCM) analysis was performed using for each protein–ligand complex, along with the unbound (apo) protein. The DCCM maps, shown in Fig. 9, display the correlation of atomic fluctuations between all residue pairs. In these matrices, positively correlated motions, where residues move in the same direction, are indicated in cyan, while anti-correlated motions, where residues move in opposite directions, are marked in magenta.

The DCCM plot of the HMPV N protein complexed with the natural compounds (a) MOLPORT-001-742-110 (b) MOLPORT-001-812-855 (c) MOLPORT-001-740-100 and reference compound (d) Gamma-Fagarine (reference) obtained after the PCA analysis.

The apo protein, which serves as the unbound reference state, displayed extensive anti-correlated motions, particularly across the loop regions and between distant domains of the protein (Supplementary Fig. 4). This pattern indicates a high level of intrinsic flexibility and dynamic motion in the absence of ligand interactions. The widespread anti-correlation suggests a lack of cohesive movement, allowing various domains to move independently, which may reflect the functional plasticity of the apo state necessary for ligand recognition or RNA interaction. In contrast, the DCCM profile of the MOLPORT-001-742-110-bound complex revealed a shift towards more localized and coordinated motions. While some anti-correlated regions persisted, particularly in the flexible loops near the binding pocket, the overall motion appeared more dampened compared to the apo state. This suggests that binding of MOLPORT-001-742-110 stabilized certain segments of the protein, reducing long-range conformational fluctuations. Similarly, the complex with MOLPORT-001-812-855 exhibited increased positive correlations within core structural elements, and a noticeable reduction in anti-correlated motion, especially between the α-helical and loop domains. This implies enhanced intra-domain communication and a more stable binding conformation. On the other hand, the reference compound Gamma-Fagarine exhibited a weaker DCCM profile, marked by lower correlated motion and persistent anti-correlated dynamics in several regions (Fig. 9).

Free energy landscape (FEL) analysis

The FEL was projected using the first two principal components, PC1 and PC2, as reaction coordinates that enable the observation of the conformational stability of protein-ligand complexes. The FEL plots in 2D and 3D presentations highlight energy minima where low energy regions coloured in yellow correspond to the most stable states of the protein.

HMPV N-MOLPORT-001-742-110 a depicted well-defined energy minimum; hence, this complex is supposed to be stably bound along the course of the simulation run. In the major energy basin, low values of free energy correspond to the conformational states of low structural deviations, representing the mainly populated state of this complex. The energy landscape of HMPV N-MOLPORT-001-812-855 was somewhat broader, including several low-energy regions. That means this compound has a moderate conformational flexibility that enables the ligand to sample different stable states within the binding pocket. The overall stability of the complex was maintained despite minor fluctuations. HMPV N-MOLPORT-001-740-100 showed more dispersed energy landscapes with additional areas of higher energy and thus assumed more conformational transitions and thus less stability in interaction with the protein, as compared to the two top candidates. Several shallow basins indicate more frequent ligand repositioning. The reference complex represents the most scattered and fragmented energy landscape, including high-energy barriers and unstable regions of this landscape. This means high conformational instability and a weak binding affinity, which agrees with the outcomes from RMSD and PCA analyses. Overall, the FEL analysis confirms that the most stable binding conformation was from MOLPORT-001-742-110, while the reference compound showed large instability, therefore reinforcing its lower potential to be a viable inhibitor (Fig. 10).

Superimposition of the minima structures with initial Docking poses

To evaluate the conformational dynamics of the protein–ligand complexes beyond RMSD analysis, we extracted representative structures from multiple free energy wells on the PCA-based 2D Free Energy Landscapes (FELs). These local minima correspond to distinct conformational states sampled during the simulation. The selected structures were superimposed onto their respective initial docking poses to assess deviations in ligand orientation and protein conformation, providing insights into dynamic rearrangements and ligand stability throughout the MD trajectory. Among the tested compounds, MOLPORT-001-740-100 demonstrated the lowest RMSD (1.367 Å) between its docked pose and the FEL minimum, indicating excellent structural retention and minimal deviation. Structural superimposition revealed high overlap of both ligand and binding site residues, with only slight adjustments in loop regions flanking the active site. These local shifts suggest minimal induced-fit adaptation, reflecting a well-preorganized and stable interaction profile.

MOLPORT-001-742-110 exhibited a moderate RMSD of 1.818 Å, with conformational changes primarily occurring in loop regions and side chains adjacent to the binding pocket. The ligand underwent mild reorientation, potentially optimizing contacts with polar residues and contributing to improved interaction geometry. The stability of the complex despite these movements suggests a favorable balance between flexibility and structural retention. For MOLPORT-001-812-855, a slightly higher RMSD of 1.898 Å was observed, indicating more pronounced conformational adaptability. Superimposed structures showed repositioning of the ligand within the pocket, accompanied by reorganization of surrounding residues. This dynamic behavior suggests that the ligand explored multiple conformational substates during the simulation, as evidenced by the multiple energy basins in the FEL, yet remained bound through persistent key interactions, highlighting potential induced-fit stabilization.

The reference compound Gamma-Fagarine displayed an RMSD of 1.855 Å, but with greater deviation in both ligand position and protein backbone compared to the natural compounds. The superimposed structure showed significant displacement, particularly in loop and coil regions, suggesting a less stable interaction. This observation aligns with the broader energy distribution seen in the FEL and supports the notion of weaker binding affinity and greater conformational drift for the reference ligand. Overall, this superimposition analysis of structures extracted from multiple FEL minima demonstrates that the protein–ligand complex sample distinct conformational states, particularly in loop and binding site flexibility. The natural compounds, especially MOLPORT-001-740-100, maintained consistent binding poses with minimal rearrangement, while MOLPORT-001-742-110 and MOLPORT-001-812-855 displayed adaptive flexibility supporting interaction refinement via induced fit. In contrast, the control compound showed signs of binding instability and broader conformational fluctuation. To further validate the diversity of conformational states sampled during the simulation, we visualized the individual 3D structures extracted from multiple FEL minima (Figs. 11 and 12). These snapshots clearly illustrated conformational transitions across distinct energy wells, including ligand repositioning and variable loop conformations. The protein complexes with the natural ligands displayed conserved core structures with localized flexibility, while the control compound demonstrated broader fluctuations across conformer.

Binding free energy and Residue-Level decomposition

To evaluate the thermodynamic favorability of ligand binding, MM-GBSA analysis was performed using Schrödinger’s Prime module on the top-ranked docked poses. The computed ΔGbind values revealed that all three natural compounds exhibited stronger binding affinities compared to the reference compound Gamma-Fagarine (Table 4). Among the candidates, MOLPORT-001-742-110 showed the most favorable ΔGbind of − 81.54 ± 4.81 kcal/mol, followed by MOLPORT-001-812-855 (–62.49 ± 3.28 kcal/mol) and MOLPORT-001-740-100 (–41.80 ± 5.35 kcal/mol). In contrast, the control compound exhibited the weakest binding free energy (–23.82 ± 1.60 kcal/mol). The energetic components of MM-GBSA further highlighted the dominant contributions of van der Waals (ΔGvdW) and lipophilic interactions, particularly in MOLPORT-001-742-110 and MOLPORT-001-812-855. For example, MOLPORT-001-742-110 had a ΔGvdW of − 56.26 ± 2.63 kcal/mol and a lipophilic contribution of − 13.39 ± 1.08 kcal/mol, which substantially favored binding. Coulombic interactions also played a critical role, especially for MOLPORT-001-742-110 (–35.90 ± 5.53 kcal/mol). Meanwhile, the control compound showed significantly weaker Coulombic and van der Waals contributions, in line with its poor ΔGbind.

Discussion

The current study aimed to identify potential inhibitors of the HMPV N protein through integrated computational drug discovery comprising virtual screening, molecular docking, molecular dynamics, and free energy landscape analysis. In this paper, the candidate compounds were screened from NP-lib database, and three candidates namely MOLPORT-001-742-110, MOLPORT-001-812-855, and MOLPORT-001-740-100 were the top three ranking, based on their high binding affinities and stability with the HMPV nucleocapsid protein. These findings are in agreement with previous studies that have previously applied computational approaches for the identification of antiviral inhibitors against RNA viruses38,39. The results of virtual screening showed that all three shortlisted compounds had binding energies ranging from − 11.3 kcal/mol to -1.2 kcal/mol, which is comparable to previous studies on viral RNA-binding proteins40. Re-docking analysis confirmed the stability of the initial binding poses, with only minor variations observed in the docking energies. In the same manner, some related approaches have been followed in screening inhibitors against SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid protein, where the lead compounds had shown strong hydrophobic and hydrogen bonding interactions with key residues41.

Molecular dynamics simulations further confirmed the stability of binding. The MOLPORT-001-742-110 complex showed the highest stability, with an RMSD of ~ 2–4 Å, low RMSF values, and minimal conformational drift. This stability agrees with previous studies where natural product-derived inhibitors of viral proteins showed RMSD values within 2–4 Å, thus ensuring that there was no significant perturbation in protein-ligand interactions42. The PCA/FEL analyses supported that MOLPORT-001-742-110 was in a well-defined low-energy basin, eluding its relatively high conformational stability with respect to other ligands. These findings are in good agreement with previous computational studies using FEL mapping to explore the stability between viral inhibitors43.

This comparison underlined important features of the persistence of the intermolecular interactions of docking with MD: the hydrogen bonds with Ser174, Thr182, Thr257, and Arg260 were conserved, while the hydrophobic contacts with Leu167, Ile176, Ala254, Trp261, and Met337 were preserved. The consistency of these interactions is indicative of strong anchoring of the ligands, which agrees with observations derived from previous studies on inhibitors of RNA viruses like RSV and Influenza A44. On the other hand, MOLPORT-001-812-855 and MOLPORT-001-740-100 had reduced hydrogen bonding, maintaining most of their stability due to hydrophobic interactions. This phenomenon is reported in some studies concerned with flexible viral protein binding pockets45.

The conformational dynamics of the HMPV N protein–ligand complexes were explored through PCA and corresponding FELs, which revealed that the protein samples multiple low-energy conformational states over the course of the MD simulation. The presence of distinct energy basins on the FEL maps indicates that the complexes do not remain trapped in a single conformational well, but rather explore a range of stable structural substates. Representative structures from these wells were extracted and compared with the initial docking poses to evaluate the extent of ligand retention and protein flexibility. The resulting structural overlays and individual 3D conformations clearly demonstrated ligand-induced conformational changes, particularly in loop regions and flexible binding site residues. Among the tested compounds, MOLPORT-001-740-100 displayed the highest conformational stability, with minimal RMSD deviation and strong overlap between the docked and simulated poses. This suggests that the compound binds in a preorganized, energetically favorable manner with minimal need for structural adjustment. In contrast, MOLPORT-001-742-110 and MOLPORT-001-812-855 exhibited moderate flexibility, reflecting dynamic adaptation of the ligand–protein interface, consistent with an induced-fit binding mechanism. These findings are well-aligned with their FEL profiles, where multiple local minima were observed, indicating transient conformational states that contribute to interaction refinement.

Dynamic cross-correlation matrix (DCCM) analysis revealed notable differences in internal residue motions between the apo protein and ligand-bound complexes. The apo form exhibited widespread anti-correlated motions, indicating higher intrinsic flexibility. Upon ligand binding, especially with MOLPORT-001-740-100, a significant increase in positively correlated motions was observed, reflecting a stabilizing effect on the protein structure. The complexes with MOLPORT-001-742-110 and MOLPORT-001-812-855 also showed enhanced residue correlation compared to the apo protein, though to a lesser extent. In contrast, the Gamma-Fagarine complex retained substantial anti-correlated motions, suggesting limited stabilization. Overall, ligand binding particularly with MOLPORT-001-740-100 promoted a more coordinated and stable protein dynamic profile, supporting its potential as a strong inhibitor.

Overall, these results indicate that MOLPORT-001-742-110 is the most potential inhibitor of HMPV nucleocapsid, combining high binding affinity with stability of structure and conservation of key interactions. This study underlines the robustness of computer-aided drug discovery in the identification of putative natural product inhibitors of RNA viruses and as such forms the basis for further experimental verification and optimization. Hence, this study underlines the importance of exploring protein conformational dynamics alongside ligand binding in structure-based drug discovery. By integrating FEL, DCCM, SASA, and RMSF analyses, we captured a comprehensive picture of how small molecule binding reshapes the structural landscape of the HMPV nucleocapsid protein, providing deeper mechanistic insights into potential inhibition modes.

Conclusion

This study successfully identified potential inhibitors targeting the nucleocapsid protein of Human Metapneumovirus (HMPV) through an integrated computational drug discovery approach. From an initial library of 1,227 natural compounds, MOLPORT-001-742-110, MOLPORT-001-812-855, and MOLPORT-001-740-100 emerged as the top candidates based on virtual screening, docking scores, and molecular interactions. Among these, MOLPORT-001-742-110demonstrated the highest binding affinity, stability, and consistency in retaining critical interactions with key residues of the nucleocapsid protein. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations validated the stability of these compounds, with MOLPORT-001-742-110 displaying the least deviation in RMSD, minimal fluctuations in RMSF, and the most well-defined energy minima in the free energy landscape (FEL). Superimposition analysis further highlighted its strong structural stability, confirming its binding effectiveness. Comparisons with the control compound underscored the superior performance of the top three ligands, especially MOLPORT-001-742-110, which maintained strong hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions throughout the simulation. While earlier studies have primarily emphasized docking or basic MD simulations, this work offers a more comprehensive and mechanistically detailed exploration of ligand–protein interactions. By integrating PCA, FEL mapping, DCCM, and per-residue MM-GBSA decomposition, we provide a deeper understanding of how small molecules influence protein flexibility, conformational stability, and inter-residue dynamics within the HMPV nucleocapsid protein. Furthermore, this is one of the first studies to apply such an integrated in silico pipeline to natural product-based screening for HMPV N protein, revealing both structural adaptations and thermodynamic preferences of promising inhibitor candidates.
