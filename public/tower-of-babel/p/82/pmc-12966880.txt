The Impact of GLP‐1 Receptor Agonists on Esophagogastroduodenoscopy Outcomes: A Retrospective Cohort Study in a Predominantly African American Population
Authors: Ahmed El Sabagh, Ahmad Abdulraheem, Ariel Jimenez, Bara Abujaber, Usman Afzal, Ghaith Almhanni, Kiandokht Bashiri, Islam Badr Ezzeldin Mohamed, Nehal Abdelbaky, Dania Qarkash, Valeria Londono, Anna Stephan, Serenity G Budd, Alireza Meighani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundGlucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists (GLP‐1 RAs) improve glycemic control and promote weight loss but may increase gastric residue, raising concerns during esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD). African American patients remain underrepresented in research on these agents.MethodsWe performed a retrospective cohort study at a single tertiary referral center, including adults (≥ 18 years) undergoing elective EGD. Participants were divided into two groups based on documented GLP‐1 RA use within 7 days of EGD versus no use. Outcomes included the presence of residual gastric contents, procedure abortion due to gastric residue, procedural bleeding, and post‐procedural hospitalization. We applied multiple imputation for missing data and entropy balancing for covariate adjustment, reporting odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI).ResultsAmong 1408 participants, 270 used GLP‐1 RAs. Compared to non‐users, GLP‐1 RA users had a higher mean body mass index (33 vs. 30 kg/m2) and hemoglobin A1c (7.43% vs. 6.54%). In the adjusted analysis, GLP‐1 RA treatment was not significantly associated with increased gastric content (OR, 1.52; 95% CI, 0.58–3.99; p = 0.395) or other procedural complications. Conversely, GLP‐1 RA use was linked to lower post‐procedural hospitalization rates (OR, 0.19; 95% CI, 0.06–0.58; p = 0.004).ConclusionIn this predominantly African American cohort, recent GLP‐1 RA therapy did not significantly elevate intra‐procedural risk or necessitate procedure abortion. These findings suggest that routine discontinuation of GLP‐1 RAs prior to EGD may not be universally required, especially for patients benefiting from improved metabolic control. Larger prospective studies are warranted to inform guideline updates for diverse patient populations.Keywords: African American population, aspiration risk, EGD, endoscopy, GLP‐1 receptor agonists, residual gastric contents

Introduction

Glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists (GLP‐1 RAs) are widely used in the management of type 2 diabetes, primarily for their role in improving glycemic control [1]. In recent years, these medications have become increasingly prominent in the management of obesity due to their stimulatory effects on proopiomelanocortin and cocaine‐ and amphetamine‐regulated transcript (POMC/CART)‐expressing arcuate nucleus neurons (POMC/CART) which leads to increased satiety and decreased caloric intake [2]. Additionally, they are associated with delayed gastric emptying, which further promotes satiety and weight loss [2].

However, the latter mechanism has raised concerns with procedures such as esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD). Increased gastric residue may hinder visualization, complicating diagnosis and intervention [3]. Furthermore, the American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) Task Force has highlighted the potential risk of perioperative aspiration, recommending the temporary discontinuation of GLP‐1 RAs in certain circumstances [4].

Multiple observational studies have demonstrated an association between GLP‐1 receptor agonist use and increased gastric content during EGD, with some also reporting a higher likelihood of aborted procedures and reduced mucosal visibility. Recent studies, including those by Kobori, Silveira, Nadeem, Chapman, and Alkabanni, consistently show greater gastric residue among GLP‐1 RA users, with Alkabanni et al. notably reporting increased rates of aborted EGDs. However, evidence regarding downstream complications remains mixed; for example, a large retrospective cohort by Barlowe et al. found no significant increase in aspiration or pulmonary complications among GLP‐1 RA users undergoing EGD [3, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9].

Similarly, a scoping review by Chang et al. failed to find a significant correlation between GLP‐1 RAs and aspiration or regurgitation events [10]. These findings challenge the necessary preoperative discontinuation of GLP‐1 RAs in order to mitigate aspiration risk, as suggested by many current clinical guidelines [11]. Given these mixed findings, further research is needed to clarify the true impact of GLP‐1 RAs on gastric residue and aspiration risk, particularly in diverse patient populations.

African American patients are disproportionately affected by obesity and diabetes [12]. However, they remain significantly underrepresented in clinical research. This may affect the generalizability of research findings and clinical recommendations for a population that might have distinctly different pathophysiological characteristics and treatment response profiles [13]. Additionally, disparities in treatment outcomes such as variations in glycemic control and weight management highlight the need for more inclusive research.

We performed a retrospective cohort study that aimed to examine the relationship between GLP‐1 RAs use and increased gastric residue in a predominantly African American cohort, a group underrepresented in current research. We also aimed to evaluate safety risks, particularly perioperative aspiration pneumonia, to provide insights for managing patients with diabetes and obesity on GLP‐1 RAs therapy.

Methods

Study Design

We conducted a retrospective cohort study at a single academic tertiary care referral center at MedStar Georgetown/Washington Hospital center between January 2016 and June 2023. The study was approved by the MedStar institutional review board under number STUDY00007167 and adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline [14]. The study received a waiver of HIPAA (Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act) authorization, as it met all applicable regulatory requirements. We aimed to investigate the relationship between the use of GLP‐1 RAs 7 days or less prior to EGD and the presence of gastric contents during the EGD procedure and the rate of procedure abortion. We also aimed to evaluate the odds of occurrence of clinical complications such as aspiration pneumonia after EGD in participants on GLP‐1 RAs.

Participants

Our study used the MedStar Health Research Institute bioinformatics database to identify who underwent elective EGD. The cohort included both inpatient and outpatient EGDs. Participants were classified in two groups, participants who were actively taking GLP‐1 RAs (i.e., semaglutide, dulaglutide, exenatide, liraglutide, tirzepatide) and those who were not. To be considered actively taking GLP‐1 RAs, participants had to have been using GLP‐RAs 7 days or less prior to the procedure. Participants with any documented use of GLP‐1 receptor agonists more than 7 days prior to the procedure were excluded from both study groups. This was ascertained by reviewing prescription records and clinical notes. All participants were adults (aged ≥ 18 years). Participants were excluded if they had a history of gastric surgery, altered gastric anatomy, active GI bleeding, non‐elective EGD procedure, or recent change in the GLP‐1 RAs dose.

Variables and Outcomes of Interest

Data collected included demographic characteristics, body mass index (BMI), hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), medication history and type of anesthesia. The primary outcome was presence and volume of gastric contents (classified to small, medium or large sized based on endoscopy report), need for gastric lavage, procedural bleeding and abortion of procedure due to gastric contents. While bleeding is not a known adverse effect of GLP‐1 RAs, it was included among the outcomes to provide a broader view of procedural safety. Secondary outcomes were occurrence of aspiration and post‐procedural hospitalization. Outcomes were manually abstracted from endoscopy procedure reports by authors. In cases of ambiguity, the principal investigator reviewed the source documentation to ensure consistency. No outcome data were obtained from the MHRI bioinformatics database.

Statistical Analysis

The independent variable was whether the patient had a GLP‐1 RAs treatment. Covariates were summarized and stratified by GLP‐1 RAs treatment, and percent missing by group was reported. Categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables were summarized using means and standard deviations (SD). Differences in patient characteristics between groups were tested using two‐sample t‐tests for continuous variables and chi‐squared tests or Fisher's exact tests for categorical variables.

Outcomes with fewer than 10 events (i.e., outcome variables where the frequency in the smallest category was less than 10) were not used in the analysis. This is because an outcome variable needs at least 10 events in order to estimate the effect of one independent variable in a regression analysis [15].

Missing data was handled using Multivariate Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE). Ten sets of imputations were created using 20 iterations. Predictive mean matching was used for continuous variables, and logistic regression imputation was used for binary variables. Convergence was assessed by comparing the distribution of the imputations to the distribution of the observed data. Figures S1–S3 highlight the distribution of imputations.

Within each imputation, entropy‐balancing weights were used to balance the covariates between participants receiving GLP‐1 RAs treatment and those not receiving GLP‐1 RAs treatment. The entropy‐balancing weights were employed in order to obtain the average treatment effect (ATE) of GLP‐1 RAs on the outcomes. Continuous covariates were balanced on the 25th, 50th, and 75th quantiles, in addition to balancing on the means. Absolute standardized mean differences (absolute SMD) and variance ratios were reported across imputations in the unweighted and weighted sample. If an absolute SMD was less than 0.10, then the covariate was considered balanced across the treatment groups. This was highlighted in Figure S4.

Logistic regression models (unadjusted odds ratios) were used to estimate the unadjusted effect of GLP‐1 RAs on the outcomes. Weighted logistic regression models (adjusted odds ratios) were used to estimate the ATE of GLP‐1 RAs on the outcomes. The models were fit on each imputed dataset and the estimates were pooled into a single set of estimates. Odds ratios (OR), 95% confidence intervals (CI), and p‐values were reported from the models. A p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

All statistical analyses were performed in R version 4.4.1. Multiple imputation was conducted using the R package MICE [16], and entropy‐balanced weighting was performed using the R package MatchThem [17].

Results

Cohort Characteristics

A total of 1408 participants were included in the analysis; 270 were using GLP‐1 RAs and 1138 were not (control group). The mean age for participants who were using GLP‐1 RAs was 59 ± 11 compared to 62 ± 12 for participants who did not (p‐value 0.002), 155 (57.40%) of the participants in the GLP‐1 RAs group were females while 601 (52.80%) were females in the control group (p‐value 0.173). In the GLP‐1 RAs group, 235 (57.40%) were non‐Hispanics and 145 (53.70%) were African American. Similarly, 1014 (89.10%) of the control group were non‐Hispanic, and 600 (52.70%) were African American (p = 0.463 and p = 0.799, respectively). Participants in the GLP‐1 RA group had both a higher body mass index (33 ± 8 vs. 30 ± 7; p < 0.001) and hemoglobin A1c levels (7.43% ± 1.83% vs. 6.54% ± 1.50%; p < 0.001). Additionally, the prevalence of diabetes and insulin use was higher among GLP‐1 RAs users (77.30% vs. 49.00% and 38.40% vs. 16.40%, respectively; both p < 0.001). There were no significant differences in the type of anesthesia administered (p = 0.144) or in the procedure duration (14–15 min on average, p = 0.683) between the two groups. Seventeen participants required endoscopic lavage, while 11 had their procedures aborted due to large gastric contents. Clinical Outcomes

In the GLP‐1 RAs group, 4 (1.48%) participants were hospitalized after the procedure compared to 30 (2.63%) participants in the control group. We performed chart review and found that most hospitalizations were due to underlying comorbidities rather than procedure‐related complications. Only one documented aspiration event was identified in the GLP‐1 RA group, while no events occurred in the control group. The unadjusted odds ratio for post‐procedure hospitalization was lower; however, it was not statistically significant (OR, 0.56; 95% CI, 0.19–1.59; p = 0.273). After adjusting for potential confounders, the odds of post‐procedure hospitalization were statistically significantly lower among participants receiving GLP‐1 RAs (adjusted OR, 0.19; 95% CI, 0.06–0.58; p = 0.004). In subgroup analysis among African American patients, GLP‐1 RAs have no statistically significant association with the clinical outcomes.

Discussion

Our retrospective cohort study showed that among 1408 participants undergoing EGD, those using GLP‐1 RAs (n = 270) were younger with a statistically significant higher BMI (33 ± 8 vs. 30 ± 7) and HbA1c levels (7.43% ± 1.83% vs. 6.54% ± 1.50%) compared to non‐users (n = 1138), with higher prevalence of diabetes and insulin use. There were no significant differences between groups in sex, ethnicity, type of anesthesia, or procedure duration. After adjustment for the covariables and applying advanced statistics (entropy‐balancing and multiple imputation) to account for missing variables, procedural outcomes—including the presence of gastric contents, need for endoscopic lavage, procedure abortion due to gastric residue, and bleeding—did not have statistically significant differences between the two study groups. However, adjusted analysis showed that GLP‐1 RAs use was associated with a significantly lower risk of post‐procedural hospitalization (adjusted OR, 0.19; 95% CI, 0.06–0.58; p = 0.004).

Our findings align with those of Singh et al., who conducted a comprehensive meta‐analysis demonstrating that GLP‐1 RA use is associated with increased odds of retained gastric contents. Importantly, their analysis did not find a significant increase in aspiration events among GLP‐1 RA users [18]. Our findings diverge from other studies that showed increased odds of gastric contents. Despite having increased odds of the presence of gastric contents during EGD, our results did not reach statistical significance (adjusted OR, 1.52; 95% CI, 0.58–3.99; p = 0.395). This is different from Kobori et al., who found that treatment with GLP‐1 RAs was associated with higher rates of gastric contents (1 participant (0.49%) in the control group vs. 11 participants (5.40%) in GLP‐1 RAs group p = 0.004) [5]. Our findings differed from those of Chapman et al., who reported that GLP‐1 RAs treatment was associated with an increase in the odds of retained gastric contents (adjusted OR, 4.62; 95% CI, 1.21–17.57; p = 0.025) [7]. Additionally, Nadeem et al. found that GLP‐1 RAs treatment was associated with higher odds of gastric contents (adjusted OR, 3.78; 95% CI, 2.93–4.87; p < 0.001) [3]. Bi et al. found similar results, reporting increased gastric residual contents with semaglutide use [19]. This divergence might be explained by a small sample size in our GLP‐1 RAs cohort and the difference in the cohort's demographics (i.e., a predominantly African American population). Notably, our study results showed a significant reduction in the rate of post‐procedural hospitalization and no increased odds of aspiration events. This is in agreement with the previous results [7].

The observation that the use of GLP‐1 RAs is associated with lower odds of post‐procedural hospitalization and no increased odds of procedural complications raises important considerations for clinical practice. One potential mechanism for the lower post‐procedural hospitalization rate among GLP‐1 RAs users may be related to the metabolic and anti‐inflammatory benefits of these agents [20]. These results suggest that the current practice of discontinuing GLP‐1 RAs prior to EGD may warrant re‐evaluation. Clinicians might consider maintaining GLP‐1 RAs therapy, particularly if it is deemed necessary for improved diabetic or weight control. This is in accordance with the American Gastroenterological Association's (AGA) recommendations [21]. Moreover, our study examines a population that is typically underrepresented in clinical research [22], and disproportionately affected by obesity and diabetes [12, 23]. These results might guide the clinical practice toward a different approach when managing GLP‐1 RAs prior to EGD.

To our knowledge, our study is the first study to evaluate the relationship between GLP‐1 RAs use and EGD outcomes in a predominantly African American cohort. By specifically examining this population, our findings offer valuable insights into the safety and efficacy of GLP‐1 RAs therapy in a group that may exhibit distinct pathophysiological characteristics. These findings can help inform more inclusive clinical recommendations and encourage tailored patient management strategies that address the unique needs of underrepresented populations, ultimately contributing to efforts to reduce healthcare disparities.

As a retrospective study, we are limited in establishing causal relationships and may be subject to selection bias. This was mitigated by a robust inclusion and exclusion criteria. Another limitation of the study was missing data in our cohort, which was mitigated by entropy‐balancing and multiple imputation; however, residual confounding from unmeasured variables may persist. Additionally, a key limitation of our study is the use of subjective, qualitative assessment of gastric contents, which increases the risk of measurement bias. Validated visualization scales, such as AR‐VIS (Augmented Reality Visualization and Information System) endoscopy [24] or PIVI (Preservation and Incorporation of Valuable endoscopic Innovations) [25], were not available retrospectively but should be incorporated into future prospective studies.

In conclusion, our study provides a unique perspective on the outcomes of GLP‐1 RAs treatment in a predominantly African American population. It shows that the current recommendations to discontinue treatment prior to EGD can be adjusted to a case‐by‐case shared decision‐making model based on patient risk profile. Multicenter, prospective randomized trials are needed to optimize care for the rapidly growing population of GLP‐1 RAs users.
