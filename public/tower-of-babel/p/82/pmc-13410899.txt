Capacity utilization of China’s elderly care service industry during 2010–2022
Authors: Xiuquan Huang, Tao Zhang, Xi Wang, Hanxiang Gong, Wenshuo Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChina’s elderly care service industry (ECSI) plays a critical role in addressing the needs of an aging population. However, the industry faces both resource shortages and waste, particularly of fixed assets, while capacity utilization (CU), which reflects fixed asset efficiency, has received limited attention.MethodsThis study develops a CU measurement model based on the data envelopment analysis framework to evaluate the CU of ECSI across 30 provinces in China from 2010 to 2022. In addition, the Dagum Gini ratio and Moran’s I are employed to analyze the spatial disparities and correlations.FindingsThe CU of China’s ECSI showed a downward trend with fluctuations, declining from 0.7426 in 2010 to 0.5889 in 2022. Central China generally exhibit higher CU than eastern and western China. Western China had the lowest CU and the most pronounced fluctuations. The Dagum Gini ratio indicated that the difference between subregions was the primary driver of the total difference (43%), followed by the difference within subregions (28%). Moran’s I analysis showed that CU in China’s ECSI exhibited increasing spatial agglomeration, with weak but significant spatial correlation. Economically developed provinces, such as Jiangsu, Zhejiang, and Hubei, formed High-High clusters. In contrast, underdeveloped western provinces, including Gansu and Xinjiang, formed Low-Low clusters.ConclusionsChina’s ECSI faces declining CU and pronounced regional imbalances. The increasing spatial agglomeration and distinct clustering patterns highlight the need for targeted policies to reduce regional disparities and improve resource allocation.Keywords: Capacity utilization, Data envelopment analysis, Elderly care service industry

Introduction

According to the data released by the National Bureau of Statistics of China, by the end of 2024, China’s population aged ≥ 60 reached 310.31 million, accounting for 22.0% of the total population, including 220.23 million aged ≥ 65 (15.6%)1.The trend of deepening aging is expected to continue in the future [1]. According to the United Nations classification, China has entered a moderately aging society in 2025 [2]. China is actively preparing to cope with population aging. By the end of 2025, China has 39,000 elderly care institutions, with 7.68 million beds and 722,000 employees2. Despite having a large total amount of resources, China’s per capita resources are below the world average [3], indicating increasing pressure on elderly care services (ECSs).

However, the existing insufficient elderly care resources have still not been fully utilized [4]. These issues include: first, approximately 50% of beds in elderly care facilities remain unoccupied nationwide. For example, in Shanghai, the city with the highest economic development and most severe aging, the occupancy rate of nursing homes was less than 45%3. Second, the structure of bed types in nursing homes is unreasonable. The Chinese government has proposed that by 2025, professional nursing care beds should account for 55% of all beds in nursing homes, but there is still a significant gap. Third, the operational capacity of nursing homes is low. According to the China Nursing home Development Report, 48.1% of nursing homes in China are barely breaking even, while 32.5% are operating at a loss4. These issues collectively reflect low efficiency in China’s elderly care sector.

Geographical variation in resource allocation is an important source of inefficiency and imbalance in nursing homes [5]. It mainly reflects regional differences in economic development, government capacity, public service provision, and the level of population aging [6]. As a result, elderly care resources are unevenly distributed across regions and between urban and rural areas. In large cities and core urban areas, nursing home resources are often insufficient. This places pressure on the elderly care market in surrounding cities and rural areas [7], reflecting a spillover effect rather than a simple crowding-out pattern. For example, in Beijing, Shanghai, and other economically developed regions, the number of people waiting for social welfare homes far exceeds available capacity. By contrast, surrounding areas tend to face excess supply, with idle beds and low utilization rates. Such spatial imbalances further reduce the efficiency of resource use. Therefore, improving the efficiency of nursing homes and narrowing regional disparities are crucial for coping with population aging, a consensus shared by both industry and academia.

Although total factor productivity, which reflects the efficiency of all resource utilization, is of great significance, for nursing homes, capacity utilization rate (CU), which reflects the efficiency of fixed asset utilization, should be given more attention. This is because the elderly care industry is a capital-intensive sector (e.g., beds and facilities), and the number of nursing homes has expanded rapidly in recent years. Therefore, CU offers a more targeted perspective for evaluating resource utilization in the elderly care service industry (ECSI). Examining the CU of nursing homes across China’s provinces, together with the spatial heterogeneity and spatial interactions, is important for addressing the challenges posed by population aging.

Literature review

Many scholars have studied resource utilization efficiency in nursing homes, focusing mainly on the comprehensive efficiency or total factor productivity of nursing homes. These studies can be divided into two groups: the first group emphasizes the measurement of elderly care resource utilization efficiency and its spatiotemporal characteristics; the second group focuses on the factors influencing elderly care resource utilization efficiency. Currently, research about the resource utilization efficiency of nursing homes in China mainly concentrates on total factor productivity. For instance, Li [8] uses panel data on nursing homes in 276 cities in China (2010–2016) to examine their spatiotemporal changes. The study found that coastal areas scored higher than inland areas, and urban areas scored higher than rural areas. Luan [9] used a data envelopment analysis (DEA) model to measure the efficiency of elderly care resources in Shanghai from 2010 to 2019, decomposing efficiency change into technical efficiency, pure technical efficiency, and scale efficiency. Zhang [10] used the BCC and SBM models to analyze the efficiency of 32 registered long-term care institutions in Xiamen in 2016, finding that private long-run care institutions had significantly higher efficiency than public ones. The second stream of research examines factors influencing elderly care resource utilization efficiency, often addressing these relationships indirectly rather than through direct analysis. For example, Stoddart [11] conducted a survey of 2,000 elderly individuals selected through stratified random sampling and used logistic regression to explore the determinants of using statutory and private domiciliary care services among elderly people living in the community. Garavaglia [12] used bootstrap DEA to assess the efficiency of 40 nursing homes in northwestern Lombardy, Italy, and analyzed the influencing factors, focusing on the impact of ownership (whether private or public) on the efficiency of nursing homes.

CU has long been a focus in academia, as it is a classic concept in production economics. Scholars have different views on how to measure or define CU. Klein [13] defined production capacity as the output level that a firm achieves when it reaches equilibrium. Kirkley [14] defined capacity as the maximum and potential output achievable using available input factors, and CU as the ratio of actual output to capacity. A CU of less than one indicates excess capacity. Cassels [15] argued that the output level corresponding to the lowest point on the short-run average cost (SAC) curve represents capacity, a view also supported by Morrison [16]. Segerson and Squires [17] considered capacity to be the output at the tangency point of the firm’s SAC and long-run average cost curves, and thus CU can be measured by calculating the minimum cost or output of a firm’s inputs and outputs. Kim [18] defined capacity as the output level at which profit is maximized. Pascoe and Tingley [19] compared several of these standards, noting that different standards correspond to different production capacities.

Benston [20] used parametric analysis to analyze bank scale and scope efficiencies, employing the Cobb-Douglas production function to estimate the parameters of the production frontier. Farrell [21] first proposed the DEA method at the micro level to measure firm efficiency. Fare [22] developed a DEA method for measuring capacity and CU, constructing a production frontier based on observable input and output values and estimating production capacity and CU for each unit based on fixed capital. It is well known that traditional DEA models, such as CCR and BCC models, calculate efficiency relative to the current production frontier, allowing only for horizontal (cross-sectional) comparisons, not vertical (cross-period) comparisons [23, 24]. Under the traditional DEA framework, the production frontier used as a reference for efficiency differs for the same decision-making unit (DMU) across different periods [25, 26]. To address this issue, scholars have developed several approaches, such as the Malmquist productivity index and the Luenberger productivity indicator [27–29]. Similarly, the cross-period comparison of CU also faces this issue, which has long remained unsolved. Yang [30] constructed a CU index for cases involving undesirable outputs using the Luenberger productivity indicator. Song [31] developed a generalized CU index for cross-period comparisons based on the Malmquist productivity index.

The current research on the resource utilization efficiency of China’s ECSI mainly focuses on total factor productivity, while there is little analysis of CU. To address this gap, this study first proposes a CU measurement method based on the DEA, providing a framework that can capture fixed-asset utilization in service-oriented and public-sector industries. The method is then applied to assess the CU of the ECSI across 30 provinces of China (excluding Hong Kong, Macao, Taiwan, and Tibet) from 2010 to 2022. Then, this study uses the Dagum Gini ratio, the global Moran’s I, and local Moran’s I to analyze the spatial disparities and spatial correlation of CU.

Materials and methods

Selection of input and output

In this study, the CU of ECSI was measured within the framework of DEA, which requires input and output indicators. The input and output indicators

First, nursing staff and management personnel are the direct providers and executors of services in nursing homes, making the number of employees an appropriate indicator to represent human resource inputs. Second, the operation of nursing homes requires material inputs, and the institutions themselves and their basic facilities are critical material support. Thus, this study uses the number of nursing homes and beds jointly as indicators of material asset inputs. Finally, financial capital is fundamental to ensure the smooth operation of ECSs and serves as the basis for both software and hardware facilities, determining the quality-of-care environments. Therefore, the number of fixed assets is selected as the indicator for financial capital.

The output indicators include economic output and service volume. We use the operating revenue of nursing homes to measure economic output. Service volume is measured by the total resident-days at year-end, which reflects the overall service volume delivered by nursing homes. Here, resident-days refer to the total number of days of care provided to residents (in person-days) at year-end.

Data source

The sample covers 30 provinces in China (excluding Tibet, Hong Kong, Macao, and Taiwan) from 2010 to 2022, obtained from the China Civil Affairs Statistical Yearbook (China Social Service Statistics).5 The data were compiled directly from official statistical sources, and basic data cleaning procedures were conducted to ensure consistency and comparability across provinces and years. No missing values were found in the dataset. In addition, all fixed asset data were adjusted for inflation using 2020 as the base year.

Results

CU in elderly care service industry at the regional level

Figure 1 shows the CU of China’s ECSI at the national level and across eastern, central, and western regions from 2010 to 20226. Overall, the CU exhibited a fluctuating downward trend during this period, decreasing from 0.7426 to 0.5889. One possible reason for this decline is the lag in the positive effects of policies related to ECSI, leading to a rise in the availability of elderly care resources outpacing the growth in demand, thereby resulting in a mismatch between the provision of and demand for ECSs. Moreover, CU was higher in central China than in the east and lowest in the west.

The capacity utilization of China’s elderly care service industry during 2010–2022

In eastern China, CU decreased from 0.7116 to 0.6287, a decline of approximately 11.64%, indicating a relatively modest decrease. This can be largely attributed to the solid foundation of ECSI in eastern China. Specifically, eastern China’s advanced economy and relatively mature ECSI have resulted in a stable supply-demand balance. Consequently, CU in eastern China remained relatively stable.

In contrast, the CU variation in central China was greater than that in eastern China. The CU in this region was 0.9110 in 2010, but it significantly decreased by 34.5% to 0.5967 by 2015. This decline was mainly attributed to the weak foundation of ECSI in central China, resulting in weak operational management capabilities. For instance, the planning of nursing homes in this region might lack long-term planning, leading to excessive expansion. In addition, the effect of the supportive policies had not yet been evident. Since 2020, the policy effect has gradually become apparent, and local governments have more mature ECSI management experience. Thus, by 2022, the CU in this region rose to 0.7810.

The CU in western China experienced the greatest change. The CU in this region dropped from 0.6511 in 2010 to 0.3481 in 2019, which was the lowest point throughout the entire period. Subsequently, the CU in western China began to gradually recover, reaching 0.4094 by 2022. The ECSI in western China started the latest and developed the slowest. Part of the reason is that the household income in this region is low, resulting in limited purchasing power for ECSs. Although the ECSI in western China received strong support from the central government, as reflected in the large investment in the industrial infrastructure, the CU remained at a relatively low. After 2020, with the gradual development of western China and the increase in household income levels, the CU began to rise. Moreover, the COVID-19 pandemic further stimulated the demand for ECSs, leading to a slight increase in the CU in western China.

CU in elderly care service industry at the provincial level

From 2010 to 2022, the CU of ECSI in China showed significant regional disparities, with generally higher utilization efficiency in eastern China than those in central and western China. A phased analysis helps better understand the trends and reasons behind these CU changes. Figures 2, 3 and 4 show the CU of each province for each year in three sub-periods: 2010–2013, 2014–2017, and 2018–2022. This segmentation provides relatively balanced time intervals and facilitates a clearer comparison of stage-wise developments. Figure 5 shows provincial CU across the three sub-periods and the full period (2010–2022).

During 2010 to 2013, the national average CU remained relatively stable, ranging from 0.70 to 0.80. Overall, the CU in the eastern and central provinces was relatively high. Some provinces in these regions reached a CU of 1 in specific years (such as Tianjin (2010), Jiangsu (2013), Shandong (2010, 2012 and 2013), Henan (2010 and 2013), and Guangdong (2010)). This may be associated with higher demand for ECSs in some eastern provinces, possibly reflecting a higher proportion of the elderly population and higher income levels.

The CU in Hainan has changed significantly. In 2010, its CU was only 0.1966, much lower than that of other provinces. This may be due to the population was not aging at a high rate at that time, resulting in insufficient demand for ECSs. However, by 2013, the CU in Hainan rose sharply to 0.8408. This might reflect that the elderly population from other provinces, supported by policy incentives, flowed into this area due to the superior natural environment and rich health and wellness resources in Hainan. Moreover, because Hainan is relatively small, it is more sensitive to the demand shock for ECSs, leading to significant fluctuations in CU.

From 2014 to 2017, the CU in various provinces showed significant fluctuations. In some eastern provinces, the CU dropped sharply, for instance, Tianjin’s index decreased from 0.9506 in 2013 to 0.5758 in 2017. This might be because the expansion rate of production capacity exceeded the growth rate of demand. In contrast, Anhui’s CU rose significantly from 0.5396 in 2014 to 0.7600 in 2017. Jiangsu maintained a relatively high CU throughout this period, consistently above 0.97 each year. The CU indices in western provinces such as Ningxia and Qinghai remained consistently low.

Ningxia is a remote and economically underdeveloped western province. During 2014–2017, its CU remained consistently low, indicating that the fixed assets of elderly care institutions were not fully utilized. This might reflect a mismatch between capacity and demand. Due to the small population of Ningxia, the development of the elderly service market is insufficient, which limits nursing home occupancy from the demand perspective. Moreover, labor shortages, poor service quality, and insufficient integration of medical and ECSs may further reduce the CU.

During 2018–2022, some provinces in eastern and central China, such as Jiangsu (0.9926), Anhui (0.9319), Shandong (0.9281), Henan (0.9424), and Sichuan (0.9534), witnessed a significant increase in their CU values. This may be related to regional differences in economic conditions, population aging, and elderly care policies. Jiangsu had the highest CU during this period, and its CU remained at 1 from 2019 to 2022, indicating that the province’s elderly care facilities have been operating at near capacity, achieving an effective match between supply and demand. The province’s strong economic foundation and fiscal capacity are related to this, which support the government’s continuous investment in ECSI and the continuous growth in elderly care demand, thereby helping to maintain a high occupancy rate in nursing homes. Additionally, the province has actively implemented elderly care policies and service integration (for example, the coordination of medical and elderly care services), which may improve operational management and reduce idle resources, enabling CU to remain near capacity for consecutive years.

However, during this period, the CU in Hainan further dropped to an extremely low value of 0.0761. This does not mean that the occupancy rate of nursing homes in Hainan is also at an extremely low level. This is inherent to the DEA method. As mentioned before, Hainan’s ECSI largely depends on the population of other provinces and is very sensitive to economic fluctuations. During this period, the economic growth rate further slowed down, the COVID-19 pandemic hindered personnel mobility, and significantly reduced the operating income of nursing homes in Hainan. For example, in 2018, the income of nursing homes in Hainan was only 54% of that in 2017. Although it improved in the following years, the gap compared to 2017 was still very large.

Over the period 2010–2022, as mentioned above, the CU in central China was greater than in eastern China, and was lowest in western China, which can also be observed in Fig. 5. Over the whole period, the top 10 provinces with the highest CU were Jiangsu, Shandong, Sichuan, Henan, Hubei, Anhui, Zhejiang, Jilin, Liaoning, and Hunan. Except for Sichuan, all other provinces were from eastern and central China. The top 10 provinces with the lowest CU were Shanxi, Yunnan, Shaanxi, Qinghai, Xinjiang, Beijing, Guizhou, Gansu, Hainan, and Ningxia, most of which are in western China. Jiangsu, Shandong, and Henan each had 5, 3, and 6 years with CU of 1, respectively, indicating maximum utilization of fixed assets. This demonstrates that elderly care facilities in these provinces were efficiently utilized against the backdrop of accelerating aging. Additionally, eastern regions benefited from strong policy support, with active social capital participation in ECSI. In contrast, provinces like Hainan, Qinghai, and Ningxia had low CU, especially Ningxia and Hainan, whose average CU for the entire period was only 0.2958 and 0.3140, respectively. In particular, Hainan’s low CU was largely related to its performance during the 2018–2022 period.

Spatial difference of CU in China’s elderly care service industry and its decomposition

Figure 6 shows that the DWS of CU in eastern, central, and western China followed significantly different trajectories. The DWS of western China was the largest among the three regions in most years, followed by eastern China, and lastly central China. Overall, the DWS of the three regions gradually increased during the sample period.

The difference within subregions of capacity utilization of elderly care service industry in eastern, central, and western China during 2010–2022

In 2010, the DWS of western China was 0.1899, peaking at 0.2870 in 2019, and then slightly declining but still remaining high at 0.2630 in 2022. The sharp increase in DWS in western China between 2017 and 2019 indicates a worsening imbalance in the utilization of elderly care facilities among the provinces in western China. This could be attributed to the effects of development support policies, particularly investment policies aimed at better-developed areas in the west, such as Sichuan and Chongqing. The disparities in western China mainly stem from regional economic inequality, lagging infrastructure, and differences in policy implementation.

In 2010, the DWS of eastern China was 0.1988, increasing to 0.2672 in 2022, indicating that the CU disparities among provinces in eastern China gradually widened. Fluctuations in eastern China were relatively moderate compared to western China. However, the DWS in eastern China intensified mainly after 2015, with notable increases in 2016, 2018, and 2021. The widening disparities in eastern China may be related to uneven economic development across provinces. Some provinces, such as Shanghai and Jiangsu, may benefit from stronger fiscal resources and policy support. Others, including Fujian and Hainan, may have developed more slowly. These differences may partly explain the increasing internal disparities over time.

In contrast, the DWS of central China was relatively stable, with much smaller fluctuations. The main increase occurred before 2014. In 2010, the DWS in central China was only 0.0263, increasing to 0.1358 in 2014, and reaching 0.1395 in 2022. Thus, the overall disparity during the 2015–2022 period remained moderate. This suggests that the provinces in central China maintained a high level of consistency in the construction and utilization of elderly care facilities. Compared to eastern and western China, the economic foundation and development levels of the central provinces are more similar, and the supply and demand for elderly care facilities are relatively balanced, resulting in consistently low disparities within the region.

Figure 7 illustrates the CU disparities between the three regions from 2010 to 2022. Overall, the DBS between the three regions increased gradually during the sample period. Focusing on the differences between eastern and central China, the disparity grew from 0.1532 in 2010 to 0.2216 in 2022, increased almost year by year, indicating a widening trend. This suggests that while CU in central and eastern China declined, the decline was faster in central China. CU in central China decreased from 0.911 in 2010 to 0.781 in 2022, while in eastern China it fell from 0.7116 in 2010 to 0.6287 in 2022. Additionally, central China experienced a decline mainly during 2010–2015, whereas eastern China saw a fluctuating decline throughout the sample period, contributing to an increased DBS between the two regions. The DBS between eastern and central China also exhibited a phased pattern, with minimal changes and no clear trend from 2010 to 2017, and a gradual increase beginning in 2018.

The difference between subregions of capacity utilization of elderly care service industry in eastern, central, and western China during 2010–2022

The DBS between eastern and western China was the largest among the three DBS values and remained so for almost the entire sample period. In 2010, the DBS between eastern and western China was 0.2022, which steadily increased to 0.3917 by 2019, before declining to 0.3504 in 2022. This trend indicates that the gap between the elderly care systems in western and eastern China widened year by year, particularly with a sharp increase in DBS from 2015 to 2019. The main reason for this is the significant decline in CU in western China from 2010 to 2019, while eastern China experienced only a slight decline. The economic advantages and policy support in eastern China may led to better utilization of elderly care facilities, whereas western China faced consistently lower utilization due to underdeveloped infrastructure and insufficient resource allocation, resulting in a significant increase in the DBS gap.

The DBS between central and western China showed a similar trajectory to that between eastern and western China, with the DBS being slightly lower than the eastern-western DBS. This was mainly due to changes in CU in western China during the sample period. The CU gap between central and eastern China was relatively small, and their CU fluctuations were less pronounced compared to those in western China, as shown in Fig. 1. The DBS between central and western China was 0.1758 in 2010, peaked at 0.3931 in 2019, and subsequently declined to 0.3518 in 2022.

Figure 8 presents the values and percentages of the three sources of the total difference. The results indicate that, from 2010 to 2022, regional disparities in CU were primarily driven by DBS. The percentage of difference between subregions (PDBS) ranged from 27% to 53% during the sample period, with an average of 43.37%, and the corresponding average value of DBS was 0.0956. Although PDBS fluctuated over time, it exhibited an overall increasing trend, suggesting a growing contribution of DBS to the total difference. High DBS values were observed in the recent years of 2019 (53%, 0.1497), 2020 (47%, 0.1229), 2021 (50%, 0.1411), and 2022 (49%, 0.1370). The DBS reached its maximum value of 0.1497 in 2019, while the PDBS also peaked at 53% during the sample period. In contrast, the minimum value of DBS was 0.047 in 2011, and at this time, DBS accounted for 26% of the total difference.

The sources of the total difference of capacity utilization in China during 2010–2022

DWS and IT show similar contributions, with average values of 0.0604 and 0.0595, each accounting for about 28% of the total difference. The proportion of within-subregion differences (PDWS) ranges narrowly between 25% and 30%, whereas IT varies more widely, from 22% to 41%. In 2011, the values of the percentage of the intensity of transvariation (PIT) and PDWS both reached their respective peaks at 41% and 32%, while PDBS dropped to its lowest value of 27%. In contrast, in 2019, when PDBS reached its maximum value of 53%, PDWS and PIT respectively dropped to their lowest levels, namely 25% and 22%. This indicates that there is a dynamic interaction among these three components, an increase in PDBS is often accompanied by a decrease in PDWS and PIT.

Analysis of spatial autocorrelation of CU of China’s elderly care service industry

Using global spatial autocorrelation, we calculated Moran’s I for CU in China’s ECSI from 2010 to 2022 (Table 2). Overall, the Moran’s I values are small in magnitude (ranging from − 0.0133 to 0.0916), indicating that the strength of spatial dependence is weak at the provincial scale. However, the corresponding Z-scores and p-values show that spatial autocorrelation is statistically significant and positive in several years (e.g., 2013 and 2016–2022), suggesting the presence of weak but significant spatial clustering and spatial dependence of CU across provinces. In terms of temporal variation, Moran’s I fluctuates over time but becomes consistently positive and significant after 2016, peaking in 2019 (I = 0.0916) and remaining positive in 2020–2022 (around 0.065). These results imply that CU exhibits limited yet non-random spatial association among provinces during the later period. We also acknowledge that using provinces as the spatial unit may attenuate detectable clustering due to the relatively coarse spatial scale, which is discussed as a limitation.

The Moran’s I of capacity utilization of China’s elderly care service industry

To analyze whether there is local clustering in the CU of ECSI across provinces in China, and to more intuitively reflect the spatial correlation of CU across regions, the Local Moran’s I was used to conduct a local spatial autocorrelation analysis on CU across provinces in 2010, 2014, 2018, and 20227. The results are shown in Fig. 9. In 2010 and 2018, the High-High clusters were mainly distributed in central and eastern China, including Jiangsu, Zhejiang, Anhui, Jiangxi, and Hubei provinces. Jiangsu and Zhejiang belong to economically developed areas. The CU of ECSI in these areas has continuously improved. This may be associated with stronger economic conditions, rising demand, and improvements in elderly care systems. The High-High clustering observed in Henan may be related to its large population, strong demand, and policy support, which have also contributed to the development of ECSI in neighboring regions, forming the High-High clustering in CU.

The local spatial autocorrelation of capacity utilization of China’s elderly care service industry

In 2010, 2018, and 2022, the Low-Low clusters were mainly distributed in western China, including Gansu, Ningxia, and Xinjiang. Due to differences in resource endowments, the development of ECSI in these regions is limited, with weak diffusion capacity and relatively low CUs, resulting in the formation of a Low-Low clustering pattern in Gansu, Ningxia, and Xinjiang.

In addition, in 2014 and 2022, High-Low clustering phenomena appeared in Shanghai and Fujian, indicating significant disparities in the development levels of ECSI compared to their neighboring regions, leading to an unbalanced spatial distribution pattern. Shanghai’s type changes across years because Local Moran’s I is evaluated relative to its neighbors; shifts in neighboring provinces’ CU can turn Shanghai into a High-Low outlier even if its own CU remains high.

Discussion

Our research results indicate that the overall CU at the national level showed a downward trend, while there are significant regional differences. This suggests that the expansion of fixed assets and the ECSI has not been matched by demand [45]. A reasonable explanation is that the speed of policy-driven capacity expansion exceeded the growth of demand for nursing homes and the improvement of operational capabilities, resulting in persistent mismatch between supply and demand in certain regions [46].

From a regional perspective, the CU in central China is higher than that in eastern China, while the CU in western China is the lowest and shows the greatest fluctuations. This indicates that CU is not only influenced by economic conditions, but also by the alignment between supply and demand, as well as productivity. Provincial cases further illustrate these relationships. Provinces with high CU are mainly concentrated in eastern and central China. For example, Jiangsu maintained a CU close to 1 throughout the sample period and had several years with a CU of 1, which is consistent with the province’s strong aging-related demand, continuous investment, and efficient service organization and management [47]. In contrast, Ningxia is a typical low-CU province in western China; limited effective demand and an underdeveloped industrial foundation have limited occupancy rates and operating revenues [48]. The CU in Hainan shows significant volatility, indicating its sensitivity to demand shocks and population mobility, especially during the period from 2018 to 2022.

The Dagum Gini decomposition indicates that DBS accounts for a substantial share of overall inequality, and DWS are particularly prominent in western China. Spatial analyses suggest that although global Moran’s I values are small, they are statistically significant in several years. The local Moran’s I identifies High-High clusters in more developed provinces and Low-Low clusters in parts of western China. Overall, the differences in CU at the provincial level exhibit distinct structural and spatial patterns [49].

These findings indicate that government policies for the ECSI need to be targeted according to the characteristics of different areas. In the western provinces with low CU, the government should focus on labor supply in the ECSI, operation management, and service quality to improve the conversion of capacity into effective services. Moreover, the ECSI development plan should be adjusted based on actual needs. In terms of demand, improving the affordability and accessibility of ECSs helps to increase CU. For provinces with fluctuating demand, policies should enhance the resilience of the ECS supply system to stabilize CU, for example, by improving coordination among elderly care facilities, communities, and home-based care services. In high CU provinces, the government’s focus should shift from expansion to improving the service structure and quality. Given the regional differences and spatial correlations, cross-regional coordination and the promotion of effective practices are also necessary.

This study has some limitations. Firstly, this study is based on provincial-level data, so the differences among cities within the province and the differences between urban and rural areas may not be fully demonstrated. However, this limitation does not necessarily undermine the provincial-level policy implications of this study, because local elderly care service plans are generally formulated under the guidance of provincial governments while retaining some local flexibility. Moreover, our policy recommendations are for the provincial government. Therefore, when applying the research results to a more specific policy environment, it is necessary to interpret them with caution. Future research can utilize more detailed data to better capture local-level differences. Secondly, the estimating CU may be sensitive to the specific settings of the DEA model and the selection of input and output variables. Moreover, due to data deficiencies, some provinces were excluded. Future research can use city-level data to test the robustness under different model settings and indicator selections, and analyze the impacts of population aging, income, migration, and policy investment on CU, in order to better reveal the underlying mechanisms.

Conclusions

This study proposes a CU measurement method within the framework of DEA to evaluate the CU of the elderly care service industry across 30 provinces in China (excluding Hong Kong, Macau, Taiwan, and Tibet) from 2010 to 2022 and to investigate its spatiotemporal characteristics. Furthermore, the study employs the Dagum Gini coefficient, global Moran’s I, and local Moran’s I to examine the spatial disparities and spatial correlation of CU. The major findings of this paper are summarized as follows.

Firstly, at the national level, the CU of ECSI shows a downward trend with fluctuations, dropping from 0.7426 in 2010 to 0.5889 in 2022. In eastern China, CU decreased from 0.7116 to 0.6287, with a decline of approximately 11.64%. In central China, CU exhibited significant fluctuations, dropping from 0.9110 in 2010 to 0.5967 in 2015, and then rebounding to 0.7810 in 2022. The CU in western China changed the most significantly, dropping from 0.6511 in 2010 to 0.3481 in 2019, and then recovering to 0.4094 in 2022. Throughout the entire period, CU in central China was usually higher than that in eastern China, while CU in western China was the lowest. Provinces with higher CU mainly came from the eastern and central China. Jiangsu, Shandong, and Henan had CU at the highest levels for many years. In contrast, provinces in the west such as Hainan, Qinghai, and Ningxia had lower CU.

Secondly, the DWS of western China was the largest among the three regions in most years, followed by eastern China, and then central China. Overall, the DWS of the three regions gradually increased during the sample period. The DBS between the three regions increased gradually during the sample period. The DBS between eastern and western China was the largest among the three regional DBS measures, and the DBS between eastern and central China was the smallest. DBS was the primary driver of the total difference, accounting for approximately 43%, while DWS and IT each accounted for about 28%.

Thirdly, China’s ECSI exhibited increasing spatial agglomeration during 2010–2022, as shown by Moran’s I. Based on local spatial autocorrelation analysis of CU across provinces in 2010, 2014, 2018, and 2022, weak but statistically significant spatial clustering was observed. High-High clusters appeared in developed provinces such as Jiangsu, Zhejiang, Anhui, and Hubei. Such clustering may be associated with strong economic conditions and population aging. In contrast, Low-Low clusters appeared in underdeveloped western provinces such as Gansu, Ningxia, and Xinjiang. These clusters are linked to economic constraints and environmental conditions.
