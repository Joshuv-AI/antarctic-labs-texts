Willingness to Pay for Treatment Attributes in Diffuse Large B-Cell Lymphoma: A Discrete Choice Experiment in Japan
Authors: Yasuyuki Arai, Timothy Bolt, Hikaru Onishi, Michael LoPresti, Victoria Arguissain, Jörg Mahlich
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectivesTreatment decision-making in relapsed/refractory diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (R/R DLBCL) is increasingly complex, especially with the emergence of novel, high-cost therapies such as chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy. Understanding how physicians weigh various clinical and nonclinical treatment attributes is essential for aligning healthcare decisions with both clinical value and economic sustainability. This study aims to quantify Japanese physicians’ preferences in health outcomes and patient experience outcomes through eliciting trade-offs in a choice experiment for specific treatment characteristics in R/R DLBCL.MethodsWe performed a discrete choice experiment (DCE) with Japanese hematologists and oncologists. Participants completed an online best–worst scaling three alternative discrete choice experiment featuring a series of hypothetical treatment outcomes for DLBCL. The DCE included two patient vignettes: patient Q, a 70-year-old male with two prior treatment lines and Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status 1; and patient R, a 58-year-old female with three prior treatment lines and ECOG performance status 2. Predefined attributes included: overall survival (OS) rates at 12 and 24 months, change in ECOG status at 6 months, CAR-T related side effects (risk of severe cytokine release syndrome (CRS), and risk of severe neurological events), duration of hospitalization, time until treatment initiation, and total treatment cost. A conditional logit model was used to calculate odds ratios (OR) for each attribute’s influence on selecting the preferred treatment recommendation. ORs were translated into willingness-to-pay (WTP) estimates for the health outcomes, changes in adverse event risks and waiting time/hospitalization duration.ResultsA total of 231 Japanese hematologists and oncologists participated, each making six treatment decisions for two patient vignettes. Across both profiles, 12- and 24-month overall survival (OS), hospitalization duration, waiting time, and treatment cost significantly influenced preferences. For patient Q, reduced CRS risk was significant, while for patient R, ECOG improvement was influential. Willingness to pay (WTP) for a 1% increase in 24-month OS was approximately JPY 1.4 million (~USD 9520) for patient Q and JPY 1.8 million (~USD 12,240) for patient R; corresponding estimates for 12-month OS were slightly lower. A 1-week reduction in hospital stay was valued at JPY 1.1–1.9 million (USD 7480–12,920), and a 1-week reduction in waiting time at JPY 600,000–1.5 million (USD 4080–10,200). Overall, physicians prioritized long-term survival more strongly for the younger, more severe patient, whereas preferences were more balanced for the older patient. Clinical improvement (ECOG) weighed more heavily for the younger patient, while safety concerns were more salient in the older case.ConclusionsOur findings suggest that Japanese hematologists are cost-aware in their treatment decision-making. While improved OS remains the most valued outcome, reducing hospital length of stay is associated with substantial WTP, highlighting the importance of logistical and economic factors. The severity of prognosis shifted physician priorities to short-term survival and rapid access to care with more tolerance of adverse event risks. Future research should explore how these physician preferences align with patient perspectives.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40261-026-01549-9.

Introduction

Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) is the most common subtype of non-Hodgkin lymphoma, accounting for approximately 30–40% of cases globally. Despite advances in frontline therapy, up to 40% of patients experience relapsed or refractory (R/R) disease, which carries a poor prognosis [1]. In recent years, treatment options for R/R DLBCL have expanded with the introduction of chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapies, bispecific antibodies, and targeted agents [2, 3]. While these therapies offer potential improvements in long-term survival, they are associated with substantial economic burden, treatment-related toxicity, and logistical challenges, including prolonged hospitalization and limited availability [4, 5]. In Japan, where health expenditures are tightly regulated through a universal health insurance system, understanding how physicians evaluate trade-offs among survival, adverse events, logistical burden, and treatment costs is critical for ensuring sustainable and equitable oncology care [6, 7]. Although clinical guidelines provide recommendations, real-world treatment decisions often require balancing multiple competing priorities on the basis of patient characteristics and health system constraints.

Discrete choice experiments (DCEs) are a robust method for quantifying preferences by presenting decision-makers with hypothetical scenarios in which multiple attributes vary [8]. DCEs have been increasingly used in oncology to assess preferences among patients including conditions such as R/R DLBCL [9, 10], but evidence on physician preferences, in this indication is still limited [11]. In the Japanese context, only a few studies have investigated hematologists’ decision-making processes using preference elicitation methods [12]. This study aims to quantify Japanese hematologists’ preferences for key treatment attributes in R/R DLBCL using a DCE framework. By evaluating two patient vignettes with differing clinical characteristics, we explore how treatment decisions are influenced by treatment attributes such as overall survival (OS), adverse event risk, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status improvement, hospitalization duration, time to treatment initiation, and total cost. These findings have important implications for value-based care strategies in oncology.

Material and Methods

This study’s choice experiment followed the good practice as outlined by ISPOR Conjoint Analysis Task Force and standard practice [13, 14].

An online DCE was conducted by INTAGE Healthcare Inc. (Tokyo, Japan) among physicians selected from a panel managed by PLAMED Inc. (Tokyo, Japan). This panel comprises approximately 900 hematologists and some oncologists, representing about 32% of all hematologists registered in Japan [15]. A survey invitation was sent by email to hematologist and oncologist members of the physician panel. Physicians were asked to complete the survey within 1 week of receipt of the request. The survey included an initial consent question and some initial questions to determine the eligibility of physicians for the study. Those meeting the eligibility criteria were asked to continue with the main survey. Physicians were eligible to participate in the study if they provided consent to participate and they reported that they are a specialist in hematology, hemato-oncology, or oncology, have administered drug therapy to patients with relapsed/refractory diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) in the last 12 months. Moreover, an effort was made to include at least 30 physicians working in facilities already using/having adopted CAR-T, awaiting manufacturer certification, or in preparation for use CAR-T products. The only exclusion criteria was not meeting the above inclusion criteria.

The sampling strategy aimed to include at least 30 physicians affiliated with institutions that had already implemented or were in the process of adopting CAR-T cell therapies. The target sample size was set using the rule of thumb proposed by Orme. This rule estimates the required sample size on the basis of the number of repetitions of DCE tasks, the number of profiles presented per task, and the maximum number of levels of the designed attribute. On the basis of the DCE profile, which included up to four attribute levels and three alternatives per task and a maximum number of completed tasks of ten, the number of respondents needed for this study can be estimated to be at least 135. The study was conducted in accordance with the approved protocol, International Council for Harmonisation (ICH) guidelines, applicable Japanese regulations and clinical research standards, and the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Prior to survey administration, the study received approval from the Toukeikai Kitamachi Clinic Ethics Review Board (institutional review board (IRB) no. KXJ09854). Beyond the sampling inclusion criteria, a rationality check was performed whereby responses were eliminated for physicians that reported a differenced of more than 2 years in their age relative to their age registered for the panel and a difference in their sex registered for the panel.

Attributes and levels of the DCE were defined by clinicians and complemented by a literature search. As support for the selection of relevant attributes, we used the criteria defined by Tesler [16] and further explained by Mühlbacher et al. [17]. Therefore, all attributes should have the following characteristics. First, only attributes that are thought to influence the overall utility (preference) of an individual and thus affect the decision should be selected (relevance). The attributes must be changeable (influenceability). This ensures that the results of the DCE can be used to optimize the object of consideration according to the preferences.

The attributes were also selected to be plausible to represent the decision situation realistically (realism and plausibility). This is particularly relevant with our clinician sample as they understand the prognosis and outcome ranges. It should be possible to compensate for the worsening of attributes with improvements in other attributes (substitutive relationship) enabling the choice experiment to estimate meaningful trade-offs and, if a cost attribute in the appropriate range is included, the marginal willingness to pay (WTP).

For the present study, attributes were selected on the basis of clinicians’ advice. For instance, cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and severe neurological events such as immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS) are typical side effects of CAR-T therapies [18]. Both are prognostic factors of efficacy outcomes such as overall survival (OS) or progression-free survival (PFS) [19]. For attributes, the range covered by the levels was based on a literature search. Clinical levels such as efficacy and adverse events were sourced from Crump et al. [20] and a Cochrane systematic literature review [21]. Economic data, including treatment costs and hospital lengths of stay, are from Wakase et al. [22]. The experimental design was a D-efficient design generated in Ngene 1.4.0 (ChoiceMetrics, Sydney, Australia), constrained to ensure realism by preventing 24-month survival probabilities from exceeding those at 12 months since patients who do not survive the first 12 months cannot survive to 24 months. This produced 24 choice sets, blocked into four groups of six, each consisting of three alternatives that physicians ranked from best to worst. Each respondent clinician was randomly assigned to one block and therefore completed six choice tasks per patient vignette. Sample size considerations were informed by established guidance for discrete choice experiments, including the rule-of-thumb proposed by Orme, on the basis of the number of choice tasks completed by each respondent, which indicated a minimum sample size of approximately 112 respondents per vignette. The recruitment target was increased beyond this minimum to enable planned subgroup analyses and exploration of preference heterogeneity across clinician characteristics.

Each vignette response could be analyzed using the traditional choice experiment approach, on the basis of the most preferred option, or by incorporating the full ranking of all three alternatives. In the present analysis, we estimate preferences using a conditional logit model on the basis of the most preferred choice in each task. This specification was selected to estimate average marginal utilities associated with treatment attributes and to derive corresponding willingness-to-pay (WTP) measures. More flexible specifications, including mixed logit models, were explored in preliminary analyses and indicated some heterogeneity in preferences across selected attributes. However, given the relatively homogeneous respondent sample of specialist clinicians, and the stability and statistical significance of the cost parameter across specifications (a necessary condition for valid WTP estimation), the conditional logit model was retained as the primary model to provide a parsimonious and interpretable representation of mean treatment preferences.

The results presented are from the conditional logit model using the most preferred choice. The choice experiment estimates how attribute levels influence the probability of selecting a treatment option on the basis of its relative expected benefit (expected utility) relative to the other attributes. With these and the use of logistic regression modeling, the estimated preference weight parameters do not have direct or inherent interpretability in isolation but their influence on probability. To aid interpretation, the results are converted and presented using odds ratios [OR = exp(β), where β is the estimated attribute coefficient] and WTP estimates. Given that our audience is largely clinical, odds ratios are particularly meaningful, providing a directly interpretable measure of how each attribute affects the likelihood of selecting a treatment. Willingness-to-pay (WTP) estimates were then calculated by dividing each non-cost attribute coefficient by the negative of the cost coefficient (WTP = −β_attribute/β_cost), yielding the monetary value associated with a one-unit change in the attribute.

WTP estimates are expressed per one-unit change in each non-cost attribute. For example, the WTP associated with 12-month OS represents the value for a 1% increase in survival probability. For adverse events, negative WTP values reflect the amount of compensation that respondents would require for a 1% increase in risk. For time-related attributes (hospitalization and waiting time), WTP corresponds to the value of each additional week within the specified range. For ECOG performance status, dummy variables were used; thus, WTP represents the change from the reference category of “no change” to the alternative level presented. Finally, for variables with odds ratios that are not statistically significant, the corresponding WTP estimates should be interpreted with caution, as they are less precise and may vary more widely.

We also perform subgroup analysis to detect differences across physician characteristics and institutional context. To do so, we conducted separate conditional logit models by subgroup and used Swait–Louviere likelihood ratio tests [23] to determine whether preference structures differed statistically across groups.

All statistical analysis, including models for the subgroup analyses, were performed using Stata 18 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA).

Results

Of the 237 physicians responding, 231 met the rationality check, and hence, the results presented here are for these 231 physicians. The survey included 231 physicians, most of whom were male (87.0%) and between the ages of 30–49 (63.6%). Respondents were predominantly hematologists (78.8%), with an average of 17 years of postgraduate experience, and the majority (84.4%) were certified by the Japanese Society of Hematology. Nearly all worked in medium-to-large hospitals, with 28.1% in facilities with more than 800 beds, and most were affiliated with university (36.8%), general (31.2%), or national/public hospitals (27.7%). Geographically, the largest proportion were based in the Kanto (37.7%) and Kansai (25.5%) regions. Three quarters (75.8%) reported access to an ICU capable of managing adverse events, and hospitals employed an average of 4.5 physicians qualified to provide CAR-T therapy. In the prior year, respondents treated a mean of 6.4 patients with refractory DLBCL using CAR-T therapy, though this ranged widely (1–100 patients). A more detailed description of the sample is available elsewhere [24].

Reported relative importance rankings were derived from the dummy coded attribute level coefficients. For each attribute, the utility range was defined as the difference between the maximum and minimum level-specific utilities (including the reference level). Relative importance was then calculated as the ratio of each attribute’s utility range to the sum of utility ranges across all attributes, representing the proportion of total explained utility variation attributable to that attribute.

Attributes that were statistically significant across both vignettes included 24-month OS, 12-month OS, cost, hospitalization, and waiting time. Among these, 24-month OS had the highest relative importance (30.1–33.9%), followed by cost (19.9–22.7%) and 12-month OS (18.3–19.7%). CRS risk (7.0–9.0%) and neurotoxicity risk (6.5–7.0%) showed moderate relative importance but were not statistically significant. Hospitalization (5.0–6.2%) and waiting time (3.9–5.2%) had lower importance but were statistically significant. ECOG improvement demonstrated the lowest relative importance (2.6–3.0%) and was not statistically significant.

WTP Equivalences

Across the sample, the attributes with the most consistent and substantial influence on physicians’ treatment preferences were extended survival (both 12-month and 24-month), treatment cost, and time-related variables. For the older patient with less advanced disease (vignette patient Q), hospitalization duration emerged as the most important time factor, whereas for the younger patient with more advanced disease (vignette patient R), both waiting time for treatment initiation and hospitalization duration were statistically significant (Table 3). In contrast, the two side-effect risk attributes, the risk of severe CRS and risk of a severe neurological event had smaller impacts on decision-making when weighed against survival, cost, and time attributes. For vignette patient Q, a reduction in CRS risk was statistically significant, suggesting that physicians placed meaningful value on lowering this toxicity risk in older, less severely ill patients. For vignette patient R, however, improvement in functional status, measured by ECOG performance score (from 2 to 0), had a more pronounced influence on choices, reflecting the importance of maintaining or restoring quality of life in advanced disease. The WTP estimates provide a more nuanced quantification of physicians’ treatment preferences across the different attributes. For survival outcomes, physicians expressed the highest valuations. Specifically, for each 1% absolute increase in 24-month OS, WTP was approximately JPY 1.4 million (~USD 9520) for vignette patient Q and JPY 1.8 million (~USD 12,240) for vignette patient R, with both estimates reaching strong statistical significance (p < 0.001). For 12-month OS, the WTP values were slightly lower but remained substantial, at JPY 1.2 million (~USD 8160) for vignette patient Q and JPY 1.3 million (~USD 8840) for vignette patient R (both p < 0.001). Physicians also placed considerable value on reductions in treatment-related time burdens. A 1-week decrease in hospitalization was associated with WTP ranging between JPY 1.1 and JPY 1.9 million (USD 7480–12,920), statistically significant for both vignettes (p = 0.006 and p < 0.001). Similarly, a 1-week reduction in waiting time translated into WTP estimates of JPY 600,000–1.5 million (USD 4080–10,200), also statistically significant (p = 0.048 and p < 0.001). These findings underscore that physicians assign meaningful economic value not only to improvements in survival but also to logistical aspects of care delivery. Overall, these results indicate that while survival gains are the dominant driver of physician preferences, efficiency-related attributes such as reducing hospital stays and treatment delays also carry substantial value, particularly in more severe cases, while toxicity and functional status considerations show more context-specific importance.

Impact of Physician and Hospital Characteristics

To explore drivers of treatment preferences for DLBCL, we prespecified subgroups with clinician stakeholders, including hospital type (university versus non-university), institutional experience with refractory DLBCL, number of CAR-T-qualified physicians, and respondent age. Subgroup analyses revealed several statistically significant differences in attribute coefficients or WTP, though effect sizes and consistency varied (Supplementary Tables S1–S5). Institutional factors were more influential for vignette patient Q (older, less advanced DLBCL). These variables were correlated as university hospitals tended to be larger, have more qualified clinicians, and treat more patients; therefore, results are discussed collectively.

University hospitals, typically larger centers engaged in advanced research and specialized care, showed a slightly higher preference and WTP for longer survival, as well as greater WTP for reducing hospitalization and waiting times (Supplementary Table S1). Hospital size (number of beds) appeared even more directly relevant than university affiliation: physicians in larger hospitals had higher odds ratios and WTP for 24-month survival in both vignettes, while physicians in smaller hospitals were more sensitive to waiting times, especially for vignette patient R (Supplementary Table S2). Experience with complex cases, measured by treating ≥ 5 patients with refractory DLBCL annually, was associated with greater WTP for reducing hospitalization, while physicians treating fewer such patients showed higher WTP for 24-month survival gains (Supplementary Table S3). Similarly, facilities with ≥ 2 CAR-T-qualified physicians placed higher value on survival gains compared with less-equipped centers (Supplementary Table S4). Physician age differences were modest for survival attributes. Both age groups (under/above 40 years) valued 12- and 24-month OS similarly though younger physicians tended to have a slightly higher WTP, particularly for vignette patient R. Efficiency-related attributes showed clearer contrasts: younger physicians strongly preferred shorter hospitalization and waiting times for both vignettes, while older physicians’ aversion to longer stays was limited to vignette patient Q, and waiting time was not significant (Supplementary Table S5).

Discussion

This study estimates the WTP of Japanese hematologists and oncologists for treatments in the DLBCL setting. We found that survival time—particularly 24-month survival—was the most influential factor in treatment decision-making. On the basis of the DCE results, a 1% increase in 24-month OS was associated with an increase in WTP of approximately JPY 1.4 million (~USD 9520) for vignette patient Q and JPY 1.8 million (~USD 12,240) for vignette patient R. This is particularly relevant in this disease space, where new treatments in the form of CAR-T cell therapies have increased survival. In the ZUMA 7 study, axi-cel demonstrated a 4-year OS of 54.6% versus 46% in standard of care [25]. In the TRANSFORM trial, liso-cel demonstrated a 73.1% 18-month OS versus 60.6% in the SOC arm [26]. CAR-Ts also come with a notable increase in treatment costs [27]. The findings on this paper emphasize physicians’ implicit WTP for these new treatment options that improve survival.

Another factor that was an important driver of WTP was reduced waiting times. Notably, this is the first study to quantify the monetary value of reduced waiting times in oncology care. A 1-week reduction in waiting time was valued at JPY 600,000–1.5 million (~USD 4080–USD 10,200). In the DLBCL space, as experience with CAR-T therapy increases, reducing vein-to-vein time (V2VT), defined as the period from leukapheresis to CAR T cell infusion [28], has been a focus point for the industry [29]. A simulation even estimated that a reduction in V2VT from 54 days as observed in the JULIET trial [30] to 24 days as in the ZUMA-1 trial led to a 3.2-year gain in life expectancy in the US healthcare context per patient [31]. The increase in efficacy observed with shorter V2VT is primarily driven by higher infusion rates of CAR-T therapy, as many patients may experience disease progression during longer waiting periods, rendering them ineligible for treatment or leading to death. In this sense, decreased waiting times save lives, which is consistent with a high WTP for a decrease in V2VT.

Reduction of hospital length of stay (LOS) was a third significant choice driver: a 1-week reduction in hospital stay was valued at JPY 1.1–1.9 million (~USD 7480– USD 12,920). For Japan, in the DLBCL context, hospitalization LOS in days were 58 in 1 L, 50 in 2 L, and 45 in 3 L [32]. This is significantly longer than in Germany, where LOS was 34 days during 1 L, 25 in 2 L, and 24 in 3 L [33]. It is well known that Japan has by far the longest LOS within the OECD [34]. A comparative study between Japan and Canada found that, even after controlling for patient demographics and comorbidities, hospital stays in Japan were significantly longer than in Canada. These findings suggest that nonclinical factors, such as cultural norms, may contribute to persistent international differences in LOS [35]. One such cultural factor might be risk aversion, where Japan is considered the most risk-averse country in the world [36, 37]. In that context, a quicker treatment response would be a feasible way of saving more resources in Japan. These culturally driven influences on WTP underscore the limited transferability of our findings, as preferences shaped by Japan’s specific risk attitudes, healthcare norms, and decision-making frameworks may differ substantially from those in other countries. Consequently, results obtained in the Japanese context should be interpreted with caution when applied to settings with different cultural or institutional characteristics.

In the interest of reducing hospital stays, in the USA, CAR-Ts have started shifting to outpatient administration led by liso-cel’s OUTREACH study where 82 patients received liso-cel, with 70% in outpatient monitoring; among those outpatient, 25% of patients have never been hospitalized after infusion [38]. Other studies have shown that outpatient administration is safe and feasible with other approved CAR-T cell therapies [39]. Therefore, outpatient administration would reduce hospital burden and hospitalization rates [40], indicating that there is a higher WTP for treatment options where outpatient administration is possible.

When comparing the patient vignettes, we found that for the younger patient with more advanced cancer (vignette patient R), long-term survival was valued more highly than short-term survival, though the difference between the two was relatively modest. For the older patient with a less severe condition and a better prognosis (vignette patient Q), the emphasis on long-term survival was more pronounced, creating a wider gap relative to short-term survival. This pattern suggests that in severe cases, even short-term survival gains are perceived as meaningful, whereas in less severe cases, greater weight is placed on the potential for extended outcomes, consistent with expectations of longer-term benefit.

These preferences were also reflected in WTP estimates. Physicians indicated a higher WTP for long-term survival in the younger, more severe case, suggesting that disease severity may be a stronger driver of treatment decisions than patient age. Time-related attributes such as hospitalization duration and waiting time were also more influential in vignette patient R, highlighting the value placed on rapid treatment initiation and minimizing inpatient burden for patients with poor prognosis. Regarding side effects, CRS was a significant factor only in the less severe, older patient case. Physicians expressed a higher WTP to reduce this risk, indicating greater concern about serious side effects when patients are otherwise expected to achieve longer survival.

Physician characteristics also appeared to influence treatment priorities in meaningful ways. Physicians affiliated with university hospitals, working in larger institutions, or with greater experience in managing DLBCL placed stronger emphasis on survival outcomes. These environments may foster greater confidence in handling adverse events, allowing survival benefits to take precedence. University physicians, in particular, showed greater aversion to prolonged hospitalizations and, in the case of the younger, more advanced patient, stronger preference for reduced waiting times, likely reflecting a deeper understanding of key determinants of CAR-T success [25]. Larger hospitals exhibited a similar pattern, prioritizing shorter waiting times for the more severe patient group, which may similarly reflect institutional expertise.

Patterns also emerged in relation to clinical experience with refractory patients. Physicians from high-volume centers, who routinely manage such cases, expressed stronger aversion to long hospital stays, potentially reflecting awareness of opportunity costs and resource pressures in busy settings. In contrast, institutions with less exposure to refractory patients showed heightened concern about CRS, suggesting lower familiarity with managing such complications. Physician age also appeared to play a role: younger clinicians placed greater weight on long-term survival and demonstrated higher WTP for extended survival, indicating generational differences in how treatment benefits are valued.

An important question arising from our findings is the extent to which physician preferences align with those of patients. While our study focused on physicians in Japan, exploring patient perspectives remains a critical avenue for future research. Evidence from other contexts suggests that survival consistently emerges as a highly valued attribute across stakeholders but also highlights areas of divergence that could have meaningful implications for shared decision-making. For example, Birch et al. [10] conducted a DCE with US patients with DLBCL and similarly found that survival was the dominant treatment attribute. Thomas et al. [41] examined preferences in follicular lymphoma and reported that progression-free survival (PFS) was the most influential factor for both physicians and patients, indicating broad agreement on the primacy of efficacy.

However, a growing body of evidence suggests that the apparent convergence on survival may obscure important differences in how trade-offs are valued. In advanced melanoma, patients ranked OS as the most important attribute, while oncologists prioritized avoiding adverse events, with patients assigning significantly more weight to efficacy measures such as PFS and objective response rates [42]. Similarly, in non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC), patients placed greater emphasis on the possibility of durable “tail-of-the-curve” survival, whereas physicians focused more on median or expected survival [43]. Other systematic reviews reinforce this pattern, showing that patients tend to place relatively higher importance on treatment process and quality-of-life attributes such as convenience, independence, or reduced toxicity, whereas physicians often emphasize the management of adverse events and predictable efficacy outcomes [44]. These discrepancies suggest that while both groups consistently value survival, physicians and patients may weigh risks and trade-offs in markedly different ways. Patients may be more willing to tolerate higher risks or burdens if there is a chance of achieving durable benefit, whereas physicians often prioritize safety and risk management. The broader oncology literature, including research in renal cell carcinoma, confirms that survival remains the highest-ranked factor in physician decision-making [45], but patient-centered studies consistently point to a more multidimensional decision framework. Taken together, these findings underscore the need for empirical work in Japan to examine whether physician and patient preferences are aligned. If discrepancies exist, they may have important implications for treatment uptake, adherence, and satisfaction. Understanding these dynamics will be essential for supporting shared decision-making and ensuring that treatment choices reflect not only clinical expectations but also the lived experiences and values of patients.

Finally, our study does also have several political implications as we can roughly defer the WTP for an additional life year gained, which is an important parameter for cost effectiveness analysis to determine drug prices. An illustrative calculation is as follows. For CAR-T therapies, survival curves appear to reach a plateau between 12 and 24 months at approximately 40%, suggesting long-term remission in this proportion of patients [46]. A hypothetical new therapy that increases 12-month survival from 40% to 100% would be associated with a WTP of 60 × 1.42 million yen (which is one of our WTP estimates) resulting in a sum of 85.2 million yen. If remission is assumed to result in an additional 10 years of life expectancy, the new therapy would result in 6 (0.6 × 10) additional years compared with the current standard of care. Therefore, the WTP per life year would be JPY 14,200,000 million yen (85,200,000 ÷ 6) . How does this relate to official WTP thresholds in Japan? In Japan, Health Technology Assessment (HTA) guidelines that were introduced in 2016 [47] recommend a baseline WTP threshold of JPY 5,000,000 (~USD 35,000), with adjustments for disease severity allowing increases up to JPY 15,000,000 (~USD 104,000). For cancer-related diseases such as DLBCL, typically, a threshold of JPY 7,500,000 (~USD 52,500) is applied [48]. Therefore, the WTP derived from our study would be approximately 90% higher than the one used in health Japanese HTA. However, it should be noted that HTA thresholds are typically conservative, as they reflect the payer perspective rather than broader societal valuation [49].

Limitations

This study specifically addresses preferences of Japanese hematologists and oncologists and as such may not generalize to other countries or healthcare systems. The DCE method explicitly considers hypothetical treatment scenarios with a limited set of attributes; therefore, real-world decisions may reflect other priorities or incentives for particular patients or treatments. As patient preferences were not assessed, WTP and attribute weighting may diverge from real-world patient-centered decision-making.

It is noted that the sampling is constrained by practical considerations in the available panel of specialist clinicians, which may have been a particular limitation in performing subgroup analysis.

Conclusions

This study provides novel evidence on the determinants of physician decision-making in the treatment of DLBCL in Japan, quantifying the relative importance of key treatment attributes and the WTP associated with improvements in these factors. By capturing how physicians trade off between survival outcomes, treatment logistics, and side effects, this work offers one of the first quantitative assessments of choice drivers in this therapeutic space in Japan. Our findings reinforce the existing literature showing that survival gains are consistently prioritized by physicians while also revealing heterogeneity in WTP across different patient profiles and physician characteristics. These insights suggest that not only clinical outcomes but also institutional contexts and individual physician experience shape treatment preferences in meaningful ways. Future research should build on this work by directly examining patient preferences in the Japanese context, as well as investigating how differences between physician and patient perspectives may influence treatment uptake, satisfaction, and outcomes.
