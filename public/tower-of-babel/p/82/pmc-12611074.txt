Cognitive frailty in urban community-dwelling older adults: Associated physical and psychological risk factors
Authors: Nicholas Hardi, Kevin Kristian, Fernandus William, Fransisca Theresia, Patricia Angelica, Juan A. Kuistono, Michelle A. Irawan, Yvonne S. Handayani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Frailty and cognitive impairment is an integral part of morbidity in older adults. Their co-occurrence raises concerns and is further conceptualized as cognitive frailty (CF). CF increases the risk of disability, hospitalization, and death. Identification and modification of CF risk factors should reduce the burden. Data on older adults in Indonesia should be explored for better prevention or management measures. The aim of the study was to explore the prevalence of CF and the risk factors in urban older adults in the community.MATERIALS AND METHODS:This cross-sectional study included 128 older adults in the community in Jakarta, Indonesia. Data was collected through interviewing the participants using a structured questionnaire. The questionnaire comprised of section on demographics, and various validated scales to assess cognitive frailty, activities of daily living, presence of comorbidities, nutritional status, physical activity, depression, anxiety, and sleep quality. Multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to predict the associated risk factors of CF.RESULTS:The prevalence estimate of CF was 5.5%. The results revealed that age (odds ratio [OR] 42.76; 95% confidence interval [CI] 2.38–768.224, P=0.011), sex (OR 31.72; 95% CI 1.277–788.046, P=0.035), the activity of daily living (ADL) (OR 30.01; 95% CI 2.09–430.694, P=0.012), and depressive symptoms (OR 23.02; 95% CI 1.813–292.255, P=0.016) were significant correlates of CF.CONCLUSION:Age, sex, ADL, and depressive symptoms were associated with CF among older adults. There should be effective surveillance of CF in the community.

Introduction

Although life expectancy is rising, physical and psychological vulnerability put individuals at risk of being frail, leading to several adverse health-related outcomes.[1] Both physical frailty and cognitive impairment are common in the older age group, thereby influencing the individual’s vulnerability and resilience to stressors.[2] Consequently the cognitive frailty (CF) concept was raised to address both issues simultaneously.[2] CF is a condition evidenced by physical frailty and cognitive impairment without a clinical diagnosis of dementia.[3] It is associated with adverse health outcomes of disability, worse quality of life, and mortality.[4] Early identification of CF, especially among older people with multimorbidity, is crucial for the reduction of the disease burden. The incidence of CF increases with age.[5] It is also influenced by physical factors such as gender, education level, nutritional status, comorbid disease; and psychological factors such as depression, anxiety, and sleep quality.[5] Early identification of the risk factors is expected to initiate preventive actions and prompt the required intervention.

The prevalence varies across some countries that have explored the magnitude of CF and this ranges from 4.8% in Singapore,[6] 11.2% in Korea,[7] 15% in China,[8] and 23.6% in Spain.[9] Frailty prevalence in Indonesia ranges from 4.3% to 52.2%, with a pooled prevalence of 26.8%[10] and cognitive impairment prevalence across the country at 56.9%.[11] Life expectancy in Indonesia will continuously rise from 72.51 in 2015 to 75.47 in 2045.[12] In addition, the pre-elderly group in Indonesia accounts for around 18.5% of the total Indonesian population, so aging will further increase the older population in Indonesia, especially in Jakarta,[13] one of the urban areas in Indonesia with a high percentage of elderly (10.18%).[13] To date, CF data in Indonesia, especially in Jakarta, is limited. It was estimated that 1.2% of older patients has experienced CF in one of the hospitals in Jakarta.[14] Different settings could have distinct factors or issues underlying CF. Hence, the current study aimed to explore the prevalence of CF in the community in Jakarta and understand its potential risk factors.

Materials and Methods

The study done from March to April 2024 was cross-sectional using a convenient sampling method. Based on Kish Leslie’s formula[15] for sample size with a 95% confidence level, a 10% margin of error, and unknown CF prevalence in Indonesian community setting (50%), the required sample size was 97. Information about the study was distributed to the local older persons in the community in Jakarta, Indonesia. Participants who volunteered to participate underwent an interview. The participants in our study were individuals aged 60 and above. The CF criteria in this study was the modified CF criteria by Won et al., which included physical frailty, cognitive impairment, marked by 1.5 standard deviation below the mean for age-, gender-, and education-adjusted norms on any cognitive functioning test, such as montreal cognitive assessment (MoCA), and independence from the Lawton-instrumental activity of daily living (IADL) assessment. Points 2 and 3 are critical to ensure the absence of dementia.[3] Participants who were unable to speak, see, write, or draw were excluded from this study. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board vide Letter No. 26/02/KEP-FKIKUAJ/2024 dated 26/02/2024, and written informed consent was taken from all participants in the study.

Sociodemographic data including age (years), sex, educational level, occupation, and marital status, were collected through physical interview. Age was categorized by the mean age of our participants (68.5 years). Evaluation of CF was based on physical frailty, assessment of cognitive function, and independence using questionnaires. The “not cognitive frailty” group consisted of participants who did not meet the CF criteria. The physical conditions included medical comorbidities, nutritional status, physical activity, and functional status. The psychological conditions evaluated were depressive mood, anxiety, loneliness, and sleep quality.

The Indonesian fatigue, resistance, aerobic capacity, illnesses and loss of weight (FRAIL) Scale (Ina-FRAIL) version was an adapted version of the FRAIL scale with good internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha: 0.951).[16] “Frail” describes an individual who has scored more than 2, while “pre-frail” describes a score of 1–2, and “robust” a 0.[16]

MoCA is a rapid screening tool for mild cognitive impairment. The highest possible cognitive score achieved on this examination is 30, while a score of < 26 indicates cognitive impairment.[17] This study used the Indonesian version of MoCA (MoCA-INA).

IADL scale assessed individual capabilities to accomplish the tasks for independent survival in the community. The questionnaire has a good internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha: 0.93). Criteria were used on a trichotomous scale with a cutoff at 9 to determine independence in the clinical and research settings.[18]

Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) was used to evaluate the presence of any medical comorbidities of the participants. Participants with any comorbidities were categorized into “medical comorbidities” group. The classic CCI (cCCI) was widely used to assess 19 comorbidities, the details were explained elsewhere.[19]

Mininutritional Assessment Short Form is a fast-screening tool for evaluating nutritional status and is valid for geriatric populations in the community and clinical settings. The result could be classified into normal nutrition (score of 12–14), at risk of malnutrition (score of 8–11), and malnourished (score of 0–7).[20] Since there were no “malnourished” cognitive frail participants in our study, we used the cutoff of 11 as a marker for “probable poor nutritional status.”

The Global Physical Activity Questionnaire by the World Health Organization is an instrument for physical activity surveillance in the community, which has been used in previous studies in Indonesia.[21] A score equal to or exceeding 600 metabolic equivalents (METs)/week is defined as “active,” while a score lower than 600 METs/week is considered “not active.”[22]

The Barthel activity of daily living (ADL) Index Indonesian version has been validated (Cronbach alpha: 0.938) and was used to measure an individual’s independence level in daily activities.[23] It has a total score of 20, with the interpretation of a score of 20 as “Independent,” a score of 15–19 as “Mild Dependency,” a score of 10–14 as “Moderate Dependency”, a score of 5–9 as “Severe Dependency” and a score of 0–4 as “Total Dependency.”[24]

The Indonesian version of Patient Health Questionnaire-9 has a good internal consistency (Cronbach alpha: 0.86) to assess depressive symptoms in the last 14 days.[25] The questionnaire consisted of 9 questions with total scores ranging from 0 to 27, with a cutoff at 5 was used to determine the presence of significant depressive symptoms.[26]

The Indonesian version of general anxiety disorder-7 (GAD-7) scale consists of seven questions to assess the presence of generalized anxiety disorder in the last 14 days. It has a good internal consistency (Cronbach alpha: 0.87).[27] The total score of GAD-7 ranges from 0 to 21 with a cutoff at 5 to determine the presence of significant anxiety symptoms.[28]

UCLA loneliness scale software version 3 (UCLA-3) is an instrument of 20 questions for measuring loneliness. The Indonesian version of UCLA-3 has a good internal consistency (Cronbach alpha: 0.922), with each correlation item ranging from 0.433 to 0.754.[29]

Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) evaluated sleep quality in the past month. The global PSQI score only includes the initial 19 questions, with a total score ranging from 0 to 21, with a cutoff at 5 indicating poor sleep quality.[30] The Indonesian version has been validated and showed good internal consistency (Cronbach alpha: 0.85).[31]

This study displayed the sociodemographic, physical, and psychological factors in frequency and proportion. The Firth’s penalized regression was computed to assess the bivariate association of CF and the independent variables (age, sex, education level, occupation, marital status, medical comorbidities, nutritional status, physical activity, ADL, depressive symptoms, anxiety symptoms, sleep quality, and loneliness scale). The Firth analyses were computed using (JASP Team, 2024; jasp-stats.org). The multivariate logistic regression included all the variables of interest to CF. Analysis utilized the backward conditional method to exclude the statistically insignificant independent variables during the analysis. It could conclude the potential risk factor for CF. The automated selection using the backward conditional method was used to omit the covariates and generate more precise estimates to describe the risk factors of the dependent variable. All the data analyses were conducted using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences software version 22.00 (SPSS, IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA), and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Seven (5.5%) of the 128 participants were categorized as having CF. The CF group were older, had more women (85.7%), lower educational level (85.7%), and more unemployed (100%) participants than the non-CF group (63.6%, 81.8%, and 80.2%, respectively). There was a significant difference in age between the two groups (P = 0.006). In contrast, the remaining characteristics of the two groups, namely sex, education level, occupation, and marital status, were not significantly different (P > 0.05). Four participants had cognitive impairment (MoCA <26) and physical dependency simultaneously (IADL <9), suspected as dementia. Detailed sociodemographic characteristics are displayed in Table 1.

In the cognitive frail group, more participants reported probable poor nutritional status, physical inactivity, and mild dependency. The proportion of these variables was higher than the non CF group. ADL and CF had a significant association (P = 0.036). There is a higher prevalence of mild dependency in the CF group compared to the non-CF group (57.1% and 20.7%, respectively). Medical comorbidities, nutritional status, and physical activity were not significantly associated with CF.

Regarding the psychological characteristics, most of the CF participants experienced depressive symptoms, had no anxiety symptoms, poor sleep quality, and loneliness. Both groups showed similar predominant poor quality of sleep. A comparison of the groups showed that the CF group showed a higher proportion of depression (71.4%) and anxiety (42.9%) compared to the counterpart (31.4% and 18.2%, respectively). There was a significant association of depressive symptoms and CF (P = 0.037). Anxiety symptoms, sleep quality, and loneliness were not significantly associated with CF. The multivariate analysis revealed that age, sex, ADL, and depressive symptoms were the significant potential risk factors for CF. Sex became significantly associated with CF only after the statistically significant variables (education level, occupation, marital status, medical comorbidities, nutritional status, physical activity, anxiety symptoms, sleep quality, and loneliness scale) were removed from the equation. Discussion

The research investigated the magnitude of CF and the factors associated with CF in older people in the Jakarta community. The prevalence estimate of CF in our study was 5.5%. It was higher than the existing studies in Singapore (4.8%)[6] but was lower than the existing studies in China (15%)[8] and Korea (11.2%).[7] Differences in the operational criteria and the measurement tools[78] of CF could partially explain the discrepancy. This study used Won et al., modified CF criteria to ensure the feasibility of the study in a community setting.[3] The concept of CF highlights the co-occurrence of physical frailty and cognitive impairment without dementia. Consequently, operational definition in our study probably reduced the number of CF compared to the recent meta-analyses, which might include dementia in the CF group. Participants who met the dementia criteria in our study were categorized as not cognitive frail. Despite the differences, CF should be concerning. Our findings were higher compared to the previous study of patients at one hospital in Jakarta (1.2%).[14] Different settings could underlie the prevalence discrepancies. CF was higher in hospitals and elderly care institutions than community-dwelling settings in China. A possible reason is that in the community settings, the older adults live in a relatively closed environment.[8]

The associated risk of CF is also quite divergent among countries. A study from China and Korea stated that old age, female, lower education, underweight, chronic diseases, and depression were the risk factors of CF.[732] A study from Spain stated that age is a nonmodifiable factor, but nutritional status, quality of life, and physical activity were modifiable.[9] Despite the different backgrounds, our findings support the existing studies, which highlight the role of age, sex, depressive symptoms, and functional status as risk factors for CF.

Consistent with a previous study, the CF group were of significantly higher age than their counterpart.[5] Both frailty and cognitive impairment are commonly associated with geriatric syndromes as a result of aging with detrimental effects on both physiological and brain function.[33] Along with aging, brain function and proficiency in activities diminishes, possibly leading to the increase of CF prevalence.[34] Recent literature reported that the risk of CF increased as age increased by 1 year.[35]

Sex was significantly predictive of CF after combining with other variables. The results corroborate a previous study that indicates that women are at a higher risk of CF than men.[7] Biological differences between women and men could explain the underlying risk. Testosterone, a hormone that promotes neuron repair, is lower in women, thereby putting them at higher risk of cognitive impairment and CF.[32] Estrogen, a hormone that enhances human brain connection and protects against hippocampal aging, was found to decrease in postmenopausal women, as is the case in older women.[36] Cortisol plays a role in responding to psychosocial stress which increases the risk of aging among menopausal women, and the effects are approximately three times greater compared to men.[37] It is known that high and chronic exposure to cortisol could put individuals at a higher risk of cognitive impairment and frailty.[38]

However, the results should be interpreted cautiously because of the greater number of women in our study. In contrast with an existing study, sex was consistently associated with CF in both bivariate and multivariate analyses.[7] Older adults could face certain gender- or age-specific issues. An older woman has a higher risk of experiencing mental problems including depression, which could influence physical health and functional ability.[39] Depression was higher among the older women than older men. Older women with depression had a significantly higher risk of difficulty in performing ADL compared to older men with depression.[40] These results indicate a clear association between age, sex, ADL, and depressive symptoms, which could partially explain the significance of sex in the multivariable analysis together with age, ADL, and depressive symptoms.

Depressive symptoms were significantly predictive of CF. Consistent with a previous study, CF was associated with depressive symptoms and older people with CF had twice the risk of depression compared to older people with no CF.[41] The prevalence of depression in community-dwelling older adults with CF was 39.2% with 8.6% of the CF group having clinical depressive symptoms.[42] However, our study revealed that most of the participants in the CF group expressed significant depressive symptoms. Individuals with depressive symptoms reported low mood, reduced activities, and also declined cognitive functions in attention, executive functions, memory, and processing speeds.[43] Thus, the symptoms are closely related to cognitive impairment and physical frailty. The association of frailty with depression was driven by both psychological and biological factors. Frail people often have physical impairment that impacts their social life, an important factor in depression, which in itself affects behavior, performance, and social participation that leads to deterioration of functional status and an increase in frailty in older people.[44] Cognitive impairment associated with depression is mainly driven by biological factors such as age, increased oxidative stress, inflammation, disturbed hypothalamus-pituitary-adrenals axis, reduced monoamines functionality, vascular-originated brain ischemia, and amyloid-beta plaque accumulation.[43] Considering the reported impact of global disability[43] and the increasing prevalence,[45] depressive symptoms should be well-handled as they are modifiable risk for frailty and cognitive impairment.[46]

Activities of daily living require good physical activities and cognitive functions. More participants in the CF group expressed mild dependency than independence. In line with previous studies, the CF group experienced more ADL dependency than the non CF group.[47] To corroborate the result, a study of older people with hypertension and diabetes showed that age and ADL served as risks for CF.[34] The result indicated the potential impact of ADL impairment on CF, which highlights the importance of improving ADL among older adults.

The results provided insight into the possibility of both modifiable (ADL and depressive symptoms) and nonmodifiable (age and sex) risks of CF. The prevention and management of modifiable factors should be encouraged, using a multimodal approach that incorporates exercises, training, and mood management. There were the demonstrated benefits of physical activities for cognitive function, frailty status, mental health,[48] and independence,[49] which contributes to a lower risk of worsening CF.[50] Physical activities have beneficial effects both in alleviating mental health distress and preventing various noncommunicable physical diseases.[51] Physical activities are important for most inactive participants in our population. Besides physical activity, a pharmacological approach should be considered for the improvement of the depressive mood.[46] After identifying the risk factors associated with CF, early intervention is warranted to mitigate the possibility of CF.

This study is not without limitations. The data collection might have been influenced by recall bias and potential cognitive impairment among older adults. Efforts through physical interview using validated questionnaire could reduce limitations. The results of our study should be interpreted with caution given the possibility of selection bias which is more inclusive for older adults who were able to come to the interview site. Larger sample size and participants from multiple sites should be considered to generalize the result in the community settings. Given the wide range of risk estimation in our study, sample size should be expanded particularly in the CF group to precisely estimate the risk of the associated factors to CF. The current study did not explore the interaction of age, sex, ADL, and depressive symptoms in the CF population. Further studies to analyze the causal relationship are encouraged. However, to our knowledge, this study is the first to explore the physical and psychological determinants of CF in older people living in the community settings in Indonesia.

Conclusion

Age, depressive symptoms, and ADL were found to be associated with CF, while sex was associated with CF in the presence of other risk factors. The prevalence estimate of CF in our study was 5.5%. Early identification and intervention of the associated risk could mitigate disability in older people and enhance their well-being.
