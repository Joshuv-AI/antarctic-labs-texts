The relationship between oncology nurses’ emotional intelligence, burnout, and patient-centered communication: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Abdulaziz M Alodhialah
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOncology nursing involves intense emotional demands, requiring nurses to balance complex clinical responsibilities with empathetic, patient-centered communication (PCC). Burnout among oncology nurses is associated with diminished communication quality, while emotional intelligence (EI) has emerged as a potential protective factor. However, limited research has explored how EI interacts with burnout to influence PCC, particularly in Middle Eastern healthcare settings.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 172 oncology nurses in three tertiary hospitals in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. Participants completed Arabic versions of the Schutte Self-Report Emotional Intelligence Test, the Copenhagen Burnout Inventory, and the Patient-Centered Communication Questionnaire. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, Pearson correlation, multiple linear regression, and moderation analysis via PROCESS macro.ResultsEI was positively associated with PCC (r = 0.45, p < 0.001) and negatively associated with personal (r = − 0.41), work-related (r = − 0.38), and client-related burnout (r = − 0.33) (all p < 0.001). In the regression model, EI significantly predicted higher PCC (B = 0.26, p = 0.001), while personal (B = − 0.32, p = 0.004) and work-related burnout (B = − 0.23, p = 0.011) predicted lower PCC. Client-related burnout showed a non-significant trend (B = − 0.18, p = 0.076). The model explained 37.3% of the variance in PCC (F(4,167) = 24.2, p < 0.001, R² = 0.373). EI significantly moderated the effects of all three burnout dimensions on PCC, suggesting a buffering effect.ConclusionEI plays a critical role in reducing the negative impact of burnout on PCC among oncology nurses. Integrating EI development into professional training and wellness initiatives may enhance both communication quality and nurse well-being in emotionally demanding care settings.Keywords: EI, Burnout, Patient-centered communication, Oncology nursing, Nurse–patient relationship, Compassion fatigue, Resilience

Introduction

Effective communication between healthcare providers and patients is a cornerstone of quality care, particularly in oncology settings, where patients face life-altering diagnoses and complex treatment trajectories. Patient-centered communication (PCC) involves attentively engaging patients in decision-making, acknowledging their emotional needs, and tailoring communication to their individual preferences and values. In the oncology context, PCC has been associated with improved treatment adherence, patient satisfaction, and psychological outcomes, such as reduced anxiety and increased trust in healthcare providers [1, 2].

Oncology nursing presents unique emotional, cognitive, and relational challenges that heighten the need for robust communication skills [3]. Unlike many other nursing specialties, oncology care often entails long-term therapeutic relationships, frequent exposure to terminal illness and death, emotionally charged end-of-life discussions, and the cumulative toll of witnessing patient suffering and loss [4, 5]. These emotionally taxing elements can impair nurses’ ability to maintain high levels of presence, empathy, and attentiveness in their communication with patients.

One factor that may buffer against the erosion of communication quality under stress is EI (EI). EI refers to an individual’s capacity to perceive, understand, regulate, and use emotions effectively in interpersonal interactions [6]. Among nurses, EI has been linked to better stress management, greater empathy, enhanced collaboration, and improved clinical decision-making [7, 8]. Nurses with higher EI are often better equipped to maintain emotional boundaries, manage their reactions to distressing situations, and respond compassionately to patients’ needs. In oncology, where emotional labor is constant, EI may play a particularly protective role in supporting PCC [9].

However, EI operates within a broader occupational context that includes job-related stress and burnout. Burnout is a psychological syndrome resulting from chronic workplace stress and is characterized by emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and a reduced sense of personal accomplishment [10]. Oncology nurses are consistently reported to be at elevated risk of burnout due to high workloads, complex patient care demands, staff shortages, and frequent encounters with death and dying [11]. Burnout can compromise cognitive performance, reduce empathic communication, and foster emotional disengagement from patients, all of which hinder the delivery of PCC [12].

Several international studies have examined the relationship between EI, burnout, and communication in healthcare. However, these findings may not be readily generalizable to Middle Eastern settings due to cultural differences in emotional expression, hierarchical communication norms, and contextual workplace dynamics [13]. In Saudi Arabia, healthcare settings are characterized by high nurse-to-patient ratios, multicultural teams, and culturally sensitive communication practices, particularly when discussing prognoses and end-of-life issues [14, 15]. Despite the high emotional and communication demands placed on oncology nurses in this context, few studies have explored how EI and burnout interact to influence PCC in Saudi Arabia. This constitutes a significant gap in the literature [7].

The present study was designed to address this gap by investigating the interconnections among EI, burnout, and PCC among oncology nurses in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. Specifically, the study aimed to (1) assess the relationships between nurses’ EI, burnout dimensions, and patient-centered communication, and (2) examine whether EI moderates the effect of burnout on PCC. Understanding these relationships can help inform targeted interventions to promote communication effectiveness and well-being among oncology nurses in emotionally demanding care environments.

Theoretical framework

This study is grounded in the Job Demands–Resources (JD-R) model, a widely used theoretical framework in occupational health psychology that explains how job characteristics influence employee well-being, motivation, and performance. According to the JD-R model, each profession is characterized by specific job demands—those aspects of work that require sustained effort and are associated with physiological or psychological costs—and job resources—those that help achieve work goals, reduce demands, or stimulate personal growth and development [16].

In the context of oncology nursing, burnout is conceptualized as a job stressor that results from high emotional labor, workload, and patient acuity, particularly in palliative and end-of-life care settings. Chronic exposure to these demands can lead to emotional exhaustion and detachment, which impair nurses’ capacity for empathetic and effective communication [17].

Conversely, EI is understood as a personal job resource that equips nurses with emotional regulation, empathy, and interpersonal competence. These skills help nurses manage emotional strain, sustain motivation, and maintain high-quality interactions with patients, even in demanding circumstances [18].

Patient-centered communication, a critical aspect of nursing performance, is treated as the behavioral outcome in this model. It reflects how effectively a nurse can engage with patients through empathy, active listening, and shared decision-making [19].

Study aim

The primary aim of this study is to explore the interrelationship between EI, burnout (across personal, work-related, and client-related dimensions), and PCC among oncology nurses in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. Specifically, the study seeks to examine:

The extent to which EI and burnout independently predict levels of PCC.

The extent to which EI and burnout independently predict levels of PCC.

Whether EI moderates the relationship between burnout and patient-centered communication, such that higher EI may buffer the negative impact of burnout on PCC.

Whether EI moderates the relationship between burnout and patient-centered communication, such that higher EI may buffer the negative impact of burnout on PCC.

This investigation is grounded in the Job Demands–Resources (JD-R) model, which posits that personal resources like EI can mitigate the detrimental effects of job demands (i.e., burnout) on performance outcomes such as communication quality.

Study hypotheses

Based on the literature and theoretical framework, the study is guided by the following hypotheses:

H1: EI will be positively associated with patient-centered communication.

H1: EI will be positively associated with patient-centered communication.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study employed a cross-sectional correlational design to examine the relationships among EI (EI), burnout, and patient-centered communication (PCC) among oncology nurses. Cross-sectional correlational designs are commonly used in psychological and occupational health research to explore associations between variables measured at a single point in time. This design enabled the researcher to collect self-reported data from a diverse sample of oncology nurses working in three major tertiary hospitals in Riyadh, Kingdom of Saudi Arabia. The approach allowed for the use of multivariate statistical analyses (i.e., Pearson correlation and hierarchical multiple regression) to identify key predictors and potential interaction effects among study variables without manipulating any conditions.

Setting

The study was conducted in Riyadh, the capital of Saudi Arabia, which hosts several major tertiary healthcare institutions. Three hospitals were selected for inclusion: (1) a large government-funded oncology center with a capacity of approximately 450 beds and a dedicated cancer institute offering inpatient and outpatient chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and palliative care; (2) a private multispecialty hospital with a 300-bed capacity that includes a specialized oncology unit primarily serving insured and international patients; and (3) a university-affiliated teaching hospital with approximately 500 beds and an academic oncology department where both clinical care and nursing education are integrated. These institutions differ in terms of staffing ratios, patient acuity, and administrative structures, offering a varied but representative sample of oncology nursing environments in Saudi Arabia. This diversity also allows for the exploration of how institutional characteristics may influence nurses’ burnout levels, emotional labor, and patient-centered communication practices.

Sample and sampling

Target population

The target population for this study comprised oncology nurses working in Riyadh, Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (KSA). Oncology nurses play a pivotal role in cancer care, providing direct patient support, managing symptoms, and facilitating patient-centered communication.

The study included oncology nurses working in tertiary hospitals, specialty cancer centers, and general hospitals with dedicated oncology departments. These institutions were selected because they provide specialized cancer care, including chemotherapy, radiation therapy, palliative care, and surgical oncology services.

Eligible participants were registered nurses (RNs) or nurse specialists currently employed in oncology departments across the selected hospitals. To ensure familiarity with the emotionally demanding nature of oncology care, participants were required to have at least one year of professional experience in oncology nursing. Proficiency in either Arabic or English was also necessary, as these were the primary languages used in the questionnaires and in local healthcare communication. All participants were required to provide informed consent and agree to voluntary participation in the study.

Nurses in administrative or managerial roles with limited direct patient contact were excluded, as were those on extended medical or maternity leave during the data collection period. In addition, nurses working in non-oncology units such as general medicine, surgery, or pediatrics were excluded due to differences in job demands and patient interaction profiles.

Data collection

Recruitment and eligibility

Participants were recruited using a convenience sampling approach from three tertiary hospitals in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, each offering specialized oncology services. Eligible participants were registered nurses working in oncology units (e.g., inpatient, outpatient, chemotherapy, or palliative care) with at least one year of clinical oncology experience. Nurses on extended leave or with no direct patient contact were excluded. Proficiency in Arabic or English was required, as the survey instruments were available in both languages.

Recruitment was facilitated through nursing department coordinators who distributed survey links and/or paper forms during staff meetings and shift handovers. Participation was voluntary, and no incentives were provided. Informed consent was obtained in written or digital form prior to survey access.

Data collection procedure

Data were collected over a two-month period using both paper-based and electronic questionnaires to maximize accessibility. Online surveys were distributed via secure links, with settings enabled to prevent multiple submissions from the same device or email/IP address. Paper-based responses were submitted anonymously using coded envelopes and collected in locked drop boxes accessible only to the researcher. Average completion time was 15–20 min.

To ensure data quality and minimize duplication:

A unique anonymous code was assigned to each paper survey.

Duplicate responses were screened and removed based on demographic consistency and submission time stamps.

A pilot test was conducted with 30 oncology nurses from a separate hospital to assess clarity, cultural appropriateness, and completion time. Minor revisions were made to item wording and formatting based on pilot feedback.

Final sample

Of the 240 nurses invited, 180 completed the survey, resulting in a 75% response rate. After removing 8 incomplete responses, the final analytic sample comprised 172 oncology nurses. Participants were distributed across the three sites as follows: 72 from the public oncology center, 56 from the private multispecialty hospital, and 44 from the university-affiliated teaching hospital. This distribution provided a diverse representation of oncology work environments in the Riyadh region.

Data collection tools

To assess the interconnection between EI, burnout, and patient-centered communication among oncology nurses, three validated instruments were used in addition to a demographic questionnaire. These tools were selected based on their established psychometric properties and relevance to the study’s objectives. Each tool was translated into Arabic, ensuring cultural appropriateness and validity for the target population in Riyadh, Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (KSA).

1. The Schutte Self-Report EI Test (SSEIT) was used to measure the EI of oncology nurses. The SSEIT was originally developed by Schutte et al. (1998) based on Salovey and Mayer’s EI model [20]. It is widely used in healthcare and psychological research to assess individuals’ ability to perceive, understand, regulate, and express emotions effectively. The choice of SSEIT was based on its robust psychometric properties and ease of administration in research settings.

The aim of the SSEIT is to evaluate EI across key domains, including self-awareness, self-regulation, social skills, empathy, and motivation. Higher EI is associated with better stress management, improved interpersonal relationships, and enhanced resilience in professional settings, which are particularly relevant to oncology nursing.

The SSEIT comprises 33 items, each rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly Disagree, 5 = Strongly Agree). These items cover four core domains of EI:

Perception of Emotion (10 items)

Perception of Emotion (10 items)

Managing Own Emotions (9 items)

Managing Own Emotions (9 items)

Managing others’ emotions (8 items)

Managing others’ emotions (8 items)

Utilization of Emotion (6 items)

Utilization of Emotion (6 items)

The scoring system ranges from 33 to 165, with higher scores indicating greater EI. The total score is calculated by summing the individual item scores, and subdomain scores can also be analyzed if needed.

For use in Arabic-speaking populations, the SSEIT was translated and validated using a standard forward–backward translation process. Previous research in Arabic healthcare settings has demonstrated that the Arabic version of SSEIT maintains strong construct validity and cultural adaptability [21]. The reliability of the Arabic SSEIT has been well-documented in the literature, with Cronbach’s alpha values ranging from 0.78 to 0.85, indicating good internal consistency. In the present study’s pilot test with 30 oncology nurses in Riyadh, Cronbach’s alpha was 0.80, confirming its reliability for the target sample.

(2005) as an alternative to traditional burnout scales, focusing on exhaustion as a core component of burnout [22]. Unlike the Maslach Burnout Inventory (MBI), which measures three dimensions of burnout, the CBI focuses on fatigue and exhaustion related to personal, work-related, and patient-related stressors, making it a highly relevant tool for assessing burnout in healthcare settings.

The aim of the CBI is to evaluate burnout across three specific domains:

The aim of the PCC questionnaire is to assess the extent to which nurses engage in effective, empathetic, and patient-focused communication. Effective PCC is associated with better patient satisfaction, improved treatment adherence, and enhanced psychological outcomes for patients undergoing cancer treatment.

The instrument consists of 15 items, each rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Never, 5 = Always). The key dimensions assessed include:

The scoring system generates a total PCC score ranging from 15 to 75, with higher scores indicating more frequent use of patient-centered communication behaviors.

For use in Arabic-speaking healthcare settings, the PCC questionnaire was subjected to a rigorous forward–backward translation process by bilingual experts, followed by a content validity assessment involving oncology nursing professionals in Riyadh. The Arabic version was adapted and pilot-tested in a prior study by the author (unpublished). The tool underwent pilot testing with 50 Arabic-speaking nurses, confirming its face validity and internal consistency. A factor analysis was conducted to verify the underlying structure of the questionnaire.

The reliability of the PCC scale in the present study’s pilot test was 0.84 (Cronbach’s alpha), indicating strong internal consistency. These results align with previous research that has validated PCC tools in Arabic-speaking healthcare settings, ensuring the instrument’s suitability for measuring patient-centered communication among oncology nurses in KSA.

Data collection procedure

Prior to formal data collection, the research team conducted a pilot test with 30 oncology nurses from a single hospital not included in the main study sample. Minor revisions to wording and layout were made for clarity. Once finalized, the questionnaires were distributed in paper-based and electronic formats (depending on nurse preference and shift schedules) during staff meetings or via institutional email systems. Data collection was conducted over a two-month period, specifically from March to April 2025. Based on pilot testing and participant feedback, the average time required to complete the full questionnaire was approximately 15 to 20 min. No participants reported undue burden or difficulty in completing the survey within this time frame, and the format (paper or electronic) was adapted to accommodate nurses’ work schedules.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS version 26.0. Descriptive statistics (means, standard deviations, ranges) were calculated to summarize participants’ EI, burnout scores, and patient-centered communication (PCC) levels.

To examine associations between study variables, Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r) was used, which is appropriate for assessing linear relationships between continuous, normally distributed variables. Prior to conducting parametric tests, assumptions of normality were evaluated using skewness, kurtosis, and Shapiro-Wilk tests, all of which indicated that the data approximated normal distribution. Homoscedasticity and linearity were assessed through residual scatterplots and were found to meet regression assumptions.

To explore predictive relationships, a hierarchical multiple linear regression analysis was conducted with PCC as the dependent variable. The independent variables included:

EI (total SSEIT score),

EI (total SSEIT score),

Three subscales of burnout from the Copenhagen Burnout Inventory (personal, work-related, and client-related burnout),

Three subscales of burnout from the Copenhagen Burnout Inventory (personal, work-related, and client-related burnout),

Covariates: age and years of clinical experience.

Covariates: age and years of clinical experience.

This modeling approach enabled assessment of both the unique contribution of each predictor and the overall explained variance (R²) in PCC. Additionally, an interaction term (EI × Burnout) was computed and entered in a moderation model to test whether EI moderated the relationship between burnout and PCC, following the guidance of Aiken and West (1991) [25]. All tests were two-tailed, and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Age, gender, and years of clinical experience were included as covariates in the regression model due to their documented influence on both emotional processing and communication behavior in clinical settings. Age and professional experience have been linked to greater emotional maturity, self-regulation, and communication efficacy, which may independently affect both EI and patient-centered communication. Gender differences in emotional labor and communication styles have also been reported in the nursing literature, potentially confounding the relationships under study. Including these covariates helped control for their potential confounding effects, thereby isolating the specific contributions of EI and burnout dimensions to the outcome variable (PCC).

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of King Saud University, Riyadh, Saudi Arabia (IRB approval number: 2025-046). As the lead institution, King Saud University’s IRB granted ethical clearance that covered all collaborating sites. In addition to this centralized approval, formal administrative permissions were obtained from the nursing directors and hospital administrators of each participating institution. Written informed consent was secured from all participants. The study adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki, and strict measures were taken to ensure anonymity, confidentiality, and the voluntary nature of participation.

Table 3 presents the bivariate correlations among EI (EI), the three dimensions of burnout (personal, work-related, and client-related), and patient-centered communication (PCC). EI was positively and moderately correlated with PCC (r = 0.45, p < 0.01), indicating that higher levels of EI among oncology nurses are associated with more effective patient-centered communication. Conversely, EI was negatively correlated with all three dimensions of burnout, with the strongest inverse relationship observed with personal burnout (r = –0.41, p < 0.01), followed by work-related burnout (r = –0.38, p < 0.01) and client-related burnout (r = –0.33, p < 0.01). These findings suggest that emotionally intelligent nurses may be more resilient to occupational stress. Notably, all three burnout dimensions demonstrated significant negative correlations with PCC, with personal burnout showing the strongest association (r = –0.47, p < 0.01). This implies that as burnout increases, particularly on a personal level, nurses’ ability to engage in effective patient-centered communication may diminish. Additionally, moderate-to-strong positive intercorrelations were observed among the three burnout domains (ranging from r = 0.61 to 0.68, p < 0.01), reflecting the interrelated nature of occupational stress experiences.

Discussion

This study examined the interrelationships among emotional intelligence (EI), burnout, and patient-centered communication (PCC) among oncology nurses in Saudi Arabia. The findings highlight the complex ways in which psychological resources and occupational stressors influence communication behaviors in emotionally demanding care environments.

The mean PCC score of 63.4 suggests that participants demonstrated moderate to high levels of patient-centered communication. This level of PCC is comparable to those reported in studies conducted in similarly high-stress clinical environments, such as intensive care and palliative care units, where relational communication is essential despite emotional strain. In the context of Saudi Arabia, where nursing staff often work in multicultural teams and navigate sensitive family dynamics, such scores may reflect culturally reinforced expectations of compassion and duty within patient care. Prior studies have indicated that nurses in Middle Eastern settings often uphold relational communication norms due to cultural values around empathy, hospitality, and family-centered care, even when faced with time constraints and high workloads [26–28].

Burnout was prevalent across all three measured domains, with personal burnout yielding the highest mean score (M = 48.2), followed by work-related (M = 44.7) and client-related burnout (M = 40.3). These findings are consistent with previous research indicating that oncology nurses are particularly vulnerable to burnout due to frequent exposure to suffering, death, and emotionally complex care situations [29]. The prominence of personal burnout suggests that the effects of occupational stress extend beyond the professional domain and may be compounded by personal life stressors, inadequate recovery, or insufficient institutional support. These patterns have been similarly reported in regional studies where systemic staffing shortages and limited access to psychological support exacerbate emotional exhaustion among healthcare workers [30].

EI emerged as both a direct predictor of PCC and a moderator of the burnout–PCC relationship. Nurses with higher EI demonstrated better communication behaviors and were more resilient in maintaining these behaviors despite experiencing burnout. This moderating effect supports the theoretical assumptions of the Job Demands–Resources (JD-R) model, which posits that personal resources like EI can buffer the negative consequences of job demands on performance outcomes. These findings are in line with international evidence suggesting that emotionally intelligent healthcare professionals are better equipped to regulate their emotions, respond empathetically to patients, and sustain engagement under pressure [6, 31, 32]. In this study, the buffering role of EI was evident across all dimensions of burnout, indicating its broad protective potential.

The contextual specificity of these findings offers a valuable contribution to the global nursing literature. Although the relationships among EI, burnout, and communication have been explored in Western and Asian settings, there remains a scarcity of research in Arab healthcare contexts. Cultural norms surrounding emotional expression, gender roles, and hierarchical communication patterns may influence both the experience of burnout and the expression of PCC in ways that differ from Western models. For instance, in Saudi Arabia, deference to authority and gendered communication expectations may shape how nurses manage emotionally charged conversations with patients and families. Additionally, many nurses working in Saudi oncology settings are expatriates who may face language barriers, social isolation, or institutional marginalization—all of which can contribute to emotional strain and limit opportunities to express PCC fully [33, 34].

These findings underscore the need to address both individual and organizational factors in improving communication quality and nurse well-being. While EI training may equip nurses with tools for emotional regulation and empathy, such interventions must be implemented alongside systemic changes—such as supportive leadership, adequate staffing, and accessible psychological resources—to meaningfully reduce burnout and enhance communication performance.

Implications for nursing practice and policy

The results of this study have several implications for oncology nursing practice and healthcare policy. First, the significant positive association between EI and patient-centered communication (PCC) highlights the need for targeted training programs that enhance EI competencies among oncology nurses. Integrating emotional regulation, empathy training, and reflective practice into professional development curricula could empower nurses to navigate emotionally charged interactions more effectively, ultimately improving communication and patient satisfaction. Second, the strong negative correlations between burnout dimensions—particularly personal burnout—and PCC suggest that burnout reduction must be prioritized not only for nurse well-being but also for communication quality. Organizational interventions such as workload adjustments, adequate staffing, access to counseling services, and the implementation of resilience programs are essential to minimize emotional exhaustion. Specifically, managers should monitor personal burnout signs among staff and create supportive environments where emotional challenges are openly addressed. Third, the study supports the theoretical premise of the Job Demands–Resources (JD-R) model, in which individual resources like EI can mitigate job stressors. This reinforces the importance of adopting a dual approach in policy development—strengthening both personal resources (e.g., EI skills) and structural resources (e.g., leadership support, clear communication channels) to foster a sustainable oncology nursing workforce.

Finally, the findings underscore the need for context-sensitive communication training in Saudi Arabia and other similar healthcare contexts. Given the cultural nuances of emotional expression and hierarchical nurse–physician dynamics in Arab countries, PCC interventions must be locally tailored to be both effective and culturally congruent. Promoting open, empathic, and patient-inclusive communication strategies can bridge relational gaps and align care with patients’ values and needs.

Limitations

Despite the valuable insights gained, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to infer causality among EI, burnout, and PCC. Longitudinal or experimental designs would be needed to determine the directionality of these relationships over time.

Second, the self-reported nature of the instruments used (SSEIT, CBI, and PCC scale) introduces the potential for social desirability bias and response subjectivity, particularly in relation to emotionally charged constructs such as burnout and communication. Participants may have underreported stress or overestimated communication competence due to professional role expectations.

Third, although the sample included nurses from three major oncology centers in Riyadh, sampling was non-probabilistic, and the generalizability of the findings beyond this urban context—or to other regions in Saudi Arabia—remains limited. Institutional cultures, patient demographics, and regional healthcare resources may differ substantially across sites.

Fourth, while the study attempted to mitigate language-related bias through validated Arabic translations of the instruments and pilot testing, data collection in both Arabic and English could have introduced linguistic variability that may affect the internal consistency of responses. Although Cronbach’s alpha values were acceptable, future studies should evaluate cross-linguistic equivalence more rigorously using confirmatory factor analysis.

While causal inferences cannot be drawn from cross-sectional data, this design provides valuable insights into potential relational patterns that can inform future longitudinal or interventional studies aimed at improving nurse well-being and communication performance in emotionally demanding care environments such as oncology.

Fifth, unmeasured confounding variables, such as nurses’ coping styles, staffing ratios, leadership climate, and emotional labor expectations, were not included in the regression model. These factors may influence both burnout and communication and warrant investigation in future multivariate models. Finally, due to privacy considerations in online and paper-based distribution, the study could not track completion times or fully eliminate the risk of duplicate participation. Although procedural safeguards were employed (e.g., unique access links and manual verification), the potential for data duplication or missing contextual details remains a methodological concer.

Conclusion

This study explored the interconnection between EI, burnout, and PCC among oncology nurses in Riyadh, Kingdom of Saudi Arabia. The findings revealed that higher EI is significantly associated with lower levels of burnout and better patient-centered communication skills. Additionally, EI was found to moderate the negative impact of burnout on communication, suggesting that emotionally intelligent nurses are more resilient in maintaining high-quality interactions with their patients despite workplace stressors. These findings highlight the crucial role of EI in promoting nurse well-being and enhancing the quality of oncology care.

The negative correlation between EI and burnout subscales (personal, work-related, and client-related burnout) suggests that nurses with greater EI can better regulate their emotions, manage occupational stress, and avoid the emotional exhaustion that often leads to professional burnout. Given the emotionally charged nature of oncology care—where nurses frequently deal with patient suffering, distress, and end-of-life issues—developing EI skills could be a vital strategy for enhancing coping mechanisms, job satisfaction, and professional fulfillment.

Moreover, the strong positive relationship between EI and patient-centered communication underscores the importance of emotional competencies in fostering trust, empathy, and effective interactions with cancer patients. Oncology nurses who exhibit higher EI are more likely to engage in active listening, provide compassionate care, and communicate effectively, all of which contribute to improved patient satisfaction and better health outcomes. As PCC is a critical determinant of patient adherence to treatment and psychological well-being, strengthening EI skills among nurses could enhance both patient experiences and clinical outcomes.
