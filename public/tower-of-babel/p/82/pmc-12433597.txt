Higher PHQ-9 score levels and neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio are associated with increased risks of stroke, all-cause mortality, and MACE in NHANES 2005–2018
Authors: Junqi Liao, Aimei Zhang, Yujun Cong, Wanhui Peng, Xiaogang Tang, Qing Zhu, Li Li, Yunze Li, Yan Liu, Hui Jiang, Jingyi Chen, Yang Han, Zhaoyao Chen, Wenlei Li, Yuan Zhu, Hongquan Liu, Minghua Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe association of neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) and depressive symptoms with stroke risk, mortality, and major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) is currently unknown. This study aimed to investigate the effects of neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) and depressive symptoms on stroke risk, mortality, and MACE in NHANES 2005–2018.MethodsThe National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) was conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics between 2005 and 2018, recruiting a nationally representative sample of participants aged 20 years and older. The NLR reflects inflammatory status and the Patient Health Questionnaire 9 (PHQ-9) indicates depressive symptoms. Independent and joint associations between NLR, depressive symptoms, and stroke and mortality were then examined, and relative risk was calculated using weight-based Cox regression analyses. Finally, mediation analysis was used to explore the indirect impact of the PHQ-9 score on stroke and mortality mediated through NLR.ResultsNLR and depressive symptoms were positively associated with stroke risk (p < 0.05). Participants with higher NLR levels had an increased risk of all-cause mortality and MACE (HR, 1.406; 95% CI 1.261–1.567; HR, 1.927; 95% CI 1.518–2.447), while those with higher PHQ-9 score were associated with an elevated risk of all-cause mortality and MACE (HR, 1.762; 95% CI 1.516–2.047; HR, 1.755; 95% CI 1.396–2.206). In addition, joint analyses indicated that participants with PHQ-9 score ≥ 10 and high NLR levels had the highest risk of all-cause mortality and MACE (HR, 2.079; 95% CI 1.673–2.585; HR, 2.858, 95% CI 2.007–4.069). Specifically, participants with elevated NLR levels and moderate-severe depressive symptoms faced the greatest risk of mortality. Mediation analyses revealed that NLR partially mediated the association between PHQ-9 score and stroke risk, all-cause mortality, and MACE by0.6%, 5.9%, and 4.8%, respectively.ConclusionOur research indicates that elevated NLR levels and more severe depressive symptoms were associated with increased risk of stroke, all-cause mortality and MACE. In addition, NLR plays a mediation role in linking depressive symptoms to stroke, MACE, and all-cause mortality.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07198-0.Keywords: Neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, Depressive symptoms, Stroke, Mortality, Major adverse cardiovascular events

Introduction

Stroke is a serious disease that threatens worldwide public health and is one of the leading causes of death and disability among adults [1]. Numerous studies have revealed the critical significance of the neuroinflammatory response in stroke pathogenesis [2]. White blood cell subtypes such as neutrophils and lymphocytes play an important part in the inflammatory response [3, 4]. Our previous research has established the relationship between inflammatory markers and stroke outcomes [5–8]. Studies have demonstrated that anti-inflammatory medications can lower the incidence of stroke and major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE, which includes deaths from heart disease and cerebrovascular disease [9, 10]. A substantial body of domestic and international research has substantiated that the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), a composite inflammation-related marker, not only can effectively reflect the progression of inflammation in patients, but also predict the occurrence of stroke. It is frequently utilized in the prognosis assessment of MACE [7, 11–15].

Depression is one of the prevalent mental disorders and the primary cause of mental health-related disability worldwide. The number of depression patients is escalating year by year on a global scale [16]. Depression has been shown in studies to be an independent risk factor for MACE and is linked with mortality [17–20]. Moreover, a positive correlation exists between moderate-severe depressive symptoms and the risk of stroke, exerting a significant influence on survival rates [21, 22]. It is worth noting that elevated inflammation markers have been demonstrated to be correlated with the severity of depressive symptoms [23, 24]. Therefore, patients with severe depression are at a higher risk of exhibiting elevated levels of inflammation, which could potentially have a detrimental impact on the subsequent survival rate of the patients. These findings suggest a possible link between NLR, depressive symptoms, and stroke and mortality.

As we know, there have been no studies that have concurrently assessed the correlation between NLR, depressive symptoms, and stroke risk within the same group of patients, as well as investigated the comprehensive impact of NLR and depressive symptoms on mortality. Therefore, this study aims not only to investigate the correlation between NLR, depressive symptoms, and stroke, but also to explore the relationship between NLR, depressive symptoms, and mortality, and to explore the mediating role of NLR.

Methods

This study used a nationally representative sample from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 2005–2018 of the National Center for Health Statistics, which has been conducted on a 2-year cycle since 1999 to monitor the health and nutritional status of the US population. This study was divided into two parts: first, a cross-sectional study to analyze the correlation between NLR and depressive symptoms and stroke; and second, a longitudinal study to further investigate the effect of NLR and depressive symptoms on all-cause mortality and MACE. All NHANES 2005–2018 protocols were approved by the Ethics Review Board of the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Study population

Participants lacking stroke questionnaire data, depression questionnaire data, complete survival data, and blood counts were excluded. Ultimately, a total of 32,680 participants were included in this analysis, with data from the NHANES 2005–2018 dataset (Fig. 1).

Study flowchart of participants selected

Assessment of depressive symptoms

The Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) was utilized to diagnose and assess the severity of depressive symptoms in participants. The PHQ-9 consists of nine questions, with each question scoring from 0 to 3 points. The total score ranges from 0 to 27, with a higher score indicating more severe depressive symptoms. Participants were classified into three categories based on their PHQ-9 score: no depression (0–4), mild depression (5–9), and moderate-severe depression (≥ 10). PHQ-9 is also an effective and reliable screening tool for depression in the elderly [25]. Furthermore, we defined the participants who scored 10 or more as having potentially clinically relevant depression based on extensive research on the accuracy of PHQ-9 [26].

Measurement of NLR

Neutrophil and lymphocyte counts were determined by conducting a complete blood count on blood specimens using a Beckman Coulter automated blood analyzer in an MEC. NLR was calculated as the absolute neutrophil count divided by the absolute lymphocyte count.

Assessment of mortality

The primary outcome of the study was all-cause mortality and the secondary outcome was MACE (includes deaths from heart disease and cerebrovascular disease). Deaths from all causes were defined as all-cause mortality and MACE was defined as ICD-10 codes I60-I69, I00-I09, I11, I13, I20-I51. The follow-up period for the study was from the date of initial diagnosis to the date of death or the end of the study period, 31 December 2019, whichever came first.

Assessment of covariates

All-cause mortality and MACE analyses considered the same set of covariates. Participant characteristics included age, sex, race, education level, history of smoking and drinking, body mass index (BMI), diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and history of cardiovascular disease (CVD). Race was categorized as non-Hispanic white, non-Hispanic black, Mexican American, and other. Education was categorized as "less than high school", "high school", or "more than high school". Smoking was categorized as never smoking, ever smoking, and current smoking. Drinking was categorized as never drinking, ever drinking, and current drinking. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in meters and was classified as normal (< 25.0 kg/m2), overweight (25.0 to 30 kg/m2), and obese (≥ 30.0 kg/m2). The definition of diabetes depends on an affirmative answer to any of the following questions: "Has your doctor ever told you that you have diabetes?". CVD includes coronary heart disease, heart failure, and myocardial infarction. Cancer and hypertension are diagnosed by self-report. The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) creatinine Eq. (2009). Chronic kidney disease (CKD) was defined as either eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 or urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) ≥ 30 mg/g.

Statistical analysis

The analysis considered the complex NHANES 2015–2018 sample design by considering appropriate sample weights, stratification, and clustering. The weight of the analyzed samples was calculated as the 2-year MEC weight of the fasted samples divided by 7. Categorical variables were compared using a survey-weighted chi-square test, while continuous variables were compared using survey-weighted linear regression.

Baseline characteristics describe different levels of NLR and depressive symptoms. Data are expressed as mean ± standard error (SE) for continuous variables and as a percentage for categorical variables. Logistic regression was applied to explore the correlation between NLR and depressive symptoms and stroke. Multivariate Cox proportional risk regression models were applied to analyze the correlation between NLR and depressive symptoms with all-cause and MACE. The multivariate models in the final stage were adjusted for age, sex, race, BMI, education, history of smoking and drinking, and health factors (hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes mellitus, and/or history of CVD). To examine joint associations, participants were categorized by NLR and depression groups, and the same set of covariates were adjusted using multivariate Cox proportional risk regression models to estimate the risk of death. The restricted cubic spline (RCS) function was also applied to analyze the linear and non-linear associations of NLR with stroke, all-cause mortality, and MACE. Participants with chronic kidney disease and malignant tumors were excluded in the sensitivity analysis. Subgroup analysis was conducted based on age. Survival analyses were performed using the Kaplan–Meier method, in which the probability of survival in the population was assessed according to the levels of NLR and depressive symptoms and compared using the log-rank test. Finally, mediation analyses were performed to assess the indirect effect of depressive symptoms on mortality and stroke mediated through NLR. Missing data were replaced by multiple interpolations. The missed data rate of demographic and clinical variables (including education level, diabetes, hypertension, CVD, history of smoking and drinking and BMI) was less than 5%, and the missed data rate of dyslipidemia was less than 15%. Multiple interpolation on missing values was performed in R with a software package called "Mice" (Buuren and Groothuis-Oudshoorn, 2011). All tests were two-sided, and a P value of 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed by the R Statistical Program (http://www.R-project.org, R Foundation), Free Statistics Analysis Platform, and Statistical Package for the Social Sciences 25.0.

Result

Baseline characteristics

A total of 32,680 US adults (weighted population 196,879,111; age 49.8 ± 17.8 years (mean ± SE); (49.1%) male) were included, and participants were classified into three groups according to NLR grading: low (n = 10,942), medium (n = 10,916), and high (n = 10,822). Of these, 14,165 (43.3%) were non-Hispanic whites, 6,538 (20.0%) were non-Hispanic blacks, 5,171 (15.8%) were Mexican-Americans, and 6,806 (20.8%) were individuals of other races or ethnicities (Table 1). Participants with high NLR appeared to be more likely to be male, white, higher BMI, higher education, current smokers and drinkers. Association between single NLR or PHQ-9 score and stroke

This study first investigated the relationship between NLR and PHQ-9 score and stroke. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed between NLR and PHQ-9 score and stroke (Table 2). After adjusting for covariates, the results showed that the risk of stroke was highest in US adults with higher NLR (OR, 1.39; 95% CI, 1.09–1.76) and PHQ-9 score > = 10 (OR, 2.32; 95% CI, 1.83, 2.95). The RCS analysis showed a non-linear correlation between NLR and stroke(P = 0.0343), whereas there was a linear correlation between PHQ-9 score and stroke(p = 0.0801) (Fig. 2).

Joint association of NLR and PHQ-9 score with stroke

In the joint analysis (Table 3), participants with PHQ-9 score > = 10 and high NLR levels had the highest risk ratio for stroke compared with lower NLR levels and PHQ-9 score < 10 (OR, 2.89; 95% CI, 1.97–4.24). Sensitivity analysis also indicated that compared with patients with low NLR levels and PHQ-9 score < 10, patients with PHQ-9 score > = 10 and high NLR levels had a higher risk of stroke (Table S1). The results of age-based subgroup analysis showed that whether ≥ 60 years old or < 60 years old, elevated NLR levels and aggravated depressive symptoms were closely related to an increased risk of stroke (Fig. S1 A). No interaction was found in subgroup.

Association between single NLR or PHQ-9 score and mortality or MACE

During a median follow-up period of 7.3 years (interquartile range 4.1–10.8 years), 3,350 (10.3%) of the 32,680 participants died, 1039 (3.2%) of whom died from MACE. RCS analysis showed that the NLR and PHQ-9 score were linearly correlated with all-cause mortality (p = 0.1477; p = 0.2727) (Fig. 3A, C). NLR was nonlinearly positively correlated with MACE, whereas PHQ9 was linearly correlated with MACE. (p < 0.0001; p = 0.1796) (Fig. 3B, D). The participants with higher NLR elevated the risk of all-cause mortality and MACE (Table 4). After adjusting for covariates, the results showed that participants with higher NLR had the highest risk of all-cause mortality and MACE (HR, 1.406; 95% CI 1.261–1.567; HR, 1.927; 95% CI 1.518–2.447) and that participants with higher PHQ-9 score were associated with increased all-cause mortality and MACE (HR, 1.762; 95% CI 1.516–2.047; HR, 1.755; 95% CI 1.396–2.206). Survival curve analyses showed that for both all-cause mortality and MACE, survival was significantly lower in the high-NLR group compared with the low-NLR group, and the same trend was observed in the group with higher PHQ-9 score (Fig. 4).

Joint association of NLR and PHQ-9 score with mortality

In the combined analysis (Table 5), participants with PHQ-9 score > = 10 and high NLR levels had the highest all-cause mortality and MACE (HR, 2.079; 95% CI 1.673–2.585; HR, 2.858, 95% CI 2.007–4.069) compared with those with low NLR levels and PHQ-9 score < 10. The Kaplan–Meier curves for all-cause and cerebrovascular deaths also showed that PHQ-9 score > = 10 and high NLR levels had the lowest survival rates (Fig. 5). Sensitivity analysis also indicated that compared with patients with low NLR levels and PHQ-9 score < 10, participants with PHQ-9 score > = 10 and high NLR levels had higher all-cause mortality and MACE (Table S2). The results of age-based subgroup analysis showed that whether ≥ 60 years old or < 60 years old, elevated NLR levels and aggravated depressive symptoms were closely related to an increased risk of all-cause mortality and MACE (Fig. S1 B, C). No interaction was found in subgroup.

Mediation analysis of NLR and PHQ-9 score for all‑cause mortality and MACE

Mediation analyses explored the mediating role of NLR between PHQ-9 score and stroke, all-cause mortality, and MACE. PHQ-9 scores were positively correlated with NLR (β ± SE = 0.0089 ± 0.0016, p < 0.001), and NLR was positively correlated with stroke, all-cause mortality, and MACE (β ± SE = 0.0418 ± 0.0189, p = 0.0274; β ± SE = 0.1875 ± 0.0146, p < 0.001; β ± SE = 0.1200 ± 0.0181, p < 0.001).

Ultimately, 0.6%, 5.9%, and 4.8% of the observed correlations between PHQ-9 score and stroke risk, all-cause mortality risk, and MACE risk, respectively, were moderated by the NLR (Fig. 6).

Discussion

The findings of our study indicate elevated NLR levels and more severe depressive symptoms were associated with increased risk of stroke, all-cause mortality and MACE. Additionally, mediation analysis revealed that NLR partially mediates the association of depressive symptoms with both stroke and mortality.

Neuroinflammation is the main factor causing brain injury [27]. Several randomized controlled trials have indicated that anti-inflammatory therapy is capable of reducing vascular risks like stroke [28, 29]. Therefore, inflammation may serve as a predictor for stroke occurrence. Our research has revealed that individuals with high levels of inflammation are more vulnerable to stroke. When a stroke occurs, the injury site releases chemokines, prompting neutrophils to cross the endothelial cell layer and reach the focal cerebral microvessels, secreting a large number of pro-inflammatory cytokines, and infiltrating the endothelial cell membrane and basement membrane [30, 31]. Simultaneously, lymphocytes are recruited to the ischemic region, damaging the blood–brain barrier. This action affects neuronal autophagy through derived cytokines, ultimately leading to neuronal damage, apoptosis, and necrosis [32, 33]. NLR serves not only as a readily accessible systemic inflammatory marker but also encompasses two complementary immune pathways. It can comprehensively reflect the dynamic relationship between innate immunity (neutrophils) and adaptive cellular immune responses (lymphocytes) in disease conditions, compensating for the deficiency of using a single indicator [34–36]. A study demonstrated that in the observational cohort study of patients with acute ischemic stroke, the higher the NLR at admission, the greater the possibility of adverse functional outcomes [37]. This perspective was further supported by research showing that elevated NLR levels indicate more severe inflammation and an increased risk of stroke mortality [38].

Meanwhile, NLR can also predict the prognosis of cardiovascular diseases [39, 40]. Five randomized trials were conducted on atherosclerosis in the cardiovascular system, and the results demonstrated that NLR could significantly forecast adverse cardiovascular events [41]. Additionally, a group study based on the age and gender of the population concluded that NLR could predict the mortality rate of coronary heart disease [42]. Moreover, NLR is frequently used to assess the prognosis of tumors including lung and colorectal cancer [43]. Patients with localized colorectal cancer and low preoperative NLR had a 5-year survival rate of 77.2%, while those with high preoperative NLR had a 5-year survival rate of 50.8%, according to a meta-analysis [44]. Moreover, studies have also shown a positive association between NLR levels and rates of mortality among the elderly [45]. Our research confirms the relationship between NLR and MACE, and all-cause mortality—indicating that higher NLR levels correspond to increased MACE and mortality.

Depression can also lead to the occurrence of stroke [46, 47]. Through the INTERSTROKE case–control study, which performed a logistic regression to determine that depression had a significant risk factor for stroke [48]. In addition, several studies have indicated that depression could elevate the risk of stroke to a certain degree [49].

In addition, some research claims that depression may increase the risk of myocardial infarction [50]. The possible reason is that depression can lead to enhanced platelet aggregation function and decreased plasma fibrinolysis activity, thereby forming thrombi to clog blood vessels. A study from public data revealed a significant positive correlation between depression and cardiovascular disease mortality in Chinese adults [51]. Depressive symptoms could also lower self-care awareness and promote unhealthy lifestyles, raising the risk of other illnesses. Additionally, a meta-analysis demonstrated that depression is conspicuously correlated with the elevated risk of stroke mortality [52]. Our research indicates that there is a correlation between depressive symptoms and MACE as well as all-cause mortality, and the severity of the depressive symptoms is positively correlated with MACE and mortality.

A longitudinal study of aging in the UK confirmed that men with depressive symptoms and high C-reactive protein (CRP) had an increase in the risk of all-cause mortality and the incidence of cardiovascular events [53]. Currently, there have been no studies that incorporate risk assessment by placing NLR and depressive symptoms within the same large-scale group of participants. Thus, this large-sample study aims to explore the relationship between NLR, depressive symptoms, and stroke while assessing their association with mortality. Our findings confirm that elevated NLR levels and more severe depressive symptoms were associated with increased risk of stroke, all-cause mortality and MACE.

It is currently unclear how NLR and depressive symptoms interact to cause stroke and fatality. Potential mechanisms may exist as follows: Firstly, the hypothesis of the hypothalamus–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis. Depressed patients often exhibit functional disorders in the HPA axis, resulting in elevated cortisol levels [54]. This, in turn, triggers actin cytoskeleton reorganization, enhances lymphocyte and neutrophil deformability, and initiates inflammation [55]. Simultaneously, elevated cortisol levels can activate cell-mediated immune activation and increase the responses of cytokines such as lymphocytes and neutrophils to stressors, causing a continuous inflammatory response [56]. Once the vascular endothelium is infiltrated by inflammatory cells, it is prone to lipid accumulation and vascular lumen stenosis, thereby leading to insufficient tissue perfusion and triggering cardiovascular events [57]. Besides, Neuroinflammation can reduce serotonin synthesis and increase neurotoxic kynurenine metabolites, thereby inducing or exacerbating depressive symptoms [58, 59]. Neuroinflammation also inhibits neuroplasticity and disrupts the HPA axis function, thereby further contributing to the pathological process of depression [60, 61]. Simultaneously, this neuroinflammatory process significantly elevates the risk of ischemic stroke. The underlying mechanism involves neuroinflammation promoting the development of atherosclerosis [62]. This manifests specifically by inducing endothelial dysfunction, and increasing plaque instability [63, 64]. These effects heighten the probability of ischemic stroke occurrence. Additionally, NLR and depressive symptoms can cooperatively reduce blood flow in blood vessels, increasing the risk of patient mortality in cases of prolonged tissue ischemia or extensive ischemic areas [65, 66]. Secondly, depression can activate microglia in the hippocampus, prompting the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines that drive neuroinflammation. Depressed patients have a more intense cytokine response to stressors, which can lead to prolonged inflammatory processes, hinder the growth of new brain cells, and reduce brain neuroplasticity, thereby inducing the progression of cardiovascular events and other diseases [67–69]. Even worse, suicidal tendencies may emerge. Maintaining lower NLR levels and mitigating depressive symptoms can improve prognosis. Nonetheless, further large-scale studies are essential to validate these findings.

However, this study also has the following limitations: Firstly, in terms of the inclusion age range, the age range of the participants was not strictly defined. Given that the occurrence age group of stroke is mainly concentrated in the elderly population, there may be a certain degree of selection bias. Secondly, the assessment of depression was accomplished by using the self-report of PHQ-9, which may not be the optimal indicator of depressive symptoms. Additionally, there are omissions in the dynamic monitoring of NLR, unable to comprehensively understand the change rules of inflammatory indicators of stroke patients in the time series.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study indicates that elevated NLR levels and more severe depressive symptoms were associated with increased risk of stroke, all-cause mortality and MACE. Moreover, the association between depressive symptoms and stroke, all-cause mortality as well as MACE may be partially mediated by NLR. These findings have important implications for public health, particularly the treatment and prevention of depression and inflammation in stroke patients. By gaining a deeper understanding of these relationships, public health practitioners and researchers may be able to develop more effective interventions and policies to improve their long-term health.
