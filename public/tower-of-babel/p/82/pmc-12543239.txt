Effect of Mental Health Diagnoses on Postoperative Outcomes and Secondary Treatment Utilization After Surgery for Adhesive Capsulitis: A Retrospective Large Database Analysis
Authors: Harmon S. Khela, Monty S. Khela, John D. Kelly, Liane Miller
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Adhesive capsulitis (AC) is a debilitating condition that markedly affects shoulder mobility and function. Mental health disorders have been associated with poorer outcomes for orthopaedic patients; however, their specific effect on postoperative outcomes after surgery for AC remains underexplored.Methods:Patients who underwent arthroscopic capsular release or manipulation under anesthesia for AC were identified in the PearlDiver database. Patients with preexisting mental health diagnoses (anxiety, depression, bipolar disorder, or schizophrenia) were matched 1:1 with control subjects based on age, sex, and Charlson Comorbidity Index. Rates of 90-day complications and 2-year surgical outcomes were analyzed through multivariable logistic regression. Kaplan-Meier analyses generated 10-year cumulative incidence rates of postoperative therapeutic modalities, and a Cox proportional hazard ratio (HR) model generated HRs and 95% confidence intervals.Results:Among 42,862 patients identified, 26.1% had a preoperative mental health diagnosis. After matching, patients with a mental health disorder experienced greater risk for 90-day hospital readmission (odds ratio [OR] = 1.33, P < 0.001), emergency department visits (OR = 1.81, P < 0.001), and several postoperative complications, including acute kidney injury (OR = 1.60, P < 0.001), wound complications (OR = 1.63, P = 0.028), pneumonia (OR = 1.46, P < 0.001), urinary tract infection (OR = 1.22, P < 0.001), and stroke (OR = 1.75, P = 0.005). Patients with mental health disorders were also more likely to undergo physical therapy (OR = 1.18, P < 0.001), intra-articular injections (OR = 1.27, P < 0.001), and any secondary treatment (OR = 1.22, P < 0.001) 2 years after surgery. In addition, preexisting mental health disorders were associated with an increased 10-year cumulative incidence and risk of physical therapy (53.7% versus 52.0%, HR = 1.09, P 0.001), intra-articular injections (32.0% versus 28.6%, HR = 1.20, P < 0.001), and any secondary treatment (64.9% versus 62.7%, HR = 1.10, P < 0.001).Conclusion:Patients with existing mental health disorders experienced increased rates of complications and a higher risk of secondary treatment utilization over a 10-year period after surgery for AC. Awareness of these risks is essential for optimizing postoperative care and addressing the complex needs of this patient population.

Adhesive capsulitis (AC), commonly referred to as frozen shoulder, is a condition affecting 2% to 5% of the general population.1 The underlying pathophysiology of AC involves fibroproliferative changes, excessive scar tissue formation, and capsular inflammation in the glenohumeral joint, leading to shoulder stiffness, pain, and restricted range of motion. AC may be idiopathic, linked to systemic conditions such as diabetes, thyroid disease, autoimmune disorders, and Dupuytren contracture, or secondary to postoperative or traumatic causes.2,3 Although the precise mechanisms driving AC remain unclear, emerging research has pointed to the role of inflammatory mediators, genetic predispositions to fibrotic changes, and hormonal factors (such as estrogen and thyroid-stimulating hormone) as key contributors to its development.4-7 Studies have yet to identify a singular best treatment, highlighting the need for individualized patient care strategies based on factors such as stage of disease and associated comorbidities. Although conservative treatments, including physical therapy, corticosteroid intra-articular injections, and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, are typically first-line options, surgical interventions such as arthroscopic capsular release or manipulation under anesthesia are considered when nonsurgical measures fail.8,9 Despite these approaches, patients often experience prolonged recovery periods and, in some cases, permanent reduction in shoulder mobility.

Given the debilitating nature of AC and its effect on quality of life, it is crucial to identify modifiable patient-level factors that may increase the risk for poor outcomes after surgery. Assessing psychosocial variables as determinants of postoperative prognosis has recently gained momentum in orthopaedic surgery. Patients with preexisting mental health disorders have worse outcomes across several orthopaedic surgeries, including hip and knee arthroplasty, rotator cuff repair, anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction, and total shoulder arthroplasty.10-30 Although well-studied in other shoulder diagnoses, the relationship between existing mental health disorders and outcomes after AC surgery remains underexplored. Preliminary investigations into the association between AC and mental health disorders have yielded conflicting results on whether an AC diagnosis increases the likelihood of developing anxiety or depression.20,21 Regarding surgery for AC specifically, however, data are even more limited. Haroun et al22 analyzed the effect of preoperative anxiety and depression on pain scores in patients who underwent arthroscopic capsular release for AC and found that those with higher psychological distress experienced greater pain scores, measured through the Visual Analog Scale, preoperatively and at 1-year follow-up after arthroscopic release. However, most existing studies have relied on single–institution-level data, and none have adequately assessed the effect of mental health disorders on complications and long-term outcomes after AC surgery.

The purpose of this study was to use a national database to assess whether preoperative mental health diagnoses (anxiety, depression, bipolar disorder, or schizophrenia) are associated with increased postoperative complications and secondary treatment utilization after surgery, specifically manipulation under anesthesia or capsular release, for AC. We hypothesized that patients with preexisting mental health conditions would be at increased risk for postoperative complications and secondary treatment utilization compared with those without these diagnoses. Considering the high prevalence of mental health disorders in orthopaedic populations, understanding their effect on postoperative outcomes could help optimize perioperative management and facilitate personalized care for patients undergoing AC surgery.

Methods

Study Design

A retrospective cohort study was conducted using the PearlDiver Mariner Claims Database (PearlDiver Technologies), a large national administrative claims database that contains diagnostic, procedural, and demographic records for more than 165 million patients from 2010 to 2022. This deidentified data set contains records from all payer types, including cash, commercial insurance, Medicaid, Medicare, and government entities. Patients are identified using Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) and International Classification of Disease (ICD) diagnosis codes. Longitudinal tracking of patients is possible and reliable in this database because patients are tracked based on unique patient identifier codes instead of insurance status. This study only extracted deidentified patient information and therefore was deemed exempt from an institutional review board.

Patient Population

Patients with AC were identified using ICD-9 and ICD-10 diagnosis codes (Supplemental Table 1, http://links.lww.com/JG9/A458). Patients with secondary causes of AC or other concomitant shoulder diagnoses were excluded: septic arthritis, prior shoulder arthroplasty, shoulder trauma, rotator cuff pathology, biceps pathology, and labral/capsule pathology (Supplemental Table 2, http://links.lww.com/JG9/A458). To limit selection bias and assess outcomes longitudinally, we required patients to be continuously enrolled in the database for at least year before and 2 years after the index diagnosis of AC. From this cohort of eligible patients with AC, a subset of patients who underwent surgical management in the form of arthroscopic capsular release (CPT-29825) or manipulation under anesthesia (CPT-23700) was acquired.

Following these criteria, these eligible patients were stratified into two cohorts based on whether they had preexisting mental health disorders before surgery. Mental health disorders were classified into anxiety, depressive, bipolar, and schizophrenic disorders based on ICD diagnosis codes (Supplementary Table 3, http://links.lww.com/JG9/A458). Patients with a diagnosis of generalized anxiety disorder, adjustment disorder with anxiety, posttraumatic stress disorder, or panic disorder before surgery were included in the anxiety cohort. Similarly, patients with a diagnosis of major depressive disorder, dysthymic disorder, cyclothymic disorder, or depressive disorder not elsewhere classified were included in the depression cohort. The bipolar disorder cohort consisted of patients with bipolar I disorder, bipolar II disorder, and unspecified bipolar disorder, while the schizophrenia cohort included patients with simple schizophrenia, disorganized schizophrenia, catatonic schizophrenia, and paranoid schizophrenia, among other related diagnoses. Collectively, patients diagnosed with any one or more of these disorders before surgery were categorized into the “mental health diagnosis” cohort. Patients without any of these documented mental health diagnoses preceding AC surgery during the same period served as the control group. Baseline demographics were collected for these two cohorts, including age, sex, and the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), a verified metric providing an overall evaluation of a patient's comorbidity status. To control for confounding variables, the mental health diagnosis and no mental health diagnosis cohorts were matched 1:1 based on age, sex, and CCI.

Outcome Variables

The primary outcomes assessed were 90-day rates of postoperative complications, hospital readmissions, and emergency department visits and 2-year postoperative utilization rates of physical therapy, intra-articular injections, and any secondary treatment (defined as physical therapy and/or intra-articular injections). Medical and surgical complications included acute kidney injury, cardiac arrest, deep vein thrombosis, wound complications, hematoma, nerve injury, pneumonia, pulmonary embolism, myocardial infarction, blood transfusion, urinary tract infection, cardiac arrhythmia, stroke, and septic arthritis occurring within 90 days after surgery. The secondary outcome included a comparison of the 10-year cumulative incidence and risk of secondary treatment utilization after AC surgery between patients with and without preexisting mental health disorders. All patients included in this analysis had a minimum of 2 years of postoperative tracking in the PearlDiver database after their index surgery. The ICD/CPT codes used to ascertain all outcomes can be found in Supplemental Table 4, http://links.lww.com/JG9/A458.

Statistical Analysis

The Welch t-test was used to assess differences between continuous variables (age and CCI), while the Pearson χ2 test with Yates continuity correction was used to compare differences between categorical demographic variables (age range and sex). Rates of postoperative complications and surgical outcomes were compared between the two cohorts using χ2 analysis. Logistic regression analyses were conducted to calculate odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals for all complications and outcomes compared between the matched cohorts. Kaplan-Meier analysis was implemented to determine the 10-year cumulative incidences of physical therapy, intra-articular injections, and any secondary treatment after surgery for AC. To compare the risk of these secondary treatment modalities between the matched cohorts, a Cox proportional hazard model analysis was used to calculate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05 for all tests. All statistical analyses were conducted using the R software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing) provided by the PearlDiver database.

Results

The initial cohort included 1,601,665 patients diagnosed with AC. After applying exclusion criteria, 1,291,716 patients remained eligible for analysis. The cohort was further narrowed based on follow-up criteria, leading to the final inclusion of 42,862 patients identified from 2011 to 2020 who underwent surgical intervention for AC and were active 1 year before and 2 years after surgery (Figure 1). Among these, 11,207 patients (26.1%) had a preoperative mental health diagnosis, whereas 31,655 patients (73.9%) did not have any documented mental health conditions. Unmatched baseline demographics showed that patients with mental health diagnoses were predominantly female (77.7% versus 65.7%, P < 0.001), had a higher mean CCI (2.0 versus 1.1, P < 0.001), and were slightly younger (mean 53.4 versus 54.6 years, P < 0.001) (Table 1). After matching based on age, sex, and CCI, the final cohort included 10,430 patients in each group. The matched groups showed no significant differences in age (53.6 ± 9.4 years), sex distribution (77.6% female), or CCI (1.7) (Table 2).

Ninety-day Complications

In the first 90 days after surgery, patients with preoperative mental health diagnoses experienced higher rates of certain complications compared with those without mental health diagnoses, whereas other complications showed no significant difference between the groups. Univariate analyses revealed that hospital readmissions were more frequent in the mental health diagnosis cohort (n = 148 [1.4%] versus n = 109 [5.4%], P = 0.017), along with a higher incidence of emergency department visits (n = 1,131 [1.4%] versus n = 109 [5.4%], P < 0.001) (Table 3). Regarding medical complications, patients with mental health diagnoses had elevated rates of pneumonia (n = 82 [0.79%] versus n = 56 [0.54%], P = 0.033) and urinary tract infections (n = 262 [2.51%] versus n = 215 [2.06%], P = 0.033) within 90 days of surgery. All other evaluated complications showed no difference between the matched cohorts (Table 3).

Two-year Surgical Outcomes

At the 2-year postoperative mark, notable differences were observed in secondary treatment utilization between the matched mental health diagnosis and control cohorts. In particular, patients with preoperative mental health diagnoses demonstrated higher utilization rates of physical therapy (n = 4,479 [42.9%] versus n = 4,139 [39.7%], P < 0.001), intra-articular injections (n = 2,356 [22.6%] versus n = 2,000 [19.2%], P < 0.001), or any secondary treatment (n = 5,580 [53.5%] versus n = 5,153 [49.4%], P < 0.001) as part of their postoperative care. This trend persisted after multivariable logistic regression because patients with preexisting mental health diagnoses had greater odds of needing physical therapy (OR = 1.18 [1.14 to 1.22], P < 0.001), intra-articular injections (OR 1.27 [1.22 to 1.32], P < 0.001), and any secondary treatment (OR = 1.22 [1.18 to 1.27], P < 0.001).

Cumulative Incidence and Risk for Secondary Treatment Utilization

The 10-year cumulative incidences of physical therapy (53.7% versus 52.0%), intra-articular injections (32.0% versus 28.6%), and any secondary treatment (64.9% versus 62.7%) were all higher among patients with mental health diagnoses (Table 5, Figures 2–4). Compared with the matched control cohort, patients with mental health diagnoses had a higher risk of using physical therapy (HR = 1.09 [1.05 to 1.13], P < 0.001), intra-articular injections (HR = 1.20 [1.14 to 1.26], P < 0.001), and any secondary treatment (HR = 1.10 [1.07 to 1.14], P < 0.001) 10 years postoperatively (Table 5).

Discussion

This study evaluated the effect of existing mental health diagnoses on postoperative outcomes after surgical intervention for AC. We found that patients with concomitant mental health disorders, including anxiety, depression, bipolar, or schizophrenic disorders, experienced markedly higher rates of complications within 90 days after surgery. In addition, these patients required more secondary treatments, including physical therapy and intra-articular injections, over a 10-year period.

These findings are consistent with the growing body of evidence showing that mental health comorbidities can negatively influence orthopaedic outcomes.10-30 In a 5-year cohort study, Jacob et al21 found that the incidence of depression in patients with AC was nearly double that of patients without the condition (17.5% versus 8.7%). In our study, the prevalence of preoperatively diagnosed mental health disorders within 1 year of the index surgery for AC was slightly higher at 26.1%. These findings suggest that AC may be associated with a higher prevalence of mental health disorders, which in turn can negatively affect both subjective pain scores and functional outcomes postoperatively. Ebrahimzadeh et al29 investigated idiopathic AC in 120 patients and determined that patients with anxiety or depression exhibited higher visual analog scale pain scores, but no surgery was involved. Our study also reinforces the findings of Haroun et al,22 who reported higher pain scores for patients with psychological distress both preoperatively and at the 1-year follow-up after arthroscopic capsular release for frozen shoulder.

Our results are also consistent with similar investigations across different orthopaedic procedures. Specifically, Lau et al12 found that 28.3% of patients undergoing arthroscopic rotator cuff repair had a preexisting diagnosis of anxiety or depression, and these patients exhibited worse preoperative and postoperative scores on the American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons scale. Despite this, patients with anxiety or depression showed similar improvements in outcome scores postoperatively compared with those without mental health disorders, although their overall scores remained lower. Similarly, Garcia et al13 reported that patients with major depressive disorder undergoing anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction had lower patient-reported functional scores (Lysholm and International Knee Documentation Committee) at both baseline and 1 year postoperatively. This trend has been consistently observed across various procedures, including arthroscopic shoulder stabilization and total joint arthroplasty, where preexisting psychological conditions negatively affect pain perception and functional recovery.16-19,24-28,30

Although much of the existing literature has focused on patient-reported outcomes, our study highlights the notable effect of mental health conditions on postoperative complications and healthcare utilization. A retrospective national database study by Freshman et al23 found that those with preoperative depression had higher rates of 90-day complications and readmissions, and 2-year revision procedures for elective hip arthroscopy. Similarly, in arthroscopic rotator cuff repair and total shoulder arthroplasty, preexisting mental health disorders were a risk factor for greater readmissions rates and numerous complications, such as urinary tract infection, pneumonia, superficial wound infection, and deep vein thrombosis.10-12,14-16,24,25 Multiple other studies in the total joint arthroplasty literature have reported similar findings as our study, where patients with mental health comorbidities are often at increased risk for complications, prolonged recovery times, and greater healthcare utilization.17-19,26-28

Potential mechanisms underlying these associations likely involve a combination of biological, psychological, and behavioral factors. The higher complication rates observed in patients with preoperative mental health disorders may be rooted in dysregulation of chronic stress responses, characterized by elevated cortisol levels, which can impair immune function and slow wound healing.31-33 Depression and anxiety have been shown to upregulate proinflammatory cytokines, such as interleukin-6 and C-reactive protein, which can exacerbate preexisting conditions and impair the body's ability to recover from surgery.34 Furthermore, the cognitive impairments associated with these disorders may hinder patients' ability to effectively manage their postoperative care, leading to an increased risk of complications. In addition, mental health disorders are often associated with behaviors that negatively affect health, such as smoking, poor nutrition, and physical inactivity, which may exacerbate comorbid conditions such as obesity and cardiovascular disease that elevate surgical risks.35 Our study also revealed an increased risk of emergency department visits postoperatively among patients with mental health disorders. This finding is consistent with previous research, which suggests that these patients may be more likely to seek emergency care due to increased levels of somatization and anxiety.28,36 The increased frequency of these visits not only reflects the heightened distress these patients may experience but also contributes to the overall burden on the healthcare system, highlighting the need for targeted preoperative counseling and optimized perioperative management strategies.

In this study, we also report a greater reliance on secondary treatment modalities (physical therapy and intra-articular injection) for patients with mental health disorders up to 10 years after surgery. This trend suggests that the presence of mental health conditions may complicate the recovery process, necessitating ongoing interventions long after the initial surgery. It is well-documented that AC can lead to chronic pain and stiffness, which often requires extended rehabilitation efforts.1-3 For patients with underlying mental health disorders, these challenges are likely compounded by factors such as increased pain sensitivity, reduced motivation, and potential nonadherence to postoperative care protocols.30,37-39 These patients may struggle more with the physical demands of rehabilitation and may experience higher levels of anxiety or depression as they face slow or incomplete recovery. This, in turn, can create a vicious cycle where ongoing pain and functional limitations worsen mental health symptoms, further prolonging the need for physical therapy and additional treatments such as intra-articular injections. Moreover, patients with mental health disorders may have a heightened perception of pain, known as pain catastrophizing.36,38 This may lead to excessive stress and anxiety that precipitates poor adherence to postoperative care guidelines including physical therapy, which is crucial for recovery after surgery for AC. Therefore, these patients may require more intensive follow-up care and interventions to achieve outcomes comparable with those without mental health conditions.

The effect of mental health disorders on healthcare costs is another crucial aspect highlighted by our findings. The increased need for secondary treatments, higher complication rates, and greater overall healthcare utilization likely contribute to the higher costs associated with the care of these patients. Our findings echo those of other studies, which showed that patients with depression undergoing orthopaedic surgeries had markedly higher postoperative costs compared with those without depression.11,17,40 This suggests that addressing mental health disorders in the preoperative setting could not only improve clinical outcomes but also reduce healthcare expenditures.

In light of these findings, it is clear that screening for and addressing mental health comorbidities should be an integral part of the preoperative and postoperative care plan for patients undergoing surgery for AC and other orthopaedic conditions. Future research should focus on developing and evaluating interventions aimed at optimizing the mental health of patients before surgery, with the goal of improving both short-term and long-term outcomes. Collaboration between orthopaedic surgeons, psychiatrists, psychologists, and physical therapists could provide a more holistic treatment approach, addressing both the physical and psychological aspects of recovery. This approach could be particularly beneficial in managing the complex interplay between pain, mental health, and physical function, ultimately leading to better patient outcomes.

There are several limitations to this study that should be acknowledged. First, the PearlDiver database relies on the accuracy of CPT and ICD codes submitted during billing, which may introduce errors in coding or underreporting of diagnoses and procedures. Although strict inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied to minimize misclassification, it is possible that some patients were miscoded in electronic health records, affecting the study's findings. Second, the binary nature of the data set precluded the ability to assess and stratify the data based on the severity or chronicity of the mental health disorders, which could influence postoperative outcomes. There is also a possibility that some mental health disorders may be undiagnosed in either cohort. Future research may benefit from incorporating more granular data regarding the duration and intensity of mental health conditions. Third, the reliance on a large commercial database restricts the scope of information that can be extracted for each patient or case. The lack of detailed surgical data in the database, such as specifics on the technique used or surgeon experience, or patient data limits the ability to control for potential confounders. However, we attempted to minimize confounders through matching and adjustment during multivariable analysis. In addition, we could not account for patient-reported outcomes or functional measures, which are important for understanding the true effect of these conditions on postoperative recovery. There may have been other mediators of poor outcomes after AC surgery, such as socioeconomic status, race, or insurance plan, that were not controlled for in this study. Finally, the retrospective design of the study limits causal inferences because the associations observed do not confirm a direct cause-effect relationship between mental health disorders and outcomes. Prospective studies are needed to further explore these relationships.

Conclusion

This study is the first large-scale national analysis to investigate the effect of mental health disorders on postoperative outcomes in patients undergoing surgery for AC. The substantial effect of mental health comorbidities on complication rates, healthcare utilization, and the need for secondary treatments highlights the importance of addressing these conditions as modifiable risk factors. Incorporating mental health evaluation and management into the preoperative and postoperative care for patients with AC is critical for enhancing outcomes and optimizing overall care.
