Natural deep eutectic solvent-based pectin-chitosan composite hydrogel films: A novel pH-responsive color indicator for food packaging systems
Authors: Hamza Alasalvar, Gulsah Karabulut, Gulden Goksen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study presents a novel intelligent, biodegradable hydrogel film for food packaging, based on a pectin–chitosan matrix, integrated with Hibiscus sabdariffa extract and a natural deep eutectic solvent (DES; choline chloride–lactic acid). Anthocyanins from H. sabdariffa enabled reversible color changes (red–purple–yellow) across pH 2–10, allowing real-time freshness monitoring. Mechanical testing revealed that DES enhanced film flexibility by increasing elongation at break and reducing tensile strength. FTIR and XRD analyses supported these findings by showing reduced hydrogen bonding interactions and a more disordered amorphous structure, which are structural features commonly associated with improved polymer chain mobility. FESEM revealed a compact morphology, with increased roughness due to chemical compounds in the extract. Antioxidant activity improved with H. sabdariffa, as shown by DPPH and ABTS assays, suggesting potential for oxidative stability enhancement in food packaging applications. However, DES raised water vapor permeability by 194 %, indicating a considerable reduction in barrier performance. These hydrogel films are envisioned for use as direct-contact layers in perishable food packaging, enabling consumers to monitor freshness through visible color changes. This synergistic system offers excellent pH responsiveness, antioxidant functionality, and mechanical flexibility, supporting its application in smart food packaging.Keywords: Intelligent packaging, pH-responsive, Deep eutectic solvents, Hibiscus sabdariffa, Biodegradable food packaging

Introduction

Food packaging plays a critical role beyond mere containment; it functions as a protective barrier against spoilage, microbial contamination, and various environmental stressors (Jahangiri et al., 2024). However, conventional plastic packaging materials present serious challenges due to their non-biodegradable nature, environmental persistence, and contribution to the global plastic waste crisis (Macheca et al., 2024). In response to growing concerns regarding food safety, environmental sustainability, and public health, there is an increasing demand for eco-friendly and intelligent packaging systems that offer functionalities beyond physical protection (Realini and Marcos, 2014; Amin et al., 2022). Among emerging strategies, the development of pH-responsive biopolymer films has gained considerable attention as a promising approach for real-time freshness monitoring in food packaging applications (Cheng et al., 2022).

Intelligent packaging technologies provide active monitoring of food quality, and pH-responsive color indicators have gained interest as non-invasive, real-time sensors for spoilage detection (Kuswandi et al., 2011). During storage, many perishable foods such as meat, fish, and dairy undergo pH changes due to microbial metabolism and enzymatic activity (Priyadarshi et al., 2021). Conventional detection methods, including microbiological and chemical assays, are often time-consuming, costly, and destructive (Zhao et al., 2022). In contrast, colorimetric indicators embedded in biodegradable films offer a rapid, simple, and visual method for freshness evaluation through pH-induced color changes (Luo et al., 2023).

Among natural pH-sensitive pigments, anthocyanins—widely present in fruits, vegetables, and flowers—are ideal for smart packaging applications (Parcheta et al., 2021; Pang et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2023). These water-soluble flavonoids exhibit vivid and reversible color changes in response to pH, ranging from red in acidic to purple in neutral and yellow/green in alkaline conditions (Pang et al., 2023). Their intrinsic antioxidant and antimicrobial properties further enhance the functionality of intelligent packaging systems (Zhao et al., 2022). Hibiscus sabdariffa is a particularly rich source of anthocyanins, primarily delphinidin and cyanidin derivatives, known for their vibrant red color and high bioactivity (Escobar-Ortiz et al., 2021). When incorporated into biodegradable films, H. sabdariffa extract can serve both as a pH indicator and as an active agent to improve shelf life and food quality monitoring (Aydin and Zorlu, 2022).

To effectively deliver pH-responsive functionality in packaging films, a suitable polymer matrix is essential. Polysaccharide-based films, particularly those comprising pectin and chitosan, are widely explored due to their biodegradability, renewable origin, and excellent film-forming properties (Huang et al., 2021; Harussani et al., 2021; Yu et al., 2023). Pectin, an anionic polysaccharide obtained from plant-based residues, offers good film-forming capacity but tends to produce brittle films due to strong hydrogen bonding (Zhang et al., 2024). Chitosan, a cationic polysaccharide derived from chitin, forms flexible, transparent films with inherent antimicrobial activity, making it highly suitable for food-contact materials (Zhou et al., 2024). When combined, pectin and chitosan form electrostatically cross-linked hydrogel films with improved mechanical strength and integrity (Duan et al., 2022; Wei et al., 2023). This dual-polymer matrix also enhances transparency, facilitating clearer visualization of pH-responsive color changes (Duan et al., 2022).

One of the major challenges in polysaccharide-based films is brittleness and poor flexibility, necessitating the incorporation of plasticizers (Grala et al., 2022). While conventional plasticizers such as glycerol or sorbitol are commonly used, they suffer from limitations including moisture sensitivity and limited bioactivity (Harussani et al., 2021). Recently, natural deep eutectic solvents (DESs) have emerged as sustainable, multifunctional alternatives for plasticization in biopolymer systems (Meenu et al., 2023; Karabulut and Goksen, 2025).

DESs are biodegradable mixtures formed by combining hydrogen bond donors (HBDs) and acceptors (HBAs), which lower the glass transition temperature (Tg) and enhance mechanical flexibility (de Souza et al., 2024; Zannou et al., 2025). Among various DESs, choline chloride–lactic acid (ChCl–LA) exhibits high compatibility with polysaccharides, forming hydrogen-bonded networks that improve elasticity, moisture retention, and barrier performance (Yu et al., 2023; Grala et al., 2022; Zannou et al., 2025). Beyond their plasticizing role, DESs also act as carriers for bioactive compounds, enhancing the solubility and stability of plant-derived antioxidants, antimicrobials, and pH-sensitive pigments (Rather and Ali, 2022; Türker and Doğan, 2021; Meenu et al., 2023). This dual function is particularly beneficial in smart packaging applications, where both structural and functional enhancements are needed (Tadele et al., 2024). Incorporating H. sabdariffa extract into a DES matrix may improve anthocyanin solubility, distribution, and stability, thereby enhancing the overall performance of pH-responsive films (Liu et al., 2022; Alasalvar et al., 2024).

Accordingly, the present study aims to develop and characterize a novel pH-responsive hydrogel film using a pectin–chitosan matrix, enriched with H. sabdariffa extract and plasticized with ChCl–LA DES. The novelty of this approach lies in the dual role of DES as a green plasticizer that improves mechanical flexibility, and as a green extraction medium for anthocyanins from H. sabdariffa. The specific objectives of this research are to: i) investigate the impact of ChCl-LA DES plasticization on the mechanical, thermal, and physicochemical properties of the pectin–chitosan composite films; ii) evaluate the influence of ChCl-LA extract incorporation from H. sabdariffa on antioxidant activity, pH-responsiveness, and structural integrity; iii) assess the visual and quantitative colorimetric response of the films to pH variations for potential application as real-time freshness indicators in food packaging. These hydrogel films are intended to be used as intelligent freshness indicator layers placed in direct or indirect contact with high-moisture foods (e.g., meat, fish). Their visible pH-induced color changes enable consumers and suppliers to monitor spoilage progression non-destructively within the package. The flexibility, antioxidant activity, and visual clarity of the films make them suitable for integration into tray linings, lids, or indicator sachets in smart packaging systems.

Materials and methods

Materials

Dried H. sabdariffa calyces were sourced from a local herbalist in Niğde, Türkiye. Prior to extraction, the calyces were milled and sieved through a 200 μm mesh. Chitosan (Ch), with a molecular weight of 200–300 kDa, was obtained from Adaga Health Chemistry Industry Inc. (Antalya, Türkiye). High methoxyl apple pectin was purchased from Tito (Smart Kimya, İzmir, Türkiye). Choline chloride (ChCl) and lactic acid (LA), used for the preparation of DES, were supplied by Acros Organics (Belgium) and Merck (Germany), respectively.

DES preparation

The DES was synthesized via a conventional heating–stirring method. ChCl was used as the hydrogen bond acceptor (HBA), while LA acted as the hydrogen bond donor (HBD). The components were accurately weighed at a molar ratio of 1:2 (ChCl-LA) and mixed in a sealed glass beaker. The mixture was stirred continuously at 300 rpm and heated to 80 °C on a magnetic stirrer until a clear and homogeneous liquid was obtained. After synthesis, the ChCl–LA DES was cooled to ambient temperature (25 °C) and stored in a dark container to minimize light-induced degradation.

Extraction procedure

DES extraction of bioactive compounds from H. sabdariffa was performed following the protocol of Alasalvar et al. (2024) with some modifications. Hibiscus powder was mixed with ChCl-LA DES at a solid-to-liquid ratio of 1:10 (w/v) in a test tube. Ultrasonic-assisted extraction was conducted at 40 °C for 30 min using an ultrasonic bath (425 W, WiseClean WUC-D06H, Daihan, South Korea). The extract was then centrifuged at 6000×g for 10 min to separate the clear supernatant, which was stored at 4 °C in the dark until further analysis.

Total phenolic content (TPC), total anthocyanin content (TAC), and antioxidant capacity assays of Hibiscus extracts

The TPC of the extract was quantified using the Folin–Ciocalteu colorimetric method as described by Ozturk et al. (2018), with slight modifications. Briefly, 1.56 mL of distilled water, 40 μL of extract, 100 μL of Folin–Ciocalteu reagent, and 300 μL of 20 % (w/v) Na2CO3 were sequentially mixed and vortexed for 1 min. The reaction mixture was incubated in the dark at room temperature for 1 h, after which the absorbance was measured at 750 nm using a UV–visible spectrophotometer (Evolution 300, Thermo Scientific, USA). TPC was expressed as milligrams of gallic acid equivalents per gram of sample (mg GAE/g).

TAC was determined using the pH differential method according to Çam et al. (2009). The extract was diluted with pH 1.0 buffer (0.025 M potassium chloride) and pH 4.5 buffer (0.4 M sodium acetate), and the mixtures were incubated in the dark for 15 min. Absorbance was recorded at 520 nm and 700 nm using the same UV–visible spectrophotometer. TAC was calculated using the pH differential equation and reported as milligrams of cyanidin-3-glucoside (C3G) equivalents per gram of sample (mg C3G/g).

The antioxidant activity of the extract was evaluated using the 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) radical scavenging assay, as described by Görgüç et al. (2019), with slight modifications. In brief, 0.1 mL of extract was mixed with 2.9 mL of 0.1 mM ethanolic DPPH solution and incubated in the dark at room temperature for 30 min. A control was prepared by substituting the extract with distilled water under identical conditions. The absorbance was measured at 517 nm using a UV–visible spectrophotometer (Evolution 300, Thermo Scientific, USA). Results were expressed as milligrams of Trolox equivalents per gram of sample (mg TE/g).

The ABTS (2,2-azinobis-(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid)) assay was conducted following the method of Re et al. (1999). The ABTS•+ radical cation was generated by reacting 7 mM ABTS solution with 5 mL of 12.25 mM potassium persulfate (K2S2O8), followed by incubation in the dark at room temperature for 12–16 h. The resulting solution was diluted with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) to adjust the absorbance to 0.700 ± 0.020 at 734 nm. For the assay, 20 μL of extract was added to 1.98 mL of the ABTS working solution and incubated in the dark for 6 min. Absorbance was then recorded at 734 nm. Results were expressed as mg TE/g.

Color response of Hibiscus extracts to pH changes

The pH sensitivity of the extract was evaluated by adding 100 μL of the extract to 20 mL of buffer solutions with pH values ranging from 2.0 to 12.0 (at intervals of 1.0). The mixtures were gently stirred and allowed to equilibrate at room temperature (25 °C) for 10 min. The resulting color changes were visually recorded using a smartphone camera (iPhone 13 Pro, Apple Inc.) under standardized lighting conditions. Images were captured against a white background in a light-controlled environment to minimize external variability.

Hydrogel film preparation

Pectin-based hydrogel films (Table 1) were prepared by dissolving 2.00 g of pectin in 100 mL of distilled water under continuous magnetic stirring at 500 rpm for 12 h. Separately, a 2 % (w/v) chitosan solution was obtained by dispersing chitosan in 100 mL of 1.5 % (v/v) acetic acid and stirring under the same conditions. The pectin and chitosan solutions were then mixed at a 1:1 (v/v) ratio and homogenized using a high-shear homogenizer (Silverson Machine Ltd., Waterside, Chesham Bucks, UK) at 7000 rpm for 5 min. The pH of the resulting blend was adjusted to 1.5 using 35–36 % (w/w) hydrochloric acid and 0.1 N HCl while stirring continuously at 500 rpm at room temperature (25 °C). To prepare the control film (PEC–CH), 20 mL of the film-forming solution was transferred into sterile Petri dishes. For modified films, DES or DESext was added at a level corresponding to 100 % of the total polymer weight (i.e., 4 g DES for 4 g polymer mixture consisting of 2 g pectin +2 g chitosan). These mixtures were stirred at 500 rpm for 30 min at room temperature before casting. Subsequently, 20 mL aliquots of the DES- and DESext-modified film-forming solutions were poured into Petri dishes and labelled as PEC–CH–DES and PEC–CH–DESext, respectively. All films were dried and conditioned in a temperature- and humidity-controlled chamber (TK 120, Nüve, Ankara, Türkiye) at 25 °C and 50 % relative humidity for at least 48 h prior to further analysis. In this section, it should be noted that with the use of high concentrations of DES beyond the standard ratios (30–50 % w/w), films produced at concentrations below 100 % w/w did not exhibit suitable flexibility properties. However, this is an advantage rather than a disadvantage in the current study because it allows for the loading of large amounts of bioactive compounds into the film system. A similar phenomenon was observed in the study of Mercadal et al. (2024).

Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) of hydrogel films

FTIR spectra of films were recorded using attenuated total reflection (ATR) mode within the spectral range of 400–4000 cm−1. Measurements were conducted under ambient conditions with an FTIR spectrometer (PerkinElmer Spectrum Two, USA). Each spectrum was acquired with 32 scans at a resolution of 4 cm−1. The air background spectrum was subtracted from the sample spectra using the instrument’s software.

X-ray diffractometer (XRD) of hydrogel films

The crystallinity of the samples was evaluated using an X-ray diffractometer (Rigaku D/Max-2200, Japan) equipped with a Cu Kα radiation source (λ = 1.5418 Å) and an Eiger 2R_500K detector operating in 0D mode. The instrument was set to 40 kV and 30 mA, with data collected at a scanning speed of 1°/min. Crystallinity percentage was determined by calculating the ratio of the crystalline area to the total area within the 2θ range of 4°– 50°, using the SmartLab Studio II software (Japan).

Thermal analysis of hydrogel films

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was performed using a Setaram Labsys Evo Thermal Analysis System (Caluire, France). The samples were heated from 40 to 600 °C at a rate of 10 °C/min under a continuous flow of dry nitrogen to prevent oxidative degradation.

Field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM) of hydrogel films

FESEM was performed using a Quanta FEG 450 (FEI, USA) to examine morphological changes in the film samples. Prior to imaging, the samples were coated with a gold/palladium layer to enhance conductivity. Observations were conducted at an accelerating voltage of 15 kV, with magnifications of 250× and 1000×.

Profilometer of hydrogel films

The surface roughness (Ra) of the films was evaluated using a 3D and 2D surface profilometer (KLA Tencor-P6, USA). Film samples were cut into 1 cm × 1 cm squares using a precision razor blade and mounted uniformly on polystyrene Petri dishes to ensure flatness. Measurements were conducted at a scanning speed of 1 mm/s across five randomly selected regions per sample. A scan area of 0.5 mm × 0.5 mm was analyzed with a vertical resolution of 1 nm. The scanned surface profile was mapped onto a charge-coupled device (CCD) array, generating high-resolution 3D topographical images. The arithmetic mean surface roughness (Ra) was calculated from the height distribution after plane correction to eliminate geometric deviations.

Thickness and optical properties of hydrogel films

Film thickness was measured using a digital micrometer (Dasqua, Italy) with an accuracy of ±0.001 mm. Measurements were taken at six randomly selected locations on each film sample, and the mean value was recorded. Color parameters, including L∗ (lightness), a∗ (red–green axis), and b∗ (yellow–blue axis), were determined using a colorimeter (CR-400, Konica Minolta, Japan). Measurements were performed in triplicate, and average values were reported in the CIE Lab color space. Antioxidant capacity of hydrogel films

The antioxidant capacity of the films was assessed using ABTS and DPPH radical scavenging assays, with modifications based on the protocol of Alasalvar et al. (2024). For sample preparation, 25 mg of each film was dispersed in 10 mL of distilled water and subjected to ultrasonic extraction for 30 min at room temperature. Statistical analysis

The experiments were conducted minimally in triplicate. Statistical analyses were performed using the SPSS software package (version 24.0, IBM Corp., Chicago, IL, USA). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied to assess significant differences among the film samples, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons. All results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Differences were considered statistically significant at a 95 % confidence level.

Results and discussion

Characterization of Hibiscus extract

As shown in Fig. 1A, the total phenolic content (TPC), total anthocyanin content (TAC), and antioxidant capacities (ABTS and DPPH assays) of the H. sabdariffa-based DES extract (DESext) were found to be 18.65 mg GAE/g, 3.64 mg C3G/g, 34.53 mg TE/g, and 18.32 mg TE/g, respectively. These values confirm the presence of bioactive compounds—particularly phenolics and anthocyanins—known for their potent antioxidant properties. Comparative findings from previous research further support these results. For example, Kurtulbaş et al. (2022) reported TPC and TAC values of 31.90 mg GAE/g and 2.96 mg C3G/g, respectively, for H. sabdariffa extracts obtained using citric acid/ethylene glycol-based DES. Similarly, Alañón et al. (2020) demonstrated the effectiveness of various DESs in extracting bioactives from H. sabdariffa, with TPC values ranging from 11.50 to 28.56 mg GAE/g and TAC values between 1.92 and 3.44 mg C3G/g. The findings of the present study fall within these reported ranges, reinforcing the efficiency of DES-mediated extraction for recovering phenolic and anthocyanin compounds.

Moreover, the high antioxidant activity of DESext, as evidenced by its ABTS (34.53 mg TE/g) and DPPH (18.32 mg TE/g) values, indicates strong radical scavenging potential. Given that antioxidant capacity is closely correlated with the concentration and structural composition of phenolic and anthocyanin compounds (Sadeer et al., 2020), the observed activity likely stems from the synergistic action of these bioactives. These results underscore the functional potential of DES-based extraction in developing nutraceutical ingredients and active materials for food packaging applications.

The pH-responsive behavior of DESext is presented in Fig. 1B, demonstrating a vivid color transition from red in acidic environments to yellow in alkaline conditions across a pH range of 2–12. This reversible color shift is attributed to structural transformations of anthocyanins under varying pH, reflecting changes in their ionic forms. At low pH (2–3), anthocyanins predominantly exist as flavylium cations, resulting in a red hue. As the pH increases to neutral and mildly alkaline conditions (pH 4–7), they shift into their quinoidal base form, producing a purple color. Under strongly alkaline conditions (pH > 10), anthocyanins convert into chalcone structures, resulting in a yellow coloration (Pang et al., 2023; Alasalvar et al., 2024).

These observations are consistent with previous reports describing similar pH-induced chromatic transitions in H. sabdariffa extracts (Fallah et al., 2025; Khezerlou et al., 2023). The pronounced and stable color variations across a broad pH range highlight the potential of DESext as a natural, pH-sensitive indicator for intelligent food packaging, real-time spoilage detection, and smart film applications.

FTIR of Hibiscus extracts and hydrogel films

As shown in Fig. 2A, the PEC–CH film exhibited characteristic FTIR bands corresponding to both pectin and chitosan, confirming their intermolecular interactions. The broad O–H/N–H stretching band (∼3400 cm−1) indicated extensive hydrogen bonding among hydroxyl and carboxyl groups of pectin and hydroxyl and amino groups of chitosan. The C=O stretching band near ∼1740 cm−1 corresponded to esterified galacturonic acid and protonated carboxyl (-COOH) groups in pectin (Chen et al., 2024; Kurek et al., 2021). Ionized carboxylate groups produced bands between 1600 and 1640 cm−1, overlapping with the N–H bending vibration of chitosan (∼1590–1630 cm−1), representing –NH2 scissoring (Khezerlou et al., 2023). Shifts and intensity changes in these peaks indicated electrostatic and hydrogen-bonding interactions between pectin and chitosan rather than covalent bond formation. The emergence of amide II (∼1540 cm−1) and amide III (∼1400 cm−1) bands reflected the presence of protonated amines and carboxylates within a polyelectrolyte complex stabilized by ionic attraction between chitosan’s –NH3+ and pectin’s –COO- groups.

At the casting pH of ∼1.5, the primary amine groups of chitosan (pKa ∼6.3) were fully protonated to –NH3+, whereas most carboxyl groups of pectin (pKa ∼3.5) existed in their protonated form (–COOH), with a small fraction ionized (–COO-). Therefore, the band near 1740 cm−1 is attributed mainly to protonated –COOH vibrations, while the region around 1600–1630 cm−1 results from overlapping contributions of partially deprotonated –COO- and N–H bending of –NH3+ groups. This coexistence of protonated and ionized species under acidic conditions supports the formation of hydrogen-bonded and electrostatically associated polymer networks.

Upon DES incorporation (PEC–CH–DES), distinct spectral changes were observed in the 3200–3400 cm−1 region. The broadening and intensity decrease suggested that part of the interpolymer hydrogen-bonding network was replaced by new hydrogen bonds between DES components (–OH-rich) and the polymers. The slight shift of the 1740 cm−1 band indicated that DES altered the hydrogen-bonding environment of pectin’s carboxyl groups (Chen et al., 2024). Broadening and intensity loss in the 1600–1630 cm−1 range suggested partial shielding of ionic interactions between –COO- and –NH3+ groups due to DES-induced changes in protonation equilibrium. The fingerprint region (1000–1150 cm−1) remained mostly unchanged, except for decreased intensity near 1010 cm−1, indicating possible DES–polymer interactions influencing glycosidic linkages (de Souza et al., 2024).

When H. sabdariffa extract was incorporated (PEC–CH–DESext), further modifications occurred, especially in the O–H stretching region, where the peak broadened and shifted to lower wavenumbers, signifying a denser hydrogen-bonding network. Compounds such as anthocyanins, flavonoids, and organic acids introduced additional –OH, –C=O, and –COOH/–COO- groups, all capable of participating in hydrogen bonding with pectin and chitosan. Anthocyanins increased absorbance in the 1500–1600 cm−1 region, corresponding to aromatic C=C vibrations, while organic acids contributed additional COO− bands enhancing ionic interactions with protonated –NH3+ groups of chitosan (Fallah et al., 2025).

At this acidic pH, anthocyanins exist predominantly as flavylium cations (Ar–C+), which are electrophilic and positively charged. These species can interact electrostatically with deprotonated –COO- groups of pectin or form hydrogen bonds with carbonyl and hydroxyl groups, thereby stabilizing the polymer network (Escobar-Ortiz et al., 2021). However, they are not nucleophilic enough to form covalent amide bonds with chitosan. Thus, the observed spectral changes in the amide II (∼1540 cm−1) and symmetric COO− (∼1390 cm−1) regions primarily reflect non-covalent hydrogen-bonding and electrostatic associations, rather than true amide linkage formation.

Overall, the combined incorporation of DES and H. sabdariffa extract modified the hydrogen-bonding and electrostatic interactions within the PEC–CH matrix. DES acted as a plasticizer and interaction modulator, while anthocyanin-rich extract functioned as a secondary hydrogen-bonding and electrostatic cross-linker, enhancing structural cohesion and stability without inducing covalent bond formation (Fig. 2B).

XRD of hydrogel films

XRD patterns of all hydrogel films (Fig. 2C) exhibited a broad amorphous halo centered around 20–22° (2θ), characteristic of non-crystalline polysaccharide-based matrices such as pectin and chitosan (Grala et al., 2022).

The absence of sharp diffraction peaks confirms the dominantly amorphous structure of the films, indicating disordered molecular packing and limited long-range periodicity. Upon incorporation of DES (PEC–CH–DES), the halo became slightly broader and shifted marginally toward lower angles. Such halo broadening and leftward displacement are generally attributed to increased intermolecular spacing and enhanced chain mobility due to plasticization, rather than to the formation of true crystalline domains (Wei et al., 2024). These changes reflect modulation of short-range order and weaker inter-chain hydrogen bonding within the pectin–chitosan matrix.

The film containing Hibiscus sabdariffa extract (PEC–CH–DESext) exhibited a minor increase in halo intensity and a small feature near ∼12° (2θ). This subtle feature may correspond to localized molecular ordering of certain extract components (e.g., phenolic or organic acid molecules) rather than a distinct crystalline phase (Ahirwar et al., 2024). Such ordering likely arises from π–π stacking or partial alignment of aromatic structures during film drying (Thakur et al., 2022). The increased hydrogen bonding and amorphous rearrangement observed by FTIR and XRD suggest internal densification of the polymer matrix, which aligns with the reduced water vapor permeability and supports the overall compactness at the bulk level.

Therefore, instead of quantifying crystallinity in percentage terms, the observed variations should be interpreted as relative changes in amorphous packing density and short-range molecular organization. Overall, the incorporation of DES and H. sabdariffa extract altered the amorphous arrangement of the pectin–chitosan network, improving molecular flexibility and confirming the films’ suitability for flexible smart packaging applications.

Thermal analysis of hydrogel films

As seen in Fig. 2D, PEC–CH film exhibited the highest initial thermal stability, with degradation occurring in a two-step pattern: an initial weight loss attributed to moisture evaporation (below 150 °C) and a subsequent major degradation phase between 200 °C and 400 °C, corresponding to the breakdown of polysaccharide chains.

Incorporation of DES in PEC–CH–DES reduced the thermal stability of the film by shifting the onset of major degradation to lower temperatures. This behavior is attributed to the plasticizing effect of DES, which disrupts intermolecular hydrogen bonding, enhances chain mobility, and lowers the energy required for thermal decomposition (Wei et al., 2024). The addition of H. sabdariffa extract (PEC–CH–DESext) slightly improved the thermal resistance compared to the DES-only film. The DESext film showed a higher residual mass and a delayed decomposition onset, indicating enhanced stability. This improvement may be attributed to non-covalent interactions between extract constituents and the polymer matrix, which likely contribute to enhanced thermal stability rather than direct structural reinforcement. Although certain polyphenols degrade at relatively low temperatures (Xu et al., 2023), their molecular interactions with biopolymers may contribute to structural resistance during the main decomposition phase.

Morphological properties of hydrogel films

The FESEM analysis of the pectin–chitosan films provide critical insights into the impact of DES plasticization and the incorporation of DESext on surface morphology. As seen in Fig. 3A, the PEC–CH film exhibited a relatively smooth and homogeneous surface, indicative of good polymer compatibility and uniform film formation. Upon the addition of DES (PEC–CH–DES), a more compact and continuous surface structure was observed, suggesting enhanced molecular interactions between the polymeric chains and the eutectic solvent. This structural modification likely contributed to improved flexibility and mechanical integrity (Yu et al., 2023). Besides, the high magnification FESEM images of all formulations appeared largely similar. Specifically, the PEC–CH–DESext films showed increased surface roughness and minor irregularities, likely due to the presence of diverse chemical compounds in the extract. These constituents may interact with the polysaccharide matrix and induce localized aggregation or phase separation (Zhang et al., 2023). Despite the compact bulk network, the profilometer and FESEM data revealed increased surface roughness, which likely arises from microphase separation or the migration of DES and bioactives toward the air interface during drying. This surface-level heterogeneity does not contradict the overall compactness but rather reflects surface relaxation effects commonly observed in plasticized biopolymer systems. While such morphological changes might influence mechanical strength, they may also enhance the film’s potential for bioactive retention and release, making the DESext films particularly promising for functional food packaging applications. Overall, these microstructural observations align with profilometric findings, indicating that while bulk compactness is retained, surface-level relaxation and roughness are locally enhanced by DES and extract incorporation.

Profilometer of hydrogel films

The surface roughness analysis of pectin-chitosan films, revealed significant structural variations, highlighting the influence of plasticization and bioactive incorporation on surface morphology. As seen in Fig. 3B and Table 2, the PEC-CH film exhibited a relatively smooth and homogeneous surface, as indicated by its lower Ra (mean roughness), Rz (maximum height difference), and Rt (total roughness depth) values. This smooth texture suggests a well-integrated polymer matrix, where strong electrostatic interactions between pectin and chitosan contribute to a uniform film structure with minimal phase separation. However, PEC-CH-DES film led to a notable increase in surface roughness. The higher Ra and Rz values indicate that the plasticizer disrupts the polymeric network, increasing molecular mobility and leading to localized aggregation and surface heterogeneity. The increase in skewness (Rsk) suggests the formation of more pronounced surface peaks, while the higher kurtosis (Rku) values indicate a surface with sharper features, possibly due to phase rearrangements induced by DES (de Oliveira et al., 2020).

The PEC-CH-DESext film, which includes H. sabdariffa extract along with DES, exhibited the highest surface roughness among the three formulations. The chemical compounds in Hibiscus extract, contribute to increased structural heterogeneity by introducing additional interactions within the film matrix. The elevated roughness parameters suggested that the extract components induce phase separation, leading to a more irregular surface. This increase in roughness may be beneficial for applications requiring enhanced surface interactions, such as bioactive coatings or antimicrobial packaging, where a rougher texture can improve adhesion and active compound release.

Profilometry analysis revealed that both plasticization and bioactive incorporation markedly influenced the surface characteristics of PEC–CH films. The addition of DES led to increased surface roughness, likely due to disruption of the polymeric network and reduced molecular packing density. Furthermore, incorporation of H. sabdariffa extract intensified this effect, potentially as a result of additional molecular interactions and localized phase separation between the extract constituents and the polysaccharide matrix.

Moisture content, swelling capacity, water solubility, and water vapor permeability of hydrogel films

As illustrated in Fig. 4A, the addition of DES and DESext notably influenced the moisture content (MC) of the PEC–CH films. The control PEC–CH film exhibited an MC of 14.18 %. Incorporation of DES led to a significant (p < 0.05) increase in MC, which can be attributed to the hydrophilic nature of choline chloride–lactic acid (ChCl–LA) and its capacity to retain water molecules through hydrogen bonding (Y. Liu et al., 2025). These findings align with previous results reported by Wei et al. (2024), who observed increased MC in zein-based films plasticized with various DESs, including ChCl–citric acid, ChCl–LA, ChCl–urea, and ChCl–ethylene glycol. Conversely, films containing DESext exhibited a relative reduction in MC compared to those with pure DES. This effect is likely due to the presence of chemical compounds in the H. sabdariffa extract, which may interact with the polymer matrix and limit free water uptake by forming additional cross-links or occupying hydrophilic sites. A similar trend was reported by Zhao et al. (2022), who found that DESext derived from Rosa roxburghii Tratt leaves decreased the MC of chitosan/zein films when compared to films containing DES alone.

SC signifies the water resistance of the films. Films with low SC provide better protection against moisture, while those with higher SC are less effective in resisting water. As indicated in Fig. 4B, PEC-CH (2978.77 %) film displayed a higher SC than PEC-CH-DES (362.37 %) and PEC-CH-DESext (415.63 %). This suggests that the water absorption capacity reduced when DES and DESext were used as plasticizers. At pH below 2, amine groups of CH and carboxyl groups of PEC are fully protonated. This makes CH positively charged while reducing the negative charge of PEC, thus weakening the electrostatic interactions between PEC and CH. Under these conditions, CH and PEC interact primarily through hydrogen bonding (Tsirtsidou et al., 2025). In the present study, the SC of the PEC-CH film was higher because of the uptake of more water molecules among the polymer chains. On the other hand, a lower SC in the other films could be attributed to the interactions between the PEC-CH system and ChCl-LA, Similarly, Mercadal et al. (2024) reported that soy protein isolate films containing DES have lower SC than pristine films. According to Rolińska et al. (2024), a decrease in the SC using DES in the CH films was linked to the formation of more compact structures in the film system. Zhou et al. (2024) also reported that hydrogen bonding interactions between the DES and the polymer matrix could impede water penetration into the film. In the present study, the decrease in SC was due to changes in hydrogen bond interactions between the DES and the polymer matrix, as indicated in FTIR analysis, and not a result of the formation of a compact structure. Overall, the lower SC in the films with DES and DESext compared to control film indicated that the films prepared using DES and DESext would remain more stable when applied to foods with high moisture content.

Water solubility (WS) reflects the film’s ability to resist disintegration and maintain structural integrity upon exposure to aqueous environments. The WS of the films displayed a different behavior in their SC. In other words, the WS increased while SC decreased with the use of DES and DESext. The WS of the control film was 37.27 %, which was lower than the films with DES (53.93 %) and DESext (54.92 %) (Fig. 4C). This increment can be related to the hydrophilic nature of DES. The increase in WS can be due to the increased diffusion of this solvent into the medium rather than the solubility of PEC and CH. Additionally, there was no significant (p > 0.05) variation between PEC-CH-DES and PEC-CH-DESext.

As shown in Fig. 4D, the incorporation of DES and DESext into the PEC–CH film matrix significantly (p < 0.05) increased the water vapor permeability (WVP) from 0.66 × 10−10 g/m2·s·Pa (control) to 1.94 × 10−10 and 1.89 × 10−10 g/m2·s·Pa, respectively. This phenomenon limits application of the current film formulations in scenarios where moisture protection is essential. The increase can be attributed to the plasticizing effect of ChCl–LA-based DESs, which reduce intermolecular interactions between polymer chains and enhance free volume and chain mobility, thereby facilitating water vapor diffusion (Grala et al., 2022). The reduction in WVP further supports that, despite localized roughness, the internal network became denser and less permeable due to improved intermolecular cohesion promoted by DES and extract components.

Overall, while the incorporation of DES and DESext improved certain water-related parameters such as reduced SC, it also resulted in increased WS and WVP. From a packaging perspective, such changes may present both advantages and limitations depending on the target application. In conventional moisture-barrier packaging systems, high solubility could compromise film integrity over time. However, the films developed in this study are intended for use in intelligent packaging applications, such as internal freshness indicators or short-term contact labels, where moderate solubility may facilitate the release of bioactive compounds, enhance visual pH responsiveness, and support biodegradation after use. Therefore, the functional performance of these films must be interpreted in the context of their designed smart packaging roles rather than as passive moisture barriers.

Optical properties of hydrogel films

The optical properties of the films are summarized in Fig. 5A–D. The addition of the DES to the film system did not cause any statistically significant (p > 0.05) changes in the L∗ (lightness), a∗ (redness), and b∗ (yellowness) values of the film. A similar observation was reported by Chen et al. (2024) for pectin films plasticized with ChCl-glycerol, -malic acid, -sucrose. However, using DESext decreased the lightness and yellowness while increasing the redness. These variations were due to the presence of chemical compounds, especially anthocyanins, in the DESext. These findings agree with those of Y. Liu et al. (2025) who reported that incorporating DESext rich in betalain from pitaya peel changed the color characteristics of sodium alginate-sodium carboxymethyl cellulose films. Meanwhile, the opacity of the films significantly (p < 0.05) increased after adding DES and DESext, indicating that the light-barrier performance of the films improved (Fig. 5E). When the performance of DES and DESext was compared, DESext presented a higher opacity value. This phenomenon was due to the presence of bioactive compounds in the DESext.

Thickness of hydrogel films

As shown in Fig. 5F, the film thickness ranged from 51.50 μm to 114.43 μm, indicating that the incorporation of DES and DESext significantly (p < 0.05) influenced the structural properties of the films. This trend is consistent with previous reports demonstrating that plasticizer addition can increase film thickness by modifying the polymer network and intermolecular spacing (Wei et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2024). An approximately twofold increase in film thickness was detected, corresponding to the added concentration of DES and DESext. However, there was no significant (p > 0.05) difference between the thicknesses of PEC-CH-DES and PEC-CH-DESext films. In contrast to these findings, several studies have reported an increase in film thickness with the use of DESext compared to DES (Chandra Roy et al., 2021; Zhao et al., 2022). The authors attributed these increases in thickness to the presence of bioactive compounds. In the present study, the statistically insignificant (p > 0.05) changes in film thickness with the inclusion of DESext could be related to the insufficient amount of DESext included to affect the thickness of the matrix. Yu et al. (2023) reported that variations in the thickness of chitosan films depended on the type and concentration of DESs. Besides, in a study by Alves et al. (2022), which examined the effectiveness of ChCl-LA as a DES and ChCl-LA extract containing green tea phenolics in chitosan film production, the use of DESext at the same plasticizer concentration (30 %) resulted in thinner films than DES. Hence, the observed variations in thickness can be attributed to the structural characteristics of the bioactive compounds, as well as the type and concentration of DES utilized.

Mechanical properties of hydrogel films

Mechanical properties such as TS, EB, and YM are crucial indicators of the structural integrity, flexibility, and applicability of food packaging films. The impacts of adding DES and DESext on the mechanical characteristics of pectin-chitosan composite hydrogel films are illustrated in Fig. 5G. The incorporation of DES and DESext notably influenced the mechanical properties of the PEC-CH films. Specifically, a significant (p < 0.05) reduction in TS values was observed with the addition of DES and DESext, while a concurrent increase in EB was recorded. This inverse relationship suggests that the presence of DES and DESext within the biopolymer matrix effectively interrupts polymer-polymer interactions, thereby enhancing molecular mobility and reducing intermolecular friction. Consequently, the rigidity of the film matrix decreases, leading to enhanced elasticity and flexibility. The decrease in the YM values of the film matched these findings. These observations are consistent with structural analyses (FTIR and XRD), which demonstrated a reduction in intermolecular hydrogen bonding and crystallinity due to DES integration. DES, particularly choline chloride-lactic acid (ChCl-LA), acts by intercalating between polysaccharide chains, reducing cohesive energy density, and subsequently increasing chain mobility. This molecular-level plasticization effect explains the observed improvement in the flexibility (EB) of the films.

The incorporation of H. sabdariffa extract in DESext further modulated the mechanical properties of the films. Bioactive polyphenols, particularly anthocyanins, likely formed additional hydrogen bonds and electrostatic interactions with the polysaccharide matrix, altering the internal structure. Although the TS decreased with DESext addition, the marked increase in EB reflects improved flexibility—an advantageous trade-off for food packaging applications, where enhanced elasticity facilitates handling, wrapping, and mechanical durability during storage and distribution.

The results presented here align closely with previous studies that explored the role of natural DES as multifunctional plasticizers. Similarly previous studies reported a reduction in TS and enhanced EB upon integration of DES and bioactive-enriched DES into biopolymer film matrices, emphasizing the common plasticizing mechanism attributable to DES-mediated polymer network modulation (Alasalvar et al., 2024; Lawal et al., 2024; Thakur et al., 2022).

Color response of hydrogel films to pH variations

The pH sensitivity of the PEC–CH–DESext film was evaluated by monitoring its color response in buffer solutions with pH values ranging from 2 to 12. As illustrated in Fig. 6A, the film exhibited both instrumental and visually perceptible color changes across the tested pH range. The ΔE values (where values greater than 5 indicate color differences) used to assess whether the color difference was perceptible to the human eye varied between 23.14 and 31.24, depending on the pH value. These variations are attributed to structural transformations of anthocyanin molecules, which undergo pH-dependent ionization, resulting in distinct chromatic shifts observable by the naked eye. At pH 2, the color was reddish. The reddish color of the film was still visible between pH 2 and 6. The redness and yellowness of the film decreased at pH 7 and 8. At pH 7–9, the film had a purplish color. After this pH range, the film exhibited a purplish-gray color. The color change of the film was different from that of DESext. A comparable result was found by Xiao et al. (2023), which was attributed to the interaction between anthocyanins and the film system. In the present study, a possible explanation for this phenomenon could be that the film was prepared at low pH. When the film is immersed in the buffer solution, the low pH of the film can create a more acidic environment within the film system compared to the pH of the surrounding buffer solution. The relationship between the color indicator properties of the films and the pH of the film preparation solution was reported by Xu et al. (2023).

Antioxidant capacity of hydrogel films

The ABTS and DPPH antioxidant capacities of the films are presented in Fig. 6B–C. All films showed inhibitory effects on ABTS and DPPH radicals. The antioxidant capacity of the PEC-CH film was attributed to the inherent antioxidative properties of its constituent polymers. The antioxidant capacity of PEC and CH films was also reported in previous studies (Kurek et al., 2021; B. Liu et al., 2023). DES incorporation did not affect the ABTS antioxidant capacity of the films, but it was improved using DESext. Similarly, Yu et al. (2023) reported a low antioxidant capacity of ChCl-LA as a DES in the chitosan films. On the other hand, in the DPPH antioxidant capacity analysis, both DES and DESext contributed to the antioxidant capacity of the films. The PEC–CH–DESext film exhibited the highest antioxidant activity, as determined by both ABTS and DPPH assays. These findings indicate that the incorporation of DESext was more effective than pure DES in enhancing the bioactive properties of the films, likely due to the synergistic contribution of phenolic compounds and anthocyanins present in the H. sabdariffa extract.

Conclusions

This study presents a promising strategy for the development of biodegradable and pH-responsive packaging films by integrating natural deep eutectic solvents and anthocyanin-rich H. sabdariffa extract into a pectin–chitosan hydrogel matrix, enabling functional performance in intelligent food packaging applications. The incorporation of DES notably enhanced the films’ mechanical properties, evidenced by increased elongation at break and reduced brittleness, though accompanied by a decrease in tensile strength and thermal stability. Hibiscus extract significantly (p < 0.05) improved the antioxidant capacities of films, achieving remarkable increases in DPPH and ABTS radical scavenging activities. FTIR analysis confirmed the formation of extensive hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions within the film matrix, which were influenced by the incorporation of DES and Hibiscus sabdariffa extract. Complementary XRD results indicated a reduction in film crystallinity, suggesting enhanced chain mobility and improved flexibility, both desirable for functional packaging applications. Furthermore, the developed films exhibited distinct and reversible color changes in response to pH variations, transitioning from red at acidic conditions (pH 2) to purple at neutral (pH 7) and yellow at alkaline environments (pH 10). The incorporation of DES and H. sabdariffa extract positioned the developed films as promising real-time indicators for monitoring food freshness and spoilage, offering a natural and practical alternative to synthetic colorimetric sensors. The observed increase in water vapor permeability indicates altered barrier behavior, potentially beneficial for applications requiring moisture exchange. While the incorporation of DESext contributed positively to bioactivity, flexibility and responsiveness, it also compromised the film’s ability to serve as an effective moisture barrier. To address the limitations identified in the current formulation, future research should explore the use of different deep eutectic solvents (DESs) within the pectin–chitosan film matrix to optimize functional performance without compromising barrier properties. Overall, this study highlights the potential of DES-plasticized, anthocyanin-enriched biopolymer films as intelligent packaging materials that advance food quality monitoring, safety assurance, and environmental sustainability.
