Eustachian valve endocarditis in the context of right atrial embryonic remnants: A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Luiza Nawrot, Marcin Szczepanik, Marcin Kuniewicz, Daniel Rams, Marta Baran, Grzegorz Karkowski, Maciej Stąpór, Barbara Gach-Kuniewicz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEustachian valve endocarditis (EVE) is a rare right-sided infective endocarditis (RSIE) manifestation. This condition has scattered and limited clinical characteristics that require collection and systematization. This meta-analysis evaluates the predisposing factors, pathogens, and associated risk profiles in EVE cases.MethodsThe meta-analysis included 68 cases of EVE from 57 reports published between 1986 and 2024 from PubMed, Embase, Scopus, Cochrane, and Web of Science. Data on demographics, clinical characteristics, risk factors, and microbiological findings were extracted and analyzed using descriptive and univariate statistical methods. Pooled prevalence rates from observational studies were calculated using a fixed effects model.ResultsThe pooled prevalence of EVE in RSIE among the analyzed cases was 2.54%. Single-valve infections accounted for 70.6% of cases, with the Eustachian valve as the sole affected structure. The most common pathogen was Staphylococcus aureus (60%), predominantly affecting younger intravenous drug users (IVDU) with an odds ratio [OR (odds ratio) = 6.27; 95% CI (confidence interval): 1.62–24.31] in univariate logistic regression analysis. Other predisposing factors, including central venous catheters (CVCs) and cardiac implantable electronic devices (CIED), were not significantly associated with Staphylococcus aureus or other bacterial non-Staphylococcus aureus or fungal infections.ConclusionsEustachian valve endocarditis is relatively rare and primarily associated with IVDU and younger age, whereas CVCs and CIEDs are insignificant predictors. Identifying embryonic remnants like the Eustachian valve or Chiari network using echocardiography may facilitate early diagnosis in at-risk populations.

Introduction

The Eustachian valve (EV), Thebesian valve (ThV), and Chiari network (CN) are remnants of the venous system that regress after embryonic circulation. However, some of these structures remain significantly reduced and may contribute to unsuspected cases of endocarditis [1]. The EV is located at the inferior vena cava (IVC) junction and the right atrium [2]. It is present in approximately one-fourth of individuals [3]. Embryologically, the EV directs the bloodstream from the IVC into the foramen ovale during fetal development [4]. After the foramen ovale closes, the valve becomes irrelevant and begins deteriorating. The remnants of the EV primarily form thin cristae or endocardial folds in a semilunar shape, extending from the anterior rim of the IVC. The lateral aspect of the fold may attach to the crista terminalis, while the medial element may connect to the ThV [2]. Sometimes, the remnant of the EV valve may take the form of a mobile, elongated structure extending several centimeters into the atrial chamber [5]. The incidence of infective endocarditis is estimated at 13.8 cases per 100,000 subjects per year, with right-sided infective endocarditis (RSIE) accounting for 5–10% of these cases [6–7]. Eustachian valve endocarditis, as an element of right atrial embryonic remnants (RAER), is rarer, with an estimated 0.13 new cases per 100,000 population per year, as reported by Cresti et al. in 2017 [8].

The frequency of RSIE may increase due to rising risk factors and could depend on the quality of medical care and social habits worldwide. The main predisposing factor for EVE is intravenous drug use (IVDU), and the most common causative agent is Staphylococcus aureus [9]. Pacemaker wires, history of rheumatic heart disease, immunodeficiency, and indwelling vascular lines/catheters/devices are essential causes as well [1, 3–8]. However, the primary factor influencing the infective endocarditis (IE) of the EV is its anatomy — specifically, its length and the presence of embryonic remnants. The frequency of RAER varies significantly. Autopsy research found the presence of EV in nearly 2/3 of the hearts, the CN in 15%, and an extended ThV in 10% [10–12]. The detection of CN is highly variable, depending on the method used. In open-heart studies, its presence is higher, varying between 4.6–28.9% [13, 14], whereas echocardiographic detection is much lower at 0.65% [15]. The length of the EV has also been analyzed in numerous studies, varying from a thin ridge up to an elongated structure, with an average of 4.9 ± 2.6 mm [14]. Additionally, EVs larger than 1 cm were present in 19.7% of patients undergoing ablation for typical atrial flutter, potentially hindering the achievement of a cavo-tricuspid isthmus block [16].

Since the first vegetation on an Eustachian valve was described in a postmortem study in 1986 and via transthoracic echocardiography in 1990, this study has specifically reviewed publications on EV with suspected endocarditis [17, 18]. Through a meta-analysis of the collected data, the aim was to characterize EV endocarditis, including the predisposing factors, pathogens, associated risk profiles, and general outcomes reported in the literature.

Methods

Search strategy, study selection

Through September 2023, literature searches were performed using the PUBMED, EMBASE, SCOPUS, Web of Science, and Cochrane databases to identify eligible articles. This review followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analysis (PRISMA) guidelines [19]. Studies eligible for meta-analysis were identified using the keywords/phrases Eustachian Valve, Endocarditis, and Right atrial endocarditis. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) studies reporting precise data that was easy to extract for further analysis, and (2) studies that were retrospective and observational, often presented as single case reports in the literature, conference abstracts, or poster sessions. The decision to include such publications was based on the specific region of inflammation and its rarity.

The following search terms were used: (“endocarditis” or “infective endocarditis”) and (“Eustachian valve” or “inferior vena cava valve”). Additionally, the search terms were adjusted to each database to optimize the quality of the research papers.

Exclusion criteria included letters to the editor, studies with incomplete and unclear data, animal studies, repeated records in databases, review articles, duplicated studies, and those unrelated to EVE. Two investigators independently evaluated all studies for inclusion. Any discrepancies arising during the assessment were resolved through a consensus among all the reviewers after consulting with the authors of the original research, when possible.

No date limits or language restrictions were applied. The references cited in the included articles were also thoroughly searched. The Critical Appraisal Tool for Anatomical Meta-analysis (CATAM) and Anatomical Quality Assessment Tool (AQUA) were used to provide the highest quality findings [20, 21]. Ethical approval was not required as all analyses were based on previously published data.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Eligible studies

Initially, 281 potentially relevant articles were identified according to the adopted search strategy. As illustrated in the flow diagram (Fig. 1), 176 papers were excluded in the first review after screening titles and abstracts, followed by the exclusion of 36 additional articles after a detailed evaluation. A total of 69 articles were thoroughly reviewed, considering timelines and originating centers with intermeshing records. Ultimately, 57 eligible cases fulfilled all inclusion criteria for EVE registry [1, 8, 17, 22–47, Suppl. ref.].

The overall data collection process can be found in Figure 1. The included studies were published between 1986 [17] and 2024 [Suppl. ref.].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using the SPSS 29.0 (Predictive Solutions, Pittsburgh, PA, USA). Continuous variables are expressed as mean and standard deviation (SD). The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to check the normality of the distribution for continuous variables. The U Mann-Whitney test was performed for nonparametric variables to assess the differences between the analyzed groups. A Chi-square test was conducted to evaluate the statistical significance of the association between two qualitative variables. Fisher’s exact test was used where expected counts were below n = 5. The Chi-square test and the I2 statistic were used to measure the level of heterogeneity among the included studies. For the Chi-square test, a Cochran’s Q p-value of < 0.10 indicated significant heterogeneity. All pooled estimates were displayed with a 95% confidence interval (CI). Two separate univariate logistic regression analyses were conducted to identify factors associated with Staphylococcus aureus infections and non-Staphylococcus aureus infections (excluding fungal infections). In these univariate analyses, the effect of each predictor was assessed independently. Logistic regression was performed using the maximum likelihood estimation method, and results were expressed as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CI.

In the first model, the dependent variable was the presence of Staphylococcus aureus, while in the second model, it was the presence of non-Staphylococcus aureus pathogens. Independent variables included intravenous drug use (IVDU), central venous catheters (CVC), cardiac implantable electronic devices (CIED), age, and sex. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Two cases with unidentified pathogens were excluded from the metanalysis [25, 44].

Results

A total of 68 patients (29 females, 42.6%) with a diagnosis of right-sided infective endocarditis (RSIE) involving the Eustachian valve were included in the analysis. The mean age of the patients was 49.06 years (18.32). The causes of RSIE were categorized into two groups: predisposing factors and blood cultures isolated pathogens. Predisposing factors included IVDU (22 cases), CVC (20 cases with hemodialysis, Hodgkin’s lymphoma, Lupus erythematosus), and the presence of CIED (9 cases). Additionally, 17 cases were associated with other nonadjustable factors, including critical care status, post-cardiac surgery of congenital heart defects, poorly controlled diabetes, Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease, actinomycosis, fungal endocarditis, and multiple abdominal surgeries. All patient characteristics are presented in Table 1.

The prevalence of EVE in RSIE was estimated from three publications (Román et al. 2001, Cresti et al. 2017, Román et al. 1994), identifying 10 cases from 394 patients. The pooled prevalence reached 2.54% (95% CI: 1.38–4.61%) (Fig. 2). Heterogeneity analysis showed I2 = 0%, Q = 0.77, and p = 0.682, indicating no significant variability between studies. Therefore, a fixed-effects model was applied, confirming the consistency of the studies and allowing for a single prevalence estimate.

Blood cultures primarily isolated bacterial pathogens. The most common were Staphylococcus aureus (n = 41, 60.3%), including MSSA (n = 14, 20.6%) and MRSA/VRSA (n = 10, 14.7%). Other bacteria identified included S. hominis (n = 2, 2.9%) [8, 27], S. epidermidis/MRSE (n = 2, 2.9%) [40, 41], E. coli (n = 3, 4.4%) [23, 29, 37], Streptococcus viridans (n = 3, 4.4%) [22, 28, 31], and Klebsiella pneumoniae (n = 2, 2.9%) [24, 26]. Additionally, several less frequently observed pathogens were detected, including single cases of infections caused by Actinomyces turicensis [30], Mycobacterium abscessus group [Suppl. ref.], Enterobacter cloacae [32], Streptococcus agalactiae [Suppl. ref.], Pasteurella multocida [36], and Staphylococcus pettenkoferi [47]. The diversity of microorganisms indicates diverse sources of infection and potential risks associated with different transmission routes.

In the analyzed data on fungal infections (n = 7, 10.3%), the dominant pathogen was Candida albicans; however, cases of infections caused by Candida tropicalis, Candida parapsilosis, and Cryptococcus neoformans were also recorded [Suppl. ref.].

In two cases, masses attached to the remnants were found to be of non-pathogenic origin (2.9%) [25, 44].

Eustachian valve endocarditis was revealed using TTE alone in 13 cases (19.12%), TTE followed by transesophageal echocardiography (TOE; according to European Association of Cardiovascular Imaging) in 21 cases (30.89%), and TOE alone in 29 cases (42.65%). In total, 50 cases (73.53%) were diagnosed with TOE.

The Eustachian valve was the sole affected structure in 48 (70.6%) cases. However, in 20 cases, it was affected alongside other structures, such as the tricuspid valve (n = 12), mitral valve (n = 6), bioprosthetic valves (n = 2), pacing leads (n = 2), a central venous catheter, and, occasionally, multiple structures simultaneously.

In the study group, 9 patients had dialysis catheters and were undergoing dialysis therapy. One patient had HIV, was an IVDU, and had a Staphylococcus aureus infection. Among the factors classified as individual cases, several unique conditions were identified. This included pneumonia found at autopsy [17], ethanol detoxification [22], mechanical aortic valve replacement combined with coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) [29], the presence of an intrauterine device (IUD) [30], and a history of ischemic heart disease [31]. Septic shock in the intensive care unit (ICU) was documented in two patients, while another had a past medical history of NG colon with ileostomy formation and deep vein thrombosis [Suppl. ref.]. Additional findings included a Roux-en-Y procedure [34], signs of acute kidney injury after a dog bite [36], hypertension with alcoholic cirrhosis [Suppl. ref.], and uncontrolled type 1 diabetes mellitus associated with bacteria in urine on admission [42], chronic bladder outlet obstruction, a distended bladder wall, and moderate bilateral hydronephrosis.

Furthermore, a history of multiple pulmonary emboli with antiphospholipid syndrome and bilateral pulmonary endarterectomy was noted [44], as well as Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease in a patient who had recently undergone total left knee arthroplasty [46]. In three cases, EVE led to sepsis. Two of them were lethal [17, Suppl. ref.].

Statistical analysis revealed that patients infected with Staphylococcus aureus were significantly younger compared to non-Staphylococcus aureus-infected individuals (44.50 (17.19) vs. 56.54 (16.65); p = 0.01), whereas those with infections caused by pathogens other than Staphylococcus aureus were significantly older than their counterparts [58.08 (18.27) vs. 45.41 (16.45); p = 0.01]. Individuals infected with Staphylococcus aureus were significantly more likely to be intravenous drug users (IVDU) compared to non-Staphylococcus aureus-infected individuals (χ2 = 8.13, p = 0.00), while those infected with non-Staphylococcus aureus subjects were significantly less likely to be IVDU (χ2 = 6.30; p = 0.01). The mean age of patients with IVDU was 39.72 (13.64) years, while the mean age of all others was 55.08 (17.3) years (p < 0.01). Young drug users with a Staphylococcus aureus infection were the predominant group in the meta-analysis, highlighting age as a significant factor in both regression models.

Univariate logistic regression analysis identified IVDU as the primary risk factor for Staphylococcus aureus infection with an odds ratio (OR) of 6.3 (95% CI: 1.62–24.31, p = 0.01), while age was the other significant factor; with each additional year, the odds of Staphylococcus aureus infection decreased (OR = 0.96; 95% CI: 0.93–0.99; p = 0.01). Similarly, the second univariate analysis for non-Staphylococcus aureus infections yielded statistically significant, inverse results: IVDU was associated with lower odds of infection (OR = 0.16, 95% CI: 0.03–0.76, p = 0.02) while increasing age was associated with higher odds (OR = 1.04, 95% CI: 1.01–1.08, p = 0.01).

Other variables, including CIED, age, CVC, and sex, did not significantly influence Staphylococcus aureus infection (Fig. 3, Tab. 2).

Univariate analysis of patients undergoing intensive chronic treatments with CVC or dialysis yielded a hazard ratio (HR) of 0.81 (95% CI: 0.27–2.40, p = 0.7) for Staphylococcus aureus and HR of 1.17 (95% CI: 0.37–3.74, p = 0.79) for other infections, indicating that CVC was not a statistically significant predictor of infection. Additionally, multivariate logistic regression analysis for the dependent variable CVC, with age and all pathogens as predictors, did not identify any significant predictors of RSIE. Neither age nor specific pathogens had a statistically significant impact on CVC-associated infections.

Discussion

The meta-analysis of right-sided infective endocarditis (RSIE) involving the Eustachian valve revealed that it is relatively low, estimated to have a pooled prevalence of 2.54%. That is why most of the cases are described as case reports. This analysis brings interesting conclusions and facts based on the most up-to-date EVE. 70.6% of cases were single-valve infections, emphasizing the Eustachian valve’s isolated involvement. Among predisposing factors, intravenous drug use (IVDU) was strongly associated with Staphylococcus aureus infections, showing a significant relation (χ2 = 8.13, p = 0.00) compared to non-Staphylococcus aureus infected individuals without IVDU history (χ2 = 6.30; p = 0.01). The Univariate analysis confirmed IVDU as the primary risk factor for Staphylococcus aureus, with an OR of 6.30. Conversely, central venous catheters or implantable cardiac devices did not significantly predict Staphylococcus aureus infections, suggesting no correlation between other known risk factors for RSIE in this cohort.

The most frequently isolated pathogen was Staphylococcus aureus (60%), including methicillin-sensitive (MSSA) and resistant strains (MRSA/VRSA). Fungal infections, primarily caused by Candida spp., accounted for 10.3% of cases. Age was inversely associated with Staphylococcus aureus infections, with younger IVDU patients being predominantly affected. Possible explanations for this were infections due to direct skin inoculation, bacterial exposure, and frequent venous trauma, increasing susceptibility to Staphylococcus aureus bacteremia [48].

The diversity of pathogens, including uncommon bacteria and fungi, underscores the varied etiologies of RSIE. Moreover, non-adjustable risk factors such as critical care, cardiac surgery, or comorbidities contributed to infection risk. Unfortunately, some reported cases were fatal, with several described as post-mortem studies [17, Suppl. ref.]. Is it necessary to search for these remnants in every patient? The titles reflect researchers’ perplexity regarding endocarditis on the Eustachian valve: “a rare consequence” [35], “rare case” [27, 30, Suppl. ref.], “unusual case” [24, Suppl. ref.], “think twice, look twice” [29], “forgotten valve” [43], “gone, but not forgotten” [45], “beware the eustachian valve” [39] and lastly, “hide and seek“ [38]. Interestingly, publication trends indicate increasing research interest in RSIE after 2010, reflecting advances in understanding its pathophysiology and risk factors from one side and technological improvement in the TOE probe resolution, significantly improving detectability of the remnants and signs of endocarditis.

The “sine qua non” condition for developing vegetation on the EV is its extensive presence. While this may seem trivial, embryological remnants play a crucial role in this context.

What constitutes RAER remains a subject of debate — is just an enlarged Eustachian valve alone sufficient for diagnosis, or is the presence of a Chiari network also necessary?

The prevalence of right atrial embryological remnants has been analyzed in various studies. Still, there is significant variability depending on the type of publication — whether post-mortem in adults, echocardiographic studies, or analyses conducted on pediatric populations [12, 14, 16]. The reason for that might be a technological gap in echocardiographic resolution. There is a large variability in EV shape, size, thickness, length, and extent (Fig. 4A). There are cases of complete closure of the inferior vena cava in the right atrium, resulting in massive thrombosis [49]. Nevertheless, the most typical, so-called remnant in the RA is the Chiari network (Fig. 4B) [16].

While the detection of an EV is frequent during echocardiographic analysis, the study of the Thebesian valve remains extremely rare and largely overlooked. The presence and extent of the Chiari network, which can span between these structures, is also highly variable and difficult to detect [12, 13].

Conclusions

The atrial embryological remnants may predispose patients to infective endocarditis in patients with predisposing factors, such as implantable devices, central venous catheters, and IVDU.

Although EVE is rare, and vegetation on the Eustachian valve is uncommon, these structures nowadays are easily detected using TOE due to their high resolution, and this approach, not TTE, is recommended. Identifying RAER in high-risk patients may improve diagnosis and treatment outcomes.

Limitations

Due to the retrospective nature of the analysis, small sample size, data from case reports, and multiple infectious agents, the study did not assess potential interactions between individual factors, which may limit the findings’ generalizability and reduce the analysis’ statistical power. Furthermore, analysis of studies in different eras of technological possibilities affect the final results. The lack of data on treatment options and sources of infection limits a comprehensive understanding of factors influencing the results.
