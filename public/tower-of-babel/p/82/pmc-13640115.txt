Estimating stage-specific lung cancer costs for economic evaluation of LDCT screening in Ireland: a patient-level microsimulation
Authors: Tatiana Bezdenezhnykh, James F. O’Mahony, Jarushka Naidoo, Koen de Nijs, Deirdre Murray, Daniel Ryan, Alan Smith, Seamus Cotter, Kathleen Bennett, Benjamin M. Jacob, Patrick Redmond
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLung cancer imposes a significant clinical and economic burden in Ireland, with low five-year survival rates and no national low-dose computed tomography (LDCT) screening. Stage-specific cost estimates are essential for economic evaluation but are lacking, particularly as novel, high-cost therapies shift treatment patterns. This study estimates stage-stratified lung cancer costs over five years from the Irish public payer perspective.MethodsA patient-level microsimulation model was developed using data from the National Cancer Registry Ireland, Health Pricing Office tariffs, and published literature. Individual treatment pathways and lines of care were modelled by stage. Progression-free survival was estimated parametrically. Costs (2024 euro) were assigned monthly and summarized as mean cost per patient. Uncertainty was assessed via probabilistic and one-way sensitivity and scenario analyses.ResultsFive-year costs per surviving patient increased with stage, ranging from €38,149 (Stage I) to €231,723 (Stage IV). Year 1 represented the highest cost component, peaking at €62,869 for Stage IV. While costs declined after Year 1 in early-stage disease, they remained high in Stage IV (~€28,489 in Year 5) due to ongoing systemic therapy. Sensitivity analyses identified immunotherapy prices as the primary cost driver in advanced stages, while surgery costs were most influential in Stages I–II. Annual lung cancer costs in Ireland were estimated at ~€120 million.ConclusionsLung cancer costs in Ireland are heavily stage-dependent. The cost differential between early and advanced disease supports the economic rationale for LDCT screening to facilitate stage shift. These findings provide critical inputs for evaluating a national risk-based screening programme.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-026-15102-z.

Background

Countries are increasingly piloting and implementing low-dose computed tomography (LDCT) lung cancer screening (LCS) programmes. Global adoption is advancing rapidly, with programmes operational or in development across North America, East Asia, and Europe [1, 2]. Within Europe, the EU Council’s 2022 update to its cancer screening recommendation for the first time included LDCT screening for current and former heavy smokers [3], and the Europe’s Beating Cancer Plan [4] has accelerated implementation across member states. However, detailed stage-specific cost data remain scarce in many European settings, hindering the reliability of economic evaluations. This study aims to provide evidence on stage-specific costs of lung cancer treatment to support economic evaluation of the national LCS programme in Ireland.

Costs by stage at diagnosis are essential inputs for informing the design and evaluation of preventive health programmes, including population-based cancer screening. In lung cancer, screening by LDCT primarily creates value through stage shift: increasing detection at earlier, potentially curable stages and reducing diagnoses at later stages where treatment is more prolonged, more resource-intensive, and increasingly dominated by high-cost systemic therapies [5, 6]. Credible stage-specific cost estimates are therefore a prerequisite for reliable cost-effectiveness analysis of screening programmes.

There is a small but growing body of European literature on lung cancer costs. A detailed patient-level costing analysis from Catalonia (Spain) reported hospital diagnosis and treatment costs by stage at diagnosis [7]. An administrative-database comparison across France, England, and Germany demonstrated higher costs among patients with metastatic disease [8]. An English study reported broad population-level cost estimates using registry data [9, 10]. However, these studies largely predate the widespread adoption of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) and tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs), which have fundamentally altered treatment pathways and associated costs [11].

Recent changes to treatment regimens have important implications for costings. From around 2017 onwards, widespread adoption of ICIs and TKIs substantially altered lung cancer management, reducing the relevance of older stage-cost estimates. Recent European real-world studies confirm rising expenditures but provide limited stage-specific detail. In Finland (2014–2019), register-based analyses showed steady cost growth for both non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and small cell lung cancer (SCLC), driven mainly by higher medicine costs and an increased proportion of patients receiving active treatment [12]. A large French claims study (2013–2021) similarly reported substantial increases in total spending for metastatic lung cancer, with rising drug acquisition costs largely offset by reduced hospitalisation, resulting in stable mean monthly costs per patient [13]. In Belgium, registry-based analyses have shown that lung cancer is among the highest-cost cancers, with expenditures concentrated in the initial phase of care and driven largely by hospital care and systemic therapies, particularly in advanced disease [14], while an economic evaluation of LDCT screening incorporating a cost analysis similarly highlights that stage shift toward earlier-stage diagnosis may offset the high treatment costs associated with late-stage disease [15].

The most comprehensive recent European real-world analysis is a nationwide Dutch study from 2024. This linked tumour-level registry information to population-wide medical expenditures (2012–2021) and reported costs by stage, histology, and first-line modality, highlighting rapidly increasing expenditures for late-stage disease in the era of treatment innovation [11, 16]. Using these inputs, the MISCAN-Lung microsimulation model found that screening became more cost-effective as it avoids increasingly expensive late-stage disease.

To overcome the limitations of incomplete longitudinal real-world utilisation data, several studies have turned to modelling approaches that synthesise local clinical evidence, treatment algorithms, and unit costs to generate internally consistent cost estimates. This is particularly relevant given that the recording and availability of utilisation and medical cost data are often subject to substantial time lags, resulting in delayed estimates of the true cost of treatment. A notable European example is an Italian study that used a guideline-based ‘whole-disease’ model to estimate direct NSCLC costs by stage for the first year following diagnosis, illustrating both the feasibility of stage-stratified costing and the dependence of results on pathway assumptions and local prices [17]. Simulation work such as the Australian txSim modelling study demonstrates how discrete-event modelling can keep pace with evolving standards of care by combining trial efficacy, real-world patient mix, and current reimbursement costs to estimate outcomes and costs under contemporary treatment algorithms [18].

Our study is directly motivated by this logic but adopts a wider, screening-relevant scope: rather than modelling systemic therapy alone in advanced disease, we simulate stage-stratified patient pathways from diagnosis across multiple treatment modalities and lines of care in accordance with current Irish practice, enabling estimation of five-year costs by stage and providing inputs aligned to economic evaluations of LDCT screening and budget impact estimates.

In Ireland, the need for up-to-date cost estimates is particularly pressing. Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer death, with approximately 2,600 new cases and 1,800 deaths annually [19]. Over 60% of cases are diagnosed at Stage III or IV, and a national LCS programme is under active consideration, with the Lung Health Check Pilot launched in Dublin in May 2025 under the EU4Health-funded SOLACE project [20].

In this study, we develop a lung cancer cost microsimulation model tailored to the Irish context, designed to generate stage- and pathway-specific cost estimates suitable for informing economic evaluations of LCS and other policy interventions.

Methods

Model overview and data sources

We developed a patient-level microsimulation model that mirrors real-world patient journeys from diagnosis through subsequent treatment lines, using Irish registry data. The model simulates a hypothetical cohort of 15 000 incident lung cancer patients from diagnosis through a five-year (60 month) time horizon using a monthly cycle length. Costs were estimated from the perspective of the Irish public healthcare payer (Health Service Executive) and expressed in 2024 euro. Mean costs were calculated per month for (i) all patients alive in that month and (ii) the total diagnosed cohort, thereby using two alternative denominators, and later aggregated to annual costs. Costs were not discounted because the study estimates annual and cumulative treatment costs over a five-year period. This approach is consistent with budget-impact-style reporting, where undiscounted annual costs are generally preferred to reflect the expected financial impact on the payer over time. Future economic evaluations using these cost inputs should apply the discount rate appropriate to the decision context.

Data inputs were drawn from four main sources: (i) National Cancer Registry Ireland (NCRI) data (2019–2022) for stage distribution, histology, and first-line treatment patterns; (ii) published clinical studies and trials for survival, with individual patient data reconstructed from Kaplan–Meier curves; (iii) Irish cost data, including Health Pricing Office (HPO) diagnosis-related group (DRG) tariffs for hospital services and Primary Care Reimbursement Service (PCRS) reimbursement prices for systemic therapies; and (iv) clinical expert input, used to inform treatment sequencing beyond first-line and validate model assumptions. A large language model (ChatGPT) was used during model development to assist with debugging and resolving coding issues. The authors retained full responsibility for the model design, implementation, and validation, and all outputs were independently verified. The model was implemented in R, and all figures were generated using RStudio.

Patient population and initial staging

Patients enter the model at diagnosis and are assigned a stage based on national distributions reported by NCRI for Ireland in 2022: for NSCLC, Stage I (24%), Stage II (7%), Stage IIIa (14%), Stage IIIb (7%), Stage IV (40%), and unknown stage (7%); for SCLC: Stage I (3%), Stage II (4%), Stage IIIa (13%), Stage IIIb (12%), Stage IV (62%), and unknown stage (5%)1. Unknown-stage patients were excluded and the stage distribution renormalised, as treatment sequencing and survival inputs could not be reliably estimated for this subgroup.

Histology is assigned at diagnosis, with 87% of patients classified as NSCLC and 13% as SCLC, consistent with NCRI 2022 data. Among NSCLC patients, molecular status is modelled explicitly: 23% are simulated as having targetable alterations (EGFR [21], ALK [22], or ROS1 [23]), which makes them eligible for targeted therapy. Mutation-specific prevalences are drawn from published meta-analyses and observational studies, restricted to alterations with reimbursed targeted therapies in Ireland.

Initial treatment allocation

Initial treatment assignment is conditional on stage at diagnosis and based on NCRI data covering 2019–2021 (Fig. 2). The NCRI records treatments given within the first year post diagnosis, which were used to define mutually exclusive first-line treatment categories: surgery alone (SR); SR plus chemotherapy (CH); SR plus CH and radiotherapy (RT); SR plus RT; CH alone; CH plus RT; RT alone; RT combined with targeted (TT) or immunotherapy (IO); TT or IO alone; and best supportive care (BSC). Initial treatment categories differ substantially across stages, with surgery-dominant pathways in early-stage disease and higher use of chemotherapy, radiotherapy, systemic therapies, or BSC in advanced stages.

Line sequencing

Subsequent treatment lines were assigned independently of the initial treatment recorded by the NCRI. Line assignment was governed by stage- and histology-specific treatment rules, informed by contemporary clinical practice and validated through expert consultation, as the NCRI does not capture treatments beyond the first year post diagnosis. Distinct sequencing rules were applied for early-stage (I–II) NSCLC and limited-stage SCLC; late-stage (III–IV) targetable NSCLC; late-stage non-targetable NSCLC; and extensive-stage SCLC (Additional file: 1 Figure S1). The maximum number of simulated treatment lines is four, with group-specific probabilities guiding the assignment (Additional file 1: Table S1). For simplicity, we assumed no RT or SR assignment was modelled at the latter lines.

Advanced-stage patients were substantially less likely to receive third- or fourth-line treatment. Receipt of BSC at any line was assumed to represent the final line of care and preceded patient mortality within the model. Modelled overall survival was calculated as the sum of assigned PFS months at each line and validated against reported NCRI overall survival data.

Disease course and survival inputs

Patients were followed from diagnosis until death or the end of the modelling horizon of 60 months. Progression from line to line was driven by PFS length. Survival inputs were derived by digitising published Kaplan–Meier curves for stage- and treatment-specific cohorts and reconstructing approximate individual patient data using the Guyot algorithm (Additional file 1: Tables S2-3) [24]. Parametric survival models were fitted to reconstructed individual patient data using standard distributions (exponential, Weibull, log-normal, log-logistic, Gompertz, and generalized gamma), with model selection guided by Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). Extrapolation beyond observed follow-up was applied where necessary to match the modelling horizon.

Targeted therapy and immunotherapy PFS inputs were obtained from pivotal randomised controlled trials for EGFR-, ALK-, and ROS1-positive disease and for immune checkpoint inhibitors. For patients diagnosed at Stage IV, PFS data for initial treatment were not available directly. We therefore approximated PFS by scaling OS-derived survival draws using a PFS: OS ratio of 0.40 for each NCRI treatment category. This ratio is broadly consistent with observed PFS: OS relationships in advanced NSCLC trials, where median PFS typically ranges from 35 to 50% of median OS for chemotherapy and immunotherapy regimens [25, 26]. For Stages II and III, stage-specific PFS estimates for initial treatment were not available across all treatment categories. In the absence of differential evidence, we assumed that PFS for Stage IIIA was 80% of Stage II PFS and that PFS for Stage IIIB was 70% of Stage II PFS, reflecting increasing disease burden and more limited treatment durability with advancing stage [27].

Following the approach of txSim [18], each patient is assigned a single latent performance quantile drawn from a uniform distribution, held constant across all treatment lines, reflecting consistent individual-level response to treatment.

Cost inputs

Costs were estimated from the perspective of the Irish Health Service Executive (HSE) and expressed in 2024 euro. The model includes diagnostic costs, ongoing surveillance, recurrence-related costs, non-drug treatment costs (surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and administration), and drug acquisition costs for targeted and immunotherapies. Costs related to the management of treatment-related adverse events (AEs) are not explicitly included; the implications of this conservative assumption are explored in scenario analyses and discussed under Limitations. Costs were estimated on a monthly basis over a maximum of 60 months from diagnosis and expressed per patient alive (“per survivor”) and per all patients (“per patient”), by dividing total costs incurred in each month by the number of patients alive at that time or by the total cohort size, respectively. Mean monthly costs were aggregated to annual and total 5-year costs, as well as into two periods: the first 6 months (months 1–6) and subsequent months (months 7–60), reported as average monthly costs within each period.

In the absence of unit-level costing data in Ireland, hospital resource use was costed using DRG tariffs from HPO Admitted Patient Price Lists [28, 29]2. Assignment of DRGs to diagnostic and treatment activities was performed in consultation with HPO specialists. Chemotherapy cycles were capped at four, and radiotherapy fractions were capped at 30. These assumptions are conservative; their impact is explored in the one-way sensitivity analysis. BSC was modelled as a monthly cost, stratified by stage. Table 1 presents the cost input parameters.

Drug costs for targeted and immunotherapies were based on HSE Primary Care Reimbursement Service (PCRS) Irish reimbursement prices (Additional file 1: Table S4) [30]. When multiple agents were available within a drug class, each was assigned equal probability, given the absence of detailed real-world utilisation data. Monthly costs were accumulated over treatment duration, and total costs were aggregated annually over five-year horizons. A similar simplified approach to costing was adopted in a previous Irish study on melanoma [31]. Drug costs reflect published PCRS reimbursement list prices. Confidential hospital-level procurement discounts, which are common for high-cost oncology medicines in Ireland, are not captured; actual drug expenditure may therefore be lower than modelled, and this limitation is explored through sensitivity analyses.

Sensitivity analyses

Probabilistic sensitivity analysis (PSA)

Parameter uncertainty was incorporated using standard distributional assumptions, following ISPOR–SMDM recommendations on parameter uncertainty and sensitivity analysis in decision models [32] (Additional file 1: Table S5). Unit costs and other positive quantities were sampled from gamma distributions (default coefficient of variation 20%), with tighter dispersion applied to regulated drug prices. Probabilities were modelled using beta distributions, with wider uncertainty assigned to survival adjustment scalars and narrower variance for biomarker prevalence estimates. Sets of mutually exclusive probabilities were sampled jointly using Dirichlet distributions, ensuring the sum-to-one constraint was maintained [33], with concentration parameters reflecting the relative strength of empirical versus expert-informed inputs. PSA results were summarised across 500 simulations with 15 000 patients each and visualised using density plots. The larger sample size relative to one-way sensitivity analysis OWSA (below) was used to ensure stable characterisation of distributional uncertainty.

One-way sensitivity analysis

Deterministic one-way sensitivity analysis (OWSA) assessed the influence of key cost inputs on modelled outcomes using a single simulated cohort of 15 000 patients. Patient characteristics and treatment pathways were generated once under base-case inputs and held fixed across all scenarios. Each cost parameter was varied individually by ± 20% around its base-case value. OWSA was not conducted on probability parameters, as these are interdependent and constrained to sum to one, making it infeasible to vary them independently. Results were summarised using tornado diagrams showing the change in cost under low and high parameter values.

Scenario analyses

Additional deterministic scenario analyses were conducted to address key structural and parameter uncertainties identified during peer review. Unless otherwise stated, scenarios were implemented as full model reruns using the deterministic input set, base-case treatment mix, a fixed random seed, and a simulated cohort of 15 000 patients. Results were reported as five-year costs per patient and per survivor, with absolute and percentage differences from the base case.

First, to assess uncertainty in the Stage IV progression-free survival assumption, the ratio of progression-free survival to overall survival was varied from the base-case value of 40% to 30% and 50%. Second, to explore the potential underestimation of early-stage survival, an exploratory Stage I survival scenario was conducted by increasing Stage I progression-free survival durations by 15% and 20% relative to the base case. Third, because immune-related AE costs were not included directly in the base-case model, a post hoc cost-bias analysis was performed for Stage IIIB/IV patients and IO-exposed Stage IIIB/IV patients. Cost shares of 5.15%, 9.73%, and 14.80%, as reported in the meta-analysis by Zhao M., et al. [34] were applied to current modelled costs using the formula p/(1 − p), where p is the AE cost share of total inclusive cost. Fourth, uncertainty in later-line treatment sequencing was explored using a constrained immunotherapy probability scenario analysis. Later-line immunotherapy probabilities in advanced-stage disease were multiplied by 0.5 and 1.5 relative to the base case, with the corresponding probability reallocated to or from best supportive care so that treatment probabilities continued to sum to one. Finally, an exploratory drug procurement discount scenario was conducted by applying discounts of 15%, 20%, and 30% to drug pack prices only.

Validation

Model inputs and structural assumptions were validated through a combination of clinical expert consultation and health system specialist review, consistent with ISPOR–SMDM recommendations on model transparency and validation [35]. Treatment pathway assumptions were reviewed with oncologists, who provided expert opinion on early-stage and late-stage treatment sequencing and probabilities. Cost inputs were validated through consultation with HPO experts, who confirmed the mapping of modelled diagnostic, surgical, chemotherapy, immunotherapy administration, and radiotherapy activities to the Australian Refined Diagnosis Related Groups (AR-DRGs) used in Ireland. Identification and service mapping were informed by HPO listings of the ten most frequently used AR-DRGs in lung cancer care, covering major thoracic surgery, bronchoscopy and diagnostic procedures, chemotherapy delivery, radiotherapy fractions, and systemic therapy administration.

Results

Validation of the model output survival

Simulated overall survival presented at Fig. 3, derived from line-specific PFS across treatment pathways, showed good agreement with stage-stratified NCRI survival estimates (C34 Lung cancers) for patients diagnosed between 2019 and 2022 (Fig. 3). Minor underestimation for Stage I beyond three years persists (3-year: 63% vs. 68%; 5-year: 52% vs. 59%), plausibly reflecting reliance on historical PFS data predating recent improvements in surgical and adjuvant care.

Underestimation of long-term Stage I survival will likely result in a slight cost underestimation, as fewer patients accrue surveillance and recurrence-related expenditure beyond three to five years. However, given that early-stage costs are largely driven by upfront surgery, the overall impact on lifetime costs is likely modest. For Stage III, modelled survival aligns well with NCRI estimates when IIIA and IIIB are combined, consistent with registry reporting conventions, although the model differentiates these sub-stages given their distinct treatment pathways and prognoses. Modelled survival was further validated by plotting survival by initial treatment modality (Additional file 1: Figure S2).

The modelled estimates of ever-use of immunotherapy (25–44% across stages) and targeted therapy (0–8%) are of plausible magnitude and direction. In a large contemporary U.S. cohort of patients with advanced lung cancer diagnosed between 2012 and 2020, first-line immunotherapy use increased from 14% to 66% by 2020, with 13 to 37% of patients receiving immunotherapy in the second line [36]. Community oncology data showed TKIs use in approximately 8–12% of real-world patients overall [37].

Total cost of lung cancer

We estimate that the average annual cost of managing people living with lung cancer in Ireland, based on a steady-state five-year prevalence approach, is approximately €120 million per year3. This compares with about €74 million for the incident cohort in the first year following diagnosis alone. A substantial share of total expenditure is driven by advanced disease (~ 53%), with Stage IV accounting for the largest proportion of costs, reflecting both higher treatment intensity and the concentration of patients diagnosed at this stage.

Stage shift from LCS could materially reduce national spending. Illustratively, if 10% of incident cases were shifted from Stage IV to Stage I, total costs over the five-year period would fall by approximately €5.61 million, highlighting the potential economic benefits of earlier detection alongside clinical gains.

Scenario analysis

Additional scenario analyses are summarised in Table 3, using five-year cost per patient as the primary comparison metric. Overall, the analyses showed that the model’s main conclusion was stable: advanced-stage lung cancer remained substantially more costly than early-stage disease across all tested assumptions. However, the absolute value of advanced-stage costs was sensitive to assumptions that affect treatment exposure, systemic therapy use, AE cost capture, and drug prices.

Stage IV cost estimates were moderately sensitive to assumptions about survival-dependent treatment exposure. Varying the Stage IV PFS ratio produced a range of − 8.5% to + 7.7% around the base-case estimate. This indicates that the 40% PFS assumption is important, but plausible variation in this parameter did not remove the large cost differential between Stage IV and earlier-stage disease. The AE cost-bias analysis suggested AE management costs underestimated Stage IV costs by 5.4% to 17.4%, depending on the assumed AE cost share. Treatment sequencing was another important source of uncertainty. Reducing later-line immunotherapy use had a larger effect than increasing it, because base-case immunotherapy uptake was already high in several advanced-stage strata. This means that lower-than-assumed immunotherapy use would materially reduce advanced-stage costs, whereas further escalation of immunotherapy use would have a smaller marginal effect under the current pathway structure. In contrast, uncertainty around Stage I survival had a modest effect on cost estimates. Increasing Stage I PFS duration raised Stage I costs by only 2.9% to 3.9%, suggesting that the observed underestimation of early-stage survival does not materially affect the overall stage-cost gradient. This reflects the structure of early-stage costs, which are driven mainly by upfront curative-intent treatment rather than prolonged systemic therapy. Finally, drug procurement discounts reduced Stage IV costs by 8.1% to 16.3%, showing that list-price-based drug costing may overestimate actual expenditure if confidential discounts are substantial. This uncertainty acts in the opposite direction to AE exclusion: omitted AE costs are likely to increase advanced-stage costs, while procurement discounts may reduce them. Taken together, the scenario analyses suggest that the exact magnitude of advanced-stage costs remains uncertain, but the conclusion that advanced disease is the dominant driver of lung cancer expenditure in Ireland is robust.

Uncertainty analysis

The probabilistic analyses indicate that uncertainty in this model is driven primarily by survival-dependent treatment exposure rather than short-term diagnostic costs. Stage at diagnosis remains the dominant determinant of both expected cost and cost uncertainty (Fig. 6).

Uncertainty increases substantially in advanced-stage, survivor-conditioned analyses, reflecting limited empirical data on long-term multi-line treatment pathways. When costs were conditioned on survival, distributions shifted markedly to the right and widened substantially, particularly in Stage IIIB and Stage IV.

Phase-specific monthly cost analyses (Additional file 1: Figure S3) demonstrated time-dependent uncertainty. Monthly costs during the first six months after diagnosis were tightly distributed and overlapped across stages and histologies, reflecting relatively standardised diagnostic and treatment initiation pathways. In contrast, costs beyond six months showed substantially wider and more right-skewed distributions in advanced disease, particularly Stage IV NSCLC.

One-way sensitivity analyses of five-year total costs and stage-specific annual costs per surviving patient identified clear and systematic patterns in the drivers of cost variation across stages (Additional file 1: Figure S4). In early-stage disease (Stages I–II), uncertainty in five-year costs was primarily driven by upfront service-related parameters, including surgical costs, radiotherapy pricing, chemotherapy delivery assumptions, and diagnostic inputs, reflecting the predominance of curative-intent treatment. From Stage III onwards, cost sensitivity shifted towards systemic therapy parameters. Immunotherapy drug pack prices and administration costs became the dominant drivers of variation in long-term survivor costs, particularly in Stages IIIA, IIIB, and IV. The overall magnitude of sensitivity increased with advancing stage. BSC was a significant driver of uncertainty throughout all stages.

Temporal analyses across Years 1–5 demonstrate consistent patterns in cost drivers. In Year 1, variation was largely determined by initial treatment intensity and service delivery parameters. By Year 3, systemic therapy inputs, particularly immunotherapy pricing and administration, had become the principal drivers in advanced stages. By Year 5, cost uncertainty across all stages was overwhelmingly determined by systemic therapy parameters, with minimal influence from diagnostic, surgical, or radiotherapy assumptions.

Discussion

This study presents, to our knowledge, the first comprehensive stage-stratified cost model of lung cancer in Ireland, filling a critical evidence gap for the economic evaluation of LDCT screening. The microsimulation framework enables tracking of individual treatment sequences and biomarker-driven therapy allocation across multiple lines of care, integrating national data sources with clinical expert validation.

In the absence of comprehensive stage-specific cost data for Ireland, we compared modelled estimates with recent European real-world studies from the Netherlands [16], France [13], and Finland [12]4. While direct comparisons are constrained by differences in healthcare systems, costing methodologies, and time horizons, several consistent structural patterns emerge. De Nijs et al. reported initial-phase treatment costs of approximately €6 000–€10 000 per patient-month for Stage III–IV disease in 2018–2021, with lower but increasing continuing -phase costs. Our Stage IV monthly costs per surviving patient (€5 386 in the first six months, €3 468 thereafter) are lower in absolute terms, likely reflecting the wider range of services captured in Dutch data, but reproduce the same phase-of-care gradient. Several factors may explain this difference, including broader cost capture in the Dutch study, particularly out-of-hospital care such as hospice and community-based services, and differences in reimbursement structures and tariff levels, notably for hospital stays, specialist consultations, and treatment administration. Finnish registry data reported mean 24-month cumulative costs of €19 000–€22 000 for NSCLC (2014–2019); when restricted to the first two years, our estimates are of similar magnitude for advanced stages. Overall, Irish Stage IV five-year costs are broadly consistent with contemporary Dutch and Nordic estimates. From the modelling perspective, our approach builds upon existing pathway simulators such as the Australian txSim [18] and the Italian whole disease model [17]. Like Buja et al., it retains explicit stage resolution and detailed treatment sequencing but extends beyond a one-year horizon; like txSim, it is simulation-based but remains more granular at the treatment-line level and explicitly propagates parameter uncertainty through PSA, OWSA, and scenario analyses.

Strengths, limitations, and structural assumptions

This study has several methodological strengths. The patient-level microsimulation captures complexity that cohort-level Markov models cannot represent, including heterogeneous progression trajectories and biomarker-driven therapy allocation, and lends itself to clinical validation. The dual reporting of costs per diagnosed patient and per surviving patient provides inputs suited to both cost-effectiveness modelling and budget impact analysis. The model architecture is designed for iterative updating as new therapies and utilisation data become available.

The main limitation arises from the gaps in the Irish real-world data. The NCRI captures stage and first-line treatment but not subsequent lines, treatment duration, drug choice, progression or toxicity, while Hospital In-Patient Enquiry registry data captures inpatient activity without stage information. As a result, second and later lines were modelled using rule-based probabilities informed by clinical practice, which may not fully reflect real-world conditional sequencing. Equal probabilities were assigned across agents within drug classes, and linking costs to pathways required mapping activity to AR-DRGs, introducing abstraction between clinical events and cost attribution.

Several cost components are not explicitly included, notably AE management, toxicity-related hospitalisations, and complex supportive care. Evidence suggests that AEs contribute meaningfully to cancer treatment costs, particularly for immunotherapy [34]. The scenario analyses suggest that excluding AE costs may underestimate Stage IV costs by approximately 5% to 17%, indicating that the base-case estimates are conservative with respect to advanced-stage disease. Conversely, drug costs are based on published PCRS list prices and may overestimate actual expenditure if confidential procurement discounts apply. The procurement discount scenarios reduced Stage IV costs by approximately 8% to 16%, showing that AE omission and list-price drug costing may bias estimates in opposite directions.

The scenario analyses also clarify the impact of key structural assumptions. Stage IV costs were moderately sensitive to the assumed PFS relationship, varying by approximately − 9% to + 8% across the tested range. Later-line immunotherapy sequencing was another important source of uncertainty, particularly if real-world immunotherapy use is lower than assumed. In contrast, increasing Stage I PFS duration had only a modest effect on costs, suggesting that the underestimation of early-stage survival does not materially alter the overall stage-cost gradient. Across all tested scenarios, advanced-stage disease remained substantially more costly than early-stage disease.

Some structural simplifications remain. BSC was modelled as a final line before death, although in practice patients may receive supportive care for variable durations. The equal-probability drug assignment may underestimate costs if prescribing is concentrated among higher-cost agents. BSC as a mortality trigger is a simplifying assumption; some patients may receive supportive care while surviving longer than modelled.

These estimates also reflect the Irish treatment landscape as of 2024. Adjuvant osimertinib, adjuvant atezolizumab, and perioperative combinations are entering practice and may alter early-stage cost profiles. Future versions of the model should ideally use linked NCRI, HIPE, PCRS, and oncology prescribing data to replace expert-informed assumptions with observed treatment sequences, drug utilisation, AE-related admissions, and actual expenditure net of procurement discounts.

Implications for economic evaluation of screening

International evidence consistently shows that screening cost-effectiveness results are sensitive to treatment cost assumptions [13, 16]. De Nijs et al. demonstrated that incorporating updated treatment costs into the MISCAN-Lung model materially improved the cost-effectiveness of LDCT screening, precisely because screening avoids the increasingly expensive advanced-stage care. Given the large cost differential in our model between Stage I (€38 149 per surviving patient) and Stage IV (€231 723), these findings suggest the economic case for LDCT screening in Ireland warrants formal evaluation using the contemporary cost inputs.

The present model should therefore be interpreted as a treatment-cost input module for future screening evaluations. A full evaluation of preventive strategies would additionally require explicit modelling of screening eligibility, invitation and uptake, adherence to repeat screening, LDCT and diagnostic follow-up costs, false-positive findings, incidental findings, overdiagnosis, quality-of-life effects, competing mortality and long-term survival data.

The scenario analyses indicate how these inputs should be used in future cost-effectiveness analysis. Rather than relying only on a single base-case cost set, future LDCT screening models should test alternative advanced-stage cost inputs reflecting Stage IV PFS uncertainty, AE inclusion, later-line immunotherapy use, and procurement-adjusted drug prices. This is important because treatment advances influence screening cost-effectiveness in complex ways: novel therapies may improve survival but also increase treatment duration and costs in advanced disease, thereby increasing the value of avoiding late-stage diagnoses through screening.

Conclusions

This study provides a transparent, stage-specific lung cancer cost model for Ireland and generates contemporary cost inputs relevant to future evaluation of LDCT screening. Costs were strongly stage-dependent, with advanced-stage disease accounting for a disproportionate share of expenditure. Scenario analyses showed that this stage-cost gradient was robust, although absolute advanced-stage costs remained sensitive to assumptions about Stage IV PFS, later-line immunotherapy use, AE cost capture, and drug procurement prices. The model can therefore be used as a first-generation Irish cost-input platform for future economic evaluation and should be updated as linked real-world treatment, survival, toxicity, and expenditure data become available.
