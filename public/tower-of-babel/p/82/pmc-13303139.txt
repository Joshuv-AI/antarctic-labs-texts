Stroke Recurrence and Long-Term Mortality in Patients Diagnosed with Cancer Following Stroke
Authors: Jin Kyo Choi, Young Dae Kim, Hyo Suk Nam, Deog Young Kim, Hye Sun Lee, Ji Hoe Heo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeSystemic cancer and ischemic stroke are major causes of mortality and share several risk factors, including older age, smoking, obesity, and inflammation. This study investigated the incidence of newly diagnosed cancers in stroke survivors and their outcomes.Materials and MethodsThis retrospective observational study included patients with acute ischemic stroke enrolled between January 2010 and December 2019. We excluded patients with active or newly diagnosed cancer within 30 days of stroke onset. Primary outcomes were the incidence of new systemic cancer, recurrent ischemic stroke, and mortality.ResultsA total of 5048 patients were included and followed for a median of 3.4 years (interquartile range 1.5–6.1 years). Among them, 242 (4.79%) were diagnosed with a new cancer, and 524 (10.4%) died during follow-up. At the time of cancer diagnosis, 48 patients (19.8%) had metastases. Factors associated with cancer occurrence included male sex [sub-distribution hazard ratio (SHR), 1.84; 95% confidence interval (CI), 1.27–2.67] and older age (SHR, 1.03; 95% CI, 1.01–1.04). During follow-up, 398 patients (7.9%) developed recurrent stroke, with a higher incidence in those with cancer than those without (14.9% vs. 7.5%, p<0.001). In multivariate Fine-Gray competing risk regression analyses, cancer was not significantly associated with recurrent stroke (SHR, 1.22; 95% CI, 0.77–1.91; p=0.395), but was significantly associated with increased mortality [hazard ratio, 2.57; 95% CI, 1.97–3.36; p<0.001].ConclusionCancer frequently developed in stroke survivors. Patients who developed cancer had a higher risk of mortality. Efforts for early cancer detection are warranted.

INTRODUCTION

Systemic cancer and ischemic stroke are common causes of mortality and poor clinical outcomes in the elderly, who also face significant disease-related and economic burdens.1 These conditions also share common risk factors, including smoking, obesity, and inflammation.2 Stroke occurs in approximately 15% of all cancer patients.34 Stroke may be an initial presentation of cancer in approximately 0.4% to 3% of patients.56 Cancer is also associated with stroke development.7 Tumor cells activate platelets and induce hypercoagulability,8 thereby increasing the risk of stroke. A paradoxical embolism through a patent foramen ovale may cause stroke in patients with cancer and venous thrombosis; furthermore, chemotherapy and radiation therapy may increase the risk of stroke.9 Conversely, aspirin use decreases the risk of cancer development, metastasis, and mortality in patients with cancer, as well as the recurrence of stroke.810

Previous studies on cancer and stroke have primarily focused on cancer-associated stroke, including its underlying mechanisms, the identification of cancer in cryptogenic stroke,1 the prediction of occult cancer in stroke,11 clinical outcomes in patients with cancer-associated stroke,41213 and characteristics of thrombus histology in stroke patients with cancer.1415161718

Given that both cancer and stroke frequently develop in the elderly and that they share several risk factors, stroke survivors may develop a new cancer over time.13 Patients who develop cancer may exhibit distinct clinical characteristics and outcomes compared to those who do not. However, the incidence and outcomes of new cancers in stroke survivors are unclear.

Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the incidence and outcomes of newly diagnosed cancers in ischemic stroke survivors.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study population

This study included patients registered in the Yonsei Stroke Registry, which included consecutive patients who were admitted to the stroke center due to acute ischemic stroke within 7 days of symptom onset.19 Among these registered patients, this study included those with cerebral infarction who were admitted between January 2010 and December 2019. Patients were excluded if they had active cancer at the time of admission or were newly diagnosed with cancer within 30 days of stroke onset. Patients with transient ischemic attack were also excluded, as confirming recurrent stroke is challenging in these cases. Active cancer was defined as cancer diagnosed within the previous 6 months; recurrent, regionally advanced, or metastatic cancer; cancer for which chemotherapy or radiation therapy had been administered within 6 months; inoperable cancer; or hematological cancer that was not in complete remission.20 This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Severance Hospital (Np: 4-2011-0731), and the requirement for patient consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study.

Patient evaluation

Patients underwent routine assessments, including standard blood tests, chest radiography, electrocardiography, brain imaging, transcranial Doppler, carotid duplex, ankle-brachial index, and cardiac evaluations. Brain imaging studies included magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and/or computed tomography (CT), and cerebral angiography (MR angiography, CT angiography, or conventional angiography). Cardiac evaluations included Holter monitoring or continuous electrocardiography during admission to the stroke unit, transesophageal and/or transthoracic echocardiography, and heart CT. Transesophageal echocardiography was part of the routine evaluation, except in patients with decreased consciousness, impending brain herniation, poor systemic condition, inability to accept an esophageal transducer due to swallowing difficulty or tracheal intubation, or lack of informed consent.21 Patients with vegetations on echocardiography were extensively evaluated for systemic cancer. Additionally, patients were evaluated for systemic cancer when they showed findings suggestive of cancer-associated stroke, such as a cryptogenic etiology with multiple embolic infarctions and elevated blood D-dimer levels. The severity of the initial neurological deficits was determined using the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS). The etiologic subtype of stroke was classified according to the Trial of Org 10172 in Acute Stroke Treatment classification system.22 All patients were treated according to the standard stroke management guidelines.

Patient follow-up

After discharge, all patients were regularly followed up at 3 months, 1 year, and annually thereafter. The censoring date was December 2021. Neurologists and clinical research assistants obtained follow-up information from the outpatient clinics through face-to-face interviews. If a patient missed a visit, information was obtained via structured telephone interviews with the patient or, if unavailable, a close relative.23 Additionally, medical records were reviewed for functional outcomes, social habits (smoking, alcohol consumption, and physical activity), vascular events, bleeding, new vascular risk factors, cancer diagnosis, and hospitalizations. Information on the cause of death was also collected.

Outcomes and data acquisition

The primary outcomes were the occurrence of new systemic cancer, recurrent ischemic stroke, and all-cause mortality in patients who developed cancer. Data on the type and time of cancer diagnosis were obtained through a thorough review of the medical records. Data on metastases and pathological findings were collected when available. Recurrent ischemic stroke was defined based on MRI or CT findings. Causes of death were categorized as stroke, cancer, cardiac disease, other causes, or unknown.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.2.3 (http://www.R-project.org). Data were presented as numbers (%), mean±standard deviation, or median (interquartile range, IQR) as appropriate. Univariate analysis was performed using the Student’s t-test for continuous variables and the chi-square test for categorical variables. Time-to-event outcomes included cancer incidence, recurrent stroke, and all-cause mortality. In analyses where death could act as a competing event, cumulative incidence functions were estimated, and competing-risk regression analyses were performed using the Fine and Gray subdistribution hazard model. To identify factors associated with incident cancer after index stroke, multivariable Fine and Gray models were fitted with death treated as a competing risk. Similarly, the associations of cancer development with recurrent stroke and all-cause mortality were evaluated using Fine and Gray subdistribution hazard models, accounting for death as a competing event. Cancer status was incorporated as a time-varying exposure in these analyses to reflect changes in cancer occurrence during follow-up. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. Statistical significance was set at p<0.05.

RESULTS

During the study period, 5779 patients were registered in the stroke registry. After excluding 315 patients with transient ischemic attack, 400 patients with active cancer at admission, and 16 patients diagnosed with cancer within 30 days of stroke onset, 5048 patients were included in this study. The patients were followed for a median of 3.4 years (IQR 1.5–6.1 years). The mean age of the included patients was 66.3±13.2 years, and 3058 patients (60.6%) were men.

Occurrence of cancer

Of the 5048 patients, 242 (4.79%) were diagnosed with cancer during a median follow-up of 3.4 years (Supplementary Fig. 1, only online). Among the 242 patients with cancer, 48 (19.8%) had metastasis at the time of cancer detection. The median time from cerebral infarction to cancer detection was 1058 days (IQR 370.0–1769.0). The most frequent type of cancer was bladder/urinary tract cancer, followed by colorectal, lung, and gastric/esophageal cancers (Table 1). Compared to patients without cancer, those with cancer were older, more frequently male, and less frequently had peripheral artery occlusive disease (PAOD) (Table 2). The laboratory findings were similar between the groups, except for lower platelet counts and higher serum creatinine levels in patients with cancer (Table 2).

Factors associated with the occurrence of cancer were male sex [sub-distribution hazard ratio (SHR), 1.84; 95% CI, 1.27–2.67] and older age (SHR, 1.03; 95% CI, 1.01–1.04) (Table 3).

Recurrent stroke

Of the 5048 patients, 398 (7.9%) developed ischemic stroke during follow-up. The median time from the initial stroke event to recurrent stroke was 1132 days (IQR 465.0–2154.0). Ischemic stroke recurred more frequently in the cancer group [36 patients (14.9%) vs. 362 patients (7.5%), p<0.001] (Fig. 1A). In the competing risk analysis for recurrent stroke, death was treated as a competing event. In the univariate analysis, the occurrence of cancer was significantly associated with an increased risk of recurrent stroke (SHR, 1.63; 95% CI, 1.16–2.29; p=0.005). However, in the multivariate Fine and Gray model adjusting for covariates, the association between cancer and recurrent stroke was not statistically significant (SHR, 1.22; 95% CI, 0.77–1.91; p=0.395). Independent predictors of recurrent stroke included male sex (SHR, 1.58; p=0.001), older age (SHR, 1.01; p=0.030), hypertension (SHR, 1.56; p=0.011), diabetes mellitus (SHR, 1.31; p=0.032), and end-stage renal disease (SHR, 2.29; p=0.012) (Table 4).

Mortality

During a median follow-up of 3.4 years, 524 patients (10.4%) died. Patients who developed cancer had a higher mortality rate than those who did not (28.9% vs. 9.4%, p<0.001) (Fig. 1B). The causes of death were stroke in 181 patients (34.5%), cancer in 43 patients (8.2%), and cardiac diseases in 41 patients (7.8%) (Supplementary Table 1, only online).

In the multivariable time-dependent Cox regression analyses, cancer was associated with increased risks of mortality (HR, 2.57; 95% CI, 1.97–3.36; p<0.001) (Fig. 1B), along with male sex, older age, diabetes, atrial fibrillation, PAOD, previous stroke, ESRD, and initial NIHSS. Higher body mass index and use of anticoagulants or antiplatelets were associated with decreased risk of mortality (Table 5).

DISCUSSION

This study demonstrated that patients with stroke frequently develop cancer during follow-up, and that patients with cancer have a higher risk of recurrent stroke and mortality than those without cancer.

In the present study, the incidence of cancer was not uncommon. The cancer incidence was 4.8% during a median follow-up of 3.4 years, approximately 0.6 times that of recurrent stroke (7.9%) in this study population. In addition, about 20% of cancer cases were diagnosed at advanced stages. Cancer is common in the elderly population. According to the Korea Central Cancer Registry, one in every seven individuals aged over 65 years is diagnosed with cancer in South Korea.24 Cancer has a prevalence of 4.4% in the total population of South Korea, and in 2020, there were 482.9 new cancer cases per 100000 people in South Korea.24 Although our data cannot be directly compared with those of the general population, the cancer incidence in this study population was higher than that in the general population. A previous study using the Danish Stroke Registry, which linked data to the Danish Cancer Registry, showed that stroke was associated with a 54% higher risk of occult cancer,25 supporting our findings.

We identified factors associated with cancer development during follow-up. In this study, male sex and older age were associated with cancer. A significant association has been observed between arterial stiffness and the occurrence of cancer in males.26 Male patients had a higher prevalence of cancer and a higher mortality rate than female patients. Although the exact reason for this is still unclear, it may be related to sex differences in lifestyle habits, such as alcohol consumption, smoking, and body mass index.2728

In this study, the recurrence of ischemic stroke was more frequent in patients who developed cancer, although it did not reach statistical significance in the multivariate analysis. Tumor cells promote thrombosis by activating platelets and inducing hypercoagulability.8 Tumor cells and monocytes/macrophages can damage vascular endothelial cells through the release of cytokines, such as tumor necrosis factor, interleukin-1, and interleukin-6, leading to the formation of blood clots. Additionally, interactions between tumor cells and macrophages activate factors X and XII and platelets, resulting in thrombin formation.1829 Tumor cells also generate thrombin.830 In fact, histological analysis of thrombi retrieved during intra-arterial thrombectomy in stroke patients showed higher proportions of platelets, thrombin, and tissue factor.1518 Our findings suggest that the risk of recurrent stroke may increase in patients who develop cancer.

Cancer is the leading cause of mortality in the general population in Korea.24 In this study population, stroke or stroke-related conditions were the most common cause of death, which was expected since the study population consisted of stroke patients.14 Cancer was also a frequent cause of death, with its proportion of mortality being similar to that of cardiac diseases. Long-term use of antiplatelet agents, such as aspirin, has been shown to reduce the risk of cancer, cancer mortality, and metastasis.810 Given that more than 80% of patients in this study had been prescribed antiplatelets, it is possible that the cancer-related mortality rate might have been reduced in this study population. However, this cannot be definitively determined from this retrospective, observational study.

In this study, approximately 20% of the patients who developed cancer during follow-up were diagnosed at an advanced stage. Cancer is the leading cause of death worldwide, including in Korea, particularly among elderly patients, such as those with stroke. Many cancers are curable when detected at an early stage. While major vascular events, such as recurrent stroke and myocardial infarction, are the primary concerns during the follow-up of stroke patients, the findings of this study highlight the need for early cancer detection efforts as well.

This study has several limitations. First, the presence of cancer may have been missed in some patients due to the retrospective nature of the study. Second, recurrent ischemic stroke events were determined based on brain imaging studies to obtain an accurate and conclusive diagnosis of ischemic stroke. Although all people in South Korea are covered by the national health insurance and have easy access to healthcare, including MRI and CT, imaging-based diagnosis of ischemic stroke might lead to an underestimation of the primary outcome events. Third, the exact causes of death were unclear for many patients. Fourth, mortality in this study was defined as all-cause mortality. Although causes of death were descriptively summarized, the study was not designed to distinguish cancer-specific from stroke-specific mortality or to formally assess causal pathways between cancer, stroke recurrence, and death. Therefore, the observed association between cancer and mortality should be interpreted as an overall prognostic association rather than evidence of a direct causal relationship. Finally, while this study was based on data from a single hospital with a homogenous ethnic group, the incidence and types of cancer vary across countries and ethnicities. Therefore, the generalizability of the findings may be limited.

In conclusion, cancer developed frequently in stroke survivors, and in some cases, it was diagnosed at an advanced stage. Both mortality and recurrent stroke tended to be higher in patients who developed cancer after stroke. These findings highlight the necessity of incorporating cancer surveillance into post-stroke management, alongside traditional vascular event monitoring, to enable earlier cancer detection.
