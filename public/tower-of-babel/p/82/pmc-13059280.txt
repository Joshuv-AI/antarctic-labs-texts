Carrier rates for recessive monogenic diseases in the Chinese Han population: a systematic review
Authors: Yirong Xia, Yinan Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveIn China, research on expanded carrier screening (ECS) has exhibited significant inconsistencies due to regional variations, with a lack of large-scale data analysis of the population nationwide. This study aimed to review the carrier rates of monogenic disorders throughout China comprehensively.MethodsThe study was conducted in two stages, with the carrier rate of monogenic diseases as the primary outcome. In the first stage, 1449 articles were identified, of which 11 were ultimately included for data extraction. In the second stage, all 307 genes identified by the first stage were specifically investigated, and both carrier rates and allele frequencies of pathogenic or likely pathogenic (P/LP) variants were extracted.ResultsNineteen genes were associated with monogenic disorders exhibiting carrier frequencies above 1/100, including GJB2 (7.78%), HBA1/2 (6.19%), PAH (2.76%), ATP7B (2.51%), DUOX2 (2.50%), HBB (2.48%), GALC (2.37%), CYP21A2 (2.11%), SLC26A4 (1.96%), POLG (1.92%), USH2A (1.91%), SLC25A13 (1.87%), SLC22A5 (1.78%), SMN1 (1.75%), MMACHC (1.66%), G6PD (1.38%), GAA (1.05%), UGT1A1 (1.02%), and TYR (1.01%). Twenty-seven conditions had carrier rates between 1/100 and 1/200, and thirty had carrier frequencies between 1/200 and 1/400. The ten most common pathogenic/likely pathogenic (P/LP) variants with the highest allele frequencies were: GJB2 c.109G>A (7.3%), POLG c.2890C>T (0.94%), GALC c.1901T>C (0.90%), SMN1 exon 7 deletion (0.87%), SLC25A13 c.2T>C (0.59%), SLC22A5 c.1472C>G (0.52%), GJB2 c.235del (0.50%), SLC22A5 c.1400C>G (0.48%), SLC22A5 c.43G>T (0.44%), and SLC25A13 c.852_855del (0.43%).ConclusionThis review provides the first comprehensive summary of carrier rates for monogenic diseases in the Chinese Han population. It not only describes but also ranks the carrier rates and variant frequencies of related genes, offering critical evidence for the tiered inclusion of gene categories in ECS panels or newborn screening programs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40246-026-00940-z.Keywords: Expanded carrier screening, Monogenic disease, Han Chinese, Carrier rate, Allele frequency

Introduction

Expanded carrier screening (ECS) is a technique that utilizes molecular testing in asymptomatic individuals or couples to identify variants in genes associated with genetic disorders [1]. Genetic carrier screening (GCS) was first introduced in the 1970s for the detection of DNA variations related to haemoglobinopathies [2]. Since then, the scope of screening has expanded to include a growing number of inheritable conditions, such as hearing loss and spinal muscular atrophy, among others [2].

With the advent of next-generation sequencing (NGS), ECS has emerged as a method capable of analyzing multiple disease-related genes simultaneously. This approach enhances detection efficiency while reducing costs [3]. As a result, ECS enables individuals to rapidly and cost-effectively assess their genetic risks, thereby supporting informed reproductive decision-making [1].

Studies have demonstrated that carrier screening for recessive genetic diseases conducted before or during early pregnancy can effectively prevent the birth of affected children. This significantly reduces the long-term economic burden on healthcare systems and society at large [4]. Carrier screening provides families with valuable informed reproductive choices. Subsequent decisions, such as the use of assisted reproductive technologies (e.g., preimplantation genetic testing), prenatal diagnosis, and intervention, effectively disrupt the chain of transmission for severe genetic disorders across generations. This not only alleviates substantial familial distress and societal caregiving burdens but also avoids the high lifetime medical and support costs associated with raising an affected child. However, research has found that screening for high-frequency genes alone can identify the majority of carriers. As the number of genes screened increases, the detection rate curve plateaus [5]. Therefore, focusing on high-frequency genes can significantly reduce screening costs and the subsequent burden of genetic counseling.

In the Chinese population, although ECS-related studies have been increasingly conducted, their findings exhibit considerable inconsistency due to regional variations. For example, one study on phenylketonuria (PKU) reported a carrier rate of 5.80% in Zhengzhou [6]—a city in central China where over 95% of the population is Han. In contrast, a study in Xinjiang, an autonomous region in northwestern China where the Han ethnic group accounts for approximately 40% and the Uygur for about 45%, reported a markedly lower rate of only 0.68% [7]. Similarly, one article indicated that 0.70% of the Hongkong population carried variants associated with carnitine deficiency [8], whereas another estimated the carrier rate for the Chinese in southern China to be 2.07% [9]. Moreover, there is a lack of large-scale data that comprehensively analyze the population across the entire country. Most ECS gene panels currently in use are based on foreign research or single-center studies focused on specific provinces. Therefore, to systematically evaluate the carrier rates of monogenic diseases throughout China, we conducted this systematic literature review.

Methods

Eligibility criteria

English and Chinese articles on monogenic diseases published before July 17, 2024, were included. Dissertations were excluded as they are not peer-reviewed. We included studies that reported carrier screening results or methodologies, the epidemiology of monogenic diseases, and genome sequencing or genetic testing conducted in the Han Chinese population. Eligible source populations encompassed neonates, individuals of childbearing age, students, and couples seeking assisted reproductive technologies. Studies focusing on families with a reported history of genetic diseases or on patients with clinical symptoms potentially related to any heritable condition were excluded. This is because such studies are based on samples with carrier rates significantly higher than those in the general population. Multi-ethnic studies were included if the Han Chinese constituted at least 95% of the study population or if carrier rates for the Han Chinese were reported separately. Review articles were excluded due to potential overlap of their study populations with other included primary studies.

Studies were included if they met all of the following conditions: (1) original research articles published in recognized Chinese or English journals before July 17, 2024; (2) contained the search keywords specified in “Article searching” section; (3) the study cohort was representative of the general population, with clear enrollment information and a Han Chinese participant proportion of no less than 95%; (4) the methodology was unbiased in the selection of target genes/variants, primarily utilizing Next-Generation Sequencing (NGS), with supplemental methods allowed for regions not covered by NGS; (5) the results provided a list of carrier genes, the carrier rate or number of positive individuals for each gene, the specific variant names carried, and allowed for pathogenicity re-classification of variants and direct calculation of variant allele frequency (number of mutant alleles/total alleles) and genotype frequency (number of mutant genotypes/total individuals); (6) the statistical methods were deemed appropriate by our institution’s biostatistics department.

Studies were excluded based on the following: (1) non-peer-reviewed literature, including but not limited to conference abstracts, dissertations, and articles in non-indexed or predatory journals; (2) articles not written in English, studies not involving human subjects, or non-original data reports; (3) study cohorts including other ethnic groups or with a Han Chinese proportion below 95% of the total sample; (4) research focusing on specific patient or disease cohorts, as these populations may be highly enriched for genetic disorders and not representative of the general population; (5) genetic testing methods exhibiting bias, such as protocols targeting only a limited set of high-frequency variants within genes; (6) studies assessed to have flawed or inappropriate statistical methodology.

Article searching

Our study was conducted in two parts: a broad review of monogenic diseases, followed by a focused analysis of genes related to each reviewed disease.

In the first part, we searched PubMed for English-language studies using the query: “(carrier testing [Title/Abstract]) OR (carrier screening [Title/Abstract]) AND (monogenic [Title/Abstract]) AND (China)”. Chinese-language articles were retrieved from the Wanfang, CNKI, and VIP databases using the Chinese search string: “(monogenic disease) AND [(China) OR (Chinese)] AND [(Incidence Rate) OR (Carrier Rate)]”.

In the second part, we performed a targeted search for articles on all diseases identified from the initial evidence synthesis. The search terms included the names of the diseases and their associated genes, combined with “carrier,” and “China” or “Chinese” in the Title/Abstract fields.

Study selection

The titles and abstracts of all identified articles were screened against the eligibility criteria. Those meeting the criteria were selected for a full-text review and quality assessment. Only references that fulfilled all eligibility criteria and were deemed to be of high quality were subsequently submitted to data extraction.

Data extraction

The extracted information included the article title, publication date, demographic and geographic characteristics of the study population, sample size, and methodology. Geographic locations to the north of the Qinling Mountains-HuaiHe River Line were defined as northern China [10–12], while areas to the south of this line were regarded as southern China. The primary outcome was the carrier rate for monogenic diseases. For studies in which the carrier rate was not directly reported, it was estimated from the allele frequency or disease incidence using the Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium principle. These calculations were based on pathogenic or likely pathogenic (P/LP) variants, while variants of uncertain significance, likely benign, or benign (VUS/LB/B) were excluded. The classifications of variants were evaluated in accordance with the 2015 guideline released by the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) and the Association for Molecular Pathology (AMP). In the second part of the analysis, the allele frequencies of common variants associated with a carrier rate greater than 1/100 were also extracted.

Quality assessment

The AHRQ (Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality) methodology checklist was used to evaluate reference quality. Articles scored 8–11 out of 11 were deemed to be high-quality, those scored 4–7 were regarded as middle-quality, and those scored 0–3 were low-quality. High- and middle-quality references were submitted to data extraction, while those with a low quality were excluded.

Statistical methods

All results were calculated by IBM SPSS Statistic 26. Chi-Square test was used to assess the significance of incidence or carrier rates for specific genetic disorders among different regions and populations.

Results

Study selection

In the first section of our study, the literature search identified 1240 English and 209 Chinese articles, resulting in a total of 1449 studies. After screening titles and abstracts, 1428 studies were excluded due to irrelevance. A subsequent full-text review led to the exclusion of 7 additional articles for not meeting the eligibility criteria and 3 for low quality. Ultimately, 11 studies were included for data extraction (Fig. 1).

Study searching and article selection

Analysis of the first phase revealed 307 genes associated with monogenic disorders (Supplementary material 1). Subsequently, the second phase of our study focused on retrieving and evaluating articles reporting carrier rates for P/LP variants in all 307 genes. The summary of the references and their comparisons with our result was shown in Supplementary Material 2.

Common variants of monogenic diseases with carrier rates of 1/100

Using the ACMG guidelines, 143 variants in 14 genes with reported carrier frequencies greater than 1/100 (ATP7B, CYP21A2, G6PD, GAA, GALC, GJB2, MMACHC, PAH, POLG, SLC22A5, SLC25A13, SLC26A4, SMN1, TYR, UGT1A1, HBB) were reclassified. Among these, 113 were classified as pathogenic variants, 25 as likely pathogenic variants, 3 as variants of uncertain significance (PAH:c.158G>A, USH2A:c.9259G>A, USH2A:c.5953G>A), 1 as benign (GJB2:c.368C>A), and 1 as a reported error (SLC25A13:c.1180+1G>A) (Supplementary Material 3). For subsequent carrier frequency statistics, variants of uncertain significance, benign variants, and reported errors were excluded.

In GJB2, the c.109G>A variant demonstrated the highest allele frequency at 7.3%, distantly exceeding all other variants (Table 5). This was followed by c.235del (0.50%).

Comparisons of carrier rates between different regions

According to prior research, thalassemia and glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency were reported to have a significantly higher carrier rate in southern than in northern China [13, 14]. Unfortunately, the data extracted for G6PD were all from studies that did not separately report its carrier rates in northern and southern China, so the comparison between different geographical regions for G6PD was unable to be calculated.

To date, we have not retrieved any literature on thalassemia carrier rates in Northern China. We identified 12 studies employing next-generation sequencing (NGS) for thalassemia screening among women of reproductive age in Southern China. The overall carrier rate was 7.37% (7519/102,030), comprising an α-thalassemia carrier rate of 5.38% (5486/102,030), a β-thalassemia carrier rate of 1.93% (1976/102030), and a carrier rate for other thalassemia variants of 0.06% (57/102,030).

Currently, in Southern China, thalassemia screening is included in government-funded preconception care programs. The standard protocol involves routine hematology examinations for both partners. If either partner presents with a mean corpuscular volume (MCV) or mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH) below the normal range, further genetic testing is recommended. Commonly employed techniques for detecting α-thalassemia carriers and β-thalassemia variants include quantitative fluorescent PCR, gap PCR, and reverse dot blot (RDB). The spectrum of thalassemia variants commonly encountered in the Chinese population includes: three deletion types for α-thalassemia(--SEA, -α3.7, -α4.2), three non-deletion types for α-thalassemia [Hb Constant Spring (Hb CS), Hb Quong Sze (Hb QS), Hb Westmead(Hb WS)], and 17 point variants for β-thalassemia [CD41–42 (c.124_127delTTCT), CD71–72 (c.216_217insA), CD17 (c.52A>T), − 28 (c.-78A>G), CD26 (c.79G>A), IVS-II-654 (c.316–197C>T), IVS-I-1 (c.92+1G>T), IVS-I-5 (c.92+5G>C), CD43 (c.130G>T), CD27/28 (c.84_85insC), − 32 (c.-82C>A), -29 (c.-79A>G), CD30 (c.91A>G), CD14-15 (c.45_46insG), Cap + 40–43(-AAAC)(c.-10_-7delAACA), Int(c.2T>G), CD31 (c.94delC)]. Regarding this screening pathway, we retrieved 19 studies from multiple provinces in Southern China, including Guangdong, Fujian, Guizhou, Yunnan, Hunan, Hubei, and Hainan. The pooled overall carrier rate was 9.05% (84,131/929,547), consisting of an α-thalassemia carrier rate of 6.28% (58417/929547), a β-thalassemia carrier rate of 2.54% (23,593/929,547), and a carrier rate for other thalassemia types of 0.23% (2121/929,547).

Studies employing integrated detection methods for thalassemia carrier rate was 8.88% (91605/1031577), comprising the α-thalassemia carrier rate of 6.19% (63903/1031577), the β-thalassemia carrier rate of 2.48% (25569/1031577), and a carrier rate for other thalassemia variants of 0.21% (2178/1031577).

Likewise, SMN1 showed a carrier rate of 1.74% in adults and 2.39% in newborn screening (χ² = 4.192, P < 0.05). For SLC26A4, the rates were 2.26% in childbearing adults compared to 1.53% in newborn screening (χ² = 195.77, P < 0.0001).

Discussion

Although efforts to determine carrier rates of recessive monogenic diseases in China have been ongoing, most existing studies are single-center investigations with limited sample sizes, leading to inconsistent results and poor representativeness of the broader Chinese population. To address this gap, the present study conducted a systematic review of research spanning the entire country, representing the first comprehensive large-scale analysis of carrier rates in China. This review incorporates both English and Chinese literature, as we contend that domestically published studies in Chinese provide essential population-specific data necessary for a nationally representative assessment.

China comprises 56 ethnic groups, with the Han ethnic majority accounting for approximately 93% of the population [15]. Since gene frequencies differ across ethnicities, this review focused on the Han population to most effectively approximate carrier rates within China.

During quality evaluation, three articles were excluded due to the limited representativeness of the source population and constrained testing methodologies. The study by Chen et al. reported notably higher carrier rates than other included studies [16]. It was excluded because, although none of the participants had a diagnosed genetic disease, they all sought medical services due to recurrent abortion or infertility—a factor that could introduce selection bias. Likewise, the study by Wei et al. in Taiwan excluded cancer patients but included individuals with a family history of genetic disease [17]. Additionally, the use of SNP chips (Single Nucleotide Polymorphism chips, SNP chips) allowed for the detection of only pre-specified known variants via hybridization, potentially missing other pathogenic variants and thereby underestimating carrier frequencies. Similarly, the study by Hu et al., although based on a disease-free population without family history, tested only 448 specific variants across 24 genes [18]. All studies ultimately included in our analysis employed next-generation sequencing (NGS) with some complementary techniques, a method less susceptible to selection bias.

In Tables 2, 4 and 5, several genes were associated with both autosomal recessive (AR) and autosomal dominant (AD) inheritance patterns. Conventionally, AD variants are expected to be pathogenic, which may appear inconsistent with the concept of “carrier rates.” However, in our analysis of clinically healthy individuals, all such genes exhibited recessive-like characteristics, likely due to underlying genetic heterogeneity. Therefore, given our exclusive focus on disease-free cohorts, the use of carrier rates to describe genes with both AR and AD patterns is methodologically appropriate in this context.

Our results identify genes responsible for monogenic disorders and categorize their associated incidence levels based on carrier rates (greater than 1/100, between 1/200 and 1/100, between 1/400 and 1/200, and less than 1/400). This study represents the first comprehensive systematic review to synthesize data on allele frequencies and carrier rates of common genetic disorders in China. These findings provide critical evidence for implementing tiered inclusion of gene categories in carrier screening programs.

Our analysis found that, in the carrier rate of pathogenic/likely pathogenic (P/LP) variants in the PAH gene, there is no significant differences between northern and southern China (P = 0.27), which is different from previous studies and recent guidelines who suggested a higher carrier rate in norther China [19–22]. However, the carrier rate of GJB2 was found to be significantly higher in northern China (2.58%) than in southern China (1.86%) (P < 0.0001), which contradicts prior reports of no significant regional difference in its variants prevalence [23]. Thalassemia and glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency were reported to have a significantly higher carrier rate in southern than in northern China [13, 14]. Unfortunately, we were unable to make a comparison between geographical regions for thalassemia and G6PD deficiency in our own review, since the references for G6PD did not separately report the carrier rate in different regions and there was a lack of studies focusing on northern thalassemia carriers.

The GJB2 c.109G>A variant exhibits a high allele frequency of 7.3%. Since there was a lack of consensus, some studies included this variant in calculating overall P/LP carrier rates, while others excluded it due to its markedly incomplete penetrance [24–26]. Consequently, studies that omitted c.109G>A generally reported lower GJB2 carrier rates. To bridge this data gap, we conducted calculations on carrier rates with and without this variant separately. While the carrier rate with c.109G>A was 15.99%, that without c.109G>A was significantly lower, at 3.00%. During genetic counseling, we recommend informing patients of the implications of c.109G>A, including its high carrier rate and low penetrance in the general population. Additionally, factors such as the occurrence of hearing loss in the family, carrier status, and age of onset should also be taken into consideration when determining whether reporting this variant or not.

Several limitations of this review should be noted. The carrier rates and allele frequencies of significant P/LP variants presented here indirectly reflect the prevalence of monogenic diseases in China. However, although disease prevalence can be estimated using the Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium, our results do not directly provide exact incidence rates.

Some genes or variants were reported in only one study. Their associated carrier rates may therefore be biased due to limited sample sizes and reliance on a single source population. These genes are marked with an asterisk (*) in Tables 2, 4 and 5.

Although this review focused on the Han Chinese population, potential biases remain. Given China’s vast territory and genetic diversity, carrier rates and variant profiles may vary even within the Han population. The lower carrier rate in the reproductive age group is primarily due to its composition of symptom-free, healthy adults. In contrast, some newborns with potential clinical symptoms might not have developed or been identified with those symptoms at the time of screening. Nonetheless, this review provides the first systematic and comprehensive overview of monogenic diseases in the Chinese population to date.

Included studies employed varied genetic testing methods—such as targeted NGS (tNGS), whole-genome sequencing (WGS), and whole-exome sequencing (WES). Differences in technical accuracy and coverage among these methods may introduce unpredictable biases. tNGS provides high sequencing depth and accuracy by targeting predefined genomic regions of interest, but is intrinsically limited in its ability to cover pathogenic variants located in deep intronic or regulatory regions and is prone to missing deletions or duplications in highly homologous genomic segments such as SMN1 and HBA1/HBA2 [27]. WES investigates the exonic regions of approximately 20,000 genes, thereby capturing variations beyond just the primary targets. However, it shares the coverage constraints and demonstrates low sensitivity in detecting copy number variations (CNVs) and complex structural variants [28]. While WGS offers the most comprehensive analysis by sequencing the entire genome including both exonic and intronic regions, it typically shows a lower sequencing depth of 30x, and standard short-read WGS still struggles to accurately resolve large-fragment CNVs, dynamic variants as well as complex structural rearrangements [9]. In addition, all these NGS methodologies are confronted with the formidable interpretive challenge posed by variants of uncertain significance (VUS) [27]. To address these shortcomings, the referenced studies employed supplementary techniques such as multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification (MLPA), gap-PCR, quantitative PCR, Sanger sequencing, and specialized bioinformatics tools like SMAca, which improved the accuracy and clinical utility of the testing [9, 27–31].

Conclusions

In response to the considerable inconsistencies in expanded carrier screening (ECS) data and the lack of large-scale studies in China, this review provides the first comprehensive summary of monogenic diseases in the Chinese Han population. It systematically categorizes and describes carrier rates and variant frequencies of relevant genes, offering critical evidence for the tiered inclusion of gene categories in ECS panels and newborn screening programs.
