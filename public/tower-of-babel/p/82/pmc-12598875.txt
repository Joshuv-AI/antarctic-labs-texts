Hydrophobic and Electrostatic Sequence Patterning Directs Hierarchical Assembly of Phenylalanine-Rich Oligopeptides
Authors: Mateusz Leśniewski, Emilia Iłowska, Justyna Sawicka, Zihan Li, Chun Tang, Adam Liwo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
While peptide aggregation and protein phase separation have been well-studied, the rules linking sequence patterning to aggregate morphology, particularly how microscopic hydrophobic/electrostatic arrangements dictate macroscopic structure, remain elusive. Here, we decode this link by systematically designing and analyzing 50, mainly phenylalanine-rich, tri- to heptapeptides with shuffled sequences excised from natural phase-separating proteins. Combining coarse-grained simulations with transmission electron microscopy, nuclear magnetic resonance, thioflavin assay and circular dichroism spectroscopy experiments, we demonstrate that peptide hydrophobicity and sequence patterning determine association propensity and show how hierarchical assembly is driven by clustering of aromatic (F) and charged residues. This patterning yields spheres, bilayers, web-like networks, and porous sponges. Notably, subtle sequence shuffling (e.g., KFF vs KDFF or KDFF vs KFFD) dramatically alters morphology, underscoring the precision of design. Our work establishes a framework for programming peptide self-assembly via sequence logic, bridging amino acid patterning to functional material properties.

Peptide aggregation and its role in forming various structures, from disordered clusters to highly organized fibrils, are central to numerous biological processes, including neurodegenerative diseases − and formation of membraneless organelles , as well as in the development of novel peptide-based nanomaterials − and peptide-based materials of medical use. , Despite significant progress in understanding this phenomenon, the precise mechanisms by which specific sequence patterns dictate the morphology and stability of peptide aggregates remain poorly understood. A critical challenge lies in discerning how subtle variations in amino acid composition and positioning influence the propensity of peptides to form distinct aggregate structures. ,,,− This knowledge is essential not only for elucidating the molecular basis of diseases such as Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s diseases but also for designing advanced biomaterials with tailored properties. To study the sequence-aggregation propensity and sequence-aggregate structure relationships, short peptides are used as model systems because many sequence patterns can be readily and systematically investigated at a relatively low cost. ,,− Since short-peptide association often results in dynamic nanoscale structures, computer simulations are indispensable in such investigations. Molecular dynamics (MD) in all-atom representation is limited to small systems or short time scales. To study systems composed of several hundred or more peptide molecules, the use of coarse-grained (CG) models is necessary.

Recently, several large-scale simulation studies, with 150 – 720 peptide molecules of the phase separation of all natural dipeptides, , tripeptides, tetrapeptides, and selected pentapeptides built by extending tetrapeptide sequences were carried out by means of canonical MD with the Martini CG force field with explicit coarse-grained water. These studies have demonstrated the essential role of hydrophobic, in particular aromatic, residues such as phenylalanine or tryptophan in aggregation − and that charged residues at the end of a phenylalanine sequence promote fibrilization. Martini makes the simulations of large-size peptide assemblies feasible, owing to coarse graining. However, it does not reproduce regular secondary structures, which make the scaffold of fibrils and other regular structures of peptide and protein aggregates. , Conversely, the coarse-grained UNRES force field , is capable of modeling regular secondary structures due to axial and not spherical symmetry of the respective interaction potentials and to the presence of correlation terms. The latter couple backbone-local and backbone-hydrogen-bonding interactions. Additionally, it has a more reduced representation than Martini (2 sites per residue) and uses implicit solvent, making the simulations faster.

In this study, we employed UNRES to determine how hydrophobicity and electrostatic interactions guide hierarchical assembly of oligopeptides. We selected 27 tripeptides (FFF, LFF, FFV, FYI, IYF, VFF, YFI, FHF, PFF, VYV, KFF, KLL, KYW, FYK, GFG, KYF, RYF, ECG, GYG, GYQ, KFG, KYY, DFN, GGG, KFD, NYQ, and KHD), 14 tetrapeptides (FFFF, DFFD, DFFK, DFKF, FDDF, FKDF, KDFF, KFDF, KFFD, FKKF, KFFE, KFFK, GGGG, and KDKD), 2 pentapeptides (KFFFD and GGGGG), 5 hexapeptides (FFDKFF, FQNQNF, QNFFQN, DKFFDK, KDFFDK), and 2 heptapeptides (FFDKDFF and DKFFFDK). These 50 sequences were mainly derived from phase-separating proteins comprising hydrophobic/aromatic residues critical for multivalent interactions. − Those rich in phenylalanine residues and with varying distributions of charged residues comprise the core of the set. All-glycine sequences were included for reference. For four peptides, KFF, KDFF, KFFD, and KDFFDK, which contain the FF sequence and charged residues, we carried out the transmission electron microscopy (TEM), circular dichroism (CD), nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) measurements, and thioflavin assays to verify the theoretical predictions. The synthesis and purification of these four peptides is described in , while the experiments are described and their results analyzed in .

As in our earlier study of dipeptide aggregation, for each system, we carried out multiplexed replica exchange (MREMD) , simulations with UNRES of 320 peptide molecules. Compared to canonical MD used in the other CG studies, − MREMD results in faster calculations of converged ensembles and, thereby, faster calculations of accurate ensemble averages; additionally, it provides temperature profiles of ensemble-averaged properties such as the degree of association. A succinct description of the UNRES model, the simulation procedure, including simulation settings, and that of the analysis of simulation results can be found in .

We begin with the analysis of the per-monomer aggregation free energies (ΔF̂ aggr ; see ), which are plotted vs hydrophobicity in Figure A (tripeptides) and Figure B (tetra- to heptapeptides) respectively, except for those with the degree of association of 1 or 0, for which ΔF̂ aggr could not be calculated. The values of ΔF̂ aggr as well as those of the degree of association are collected in . For tripeptides, ΔF̂ aggr is correlated with hydrophobicity, as found in our previous work on dipeptides. Some correlation still exists for tetra- to heptapeptides (Figure B). However, most of the 4 – 7 residue sequences selected for this study contain two or three phenylalanine residues and have a similar hydrophobicity around −2 kcal/mol but their ΔF̂ aggr values span a range from −1.24 kcal/mol (for DKFFFDK) to 1.72 kcal/mol (KFFK). In particular, for the tetrapeptides that contain two phenylalanine residues, lysine, and aspartic acid, the ΔF̂ aggr increases in the order KDFF (0.15 kcal/mol) < KFDF (0.58 kcal/mol) < FKDF (1.01 kcal/mol) < DFFK (1.23 kcal/mol) < KFFD (1.32 kcal/mol). The corresponding free energies, ΔF A,∞ , determined by NMR titration for KDFF (−4.34 kcal/mol) and KFFD (−3.56 kcal/mol) () are arranged in the same order as the respective ΔF̂ aggr s from MREMD. The greater change of the CD spectra of KDFF compared to those of KFFD with incubation time () also suggest stronger association of KDFF. Therefore, placing a phenylalanine residue at one end of the sequence and charged residues at the other end seems to promote association by separating residues capable of hydrophobic and electrostatic interactions.

As can be seen from Figure C, the content of the β-structure of the tetra- to heptapeptides (the secondary-structure content cannot be determined for tripeptides at the coarse-grained level) is not necessarily correlated with the aggregation degree. In particular, FFFF has a very small β-sheet content despite the association degree of nearly 1. Conversely, the peptides with the FF or FFF patterns in the middle of the sequence and charged or polar residues at the ends, as well as those with the FF sequences at the termini and charged residues inside exhibit high β-sheet contents, although their degrees of association vary from about 0.1 to nearly 1. A high β-sheet content indicates (although it is not a necessary condition for) regular structural patterns. The propensity of oligopeptides with the FF pattern inside and charged residues outside to form β-sheets was also found in all-atom simulations. , The consistent trends observed across both HN and Hα chemical shifts mutually reinforce the conclusion of progressive structure formation. The formation of β-structure in KFF, KDFF, and KFFD is primarily indicated by the concentration-dependent increase of the chemical shifts of the HN and Hα resonances, specifically observed at F2 in KFF and KFFD and F3 in KFFD (). Additionally, the consistently high 3 J H N –H α values for these phenylalanine residues () suggest that their backbone ϕ dihedral angles preferentially sample β-sheet-like conformation and, in conjunction with these HN and Hα chemical-shift values, support their role as the nucleation site for the β-sheet assembly. See also the detailed discussion of NMR results in . On the other hand, the β-structure seems to be fluctuating because it was not detected by ThT fluorescence assays carried out for KFF, KDFF, KFFD, and KDFFDK (). Because of a strong contribution from phenylalanine-ring stacking, the CD spectrum patterns characteristic of β-structure are distorted, as observed in earlier studies of phenylalanine peptides; , see also the discussion in .

The morphologies of the assemblies of the 50 peptides studied can be divided into three main categories: (1) compact, (2) noncompact with regular patterns and (3) nonregular, the latter encompassing a variety of structures from elongated aggregates (e.g., KFF) to isotropic ensembles containing monomers and small oligomers. Each of these main categories is divided into three subcategories which are listed, along with sample structures, in Table . The structures of all systems at T = 277, 300, and 310 K (the highest temperature at which measurements were carried out) are shown in . The structures shown in Table correspond to T = 277 K, which is the lowest temperature at which the experimental measurements were carried out; at higher temperatures small clusters were formed except for strongly associating systems. It can be seen from Table that the structures with regular patterns largely correspond to those with a high content of secondary structure (Figure C). The KDFF peptide is an exception, forming bilayers and oblate micelles while having a low β-structure content. Peptides with all-hydrophobic sequences form compact spheres with no internal pattern. It can also be noted that none of the tripeptides form aggregates with a regular pattern.

The aggregates, especially those with regular structural patterns, largely differ in thermal stability. A characteristic feature of the maps are the horizontal lines in low-temperature regions, which indicates the splitting of large clusters into smaller ones rather than their gradual dissolution by losing monomers, observed in our study of dipeptides. For strongly associating systems, such as FFF (spherical aggregates) or FFDKDFF (spongiform multilayer aggregates) and largely for KDFF (bilayers and oblate micelles), this heatmap pattern persists at all temperatures. For the other ones it becomes continuous with increasing the temperature, indicating that gradual dissolution occurs at higher temperatures. These two types of cluster-size distributions can also be seen in the distribution plots (). Two systems, KFFD (patterned monolayer sheets) and KDFFDK (networks of stacks) show abrupt transitions, around T = 280 K, from assemblies of a few clusters with large or considerable size to a multitude of clusters (Figure D,G). This feature is shared by all peptides with two or three phenylalanine residues in a row in the middle of the sequence and charged or polar residues outside ().

As can be seen from Figure A, a KFFD monolayer consists of stacks of extended chains held together by backbone hydrogen bonds, which results in the formation of an extensive β-sheet structure, which is additionally stabilized by the phenylalanine side chains diagonally linking the neighboring stacks (Figure A). The neighboring stacks are shifted with respect to each other by about half the distance between the chains to maximize the diagonal interactions between the phenylalanine side chains. The same pattern of interactions occurs in the model of the KFFE protofibrils proposed in an earlier study based on all-atom replica-exchange MD simulations. The KDFF bilayer (Figure B) exhibits a similar pattern of interactions in each of the two layers except that the side chains of F3 are arranged in rows holding a stack together, which makes backbone hydrogen-bonding interactions weaker due to increased distances between strands (cf. Figure C which shows a reduced β-sheet content for this tetrapeptide). The two layers and the neighboring stacks are held together by the side chains of F4. It should be noted that KFF, which also has two phenylalanine residues at the termini, forms disordered albeit large and stable aggregates (Figure A and Table ). The formation of bilayers by KFF is likely to be impeded by close packing of positively charged lysine side chains on the surface. Similar as for KFFD and KDFF, the hydrophobic interactions between the phenylalanine side chains hold together each stack of KDFFDK (Figure C). The presence of two charged residues at each end likely prevents the formation of layers (as opposed to KFFD); however, a number of stacks can form a branched structure by the insertion of a loose phenylalanine side chain into another stack.

The spongiform structures of FFDKDFF and of the like peptides containing an FF sequence at each end and charged residues inside, are composed of curved layers of stacks, each held together by hydrogen bonds and by the interactions between phenylalanine side chains, which also hold together the layer and the neighboring layers (Figure D). The multilayer structure is similar to a tree-cake or a roll. The chains in a stack are significantly rotated with respect to each other to create space between the neighboring stacks, which makes channels paved with charged residues. It should be noted that the peptides with single F residues outside and oppositely charged residues inside, FDDF and FKDF, form distorted spheres without a regular pattern. Therefore, the interactions between single phenyalanine side chains seem to be insufficient to hold the stacks of extended chains and assemble them into multilayer structure.

In summary, the present study has demonstrated the dependence of the aggregation propensity and the morphology of aggregates on the distribution of phenylalanine and charged or polar residues in the sequences with the same content of the residues of each kind. Although the propensity to aggregation primarily depends on the sum of hydrophobicities of amino-acid residues (Figure ), placing the phenylalanine residues at one end (the KDFF vs the KFFD sequence) increases it. This observation was confirmed by NMR measurements. Different distributions of charged or polar residues in phenylalanine-based sequences result in diverse aggregate shapes, including monolayers and bilayers with charged residues on the surface, micelles, web-like structures characteristic of gels, and sponges with channels padded with charged residues that could transport ions or water molecules (Figure ). The formation of these diverse shapes seems to be governed by the same driving forces as those that govern protein folding. These forces lead to the formation of a hydrophobic core and exposing of the polar and charged residues. The local and backbone-hydrogen-bonding interactions determine the details of the structural pattern. The obtained results provide the framework for rational design of peptide-based nanomaterials. However, it should be noted that the shapes of peptide aggregates also depend on solvent choice, pH, and the surface on which aggregation occurs. ,
