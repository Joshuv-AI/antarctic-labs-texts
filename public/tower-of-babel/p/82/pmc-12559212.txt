Cost effectiveness analysis of low dose computed tomography lung cancer screening in Chinese population
Authors: Youjian Yao, Yan Jin, Caifa Ji, Mei Gui
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study evaluated the economic benefits, optimal starting age, and most appropriate screening strategy for low-dose computed tomography (LDCT) lung cancer screening in the Chinese population from the perspective of the Chinese healthcare system. A Markov model was developed using previously published epidemiological and cost data from China to simulate the screening process for individuals aged 40 to 80. The analysis considered different starting ages (40, 45, 50, and 55 years) and screening intervals (annually, every two years, and every three years), resulting in 12 screening strategies and one no-screening strategy. The primary outcome was the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER). To validate the model, predicted lung cancer incidence rates were compared with actual incidence data from China, and the results showed consistency. The analysis identified age 55 as the optimal starting point for screening, with the strategy of screening every three years from age 55 to 80 emerging as the most cost-effective. Age 55 was thus determined to be the most appropriate starting age for initiating LDCT screening. This strategy yielded an ICER well below the per capita GDP of China in 2019, which was used as the willingness-to-pay (WTP) threshold in this study. One-way sensitivity analysis highlighted the cost of LDCT screening as the most influential parameter affecting the ICER. Probabilistic sensitivity analysis further confirmed the robustness of the findings: at a WTP threshold equal to the 2019 per capita GDP of China (71,453 CNY/QALY), the strategy of screening every three years from age 55 to 80 was cost-effective in nearly all simulations compared with no screening. In conclusion, when using the per capita GDP of China in 2019 as the WTP threshold, the optimal lung cancer screening strategy for the Chinese population was triennial LDCT screening from age 55 to 80. Introduction

According to the latest data report from the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC), there were approximately 2.48 million new cases of lung cancer and 1.81 million deaths in 2022, accounting for 12.4% and 18.7% of all cancer cases and deaths, respectively1. In China, the number of new lung cancer cases was 1.0606 million, with 733,300 related deaths in 2022, representing about 28% of all cancer-related deaths2. This indicates a severe burden of lung cancer in China. In recent years, advancements in screening technology and healthcare improvements have significantly increased the survival rates of lung cancer patients3,4. The five-year survival rate in developed countries was approximately 16.3%5, while in China, it was about 15%6. Previous studies have shown that patients diagnosed with stage 1 A lung cancer have a five-year survival rate of up to 75%7, highlighting the importance of early detection and treatment. In recent years, numerous lung cancer screening guidelines have been developed in China based on recommendations from various experts. These guidelines have undergone multiple revisions due to ongoing research. However, the optimal starting age and screening strategy for lung cancer screening in China remain unclear. Therefore, exploring the best starting age and strategy for lung cancer screening in China is crucial for alleviating the increasingly severe burden of lung cancer8–10.

Many studies have confirmed the effectiveness of low-dose computed tomography (LDCT) in early lung cancer screening. LDCT screening has been shown to reduce lung cancer mortality and improve patient prognosis11,12. The National Lung Screening Trial (NLST) in the United States showed that LDCT screening could reduce lung cancer mortality by 20% compared to X-ray screening in heavy smokers aged 55–7413. A Dutch study found that lung cancer mortality was significantly lower among past and current smokers aged 50–74 who underwent LDCT screening compared to those who did not14. In China’s latest lung cancer screening guidelines, LDCT is recommended as the primary screening method for lung cancer10. Therefore, the effectiveness of LDCT lung cancer screening has been supported by numerous studies both domestically and internationally.

Although many studies have confirmed the effectiveness of LDCT screening, its economic viability still requires further exploration. Currently, many scholars worldwide focus on the health economics evaluation of LDCT lung cancer screening, but most studies target high-risk populations and come from developed countries15–19. Due to differences in economic development levels and epidemiological characteristics of lung cancer, results from other countries and regions may not be directly applicable to China. One study reported that China’s lung cancer incidence had exceeded that of the United States and continued to rise, which could affect the number of lung cancer cases detected by LDCT screening and influence model analysis results20. Therefore, China’s optimal starting age and strategy for lung cancer screening cannot be directly inferred from studies conducted elsewhere.

China’s healthcare system is primarily government-led, with public hospitals playing a dominant role in providing medical services. The country has achieved nearly universal health insurance coverage through two major schemes: the Urban Employee Basic Medical Insurance (UEBMI) and the Urban and Rural Resident Basic Medical Insurance (URRBMI)21,22. However, significant disparities in healthcare resource distribution and accessibility still exist between urban and rural areas. In August 2024, the National Health Commission released the Lung Cancer Screening and Early Diagnosis and Treatment Program (2024 Edition), targeting high-risk populations23. While this represents a step forward, there is still no officially implemented nationwide screening policy for the general population. These contextual factors must be considered when evaluating the feasibility and cost-effectiveness of implementing LDCT screening strategies in China.

Currently, it remains unclear whether LDCT lung cancer screening is cost-effective in the Chinese population. Therefore, it is necessary to conduct a modeling analysis based on the epidemiological characteristics of lung cancer in China to provide reference data for policymakers to choose the best starting age and screening strategy. This study aims to evaluate the economic benefits, optimal starting age, and best screening strategy for LDCT lung cancer screening in the Chinese population from the perspective of the China health care sector.

Methods

Target population

The target population for this study was defined as a fixed cohort of 100,000 asymptomatic individuals aged 40–80 years, without a history of lung cancer. For the different screening strategies, screening was initiated at ages 40, 45, 50, or 55 years, while individuals accrued identical health outcomes and costs as the no-screening group before reaching their assigned initial screening age. The cohort was closed, meaning no new individuals were added over time, and individuals exited the model only upon death or at the end of the simulation period. All screening strategies were applied to the same cohort to ensure comparability. The upper age limit of 80 years was based on the average life expectancy of Chinese women, as reported in the World Health Statistics Report 202124.

Markov model and screening strategies

This study used a Markov model cohort simulation method to analyze LDCT lung cancer screening. The Markov model includes the following states: non-lung cancer, preclinical stage I (asymptomatic and undiagnosed), preclinical stage II, preclinical stage III, preclinical stage IV, clinically stage I, clinically stage II, clinically stage III, clinically stage IV, death from other causes, and death from lung cancer. The Markov model structure was shown in Fig. 1. The screening initiation ages were set at 40, 45, 50 and 55 years, with a termination age of 80 years. Screening was conducted at intervals of one, two, or three years, yielding a total of 12 distinct screening strategies in addition to a no-screening scenario. The Markov model cycle length is set to one year, with a total of 41 cycles. The cycle stops when the patient reaches death or age 80. All strategies, including the no-screening strategy, are simulated from age 40 to 80. Although the screening initiation ages vary, the model followed the entire cohort from age 40 onward, ensuring that each strategy shares the same time horizon and number of cycles. A half-cycle correction was applied to all Markov model calculations to account for the timing of transitions occurring throughout each cycle. To ensure comparability, the same time horizon of 41 years (from age 40 to 80) was applied to all strategies, including the no-screening group. This evaluation was conducted from the healthcare system perspective, and all intervention strategies were compared against the no screening strategy. In contrast to the Chinese guidelines, which recommend a starting age of 50 for lung cancer screening, this study chooses to initiate screening at the age of 408,10. This decision was based on studies indicating a trend of early-onset lung cancer in China in recent years, as well as the recommendations of certain Chinese experts who suggest screening younger individuals to maximize the benefits of LDCT screening25,26.

Markov model of lung cancer screening.

Markov model assumptions

(1) Studies suggest that although the impact of LDCT radiation on the human body cannot be entirely ignored, it is acceptable considering the reduction in lung cancer mortality brought about by LDCT screening27. According to the statement from the American Physical Society, epidemiological evidence is not clear regarding increased cancer incidence or mortality at radiation doses below 100 mSv. Therefore, participants in the screening will not have an increased risk of cancer due to LDCT (low-dose CT) radiation28,29; (2) The compliance rate of the screening population is assumed to be 100%, and participation in the screening will continue until the age of 80. In the base-case analysis, we assumed 100% compliance with LDCT screening until age 80. This idealized assumption provides a standardized reference scenario, allowing comparison of different screening strategies under maximum achievable adherence. To explore more realistic conditions, scenario analyses were conducted with compliance rates of 100%, 80%, 60%, 40%, and 20%.

Model parameters

The age-specific incidence rates of lung cancer in China are sourced from the global burden of disease study in 2019 (GBD 2019)30. The age-specific all-cause mortality rates for the Chinese population are obtained from the China Health and Hygiene Statistical Yearbook 202031. The transition probabilities between different metastatic states of lung cancer are derived from previous research19,32–34. Sensitivity and specificity parameters are sourced from a study by Wang et al.35. The annual mortality probabilities for different stages of lung cancer are based on research published by Yuan et al.36. The age-specific mortality rate from other causes in China was obtained by subtracting the age-specific lung cancer mortality rate from the age-specific all-cause mortality rate. The cost parameters for LDCT screening and for confirming positive cases during screening are derived from a study by Zhao et al.32. The treatment cost parameters for lung cancer at different stages are sourced from a multicenter, hospital-based retrospective survey in China37. The outcomes are measured in quality-adjusted life years (QALYs), with utility values ranging from 0 to 1, where 0 represents death and 1 represents full health. The utility value parameters for different stages of lung cancer are based on previously published studies36. All cost and utility value parameters are discounted at an annual rate of 5%, as shown in Table 1.

Outcome measures

This study compares the cost-effectiveness of different screening strategies using the Incremental Cost-Effectiveness Ratio (ICER), defined as the additional cost per additional Quality-Adjusted Life Year (QALY) gained. In line with the China Guidelines for Pharmacoeconomic Evaluations (2020 edition), which recommend using a cost-effectiveness threshold of 1–3 times the national per capita GDP, we conservatively adopted one times the per capita GDP (71453 CNY in 2019) as the willingness-to-pay (WTP) threshold in the base-case analysis38. all costs are adjusted to 2019 prices based on China’s Consumer Price Index (CPI)39.

Model calibration

The initial state distribution and age-specific non-lung cancer mortality rates are adjusted using model calibration methods to ensure that the model’s predicted life expectancy aligns with China’s life expectancy in 2019. Additionally, the model’s predicted incidence rates for each age group are calibrated to match real-world reported values, thereby forming a China-specific lung cancer natural history model with good predictive validity. By calibrating the predicted lung cancer incidence rates for the target screening age group generated by the Chinese lung cancer screening model developed in this study to ensure that the rate difference ratio [(model fitting value - target value)/target value *100%] remains within ± 20%, a high degree of consistency is achieved, indicating good model fit. Furthermore, the predicted and observed age-specific lung cancer incidence rates were compared using the Log-rank goodness-of-fit test at a significance level of 40. A P-value exceeding 0.05 indicated that the difference between the two curves was not statistically significant, thereby confirming the model’s good fit and suggesting that it accurately represents the actual lung cancer incidence in China.

CUA & sensitivity analysis

A Markov model for the cost-effectiveness analysis of LDCT lung cancer screening in the Chinese population was constructed using TreeAge Pro software (version 2024 R1.1). One-way sensitivity analysis (OSA) and probabilistic sensitivity analysis (PSA) were conducted to identify influential parameters and assess the robustness of the model results. The variation ranges for screening cost were derived from Zhao et al.32, the ranges for treatment costs were based on Zhang et al.37, and the utility value ranges were informed by Yuan et al.36. The sensitivity and specificity of LDCT screening were also included in the analysis35. The discount rate varied between 0% and 8%38. PSA was conducted on all input parameters using appropriate probability distributions to reflect parameter uncertainty. This analysis tested the stability of the model outcomes under stochastic conditions. As this study was based entirely on publicly available secondary data, no patient-level data were used, and ethical approval was not required.

Results

Model calibration

This study calibrated the predicted lung cancer incidence rates for various age groups in China against the actual incidence rates for 2019. Except for the 75–79 age group, which showed a relatively high discrepancy of −28%, the rate differences for all other age groups remained within ± 20%, indicating a satisfactory model fit. The detailed results are presented in Table 2. The incidence predicted by the model of each age group and the actual incidence curve of each age group are shown in Fig. 2, the blue curve represents the model-predicted age-specific lung cancer incidence rates, while the red curve represents the actual age-specific lung cancer incidence rates for China in 2019. The two curves are in close alignment, with a Log-rank test statistic of LR 13.239 and P = 0.066 greater than 0.05. This indicates that the difference between the two curves is not statistically significant. Overall, the goodness-of-fit results indicate that the model’s predictions for age-specific lung cancer incidence can represent the actual incidence in China.

Incremental cost-effectiveness ratio

The results of this cost-effectiveness analysis are summarized as follows: compared with the non-screening strategy, implementing LDCT screening in the Chinese population was cost-effective. Among the various screening strategies, the most expensive was annual screening from ages 40 to 80, with a total cost of 6,311.65 CNY, whereas the least costly strategy was the non-screening approach, costing only 163.19 CNY. The highest number of quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) was also achieved by annual screening from ages 40 to 80, yielding 16.90 QALYs, while the non-screening strategy resulted in the lowest QALYs at 16.87. The most cost-effective strategy was screening every three years for individuals aged 55 to 80, with an incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) of 25,542.33 CNY, which was well below the threshold of China’s per capita GDP in 2019, as shown in Table 3.

Sensitivity analysis

Willingness-to-pay

The results of this study showed that at a willingness-to-pay (WTP) threshold of 21,435.9 CNY/QALY, the probability of the non-screening strategy being cost-effective was 72.89%, while the triennial LDCT screening strategy for individuals aged 55–80 had a probability of 27.11%. As the WTP threshold increased to 42,871.8 CNY/QALY, the acceptability of the screening strategy rose markedly to 68.21%, while that of the non-screening strategy decreased to 31.79%. At a threshold of 64,307.7 CNY/QALY, the screening strategy’s probability of being cost-effective further increased to 94.13%, and ultimately reached 100% at 15,0051.3 CNY/QALY, as illustrated in Fig. 3.

Acceptability curve.

One-way sensitivity analysis

The tornado diagram in Fig. 4 from the one-way sensitivity analysis shows that the cost of LDCT screening had the greatest impact on the ICER of the screening strategy. This was followed by the compliance rate, the specificity of LDCT screening, and the discount rate. Other influential parameters included the cost of biopsy for diagnosis and the utility values for stage III and IV lung cancer. In contrast, the sensitivity of LDCT and the treatment costs for different stages had only marginal effects on the cost-effectiveness results.

Tornado diagram.

Probabilistic sensitivity analysis

The results of this study indicated that, following 10,000 Monte Carlo simulations, the probabilistic sensitivity analysis (PSA) demonstrated that, compared to the non-screening strategy, the screening strategy that began at age 55 and ended at age 80, with screenings every three years, had a 100% probability of being cost-effectiveness, assuming a WTP threshold of 71,453 CNY/QALY (one times the 2019 per capita GDP), as shown in Fig. 5.

Scenario analysis

To further evaluate the robustness of the model, scenario analyses were conducted by varying the compliance rates of LDCT screening. Specifically, compliance was assumed at 100%, 80%, 60%, 40%, and 20%. These values were selected to represent a gradient from ideal adherence to extremely poor adherence. Although pilot trials of LDCT screening in China have reported compliance rates of approximately 80%41, lower compliance scenarios were also tested to account for potential variations in real-world practice. For each scenario, the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) was calculated and compared with the willingness-to-pay (WTP) threshold. Notably, as compliance decreased, screening-related costs declined more steeply than health benefits, which in turn led to lower ICER values under lower compliance assumptions. Importantly, across all compliance levels, triennial LDCT screening for individuals aged 55–80 years consistently remained the most cost-effective strategy, suggesting that the study did not overestimate the cost-effectiveness of LDCT screening. Detailed results of the scenario analyses are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Discussion

The economic benefits of LDCT lung cancer screening and the optimal starting age for screening in the Chinese population remain uncertain. This study utilized a Markov model to evaluate the cost-effectiveness of various screening strategies based on different starting ages and intervals. The results indicated that the optimal starting age for screening in China is 55 years, with the most cost-effective strategy being LDCT screening every three years, starting at age 55 and continuing until age 80. Sensitivity analysis consistently supported this conclusion. Moreover, scenario analyses varying screening compliance rates (100%, 80%, 60%, 40%, and 20%) further confirmed that triennial LDCT screening for individuals aged 55–80 years remained the most cost-effective strategy across all compliance levels. This provides additional evidence that our conclusions are robust even under different real-world adherence assumptions. Additionally, to validate the Markov model for lung cancer screening in China, calibration methods were applied to align the predicted incidence rates for different age groups with the actual observed values, confirming the model’s validity. In determining the willingness-to-pay (WTP) threshold, we followed the China Guidelines for Pharmacoeconomic Evaluations (2020 edition), which recommend using a range from 1 to 3 times the national per capita GDP38. To ensure the robustness and policy relevance of our findings, we conservatively selected one time the per capita GDP (71453 CNY in 2019) as the threshold in our base-case analysis. Although higher thresholds may be used in some international studies, we believe this conservative approach better reflects China’s health policy context and provides more stringent evidence for decision-making. The goodness-of-fit test indicated that the model’s predictions were consistent with the age-specific lung cancer incidence rates in China. For the 75–79 age group, although the prediction error exceeds the 20% threshold for good performance, the impact of this discrepancy is minimal due to the lower remaining life expectancy in this age group.

Most health economic evaluations of LDCT screening for lung cancer have predominantly focused on developed countries such as the United States, Germany, and Australia, with target populations often being high-risk groups18,19,42. A cost-effectiveness analysis comparing LDCT lung cancer screening between Japan and the United States in 2022 showed that among never-smokers, LDCT screening was cost-effective in Japan compared to the United States. In the male cohort, spending $20,446 resulted in 17.8812 QALYs and 18.0655 LYs, while in the female cohort, spending $30,065 resulted in 17.665 QALYs and 17.9603 LYs43. Although this study’s findings are consistent with ours regarding the cost-effectiveness of LDCT screening among non-smokers, there are several reasons why a direct comparison may not be appropriate, including variations in lung cancer incidence rates and economic development levels across countries and regions44. First, differences in lung cancer incidence rates and economic development levels across countries and regions may contribute to variations in cost-effectiveness. Second, differences in modeling approaches should be noted. The modeling states used in the aforementioned studies focus solely on different stages of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), whereas our model accounts for both preclinical and clinical stages of lung cancer. Finally, our study explored 12 distinct LDCT screening strategies, incorporating various initial screening ages and screening intervals.

A cost-effectiveness analysis of LDCT screening for lung cancer based on a Chinese healthy population explored optimal screening initiation ages and strategies by gender specificity. The study reported that in the male cohort, the ICERs for LDCT screening ranged from $16,086 per QALY to $3,675,491 per QALY, while in the female cohort, the range was $36,624 per QALY to $5,943,556 per QALY45. This study categorized metastatic states into “local metastasis,” “regional metastasis,” and “distant metastasis,” which could directly influence QALY estimates. Compared to the above studies, this research has validated the model’s effectiveness. Although there are differences in screening frequency and transition state modeling, the conclusion that LDCT screening is cost-effective for the Chinese population remains consistent.

A secondary analysis study has indicated that the radiation exposure and cancer risk associated with LDCT screening for lung cancer, considering its benefits in cancer screening, are deemed acceptable27. Additionally, findings from a study using Monte Carlo simulation to model cumulative cancer risk induced by X-ray and LDCT radiation exposure suggest that annual LDCT lung cancer screening slightly increases the lifetime cumulative radiation-induced cancer risk in the screened population. However, due to uncertainties related to radiation cancer risk factors and organ dose data used in the study, further research is required to confirm these results46. Most experts agree that it is practically impossible to accurately estimate radiation risks for populations exposed to doses below 100 mSv using existing data, making estimates of radiation risk in the low-dose range highly speculative47. Evidence regarding the impact of LDCT radiation on cancer incidence and mortality rates remains inconclusive. Given the lack of clear evidence that LDCT radiation exposure increases cancer risk, this study did not consider the influence of LDCT radiation exposure on lung cancer screening when constructing the model. These findings can serve as a reference for optimizing lung cancer screening strategies in China. In particular, initiating LDCT screening at age 55 with a three-year interval provides a cost-effective solution for early detection and mortality reduction in the general population. Policymakers could consider implementing pilot screening programs based on this strategy, especially in provinces with high lung cancer incidence and adequate healthcare infrastructure.

Study limitations

Firstly, while previous studies report screening compliance rates ranging from 34.94% to 47.54%48–50, this study assumes a 100% compliance rate in order to maximize the potential benefits of screening. This idealized assumption may overestimate the cost-effectiveness of LDCT screening; however, scenario analyses incorporating lower compliance rates (100%, 80%, 60%, 40%, and 20%) showed that the optimal strategy triennial LDCT for individuals aged 55–80 years remained consistent, suggesting that the study did not substantially overestimate screening benefits. Secondly, key risk factors such as second-hand smoke exposure, air pollution, family history, and occupational exposures were not incorporated, which may result in an underestimation of lung cancer incidence. Lastly, as the study’s findings are based on model simulations, they may not fully capture real-world conditions. Therefore, additional randomized controlled trials on LDCT lung cancer screening are necessary to validate the results of this study. In addition, the results of this study should be interpreted with caution when used to inform policy or clinical practice. The model is based on certain assumptions and simplifications, and real-world effectiveness may differ due to variations in compliance, healthcare access, and demographic characteristics. Before nationwide implementation, further validation using real-world data and prospective studies is essential.

Conclusion

This study evaluated the cost-effectiveness of low-dose spiral CT (LDCT) lung cancer screening in China. The results indicated that the optimal starting age for screening is 55 years, and the screening strategy of conducting LDCT every three years for individuals aged 55 to 80 demonstrated the best cost-effectiveness, with the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) significantly lower than the per capita GDP of China in 2019, indicating substantial economic benefits. The validity of the model was confirmed, and the goodness-of-fit was satisfactory. In conclusion, this study provides evidence-based recommendations for the effective allocation of lung cancer screening policies and medical resources in China.
