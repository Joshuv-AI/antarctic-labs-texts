Preserving Hip Stability Yields Better Cartilage Repair With Microfracture Treatment: A Rabbit Study
Authors: Elshan Hajiyev, Ozgur Aydin, Selahaddin Aydemir, Pinar Akokay Yilmaz, Guven Erbil, Ahmet Kaan Arslan, Burak Duymaz, Onur Hapa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo compare the individual and combined effects of microfracture and hip stability achieved through preservation of the soft tissue on cartilage repair in rabbit femoral head osteochondral defect.MethodsTwenty-four male New Zealand white rabbits were divided into 4 groups. In group 0, the untreated right femoral heads of 6 rabbits served as healthy controls. Osteochondral defects (5 × 3 mm) were created on the left femoral heads of all 24 animals. Group 1 (n = 6) received microfracture, with the labrum preserved and capsule repaired. Group 2 (n = 6) received microfracture, but the labrum was excised and the capsule left unrepaired; additionally, a 2 × 6-mm portion of the capsule was excised. Group 3 (n = 6) had no microfracture, but the labrum and capsule were preserved. Group 4 (n = 6) had no microfracture and additionally the labrum was excised and capsule left unrepaired; as in Group 2, a 2 × 6-mm portion of the capsule was also excised. After 4 weeks, cartilage healing was assessed using the International Cartilage Regeneration & Joint Preservation Society (ICRS) and modified O’Driscoll scores and via immunohistochemically with collagen type I, II, and aggrecan staining.ResultsGroup 1 demonstrated significantly better cartilage healing than the other groups (P < .05), with better outcomes in at least one of the following parameters: ICRS score, defect filling ratio, or modified O’Driscoll score. Group 3 scored better than group 2 in ICRS assessment (P = .048). Type II collagen and aggrecan expression were significantly greater in Group 1 than in groups 2 and 4 (P < .05).ConclusionsPreservation of the hip stability enhances the effectiveness of microfracture treatment, promoting superior cartilage regeneration compared with microfracture alone in a rabbit model.Clinical RelevanceMaintaining or restoring the hip stability leads to improved cartilage healing beyond the effect of isolated microfracture.

Chondral injuries may occur on the articular surface of the femoral head more commonly as the result of acute trauma or on the acetabulum.1 Cartilage defects of the femoral head differ from those of the acetabulum with respect to etiology, association with labral pathology, lesion localization, available treatment options, and the expected response to intervention.2 The primary goal in the treatment of cartilage defects is to achieve the formation of hyaline cartilage that integrates well with the healthy surrounding tissue, completely fills the defect area, and possesses the desired mechanical strength.3 However, this outcome can generally only be achieved through techniques such as mosaicplasty or autologous chondrocyte implantation.4 Various methods that support the repair of cartilage tissue, which has limited healing potential, have been extensively investigated in both clinical and experimental studies; techniques such as joint debridement, grafting, and microfracture have been discussed.5 Among these, the microfracture technique stands out as a primary bone marrow stimulation−based surgical approach for full-thickness cartilage lesions because of its low cost, ease of application, and favorable outcomes, particularly in younger patients.6 It promotes fibrous cartilage repair by enabling pluripotent bone marrow cells to migrate into the defect area through subchondral bone perforations.7,8 This study aimed to compare the individual and combined effects of microfracture and hip stability achieved through preservation of the soft tissue on cartilage repair in rabbit femoral head osteochondral defect. We hypothesized that in stable hips with preserved soft-tissue integrity, the rate of cartilage healing would be greater and the regenerated cartilage tissue would more closely resemble native cartilage in both structure and composition.

Methods

This study was conducted in the Multidisciplinary Laboratory for Experimental Animals at our University Faculty of Medicine, following the approval of the Local Ethics Committee for Animal Experiments under protocol number 52/2023.

Animal Model

A total of 24 rabbits were obtained from the Multidisciplinary Laboratory for Experimental Animals, University Faculty of Medicine. The experimental animals were male, white New Zealand rabbits weighing between 3100 and 4300 g, skeletally mature (56 weeks). Before inclusion in the study, the animals were subjected to a 1-week quarantine period. During this period, they were housed under standard laboratory conditions (50%-60% humidity, ambient temperature of 20-22°C, with a 12-hour light/dark cycle). The 24 rabbits were randomly assigned into 4 groups, with interventions applied to their left hips. To establish a healthy control group, the right femoral heads of 6 randomly selected rabbits killed humanely at the time of assessment were excised and evaluated, without any surgical intervention (Table 1).

Surgical Procedure

Before surgery, all experimental animals were administered general anesthesia via intramuscular injection of 5 mg/kg xylazine (Alfazyne, Ege Vet Hayvancılık San. ve Tic) and 35 mg/kg ketamine (Ketalar, Eczacıbaşı İlaç San. ve Tic). An anterolateral approach to the left hip was used, with a vertical skin incision approximately 6 to 8 cm in length. After dissection of the subcutaneous soft tissue, the joint capsule was visualized. A 2-cm transverse incision was made in the joint capsule over the femoral head. The left thigh of the subjects was externally rotated and abducted to bring the femoral head into a subluxated position (Fig 1). A 3-mm-deep osteochondral defect was created on the femoral head, corresponding approximately to zone 6 according to the geographic zone classification by Ilizaliturri et al.,9 using a 5-mm diameter flattened drill bit and a rotary tool. The defect was considered contained, as it was surrounded by intact cartilage borders. A depth gauge marked at 3 mm was used on the drill bit to ensure consistency in the depth of the defect (Fig 2). The microfracture procedure was performed using a 0.9-mm diameter and 3-mm deep awl prepared in advance. In the randomized groups, to reduce the joint’s “suction seal (SS)” effect, both a 1-cm labral excision (between the 1-o’clock and 3-o’clock positions on the anterior quadrant of the left acetabulum) and a 2 × 6-mm capsular excision were performed in 12 animals from group 2 (microfracture [MF]) and Group 4 (Control), with the capsule left open and unrepaired. In contrast, in groups 1 ( SS + MF) and 3 (SS), the labrum remained intact, and capsular repair was carried out to preserve the joint’s negative pressure effect. Capsular closure, including the joint capsule and aponeurosis, was performed using absorbable polyglycolic acid sutures (VICRYL 2-0; Ethicon). After thorough sterile irrigation and hemostasis, the tissue layers were closed appropriately. The animals were monitored during the postoperative period. The rabbits were fed ad libitum with standard feed and water. Daily wound care and clinical observations were performed regularly. All animals were allowed unrestricted movement within their cages. No antibiotic or analgesic treatment was administered postoperatively.

Macroscopic Evaluation

The left femoral heads of the subjects were macroscopically evaluated. The International Cartilage Regeneration & Joint Preservation Society (ICRS) scoring system was used for the grading.10 The materials were examined and assessed by 2 orthopaedic surgeons and 2 histologists. The average of the evaluation scores from all 4 individuals was calculated. In addition, the reliability of the scores given by both observers was measured using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). Rulers were placed alongside the femoral head specimens, and photographs were taken using a camera. The extent of the repair tissue covering the defect area was quantitatively evaluated in a digital environment using software (Image J version 1.54g) on the basis of the photographs taken (Fig 3).

Quantitative calculation of the extent of the defect area in the rabbit femoral heads filled with repair tissue using digital software (Image J version 1.46; National Institutes of Health).

Histologic Analysis

All tissue samples were preserved in formaldehyde. Bone tissues were decalcified using an ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid solution and subsequently embedded in paraffin. The paraffin blocks were sectioned into 5-μm slices using a rotary microtome (RM 2255; Leica). The sections were then stained with hematoxylin-eosin, Masson trichrome, and Safranin O for histomorphologic analysis under a light microscope. The images were examined using a fluorescence microscope (BX-51; Olympus).

Immunohistochemical Analysis

To assess the similarity between the newly formed tissue and hyaline cartilage, as well as the structural composition of matrix proteoglycans, histologic analysis was performed using immunohistochemical staining. Collagen deposition was evaluated through immunohistochemical labeling with anti-type I and anti-type II collagen, whereas proteoglycan deposition in cartilage tissue was identified using anti-aggrecan.

Histomorphologic Evaluation

The modified O’Driscoll scoring system was used to evaluate cartilage tissue healing, the amount of repair tissue, and cell quality.11 Each preparation was scored between 0 and 27 points on the basis of criteria such as surface regularity, cartilage morphology, integration of the regenerative structure, its relationship with surrounding tissue, and its distance from healthy cartilage. The connection of the newly formed cartilage with neighboring tissues was separately scored from 0 to 2 as a subparameter of the scoring system. The healing tissue in sections stained with anti-type I collagen, anti-type II collagen, and antiaggrecan antibodies was examined using an Olympus BX51 fluorescence microscope. Collagen I, II, and aggrecan immunoreactivities in the regenerative area were scored on a scale from 0 (none) to 3 (strong).

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics V25 (IBM Corp.). Descriptive statistics were presented as number of units (N), percentage (%), mean, standard deviation, and standard error. The normality of numerical data was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test and Q-Q plots. For variables with normal distribution, between-group comparisons were performed using one-way analysis of variance; when a significant difference was found, multiple comparisons were performed using the Tukey HSD test. The ICC was calculated to evaluate interobserver agreement. P < .05 was considered statistically significant. The data underlying this article will be shared on reasonable request to the corresponding author.

Results

All groups with osteochondral defects were macroscopically evaluated on the basis of the ratio of repair tissue to defect area and ICRS score. In group 1 (SS+MF), it was observed that 79.6% of the defect area was filled with repair tissue, whereas in group 3 (soft tissue preserved [StP]), 67.5%, in group 2 (MF), 51.7%, and in group 4 (control), 36.9% of the defect area was filled with repair tissue (Table 2). The average ICRS scores were 7.08 in group 1 (SS+MF), 6.08 in group 3 (StP), 3.33 in group 2 (MF), and 2.75 in group 4 (control) (Table 2).

When the median of the modified O'Driscoll score between groups was statistically evaluated, group 1 (SS+MF) had significantly greater mean scores compared with all other groups. Group 4 (control) exhibited significantly lower mean scores compared with all other groups. No statistically significant difference was observed between group 2 and group 3 (P = .076). The largest mean difference was observed between group 1 and group 4 (14.83) (Table 3).

Histologic evaluations of the groups revealed that, compared with the healthy control group, cartilage in group 4 was notably irregular and thinner. The defect area was filled with a mixed type of cartilage composed of fibrous and fibrocartilaginous-like tissue. The repaired tissue showed an irregular surface in the central area of the defect. Although group 3 (StP) exhibited relatively better repair, it showed similar characteristics to group 2 (MF). In the superficial layer, the repaired tissue demonstrated weak Masson trichrome staining, whereas in the deeper layers, the cartilage extracellular matrix showed moderate-to-strong Masson trichrome staining. In group 2 (MF), collagen fibril density in the defect area was greater compared with group 3 (StP). In both groups, although the hyaline cartilage area did not completely cover the defect, the regeneration rate in group 3 (StP) was superior to that in group 2 (MF). In group 1 (SS+MF), the repaired cartilage was smoother and more complete, and the repaired subchondral bone area was denser. Hyaline cartilage tissue and subchondral bone plates were observed to be repaired. The repaired tissue exhibited a smooth surface and good integration with the adjacent cartilage. Greater magnification of the repaired tissue revealed that the cells in the healthy hyaline cartilage were arranged in columns (B Column). The morphology of the cartilage cells resembled the morphology of normal chondrocytes. In addition, both the intracellular and extracellular matrices were prominent. Furthermore, it was observed that the cartilage extracellular matrix was normally and uniformly stained with Masson trichrome (Fig 4).

Immunohistochemical Evaluation

Immunohistochemical assessments for type 1 collagen, type 2 collagen, and aggrecan were performed on tissue samples from all groups, with scores ranging from 0 to 3 (0: no reaction, 3: very strong reaction). The average type 1 collagen immunoreactivity of the groups, from strongest to weakest, was as follows: group 1 (mean: 1.67), group 3 (mean: 1.67), group 4 (mean: 1.50), and group 2 (mean: 1.33) (Table 2). After evaluating type 2 collagen, group 1 (SS+MF) showed the strongest immunoreactivity, with an average score of 2.83, whereas group 4 (control) had the weakest immunoreactivity, with an average score of 1.17. Group 3 (StP) scored an average of 2.00, and group 2 (MF) scored an average of 1.67. Excellent interobserver agreement was observed in the type 2 collagen immunoreactivity scores (ICC = 0.88). After the aggrecan evaluation, group 1 (SS+MF) showed the greatest immunoreactivity, with an average score of 2.58. The other groups scored as follows: group 3 (StP) (mean: 2.25), group 2 (MF) (mean: 1.67), and group 4 (control) (mean: 1.33) (Table 2). In the comparison of type 2 collagen and aggrecan scores between groups, group 1 showed significantly greater scores than groups 2 and 4, whereas group 3 had significantly greater scores than group 4. Group 3's scores were greater than those of group 2, although this difference was not statistically significant. Aggrecan immunoreactivity also showed excellent interobserver agreement (ICC = 0.87).

In the immunohistochemical examinations, when the immunoreactivities of type I collagen, type II collagen, and aggrecan in the cartilage repair tissue were evaluated, the distribution of immunopositive cells was observed to be more intense in groups 1 and 3 (Fig 5).

Discussion

In this experimental model, microfracture combined with hip stability achieved through preservation of the soft tissue resulted in superior cartilage repair compared with other interventions. The group with hip stability maintained solely through soft-tissue preservation (group 3) demonstrated better cartilage repair outcomes than the group treated with isolated microfracture (group 2).

The "suction seal" is a biomechanical phenomenon that arises from the interaction between the acetabular labrum and the femoral head, and it describes the role of the labrum and capsule, the soft-tissue components of the hip, in maintaining hip joint stability through negative intra-articular pressure. This effect is important in enhancing joint stability and limiting excessive motion.8,12 Similarly, recent studies have highlighted the role of capsular closure in preventing iatrogenic instability.13

In the present study, the effects of hip stability achieved through preservation of soft tissues and application of the microfracture technique on repair tissue formation in osteochondral defects were evaluated. When Table 2 is examined, it is observed that the defect fill ratio and ICRS scores, used as macroscopic evaluation parameters, were greater in the group where only the SS effect was preserved (group 3) than in the group in which microfracture was applied but the SS was not maintained (group 2). This suggests that the positive impact of SS preservation on cartilage healing may be more prominent than the effect of microfracture, which is already an accepted and established treatment technique. In the comparison of ICRS scores between groups, a statistically significant difference was observed between group 1 and group 2, whereas no statistically significant difference was found between group 1 and group 3. In addition, the difference between group 3 (StP) and group 2 (MF) was statistically significant (P = .048) (Table 3). These findings again demonstrate that preserving the hip stability effect alone yields better outcomes than applying microfracture alone. The favorable results observed in the SS group (Group 3) are also consistent with previous studies indicating that the labrum and capsule function together to provide a more stable hip joint.14,15 When we evaluated cartilage repair quality histologically using the modified O'Driscoll score, as well as type II collagen and aggrecan scores, the greatest scores were consistently observed in group 1, the second greatest in group 3, and the lowest in group 4 (Table 2). This indicates that, from a histologic perspective, preserving the SS effect leads to better outcomes than microfracture alone. The lower quality of repair tissue in cases where microfracture was applied without preserving the SS effect (group 2) highlights the critical role of the hip stability achieved through preservation of soft tissues in joint biomechanics and the healing process. This observation is in line with previous literature supporting the role of SS in regulating intra-articular pressure changes, load distribution, and synovial fluid dynamics.16,17 When compared with the control group (group 4), group 2 (MF) showed significantly greater modified O'Driscoll scores. Similarly, when compared with group 3 (StP), group 1 (SS+MF) also demonstrated significantly greater scores (P < .05) (Table 3). Furthermore, type II collagen immunoreactivity was greater in group 1 (SS+MF) compared with group 3 (StP). These results support the widely accepted short-term benefits of the microfracture technique reported in the literature.18, 19, 20 In addition, previous studies have reported similar or even better outcomes in patients who underwent capsular repair compared to those who did not.21,22 Domb et al.22 reported more cartilage defects in the groups without capsule repair. Also, Maldonado et al.21 showed that the condition of the femoral head cartilage is a predictor of secondary surgical procedures after at least 5 years of follow-up. In our study, the better macroscopic and microscopic healing of cartilage in the groups with capsule closure (group 1 and group 3) demonstrates the clinical importance of capsule closure and the "suction seal" (SS) effect. Soft-tissue preservation helps maintain the suction seal, which in turn helps retain joint fluid in the defect area, allowing stem cells, growth factors, and nourishing molecules to remain in this region for a longer period. In addition it reduces the negative effects of intra-articular pressure changes on the repair tissue, thus creating a stable microenvironment at the defect site. In our study, the best scores were achieved in group 1, the second-best scores in group 3, and the worst scores in group 4 in all macroscopic and microscopic evaluations (Table 2). This shows that both the greatest proportion of repair and the repair closest to the original cartilage tissue were achieved by applying the microfracture procedure while preserving the SS effect. This highlights the importance of maintaining the SS effect in every cartilage injury procedure where microfracture is applied in current practice. Although our results suggest that preservation of soft-tissue structures and the resulting suction seal effect contribute to improved cartilage healing, we did not include groups that would allow us to evaluate the individual effects of labral repair/reconstruction or capsular closure. Although the study was designed to compare fully preserved and fully disrupted soft-tissue conditions to better observe the effect of soft-tissue preservation and suction seal, we did not incorporate intermediate groups that could help isolate the separate contributions of the labrum and capsule. Future studies including such models are necessary to provide more clinically relevant insights.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, an acute osteochondral defect model was utilized, which may not fully replicate the chronic cartilage lesions commonly observed in human clinical settings. Second, although numerous previous studies have conducted histological assessments at the 4-week time point in rabbit models, the relatively short follow-up period in our study limited the evaluation to early-stage cartilage healing.23, 24, 25 Third, while the cartilage defect created in this study may correspond to a weight-bearing region in bipedal humans, anatomical and postural differences between bipeds and quadrupeds mean that this localization may not accurately reflect a true weight-bearing zone in rabbits; therefore, this may affect the translational applicability of the findings to humans. Fourth, the absence of additional experimental groups such as labral excision combined with capsular repair or labral repair/reconstruction with capsular repair limits the capacity to compare the findings with current clinical practice.

Conclusions

Preservation of the hip stability enhances the effectiveness of microfracture treatment, promoting superior cartilage regeneration compared to microfracture alone in a rabbit model.
