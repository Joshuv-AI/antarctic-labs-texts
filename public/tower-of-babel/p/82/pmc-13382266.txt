Whole-brain biodistribution of AAV2, AAV8, and AAV-B10 after unilateral intracerebroventricular injection
Authors: Dezhuang Ye, Abhinav Kayithi, Jinyun Yuan, Isaac Wang, Tianqi Xu, Marc Boisvert, Marie-Eve Paquet, Hong Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdeno-associated virus (AAV) vectors represent promising tools for central nervous system (CNS) gene therapy; however, achieving efficient and widespread brain gene distribution while minimizing peripheral off-target exposure remains challenging. This study compares the brain and peripheral biodistribution patterns of three AAV serotypes, AAV2, AAV8, and AAV-B10, following intracerebroventricular (i.c.v.) administration in adult female mice. Using advanced tissue clearing methods with high-resolution light-sheet fluorescence microscopy, we visualized whole-brain AAV enhanced green fluorescent protein (eGFP) reporter distribution at single-cell resolution and employed semi-automated spatial quantification to analyze regional eGFP reporter signal. Each vector carried identical eGFP expression cassettes under the ubiquitous CAG promoter. AAV-B10 demonstrated superior parenchymal eGFP signal across multiple brain regions, with significantly higher expression in the cortex, midbrain, striatum, and thalamus compared to other serotypes. In contrast, AAV2 exhibited distinctive tropism for ventricular structures, with robust eGFP signal of ependymal cells lining the ventricular walls and choroid plexus. AAV8 showed intermediate eGFP distribution targeting the choroid plexus epithelium. Following i.c.v. administration, peripheral leakage of AAV genomes was observed, with AAV8 showing strong liver enrichment of eGFP transgene and AAV-B10 displaying broader yet weaker distribution across peripheral tissues. These serotype-specific distribution profiles provide critical insights for optimizing vector selection based on therapeutic requirements and target regions, potentially enhancing the efficacy of AAV-based gene therapies for neurological disorders.Keywords: adeno-associated virus, AAV, intracerebroventricular administration, glymphatic transport, brain, gene therapy, serotypes, ventricular system tropism, AAV biodistribution, tissue clearing, light-sheet microscopy

Introduction

Adeno-associated virus (AAV) vectors are among the most promising tools for central nervous system (CNS) gene therapy, offering long-term transgene expression, low immunogenicity, and the ability to transduce both neuronal and glial cells.1,2,3,4 Current CNS-targeting strategies employ several delivery routes, including intravenous (i.v.), intraparenchymal, and intra-cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) administration.5 An i.v. delivery is minimally invasive but suffers from two major limitations: the blood-brain barrier (BBB) restricts vector entry into the CNS, and the high systemic doses required to achieve therapeutic brain levels increase the risk of peripheral toxicity.6,7,8 In contrast, intraparenchymal injection bypasses the BBB locally but is inherently invasive and yields only limited vector spread, often requiring multiple injection sites to reach therapeutically relevant volumes.5 CSF-based delivery methods, including intrathecal, cisterna magna, and intracerebroventricular (i.c.v.) injections, have emerged as attractive alternatives because they bypass the BBB and enable access to widespread CNS regions while reducing peripheral exposure.5 Among these CSF-targeted approaches, i.c.v. delivery is particularly notable in two key respects: first, it provides direct access to the lateral ventricles, the primary site of CSF production, facilitating brain-wide vector distribution through natural CSF circulation. An i.c.v. delivery enables therapeutic vectors to reach multiple brain regions simultaneously, eliminating the need for multiple injections. Second, i.c.v. delivery can strategically target ependymal cells and choroid plexus, creating biological “protein pumps” that continuously secrete therapeutic proteins into the CSF.9 For example, this mechanism could be effective for treating enzyme deficiency disorders like lysosomal storage diseases through paracrine signaling, where secreted proteins can diffuse throughout the brain and be taken up by surrounding cells.10

The efficiency of CNS gene delivery via i.c.v. depends critically on AAV serotype, as capsid characteristics influence vector tropism, diffusion, and cellular targeting.3,11,12,13 In neonatal animals, comparative studies have demonstrated that serotypes such as AAV8 and AAV9 achieve much broader distribution than AAV1, AAV2, AAV5, or AAV7 following i.c.v. injection.14 However, these serotype evaluation studies for i.c.v. injection have been performed mainly in neonatal or juvenile animals with limited studies performed in adult animals.14,15,16,17 Developmental stage influences both the distribution and the cellular tropism of AAV vectors in the CNS.14 Since the adult brain differs markedly from the developing brain in CSF dynamics, ependymal surface area, myelination patterns, and barrier properties, findings from early developmental stages may not directly translate to adult applications. Given that many intended recipients of CNS gene therapy are adults, there is a pressing need to evaluate the outcomes of i.c.v. delivery with diverse AAV serotypes in adult animal models.

The present study, therefore, compares the serotype-dependent patterns of whole-brain eGFP reporter distribution produced by three representative AAV vectors, AAV2, AAV8, and the engineered capsid AAV-B10, following i.c.v. delivery in adult mice. Each vector carried an identical eGFP reporter driven by the ubiquitous CAG promoter. We employed whole-brain tissue clearing combined with high-resolution light-sheet fluorescence microscopy for three-dimensional (3D) imaging at single-cell resolution, coupled with semi-automated spatial quantification to analyze eGFP reporter signal distribution across distinct brain regions. Peripheral organ biodistribution was also assessed to evaluate off-target effects. These results provide detailed insights into serotype-specific transduction profiles in the adult brain, guiding the rational selection of vectors for CSF-based therapies in neurodegenerative and neurodevelopmental diseases.

Results

AAV serotype-specific brain distribution following i.c.v. administration

To visualize AAV distribution throughout the brain, we performed light-sheet microscopy on cleared whole-brain specimens from all experimental groups (control without AAV injection: n = 4, AAV2: n = 5, AAV8: n = 5, AAV-B10: n = 4) to visualize the eGFP gene expression across anatomically distinct brain regions. The eGFP signal was used as a reporter of AAV-mediated gene transduction. The eGFP fluorescence intensity across different brain regions was quantified as the ratio of eGFP-positive pixels to the total pixel number of a specific brain region.

Quantitative comparison of eGFP expression (Table S1) across different brain regions demonstrated that AAV-B10 achieved significantly superior eGFP reporter signal in multiple regions compared to other serotypes (Figure 1B). In the cortex, AAV-B10 showed significantly higher expression (mean ± SD: 0.05667 ± 0.03352 normalized fluorescence units) compared to control (0.000071 ± 0.0000879, p < 0.001), AAV2 (0.004849 ± 0.00354, p = 0.0011), and AAV8 (0.002202 ± 0.001008, p < 0.001). Similar patterns were observed in the midbrain, striatum, and thalamus, with AAV-B10 consistently demonstrating significantly higher expression across all regions. Across the whole brain, AAV-B10 exhibited significantly higher overall eGFP reporter signal compared to control (0.05163 ± 0.03177, p = 0.0014), AAV2 (0.01028 ± 0.007068, p = 0.0058), and AAV8 (0.003016 ± 0.001516, p = 0.0015). Both AAV2 and AAV8 exhibited limited parenchymal eGFP reporter signal. AAV2 showed slightly higher expression, primarily within cortical, striatal, and thalamic areas, although these differences were not statistically significant compared with either the control or AAV8.

Interestingly, the viral distribution within the ventricular system revealed a notably different pattern (Figure 1C). AAV2 demonstrated the strongest ventricular tropism overall, with significant elevation over control and AAV8 in the total ventricular system. Differences between AAV2 and AAV-B10 were compartment-dependent, with the most prominent AAV2 enrichment observed in the third ventricle and cerebral aqueduct. In terms of the overall ventricular system, AAV2 (0.1243 ± 0.0422) showed significantly higher expression compared to both control (0.00001825 ± 0.00003584, p = 0.0062) and AAV8 (0.02814 ± 0.01314, p = 0.0243), while AAV-B10 (0.08614 ± 0.08567) showed moderate but non-significant ventricular expression.

Microscopic comparison of AAV serotype-specific eGFP reporter patterns of brain parenchymal

To further characterize the cellular tropism and detailed distribution patterns of each AAV serotype, we examined higher-magnification microscopic images from the same brain specimens shown in Figure 1, focusing on selected regions of interest. It should be noted that, in the absence of secondary immunolabeling, all cell-type identification in this analysis relies on cellular morphology and anatomical location rather than direct marker-based evidence.

Figure 2 presents a microscopic comparison of different AAV serotypes (AAV2, AAV8, and AAV-B10) following i.c.v. injection. Horizontal brain sections (first three panels for each serotype) indicated via colored boxes, the region of interest (ROIs) from which high-magnification images were obtained, enabling evaluation of regional and presumed cell-type-specific transduction. AAV2 exhibits limited parenchymal eGFP reporter signal besides olfactory and cerebellum regions. In the olfactory bulb, occasional eGFP-positive cells and their associated fibers were observed, consistent with reporter expression in neuronal-like cells and their processes, while the cerebellum displays only sparse fluorescence of uncertain cell type (Figure 2B). AAV8 similarly demonstrates minimal parenchymal eGFP reporter signal throughout the brain, with no detectable eGFP-positive cells observed in most brain regions. Only in the cerebellum were sparse, weakly labeled cells occasionally detected, whose cell type could not be definitively determined (Figure 2C).

AAV-B10 displays the most extensive and robust eGFP reporter expression pattern across brain parenchyma (Figure 2D). In the cortex, it induces strong eGFP expression in cells with morphological features suggestive of neurons and, in some cases, larger, rounded cells with multiple processes (white arrow) that may resemble astrocytes, although additional studies are needed to definitively identify these cell types. The cerebellum shows prominent labeling in cells morphologically consistent with neurons alongside linear, stripe-like eGFP-positive structures (white arrow), which may correspond to anatomical features such as parallel fibers, climbing fibers, and white matter tracts. This pattern suggests that eGFP may be distributed throughout neuronal processes traversing these regions, although additional marker-based analyses would be required to determine whether the corresponding cell bodies are locally transduced or located in other brain regions. In the striatum, eGFP fluorescence predominantly highlights fiber-rich areas, rather than discrete cell bodies, and the overall intensity of the eGFP signal is relatively weak compared to other regions. This pattern suggests that the observed labeling likely results from the transport of eGFP along axonal processes originating from neurons in other brain areas. Similarly, in the midbrain, eGFP labeling is largely restricted to fiber tracts and neuropil, with only limited labeling of cell bodies and generally weaker fluorescence. The thalamus exhibits clear eGFP-positive cells with morphological features typical of neurons (prominent cell bodies and dendritic processes), accompanied by additional labeling of fiber-like structures. The olfactory bulb displays robust cellular transduction, with eGFP clearly localized to cellular somata and elaborate dendritic arbors, suggesting effective reporter expression in neuronal-like cells and their processes within the olfactory bulb.

Overall, AAV-B10 achieved broader and deeper brain parenchymal eGFP reporter distribution, with GFP-positive structures including both cell-body-like and fiber-like morphologies based on morphological analysis. In contrast, AAV2 appeared to favor neuronal-like cells with restricted distribution, and AAV8 showed minimal parenchymal access. These findings suggest AAV-B10 to be a promising vector for broad CNS gene delivery targeting diverse neural populations and connectivity patterns through i.c.v. injection.

Differential tropism of AAV serotypes in ventricular structure transduction

To evaluate serotype-specific targeting of ventricular structures, we performed aquaporin-1 (AQP1) immunostaining of choroid plexus epithelium in combination with high-resolution 3D light-sheet microscopy (12×). The choroid plexus, a specialized epithelial structure responsible for CSF production and forming the blood-CSF barrier, was labeled with AQP1 (red) to specifically identify these CSF-secreting cells, while eGFP (green) indicated AAV-mediated reporter gene expression. AAV vectors were injected into the right lateral ventricle. Representative images of the lateral ventricular choroid plexus shown in Figure 3A were selected from regions away from the injection site to minimize the influence of a strong local eGFP signal along the needle tract. Importantly, quantitative analysis of lateral ventricular choroid plexus transduction included both the injection-side and contralateral lateral ventricles. Transduction patterns were further assessed in the third ventricle (Figure 3B) and fourth ventricle (Figure 3C). Negative control images confirm the specificity of both eGFP and AQP1 signals, with no detectable eGFP fluorescence in the control group.

AAV2 exhibits the most extensive ventricular tropism, with robust eGFP expression observed throughout all ventricular compartments. Strong eGFP signal colocalizes extensively with AQP1-positive choroid plexus structures in the lateral, third, and fourth ventricles. Most notably, AAV2 produced strong eGFP signal along the ependymal cell layer lining the ventricular walls, producing a continuous eGFP signal that delineates a distinct boundary at the ventricle-parenchyma interface (Videos S2 and S6), highlighting its exceptional capacity for transducing both choroid plexus and ependymal cells in ventricles.

In contrast, AAV8 demonstrates a markedly restricted ventricular tropism with limited cellular targeting. The eGFP signal was weak and primarily confined to restricted regions within the lateral ventricle choroid plexus (Figure 3A). The choroid plexus located in the third and fourth ventricles showed only faint eGFP signals slightly above control levels (Figures 3B and 3C). Unlike AAV2, AAV8 fails to transduce the ependymal cell layer within all the ventricular compartments. The high magnification visual observation is consistent with the quantification shown in Figure 1C, where AAV8 ventricular transduction is not significantly different from controls. This pattern reflects an intrinsic serotype-specific cellular selectivity rather than accessibility issues, as AAV8 is circulating in the CSF but displays minimal effective transduction in ventricular structures.

AAV-B10 exhibits intermediate transduction of the choroid plexus epithelium but reduced ependymal cell targeting compared to AAV2. While AAV-B10 shows superior and widespread transduction efficiency in brain parenchyma regions, its efficacy in ventricular compartments is comparatively limited, particularly for cells lining the ventricular walls.

Biodistribution of AAV in major organs following i.c.v. injection

To assess potential off-target effects of AAV vectors following i.c.v. administration, we quantified vector genome copies in major peripheral organs using droplet digital PCR (ddPCR). This highly sensitive method allowed precise measurement of transgene copy numbers across diverse tissue types, providing critical information about the extent of vector leakage from the CNS into systemic circulation (Table S2). Figure 4 illustrates the biodistribution of different AAV serotypes (AAV2, AAV8, and AAV-B10) in major organs following i.c.v. injection compared to control (control: n = 4, AAV2: n = 5, AAV8: n = 5, AAV-B10: n = 5).

Differential biodistribution of AAV serotypes in peripheral organs following i.c.v. injection

Quantitative analysis of eGFP transgene concentrations (copies per ng genomic DNA) in major organs (liver, lung, heart, kidney, spleen, and intestine) following intracerebroventricular injection of different AAV serotypes (control, AAV2, AAV8, and AAV-B10) to adult female mice. AAV8 demonstrated remarkable hepatotropism with significantly higher liver accumulation compared to other serotypes, while AAV-B10 showed superior accumulation in lung, heart, kidney, and spleen. Intestine showed minimal differences between serotypes (data are presented as mean ± SD with individual data points overlaid. Statistical significance determined by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test: ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001).

In the liver, AAV8 demonstrated the highest hepatotropism with significantly higher eGFP transgene copies compared to control and other serotypes: control (ratio of mean eGFP DNA concentrations, AAV8/control = 3994, p < 0.0001); AAV2 (AAV8/AAV2 = 441, p < 0.0001); and AAV-B10 (AAV8/AAV-B10 = 602, p < 0.0001). In contrast, AAV-B10 demonstrated superior accumulation in multiple organs.

In the lung, AAV-B10 showed significantly higher transgene accumulation compared to control (AAV-B10/control = 11.3, p < 0.001); AAV2 (AAV-B10/AAV2 = 29.1, p < 0.001); and AAV8 (AAV-B10/AAV8 = 3.8, p = 0.0027).

Similarly, in the heart, AAV-B10 exhibited substantially higher cardiac accumulation compared to control (AAV-B10/control = 16.8, p < 0.001); AAV2 (AAV-B10/AAV2 = 50.5, p < 0.001); and AAV8 (AAV-B10/AAV8 = 7.1, p < 0.001).

In the kidney, AAV-B10 also demonstrated superior accumulation compared to control (AAV-B10/control = 5.3, p = 0.0028) and AAV2 (AAV-B10/AAV2 = 14.5, p < 0.001). The difference between AAV-B10 and AAV8 in kidney accumulation approached but did not reach statistical significance (AAV-B10/AAV8 = 2.0, p = 0.0585).

In the spleen, moderate differences were observed. AAV-B10 showed higher splenic accumulation than AAV2 (AAV-B10/AAV2 = 48.5, p = 0.0491). The differences between AAV-B10 and control (AAV-B10/control = 80.2, p = 0.0640) and between AAV-B10 and AAV8 (AAV-B10/AAV8 = 32.7, p = 0.0518) approached but did not reach statistical significance.

In the intestine, despite an overall significant difference detected by ANOVA (p = 0.0496), post hoc analysis revealed no statistically significant differences between individual serotypes. AAV8 showed the highest mean accumulation, with the difference between AAV8 and AAV2 approaching significance (AAV8/AAV2 = 4.1, p = 0.0508).

Notably, while AAV-B10 demonstrated higher accumulation in multiple peripheral organs, the absolute transgene copy numbers remained relatively modest compared to the exceptional hepatotropism of AAV8. Even the highest mean peripheral eGFP transgene copy level observed for AAV-B10 was approximately 77-fold lower than the corresponding level detected for AAV8 in the liver.

Discussion

This study compared the brain and peripheral biodistribution profiles of AAV2, AAV8, and AAV-B10 following i.c.v. administration in adult mice. It revealed distinct serotype-specific distribution patterns that have important implications for CNS gene therapy applications. By utilizing advanced tissue clearing techniques combined with high-resolution light-sheet microscopy, we achieved 3D visualization at single-cell resolution and performed semi-automated quantitative analysis of eGFP reporter-signal distribution across intact brain tissue. The differences in cellular tropism and regional specificity observed among these serotypes underscore the importance of serotype selection in optimizing therapeutic outcomes for specific neurological disorders.

The quantitative biodistribution data and high-resolution imaging demonstrate that AAV-B10 achieves superior parenchymal eGFP reporter signal throughout multiple brain regions compared to conventional serotypes. Widespread eGFP reporter distribution and expression after i.c.v. delivery has primarily been reported in neonatal mice, as mature barriers limit homogeneous gene transduction in adult animals.14,15,16,17 Our comprehensive 3D whole-brain evaluation demonstrated that AAV-B10 achieves highly effective and widespread gene transduction throughout the adult female mouse brain after i.c.v. injection. While a prior study by Chauhan et al. showed that AAV-DJ delivery via i.c.v. injection in adult mice can transduce different brain regions, their analysis used conventional sectioning and fluorescence microscopy with inherently limited spatial coverage.18 In contrast, our whole-brain imaging approach, combined with semi-automated spatial quantification, revealed that AAV-B10 achieves uniform and intense transduction across the cortex, midbrain, striatum, and thalamus. This comprehensive spatial analysis enables the detection of regional distribution patterns that might be missed using conventional section-based approaches. The demonstration of uniform transduction across multiple brain regions in adult animals has important implications for clinical translation. Many neurological disorders, including neurodegenerative diseases, metabolic disorders, and epilepsy, affect distributed neural networks rather than focal brain regions. Our findings suggest that i.c.v. delivery with appropriate vector selection could provide therapeutic coverage for such widespread pathologies using a single injection approach, offering advantages over stereotactic approaches requiring multiple injection sites.

Previous studies using i.v. administration have demonstrated that AAV-B10 exhibits significantly enhanced penetration of the CNS, achieving brain-wide transduction with a strong neuronal preference and markedly reduced off-target expression in peripheral organs, particularly the liver.19,20 Our current results extend these findings by showing that AAV-B10 also displays robust and widespread transgene expression throughout the brain in adult female mice following i.c.v. administration, while only low levels of vector genomes are detected in peripheral organs, including the liver. Although we did not perform cell-type-specific immunostaining in this study, qualitative assessment of cellular morphology suggests that neuron-like cells predominantly comprise the transduced population, which is consistent with the previously reported neuronal bias observed after i.v. delivery of AAV-B10. These observations indicate that the capsid modifications responsible for improved BBB traversal may likewise facilitate efficient passage across other mature CNS barriers, such as the ependymal or epithelial layers present in adult animals. It is plausible that these processes rely on similar molecular interactions, particularly the binding of AAV-B10 capsids to cell surface receptors or associated molecules that regulate entry across different types of biological barriers. Nevertheless, the precise mechanisms underlying this broad parenchymal distribution remain to be fully elucidated. Several potential pathways may contribute, including vector transit from the ventricular system into systemic circulation, followed by enhanced BBB re-entry, glymphatic transport facilitating direct migration from CSF into parenchyma along perivascular spaces,21 and axonal transport from initially transduced cells.22 Our microscopic analysis reveals GFP-positive structures with morphologies consistent with both neuronal and glial cell populations, indicating that axonal transport alone cannot fully explain the widespread distribution pattern observed. Further mechanistic studies are needed to elucidate the relative contributions of these pathways to the exceptional parenchymal penetration of AAV-B10.

AAV2 showed robust expression in ventricular structures, particularly the extensive and continuous eGFP labeling along the ependymal lining visualized in our brain images and videos, which represents a unique therapeutic opportunity. Our study provides a comprehensive 3D visualization of complete ventricular wall transduction (Videos S2 and S6), revealing the extent to which AAV2 can target this specialized cell population throughout the ventricular system. The ependymal cells lining the ventricular system play crucial roles in CSF dynamics and serve as a potential “protein factory” for secreting therapeutic proteins into the cerebrospinal fluid. The therapeutic implications of this tropism pattern are particularly relevant for enzyme replacement therapies and diseases where protein secretion into CSF can provide widespread therapeutic benefit. For lysosomal storage diseases such as mucopolysaccharidosis, metachromatic leukodystrophy, or neuronal ceroid lipofuscinosis, AAV2-mediated delivery of recombinant enzymes to ependymal cells could facilitate enzyme distribution throughout the CNS via CSF circulation. The rapid turnover of CSF (three to five times daily) that typically limits drug retention may enhance therapeutic protein distribution when the protein source is anchored in ependymal cells.23 Furthermore, the choroid plexus, which AAV2 also efficiently transduces, could serve as a complementary CSF secretion source alongside ependymal cells.3,24

Age-dependent differences in transduction were evident when comparing our adult mouse data with previously published neonatal studies. In neonatal animals, particularly at postnatal day 0 (P0), i.c.v.-delivered AAV8 has been shown to produce widespread neuronal transduction across the CNS.14 In contrast, in our adult female mice, AAV8 exhibited markedly restricted parenchymal penetration with greatly diminished brain-wide distribution. This observation is consistent with reports that AAV biodistribution becomes progressively more limited as animals mature, likely due to several converging factors: the maturation and tightening of the ependymal cell lining, alterations in CSF flow dynamics, and other age-related changes in brain barrier structures that collectively reduce vector migration from ventricular CSF into brain parenchyma.14 Notably, the superior performance of AAV-B10 in adult female mice suggests that engineered capsids may better overcome these mature barriers that limit conventional serotypes in the adult CNS.

Despite direct i.c.v. administration targeting the brain, our analysis revealed significant peripheral organ distribution with distinct serotype-specific patterns. AAV8 demonstrated extraordinary hepatotropism, showing dramatically higher hepatic accumulation than all other serotypes tested. This pronounced liver targeting following i.c.v. delivery underscores the challenge of restricting gene delivery to the CNS and raises critical safety considerations regarding off-target effects. The mechanisms by which i.c.v.-injected AAV reaches peripheral tissues likely involve multiple pathways: cerebrospinal fluid drainage through arachnoid granulations into venous circulation, lymphatic clearance via cribriform plate routes, and possible direct vascular uptake at the blood-cerebrospinal fluid barrier.25,26 This substantial peripheral distribution indicates that viral vectors can access systemic circulation while maintaining their inherent tissue tropism. Each serotype showed distinct organ preferences consistent with known tropisms. AAV8 exhibited extraordinary hepatic targeting, consistent with its well-characterized liver tropism.27 In contrast, AAV-B10 demonstrated significantly enhanced accumulation across multiple peripheral organs, including the heart, kidney, lung, and spleen. Notably, although AAV-B10 distributed more widely to peripheral organs, absolute transgene copy numbers remained markedly lower than the hepatic levels observed with AAV8. For example, AAV-B10 reached its highest peripheral eGFP transgene copy level in the heart, yet this level was still approximately 77-fold lower than the expression of AAV8 in the liver. This pattern suggests AAV-B10 presents a more balanced peripheral distribution profile without the extreme organ-specific accumulation characteristic of AAV8’s hepatotropism. Previous studies comparing AAV-B10 to AAV9 following i.v. administration found that AAV-B10 had significantly reduced targeting to various non-CNS organs.20 This relatively modest off-target expression profile, combined with its enhanced CNS penetration capabilities, may underlie the improved safety and efficacy balance attributed to AAV-B10, although further dedicated safety evaluations are necessary to substantiate this potential advantage.

Our findings provide important guidance for clinical vector selection among the serotypes evaluated in this study. For applications requiring broad CNS transduction, such as gene replacement for metabolic disorders affecting multiple brain regions, AAV-B10 emerges as the preferred choice despite moderate peripheral exposure. For applications specifically targeting CSF-accessible cell populations or requiring secreted therapeutic proteins, AAV2 with ventricular tropism offers unique advantages.

This study has several limitations. First, we utilized only a single mouse strain (C57BL/6) and a single sex. Previous studies have shown that AAV tropism can differ across genetic backgrounds and between sexes.28,29 Future work is needed to evaluate serotype performance in multiple strains and both sexes to better predict outcomes for clinical translation. Second, due to the scope of this study, our comparative analysis focused on three representative serotypes (AAV2, AAV8, and AAV-B10). Future studies should evaluate a broader panel of AAV vectors, including established natural serotypes (e.g., AAV9) and engineered variants (e.g., AAV-DJ, AAV-PHP.eB, and AAV-PHP.S), to better guide vector selection for i.c.v. delivery in CNS gene therapy. Third, neuronal and glial cell populations were primarily identified based on morphological and locational criteria after whole-brain tissue clearing. To further improve the precision of cell-type identification, future studies could integrate tissue clearing with compatible immunostaining or multiplexed in situ hybridization approaches. Last, whole-brain eGFP-positive pixel quantification used in this study cannot distinguish eGFP localized to transduced cell bodies from eGFP present in axons or other cellular processes. Future studies need to incorporate nuclear counterstaining, cell-body segmentation, and cell-type-specific markers to determine the precise cellular tropism and regional transduction efficiency of each serotype.

Materials and methods

Animal and study design

All animal procedures were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Washington University in St. Louis, in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guidelines for animal research. Female C57BL/6 mice (10–12 weeks, ∼25 g body weight) were purchased from Charles River Laboratory (Wilmington, MA, USA). Animals were housed in a temperature-controlled environment (22°C ± 2°C) with 55% relative humidity, maintained on a 12-h/12-h light/dark cycle, and provided access to standard laboratory chow and water ad libitum. Mice were randomly divided into four experimental groups: (1) Control (n = 4, no AAV delivery); (2) AAV2 (n = 5, i.c.v. injection of AAV2 to the right lateral ventricle); (3) AAV8 (n = 5, i.c.v. injection of AAV8 to the right lateral ventricle); and (4) AAV-B10 (n = 5, i.c.v. injection of AAV-B10 to the right lateral ventricle). One brain from group 4 was excluded from quantification analyses in Figure 1 due to tissue processing issues. The administered vector genome dose was standardized across all AAV groups.

AAVs

AAV-B10 vectors were generated using RepCap-B10 plasmids originally developed by the Gradinaru laboratory20 and provided by COVF Viral Vector Core (RRID: SCR_016477). The following viral preparations were used: AAV2-CAG-eGFP (6.0 × 1012 CG/mL), AAV-B10-CAG-eGFP (1.0 × 1013 CG/mL), and AAV8-CAG-eGFP (6.1 × 1012 CG/mL). The titer of the viral vectors was provided by the manufacturer. All vectors carried identical expression cassettes containing eGFP under the control of the ubiquitous CAG promoter. Prior to use, the AAVs were aliquoted into single-use 10 μL volumes and stored at −80°C. Each mouse received 9.0 × 109 viral genome copies regardless of serotype, with injection volumes adjusted accordingly to standardize viral load across experimental groups.

i.c.v. injection of AAVs

One day prior to vector administration, mice underwent magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) to precisely locate the right lateral ventricle relative to skull landmarks (bregma and lambda). T2-weighted images were acquired using a 9.4T small animal MRI scanner (Bruker, Billerica, MA, USA) with a mouse brain surface coil. The acquired images were used to determine the stereotaxic coordinates and injection depth for each animal. For the i.c.v. injection procedure, mice were anesthetized via intramuscular (i.m.) injection of a ketamine (0.10 mg/g body weight) and xylazine (0.01 mg/g body weight) mixture. Before the virus injection, buprenorphine (buprenex, 0.1 μg/g body weight) and carprofen (Rimadyl, 5 μg/g body weight) were injected subcutaneously. During injection, the mouse’s head was secured in a stereotaxic frame (David Kopf Instruments, Tujunga, CA, USA). The surgical site was cleaned with povidone-iodine solution followed by 70% ethanol. A short midline scalp incision was made to expose the skull, followed by drilling of a burr hole with a 0.7-mm drill bit at the predetermined coordinates targeting the right lateral ventricle. AAV vectors were injected using a microinjector (Nanoject II, Drummond Scientific, Broomall, PA, USA) with a pulled glass capillary. The injection needle was slowly lowered to the predetermined depth, and the vector solution was delivered at a rate of 0.69 μL/min. Following injection, the needle was maintained in position for 10 min to allow for vector diffusion and then slowly withdrawn at a rate of approximately 0.5 mm/min to minimize reflux. The scalp was closed with veterinary tissue adhesive (Vetbond, 3M, St. Paul, MN, USA) followed by sutures, and the animal was placed on a heating pad for recovery. Following the surgery, sulfamethoxazole (1 mg/mL), trimethoprim (0.2 mg/mL), and carprofen (0.1 mg/mL) were chronically administered in the drinking water throughout the experiment for anti-inflammatory and antibiotic purposes. Mice were sacrificed 4r weeks post-injection to allow for optimal transgene expression. Brains and major organs were collected for subsequent analyses.

Tissue clearance

For whole-brain tissue clearing, samples were processed using a modified LifeCanvas Technologies protocol. Briefly, animals were transcardially perfused with ice-cold PBS followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), with brains post-fixed in 4% PFA overnight at 4°C. Tissues then underwent SHIELD preservation by incubation in SHIELD-OFF solution for 48 h at 4°C with gentle shaking, followed by SHIELD-ON solution at 37°C for 24 h to initiate stabilization chemistry. For delipidation, samples were immersed in LifeCanvas Delipidation Buffer (non-SDS based) and processed actively using a SmartClear II Pro electrophoretic device (45°C for 30 h). Samples were washed with PBS for 24 h, and were refractive index matched in EasyIndex RI matching solution.

Immunostaining of choroid plexus

For visualization of the choroid plexus, SHIELD-cleared whole brains were immunostained for AQP1. Tissues were first permeabilized in a solution containing 0.2% v/v Triton X-100 and 10% v/v DMSO in 1× PBS for 2 days at 37°C with gentle rocking. Following permeabilization, tissues were transferred to a blocking solution comprising 10% v/v donkey serum and 0.2% v/v Triton X-100 in 1× PBS and incubated overnight at room temperature. For primary antibody labeling, tissues were incubated with rabbit polyclonal anti-AQP1 antibody (1:200 dilution; AB3272, Millipore) in a solution containing 10% donkey serum and 0.2% Triton X-100 in 1× PBS at 37°C for 4 days with gentle rocking. The containers were sealed with plastic wrap to prevent evaporation during this extended incubation. After primary antibody incubation, tissues were transferred to 50 mL tubes and thoroughly washed in 1× PBS for 24 h with a minimum of 5–6 solution exchanges. Subsequently, tissues were incubated with Alexa Fluor 647-conjugated donkey anti-rabbit secondary antibody (1:100 dilution; Jackson ImmunoResearch) in 1× PBS at 37°C for 4 days with gentle rocking. Following secondary antibody incubation, tissues were again washed in 50 mL tubes with 1× PBS for 24 h with 5–6 solution exchanges to reduce background signal. Finally, tissues were immersed in EasyIndex refractive index matching solution for at least 24 h before imaging.

Light sheet microscopy

Cleared whole-brain specimens were excited using dual-sided illumination and visualized on a Miltenyi Ultra Microscope Blaze light sheet microscope (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany). For whole-brain scanning, a 4×/0.35 NA detection objective was used with 488 nm excitation and 525 nm emission, and images were acquired with 1.625 × 1.625 × 2.4 μm voxel resolution. For high-resolution imaging of ventricular structures, a 12×/0.53 NA detection objective was used with 488 nm excitation and 525 nm emission and 640 nm excitation and 680 nm emission. The images were acquired with 0.54 × 0.54 × 2 μm voxel resolution. All acquisition parameters, including exposure time, laser power, and z-step size, were kept consistent across all samples within each magnification setting. All images were acquired using Imspector v.7.7.2, stitched with Stitchy v.1.8 (Translucence Biosystems), and exported to Imaris (.ims) format for downstream analysis.

Fluorescence quantification

Whole-brain images were processed through a multi-step pipeline for accurate quantification of fluorescence distribution following the published protocol.30 Briefly, after light-sheet imaging, images were processed in Imaris with consistent parameters adjusted for eGFP visualization across all samples. The 3D whole brain images were then converted to downsized tagged image file format (TIFF) stacks using a custom MATLAB script, allowing selection of specific frames and intervals to standardize the analysis to 51 representative slices per brain. These downsized TIFF stacks were subsequently processed in Fiji/ImageJ software for conversion to portable network graphics (PNG) format compatible with segmentation and atlas registration software, maintaining uniform contrast parameters across all samples. Two parallel workflows were then implemented: segmentation and atlas registration. For segmentation, Ilastik software was employed to differentiate eGFP-expressing foreground from background tissue. This machine learning-based approach utilized 20 training images to establish parameters for automated segmentation across all 1,173 images (23 brains × 51 slices), followed by batch processing in Fiji/ImageJ software using the “Glasby” function to generate segmentations with color-coded foreground (blue) and background (red). For atlas registration, the preprocessed images were proportionally downsized to meet the 16-megapixel requirements of the registration software. An extensible markup language (XML) descriptor file was created for each brain using FileBuilder, followed by linear registration to the Allen Brain Mouse Atlas 2017 using QUICKNII, generating a javascript object notation (JSON) descriptor. Further refinement through VisuAlign allowed precise non-linear alignment to compensate for anatomical variations between the atlas and individual brain samples and generated a new, modified JSON descriptor. The quantification process integrated the segmentations, atlas maps, VisuAlign JSON descriptors, and foreground color information using Nutil software. This generated comprehensive quantitative data on eGFP distribution, expressed as “load” (ratio of eGFP pixels to total object pixels) for both general brain regions (custom report) and specific neuroanatomical structures (reference report). The resulting data was compiled into Excel format for subsequent analysis and comparison across samples. This systematic approach enabled detailed spatial quantification of AAV-mediated gene expression throughout the entire brain, providing critical information on viral vector biodistribution patterns.

eGFP transgene quantification with ddPCR

Custom sequence-specific primers and fluorescent probes were designed and synthesized for eGFP detection. The forward and reverse primer sequences for eGFP are 5′-GACCACTACCAGCAGAACACC-3' and 5′-CCAGCAGGACCATGTGATCG-3′, respectively. The eGFP probe sequence is 5′-CCGACAACCACTACCTGAGCACCCAGTC-3' with the hexachlorofluorescein (HEX) fluorophore and the Black Hole Quencher 1 (BHQ1). Genomic DNA (gDNA) was extracted from mouse organs using QIAamp DNA FFPE Tissue Kit (QIAGEN, #56404) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The concentration and purity of gDNA were measured using the NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Wilmington, DE, USA). Around 100–500 ng gDNA was used for ddPCR analysis to quantify AAV eGFP transgene copies from different mouse tissues. ddPCR reactions were conducted using Bio-Rad QX200 according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Data were acquired on the QX600 droplet reader (Bio-Rad) and analyzed using QX Manager Software (Bio-Rad). All results were manually reviewed for false positive and background noise droplets based on negative and positive control samples. eGFP transgene copies (eGFP copies/ng gDNA) were calculated by dividing the concentration (provided by QX Manager) by the amount of input gDNA.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism (v.8.3, La Jolla, CA, USA). Differences between multiple groups were determined by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons. All groups passed the normality test. p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
