Combined adductor canal (ACB) and sacral erector spinae plane (S-ESP) blocks for total knee arthroplasty pain in hemophilic arthropathy
Authors: Francesco Marrone, Pierfrancesco Fusco, Saverio Paventi, Carmine Pullano
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
We present the case of a successful application of combined adductor canal block (ACB) and sacral erector spinae plane (S-ESP) block for the management of a patient suffering from severe hemophilia A with an end-stage arthropathy who underwent total knee replacement. The implementation of a tailored protocol, not incorporating neuraxial techniques, such as spinal anesthesia, facilitated optimal intra- and postoperative pain management and expedited postoperative recovery and rehab without motor weakness and side effects, highlighting the potential benefit of such strategy in selected cases.

Introduction

Hemophilia is a hereditary hemorrhagic disease (X-linked recessive) characterized by the deficiency or the absence of one of the clotting factors, including factor VIII (hemophilia A) or IX (B). Its incidence is 1/5000 male live births. The clinical manifestations include spontaneous hemorrhages into muscles and vital organs and recurrent hemarthrosis.[1] As a result of repeated bleeding, hemophilic arthropathy is described leading to joint degeneration, severe pain, and deformity. In total knee arthroplasty (TKA), evidence supports the use of adductor canal block (ACB) as postoperative analgesic technique in a multimodal protocol but this technique does not fully cover the knee joint and a complementary block is suggested to get full knee analgesia.[2]

In this case, we provide a description of combining ACB and sacral erector spinae plane (S-ESP) block to get optimal intra- and postoperative pain management in a multimodal approach for a patient with severe hemophilia A who underwent TKA as treatment of his end-stage hemophilic arthropathy.

Case Report

A 44-year-old man with severe hemophilia A and end-stage knee arthritis [Figure 1] was scheduled for TKA. He was 180 cm in height and weighed 95 kg (body mass index (BMI) 29 Kg.m-2). His comorbidities included hepatitis C with a history of drugs’ abuse and cigarette smoking. Blood tests (the day before the surgery) revealed an activated partial thromboplastin (APTT) time of 82 sec with normal prothrombin time (PT) and platelet count. According to the hematologist, to have appropriate peri- and postoperative hemostasis, starting 1 hour before the surgery (T0), the patient received turoctocog alfa, a recombinant factor VIII at the dose of 8000 IU and then 4000 IU at T12-T24 and 3000 IU at T36-T48-T60. The replacement therapy was recommended for major surgery (such as intra-abdominal, cardiovascular, and orthopedic), in which peak factor levels should be 80% to 100% before the procedure with gradual reduction in peak factor level goals over the first 14 postoperative days.[3] Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) were not advised. With the informed consent of the patient, we planned a combination of ACB and S-ESP block in a multimodal anesthesia/analgesia protocol for the optimal intra- and postoperative pain control, avoiding neuraxial techniques for the surgery and providing general anesthesia via laryngeal mask. We monitored vital parameters and administered to the patient intravenous 1mg midazolam. Then, with the patient in the prone position, aseptically, we placed ultrasound (US)-guided S-ESP block at the S1 intermediate crest with cranio-caudal approach. After aspiration, we administered 0,375% ropivacaine 30 ml with dexmedetomidine 25 mcg and dexamethasone 3 mg. No side effects were recorded [Figure 2]. Then, with the patient in the supine position, we placed US-guided ACB with mid-tight subsartorial approach. We injected plain 2% mepivacaine 15 ml. No side effects were recorded [Figure 3]. After that, general anesthesia was provided as per the standard practice via intravenous propofol 120 mg and rocuronium 40 mg. A laryngeal mask was used to provide ventilation and oxygenation with sevoflurane at minimum alveolar concentration (MAC) 0,5. The surgery lasted 134 min, with limited blood loss, and it was uneventful. Vital parameters remained stable. Postoperatively, the patient received intravenously paracetamol 1 g and morphine 3 mg. Then, the patient was monitored in the post-anesthesia care unit (PACU) for 1 hour and then discharged with Aldrete score of 10. The numeric rating scale (NRS) score was reported to be ≤4/10 for 72 h. The patient could start rehab without complications.

Discussion

Postoperative pain following TKA surgery is usually severe, and ineffectively, pain management impacts on rehab and patient’ satisfaction. A multimodal analgesia inclusive of regional anesthesia proves to have multiple benefits, and it is recommended for improved outcomes.[4]

The femoral nerve block (FNB) has been widely utilized for postoperative pain and reducing opioid consumption following TKA. However, it primarily addresses pain in the anteromedial region of the knee, leaving the posterior compartment untreated and leading to quadriceps weakness. Concerns also raised regarding the increased risk of falls associated with continuous blocks. Consequently, FNB has been supplanted by ACB, which offers comparable analgesic efficacy while better preserving quadriceps function. Despite this, ACB primarily targets pain in the anteromedial knee, escaping the lateral-posterior compartments. Thus, the use of an adjunctive block is recommended.[2] In our patient, we chose to combine ACB and S-ESP block to offer an optimal multimodal option facilitating intra- and postoperative pain control, and postoperative rehab and recovery.

Saphenous is the terminal sensory branch of the femoral nerve and targets the medial aspect of the leg and the knee joint through the infrapatellar rami. To cover it, we used the subsartorial approach at the adductor canal[5] around mid-thigh level. We used plain 2% mepivacaine to get faster analgesia for surgery, while S-ESP began to take effect gradually sustaining analgesia. The S-ESP was first described for the sensory block of the posterior branches of sacral nerves,[6] and since then, applications in different surgeries have been reported. In major gender reassignment surgery, a S2-S4 ESP was performed to cover ilioinguinal, genitofemoral, and the pudendal nerves.[7] In orthopedics, it was introduced for pelvic and sacral fracture surgery[8] showing sensory T12-S3 dermatome coverage and revealed to effectively control pain following lower limb surgeries.[9] Using unilateral S-ESP, a sensory block between T12-S2 dermatomes was described in the case of hip fracture surgery.[10] Recently, it proved to be successful in a multimodal analgesia protocol for motor-sparing pain control in TKA without side effects.[11] The observed efficacy of S-ESP block for pelvic and lower limb surgeries, which engage not only sacral but also lumbar dermatomes, demonstrates the potential for anterior drug diffusion, particularly with the administration of large volumes. A recent study showed that the median approach to S-ESP (at computed tomography [CT] scans and dissection) covered subcutaneous tissue between S1-S5 horizontal planes, while the intermediate approach pushed the injectate (at CT and anatomical studies) along erector spinae muscles bilaterally S3-L2 levels. The transition of the solution toward anterior sacrum was demonstrated at CT scans in median approach with the injectate moving through the sacral foramina via spinal nerves S2-S5.[12]

In this case, the efficacy of S-ESP is supported by the fact that the patient had NRS score ≤4/10 in the 72 h following the surgery with only intravenous paracetamol. In combination with ACB, S-ESP block revealed a simpler and less invasive technique than lumbar or neuraxial approaches, rendering it a better safety profile. It could be safely placed also in patients affected by comorbidities and coagulation disorders (such as hemophilia) or taking anti-coagulant/antiplatelet medications as it is a superficial technique with a bony landmark. S-ESP together with ACB produced full knee analgesia (covering antero-medial, lateral, and posterior compartments) without a motor block, thus representing an advantage for rehab and comfort. Potential disadvantages of the S-ESP include the unpredictable dermatomal coverage, slow onset time, and its limitation to analgesic potential (not letting us to use it as anesthetic solution).

In conclusion, in our patient, the combination of ACB and S-ESP revealed success in a multimodal analgesia protocol for TKA. The S-ESP may result a useful, technically easy, and safe alternative technique as a component of a multimodal analgesia regime. Further investigation is warranted to prove its efficacy and define the optimal strategy.
