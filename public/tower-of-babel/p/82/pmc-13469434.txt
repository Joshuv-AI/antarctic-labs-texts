Widespread controls of precipitation sensitivity on drought recovery of global forests
Authors: Yixue Hong, Matthew D. Petrie, Yu Zhang, Yaping Chen, Heng Huang, Hao Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Increases in drought-induced forest mortality have raised worldwide concerns regarding forest drought recovery. Precipitation sensitivity (SPre), the magnitude of forest vegetation growth response to precipitation variation, is believed to strongly influence drought recovery. However, direct evidence is lacking, limiting its use in forecasting forest resilience under climate change. Using multiple remote-sensing datasets, we reveal that SPre is a pivotal factor shaping global forest recovery following extreme drought years, comparable to and even surpassing other well-known factors. In contrast to previous expectations, drought recovery is highest at intermediate SPre, and declines at lower and higher values. Over the past three decades, climate change increases SPre in 56% of global forests, and 30% experience SPre increases associated with a decline in drought recovery—particularly sub-tropical forests. Our results illuminate the widespread importance of SPre in global forests, and highlight incorporating SPre into ecological models to improve predictions of forest resilience.

Introduction

Forest ecosystems play a crucial role in mitigating global warming through their uptake and storage of carbon1. However, their stability and persistence are increasingly threatened by droughts2,3. Drought recovery, the ability of forests to return to a pre-drought growth level after experiencing growth declines caused by drought, is a crucial measure of ecosystem resilience4–6. Forests with low drought recovery are susceptible to environmental disturbances that may subsequently lead to abrupt and irreversible ecosystem state shifts7, resulting in a reduction in climate-mitigating services8,9. Given the substantial influence of drought recovery on ecosystem stability and carbon balance, understanding the patterns and drivers of forest drought recovery is imperative for assessing climate impacts and formulating effective adaptation strategies.

Precipitation sensitivity (SPre), which quantifies the magnitude of forest vegetation growth response to precipitation variation, is a key metric influencing the drought recovery of different global forest types10,11. Higher SPre indicates a greater sensitivity of vegetation growth to interannual precipitation variation10,12,13. Theoretically, forests with higher SPre may recover more quickly following drought events because they are more sensitive to changes in water availability during the post-drought recovery phase, when favorable water conditions resume. This expectation is often linked to the drought resistance–recovery tradeoff hypothesis, which assumes a negative relationship between drought resistance and recovery14,15. Because high SPre forests generally exhibit lower drought resistance (i.e., the capacity to withstand drought)16, they are, under the assumed negative relationship between drought resistance and recovery, expected to have higher drought recovery potential. However, high SPre does not necessarily guarantee rapid drought recovery. In cases where post-drought water availability remains insufficient to support forest regrowth17,18, recovery in high SPre forests can be markedly delayed, challenging the assumed positive relationship between SPre and drought recovery and underscoring the need to better understand its dynamics, especially over longer, multi-decade time periods. Previous studies have demonstrated that multiple factors, including climatic conditions19,20, phenology21, and soil fertility22,23, are tightly coupled with drought recovery. However, direct investigations linking SPre with drought recovery are lacking, especially on a global scale, where declines in forest cover are strongly associated with changes to terrestrial carbon sequestration.

Global change may alter forest SPre and consequently drought recovery10,11,13. Global change, including warming24 and precipitation regime shifts25,26, is expected to further intensify in the 21st century with significant impacts on terrestrial ecosystems27. Global change has been suggested to alter forest SPre by changing water availability and water demand of forests10,28, but the direction, magnitude, and timescales of changes in forest SPre with climate are not well defined, and results are often limited to a subset of global forest types. For example, some studies report an increase in SPre over the past two decades in deciduous coniferous and shrub forests10, whereas others report a decrease in SPre in evergreen broadleaf and coniferous forests11,29,30. It remains unclear how global change-induced variations in SPre could affect forest drought recovery, and if changes in recovery imposed by SPre will be similar or different among global forest types. Empirical evidence of these relationships could greatly improve understanding of the trajectories of global forests in a changing climate10,11.

To address these knowledge gaps, we investigate the effect of SPre on drought recovery across global forests using multiple satellite-derived datasets. We conduct a driver analysis to elucidate the relative importance of SPre on gross primary productivity (GPP)-based and Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI)-based measures of drought recovery compared to other established climatic and biotic factors influencing drought recovery. We then quantify relationships between SPre and GPP- and NDVI-based drought recovery, and examine whether the SPre–drought recovery linkage follows a positive relationship. Lastly, we quantify change to SPre and drought recovery for different global forest types between 1985–2000 and 2001–2018 time periods, providing evidence of global change effects on the sensitivity of global forests to drought. Together, our results—based on three decades of global-scale remote-sensing observations—provide more detailed insights into the relationships between SPre and drought recovery for global forests, and illuminate how the impacts of global change on SPre and drought recovery may alter the resilience of different forest types.

Relationships between SPre and drought recovery

We used generalized additive models (GAMs) to capture potential non-linear SPre–recovery relationships, and linear models (LMs) where SPre–recovery relationships were approximately linear, based on model diagnostics. Across global forests, the relationship between SPre and drought recovery was non-linear (Fig. 2, Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Tables 2, 3). As SPre increased, drought recovery initially increased with higher SPre values, peaked at approximately 0.1, and decreased at higher values (Fig. 2a, b). This non-linear pattern and the threshold (0.1) were relatively stable and robust over time (Supplementary Fig. 2). Further analysis revealed distinct relationships between SPre and drought recovery among different forest types. A significant non-linear pattern was observed in sub-tropical forests (GPP-based: edf = 7.10, Fig. 2a; NDVI-based: edf = 5.73, Fig. 2b; Supplementary Table 2). Effects of global change on SPre and drought recovery

We explored the effect of global change on SPre and drought recovery by comparing 1985–2000 and 2001–2018 time periods (Fig. 3). Both GPP- and NDVI-based assessments showed that tropical and sub-tropical forests experienced a significant increase in SPre and a significant decrease in drought recovery between these time periods (Fig. 3a, b). Spatial distribution analysis verified that increased SPre and decreased drought recovery were widely distributed among global forests, and particularly in tropical and sub-tropical forests (Fig. 3c, d). Overall, more than 56% of global forests experienced an increase in SPre post-2000 (GPP-based: 56.2%, NDVI-based: 55.8%; Fig. 3c, d). We further examined the relationships between changes in SPre and drought recovery (ΔSPre vs. ΔRecovery) for each forest type (Fig. 3e, f). Increasing SPre was associated with an increase in drought recovery in tropical, temperate, and boreal forests (GPP-based: β = 0.12–0.33; NDVI-based: β = 0.17–0.54), but was associated with a decline in drought recovery in sub-tropical forests (GPP-based: β = -0.08; NDVI-based: -0.94; Fig. 3e, f; Supplementary Table 4). Discussion

SPre is a key driver of drought recovery

Our results identify widespread effects of SPre on forest drought recovery on a global-scale for the first time. This observation may be attributable to the fact that SPre encompasses signals of precipitation variability and biotic response, resulting in a more pronounced influence on drought recovery than individual climatic or vegetation factors, whose effects can vary across climatically varying forest ecosystems worldwide11,31. Since SPre reflects vegetation growth sensitivity to local environmental changes12,13, our findings underscore the significance of both long-term responses of forest vegetation at the ecosystem scale and their role in shaping forest resilience across broad scales. Furthermore, our results suggest that tracking changes in SPre can help identify forests vulnerable to impending ecological changes32. Consistent with this, changes in SPre are observed to precede tree mortality events (Supplementary Fig. 6; Supplementary Methods).

SPre is more strongly associated with forest drought recovery when using NDVI-based assessments compared to GPP-based assessments. We postulate that this difference is likely associated with the decoupling between greenness and productivity33,34. Forest canopy greenness is generally more sensitive to water availability than productivity35. Additionally, aggregating forests with contrast phenologies (deciduous vs. evergreen) may introduce further variation due to their distinct GPP–NDVI relationships36. However, a stronger response in NDVI does not necessarily confirm that it is a superior index compared to GPP. Relying on a single vegetation index can provide useful and important insights (see21,37 for recent examples) but may also lead to biases in assessing resilience and its drivers across various functional processes. Ultimately, all vegetation remote sensing indices can only provide an approximation of functioning and status. Therefore, we highlight the value of investigating resilience patterns and drivers using additional vegetation indices, such as Enhanced Vegetation Index (EVI) and Solar-Induced Chlorophyll Fluorescence (SIF), to provide complementary insights on drought recovery and to motivate the development of new indices.

Unexpected non-linear effect of SPre on drought recovery

We reveal a non-linear effect of SPre on drought recovery across global forest ecosystems and identify a threshold beyond which higher SPre may constrain, rather than promote, drought recovery. Evidence for this pattern is further supported by analyses of drought recovery time (Supplementary Fig. 7). We postulate that the non-linear relationship between SPre and drought recovery may reflect different water-use strategies across the SPre gradient exhibited by global forests at the ecosystem scale. At low SPre, forests exhibit limited capacity to utilize post-drought precipitation, constraining recovery. At intermediate SPre (around 0.1), forests can efficiently convert available water into growth, thereby buffering short-term water deficits and maximizing recovery. In contrast, at high SPre, forests become increasingly sensitive to interannual precipitation variation, heightening vulnerability to prolonged or repeated droughts and extending recovery periods. Our paired-site analysis further supports this interpretation: drought-induced mortality sites more frequently exceed this SPre threshold in the years preceding the event compared with paired background locations (Supplementary Fig. 8; Supplementary Methods). Thus, our identification of a SPre threshold holds significant implications for detecting forests vulnerable to post-drought recovery decline and offers a conceptual framework for exploring how ongoing global changes—from local disturbances to broad-scale climate shifts—affect forest drought resilience.

However, the global non-linear pattern appears to be largely driven by sub-tropical forests, where the non-linear relationship between SPre and drought recovery are most pronounced. In contrast, relationships in tropical, temperate, and boreal forests are predominantly linear. This difference is likely due to the higher magnitude and broader range of SPre values in sub-tropical forests, which contrast with the lower and less variable SPre values in the other forest types that less frequently exceed the threshold (Supplementary Fig. 4). Overall, the demonstrated non-linear relationship challenges the common theoretical assumption of a strictly linear relationship10,11, highlighting the need for models to move beyond simple linear assumptions, especially at broad spatial scales. Simultaneously, the variation in SPre–recovery relationships among forest types underscores the importance of incorporating biome- and scale-specific dynamics, and the need to determine when and under what conditions future changes in SPre may cease to be positively and/or linearly correlated with drought recovery in forest types that currently exhibit these relationships.

Global change alters SPre and drought recovery

We find that the influence of SPre on drought recovery increases after 2000, suggesting that climate change may play a critical role in modulating SPre and consequently drought recovery. This trend likely reflects a long-term intensification of water limitation under ongoing warming, as rising atmospheric evaporative demand and increasing precipitation variability enhance forest dependence on water availability10,38. Consistent with this interpretation, more than half of the world’s forests became more sensitive to precipitation over the past three decades. In regions where SPre has increased, nearly one-third of forests have exhibited declines in drought recovery, with particularly pronounced declines in sub-tropical forests.

We demonstrate that increasing SPre can significantly contribute to recovery loss in sub-tropical forests, as evidenced by the negative relationship between ΔSPre and ΔRecovery. Most sub-tropical forests already exhibited relatively high SPre before 2000 (exceeding 0.1, Supplementary Fig. 9), and experienced further increases after 2000. This pattern suggests that these forests are increasingly prone to substantial declines in post-drought recovery. These results align with previous evidence that sub-tropical forests have already experienced substantial drought impacts39 and may face elevated risks of resilience loss or mortality under future water limitation22,40,41. Additional support is provided by global tree-mortality datasets (Supplementary Fig. 10a), which reveal increasing mortality in sub-tropical forests with elevated SPre and reduced drought recovery.

In contrast to subtropical forests, rising SPre may enhance drought recovery in tropical, temperate, and boreal forests, where ΔSPre and ΔRecovery tend to be positively related. However, this positive effect does not necessarily translate into improved drought recovery. In tropical forests, SPre has increased overall while drought recovery has declined after 2000, implying that the positive effect of increasing SPre can be offset by the negative impact of intensifying climate stress and additional agents of forest decline42,43. Interpretation of these patterns is further complicated by inherent limitations of satellite-derived GPP and NDVI, particularly in boreal forests where canopy greenness can persist during dormancy despite minimal growth, introducing uncertainty into recovery estimates. Together, these findings highlight the complex interactions between SPre, drought recovery, and climate change, and underscore the need to incorporate multiple factors (e.g., climatic variables, vegetation traits, soil properties) when predicting forest responses to drought, particularly given the constraints of satellite-based observations and the unique characteristics of different global forest types.

Limitations, implications, and applications

Uncertainties remain with our study. First, although our method can quantify the static drought recovery following extreme drought years, its application to assess dynamic changes in resilience is limited by the short-term and discrete nature of extreme events37,44, which in this context may capture drought severity and spatial extent, but may not fully capture drought duration45. To address this, future studies should employ quantification indices such as temporal autocorrelation and potential well volume44,46, which rely on time series-based data to capture recovery dynamics37,47. Second, using fixed vegetation traits (e.g., isohydricity, forest type) may overlook temporal shifts resulting from species turnover or climate acclimation48–50, potentially influencing our estimates in models. However, SPre consistently emerges as a dominant predictor of drought recovery across two independent time periods and multiple global forest types, underscoring the robustness of our main conclusions. Third, the spatial and temporal relationships between SPre and drought recovery may be confounded by unaccounted covariates, including extrinsic disturbances (e.g., fire, insect outbreaks), global change factors (e.g., land-use change, nitrogen deposition), and vegetation-intrinsic characteristics such as forest composition (deciduous vs. coniferous) and human forest management (e.g., thinning, prescribed burning). Although additional analyses are conducted to account for some of these factors and support the robustness of our conclusions in both spatial (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 3) and temporal analyses (Supplementary Fig. 5), these approaches cannot capture all potential interactions, and some uncertainties inevitably remain. We suggest that additional, more constrained studies conducted on finer scales and specific forest types are needed to further elucidate the impact of SPre on recovery and to determine its underlying mechanisms.

Despite these limitations, our study provides unique insights into understanding forest drought recovery. In a warmer and drier world, changes in SPre may threaten the stability and sustainability of forest ecosystems51–53, particularly sub-tropical forests39,54. As such, addressing these trends in the design of forest-based mitigation strategies (e.g., forest protection, afforestation, and restoration) is essential to prevent and/or remediate the effects of future drought55,56.

Our results have important implications for the development of process-based models. Several well-known factors, including temperature, precipitation, and phenology, are commonly used in current ecological models to predict forest drought recovery51–53. SPre, as a potential indicator of drought recovery, has long been overlooked in ecological models57. Given the role of SPre as a mediator of climate-vegetation trait interactions, ecological models focused on resolving subregional to global forest dynamics should test the benefits of integrating precipitation-trait-drought recovery linkages through regression-based parameterization. Integrating spatiotemporal variability via biome-specific hierarchical frameworks58 has the potential to substantially enhance the predictive capacity of models for long-term drought recovery trajectories under global environmental change.

Methods

Vegetation metrics

Two vegetation metrics were used to quantify drought recovery: GPP, which estimates vegetation carbon uptake via photosynthesis, and NDVI, which estimates canopy greenness and structure. The GPP dataset was derived from the Advanced Very High Resolution Radiometer (AVHRR) using remotely sensed near-infrared reflectance59. The GPP dataset covers the period from 1982 to 2018 with a spatial resolution of 0.05°. The dataset exhibits high spatial resolution and considerable capacity to capture interannual variations in terrestrial GPP on a global scale60,61. The third-generation global NDVI dataset was accessed from Global Inventory Monitoring and Modeling Studies (GIMMS) group62. The third-generation NDVI data have been widely used to monitor changes in vegetation greenness growth both regionally and globally63,64. This biweekly NDVI dataset spans all years between 1982 and 2015 with a spatial resolution of 0.083°. The dataset has been corrected for errors and noise arising from atmospheric aerosols, viewing angle effects, cloud cover, and solar zenith angle, following standard procedures established by data providers65,66.

Assessment of drought recovery required estimates of vegetation dynamics at the annual time step. To match the temporal resolution, we resampled the GPP and NDVI datasets to an annual scale using the annual sum for GPP and the annual mean for NDVI, respectively. To match the spatial extent of global forests, we obtained global forest cover data (i.e., the extent or area covered by forests) from NASA Making Earth System Data Records for Use in Research Environments Vegetation Continuous Fields Version 1 data product at a spatial resolution of 0.05°67. Both the GPP and NDVI datasets were extracted using the global forest cover data as a mask. The NDVI dataset was further resampled to 0.05° to align with the spatial resolution of the forest cover data. Global forest type data were obtained from Xu et al.68, and forests were classified into four major types based on bioclimatic zones: tropical forests, sub-tropical forests, temperate forests, and boreal forests (Supplementary Fig. 11). This bioclimate-based classification captures broad differences in water availability and seasonality, which are central to how SPre and drought recovery interact at broad spatial scales. To further account for vegetation structural differences, forests were stratified into five plant functional types (i.e., evergreen broadleaf, evergreen needleleaf, deciduous broadleaf, deciduous needleleaf, and mixed) using Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer (MODIS) land cover type product (MCD12Q1, Version 6.1)69.

Environmental and vegetation datasets

Environmental data included variables associated with climate, soil properties, nitrogen deposition, global human modification (gHM) index, and global fire weather (FWI) index. The climate data included precipitation, temperature, dry days, and surface net solar radiation at a spatial resolution of 0.1°. These gridded climate data, soil water volume and relative humidity from 1985 to 2018 were obtained from the Copernicus Climate Change Service Data Store (https://cds.climate.copernicus.eu/)70,71. Vapor pressure deficit was calculated using relative humidity and temperature according to Campbell and Norman72. The cation-exchange capacity was obtained from the Regridded Harmonized World Soil Database v1.273. Nitrogen deposition data was acquired from the Inter-Sectoral Impact Model Intercomparison Project74,75. Global human modification index, which quantifies cumulative anthropogenic pressures on terrestrial ecosystems, was derived from Kennedy et al.76. Global fire weather index was obtained from the Global Fire Weather Database (GFWED)77 and represents the meteorological potential for fire danger.

Vegetation data included variables of vegetation nutrients, canopy properties, and hydraulic traits. Specifically, leaf nitrogen and phosphorus concentrations (per unit dry mass) were derived from the trait map developed by Butler et al.78. Canopy height and leaf area index were extracted from the global forest canopy height dataset79 and the fourth-generation GIMMS leaf area index (LAI) V2.0 product80,81, respectively. Root-shoot ratio, a variable influencing nutrient and hydraulic distributions in drought survival22, was acquired from Ma et al.82. Moreover, isohydricity data, a variable for understanding plant water regulation under drought stress, was obtained from Konings and Gentine83. All above data were extracted using global forest cover data as a mask and resampled to 0.05°, to ensure consistency in both temporal and spatial resolution.

Data analysis

Drought recovery was quantified by calculating the recovery rate during and following extreme drought years14,84. Soil water volume was used to identify extreme drought years because soil moisture constitutes the primary mechanistic link between drought and ecosystem production85,86. Extreme drought years were identified as those with soil water volume values equal to or less than the 10th percentile of the time series (i.e., two extreme drought years identified per grid cell)87–89. Sensitivity analyses using a more stringent threshold (5th percentile) indicate that our results are robust to this parameter choice (Supplementary Fig. 12). Detected drought events were cross-referenced with major droughts documented in literature and media reports (Supplementary Table 6). For grid cells experiencing multiple drought events, we first calculated recovery rates for each event and then averaged them to obtain an overall recovery rate for that period. Recovery rates at or below the 20th percentile of the global recovery rate were excluded as outliers to focus on typical recovery patterns and to reduce the impact of sustained, long-duration droughts on recovery rates. SPre is a proxy for the sensitivity of GPP and NDVI to interannual precipitation variability. To calculate SPre, we first standardized the datasets of GPP, NDVI, and precipitation using Z-score normalization. Then, we detrended the precipitation dataset by applying the differencing method to remove long-term trends11,91. A linear regression was subsequently performed between the annual growth (GPP and NDVI) and precipitation for each forest pixel over the time periods (Supplementary Fig. 14). The slope of the regression was used as SPre12,92. To evaluate whether long-term vegetation trends and pulse disturbances influence SPre, we also calculated SPre using linearly detrended and disturbance-simulated (30% growth reduction in a random year) GPP and NDVI time series for each pixel. The resulting SPre estimates were highly consistent with the original values, indicating that SPre is robust to both long-term vegetation trends and short-term disturbances (Supplementary Fig. 15).

To quantify the relationship between SPre and drought recovery across forest types, we used two modeling approaches: (1) Generalized additive models (GAMs) to detect non-linear relationships, with the effective degrees of freedom (edf > 1) indicating deviation from linearity. Higher edf values suggest stronger non-linear relationships93,94; (2) Linear regression models (LMs) to assess the directionality (positive or negative) of the linear relationship based on slope coefficients (β). Positive β values indicate that forests with higher (lower) SPre show higher (lower) drought recovery, whereas negative values indicate the opposite. To assess whether the observed relationships between SPre and drought recovery are sensitive to confounding influences, we conducted additional analyses including proxies for human modification and fire-related conditions (gHM and FWI) as covariates.

To explore the effect of global change on SPre and drought recovery, we divided our analysis into two time periods: 1985–2000 and 2001–2018. The year 2000 was selected as the division point due to the observed increase in warming and drying trends following this year, and an increase in the prevalence of global tree mortality events (Supplementary Fig. 10b). The GPP data were evaluated through 2018, whereas NDVI data were evaluated through 2015 due to data availability. For each of the four forest types, we calculated SPre and recovery rates independently for both periods. We then calculated the temporal changes of SPre (ΔSPre) and recovery rate (ΔRecovery) between the post-2000 period and the pre-2000 period (e.g., \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta {S}_{{\mbox{Pre}}}={S}_{{\mbox{Pre}}({\mbox{post}}-2000)}-{S}_{{\mbox{Pre}}({\mbox{pre}}-2000)}$$\end{document}ΔSPre=SPre(post−2000)−SPre(pre−2000)). All calculations of ΔSPre and ΔRecovery were performed at the individual pixel level to capture spatially explicit changes, before summarizing results across forest types. Welch’s t-test was used to identify significant temporal trends in SPre and drought recovery. LMs were used to assess how ΔSPre relates to ΔRecovery, with ΔSPre as the predictor variable and ΔRecovery as the response variable. Positive slope coefficients (β > 0) represent that increasing SPre promotes drought recovery, while negative slope coefficients (β < 0) indicate the opposite. To further assess the robustness of these relationships, multiple linear regression models were applied, with concurrent changes in temperature, precipitation, and FWI included as covariates.

To investigate the relative importance (i.e., % of Gini Importance) of SPre and other predictors (i.e., environmental and vegetation variables) on forest drought recovery, we employed random forest (RF) regression models. RF models are nonparametric and data-driven, providing a nonlinear approach to analysis. We ran RF models for each of the 20 groups, including two study periods, two vegetation metrics, and five subsets (four forest types plus the overall global dataset). Each model used recovery rate as the response variable. Predictor variables included time-varying variables (i.e., SPre, multiyear climate averages), which were assigned period-specific measurements, and time-static variables (i.e., soil properties and vegetation traits), which were assigned invariant values due to limited temporal data (see Supplementary Table 7 for a summary of the temporal treatments of these variables). To develop the RF models, we first conducted a variance inflation factor (VIF) analysis using the car R-package in R version 4.3.395. Variables with a VIF > 5 were excluded due to high multicollinearity96, including leaf phosphorus concentration, temperature, and relative humidity (Supplementary Fig. 16). We then randomly divided the observational dataset into training and validating samples in the ratio of 80% to 20%. For each independent RF model, we used GridSearchCV to identify optimal model parameters and performance metrics (i.e., coefficient of determination and mean squared error). We further developed the optimized RF models using the sklearn package in Python 3.11. To identify key drivers, we ranked variables based on their importance scores.
