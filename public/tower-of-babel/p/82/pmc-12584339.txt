Can symptom-severity phenotypes identify depression risk after mild traumatic brain injury? A cluster-based approach
Authors: Hung-Ju Chen, Ching-Yuan Ma, Li-Fan Lin, Cheng-Chiang Chang, Dueng-Yuan Hueng, Yue-Cune Chang, Hui-Hsun Chiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMild traumatic brain injury (mTBI) affects millions worldwide and frequently leads to secondary depression. Early identification of high-risk individuals is critical for targeted mental-health screening in this population. Data-driven phenotyping offers a promising avenue to unmask hidden symptom patterns, but few studies have combined unsupervised clustering of post-concussion profiles with established clinical and psychosocial metrics. We aimed to classify post-concussion symptom-severity profiles in adults with mTBI and to evaluate their association with secondary depression risk, adjusting for Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score, psychological resilience, age, sex, and time since injury.MethodsIn this cross-sectional analysis, 249 adults with mTBI (GCS 13–15) were recruited from a tertiary hospital in northern Taiwan. We performed hierarchical clustering using Ward’s method with Euclidean distance (with BIC support) to derive three symptom-severity phenotypes from the Rivermead Post-Concussion Questionnaire items, then used k-means clustering to assign individuals to these by minizing within-cluster variance. Depression, defined as a Beck Depression Inventory-II ≥ 13, was modeled as an outcome in generalized linear models, adjusting for GCS and psychological resilience. Model discrimination was evaluated via area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC).ResultsThree distinct symptom clusters (mild, moderate, severe) were identified. The severe cluster was characterized by prominent visual symptoms, including light sensitivity and double vision. Compared with the mild cluster, the moderate cluster had 5.06-fold higher depression odds (95% CI [2.08–12.31]; p < .001) and the severe cluster 17.17-fold higher odds (95% CI [5.66–52.14]; p < .001). Higher resilience was independently protective (OR = 0.95, 95% CI [0.93–0.96]; p < .001), as was each additional GCS score (OR = 0.20, 95% CI [0.06–0.62]; p = .005). The full model showed excellent discrimination with an AUC of 88%, 95% CI [0.83–0.92].ConclusionsOur data-driven approach shows that distinct post-concussion symptom-severity phenotypes, when integrated with GCS and resilience metrics, yields a robust tool for identifying mTBI survivors at high risks of depression. These findings support early, targeted mental-health screening and lay the groundwork for prospective validation and personalized intervention strategies.Clinical trial numberNCT04243226. Introduction

Mild traumatic brain injury (mTBI) affects millions each year and is the most common manifestation of traumatic brain injury (TBI). Approximately 15%−30% of mTBI survivors develop persistent post-concussion symptoms, placing them at markedly increased risk for subsequent depression [1]. Although most individuals recover within three months, a subset experience persistent post-concussion symptom that markedly increase the risk of secondary depression and long-term functional limitation, such as work absenteeism and reduced independence; early identification of individuals at high risk is essential to mitigate disability [2]. Beyond immediate injury, mTBI frequently precipitates functional limitations, including decreased productivity, dependence on caregivers, and increased healthcare utilization that cumulatively tax social support systems [3]. Because depression is a leading cause of long-term disability after mTBI and correlates with both symptom severity and recovery trajectory, early identification of high-risk subgroups is essential to guide targeted interventions.

Depression after mTBI arises from a complex interplay of brain dysfunction, psychological trauma, and interactions among cognitive, emotional, and physical symptoms [4]. Post-traumatic headache alone affects over 60% of mTBI patients at two weeks and persists in nearly one‐third at one year, further exacerbating psychological distress and functional decline [5]. Persistent post-concussion symptoms, such as headaches, cognitive impairment, and sleep disturbances, further exacerbate psychological distress and are associated with a 3.29-fold increase in the long-term risks of depression and other psychiatric disorders [6, 7]. Given the multifactorial nature of the pathophysiology of depression, the assessment and classification of symptom severity have been imperative for identifying patients at risk of poor mental health outcomes.

Symptom severity during the early phase has been linked to prolonged recovery and emotional distress, underscoring the importance of stratifying patients by symptom profiles to guide mental health monitoring and intervention [8]. Early Rivermead Post-Concussion Questionnaire (RPQ) total-score bands reliably stratify patients by long‐term symptom persistence and functional outcomes, suggesting that RPQ-derived severity profiles can serve as pragmatic indicators of later mood and disability trajectories [9]. Indeed, individuals classified into more severe or enduring symptoms clusters face substantially higher risks of emotional disorders or depression, a pattern that holds across the 1 year post-injury and is comparable between mTBI and moderate-severe TBI cohorts [10]. However, current screening tools, which often focused on single symptom domains or rely on clinician judgment, fail to capture the heterogeneity of post-concussion presentations and frequently miss those at high-risk individuals [11].

Recent longitudinal studies document substantial heterogeneity in post-concussion symptom trajectories, revealing distinct cognitive, emotional, and somatic recovery profiles over time [12]. Growth-mixture modeling in 1,074 TBI survivors uncovered low, high and delayed depression trajectories, linked to different demographic and injury severity. This findings underscore the clinical heterogeneity that uni-domain screening tools fail to capture [13]. Moreover, total scores on standard symptom inventories often poorly predict subsequent depression, underscoring the need for more nuanced, multivariable stratification [11]. In a separate cohort of first-time mTBI survivors, depressive symptoms were strongly associated with persistent post-concussive complaints and incomplete functional recovery over six months, with women and persons of color disproportionately represented in the high-symptom groups, illustrating that simple symptom counts alone miss key at-risk subgroups [14]. However, none of these studies have combined unsupervised clustering of post-concussion symptoms with acute neurological assessments and psychological metrics to directly predict depression risk in mTBI survivors.

Although symptom-severity profiles offer valuable prognostic information, they do not account for acute neurological status or psychosocial factors known to influence post-mTBI depression. In addition to symptom severity, both acute neurological status and psychosocial resilience influence depression outcomes after mTBI. The Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), a widely used clinical index of injury severity, has been linked to long-term psychological and cognitive outcomes [15]. Lower GCS scores are associated with poor functional recovery and higher rates of post-injury depression; patients with GCS 13 to 14 experience higher rates of post‐concussion syndrome than those with GCS 15, underscoring GCS’s continued relevance even within the mild range [16]. Psychological resilience, defined as the ability to maintain or regain mental well-being after adversity, may buffer depression risk in post-injury recovery [17]. Higher resilience supports brain plasticity (neurogenesis and neural circuit remodeling) and represents psychosocial resources that promote adaptive responses, reducing cognitive, emotional, and social adversity [18]. Resilience has emerged not merely as a personal trait but as a modifiable target; early psychosocial interventions such as peer support and cognitive‐behavioral skill training can bolster resilience and alter long‐term trajectories in disability populations [19]. These findings suggest that resilience may mitigate the negative psychological effects of symptom burden and neurological impairment after mTBI.

Despite evidence that symptom severity, GCS score, and psychological resilience are correlated with long-term psychological outcomes. However, the interplay between GCS, resilience, and symptom-based recovery profiles has not been explored, underscoring the need to include both variables in models predicting secondary depression. Although symptom severity and psychological resilience individually predict depression, few studies have employed severity-based clustering of post-concussion symptoms to assess how these factors jointly stratify depression risk. By stratifying patients into data‐driven phenotypes, we can move beyond one‐size‐fits‐all follow‐up protocols toward tailored screening that aligns with each individual’s functional profile and psychological resources. This study aimed to (1) cluster individuals with mTBI into distinct symptom severity phenotypes and (2) evaluate how initial GCS scores and psychological resilience influence depression risk and overall model performance.

Methods

Design

This study is a cross-sectional analysis of baseline data collected during the pretest phase of a randomized controlled trial. The same dataset was also used in a previous cross-sectional data analysis [20]. The study protocol was approved by the institutional review board of our hospital (approval number: A202105008). All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment. This study was conducted following the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines.

Participants and settings

The inclusion criteria were as follows: being aged ≥ 20 years, receiving a diagnosis of mTBI within 6 months, having a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score of 13 to 15 within 1 h after injury, being able to communicate in Mandarin Chinese or Taiwanese. The exclusion criteria were: nontraumatic TBI, prior depression or psychological hospitalization, penetrating injury, critical illness that may interfere with the completion of study questionnaires and being unable to provide informed consent. Participants were consecutively recruited from the emergency and neurosurgical outpatient departments of a medical center in northern Taiwan between September 2021 and April 2023. Of 370 approached, 249 consented and completed self-administrated questionnaire with a response rate of 67.3%. In total, 121 declined due to time constraints.

A minimum sample size of 20 per cluster was deemed necessary to achieve sufficient statistical power for k-means clustering⁠ [21]. On the basis of the literature and simulations performed to form distinct symptom severity clusters, we selected a threshold of 4 to ensure distinct symptom severity clusters. To ensure sufficient power for logistic regression, additional sample size calculations were performed using the event-per-variable approach [22]. In this approach, a minimum of 10 events per predictor variable is recommended for detecting significant ORs. The expected rate of depression in our cohort suggested that our study achieved sufficient statistical power. The minimum required sample size was calculated based on an expected depression rate of 30% and 3 predictors (symptom severity cluster, psychological resilience, and GCS). The results indicated a requirement for at least 100 participants. The final sample size exceeded this requirement, ensuring adequate power for logistic regression analysis. Our sample size met this requirement, enabling the detection of statistically meaningful associations.

Measures

Demographic data The following sociodemographic data were collected: age, sex, education level, occupation, marital status, and GCS score at the time of injury.

Beck depression inventory-II. Depression was assessed using the Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI-II), a revised version of the original 21-item Beck Depression Inventory [23]. In the BDI-II, items are scored on a scale with end points ranging from 0 to 3. The total score ranges from 0 to 63, with a higher score indicating more severe depressive symptoms. Scores of 0 to 13, 14 to 19, 20 to 28, and 29 to 63 indicate minimal, mild, moderate, and severe depression, respectively. A score of > 13 indicates the presence of depression [24]. The Chinese version of the BDI-II includes 2 subscales that measure cognitive–affective and somatic symptoms. Lu et al.25 demonstrated the high internal consistency of the BDI-II, with a Cronbach alpha value of 0.94 and a split-half reliability of 0.91. In the present study, the Cronbach alpha value for the BDI-II was 0.93.

Rivermead post-concussion symptom questionnaire Post-concussion symptoms were evaluated using the Chinese version of the 16-item Rivermead Post-Concussion Symptom Questionnaire (RPQ) [26] via one-on-one, researcher-administered interviews to accommodate those with visual or cognitive difficulties. Each item on this scale is rated on a 5-point ordinal scale (0 = never experienced; 1 = no longer a problem; 2 = mild problem; 3 = moderate problem; 4 = severe problem), with higher scores denoting greater symptom severity. The RPQ exhibits high construct validity [27] and excellent reliability, with an internal consistency of 0.95 [28]. In the present study, the Cronbach alpha value for the RPQ was 0.92.

Resilience Scale for Adults Psychological resilience was assessed using the Resilience Scale for Adults (RSA), originally developed by Friborg et al.[29] The RSA comprises 29 items rated on a 7-point Likert scale and detects intrapersonal and socioenvironmental resources that promote tolerance to stress and negative events. Higher total scores indicate greater resilience. For this study, which has demonstrated robust psychometric properties for assessing stress recovery capacity [30]. In the present sample, Cronbach’s alpha for the RSA was 0.92.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS Version 26.0 and R version 4.3.1. Statistical significance was set at P < .05. Descriptive statistics were used to analyze basic demographic characteristics. Continuous data (e.g., age and GCS score) are presented in terms of mean ± SD values, whereas categorical data are presented in terms of frequency (percentage) values. Group comparisons used t tests/ANOVA for normally distributed variables and χ² or Kruskal–Wallis tests otherwise.

To derive distinct clusters of symptom severity, we performed hierarchical cluster analysis of RPQ items using Ward’s method and Euclidean distance. The number of clusters was selected by Schwarz’s Bayesian Information Criteria (BIC), which supported a three-cluster solution. K-means clustering then refined participants allocation to symptom clusters, minimizing within-cluster variance and visualizing results in R. ANOVA and Kruskal–Wallis tests validated differences between these clusters.

Depression was operationalized as a binary outcome on the basis of a cutoff BDI-II score of 13 [24]. Generalized linear model (GLM) evaluated the independent contributions of symptom severity clusters, GCS and resilience on depression risk, adjusting for age, sex and time since injury. Results are reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Overall improvement over the null model was assessed by the omnibus likelihood-ratio χ² test, and discrimination by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC-ROC). Pre-specified two-way interactions (cluster × resilience, cluster × GCS, resilience × GCS) were tested and retained only if significant. No missing data were present on key outcome measures; thus, complete-case analysis was employed. Sensitivity analyses confirmed the stability and reproducibility of cluster assignments.

Clusters of symptom severity

The optimal number of clusters was determined using hierarchical clustering (Ward’s method with Euclidean distance) and model-based evaluation. While the dendrogram suggested a possible 2- to 3-cluster structure (see Supplementary eFigure 1), we compared models using Schwarz’s Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). The 3-cluster solution yielded the lowest BIC value (2279.665), indicating the best overall model fit (Table 2). While other clustering indices such as the silhouette coefficient (0.21) and Calinski–Harabasz index (82.97) were reviewed, the final number of clusters was selected based primarily on BIC. Figure 1 depicts the 3 clusters (mild, moderate, and severe; orange, green, and blue) identified through the k-means cluster analysis of RPQ scores. Significant differences were noted in symptom severity among the clusters (P < .001; see Table 3). A radar plot in Fig. 2 highlights that light sensitivity, double vision, and restlessness were unique to the severe symptom cluster.

Prediction of depression risk on the basis of symptom severity, GCS and psychological resilience

The GLM analysis demonstrated an adequate model fit, with the Omnibus Test (χ² = 104.70, p < .001) confirming significant improvement over the null model. To adjust for potential confounding, age, sex, and time since injury were included as covariates in the initial model. None of these variables were statistically significant. Additionally, we also tested three two-way interaction terms: symptom severity cluster × psychological resilience, symptom severity cluster × GCS score, and GCS score × psychological resilience. None of the interaction terms reached statistical significance (Supplementary eTable 1).

As shown in Table 4, compared with the mild symptom cluster, patients in the moderate cluster had 5.06-fold increased odds of depression (OR = 5.06; 95% CI, 2.08 to 12.31; p < .001), and patients in the severe cluster had a 17.17‐fold increase (OR = 17.17; 95% CI, 5.66 to 52.14; p < .001). Higher GCS scores were significantly associated with a lower risk of depression (OR: 0.20; 95% CI, 0.06 to 0.62; p = .005), as was greater psychological resilience (OR = 0.95 per unit increase; 95% CI, 0.93 to 0.96; p < .001).

Model predictive performance (AUC analysis)

Figure 3 shows the ROC curve for three prediction models. The symptom-clusters-only model yield an AUC of 0.74 (95% CI, 0.67–0.82, p < .001). Adding GCS scores modestly improved the AUC to 0.76 (95% CI, 0.69–0.83, p < .001). The full model including symptom clusters, GCS and resilience demonstrated the best performance with an AUC of 0.88 (95% CI, 0.83–0.93, p < .001).

Discussion

In this cross-sectional study of adults with mTBI, data-driven symptom severity phenotypes robustly stratified depression risk with an AUC of 0.88. After adjustment for age, sex, and time since injury, individuals in the severe cluster exhibited a 17.17-fold higher odds of depression, and those in the moderate cluster exhibited a 5.06-fold higher odds of depression, compared to those in the mild cluster. A mean silhouette score of 0.21 reflects some statistical overlap, reflecting a continuum of symptom presentation, yet each phenotypes showed distinct clinical profiles and depression risk. Notably, the severe phenotype was characterized by prominent visual disturbance such as light sensitivity and double vision, identifying a particularly high-risk subgroup. Lower initial GCS scores further amplified depression risk, underscoring the impact of early physiological stress to later mood disturbance. Furthermore, individuals in the severe cluster with low psychological resilience exhibited the highest depression risk, indicating that resilience buffers the psychological impact of severe symptom burden.

Our results accord with prior research showing that persistent postconcussion symptoms to adverse mental-health trajectories [20]. Early, severe symptom burden not only forecasts prolonged emotional distress but also identifies candidates for prompt psychiatric referral and intervention. The association may be attributable to the fact that depressive symptoms occurring after a concussion may directly result from alterations in the autonomic nervous system and neurohormonal functions, particularly involving the amygdala [31, 32]. Similarly, consistent with de Neeling et al.,8 our results confirm that early, severe symptom burden foreshadows prolonged emotional distress. These findings are consistent with previous research showed the presence of somatic symptom disorder after mTBI is linked to higher rates of comorbid depression and anxiety, as well as greater overall disability [33]. Moreover, Eagle et al., demonstrated that individuals with more severe post-concussion symptoms following mTBI are at significantly higher risk for emotional distress and report poorer quality of life [34]. Therefore, early evaluation of symptom severity and timely implementation of targeted interventions are crucial for reducing depression risk in patients with mTBI presenting with severe symptoms.

Headache severity increased progressively across clusters, aligning with Green et al.’s systematic review showing that persistent somatic symptom, especially headache, are highly prevalent after mTBI [35]. Participants in the severe cluster experienced the greatest headache burden and the highest odds of depression, underscoring the clinical importance of this phenotype. Neuroimaging studies implicate dysfunction in the descending pain modulatory system in post-concussion headache [36]. In our study, headache severity co-occurred with prominent visual disturbances, including light sensitivity and diplopia, suggesting a combined somatosensory-visual phenotype at uniquely high risk for depression. This phenotype mirrors prior studies that diplopia and other visual disturbances occur predominately in patients with severe post-concussion profiles and are linked to long-term recovery challenges [37]. Such ocular symptoms may reflect damage to brain regions involved in visual and somatosensory processing, including the parietal and occipital lobes, either from direct contusion or the shear forces of injury [38]. Because these visual complaints mark a distinct high-severity phenotype, their early identification can flag individuals at greatest risk for secondary depression. Beyond purely sensory damage, the relentless pain of persistent headache may exacerbate limbic dysregulation, amplify emotional distress and lower thresholds for depressive symptoms. Early identification of this dual‐symptom phenotype could therefore guide timely psychiatric assessment and targeted interventions.

Vision-related complaints, especially photophobia and diplopia, impair daily activities such as work, reading and driving, often leading to social withdrawal and reduced independence [39]. Neurobiologically, the visual processing streams (parietal‐occipital regions) are closely interconnected with limbic structures (amygdala, prefrontal cortex) that regulate mood, so post‐mTBI disruption of these pathways may directly predispose individuals to depression [40]. In addition, chronic visual strain often leads to secondary sleep disturbances and fatigue, further exacerbating emotional distress [41]. These functional and neurocircuitry factors help explain why photophobia and diplopia are especially predictive of secondary depression in this population.

Lower GCS scores were significantly associated with increased depression risk, reinforcing the link between acute injury severity and long-term psychological outcomes [42]. Even within the “mild” range of 13–15, variations in GCS can predict long term outcomes. Compared with a GCS of 15, scores of 13–14 often indicate more diffuse neural disruption and subtle impairments in autonomic and neurohormonal impairment, which can destabilize emotional processing [43]. This suggests that even subtle neurological impairment can disrupt mood-regulating neural networks and contribute to longer-term psychological deficits [44]. In clinical settings, GCS provides a rapid, objective, and reproducible metric to identify patients warranting closer follow-up. Even modest reductions in GCS within the mild range can prompt more intensive monitoring or referral to neuropsychology, which is particularly important in populations at risk for disability and health disparities. By including GCS in predictive models, we ensure that initial injury severity, however mild, helps distinguish those more likely to experience adverse psychological sequelae, thereby refining early prognostication and triage.

Conversely, psychological resilience served as a protective buffer in the mTBI population, and individuals with higher resilience scores had markedly lower odds of secondary depression despite similar GCS-defined injury severity [45]. Resilience entails adaptive coping mechanisms, social support engagement, and cognitive reframing, all of which can mitigate the emotional impact of neurologic deficits. Moreover, echoing Buenrostro-Jáuregui’s work on resilience,[18] we observed that higher resilience scores attenuated depression risk even among patients with comparable symptom severity and GCS scores. These parallels bolster the argument for integrating symptom-based stratification, GCS, and resilience assessment into routine mTBI follow‐up. By incorporating psychological resilience, the model captures coping differences that are not reflected by symptom severity or GCS alone. For example, among patients in the moderate cluster with similar GCS scores, those with higher resilience may seek support, reframe stress, and engage fully in rehabilitation, whereas those with lower resilience may withdraw and ruminate, increasing their depression risk. Including resilience corrects misclassifications by reclassifying resilient individuals who remain well and low-resilience individuals who develop depression. This added variance accounts for the AUC increase from 0.76 to 0.88, substantially improving the model’s ability to distinguish who will develop secondary depression. By evaluating both GCS and resilience together, clinicians can identify those most biologically predisposed to poor outcomes as well as those with the psychosocial resources to counterbalance risk, thereby informing targeted, personalized rehabilitation strategies.

Limitations

Although our results are informative, this study has several limitations. First, the single center and relatively small sample size may limit generalizability; cultural attitudes toward mental health and available support structures differ from other regions. Future multicenter studies are needed to validate findings. Second, the cross-sectional design of our study precluded the establishment of causal relationships between symptom severity, psychological resilience, and depression risk. Longitudinal studies are recommended to confirm the temporal relationships. Third, 121 of 370 eligible patients declined participation, often citing time constraints, potentially underrepresenting individuals with greater symptom burden or lower health literacy. Taiwan’s societal stigma regarding mental health may also have influenced self-reports of depression and resilience. Future international cohorts should compare cluster characteristics across cultures. Finally, all symptoms and resilience measures were self-reported and thus subject to recall bias; future studies should incorporate objective measures of physical activity.

Conclusion

These findings suggest that symptom cluster severity effectively identify mTBI survivors at elevated risk for secondary depression. Individuals in the severe cluster, particularly those reporting visual disturbances such as light sensitivity and double vision, may benefit from early psychological screening and targeted support. Lower GCS scores were correlated with higher depression risk, while greater psychological resilience was protective. Symptom-based stratification during initial rehabilitation may facilitate timely mental health interventions. Clinicians are encouraged to integrate both symptom severity clustering and resilience assessments into routine post-mTBI evaluation to improve early detection and guide personalized rehabilitation. In future practice, especially when telehealth or community outreach programs expand, identifying high risk phenotypes, for example severe symptoms with visual disturbances, could help focus limited mental health resources on those most likely to need them, thereby reducing disparities in post mTBI outcomes.
