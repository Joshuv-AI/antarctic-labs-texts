Tissue-adaptive bioelectronic fibers with temperature-induced self-tightening enable ultrastable neural interface
Authors: Tao Zhou, Rouhui Yu, Xiaowen Bai, Yiqing Yang, Zhongyao Fan, Xiangheng Du, Yaqi Chen, Yong Wang, Zhonghua Yang, Xuemei Sun, Meifang Zhu, Shaowu Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeural interfaces are essential for brain-machine communication and closed-loop neuromodulation. However, achieving durable interfaces between neural tissue and bioelectronics remains a key challenge, as conventional electronics do not actively conform to the soft, tortuous 3D architecture of neural tissue. We report a tissue-adaptive bioelectronic fiber that actively contracts to wrap around neural tissues, forming ultrastable neural-electronic interfaces, and enabling highly reliable neural stimulation and recording. This fiber is fabricated via wet spinning from a precursor integrating a thermoresponsive polymer and electroactive materials, and exhibits an ultralow modulus of 0.16 MPa and a phase transition temperature of 26.7 °C. Upon contact with rat tissue, the polymer chains undergo a hydrophilic-to-hydrophobic transition, expelling water and contracting the fiber to conform tightly to the sciatic nerve. This ultrastable biointerface demonstrates reliable neural stimulation, producing stable hindlimb bending responses, while sciatic nerve action potential recordings show 99.5% signal retention under successive stimulations.Subject terms: Sensors and biosensors, Gels and hydrogels

Introduction

Bioelectronic-tissue interfaces are essential for neuroscience research, clinical therapies, and neuromodulation1–11. As a crucial branch of neuromodulation, peripheral nerve modulation has shown significant potential for regulating physiological functions and treating various diseases12,13. For instance, vagus nerve stimulation has been applied to regulate heart rate, modulate inflammatory responses, and manage drug-resistant epilepsy14,15. Similarly, stimulation of the ulnar and sciatic nerves has proven effective in relieving chronic pain and restoring muscle function16–18. Despite these promising applications, the clinical efficacy of peripheral nerve modulation critically depends on the quality of the electrode-tissue interface, since instability at the interface can degrade signal quality, reduce stimulation efficiency, and elevate stimulation thresholds, ultimately limiting the reliability and therapeutic effectiveness of neuromodulation19–21. Most therapeutic electrodes are fabricated from rigid materials such as platinum and silicon22,23. These devices typically require partial penetration into neural tissue, increasing the risk of inflammation and mechanical damage and thereby posing challenges for long-term therapeutic efficacy24,25. In contrast, flexible film electrodes based on polymer substrates such as polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) or parylene-C, combined with electroactive materials including gold nanoparticles or polypyrrole (PPy), have emerged as promising alternatives26,27. Owing to their soft and flexible structure, these electrodes can conform closely to neural tissue, thereby minimizing implantation-induced damage while maintaining high electrical conductivity. However, under dynamic physiological conditions, such film electrodes often lose intimate contact with surrounding tissue, resulting in interfacial gaps and subsequent signal degradation or even therapeutic failure in clinical applications. To address this limitation, enhancing adhesion at the electrode-tissue interface has become an effective strategy for ensuring stable and continuous bioelectronic integration under wet and/or dynamic environments.

Current approaches to improve electrode-tissue interface stability mainly rely on interfacial adhesion mechanisms that involve physical interactions and covalent bonding28,29. Physical interactions, including hydrogen bonding, van der Waals forces, electrostatic attraction, and mechanical interlocking, provide reversible adhesion but only moderate adhesion strength30,31. Covalent bonding, typically introduced through reactive groups such as amino (–NH2), carboxyl (–COOH), and thiol (–SH) on electrode surfaces, forms irreversible chemical linkages with tissues32,33. This markedly enhances electrode-tissue interfacial stability, even under dynamic conditions. However, the irreversible nature of such bonding can hinder device removal and increase the risk of tissue damage.

To address these limitations, active conformal strategies based on smart actuation have been developed. Smart polymers play a crucial role in these approaches, as their stimulus-responsive deformation enables active conformity to soft tissue. For example, shape-memory polymer thin-film electrodes achieve conformal tissue contact through thermally triggered shape recovery of a deformable polymer substrate carrying metal nanofilm electrodes34. Electrochemically actuated PPy-based electrodes regulate electrode-tissue contact through voltage-induced redox reactions that cause reversible expansion and contraction27. Similarly, water-responsive polymer electrodes based on oriented semicrystalline poly(ethylene oxide) undergo rapid contraction upon exposure to tissue fluids, enabling tight tissue wrapping through hydration-induced recoiling of amorphous polymer chains35. Despite these advances, most existing designs still rely on externally applied stimuli to achieve conformal adaptation. Moreover, film-type electronic devices often struggle to interface effectively with the intricate, small-scale structures of neural tissues due to geometric mismatch. Therefore, it is critical to develop advanced bioelectronic devices that can actively adapt to the complex geometry of small neural tissues through endogenous stimuli, enabling more stable and efficient signal transmission.

Here, we report a tissue-adaptive bioelectronic fiber that undergoes thermo-triggered contraction driven by endogenous tissue temperature, allowing the formation of stable biointerfaces. When interfaced with living tissue, the fiber responds to intrinsic temperature by undergoing a phase transition, resulting in autonomous contraction and self-tightening without external actuation. The bioelectronic fiber is designed as a composite hydrogel system integrating a thermoresponsive poly(N-isopropylacrylamide) (PNIPAm) matrix, polypyrrole-modified carboxylated multi-walled carbon nanotubes (PPy@CNT), and biocompatible sodium alginate (SA), fabricated via a scalable wet-spinning process. The resulting PNIPAm/SA/PPy@CNT (PSPC) fibers possess mixed ionic-electronic conductivity while maintaining mechanical compliance with soft tissues. Owing to its one-dimensional microscale geometry and thermo-adaptive behavior, the bioelectronic fiber enables stable and adaptive interfacing with soft and curved tissues. We further demonstrate its potential for reliable neural interfacing in vivo, highlighting bioelectronic fibers as a promising platform for constructing stable electrode-tissue interfaces.

Results

Fabrication and characterization of tissue-adaptive bioelectronic fibers

The tissue-adaptive bioelectronic fibers exhibit pronounced thermo-adaptive contraction, leading to autonomous tightening around surrounding tissues. This contraction behavior is functionally analogous to the active tightening of a snake coiling around its prey, resulting in stable interface (Fig. 1a, b). To evaluate this self-tightening and conformal behavior across tissues with different geometries and curvatures, the sciatic nerve, kidney, skeletal muscle, and heart of rats were selected. In all cases, the tissue-adaptive bioelectronic fibers formed stable conformal contact with these tissue surfaces (Fig. 1c).

The diameter of bioelectronic fiber is ~450 μm.

To fabricate bioelectronic fibers, the spinning solution consisting of PNIPAm, SA, and polypyrrole-modified carboxylated multi-walled carbon nanotubes (PPy@CNT), was extruded into an aqueous Ca2+-based coagulation bath (Fig. 2a)36,37. The synthesis and characterization of the individual components are detailed as follows. PNIPAm was selected as the temperature-sensitive polymer owing to its good biocompatibility and a phase transition temperature of ~ 32 °C close to physiological temperature38,39. PNIPAm was synthesized via free-radical polymerization of NIPAm in aqueous solution and subsequently purified by dialysis, yielding a polymer with a molecular weight of ~ 4.9 × 105 g/mol and a dispersity of 2.3 (Supplementary Fig. 1). PPy@CNT was obtained through Fe3+-catalyzed in situ polymerization of pyrrole on carboxylated carbon nanotubes. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images revealed a coating layer on the CNT surface, suggesting successful PPy deposition (Supplementary Fig. 2). By tuning the concentration of pyrrole monomers, PPy@CNT composites with different PPy loadings were obtained. The samples were denoted as PPyx@CNTy, where x and y represent the mass ratio of PPy to CNT, respectively. Details are provided in the “Methods” section. The Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectra showed that the C-O stretching band of –COOH groups in CNT at 1168 cm−1 became indistinct in PPy@CNT. Meanwhile, the characteristic absorption peaks of PPy (1459, 1307, and 1045 cm−1) exhibited a blue shift compared with those of pure PPy and CNT, indicating interfacial interactions between CNT and PPy (Supplementary Fig. 3). The obtained PNIPAm/SA/PPy@CNT (PSPC) fiber exhibited reversible volume changes, with pronounced shrinkage observed above its volume phase transition temperature (VPTT) (Fig. 2b). The fiber contracted at elevated temperatures and recovered upon cooling, due to the thermally induced collapse of the polymer network.

The morphology and mechanical properties of the PSPC fiber were further characterized. The dried fiber showed a diameter of approximately ~345 μm. SEM images revealed a smooth surface and a crack-free cross-section (Fig. 2c–f). The elongation at break and Young’s modulus of the PSPC fiber were 168% and 160 kPa, respectively, compared with 91% and 60 kPa for PNIPAm/SA (PS) fibers (Supplementary Fig. 4). This increase in mechanical properties is associated with hydrogen bond interactions between PPy@CNT and the polymer matrix40,41. To verify these interactions, FTIR analysis was performed. Compared with PS, the –OH stretching band in PSPC shifted from 3438 to 3424 cm−1, and the C = O stretching band shifted from 1650 to 1629 cm−1, suggesting enhanced hydrogen-bonding interactions between PPy@CNT and the PNIPAm-SA composite (Supplementary Fig. 5). The Young’s modulus of the PSPC fiber was ~160 kPa, smaller than that of rat sciatic nerve tissue ~580 kPa42, which may facilitate better mechanical compliance and highlight its potential for neural applications. To evaluate the structural integrity of PSPC fibers under physiologically relevant conditions, immersion tests were conducted in PBS at 37 °C for up to 12 h. Only minor changes in mass and diameter were observed over time, indicating stable fiber structure (Supplementary Fig. 6).

Thermoresponsive properties and finite element analysis

The thermoresponsive behavior of PSPC fibers was first evaluated to examine their potential for temperature-induced self-adaptive conformability to neural tissues. Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) was performed from 10 to 50 °C at a heating rate of 5 °C/min. The endothermic peak observed in the DSC curve corresponds to the phase transition of the polymer network. PNIPAm aqueous solutions are widely reported to exhibit a phase transition at ~32 °C43. In contrast, as shown in Fig. 3a, the phase transition temperature of PS fibers decreased to 23.5 °C, consistent with the salt-induced reduction in transition temperature44,45. This shift is likely caused by interactions between Ca2+ and the amide oxygen atoms of PNIPAm, which weaken PNIPAm-water hydrogen bonding and promotes hydrophobic aggregation and chain contraction at lower temperatures. Incorporation of PPy@CNT partially offset this decrease, raising the transition temperature to 26.7 °C in PSPC fibers. This increase can be attributed to hydrogen-bonding interactions between hydrophilic functional groups on PPy@CNT and the PNIPAm/SA matrix, which enhance water retention within the polymer network. The increased hydration suppresses PNIPAm chain aggregation, resulting in an upward shift in temperature. Importantly, the transition temperature of PSPC fibers remained well below physiological temperature, indicating their suitability for implantable bioelectronic applications. As mentioned above, the PSPC fibers experience water expulsion and shrinkage when exposed to heat. Therefore, the water loss ratio and fiber diameter were measured as indicators of its thermo-responsive deformation. Owing to its low transition temperature of 26.7 °C, the fibers responded quickly when placed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at 37 °C. Within 30–50 s, the water loss ratio increased from 10 to 20%, while the diameter decreased from 410 to 360 μm (Fig. 3b). This rapid dehydration and shrinkage indicate the high sensitivity of fiber to physiological temperature. Moreover, the PSPC fibers showed reversible size changes during repeated heating and cooling. Its diameter decreased from 450 μm at 25 °C to 350 μm in PBS at 37 °C, and returned to 450 μm upon cooling back to 25 °C (Fig. 3c). These reversible volume changes confirm the thermo-responsive behavior of the PSPC fibers and demonstrate their stability and reusability under cyclic conditions.

In vitro simulation tests were carried out to evaluate the tissue-adaptive behavior of PSPC fibers. To mimic the morphology and mechanical properties of sciatic nerve fibers, soft PDMS was used to prepare fiber-like structures similar to native nerves, with a modulus of ~380 kPa and a diameter of ~1.3 mm (Supplementary Fig. 7). PSPC and SA/PPy@CNT (SPC) fibers were first wrapped around PDMS fibers. Due to their softness, both types of fibers could be physically wound onto the PDMS. The samples were then immersed in PBS at 37 °C to mimic the physiological conditions. Upon immersion, the SPC fibers began to detach from the PDMS because of weak physical interactions. In contrast, the PSPC fibers underwent thermally induced contraction, forming a tighter interface with the PDMS. When the PDMS fibers were shaken in PBS, PSPC fibers remained firmly attached, whereas nearly half of the SPC fibers detached. Furthermore, when one end of the fibers was lifted from the solution, the PSPC fibers pulled the PDMS fiber up together, while the SPC fibers immediately detached (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Movies 1 and 2). These results indicate that the PSPC fibers can self-adaptively tighten around PDMS fibers, forming a stable and robust interface. To further evaluate their dynamic thermoresponsive behavior, a PSPC fiber was wrapped around a PDMS fiber and placed in a temperature-controlled chamber at 37 °C. The fiber completed self-adaptive contraction within 3 s (Supplementary Movie 3). Finite element analysis showed that the normal stress generated by PSPC fibers during deformation was only ~3 kPa (Fig. 3e), which is below commonly reported thresholds associated with acute nerve compression injury of ~9 MPa46. In addition, blood flow after PSPC implantation was systematically evaluated. No statistically significant (P = 0.08) differences were observed compared with the sham-operated control group, as indicated by the relative blood flow index derived from laser speckle contrast imaging (Supplementary Fig. 8). These results suggest that PSPC implantation does not significantly affect local blood flow.

Electrochemical characterization

The electrochemical property of bioelectronic fibers in PBS were tested to assess their reliability in neural electrical stimulation and recording. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was carried out under sinusoidal conditions with an amplitude of 5 mV and frequency range of 1 Hz–100 kHz. The impedance characteristics of PS, PNIPAm/SA/PPy (PSP), and PSPC fibers were comparatively evaluated. At 1 kHz, PS fibers exhibited a high impedance of 8064 Ω, which decreased to 4370 Ω after the incorporation of PPy in PSP fibers. A further reduction was observed for PSPC fibers containing PPy@CNT (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 9). Specifically, as the PPy-to-CNT mass ratio in PSPC fibers increased from 0.3 to 1.2, the impedance gradually decreased to ~2.0 kΩ at 1 kHz (Supplementary Fig. 10). These results show that CNT incorporation effectively reduces the impedance of fibers. The stretchability of PSPC fibers initially increased with increasing PPy-to-CNT mass ratio, followed by a decline at higher ratios (Supplementary Fig. 11). Considering both impedance and mechanical properties, a PPy-to-CNT mass ratio of 0.8 was identified as the optimal ratio. Unless otherwise specified, PSPC fibers in the following sections refer to this composition. In addition, PSPC fibers exhibited lower phase angles at low frequencies compared with PS fibers, indicating a reduced phase delay between current and voltage. This reduction in phase lag improves the temporal responsiveness of the current, thereby facilitating rapid charge delivery during neuroelectric stimulation (Fig. 4b). Other nanomaterials, including silver nanowires (AgNW) and polyaniline (PANI) nanorods, were also used as conductive fillers to fabricate similar fibers. These fibers exhibited comparable initial impedance. However, the impedance of the PANI- and AgNW-based fibers increased during immersion in PBS, whereas the CNT-based fibers remained stable (Supplementary Fig. 12), owing to the higher chemical and physicochemical stability of CNT.

A higher charge injection capacity (CIC) enables more efficient charge transfer at the electrode-tissue interface, thereby allowing more effective electrical stimulation. The CIC of PSPC fibers increased markedly to 10 μCcm−2, about 35 times higher than that of PS fibers, which had a CIC of 0.28 μCcm−2 (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Fig. 13). In addition, the charge storage capacity (CSC) of PSPC fibers was 0.84 mCcm−2, nearly nine times higher than that of PS fibers, which was 0.093 mCcm−2 (Fig. 4d). It also exceeds the 0.65 mC cm−2 observed for PSP fibers, indicating the important role of CNT in improving the electrochemical performance (Supplementary Fig. 14). This enhanced CSC can promote signal transmission, ensuring efficient stimulation and reliable recording. The improved performance results from the synergistic combination of the highly conductive CNT and the electrochemically active PPy. Notably, the electrochemical performance under physiological conditions is a critical factor for neural interfacing. The charge storage and transport characteristics of PSPC fibers remained stable at 37 °C, showing negligible change, which indicates good suitability for in vivo applications (Supplementary Figs. 15–17).

Electrochemical stability of PSPC fibers at physiological temperature was evaluated by cyclic voltammetry (CV). Notably, the CV curves obtained during cycling were highly overlapping, and a 97% CSC retention was observed after 100 cycles, indicating the excellent electrochemical stability of PSPC fibers (Fig. 4e, f). Moreover, CV curves showed a gradual increase in the enclosed area as the scan rate increased from 20 to 500 mV s−1, indicating an enhanced current response. This behavior suggests a hybrid capacitive mechanism governed by both surface redox reactions and ion diffusion processes (Fig. 4g). Galvanostatic charge-discharge (GCD) measurements were performed at various current densities within the potential window of −0.5 to 0.5 V to evaluate the charge-discharge behavior of the fibers. All GCD curves showed nearly symmetrical triangular shapes across different current densities, indicating well-matched charge and discharge processes and good electrochemical reversibility of the bioelectronic fibers (Fig. 4h). Furthermore, the electrochemical stability of the bioelectronic fibers was evaluated by soaking them in PBS at 37 °C for different durations. They exhibited good stability, with only a slight impedance change observed after 1 h of soaking (Fig. 4i).

Cytocompatibility and in vivo biocompatibility

The cytocompatibility of the bioelectronic fiber was systematically evaluated. PSPC fibers were cultured with mouse fibroblasts (L929) to assess cell viability, and cytotoxicity was examined using CCK-8 assays. Live/dead staining showed that the density of viable L929 cells increased progressively during the culture period, comparable to that of the control group (Fig. 5a). Consistently, the optical density (OD) values at 450 nm of cell cultures with PSPC fibers also increased over time, showing no significant difference from the control group (Fig. 5b). In addition, the survival rates of cells in the PSPC group remained above 90% (Supplementary Fig. 18), confirming the non-toxicity of PSPC fibers. Likewise, SPC fibers also exhibited good cytocompatibility after co-culture with L929 cells (Supplementary Figs. 19–21). To assess in vivo biocompatibility, PSPC fibers were implanted subcutaneously in the dorsal region of rats for one week. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining showed no apparent inflammatory response around the implanted fibers compared with the sham controls. Immunofluorescence staining for α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) and cluster of differentiation 68 (CD68) further revealed no significant (P = 0.82 for α-SMA; P = 0.75 for CD68) inflammatory or foreign body responses around the implanted fibers, indicating good in vivo biocompatibility (Fig. 5c–e).

In vivo electrical stimulation and electrophysiological recording

Electrical stimulation of the sciatic nerve was performed to evaluate the in vivo stimulation performance of the bioelectronic fibers. During stimulation, hindlimb muscle contractions produced mechanical motion that could destabilize the electrode-nerve interface. PSPC fibers were wound around the sciatic nerve of rats and closely conformed to its highly curved surface owing to their thermally induced contraction. This self-adaptive behavior formed a stable and intimate fiber-nerve interface under dynamic conditions. Under electrical stimulation, clear hindlimb bending was observed (Fig. 6a, b), indicating effective neural activation and motor responses.

To further evaluate the role of the electrode-nerve interface in stimulation effect, SPC fibers, which lack the self-contraction function but exhibit comparable electrochemical characteristics, were used as controls (Supplementary Figs. 13, 22–24). SPC and PSPC fibers were wrapped around the sciatic nerve and subjected to the same stimulation conditions. As shown in Fig. 6c, both types of fibers exhibited similar stimulation effects, including movement angle and tension force, at the beginning of stimulation. After multiple stimulations, however, the response induced by SPC fibers declined markedly, with the movement angle and tension force dropping to 25.5 and 35.2% of their initial values, respectively. In contrast, PSPC fibers induced stable responses with minimal variation (Fig. 6d, e). Notably, stable responses were also maintained over a longer period of three hours and across different stimulation frequencies, with stimulations delivered at one-hour intervals (Supplementary Fig. 25). Furthermore, the movement angle increased progressively with higher stimulation currents (Supplementary Fig. 26), indicating effective and controllable stimulation. These results demonstrate that the self-adaptive PSPC fibers form a stable fiber-nerve interface that enables effective electrical stimulation.

The bioelectronic fibers were employed to synchronously record compound nerve action potentials (CNAP) evoked by neural stimulation. The recording system consisted of a recording electrode, a reference electrode, and a ground electrode, all of which self-adaptively conformed to the sciatic nerve to form an ultrastable and intimate interface, thereby enabling reliable signal acquisition (Fig. 6f). The CNAP of sciatic nerve were synchronously recorded in response to pulsed current stimulation at different amplitudes. With increasing stimulation amplitude, the CNAP signals were progressively enhanced, indicating that the stable and conformal fiber-nerve interface enables accurate and reliable neural recording (Fig. 6g). The cycling stability of neural recordings was also evaluated. As shown in Fig. 6h, the CNAP signals recorded by PSPC fibers remained stable over 1000 consecutive stimulations, retaining 99.5% of the signal intensity (Supplementary Fig. 27). The corresponding power spectral density remained nearly unchanged, indicating stable interfacial contact and signal acquisition. The stable interface formed by the self-adaptive bioelectronic fibers supports reliable neural monitoring under tissue motion.

In addition to forming stable neural interfaces for signal recording, PSPC fibers can also be applied at muscle interfaces to monitor other bioelectrical signals, such as compound muscle action potentials (CMAP). To evaluate their recording performance, PSPC fibers were wound around the extensor digitorum longus muscle (EDL) of rats. A stimulation current was delivered to the sciatic nerve using commercial needle electrodes, and clear CMAP signals were simultaneously recorded from the EDL using PSPC fibers (Supplementary Fig. 28a). The PSPC fibers maintained stable signals with up to 99.8% intensity retention over a 3 h period, owing to the stable electrode-muscle interface formed by self-contraction. For comparison, SPC fibers without the self-contraction function were used as control bioelectrodes. Signals recorded with SPC fibers gradually decreased, with only 34.2% intensity retention (Supplementary Fig. 28b, c).

Discussion

In this work, we report tissue-adaptive bioelectronic fibers that address the need for stable and adaptive bioelectronic interfaces. These fibers autonomously conform to rat sciatic nerves at physiological temperature, forming ultrastable neural interfaces for efficient stimulation and reliable recording of nerve action potentials. This adaptive behavior, driven by tissue-temperature-triggered self-contraction, enables mechanical compatibility with neural tissues and stable electrode-tissue integration. The fibers can be fabricated via a scalable wet-spinning process. As a result, the fibers exhibit good biocompatibility, effective conformability, and stable interfacing with negligible interfacial stress during contraction, supporting efficient stimulation, reliable signal recording, and robust interfacing performance. This highlights the potential of this approach for bioelectronic applications, with potential for broader applicability and long-term use.

For comparison, the present system is compared with representative bioelectrodes for tissue interfacing, as summarized in Supplementary Table 1. Conventional metal electrodes exhibit moduli in the GPa range, leading to mechanical mismatch with soft tissues. While metal-coated polymer electrodes reduce stiffness, achieving stable tissue contact often requires external stimuli such as heat or voltage. Hydrogel-polymer hybrid and hydrogel electrodes have further lowered the modulus into the tens to hundreds of kPa range. However, these systems rely on passive, noncovalent adhesion, limiting their ability to achieve active interfacial adaptation. In contrast, the PSPC fibers possess an ultralow modulus and utilize endogenous tissue heat to enable self-adaptive, conformal interfacing. In addition, their small, one-dimensional fiber shape allows better conformity to curved and irregular tissue surfaces. The fiber bioelectrode also demonstrates stable signals over repeated cycles.

While the feasibility of tissue-adaptive fibers has been demonstrated in this study, further validation over extended implantation periods will be important for more comprehensive signal acquisition and studies of neurological disorders and neuromodulation. The current single-fiber configuration offers opportunities for further improvement in signal throughput and spatial resolution, and future efforts will focus on high-density and multi-fiber integration, as well as wireless and miniaturized systems. In brief, this work demonstrates the potential of tissue-adaptive fibers as a promising strategy for achieving stable and reliable bioelectronic interfaces, supporting advanced and integrated bioelectronic systems.

Methods

Materials

Polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS, Sylgard 184) was obtained from Dow Corning Corporation. Anhydrous calcium chloride (CaCl2), N-isopropylacrylamide (NIPAm), pyrrole (99%), chloral hydrate, N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF) and sodium dodecyl benzene sulfonate (SDBS) were purchased from Shanghai Titan Technological Co., Ltd. Potassium persulfate (KPS) was obtained from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. Physiological saline (pH = 7.0, 0.9%) was supplied by Shanghai yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. The cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) and calcein-AM/propidium iodide (PI) double staining kit were obtained from Dojindo Molecular Technologies, Inc. (Japan). The male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats (180–200 × g) were supplied by Shanghai SLAC Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. The yellow dye for staining the PDMS fibers was purchased from Shenzhen Magic Color Technology Co., Ltd. The mouse fibroblasts (L929) were obtained from the Stem Cells Bank (Chinese Academy of Sciences, China). Stimulating electrodes made of stainless steel were purchased from Wuxi Jiajian Medical Instrument Co., Ltd. (China).

Preparation of PNIPAm solution

0.5 × g of NIPAm was dissolved in 6.0 × g of deionized water, followed by the addition of 0.02 × g of KPS (initiator) and 0.015 mL of TEMED (catalyst) and stirred thoroughly to obtain the mixed solution. Ultrasonication was then performed in an ice bath (<5 °C) for 30 min and then allowed to react for 12 h for free radical polymerization (72% yield). A pure PNIPAm solution with a polymer content of 6% was obtained by dialysis, which ensured the complete removal of residual monomer.

Preparation of PPy@CNT

CNT (1.0 × g) was added to 5 mL of 0.03 M SDBS solution, stirred for 1 h, followed by ultrasonic dispersion for 3 h. Subsequently, 0.58, 1.45, and 2.03 × g of pyrrole monomer was added to the dispersion and stirred for 30 min under an ice bath. Polymerization was initiated by the slow dropwise addition of 1 mL of 5 M FeCl3 solution using a dropping funnel, and the reaction was maintained under ice-bath conditions for 12 h. After completion, the products were washed with anhydrous ethanol until colorless and dried under vacuum at 50 °C for 24 h to obtain PPy@CNT composites with different PPy-to-CNT mass ratios. The PPy content was determined by weighing the CNT before and after the polymerization of pyrrole. The composite samples were denoted as PPyx@CNTy, where x and y represent the mass ratio of PPy to CNT, respectively. PPy nanoparticles were synthesized by polymerization of pyrrole (140 μL) in 50 mL of 5 M FeCl3 aqueous solution under an ice bath for 12 h, followed by centrifugation and washing with anhydrous ethanol.

Preparation of fibers

All fibers were fabricated by injecting the corresponding spinning solutions into a 5 wt% CaCl2 coagulation bath. The PNIPAm/SA (PS) spinning solution was prepared by mixing 5.0 × g of PNIPAm aqueous solution, 0.08 × g of SA, and 1.0 × g of deionized water. For the preparation of PNIPAm/SA/PPy (PSP) fibers, the spinning solution was obtained by mixing 5.0 × g of PNIPAm aqueous solution, 0.08 × g of SA, 10 mg of PPy, and 1.0 × g of deionized water. A PPy@CNT dispersion was first prepared by dispersing 10 mg of PPy@CNT in 1.0 × g of deionized water. Then, the resulting dispersion was mixed with 0.08 × g of SA and 5.0 × g of water to obtain the SA/PPy@CNT (SPC) spinning solution. The PNIPAm/SA/PPy@CNT (PSPC) spinning solution was prepared by mixing 5.0 × g of PNIPAm aqueous solution, 0.08 × g of SA, and 1.01 × g of the PPy@CNT dispersion. Three spinning solutions (PNIPAm/SA/CNT, PNIPAm/SA/AgNW, and PNIPAm/SA/PANI) were prepared by mixing of 5.0 × g of PNIPAm aqueous solution, 0.08 × g of SA, 1.0 × g of water, and 10 mg of the corresponding conductive material. Each spinning solution was injected into the coagulation bath using a syringe with a 0.26 mm needle at an injection rate of 0.2 mL/min. Fibers formed rapidly when the spinning solution was injected into the coagulation bath. After ~ 20 min of gelation, the fibers were collected and rinsed three times with deionized water.

Preparation of stained PDMS fiber

Stained PDMS fibers with a diameter of 1.3 mm were fabricated using a polymer mold by curing a PDMS prepolymer/curing agent mixture containing yellow dye. The mixture, prepared at a 20:1 mass ratio of base to curing agent, was cured at 100 °C for 3 h, followed by demolding.

Electrochemical property measurement

The electrochemical workstation (CHI660E, Shanghai Chenhua Instrument Co., Ltd., China) was used to perform electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS), cyclic voltammetry (CV), charge injection capacity (CIC), and galvanostatic charge-discharge (GCD) measurements in a 0.1 M PBS solution with a standard three-electrode configuration. A 1 cm bioelectronic fiber with a diameter of approximately 450 μm, containing 2.8 wt% PPy@CNT in the polymer matrix, was immersed in PBS and placed between sandwiched between two platinum (Pt) electrodes as the working electrode. An Ag/AgCl served as the reference electrode, and a Pt sheet (1 × 1 cm) was used as the counter electrode. For EIS measurements, a 5 mV amplitude was applied with a frequency sweep from 100 to 105 Hz. CV tests were conducted in the range of −0.5 ~ 0.5 V at scanning speed of 20–500 mV/s. CIC was measured by applying biphasic pulses of ±0.5 V with a duration of 0.5 ms versus the Ag/AgCl reference electrode. GCD measurements were conducted between −0.5 and 0.5 V (vs Ag/AgCl) at current densities from 0.1 to 1 mA cm−2.

Characterization

The structure was observed using the cold field emission scanning electron microscope (SU-8010, Hitachi). Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) spectra were recorded using the Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (Nicolet iS50, PerkinElmer). The mechanical properties of fibers were tested by a universal tensile testing machine (UTM2103, Shenzhen Suns Technology). The tension generated during rat hindlimb movement was recorded with a biometric signal acquisition and analysis system (RM6240E, Chengdu Instrument Factory) with a muscle tension transducer (JZJ01, Chengdu Instrument Factory). The phase transition temperature of the fibers were obtained by differential scanning calorimetry (DSC 3500, NETZSCH Instruments). The Molecular weight of PNIPAm was determined by gel permeation chromatography (Agilent 1260 Infinity Ⅱ, Agilent Technologies) with N,N-dimethylformamide as solvent at 25 °C.

Stability of PSPC fibers

PSPC fibers were immersed in PBS at 37 °C for different times. At each time point, fibers were removed, blotted dry, and weighed. Fiber diameters were measured at 37 °C by optical microscopy at multiple positions, and the mean values were calculated.

Blood flow detection

Blood flow was assessed using a 2D laser speckle imaging system (OMEGAZONE OZ-2, OMEGAWAVE, Inc.). The CCD camera captured speckle images of blood flow with an acquisition time of 1 s at a defined blood vessel location on the nerve surface. The laser speckle intensity scattered by flowing blood corpuscles was visualized as color-coded speckle maps, from which the relative blood flow index (arb. units) was calculated over a 30 s measurement period. All measurements were repeated for four independent samples, and the results are presented as mean ± standard deviation.

Cytocompatibility assessment

The L929 was used as the cell model to evaluate the effects of PSPC and SPC fibers on cell behaviors. The PSPC and SPC fibers were cut into 2 cm segments, sterilized with 75% alcohol for 30 min, and then irradiated with UV light for 1 h. The fibers were washed with PBS before cell seeding. L929 cells were cultured in DMEM with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin solution. Then, cells were seeded on the sterilized fibers at the density of approximately 2 × 104 cells/well and cultured in the a 5% CO2 incubator at 37 °C. After culturing for 1, 3, and 5 days, the cell viability and proliferation were assessed using the CCK-8 assay. Briefly, the CCK-8 reagent was added to each well followed by incubation for 1 h. Then, the absorbance of the supernatants was measured at 450 nm using the microplate reader (Multiskan FC, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). At the same time, the fibers were stained using propidium iodide and calcein-AM (2 μM, Beyotime Biotech, China) for 30 min at 37 °C. Lastly, the live/dead cells were observed with the fluorescence microscope (Ti-s, Nikon, Japan).

Biocompatibility assessment

In vivo biocompatibility was evaluated by assessing the inflammatory and immune response of dorsal subcutaneous tissue after implantation of PSPC fibers for one week, with a sham-operated group serving as the control. Each group consisted of four male SD rats (6–8 weeks old). Rats were anesthetized with 0.8 mL of 10 wt.% chloral hydrate, and a 1–2 cm dorsal incision was made to create a subcutaneous pocket for implantation of PSPC fibers (~5 mm in length); sham animals underwent identical procedures without implantation. Incisions were sutured and saline was injected subcutaneously. After one week, peri-implant and sham tissues were collected, fixed, paraffin-embedded, sectioned, and subjected to H&E staining. For immunofluorescence, sections were antigen-retrieved using citrate buffer for α-SMA or EDTA buffer for CD68, incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by Cy3-labeled secondary antibodies. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and images were acquired using a Nikon Eclipse fluorescence microscope. Image quantification was performed using ImageJ (v1.53k), and statistical analysis was conducted with Origin 2021.

Stimulation and recording experiment

For sciatic nerve stimulation, CNAP recording, and CMAP recording, four male SD rats (6–8 weeks) were used for each experiment. The SD rats were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of chloral hydrate at a dose of 350 mg/kg body weight before surgery. The rats were then fixed on a stereotaxic apparatus. The hair over the surgical area was shaved, and the skin was incised to expose the sciatic nerve. PSPC fibers were gently positioned alongside the exposed sciatic nerve and manually wound around the nerve with fine forceps, forming three to five closely spaced turns. After self-shrinking to achieve conformal and stable contact with the nerve, cathode-first, charge-balanced electrical pulses (150 μA, 1–5 Hz) were delivered through PSPC fibers using a multichannel biosignal acquisition system (RM6240E, Chengdu Instrument Factory). For action potentials recording, commercial needle electrodes were used for sciatic nerve stimulations (300 μA, 0.5–1 Hz). CNAP and CMAP recordings were performed in separate experiments. CNAP was recorded from PSPC fibers integrated with the sciatic nerve as described above, whereas CMAP was recorded from fibers integrated with the EDL using the same procedure. The signals were acquired with an RM6240E system and analyzed in MATLAB 2024b.

Action potential signal retention analysis

The action potential signal retention ratio was calculated as the ratio of the signal amplitude after repeated electrical stimulation to that after the initial stimulation. The signal amplitude was determined as the difference between the maximum positive peak and the minimum negative trough.

Statistical analysis

All measurements were performed on four independent samples, and data are presented as mean ± SD. Statistical analysis between two groups were conducted using a two-sided Student’s t-test. Statistical significance was defined as p ≤ 0.05.
