Does including multiple hormones and family context together clarify hormone-behavior associations? A systematic review☆
Authors: Kristine Marceau, Jennifer Hu, Sohee Lee, Prudence Regacho, Gina Canino-Quiñones
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The objective of this study was to review the literature to determine whether simultaneously considering the hormonal milieu and the parenting domain of family context clarifies hormone-behavior associations in children and adolescents. We conducted a systematic review of PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science in May 2024 and PsycINFO in May 2025. Inclusion criteria: Studies included variable(s) assessing family context in the parenting domain, measures of multiple hormones in children/adolescents where at least one was from the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) or hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal (HPG) axis, and a child/adolescent behavioral outcome, and children/adolescents studied were under 18 years. Reviews and animal studies, studies that were not peer reviewed (e.g., dissertation/theses, chapters) or not written in English were excluded. Our search and screen yielded 27 articles. The Robins-E tool was used to assess risk of bias; we provide a narrative synthesis of results. Most studies examined multiple hormones, but independently. Fewer studies included measures of hormonal milieu, using ratios most often, but also data aggregation (e.g., factors, profiles), and/or by calculating interactions among hormones. Childhood maltreatment was the family context construct most often assessed, followed by parent psychopathology, and less often, measures of parent-child relationship quality. Results provided some evidence that 1) hormonal milieu-behavior associations differ in the context of family conflict than social evaluative threat. 2) Less positive family environments were associated with high cortisol-to-(low) DHEA ratios cross-sectionally, but to lower cortisol-to-DHEA ratio longitudinally, and cortisol-to-DHEA ratios can be associated with behavior problems, though inconsistently across studies. And, 3) maltreatment can strengthen single hormone-behavior associations but may not moderate the association of hormonal milieu with behavioral outcomes. There was a surprising lack of longitudinal studies, and very few replication opportunities across specific findings given the heterogeneity in which phenotypes were measured and the construction of models. We provide recommendations for future research including a framework for future work to build a more systematic literature base regarding the role of family context in multihormone-behavior associations.

Introduction

The field of developmental psychopathology has long been interested in the biological underpinnings of child and adolescent emotional and behavioral problems [1], often including hormone-behavior associations [[2], [3], [4]]. Across childhood and adolescence, both hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis hormones indexing stress responding [2,5] and hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal (HPG) axis hormones indexing puberty [6] have emerged as important for the development of emotional and behavior problems [3,4]. Hormone responses are fundamental biological signals that help to organize social behaviors across species and are thus highly context-dependent [7]. The family context is particularly salient for shaping children's social context and hormone responses [8] and remains important while changing across childhood and adolescence [9], yet is often overlooked in hormone-behavior associations. Within the family context, the parenting domain (e.g., parenting strategies like scaffolding and support, parent-child relationship quality like warmth, hostility, closeness, conflict, and parent characteristics that shape the family like parent psychopathology) has emerged as particularly significant for developmental programming of HPA and HPG axes and behavior [[8], [10], [11]].

Studies increasingly assess both HPA and HPG hormones with the goal of indexing the balance of multiple hormones (e.g., hormonal milieu) that together serve to facilitate responses to social situations to clarify and better understand hormone-behavior associations, though such studies are less common than studies of single hormones. In the present review, we sought to investigate a) how the literature on hormone-behavior associations in the context of family influences have used multiple hormone measurements and b) how family factors are integrated into studies of hormone-behavior associations (e.g., as a moderator of hormone-behavior associations). We thus review the evidence from studies that included more than one hormone from the HPA and/or HPG axes and a measure in the parenting domain of family context in relation to child emotional/behavioral outcomes.

Parenting and parent-child relationships and the development of hormonal milieu

There is evidence that HPA-HPG hormonal milieu changes with development (see Ref. [12] for review), and social development, including changes in parenting and parent-child relationships, helps to organize the development of HPA and HPG axes from birth beyond adolescence [11]. During middle childhood (e.g., age 6 to puberty), when parents are key regulators of children's HPA axes, both axes are less active – particularly when buffered by social stress regulators like supportive parenting [13]. Secure and sensitive parent-child relationships are associated with better stress-response (e.g., cortisol) regulation whereas parental depression, poorer-quality parent-child relationships, and the lack of supportive care environments are linked to worse HPA stress regulation and earlier increases in pubertal hormones [8]. As children develop more self-regulatory capacity across childhood and into adolescence, so does their HPA-related stress responding decline [13], and parental buffering of HPA activity is less effective [14]. In late childhood/early adolescence, increases in DHEA drive adrenarche and pubertal development including HPG activity [15], and puberty can recalibrate the HPA axis, particularly when the environment differs from the early rearing environment [16]. Pubertal recalibration of the HPA axis was stronger for children who experienced lack of supportive care in early childhood [10], and was disrupted by concurrent family conflict [17]. Together, family environmental inputs are important for shaping developmental trajectories of both axes and the hormonal milieu [11].

Measurement and operationalization of hormonal milieu

The hormonal milieu can be captured by the momentary balance of multiple hormones using single measurements (e.g., cross-sectional), for example, using ratios [18] and interaction terms to establish profiles of the hormonal milieu at single assessments (“synergy” parameters; [[19], [20], [21]]). These methods are well-suited to testing whether individual differences in momentary hormonal milieu are linked to individual differences in psychopathology outcomes. For example, DHEA has shown to offset the risks associated with high cortisol for several developmental psychopathology outcomes – particularly internalizing problems [2]. In contrast, the combination of low cortisol with higher testosterone has been linked to aggression [22], though this effect is small [23]. These studies typically examine only two hormones at a time. More comprehensive approaches to operationalize hormonal milieu from single measurements include modeling multiple hormones or ratios together (e.g., controlling for other hormones to isolate effects; [21]) and using data aggregation techniques (e.g., factor analysis; Bernhard et al., 2021).

In addition to the cross-sectional measures described above, hormonal milieu can also be captured with repeated measures indexing the (un)coordinated responses of multiple hormones to environmental cues. This is often via ‘coupling’ parameters derived from multilevel models that quantify the extent to which two hormones are co-recruited in response to a task or over time (e.g., positive coupling) or when one hormone is recruited in tandem with another other being suppressed (e.g., negative coupling) [20]. Zakreski and colleagues [12] reviewed evidence of hormone coupling and concluded that positive associations of HPA and HPG hormones occur both between and within-individuals and are observable at multiple developmental stages across the lifespan. Coupling parameters can capture within-person associations of (only) two hormones on a shorter timescale, as well as whether individual differences in (un)coordinated hormone responses to environmental cues or changes across a day or development are linked to individual differences in psychopathology outcomes. For example, youth with higher interpersonal psychopathy had stronger positive coupling of HPA-HPG hormones across the day (more coordinated diurnal changes) than youth without interpersonal psychopathy, whereas youth with higher callus-unemotional traits had less coordinated diurnal changes during adolescence [24]. Another study found no associations between the severity or directionality (e.g., primarily exhibiting internalizing vs. externalizing symptoms) of psychopathology with diurnal coupling of HPA-HPG hormones [19].

Coupling may also be shaped by family context. Early adversity and aggressive parenting diminished positive coupling in females but increased cortisol-testosterone and decreased cortisol-DHEA coupling in males [25]. Earlier family stress predicted weaker cortisol-DHEA coupling during adolescence for females but tighter positive coupling for males, whereas concurrent family psychopathology associated with tighter positive cortisol-DHEA coupling for females but weaker coupling for males [26]. A next step, now that associations have been demonstrated between measures of hormonal milieu with both family context and behavior, is to examine the extent to which family context shapes hormonal milieu-behavior associations.

Present study

We posit that family context, particularly in the parenting domain, is important to consider for understanding how hormone-behavior associations develop across childhood and adolescence. However, at present the literature is sparse and located across several disciplines and journals with diverse audiences (e.g., psychiatry, endocrinology, developmental science, family science). We conducted a systematic review to understand the state of the literature with regard to the role of family context, particularly the parenting domain, in studies of associations between HPA and HPG hormones (where multiple hormones were measured to capture hormonal milieu) with child and adolescent emotional/behavioral outcomes (hereafter termed hormone-behavior associations). We sought to 1) characterize how the literature of hormone-behavior associations in the context of family influences a) operationalizes the hormonal milieu and b) conceptualizes relations among the three major constructs (e.g., hormones, family, and behavior), and 2) summarize what has been learned from these studies. We hypothesized that studies would examine family context measures as moderators contextualizing multi-hormone behavior relationships given that hormones are context-dependent and help to organize social behaviors (H1), and that examining hormonal milieu while considering the family context (e.g., primarily as a moderator, and/or by study design with family interactions as the context of hormone assessment) would clarify hormone-behavior associations (H2).

Materials and method

This review and protocol described below were not pre-registered. All materials (e.g., endnote libraries, excel file coding sheets) are available upon author request.

Search strategy

Fig. 1 depicts the flow chart documenting our search procedures and outcomes (PRISMA; [27]). In brief, we included three rounds of searching. First, to assess all the published studies on the relations between hormonal milieu, family relationships, and internalizing and externalizing mental health outcomes during childhood and adolescence we searched four databases. These databases were selected because they together broadly index literature in social, behavioral, health and life sciences and that include good coverage of both behavioral endocrinology and developmental and psychological literature bases – the areas most relevant to the research questions included in this study. PubMed, Scopus and Web of Science were searched on May 15, 2024 with no restriction on publication date (described in “round 1” below). A search of PsycINFO was added April 11, 2025 (described in “round 3” below). After two independent raters screened title/abstracts, we subjected the full-texts selected for inclusion (e.g., “included studies”) from the initial search to two additional searches (described in “round 2” below): 1) We used PubMed's “similar to” feature to identify articles similar to the included studies. 2) We used Google Scholar's “cited by” feature to identify articles that cited the included studies. Google scholar was excluded as a primary database for the search because initial searches conducted when developing search terms yielded over 17,000 results, and the top few pages of results did not produce relevant results.

Inclusion/exclusion criteria

To be included in the review, studies had to meet the following criteria: (1) Studies must have included variables directly associated with the parenting domain of family context. Specifically, we included measures of family relationships (e.g., parent-child conflict, global family function that includes the parent-child relationship) or/and parenting (e.g., maltreatment, parenting styles), or/and parental psychopathology (which has shown to directly predict parenting and parent-child relationships; [28,29]). Measures that did not tap into the processes between parents and their children (e.g., measures of prenatal stress) or that have been shown to be indirectly associated with the parenting domain of family context (e.g., family economic stress; [30,31]) were excluded. (2) Studies must have included measures of multiple hormones where at least one was from the HPA- or HPG-axis, and hormone measurements must have been for children or adolescents. Studies that did not include multiple hormones, such as only measuring cortisol or DHEA or testosterone, were excluded. Studies reporting parents’ hormones only (e.g., not reporting child/adolescent hormones) were excluded. (3) The age of included children and/or adolescents must be under 18 years. Because longitudinal studies can span a period of multiple years, longitudinal studies that conducted their first measurement wave in childhood or adolescence and followed children until adulthood (>18yrs) were included. Studies of college students (only) were excluded. (4) Animal studies were excluded. (5) Studies must have included some kind of behavioral outcome for children. (6) Studies must have been peer reviewed empirical studies. Review papers, book chapters, or dissertations/theses were excluded. (7) Studies must have been in English.

Screening eligible studies

Round 1

As shown in Fig. 1, the initial database searches yielded 336 records identified through database searching. After duplicates were removed, 279 unique records were screened for inclusion via abstract review. Two trained raters screened these abstracts, and abstracts moved forward for full text review if one or both determined the study may be relevant. Of these, 219 were excluded due to topic irrelevance (e.g., variables associated with family relationships or child mental health-related outcomes were not included, animal studies), age of the sample (i.e., age of adolescents older than 18 years), or manuscript type (review papers, book chapters, or dissertations rather than empirical studies). The inter-rater agreement was high at 98.21 %. Disagreements were discussed by both raters and the first author until all three individuals reached consensus. In total, 60 studies of the database search were deemed potentially eligible.

Next, full-texts were screened by two trained raters. The same inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied. Of the 60 that were screened, 46 were excluded due to topic irrelevance (primarily due to only prenatal variables, no familial measures, no behavioral measures, or only one hormone measured). The inter-rater agreement was high at 91.67 %. The first author examined full texts for which there were disagreements and engaged in discussion with both raters until consensus was reached. In total, 14 studies were deemed eligible and coded.

Round 2

The 14 eligible articles were then run through PubMed's “similar to” feature and Google Scholar's “cited by” feature (see Fig. 1), which yielded 2421 new articles for screening. The first author independently screened these citations. First, reviews, book chapters, and dissertation/theses were excluded. Next, animal studies and studies not in English were excluded. Finally, titles and abstracts were screened using the aforementioned inclusion and exclusion criteria. An additional 21 potentially eligible studies were found (2397 were excluded, most of which included only one hormone or adult samples). Next, full-texts were screened by two trained raters. The same inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied. Of the 21 that were screened, 8 were excluded due to topic irrelevance (primarily due to only prenatal variables, no familial measures, no behavioral measures, only one hormone measured). The first author examined full texts for which there were disagreements and engaged in discussion with both raters until consensus was reached. In total, 13 additional studies were deemed eligible and coded though the second round of searching and screening.

Round 3

We added the database PsycINFO, performing the search using the same terms and screening procedures described above on April 11, 2025. This search yielded 14 new articles for screening, all of which were not eligible (5 were theses, 5 were reviews, 1 was an addendum, 3 measured hormones in adults). Note that these numbers are included in the left side of the PRISMA flow chart, as a part of the identification of studies via databases. Thus, the final selection consisted of 27 eligible articles that were included and coded for this review.

Coding for eligible studies

Sample characteristics were coded, such as sample size, age and gender. Study type (cross-sectional or longitudinal) was also coded. Regarding the hormonal measurements in the articles, we coded for the hormones assessed (e.g., cortisol, DHEA, testosterone), biospecimen (e.g., saliva, hair, blood), timing and number of hormone measurements, as well as how the authors integrated information from multiple hormone measurements in their analyses (e.g., check all from the following options: examined in separate models; treated independently but within the same model; bivariate correlations/between-person association; coupling parameter/within-person association, interactions among hormones, ratios, other data aggregation such as factor/latent profile analysis). We categorized family context variables into three types of constructs. First was parenting environment/strategies (e.g., parental monitoring, etc.). Notably, many studies include measures of maltreatment, and for the purposes of this review, we categorized maltreatment as a parenting environment/strategy (but see section 4.2). The other categories were family relationships (e.g., parent-child conflict, family functioning, etc.), and parental psychopathology (e.g., parental depression, parental substance use disorder, etc.). We also coded whether the family context variable was about mothers, fathers, and/or the family in general, and recorded the specific constructs measured. We recorded the specific child behavior construct measured.

Finally, we coded how the three (hormones, family factor, child/adolescent behavior) constructs were conceptualized/analyzed (e.g., associations, mediation, moderation), and recorded which constructs were predictor (X), mediator (Med), moderator (Mod) or outcome (Y) variables. We summarized the nature of associations (e.g., the presence or absence, direction of the association – positive or inverse), and whether there were sex and age differences in the associations investigated.

Bias assessment

One author coded and a second author checked the quality of each primary study using the ROBINS-E tool [32], designed to assess risk of bias in observational studies in seven domains: bias due to confounding, arising from measurement of the exposure, in selection of participants into the study/analysis, due to post-exposure interventions, due to missing data, arising from measurement of the outcome, and election of the reported result (Fig. 2). Overall, just over half of the analyses were judged to have high risk of bias, most often driven by bias due to missing data. The rest of the studies were judged to have some concerns, most often due to selection of the reported results, and measurement of the exposure. The included studies generally had low risk of bias arising from confounding, due to post-exposure interventions, and measurement of the outcome.Fig. 2Risk of bias.Fig. 2

Data synthesis

A narrative synthesis was conducted given the wide variation in measurement and analytic strategies included in the reviewed studies. This heterogeneity precluded statistical synthesis.

Family context variable information (Table 1 “family variable”)

Eleven studies (40.74 %) measured family relationships, 16 (59.26 %) measured parenting environment/strategies, and seven (25.93 %) measured parental psychopathology. Twenty one (77.77 %) included measures that didn't make a specific distinction between which family members were included, that is, they measured the family in general or did not specify a specific family member (e.g., measuring maltreatment generally but not by whom; “parent-child” conflict), four measured both maternal and paternal psychopathology/substance use phenotypes (14.81 %), and four (14.81 %) measured maternal phenotypes exclusively (i.e., mother-child conflict, [41]; aspects of mother-child interactions, [49,51]; maternal depression, [38]). None exclusively measured a paternal phenotype as the family construct.

Adolescent behavior or mental health phenotypes (Table 1 “Child/Adolescent Mental Health Phenotype”)

Most of studies (n = 10, 37.04 %) measured both externalizing and internalizing problems, while some measured both internalizing behavior and callous-unemotional traits (n = 1, 3.70 %), externalizing behavior and callous-unemotional traits (n = 2, 7.41 %), or substance use and externalizing behavior (n = 1, 3.70 %). Other studies measured only externalizing (n = 6, 22.22 %), internalizing (n = 2, 7.41 %), substance use (n = 2, 7.41 %), negative emotionality (n = 1, 3.70 %), socioemotional problem (n = 1, 3.70 %), or callous-unemotional traits (n = 2, 7.41 %). Most studies (n = 18, 66.67 %) treated adolescent behavior or mental health variables as outcomes, while five (18.52 %) treated them as antecedent and two (7.41 %) treated adolescent behavior or mental health variables as a covariate.

Associations

Ten studies examined primarily correlations, including studies wherein hormone-behavior, family-behavior, and hormone-family associations were completely independent, and several studies examining more complex models included correlations. In the sections below, we omit the details of findings when analyses could not support our study objective: to understand the role of family context in studies that index the hormonal milieu by measuring multiple hormones simultaneously in association with child or adolescent behavior. Most often this omitted single hormone-behavior associations.

Independent associations

Han et al. [41] was unique in that the focus was on multi-hormone behavior associations in the context of a parent-child conflict discussion task, and therefore the family relationship was contextual by study design rather than measured. They measured hormone responses by computing lower area under the curve with respect to ground (AUCg) from pre-conflict, +20m, and +40m saliva samples, and included cortisol, testosterone, and DHEA as correlated outcomes in association with child internalizing and externalizing problems. They found that youth with more internalizing problems during adolescence had blunted cortisol activity to the parent-child conflict discussion but youth with more externalizing and fewer internalizing symptoms had increased cortisol and testosterone activity in response to the task. There was a different pattern of finding in response to the Trier Social Stress Test, indicating that the family context, in this case acute parent-child conflict, can impact the hormonal milieu and behavior problems. Finally, although Taylor-Cavelier and colleagues [52] initially included adolescent depression a covariate, it was excluded (because it was a null association) in the final model that found that the effect of child maltreatment on peak cortisol levels in response to stress was amplified in the context of high DHEA/cortisol ratios but diminished in the context of high levels of DHEA alone [52].

Correlations among all three constructs

Reindl et al. [49] examined associations between a family variable and hormone measures, and separate associations between the family variable and behavioral outcome (but did not test hormone-behavior associations). They found that caregiving quality among youth in foster care was related both to hormonal milieu (i.e., children in lower-quality foster care had lower cortisol-to-DHEA ratios, and perhaps lower cortisol levels, but no difference in DHEA or progesterone levels) and to behavioral problems [49]. Several studies included associations between a family variable and hormone measure, and separate associations between hormone measures with child outcomes (Clemens et al., 2020; Dawes et al., 1999; [33,51]; Mizuchima et al., 2015). Only two of these studies included measures of hormonal milieu–the combination of cortisol and DHEA–and found that child maltreatment and frequent change of caregivers was cross-sectionally associated with the combination of high cortisol and low DHEA (Clemens et al., 2020; [33]). However, multi-hormone behavior associations were mixed: in one study the combination of high cortisol and low DHEA was associated with psychopathology symptoms (Clemens et al., 2020), but in the other youth with this profile were also viewed as being more likeable [33], whereas youth with high DHEA but average (not low) cortisol had increased internalizing symptoms [33]. Together, these studies suggest that more severe family-related phenotypes may be consistently associated with higher cortisol-to-DHEA ratios, and that although there seem to be some signal such that cortisol-to-DHEA ratios are associated with behavior problems, the findings may differ by outcome and/or study.

There were three cross-sectional studies examining multiple hormones separately but within the same statistical model (e.g., accounting for hormonal milieu by adjusting for other hormone levels/responses), including different family variables and hormones. First, family stress was associated with blunted cortisol responses to a corticotropin-releasing hormone infusion, whereas maternal psychopathology was associated with elevated ACTH but lower cortisol responses to the infusion; blunted ACTH response but normal cortisol response to CRH infusion in adolescence associated with both childhood and later depression [51]. Second, maltreated children who were “unsettled” (still living with biological parents or in residential care for <1y) had higher awakening cortisol than “settled” (in residential home care for >1y) and non-maltreated youth, and “settled” maltreated youth had higher bedtime salivary oxytocin than non-maltreated youth [48]. The “unsettled” group also had higher depression than “settled” and non-maltreated youth but oxytocin and cortisol levels were unassociated with depression [48]. Finally, parent substance use disorder was associated with decreased testosterone, dihydrotestosterone, pre-task cortisol, and cortisol reactivity to an auditory evoked potential task, and family dysfunction was associated with lower pre-task cortisol and cortisol reactivity, however hormonal measures were unassociated with adolescent behavior (Dawes et al., 1999). Together, these studies suggest that diverse family-related factors are associated with individual differences in specific hormone levels when controlling for the levels of other hormones, but those hormone levels were not consistently associated with behavior.

Longitudinal associations

Several studies longitudinally examined the predictive effect of family factors on hormonal milieu (some included in the context of more complex models). Results demonstrated that unstable, negative family environments (i.e., abuse/parental separation; foster care placement) was associated with later individual differences in hormonal milieu (e.g., low cortisol-to-DHEA ratio: [49,46]; reduced ACTH-to-cortisol ratio: [51]), but there were no direct associations between parent substance use and testosterone or cortisol (Tarter et al., 2013). Lee et al. [46] also found that lower cortisol-to-DHEA ratio was associated with higher levels of externalizing problems. Cho et al. [38] found that higher testosterone was negatively associated with infant socioemotional problems while cortisol levels were not associated with infant socioemotional problems. Tarter et al. (2013) found that high testosterone (but not cortisol) levels mediated the association between increased adolescent externalizing problems and substance use in young adulthood, suggesting bidirectional hormone-behavior associations.

Summary

Across these studies, results suggest that hormone-behavior associations are concurrent and most likely bidirectional over time. However, specific findings remain mixed: the “risk” profile for cortisol-to-DHEA ratio may differ concurrently (Clemens et al., 2020) vs. longitudinally [46], and the sample context may be particularly important to consider in how hormonal milieu is associated with behavioral and mental health.

Moderation

Nine studies examined moderation including all three constructs. Four treated family variables as moderators (as hypothesized); examining whether family context in the parenting domain modified the relationship between hormonal milieu and adolescent emotional/behavioral problems. Two treated hormone variables as a moderator of the relationship between family variables and adolescent behavioral and mental health. Finally, three studies examined children's mental health as a moderator of family adversity – (multi)hormone associations. Again, we focus our text on study findings that directly address our research question and omit reporting of findings from studies examining one hormone at a time.

Family variables as moderators of multi-hormone-behavior associations

Maltreatment did not moderate associations of cortisol-to-DHEA ratios on resilience (though maltreatment did moderate associations of cortisol levels alone; [35]). Associations between a measure of allostatic load that included low morning cortisol, flattened cortisol diurnal slope and high DHEA among other measures and multiple domains of psychopathology symptoms were stronger among maltreated than non-maltreated youth [50]. In another set of analyses, depressive symptoms were highest in maltreated youth with low morning cortisol and high morning DHEA [50]. Other studies examined hormone levels only independently (Black et al., 2019; [34]), with mixed findings. Within each study there was a mixture of significant and null moderation effects presented. Taken together, the evidence that familial factors moderate multi-hormone-behavior associations are sparse and inconsistent across studies.

Hormones as moderators of family context-behavior associations

Two studies found that the deleterious effects of a negative family environments on behavior problems were amplified in the context of certain hormonal levels or milieus. Kimonis et al. [44] found that among youth with high DHEA/cortisol ratios, childhood maltreatment was associated with more internalizing problems. While Del Puerto-Golzarri et al. [39] examined cortisol and testosterone as moderators of associations of authoritarian parenting and aggression, these hormones were examined in separate models and did not index hormonal milieu.

Child behavior as moderators of family context-hormone associations

One study tested whether several family factors – parental dysfunction (distress and hostility), negative parenting practices, family conflict, and family stress (life events) were differentially associated with several hormones (cortisol, androstenedione, DHEA, testosterone, but examined in separate models and thus not indexing hormonal milieu) in youth with vs. without disruptive behavior [40]. Few of the tested interactions were significant, and the significant findings were not hypothesized. Another study examined callous-unemotional traits as a moderator of adverse familial backgrounds and hormonal response to treatment (e.g., steeper diurnal declines in testosterone, increases in cortisol pre-to-post treatment, also examined in separate models and thus not directly indexing hormonal milieu; [43]). The only study in this group to measure the hormonal milieu, Micol and colleagues [47], found that children's own depressive symptoms did not moderate the association between familial risk of depression and cortisol-to-DHEA ratios.

Mediation

Seven studies examined mediation, most often examining hormonal mediators of associations between family influences (typically broad early adversity-type influences) with child psychopathology outcomes.

Cross-sectional evidence

Bernhard et al. (2021) found that family factors such as adverse family situation and parental psychopathology discriminated between youth with conduct disorder vs. healthy controls, and that youth with conduct disorders had different multi-hormone factors. Specifically, for females the interaction of factor 3: cortisol/alpha-amylase and 4: oxytocin from a principal components analysis predicted conduct disorder risk such that only for girls with high cortisol/alpha-amylase and low oxytocin was conduct disorder risk elevated. For boys, factor 2: elevated testosterone + DHEA-sulfate predicted conduct disorder. However, there was no evidence that hormone profiles mediated associations between family factors and youth psychopathology assessed contemporaneously. A second study in the same sample examined cortisol, oxytocin, and testosterone reactivity and recovery to the Trier Social Stress Test (TSST; Kirschbaum et al., 1993) independently and thus did not index the hormonal milieu [36].

Longitudinal evidence

Three studies included multiple hormones in longitudinal models as mediators of interpersonal violence exposure on externalizing and internalizing problems [37], testing bidirectional longitudinal associations between hormone reactivity, family problems, and negative emotionality (Marceau et al., 2012) and in complex moderated mediation models [53], but examined hormones separately – not indexing hormonal milieu. Lee et al. [46] found that more severe early life stress exposure was associated with externalizing problems through a lower cortisol-to-DHEA ratio, though only for boys.

Sex and age differences

Relatively few studies examined sex differences (n = 15, 18.52 %) and/or age differences (n = 3, 11.11 %) in their focal analyses (i.e. the association of multi-hormone and family influences). Four studies (14.81 %) found gender differences in the focal analysis. Findings including measures of hormonal milieu (not single hormones) showed that the combination of high cortisol and low DHEA was associated with more pervasive maltreatment experiences [33]. In addition, mediation of environmental risk on conduct disorder by neuroendocrine profiles was stronger in females (Bernhard et al., 2021). Only among males, a lower cortisol-to-DHEA ratio mediated the association between early-life-stress (ELS) and externalizing problems [46]. One study (3.70 %) found that gender didn't moderate the relationship between familial risk and cortisol-to-DHEA ratio [47]. These studies (except [33]) controlled for pubertal status.

Three studies examined age differences, with one looking at a narrow (i.e. 12 months) range in early childhood [49], one wide range (i.e. 4 years) in middle childhood to early adolescence [41], and one wide range (i.e. 4 years) in adolescence [47]. One study found older adolescents exhibited more DHEA and testosterone activity in the context of an anger induction during a parent-child conflict [41], whereas two did not find any differences (e.g., no age difference in the association of caregiving quality and cortisol-to-DHEA ratio: [49]; familial risks and cortisol-to-DHEA ratio: Micol et al., 2019). Han et al. [41] did not control for pubertal status whereas Micol et al. [47] did. In addition, Micol et al. [47] tested for moderation by pubertal status and did not find any effect.

Discussion

We identified and reviewed twenty-seven studies that examine the interplay between family factors, multiple hormones, and child and adolescent mental and behavioral health. We hypothesized that studies would examine family relationship measures as moderators contextualizing multi-hormone behavior associations (H1). However, this set of studies encompassed a very wide variety of phenotypes and strategies for assessing family-multihormone-behavior interplay, not only conceptualizing family relationship measures as contextual influences on multi-hormone behavior associations. In addition, this set of studies treated multiple hormones in several different ways – from completely separately in independent models to measures of hormonal milieu. The most common family context constructs (which we categorized into the parenting domain) measured in the included studies were childhood maltreatment (which here is presumed to be a familial influence, but does not always reflect familial influences, see section 4.2), parent psychopathology, and in fewer instances, measures of parent-child relationship quality. Finally, although most studies (due to our inclusion/exclusion criteria) examined psychopathology symptoms in the internalizing and externalizing domain, there was a mix in terms of treatment of mental health as predictors, moderators, and/or outcomes. The diversity in measurement strategies and analyses speaks to the complexity of the interplay between family factors, multiple hormones, and child and adolescent mental and behavioral health, and causes challenges in replication of results and in summarizing the findings across studies. Thus, we cannot at this time conclude support for our second hypothesis, that examining multiple hormones simultaneously while considering the family context would clarify developmental hormone-behavior associations (H2).

The most consistent findings (still in need of replication)

Most studies reported correlations among child, family, and hormone phenotypes. However, few quantified the hormonal milieu – most either examined multiple hormones but in separate models or statistically controlled for other hormone levels by including multiple hormones in the same model. This strategy does not capture the hormonal milieu, rather it quantifies the effect of one hormone while holding levels of another constant (partialling out covariation to get a clearer estimate of one hormone of interest). Of those that quantified the hormonal milieu, ratios by far were the most frequently studied indicators of hormonal milieu, likely because of the ease of creating and relative ease of interpreting ratios compared to more complex combinations of hormones. High cortisol-to-(low) DHEA ratios were generally associated with less positive family environments cross-sectionally (Clemens et al., 2020; [33]), but less positive family environments were longitudinally associated with lower cortisol-to-DHEA ratio [49,46]. Although cortisol-to-DHEA ratios have also been somewhat robustly linked to internalizing problems [2] such evidence was inconsistently found in the studies reviewed here. Combined with the finding that stronger coupled changes in cortisol and testosterone in response to parent-child conflict (but not social evaluative threat) was associated with externalizing problems [41], the reviewed studies corroborate general evidence that the family environment is associated with the hormonal milieu and also child adjustment, although the ways in which these associations present likely varies over time and context.

Second, there was some general evidence of moderation. Moderation is a test of contextualization – whether a focal association is strengthened or weakened in the context of a third variable. It was interesting that all three constructs were considered as contexts for the ways the other two are related. Of studies including a family context variable as a moderator, maltreatment was the most common (though it was also examined as a predictor), and there was some evidence that maltreatment strengthens hormone-behavior associations, but not for measures of hormonal milieu – rather, for individual hormones (e.g. low cortisol but not cortisol-to-DHEA ratio; [35]). There was also some evidence that less positive family environments are more strongly associated with behavioral problems in the context of certain hormonal milieus (though no two findings were replicated). Across these studies, while there were typically one or two statistically significant moderation findings, there were more within the same study that were not statistically significant, and other studies found no moderation effects (e.g., Ref. [34,47]). Further, there was little consistency of evidence in one direction or another. This pattern of evidence is more consistent with the prevalence of false positives rather than powerful moderating effects. In some (but not all) studies, small sample sizes could also contribute to the null and inconsistent findings.

Finally, there was some evidence of the mediating role of hormones between familial influences and child psychopathology. Mediation is a test of process – whether the association between focal predictors (e.g. familial influences and child/adolescent psychopathology) happens through a third variable (hormonal milieu). The best test of mediation requires a longitudinal model, where temporal ordering ensures that the associations flow in the direction hypothesized, preferably while also controlling for earlier levels of the mediator and outcome to prevent reverse causation. Cross-sectional applications of mediation are limited because the researcher determines the ordering of variables, but the test is statistically equivalent to alternate variable ordering. While some studies support hormonal mediation, findings were both nuanced and limited by studies examining mediation effects in cross-sectional designs and/or using single hormones rather than measures of hormonal milieu. The strongest longitudinal evidence was that the cortisol-to-DHEA ratio mediated the effects of early life stress on externalizing problems – but only in boys [46].

The family context

We expected more studies would include measures of parenting strategies or family relationships than we found in the set of studies that met requirements for inclusion. Although our search terms did not include maltreatment, a large proportion of the studies included a child maltreatment phenotype as the indicator of family relationships. Although maltreatment does not need to be committed by a family member, it often is, and several questionnaire measures (e.g., the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire) specifically includes items about “people in my family” and “my parents” in addition to non-specific items with regard to perpetrator. Thus, we decided to retain maltreatment as a “family” phenotype for the purposes of this review. Similarly, the next largest category of “family influences” was parent psychopathology. Given evidence that parent psychopathology symptoms contribute to differences in family context and parenting behaviors [54,55], we elected to retain these studies as well. We urge caution in interpreting parent psychopathology as a holistic indicator of the family context rather than a family environmental influence – parent psychopathology is bi-directionally associated with youth psychopathology and genetically influenced [56,57]. Family contexts can be a cause and a consequence, or a proxy for unmeasured constructs (e.g., genetics, overall living conditions, poverty, resources, or other conditions) that could affect neuroendocrine function and mental health in parents and kids.

Current limitations of the field

Our review revealed that the field is haphazardly testing a very wide variety of hormones and family influences in association with child/adolescent psychopathology symptoms. Some differences in findings may be driven by methodological issues, including inconsistent treatment of hormone data [58], and small sample sizes that cannot reliably detect small associations. Together, the literature as it stands provides very little in terms of replication opportunities – replication attempts are critical, suggesting that more systematic work is needed moving forward. Even when studies include multiple hormones, it is still relatively rare to capture measures of hormonal milieu. It should be noted that most studies were cross-sectional, and if there were repeated hormone assessments, these were aggregated into single indicators (e.g., area under the curve, diurnal slopes, reactivity and/or recovery measures). We echo others [2,12,59] in reiterating how important a developmental perspective is in this area. We were surprised how few of the studies were longitudinal – for mediation in particular longitudinal data is imperative.

Looking forward: systematic evaluation of multi-hormone-behavior associations in the family context

Our recommendations for the field are threefold: First, it will be important to move toward a more consistent operationalization of the family context. We derived the categories of parenting environment/strategies, family relationships, and parental psychopathology from the review of the extant literature. Missing from the literature are studies examining parenting dimensions (e.g., responsiveness, warmth, involvement, demandingness, control, hostility) and styles (e.g., authoritarian, authoritative, permissive/indulgent, and neglectful) (Oropesa Ruiz, 2022), which may better capture the environmental backdrop in which hormone-behavior associations are developing across childhood and adolescence. That is, measuring the family context at this broader level, rather than at the level of specific parenting behaviors, may be a more potent moderator of hormone-behavior associations particularly on a longer or general timescale.

In contrast, specific parenting behaviors that elicit aspects of the social environment that have been previously linked to specific hormone responses may matter more for mediation models. For example, it is well-established that social-evaluative threat elicits specifically cortisol responses (Dickerson & Kemeny, 2004). Challenge has been shown to precipitate increases in testosterone [60]. Specific parenting behaviors tapping both social evaluative threat and challenge, such as displays of hostility or behavioral control particularly in front of peers, may therefore set off the increase of multiple hormones (in this case we would hypothesize both cortisol and testosterone), which could support specific responses in adolescents (e.g., increased antisocial behavior; [61]). In sum, we posit that examining parenting dimensions and styles will be more fruitful as contextual moderators of hormone-behavior associations at longer-term, more global timescales, but that hormonal milieus may be better mediators of specific parenting behaviors to adolescent behaviors during interactions/on shorter, daily timescales.

Second, we have defined hormonal milieu as the balance of multiple hormones that together serve to facilitate responses to social situations. Since a primary feature of hormone responses are to respond to and help to organize behaviors in response to environmental cues, longitudinal operationalizations are particularly valuable to the field moving forward. Further, the hormonal milieu is inherently within-person and should be measured using methods that capture within-person variance [21]. There is a long-standing debate on the utility of hormone ratios especially as a within-person measure, with several recommendations for construction (e.g., using log-ratios) or forgoing ratios in favor of interactions (Sollberger et al., 2016; Del Guidice et al., 2022). Novel approaches such as latent profiles, or machine learning techniques such as temporal network analysis [62], recurrent neural network analysis [63] (depending on the data) may improve the quantification of hormonal milieu. Clearly describing the measurement and interpretation in each study is crucial in this endeavor. In addition, most (but not all) studies examine only one operationalization of hormonal milieu—such as ratios, interactions, or coupling—rather than comparing the fit and utility of multiple approaches. When different studies examine different operationalizations of hormone milieu – and measure different family context and child behavior phenotypes – even conceptual replication becomes challenging. We urge researchers to test competing operationalizations of hormonal milieu within the same study (accounting for multiple testing) with the aim of converging on the most robust patterns of findings rather than over-interpreting the few significant findings if most findings are null.

Finally, a systematic approach in the field should consider the temporal ordering and timescales of multi-hormone-behavior associations. For fully understanding the temporal ordering of mediation, panel models that allow for bidirectional effects are imperative, and allow for simultaneous comparison of which direction of effects best fits the data. Longitudinal investigations are imperative.

Strengths and limitations of the present review

Strengths of this review include a systematic search of articles across several fields, synthesis of a complex and multifaceted literature, and focus on family context as a context in which to understand associations of the hormonal milieu and behavior during childhood and adolescence. Limitations include that in our search, there were a substantial number of studies that examined cross-system stress responses (e.g., peripheral-nervous system [PNS] and HPA) – this was out of the scope of the present review but represents another way of integrating biological systems. Our search terms focused on negative outcomes for child phenotypes, while paying less explicit attention to positive outcomes. Focusing on positive phenotypes is important, as it can guide future family research to integrate physiological perspectives and help children reach their full potential. Finally, since the articles included in this study adopt different statistical approaches and measures, we were unable to obtain meta-analytic estimates of effect sizes of the included associations.

Conclusions

As a field we need to work toward clarity in how we conceptualize family-multihormone-behavior interplay. The reviewed studies suggests that 1) hormonal milieu-behavior associations differ in the context of family conflict than in the context of social evaluative threat, 2) less positive family environments were associated with high cortisol-to-(low) DHEA ratios cross-sectionally, but lower cortisol-to-DHEA ratio longitudinally, and cortisol-to-DHEA ratios can be associated with behavior problems, and 3) maltreatment can strengthen single hormone-behavior associations, but may not moderate the association of hormonal milieu with behavioral outcomes.

We proposed a framework for future studies to build a more systematic literature base: Longitudinal studies that capture within-person hormonal milieu, and that compare multiple operationalizations of hormonal milieu are critical for improving the measurement and interpretation of this construct. We posit that parenting dimensions and styles may moderate hormonal milieu-behavior associations on developmental timescales, and that hormonal milieus may mediate of specific parenting behaviors to adolescent behaviors during interactions/on shorter, daily timescales. The hormonal milieu may best be considered a mechanism of the social environment – that is, positioned as a mediator of how family influences shape child development. In cases of moderation, because hormone responses organize behavior in response to social cues, the family environment should theoretically serve as the moderator of hormone-behavior associations. Beginning with these models may give the field a more systematic framework for understanding which contexts act as mediators and/or moderators, and of which hormonal milieus. Systematically testing this framework with longitudinal data on the appropriate timescale will lead to advances in our understanding because it will afford conceptual replications and illuminate which hormonal milieus are responsive to or modified by family context in a way that has a bearing on behavior.
