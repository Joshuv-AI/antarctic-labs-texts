Joint involvement in VEXAS and non-VEXAS clonal haematopoiesis: two clusters from a multicentre regional cohort
Authors: Baptiste de Maleprade, Fabien Jastrzebski, Ygal Benhamou, Gaetan Sauvetre, Benjamin Membrey, Marine Avenel, Alexandre Curie, Guillaume Armengol, Pauline Brevet, Vincent Langlois, Thierry Lequerre, Aspasia Stamatoullas-Bastard, Fabrice Jardin, Olivier Vittecoq
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjective: To describe the joint manifestations associated with clonal haematopoiesis and to compare patients with and without VEXAS (vacuoles, E1 enzyme, X-linked, autoinflammatory, somatic) syndrome. Methods: Patients screened for UBA1 mutations between December 2019 and January 2023 in 7 Normandie (France) hospitals were recruited retrospectively. Results: Thirty patients with a haematological disorder associated with dysimmune manifestations were included: 17 (57%) without UBA1 mutations (non-VEXAS) and 13 (43%) with UBA1 mutations (VEXAS). Thirteen (77%) non-VEXAS patients had joint involvement (arthralgia/arthritis), compared with 4 (31%) VEXAS patients (p value: 0.016). Unsupervised clustering using hierarchical clustering identified two clusters. Cluster 1 (14 patients) had more joint involvement (93% vs 25%, p value: 0.001), less UBA1 mutation (14% vs 69%, p value: 0.008), pulmonary involvement (7% vs 50%, p value: 0.017), chondritis (0% vs 50%, p value: 0.003) and unprovoked venous thrombosis (7% vs 43%, p value: 0.039) than cluster 2 (16 patients). Cluster 1 had more ASXL1 mutations (21% vs 0%). Conclusion: Joint involvement was more frequent in patients with clonal haematopoiesis and dysimmune manifestations unrelated to VEXAS syndrome.Keywords: Arthritis, Hematology, Inflammation

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

Arthralgia and arthritis are more frequently associated with clonal haematopoiesis without VEXAS syndrome.

HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY

Arthralgia and arthritis are frequent in non-VEXAS clonal haematopoiesis unlike in VEXAS, thus clonal haematopoiesis should not be overlooked in late-onset rheumatic diseases.

Introduction

Clonal haematopoiesis is a haematological disorder characterised by expanded clones of haematopoietic stem and progenitor cells.1 Its frequency increases with ageing, reaching 10%–30% after 70 years old.2 Within clonal haematopoiesis, clonal haematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP) is defined by somatic mutations in haematopoietic cells that are associated with cancer such as DNMT3A, TET2, ASXL1, JAK2, TP53 and SF3B1, without haematopoietic cancer.2 3 Myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS) is defined by morphologic dysplasia in haematopoietic cells associated with bone marrow failure, resulting in refractory peripheral cytopenia.1 4 Both CHIP and MDS are associated with an increased risk of acute myeloid leukaemia,5 all-cause mortality6 and dysimmune manifestations7 8 (up to 30% of MDS are associated with systemic autoimmune disease and dysimmunity (dMDS)9,11). The range of these manifestations is wide, from isolated biological abnormalities6 to authentic autoimmune disease.811,13 They can affect several organs and mimic vasculitis,14 connective tissue diseases,11 inflammatory arthritis10 or neutrophilic disease15 but often fail to meet classification criteria of systemic autoimmune disease. VEXAS (vacuoles, E1 enzyme, X-linked, autoinflammatory, somatic) syndrome, defined by UBA1 mutations in haematopoietic cells associated with dysimmunity, was recently described and is still being characterised.16 17 While joint involvement is relatively rare in VEXAS (30%),18 19 it was reported to reach up to 70% in dMDS.10 11 Nevertheless, joint involvement is not the only rheumatic manifestations seen in the patients with VEXAS and non-VEXAS clonal haematopoiesis, and other musculoskeletal involvement could be of importance as suggested by recent review finding 34% arthralgia but 47% musculoskeletal involvement in VEXAS.20 Those manifestations could include myalgias, enthesitis and polychondritis.21 But dMDS studies were carried out before 2020, the year in which UBA1 mutations were linked to autoimmune manifestations. Thus, joint involvement frequency and characteristics have to be reassessed.10 11 The aim of this study was to characterise joint involvement in patients with dysimmune manifestations associated with clonal haematopoiesis and to compare patients with VEXAS syndrome and those without VEXAS syndrome.

Methods

This retrospective, multicentre, observational and case–control study was conducted in Normandy, France. Patients screened for UBA1 mutations (carried out at clinician’s discretion) between December 2019 and January 2023 in Caux-Maritime hospital, Elbeuf-Louviers-Val de Reuil hospital, Eure-Seine hospital, Le Havre hospital, Saint Hilaire clinic, Henri Becquerel Center and Rouen University Hospital were recruited. All patients had a myelogram and karyotype evaluation as part of their medical follow-up. Patients with clonal haematopoiesis (variant allele frequency ≥2%) or dysplasia (>10%) affecting at least one lineage and dysimmune manifestations (clinical or biological) were included. Patients with established or clearly identified auto-immune diseases were excluded.

Patients’ characteristics were collected from standardised observations and medical files. Patients were examined by physician specialised in rheumatoid diseases, either a rheumatologist or an internist. Only inflammatory arthralgia or arthritis as labelled by the clinician, was considered relevant. Immunological lab tests were performed for all patients, and centralised in Rouen University Hospital. Genomic DNA extracted from patients’ bone marrow was analysed by Sanger sequencing or next-generation sequencing to detect UBA1 mutations and myeloid mutations (panel is attached in online supplemental table 1). Missing data were extremely low thanks to the interdisciplinary meetings required before carrying out genetic research in these hospitals.

Regarding treatment response, complete response was defined as C reactive protein (CRP) <5 mg/L, the absence of clinical dysimmune symptoms and a prednisone dose of less than 10 mg/day. Partial response was defined as the absence of clinical dysimmune symptoms with a 50% decrease of CRP from baseline. No response was defined as the persistence of clinical symptoms and/or a decrease of CRP of less than 50%. Severe adverse events were defined as adverse events, leading to treatment discontinuation or death of the patient.

Qualitative variables were compared using Fisher’s exact test. P values below 0.05 were considered significant. Unsupervised clustering was performed on clinical manifestation using Gower matrix and hierarchical clustering. We used only clinical manifestations to limit the number of entries, due to our low number of patients. Statistical analyses and graphs were generated using Python (Python Software Foundation—packages: scikit learn 1.1, matplotlib 3.6.0, seaborn 0.12.1, lifelines 0.27.7, sksurv 0.21.0, gower 0.1.2, scipy 1.15.1).

The study was approved by the local ethics committee for research on existing data at Rouen University Hospital (E2024-31) and by the CNIL French agency (2 237 189v0 following reference methodology MR004).

Results

The study included 30 patients who had a haematological disorder associated with clinical and/or biological dysimmune manifestations. Patients’ characteristics are described in table 1: 17 patients (56%) had no UBA1 mutations (non-VEXAS group) and 13 patients (43%) had UBA1 mutations (VEXAS group). There was no difference between groups regarding age and smoking. Women were only found in the non-VEXAS group (n=5 (29%) vs n=0 in the VEXAS group). 13 patients (77%) in the non-VEXAS group and 4 patients (31%) in the VEXAS group had joint involvement defined as arthralgia or arthritis (table 1) (p value: 0.016). This joint involvement was characterised by peripheral arthralgia in all patients. There was no axial involvement in our cohort. In the non-VEXAS group, 10 patients (77%) had large joint involvement and 6 patients (46%) had small joint involvement. In the VEXAS group, one patient (25%) had large joint involvement and three patients (75%) had small joint involvement. X-rays were available for 14 patients, and none showed erosions suggestive of inflammatory disease. No patients in the VEXAS group developed arthritis compared with four patients (24%) in the non-VEXAS group, and three of the four joint fluid analyses were available. It consisted mainly of neutrophils in two patients and lymphocytes in one patient. Patients in the non-VEXAS group had less cutaneous involvement (7 patients—41% vs 11 patients—85%; p value: 0.019), less venous thrombosis (1 patient—6% vs 7 patients—54%; p value: 0.005) and less chondritis (one patient—6% vs 7%–54%; p value: 0.005) compared with patients in the VEXAS group.

Haemoglobin level was higher in the non-VEXAS group (11.1 g/dL±2.4 vs 9.4 g/dL±1.4, p value: 0.020) (online supplemental table 2). In the VEXAS group, two patients had monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance. Karyotype evaluation found only one abnormality in the VEXAS group (loss of Y chromosome) and five in the non-VEXAS group (listed hereafter: 7q deletion, 17q deletion, 20q deletion, chromosome 7 monosomy and 11q deletion with loss of MLL). There was no trisomy 8. Regarding haematological disease characteristics, there was more MDS and more intermediate to high Revised International Prognostic Scoring System scores in the non-VEXAS group compared with the VEXAS group, 13 patients (77%) versus 4 patients (31%) p value: 0.015, and 6 patients (35%) versus 0 patients; p value: 0.020, respectively. There was no patient with significant titre of autoantibodies associated with rheumatoid arthritis, lupus, anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA) vasculitis, systemic scleroderma, myositis and Sjögren syndrome. Of interest, four patients had dysimmune manifestations with clonal haematopoiesis without evidence of MDS and UBA1 mutation, and 3 (75%) of them had joint involvement.

All patients received corticosteroids as first-line and second-line treatment. Nine patients received azacytidine (eight in the non-VEXAS group and two in the VEXAS group), and only two patients had no response to treatment (online supplemental table 3). Severe adverse events were frequent, 23% in the non-VEXAS group and 45% in the VEXAS group.

17 patients had joint involvement and 13 patients did not have joint involvement. Joint involvement was associated with a lower frequency of pulmonary involvement (two patients with joint involvement (12%) vs seven patients without joint involvement (54%); p value: 0.018) (online supplemental table 4). Among the most frequent mutations associated with clonal haematopoiesis with dysimmune manifestations (figure 1A, B), SRSF2 mutations were found in two patients with joint involvement compared with no patients without joint involvement, TET2 mutations in three patients with joint involvement compared with two patients without joint involvement, and IDH2 mutations in one patient with joint involvement compared with one patient without joint involvement. We did not find any IDH1 mutations in our cohort. NRAS and ASXL1 mutations were the mutations most frequently associated with a joint phenotype (both present in three patients with joint involvement).

Next, we performed unsupervised clustering using hierarchical clustering and identified two clusters (figure 1C – table 2). Cluster 1 (14 patients) had more joint involvement (93% vs 25%, p value: 0.001), less UBA1 mutation (14% vs 69%, p value: 0.008), less pulmonary involvement (7% vs 50%, p value: 0.017), less chondritis (0% vs 50%, p value: 0.003) and less unprovoked venous thrombosis (7% vs 44%, p value: 0.039) than cluster 2 (16 patients). ASXL1 was the most frequent mutation in cluster 1 (3 patients—21% vs 0% in cluster 2).

Discussion

In this study, we compared features of patients with clonal haematopoiesis (including CHIP or MDS) according to their UBA1 mutational status. In our regional cohort, patients without UBA1 mutations presented more joint involvement, and patients with UBA1 mutations presented more chondritis, venous thrombosis and cutaneous involvement as previously described. Chondritis, a common symptom in VEXAS, seems to be highly specific to the latter in our cohort (specificity: 0.94). No MDS was observed in 24% of patients in the non-VEXAS group, suggesting that the presence of MDS is not mandatory to develop dysimmune manifestations. Unsupervised analysis revealed two phenotypes: one without UBA1 mutation, with joint involvement, and the other closer to VEXAS with more UBA1 mutation, chondritis, venous thrombosis and pulmonary involvement. The main limitation of this study was its small sample size, and it was not designed to assess causality of clonal haematopoiesis on dysimmune symptoms. Unsupervised analysis results should be considered with caution, as it was realised on a cohort of small size. Retrospective data collection may also introduce some bias, but standardised examination in those rare diseases resulted in very low levels of missing data. Nevertheless, as mortality is high in these diseases,11 clonal haematopoiesis must be investigated in these patients despite a distinctive clinical profile. The diagnosis must be made as early as possible to enable the introduction of appropriate treatment and follow-up. Recent evidence of an association between IDH mutations and seronegative rheumatoid arthritis22 has highlighted the complex relationship between these diseases and clonal haematopoiesis. A better understanding of these diseases will enable the development of new targeted treatments. As TET2 and ASXL1 seem to be involved in these dysimmune manifestations, the use of hypomethylating agents such as azacytidine could be a medication to consider in these patients who have no alternative therapeutic options.
