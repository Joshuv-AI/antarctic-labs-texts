Host phylogeny and diet shape gut microbiome and virome in wild small mammals of Gongga Mountain, China
Authors: Le-Wei He, Rui-Xiang Tang, Shao-Ying Liu, Zhen-Jie Zhang, Yi Li, Xu-Ming Wang, Bi-Song Yue, Zhen-Xin Fan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Gut microbiotas play pivotal roles in host adaptation, yet their composition and function in high-altitude small mammals remain poorly characterized. This study investigated how host phylogeny (order-level) and dietary habits shape the gut microbiome and virome of three mammalian orders (Eulipotyphla, Rodentia, Lagomorpha) in Gongga Mountain, a biodiversity hotspot on the Qinghai-Xizang Plateau. Metagenomic sequencing of 219 samples from 22 species revealed order-specific microbial signatures: Eulipotyphla (carnivorous) harbored higher abundances of potential pathogens (e.g., Helicobacter, Hafnia) and Retroviridae; Lagomorpha (herbivorous) was enriched in cellulolytic bacteria (e.g., Lachnospiraceae, Prevotella) and carbohydrate-active enzymes (CAZymes); Rodentia (omnivorous) showed intermediate traits. We reconstructed 1 385 high-quality metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs), 1 328 representing novel species, and identified 749 viral operational taxonomic units (vOTUs), >80% being Caudoviricetes. Crucially, Retroviridae abundance in Eulipotyphla suggests zoonotic risk. Phage-host network analysis indicated Caudoviricetes regulates cellulolytic bacteria in Lagomorpha. Host phylogeny and diet jointly drive gut microbiome divergence in small mammals. We establish the first gut microbiome and virome resource of small mammals in the high-altitude area of Gongga Mountain, highlighting Eulipotyphla as a potential vector for zoonotic pathogens.

INTRODUCTION

Gongga Mountain is situated in the transitional region between the southeastern Qinghai-Xizang Plateau and the Sichuan Basin, with elevations ranging from approximately 1 000 to 7 556 m above sea level (a.s.l.). It is one of the highest mountain systems in southwestern China (Zu et al., 2019). Its substantial elevational gradient of more than 6 000 m a.s.l. has given rise to a pronounced spectrum of vertical vegetation zones, extending from the subtropical to the alpine frigid zone, and supports highly diverse and complex biotic communities (Zu et al., 2019). Previous studies have identified the region encompassing Gongga Mountain as part of a globally important biodiversity hotspot (Myers et al., 2000; Wu et al., 2013). Small mammals play important ecological roles in terrestrial ecosystems worldwide (Searing et al., 2023). Their distinctive ecological niches and contributions to energy flow and nutrient cycling are fundamental to ecosystem functioning (Searing et al., 2023). Within food webs, small mammals may act both as predators of invertebrates and as prey for medium-sized carnivores and raptors, thereby enhancing trophic complexity (Dorigo et al., 2021; Paniccia et al., 2022). Moreover, their foraging and burrowing activities can facilitate vegetation regeneration and improve soil aeration (Dorigo et al., 2021; Paniccia et al., 2022).

Previous studies have revealed substantial differences in morphology and dietary habits among small mammals belonging to different taxonomic orders. Members of Eulipotyphla primarily feed on invertebrates, although some species also consume small vertebrates; thus, they are generally considered insectivorous or carnivorous mammals (Symonds, 2005). Rodentia is the largest mammalian order worldwide, comprising more than 2 000 species and accounting for approximately 40% of all mammalian species (Kay & Hoekstra, 2008). Studies of rodent feeding ecology have suggested that the adaptive evolution of their mandibular and dental structures is closely associated with dietary diversification, including omnivory (Landry, 1970). Rodents possess a distinct diastema between the incisors and cheek teeth, while their continuously growing incisors enable them to gnaw and process a wide variety of food items, including grasses, seeds, and chitin-rich insects (Hunter & Jernvall, 1995; Kay & Hoekstra, 2008). In contrast, lagomorphs, including rabbits, hares, and pikas, are predominantly herbivorous mammals (Iraçabal et al., 2024). They possess highly specialized jaw and dental morphologies, characterized by the absence of canines and the presence of sharp, continuously growing incisors adapted for gnawing plant material (Zachos, 2016). Gut morphology also varies considerably among small mammals with different dietary strategies. Because herbivorous mammals require relatively large fermentation chambers to digest fibrous plant material, their digestive tracts are generally longer and more complex than those of carnivorous mammals (Duque-Correa et al., 2021). Moreover, the cecum is absent or greatly reduced in many carnivorous mammals but is relatively well developed in herbivorous mammals, in which it plays an important role in microbial fermentation (Mcgrosky et al., 2019).

The gut microbiota is a highly diverse community comprising bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and viruses, and it plays a crucial role in maintaining intestinal homeostasis and overall health in mammals (Puschhof et al., 2021). In contrast to the relatively stable host genome, the gut microbiome exhibits considerable plasticity and can respond rapidly to environmental changes and host-derived stimuli (Zmora et al., 2019). Among the numerous endogenous and exogenous factors that shape the gut microbiome, diet is regarded as a major determinant of both the composition and functional capacity of the host-associated microbial community (Groussin et al., 2017; Youngblut et al., 2019; Zmora et al., 2019). Comparative studies of herbivorous, omnivorous, and carnivorous mammals have revealed pronounced differences in gut microbial communities among hosts with distinct dietary strategies (Zmora et al., 2019). Although host phylogeny contributes to the assembly of the gut microbiome, dietary preferences can exert a strong influence on microbial community composition and may result in convergent microbial characteristics among phylogenetically distant hosts with similar diets. Specifically, gut bacterial diversity generally tends to decrease along the dietary gradient from herbivory to omnivory and carnivory, accompanied by the emergence of diet-associated patterns in microbial composition and function (Degregori et al., 2024b; Ley et al., 2008; Muegge et al., 2011; Youngblut et al., 2019). Unlike marker gene-based approaches such as 16S rRNA gene sequencing, shotgun metagenomic sequencing does not rely on taxon-specific amplification primers but instead randomly sequences the total microbial DNA present in a sample (Fischbach & Segre, 2016; Wang et al., 2022). This approach provides comprehensive information on both the genetic composition and functional potential of the gut microbiota. It also enables more detailed taxonomic characterization than marker gene sequencing, often achieving species- or even strain-level resolution (Gao et al., 2021; Schloissnig et al., 2013). Therefore, shotgun metagenomic sequencing represents a powerful approach for investigating the taxonomic composition, functional capacity, and dietary adaptations of the gut microbiota in small mammals.

In this study, we performed shotgun metagenomic sequencing of the gut microbiota of 22 small-mammal species representing three orders: Eulipotyphla, Rodentia, and Lagomorpha. Using the resulting metagenomic data, we characterized and compared the taxonomic composition and functional profiles of the gut microbial communities among these three host orders in the Gongga Mountain region. We also reconstructed metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) and characterized the gut viromes. This study aimed to elucidate the extent to which host taxonomic affiliation at the order level shapes the gut microbiome and to establish the first comprehensive gut microbiome and virome resource for high-altitude small mammals.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Sample collection

All small-mammal specimens used in this study were collected from Gongga Mountain, Sichuan Province, China. Between September and October 2022, a total of 219 individuals were captured using cage traps randomly placed across the sampling sites. The captured animals were anesthetized with ether in the field, and relevant environmental information, including elevation and habitat type, was recorded for each sampling site (Supplementary Figure S1 and Table S1). The anesthetized animals were subsequently transported to the laboratory and euthanized by cervical dislocation. All procedures involving the capture and euthanasia of small mammals were authorized by the Sichuan Academy of Forestry Sciences. During sample collection, sterile gloves and instruments were used to aseptically excise a central section of the intestinal tract and collect its luminal contents. One intestinal-content sample was obtained from each individual, yielding a total of 219 samples. The samples were immediately placed on ice packs after collection and subsequently stored at −80°C until DNA extraction. Total DNA was extracted from each intestinal-content sample using an Intestinal Genomic DNA Extraction Kit (Tiangen Biotech, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

The small mammals collected in this study represented three orders: Eulipotyphla, Rodentia, and Lagomorpha. The Eulipotyphla samples comprised 58 individuals representing two families, seven genera, and nine species. The Rodentia samples included 125 individuals representing three families, seven genera, and 11 species, whereas the Lagomorpha samples consisted of 36 individuals from one family, one genus, and two species (Supplementary Table S1). The relatively limited species representation within Lagomorpha should be acknowledged as a potential limitation of this study.

All experimental protocols and sampling were approved by the Ethics Committee of College of Life Sciences, Sichuan University (No. 20210309009) and Gongga Mountain National Nature Reserve Administration Bureau (No. 513320202100081).

Metagenomic sequencing and quality control of raw data

A total of 0.2 μg of DNA from each metagenomic sample was used as the input material for library preparation. Sequencing libraries were constructed using the NEBNext® Ultra™ DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina® (New England Biolabs, USA; catalog no. E7370L) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Metagenomic DNA was fragmented by sonication to an average size of approximately 350 bp. The resulting DNA fragments were end-repaired and ligated to full-length Illumina-compatible adapters, followed by PCR amplification. The amplified products were purified using the AMPure XP system (Beckman Coulter, USA). Library quality was assessed using an Agilent 5400 system (USA), and library concentrations were quantified by quantitative PCR (qPCR). Qualified libraries were pooled according to their effective concentrations and the required sequencing depth. Paired-end sequencing with a read length of 150 bp (PE150) was subsequently performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (USA). Detailed sequencing information for all metagenomic samples is provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Raw fluorescence images generated by the Illumina platform (USA) were converted into raw sequencing reads through base calling and stored in FASTQ format. Quality filtering and preprocessing of the raw reads from all metagenomic samples were performed using fastp v.0.23.4 (Chen et al., 2018), yielding high-quality clean reads. Because assembled reference genomes were unavailable for most of the small-mammal species examined in this study, phylogenetically closely related species were identified based on previously published phylogenetic studies. Reference genomes representing 13 closely related small-mammal species were subsequently downloaded from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI). The reference genome used for host-sequence removal for each sampled species is listed in Supplementary Table S3. The clean reads from each sample were aligned against the corresponding reference genome using Bowtie2 v.2.4.5 (Langmead & Salzberg, 2012), and reads that mapped to the reference genomes were removed to minimize host-derived sequence contamination in the metagenomic datasets.

Identification of taxa in metagenomes

Taxonomic identification and quantification of microorganisms in all small-mammal gut metagenomic samples were performed using Kraken2 v.2.1.3 (Wood et al., 2019) against a standard microbial reference database containing bacterial, archaeal, viral, fungal, and protozoan sequences. The number of clean reads assigned to each microbial taxon was recorded as its read count and used to estimate taxonomic abundance.

Reconstruction and quality assessment of MAGs

Contigs generated through both individual-sample assembly and species-level co-assembly were used for genome binning. For individually assembled contigs, the corresponding clean reads were mapped back to the contigs using Bowtie2 v.2.5.4 (Langmead & Salzberg, 2012). The resulting SAM files were converted to BAM format and sorted using SAMtools v.1.22.1 (Danecek et al., 2021). For each species-level co-assembly, the alignment files generated from all corresponding samples were merged using the “samtools merge” command. The resulting BAM files were subsequently sorted and indexed for downstream binning analyses. To recover as many high-quality microbial genomes as possible from the small-mammal gut metagenomes, contigs were independently binned using MetaBAT2 (Kang et al., 2019), CONCOCT (Alneberg et al., 2014), and MaxBin (Wu et al., 2016), based primarily on sequence composition and contig coverage profiles. The binning results generated by these three programs were then evaluated, integrated, and refined using DAS Tool v.1.1.7 (Sieber et al., 2018), with the parameter “--score_threshold 0”, to produce a consensus bin set. In parallel, MetaBinner v.1.4.4 (Wang et al., 2023), a deep learning-based metagenomic binning tool, was used to generate an additional set of genome bins by integrating multiple sequence and abundance features. Ultimately, the consensus bins obtained using DAS Tool and the bins predicted using MetaBinner were retained as two initial bin sets for subsequent quality assessment, dereplication, and filtering.

To obtain a high-quality, non-redundant set of metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs), the initial bin sets were dereplicated using dRep v.3.4.2 (Olm et al., 2017) with the following parameters "dereplicate_wf -comp 70 -con 10 -sa 0.99 -nc 0.1 --S_algorithm fastANI". Pairwise average nucleotide identity (ANI) among genomes was calculated using fastANI (Jain et al., 2018). Genomes sharing an ANI of ≥99% were clustered together, whereas genomes with an ANI of <99% were retained as distinct strain-level representatives. Genome completeness and contamination were assessed using CheckM v.1.2.4 (Parks et al., 2015) within the dRep workflow. Ultimately, MAGs with an estimated completeness of >70% and contamination of <10% were retained, yielding a non-redundant, strain-level genome catalog for downstream analyses.

Taxonomic identification and abundance calculation of MAGs

GTDB-Tk v.2.5.2 (Genome Taxonomy Database Toolkit) (Chaumeil et al., 2022) is a software package for the taxonomic classification of bacterial and archaeal genomes based on the Genome Taxonomy Database (GTDB) (Parks et al., 2020). In this study, all MAGs were taxonomically classified using the “classify_wf” workflow of GTDB-Tk against GTDB release R08-RS214. Each MAG was assigned to the lowest taxonomic rank supported by the classification results. A phylogenetic tree of the MAGs was subsequently constructed using the “de_novo_wf” workflow of GTDB-Tk, which employed FastTree to infer the phylogeny from concatenated marker-gene alignments (Price et al., 2009).

Mobile genetic element (MGE) prediction of MAGs

Insertion sequences (ISs) in the MAGs were identified using the Python-based tool ISEScan v1.7.3 (Xie & Tang, 2017). Bacterial plasmid sequences were downloaded from the PLSDB database and used as a reference database for plasmid prediction. The MAG sequences were aligned against this plasmid database using DIAMOND v.2.1.8 to identify putative plasmid-associated sequences (Buchfink et al., 2015; Schmartz et al., 2022). Transposon sequences in the MAGs were predicted using the Perl-based tool TransposonPSI v.1.0.0.

Identifying gut viruses of small mammals from metagenomes

Contigs used for viral genome prediction were generated by individually assembling the clean reads from each metagenomic sample using MEGAHIT v.1.2.9 (Li et al., 2015), retaining contigs longer than 300 bp. Viral genomes were predicted from the metagenomic contigs using three complementary tools: DeepVirFinder v.1.0 (Ren et al., 2020), VirSorter2 v.2.2.4 (Guo et al., 2021), and VIBRANT v.1.2.1 (Kieft et al., 2020). For each sample, viral sequences predicted by the three tools were merged according to their contig assembly source and dereplicated using CD-HIT v.4.8.1 (Fu et al., 2012). The quality of the non-redundant viral sequences from each sample was assessed using CheckV v.1.0.3 (Nayfach et al., 2021a). Based on the CheckV quality-assessment results, viral sequences were retained if they met the following criteria: length >5 000 bp, completeness ≥50%, contamination <5%, k-mer frequency ≤1.0, and more viral genes than host genes. After quality filtering, all high-quality viral genome sequences from individual samples were pooled and dereplicated again using CD-HIT v.4.8.1 (Fu et al., 2012). The resulting cross-sample non-redundant viral sequence set was defined as viral operational taxonomic units (vOTUs).

Taxonomic identification and abundance calculation of vOTUs

Taxonomic classification of vOTUs was primarily performed using the ictv-mmseqs2-protein-database, which provides scripts and instructions for constructing an MMseqs2-compatible viral protein database with taxonomic annotations. This database enables the assignment of viral sequences to taxonomic groups defined by the International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses (ICTV) (Siddell et al., 2023). The abundance of vOTUs was estimated using CoverM, a tool designed for rapid calculation of read coverage and relative abundance in metagenomic datasets. Quality-controlled clean reads from each metagenomic sample were mapped to the vOTU sequences, and the read-mapping results were used to calculate the abundance of each viral genome. The resulting abundance profiles were compiled into a vOTU abundance table for downstream analyses.

Comparison with viral sequences in other databases

Three publicly available gut virome databases were downloaded for comparative analysis: (1) the Gut Virome Database (GVD), which was constructed from 2 697 virus-like particle or microbial metagenomes derived from 1 986 individuals across 16 countries (Gregory et al., 2020); (2) the Gut Phage Database (GPD), comprising approximately 142 000 non-redundant viral genomes recovered by mining 28 060 human gut metagenomes and 2 898 cultured gut bacterial reference genomes (Camarillo-Guerrero et al., 2021); and (3) the Metagenomic Gut Virus catalog (MGV), which contains 189 680 viral genomes identified from 11 810 publicly available human fecal metagenomes (Nayfach et al., 2021b). The vOTUs identified in this study were compared pairwise against the viral genome sequences in these three gut virome databases using BLASTN. A vOTU was considered a putatively novel viral genome if it showed an average nucleotide identity (ANI) of <95% or an alignment fraction (AF) of <85% to all reference viral genomes in the databases.

Host prediction and lifestyle prediction of Caudoviricetes vOTUs

Microbial hosts were predicted for all vOTUs identified as phages using CHERRY (Shang & Sun, 2022). In addition, CHERRY was used to infer MAG hosts for vOTUs assigned to the class Caudoviricetes. The lifestyles of Caudoviricetes vOTUs were predicted using PhaTYP (Shang et al., 2023).

Gene assembly, functional prediction, and quantification

Clean reads from each metagenomic sample were individually assembled using MEGAHIT v.1.2.9 (Li et al., 2015) with the parameter set as "--min-contig-len 300", retaining contigs with lengths of at least 300 bp. Genes were predicted from the assembled contigs of each sample using Prodigal v.2.6.3 (Hyatt et al., 2010). Redundant genes were removed using CD-HIT v.4.8.1 (Fu et al., 2012) at thresholds of 95% sequence identity and 90% coverage, resulting in a non-redundant gene catalog. The predicted nucleotide sequences were subsequently translated into amino acid sequences. Functional annotation was performed by aligning the non-redundant amino acid sequences against the Carbohydrate-Active enZYmes (CAZy) database and the Virulence Factor Database (VFDB) using DIAMOND v.2.1.8 (Buchfink et al., 2015), thereby identifying carbohydrate-active enzyme-related genes and virulence factor genes in the metagenomes. The abundance of non-redundant genes was quantified using Salmon v.1.10.1 (Patro et al., 2017), and gene abundance values were normalized as transcripts per million (TPM).

Whole-genome annotation of all MAGs was performed using Prokka v.1.14.5 (Seemann, 2014) with the parameters "--metagenome --force --gcode 11". To improve annotation accuracy, the most specific taxonomic assignment available for each MAG was incorporated during genome annotation. To investigate differences in the abundance of gene functions encoded by MAGs among small mammals from different orders, functional prediction was performed based on the CAZy database and the Virulence Factor Database (VFDB). Before functional annotation, gene sequences predicted from all MAGs were dereplicated using CD-HIT v.4.8.1 (Fu et al., 2012) to construct a non-redundant gene set. Protein-coding sequences were then predicted from the non-redundant gene sequences using Prodigal v.2.6.3 (Hyatt et al., 2010), generating a non-redundant amino acid sequence set for downstream functional annotation.

Data statistical analysis

β-diversity analysis and permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) were performed using the vegan package in R, with Bray-Curtis dissimilarity used as the β-diversity metric. The α-diversity of the gut microbiota at the genus and species levels was calculated using the R package microeco v.1.7.1, with the Shannon index used as the diversity metric (Liu et al., 2021). Linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) was used to identify microbial and functional biomarkers associated with small mammals from different orders (Segata et al., 2011). During this analysis, the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) method was applied to control the false discovery rate (FDR). Features with an FDR-adjusted P-value of <0.05 and an LDA score of >2 were considered statistically significant biomarkers. Gut microbial co-occurrence networks for small mammals from different orders were constructed using the R packages phyloseq v.1.42.0 (Mcmurdie & Holmes, 2013) and ggClusterNet v.0.1.0 (Wen et al., 2022). Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was used to calculate pairwise correlation coefficients among microbial taxa. Procrustes analysis was performed to assess the relationship between the composition of Caudoviricetes vOTUs and that of their predicted MAG hosts, based on relative abundance profiles. Unless otherwise specified, statistical significance was assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test.

Data visualization

Data visualization was completed using the R packages ggplot2 v.3.5.1, ggpubr v.0.6.0, ggvenn v.0.1.10, pheatmap v.1.0.12, amplicon v.1.19.0 (Liu et al., 2023), and UpSetR v.1.4.0 (Conway et al., 2017).

RESULTS

Composition and functions of gut microbiota in small mammals of different orders

We first used permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) to identify the factors contributing most strongly to variation in the β-diversity of gut microbiota in small mammals. The results showed that host taxonomic identity at multiple levels had significant effects on gut microbial β-diversity, with order-level classification explaining the largest proportion of variation (Supplementary Figure S2A). In contrast, neither elevation nor habitat type at the sampling sites was identified as a significant predictor of gut microbiota variation after accounting for other factors (Supplementary Figure S2A). Nevertheless, we acknowledge that the potential influence of elevation, particularly through its association with dietary shifts, could not be fully disentangled in this study, as suggested by previous research (Degregori et al., 2024a).

We further examined differences in the composition and relative abundance of gut microbiota among small mammals from different orders. At the phylum level, Pseudomonadota, Actinomycetota, Campylobacterota, and Bacillota were the dominant phyla in Eulipotyphla, whereas Bacillota, Pseudomonadota, Bacteroidota, and Actinomycetota dominated the gut microbiota of Rodentia and Lagomorpha (Figure 1A). LEfSe analysis at the phylum level (LDA>3, FDR<0.05) further revealed that Pseudomonadota, Campylobacterota, Actinomycetota, and several other phyla were significantly enriched in Eulipotyphla compared with Rodentia and Lagomorpha. In contrast, Bacillota, Bacteroidota, and other phyla were significantly enriched in Lagomorpha relative to Eulipotyphla and Rodentia (Supplementary Figure S2B and Table S4).

At the genus level, the gut microbiota of Eulipotyphla was dominated by Helicobacter, Pseudomonas, Streptomyces, Ralstonia, and other genera. In Rodentia, the dominant genera mainly included Pasteurella, Paenibacillus, Blautia, and Clostridium, whereas Lagomorpha was primarily characterized by Paenibacillus, Blautia, Alistipes, Prevotella, and related genera (Figure 1B). Genus-level α-diversity analysis showed that both the Shannon and Chao1 indices were significantly higher in Lagomorpha than in Eulipotyphla and Rodentia. In addition, Rodentia exhibited significantly higher Shannon and Chao1 indices than Eulipotyphla (Figure 1C, D). LEfSe analysis at the genus level (LDA>3, FDR<0.05) identified Pseudomonas, Hafnia, Ralstonia, Streptomyces, Campylobacter, Serratia, and other genera as microbial biomarkers of Eulipotyphla. Pasteurella was identified as a microbial biomarker of Rodentia, whereas Alistipes, Prevotella, Flavonifractor, Paenibacillus, Faecalibacterium, and other genera were identified as microbial biomarkers of Lagomorpha (Figure 1E; Supplementary Table S5).

At the species level, the dominant taxa in Eulipotyphla included Ralstonia insidiosa, Hafnia alvei, Helicobacter enhydrae, Helicobacter cholecystus, and related species. In Rodentia, the predominant species were Pasteurella multocida, Paenibacillus larvae, Flavonifractor plautii, Dysosmobacter welbionis, and others. In Lagomorpha, the dominant species included Paenibacillus larvae, Flavonifractor plautii, D. welbionis, Lawsonibacter asaccharolyticus, and related taxa (Supplementary Figure S2C). Species-level α-diversity analysis showed that the Shannon index was significantly higher in Lagomorpha than in both Eulipotyphla and Rodentia, whereas no significant difference was observed between Eulipotyphla and Rodentia (Figure 1F). The differences in the Chao1 index among the three orders were consistent with the genus-level results (Figure 1G). LEfSe analysis at the species level (LDA>3, FDR<0.05) identified Hafnia alvei, Helicobacter enhydrae, R. insidiosa, Helicobacter cholecystus, Serratia liquefaciens, and other species as microbial biomarkers of Eulipotyphla. Pasteurella multocida, Natranaerofaba carboxydovora, Acutalibacter muris, and related species were identified as microbial biomarkers of Rodentia. In contrast, Flavonifractor plautii, Paenibacillus larvae, L. asaccharolyticus, D. welbionis, Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, and other species were identified as microbial biomarkers of Lagomorpha (Figure 1H; Supplementary Table S6).

Analysis of gut microbial co-occurrence networks based on metagenomic data can help identify core gut microbial taxa and infer potential associations among species. We selected the top 150 gut microbial taxa with the highest relative abundance within each order, namely Eulipotyphla, Rodentia, and Lagomorpha, to construct order-specific gut microbial co-occurrence networks. After microbial abundance data were normalized using the trimmed mean of M-values (TMM) method, pairwise correlations among microbial taxa were calculated. Correlation edges were retained using thresholds of correlation coefficient >0.8 and P<0.05. The resulting co-occurrence networks showed only positive correlations among microbial taxa in all three orders (Supplementary Figure S2D–F). The Eulipotyphla network contained taxa from the largest number of microbial phyla, with 14 phyla represented, followed by Rodentia, whereas Lagomorpha showed the lowest phylum-level diversity in its network, with only six phyla represented (Supplementary Figure S2D–F). In the gut microbial co-occurrence networks of Lagomorpha and Rodentia, Bacillota and Bacteroidota were the dominant phyla, whereas Pseudomonadota and Campylobacterota made the greatest contributions to the Eulipotyphla network (Supplementary Figure S2D–F). Furthermore, the species-level microbial biomarkers identified by LEfSe for each order occupied central positions in the corresponding gut microbial co-occurrence networks (Supplementary Figure S2D–F).

Carbohydrate-active enzyme (CAZyme) genes in the mammalian gut microbiota play important roles in host health and disease. Therefore, we performed functional annotation and abundance estimation of CAZymes across all small-mammal gut metagenomic samples. Comparison of the total CAZyme abundance among the three orders showed that Lagomorpha had a significantly higher total abundance of CAZymes than Rodentia and Eulipotyphla (Supplementary Figure S2G). We further compared the abundances of the six major CAZyme categories across different orders and found that all six categories were significantly more abundant in Lagomorpha than in Rodentia and Eulipotyphla. LEfSe analysis was subsequently performed to identify CAZyme biomarkers associated with small mammals from different orders (LDA>3, FDR<0.05). Multiple glycosyltransferase (GT) family members, together with GH56 and CE11, were identified as CAZyme biomarkers of Eulipotyphla. Among these biomarkers, GT9 is primarily associated with heptosyltransferase activity, GT51 with peptidoglycan glycosyltransferase activity, and CE11 with UDP-3-O-acyl-N-acetylglucosamine deacetylase activity. GT2, GH13, and GH25 were identified as CAZyme biomarkers of Rodentia, with GH13 mainly associated with enzyme families such as α-glucosidases. In contrast, CAZyme biomarkers of Lagomorpha were predominantly glycoside hydrolase (GH) family members, including GH3, GH43, and GH2, which are mainly associated with xylosidase, arabinofuranosidase, and glucosidase activities (Figure 2B; Supplementary Table S7).

The Virulence Factor Database (VFDB) provides a comprehensive collection of major virulence genes from well-characterized bacterial pathogens. Based on this database, we performed functional prediction and abundance estimation of virulence genes across all small-mammal gut metagenomic samples. LEfSe analysis was then used to compare differences in the relative abundance of virulence genes among small mammals from different orders and to identify order-associated virulence gene biomarkers (LDA>3, FDR<0.05). Several virulence gene biomarkers enriched in Eulipotyphla, including VFG019550, VFG006522, VFG019549, and VFG019551, were associated with the encoding of flagellin B (FlaB) (Supplementary Figure S2H and Table S8).

Correlation analysis between species-level microbial biomarkers and functional biomarkers based on metagenomic data can help identify potential microbial contributors to key gut microbial functions. We therefore performed correlation analyses between species-level microbial biomarkers of Eulipotyphla (LDA>3, FDR<0.05) and their CAZyme biomarkers (LDA>3.5, FDR<0.05), as well as between these microbial biomarkers and virulence gene biomarkers (LDA>3.5, FDR<0.05).

In Eulipotyphla, the relative abundances of five Helicobacter species, namely Helicobacter mustelae, H. canis, H. pylori, H. cholecystus, and H. enhydrae, were significantly positively correlated with the relative abundances of GT9, CE11, GT30, and GT19 (Figure 2C). Among these CAZyme families, GT30 is mainly associated with α-3-deoxy-D-manno-octulosonic acid (KDO) transferase activity, whereas GT19 is primarily associated with lipid A disaccharide synthase activity. In addition, the relative abundances of these five Helicobacter species were significantly positively correlated with the relative abundances of four virulence genes encoding flagellin B, namely VFG019550, VFG006522, VFG019549, and VFG019551 (Figure 2D).

In Rodentia, species-level gut microbial biomarkers (LDA>3, FDR<0.05) showed generally significant positive correlations with CAZyme biomarkers (LDA>3.5, FDR<0.05). Among these CAZyme families, GH1 includes β-glucosidases, whereas GH19 includes class I, II, and IV chitinases (Figure 2E). In Lagomorpha, correlation analysis between species-level microbial biomarkers (LDA>3, FDR<0.05) and CAZyme biomarkers (LDA>3.5, FDR<0.05) showed that the relative abundances of species such as Flavonifractor plautii and Faecalibacterium prausnitzii were significantly positively correlated with the relative abundances of GH2 and GH3 (Figure 2F).

Reconstruction of MAGs in gut metagenomes of small mammals

During genome reconstruction of the gut microbiota from small mammals, a total of 13 259 bins were assembled from 219 metagenomic samples. To obtain strain-level metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs), we applied filtering and dereplication thresholds of ANI <99%, completeness ≥70%, and contamination <10%, resulting in 1 385 high-quality, non-redundant MAGs. Among these, 229 MAGs were recovered from species-level co-assembled bins. According to the criteria for high-quality draft genomes proposed by Bowers et al. (Bowers et al., 2017), defined here as genome completeness ≥90% and contamination ≤5%, 634 of the 1 385 MAGs met the standards for high-quality draft genomes. In addition, 408 MAGs had completeness values greater than 95%, 1 147 MAGs had contamination levels below 5%, and three MAGs achieved 100% completeness with 0% contamination (Supplementary Figure S3A). Taxonomic classification of the 1 385 MAGs was performed using GTDB-Tk (Chaumeil et al., 2022), with taxonomic assignments based on the standardized Genome Taxonomy Database (GTDB) nomenclature (Parks et al., 2020). Only one MAG was classified as an archaeal genome, whereas the remaining 1 384 MAGs were classified as bacterial genomes. The bacterial MAGs were assigned to 12 phyla and 16 classes. Among them, 1 383 MAGs were further classified into 32 orders, 1 374 into 52 families, 1 325 into 163 genera, and only 56 into 34 species (Supplementary Table S9). A phylogenetic tree of the bacterial MAGs was constructed using the approximate maximum-likelihood method implemented in FastTree through GTDB-Tk. The resulting phylogeny was broadly consistent with the bacterial phylum-level taxonomic annotations (Figure 3A). Among the bacterial MAGs, 828 were assigned to Bacillota, accounting for approximately 59.83% of all MAGs (Supplementary Table S9).

We compared the composition and abundance of gut microbiota among small mammals from different orders at the bacterial genome level. At the phylum level, Pseudomonadota and Campylobacterota were dominant in Eulipotyphla, whereas Bacillota_A and Bacteroidota were dominant in Rodentia. In Lagomorpha, the predominant phyla were Bacillota_A, Bacteroidota, and Spirochaetota (Figure 3B). LEfSe analysis at the phylum level (LDA>2, FDR<0.05) showed that Pseudomonadota and Campylobacterota were significantly enriched in Eulipotyphla compared with Rodentia and Lagomorpha, whereas Bacteroidota, Spirochaetota, and several other phyla were significantly enriched in Lagomorpha relative to Eulipotyphla and Rodentia (Supplementary Figure S3B). At the genus level, the dominant genera in Eulipotyphla included Helicobacter_G, Hafnia, Serratia_J, and Ralstonia. In Rodentia, the gut microbiota was mainly represented by UBA3282, COE1, and 1XD8-76, which are genera within the family Lachnospiraceae. In Lagomorpha, the predominant genera included UBA3282, a genus within Lachnospiraceae, as well as Acetatifactor, Treponema_D, Enterenecus, and Prevotella (Figure 3C). The α-diversity patterns based on MAG profiles were broadly consistent with those inferred from metagenomic taxonomic profiling (Supplementary Figure S3C, D). LEfSe analysis at the genus level (LDA>3, FDR<0.05) identified Helicobacter_G, Hafnia, Serratia_J, Ralstonia, and other genera as marker taxa for Eulipotyphla. UBA3282, 1XD8-76, COE1, Pelethomonas, and related genera were identified as marker taxa for Rodentia, whereas Treponema_D, Acetatifactor, Prevotella, Enterenecus, and other genera were identified as marker taxa for Lagomorpha (Figure 3D; Supplementary Table S10). LEfSe analysis at the MAG level (LDA>3, FDR<0.05) further identified several order-associated marker MAGs. MAG1225, assigned to the genus Hafnia, Serratia_J liquefaciens_A, Ralstonia sp001078575, MAG1147, assigned to the genus Helicobacter_G, and other MAGs were identified as markers of Eulipotyphla. Several members of Lachnospiraceae, including MAG499, MAG121, and MAG479, together with MAG1079, assigned to the genus Alistipes, were identified as marker MAGs of Rodentia. In contrast, MAG677, assigned to the genus Enterenecus, MAG145, assigned to Acetatifactor, and other MAGs were identified as marker MAGs of Lagomorpha (Supplementary Figure S3E and Table S11).

We performed functional prediction of carbohydrate-active enzymes (CAZymes) based on MAGs recovered from the gut microbiota of all small mammals. The total abundance of CAZymes and the abundance patterns of each major CAZyme category across different orders were broadly consistent with those observed in the metagenomics-based analysis (Figure 3E; Supplementary Figure S3F). LEfSe analysis was used to identify order-associated differences in the relative abundance of CAZyme-related genes encoded by MAGs (LDA>3, FDR<0.05). GT9, GT51, CE11, and related CAZyme families were identified as significantly enriched markers in Eulipotyphla, consistent with the metagenomics-based results. GT4, GH13, GT8, GT2, and other CAZyme families were identified as markers of Rodentia. In contrast, the CAZyme markers enriched in Lagomorpha were mainly members of the glycoside hydrolase (GH) family, including GH77, GH3, GH2, GH25, and GH5 (Figure 3F; Supplementary Table S12).

We performed virulence gene prediction for all MAGs. PCoA analysis showed that the virulence gene profiles of Lagomorpha were clearly separated from those of Rodentia and Eulipotyphla, whereas the profiles of Rodentia and Eulipotyphla partially overlapped (Supplementary Figure S3G). Further LEfSe analysis was conducted to examine differences in the relative abundance of virulence genes among small mammals from different orders. Several virulence gene markers enriched in Eulipotyphla MAGs, including VFG006522, VFG006524, VFG019549, VFG019550, and VFG019551, were associated with the encoding of flagellin B (FlaB) (Supplementary Figure S3H and Table S13).

To infer the potential functions of specific microbial genomes and support the results of the metagenomics-based association analysis, we performed correlation analyses between the relative abundances of high-quality MAGs and those of CAZyme and virulence factor gene (VFG) families. Specifically, we analyzed correlations between the MAG biomarkers of Eulipotyphla (LDA>3, FDR<0.05) and their CAZyme biomarkers (LDA>3.5, FDR<0.05), as well as between these MAG biomarkers and VFG biomarkers (LDA>3.5, FDR<0.05). In Eulipotyphla, the relative abundances of multiple members of the genus Helicobacter_G, including MAG1159, MAG1156, MAG1144, MAG1143, MAG1145, MAG1148, MAG1146, MAG1154, and MAG1153, were significantly positively correlated with the relative abundances of GT51 and CE11 (Figure 3G). Furthermore, the relative abundances of several Helicobacter_G MAGs, including MAG1149, MAG1159, MAG1156, MAG1144, MAG1143, MAG1145, MAG1148, MAG1146, MAG1154, and MAG1153, were significantly positively correlated with the relative abundances of one chemotaxis response regulator-related gene, VFG043386, and four flagellin B-encoding genes, namely VFG006522, VFG006524, VFG019549, and VFG019550 (Figure 3H). Correlation analysis between Rodentia MAG biomarkers (LDA>3, FDR<0.05) and their CAZyme biomarkers (LDA>3.5, FDR<0.05) revealed significant positive correlations between members of the family Lachnospiraceae, such as MAG395, MAG302, and MAG551, and the CAZyme families GH13 and GH94 (Supplementary Figure S3I). GH94 is mainly associated with enzyme families such as cellobiose phosphorylases, cellodextrin phosphorylases, and cellobionic acid phosphorylases. In Lagomorpha, correlation analysis between MAG biomarkers (LDA>3, FDR<0.05) and CAZyme biomarkers (LDA>3.5, FDR<0.05) showed that members of Lachnospiraceae, including MAG504, MAG536, and MAG228, were significantly positively correlated with one or more members of the GH2, GH3, and GH5 families (Supplementary Figure S3J).

Comparison of Helicobacter MAGs and cellulolytic bacteria MAGs from different sources

In the taxonomic classification of MAGs, Helicobacter-assigned MAGs derived from Eulipotyphla were annotated as Helicobacter_G, whereas those derived from Rodentia and Lagomorpha were annotated as Helicobacter_C and Helicobacter_D, respectively (Supplementary Table S9). To compare genomic differences among Helicobacter MAGs from different sources, we performed comparative genomic analysis of 62 Helicobacter MAGs assembled in this study and eight Helicobacter MAGs downloaded from the integrated Mouse Gut Metagenome Catalog (iMGMC). We first constructed a phylogenetic tree of these 70 Helicobacter MAGs. All 20 MAGs from Eulipotyphla were annotated as Helicobacter_G and clustered into an independent clade (Figure 4A), indicating that they were phylogenetically distinct from Helicobacter MAGs derived from Rodentia, Lagomorpha, and laboratory mice. In addition, Helicobacter MAGs from Rodentia and Lagomorpha were assigned to Helicobacter_C and Helicobacter_D, respectively, and these MAGs also clustered into largely source-specific clades (Figure 4A). To compare functional differences among Helicobacter MAGs from different sources, we further predicted CAZymes and virulence genes in these MAGs. In the CAZyme prediction results, the CAZyme markers of Eulipotyphla identified in both the metagenomics- and MAG-based analyses, including GT9, GT51, and CE11, were annotated in all Helicobacter MAGs (Figure 4B). However, considering the overall CAZyme profiles, Helicobacter MAGs from Eulipotyphla encoded fewer CAZymes than those from Rodentia, Lagomorpha, and laboratory mice. For example, GH73 was detected only at low levels in Helicobacter MAGs from Eulipotyphla but was more frequently detected in those from other small mammals (Figure 4B). β-diversity analysis further revealed that the CAZyme functional profiles of Helicobacter MAGs from Eulipotyphla differed significantly from those of Helicobacter MAGs from other sources (Supplementary Figure S4A). In the virulence gene prediction results, flagellin-related genes, including fliE, fliS, flgG_2, fliM, flgC, and fliP, were detected in Helicobacter MAGs from most small mammals (Figure 4C). However, several flagellin-related genes were not consistently detected across all Helicobacter MAGs. For instance, fliN, fliG, and fliQ were detected only at low levels in Eulipotyphla-derived MAGs, whereas flaB and fliA were detected only at low levels in Rodentia-derived MAGs. Overall, fewer virulence genes were detected in Helicobacter MAGs from Eulipotyphla than in those from Rodentia, Lagomorpha, and laboratory mice. β-diversity analysis also showed clear differences in virulence gene profiles between Helicobacter MAGs from Eulipotyphla and those from other sources (Supplementary Figure S4B). These results suggest that Eulipotyphla-derived Helicobacter MAGs differ markedly from Helicobacter MAGs from other small mammals in terms of gene functional potential. We further compared the relative abundance of Helicobacter MAGs, including Helicobacter_G, Helicobacter_C, and Helicobacter_D, among the three small-mammal orders. The relative abundance of Helicobacter genomes was highest in the gut microbiota of Eulipotyphla (Figure 4D). This pattern suggests that the invertebrate-based diet of Eulipotyphla may be associated with greater gut colonization by Helicobacter and with the expression or retention of specific virulence-related functions. Finally, we predicted three types of mobile genetic elements (MGEs), including insertion sequences, plasmids, and transposons, in Helicobacter MAGs. The association between MGEs and virulence genes was assessed by calculating the ratio of virulence genes co-localized with MGEs to the total number of virulence genes in each genome. This analysis revealed distinct MGE-virulence gene association patterns across host orders. In Helicobacter MAGs from Rodentia and Lagomorpha, virulence genes showed stronger associations with plasmids than with other MGE types. In contrast, virulence genes in Helicobacter MAGs from Eulipotyphla were more frequently associated with transposons. In Helicobacter MAGs from laboratory mice, virulence genes were primarily associated with both plasmids and transposons. Insertion sequences showed relatively weak associations with virulence genes across Helicobacter MAGs recovered from the gut microbiota of small mammals (Supplementary Figure S4C).

A large number of putative cellulolytic bacterial MAGs were recovered from Rodentia and Lagomorpha, including MAGs assigned to the family Lachnospiraceae and the genera Treponema and Prevotella. Because the goat gut microbiota has been extensively studied as an important model for ruminant microbial fiber degradation, numerous cellulolytic bacterial genomes have been reconstructed from goat gut metagenomes. To compare cellulolytic bacterial MAGs from different host sources, we downloaded MAGs of cellulolytic bacteria reconstructed from goat gut metagenomes by Peng et al. (Peng et al., 2021) and compared them with the cellulolytic bacterial MAGs recovered from Rodentia and Lagomorpha in this study. A total of 523 Lachnospiraceae MAGs were reconstructed from our metagenomic data, including 419 from Rodentia and 104 from Lagomorpha. We constructed a phylogenetic tree comprising these small-mammal-derived Lachnospiraceae MAGs and 51 Lachnospiraceae MAGs from the goat gut. The results showed that Lachnospiraceae MAGs did not cluster strictly according to host source (Figure 4E). To compare their functional potential, CAZymes were identified in all 574 Lachnospiraceae MAGs, and gene function prediction was further performed based on the UniRef50 database. The CAZyme heatmap showed broadly similar CAZyme profiles among Lachnospiraceae MAGs from different host sources (Supplementary Figure S4D). Consistently, the NMDS plot based on UniRef50 functional profiles indicated no clear separation in gene functional composition among these Lachnospiraceae MAGs (Figure 4F). In the MAG-based analysis of gut microbial relative abundance, Treponema was identified as a marker genus of Lagomorpha. Among the 121 Treponema MAGs reconstructed in this study, 63 were derived from Lagomorpha and 58 from Rodentia. We constructed a phylogenetic tree including these Treponema MAGs and three goat gut-derived Treponema MAGs. Similar to the Lachnospiraceae results, Treponema MAGs did not cluster completely according to host source (Supplementary Figure S4E). Further comparison of CAZyme profiles showed that Treponema MAGs from different sources displayed broadly similar CAZyme detection patterns (Supplementary Figure S4F). We also reconstructed 16 and 19 Prevotella MAGs from Rodentia and Lagomorpha, respectively. A phylogenetic tree was constructed using these 35 small-mammal-derived Prevotella MAGs together with five goat gut-derived Prevotella MAGs. The results showed that Prevotella MAGs also did not cluster strictly by host source (Supplementary Figure S4G). Comparison of CAZyme profiles further indicated that Prevotella MAGs from different sources had relatively similar CAZyme detection patterns (Supplementary Figure S4H).

Prediction and characteristics of gut virome in small mammals

Using an integrated approach combining three viral sequence prediction tools, namely DeepVirFinder (Ren et al., 2020), VirSorter2 (Guo et al., 2021), and VIBRANT (Kieft et al., 2020), we identified a total of 52 768 putative non-redundant viral sequences from all samples. The quality of these non-redundant viral sequences was assessed using CheckV (Nayfach et al., 2021a). Viral sequences were retained if they contained more viral genes than host genes, had a contig length >5 000 bp, completeness >50%, contamination <5%, and k-mer frequency ≤1.0. After quality filtering, 749 high-quality gut viral operational taxonomic units (vOTUs) were obtained from small mammals. The average length of these vOTUs was 37 021.88 bp, with a median length of 26 237 bp. Approximately 36% of the vOTUs were classified as complete or high-quality according to CheckV quality estimates (Supplementary Figure S5A, B). Comparative analysis against three existing human gut virome databases, namely GVD, GPD, and MGV, showed that 150 vOTUs could be matched to viral genomes in at least one of these databases. Among them, 50 vOTUs were shared across all three databases, whereas 599 vOTUs did not cluster with any viral genomes from the reference databases, suggesting that they may represent putatively novel gut vOTUs (Figure 5A).

Taxonomic classification of the 749 vOTUs was performed using the ictv-mmseqs2-protein-database in combination with multiple tools. In total, 722 vOTUs could be taxonomically assigned at least at the domain level, whereas 27 vOTUs could not be assigned to any viral domain (Supplementary Table S14). Among the 722 domain-level classified vOTUs, 711 were further assigned at the kingdom level, 711 at the phylum level, 710 at the class level, 111 at the order level, 119 at the family level, 24 at the genus level, and four at the species level (Supplementary Table S14). At the domain level, the vast majority of vOTUs were classified as Duplodnaviria, accounting for approximately 80.91% of all vOTUs (Figure 5B; Supplementary Table S14). In our classification results, vOTUs assigned to Duplodnaviria were further classified into one kingdom, Heunggongvirae; one phylum, Uroviricota; and one class, Caudoviricetes. At the order level, most vOTUs could not be assigned to a known order, accounting for 85.18% of all vOTUs. The two most frequently identified orders were Tubulavirales and Ortervirales, comprising 49 and 44 vOTUs, respectively (Supplementary Figure S5C and Table S14). Similarly, at the family level, most vOTUs could not be assigned to a known family, accounting for 84.11% of all vOTUs. The two most frequently identified families were Inoviridae and Retroviridae, comprising 49 and 44 vOTUs, respectively (Supplementary Figure S5D and Table S14). Notably, none of the vOTUs classified as Retroviridae clustered with viral sequences from existing virome databases, suggesting that the Retroviridae vOTUs identified from the gut metagenomes of small mammals may represent putatively novel viruses (Supplementary Table S14).

We compared the composition and relative abundance of gut viromes among small mammals from different orders. At the viral class level, Caudoviricetes represented an important component of the gut virome in Lagomorpha, with a significantly higher relative abundance than in Rodentia and Eulipotyphla (Figure 5C, D). In contrast, Revtraviricetes dominated the gut viromes of Eulipotyphla and Rodentia, showing significantly higher relative abundances than in Lagomorpha (Figure 5C, D). At the viral order level, Ortervirales was the dominant order in the gut virome of Eulipotyphla, whereas Ortervirales and Tubulavirales were the dominant orders in Rodentia (Figure 5E, F). In Lagomorpha, the gut virome was mainly dominated by Tubulavirales and Petitvirales (Figure 5E, F). Ortervirales exhibited the highest relative abundance in Eulipotyphla, and its relative abundance in Rodentia was significantly higher than that in Lagomorpha. In contrast, Petitvirales and Tubulavirales showed the highest relative abundances in Lagomorpha, and their relative abundances in Rodentia were significantly higher than those in Eulipotyphla (Figure 5E, F). At the viral family level, Retroviridae dominated the gut virome of Eulipotyphla (Figure 5G). In Rodentia, the dominant families were Retroviridae, Inoviridae, and Adenoviridae, whereas the gut virome of Lagomorpha was mainly characterized by Inoviridae, Herelleviridae, and Microviridae (Figure 5G). Retroviridae had the highest relative abundance in Eulipotyphla, and its relative abundance in Rodentia was significantly higher than that in Lagomorpha. The relative abundance of Inoviridae was significantly higher in both Lagomorpha and Rodentia than in Eulipotyphla, with no significant difference between Lagomorpha and Rodentia. Herelleviridae and Microviridae showed the highest relative abundances in Lagomorpha (Figure 5H).

We performed Gene Ontology (GO) functional annotation for all gut vOTUs identified from small mammals. PCoA analysis showed partial overlap in GO functional profiles among vOTUs from the three host orders (Supplementary Figure S5E). We further compared differences in the abundance of GO function-related genes among small mammals from different orders using LEfSe analysis. In Eulipotyphla, genes associated with symbiosis- and interaction-related GO terms, including GO:0044419, GO:0044403, and GO:0051701, showed significantly higher relative abundances than those in Rodentia and Lagomorpha (Supplementary Figure S5F). In Lagomorpha, genes associated with icosahedral capsid-related GO terms, including GO:0019030, GO:0039617, and GO:0046729, were significantly more abundant than those in Eulipotyphla and Rodentia (Supplementary Figure S5F).

Interactions between gut Caudoviricetes and microbial hosts in small mammals

Given the important role of Caudoviricetes in the gut virome of small mammals, we further examined differences in Caudoviricetes vOTUs among small mammals from different orders. The lifestyle of each vOTU classified as Caudoviricetes was predicted using PhaTYP (Shang et al., 2023). Among the 606 Caudoviricetes vOTUs, 239 were predicted to be virulent phages, 366 were predicted to be temperate phages, and the lifestyle of only one vOTU could not be determined (Supplementary Table S15). We then compared the relative abundances of Caudoviricetes vOTUs with different predicted lifestyles among host orders. In Eulipotyphla, virulent Caudoviricetes showed significantly higher relative abundance than temperate Caudoviricetes. In Rodentia, the opposite pattern was observed, with temperate Caudoviricetes being significantly more abundant than virulent Caudoviricetes. In Lagomorpha, no significant difference was observed between the relative abundances of virulent and temperate Caudoviricetes (Figure 6A).

Microbial hosts of Caudoviricetes vOTUs were predicted using CHERRY (Shang & Sun, 2022) (Supplementary Table S16). We further analyzed the predicted host composition of Caudoviricetes and the corresponding abundance differences of Caudoviricetes vOTUs among small mammals from different orders. At the phylum level, the predominant predicted hosts of Caudoviricetes vOTUs were Bacillota, Pseudomonadota, and Bacteroidota (Figure 6B; Supplementary Table S16). The predicted phylum-level host profiles of Caudoviricetes vOTUs differed markedly among host orders. In Eulipotyphla, the predicted hosts were mainly assigned to Bacillota, Pseudomonadota, and Cyanobacteriota (Figure 6B). In Rodentia, the dominant predicted host phyla were Bacillota, Pseudomonadota, Bacteroidota, and Cyanobacteriota, whereas in Lagomorpha, the main predicted hosts included Bacillota, Pseudomonadota, Bacteroidota, Actinomycetota, and Spirochaetota (Figure 6B). We further examined the predicted genus-level host composition of Caudoviricetes and the corresponding abundance differences of Caudoviricetes vOTUs among small mammals from different orders. At the genus level, the predicted hosts of Caudoviricetes vOTUs in Eulipotyphla were mainly Streptococcus, Cylindrospermopsis, Paenibacillus, and Veillonella (Figure 6C). In Rodentia, the dominant predicted hosts included Enterocloster, Clostridium, Fischerella, and Selenomonas, whereas in Lagomorpha, the predicted hosts were mainly Paenibacillus, Streptococcus, Acinetobacter, and Treponema (Figure 6C). LEfSe analysis of the top 20 discriminative features (FDR<0.05) showed that, in Eulipotyphla, the relative abundances of Caudoviricetes vOTUs predicted to infect Veillonella, Rhodoferax, and Aeromonas were significantly higher than those in Rodentia and Lagomorpha (Figure 6D). In Rodentia, Caudoviricetes vOTUs with predicted hosts assigned to Enterocloster, Fischerella, Selenomonas, Lawsonibacter, Alicyclobacillus, and Microcystis showed significantly higher relative abundances than those in Eulipotyphla and Lagomorpha (Figure 6D). In Lagomorpha, the relative abundances of Caudoviricetes vOTUs predicted to infect Paenibacillus, Prevotella, Treponema, and Streptomyces were significantly higher than those in Eulipotyphla and Rodentia (Figure 6D).

Given the potential influence of gut Caudoviricetes diversity on mammalian gut microbial communities, we investigated the relationship between the diversity of gut Caudoviricetes vOTUs and that of gut microbial MAGs in small mammals. At the species level, the α-diversity of Caudoviricetes vOTUs was significantly positively correlated with MAG α-diversity in Rodentia and Lagomorpha, whereas no significant correlation was observed in Eulipotyphla (Figure 6E). We further compared the community distributions of gut Caudoviricetes vOTUs and MAGs based on Bray-Curtis dissimilarities using Procrustes analysis. Although significant correlations between Caudoviricetes vOTU and MAG community structures were observed across all three orders, the correlation was relatively weak in Eulipotyphla (r=0.3722419; Supplementary Figure S5G). In contrast, stronger correlations were observed in Rodentia (r=0.8117904; Supplementary Figure S5H) and Lagomorpha (r=0.7251562; Supplementary Figure S5I). We used CHERRY (Shang & Sun, 2022) to predict the MAG hosts of Caudoviricetes vOTUs, thereby investigating potential interactions between gut Caudoviricetes and microbial genomes in small mammals. A total of 423 vOTUs were linked to predicted MAG hosts (Supplementary Table S17). We further investigated the correlations between the relative abundances of Caudoviricetes vOTUs and their predicted MAG hosts in small mammals. In Eulipotyphla, temperate Caudoviricetes vOTUs showed stronger correlations with their predicted MAG hosts than did virulent Caudoviricetes vOTUs (Figure 6F). In contrast, the correlations between the two lifestyle groups of Caudoviricetes vOTUs and their predicted MAG hosts were comparable in Rodentia and Lagomorpha (Figure 6G, H). Rodentia exhibited the largest number and diversity of MAG hosts significantly correlated with Caudoviricetes vOTUs, including 324 MAGs assigned to 19 families and 65 genera (Figure 6I; Supplementary Table S18). In contrast, Eulipotyphla showed the lowest number and diversity of such MAG hosts, with 45 MAGs assigned to 11 families and 23 genera (Supplementary Figure S5J and Table S19). In Rodentia and Lagomorpha, Caudoviricetes vOTUs showed significant positive correlations with MAGs assigned to putative cellulose-degrading bacterial groups, including Lachnospiraceae and Treponema_D (Figure 6I, J; Supplementary Tables S18, S20).

DISCUSSION

The structural characteristics of gut microbial communities in mammals from different orders can reflect the combined effects of host phylogeny, physiological traits, ecological niches, and feeding habits on gut microbiota assembly. Studies based on 16S rRNA gene sequencing have shown that both diet and host phylogeny can influence the diversity of mammalian gut microbiota. Multiple studies have further demonstrated that, as mammalian diets shift from carnivory to omnivory and herbivory, gut microbial diversity generally increases, and gut microbial communities tend to co-diversify with their mammalian hosts (De Jonge et al., 2022; Ley et al., 2008; Xie et al., 2024). This pattern may be associated with the greater complexity of carbon sources in plant-based diets, which require a broader range of microorganisms to participate in the hydrolysis and utilization of complex substrates (Bayané & Guiot, 2011). Although omnivores consume mixed diets, herbivorous mammals often possess digestive systems specifically adapted for microbial degradation of complex plant material, including specialized gut morphology, prolonged digesta retention time, and well-developed fermentation chambers, thereby fostering a more diverse gut microbiome (Huang et al., 2025). In contrast, omnivores may rely more heavily on host-derived digestive enzymes and less on extensive microbial fermentation, which may constrain microbial niche diversity.

Based on metagenomic sequencing, metagenome-assembled genome (MAG) reconstruction, and virome prediction, we investigated the characteristics of gut microbial communities in small mammals from different orders in the Gongga Mountain region (Chen et al., 2021; Lou et al., 2024; Qin et al., 2010; Wang et al., 2019; Zhou et al., 2022). We found that gut microbial species diversity gradually increased with the increasing proportion of plant-derived components in the diet of small mammals. This pattern is generally consistent with previous findings showing that mammalian gut microbial diversity varies among hosts with different dietary strategies (Ley et al., 2008). Previous phylogenetic studies and the “Glires” hypothesis have suggested a close evolutionary relationship between Rodentia and Lagomorpha (Nishihara et al., 2006). The convergence in gut microbial composition observed between these two orders may therefore reflect, at least in part, the influence of host phylogenetic relationships on gut microbiota assembly in small mammals.

The gut microbiota of Eulipotyphla exhibited higher relative abundances of bacteria carrying virulence-associated genes, as well as members of the family Retroviridae. This pattern may be associated with the feeding behavior of Eulipotyphla, which primarily consume invertebrates and may occasionally feed on carrion (He et al., 2023; Lee et al., 2023; Silby et al., 2011). In the gut metagenomes of Eulipotyphla, the CAZymes showing higher abundances were mainly associated with the biosynthesis of lipopolysaccharides (LPS) and peptidoglycan (Cote & Taylor, 2017; Dahmane et al., 2018; De Falco et al., 2015; Fujita et al., 2022). Moreover, members of the genus Helicobacter in the gut of Eulipotyphla showed significant positive correlations with genes involved in the regulation of LPS biosynthesis and flagellin B production. LPS is a major component of the outer membrane of most Gram-negative bacteria and plays important roles in bacterial motility, gut colonization, biofilm formation, and the virulence of pathogenic bacteria (Kaito et al., 2020; Raetz & Whitfield, 2002). In Helicobacter pylori, LPS has been identified as an important virulence factor involved in host-microbe interactions and the establishment of chronic infection (Rubin & Trent, 2013). Flagellin B is a structural component of the flagellar filament, and strains lacking the flaB gene, which encodes flagellin B, exhibit reduced motility (Eletto et al., 2021; Josenhans et al., 1995). Because certain Helicobacter species require flagella for host colonization and infection (Ottemann & Lowenthal, 2002), the Helicobacter species detected in the gut of Eulipotyphla may possess genetic potential related to virulence-associated mechanisms, including LPS biosynthesis and flagellin B production.

Through comparative genomic analysis, we further revealed a high degree of specificity of Helicobacter in the gut of Eulipotyphla. The invertebrate-dominated diet of Eulipotyphla, including insects and other small invertebrates, may facilitate the colonization and persistence of Helicobacter species in their gut, potentially contributing to the enrichment of virulence-associated traits. Hafnia (Zhang et al., 2019) and Helicobacter (Mladenova-Hristova et al., 2017) have been recognized as important genera containing zoonotic pathogenic bacteria. Emerging infectious diseases (EIDs) of wildlife origin have received increasing attention in public health research and surveillance (Jones et al., 2008). For example, Cutuli et al. (Cutuli et al., 2021) reported that Hafnia alvei can cause pulmonary infection and disrupt the lung microbiota, while Okushin et al. (Okushin et al., 2018) demonstrated that Helicobacter pylori infection is closely associated with the occurrence and progression of liver diseases. In addition to bacterial pathogens, members of the family Retroviridae represent an important group of human and veterinary pathogens (Balvay et al., 2007; Coffin et al., 2021). Retroviruses are present in the genomes of many mammals, including rodents, cats, non-human primates, and humans (Balvay et al., 2007; Coffin et al., 2021), and retroviral infections can contribute to various pathological conditions, including cancer and immunodeficiency (Balvay et al., 2007; Coffin et al., 2021). Therefore, the high relative abundances of potential pathogenic bacteria and Retroviridae in the gut of Eulipotyphla suggest that these small mammals may act as potential reservoirs or carriers of zoonotic pathogens, underscoring their potential importance in public health surveillance. Establishing a long-term monitoring system for the gut microbiome and virome of small mammals in the Gongga Mountain region, particularly species of Eulipotyphla, and regularly assessing the dynamics of potential pathogens such as Helicobacter, Hafnia, and Retroviridae may contribute to both biodiversity conservation and the development of early-warning and prevention strategies for wildlife-associated pathogens.

Resident gut microbiota are widely involved in cellulose degradation in the large intestine of herbivorous mammals (Froidurot & Julliand, 2022). In the gut of Lagomorpha, many colonizing microorganisms include bacteria with strong capacities for cellulose degradation and short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) production (Fischer et al., 2020; Li et al., 2017; Mukherjee et al., 2023; Rosés et al., 2021). High relative abundances of Lachnospiraceae were detected in both Rodentia and Lagomorpha, and this family represents an important group of SCFA-producing and cellulose-degrading bacteria (Jalanka-Tuovinen et al., 2011; Xu et al., 2017). Gut microbiota primarily degrade cellulose in mammalian hosts through CAZymes encoded by their own genomes (Flint et al., 2012). Compared with carnivorous animals, herbivorous animals consume a higher proportion of cellulose-rich plant material; consequently, their gut microbial communities may harbor a greater abundance of cellulase-related enzymes (Alcaide et al., 2012; Froidurot & Julliand, 2022; Zhou et al., 2020). In this study, we found that from Eulipotyphla to Rodentia and then to Lagomorpha, the total abundance of CAZyme genes in the gut metagenome increased with the increasing proportion of plant-derived components in the diet. This pattern was consistent with the observed changes in gut microbial diversity. In the gut metagenomes of Lagomorpha, glycoside hydrolases (GHs) were detected at relatively high abundances. These abundant CAZymes likely play important roles in cellulose degradation (Guo et al., 2024; Mendonça et al., 2023; Zayulina et al., 2020). Together, the composition and functional potential of the gut microbiota in Lagomorpha reflect its dietary specialization as a typical herbivorous mammal. Significant positive correlations were also observed between gut microbial biomarkers and CAZyme biomarkers in Rodentia. β-glucosidases belonging to GH1 and class I, II, and IV chitinases belonging to GH19, which were more abundant in the gut microbiota of Rodentia, can catalyze the metabolism of cellulose and chitin, respectively (Hemati et al., 2022; Udaya Prakash et al., 2010; Yano et al., 2005). These associations between microbial species and CAZymes in Rodentia are consistent with its omnivorous dietary characteristics. During mammalian evolution, the composition and functions of gut microbiota among closely related herbivorous mammals have shown extensive convergence (Ley et al., 2008; Muegge et al., 2011). The phylogenetic and functional convergence of cellulose-degrading MAGs from the gut metagenomes of Rodentia and Lagomorpha with those from goat fecal metagenomes further suggests that cellulose-rich diets can shape similar gut microbial functional traits across distantly related mammals. Given that Lagomorpha rely heavily on fiber-rich plants and harbor specialized cellulose-degrading microbial communities, including Lachnospiraceae and Prevotella, protecting native vegetation in their habitats may be important for maintaining both host nutritional ecology and gut microbial stability. Therefore, destructive activities such as overgrazing and land reclamation should be minimized in these habitats.

In this study, more than 80% of the vOTUs predicted from the gut metagenomic data of small mammals were classified as Caudoviricetes, suggesting that this viral class may play an important role in the gut virome of small mammals. A similar pattern has also been observed in the gut viromes of yaks from the Qinghai-Xizang Plateau and domestic pigs (Lu et al., 2024). Most phages are considered important candidates for regulating bacterial and archaeal communities in the mammalian gut (Cao et al., 2023). In the gut of Lagomorpha, Caudoviricetes vOTUs predicted to infect Paenibacillus, Prevotella, and Treponema showed relatively high abundances. Combined with the metagenomic and MAG-based results showing that these genera are characteristic components of the Lagomorpha gut microbiota, we hypothesize that Caudoviricetes may contribute to the regulation of cellulose-degrading bacterial communities and their associated functions in the gut of Lagomorpha (Jiang et al., 2022; Mukherjee et al., 2023; Su et al., 2022). We further predicted the MAG hosts of all Caudoviricetes vOTUs and found that the associations between Caudoviricetes vOTUs and bacterial MAGs appeared to be weaker in Eulipotyphla than in Rodentia and Lagomorpha, suggesting a potentially weaker phage-mediated regulatory effect in the gut microbiota of Eulipotyphla. In addition, significant positive correlations were observed between Caudoviricetes vOTUs and MAGs assigned to Lachnospiraceae and Treponema in Rodentia and Lagomorpha. Although such positive correlations may seem inconsistent with a simple predator-prey relationship between phages and bacteria, they may reflect a stable long-term coexistence between viruses and their bacterial hosts, in which phages regulate rather than completely eliminate dominant bacterial populations. This dynamic interaction suggests that phage predation may help modulate bacterial community structure while maintaining dominant functional groups, thereby contributing to the stability and functional redundancy of gut microbial communities.

Compared with the extensively studied gut microbiota of humans and other medium- to large-sized mammals, the gut microbiota of small mammals has received relatively limited attention, and available metagenome-assembled genome (MAG) resources remain scarce (Chen et al., 2021; Deng et al., 2023; Hess et al., 2011; Peng et al., 2021; Svartström et al., 2017). High-quality gut microbial MAG resources for Rodentia have mainly been derived from mouse models under experimental conditions (De Nies et al., 2022; Lesker et al., 2020). For wild rodent species, MAG-based studies of the gut microbiota have so far been reported only for the bank vole (Myodes glareolus), from which 254 MAGs were recovered. In that study, Bacteroidota was the most abundant phylum in the gut microbiota, followed by Bacillota, Spirochaetota, and Pseudomonadota (Lavrinienko et al., 2020). Several studies have characterized the gut microbial communities of Ochotonidae and Leporidae within Lagomorpha; however, most of these studies have not reached the microbial genome level. To date, European hare samples included in the wildlife gut MAG database constructed by Levin et al. represent one of the few available MAG resources for wild lagomorphs (Kohl et al., 2018; Levin et al., 2021; Paës et al., 2022). In Eulipotyphla, gut microbiota studies have been conducted only for Suncus murinus and Suncus ater, and neither has provided microbial genome-level resources (Koziol et al., 2022; Shinohara et al., 2019). Given the currently limited research effort devoted to the gut microbiota of small mammals, MAG reconstruction has great potential to reveal previously undescribed gut microbial diversity in these hosts. In this study, microbial genome assembly and quality screening were performed using 219 gut metagenomic samples from small mammals collected in Gongga Mountain, thereby establishing the first gut microbial genome resource for high-altitude small mammals. A total of 1 385 MAGs were reconstructed, of which 1 384 were classified as bacterial genomes. Compared with microbial genomes in the GTDB database, 1 328 MAGs could not be assigned to known species, suggesting that they may represent previously uncharacterized species-level genomes. The recovery of these potentially novel microbial genomes substantially expands the available microbial reference genome resources and provides new insights into the gut microbial diversity of small mammals.

As an important group of mammals, small mammals have received limited attention in studies of gut viromes and bacteriophage genomes, with most existing research relying on mouse models in the context of human disease treatment (Mao et al., 2024; Ritz et al., 2024). In the present study, viromes were predicted and quality-filtered from the gut metagenomic sequencing data of 219 small mammals, resulting in the construction of 749 vOTUs. These vOTUs provide a valuable supplement to the currently limited gut virome resources available for small mammals. Comparison with three established gut virome databases, namely GPD, GVD, and MGV, showed that 599 vOTUs did not cluster with viral genomes deposited in these databases. This result suggests that most of the vOTUs recovered in this study may represent putatively novel gut viruses. Ritz et al. (2024) detected a high abundance of Caudoviricetes in the gut virome of mouse models but did not identify members of Retroviridae. Similarly, Mao et al. (2024) did not detect Retroviridae in the fecal virome of mice. In contrast, we predicted 44 vOTUs assigned to Retroviridae from the gut metagenomic samples of small mammals collected in Gongga Mountain. Notably, none of these Retroviridae vOTUs matched viral genomes in the existing gut virome databases examined in this study, including GPD, GVD, and MGV. The recovery of these previously unreported Retroviridae vOTUs may reflect distinctive features of the gut virome of small mammals inhabiting the high-altitude Gongga Mountain region. These findings expand current knowledge of small-mammal-associated gut viruses and provide valuable genomic resources for future studies of retroviruses in wildlife hosts.

In conclusion, we conducted an in-depth investigation of the gut microbiota of 219 small mammals using shotgun metagenomic sequencing. This study established a compositional and functional landscape of gut microbial communities across three orders of small mammals inhabiting the high-altitude Gongga Mountain region. By comparing gut microbial composition and functional profiles among these host orders, we identified order-specific microbial taxa and functional biomarkers. Notably, the gut microbiota of Eulipotyphla harbored higher abundances of potential pathogens and Retroviridae, suggesting that these mammals may serve as potential carriers of zoonotic pathogens and highlighting their possible value in public health surveillance and early-warning systems. Through metagenome-based microbial genome reconstruction and viral prediction, we further established the first gut microbiome and virome resource for high-altitude small mammals, providing an important data foundation for future studies. Nevertheless, this study was primarily based on metagenomic inference and lacked direct functional validation. Future investigations integrating experimental approaches, such as metabolomic analysis and targeted functional assays, will be valuable for confirming the predicted microbial functions and ecological interactions inferred from metagenomic sequencing.
