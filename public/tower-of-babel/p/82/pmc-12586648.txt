Claus waste-derived TiO2 coupled with UiO-66 for an S-scheme heterojunction with enhanced photocatalytic activity
Authors: Maedeh Kouhfallah, Bita Ayati, Habibollah Younesi, Hossein Shekari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This research investigates the decoration of UiO-66 with TiO2 derived from recycling units of the gas refinery Claus process, using a solvothermal technique. Comprehensive materials characterization by XRD, FT-IR, TGA, FE-SEM, and BET confirmed the successful formation of a well-defined UiO-66/TiO2 heterojunction, with TiO2 nanoparticles uniformly distributed on the UiO-66 surface. The nanocomposite exhibited a reduced optical bandgap (2.92 eV) compared to pristine UiO-66 (3.97 eV) and TiO2 (3.20 eV), as well as enhanced surface. The constructed UiO-66/CWT S-scheme heterojunction facilitates efficient charge separation via interfacial recombination of UiO-66 photogenerated electrons and CWT photogenerated holes, thereby improving photocatalytic efficiency. The resulting UiO-66/TiO2 nanocomposite exhibited enhanced photocatalytic performance under visible light compared to pristine UiO-66 and TiO2 particles. The optimal conditions for Rhodamine B (RhB) mineralization with the highest photocatalytic removal efficiency were determined by evaluating the effects of the Claus process waste (CWT) to UiO-66 weight ratio, pH, photocatalyst dose, initial dye concentration, and reaction temperature. Under optimal conditions, with a CWT to UiO-66 weight ratio of 2:1, a pH of 3, a photocatalyst dose of 500 mg/L, an initial dye concentration of 25 mg/L, and a temperature of 25 °C, the dye removal efficiency increased to 97.42%. In other words, the formation of the nanocomposite can improve charge carrier separation and enhance photocatalytic activity. Furthermore, the decolorization kinetics followed accurately the Langmuir-Hinshelwood (L-H) first-order model. In conclusion, high-purity TiO2 was successfully produced using as-recycled TiO2 sourced from the gas refinery Claus process, demonstrating the potential of converting low-grade TiO2 from spent catalysts. The approach outlined here offers new perspectives on the value-added recycling of titanium resources in the Claus unit.

Introduction

One of the important and valuable steps in solving various environmental problems, especially in the field of water pollution and producing safe drinking water, is the treatment of wastewater containing persistent dyes, antibiotics, microorganisms, and micropollutants1. Therefore, the release of organic pollutants such as pharmaceuticals, organic dyes, pesticides, herbicides, and personal care compounds into water resources continues to be a major concern worldwide due to the threat to the public health of living organisms and ecological systems2. synthetic dyes pose carcinogenic risks to humans. Additionally, they tend to accumulate in both terrestrial and aquatic environments, leading to a range of ecological concerns3. The presence of pigment molecules in water not only hinders sunlight penetration into deeper aquatic zones but also disrupts the biological processes of marine life4. This disruption can result in issues such as stunted growth, heightened enzyme activity, respiratory challenges, and abnormal eye movements in both aquatic and terrestrial organisms due to the presence of toxic pollutants5. Therefore, the detoxification of these dyes is imperative, given their slow biological degradation, chemical stability, toxicity, and the complexities inherent in their molecular structure. In recent years, a variety of methods have been explored for the treatment of dyes, yet many face challenges such as high expenses and the incomplete removal of specific dyes6. The treatment methods for dye-laden wastewater can generally be categorized into physical, chemical, and biological approaches. Among the chemical methods, advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) are particularly significant for the removal of emerging pollutants, largely because they require relatively low energy7. However, AOPs are based on the production of hydroxyl radicals are mostly used for the mineralization of pollutants with some limitations due to toxic metals and secondary pollution especially catalysts8. Advanced oxidation processes (AOPs), of course, can also be described as promising technologies that aim to decolorize and mineralize organic compounds or convert them into biodegradable and environmentally friendly products. Among advanced oxidation methods, the use of homogeneous photodegradation or degradation systems such as Fen+/H2O2 (Fenton), H2O2/UV, and Fen+/H2O2/UV (Photo-Fenton) has attracted much attention due to its excellent performance in the oxidation of various pollutants including dyes9,10. Of course, it should be noted that Fenton processes have a very important limitation, which is the external addition of hydrogen peroxide to produce hydroxyl radicals11. In contrast, photocatalysts have garnered considerable interest in recent years due to their affordability and eco-friendly characteristics. Among various catalysts, heterogeneous catalysts offer distinct advantages, including their longevity, ease of separation, and efficient recyclability12. Metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) have numerous applications within the realm of heterogeneous catalysis. These materials are characterized by robust three-dimensional crystalline structures featuring large pores, constructed from metal nodes and organic linkers13. MOFs exhibit capabilities such as adsorption, heterogeneous catalysis, and ion exchange12,14 and play an important role in enhancing the photocatalytic activity from the UV light range to the visible range15. When exposed to light, these materials function as semiconductors, making them viable photocatalysts. Additionally, they possess a remarkable ability to effectively eliminate persistent pollutants16,17. However, a primary drawback of semiconductors is their limited efficiency, stemming from the rapid recombination of electron-hole pairs18. Heterogeneous connections with dual Z-design are a desirable and advanced approach to increase load carrier separation with the help of direct or indirect load transfer mechanisms19. As a general rule, achieving appropriate quantum efficiency with Z-mode mechanisms is a result of the appropriate selection of semiconductors, such that one semiconductor is responsible for reduction performance and high stability, while the other provides oxidation stability and high performance under light irradiation20. Among the various photocatalysts, titanium dioxide (TiO2) stands out due to its exceptional physical and chemical properties, including high chemical stability, resistance to corrosion, effective photocatalytic performance21, non-toxicity, low energy consumption, and insolubility in water. Its availability and environmentally friendly nature make TiO2 a widely adopted photocatalyst for pollutant removal22. However, there are certain limitations associated with it, such as its reactivity only in the UV light spectrum due to high bandgap energy, limited light transmission ability, low photon efficiency, and relatively weak absorption capabilities when compared to organic pollutants23. Research has demonstrated that combining TiO2 with porous materials such as activated carbon, zeolite, and mesoporous silica can significantly enhance its photocatalytic performance relative to bare TiO224. In a study conducted by25 showed that substantial advancements have been made regarding catalysts derived from metal-organic frameworks, possessing well-defined morphologies, owing to their promising applications in photocatalysis and electrocatalysis. In another study done by26, the researchers achieved an impressive 95% removal of Rhodamine B (RhB) using a porous TiO2/ZrO2photocatalyst with a 100 W LED lamp. Additionally, other researchers, for example27, successfully eliminated 97.58% of RhB dye and 66.59% of toluene using a TiO2@UiO-66 variant with UV light. Furthermore, in a study reported by28 supported a UiO-66-NH2/TiO2/ZnO nanocomposite (UTZ), achieving a notable removal efficiency of 90.03% for benzene, toluene, and xylene (BTX) along with an 85.16% reduction in total organic carbon (TOC). Also researchers such as29 removed methylene blue and methyl orange with simulated sunlight, reaching a 60% efficiency using a CoFe2O4@UiO-66 nanocomposite effectively. Furthermore, for instance30, reported a removal efficiency of 76.4% for RhB, 94.1% for methylene blue, 62.8% for tetracycline, and 58.5% for ofloxacin with a CFc/TiO2/NH2-MIL-125(Ti) system under sunlight conditions.

This paper presents a novel nanocomposite, UiO-66/CWT, which has been developed from the waste generated by the Claus process at the Pars Asalouye Special Economic Energy Zone Gas Refinery. This composite enhances the metal organic framework of UiO-66 through the incorporation of TiO2 nanoparticles. The study evaluates the synthesis of the nanocomposite and its effectiveness in removing RhB pollutants under visible light conditions. The Claus process employs various catalysts, including TiO2, to accelerate reaction rates. Despite the substantial presence of TiO2 as a catalyst, the waste produced during this process has often been left unused, contributing to environmental contamination. This research innovatively repurposes this industrial byproduct as a valuable photocatalyst. Instead of synthesizing new TiO2 nanoparticles, this approach integrates the existing waste material to create the UiO-66/CWT nanocomposite. Moreover, the band gap of the photocatalyst was tuned to align with the visible spectrum by incorporating terephthalic acid, an economical ligand, into the structure. The study also utilizes cutting-edge COB 50 W (Chips on Board) LED lamps, emphasizing the importance of energy efficiency and reduction in consumption.

Materials and methods

Chemicals

This study utilized RhB dye powder (C28H31ClN2O3) as a pollutant, 96% pure ethanol (C2H5OH) for washing synthetic materials and separating impurities, and titanium dioxide (TiO2) obtained from the waste of the Claus process at the Pars Asalouye Economic Co., Ltd. special energy zone gas refinery to create a nanocomposite with industrial photocatalytic properties. The chemicals, sourced from Merck Germany with synthetic purity, included zirconium tetrachloride powder (ZrCl4), terephthalic acid powder (C8H6O4), dimethylformamide solvent (DMF, C3H7NO), and acetic acid solvent (CH3COOH) for UiO-66 synthesis and nanocomposite production. Additionally, a 0.1 M hydrochloric acid (HCl, 37 wt%) solution and a 0.5 M sodium hydroxide (NaOH) solution were used for pH adjustment, while distilled water (D H2O) was employed for preparing the dye solution and recycling TiO2.

Synthesis of UiO-66

The synthesis of UiO-66)Zr-based Metal-Organic Frameworks (followed a solvothermal method, as described by27. In this process, 0.234 g of ZrCl4 was vigorously stirred with 15 mL of DMF using a magnetic stirrer for 1 h. Concurrently, a terephthalic acid solution was prepared by dissolving 0.166 g of terephthalic acid in 10 mL of DMF over 30 min. Subsequently, 6 mL of acetic acid was added to the ZrCl4 solution and stirred for 15 min. The terephthalic acid solution was then combined with the zirconium tetrachloride solution and stirred for an additional h. The resulting mixture was transferred to an autoclave and placed in an oven at 120 °C for 24 h. After cooling to ambient temperature, the solution was washed multiple times with 96% pure ethanol and DMF to remove impurities. Lastly, the solution was dried for 24 h at 60 °C in a vacuum oven, yielding white powder representing the synthetic UiO-66 particles.

CWT recycling method

Initially, a specific quantity of TiO2 obtained from the Claus process waste (CWT) underwent drying in an oven at 110 °C for 10 h. Subsequently, it was pulverized with a mortar until it could pass through a 230-mesh sieve (63 μm). This product was then subjected to washing with regular HCl to eliminate impurities, followed by placement in an ultrasonic device for one h, and subsequent multiple washings with distilled water. Ultimately, the cleansed TiO2 was dried in an oven at 110 °C for 3 h and then subjected to calcination at 550 °C31.

Synthesis of UiO-66/CWT nanocomposite

Nanocomposite particles of UiO-66/CWT were synthesized using the solvothermal method, following a procedure similar to the procedure done by27. To begin, 0.234 g of ZrCl4 was combined with 15 mL of DMF to create a uniform solution. Concurrently, in a separate beaker, 0.166 g of terephthalic acid was mixed with 10 mL of DMF. Subsequently, 6 mL of acetic acid was added to the zirconium tetrachloride solution, followed by the addition of the terephthalic acid solution. Recycled CWT powder, in weight ratios of CWT to UiO-66 ranging from (0.5:1, 1:1, 1.5:1, 2:1, 2.5:1, 3:1, 4:1), was then added to the solution, and thorough mixing was carried out for 2 h. The resulting solution was transferred to an autoclave and placed in an oven at 120 °C for 24 h. After cooling and reaching ambient temperature, the solution was washed multiple times with DMF and 96% ethanol. Finally, the solution was dried in a vacuum oven at 60 °C for 24 h, yielding the white synthesized nanocomposite particles.

Photocatalytic degradation experiments

The experiments involved utilizing a discontinuous system to assess the photocatalytic removal of RhB dye and to evaluate the effectiveness of the UiO-66/CWT nanocomposite. Initially, a 100 mL dye solution with a concentration of 25 mg/L was prepared at room temperature and pH 3. Subsequently, the solution was transferred to a reactor measuring 14 cm*14 cm*3 cm, with a working volume of 200mL, and positioned on a magnetic stirrer. After introducing 300 mg/L of photocatalyst into the solution to generate free radicals such as ȮH and prevent settling of the photocatalyst particles, the solution was thoroughly mixed. Prior to commencing the removal process using the COB 50 W visible lamp, a novel LED lamp technology, the dye solution with the added photocatalyst was left in the dark for 40 min, covered with foil, and the resulting sample was referred to as the “dark” sample for the purpose of achieving absorption-desorption equilibrium. Subsequently, the lamp was activated for 180 min, and sampling was carried out at one-h intervals using a Pasteur pipette. The samples underwent centrifugation to partition the photocatalyst from the dye solution. Following the completion of the removal process in the photoreactor, the light absorption was quantified using a spectrophotometer at the dye’s maximum wavelength (554 nm). To enhance the photocatalytic activity of the nanocomposite and to assess the impact of reinforcing UiO-66 with TiO2 nanoparticles, the removal efficiency of the dye solution was estimated in the nanocomposite structure for various weight ratios of CWT to UiO-66 (0.5:1, 1:1, 1.5:1, 2:1, 2.5:1, 3:1, and 4:1), while maintaining pH, photocatalyst dose, initial dye concentration, and reaction temperature as constant parameters. Subsequently, the photocatalytic process of UiO-66/CWT for RhB removal was conducted under different conditions of solution pH (2, 3, 4, 5, 7, and 9), photocatalyst dose (100, 200, 300, 400, and 500 mg/L), initial dye concentration (15, 25, 35, 50, 75, and 100 mg/L), and reaction temperature (25, 35, 45, and 55 °C) using visible light. One factor at a time (OFAT) tests were carried out with three repetitions and an acceptable error margin of less than 5%. Main equipment

X-ray Diffraction Spectrometry (XRD) was employed to analyze the crystal structure of the particles using a Philips X/Pert Pro. Additionally, Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FT-IR) was utilized to examine the functional groups in each component and establish the correlation among the synthetic particles. The investigation also involved the use of a Bruker model TENSOR II for FT-IR analysis, operating within a wavelength range of 400–4000 cm−1. Furthermore, a Hitachi Model 300 STA Thermal Gravimetric Analysis (TGA) instrument was utilized to identify volatile organic and inorganic substances and study the samples’ behavior at various temperatures. The TGA device featured a temperature range from ambient temperature up to 1200 °C and a scanning speed ranging from 2 to 100 °C per minute. In addition, Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscopy (FE-SEM) was applied to determine the size, structural characteristics, and morphology of the synthesized particles. Finally, for the identification of chemical compounds and elements present in the synthetic nanoparticles, EDS-Mapping analysis was performed using the TESCAN company’s MIRA3 model. The energy gap was determined using a Diffuse Reflection/Transmittance Spectroscopy (DRS) SHIMADZU UV-1800 model, which recorded absorption intensity and reflection percentage in the 200–1100 nm range. The specific surface area of the synthesized particles was measured through Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) analysis, using the BELSORP MINI II model manufactured by BEL company. The UV/Vis Spectrophotometer, Optizen alpha model, was utilized to determine dye concentration, while the Elmasonic 120 H ultrasonic dispersed particles during the synthesis process. An autoclave and Teflon with a 50 mL volume were employed to transfer the solution prepared for the synthesis of UiO-66 and nanocomposite UiO-66/CWT. Heat of nanoparticles and their synthesis was observed using the Memert company’s UF110 oven, and synthetic materials were dried using the Memert company’s VO400 vacuum oven with its Platinum model vacuum pump. Chemicals were weighed using the Bel digital scale model M124i, and two-phase solutions were processed using the Hettich company’s centrifuge Universal 320R model. The TiO2 calcination in the Paragon Q11 furnace involved the use of a COB 50 W visible lamp, which was then cooled with a fan. Solution mixing during the experiment was facilitated by the Heidolph company’s MR silver magnetic stirrer, while the pH of the reaction was adjusted using the Hanna digital pH meter model HI83141. Additionally, the JULABO heating-cooling circulator bath model F33-EH was employed to control the reaction temperature.

FT-IR analysis

The FT-IR spectrum in Fig. 1b illustrates the characteristics of synthetic nanoparticles. In the spectrum of UiO-66, a broad peak at 3419 cm−1 within the range of 3000–3700 cm−1 indicates the presence of O–H bonds from water molecules, a feature also Present in the spectra of TiO2 and the nanocomposite. The carboxylate group of the terephthalic acid ligand exhibited two strong C–O bonds, leading to distinct peaks at 1584 cm−1 and 1398 cm−1 representing asymmetric and symmetric C–O vibrations, respectively. Additionally, a minor peak at 1509 cm−1 indicated the presence of C = C bonds within the benzene ring of the terephthalic acid ligand in the structure of UiO-66. The C = O stretching vibrations of the carboxylic acid in terephthalic acid were observed at 1660 cm−1, suggesting bonding between the zirconium metal and the organic component of terephthalic acid. Furthermore, peaks at 811 cm−1 and 744 cm−1 were associated with C–H and O–H vibrations in the terephthalate ligand, respectively. The asymmetric stretching vibration of Zr-(OC) was also evident at 553 cm−1, while the peaks at 675 cm−1 and 479 cm−1 were assigned to the stretching vibrations of µ3-O and µ3-OH, respectively45. In the FTIR spectrum of recycled titanium dioxide, a broad peak within the range of 400–900 cm−1 indicated the stretching vibration of Ti–O–Ti on TiO2. Additionally, the hydroxyl stretching vibration of the Ti–OH bond was identified at 1645 cm−1 27. Analysis of the FT-IR spectrum of the UiO-66/CWT nanocomposite revealed shifts in peak locations compared to bare UiO-66; for instance, from 1660 cm−1 to 1658 cm−1 and from 1584 cm−1 to 1588 cm−1. Furthermore, the relative intensity of characteristic peaks in the nanocomposite was noticeably diminished compared to bare UiO-66 and bare recycled titanium dioxide. The spectrum of the nanocomposite strongly supports the presence of UiO-66 and titanium dioxide. By reinforcing UiO-66 with TiO2, it can be inferred that Ti-–O–Zr groups were formed, establishing strong surface contact between Zr+4 and TiO2. The successful synthesis of the nanocomposite is evident.

TGA analysis

The findings presented in Fig. 1c illustrate the outcomes of thermal weight analysis conducted to assess the stability and thermal resilience of synthetic nanoparticles. This assessment took place in a nitrogen gas environment within a temperature range of 25 to 800 °C. The UiO-66 sample exhibited a total weight reduction of 56.12%. Within the temperature range of 25–160 °C, a 5.58% weight loss occurred attributable to the evaporation of water molecules. In the temperature range of 160–410 °C, a 6.42% weight loss was observed, attributed to the release of the DMF solvent trapped in the UiO-66 structure. The most substantial weight loss, amounting to 32.36%, occurred in the 410–574 °C temperature range, resulting from the decomposition of the UiO-66 terephthalic acid ligand. Studies by46, showed the thermal stability of the metal organic framework UiO-66 is notable, withstanding temperatures up to 773 K (approximately 500 °C). There was a marginal decrease in sample weight within the 574–794 °C range, amounting to 11.76%, attributed to the residual presence of Zr metal in the UiO-66 structure47,48. In the case of TiO2, its TGA pattern exhibited a 3.43% reduction in total sample weight, with 1.31% loss occurring between 25 and 154 °C due to water molecule evaporation. Additionally, a 0.71% weight loss within the 154–400 °C range resulted from solvent volatilization and residual organic substances in the TiO2 sample. These findings suggest the efficient recyclability of TiO2 derived from the Claus process waste. Moreover, beyond 400 °C, the amorphous phase of TiO2 transitioned into a crystalline state49,50. The TGA analysis of the UiO-66/CWT nanocomposite revealed a total weight loss of 23.93%. The weight loss of the nanocomposite in the range of 25–187 °C has been 7.9% due to the evaporation of water molecules in the structure of the nanocomposite. The nanocomposite displayed weight reductions of 3.85% and 11.46% within the respective temperature ranges of 187–420 °C and 420–620 °C, while a minimal 0.69% decrease was observed in the 620–794 °C range, indicating enhanced thermal resistance compared to bare UiO-66. This suggests a successful synthesis of the UiO-66/CWT nanocomposite through the reinforcement of UiO-66 with recycled TiO2 nanoparticles.

FE-SEM and EDS analysis

Figure 2 depicts the FE-SEM images of UiO-66, TiO2, and UiO-66/CWT nanoparticles at a magnification of 200,000 times, revealing a particle size of 200 nm. The metal-organic framework particles exhibited the characteristic octahedral morphology of UiO-66 with relatively uniform sizes, indicating a successful synthesis51. The TiO2 nanoparticles displayed a crystalline, spherical structure, appearing agglomerated and aggregated52. Within the nanocomposite image, the TiO2 particles adhered well to the UiO-66 particles, exhibiting crystalline, spherical, and agglomerated structures. This interaction potentially facilitated the formation of the nanocomposite and provided reinforcement to the metal organic framework of UiO-66 with TiO2. Figure 3 presents the EDS spectrum, while Table 1 lists the constituent elements of synthetic nanoparticles along with their respective weight percentages. The weight% of elements (C) and (Zr) in UiO-66 decreased from 28.54% to 32.11% to 8.21% and 10.70%, respectively, when comparing UiO-66 to UiO-66/CWT nanocomposite. This reduction was also evident in the XRD analysis, showing decreased characteristic peaks in the nanocomposite compared to bare UiO-66. Furthermore, the weight% of element (Ti) in TiO2 decreased from 64.69% to 39.09% when comparing it to the nanocomposite. Additionally, the weight% of element (O) increased in the nanocomposite due to its presence in both UiO-66 and TiO2 structures. Thus, the decrease in the weight% of the elements in UiO-66 and TiO2 in comparison to the UiO-66/CWT nanocomposite can be attributed to the effective coating of UiO-66 with TiO2, affirming the successful synthesis of the nanocomposite.

Bandgap energy

Figure 4a illustrates the graph depicting a decrease in the band gap measurement after the nanocomposite formation. The energy gap of TiO2 is 3.20 eV, UiO-66 is 3.97 eV, and UiO-66/CWT nanocomposite is 2.92 eV. The reduction in the nanocomposite’s energy gap indicates successful synthesis, resulting in improved photocatalytic dye removal efficiency under visible lamp irradiation.

Figure 5 illustrates the influence of nanoparticles on the RhB removal process under particular conditions: a 40-minute period of darkness succeeded by exposure to 50 W COB visible light with a radiation energy intensity of about 1.79 W/m2 for 180 min. In the initial 40 min of darkness during each trial, the efficacy of the removal process was considerably limited, attributed to the absorption traits of the nanoparticles. The findings highlight the superior absorption capacity of UiO-66 nanoparticles. Conversely, the creation of nanocomposites resulted in a notable reduction in absorption compared to bare UiO-66 particles.

In Fig. 5a, the photocatalytic activity of the bare UiO-66 nanoparticles, bare TiO2, and various CWT to UiO-66 weight ratios (0.5:1, 1:1, 1.5:1, 2:1, 2.5:1, 3:1, 4:1) in the nanocomposite was assessed under specific test conditions: pH 3, photocatalyst dose of 300 mg/L, initial dye concentration of 25 mg/L, and a temperature of 25 °C. The nanocomposite exhibited significantly enhanced efficiency in the photocatalytic removal of dye compared to bare UiO-66 (62.82%) and TiO2 (14.42%) nanoparticles. This improvement can be attributed to the effective separation and transfer of photoelectrons within the structures of TiO2 and UiO-66, as well as the synergistic effect between the two materials that provides active sites for photocatalytic reactions under visible light. The formation of the nanocomposite promoted carrier separation and improved photocatalytic activity27. Notably, the nanocomposite with a CWT to UiO-66 weight ratio of 2:1 demonstrated the highest photocatalytic removal efficiency at 94.92%, outperforming other weight ratios. Specifically, increasing the weight ratio from 0.5:1 to 2:1 resulted in an increase in dye removal from 82.82% to 94.92%. However, further increasing the ratio from 2:1 to 4:1 led to a decrease in the removal efficiency to 22.27%. This reduction can be attributed to an excessive loading of TiO2 nanoparticles on the surface of the UiO-66 metal organic framework, which can cover active sites and lead to an increase in electron-hole formation centers, ultimately reducing pigment removal56.

Kinetics of the photocatalytic elimination reaction

The data presented in Table 2 illustrates the removal percentage, oxidation rate constant (Kapp), and correlation coefficient (R2). The analysis indicates a non-linear relationship between Ct and irradiation time, demonstrating that the Langmuir-Hinshelwood (L-H) model’s first-order kinetics effectively describe the data related to pH, photocatalyst dose, initial dye concentration, and reaction temperature. The results from Table 2 reveal that the oxidation rate constant of the nanocomposite in CWT to UiO-66 weight ratios exceeded that of bare UiO-66 and bare TiO2 nanoparticles up to 3:1. Furthermore, the rate constant displayed an increasing trend up to pH 3, followed by a decrease as the pH of the reaction increased, with the highest rate constant observed at pH 3. Additionally, as the photocatalyst dose increased, the reaction rate constant also increased, while an increase in the initial dye concentration led to a decrease in the reaction rate. Moreover, the removal efficiency at temperatures of 25 °C, 35 °C, 45 °C, and 55 °C was found to be 97.42%, 94.30%, 93.40%, and 98.46%, respectively. The rate constant exhibited a decreasing trend until 35 °C, followed by an increase with rising temperatures up to 55 °C. It is noteworthy that at lower temperatures, the rate constant generally decreases due to certain chemical reactions not following the Arrhenius theory, while at higher temperatures, an increasing trend in the rate constant is observed. This suggests that at higher temperatures, dye removal adheres to the Arrhenius theory, indicating a positive influence of temperature on photocatalytic activity. Consequently, the highest removal efficiency was achieved at 55 °C with a rate of 98.46%, slightly differing from 97.42% at 25 °C. Therefore, the optimal reaction temperature is determined to be 25 °C.

Charge transfer mechanism based on the S-scheme model

Recent advances in heterojunction engineering have introduced the S-scheme (step-scheme) charge transfer model as a more precise description of interfacial electron migration in composite photocatalysts such as UiO-66/TiO2. In an S-scheme heterojunction, both UiO-66 and TiO2 are photoexcited under visible light, generating electron–hole pairs. At the interface, the photogenerated electrons with lower reduction potential in the conduction band (CB) of UiO-66 recombine selectively with the holes of the valence band (VB) of TiO2, due to the appropriate alignment of their Fermi levels and band structures. This selective recombination effectively filters out the less energetic charge carriers, leaving only the electrons in the CB of TiO2 (with stronger reduction ability) and the holes in the VB of UiO-66 (with stronger oxidation ability), which are retained for the subsequent redox reactions. As a result, the S-scheme mechanism not only promotes more effective charge separation but also preserves the high redox capability of the system, thus enhancing the overall photocatalytic performance. Recent studies have demonstrated that such an S-scheme transfer process is particularly beneficial for photocatalytic degradation and other redox reactions, as shown in similar systems65,66.

Energy band structures and proposed S-scheme photocatalytic mechanism of UiO-66/CWT nanocatalyst

As shown in Fig. 7, the UiO-66/CWT composite operates via an S-scheme pathway rather than a type-II transfer. Interfacial recombination occurs selectively between e⁻ in UiO-66’s CB and h⁺ in CWT’s VB, leaving the photogenerated e⁻ in CWT’s CB and h⁺ in UiO-66’s VB. This preserves the stronger reduction and oxidation potentials at the respective ends, which accounts for the enhanced photocatalytic performance. Under light irradiation, both UiO-66 and CWT generate electron–hole pairs. Due to the internal electric field and band alignment, the photogenerated electrons in the CB of UiO-66 recombine with the photogenerated holes in the VB of CWT at the interface. This S-scheme charge transfer pathway allows the photogenerated electrons with stronger reducing ability (in the CB of CWT) and holes with stronger oxidizing ability (in the VB of UiO-66) to be preserved, thereby enhancing the photocatalytic activity65,67,68. The photogenerated electrons with high reducing capability remain in the CB of TiO2, while the holes with strong oxidizing potential accumulate in the VB of UiO-66, leading to enhanced separation and redox activity (reaction 7). Therefore, the S-scheme model best explains the performance improvement in this system. This S‑scheme recombination pathway preserves the highly reducing electrons in the CB of CWT (–0.33 V), which are thermodynamically capable of reducing O2 to superoxide radicals (O2•−) (Reaction 8), and the strongly oxidizing holes in the VB of UiO‑66 (+ 2.90 V), which can oxidize H2O or OH⁻ to hydroxyl radicals (ȮH) (reactions 9 and 10). The reaction of the produced superoxide ion with hydrogen ion leads to the production of hydroperoxide radical. Further along the pathway, the reaction of the hydroperoxide ion with a proton leads to the formation of hydrogen peroxide (reaction 11). Hydroxyl radicals and hydroxyl ions are also produced by the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide by conduction band electrons (Reaction 12). The radicals produced on the surface of UiO-66 and TiO2 react with the Rhodamine B dye and decompose its molecular structure into simpler compounds CO2 and H2O (Reaction 13)69. Conclusion

The study presented a new photocatalyst developed from MOFs, showcasing its high efficiency in environmental applications such as dye removal under visible light. The results demonstrated that the innovative UiO-66/CWT nanocomposite significantly outperformed bare UiO-66 and recovered waste TiO2 from the Claus process in terms of photocatalytic degradation of RhB cationic dye. Various experiments were conducted to determine the optimal conditions for the photocatalytic oxidation of RhB, including the weight ratio of CWT to UiO-66, reaction pH, photocatalyst dose, initial dye concentration, and reaction temperature. The findings revealed that the nanocomposite with a weight ratio of 2:1 (CWT to UiO-66), pH 3, 500 mg/L photocatalyst dose, 25 mg/L dye concentration, and 25 °C temperature exhibited the highest removal efficiency (97.42%) and the highest oxidation rate constant (0.01873 min−1). Furthermore, the synergistic effect between TiO2 and UiO-66 within the nanocomposite was found to provide ample active sites for photocatalytic reactions under visible light, facilitating carrier separation. The experimental results aligned well with the first-order kinetics of the Langmuir-Hinshelwood model, indicating the adaptability of the model to the data.
