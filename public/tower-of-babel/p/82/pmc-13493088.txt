Clinical Outcome and Safety of Amnioreduction in Singleton Pregnancies with Severe Polyhydramnios
Authors: Laia Grau, Míriam Pérez-Cruz, Mar Bennassar, Olga Gómez, Elisenda Eixarch, Narcís Masoller, Montserrat Izquierdo Renau, Eduard Gratacós, Josep M. Martínez, Joan Sabrià
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSevere polyhydramnios is associated with fetal anomalies and adverse perinatal outcomes. The safety of amnioreduction in singleton pregnancies remains unclear. We aimed to evaluate pregnancy outcomes and procedure-related complications of amnioreduction in singleton pregnancies with severe polyhydramnios, excluding other invasive procedures apart from diagnostic amniocentesis.MethodsRetrospective analysis of amnioreduction procedures performed in 40 singleton pregnancies at a fetal therapy referral center (2013–2024). Inclusion criteria were severe polyhydramnios (defined by an Amniotic Fluid Index ≥35 cm) associated with maternal intolerance and/or short cervix evaluated by ultrasound (with a cut-off <15 mm). Procedure-related complications occurring within 48 h, including premature rupture of membranes, placental abruption, chorioamnionitis, preterm delivery below 37 weeks, and fetal demise were recorded. Maternal and pregnancy data, technical procedural characteristics, and perinatal outcomes were compared between complicated and uncomplicated procedures.ResultsIn 90% of the cases, a recognizable etiology for polyhydramnios was identified, with fetal structural anomalies being the most frequent cause, accounting for 62.5% of cases. Overall complication rate was 8.8%, including 3 placental abruptions and 2 cases of premature rupture of membranes. Complicated procedures were associated with earlier delivery (31.9 ± 3.7 vs. 36.8 ± 1.9 weeks, p = 0.016) and shorter procedure-to-delivery interval (8.6 ± 14.7 vs. 33.4 ± 16.4 days, p = 0.003). Repeated amnioreductions did not increase complication rates. No significant association was observed between pregnancy- or amniodrainage-related variables – including the number of amnioreduction sessions – and the occurrence of complications.ConclusionThese findings support that amnioreduction performed by highly trained personnel following a standardized protocol is a safe intervention in singleton pregnancies complicated by severe polyhydramnios. These data may provide valuable guidance in prenatal counseling for cases in which prolonging gestation is crucial due to fetal structural anomalies.

Introduction

Polyhydramnios, defined as an excessive accumulation of amniotic fluid in the second or third trimester, complicates 1–2% of singleton pregnancies [1–6]. While well-established causes include fetal congenital anomalies, gestational and pregestational diabetes, alloimmunization, and congenital infections, up to nearly 60% of cases of polyhydramnios are classified as idiopathic [7–11].

Severe polyhydramnios, defined by an Amniotic Fluid Index (AFI) ≥35 cm, may result in maternal intolerance due to abdominal distension and dyspnea or precipitate regular uterine contractions, thereby increasing the risk of preterm birth, preterm premature rupture of membranes (PPROM), and even spontaneous placental abruption – complications that are associated with increased perinatal mortality [4, 6–9, 12]. Fetal anomalies – primarily structural or genetic – represent the most frequent underlying causes of severe polyhydramnios. An etiology can be identified in up to 90% of severe cases – markedly higher than in milder forms – with structural anomalies being the most common finding. These anomalies not only account for a substantial proportion of severe cases but are also associated with a significantly worse overall prognosis [2].

Amnioreduction, also referred to as therapeutic amniodrainage, has been proposed as an effective intervention to relieve maternal discomfort and reduce the risk of preterm delivery in pregnancies complicated by severe polyhydramnios [9, 13–16]. Historically, this procedure was widely used in the management of twin-to-twin transfusion syndrome prior to the advent of laser therapy, and consequently, most of the literature on its complications originates from such cases [17–19]. Currently, amnioreduction is more frequently indicated in singleton pregnancies, although only a limited number of studies – with small sample sizes – have evaluated its safety and outcomes in the specific context of severe polyhydramnios caused by fetal structural anomalies. Furthermore, no consensus has been established regarding the technical aspects of the procedure, including the optimal volume of amniotic fluid removal at different gestational ages (GAs), the appropriate drainage rate, and the recommended interval between procedures [20].

The aim of this study was to evaluate pregnancy outcomes and procedure-related complications following amnioreduction performed under a standardized technical protocol in a highly selected cohort of singleton pregnancies with severe polyhydramnios, mainly due to fetal structural anomalies, in which no other invasive procedures- apart from diagnostic amniocentesis-were performed.

Methods

This retrospective study included all amnioreduction procedures performed in singleton pregnancies complicated by severe polyhydramnios, defined as an AFI ≥35 cm, between January 2013 and June 2024 at BCNatal (Hospital Clinic and Hospital Sant Joan de Déu), a tertiary referral center for fetal therapy.

Amnioreduction was performed during the third trimester in the presence of maternal intolerance to abdominal distension (abdominal discomfort or dyspnea), a high risk of preterm delivery (presence of uterine contractions and/or shortened cervix, defined in our institutional protocol as a cervical length ≤15 mm on transvaginal ultrasound, a threshold considered indicative of increased risk for preterm birth in the setting of uterine overdistension), or both. Cases requiring additional procedures such as fetoscopy, cordocentesis, or fetal shunt placement (apart from previous diagnostic amniocentesis) were excluded.

In all pregnancies, GA was calculated based on crown-rump length at first-trimester ultrasound [21]. All patients underwent a comprehensive evaluation for polyhydramnios according to our institutional protocol, which included a detailed ultrasound examination to rule out structural anomalies, screening for red cell antibodies, a 100 g oral glucose tolerance test to exclude gestational diabetes, and screening for parvovirus B19, rubella, toxoplasmosis, and syphilis. Genetic assessment, including fetal karyotype or microarray analysis and, in selected cases, exome sequencing, was performed via amniocentesis either prior to or during the procedure, depending on the suspected diagnostic and parental preferences.

The amnioreduction technique was consistent throughout the study period, with all procedures performed by maternal-fetal medicine specialists. Prophylactic cefoxitin (2 g) was administered 1–2 h before the procedure, and antenatal corticosteroids for fetal lung maturation (two doses of betamethasone 12 mg, administered 24 h apart) were given beforehand according to institutional protocol. Tocolysis with oral nifedipine or intravenous tractocile was administered in all cases. Under continuous ultrasound guidance, an 18-gauge needle was introduced transabdominally, avoiding the placenta, the uterine fundus, and any fetal or cord contact. Local anesthetic was infiltrated at the puncture site, and the needle was connected to a vacuum-aspiration device to allow controlled fluid removal. Real-time sonographic monitoring was maintained to ensure correct needle-tip position, prevent fetal/cord contact, and document progressive fluid reduction. In all procedures, the aim was to drain between 3 and 4 L of amniotic fluid. Once this target range was reached, the procedure was discontinued at the operator’s discretion, taking into account the objective of maintaining a residual fluid volume within normal limits or achieving mild oligohydramnios, as well as maternal tolerance and the absence of an accessible fluid pocket at the puncture site. When indicated, an amniotic fluid sample was submitted for microbiological testing to exclude congenital infection.

Maternal and pregnancy data were recorded for all cases. Procedural variables included the number of amniodrainage sessions performed during gestation, placental location, AFI before and after the procedure, the volume of amniotic fluid removed, and procedure duration. Clinical information such as the underlying cause of polyhydramnios, the clinical indication, GA, and cervical length at the time of amnioreduction was also collected. Procedure-related complications were defined as events occurring within 48 h of the amnioreduction procedure, including PPROM, placental abruption, chorioamnionitis, preterm delivery before 37 and 34 weeks, and fetal demise. Other variables such as GA at delivery, latency from the procedure to delivery, mode of delivery, birthweight, umbilical artery pH, and neonatal outcomes were analyzed as obstetric and perinatal outcomes. The occurrence of at least one of these complications defined a complicated amnioreduction. A comparative analysis was subsequently performed to evaluate maternal and procedural characteristics between complicated and uncomplicated cases.

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 29 Package®. Data were reported as mean ± SD and n (%), after confirming normality distribution by the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. The Student’s t test, the chi-square test, and Fisher’s exact test were used where appropriate. A p value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 40 pregnant women with severe polyhydramnios were included in the study. Most were multiparous and had a body mass index within the normal range. Polyhydramnios was typically detected at the beginning of the third trimester, and an underlying etiology was identified in 90% of severe cases, most commonly a structural fetal anomaly (62.5%). Across these 40 women, 57 amnioreductions were performed, with a mean GA of 32 weeks at the time of the first procedure.

Among the 57 amniodrainages performed, 27 patients (67.5%) underwent only one amniodrainage, while the remaining 13 women (32.5%) required 2 to 4 amnioreductions. A total of 52 procedures (91%) were complication-free within 1 week, while 5 procedure-related complications (8.8%) occurred within 48 h after the procedure and led to early finalization of pregnancy. These included 3 cases of placental abruption (5.3%) and 2 cases of PPROM (3.5%). Most complications (4/5, 80%) occurred within the first 12 h following the procedure; specifically, all events occurred within this time frame except for one case of PPROM, which developed between 12 and 48 h after the procedure. PPROM occurred after a second amniodrainage, whereas placental abruption occurred after the first procedure. There were no instances of chorioamnionitis or fetal demise following the amnioreductions. Six neonatal deaths were recorded, all related to the underlying condition rather than to the amnioreduction procedure or its complications (3 neonates with congenital diaphragmatic hernia, one with Steinert’s disease, one with Kagami-Ogata syndrome, and one with an orofacial tumor).

Discussion

In our series, which consisted predominantly of singleton pregnancies complicated by severe polyhydramnios secondary to fetal structural anomalies, amnioreduction performed during the third trimester was associated with a low complication rate. Furthermore, no significant associations were observed between pregnancy- or amniodrainage-related variables – including the number of amnioreduction sessions – and the occurrence of complications. These findings support that amnioreduction performed by trained specialists following a standardized protocol is a safe intervention, which may be particularly valuable in cases where prolonging gestation is essential due to fetal structural anomalies.

The amnioreduction complication rate observed in our series was 8.8%, slightly lower than the rates reported in recent literature. For instance, Erfani et al. [17] described peri-procedural complications in 10.6% of amnioreductions, while more recently, Soni et al. [22] reported complications in 10.9% of the procedures in a cohort of 110 patients who underwent amniodrainage. Although the difference between our findings and those of these studies is subtle, direct comparisons may be limited due to differences in study populations. Both previous studies included more heterogeneous cohorts regarding polyhydramnios severity and drainage indications. In contrast, our cohort was highly homogeneous, consisting exclusively of singleton pregnancies with severe polyhydramnios, without any additional invasive or fetal surgical procedures other than prior diagnostic amniocentesis, with well-defined clinical indications for amnioreduction, and the underlying etiology was determined in 90% of cases. This homogeneity may reduce potential confounding and improve the internal consistency of our findings.

An additional relevant finding in our series was the timing of complications. Most of them (80%) occurred within the first 12 h following the procedure, with only 1 case of PPROM developing between 12 and 48 h. This observation may have practical implications for post-procedure surveillance, suggesting that closer monitoring during the first hours after amnioreduction may be particularly important. Furthermore, this information may be valuable when counseling patients regarding the expected timing of potential complications after the procedure.

As consistently reported in the literature, 82.5% of our cases were diagnosed with a significant structural and/or genetic anomaly [22]. The most frequent structural anomalies in our series were congenital digestive obstructions and diaphragmatic hernia. Polyhydramnios is well established as an independent risk factor for perinatal mortality and morbidity, with the prognosis largely determined by its underlying cause and severity. Thus, when a fetal anomaly is present, it is essential to consider both the intrinsic risks and neonatal mortality associated with severe polyhydramnios. This overlap makes it challenging to ascertain whether amnioreduction-related complications arise from the condition itself or from the procedure. In this context, Kleine et al. [12] evaluated the risk of complications following amniodrainage relative to the baseline risks of severe polyhydramnios and concluded that amnioreduction performed to alleviate maternal symptoms does not increase the overall complication rate in this specific clinical scenario.

Although our study did not include a control group managed expectantly, demonstrating the potential benefit of amniodrainage in prolonging gestation in high-risk pregnancies – thereby improving perinatal outcome – is essential. In our series, amnioreduction performed at lower GA in cases with maternal symptoms and/or high risk of preterm delivery resulted in an average interval of more than 4 weeks between the initial procedure and delivery. These observations suggest a clinically meaningful latency; however, causality cannot be inferred. Shorter latency in the complicated group is expected, as early delivery constituted part of the complication definition in this study. By contrast, other studies conducted in more heterogeneous populations that compared amniodrainage with expectant management concluded that amnioreduction does not significantly prolong pregnancy [13]. Further research in more homogeneous populations could help clarify this issue. Nonetheless, these previous studies highlighted other important benefits, such as a lower rate of uterine atony and a higher rate of vaginal delivery in the group managed with amniodrainage [22]. Additionally, the improvement in maternal comfort should not be underestimated, as it substantially enhances quality of life during the final stages of pregnancy.

Regarding the amnioreduction technique protocol and its potential association with procedure-related complications, there is a lack of randomized studies [14, 16, 17]. At present, no consensus exists on the maximum safe volume of amniotic fluid that can be aspirated, the optimal aspiration rate, or the use of tocolytics and antibiotics. Improving outcomes and reducing complication rates through better patient selection or technical refinement remains challenging. In our series, amnioreduction was performed by highly trained staff following a stringent protocol, and no significant differences were identified between complicated and uncomplicated procedures with respect to maternal age, body mass index, GA at the time of amniodrainage, volume of amniotic fluid removed, procedure duration, or the time interval between sessions. Interestingly, complication rates remained low even in procedures involving drainage volumes greater than 3 L. While our study was not powered to formally assess the relationship between drainage volume and complication risk, this observation may be reassuring when counseling patients who require large-volume amnioreduction.

Although both our findings and previous reports suggest that repeated amniodrainage procedures may be as safe as a single intervention, this interpretation should be approached with caution. In our series, the 2 cases of PPROM occurred during consecutive amnioreductions, whereas the three cases of placental abruption were observed during the first procedure. Recently, Laoreti et al. [20] reported a low complication rate in 121 procedures performed on 86 patients, including both singleton and twin pregnancies with polyhydramnios of varying severity. Their study documented one case of placental abruption after a second amnioreduction and 1 case of PPROM at 36 weeks following the first drainage [20]. The absolute number of complications is small, and studies to date – including ours – are retrospective and observational, which limits the ability to draw definitive conclusions. Nevertheless, current data indicate that repeated interventions may be justified in patients experiencing recurrent clinical symptoms – such as severe abdominal distension, dyspnea, and threatened preterm labor with a shortened cervix – due to persistent polyhydramnios.

This study has several limitations and considerations, including its retrospective design, limited sample size, and the absence of a control group for comparative analysis. In addition, the relatively small number of complications limits the statistical power to detect potential associations between clinical or procedural variables and adverse outcomes; therefore, these findings should be interpreted with caution. The primary strength lies in its targeted selection criteria. Although the number of cases included is modest, to date, this represents the most homogeneous series reported. To our knowledge, this is the first study to exclusively include women with singleton pregnancies complicated by symptomatic severe polyhydramnios, without any additional invasive procedures or fetal interventions apart from diagnostic amniocentesis, all managed under a strict study protocol with postnatal follow-up. Furthermore, all amniodrainage procedures were performed by a maternal-fetal expert team following a standardized protocol at a tertiary referral center.

In conclusion, we observed a low complication rate associated with amnioreduction in a well-selected and homogeneous cohort of singleton pregnancies complicated by severe polyhydramnios, with no cases of perinatal death or severe maternal complications. Additionally, no identifiable differences were found between complicated and non-complicated groups, which reinforces the importance of establishing a strict technical protocol in a procedure that should be performed by trained specialists. These findings may serve as a valuable resource for counseling potential candidates for amnioreduction in cases involving fetal anomalies, where prolonging gestation could substantially improve neonatal outcomes.
