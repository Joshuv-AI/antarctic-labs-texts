Cochlear endoplasmic reticulum stress causes connexin 26 degradation is involved in age-related hearing loss
Authors: Xue Bai, Bing Liao, Wen-Hui Hu, Mei-Qun Wang, Yu Sun, Xu-Bo Chen, Kai Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryAge-related hearing loss (ARHL) represents a progressive auditory disorder. Growing evidence indicates that degradation of connexin 26 (Cx26) in cochlear may constitute one of the pathogenic mechanisms in ARHL. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying age-related Cx26 degradation remain unclear. We systematically analyzed Cx26 expression patterns in C57BL/6J mice across different age groups and confirmed an age-dependent downregulation of Cx26 in the mouse cochlea. Notably, we observed concurrent upregulation of endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress markers Glucose-regulated protein 78 (GRP78) and protein disulfide isomerase (PDI) in aged cochlear tissues. Furthermore, in vitro experiments demonstrated that ER stress significantly reduced Cx26 protein levels in cochlear explants. Mechanistically, we observed enhanced expression of ubiquitination-related proteins Nedd4 and Eps15 with evident colocalization with Cx26 in an ER stress mouse model. In summary, our findings demonstrate that Cx26 ubiquitination mediated by cochlear ER stress is the main cause of Cx26 degradation in the cochlea of aged mice.

Introduction

Age-related hearing loss (ARHL), clinically designated as presbycusis, is a progressive and irreversible auditory dysfunction marked by diminished high-frequency sound perception and impaired speech-processing capabilities.1,2 Epidemiological studies indicate that ARHL prevalence escalates with aging, affecting over one-third of individuals beyond 60 years of age.3,4 Notably, this sensory deficit has been linked to systemic comorbidities in older adults, including social isolation, depressive disorders, and accelerated cognitive decline.5 In addition, more and more evidence is implicating ARHL as a modifiable risk factor for Alzheimer’s disease pathogenesis.6,7 The etiological complexity of ARHL arises from multifactorial interactions between genetic predisposition, endocrine dysregulation, ototoxic agents, and noise exposure.8,9 In recent years, clinical pharmacogenetic analysis based on individual genetic information has been of significant importance for the prevention of sensorineural hearing loss, particularly in guiding the use of medications that pose a risk of hearing loss.10 While current clinical management predominantly relies on hearing aids and cochlear implants to amplify auditory signals, no FDA-approved pharmacotherapeutics exist to halt or reverse cochlear degeneration. This therapeutic gap underscores the critical need to elucidate molecular mechanisms driving ARHL progression and develop mechanism-driven interventions. Auditory senescence manifests through multilevel dysfunction, spanning peripheral cochlear structures to central neural networks. Particularly, cochlear pathology characterized by lateral wall atrophy, hair cell loss, and spiral ganglion neuron degeneration has been identified as a cardinal feature of ARHL.2,11,12 Nevertheless, the molecular cascades initiating these degenerative processes remain incompletely characterized, necessitating systematic investigation into early pathogenic events.

Emerging genetic evidence underscores the critical role of endogenous mutations in ARHL pathogenesis. Rodriguez et al. demonstrated that compound heterozygous mutations in GJB2 predispose individuals to accelerated ARHL onset.13 Pioneering work by Fetoni et al. established the first causal link between connexin 26 (Cx26) deficiency and ARHL through a Gjb2+/− murine model, which simulate the heterozygous human mutation profile and exhibited premature high-frequency hearing deterioration.14 Mechanistically, Cx26-containing gap junctions are essential for maintaining endocochlear potential (EP) and cochlear amplification, with Zhu et al. revealing that Cx26 insufficiency disrupts these processes, culminating in progressive hearing loss.15 Furthermore, Tajima et al. further identified Cx26 degradation and post-translational modifications as early biomarkers in ARHL pathology.16 Our recent investigations demonstrated age-dependent reduction of cochlear gap junction plaques (GJPs) correlating with accelerated ARHL progression in murine models.17 However, the cause of age-related degradation of cochlear Cx26 is unclear. Notably, connexins exhibit rapid turnover (half-life, 1.5–5 h) with tightly regulated synthesis-degradation equilibrium under physiological conditions.18 Aging disrupts this homeostasis through altered expression profiles, intracellular trafficking defects, and aberrant post-translational modifications, ultimately driving pathological gap junction remodeling.19 The endoplasmic reticulum (ER) is the main site of protein synthesis, and the homeostasis of the ER is constantly challenged by pathological damage, which leads to the accumulation of misfolded proteins in the lumen of the ER, which is known as ER stress. ER stress is involved in the pathological process of many chronic diseases. In order to maintain the function of the ER, the unfolded protein response (UPR) is activated to reduce the load of misfolded proteins and intracellular cell homeostasis.20 Intriguingly, cochlear ER stress activation has been documented in aging murine models, implicating this pathway in ARHL development.21 Nevertheless, the causal relationship between ER stress and Cx26 dysregulation remains undefined. Based on these findings, we hypothesize that ER stress-mediated mechanisms drive age-related depletion of Cx26 GJPs in the cochlea, representing a novel pathogenic axis in ARHL.

In this investigation, we systematically delineated age-associated alterations in Cx26 expression and elucidated the molecular mechanisms underlying GJP degradation within the aging cochlea. Crucially, we established a causal relationship between ER stress activation and progressive Cx26 depletion through complementary in vivo and in vitro models. Pharmacological induction of ER stress consistently reduced Cx26 protein levels and disrupted GJP integrity across experimental paradigms. Mechanistically, transcriptomic profiling combined with immunofluorescence co-localization analyses revealed enhanced ubiquitination of Cx26 under ER stress conditions. Our data demonstrate that ER stress triggers Nedd4 and Eps15 mediated ubiquitination, thereby accelerating Cx26 proteasomal degradation and subsequent GJP disassembly. This study provides the first evidence that ER stress-induced ubiquitin-proteasome system activation drives age-related Cx26 degradation in cochlear GJPs. Our results propose a novel therapeutic paradigm wherein modulation of cochlear proteostasis, particularly through targeting ER stress pathways or ubiquitination regulators, could mitigate GJP degeneration and potentially delay ARHL progression.

Results

Age-dependent decline of cochlear Cx26 expression and GJP remodeling in C57BL/6J mice

C57BL/6J mice exhibit high-frequency hearing loss in middle age, which can be used as an ideal ARHL model.22 In this study, auditory brainstem-response (ABR) measurements revealed significant threshold increases at six months compared to one-month-old control, with further deterioration observed at 12 months (Figure 1A). To investigate age-related changes in Cx26 of the cochlea at different stages, we firstly performed the expression level analysis of Cx26 mRNA by RT-qPCR. As show in Figure 1B, RT-qPCR analysis demonstrated stable Gjb2 mRNA levels across age group, suggesting post-transcriptional regulation of Cx26 expression. Then, we performed immunofluorescent staining to label Cx26 for a detailed analysis of cochlear GJPs in different groups. In one-month-old C57BL/6J mice, gap junction proteins exhibited characteristic linear arrangements along the apposed plasma membranes of inner sulcus cells (ISCs). Comparatively, six-month-old mice demonstrated significant structural disorganization of ISC GJPs, manifesting as scattered distribution patterns with prominent fragmentation. This age-dependent deterioration progressed markedly in 12-month-old cohorts, where GJPs were reduced to punctate remnants distributed sporadically along cellular interfaces (Figure 1C). We also quantitatively analyzed the expression of Cx26 plaques in Deiter cells (DCs), and the results showed that the expression of Cx26 in DCs decreased significantly with age. To quantify this difference, we measured the length of the GJPs along a single-cell border and the Cx26 fluorescence density of DCs in different groups. The average lengths of GJPs of ISCs in 12-month-old mice were significantly shorter than those in other two groups (Figure 1D). Quantitative immunofluorescence outcomes displayed that the expression of Cx26 at the boundary of DCs gradually but significantly declined with the aging of mice (Figure 1E). To quantify Cx26 reduction during cochlear aging, western blot analysis of whole cochlear lysates revealed progressive decreases in connexin 26 protein levels. Quantitative densitometry showed 50.7 ± 7.2% and 89.4 ± 5.8% reductions at six- and 12-month postnatally compared to one-month controls (Figures 1F and G). Collectively, these data establish occurrence of a progressive age-related decline in cochlear Cx26 expression and concomitant ultrastructural disintegration of ISC gap junctions. ER stress signalings are activated in the cochleas of aged C57BL/6J mice

Previous studies have shown that ER stress is involved in the processes of various aging diseases, such as the Alzheimer’s disease, sarcopenia, heart failure, etc.23,24,25,26 In order to further clarify whether ER stress is directly involved in the occurrence of ARHL, we analyzed the multiple ER stress relational markers in the cochlea of aging C57BL/6J mice. Glucose-regulated protein 78 (GRP78 ) and protein disulfide isomerase (PDI) are important molecular chaperones in the ER and play a vital role in modulation of the UPR. The transcription levels of GRP78 and PDI were measured by RT-qPCR. As shown in Figures 2A and 2B, the mRNA expression levels of GRP78 and PDI were significantly increased in the cochleas of aged C57/6J mice. Immunofluorescence analysis further corroborated these findings, revealing intensified GRP78 and PDI fluorescence signals localized predominantly in the organ of Corti of aged mice (Figures 2C–2E). Furthermore, western blot analysis of whole cochlear lysates also demonstrated age-dependent elevations in ER stress-related proteins, GRP78, GRP94, PDI, and CHOP (Figures 2F and 2G). ER stress results in decreased Cx26 expression in Bxpc3 cells and cochlear explant culture

Incubation of Bxpc3 cells with dithiothreitol (DTT),which interferes with protein folding by blocking disulfide bond formation, decreased the expression of Cx26 in a concentration-dependent manner (Figure 3A). The expression of Cx26 was significantly decreased when incubated with 8 or 10 mM DTT for 6 h (Figure 3B). Another ER stress inducer, tunicamycin (TCM), also decreased the expression of Cx26 in a concentration-dependent manner (Figure 3C). Quantitative results revealed that TCM effectively suppressed Cx26 expression at concentrations as low as 30 μg/mL (Figure 3D). Immunofluorescence staining also confirmed that a low concentration of DTT (2 mM) and TCM (10 μg/mL) did not affect the expression of Cx26, while treatment with a high concentration of DTT (10 mM) and TCM (50 μg/mL) significantly decreased the expression of Cx26 (Figure 3E). In addition, to investigate the effects of ER stress on Cx26 expression in cochlear explants, cochlear explants were treated with cycloheximide to inhibit protein synthesis, and the expression of Cx26 in the presence or absence of ER stress inducers was evaluated. As shown in Figure 3F, the results of immunofluorescence staining showed that Cx26 expression of DC and ISC in cochlea decreased significantly after TCM treatment. Western blot quantification of cochlear explant cultures demonstrated a reduction in Cx26 protein levels after TCM treatment, indicating that Cx26 was degraded by proteasome mediated by the ER stress (Figures 3G and 3H). Notably, this degradation was attenuated by co-treatment with the proteasome inhibitor MG132(Figures 3I and 3J). ER stress decreased Cx26 expression in cochlea in C57BL/6J mice

To investigate the influence of ER stress on cochlea Cx26 in vivo, C57BL/6J mice were intraperitoneally injected with TCM for five days as the ER stress mouse model. RT-qPCR indicated that there were no significant differences in the transcription level of Cx26 between the control and TCM groups (Figure 4A). ABR test results showed that continuous TCM treatment had no significant effect on the hearing of mice (Figure 4B). Immunofluorescence staining was performed on the cochlear basilar membrane indicating there was no loss of hair cells in the organ of corti (Figure 4C). Further, we examined the expression of cochlea Cx26 in different groups; western blot analysis showed TCM treatment significantly reduced the level of Cx26 protein in cochlea compared with the control group (Figure 4D). Subsequently, we performed immunofluorescent staining to label Cx26 for a detailed analysis of cochlear GJPs of ISCs in different groups. In vehicle-treated controls, Cx26-containing GJPs exhibited characteristic hexagonal arrays along the plasma membrane, maintaining continuous linear configurations. On the contrary, the TCM treatment group presented as fragmentation of linear plaques into discontinuous puncta (Figure 4F). Morphometric analysis demonstrated significant TCM-induced GJP remodeling, the quantitative analysis results showed that the length of GJPs decreased significantly and the number of patches increased significantly in TCM treatment group (Figures 4G and 4H). ER stress leads to increased Cx26 ubiquitination in cochlea

The ubiquitinated proteasome pathway is an important protein quality control system in cells, which regulates various metabolic processes by regulating the turnover of functional proteins or degrading abnormal proteins. Ubiquitin ligases are core regulatory factors in the ubiquitin-proteasome system, responsible for the specific recognition of target proteins and mediating their ubiquitination, thereby marking these proteins for proteasomal degradation. To investigate the molecular mechanism by which TCM reduces Cx26 expression, we analyzed changes in the transcriptional levels of ubiquitin ligases following TCM treatment using real-time qPCR. The results revealed significant upregulation of a series of ubiquitination-related enzymes and protein degradation-associated genes, including NEDD4, EPS15, RBX1, SUMRF2, TRIM21, UCHL1, FBXO2, and SKP1, among others. In contrast, the expression of certain ubiquitination-related genes, such as SKP2, DNM2, and CUL1, showed no significant alterations (Figure 5). Previous studies have shown that the ubiquitinating ligase Nedd4 can mediate the ubiquitination of connexin, the ubiquitinated membrane proteins usually bind to recruited adapter proteins containing ubiquitin-binding domains and initiate the internalization and degradation of connexin 43.27,28 We performed the immunohistochemical analysis by staining Cx26, Nedd4, and Esp15 proteins for observing the relationship between GJPs and ubiquitination of cochlear ISCs in C57BL/6J mice. As shown in Figure 6A, GJPs formed linear structures along adjacent ISCs, and Nedd4 is expressed on the cell membrane and co-localized with the Cx26 control group. However, GJPs of ISCs in the TCM group appeared drastically fragmented, and the expression of Nedd4 was significantly increased at the cell membrane (Figure 6A). Western blot results also confirmed that the expression of total Nedd4 protein in the cochlea was significantly increased (Figures 6B and 6C). In addition, we observed that the expression of Esp15 was abundant in ISCs in the cochlea after TCM treatment, and co-localized with fragmented gap junctions (Figure 6D). Quantitative results showed that the expression of Esp15 was significantly increased in the TCM group (Figures 6E and 6F). The above results indicate that the decrease of Cx26 expression in cochlea caused by ER stress is accompanied by the increase of ubiquitination modification of Cx26. Discussion

Progressive auditory deterioration, a hallmark of presbycusis, manifests through distinct spatiotemporal progression of cellular degeneration in the cochlea. The C57BL/6J murine model has emerged as the gold standard for ARHL research due to its recapitulation of human presbycusis pathology, including analogous cochlear degeneration dynamics and hearing threshold trajectories.9,29 In this study, we observed that C57 BL/6J mice developed moderate deafness at six months of age and escalated to severe hearing loss at 12 months of age, which was consistent with the results of other studies.16 The gap junction channel is the main way for intercellular substance and information exchange, which is essential for maintaining inner ear homeostasis. Our previous studies have shown that the degradation of cochlear gap junction channels is involved in the occurrence of ARHL. However, with the increase of age, the mechanism leading to the degradation of cochlear gap junction protein is not clear. Here, we explored the degradation mechanism of cochlear gap junctions. Initial quantification of Gjb2 mRNA levels in cochlear tissues from C57BL/6J mice across age cohorts revealed stable transcriptional activity, with no significant variation observed between one-month, six-month, and 12-month groups. Then we detected the variation of protein levels of Cx26 in inner ear of aging models, and immunofluorescence-labeled Cx26 for analyzing the GJPs in ISCs and DCs. The GJP sizes of ISCs were significantly reduced, and the gap junction proteins Cx26 were significantly decreased in 12-month-old compared with one-month-old mice. Furthermore, western blot results also indicated that with the increasing age of mice, the total amount of Cx26 protein in the cochlea statistically decreases. Although we observed decreased Cx26 expression in DCs in aged mice, this finding does not directly establish a causal link between hair cell degeneration and Cx26 degradation. DCs and outer hair cells (OHCs) form a one-to-one pairing, providing mechanical support and metabolic sustenance to the OHCs.30 Our previous research demonstrated that specific knockout of Cx26 in DCs led to degeneration of the corresponding OHCs, indicating that Cx26 expression in DCs plays a critical role in OHC survival.31 It is currently hypothesized that Cx26 deficiency-induced hair cell damage may be associated with disruptions in potassium (K+) recycling, disturbances in ATP-calcium signaling propagation, and energy supply dysfunction.32 The aging of auditory cells is a highly complex physiological process. Future investigations will require more suitable animal models or advanced detection methodologies for deeper exploration. Connexin is a short-lived protein with a half-life of 1.5–5 h. The synthesis and degradation of connexin in the cell membrane exist in a dynamic equilibrium. New Cx26 is synthesized in the ER, modified, and then transported to the cell membrane, while removed connexin is internalized into lysosomes for degradation. Within the connexin family, Cx26 and Cx30 are most closely associated and typically co-assemble to form gap junction channels. Consequently, Cx30 may undergo coordinated alterations with Cx26. Tajima et al. demonstrated significantly reduced Cx26 and Cx30 levels in 32-week-old versus four-week-old C57BL/6J mice, concurrent with an age-dependent shift in Cx26 hydrophobicity.16 Paciello et al. further confirmed that Cx30 deficiency accelerates cochlear aging and age-related hearing decline, accompanied by stria vascularis dysfunction and sensory cell degeneration.33 Similarly, Pineros et al. observed significant downregulation of Cx30 and Cx43 expression during aging in CBA/CaJ mice, reporting strong correlations between hearing-loss severity and altered Cx30 expression.34 Collectively, these findings implicate connexin family members (Cx26, Cx30, and Cx43) in the pathophysiological processes of age-related hearing loss. Connexins have a half-life of 1.5–5 h, categorizing them within the short half-life protein family.18 Under physiological conditions, the self-renewal of cellular connexins is dynamically balanced. New Cx26 is synthesized in the ER, modified, and transported to the cell membrane for assembly, while eliminated connexins are internalized into lysosomes for degradation.35 Therefore, the decreased expression of Cx26 in aged C57BL/6J mice may be due to decreased protein synthesis or increased degradation. The ER is the main place for protein synthesis and processing. The accumulation of unfolded proteins in the lumen of the ER can lead to ER stress, and the UPR is then initiated to restore ER homeostasis. In addition, sustained accumulation of unfolded proteins initiates ER-related degradation. Studies have demonstrated that ER stress is involved in the aging process,36 and our findings reveal a significant upregulation of ER stress-related proteins in the cochlea. We therefore speculate that the decreased Cx26 expression observed in the cochleas of aged mice may be associated with ER stress. While our data provide insights into potential degradation mechanisms of cochlear Cx26 and contribute valuable information on ARHL, several questions remain unresolved. For instance, though Cx26 plaque disruption was observed, its functional consequences for gap junction activity remain unclear. Given that cochlear gap junction channels are essential for potassium recycling and EP maintenance,37 whether Cx26 degradation directly impairs EP requires experimental validation. Furthermore, beyond its expression in supporting cells of the organ of Corti, Cx26 is also present in intermediate cells of the stria vascularis and spiral ligament.38 However, whether vascularis intermediate cells and spiral ligament exhibit parallel Cx26 alterations in aged mice remains unreported. It is well-established that stria vascularis degeneration constitutes a primary pathological feature of ARHL. Future studies should explore potential links between this degeneration and connexin degradation.

To validate the hypothesis that the reduction of Cx26 in the cochleas of aged mice is directly linked to ER stress, we constructed a cellular ER stress model, and the results showed that both TCM and DTT ER stress inducers could lead to a significant decrease in Cx26 protein level in Bxpc3 cells. Furthermore, the results of immunofluorescence staining of cochlear culture showed that ER stress could lead to the decrease of Cx26 and the disruption of GJPs in the DCs and ISCs. In the mouse model, the expression level of Cx26 total protein in the cochlea was significantly decreased after TCM injection, and the GJP of ISCs was fragmented, which was similar to the pathological phenotype of the cochlea of aged C57BL/6J mice. Interestingly, the mice did not show significant hearing loss at this time. These results further demonstrate that the ER stress in the cochlea is directly related to the decreased expression of Cx26, and the decrease of Cx26 and the disruption of GJPs occur before hearing loss. To validate the functional consequences of Cx26 plaque disruption, extended monitoring was performed. Longitudinal assessment at 30 days post-TCM administration was conducted to evaluate auditory functional outcomes following Cx26 plaque disruption. As presented in Figure S1, high-frequency hearing loss and OHC loss in the basal turn of the cochlea were observed 30 days post-TCM administration. Tajima et al. showed that the degradation of Cx26 in the cochlea preceded the loss of hair cells in C57BL/6J mice. The degradation of Cx26 and the destruction of GJPs were the factors of early hearing loss in ARHL.16 Combined with our latest research, the decreased expression of Cx26 in adult mice leads to a pathological type similar to ARHL, and the degradation of Cx26 in the cochlea accelerates the development of ARHL.17 Therefore, elucidating the molecular mechanism of cochlear Cx26 degradation and mechanism-based regulation to slow down the degradation of Cx26 may become a treatment for ARHL. In this study, we analyzed the changes in the transcriptional level of the cochlea in the TCM-treated group by transcriptome sequencing. The results showed that the transcription levels of genes related to ubiquitination and proteasome degradation pathways were significantly increased. The ubiquitinated proteasome degradation pathway is one of the main ways to regulate intracellular protein homeostasis.39 The ubiquitin-activating enzyme E1 activates the ubiquitin molecule, which is then transferred to the ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme E2. Finally, the ubiquitin ligase E3 recognizes and modifies the target protein, and the ubiquitinated protein is then degraded in the proteasome.40 Our western blot results showed that the level of total ubiquitinated proteins in the cochlea increased after TCM treatment, indicating that ER stress in the cochlea leads to misfolded protein accumulation and activation of ubiquitination degradation pathways. Studies have shown that the ubiquitination ligase Nedd4 is involved in the modification of gap junction proteins, and knockdown of Nedd4 using siRNA resulted in a decrease of the amount of the ubiquitination of Cx43.27,41 In order to determine whether the degradation of Cx26 in the cochlea was related to the ubiquitination modification of Nedd4, we investigated Cx26 of ISCs and Nedd4 together by immunofluorescent staining. The results showed that ER stress could lead to a significant increase in the expression of Nedd4 in the cochlea, and the Nedd4 interacts with Cx26 mainly at the plasma membrane of ISCs. In addition, Nedd4-mediated connexin ubiquitination is required to recruit Eps15 and to initiate the degradation pathway of connexin.42,43 Here, we investigated the interaction of Eps15 with Cx26 in the ISCs after ER stress. The immunofluorescence results show that a significant amount of Eps15 colocalizes with Cx26 of ISCs. Based on the above results, we propose that Nedd4- and Esp15-mediated Cx26 ubiquitination may be an important molecular mechanism of Cx26 degradation in the cochlea caused by the ER stress. Our previous paper reported that ROS accumulation in cochlea is a significant contributor to the decline in Cx26 expression levels. We proposed that accumulated ROS directly attacks critical membrane proteins, leading to their degradation via proteasomal or lysosomal pathways.17 In this current study, we focus on investigating the impact of ER stress on cochlear Cx26 expression. Multiple studies indicate an interplay between oxidative stress and ER stress, where ER stress impairs mitochondrial function, thereby increasing oxidative stress.44,45 Consequently, ER stress may induce endogenous ROS accumulation, thereby triggering Cx26 degradation and cellular damage. However, the pathophysiological changes in the aging cochlea are highly complex. The molecular mechanisms underlying Cx26 degradation and its impact on auditory function remain to be further elucidated. Therefore, exploring antioxidant strategies and modulating intracellular ER stress holds significant promise for restoring cochlear Cx26 expression and inner ear function. Future research will be conducted in this direction.

Our findings demonstrate that the progressive reduction of connexin proteins and the subsequent dysregulation of GJPs in the cochlea are directly associated with ER stress. Mechanistically, we identify that ER stress-induced ubiquitination of Cx26 serves as the primary mechanism driving its proteasomal degradation. This disruption of connexin homeostasis, particularly Cx26, appears to critically impair cochlear function through compromised intercellular communication. Consequently, therapeutic strategies aimed at preserving Cx26 expression and maintaining GJP integrity through targeted modulation of ER stress pathways may represent a promising avenue for mitigating ARHL and potentially other degenerative conditions linked to connexin dysregulation.

Limitations of the study

Although our data provide insights into the potential mechanisms underlying age-related degradation of Cx26 in the cochlea, several questions remain unresolved. In addition to its expression in supporting cells of the organ of Corti, Cx26 is also present in intermediate cells of the stria vascularis and the spiral ligament. However, whether alterations in Cx26 also occur in the stria vascularis and spiral ligament of aged mice have not been examined in this study. Despite the observed disruption of Cx26 plaques, the functional impact on gap junction activity remains unclear. Furthermore, although we observed a reduction in Cx26 expression in the cochleas of aged mice, this finding does not directly demonstrate a causal relationship between hair cell degeneration and Cx26 degradation. Aging of the auditory system is an exceedingly complex physiological process. Future studies will require more suitable animal models or advanced detection methods to enable deeper exploration.

Experimental model and study participant details

Animals

Male C57BL/6J mice at defined developmental stages (4-week-old, 6-month-old, and 12-month-old) were sourced from the Specific Pathogen-Free (SPF) Experimental Animal Center of Nanchang University. Neonatal mice at postnatal day 3 (P3) were generated through timed mating of adult breeders and maintained under identical SPF conditions. Mice in the tunicamycin (TCM, 11089-65-9, MedChemExpress) group received intraperitoneal injections of 10 mg/kg TCM for five consecutive days. All experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Nanchang University (NCULAE-20231031214). We try our best to minimize the number and suffering of animals.

Cell culture

The pancreatic adenocarcinoma cell line BxPC3 was obtained from Wuhan Procell Life Technology Co., Ltd. and was authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) analysis, which confirmed the absence of cross-contamination. We confirm that the cell lines used have tested negative for mycoplasma contamination. BxPC3 cells were maintained in high-glucose Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (11995500, Gibco) supplemented with 5% (v/v) heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (11054001, Gibco), incubated under standard culture conditions. In order to explore the effect of endoplasmic reticulum stress on the expression of Cx26 in cells, we selected dithiothreitol (DTT,16096-97-2, MedChemExpress) and TCM to construct an endoplasmic reticulum stress model. BxPC3 cells were exposed to different concentrations of DTT (2-10 Mm) or TCM (10-50 μg/ml) for 12h. The treated cells were recovered in normal medium and collected for further detection. Each experimental groups consisted of a minimum of three independent biological replicates.

Method details

Culture of cochlear explants and drug treatments

Cochlear basilar membranes were dissected from postnatal day 3 (P3) wild-type C57BL/6J mice under sterile conditions and cultured on collagen IV-coated dishes (356401, Corning® BioCoat™). Explants were maintained in defined culture medium containing: basal medium, 1% bovine serum albumin (A8020, Solarbio), 2 mM L-glutamine (G7513, Sigma-Aldrich), and D-glucose, with equilibration in a humidified 37°C/5% CO2 incubator for 24 hours pretreatment. Pharmacological interventions were initiated after this acclimatization period, tunicamycin TCM group were treated with 100μg/mL TCM for 48 h, and the TCM + MG132 group was treated with 100 μg/ml TCM combined with 10μM MG132. After treatment, samples were collected for immunofluorescence and western blot detection. Each experimental groups consisted of a minimum of three independent biological replicates.

Auditory brainstem response (ABR)

All auditory testing was conducted in a relatively sound-attenuated shielded chamber to minimize the influence of external sounds. For ABR test, each experimental group contained five mice. Concisely, mice were deep anesthetized, then maintained the body temperature at 37°C by a heating pad during the whole experiment. The electrodes were respectively inserted in the vertex (active) and the tested ear (reference). ABR thresholds were recorded with a tone burst stimulus at six frequences of 8, 16, 24, 32 and 40 kHz through using Tucker-Davis Technology System. The SigGen32 software (Tucker-Davis Technologies, USA) was used to amplify and recorded the ABR signals at different frequencies.

Cochlear cryosections preparation

Following deep anesthesia induced by intraperitoneal pentobarbital, bilateral temporal bones were surgically excised and cochleae meticulously harvested via fenestration approach. Specimens were immediately immersion-fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer for 60 min at 4°C. Subsequent decalcification was performed using 10% EDTA disodium salt at 4°C for 48-72 h, with solution renewal every 12 h. For cryosection preparation, decalcified cochleae underwent graded sucrose dehydration: sequential immersion in 20% and 30% sucrose-PBS solutions until complete tissue equilibration. Specimens were then oriented in mold chambers and embedded in Tissue-Tek® OCT compound under stereomicroscopic guidance, followed by overnight cryopreservation at -20°C. Axial modiolar sections (5 μm thickness) were obtained using a cryostat microtome maintained at -20°C chamber temperature. The slides are collected for immunofluorescence.

Immunofluorescence

Each experimental group contained five mice. Briefly, the organs of Corti were dissected in PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, then permeabilized with 3 % Triton X-100 for 20 min, and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin for 1 h. The above steps were all carried out at room temperature. Afterwards, the samples were incubated with mouse anti-Cx26 (710500, Thermo Fisher Scientific, RRID: AB_2533971), anti-Eps15 (sc-135898, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, RRID: AB_2098694), and anti-NEDD4 (sc-518160, Santa Cruz Biotechnology) at 4°C for overnight. The next day, the tissues were washed with PBS and incubated with the secondary antibody at 25°Cfor 4h. Finally, Phalloidin (P5282, Sigma-Aldrich) and DAPI (C0065, Solarbio) was used to label the cuticular plate and nucleus of hair cells. After sealing, the samples were visualized and imaged through confocal fluorescence microscope (Olympus FV1000).

RNA preparation and analysis

Total RNA were obtained from mice cochleae lysed with Trizol Reagent according to the instructions of the RNAiso Plus (TaKaRa, Shiga, Japan). Subsequently, samples were used to synthesized cDNA by using a PrimeScript RT reagent kit with gDNA eraser (Takara Bio Inc.). The mixtures containing appropriate primers and the LightCycler 480 SYBR Green I Master were preformed on a LightCycler™ 480 system (Roche Diagnostics Ltd) with activation at 95°C for 15 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 10 s, 60°C for 15 s and 72°C 30s. A normalized mRNA level of each gene were calculated by 2−ΔΔCt and the expression levels were normalized to GAPDH. Each experimental group consisted of five independent biological replicate samples.

Protein extraction and western blotting

Protein lysates from cellular or cochlear explant samples were extracted using ice-cold RIPA buffer (P0013B, Beyotime Biotechnology). Lysates were centrifuged at 12,000×g for 15 min at 4°C to remove debris, then mixed with Laemmli buffer (1610747, Bio-Rad) containing 4% SDS and 100 mM Tris-HCl, followed by denaturation at 95°C for 10 min. Proteins were electrophoresed through 10% SDS-polyacrylamide gels under constant voltage and transferred to 0.45 μm PVDF membranes (IPFL00010, Millipore) using semi-dry transfer at 25 V for 45 min. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 2 h at room temperature, then incubated with anti-Cx26 antibody (710500, Thermo Fisher Scientific, RRID: AB_2533971), anti-β-actin antibody (A5316, Merck-Millipore, RRID: AB_476743), anti-Grp78 (sc-13539, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, RRID: AB_627698), anti-CHOP (15204-1-AP, Proteintech, RRID: AB_2292610), anti-Grp94 (14700-1-AP, Proteintech, RRID: AB_2233347), anti-PDI (sc-376370, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, RRID: AB_10988269), anti-Eps15 (sc-135898, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, RRID: AB_2098694), anti-NEDD4 (sc-518160, Santa Cruz Biotechnology) and GAPDH (10494-1-AP, Proteintech, RRID: AB_2263076) overnight at 4°C. Next, the samples were incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody for 2h, Enhanced chemiluminescence reaction kit was used for detecting the immunoreactive bands, and densitometric analyses of the band were conducted by using NIH Image J. Each experimental groups consisted of a minimum of three independent biological replicates.

Quantification statistical analysis

The number of mice included in each experiment were indicated in the corresponding figure legends. All data are presented as means ± SEM and analyzed using GraphPad Prism statistical software (version 8, GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego). Statistical power was calculated and normality verified before parametric comparisons using one-way ANOVA or Student's t-test as appropriate. Statistical significance was defined as NS: no significant, ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, and ∗∗∗p < 0.001.
