Impact of different sufentanil concentrations in epidural labor analgesia on maternal and neonatal outcomes: a propensity score-matched cohort study
Authors: Lixia Xue, Ludi Zhang, Yanying Wang, Xiaoting Ma, Huayong Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe optimal sufentanil concentration for patient-controlled epidural analgesia (PCEA) during labor remains uncertain. This study evaluated the association between sufentanil concentration strategies and maternal-neonatal outcomes.MethodsThis single-center retrospective cohort study included parturients receiving sufentanil-containing epidural labor analgesia. Patients were stratified by fixed sufentanil concentration in the PCEA formulation. The observed concentration range was 0.25–0.50 µg/mL, and the median value of 0.30 µg/mL defined the Low-concentration group (≤ 0.30 µg/mL) and High-concentration group (> 0.30 µg/mL). One-to-one propensity score matching (PSM) was used to balance baseline confounders. The single primary endpoint was a composite safety outcome defined as any analgesia-related adverse event, including nausea and vomiting, pruritus, or lower limb motor blockade. Secondary outcomes included Visual Analog Scale (VAS) pain scores, breakthrough pain, PCEA bolus use, maternal satisfaction, labor outcomes, and neonatal metrics.ResultsA total of 314 matched patients were analyzed. Both groups showed similar end-stage VAS scores and maternal satisfaction (P = 0.412), although the Low-concentration group required more PCEA boluses (median: 5 vs. 3; P = 0.004). The High-concentration group had more opioid-related maternal adverse events, including pruritus (31.8% vs. 14.6%; P < 0.001), nausea and vomiting, and minor motor blockade. High-concentration sufentanil was associated with higher odds of prolonged second stage of labor (adjusted odds ratio = 1.85; 95% confidence interval: 1.08–3.16).ConclusionsLow-concentration sufentanil was associated with broadly comparable analgesic effectiveness and fewer opioid-related maternal adverse events. The second-stage difference was statistically significant but clinically modest and did not translate into significant differences in delivery mode, postpartum hemorrhage, or neonatal outcomes. These findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating real-world evidence.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12884-026-09572-6.

What does this study adds to the clinical work

This study suggests that a low-concentration sufentanil strategy for patient-controlled epidural analgesia (PCEA) was associated with broadly comparable analgesic effectiveness and fewer opioid-related maternal adverse events compared with a high-concentration strategy. The observed difference in second-stage duration was statistically significant but clinically modest and did not translate into significant differences in instrumental delivery, cesarean delivery, postpartum hemorrhage, or neonatal outcomes. These findings support cautious consideration of lower-opioid PCEA formulations in routine practice, while prospective studies are needed to confirm causal effects.

Introduction

Labor pain is recognized as one of the most severe forms of acute pain experienced by women, with a profound impact on maternal psychological and physiological well-being. Consequently, effective pain management during childbirth is a critical component of modern obstetric care. Among the available analgesic methods, neuraxial analgesia, particularly epidural analgesia, is globally recognized as the gold standard due to its superior efficacy and safety profile [1]. Its widespread adoption is evident from high utilization rates in developed nations, such as the United States, where over 70% of parturients receive neuraxial analgesia [1]. Furthermore, its use is on a significant upward trend globally, including in countries where it was previously less common, underscoring a growing international consensus on its importance [2].

The established principle of modern epidural analgesia involves a balanced combination of a low-concentration local anesthetic and a potent opioid. This synergistic approach effectively reduces the total dosage of each agent, thereby minimizing motor blockade and other dose-dependent side effects. Sufentanil, a highly potent and lipophilic opioid, is frequently selected for this purpose. Its co-administration with a local anesthetic like ropivacaine or bupivacaine has been proven to accelerate the onset of analgesia, enhance the quality of pain relief, and significantly spare the required dose of the local anesthetic [3–5]. A recent network meta-analysis has confirmed that analgesic regimens containing sufentanil are among the most effective and fast-acting options for labor pain management [4]. This dose-sparing effect is particularly crucial as it allows for satisfactory analgesia with minimal motor impairment, facilitating maternal mobility and participation in labor [5].

More broadly, contemporary labor analgesia has evolved from uniform neuraxial protocols toward individualized analgesia strategies. Recent literature emphasizes that modern maternity analgesia includes multiple neuraxial techniques, such as epidural analgesia, combined spinal-epidural analgesia, dural puncture epidural analgesia, and continuous spinal analgesia, together with patient-controlled dosing, individualized timing, maternal preferences, and non-technical aspects of care [6]. Within this evolving framework, optimizing the sufentanil component of patient-controlled epidural analgesia is clinically relevant because small differences in opioid concentration may influence both analgesic adequacy and opioid-related maternal adverse events while preserving the advantages of low-concentration local anesthetic techniques.

Despite the well-documented benefits of incorporating sufentanil into epidural solutions, a significant knowledge gap persists regarding its optimal concentration. The majority of recent high-quality research has concentrated on comparing different drug combinations or adjusting the concentration of the local anesthetic while keeping the sufentanil dose constant [7, 8]. For instance, a retrospective study highlighted improved maternal outcomes by reducing the ropivacaine concentration but did so with a fixed dose of sufentanil, reflecting a common research design that overlooks the opioid’s own dose-response relationship [8]. Methodological limitations and the risk of bias in prior opioid-related studies have also been noted [9]. Indeed, the lack of dose-ranging studies is a recognized limitation in the sufentanil literature. High-level evidence, such as systematic reviews of randomized controlled trials, highlights that many studies do not adequately assess the dose-response relationship, explicitly concluding that future research is warranted to determine optimal dosages [10]. This directly points to an unresolved clinical question and a clear research gap in the field.

Therefore, the primary objective of this retrospective cohort study is to address this gap by evaluating the association between different sufentanil concentrations used in clinical practice and maternal-neonatal outcomes. This research aims to provide real-world evidence to inform the optimization of sufentanil dosing in epidural labor analgesic solutions.

Methods

Study design and ethics

This single-center, retrospective cohort study was conducted at Handan First Hospital to evaluate the association between different sufentanil concentrations used in epidural labor analgesia and maternal-neonatal outcomes. Data were comprehensively extracted from the Electronic Medical Record (EMR) system and the Anesthesia Clinical Information System (ANCIS) spanning from October 1, 2022, to June 30, 2025. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Handan First Hospital (Approval No. HDYY-LW-26021). Given the retrospective nature of the analysis and the utilization of de-identified data, the requirement for written informed consent was waived by the IRB. The study was conducted in strict accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study population

A three-stage validation process was employed to ensure the accuracy of participant selection. First, a preliminary cohort was generated through an automated query using predefined criteria. Second, medical records were manually reviewed by two independent researchers using a standardized data abstraction form. Finally, eligibility discrepancies were resolved by a senior anesthesiologist through consensus.

The inclusion criteria were: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status I or II [11]; (3) singleton pregnancy at term (gestational age ≥ 37 0/7 weeks) with an intended vaginal delivery [12]; and (4) epidural labor analgesia (ELA) initiated with a sufentanil-containing solution.

Exclusion criteria included: (1) documented failure of epidural catheter placement or catheter replacement due to inadequate analgesia (defined as persistent Visual Analog Scale [VAS] score > 3 despite rescue boluses or unilateral block confirmed by the attending anesthesiologist); (2) administration of systemic opioids (e.g., pethidine, nalbuphine) within 4 h prior to epidural placement; (3) known allergy to local anesthetics or opioids; (4) severe pre-existing systemic comorbidities (e.g., significant cardiac, pulmonary, hepatic, or renal disease); (5) major obstetric complications, including hypertensive disorders of pregnancy [13], placenta previa, or active maternal hemorrhage; (6) contraindications to neuraxial anesthesia (e.g., infection, coagulopathy) [14]; and (7) incomplete medical records lacking mandatory core data, specifically: sufentanil concentration, baseline demographics, intrapartum VAS scores, delivery mode, or neonatal Apgar scores.

Grouping strategy and sample size estimation

Following the identification of the study cohort, eligible participants were stratified into two distinct groups for comparative analysis based on the fixed concentration of sufentanil in the initial patient-controlled epidural analgesia (PCEA) formulation. To ensure the strict definition of the exposure variable, the analysis was restricted to patients who received a constant concentration of sufentanil throughout the entire course of labor analgesia; patients who required a change in the background infusion concentration or replacement with a PCEA bag of a different concentration during labor were excluded from the final grouping. The observed sufentanil concentrations used in routine clinical practice during the study period ranged from 0.25 to 0.50 µg/mL. The median concentration was 0.30 µg/mL; therefore, patients were categorized into a Low-concentration group (≤ 0.30 µg/mL) and a High-concentration group (> 0.30 µg/mL). This threshold was used primarily as a data-driven cut-off to ensure balanced group sizes and sufficient statistical efficiency in this retrospective cohort. Clinically, however, the cut-off also separates lower-dose PCEA formulations from higher-opioid exposure regimens approaching the 0.5 µg/mL sufentanil concentration commonly evaluated in epidural labor analgesia studies [15, 16]. Because no universally accepted optimal sufentanil concentration threshold has been established for PCEA-based labor analgesia, this grouping should be interpreted as a pragmatic exposure contrast reflecting real-world institutional practice rather than as a definitive clinical dosing boundary. The distribution of actual sufentanil and ropivacaine concentrations used in the unmatched and matched cohorts is summarized in Supplementary Table 1.

Because this was a retrospective cohort study, the study population was determined by all eligible parturients who received sufentanil-containing epidural labor analgesia during the predefined study period and met the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Therefore, the sample size calculation was not used to prospectively determine enrollment. Instead, a post hoc adequacy assessment was performed to evaluate whether the available matched cohort provided sufficient statistical power for the single primary composite safety endpoint, defined as any analgesia-related adverse event. Based on institutional preliminary data and clinical expectations, the anticipated incidence of the primary composite safety endpoint was approximately 20% in the Low-concentration group and 38% in the High-concentration group. Using a two-sided two-sample proportion test with α = 0.05, 1-β = 0.80, and an allocation ratio of 1:1, at least 98 patients per group were required to detect this difference. The final propensity score-matched cohort included 157 patients per group, exceeding this minimum requirement. This analysis was therefore interpreted as a post hoc justification of dataset adequacy rather than as an a priori sample size determination.

Data collection and outcome definitions

Data extraction utilized the institution’s EMR and ANCIS to populate a standardized electronic case report form (CRF). The CRF systematically captured baseline maternal characteristics, obstetric parameters, detailed anesthetic management data, and all prespecified outcome variables.

A prespecified outcome hierarchy was used to improve interpretability. The single primary endpoint was a composite safety outcome, defined as the occurrence of any analgesia-related adverse event, including nausea and vomiting, pruritus, or lower limb motor blockade. This endpoint was selected because the principal clinical concern when increasing the sufentanil concentration in epidural labor analgesia is the potential increase in opioid-related maternal adverse effects. Component adverse events were also analyzed separately to clarify the safety profile. Motor blockade was quantitatively assessed using the modified Bromage scale (0 = no motor block to 3 = unable to flex ankle) assessed at the same time points as pain scores [17].

Key secondary efficacy outcomes included analgesic effectiveness assessed by the 100-mm Visual Analog Scale (VAS), where 0 mm indicates no pain and 100 mm indicates the worst imaginable pain [18], breakthrough pain, total effective PCEA boluses, time to first PCEA bolus, onset time, and maternal satisfaction. Given the potential for missing time-point data in retrospective records, VAS scores were collected where available at baseline, 30 min post-bolus, and at the end of the first and second stages of labor. Breakthrough pain was defined as a VAS score > 30 mm requiring physician-administered rescue boluses. Other secondary outcomes included maternal obstetric outcomes and neonatal outcomes.

Secondary outcomes encompassed a comprehensive panel of maternal and neonatal metrics. Maternal obstetric outcomes included the mode of delivery (spontaneous vaginal, instrumental vaginal [forceps or vacuum extraction], or cesarean section); the duration of the second stage of labor, with a prolonged second stage defined according to American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) guidelines as > 3 h in nulliparous women and > 2 h in multiparous women [19]; and the incidence of postpartum hemorrhage (PPH), defined as cumulative blood loss ≥ 1,000 mL within 24 h of delivery [20]. Overall maternal satisfaction with labor analgesia was evaluated using a self-reported 11-point Numerical Rating Scale (NRS) ranging from 0 (completely dissatisfied) to 10 (completely satisfied) collected 24 h postpartum [21]. Neonatal outcomes included Apgar scores at 1 and 5 min [22] (analyzed both as continuous values and categorical scores < 7); umbilical artery blood gas analysis including pH values and base excess (BE); and the rate of admission to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU).

A comprehensive set of variables was collected to rigorously control for potential confounding. Maternal baseline characteristics included age, parity, gestational age, and body mass index (BMI) at the time of admission for labor. Obstetric parameters at the time of epidural placement included cervical dilation, Bishop score, and status of membranes (intact or ruptured). Intrapartum management variables included the administration of oxytocin (categorized as induction or augmentation, with total cumulative dose recorded), which significantly influences labor progression and pain. Fetal characteristics included birth weight, as it can affect labor duration and mode of delivery. Anesthetic management variables explicitly included the specific concentration of ropivacaine (to control for its potential synergistic effect), the volume of the initial bolus, and PCEA pump settings.

Data quality control and management of missing data

To ensure the integrity and reproducibility of the retrospective dataset, a stringent multi-tiered data quality control protocol was implemented. The initial data extraction was primarily automated using structured queries from the EMR and ANCIS databases, incorporating built-in logical consistency checks and biological range checks. Following the initial automated extraction, a validation audit was performed on a random sample of 20% of the total cohort. This audit involved manual verification of the source records by two independent clinical researchers who were blinded to the study outcomes to prevent observer bias. Inter-rater reliability was formally assessed for key predictor variables and primary outcomes. The audit yielded a Cohen’s Kappa coefficient of 0.88 for categorical variables and an Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) of 0.94 for continuous variables, indicating high data reliability. Any discrepancies identified during this process were adjudicated by a senior obstetric anesthesiologist to reach a consensus. Regarding missing data management, a complete-case strategy was adopted for mandatory core variables required for cohort eligibility and primary analysis. Patients were excluded if they lacked any of the following core variables: sufentanil concentration, baseline demographic data, baseline VAS score, primary composite safety endpoint, delivery mode, or neonatal Apgar scores. These variables were therefore complete in the final unmatched cohort. Missingness was then assessed for all remaining covariates and secondary outcomes before propensity score matching. Variables with missingness < 5% were imputed using mean imputation for normally distributed continuous variables, median imputation for non-normally distributed or ordinal variables, and mode imputation for categorical variables. No covariate had missingness > 5%; therefore, MICE was not required in the final analysis. Secondary outcome variables that were not mandatory for cohort eligibility, particularly umbilical artery blood gas parameters and selected time-point VAS measurements, were not imputed as outcomes; analyses involving these variables were performed using available cases.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Version 26.0 and R Version 4.5.1, with a two-sided P value less than 0.05 considered significant. Continuous variables were compared using Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U test, while categorical variables were assessed using Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests.

The primary reference analysis was based on the 1:1 propensity score-matched cohort. This analysis was selected as the main comparative framework because it provided a clinically interpretable comparison between the Low- and High-concentration groups after balancing measured baseline maternal, obstetric, pain-related, and anesthetic characteristics. To address measured selection bias, we conducted one-to-one propensity score matching (PSM) using a nearest-neighbor algorithm without replacement and a caliper width of 0.02. The propensity score model included prespecified baseline maternal, obstetric, pain-related, and anesthetic variables available before or at epidural initiation, including maternal age, parity, BMI at admission, gestational age, baseline VAS score, cervical dilation, Bishop score, membrane status, oxytocin use, birth weight, fetal head station, ropivacaine concentration, initial bolus volume, background infusion rate, PCEA bolus volume, and lockout interval. A standardized mean difference (SMD) less than 0.1 was considered to indicate adequate balance. Outcomes in the matched cohort were analyzed using paired tests or generalized estimating equations, and longitudinal VAS scores were evaluated using linear mixed-effects models.

All analyses outside the matched cohort were considered supportive or sensitivity analyses rather than primary reference analyses. Supportive multivariable regression analyses were performed in the full unmatched cohort using the same covariate adjustment set to evaluate whether the associations observed in the primary PSM analysis remained consistent after covariate adjustment. Additional sensitivity analyses included restricted-cohort analyses excluding predefined outliers, inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW), and continuous-exposure modeling of sufentanil concentration. To further address the potential arbitrariness introduced by dichotomizing sufentanil concentration at the median, the actual sufentanil concentration was modeled as a continuous exposure variable in the full unmatched cohort. Adjusted regression models evaluated the association between each 0.1 µg/mL increase in sufentanil concentration and the key outcomes, using the same covariate adjustment set as the primary multivariable models. Subgroup analyses stratified by parity, BMI, and oxytocin use were considered exploratory and used interaction terms to assess potential effect modification.

Results

Patient screening and baseline characteristics

Of 628 parturients screened, 213 were excluded, leaving an initial unmatched cohort of 415 patients (Low-concentration: n = 198; High-concentration: n = 217) (Fig. 1). The administered sufentanil concentration ranged from 0.25 to 0.50 µg/mL, with a median value of 0.30 µg/mL; thus, 198 patients receiving concentrations ≤ 0.30 µg/mL were assigned to the Low-concentration group and 217 patients receiving concentrations > 0.30 µg/mL were assigned to the High-concentration group. The actual distributions of sufentanil and ropivacaine concentrations in the unmatched and matched cohorts are shown in Supplementary Table 1. Before PSM, the High-concentration group had significantly higher admission BMI, a greater proportion of nulliparity, advanced cervical dilation, lower Bishop scores, and higher baseline VAS scores and oxytocin usage (all P < 0.05). After 1:1 PSM, 157 matched pairs (n = 314) were generated. All baseline demographic, obstetric, and anesthetic variables were well-balanced between the two groups (SMD < 0.10, P > 0.05) (Table 1).

The missingness profile of the final unmatched cohort is summarized in Supplementary Table 2. Mandatory core variables, including sufentanil concentration, baseline demographics, baseline VAS score, primary composite safety endpoint, delivery mode, and neonatal Apgar scores, were complete after application of the eligibility criteria. Analgesic efficacy and safety profile

Outcomes related to analgesic efficacy and safety in the propensity score-matched cohort are presented in Table 2 and Table 3. Regarding key secondary efficacy outcomes (Table 2), patients in the High-concentration group exhibited a faster onset of analgesia compared to the Low-concentration group (median [IQR]: 10 [8, 12] min vs. 12 [10, 15] min; P < 0.001). Although the total volume of analgesic solution consumed was comparable, total sufentanil consumption was significantly higher in the High-concentration group (48.5 ± 8.2 µg vs. 34.2 ± 6.5 µg; P < 0.001). Patients in the Low-concentration group required significantly more effective PCEA boluses (median: 5 vs. 3; P = 0.004) to maintain comfort. However, the incidence of breakthrough pain requiring physician rescue was similar between groups (12.1% vs. 10.2%; P = 0.582). Maternal satisfaction scores at 24 h postpartum were comparable (8.8 ± 1.2 vs. 8.9 ± 1.1; P = 0.412). Longitudinal analysis (Fig. 2) showed that while the High-concentration group had statistically lower VAS scores at 30 min (P = 0.021), the clinical difference was minimal (< 10 mm) and scores were similar at all subsequent time points.

Regarding the primary composite safety endpoint (Table 3), the incidence of any analgesia-related adverse event was significantly lower in the Low-concentration group than in the High-concentration group (31/157 [19.7%] vs. 60/157 [38.2%]; P < 0.001). Component analysis showed that the Low-concentration group had a significantly lower incidence of pruritus (14.6% vs. 31.8%; P < 0.001), nausea and vomiting (5.1% vs. 12.7%; P = 0.018), and minor motor blockade (1.9% vs. 7.0%; P = 0.035). These matched-cohort results were considered the primary reference findings for analgesic efficacy and safety outcomes.

Maternal and neonatal outcomes

Maternal and neonatal outcomes are summarized in Table 4 and Table 5. Overall mode of delivery (P = 0.312) and intrapartum cesarean section rates (5.1% vs. 7.0%) did not differ significantly between groups. The High-concentration group had a statistically longer second stage of labor than the Low-concentration group, but the absolute median difference was modest (56 min vs. 48 min; absolute difference, 8 min; P = 0.035). The incidence of a guideline-defined prolonged second stage was numerically higher in the High-concentration group, but this difference did not reach statistical significance (12.1% vs. 7.6%; P = 0.185). This difference also did not translate into statistically significant increases in instrumental vaginal delivery (12.7% vs. 9.6%; P = 0.358), intrapartum cesarean delivery (7.0% vs. 5.1%; P = 0.467), postpartum blood loss, or PPH incidence (≥ 1000 mL). Neonatal safety profiles were robust across both groups, with no significant differences observed in 1-minute (P = 0.654) and 5-minute (P = 0.812) Apgar scores, umbilical artery pH and base excess, or NICU admission rates (3.2% vs. 3.8%; P = 0.763). These matched-cohort comparisons were considered the primary reference findings for maternal obstetric and neonatal outcomes.

Multivariable regression analysis

Supportive multivariable logistic regression analyses in the full unmatched cohort (n = 415) are presented in Tables 6 and 7 to evaluate whether the associations observed in the primary PSM analysis remained consistent after covariate adjustment. Sufentanil concentration was not an independent predictor of high maternal satisfaction (NRS ≥ 9) (aOR = 1.12, 95% CI: 0.72–1.75; P = 0.612); instead, end-stage 2 VAS ≤ 30 mm and spontaneous vaginal delivery were identified as significant positive predictors (Table 6). After adjusting for measured clinical confounders, the High-concentration strategy was associated with higher odds of a prolonged second stage of labor (aOR = 1.85, 95% CI: 1.08–3.16; P = 0.025) (Table 7). Nulliparity remained the strongest predictor of both a prolonged second stage and instrumental delivery. However, the association between the High-concentration strategy and instrumental vaginal delivery did not reach statistical significance (aOR = 1.45, 95% CI: 0.82–2.56; P = 0.198), indicating that the observed second-stage prolongation was not accompanied by a statistically demonstrable increase in operative vaginal delivery in this cohort.

Subgroup analyses and effect modification

Prespecified subgroup analyses stratified by parity, BMI, and oxytocin usage (Supplementary Fig. 1) revealed a significant interaction solely for parity (P for interaction = 0.038). Specifically, the High-concentration strategy was an independent risk factor for a prolonged second stage in nulliparous women (aOR = 2.15, 95% CI: 1.12–4.15; P = 0.021), whereas this association was not statistically significant among multiparous women (aOR = 1.25, 95% CI: 0.45–3.50; P = 0.665). In contrast, neither BMI (P for interaction = 0.582) nor oxytocin usage (P for interaction = 0.714) significantly modified the treatment effect, suggesting that the labor-prolonging risk of high-concentration sufentanil persists regardless of maternal body habitus or oxytocin augmentation.

Sensitivity analysis

To evaluate the robustness of the primary PSM findings against potential selection bias and modeling assumptions, a series of supportive sensitivity analyses were conducted using alternative statistical approaches. These included multivariable logistic regression in the full unmatched cohort (n = 415) adjusting for the complete set of identified confounders, a restricted analysis excluding patients with extreme clinical characteristics (BMI < 18.5 or > 35 kg/m²) or non-analgesia-related labor arrest, and an IPW analysis utilizing the full dataset to maximize statistical power. As presented in Supplementary Table 3, the direction and statistical significance of the associations between the High-concentration strategy and key adverse outcomes, specifically the increased risk of pruritus, nausea/vomiting, and the prolongation of the second stage of labor, remained consistent across all three alternative models.

In addition, to reduce reliance on the median-based grouping strategy, actual sufentanil concentration was modeled as a continuous exposure variable in Model 4. Each 0.1 µg/mL increase in sufentanil concentration was associated with higher odds of pruritus (aOR = 1.65, 95% CI: 1.25–2.18; P < 0.001), nausea and vomiting (aOR = 1.52, 95% CI: 1.08–2.15; P < 0.05), and prolonged second stage of labor (aOR = 1.35, 95% CI: 1.04–1.75; P < 0.05). These findings were directionally consistent with the dichotomized analyses in Models 1–3, suggesting that the observed associations were not solely driven by the median-based exposure classification.

Discussion

This study demonstrated that in the context of epidural labor analgesia with PCEA, a low-concentration sufentanil strategy offers a favorable risk-benefit profile compared to a high-concentration strategy. Using a clarified outcome hierarchy, the primary composite safety endpoint was significantly lower in the Low-concentration group, while key secondary efficacy outcomes indicated broadly comparable analgesic effectiveness and maternal satisfaction between groups. The low-concentration approach was also associated with less impact on labor progression. The broadly comparable analgesic effectiveness observed in the Low-concentration group appeared to be supported by greater patient-titrated PCEA bolus use, whereas the more favorable safety profile was characterized by a lower incidence of opioid-related maternal adverse events, namely pruritus and nausea and vomiting. Finally, and perhaps most clinically significant, our results identified the high-concentration strategy as an independent risk factor for a prolonged second stage of labor, a risk that was particularly pronounced in the nulliparous subgroup. These findings collectively suggest that a low-concentration regimen optimizes the balance between effective pain relief and maternal and fetal well-being.

The exposure definition should be interpreted in the context of real-world institutional dosing practice. Although the 0.30 µg/mL cut-off was derived from the median value of the observed cohort, it also provides a clinically interpretable distinction between lower-dose PCEA formulations and higher-opioid formulations closer to the 0.5 µg/mL concentration frequently evaluated in epidural labor analgesia studies [15, 16]. Therefore, the grouping strategy was intended to compare two pragmatic opioid exposure strategies rather than to define a universal therapeutic threshold. The continuous-exposure sensitivity analysis further supported this interpretation, showing that each 0.1 µg/mL increase in sufentanil concentration was associated with directionally consistent increases in opioid-related adverse events and prolonged second stage of labor. This reduces the likelihood that the main findings were merely an artifact of median-based dichotomization.

A central finding of our study is that the lower-concentration sufentanil regimen was associated with broadly comparable analgesic quality in this real-world cohort. This can be primarily attributed to the PCEA modality that empowers parturients to self-titrate analgesia according to their individual pain thresholds. Our study showing a higher number of PCEA demands in the low-concentration group (approximately 5 vs. 3), supports this concept of compensatory self-dosing. This aligns with the principle of personalized medicine, where the analgesic regimen is flexible and responsive to the unique experience of labor pain. This self-titration mechanism effectively closes the potential analgesic gap that a lower background infusion might otherwise create. Our observation is consistent with a growing body of evidence advocating for low-concentration, high-volume techniques or PCEA-dominant strategies. For instance, a systematic review comparing programmed intermittent epidural bolus (PIEB) with continuous epidural infusion (CEI) concluded that PIEB improves maternal satisfaction and reduces total anesthetic consumption without compromising analgesic efficacy [23]. Similarly, studies investigating the minimum local analgesic concentration (MLAC) of sufentanil have shown that effective analgesia can be maintained at very low concentrations when combined with a local anesthetic [24]. The flexibility of PCEA allows for achieving adequate pain control while minimizing total drug exposure, which is a cornerstone of modern obstetric anesthesia [25, 26].

The significant reduction in pruritus and nausea in the Low-concentration group highlights the dose-dependent nature of these opioid-related maternal adverse events. Our results show that the total sufentanil consumption was significantly higher in the high-concentration group, providing a direct explanation for the increased incidence of these adverse events. This dose-response relationship is a well-established pharmacological principle for opioids [27]. The underlying mechanism for opioid-induced pruritus is complex, involving central pathways more than peripheral histamine release. Recent research suggests that the activation of µ-opioid receptors in the dorsal horn of the spinal cord can trigger itch-specific neurons [28, 29]. A 2025 study further elucidated this pathway, identifying that neuropeptide Y neurons in the arcuate nucleus can mediate opioid-induced itch, offering novel therapeutic targets [30]. Our clinical findings are consistent with this understanding; a higher dose leads to greater µ-receptor activation and consequently more pruritus. When comparing our findings to other studies, a meta-analysis comparing sufentanil and fentanyl for labor analgesia found that sufentanil may be associated with a slightly different side profile, but pruritus remains a common issue with both [9]. Our study adds to this literature by demonstrating that manipulating the concentration and delivery method can substantially mitigate these side effects.

The association between higher sufentanil concentration and second-stage duration should be interpreted cautiously. In the matched cohort, the High-concentration group had a statistically longer second stage than the Low-concentration group; however, the absolute median difference was 8 min, which is modest in routine obstetric practice. In addition, the numerically higher rate of guideline-defined prolonged second stage did not reach statistical significance, and the observed difference in second-stage duration did not translate into statistically significant increases in instrumental vaginal delivery, intrapartum cesarean delivery, postpartum hemorrhage, or adverse neonatal outcomes. Therefore, this finding may be most appropriately interpreted as a signal of possible influence on labor progression rather than as evidence of clinically important obstetric harm.

Several explanations may be considered. Individual analgesic requirements during labor vary according to pain intensity, labor progression, and obstetric context, which may influence both the selected epidural regimen and subsequent labor outcomes [31]. Although higher local anesthetic concentrations have been associated with less favorable obstetric outcomes in prior evidence, particularly instrumental delivery, our study specifically evaluated sufentanil concentration while ropivacaine concentration was balanced between groups [32]. Therefore, the observed association may reflect subtle opioid-related effects, interaction with the overall neuraxial regimen, or residual confounding by indication rather than a direct causal effect of sufentanil concentration itself. This interpretation is also consistent with current obstetric guidance, which recognizes that longer second-stage durations may be acceptable in the setting of regional analgesia when maternal and fetal conditions remain reassuring [33]. Prospective dose-ranging studies with standardized provider practice, timing of epidural initiation, dosing adjustments, and labor management are required to determine whether reducing sufentanil concentration causally affects labor progression.

While the high-concentration regimen was associated with increased maternal side effects and a longer second stage of labor, it is crucial to confirm that it did not result in severe adverse neonatal outcomes. Our findings are reassuring in this regard. There were no significant differences in neonatal outcomes, including Apgar scores and umbilical artery pH, between the two groups. This is consistent with the known pharmacokinetic profile of sufentanil. As a highly lipophilic opioid, epidurally administered sufentanil is readily absorbed into the epidural fat and systemic circulation of the mother but exhibits minimal placental transfer [34]. Pharmacokinetic studies have consistently demonstrated a low fetal-to-maternal concentration ratio for sufentanil [35]. One population pharmacokinetic study in laboring women confirmed that while placental transfer occurs, umbilical cord concentrations of sufentanil remain exceptionally low due to a very slow transfer rate [36]. This pharmacological property is a key reason for sufentanil’s favorable neonatal safety profile. A 2026 retrospective study further corroborates our findings, showing that the use of a sufentanil-containing epidural solution did not negatively impact 5-minute Apgar scores or increase the rate of admission to the neonatal intensive care unit compared to parturients without epidural analgesia [37].

Our findings should also be interpreted within the broader landscape of evolving labor analgesia strategies. Neuraxial analgesia remains the most effective and widely used approach for intrapartum pain relief, but emerging alternatives and adjuncts are being explored for women with contraindications to neuraxial techniques, technical difficulty, or specific preferences. Peripheral nerve blocks and fascial plane blocks, including pudendal, paracervical, quadratus lumborum, and erector spinae plane approaches, have recently been discussed as potential options in selected labor settings [38]. At present, however, their use in labor remains supported mainly by limited evidence, including case reports, case series, and preliminary studies, and they cannot be considered replacements for standard neuraxial analgesia. Their potential relevance lies in expanding individualized analgesia pathways while maintaining attention to maternal satisfaction, fetal safety, and obstetric outcomes. In this context, our study addresses a narrower but clinically practical question within standard PCEA-based neuraxial analgesia: whether reducing sufentanil concentration can maintain broadly comparable analgesic effectiveness while lowering opioid-related maternal adverse events.

The strengths of this study lie in its use of multiple analytic approaches to evaluate the consistency of the observed associations. The 1:1 PSM analysis served as the primary reference analysis, while multivariable regression, IPTW, restricted-cohort analysis, and continuous-exposure modeling were used as supportive analyses to assess the consistency of the findings across different modeling assumptions. This analytic hierarchy improves interpretability while avoiding overreliance on any single observational method. Nevertheless, these methods cannot establish causality or fully eliminate residual confounding. The detailed subgroup analysis focusing on parity is another key strength, providing valuable insights that support a more personalized approach to labor analgesia. Furthermore, our study utilized a combination of subjective outcomes and objective measures, offering a more comprehensive assessment of analgesic efficacy.

We must also acknowledge several limitations. First, because this was a retrospective observational study, residual confounding and confounding by indication cannot be fully excluded. In routine practice, parturients with more severe baseline pain, more complex labor progression, greater need for oxytocin augmentation, or anticipated difficulty in maintaining adequate analgesia may have been more likely to receive higher sufentanil concentrations. This concern is supported by the baseline differences observed before matching, including differences in baseline VAS score, cervical dilation, Bishop score, oxytocin use, parity, and BMI. Although these measured factors were included in the propensity score and multivariable models and were well balanced after matching, unmeasured labor management factors may still have influenced both the choice of sufentanil concentration and clinical outcomes. Such factors include provider-level practice patterns, timing of epidural initiation relative to labor progression, real-time dosing decisions, undocumented rescue adjustments, and subtle assessments of labor complexity that were not fully captured in the electronic records. Therefore, the observed associations should be interpreted as real-world comparative effectiveness and safety signals rather than definitive causal effects.

Second, this was a single-center study conducted in China, where epidural labor analgesia protocols, sufentanil concentrations, background infusion settings, PCEA bolus settings, obstetric management, and thresholds for intervention may differ from those used in other healthcare systems, including Western obstetric anesthesia practice. Although the study reflects routine institutional practice and the observed sufentanil concentrations were explicitly reported, the applicability of these findings to centers using different neuraxial drug combinations, dosing protocols, labor management pathways, or provider staffing models may be limited. Future multicenter studies across different practice environments are needed to determine whether the observed associations are reproducible under alternative epidural analgesia protocols and obstetric management patterns.

Third, the retrospective dataset did not capture several patient-centered or longer-term outcomes that may be relevant when evaluating labor analgesia strategies. These include maternal mobility during labor, ability to participate in pushing, urinary catheterization burden, breastfeeding initiation and success, maternal postpartum recovery experience, and long-term neonatal neurobehavioral outcomes. Because these outcomes were not systematically documented in the electronic medical records, they could not be reliably analyzed. Prospective studies should incorporate standardized patient-centered outcome measures and longer neonatal follow-up to provide a more comprehensive assessment of the clinical trade-offs associated with different sufentanil concentration strategies.

Conclusion

This study suggests that a low-concentration sufentanil regimen combined with patient-controlled epidural analgesia may offer a favorable risk-benefit profile in routine clinical practice. This strategy was associated with broadly comparable analgesic effectiveness and a lower incidence of opioid-related maternal adverse events compared with a high-concentration regimen. The low-concentration approach was also associated with a statistically but clinically modestly shorter second-stage duration, particularly among nulliparous women, and this difference did not translate into statistically significant differences in instrumental delivery, cesarean delivery, postpartum hemorrhage, or adverse neonatal outcomes. Given the possibility of residual confounding and confounding by indication, these findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating real-world evidence. Prospective dose-ranging or randomized studies are warranted to confirm whether reducing sufentanil concentration causally improves maternal adverse events and labor progression.
