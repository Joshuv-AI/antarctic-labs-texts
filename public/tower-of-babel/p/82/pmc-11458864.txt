Tailoring silk-based covering material with matched mechanical properties for vascular tissue engineering
Authors: Yangxiao Yu, Guangzhou Song, Mengnan Dai, Peixuan Li, Jianmei Xu, Yin Yin, Jiannan Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractVascular covered stents play a significant therapeutic role in cardiovascular diseases. However, the poor compliance and biological inertness of commercial materials cause post-implantation complications. Silk fibroin (SF), as a biomaterial, possesses satisfactory hemocompatibility and tissue compatibility. In this study, we developed a silk film for use in covered stents by employing a layer-by-layer self-assembly strategy with regenerated SF on silk braiding fabric. We investigated the effects on the mechanical properties of the silk films in detail, which were closely correlated with fabric parameters and layer-by-layer self-assembly. The results showed that there was a significant relationship between these factors and both the compliance and mechanical strength. The 1 × 2/90°/100/SF6 film exhibited excellent mechanical properties. Notably, compliance reached 2.6%/100 mmHg, matching that of the human saphenous vein. Thus, this strategy shows promise in developing a novel covered stent, with biocompatible and comprehensive mechanical properties, and significant potential for clinical applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-75343-y.Keywords: Silk fibroin, Silk fabric, Covering material for vascular stent, Fabric parameters, Mechanical propertiesSubject terms: Biomaterials, Materials for devices, Structural materials

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease has witnessed a gradual increase in prevalence, and is the major cause of mortality among non-communicable diseases. Current treatments include open surgery and interventional therapy. Interventional therapy has been the prevailing surgical treatment for cardiovascular diseases over the past 30 years1, due to decreased trauma, shorter procedure duration, and reduced blood loss, all of which have resulted in decreased mortality rates. The research and innovation of vascular stents have been paid more and more attention2. Recently, covered stents, which combine a metal stent with a polymer covering material, have received more attention. As a physical barrier between the vessel wall and the blood, the polymer covering materials play important roles in preventing excessive tissue proliferation, dissection formation, and isolating hemangioma3. Moreover, the presence of covering materials can relieve stress concentration and turbulence on the vessel wall, induced by the uneven structure of a bare stent, effectively reducing the risk of thrombus formation4.

In clinical practice, currently available covering materials mainly include polyethylene terephthalate (PET) or polytetrafluoroethylene (PTEF), both of which are synthetic materials that possess excellent mechanical strength and biostability5,6. These materials have been used in the field of large and medium caliber artificial blood vessel transplantation for more than 60 years7, and they are also considered covering materials for covered stents. Gordon et al. used a PTFE covered stent to evaluate the tissue response in a porcine abdominal aorta model, and the results showed no restenosis or thrombus formation after 1 month8. The study by Schofer et al. showed the feasibility of a PET covered stent in the treatment of carotid lesions, which was a thin strut nitinol stent combined with a PET mesh9. After a 30-day follow-up period, none of the patients experienced major adverse cardiac events. However, these materials prepared for covered stents can cause stent migration and luminal surface fibrosis, owing to poor compliance and biological inertness, as well as inability of the luminal surface to endothelialize, which cannot comprehensively address the balance of the coagulation system10. Recent clinical analysis reports indicated that there was a relatively high incidence of postoperative re-interventions in long-term follow-up results for both PET covered stents and PTFE covered stents11,12. These materials do not comply with the development of tissue regenerative medicine, thus there is an urgent need to replace these materials with advanced biodegradable biomedical materials.

Silk fibroin (SF) is a fascinating biomaterial that has been continuously developed over the past 30 years; it has been widely investigated in the field of tissue engineering applications13,14. Numerous studies have confirmed that silk fiber, a natural biomacromolecule, exhibits excellent mechanical properties, including a high breaking strength ranging from 300 to 740 MPa and a breaking strain of 4 to 26%15. Recently, vascular grafts fabricated from silk fibers using braiding techniques possess excellent mechanical properties, rapid endothelialization, and long-term patency without intimal hyperplasia16–18. In our previous study, we devised a novel silk film prepared by coating regenerated SF on the surface of silk braiding fabrics through layer-by-layer self-assembly with rotary drying, which was then used as a covering material for the construction of covered stents19. The preliminary results showed that the silk films exhibited excellent resistance to water leakage and had no cytotoxicity, suggesting potential applicability as a covering material for covered stents.

On the basis of a previous pre-study and aimed at the problem of poor compliance with commercial covering materials, we focused on studying effective improvements in compliance and compressive resistance by regulating fabric braiding parameters and self-assembly. Furthermore, we investigated the burst strength and tensile properties in detail, and analyzed the mechanical failure mechanisms of the silk films. This study provided a novel material and technique for advancing the development of vascular covered stents that matched the biomechanics of blood vessels and bioactivity.

Materials and methods

Materials

Bombyx mori raw silk (20/22D, 6 A) was purchased from Haian Tian Xin Silk Industry. Sodium carbonate (Na2CO3) was purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co. Ltd. Lithium bromide (LiBr) was purchased from Finecollection Institute of Chemical Industry. Polyethylene glycol diglycidyl ether (PEG-DE; MW500 Da) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich Co. Ltd. Silk yarn was purchased from Suzhou Huaxian Co. Ltd.

Preparation of silk films

Silk films were prepared as described previously19. In brief, raw silk was degummed by Na2CO3, and then dissolved in 9.3 M LiBr solution. The resulting SF aqueous solution was dialyzed against deionized water, concentrated to 80 mg/mL, and cross-linked using PEG-DE at a mass ratio SF: PEG-DE = 1.0:0.8. Silk tubular fabrics with various structural parameters were braided by adjusting braiding angles (25°, 65° or 90°), the number of axial yarns (40, 60 or 100 threads/10 cm along the axial direction) and silk yarn sizes (1 × 2 or 3 × 2 silk yarn). Both 1 × 2 and 3 × 2 silk yarns were degummed by Na2CO3. The braiding angle represents the angle formed by the interlacing of silk yarns within the braided structure in the radial direction. The braided silk fabric was then fully immersed in the cross-linked SF solution for 30 s and dried on a rotary drying device (50 rpm, 60 °C) for 20 min, forming an SF self-assembled layer. Finally, a tubular silk film (10 mm in diameter) was obtained after 1–6 immersing-drying cycles. The silk films were named, for example, the 1 × 2/90°/60/SF6 film, which indicated a silk yarn size of 1 × 2, a braiding angle of 90°, an axial yarn number of 60 threads/10 cm, and a self-assembly of 6 layers.

Tensile test

The tensile properties of silk films were determined as previously described16. In brief, tensile tests were performed using a tensile tester (YG065, Laizhou Electronic Instrument Co., Shandong, China). The pre-load applied was 1 N and the elongation rate was 100 mm/min. Samples 30 mm in length were cut for the radial tensile test and 60 mm in length for the axial tensile test. Young’s modulus was obtained by calculating the slope of the initial straight line portion of the stress-strain curve. Four replicate samples were analyzed for each material.

Burst strength test

Following International Organization for Standardization (ISO) 7198: 2016, a custom-made pressurized burst device was used to measure the burst strength of silk films. Samples were cut into 30 mm×30 mm square pieces, and were then attached to a tester platform, where deionized water was incrementally introduced to achieve a controlled pressure increase at a rate of 10 kPa/s until bursting. The maximal pressure of each sample was recorded and four replicate samples were analyzed for each material.

Compressive resistance test

As described previously16,20, the compressive resistance of silk films was performed using a texture analyzer (TMS-Pro, Food Technology Corporation, VA, USA). Samples (60 mm in length) were compressed by 8 mm perpendicular to the axial direction at a strain rate of 10 mm/min, after which the load flat plate returned to the initial height. Compressive force and elastic recovery rate were recorded, and the compressive modulus was obtained by calculating the slope of the initial straight line portion of the load-displacement curve. Four replicate samples were analyzed for each material.

Compliance test

Following ISO 7198: 2016, a test system was custom-made to measure the compliance of silk films. One end of the silk film was blocked, and the other end was connected to an MP2000 peristaltic pump (Rikakikai, Tokyo, Japan) to maintain a defined pressure (80 mmHg or 240 mmHg). Images of silk films were captured at the defined pressure using a CLS100 stereoscopic microscope (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany) and the outer diameters of silk films were measured by Nano Measurer 1.2 software (Fudan University, Shanghai). COMSOL simulation

To evaluate the effect of braiding parameters on the burst strength and compliance of silk films, finite element models were designed using SolidWorks 2020 (Dassault Systemes SolidWorks Corp., MA, USA) and finite element analysis was performed in COMSOL Multiphysics 5.6 (COMSOL Inc., MA, USA)21. In the simulation, the Solid Mechanics interface was selected to calculate the deformation of silk films.

Statistical analysis

All the quantitative results were presented in mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance was determined by one-way analysis of variance using SPSS software (Version 26, IBM, IL, USA). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Radial strength resistance

Covered stents are required to maintain a normal hemodynamic environment after implantation, including vasoconstriction, vasodilation, and continual hemokinesis22. The covering materials play a key role in withstanding the radial stresses generated by the blood flow. The radial tensile test mode was performed as shown in Fig. 1A. The representative stress-strain curve of a 1 × 2/90°/100/SF6 film is shown in Fig. 1B. The stress increased slowly at low strain, resembling the typical “toe region” of natural blood vessels23. This characteristic provides blood vessels with the capacity to endure repetitive blood pulses and preserve blood pressure. As the strain continued to increase, the stress rapidly rose until the silk film ruptured. Figure 1C and D show the radial tensile properties at failure of silk films, increasing the braiding angle resulted in a decline in radial breaking strength but a significant increase in breaking elongation for the silk films. The radial breaking strength of the 1 × 2/90°/100/SF6 film decreased by 32%, while the breaking elongation significantly increased by 57% compared to the 1 × 2/25°/100/SF6 film, indicating superior flexibility in the former. Under the same braiding angle, a silk film with a higher number of axial yarns had higher radial breaking strength and breaking elongation. The radial breaking strength and breaking elongation of the 1 × 2/90°/100/SF6 film were 1.9 and 1.6 times that of the 1 × 2/90°/40/SF6 film, respectively. In addition, silk yarn sizes also affected the mechanical properties of the silk films. Figure S1 shows that the 3 × 2 group slightly excelled over the 1 × 2 group in radial tensile properties. As exemplified by the 90°/60/SF6 film, the 3 × 2 group exhibited a 10% increase in breaking strength and a 61% increase in breaking elongation.

Different lowercase letters in the figure represent significant difference (p < 0.05).

Figure 1E and F show the radial tensile behavior of the 1 × 2/90°/60 silk films with varying self-assembly layers. An increase in layers significantly raised radial breaking strength, while it led to an initial increase followed by a decrease in breaking elongation. The silk film with self-assembly of two layers showed a 2.8 times increase in breaking strength compared to the single-layer self-assembly film, accompanied by a slight increase in breaking elongation. As the number of self-assembly layers increased, the silk film’s breaking strength showed a gradual increase and eventually stabilized, while the breaking elongation decreased. Upon reaching self-assembly of four layers or more, the silk film’s breaking strength exceeded 4 MPa and the breaking elongation was more than 90%, suggesting excellent deformability and blood pressure tolerance.

Any structural weakness in covering materials will cause the failure of endovascular graft exclusion. Therefore, we used the burst strength test to evaluate the homogeneity and structural integrity of silk films (Fig. 2A), in order to ensure the effectiveness for future application in vascular disease treatment. As shown in Fig. 2B, varying braiding angles and the number of axial yarns were closely related to the burst strength of silk films, which significantly increased with the increase in braiding angle and the number of axial yarns. The burst strength of the 1 × 2/90°/100/SF6 film was 2.3 times that of the 1 × 2/25°/100/SF6 film and about 4.2 times that of the 1 × 2/90°/40/SF6 film. Similar to the radial tensile properties, the burst strength of the 3 × 2 group surpassed that of the 1 × 2 group (Fig. S2). As shown in Fig. 2C, the burst strength of the silk films exhibited a significant increase with the addition of self-assembly layers. When self-assembled with two layers, the 1 × 2/90°/100/SF2 film exhibited excellent burst strength (737 ± 177 mmHg). With the self-assembly of six layers, the burst strength increased 2.9 times compared to that with two layers. After the self-assembly of four layers, the burst strength of the films surpassed the current clinical gold standard (saphenous vein, 1599 ± 877 mmHg)24.

Axial tensile properties

The axial tensile properties of silk films reflect their capacity to respond to the shear stress generated by blood flow, which plays an important role in influencing the morphology and function of endothelial cells16. According to the Fig. 3A test mode, the impact of fabric structural parameters on the axial tensile properties of silk films was investigated (Fig. 3B and C). In contrast to radial tensile, as the braiding angle increased, the breaking strength of silk films tended to improve, while the breaking elongation declined sharply. The 1 × 2/90°/100/SF6 film exhibited a breaking strength of 11.3 MPa and was approximately 4.8 times that of the 1 × 2/25°/100/SF6 film. However, the breaking elongation of the 1 × 2/90°/100/SF6 film was significantly lower than that of the 1 × 2/25°/100/SF6 film. On the other hand, a higher number of axial yarns resulted in a higher breaking strength and a slightly lower breaking elongation. The breaking strength of the 1 × 2/90°/100/SF6 film was approximately 1.6 times that of the 1 × 2/90°/40/SF6 film, while the breaking elongation slightly decreased by15%. Moreover, the axial tensile properties improved with the increase in the number of silk yarn sizes (Fig. S3). Figure 3D and E show the impact of varying self-assembly layers on the axial tensile properties of silk films. As the number of self-assembly layers increased from one to six, the results showed an 11% increase in breaking strength and a 37% decrease in breaking elongation.

Compressive resistance

During interventional therapy, the stent is inserted into a catheter and delivered to the target lesion. The flexibility of a covered stent is very important, as it must be radially compressed into a smaller size to fit into the catheter4. We investigated the influence of fabric structural parameters and self-assembly layers on the flexibility of silk films using in vitro compressive resistance tests (Fig. 4A). As shown in Fig. 4B, compressive resistance increased with a larger braiding angle and a higher number of axial yarns. The compressive force of the 1 × 2/90°/100/SF6 film was 2.1 times that of the 1 × 2/25°/100/SF6 film and 2.5 times higher than that of the 1 × 2/90°/40/SF6 film. The silk yarn size had a significant impact on compressive resistance (Fig. S4). The compressive force of the 3 × 2 group was more than twice as high as that of the 1 × 2 group. Moreover, all silk films prepared with various fabric parameters showed a recovery rate of around 80%, indicating excellent elasticity without any risk of rupture (Fig. 4C). Figure 4D shows the compressive resistance of the silk films at various self-assembly layer numbers. With the increase in the number of self-assembly layers, the compressive force gradually increased, while the elastic recovery rate showed no significant change (Fig. 4E).

Compliance

Compliance refers to the ability of submissiveness to changes in blood pressure25. Hence, we evaluated the compliance by measuring the lumen’s deformation at various pressures, and investigated the effect of various fabric structural parameters on the compliance of silk films. As shown in Fig. 5A, the compliance increased significantly with an increase in braiding angle. Compliance of the 1 × 2/90°/100/SF6 film was 3.8 times higher than that of the 1 × 2/25°/100/SF6 film. It was noted that the films with a lower number of axial yarns and a finer silk yarn size tended to improve compliance. The 1 × 2/90°/40/SF6 film exhibited a higher compliance which was 2.5 times that of the 1 × 2/90°/100/SF6 film and 3.1 times higher compared to the 3 × 2/90°/40/SF6 film (Fig. S5). Furthermore, the compliance decreased significantly with an increase in SF self-assembly layers (Fig. 5B).

Mechanism and discussion

Since the first clinical application in the 1990s, covered stents have effectively reduced the risk of thrombus formation and restenosis, which are caused by the uneven structure of a bare stent. Moreover, covered stents have shown marked efficacy in treating aneurysms and dissections, as well as in sealing vessel perforations and ruptures26. However, postoperative re-intervention rates for covered stents remain relatively high. Due to the mismatch of compliance and biologically inert surfaces, the commercially available covered stents trigger inflammatory responses, and this pathology may be further exacerbated, which again leads to embolism27. Hence, there is an urgent need for a material with bioactivity and flexibility to revolutionize the treatment of vascular disease, addressing the clinical issues that have arisen since the application of covered stents. In our work, we designed an SF-based covering material and optimized the mechanical strength and compliance, in order to develop a covered stent with excellent comprehensive mechanical properties. Furthermore, we revealed the mechanical failure mechanism of the silk film.

The covering material was composed of two components: the SF film and the silk braiding fabric, with the silk braiding fabric embedded within the SF film to provide mechanical reinforcement. Due to the braiding process, the silk yarns follow helical paths interlacing with each other, and there is a clear correlation between the interlacing angle and the arrangement of silk yarns, resulting in changes of stress decomposition with different braiding angles. The tensile test results showed that when the braiding angle increased, the radial breaking strength decreased while the axial force increased, and the radial elongation increased while the axial elongation decreased. The ultimate force of silk films depends on the breaking force of the silk yarns. As shown in Fig. 6A, the force f on the silk yarn can be decomposed into a force fr in the radial direction and a force fa in the axial direction. When the silk yarn is stretched to break, the ultimate radial force Fr and the ultimate axial force Fa on the silk film can be expressed by the following equation:

where Fr is the ultimate radial tensile force (N), n is the number of axial yarns (threads/10 cm), l is the length of film (cm), fr is the component force along the radial direction (N), f is the ultimate force on the silk yarn (N), α is the braiding angle (°), Fa is the ultimate axial tensile force (N), D is the diameter of film (cm), and fa is the component force along the axial direction (N).

According to this equation, an increase in the braiding angle α results in a decrease in the ultimate radial force Fr and an increase in the ultimate axial force Fa, leading to a reduction in radial breaking strength and an increase in axial breaking strength. At the minimum braiding angle of 25°, the silk yarns tended to align in the radial direction. As a result, the radial deformation of the silk film was limited. When stretched along the radial direction, both the silk yarns and the SF film ruptured at the same time (Fig. 6B); however, under the axial force, there was a separation between the silk yarns and the SF film at first, then the silk yarns slipped drastically before breaking, resulting in a significant increase in breaking elongation (Fig. 6C). As the braiding angle α increased, the SF film broke before the silk yarns under radial force, and the slippage of silk yarns gradually increased, resulting in an increase in radial breaking elongation (Fig. 6D); the deformation was gradually limited under axial force, leading to the breaking of silk yarns and the SF film at the same time during axial stretching (Fig. 6E). Based on the above equation, it can also be observed that as the number of axial yarns n increases, both Fr and Fa increase, resulting in an increase in the breaking strength. A higher number of axial yarns had a tighter arrangement of silk yarns, delaying the occurrence of breaking and resulting in a higher breaking elongation. In particular, there was a more significant increase in radial breaking elongation, due to the slippage of silk yarns which formed easily at a large braiding angle.

In addition to the need for adequate tensile properties to maintain hemodynamic stability, covered stents should also have the ability to isolate blood flow and withstand acute hypertension caused by pathological or physiological stimuli. During the burst testing, each part within the silk film’s lumen withstood uniform normal forces acting perpendicular to its surface, resulting in overall expansion until bursting (Fig. 7A). Due to the uneven structure, this will cause rupture in areas with large strain or weak structures. Regarding the braiding angle, the distance between neighboring interlacing points in the radial direction is longer than that in the axial direction at 25°. This difference resulted in larger deformation in the radial direction (Fig. 7B), causing the formation of radial cracks (Fig. 7C). In contrast, when the braiding angle was 90°, the distance was equal in both the axial and radial directions, leading to a uniform stress distribution. Thus, the silk film exhibited a higher burst strength, and showed a small point in the center of the SF film when it burst.

Burst mechanism of silk films. (A) Surface morphology, (B) simulation of deformation distribution under burst pressure, and (C) burst behavior.

The effect of braiding parameters on the burst strength also depends on the number of axial yarns. A higher number of axial yarns increased the mechanical reinforcement points, improving the burst strength. Regarding the relationship between the number of self-assembled layers and burst strength, with fewer self-assembly layers, the SF film was inadequate to entirely cover the braiding fabric. There was a significant height difference between the silk yarns, especially at the interlacing points, and the SF film interface, resulting in a weak binding force between the SF film and silk yarns at the pennation angle area. These weak structures were leading factors for bursting under pressure (Fig. 7A). As the number of self-assembly layers increased, the thickness of the SF film also increased and the braiding fabric was eventually embedded in the SF film, eliminating the weak structures and reducing the uneven stress distribution, resulting in a significant increase in burst strength.

Unmatched compliance may hinder the covered stent from adhering closely to the inner wall of vessels, leading to its migration, and may cause hemodynamic fluctuations and induce the aggregation of vascular cells, thereby increasing the risk of in-stent restenosis4,28,29. Figure 8A shows the simulation results for the deformation of silk films with varying fabric structural parameters. When the braiding angle was 25°, the silk yarns tended to align in the radial direction, limiting the silk film’s radial deformation, and resulting in an uneven expansion and a bump surface under pressure. When the braiding angle was increased to 90°, the silk yarns interlaced uniformly in the radial and axial directions, leading to a larger deformation space in the radial direction. Thus, the deformability was improved and the surface of the silk film was flat under pressure. With increasing pressure, the difference in deformation was more significant between these two groups of silk films. With a lower number of axial yarns, more bulges formed on the film surfaces under pressure, due to the larger area of the protein film lacking the support of silk yarns. This can interfere with stable blood flow, causing the formation of turbulent flow. A higher number of axial yarns improved the uneven surface with bulges, but led to a decrease in compliance. The simulations of deformation were consistent with the morphologic changes of silk films at a pressure of 240 mmHg (Fig. 8B). The compliance results (Fig. 8C), calculated according to the simulation of deformation under different pressures, were also consistent with the experimental values (Fig. 5A). In addition, the number of self-assembled layers is an important factor affecting the compliance of silk films. As the self-assembly layers increased, the SF film gradually thickened and covered the silk yarns and interlacing points, which limited the deformation of the braiding fabric and reduced the compliance (Fig. 5B).

The radial Young’s modulus and compressive modulus results also reflected the compliance of the silk films, which affected the constriction and expansion of the covered stents. According to the tension curve (Fig. S6) and the compressive curve (Fig. S7), a higher braiding angle showed lower radial Young’s modulus and compressive modulus, and a low number of axial yarns showed lower radial Young’s modulus and compressive modulus (Fig. 8D, E). These results were consistent with the compliance results.

In addition, we also observed a relationship between the silk yarn sizes and the mechanical properties of the silk film. In the silk film preparation process, as the silk braiding fabric was immersed, the SF solution permeated the inside of the silk yarns, leading to cohesion between silk fibers. Due to the higher number of fibers and surface area in the 3 × 2 silk yarn compared to the 1 × 2 silk yarn, there was stronger cohesion within the 3 × 2 silk yarn, thereby improving the mechanical strength of the silk film. On the other hand, it limited the flexibility of the silk film.

According to reports, the commercially available covered stents possess excellent mechanical strength, but their compliance cannot match that of native blood vessels. Commercially available PET covered stents showed a low compliance of 0.1–0.2%/100 mmHg, and commercial expanded PETF covered stents had a compliance of 1.7–2.0%/100 mmHg30. The covering materials developed herein, such as the 1 × 2/90°/60/SF6 film, showed excellent compliance (2.6%/100 mmHg), which matched the compliance of the clinical gold standard (human saphenous veins, 0.7–2.6%/100 mmHg)31. Moreover, the 1 × 2/90°/60/SF6 film also showed sufficient tensile strength and burst strength, qualifying it to maintain normal blood flow.

Conclusions

In the work, we designed a novel SF-based covering material and optimized its mechanical properties. Through the regulation of braiding parameters and the number of self-assembled layers, the 1 × 2/90°/60/SF6 film exhibited excellent mechanical properties. Its compliance was significantly higher than that of commercial synthetic materials and matched the compliance of human saphenous veins, making it a promising candidate for the covering materials of vascular stents. In our future work, we will concentrate on its combination with metal support and evaluate the implantation feasibility in animal experiments.
