Id1 promotes clonal hematopoiesis in mice with Tet2 loss of function
Authors: Shweta Singh, Kristbjorn O. Gudmundsson, Tanmoy Sarkar, Brad L. Jakubison, Holly M. Morris, Sandra Burkett, Karim Baktiar, Gary T. Pauly, Dina M. Sigano, Joel P. Schneider, Lino Tessarollo, Jonathan R. Keller
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Hematopoietic malignancies emerge through the acquisition of genetic mutations within hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs). Some mutations impart a selective growth advantage to HSPCs, which expand and contribute to mature blood cells. This expansion is termed clonal hematopoiesis (CH). Inhibitor of DNA binding 1 (ID1) protein is a transcriptional regulator of proliferation/differentiation of hematopoietic cells. HSPCs express low levels of Id1 that is induced by growth factors and other mediators of inflammatory during stress to promote HSPC expansion. Since chronic inflammation is associated with the progression of hematopoietic malignancies, reducing Id1 expression may reduce CH. Genetic ablation of Id1 in Tet2−/− HSPCs reduces HSPC expansion/CH, extramedullary hematopoiesis, myeloid skewing, and genetic instability and delays the onset of disease. Mechanistically, p16 expression, senescence, and apoptosis were increased, and proliferation decreased in Tet2−/−;Id1−/− HSPCs. Thus, ID1 may represent a therapeutic target to reduce CH and delay the onset of disease.

INTRODUCTION

Hematopoietic malignancies arise, in part, from the accumulation of somatic mutations in HSPCs as individual age (1–3). Mutations that occur early in disease progression can impart an increased fitness/survival advantage to HSPCs, which allows them to expand relative to normal HSPCs. Consequently, during the early preleukemic stages of hematopoietic malignancies, the frequency of mature blood cells with these founding mutations is increased in patients without clinical signs of hematologic dysfunction, a condition known as clonal hematopoiesis (CH) (4–6). Patients with CH have a roughly 10-fold increased frequency of progressing to hematopoietic malignancies via accumulation of additional mutations during aging, indicating the need for therapeutic interventions during this early preleukemic phase of the malignancy (1, 4, 7). Loss-of-function mutations in the epigenetic modifying enzyme, ten-eleven translocation 2 (TET2), are frequently observed in patients with CH, myeloproliferative neoplasms (MPN), chronic myelomonocytic leukemia (CMML), systemic mastocytosis, myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS), and acute myeloid leukemia (AML) (8–13). Mice with Tet2 loss of function (Tet2−/−) show CH that progresses to myeloid and lymphoid hyperplasia and leukemia in some mice after a long latency (14–22). Tet2−/− mice are characterized by hyperinflammatory environments with increased production of proinflammatory molecules including interleukin-6 (IL-6), IL-1, tumor necrosis factor–α, and others, which contribute to HSPC expansion and atherosclerosis (21, 23–28). Inhibition of inflammatory cytokines, their receptors, and downstream signaling proteins reduces HSPC expansion and CH in Tet2−/− mice, suggesting that cytokines drive preleukemic HSPC expansion (23, 27, 29, 30). Chronic inflammation has been implicated in promoting the progression of myeloid malignancies and suggests that reducing inflammation could slow the progression of hematopoietic malignancies (31–35).

Inhibitors of DNA binding proteins (ID1-4) are helix-loop-helix transcription factors that regulate the proliferation and differentiation of neuronal, muscle, endothelial, and hematopoietic cells in other tissues and are frequently deregulated in cancer (36–38). The founding member of this family, ID1, is expressed at low levels in HSCs and is induced by hematopoietic growth factors (HGFs) that promote myeloid proliferation and differentiation including IL-3 and granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (39, 40). Enforced expression of Id1 in HSPCs enhances HGF-induced proliferation, immortalizes hematopoietic progenitor cells (HPCs) in vitro, and promotes a myeloproliferative disease after a long latency in vivo (41, 42). Id1 is induced in HSPCs during bone marrow transplantation (BMT), genotoxic and inflammatory stress, and aging in vivo (43). Since Id1 is induced in HSPCs by proinflammatory cytokines in vitro and in vivo and during BMT and inflammatory stress and promotes HSPC proliferation, we reasoned that ID1 may represent a potential therapeutic target to reduce CH (39, 41, 44). We show here that ablation of Id1 expression in Tet2−/− mice (i) reduces expansion/self-renewal of HSPCs, (ii) reduces extramedullary hematopoiesis, (iii) reduces myeloid skewing, (iv) reduces genomic instability, and (v) delays the onset of hematopoietic malignancy. Thus, inhibiting ID1 in patients with CH and chronic inflammation could be therapeutic and reduce HSPC expansion, MPN, and the onset of hematopoietic disease.

RESULTS

Id1 expression is increased in TET2−/− HSPCs and intrinsically promotes the expansion of hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells

Analysis of previously published RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) datasets demonstrate that Id1 RNA is increased in murine Tet2−/− HSPCs compared to Tet2+/+ controls (fig. S1A) (20, 45). To confirm that Id1 expression is increased in Tet2−/− HSPCs in vivo, we crossed Tet2−/− mice with a Id1-GFP reporter mouse model and found that ID1–green fluorescent protein (GFP) expression is increased in HSCs, multipotent progenitors (MPPs), and HPCs in Tet2−/−;Id1GFP/+ mice (Fig. 1A) (46). We show that Id1 mRNA is up-regulated in donor Tet2−/− HPCs from competitively transplanted recipients and validate that Id1 is ablated in Tet2−/−;Id1−/− HPCs (Fig. 1, B and C). Furthermore, we show that inflammatory cytokines induce ID1 (ID1-GFP) in Tet2+/+ and Tet2−/− HSPCs in vitro (Fig. 1, D and E). While gene expression datasets of HSPCs from patients with CH and TET2 mutations are currently limiting, ID1 expression is increased in AML patient samples with TET2 mutations and in CD34+ HSPCs from patients with MDS and CMML and TET2 mutations compared to controls (Fig. 1, F to H) (47, 48). Collectively, these data demonstrate that Id1 expression is increased in murine Tet2−/− HSPCs and in AML, MDS, and CMML patient samples with TET2 mutations.

To investigate if Id1 intrinsically contributes to CH, we evaluated hematopoietic development in competitive repopulation assays to rule out any contribution that loss of Tet2 function might have in the hematopoietic microenvironment. Furthermore, γ-irradiation (γ-IR) conditioning for BMT induces proinflammation that promotes murine HSPC expansion, and CH is increased in human donor HSPCs after allogeneic BMT (49–51). Tet2−/− BMCs have a competitive repopulation advantage in vivo (15, 17, 19); therefore, we transplanted equal numbers of Tet2+/+;Id1+/+(WT), Tet2+/+;Id1−/− (Id1−/−), Tet2−/−;Id1+/+ (Tet2−/−), and Tet2−/−;Id1−/− donor BMCs with host BMCs to evaluate if reducing Id1 levels in Tet2−/− BMCs could affect donor reconstitution (Fig. 2A). As expected, we observed an increase in the total number of donor-derived (CD-45.2+) Tet2−/− BMCs in primary recipient mice 12 to 16 weeks after BMT compared to mice transplanted with Tet2+/+ and Id1−/− BMCs (Fig. 2B). Tet2−/−;Id1−/− BMCs showed reduced donor BM repopulation, indicating that Id1 is intrinsically required for the enhanced repopulation and expansion of Tet2−/− BMCs. Immunophenotype analysis of Tet2−/− repopulating cells revealed increased numbers of donor-derived lineage-negative, Sca-1–positive, c-Kit–positive (LSK) cells and LSK/Flk2-neg (Flk2−) cells that are enriched for primitive HSPCs including HSCs, short-term HSC (STHSCs), and MPPs and LSK/Flk2+ (MPP-4) cells that are enriched for lymphoid repopulation compared to Tet2+/+ and Id1−/− repopulating BMCs (Fig. 2C and fig. S1B). In addition, primitive HSCs, STHSCs, and MPP-2/MPP-3s (enriched for MPPs with myeloid/erythroid repopulation) were increased in Tet2−/− repopulating BMCs compared to controls (Fig. 2D and fig. S1B). These HSPCs were significantly reduced and rescued in mice transplanted with Tet2−/−;Id1−/− donor BMCs demonstrating that Id1 is required for the expansion of immunophenotypic Tet2−/− HSPCs. Last, donor-derived Lin−/c-Kit+ (LK) and committed progenitors [common myeloid progenitors (CMP) and granulocyte/macrophage progenitors (GMP)] were increased in the bone marrow (BM) of Tet2−/− chimeric mice and reduced and rescued in the BM of Tet2−/−;Id1−/− chimeric mice (Fig. 2, E and F).

Tet2−/− mice show extramedullary hematopoiesis (15, 17, 19); therefore, we analyzed donor hematopoietic repopulation in spleens and confirmed that mice competitively transplanted with Tet2−/− BMCs show increased size, weight, and total spleen cell numbers compared to controls (Fig. 2G and fig. S1, C and D), which were reduced and rescued in mice transplanted with Tet2−/−;Id1−/− BMCs. Increased numbers of donor (fig. S1E) and donor-derived LK and CMP and GMP cells were observed in the spleens of mice transplanted with Tet2−/− (Fig. 2, H and I, and fig. S2A), which were reduced and rescued in mice transplanted with Tet2−/−;Id1−/− donor BMCs, demonstrating that Id1 intrinsically promotes extramedullary hematopoiesis in Tet2−/− chimeric mice.

To confirm that Id1 contributes to CH in Tet2−/− mice, we used an experimental model of CH (23), where host cells were competitively transplanted in fivefold excess to donor-derived experimental cells into γ-IR recipient mice (Fig. 2J). The frequency of donor Tet2−/− BMC reconstitution (donor/host ratio) increased 3- to 4-fold in peripheral blood cells (PBCs) over time, while the frequency of Tet2+/+ and Id1−/− BMC reconstitution was less than 0.5-fold over the 16 weeks of analysis (Fig. 2K). In comparison, transplantation of Tet2−/− HSPCs that lack Id1 expression showed significantly reduced repopulation potential (1.5-fold) compared to Tet2−/− BMCs, indicating that Id1 contributes to CH of preleukemic Tet2−/− HSPCs.

Ablation of Id1 in Tet2−/− HSPCs reduces myeloid skewing in vitro and in vivo

To investigate if Id1 intrinsically regulates myeloid skewing, we evaluated lineage development in noncompetitively transplanted mice with Tet2+/+, Id1−/−, Tet2−/− and Tet2−/−;Id1−/− BMCs (fig. S2B). CBC analysis of PBCs 12 to 16 weeks after transplantation of Tet2−/− BMCs showed elevated levels of white blood cells, neutrophils, and monocytes (fig. S2C), which were reduced and rescued in mice transplanted with Tet2−/−;Id1−/− BMCs. Similarly, in competitively transplanted mice, donor-derived BM neutrophils, monocytes, macrophages, and dendritic cells were increased in mice transplanted with Tet2−/− BMCs (figs. S2D and S3A), while donor CD19+ B cells and Ter-119+ erythroid cells were decreased (fig. S3B). Mice transplanted with Tet2−/−;Id1−/− BMCs showed a decrease and rescue of all myeloid lineage cells in the BM (fig. S3A), while CD19+ B cells and erythroid cells were increased (fig. S3B). Furthermore, mice transplanted with Tet2−/− BMCs showed increased donor myeloid cell repopulation in the spleen, and myeloid skewing in the spleen was reduced and rescued in mice transplanted with Tet2−/−;Id1−/− BMCs (fig. S3C). Last, Tet2−/− Lin− BMCs showed increased numbers of differentiating monocytes and macrophages after 6 days of culture compared to control Lin− BMCs but was reduced and rescued in Tet2−/−;Id1−/− Lin− cells (fig. S4, A and B). Collectively, these results suggest that Id1 intrinsically promotes myeloid development and skewing in Tet2−/− mice. In agreement with this conclusion, forced expression of Id1 in normal HSPCs promotes myeloid and inhibits lymphoid development in vitro and in vivo (39, 40).

Myeloid skewing in Tet2−/− mice is correlated with an increase in committed myeloid progenitors. We show that donor-derived LK and committed progenitors (CMP and GMP) are increased in BM (Fig. 2, E and F) and spleen (Fig. 2, H and I) of Tet2−/− chimeric mice and reduced and rescued in the BM and spleen of Tet2−/−;Id1−/− chimeric mice. This suggests that Id1 contributes to myeloid skewing in Tet2−/− mice, in part by promoting myeloid progenitor cell expansion.

Id1 promotes progenitor expansion and self-renewal of Tet2−/− HSPCs by inhibiting cell differentiation

To confirm that Id1 is critical for the expansion of functional Tet2−/− HSPCs, we examined HSPC function in colony formation assays (colony forming unit-culture, CFU-c), self-renewal, and single-cell growth assays (Fig. 3A). BMCs from Tet2−/− chimeric mice showed increased colony formation compared to control mice, and the number of colonies was decreased and rescued in Tet2−/−;Id1−/− BMCs (Fig. 3B). Thus, Id1 is required for the expansion of functional HSPCs in Tet2−/− chimeric mice in vivo.

Tet2−/− HSPCs have enhanced self-renewal potential in vitro (17); therefore, we evaluated the replating potential of 5 × 103 cells from CFU-c plates initiated from 250 fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS)–sorted HSCs (Fig. 3A). As predicted, Tet2−/− BMCs showed extensive replating potential in CFU-c assays, while control BMCs showed limited replating potential that was significantly reduced after the third and fourth replating since these cells differentiate with little self-renewal potential in CFU-c assays. Tet2−/− BMCs that lack Id1 lost serial replating potential, indicating that Id1 is required for the self-renewal of Tet2−/− BMCs (Fig. 3C). We found that 90 to 95% of the cells in Tet2−/− colonies harvested from the fourth serial replating were Lin−/c-kit+ (LK-like), while 40 to 45% of the cells in Tet2−/−;Id1−/− colonies were LK-like (Fig. 3D). Similarly, the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of c-Kit expression was decreased on cells in Tet2−/−;Id1−/− colonies compared to cells in Tet2−/− colonies (Fig. 3D). Further analysis revealed that cells in Tet2−/−;Id1−/− colonies showed increased numbers of differentiated Lin+ cells compared to cells Tet2−/− colonies, suggesting that Tet2−/− HSPCs had undergone increased differentiation in the absence of Id1 (Fig. 3E). Analysis of cells from early passage WT and Id1−/− colonies confirms that these cells have differentiated compared to cells from Tet2−/− and Tet2−/−;Id1−/− colonies (fig. S4C). Thus, ID1 may contribute to the self-renewal of Tet2−/− cells by preventing their differentiation. In agreement with these observations, enforced expression of Id1 in normal HSPCs promotes proliferation, prevents differentiation, and immortalizes SCF-dependent LK-like HSPCs in vitro, which further differentiate in response to HGFs in vitro and in vivo (41).

The requirement for Id1 in the expansion of Tet2−/− HSPCs was confirmed by direct examination of single-cell growth in Terasaki plates (20 μl per well) by bright-field examination. Single Tet2−/− LK cells showed a significant increase in wells with cell growth covering >25% of the well and a reduction in wells with cell growth covering <25% of the well compared to controls, indicating that Tet2−/− LK cells have increased growth potential (Fig. 3F). Ablation of Id1 in Tet2−/− LK cells reduced and rescued cell growth resulting in a decrease in wells with cell growth >25% and increase in wells with cell growth <25% of the well. Similar results were observed in single-cell assays with Tet2−/− HSCs that have a higher growth potential overall in this assay (fig. S4D). Thus, Id1 promotes the expansion of Tet2−/− HSPCs in vitro.

Id1 inhibits apoptosis and senescence of Tet2−/− HSPCs

To understand how Id1 contributes to HSPC expansion, we examined cell cycling, proliferation, apoptosis, and senescence of HSPCs in Tet2−/− chimeric mice. Tet2−/− MPP BMCs in chimeric mice showed increased bromo deoxyuridine (BrdU) incorporation compared to control cells that was reduced and rescued in Tet2−/−;Id1−/− MPPs, while Tet2−/− HSCs/STHSCs from the same mice did not show increased BrdU incorporation compared to controls (fig. S5, A and B). Splenic Tet2−/− HSCs/STHSCs/MPP2s showed significantly increased BrdU incorporation compared to controls, and loss of Id1 expression reduced and rescued the proliferation of spleen HSPCs (fig. S5C). Overall, ID1 promotes the expansion of Tet2−/− HSPCs, in part, by regulating cell proliferation.

Analysis of apoptosis in Tet2−/− BMCs mice showed a decrease in annexin+ apoptotic and increased numbers of live HSCs, STHSCs, and MPPs compared to controls (Fig. 4, A and B). Loss of Id1 expression in Tet2−/− HSPCs resulted in increased apoptosis and reduced the number of live cells. These results were confirmed in Lin− expansion assays in vitro, where we can evaluate loss of Id1 function in STHSCs and MMPs that survive in these cultures (fig. S6A). Tet2−/− MPPs showed reduced apoptosis and increased viability compared to controls, while Tet2−/−;Id1−/− MPPs showed increased apoptosis and decreased survival (Fig. 4, C to E). Tunel assays confirmed that Tet2−/− LK cells from chimeric mice show reduced apoptosis compared to control cells, while apoptosis was rescued and increased in Tet2−/−;Id1−/− LK cells (Fig. 4, F to H). Reduced levels of apoptosis have been observed in Tet2−/− Kit+ progenitors and in Tet2−/− HSPCs in studies examining the mechanistic role of IL-6 in CH (16, 23). Together, these results suggest that Id1 contributes to the expansion of Tet2−/− BM HSPCs by inhibiting apoptosis and promoting cell survival.

ID1 inhibits cell senescence in other cell types (52–54); therefore, we evaluated senescence in Tet2−/−chimeric mice (Fig. 4, I and J). We found that Tet2−/− LK BMCs showed a significant decrease in the expression of senescence-associated marker, β-galactosidase, compared to control mice, which was increased and rescued in Tet2−/−;Id1−/− LK progenitors. Together, these results suggest that Id1 intrinsically promotes expansion of Tet2−/− HSPCs and contributes to CH, in part by inhibiting apoptosis and senescence of HSPCs.

Ten-eleven translocation (TET) proteins catalyze the conversion of 5-methylcytosine to 5-hydroxymethylcytosine (5hmC) that leads to DNA demethylation and were determined by previously published methods (55–58). The levels of 5hmC levels are reduced in Tet2−/− HSPCs and contribute to altered gene expression and multiple phenotypes observed in mice that lack Tet2−/−. As expected, we found that 5hmC levels are reduced in Tet2−/− LK cells compared to controls; however, ablation of Id1 did not rescue 5hmC levels (fig. S6, B and C). Thus, Id1 expression does not affect the levels of 5hmC in Tet2−/− HPCs, which could contribute to the hematopoietic phenotypes observed in Tet2−/− mice.

Id1 reduces p16 expression in Tet2−/− chimeric bone marrow cells that results in reduced apoptosis and senescence

To gain mechanistic insight into how ID1 promotes CH in Tet2−/− chimeric mice, we first evaluated the expression of canonical ID1 targets known to regulate apoptosis, senescence, and proliferation and then performed bulk RNA-seq of Tet2−/−, Tet2−/−;Id1−/− and control LK progenitors. ID proteins regulate cell growth, in part by restraining the transcriptional activity of E and ETS proteins and expression of their downstream targets including cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors (Cdki) (38, 43, 53, 59–61); therefore, we evaluated p16, p21, p27, and p57 expression in HSPCs by flow cytometry. The expression of p16 was significantly down-regulated in Tet2−/− donor LK, LSK, Flk2−, and MPP cells compared to controls, while p16 expression was increased and rescued in Tet2−/−;Id1−/− HSPCs (frequency and MFI − LK/LSK) (Fig. 5, A to E). Additional analysis of CIP/KIP proteins showed that expression of p57 was decreased in Tet2−/− LSK, LSK/Flk2−, MPP-4, STHSCs, and MPP3 cells and was increased and rescued in Tet2−/−;Id1−/− LK, LSK/Flk2−, and MPP progenitors (fig. S7, A to C). Last, p21 and p27 expression was down-regulated in Tet2−/− HSCs and MPPs compared to control mice and increased and rescued in Tet2−/− HSCs and MPPs that lack Id1 (fig. S7, D and E). Thus, Id1 may contribute to the expansion of Tet2−/− HSPCs and CH by inhibiting p16 and p57 and other CIP/KIP family members.

To support the notion that ID1 promotes the expansion of Tet2−/− HSPCs by inhibiting p16 expression and reducing apoptosis and senescence, we knocked down (KD) Id1 expression in Tet2−/− Lin− cells using adenoviral vectors that express shId1 and shScr control (CTL) sequences and GFP and evaluated apoptosis, senescence, and p16 expression in vitro by flow cytometry (Fig. 5F). KD of Id1 in Tet2−/− Lin− expansion cultures resulted in a significant increase in apoptosis of STHSCs and MPPs after 6 days (Fig. 5, G and H) and increased the MFI and percentage of senescent STHSCs and MPP3s using a β-gal–sensitive fluorescent probe, DDAOG (Fig. 5, I to K). In addition, KD of Id1 in Tet2−/− Lin− cells increased the expression (frequency and MFI) of p16 in HSCs, STHSCs, and MPP3 by flow cytometry (Fig. 5, L to N). Thus, reducing Id1 expression in Tet2−/− HSPC BMCs increases p16 expression, apoptosis, and senescence of HSPCs.

To establish that p16 expression regulates apoptosis and senescence of Tet2−/− HSPCs, we KD p16 expression in Tet2−/−;Id1−/− Lin− cells using lentiviral vectors that express shp16 or shScr (Fig. 6A). The expression of p16 was significantly reduced in p16 KD Lin− cells (Fig. 6B), which resulted in a significant decrease in the percentage of early apoptotic Lin− cells (Fig. 6C) and C12FDG+ senescent STHSCs and MPP3 cells by flow cytometry (Fig. 6D). Together, p16 promotes apoptosis and senescence of Tet2−/−;Id1−/− Lin− cells and suggests that ID1 inhibits apoptosis and senescence of Tet2−/− cells by inhibiting p16 expression.

To gain further mechanistic insights into how ID1 promotes CH in Tet2−/− mice, we analyzed differentially expressed genes in LK RNA-seq datasets by Ingenuity Pathway and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) (table S1 and figs. S7F and S8, A and B). As predicted, ID1 signaling pathway genes were up-regulated in Tet2−/− compared to Tet2−/−;Id1−/− LK cells. Extracellular signal–regulated kinase/mitogen-activated protein kinase, IL-8/IL-1/IL-6, chemokine, and mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) signaling pathway genes were up-regulated in Tet2−/− LK compared to Tet2−/−;Id1−/− LK cells, indicating that the Tet2−/− LK cells were exposed to an inflammatory environment that was rescued by loss of Id1, which is likely due to a reduction in the number of proinflammatory cells. p53 pathway and apoptosis pathway genes and cell cycle checkpoint/G2/M phase/kinetochore pathway genes were decreased in Tet2−/− LK cells compared to Tet2−/−;Id1−/− LK cells, which could contribute to decreased genomic stability in Tet2−/− mice. Last, ingenuity pathway analysis (IPA) showed that RHO guanosine triphosphatase (GTPase) cycle genes were significantly up-regulated in Tet2−/− LK compared to Tet2−/−;Id1−/− LK cells, which suggest an unexplored mechanism of Id1 action in promoting cell survival, proliferation, and self-renewal (62). GSEA analysis confirmed the IPA analysis and found that Tet2−/−; Id1−/− LK cells show (i) decreased inflammatory gene expression, (ii) decreased mTOR signaling pathway genes, and (iii) increased DNA repair, chromosome separation, and G2M checkpoint gene expression compared to Tet2−/− LK cells (fig. S9, A to D). Together, IPA and GSEA pathway analysis confirms that (i) inflammatory signaling pathways are down-regulated in Tet2−/−;Id1−/− LK cells, (ii) that Id1 may regulate the p53 pathway and contribute to the decreased apoptosis and genomic instability of Tet2−/− LK cells, and (iii) Id1 may promote CH by up-regulating RHO GTPase signaling and function. Last, since complete rescue of Tet2−/− phenotypes was not observed in p16 short hairpin RNA (shRNA) rescue experiments, additional mechanism(s) including other epigenetic modifications could compensate for the loss of 5hmC in the absence of Id1.

Ablation of Id1 rescues chromosomal instability of Tet2−/− BMCs

TET proteins regulate DNA damage response pathway protein expression, and cells with loss of TET function show increased DNA strand breaks and genomic instability and develop myeloid malignancies (20, 63–66). Therefore, we cultured Tet2−/− and Tet2−/−;Id1−/− BMCs in HGFs in vitro and analyzed mitotic cells for chromosomal aberrations (Fig. 6E). There was a significant increase in chromosomes with gaps, breaks, arm loss, and translocations in Tet2−/− BMCs compared to controls (Fig. 6, F and G). Chromosomal aberrations were reduced and rescued in Tet2−/−;Id1−/− BMCs in three separate experiments, demonstrating that ablation of Id1 reduces genomic instability of Tet2−/− BMCs. Since TET1 regulates the expression of DNA repair genes including BRCA1, RAD51, and 53BP1, and loss of TET1/2 leads to genomic instability, we evaluated RAD51 and 53BP1 expression in Tet2−/− chimeric mice by flow cytometry (fig. S10A). The expression of RAD51 and 53BP1 was significantly reduced in Tet2−/− HSPCs compared to controls and was increased and rescued in Tet2−/−; Id1−/− HSPCs (fig. S10, B to E). Thus, loss of Id1 expression results in increased DNA repair protein expression that is correlated with increased genomic stability in Tet2−/− HSPCs.

Since Tet2−/− BMCs show reduced apoptosis (Fig. 4, A to E), increased genomic instability (Fig. 6, E to G), reduced expression of p53 pathway signaling genes (fig. S9B), and ID1 down-regulates p53 expression in other cell types (67, 68), we evaluated p53 and p19 expression in HSPCs from chimeric mice (Fig. 7A). The expression of p53 was reduced in Tet2−/− LK, LSK, STHSC, and MPP cells compared to control cells (Fig. 7B and fig. S10, F and G); however, p53 expression was selectively increased and rescued in Tet2−;Id1−/− LK cells but not in LSK cells. The expression of p19, a positive regulator of p53, was similarly reduced and rescued in LK cells (Fig. 7C and fig. S10H). Together, p53 expression is down-regulated in primitive and committed Tet2−/− HPCs and rescued by loss of Id1 function in committed Tet2−/− progenitors, suggesting that p53 may function to increase genomic stability in more committed progenitors.

Id1 ablation delays the onset of disease progression in Tet2−/− chimeric mice

Ablation of Id1 in Tet2−/− chimeric mice reduces (i) HSPC expansion/self-renewal, (ii) extramedullary hematopoiesis, (iii) myeloid skewing, and (iv) chromosomal abnormalities; therefore, we reasoned that loss of Id1 may delay or reduce the onset or incidence of leukemia. Tet2−/− mice develop myeloid and lymphoid hyperplasia that leads to hematopoietic malignancies in some mice after a long latency (17, 19, 69–71); therefore, we treated chimeric mice with multiple sublethal doses of 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) to accelerate aging, by promoting proliferative stress and accumulation of secondary mutations and disease (Fig. 7D) (72). Tet2−/− chimeric mice became sick and moribund beginning 100 days after transplantation and treatment with 5-FU, while control chimeric mice survived (Fig. 7E). Histopathological examination of moribund Tet2−/− chimeric mice showed BM hyperplasia with densely packed BM and splenic hyperplasia that altered the normal architecture of the spleen (Fig. 7F). Increased cardiac inflammation/vasculitis was observed in some sick Tet2−/− chimeric mice compared to aged matched healthy Tet2+/+ mice, which is likely due to increased cardiac inflammation caused by γ-IR conditioning for BMT and made worse by hyperinflammatory Tet2−/− macrophages (Fig. 7F) (21, 26, 49). Thus, loss of Id1 in Tet2−/− chimeric mice reduces hematopoietic hyperplasia and inflammation and delays the onset of leukemia.

DISCUSSION

Chronic inflammation promotes HSPC expansion and contributes to CH, MPN, and AML (31, 33, 73–75). Cytokines and proinflammatory mediators produced during chronic inflammatory stress induce Id1 in HSPCs and promote proliferation, suggesting that ID1 could contribute to CH (39, 40, 43, 76). We show that genetic ablation of Id1 in a mouse model of CH reduces HSPC expansion and self-renewal, myeloid skewing, extramedullary hematopoiesis, genetic instability, and disease onset, which is correlated with increased differentiation, apoptosis, and senescence and decreased proliferation (53, 54). Mechanistically, Tet2−/− HSPCs show reduced p16/CDKI expression, and loss of Id1 expression in Tet2−/− HSPCs in vivo and in vitro increases p16/CDKI expression, promotes senescence and apoptosis, and reduces cell growth, which collectively contribute, in part, to reducing CH. We speculate that ID1 increases p16/CDKI expression via the canonical pathway of antagonizing E protein transcriptional activity (ID-E-CDKI pathway) (53, 59–61, 77). Thus, small-molecule inhibitors of ID1could be used as a first-line defense to limit the expansion of preleukemic clones in patients with CH with high-risk progression to AML.

Inhibition of inflammation, inflammatory cytokines, and downstream intracellular signaling pathways are potential therapeutic targets to reduce CH (23, 24, 31–33, 74, 78). Administration of neutralizing IL-6 antibodies and inhibitors of signaling proteins downstream of IL-6 suppresses CH in Tet2−/− mice (23, 25). Ablation of IL-1r1 expression in Tet2−/− HSPCs rescues LSK expansion, myeloid skewing, and the proinflammatory state (29). Thus, inhibiting inflammatory signaling pathways in patients with TET2 mutations and CH could reduce HSPC expansion and delay progression to MPN and hematopoietic malignancies. However, cytokines can act alone or in combination to promote CH; therefore, it is difficult to predict which cytokines or cytokine combinations to inhibit, and some patients might show increased risk of infection after blocking cytokine function. Since Id1 is expressed at low levels in primitive HSPCs during steady-state hematopoiesis and is induced after BMT and inflammatory and genotoxic stress by proinflammatory cytokines (39, 40, 43, 76), inhibitors of ID1 represent attractive alternative therapies to reduce CH. Thus, it will be important to evaluate whether small-molecule inhibitors of ID1 including AGX51 and cannabidiol inhibit CH in Tet2−/− mice in vivo and human models of CH, MPN, AML, and CMML in vitro and vivo (79–81). ID1 inhibitors might be therapeutic in patients with other mutations that promote CH including JAK2 or more broadly in patients with MPN with chronic inflammation and MDS, since Id1 is induced downstream of JAK2 signaling and other proinflammatory signals (76, 82). Id1 could potentially affect cytokine levels directly or indirectly by reducing the expansion of inflammatory cells; therefore, additional studies are needed to determine whether ablation of Id1 in Tet2−/− mice results in reduced cytokine levels. ID1 is frequently overexpressed in many cancers, and clonal expansion and chronic inflammation are observed in other tissues during aging and tumor progression, suggesting that inhibiting ID1 may slow early-stage progression of other cancers (83–87).

Current studies support the hypothesis that Tet2−/− immune cells are hyperinflammatory and produce elevated levels of proinflammatory cytokines that indirectly promote HSPC expansion and contribute to CH. However, loss of TET2 function could intrinsically regulate Id1 gene expression; therefore, future studies will examine whether loss of TET2 function intrinsically regulates Id1 gene methylation and expression in HSPCs shortly after Tet2 gene deletion in vitro and in vivo.

Patients with TET2 mutations and CH have an increased risk of developing atherosclerosis and cardiovascular disease (CVD) (88–90). Atherosclerosis-prone Ldlr−/− mice transplanted with Tet2−/− BMCs show increased inflammation and hyperinflammatory macrophages that promote atherosclerotic plaque formation (4, 21, 88). Tet2−/−;Id1−/− chimeric mice show reduced myeloid cell expansion, suggesting that genetic ablation of Id1 in Tet2−/−;Ldlr−/− mice may reduce and rescue atherosclerosis (22, 91). Thus, inhibiting Id1 expression or function in patients with TET2 mutations and CH might reduce the risk of atherosclerosis and CVD.

Tet2−/− HSPCs show increased genomic instability that is correlated with a decrease in the expression of DNA repair proteins (20, 63, 65). Unexpectedly, we found that ablation of Id1 expression in Tet2−/− HSPCs increases genomic stability that was correlated with (i) an increase in DNA repair protein gene expression, RAD51 and 53BP1, (ii) increased p53 protein expression (LK cells), (iii) increased G2M checkpoint pathway gene expression, and increased sister chromatin separation gene expression, and (iv) decreased proliferation, which could contribute to increased genomic stability. Id1 has been previously reported to inhibit p53 expression (67, 68); however, the precise mechanism(s) that ID1 regulates p53 expression in these cells, and LK cells but not LSK, and DNA repair protein expression in HSPCs, is now not known. Additional experiments are needed to uncover whether these, or other proteins and pathways not yet identified, mediate the increase in genomic stability observed in Tet2−/−; Id1−/− HSPCs.

In summary, we provide evidence that genetic ablation of Id1 in Tet2-deficient HSPCs rescues hematopoietic phenotypes associated with CH in these mice. Id1 ablation reduces chronic proliferative stress in Tet2−/− HSPCs and increases apoptosis/senescence that results in reduced LSK expansion and reduced accumulation of genetic changes, which could contribute to disease progression and onset. Furthermore, normal HSPCs express low levels of Id1 that are increased during stress, and mice that lack Id1 develop and age normally, suggesting that specific inhibitors of ID1 may be therapeutic and well tolerated in patients with TET2 mutations or other mutations that promote inflammation and CH (92).

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Mice

Id1−/− mice on a C57BL/6 background were derived from Id1−/− mixed background mice (B6;129 provided by R. Benezra) after 10 (or greater) generations of backcross with C57BL/6 mice (Charles River). Tet2−/− mice were purchased from the Jackson laboratory (023359). Id1−/− mice were bred with Tet2−/− mice to generate Tet2−/−;Id1−/− mice. C57BL/6 Id1-EGFP knock-in reporter mice (Id1GFP/+) were provided by X.-H. Sun, Oklahoma Medical Research Foundation, Oklahoma, US. C57BL/6 Ly5.2 (CD45.1) mice were obtained from Charles River. All mice were maintained on a C57BL/6 genetic background. Recipient mice used in BMT experiments were 8- to 12-week-old females. (Experiments involving the use of mice were approved by the National Cancer Institute at Frederick Animal Care and Use Committee in accordance with the eighth edition “Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.”)

Cell lines

Human embryonic kidney (HEK) 293T/17 cells were cultured in 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, 15140) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium. Transfection studies in HEK 293T/17 cells were performed following the guidelines outlined for LipoD293 In Vitro DNA Transfection Reagent (Signagen, SL100668) in a 100-mm dish. Mo7e (DSMZ-cell collection, #ACC 104), K562 [American Type Culture Collection (ATCC), #CRL-3343], and KG1a (ATCC, #CCL-246.1) cells were grown as described by the supplier.

Bone marrow transplantation

For competitive bone marrow transplantation assays, 1 × 106 BMCs from wild-type (WT) (Tet2+/+;Id1+/+), Tet2−/− (Tet2−/−;Id1+/+), Id1−/− (Tet2+/+;Id1−/−), and dKO (Tet2−/−;Id1−/−) mice (CD45.2) were mixed with 1 × 106 C57BL/6 (CD45.1) BMCs and transplanted into γ-irradiated (10 Gy, 137Cs source) congenic CD45.1 mice by tail vein injection (intravenously). Recipient mice were pretreated 1 week before and 2 weeks after transplantation with antibiotic-containing water [(pH 2.5 to 3.0) amoxicillin (0.5 mg/ml) and enrofloxacin (0.17 mg/ml)]. Donor reconstitution was assessed by flow cytometry in the bone marrow and/or peripheral blood after 12 to 16 weeks for all BMT recipient mice.

Flow cytometry analysis of hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells

Single-cell suspensions were prepared from WT, Tet2−/−, Id1−/−, or dKO BMCs or from BMCs of animals transplanted with them. BMCs were harvested from femurs and tibias by flushing with 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) using 25-gauge needle and syringe filtered through a 40-μm membrane. The light density BMC fraction was isolated using lymphocyte separation media (LSM) (MO Biomedicals).

PBCs collected from mandibular bleed were stained with cell surface markers, incubated with BD Pharm lysis buffer for 10 min to remove RBCs, and washed in 1% BSA PBS solution, before acquiring. For lineage analysis, PBCs and BMCs were incubated with the following fluorochrome-conjugated anti-mouse monoclonal antibodies: CD45.2 (104), CD11b (M1/70), Gr-1 (RB6-8C5), CD19 (1D3), CD11c (HL3), F4/80 (AG BM8), Ly6C (AL-21), Ly6G (1A8), CD4 (GK1.5), CD8 (53-6.7), CD71 (R17217), and Ter119 (Ter119). For HSPC analysis, LSM-purified BMCs were treated with Fc receptor blocking antibodies (anti-mouse CD16/32) and then incubated with fluorochrome-conjugated mouse lineage antibody cocktail from BD, c-Kit (ACK2), Sca-1 (D7), CD150 (mShad150), CD48 (HM48-1), CD34 (RAM34), Flk2 (A2F10), and anti-FcgRII/III (FcR) (93). Lin-negative (Lin−) c-Kit+ and Sca-1+ (LSK) cells were analyzed according to the following marker expression: HSC: LSK/Flk2−/CD150+/CD48−, STHSCs: LSK/Flk2−/CD150−/CD48−, multipotent progenitors (MPP2): LSK/Flk2−/CD150+/CD48+, and multipotent progenitors (MPP3): LSK/Flk2−/CD150−/CD48+. All cells were incubated in staining buffer (1% BSA PBS) for 45 min on ice with intermittent mixing and then washed in buffer before acquiring. All the antibodies used were purchased from BD Biosciences (San Jose, CA) or eBioscience (San Diego, CA).

Id1-GFP reporter cell assays

For the evaluation of Id1 expression in the Tet2−/− mouse model, Id1GFP/+ mice (Tet2+/+;Id1GFP+) were crossed to Tet2−/− mice resulting in a Tet2+/+;Id1GFP/+ mouse model. BMCs were harvested from Tet2+/+;Id1GFP/+ and Tet2−/−;Id1GFP/+ mice and fractionated using LSM. The cells were then stained for HSCs, STHSCs, MPPs, CMP, Lin− c-Kit+ (LK) CD34+ FcR−, GMP, LK CD34+FcR+, and megakaryocyte erythroid progenitors (MEP) LK CD34−FcR−. The cells were fixed and permeabilized with BD cytofix/perm buffer, then blocked with 5% BSA in PBS for 45 min on ice, and then stained with primary antibody, chicken (chk) anti-GFP (ab 13970) for 1 hour. The cells were then washed and incubated with secondary antibody, anti-chk AF488 (ab 150169) for 30 min, washed twice with PBS, and analyzed for GFP expression by flow cytometry.

Intracellular staining

For analysis of cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor (CDKI) and DNA damage response protein expression, all antibodies were purchased from Santa Cruz Biotech. BMCs stained for HSPC analysis were fixed and permeabilized with BD cytofix/perm buffer, then blocked with respective purified isotype control antibody for 40 min on ice, and then stained with p16 [sc-166760 phycoerythrin (PE)], p21 [sc-6246 Alexa fluor (AF) 488], p27 (sc-1641PE), p57 (sc-56341 AF488), p53 (sc-126 PE), p19 (sc-32748 AF488), Rad51 (sc-398587 AF594), and 53BP1 (sc-515841 AF488) for 45 min on ice and analyzed by flow cytometry.

Survival assay

Chimeric mice were generated by transplanting 2 × 106 BMCs from WT, Tet2−/−, Id1−/−, and Tet2−/−;Id1−/− mice (CD45.2) into γ-irradiated (10 Gy, 137Cs source) recipient mice by tail vein injection (intravenously). Twelve weeks after BMT, recipient mice were treated with three doses of 5-FU (135 mg/kg) via intraperitoneal injections, at an interval of 2 and 3 weeks, respectively, and monitored for survival. Sick mice were euthanized in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Proliferation and cell cycle analysis

Chimeric mice were generated by transplanting 2 × 106 (1:1 donor + competitor) WT, Tet2−/−, Id1−/−, and Tet2−/−;Id1−/− BMCs into 10-Gy irradiated recipient mice. Twelve weeks posttransplantation, the recipient mice were subjected to one intraperitoneal BrdU injection (1 mg/6 g mice) (Sigma-Aldrich, B5002). These mice also received BrdU [BrdU (1 mg/ml) + 1% glucose] in drinking water for 2 days. The BMCs were harvested and stained for HSPC markers, fixed, permeabilized, and refixed with Cytofix/Cytoperm buffer (BD Biosciences) followed by deoxyribonuclease I (Sigma-Aldrich, D4513) treatment (300 μg/ml) for 1 hour at 37°C. The cells were washed and stained with anti-BrdU antibody (BD Biosciences) for 30 min at room temperature (RT), washed, and analyzed by flow cytometry. For cell cycle/quiescence intracellular staining, BMCs stained for HSPC analysis were fixed and permeabilized with Cytofix/Cytoperm buffer (BD Biosciences) followed by 40-min intracellular staining with Ki-67–PE (B56) (BD Biosciences, 567719). The cells were then incubated overnight with FxCycle Violet dye (Thermo Fisher Scientific, F10347) and acquired the next day. BD LSRII SORP, BD Fortessa, or BD FACSymphony A5 was used for FACS acquisition and Aria II for cell sorting. Data analysis was done using FlowJo V9 software (Tree Star Inc., Ashland OR).

Apoptosis assays

Chimeric mice were generated by transplanting 2 × 106 (1:1 donor + competitor) WT, Tet2−/−, Id1−/−, and Tet2−/−;Id1−/− BM cells into 10-Gy irradiated recipient mice. Twelve weeks posttransplantation, the BMCs were harvested and stained for HSPC markers, fixed, permeabilized, and labeled with terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase–mediated deoxyuridine triphosphate nick end labeling reaction mixture, as per the protocol (Sigma-Aldrich, 11684795910). The cells were washed twice and analyzed by flow cytometry. For annexin V staining, BMCs were incubated with annexin V–PE and 7-aminoactinomycin D (7AAD) (BD Biosciences 559763) for 15 min at RT, further stained for HSPC analysis, and immediately acquired on a flow cytometer.

Senescence assay

In vitro cultured cells or BMCs from transplanted recipient mice were harvested and stained for HSPC markers. The cells were then resuspended in growth media and treated with a final concentration of 100 nM bafilomycin A1 (Sigma-Aldrich, B1793) for 1 hour at 37°C, and C12FDG (Thermo Fisher Scientific, D2893) at a final concentration of 33 μM or DDAOG (Thermo Fisher Scientific, D6488) at a final concentration of 20 μM was added onto the cells and incubated for 1 hour at 37°C. The cells were further stained with the HSPC markers and analyzed by flow cytometry.

Hematopoietic stem and progenitor expansion assays

In vitro studies

LSM BMCs were isolated from experimental mice and incubated with biotinylated Mac-1 (M1/70), Gr-1 (RB6-8C5), B220 (RA3-6B2), CD4 (GK1.5), CD8 (53-6.7), IL-7R (A7R34), and Ter119 (Ter119), and Lin+ IL-7R+ cells were subsequently removed with streptavidin beads. Lineage negative (Lin−) cells were then cultured in StemSpan (09650) medium containing mSCF (PeproTech, 250-03) (100 ng/ml), hTPO (PeproTech, 300-18) (100 ng/ml), mFGF1 (PeproTech, 450-33A) (10 ng/ml), hIGFII (PeproTech, 100-12) (20 ng/ml), and angiopoietin 2 (PeproTech, 130-07) (50 ng/ml). Cells were removed from culture after 5 to 6 days and stained with HSPC markers. To monitor proliferation, using BrdU, cultured Lin− cells were pulsed with 1 mM BrdU (BD Biosciences, 559619) 30 to 40 min. Lin− cells were harvested and stained for HSPCs, fixed, and permeabilized with Cytofix/Cytoperm buffer (BD Biosciences) followed by 40-min intracellular staining with anti-BrdU antibody and analyzed by flow cytometry. Alternatively, Lin− cells were isolated from LSM Tet2+/+;Id1GFP/+ and Tet2−/−;Id1GFP/+ BMCs and then cultured in StemSpan media containing SCF, SCF and IL-6 (PeproTech, 216-16) (50 ng/ml), or IL-1β (PeproTech, 211-11b) (20 ng/ml) or tnterferon-γ (PeproTech, 315-05) (20 ng/ml). Cells were removed from cultures after 36 hours and stained with antibodies that recognize HSPCs and GFP.

In vitro differentiation culture

LSM BMCs were isolated from WT, Tet2−/−, Id1−/−, and Tet2−/−; Id1−/− mice. Lin− cells were cultured in IMDM + 10% fetal bovine serum containing mSCF (50 ng/ml), mFlt3 (10 ng/ml), mIL-3 (10 ng/ml), macrophage colony-stimulating factor (10 ng/ml), granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (10 ng/ml), and granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (10 ng/ml). After 6 days of culture, the cells were harvested, stained with lineage-specific antibodies, and analyzed on a flow cytometer.

Colony formation assay

BMCs were harvested from WT, Tet2−/−, Id1−/−, and Tet2−/−;Id1−/− mice and labeled with antibodies that recognize HSPCs as described above, and HSCs (LSK/FLT3−/CD150+/CD48−) were FACS-sorted. HSCs per plate were seeded in methocult (STEMCELL Technologies, M3434) containing 35-mm gridded dishes. Colonies were scored 7 to 10 days after the seeding, under a microscope. For serial replating, colonies in a single plate were harvested together and counted, and 5000 cells per plate were reseeded in the methocult for screening after another 7 to 10 days. Up to seven replatings were performed for the experiments.

Single-cell division kinetic assay

BMCs were harvested from WT, Tet2−/−, Id1−/−, and Tet2−/−;Id1−/− mice and labeled with antibodies that recognize HSPCs as described above. LK (Lin− c-kit+) and HSCs (LSK/FLT3−/CD150+/CD48−) were sorted and single cell deposited directly into Terasaki plates (Greiner Bio-One) containing StemSpan serum-free media supplemented with mSCF (100 ng/ml), hTPO (100 ng/ml), IL-6 (50 ng/ml), and mIL-3 (30 ng/ml) in vitro. Percentage confluency was determined 7 days after seeding.
