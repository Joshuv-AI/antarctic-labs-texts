Physics of North Indian Ocean tropical cyclones
Authors: Vadlamudi Brahmananda Rao, Mohan S. T, V. S. Lakshmi Bhargavi, Dandu Govardhan, C. V. Srinivas, V. S. Prasad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This research paper focuses on the applicability of two well-known concepts, Convective Quasi Equilibrium (CQE) and Convective Instability of the Second Kind (CISK), in the genesis of tropical cyclones (TCs), over the North Indian Ocean (NIO). Using high-resolution regional reanalysis IMDAA data from NCMRWF, we study the applicability of CQE and CISK during pre-monsoon (April and May) and post-monsoon (October and November) for the 1979 to 2020 period. In this study we found that CQE seems to be applicable over some regions of Arabian Sea (AS) in the pre-monsoon season only. However, TCs form over the entire AS, thus CISK mechanism may be vital in pre-monsoon over the AS. In post-monsoon season, over the Bay of Bengal (BB), CQE is found to be applicable. Also, we found the applicability of the CISK, over the BB, both in the pre- and post-monsoon seasons. The study of individual TCs confirms these results regarding the genesis mechanisms of TCs over NIO. Another important, feature we discovered over the AS is the strong, diurnal variation of humidity.

Introduction

The North Indian Ocean (NIO) is one of the global tropical ocean basins where Tropical Cyclones (TCs) form primarily only during the pre- and post-monsoon seasons. Although these NIO TCs represent only 6% of the global total1, they cause significant damage to the lives and livelihoods of more than a billion people living along the coastal regions of the Arabian Sea (AS) and the Bay of Bengal (BB). A recent study by2, involving a large group of global experts, highlighted the increasing exposure to extreme climate events such as TCs, with the largest projected increase in overall exposure occurring in South Asia, i.e., in the Indian subcontinent and its neighbouring regions. Therefore, understanding the fundamental physical processes responsible for the genesis and further development of TCs in the NIO is crucial for the well-being of the region’s population, especially the vulnerable coastal communities. This understanding is essential for improving the accuracy of TC predictions3.

Extensive research in the Atlantic and Pacific Ocean basins has identified various dynamic and thermodynamic processes, working across multiple scales, that influence the intensification or weakening of TCs. However, there has been relatively limited theoretical research on these processes in the North Indian Ocean (NIO) region. Some of the key factors include large-scale circulations in the TC environment, vortex-scale convective circulations, microscale turbulence, and cloud microphysical processes4–10(Vertical deep-layer wind shear, mid-tropospheric humidity, and sea surface temperature (SST) are also critical in determining TC development and intensification when other environmental conditions are favourable9–11.

Despite various studies on NIO TCs12–29(), the fundamental physics of convectively coupled systems, especially their thermodynamics and related theories, remain insufficiently studied and understood. In contrast, the dynamics of mid- and high-latitude cyclones, which are primarily driven by baroclinic processes, were first explored in the seminal works of30,31. In fact, the Charney ‘paradigm’ of baroclinic instability remains valid even today, indicating that the basic physics of these systems is well understood. Additionally, in mid- and high-latitudes, there is no significant variation in the underlying physics with longitude, and the energy for the intensification of synoptic systems is released over horizontal scales of several thousand kilometres.

This study aims to verify the physical processes responsible for NIO TCs during the pre- and post-monsoon periods when TCs are most likely to occur. We investigate the applicability of Convective Quasi Equilibrium (CQE) in the AS and BB regions during these seasons. Additionally, we explore the Convective Instability of the Second Kind (CISK) mechanism for NIO TCs, as we observed that CQE did not consistently apply in some cases, and CISK may be important for these cases of TCs over NIO.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: Sect. 2 discusses the data and methods used in the study. Section 3 presents the results, while Sect. 4 offers a discussion of the findings. Section 5 provides an analysis of individual cyclones, and Sect. 6 concludes the research with a comprehensive summary of the findings.

Materials and methods

Datasets used

In the present analysis, we have utilized high-resolution reanalysis data for the Indian monsoon regions known as Indian Monsoon Data Assimilation and Analysis reanalysis (IMDAA). The reanalysis data sets are freely available at the National Centre for Medium Range Weather Forecasting (NCMRWF) (https://rds.ncmrwf.gov.in/).

The IMDAA reanalysis datasets with high-resolution of 12 km over the Indian subcontinent are developed in a collaborative research project among the NCMRWF, the United Kingdom Met Office (UKMO) and the India Meteorological Department (IMD). The project is supported by the Ministry of Earth Sciences (MoES) under the National Monsoon Mission project. The reanalyses data spans over the period 1979–2020 (42 years) and it is based on the UKMO unified limited area modelling system (Version 10.2) with 63 terrain-following vertical hybrid levels extended up to ~ 40 km height of the atmosphere. This regional reanalyses data covers the region from 30° to 120° E (East Africa to East Asia) and 15°S to 45°N with 800 by 576 grid points in the horizontal direction32,33 (). The reanalysis model uses the Even Newer Dynamics (ENDGame) dynamical core, whilst employing various types of physical parametrization schemes34,35 ). The lower boundary for the IMDAA reanalysis is utilized from HadISST2 (Version-2 of Hadley Centre Ice and Sea Surface Temperature data) until 2010 and later UKMO operational high-resolution SST and Sea Ice Analysis (OSTIA) data is used and the lateral boundary conditions are from European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF)-Interim Reanalysis (ERA-I). The reanalysis provides high-frequency (hourly to 3 hourly) 2-D and 3-D atmospheric data and is freely available for research purposes (https://rds.ncmrwf.gov.in). In addition, the large domain of IMDAA helps study the summer monsoon features36 and used for analysing the severe weather systems like cyclones over the NIO region. Further, in a very recent study37, showed that IMDAA data can reproduce well the characteristics of TCs over NIO.

For the present analysis, we have used nearly 25 years (1996–2020) of temperature (T), specific humidity (q), and zonal winds (u) data from IMDAA, during April, May, October and November, which are usually regarded as cyclone periods over the Indian subcontinent. Theoretical understanding of Convective Quasi Equilibrium and Convective Instability of the Second Kind mechanisms of Tropical Cyclones

The pioneering studies by39,40 well established that the formation and intensification of TCs require heat transfer from the ocean. Surface enthalpy fluxes are essential for a TC to develop, as these fluxes create energy stored in a conditionally unstable atmosphere. Miller41 was the first to assert that the energy source for TCs is the latent heat of condensation, similar to the theory manifested by Bjerknes42 and Lilly43. In TCs, the energy released at the scale of cumulus clouds is transferred to the synoptic scale, leading to a two-scale interaction that ultimately results in the development of TCs on a synoptic scale. This concept forms the basis of the CISK theory ()44,45.

A careful review of46 reveals that the mechanisms of TC genesis and maintenance vary significantly by region. Most of the literature on TCs focuses on regions other than the NIO, and there has been relatively little work on NIO TCs, particularly regarding the ‘theory of genesis and maintenance’ using observed thermodynamics in this region. The NIO is shallower, warmer, and smaller in size compared to the Atlantic and Pacific Oceans47. The present study aims to initiate research in this direction. Unlike baroclinic disturbances in mid- and high-latitudes, the genesis and maintenance of TCs are highly dependent on both region and season 48. In an insightful article48, discussed the utility of the Quasi-Equilibrium Approximation (QEA) for tropical synoptic-scale motions, including TCs. Several subsequent studies have examined the applicability of CQE to these systems.

As mentioned above, the dynamics of large-scale motions in the mid and high latitudes are relatively straightforward, unlike tropical atmosphere where dynamics are more complex40. Riehl40 proposed that the latent heat released during moist convection drives large-scale circulations in the tropics, such as tropical cyclones, making the interaction between moist convection and large-scale circulations a fundamental aspect of tropical meteorology. Later in 196445, , along with (1969)44, independently proposed a theory known as CISK. This theory describes the interaction between moist convection and circulation within tropical low-pressure systems, suggesting that low-level convective inhibition will be overcome by moisture dynamics due to convection. This inhibition occurs because the equivalent potential temperature in the tropical atmosphere is lower than the corresponding saturated value in the boundary layer (please refer Fig. 2 of44. Ooyama44 first showed that indeed this mechanism can generate realistically TCs of Hurricane intensity. In45, cumulus heating is taken as a forcing and38 criticized this CISK theory, saying in tropics conditional instability is rare. But, as we show later it does occur over NIO, in some cases.

Arakawa and Schubert49 introduced an alternative theory describing the interaction between moist convection and large-scale tropical motions. They proposed that the time scale for generating Convective Available Potential Energy (CAPE) relative to its release by moist convection is significantly longer, meaning that moist convection rapidly adjusts the tropical atmosphere toward a moist adiabatic lapse rate. This process, termed CQE by49, maintains the neutral buoyancy of air lifted from the sub-cloud layer to levels above the cloud base. Emanuel48 provided observational support for this theory. A simple method to test CQE is to compare changes in sub-cloud entropy (Sb) with the saturation entropy of the free tropospheric convective layer (S*), as proposed by Emanuel50. If the peaks of Sb and S* coincide, it indicates the maintenance of a moist adiabatic temperature profile with height, a condition for the occurrence of CQE as termed by Arakawa and Schubert49.

However51, observed that moist convection could act as a source of kinetic energy for large-scale disturbances, based on empirical evidence. They found that latent heating from moist convection is positively correlated with temperature anomalies in the tropical Pacific, suggesting that convection not only adjusts in response to instability but can also generate available potential energy (APE) and drive circulations.

From the CISK perspective, given the assumed widespread presence of convective inhibition, convection is mainly driven by low-level moisture convergence, which overcomes the low-level barrier by lowering the ‘lifting condensation level.’ A straightforward way to test the applicability of CQE is to compare estimates of S* and Sb and see if they coincide, as done by Boos and Emanuel52. If they do, it indicates that the equivalent potential temperature (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\theta\:}_{e}$$\end{document}) does not vary with height, confirming the occurrence of ‘Neutral Buoyancy’ as previously mentioned. In this study, we employ a similar approach to test the applicability of CQE for tropical cyclones over the NIO.

Although the applicability of CQE has been tested in some regions, to our knowledge, a similar evaluation has not been conducted for tropical TCs in the NIO. The NIO is particularly prone to TC formation during two key seasons: April to May (pre-monsoon season) and October to November (post-monsoon season). In contrast, during the boreal summer monsoon season from June to September, TCs generally do not form due to the strong vertical wind shear prevalent during this period53. However, weaker Monsoon Depressions (MDs) do develop during the summer monsoon, and these are crucial for India’s annual rainfall, accounting for 80% of the country’s rainfall54. The atmospheric conditions during the pre- and post-monsoon seasons are conducive to TC formation over the NIO. Despite NIO TCs making up only 6%1 of global TCs, they are responsible for 80% of TC-related fatalities worldwide55. This underscores the importance of understanding the underlying physics that drive TC formation and intensification in the NIO.

In the case of CQE, it is assumed that the spatial and temporal scales are significantly larger than the convective scales, allowing convection to be considered in quasi-equilibrium with the large-scale environment. Strong meridional overturning circulations typically feature a narrow region of intense ascent and a broad area of slow sinking motion, which maintains mass balance. Since the precipitating ascent zone is expected to be near the free tropospheric temperature maximum, CQE suggests that the maximum equivalent potential temperature of the sub-cloud layer (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\theta\:}_{eb}$$\end{document}) should coincide with the saturated equivalent potential temperature (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\theta\:}_{e}^{*}$$\end{document}) of the free atmosphere. However, whether this alignment of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\theta\:}_{eb}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\theta\:}_{e}^{*}$$\end{document} occurs in TC regions over the NIO has ‘not yet been verified’.

Duan et al.60 show the strong influence of low vertical shear in May over both seas (). They also noticed that a low wind shear and higher relative humidity are two factors over the BB which probably explain higher TCs in the post-monsoon season. The two peaks in TCs (“Bimodal”) in the pre-monsoon and post-monsoon seasons are, closely related to the South Asian summer monsoon in the intervening period June to September. During the summer monsoon, although humidity is high, the strong vertical shear inhibits the development of TCs over NIO. Over the BB, the TCs number is higher in the pre-monsoon and post-monsoon seasons mainly because of higher relative humidity and lower vertical wind shear compared to that over the AS.

As commented by Emanuel57, the exact processes that cause the genesis of TCs remains still elusive. The TC genesis has two facets, namely (1) the nature of the mean structure of the background large-scale environments to the location of TCs formation and (2) the dynamic and thermodynamic paths taken by individual cyclones. Thus, our discussion above so far gives the mean structure. Now, we discuss, the cases of individual cyclones as given in37 (hereafter as R2023) during the pre-and post-monsoon seasons, the epochs of TCs over NIO.

To get a general idea of the background ambient, results of Emanuel57, gives the annual mean global genesis index (GI) calculated from the monthly averages of ERA-Interim data (Dee et al. 2011) for the period 1979–2016. In this figure black dots show the locations of the first points of TCs. For, NIO, we see TCs formation over the AS and BoB as expected, with the latter showing higher numbers. One important point to note in this figure is the first or starting points over the AS occur in addition to the region of GI, outside this region also. But most of the TC’s first points are captured over NIO by this index. Thus, one might postulate that the overall frequency of TC genesis in a given climate is governed by some combination of mean large-scale environmental susceptibility to genesis (as measured for example by a GI) and the frequency structure and amplitude of individual disturbances that serve as catalysis to the genesis. Few recent studies have demonstrated that multiple factors influencing the TC gensis and frequency over NIO. For instance, easterly waves and the occurrences of Madden Julian Oscillation (MJO)61,62; strong linkage was found between TC activity and Atlantic meridional mode, Indian Ocean dipole, and El Niño–Southern Oscillation63; the impact of anthropogenic aerosols and Green House Gases (GHGs) on the low-level convergence patterns over Tropical west pacific and NIO TC genesis64.

In the study by R2023, they used the same data as we used for the study of mean structure in Figs. 1 and 2. In their study, R2023 examined 197 individual storms of different categories (depressions to Super cyclones) during the period 1979–2018, over NIO. These authors evaluated the performance of IMDAA reanalysis in the distribution of genesis points of TCs over NIO. They found the location of genesis points is a little displaced compared to the IMD best-estimated tracks. The errors are less, over BoB compared to those over AS. They further mentioned, that from their figures, the location of the storms is reasonably close to the observed pattern in both pre- and post-monsoon seasons over BoB and AS. Also, for our present study, the location of TCs using IMDAA relative to the observations is not a major concern, because, in our study, we are interested more in the genesis mechanism than the location. However, from Figs. 1(b)-(e) of R2023, we infer, that the genesis location, tracks and overall density and number of storms per km square are very similar for observed and IMDAA data.

A comparison of Fig. 1b with the results of57, shows, there is an agreement between the GI and the locations in R2023. Although, most of the genesis locations in Fig. 1b, of R2023 over BoB lie within the GI isolines, over AS, some TCs form outside over western part away from GI. Further, the GI is the highest overhead BB, but the higher number of TCs form to the south between 5⁰N and 15⁰N and east of 85⁰E. Thus, the GI gives only a rough qualitative idea of the region of TC formation, even over NIO. A common problem with the GI is in the calculation of the index, many authors use relative humidity65. However, the genesis depends more nearly on the saturation deficit of the mid-troposphere than relative humidity57. Discussion

In a careful analysis above, we examined the applicability of the CQE concept for NIO, TCs both in pre-and post-monsoon seasons, the epochs when TCs occur over NIO. First, we studied long-term means for 25 years (1996–2020). Emanuel57 commented that TCs form in a combination of mean background environment together with thermodynamic features that occur in individual cases of cases of TCs. To get an idea of the TCs formation regions in the mean background, we examined three sources, namely Emanuel57 (hereafter E2018), R2023 and Perez-Alarcon56 (hereafter PA2023).

In R2023, the authors found genesis over BB accounts for 70% of TCs over NIO during the period,1980–2018. In their, results, the cluster of TC points in BB and AS are shown. In BB, TC genesis points are quite well spread with most points to the west of 90⁰E and spread between 76⁰E to 100⁰E, while in AS, the spread of TC points is less dense and located mostly between 52⁰E to 75⁰E. In R2023, we see, in IMDAA data (which we used in the present work) for the period 1979–2018, almost the same period used in PA2023. In R2023, since both IMDAA and observation data are used, the cluster looks dense. But, in IMDAA data over BB, the genesis points lie between 5⁰N to 15⁰N and east of 85⁰E. In AS maximum points are between 9⁰N to 15⁰N and 50⁰E to 75⁰E. In E2018, the cluster for the period 1979–2016, seems to agree with the above works, except that in E2018 over BoB the higher number is mostly south and in AS, the genesis points generally agree in position as shown in PA2023 and R2023.

One important feature noted in E2018 is that the number of genesis points do not agree with the GI value. In AS, the region of the GI’s high value occurs near the Peninsula’s west coast, where the number of TCs are low. In BB also, more genesis points occur in the region of lower values of the GI. Further, over the Red Sea and the Persian Gulf, no TCs form, as commented by E2018, though the Genesis Potential Index (GPI) is high here. The GI is purely empirical, and we can expect only a qualitative agreement between GPI and genesis points.

From the results of61we clearly noticed that ( e.g., in their result of Fig. ) month 3for April, the genesis points lie between 15 N to South and 80 E to 110E, but only in Bay of Bengal. Whereas in May, there are genesis points both in BOB and Arabian Sea. And In Arabian Sea, they extend from 5 N to the North. In November, the genesis points occur East of 65 E to 100E;Equator to 15 N. Overall, in the post monsoon season there are more genesis points in BOB and as the season advances, these move southward. A careful comparison of genesis points with rainfall shows a good agreement. Rainfall may overestimate the genesis point number, but it surely includes the ‘genesis regions’.

Our above analyses to test the agreement of NIO atmospheric thermodynamics for the CQE concept using both the time mean and composite of individual cases showed that CQE does not seem to apply to NIO in the pre-monsoon. Even during the post-monsoon, there is only qualitative agreement. Then the question that arises is how TCs form over NIO, during the pre-monsoon and post-monsoon as the observations show. Here, a recent paper by Tomassini69 might be useful to explore other alternative mechanisms for forming TCs over NIO. In the middle and high latitudes, unlike in the global tropics, there is mostly one-scale dominance, namely large-scale baroclinic Rossby waves of around wave number 5–7. While in the tropics, observations and theory indicate that there is an interaction (cooperative as45 suggest) between small-scale moist convection and large-scale synoptic motions, such that the former gives energy to the latter or sustains the latter. Further the latter also makes the tropical atmosphere favorable for moist convection. Regarding the actual mechanisms that explain these processes, there are two hypotheses, which are tested and seem to work in the tropical moist atmosphere. One that stresses lower-level stability to be superseded and generate buoyancy, (well explained in44 and the other, in which convection is an adjustment process (throughout the vertical column) to the large-scale instabilities, called CQE, discussed above. In the former, the so-called, CISK44,45 ) conditional nature atmospheric instability allows convective vertical motion and liberation of latent heat of condensation to drive and give sufficient energy to largescale disturbances. In the second, CQE convection is thought to dampen scale at which the instability at a rate much shorter than the time scale the instability is created38,49,70. Arakawa in their study70 compares CQE concept to Quasi-Geostrophic adjustment by the dispersion of gravity waves (whenever the quasi-geostrophy is destroyed gravity waves help in restoring the adjustment). The ratio of the time scales of the two is of the order of Rossby number, Ro which is 1/10. In any case, the main difference between the CISK and CQE is (1) to what degree moisture dynamics and related lower tropospheric instability are important versus (2) vertically integrated instability. That is whether moist convection leads to adjustment and stabilization of the atmospheric column or drives the circulation. Another important aspect of the tropical atmosphere is that meteorological conditions (e.g., seasonal flows) vary under different circumstances such as pre- and post-monsoons and over different regions such as AS and BB. In a study by PA2023, they found that NIO TCs require more atmospheric moisture during the growing stages than later. They also found specific humidity or moisture addition in the genesis stage with maximum value for the Hurricane category. However, in our analysis discussed above, we found, that the concept of CQE with limited application to NIO TCs. We obtained these results using R2023 data for all categories of TCs and for the entire life cycle of TCs, not only in the genesis stage but to the entire life period of TCs.

However, genesis stage may be highly variable, because the initial trigger could be different for different TCs. But, after the initial trigger, the subsequent evolution of the life cycle is expected to depend on factors other than the trigger. Thus, we study the entire life cycle of TCs, which is expected to be similar and depends only on the thermodynamics of the atmosphere of the season and the region. So, we studied the entire” life cycle”, which may give reliable results for the TCs over NIO. Thus, the data of R2023, we used here covering all stages of TCs over NIO, are well suited.

Now we discuss how well CISK hypothesis is valid for the genesis and development of TCs over NIO. The high values of θe at the lowest level, show for a parcel rising from this level, can reach above with θe more than θe*, and the parcel becomes buoyant. The intersection point with θe* is the Level of Free Convection (LFC). As discussed earlier for the climatological case, the area of CIN is, a triangular area between θe point 403 K to θe* point 419 K to 850 hPa, 403 K point on θe* curve, while, CAPE is the area to the left between θe* curve at 850 hPa from point 403 K up to θe* curve at 200 hPa, point 403 K. The area of CAPE becomes a minimum around 3 May. Later, as θe* increases, the area of CAPE increases again. Interestingly, this behaviour of TC, FANI regarding the CIN and CAPE agrees very well with what happened to the life cycle of FANI, as reported by IMD (2019). Their Fig. 3a shows that the storm maintained without decay, 21 h even after landfall.

For, the storm, AMPHAN (16–21 May 2020) also the time variations of θe and θe*, are qualitatively similar to those of FANI. Also, we note in the case of AMPHAN, the decrease of θe, above LFC, is quicker than that in the case of FANI. These differences, make the “Storm, AMPHAN, “Rapid Intensification” shorter than in the case of FANI, as we noted in our study71.

In the case of TC, TITLI (8–13 October 2018) occurring in the Post monsoon season over BOB, similar to the climatology, the possibility of a parcel rising from the lowest level, with values of θe higher than θe* is remote. Also, In the case of TC, GAJA (10–19 November 2018), since θe* in Fig. 13d, from 10 to 15 November 2018, is high, the possibility of CISK is remote. So, in the case of Post-monsoon TCs over BOB, since both TITLI and GAJA did intensify into Super TCs, CQE, the other alternative physical mechanism, may be important.

In the case of AS, super cyclones, MEKUNU (22–27 May 2018) in the pre-monsoon season, LUBAN (6–15 October 2018 ) and MAHA ( 30th October − 7th November 2019) in the post-monsoon season, the picture of θe and θe* shown in Figs. 14 and 15, very clearly indicate, a parcel rising from the top of the boundary layer ( around 1000 hPa level ) with θe around 400 K can reach higher up around 600 hPa with θe* lower ( 320 K ), that is LFC, is above this level. Also, in all the 3 cases TCs above, CAPE is much higher than CIN. However, in the climatology (Figs. 9 and 10), the values of θe are very low (around 340 K), while in the case of above 3 TCs, they are much higher than 400 K. That is in the case of 3 TCs, the low-level specific humidity is much higher than climatology. Also, θe*, higher up around 600 hPa in the three cases is around 320 K, while in the climatology it is 340 K. These, results show, that for the cases of 3 TCs over AS, the area of CAPE is much higher than that of CIN. The higher value of θe in the lower levels as noted for 3 TCs, makes CIN less, while the lower value of θe* higher up, makes CAPE higher. Thus, in the case of AS, our analysis of observations shows in both the pre-monsoon and post-monsoon, the Physical process CISK is highly relevant for developing intense TCs.

In a recent paper, Tomassini69 discussed the main differences between the two concepts we studied here, namely CQE and CISK. He mentioned that the meteorological conditions can vary greatly under different circumstances and in different tropical regions. As suggested by him, we found that the meteorological conditions vary substantially between pre- and post-monsoon seasons and also even between nearly AS and the BoB. In the case of NIO TCs, we see only a limited application of CQE. In both AS and BoB, the vertical profiles of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\theta\:,\:$$\end{document}\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\theta\:e\:and$$\end{document} θe* both pre- and post-monsoon seasons are very similar to those of Ooyama44. This suggests, at least in NIO in pre- and post-monsoon seasons the ambient of TCs genesis and development is more akin to the applicability of the CISK concept. We also examined individual cases of 7 TCs over NIO. These also seem to confirm our conclusion. Thus, both the time mean, and individual case do not suggest a moist adiabatic profile, as found over the west tropical Pacific. While over the Sahel, the thermodynamic structure is again different69. In any case, Tomassini pointed out to a consensus in which the situation can be called a hybrid, “Lower-Tropospheric Quasi equilibrium”.

Concluding remarks

This study focuses on examining the validity of the CQE and CISK concepts over the NIO during the pre-Monsoon (April and May) and post-Monsoon (October and November) seasons. We observe the TC formation is more favourable in the Bay of Bengal (BB) and weaker in the Arabian Sea (AS) regions. The CQE theory basically presumes that ‘deep convection’ induces the lower tropospheric moisture convergence required to maintain a sufficient moisture supply for further convection. While in CISK theory, the presence of low - level inhibition and the ‘conditional’ nature of atmospheric instability allows for convective vertical motion and latent heating to drive and intensify synoptic - scale disturbances as well as overturning circulations. Whether it may be CQE or CISK, the main energy source for the intensification from the genesis stage, is the latent heat of condensation. The positive correlation between the condensation heating and temperature variations, generates the ‘Perturbation Available Potential Energy’ (PAPE). Then, the PAPE is converted into Perturbation Kinetic Energy (PKE) by warm air rising and cold air sinking (kindly refer to atmospheric energy cycle equations, 1 to 4, in72.

It is the wind and intense rainfall of Tropical Cyclones, which cause severe damages to life and property. That is, the increase of wind (increase of PKE) and increase of intense rainfall (release of latent heat strongly positively correlating with temperature or increase of PAPE, which gets converted to PKE) are thus can be due to CQE or CISK, finally.

In the case of middle and high latitudes, PAPE is generated due to poleward transport of sensible heat by ‘unstable’ baroclinic perturbations or eddies. The conversion from PAPE to PKE in middle and high latitudes, also like the case of tropical latitudes, is due to warm air rising and cold air sinking (These two types of energy conversions in the tropical and higher latitudes are given in the Schematic diagrams of72.

To summarise, in this article, the applicability of CQE and CISK concepts during the tropical cyclone seasons of pre and post monsoon seasons for the period 1979 to 2020 are focussed. The atmospheric thermodynamics revealed that only in specific regions of the Arabian sea, CQE is major driver of generation of Tcs. For the rest of the region CISK seems to be important. The concept of CISK seems to be highly relevant for intense TCs over AS. Over BOB, CISK and CQE both seems to be applicable with more dominance from CQE.

As this study focusses on the physics driving TCs, the results will be definitely useful in improving the prediction accuracy and possibly early warning of tropical cyclone formation and intensification. And we also acknowledge the limitation of ‘ not stressing oceanic processes’ in this study. The concept of this study, primarily focusses on the processes where the surface enthalpy fluxes that generate energy, are prominent in a conditionally unstable atmosphere. So, this study couldn’t particularly stress the role of Ocean.

However, further research is essential to explore how recent human-induced warming might be influencing the physical processes of TCs across different geographical regions. Understanding whether these changes are leading to an increase or decrease in the frequency and intensity of TCs, alongside the applicability of CQE and CISK theories, remains a critical area of study.
