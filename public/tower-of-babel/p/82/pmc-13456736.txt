Clinical and radiological features of primary breast sarcoma: a 14-year retrospective case series from a single institution
Authors: Rujira Patanawanitkul, Varali Ariyadej, Voraparee Suvannarerg, Shanigarn Thiravit, Kobkun Muangsomboon, Pornpim Korpraphong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Primary breast sarcoma (PBS) is a rare and aggressive malignancy arising from the breast’s mesenchymal tissues. Its clinical and imaging features are non-specific, often mimicking benign or epithelial malignancies, which makes early diagnosis challenging. Given the rarity of PBS, detailed case series are essential to enhance understanding and guide management. This study presents a case series of PBS, focusing on its clinical, radiological, and pathological characteristics.Methods:A retrospective review was conducted at a single tertiary care institution between January 2008 and December 2022. Nine pathologically confirmed PBS cases were included. Mammographic and ultrasound findings were reviewed by experienced radiologists. Clinical presentations, imaging features, histopathology, and outcomes were analyzed.Results:Nine cases of PBS were identified, including five angiosarcomas, one myxofibrosarcoma, one liposarcoma, one rhabdomyosarcoma, and one spindle cell sarcoma. The patients ranged in age from 18 to 83 years (mean age: 38.3 ± 21.1 years). Most presented with palpable breast masses, and three experienced rapid tumor growth. Imaging commonly demonstrated oval or round masses with obscured or indistinct margins. Tumor sizes ranged from 1.5 to 14.0 cm (mean: 7.0 ± 4.9 cm). Initial pathological sampling suggested sarcoma or malignant mesenchymal neoplasm in five of nine cases (55.6%), with immunohistochemistry (IHC) aiding final subtype classification. Three cases demonstrated local breast recurrence, and four patients experienced distant metastases.Conclusion:PBS often presents with nonspecific clinical and radiological features, with rapid tumor growth being a key characteristic. Core biopsy remains essential for diagnosis, frequently supported by IHC for subtype differentiation. Greater awareness of these aggressive tumors may facilitate earlier diagnosis and improved clinical management.

Introduction

Primary breast sarcomas (PBSs) are rare malignancies, accounting for less than 1% of all breast cancers and under 5% of sarcomas[1,2]. These tumors arise from mesenchymal tissue and differ significantly from epithelial breast cancers in their pathology, behavior, and prognosis. Subtypes include angiosarcoma, myxofibrosarcoma, liposarcoma, rhabdomyosarcoma, and spindle cell sarcoma[3]. Unlike therapy-related sarcomas, PBS arises de novo, predominantly affecting younger women[4,5]. Clinical and imaging findings are often nonspecific, posing diagnostic challenges[6–8].

Despite their rarity, PBS demands attention due to its aggressive nature and high recurrence rates[9–11]. This study investigates the clinical, radiological, and pathological features of PBS to improve diagnostic accuracy and therapeutic outcomes.

Materials and methods

A retrospective consecutive case series was conducted at a single tertiary care institution, including patients diagnosed with PBS between 2008 and 2022. Patients were required to have histopathologically confirmed PBS and available mammography or ultrasound imaging. Clinical data were collected, and imaging features were analyzed using the BI-RADS lexicon. Pathological evaluation included immunohistochemistry (IHC) for subtype confirmation.

Descriptive statistics were used to analyze the data. Continuous variables, such as age and tumor size, were reported as mean values with standard deviations, while categorical data were presented as frequencies and percentages. This study was retrospective and noninterventional. The study was approved by the Siriraj Institutional Review Board (Certificate of Approval Number Si 348/2023). The present case series was conducted and reported in accordance with the PROCESS (Preferred Reporting of Case Series in Surgery) guidelines for the age of Artificial Intelligence[12].

Results

The age distribution of the patients ranged from 18 to 83 years. Seven patients were aged 43 or younger, while two patients were postmenopausal (aged 56 and 83 years). Clinically, eight patients presented with palpable breast masses, three of which were noted to grow rapidly. The duration of symptoms ranged from 2 weeks to 6 years, with a median duration of 1 year (mean: 1.4 years). Case 1: Angiosarcoma (high grade)

A 32-year-old woman presented with an 11-month history of a rapidly growing right breast mass, associated with pain and bloody nipple discharge. On examination, a large, movable mass measuring 13.5 cm was noted. Mammography revealed an oval mass with obscured margins and diffuse skin thickening, while ultrasound showed a heterogeneous hypoechoic mass with internal vascularity and cystic portions (Fig. 1). Initial core biopsy performed at an outside hospital yielded only hemorrhagic material and necrotic debris. Repeat ultrasound-guided core biopsy at our institution demonstrated an atypical vascular lesion, and immunohistochemical analysis supported the diagnosis of high-grade angiosarcoma (CD31+, Fli-1+, and ERG+). The patient underwent modified radical mastectomy with axillary dissection, which revealed metastatic axillary lymph nodes and widespread lung, liver, and bone metastases on subsequent computed tomography (CT) scans. She died shortly after surgery due to progressive metastatic disease.

Case 2: Angiosarcoma (low grade)

A 39-year-old woman presented with a 6-month history of a progressively enlarging left breast mass. Imaging showed an oval, high-density mass with obscured margins on mammography and a hypoechoic mass with indistinct margins and rim vascularity on ultrasound.

Core biopsy revealed low-grade angiosarcoma with CD31+ and Fli-1+ markers. She underwent total mastectomy, with pathology confirming clear margins. Three years later, the patient developed a new mass in the contralateral breast, diagnosed as high-grade angiosarcoma, and subsequently passed away from complications.

Case 3: Angiosarcoma (low grade)

A 37-year-old woman, previously treated for low-grade angiosarcoma of the right breast, presented with a new palpable mass in the left breast. Follow-up mammography revealed two round isodense nodules with partially indistinct margins, while ultrasound confirmed the presence of hypoechoic nodules.

Core biopsy identified recurrent low-grade angiosarcoma. She underwent re-excision and adjuvant radiotherapy but later developed scalp metastases, which ultimately led to her demise. This case emphasizes the recurrence risk of low-grade angiosarcomas, even after aggressive treatment.

Case 4: Angiosarcoma (intermediate grade)

A 19-year-old woman reported a 10-month history of a rapidly enlarging left breast mass, accompanied by overlying skin discoloration. Physical examination revealed an 8-cm mass with erythematous skin changes. Imaging studies showed an oval isodense mass with obscured margins on mammography and an irregular hypoechoic mass with indistinct margins and rim vascularity on ultrasound (Fig. 2). Case 5: Angiosarcoma (intermediate grade)

A 56-year-old postmenopausal woman initially presented with a painful right breast mass and underwent an excisional biopsy at an outside hospital, which was interpreted as fibrocystic change. Due to progressive enlargement, re-excision was later performed and diagnosed as a hemangioma. One year later, she developed another enlarging mass at the prior surgical site. Mammography revealed an irregular, indistinct mass with architectural distortion and adjacent skin thickening, while ultrasound demonstrated an irregular, hypoechoic mass with spiculated margins and internal vascularity.

Pathology review of outside slides and repeat core biopsy at our institution confirmed intermediate-grade angiosarcoma. The patient underwent a right total mastectomy followed by radiotherapy. Thirteen months later, a local chest wall recurrence developed, requiring re-excision and additional radiotherapy. She is currently disease-free.

Case 6: Myxofibrosarcoma (intermediate grade)

A 43-year-old premenopausal woman presented with a palpable left breast mass. Initial ultrasound demonstrated a well-defined, complicated cyst with internal septations and turbid content, without vascularity. Four months later, interval enlargement was observed, and repeat ultrasound demonstrated a heterogeneous, hypoechoic mass with internal flowing echoes and vascularity.

Open excision was performed, and pathology revealed intermediate-grade myxofibrosarcoma with positive margins. The patient subsequently received adjuvant chemotherapy and radiotherapy. Although she remains alive, she later experienced multiple local recurrences and chemotherapy-related hemorrhagic cystitis.

Case 7: Liposarcoma (low grade)

An 18-year-old woman presented with a slowly growing right breast lump over 6 years. Ultrasound revealed a large hypoechoic mass with indistinct margins. A previous core biopsy from an outside hospital was interpreted as benign fat tissue. A repeat ultrasound-guided core biopsy demonstrated a low-grade myxoid neoplasm, and excision confirmed low-grade liposarcoma with S100+ and Desmin+ markers.

Following surgery, radiotherapy, and later breast reconstruction with lipofilling, the patient remains disease-free. This case highlights the slow progression and good prognosis of well-differentiated liposarcomas with appropriate treatment.

Case 8: Rhabdomyosarcoma (alveolar type)

An 18-year-old woman presented with a 1-year history of a left breast mass. Initial fine-needle aspiration from a supraclavicular lymph node at an outside hospital suggested a poorly differentiated neuroendocrine tumor, and the patient received chemotherapy. Despite treatment, the tumor progressively enlarged. Mammography demonstrated extremely dense breast tissue without suspicious calcifications, while ultrasound revealed numerous macrolobulated hypoechoic masses and heterogeneous nodules scattered throughout the left breast (Fig. 3). A left mastectomy subsequently confirmed high-grade spindle cell sarcoma. Follow-up CT demonstrated lung metastases.

Discussion

PBS is a rare malignancy with no established guidelines for standardized treatment due to its rarity[4]. Early detection and accurate diagnosis are critical to improving patient outcomes. Detection was achieved using multimodality imaging, primarily mammography and ultrasound, although imaging findings remained non-specific.

In our case series, the most frequent clinical presentation was a painless breast mass, consistent with prior studies[13–17]. Yang et al reported that 12% of PBS cases were asymptomatic and incidentally detected on mammograms[7]. Pain has been reported in 7–21% of cases[8,9]. In our series, pain typically occurred in patients with larger tumors (6, 9, and 14.3 cm), all of which were angiosarcomas.

Radiological features of PBS remain non-specific. Mammography commonly reveals round or oval masses with obscured, indistinct, or circumscribed margins. Spiculated margins and calcifications are uncommon, aligning with findings from previous studies[6]. One case was not identified on mammography due to extremely dense breast tissue. On ultrasound, PBS often presents as hypoechoic, round, or oval lesions with indistinct margins, without posterior attenuation. A smaller subset of cases exhibits hyperechoic patterns or heterogeneous echotextures, which some studies also reported[7,18].

A retrospective study by Kieferd et al. compared the diagnostic accuracy of 91 incisional biopsies and 102 core needle biopsies (CNBs). The analysis found that incisional biopsies and CNBs achieved comparable accuracy rates, with 96% and 97.6%, respectively. Importantly, CNBs were also associated with faster diagnosis and less physical burden on patients, making them a more practical choice[19]. Although tissue biopsy remains essential for diagnosis, PBS may still be difficult to diagnose because of tumor heterogeneity, vascularity, and overlapping histological features[20,21]. In our series, two lesions were initially diagnosed as benign entities, including fibrocystic change/hemangioma and benign fat tissue. One additional case yielded non-diagnostic hemorrhagic material on the initial biopsy, while another was initially diagnosed as a poorly differentiated neuroendocrine tumor on fine-needle aspiration before subsequent core biopsy and immunohistochemical analysis confirmed alveolar rhabdomyosarcoma. These discrepancies were likely related to inadequate tissue sampling and overlapping histopathological features. In several cases, the initial biopsy suggested a malignant mesenchymal lesion but was insufficient for definitive subtype classification until repeat biopsy, surgical excision, or additional immunohistochemical analysis was performed.

This study’s limitations include a small sample size and its retrospective design from a single institution, which may limit generalizability. Additionally, not all patients underwent imaging with all modalities, potentially affecting the completeness of imaging data. Despite these limitations, this case series contributes valuable insights into the clinical, radiological, and pathological characteristics of PBS.

Conclusion

PBSs are rare, aggressive tumors often defined by their rapid growth, making early recognition and intervention crucial. Effective management requires accurate diagnosis through imaging and biopsy, with IHC providing vital confirmation.
