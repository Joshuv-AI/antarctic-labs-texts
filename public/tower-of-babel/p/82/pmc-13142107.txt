Residue depletion and withdrawal period determination of fluralaner in edible tissues and eggs following oral administration in laying Hens
Authors: Tingting Chen, Luyao Wang, Yinuo Zhang, Mengdi Zhang, Zhuo Chen, Shuai Zhang, Fengting Lang, Zhihui Hao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To investigate the residue depletion kinetics and establish the withdrawal period of fluralaner in chickens and eggs, a sensitive and reliable liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) method was developed for the quantitative determination of fluralaner residues in edible chicken tissues and eggs. Furthere more, fifty healthy Hy-Line Brown laying hens were selected and received fluralaner solution via drinking water at a dosage of 0.5 mg/kg body weight on the 1st day and 8th day. At 7 days after the final administration, fluralaner residues in all edible tissues declined to levels below their respective maximum residue limits (MRLs). Using WT1.4 software and calculations based on the 95 % confidence interval, the withdrawal periods for fluralaner in muscle, liver, kidney, skin/fat were 12.19, 8.06 10.67 and 4.60 days, respectively. On day 7 after the final administration, the maximum observed fluralaner residue concentration in eggs was 890.72 µg/kg. This level was below the established MRL for eggs (1300 µg/kg) as stipulated by both the Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Affairs of China and the European Union. Moreover, the fluralaner concentration at each time point in eggs remained below 1300 µg/kg at 95 % confidence interval. Considering inter-individual variability, age related metabolic differences, and in reference to the approved withdrawal and egg-discard periods of the originator formulation Exzolt®, a withdrawal period of 14 days was established for the self-prepared fluralaner solution in chickens, while the egg-discard period was determined to be 0 days. Current study provides a scientific and regulatory basis for the rational use of fluralaner in laying hens and supports the establishment of an appropriate withdrawal period and egg-discard strategy in layer production systems.

Introduction

Fluralaner, carbamoyl benzamide phenyl isoxazoline, is an ectoparasiticide of the isoxazoline class which acts as an antagonist on ligand-gated chloride channels (gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) receptor and glutamate receptor) (Gassel et al., 2014; Yamato et al., 2020; Zhou et al., 2022). It has demonstrated a favorable safety profile and prolonged efficacy against Dermanyssus gallinae (poultry red mite) in controlled experimental and field studies (Mul et al., 2009; Sparagano et al., 2009; Williams et al., 2014). Fluralaner was first launched in 2014 (EMA, 2014). At present, fluralaner (Bravecto, Merck Animal Health) is approved in the United States for the treatment of flea and tick infestations in dogs and cats, and is available as chewable tablets and a topical spot-on formulation. In Europe and Australia, fluralaner (Exzolt®, Merck Animal Health) is the only isoxazoline-class medicinal product approved for use in poultry (CVMP, 2017). It can be administered via drinking water on farms for the treatment of poultry red mite infestation in chicks, breeder flocks, and laying hens (Australian Pesticides and Veterinary Medicines Authority, 2019; EMA, 2017; González-Morales et al., 2023). Due to its relatively favorable safety profile, it has been widely used overseas (Prohaczik A et al., 2017).

Fluralaner is metabolized in animals primarily by the liver; however, it has disadvantages including a low clearance rate, a propensity for accumulation, and relatively high residue levels. Prolonged use at supratherapeutic doses may induce neurological dysfunction in mammals (Gaens D et al., 2019). Reportedly, fluralaner residues are highest in the liver, skin/fat, followed by the kidneys, and are lowest in muscle. Fluralaner is metabolized into multiple metabolites. According to EMA evaluations, fluralaner undergoes oxidative metabolism followed by conjugation, yielding hydroxylated and sulfate-conjugated metabolites (EMA, 2017). Among all metabolites formed in chickens, the parent compound fluralaner and the major metabolite fluralaner carboxylic acid constitute the predominant components. Fluralaner is eliminated mainly via feces, with only limited excretion in urine. To standardize the use of fluralaner and reduce the potential risk of drug residues to human health, countries worldwide have progressively established maximum residue limits (MRLs) for fluralaner in various animal tissues. The marker residue is the parent compound fluralaner. The European Union specifies MRLs for fluralaner in chicken skin/fat, liver, kidney, muscle, and eggs of 650, 650, 420, 60 and 1300 µg/kg, respectively, whereas China specifies MRLs for chicken skin/fat, liver, kidney, and eggs of 650, 650, 420, and 1300 µg/kg, respectively (European Union, 2017; Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Affairs, 2023).

Recently, we developed a oral fluralaner solution for the treatment of ectoparasitic mite infestations in chickens, and the residue depletion profile and withdrawal period of this formulation remain to be investigated. Although maximum residue limits for fluralaner have been established in edible tissues (muscle: 65 μg/kg; kidney: 420 μg/kg; skin/fat:650 μg/kg; liver: 650 μg/kg) and eggs (1300 μg/kg), the absence of scientifically validated withdrawal periods and egg discard strategies represents a critical gap for risk assessment and regulatory decision making. Given the compound's prolonged persistence, low clearance rate, and propensity for accumulation in metabolically active tissues, a systematic evaluation of its residue depletion behavior in laying hens is essential. Therefore, the present study aimed to develop and validate a sensitive LC-MS/MS method for the quantification of fluralaner residues in edible tissues and eggs, to characterize its depletion kinetics following oral administration, and to establish scientifically justified withdrawal periods and egg discard recommendations. The findings are intended to support rational drug use, ensure food safety, and provide a regulatory reference for the potential future authorization of fluralaner in layer production systems..

Materials and methods

Reagents and chemicals

Fluralaner solution (Lot: 202401061, 1 %) was supplied by Shandong Sinder Technology Co., Ltd. (Weifang, China); Fluralaner working standard (Lot: FLLN-202201; purity 99.6 %) was provided by China Agricultural University (Beijing, China); Internal standard Fluralaner-D4 (Lot: FS1649005, 99.9 % purity) was purchased from Alta Scientific Co., Ltd. (Tianjin, China). All other reagents and chemicals were of analytical grade or higher and were obtained from certified commercial suppliers.

Animal experiments

Fifty healthy Hy-Line Brown laying hens with a body weight exceeding 1.5 kg, including twenty hens at the peak of egg production and thirty hens at the pre-lay stage, were maintained under controlled environmental conditions (temperature 15-28°C; relative humidity 55-75 %) with free access to feed and water. All the hens were allowed a one-week acclimation period to adjust to the new environment. All experimental procedures were approved by the Animal Use and Protection Committee of China Agricultural University (Approval No. AW61705202-2-02) and were conducted in accordance with national guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals. Thirty laying hens were allocated to the tissue residue depletion study, while twenty hens at peak lay were assigned to the egg residue depletion study to account for physiological differences in drug distribution and elimination associated with egg production. All experimental hens received fluralaner solution via drinking water at a dosage of 0.5 mg/kg body weight on the 1st day and 8th day. Prior to each administration, the hens were subjected to a two-hour fasting period and a six-hour water restriction. Throughout the experimental period, the hens were provided with a standard complete feed free of any additional medications, and water was available ad libitum.

The dosing regimen was as follows: For the tissue residue depletion study, thirty laying hens were divided into six cages, with five hens per cage. Based on a typical autumn/winter water-to-feed ratio of 1.8∼2.0:1 and an average feed intake of 110–120 g, the daily water consumption for each laying hen is approximately 200-240 mL. According the minimum volume of 200 mL, the total water intake for five hens was calculated as 1 L. Based on a dosage of 0.05 mL/kg b.w., the total volume of fluralaner solution required for each cage was calculated, diluted 10-fold with sterile water, and accurately measured using a pipette. This diluted solution was then mixed into 500 mL of drinking water (half of the daily intake) to ensure complete consumption within 2 hours to guarantee dosing accuracy; For the egg residue depletion study, twenty laying hens were housed individually (one per cage). The fluralaner solution was administered via gavage (oral intubation) using the same dosage calculation method.

Hens were humanely euthanized by exsanguination following jugular vein severance in accordance with approved ethical protocols. Egg Residue Elimination Test: egg samples (one egg per hen) were collected daily throughout the entire dosing period, as well as on 1st, 3rd, 5th, 7th, 9th, and 12th days after the second administration. Sample extraction method

Weighed (2 ± 0.02) g of muscle tissue and (1 ± 0.01) g each of liver, kidney, skin/fat, and egg into 50 mL centrifuge tubes separately. Then, 10 μg/mL of the internal standard solution of fluralaner-D4 was added: 30 μL to the muscle sample and 100 μL to the other samples. Subsequently, 10 mL of 80 % acetonitrile was added to each tube, followed by vortex mixing and ultrasonic extraction for 15 min. Then placed 1 g of sodium chloride and centrifuged the samples at 12,000 rpm for 10 minutes. Added 5 mL of n-hexane to the supernatant and centrifuged at the same condition. Discard the n-hexane layer and make up the extract to 10 mL with 80 % acetonitrile solution. Take a specified volume of extract (muscle: 5 mL, other tissues: 1 mL) and evaporate to dryness under nitrogen at 40°C. The residue was then reconstituted in 1 mL of 0.2 % formic acid in a water-acetonitrile mixture (20:80, v/v). The solution was centrifuged, filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane and subjected to LC-MS/MS analysis.

Method validation

Specificity. Specificity was evaluated by comparing chromatograms of solvent, blank tissues and egg, spiked tissues and standard solutions, to determine whether any solvent or matrix components interfered with the target analytes at their respective retention times.

LOD and LOQ. Standard working solutions at known concentrations were spiked into blank tissue samples in decreasing concentration levels. Each concentration was tested in six replicates. The processed samples were analyzed by LC-MS/MS. The lowest concentration in the tissues and egg with a signal-to-noise ratio (S/N) ≥ 3 was defined as the limit of detection (LOD), while the lowest concentration with S/N ≥ 10 defined as the limit of quantification (LOQ).

Calibration Curve. Matrix-matched calibration curves were constructed by spiking blank samples of muscle, liver, kidney, skin/fat, and egg with fluralaner working standard solutions at six concentration levels. After processing according to the described method, samples were analyzed by LC-MS/MS. Calibration curves were generated by plotting the peak area ratio of the analyte to the internal standard (y-axis) against the spiked concentration (x-axis). Linear regression analysis was performed using a 1/x weighting factor, and regression equations and correlation coefficients were calculated.

Accuracy and Precision. Accuracy and precision were assessed by spiking blank samples of muscle, liver, skin/fat, kidney, and egg with fluralaner standard at four concentration levels. For each concentration, six replicates were prepared, and the experiments were repeated in three batches (at least two days). Intra-batch and inter-batch accuracy (expressed as recovery) and precision (expressed as coefficient of variation) were evaluated in accordance with international residue analytical validation guidelines.

Stability. The stability of fluralaner in edible tissues and eggs was evaluated under three conditions: three freeze-thaw cycles, room temperature storage, and frozen storage at −20°C. Analyte concentrations were quantified using accompanying freshly prepared standard curves, and changes in concentration were assessed by comparing treated samples with their initial concentrations.

Statistical Analysis

Residue concentrations are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Withdrawal periods were calculated using WT 1.4 software based on the 95 % confidence interval, while statistical analyzes were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 22.0.

No interfering peaks were observed in solvent blanks or blank matrix samples of muscle, liver, kidney, skin/fat, or eggs at the retention times of fluralaner and the internal standard (fluralaner-D4). The limits of detection (LOD) and quantification (LOQ) in muscle were 5 and 10 μg/kg, respectively. In liver, kidney, skin/fat, and egg, the LODs were all 20 μg/kg and LOQs reached 50 μg/kg. Refer to the 14-day withdrawal period of the original reference drug, in consideration of inter-individual variability in drug disposition, age related metabolic differences among laying hens, and the application of a conservative regulatory safety margin, a withdrawal period of 14 days was established for fluralaner residues to adequately guarantee food safety in edible chicken tissues. Discussion

The present study comprehensively characterized fluralaner residue depletion in edible tissues and eggs of laying hens following oral administration via drinking water and established scientifically justified withdrawal and egg-discard periods based on statistically robust residue depletion modeling. Using a validated LC-MS/MS analytical method and WT 1.4 software at the 95 % confidence level, this work addresses a critical data gap for fluralaner use in poultry under conditions relevant to regulatory evaluation and food safety assurance.

At present, relatively few studies have reported on fluralaner residues. The available literature indicates that fluralaner metabolism and depletion vary across animal species. Following oral administration in dogs, the elimination half-life ranges from approximately 11 to 13 days; in cats the half-life appears to be longer, whereas in black bears the elimination half-life (t1/2) is approximately half of that observed in dogs (Kilp et al., 2016; Wilkinson et al., 2021). Previous studies have reported that fluralaner is metabolized predominantly as the parent compound in vivo, with about 90 % excreted via feces (Sari AB et al., 2022). In addition, fluralaner is eliminated more rapidly in rats than in dogs. The duration of fluralaner activity in captive wild carnivores may also differ by species. In general, larger animals exhibit longer elimination times (Wilkinson V et al., 2021): lions have a substantially longer elimination half-life than other species, whereas fluralaner disappears rapidly from the feces of smaller species (meerkats, coatis, and otters).

Metabolism studies in laying hens have shown that fluralaner exhibits the highest concentrations of total radioactive residues (TRR) in liver tissue, followed by skin/fat, kidney, and muscle, and it is eliminated primarily via feces (Xiong et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2020). The assessment report for Exzolt® approved by the Committee for Medicinal Products for Veterinary Use (CVMP), as published on the EMA website (European public MRL assessment report (EPMAR) Fluralaner (poultry), 2017), indicated that in a laying-hen study, the highest fluralaner residue levels were observed 1 day after dosing in the liver (906.60∼1963.00 µg/kg) and skin/fat (977.80∼1526.00 µg/kg), followed by the kidney (582.10∼1203.00 µg/kg), with lower concentrations in muscle (75.60∼152.20 µg/kg); In this study, an evaluation of the residue depletion kinetics of fluralaner in the edible tissues of laying hens showed that, at 6 hours after the second administration, fluralaner concentrations in all tissues reached their peak levels. The residue levels ranked, from highest to lowest, as liver (947.48∼1121.55 µg/kg), kidney (673.39∼1046.55 µg/kg), skin/fat (205.79576.44 µg/kg), and muscle (198.59∼315.35 µg/kg). Residue levels in tissues other than skin/fat were consistent with those reported for Exzolt®.

Subsequently, residues in all tissues depleted at the same rate. By day 10 after the final administration of Exzolt®, concentrations had decreased to 133.10∼348.60 µg/kg in liver, 122.10∼418.10 µg/kg in skin/fat, 83.71∼253.50 µg/kg in kidney, and 16.32∼40.39 µg/kg in muscle, all below the respective maximum residue limits (MRLs). By day 7 post-treatment cessation, fluralaner residues in all tissues of the self-formulated solution had fallen below maximum residue limits (MRLs). This indicates that fluralaner depletion from tissues is faster in the self-formulated solution than in Exzolt®, potentially attributable to formulation characteristics. These findings are generally consistent with those reported in the literature (Enomoto H et al., 2024). Ling Yihan et al. (2024) investigated the residue depletion of fluralaner tablets in laying hens and found that fluralaner residues were highest in chicken skin/fat, were broadly comparable in liver and kidney, and were lowest in muscle. This is due to the difference in dosage form between tablets and solutions. It is also reported that fluralaner residue concentrations were higher in males than in females. Our study did not investigate the effect of gender on the elimination of fluralaner residues in chickens, further exploration in this area may be pursued in subsequent research.

Consistent with previous studies, this study has found that the depletion rate of fluralaner varies significantly across different tissues, which may be attributable to the physicochemical properties of fluralaner and tissue-specific physiological factors. Fluralaner is a highly lipophilic compound with an octanol–water partition coefficient (logP) of approximately 5.1, indicating a tendency to distribute into and persist within lipid-rich environments (EMA, 2022). In addition, fluralaner exhibits near-complete plasma protein binding (approximately 100 %) and therefore circulates predominantly in the bound form, with an extremely low free fraction; these further influence inter-tissue distribution kinetics and metabolic clearance efficiency (Kilp et al., 2016). Adipose tissue exhibits relatively low blood flow and sluggish metabolic activity, resulting in the slow release of drugs upon entry. Consequently, these substances are difficult to promptly transport to metabolic organs such as the liver for biotransformation and excretion. This mechanism contributes to the slower elimination of fluralaner from the liver and skin/fat.

This study found that fluralaner residues in eggs peaked on day 7 after treatment cessation (890.72 µg/kg). However, throughout the entire sampling period, fluralaner residues in all egg samples did not exceed the maximum residue limit (MRL; 1300 µg/kg) stipulated by the national standard and the European Union. A residue depletion study of Exzolt® in laying hens reported that the maximum mean level in eggs was reached on day 7 after the first administration (610.3 µg/kg). After the second administration, the highest fluralaner concentration in eggs was 828.4 µg/kg, and all values remained below the limit of quantification. This is consistent with the findings of the present study, indicating that after oral administration fluralaner distribution in egg products is relatively stable and does not exhibit a sustained accumulation trend. This may be closely related to the physicochemical characteristics of fluralaner: although it has very high plasma protein binding and strong lipophilicity, its transport from the ovary to the yolk appears to be limited, resulting in only limited accumulation in eggs. In addition, it has been reported that the withdrawal intervals for fluralaner in whole eggs following intravenous (0.5 mg/kg) and transdermal administration (mean 58.7 mg/kg) were 7 days and 81 days, respectively. This suggests that different routes of administration, even in the same breed of laying hens, can influence the depletion of fluralaner residues in eggs.

Based on the evaluation of fluralaner residue depletion in different tissues and in eggs, this study established a 14-day withdrawal period for the fluralaner solution in chickens. The egg-discard period was set at 0 days, which is consistent with the requirements specified for the originator product. During clinical use of fluralaner, the withdrawal- and egg-discard-period management recommendations proposed in this study should be strictly followed, and overdosing or overly frequent administration should be avoided to prevent the risk of residue exceedances. Regulatory authorities may also use the findings of this study as a reference when conducting risk assessments and developing relevant standards, thereby more effectively safeguarding consumer health and ensuring the quality and safety of poultry and egg products.

Conclusion

A sensitive and reliable LC-MS/MS method was developed and validated for the determination of fluralaner residues in chicken muscle, liver, kidney, skin/fat, and eggs. Residue depletion results showed that fluralaner concentrations in all edible tissues declined below their respective maximum residue limits by day 7 after the final administration and fell below the limits of quantification by day 14. Withdrawal periods calculated using WT 1.4 software at the 95 % confidence level ranged from 4.60 to 12.19 days across tissues; therefore, applying a conservative food safety margin, a unified withdrawal period of 14 days was established for edible tissues. Residue levels in eggs remained consistently below the regulatory maximum residue limit throughout the study, supporting a zero-day egg-discard period. The residue depletion was evaluated under a single dosing regimen and administration route, and extrapolation to alternative dosing scenarios or field conditions could be approached in further studies.
