Recent aqueous alteration associated to sedimentary volcanism on Mars
Authors: M. Pineau, J. Carter, A. Lagain, E. Ravier, N. Mangold, L. Le Deit, C. Quantin-Nataf, A. Zanella
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Sedimentary volcanism, whereby material is brought to the surface by fluid overpressure, has been proposed to explain some of the periglacial landforms, including pitted cones, in the Northern Plains of Mars. However, in the absence of convincing mineralogical evidence, the origin for these deposits has never been conclusively determined. Here we conduct a remote sensing-based mineralogical survey to identify hydrated minerals within the Thumbprint Terrains and neighbouring Vastitas Borealis Formation. We detect several occurrences of hydrated silica along with sulfate salts in candidate mud volcano-like morphologies which likely formed during the Early Amazonian period, supporting the sedimentary volcanism origin. Buoyancy-driven analytical modelling suggests the hydrated silica and sulfate salts are sourced from reservoirs at depths of several 10 s and 100 s of metres, respectively below the Thumbprint Terrains and Vastitas Borealis Formation. The exposed sulfates may have been derived from ancient buried evaporite deposits suggesting, at least locally, a salt-rich aqueous origin for the Vastitas Borealis Formation, and would be consistent with the presence of a past northern ocean on Mars.

Introduction

The Noachian (4.1−3.8 Ga) is a geological period where comparatively warmer and wetter climates enabled at least intermittently liquid water to chemically modify the crust of Mars1–3. Around the Noachian-Hesperian transition (3.8−3.6 Ga), Mars has undergone a loss of its atmosphere, declining surface water activity, and experienced increasingly arid and cold climates1,4. However, there is an emerging perception that the Martian hydrosphere, while waning since the Hesperian, may have been able to persist and occasionally leave behind traces of aqueous activity well into the Hesperian and perhaps even later during the Amazonian (<3.1 Ga)5–13. There is however little indication for late-stage surface water activity, especially in the lowlands and Northern Plains (NPs) of Mars, which are the dominant Amazonian units14. Exploration of the young NPs has the potential to answer key questions regarding the fate of water on Mars and the timing of its activity, including the presence of lingering subsurface aqueous reservoirs that have not been subject to atmospheric escape15. Quantitatively, such reservoirs remain poorly constrained for lack of direct evidence. Their ability to have been remobilized in liquid form at or below the surface is also a critical knowledge gap because this would imply a heightened habitability potential for recent Mars16, as well as provide anchor points in the Mars timeline with respect to the stability of water in the near subsurface and surface.

To address these questions, we conducted a mineralogical survey of the NPs in search of specific tracers of aqueous activity. We focused our survey on the Late-Hesperian to Early-Amazonian “Vastitas Borealis Formation” (VBF), which has been dated to 3.75–3.40 Ga17–19 (Fig. 1). The VBF has been interpreted as a depositional unit resulting from outflow channel floods or an ancient hydrological reservoir20,21 (Fig. 1A). In regions such as Acidalia, Utopia, Isidis and Chryse Planitiae, the VBF is associated with the striking morphological unit called the Thumbprint Terrains (TT) that exhibits sets of parallel ridges and curvilinear features overprinted by clusters or chains of pitted cones and mounds22,23. Several hypotheses have been proposed for the TT formation, among others: explosive volcanism resulting in cinder cones, pseudo-craters24 or glacial processes resulting in pingos and moraines22,25. Others suggest the presence of mudflows coupled with mud volcanism26–29. Impact-generated tsunami deposits in a Hesperian-Amazonian paleo-ocean were also proposed30–33, in agreement with Parker et al. (1993) hypothesis34. While these features are suggestive of water activity, conclusive compositional evidence was lacking as no hydrated minerals were detected (Supplementary Text 1). Summarized methods

Our survey was driven by remote sensing data from the Compact Reconnaissance Imaging Spectrometer for Mars (CRISM)35 onboard the Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter (MRO) spacecraft. Expanding from a coarser survey across the entire surface of the planet12, we relied on high-resolution observations (spatial resolution <40 m/pixel), searching for hydrated mineral spectral signatures as tracers of aqueous alteration. The geomorphological context of each mineral detection was ascertained by combining ortho-rectified CRISM mineral maps with orbital imagery from the Context Camera (CTX)36, the High-Resolution Imaging Science Experiment (HiRISE)37 as well as the Colour and Stereo Surface Imaging System (CaSSIS)38. We also estimate grain sizes at the surface based on models of thermal inertia (TI) of the surfaces derived from Mars Odyssey’s Thermal Emission Imaging System (THEMIS)39. Surface ages were also estimated by model age standard crater counting. The methods are detailed in Methods section and Supplementary Text 2.

HySi is found within the TT to be associated with 10 s of cones which are several meters high, and at times feature a pitted summit. HySi is preferentially found on the cone summits (Fig. 2A–D; Fig. 3; Supplementary Fig. 1). Figure 2A also shows HySi as surficial fine-grained materials that are eroded or expelled from the nearby cones. Despite this example, for most HySi-bearing cones, no obvious extensive flow-like features are observed. While CRISM data do not show systematic detections of hydrated minerals on all cones, the shared similarities between CRISM-based detections and cones and others observed in HiRISE and CaSSIS images (textures, grain-sizes, colour hues) do suggest that many more pitted cones and related surrounding material in the region could contain HySi. In rare cases, small impact craters exhuming the flanks of cones give us a glimpse into their internal hydrated composition (Fig. 3, FRT0000A386 (2) inset).

In contrast to HySi, PHS are located in morphologically young impact craters, where the strongest signatures are in their flanks and ejecta deposits (Fig. 2A, C, D; Supplementary Fig. 1). These craters, ranging from 300 m to 3 km in diameter, generally present lobate ejecta suggesting that they impacted volatile-rich material43. Another context for PHS is illustrated in Fig. 2B which displays PHS detections in association with a large 165 meters-high mound (unpitted) with HySi at its base. In-depth spectral characterization of the minerals

Our main finding is the presence of widespread detections of HySi within and around pitted cones. Their nature and origin can be further inferred from the type of silica and geochemical conditions in which they have formed. To do this, we used spectral discrimination criteria on the CRISM data that are adapted to HySi spectral signature44–47: the minima position of their absorption bands, their relative area, and their shape using the Concavity-Ratio-Criterion (CRC) (see Methods and Supplementary Text 3). In the diagram of Fig. 8A, observations fall within the bottom-left corner that is characteristic of HySi formed through low-temperature (low-T) geological processes (T < 60 °C) such as surface weathering or low-T hydrothermal activity if we consider that the dehydration was limited, which is unfortunately impossible to verify with current orbital instruments. The data also fall in the cluster that is indicative of rather poorly hydrated or dehydrated varieties of HySi.

In the truncated ternary diagram in Fig. 8B, HySi detections associated with pitted cones exhibit properties diagnostic of poorly-hydrated silica (e.g., volcanic glasses or chalcedony) and/or moderately to highly dehydrated species (opal-A/CT under Mars atmospheric conditions), the data points have an intermediate position between the two clusters.

Considering these spectral observations, we favour the presence of poorly hydrated HySi varieties, mostly in an amorphous state, such as silica gels, altered volcanic glasses (e.g., fine-grained altered ashes or pumices), or HySi-rich silt-to-sand material that underwent low-T aqueous alteration and/or water-limited fluid-rock interaction. Essentially, we rule out hydrothermal alteration as their likely origin.

While the detection of PHS-rich deposits associated to impact craters and large mounds is straightforward, it is unfortunately difficult to assess their precise composition. Indeed, PHS spectra observed in the CRISM spectral range lack sufficient features to make a precise identification, other than the generic absorptions at 1.4, 1.9 and 2.4 µm (e.g., ref. 48; Figs. 4, 6). These generally agree with Ca- or Mg-rich poly-hydrated salts. We can confidently exclude Fe-rich salts, hydroxylated sulfates such as jarosite or alunite, or hydrated chlorinated salts.

Characteristics and origin of sedimentary volcanism

Our datasets demonstrate that the pitted cones, previously interpreted as mud volcano-like structures on the basis of morphology only29, can be confidently linked to the presence of hydrated minerals supporting an origin by sedimentary volcanism. On Earth, the mineralogy observed at the surface of sedimentary volcanoes depends on the lithology of the buried source (e.g., clay-rich versus sand-rich) and the composition of the fluids accompanying the remobilized sediments. In terms of alteration mineralogy, with a notable NIR signature, terrestrial mud volcanoes would show a composition dominated by phyllosilicates (micas, smectites, kaolins), carbonates, sulfates and a low-to-moderate amount of amorphous phase (e.g., HySi)49–51. Injectites, extrudites and sand volcanoes, with coarser particles, are rather enriched in carbonates, clays, micas, and siliceous cements (e.g., chalcedony, HySi)52–54. The mineralogy we observe on Mars is devoid of clays and carbonates, thus different from that expected for terrestrial mud volcanoes. We investigate below all stages of the proposed sedimentary volcanism process: the nature and origin of the source material, the intrusion mechanism, and the extrusion conditions.

HySi formation and source reservoirs depths

In the context of sedimentary volcanism, several hypotheses could explain the HySi occurrence: i) pre-existing and pre-altered buried Si-rich sediments (such as altered ashes or pumices, silts or sands), ii) formation of aqueous minerals through alteration of Si-rich sediments during upwelling, or iii) formation at the surface after emplacement, iv) direct HySi precipitation from Si-saturated fluids reaching the surface. The process of aqueous alteration may or may not have been coeval with the upwelling process of the fluidized HySi reservoirs.

In the context of continental alteration by surface agents, we would expect the formation of hydrated phases over large areas and not localized in the pitted cones and observed ridges. This hypothesis is not consistent with the observations and therefore is ruled out. Instead, we propose that the surface occurrences of HySi are linked to the upwelling of sediments and fluids to the surface by sedimentary volcanism. This may have occurred from the extrusion of buried HySi-bearing sediments, and/or by direct precipitation of HySi from siliceous fluids.

Although it is difficult to estimate the amount of fluid associated with HySi precipitation, its presence is indicative of siliceous solutions from which HySi gels were formed. It is also possible to propose that pre-existing subsurface HySi-rich materials were extruded. Such types of Si-rich cones may be indicative of clastic pipe-like features52,55,56 such as in terrestrial sand-rich sedimentary volcanoes33,57–61 and/or hydrodynamic blowouts62–65. In the latter case the HySi sources are constrained by the thickness of the TT (< 100 m31,66) and therefore may be shallow.

We assess the depth of the sourced materials using a buoyancy-driven upwelling model applied to sedimentary volcanism67,68 (see Methods, Supplementary Text 5, and Supplementary Data 4). Given the limits allowed by the assumed thickness of the TT (< 100 m31,66), and whatever the considered densities for extruded materials and host rocks, we found that HySi-rich materials are sourced from several 10 s of meters underground (between 10 to 80 m), from subsurface reservoirs that are internal to the TT. Regarding other HySi-rich cones outside the TT, depths are less well constrained, but are likely ranging from a few tens of meters to a maximum of 200–300 m. These depths, in agreement with previous estimates68,69, attest to the presence of HySi-rich lithologies or siliceous aqueous reservoirs within the TT units (Fig. 9, top), but also to a possible presence of similar HySi-bearing sediments in the older VBF units. PHS formation and source reservoirs depths

PHS are in majority associated with impact craters with diameters ~1-3 km or less, and must have been formed through the excavation and subsequent sublimation of salt-rich ice in the regolith70. However, such small impacts also excavate materials beyond 80-90 meters depth71. In this case, the presence of PHS may indicate sulfate-rich reservoirs in the TT subsurface and/or in the surrounding/underlying VBF unit (Fig. 9, top). This could be inferred from observations where different impacts excavate PHS-rich materials in different settings (Fig. 2A,C,D,F).

PHS are also observed in association with the large Acidalia and Utopia mounds (Fig. 2B, F; Fig. 5; Fig. 7). Following the same rationale as for HySi formation, we rule out sulfate formation from regional continental weathering, and the positive topography excludes playa-like evaporitic settings. We discuss PHS formation through fluid-rich sedimentary volcanism independently for the Acidalia and Utopia edifices.

Regarding Acidalia, no flow feature is observed. Thus, this PHS-rich mound may have formed through sedimentary volcanism involving low amounts of fluid. The presence of surface fractures indicates possible gas-rich extrusion (Fig. 5). This could be indicative of PHS formation in fumarolic-like setting, but this hypothesis is not supported by the amagmatic geological context and mineralogy. Gas is common in terrestrial sedimentary volcanism as fluid overpressure of volatile-bearing sediments will be the main trigger of the extrusion29,50,55,72. Here again, the presence of pre-existing subsurface sulfates that were extruded through gas-rich (possibly explosive but unconfirmed here73–75) sedimentary volcanism is the most favoured hypothesis (Fig. 9, top). The boulder-rich nature of the materials constituting the mound is also in line with this (Fig. 5). When sedimentary volcanism occurs, it is common to have remobilization and incorporation of the intersected layers by the injected materials, resulting in a brecciated mixture of different lithologies29,72,76,77. The presence of HySi in the basal part of the cone from silica intake within the rising materials during the sedimentary volcanism activity is a case in point (Fig. 2B; Fig. 9, top).

In Utopia, geomorphological observations support more fluid-rich activity compared to Acidalia. The observation of radially extending surface flow features with PHS detections (Fig. 2F; Fig. 7) may indicate that the sedimentary volcanism context here is accompanied with higher fluid quantities (Fig. 9, bottom). The PHS in the flow features could represent detrital sulfate-rich materials remobilized from the cone or sulfates formed through evaporation of upwelled saline waters stagnating in ponds78. We do not observe ponding/evaporitic features such as small depressions or basin-like morphologies that may have been able to sustain stagnant salty waters. A more likely explanation would be that the mound represents upwelled sulfate-rich materials in an intrusive context, indicating that the subsurface may contain a deep salt-rich reservoir79–81 (Fig. 9, bottom).

Applying the same buoyancy-driven model to the PHS-rich mounds and cones of Acidalia and Utopia Planitiae respectively, we found that the PHS sources are 100 s meters deeper than the HySi sources, reaching up to a few kilometres. Their origin is therefore linked to deeper geological reservoirs within the VBF units (Supplementary Text 5; Fig. 9). These results are in the predicted ranges for Martian edifices69 and would imply the presence of sulfate salt-rich lithologies within the VBF layers (Fig. 9), possibly in the form of ancient evaporites.

Mechanism triggering sedimentary volcanism

The mechanisms that triggered the sedimentary volcanism responsible for the formation of the pitted cones and mounds remains uncertain. Although this study does not claim to identify a specific process, we do suggest that geothermal activity is unlikely. No igneous volcanic edifice exists in proximity to the sedimentary volcanoes and we do not observe evidence of hydrothermal processes such as found elsewhere at Mars based on the morphology, mineral makeup, and precise composition of the HySi. Further studies, including terrestrial analogue field investigations, will be required to more accurately identify the geological processes responsible for the fluid overpressure and subsequent mobilization of volatile-bearing sediments. Contenders include rapid and sudden sedimentation, long-term load and subsidence, seismic shaking (including impact-induced marsquakes), tectonic shortening (i.e., fractures/faults formation, possibly induced by large-scale sediment compaction), mineral phase changes, permafrost development/destabilization and/or (peri)glacial processes, among others25,27,29,31–33,42,56,69,82–88.

Model age measurements suggest ancient sediments in young structures

Crater count-based dating of the Acidalia’s TT (see Methods, Fig. 10 and Supplementary Text 2,6) indicate an Early-Amazonian age (2.3 + /−0.3 Ga), similar to what was previously proposed33. For Utopia, we found similar ages (2.2 + /−0.5 Ga), consistent with the Early Amazonian period. While TT are Amazonian in age, the surrounding VBF terrains are dated back to the Early Hesperian period20. There is a correlation between observed structures, their potential ages of formation, and the hydrated phases extruded by sedimentary volcanism. Indeed, the HySi-bearing cones are related to shallow reservoirs (<100 m) and are mostly associated with the youngest (Early Amazonian) TT. It is important to note that the depths that are suggested here represent first-order minimal depths. Thus, it is possible that even deeper sources are involved85, which would then indicate access of even older materials and sediments in these sedimentary volcanoes. Notably, model ages for PHS-rich mounds are Early-Amazonian, but extraction depths indicate that they are able to sample deeply buried and therefore significantly older sediments. Conclusions

The sites reported here exhibit similar morphological and mineralogical characteristics indicating shared geological processes that have long been proposed as being indicative of sedimentary volcanism18,19,26,29,56,74,82,88–93. This study presents the first evidence for the presence of hydrated phases, namely hydrated silica and sulfates, in association with pitted cones and mounds in the NPs, confirming the sedimentary volcanism origin for these structures. The ages of these landforms imply that Early Amazonian Mars allowed conditions for fluids to reach the surface and flow to some limited spatial and temporal extents. In addition, we demonstrate the existence of buried aqueous reservoirs of both siliceous- and sulfate-rich materials. These were present in the NPs by the Amazonian and Hesperian periods, respectively.

While our study focused on the NPs, we did also look at other geological edifices in other lowlands (including pitted cones) that have been proposed to be Hesperian- and Amazonian-age sedimentary volcanoes. These are located in Chryse Planitia94,95 (19°8’N; 37°4’W) and Hydraotes Chaos87 (0°13’N; 33°49’W). In both cases, the pitted cones exhibit similar HySi detections (Supplementary Fig. 3). This strongly suggests that, in the same manner, sedimentary volcanism existed even more pervasively during Late Mars (see Supplementary Text 4,5 and Supplementary Data 1,2,4 for details and data).

Our findings may open up new perspectives for future Mars science and exploration missions. Exploring these relatively accessible sediments will enable access to aqueous subsurface reservoirs of the NPs that may resolve the debate about the presence of putative buried ocean-related sediments. This would also provide inputs on climate modelling to understand fluid stability in Late Martian history and to better characterize the nature of these subsurface fluids95.

The presence of HySi-rich deposits in such contexts is of major astrobiological relevance. The buried sediments were protected from solar and cosmic radiation for most of Mars’ history96,97. The oldest terrestrial biosignatures have been observed in HySi phases, testifying to silica’s great capacity to protect biomolecules over geological time scales97–103. Martian HySi-bearing sedimentary volcanoes therefore represent a double benefit for past water and astrobiological studies.

Methods

Mineral identifications at Mars

The primary data source for the Mars mineral detections is from the CRISM instrument onboard MRO35. We processed quasi-nadir CRISM (onboard MRO) TRDR “targeted” observations, with observing modes “FRT”, “HRL”, “FRS” and “HRS” as described in Murchie et al. (2007)35 and on the CRISM website (http://crism.jhuapl.edu/data/publicData.php). These correspond to small areas of Mars (less than 200 km²) mapped at better than 40 m/pixel spatial sampling, and with spectral sampling around 6.56 nm in the NIR range used in this study (1.0–2.6 µm). CRISM also comprises a visible-near infrared “S” channel which was also processed at each location of the Northern Plains (NP) where alteration minerals were found, to search for spectral counterparts in the visible range spectrum.

Lower spatial and spectral resolution “multispectral” CRISM nadir infrared data were occasionally used to search for mineralogy in the largest structures of interest (using observing modes “MSP” and “HSP”), but yielded no additional detections and so were not reported in this study. OMEGA data from Mars Express was also used at its highest achievable spatial resolution, which did not contribute additional detections.

We processed a large fraction of the available CRISM infrared TRDR in the NP. The targeted nature of the CRISM observations does not preclude bias in the sample selection because the NP of Mars are not contiguously covered by CRISM, and certain landforms were likely preferentially targeted. However, over the 100 s of observations analysed, the full range of longitudes and latitudes were investigated, as well as many recognized geological units and landforms, mitigating that bias. It does however imply that the altered landforms reported here are likely many orders of magnitude more common on Mars than detected currently, as they were not mapped with (good quality) CRISM data. We do not report any spectral counterpart in the visible channel of CRISM which correlates uniquely with the alteration signatures reported in the near-infrared channel. In particular, we searched for the crystalline hematite absorption band near 0.88 µm but found none in our detection dataset.

Data processing of the TRDR data follows similar steps as described in Carter et al. (2013)104. CRISM I/F data was retrieved through the ASU-hosted CRISM portal, then processed to reflectance, by accounting for the incidence angle and atmospheric absorptions using the “volcano-scan” method of Langevin et al. (2005)105. Our custom data reduction pipeline produces a “flat” spectral cube wherein the neutral mineralogy of the whole cube is assessed and removed at each pixel by a spectral division. The process is described in Torres et al. (2024)106. The mineral absorption bands are detected both using traditional band ratios and by measuring the spectral angles induced by the bands. Combination of spectral maps are made in order to extract more reliable and specific mineral detections, which produces the mineral maps of this paper, through a process described in Carter et al. (2023)12 which allows extracting average spectra for each spatially correlated cluster of detection, so-called “ROI spectra”. These are then compared to laboratory spectra of alteration minerals.

Terrestrial opaline silica reference samples

To better characterise opaline silica CRISM detections, we compared the extracted spectra with different terrestrial reference samples (terrestrial analogues spectra are available in Supplementary Data 3). We selected near infrared spectra from various studies44–47,107,108. The samples were classified according to the type of silica (volcanic glasses, opal-A, opal-CT, chalcedony) and their geological origin (low-temperature surface alteration or continental weathering, high-temperature hydrothermal activity, high-temperature volcanic activity, unknown or uncertain geological origin). Some samples were in the form of powders, others were in the form of polished sections/chips. Depending on the studies, spectra were acquired under terrestrial ambient atmospheric conditions (20–22 °C, 1 bar), or in Mars-relevant atmospheric conditions. Low-pressure experiments were conducted at 20 °C and 1–7 mbar to simulate non-systematic dehydration of silica at the surface of Mars. Low-temperature experiments were conducted at −90 to −50 °C at 1 bar to observe the signature of frozen opals (when structural water molecules turn into ice in cryogenic conditions). We also selected some spectra acquired at very low atmospheric pressure (22 °C; 0.016 mbar) to simulate extreme non-systematic dehydration of silica. The list of all terrestrial samples used as references is available in Supplementary Data 3, where the data are classified according to acquisition procedures and presented along with the calculated values of the various spectral criteria presented below.

Processing of near infrared spectra for HySi specie identification

Since terrestrial data were not acquired using the same instruments, and therefore not at the same spectral resolutions, all the terrestrial spectra have been degraded to the spectral resolution of the CRISM instrument (≈ 6.56 nm). Then, Martian CRISM data and terrestrial reference spectra were all processed in the same way before any calculation of spectral criteria. First, all spectra were smooth using the Savitzky-Golay method109,110. Least square polynomial regression of adjacent points was conducted with 3rd-degree polynomial functions and a moving average window of about 7 to 9 points/spectels, up to 12 in rare cases.

After smoothing, the three absorption bands of silica at 1.4, 1.9 and 2.2 microns were isolated from the smoothed spectra using two anchor points for each and fitted using two gaussian functions related to A-type or B-type of water in hydrated silica44,46,107,111. The anchor points used to truncate each absorption are the following: 1.315 and 1.605 µm for the 1.4 µm band, 1.815 µm and 2.132 µm for the 1.9 µm band, and 2.125 and 2.370 for the 2.2 µm band. To minimise the influence of spectral noise, instrumental artefacts and spike generated by the atmospheric correction in CRISM data, and continuum variations, Gaussian modelling was assisted by manual continuum adjustments, adding anchor points when necessary. Anchor points were added without modifying the shapes and areas of absorption bands.

Each processing step (e.g., smoothing, then modelling) has been tested multiple times by slightly modifying the parameters, while manually checking the obtained results. We found that the processing parameters described above give the best results without significantly and unrealistically modifying the spectra: band shapes, inflection points, local band minima were kept while maintaining a good signal-to-noise ratio, especially for CRISM data. In most cases, and regardless of the chosen parameters, the spectral results were always very close to each other.

Spectral criteria calculations

Band minima positions (minimum of reflectance)

The band minima position is useful in defining the type of silica, (de)hydration behaviour, or state of pore water44,46,107,108,112. The more the minimum is shifted at high wavelengths, the more crystalline the silica (opal-CT, chalcedony). Conversely, amorphous silica phases tend to be identified with minima at low wavelengths (opal-A, volcanic glasses). Shifts of the band minima position are also helpful for tracing any silica dehydration (shifts at lower wavelengths) or crystallisation of ice inside the silica porosity (shifts at higher wavelengths due to water ice signatures). Minimum search was performed over large ranges for the 1.4 and 1.9 µm absorption band to account for possible large shifts due to the presence of ice bands in possibly “frozen silica”108. Ranges are the following: from 1.34 µm to 1.58 µm for the 1.4 absorption and 1.87 µm to 2.05 µm for the 1.9 band.

Diagrams combining band minima positions of the 1.4 and 1.9 bands are available for terrestrial reference data in Supplementary Text 3. The fields are taken from previous studies44,46,47, refined with the present dataset. Results for CRISM data for the different geomorphological classes and prospected regions are presented in Supplementary Text 4.

Concavity-Ratio-Criterion (CRC)

Concavity-Ratio-Criterion (CRC) quantifies the shape of the high-wavelength side of the 1.4 and 1.9 absorption bands. This spectral parameter is useful to discern geological origins of opals regardless of their crystallinity. According to previous works44,46,47, high CRC values indicate opals formed by medium-to-high temperature hydrothermal activity (T > 60 °C) whereas low values are mostly indicative of opals formed by low-temperature hydrothermal activity or continental surface alteration (T < 60 °C).

For the 1.4 µm absorption, we chose two anchor points at λS ≈ 1.34 µm and λL ≈ 1.58 µm. Then, two band depths were calculated: BDC, the real band depth of the inflection point at ≈ 1.46 µm, and BDC*, the virtual band depth of the same inflection point using a linear continuum between the band minimum and the anchor point λL.

The retained value for the CRC is the average of all calculated values, and the error represents the standard deviation (1σ).

The obtained CRC values for terrestrial reference data can be combined with the band minima positions (MIN1.4 vs. CRC1.4 or MIN1.9 vs. CRC1.9) or between them (CRC1.4 vs. CRC1.9). The first combinations are useful to decipher between silica phases using band minima positions and geological origins using CRC values. The second combination is useful to assess geological origins with higher certainties. Note that in both cases, these diagrams can be helpful to determine putative occurrence of dehydrated silica or frozen silica46,108. The results for CRISM data for the different geomorphological classes and prospected regions are presented in Supplementary Text 4.

Relative area of absorption bands

The area of each absorption band was calculated as the sum of the two gaussian functions used for the fitting process. These areas were then converted into relative areas where the area of a single band is normalised to the sum of all band areas. Relative area contributions were then given in percent. These relative area values were used in the classification of silica-rich materials from Pineau et al. (2020)46, which is in the form of a ternary diagram, refined with the present dataset. The various fields obtained using terrestrial reference data are shown in Supplementary Text 3, and the results for CRISM data for the different geomorphological classes and prospected regions are presented in Supplementary Text 4. Note that this diagram is only helpful to decipher silica phases and degree of (de)hydration. No information on geological origin can be obtained here (except for volcanic glasses that are indicative of volcanic activity at first glance).

Surface grain size estimates

We have estimated possible particle size of extruded materials and surrounding embedding units in order to provide lithological constraints on these two. Particle size was estimated from empirical laws and thermal inertia (TI) observations following the method provided by Presley & Christensen (1997)113. The reasoning and procedure we used is detailed in Supplementary Text 2. The TI of the different surfaces of interest were extracted from TI maps generated from THEMIS data by the Thermophysical Analysis Tools for Mars Research (MARSTHERM, https://marstherm.psi.edu/) developed by the Planetary Science Institute in Tucson, Arizona, and other institutions114. Several dozen maps have been requested for different areas in Acidalia and Utopia Planitiae, where we detect mineral phases associated with sedimentary volcanism. For each mapped zone, TI are extracted from tables and presented as a +/− symmetrical normal distribution. Three TI values are calculated: an average TI, as well as minimum and maximum values (average +/− 2σ). These values are then injected into the equations provided in Presley & Christensen (1997)113 to calculate a grain size range (from minimum and maximum TI) and a central mean grain size. TI values and calculated grain sizes are synthesized in Supplementary Data 2.

Surface model age measurements

To check whether the sedimentary volcanism events investigated in this study occurred at the same periods on Acidalia and Utopia Planitiae, we carried out crater-count model age dating. We identify the regional units associated with the hydrated minerals local detections presented in this study using THEMIS day/night observations39 and refined crater count areas using CTX V01 mosaics118 [NASA/JPL/MSSS/The Murray Lab; https://murray-lab.caltech.edu/CTX/]. We derived their model age as “closest putative age” of sedimentary volcanism and formation of hydrated phases presented in this study. Given that the hydrated phases are observed in these cones/mounds, their exhumation is synchronous with the TT’ sedimentary volcanism. Under the assumption that these phases were therefore present within the TT’ stratigraphy, they predate sedimentary volcanism events and have an age comparable to the deposition age of the TT. It is possible that the massive activity of sedimentary volcanism, aeolian erosion and/or sedimentation may have erased traces of meteoritic impacts since the TT were deposited. Thus, the calculated ages represent a “retention age”, at best slightly posterior to the initial TT’s deposition, and anterior to the sedimentary volcanic events that extruded the hydrated phases. Further information on how the surfaces have been mapped are provided in Supplementary Text 2.

Areas are too small (<50,000 km²) to have retained enough kilometric craters, thus preventing the use of manual crater databases119,120 for age-dating121. The presence of too many circular structures (i.e., pitted cones and mounds) prevents the use of automatic crater mapping method122–125 for model age derivation. Moreover, units located in Acidalia Planitia are contaminated by secondary crater clusters originating from Mojave crater124. For these reasons, impact craters superimposed on, or whose ejecta overlap the various units, were manually mapped using the “CraterTools” add-on implemented in the ArcGIS software126. Crater counts were then processed into the CraterStatsII software127, where the crater size-frequency distributions (Crater SFD) were displayed in differential form and fitted with a production function from the Hartmann (2005)’s chronology system128 using the Poisson timing analysis method129. Crater SFDs, and results summarized in Supplementary Text 6, show a relatively good alignment with a production function (PF) for craters as small as 400-700 metres, with no correlation between the minimum diameter used to fit a PF with the crater data and obtained model ages. No areas smaller than 1000 km² were used to derive model ages as advocated by Warner et al. (2015)121. Model Age Derivation results are detailed in Supplementary Text 6.
