Navigating the HIV/AIDS epidemic in Bangladesh: A comprehensive retrospective analysis of epidemiological trends and sex-specific disparities (1990–2021) with long-term ARIMA forecasting to 2050 for strategic public health planning
Authors: Ibrahim Khalil, Md Imran Hossain, Sakib Abrar, Mst Mahmuda Akter, Supoma Ghosh Ria, M Rafiqul Islam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe HIV/AIDS epidemic remains a critical public health challenge in Bangladesh, with complex epidemiological trends and sex-specific disparities requiring detailed investigation to guide effective interventions. This study comprehensively analyzes the temporal dynamics of HIV/AIDS burden from 1990 to 2021 and employs advanced statistical modeling to forecast future trends up to 2050, aiming to inform targeted public health strategies.MethodsData on Disability-Adjusted Life Years (DALYs), deaths, incidence, prevalence, Years Lived with Disability (YLDs), and Years of Life Lost (YLLs) were sourced from a robust epidemiological database for Bangladesh. Joinpoint regression analysis was utilized to detect significant trend changes, calculating the average annual percent change (AAPC) and annual percent change (APC) with 95 % confidence intervals (CIs). Sex-stratified analyses elucidated disparities between males and females. Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA) models were applied to project age-standardized rates (ASRs) for each metric through 2050, incorporating historical trends and variability to ensure robust predictions.ResultsFrom 1990 to 2021, the HIV/AIDS burden in Bangladesh increased significantly across all metrics, with distinct sex-specific patterns. The overall AAPC for DALYs was 19.0332 % (95 % CI: 15.8145, 23.0026, p < 0.000001), with females showing a higher AAPC (21.7252 %, 95 % CI: 18.2308, 25.7026) than males (18.4703 %, 95 % CI: 15.1896, 22.5646). Deaths exhibited a similar trend, with an overall AAPC of 18.9645 % (95 % CI: 15.7008, 23.2166), higher in females (21.8808 %, 95 % CI: 18.4195, 25.9932) than males (18.4655 %, 95 % CI: 15.1992, 22.6832). Incidence rose with an AAPC of 15.7929 % (95 % CI: 11.6467, 19.1922), slightly higher in females (16.2581 %, 95 % CI: 12.1795, 20.0487) than males (15.1639 %, 95 % CI: 11.2952, 18.4817). Prevalence increased markedly (AAPC: 18.2239 %, 95 % CI: 14.5688, 22.2078), with females at 20.2887 % (95 % CI: 16.6453, 24.3006) and males at 18.1837 % (95 % CI: 14.6734, 21.841). YLDs and YLLs followed similar patterns, with females consistently showing higher AAPCs. Joinpoint analysis identified peak APCs in the 1990s and early 2000s, followed by moderated growth and declines post-2016, particularly from 2019 to 2021, reflecting potential intervention impacts. ARIMA forecasts project a decline in DALYs, deaths, and YLLs ASRs to negligible levels by 2050 for both sexes, with wide CIs indicating substantial uncertainty (e.g., DALYs overall: negligible, 95 % CI: 0, 583.3697). Incidence ASRs are expected to stabilize (e.g., overall: 0.963899, 95 % CI: 0, 3.819279), while prevalence ASRs are projected to rise dramatically, particularly for males (614.7463, 95 % CI: 0, 695680.4), highlighting significant long-term challenges.ConclusionsThe HIV/AIDS burden in Bangladesh has escalated significantly from 1990 to 2021, with females bearing a disproportionately higher burden across all metrics. Recent declines suggest the efficacy of public health interventions, but persistent sex disparities and projected prevalence increases underscore the need for targeted, sex-specific strategies. The considerable uncertainty in long-term forecasts emphasizes the importance of sustained surveillance, adaptive interventions, and resource allocation to mitigate the epidemic's impact by 2050, ensuring equitable health outcomes.Keywords: HIV/AIDS, Epidemiological burden, Disparities, ARIMA forecasting, Retrospective analysis

Introduction

The human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) and acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS) constitute a persistent global health challenge, with profound implications for morbidity, mortality, and socioeconomic burdens.1 In Bangladesh, a densely populated South Asian nation with over 170 million people, the HIV/AIDS epidemic remains concentrated among key populations such as people who inject drugs (PWID), sex workers, men who have sex with men (MSM), and transgender individuals, with an estimated national adult prevalence of less than 0.1 % as of 2023, according to UNAIDS data—significantly lower than the 4.9 % average in sub-Saharan Africa.1,2 However, this low prevalence masks a rising burden, with new infections increasing by 21 % between 2010 and 2022, contrasting with a 49 % global decline in new infections since 2010.1 The epidemic has evolved within a complex interplay of socio-cultural, economic, and healthcare system dynamics, including rapid urbanization, labor migration to high-prevalence countries like those in the Middle East, and limited access to harm reduction services.2,3 Despite a relatively low prevalence compared to high-burden regions like sub-Saharan Africa, Bangladesh faces unique challenges due to its high population density, limited healthcare infrastructure, and socio-behavioral risk factors such as stigma, gender inequality, and migration.3,4 Unlike sub-Saharan Africa, where heterosexual transmission predominates and prevalence is driven by widespread community-level spread, Bangladesh's epidemic is largely driven by injection drug use (with HIV prevalence among PWID reaching 27 % in some urban areas) and commercial sex networks, exacerbated by low condom use (below 50 % in high-risk groups) and cultural taboos that hinder open discussions on sexual health.2,3 National strategies, such as the National AIDS/STD Programme (NASP) established in 1985 and updated with the 4th National Strategic Plan for HIV and AIDS (2018–2023), have focused on targeted interventions like needle exchange programs, voluntary counseling and testing, and antiretroviral therapy (ART) rollout since 2004, covering over 80 % of diagnosed cases by 2022.2 However, challenges for females include low knowledge levels (only 14 % of married women have comprehensive HIV awareness) and barriers to services due to patriarchal norms, leading to delayed diagnosis and higher vulnerability.4,5 The epidemic's trajectory in Bangladesh has been shaped by increasing incidence and prevalence, with notable sex-specific disparities that necessitate targeted public health interventions.5

Globally, HIV/AIDS has claimed millions of lives and continues to impose significant disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) and years of life lost (YLLs).6 In low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) like Bangladesh, the burden is exacerbated by delayed diagnosis, limited access to antiretroviral therapy (ART), and inadequate prevention programs.7 Epidemiological data from 1990 to 2021 indicate a rising trend in HIV/AIDS metrics in Bangladesh, including incidence, prevalence, deaths, DALYs, years lived with disability (YLDs), and YLLs.8 These trends underscore the urgency of understanding temporal dynamics and sex-specific differences to inform evidence-based strategies.

Sex disparities in HIV/AIDS outcomes are well-documented globally, with females often facing higher vulnerability due to biological, social, and structural factors.9 In Bangladesh, gender norms, limited healthcare access for women, and occupational migration among men contribute to differential disease burdens.10 Previous studies have highlighted the role of key populations—such as sex workers, men who have sex with men (MSM), and people who inject drugs (PWID)—in driving HIV transmission, yet comprehensive analyses of sex-stratified trends remain sparse.11 Furthermore, long-term forecasting of HIV/AIDS metrics is critical for strategic resource allocation and policy planning, particularly in resource-constrained settings.12

This study aims to address these gaps by conducting a retrospective analysis of HIV/AIDS epidemiological trends in Bangladesh from 1990 to 2021, with a focus on sex-specific disparities. By employing joinpoint regression to identify temporal shifts and autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) models for forecasting through 2050, this research seeks to provide actionable insights for public health planning. The objectives are threefold: (1) to quantify the temporal evolution of HIV/AIDS burden across key metrics, (2) to elucidate sex-specific differences in epidemiological trends, and (3) to project future disease burden to guide targeted interventions. This analysis is poised to inform policymakers, clinicians, and public health practitioners in mitigating the epidemic's impact in Bangladesh.

Materials and methods

Data source

Epidemiological data on HIV/AIDS in Bangladesh from 1990 to 2021 were obtained from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) Study 2021, a comprehensive database managed by the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME).13 The GBD dataset provides robust, age-standardized estimates for DALYs, deaths, incidence, prevalence, YLDs, and YLLs, adjusted for population size and age structure to ensure comparability.14 Data were extracted for both sexes combined and stratified by sex (male and female) to enable detailed analysis of disparities. All metrics were reported as age-standardized rates (ASRs) per 100,000 population, with 95 % confidence intervals (CIs) to account for uncertainty.

Statistical analysis

Joinpoint regression analysis

To evaluate temporal trends and identify significant changes in HIV/AIDS metrics, joinpoint regression analysis was performed using the Joinpoint Regression Program (version 5.0.2, National Cancer Institute).15 This method detects inflection points (joinpoints) where statistically significant changes in trend occur, estimating the annual percent change (APC) for each segment and the average annual percent change (AAPC) over the entire period (1990–2021).16 The AAPC was calculated as a weighted average of segment-specific APCs, with weights proportional to segment length. Statistical significance was assessed at p < 0.05, with 95 % CIs computed using a log-linear model. The Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) was employed to select the optimal number of joinpoints, balancing model fit and complexity.17 Sex-stratified analyses were conducted to elucidate disparities in trends between males and females.

ARIMA forecasting

Long-term forecasting of HIV/AIDS ASRs through 2050 was conducted using autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) models, implemented in R (version 4.3.2) with the forecast package.18 ARIMA models were selected for their ability to capture temporal dependencies, trends, and seasonality in time-series data.19 For each metric (DALYs, deaths, incidence, prevalence, YLDs, YLLs), the optimal ARIMA(p,d,q) model was determined using the Weighted Bayesian Information Criterion (WBIC), which penalizes model complexity while maximizing fit.20 The parameters p (autoregressive order), d (differencing order), and q (moving average order) were automatically selected via the auto.arima function, ensuring stationarity and minimizing residual autocorrelation.21 Historical data from 1990 to 2021 were used to fit the models, and forecasts were generated with 95 % prediction intervals to quantify uncertainty. Sex-stratified forecasts were produced to assess differential trajectories.

Data validation and sensitivity analysis

Data quality was assessed by cross-referencing GBD estimates with national surveillance reports from the Bangladesh Ministry of Health and Family Welfare and UNAIDS.22,23 Sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate the robustness of ARIMA forecasts by varying model parameters (e.g., differencing order) and assessing the impact of outliers. All statistical analyses adhered to GBD protocols for handling missing data and uncertainty.24

Ethical considerations

As the study utilized aggregated, de-identified data from a publicly available database, ethical approval was not required. Data handling complied with IHME guidelines for confidentiality and transparency.25

Results

Disability-adjusted life years (DALYs)

Overall

The burden of HIV/AIDS, measured in DALYs, increased significantly from 1990 to 2021, with an average annual percent change (AAPC) of 19.0332 % (95 % CI: 15.8145, 23.0026, p < 0.000001). With six joinpoints, DALYs rose sharply (Fig. 1, Table 1) from 1990 to 1994 (APC: 36.9532 %, 95 % CI: 35.7305, 39.0647, p < 0.000001), followed by a sustained increase from 1994 to 2004 (APC: 33.9144 %, 95 % CI: 33.5268, 34.2542, p < 0.000001). The growth rate moderated from 2004 to 2007 (APC: 25.3509 %, 95 % CI: 24.4796, 26.7185, p < 0.000001) and further slowed from 2007 to 2012 (APC: 12.3716 %, 95 % CI: 11.8995, 12.862, p < 0.000001). A modest increase occurred from 2012 to 2016 (APC: 4.5661 %, 95 % CI: 4.073, 5.1967, p < 0.000001), followed by a decline from 2016 to 2019 (APC: −0.9481 %, 95 % CI: −1.6084, −0.0957, p = 0.041592) and a sharp decrease from 2019 to 2021 (APC: −11.2299 %, 95 % CI: −12.313, −10.2496, p < 0.000001).

Discussion

The findings of this study provide a comprehensive overview of the HIV/AIDS epidemic in Bangladesh from 1990 to 2021, revealing a significant increase in disease burden across all metrics, with pronounced sex-specific disparities. The AAPC for DALYs (19.0332 %, 95 % CI: 15.8145, 23.0026) and deaths (18.9645 %, 95 % CI: 15.7008, 23.2166) underscores the epidemic's escalation, particularly in the 1990s and early 2000s, driven by limited ART access, inadequate prevention programs, and high-risk behaviors among key populations.26 These increases contrast with global trends, where the AAPC for HIV/AIDS DALYs declined by 0.7 % annually from 1990 to 2019, and in neighboring South Asian countries like India, where incidence rates stabilized earlier due to more robust national programs.6,8 In Bangladesh, the underlying reasons include persistent stigma (with 70 % of high-risk groups reporting discrimination), low testing rates (less than 20 % among PWID), and migration-driven transmission, which differ from sub-Saharan Africa's broader heterosexual spread by concentrating risk in urban hotspots like Dhaka.2,3,10 National policies, such as the expansion of NASP's targeted interventions since 2010, including opioid substitution therapy and community-led testing, have contributed to post-2016 declines, but gaps in female-specific services—where less than 10 % of women in rural areas access HIV education—exacerbate disparities.4,5,22,23 The higher AAPCs for females across all metrics (e.g., 21.7252 % for DALYs, 21.8808 % for deaths) reflect structural vulnerabilities, including gender-based violence, limited healthcare access, and socio-cultural barriers to testing and treatment.27 These findings align with global evidence highlighting women's disproportionate HIV burden in patriarchal settings.28

Joinpoint analysis identified critical inflection points, with peak APCs in the 1990s reflecting the epidemic's early expansion, followed by moderated growth in the 2000s and declines post-2016, particularly from 2019 to 2021. These declines likely reflect the scale-up of ART, improved testing, and prevention programs, such as needle exchange initiatives and condom promotion.29 However, the persistent rise in prevalence (AAPC: 18.2239 %, 95 % CI: 14.5688, 22.2078) highlights ongoing transmission, particularly among key populations like PWID and MSM.30 The stabilization of incidence rates post-2017 suggests that prevention efforts may be curbing new infections, but the high prevalence forecasts (82.83243 per 100,000 by 2050) indicate a growing population living with HIV, necessitating expanded ART coverage and chronic care infrastructure.31

ARIMA forecasts project a decline in DALYs, deaths, and YLLs to negligible levels by 2050, but the wide CIs (e.g., DALYs: 0, 583.3697) underscore substantial uncertainty. This variability may stem from unpredictable factors such as funding for HIV programs, emergence of drug resistance, or changes in migration patterns.32 The dramatic projected increase in prevalence, particularly for males (614.7463 per 100,000, 95 % CI: 0, 695680.4), suggests that current interventions may not adequately address long-term transmission dynamics, especially among high-risk groups.33 Compared to global projections, where prevalence is expected to decline by 30 % by 2030 under sustained interventions, Bangladesh's rising male prevalence underscores urgency, as unaddressed increases could strain limited resources (with HIV funding at only $0.20 per capita annually) and reverse recent gains if not tackled through enhanced NASP strategies like PrEP rollout.1,6,12,23 These trends are being partially addressed via the 5th National Strategic Plan (2024–2028), but urgency arises from the potential for epidemic resurgence amid population growth and climate-induced migration.2,3 These projections emphasize the need for sustained surveillance and adaptive strategies to manage a growing population of people living with HIV (PLHIV).

Sex-specific disparities warrant particular attention. Females exhibited higher AAPCs across all metrics, likely due to biological factors (e.g., higher mucosal susceptibility) and social determinants (e.g., stigma, economic dependence).34 The sharper decline in male deaths and YLLs post-2019 may reflect better healthcare access for men, highlighting inequities in service delivery.35 These findings underscore the need for gender-sensitive interventions, such as women-centered testing and treatment programs and male-focused prevention campaigns targeting high-risk behaviors.36

Study limitations

This study has several limitations. First, reliance on GBD data introduces potential biases due to modeling assumptions and incomplete national surveillance data, particularly in the early 1990s when HIV reporting in Bangladesh was limited.37 Second, the ARIMA models, while robust for time-series forecasting, assume stationarity and may not fully capture non-linear dynamics or external shocks (e.g., policy changes, pandemics).38 Third, the wide CIs in long-term forecasts reflect inherent uncertainty, limiting precision for 2050 projections. Fourth, the study did not account for subnational variations or key population-specific data, which could mask localized epidemic drivers.39 Finally, the lack of data on ART adherence, viral suppression rates, and behavioral risk factors limits the ability to assess intervention effectiveness comprehensively.40

Future directions and recommendations

Future research should prioritize granular analyses of subnational and key population data to identify localized epidemic drivers and tailor interventions.41 Integrating real-time surveillance data with machine learning models could enhance forecasting accuracy and capture non-linear trends.42 Additionally, studies exploring the impact of ART scale-up, drug resistance, and social determinants (e.g., stigma, migration) are critical to understanding long-term epidemic dynamics.43 Public health strategies should focus on:

1.Gender-Sensitive Interventions: Develop women-centered HIV testing and treatment programs to address structural barriers, alongside male-focused campaigns targeting high-risk groups like MSM and PWID.44

Conclusion

This study provides a robust analysis of HIV/AIDS epidemiological trends in Bangladesh from 1990 to 2021, revealing a significant increase in disease burden with marked sex-specific disparities. Females consistently exhibited higher AAPCs across DALYs, deaths, incidence, prevalence, YLDs, and YLLs, underscoring the need for gender-sensitive interventions. Recent declines in key metrics suggest the efficacy of public health efforts, but the projected rise in prevalence by 2050 highlights ongoing challenges in managing a growing population of PLHIV. The considerable uncertainty in long-term forecasts emphasizes the importance of sustained surveillance, adaptive interventions, and equitable resource allocation. By prioritizing gender-specific strategies, ART expansion, and enhanced data systems, Bangladesh can mitigate the HIV/AIDS epidemic's impact and achieve equitable health outcomes by 2050.
