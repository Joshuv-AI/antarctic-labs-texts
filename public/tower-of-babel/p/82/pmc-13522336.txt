A dataset from ten disasters for studying location descriptions and training AI models
Authors: Kai Sun, Yingjie Hu, Kenneth Joseph
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPeople have been increasingly using social media to post messages during a natural disaster, and describe the locations of victims, damages, difficult situations, and relief resources. Many of these location descriptions are in the forms of detailed and multi-entity descriptions, such as door number addresses, road intersections, and highway exits. Currently, there is limited availability of datasets that contain these detailed location descriptions labeled in disaster-related messages. A lack of these datasets hinders the understanding of how people describe locations during disasters and the automatic extraction of these location descriptions. This paper fills this gap by providing a dataset that covers ten disasters in the United States and in five disaster types: hurricanes, floods, wildfires, tornados, and winter storms. The messages containing location descriptions are collected from the social media platform Twitter/X, and we describe the collection, labeling, and validation of this dataset. This dataset can be used for studying the ways people describe locations under disaster contexts and for training AI models to extract these important locations.

Background & Summary

Location information is highly important during a natural disaster for reaching victims and coordinating response and relief efforts. In recent years, people have been increasingly using social media platforms during a disaster to post messages and describe important locations1–4. Two example messages (tweets) from two different disasters are provided below (the content of the tweets is slightly modified for privacy protection):

Accurately extracting and geo-locating these location descriptions can help disaster responders reach victims more quickly and even save lives. In addition to locations of victims, people also describe locations of disaster damages, difficult situations, and relief resources.

Currently, there is limited availability of datasets containing these detailed location descriptions from disaster-related messages. While a number of disaster-related social media datasets have been created over the past years, we cannot directly extract labeled location descriptions from existing datasets, for two major reasons. First, some existing datasets do not label location descriptions, and focus on labeling other types of information from disaster-related messages. For example, the HumAID dataset5 labels the main topics of disaster-related messages (e.g., whether the main topic of the message is about “sympathy and support” or “injured or dead people”), while the dataset by Huang & Xiao1 labels the disaster phase of a message (e.g., whether a message was posted during the disaster response phase or recovery phase). Second, while some datasets focus on labeling location descriptions, they contain mostly coarse location descriptions (e.g., country names, state/province names, and city names) rather than fine-grained and detailed descriptions. For example, less than 5% of location descriptions in the GeoCorpora dataset are fine-grained6,7, while fine-grained descriptions account for only 9.77% of the IDRISI-RE dataset8. In addition, existing datasets are often based on disaster-related messages from all over the world and have limited focus on one particular country. Examples of worldwide datasets include HumAID5, IDRISI-RE8, and DLRGeoTweet7. While such worldwide coverage is highly valuable for some applications, it limits our ability to study the typical patterns of location descriptions used by people in a specific country, since these descriptions often vary from one country to another due to different address formats and languages used.

To fill this gap, we create a dataset with detailed location descriptions from 10 major disasters in the United States. By “creating” a dataset, we mean collecting and curating real messages from disaster events, and labeling the location descriptions contained in these messages; we do not mean “creating” messages in a synthetic sense. An early version of this dataset has been used in our previous studies9,10 and has also been used or discussed by other researchers7,8,11. That early version, however, contained only 1,000 tweets from a single disaster (Hurricane Harvey). The dataset presented in this paper is a substantial extension of the early version. Creating this dataset requires substantial amounts of financial, labor, and time resources. By sharing this dataset, we hope that other researchers and practitioners do not need to spend such resources and can directly use this dataset to advance their work.

This dataset can be used for two major purposes in disaster research and practical applications. First, it enables researchers to study how people describe locations under disaster contexts. For example, researchers can identify typical location description patterns associated with different types of disasters, and can explore how location descriptions may vary across different message topics. Knowledge gained through these studies may help disaster managers develop strategies to geo-locate victims and coordinate disaster response efforts. Disaster managers may also identify effective and less effective location descriptions used by people, and conduct community outreach activities to help people learn effective ways to communicate their locations under emergency situations. Second, this dataset can be used for training AI models to automatically extract detailed location descriptions from disaster-related messages. Specifically, AI models can be trained for identifying location descriptions from a message and classifying them into different categories, e.g., door number address and road intersection. The extracted and categorized location descriptions can then be located via corresponding geospatial operations, such as street-based linear geocoding, intersection identification, and gazetteer matching. The trained AI models could be further integrated into real-time disaster response systems to assist in tasks such as locating disaster victims and mapping incidents. While this dataset is based on tweets, we believe that it can also be used in projects involving disaster-related messages from other social media platforms, since the ways people describe locations follow social norms and are unlikely to change substantially across these platforms. We hope that this dataset can serve as a useful resource for supporting future research and disaster response efforts.

Methods

A workflow was designed to create this dataset, which consisted of three major steps (Fig. 1). In Step 1, we collected disaster-related messages using the application programming interface (API) of Twitter/X, and focused on five types of natural disasters in the United States in recent years. In Step 2, we prepared the data for annotation by conducting data filtering and data sampling, and further split the data into batches to facilitate the data annotation process. In Step 3, we hired a group of human annotators with geographic information system (GIS) and disaster management backgrounds to collaboratively annotate the data, and we further performed quality checks on the annotated results. In the following, we present details about each step. The code for collecting and preparing this dataset, including the Twitter/X API end point used, is shared on GitHub: https://github.com/geoai-lab/DisasterLocDesc.

Step 1: Data collection

In the first step, we collected social media messages posted by people during recent major disasters in the U.S. We used the Billion-Dollar Disasters website (https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/access/billions/) from the U.S. National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) to identify major disasters, and focused on five types of disasters that have devastated many communities: hurricanes, wildfires, floods, tornados, and winter storms. To ensure a balanced representation, we selected two disaster events per disaster type. The selected disasters are:

Hurricanes: 2017 Hurricane Harvey, 2022 Hurricane Ian

Hurricanes: 2017 Hurricane Harvey, 2022 Hurricane Ian

Wildfires: 2018 California Camp Fire, 2023 Hawaii Firestorm

Wildfires: 2018 California Camp Fire, 2023 Hawaii Firestorm

Floods: 2022 St. Louis Floods, 2022 California Floods

Floods: 2022 St. Louis Floods, 2022 California Floods

Tornados: 2020 Easter Tornado Outbreak, 2021 Kentucky Tornado

Tornados: 2020 Easter Tornado Outbreak, 2021 Kentucky Tornado

Winter storms: 2021 Texas Winter Storm, 2022 Buffalo Blizzard

Winter storms: 2021 Texas Winter Storm, 2022 Buffalo Blizzard

These 10 disasters cover diverse geographic regions in the country, including the East Coast, the West Coast, the Great Lakes region, the Gulf Coast, the Middle States region, and the Hawaiian Islands.

With the disaster events selected, we used the Twitter/X API to collect public tweets that were posted during these disasters. Due to the recent changes of Twitter/X, using its API requires a fee. To create this dataset, we purchased one-month usage of the Twitter/X API. We carefully constructed search queries for each of the 10 disasters. Here, the data we need are tweets that are related to the target disaster and that also contain location descriptions. With this consideration, we constructed each search query by linking three components: (1) time range of the disaster, (2) disaster-related keywords, and (3) location-related keywords. For (1) time range of the disaster, we first focused on the recorded disaster days to retrieve relevant tweets. In cases when the number of initially retrieved tweets was small, we conducted additional data retrieval iterations by covering the days in the disaster recovery and disaster preparation phases. The full time ranges of the retrieved data for the 10 disasters are summarized in the Data Overview section. For (2) disaster-related keywords, we used keywords and hashtags associated with each disaster. For example, for the 2023 Hawaii Firestorm, the disaster-related keywords include “#MauiFires”, “#HawaiiFires”, “#LahainaFires”, “Hawaii wildfires”, and others. These keywords and hashtags were identified based on manual searching and inspection of the tweets posted for each of the ten disasters. For (3) location-related keywords, we utilized a list of terms commonly used by people in the U.S. to describe detailed locations, such as “street”, “avenue”, “park”, “square”, “bridge”, “rd”, and “ave”9. This approach allows us to focus on retrieving the tweets that contain detailed location descriptions. We note that using these keywords may exclude some location descriptions that do not contain explicit location terms. It is necessary to do so for creating this dataset because only a small percentage of tweets contain fine-grained location descriptions. Without using location keywords, we would end up with a dataset containing very few location descriptions. Nevertheless, this limitation may need to be considered in future use of this dataset. We also excluded retweets and focused on English tweets in the search queries. The data collection was conducted from May to June 2024, and a total of 147,976 tweets that met all search criteria were collected.

Step 2: Data preparation

In the second step, we preprocessed and prepared the collected tweets with the goal of making them ready for data annotation. We first applied three filters to the collected data: a tweet-length filter, a deduplication filter, and a location-term filter. The tweet-length filter removed tweets with fewer than three words, which were less likely to contain meaningful information5. The deduplication filter removed tweets that had exactly the same textual content. These duplications were often found when two tweets had the same textual content but included different URLs. We kept only the first tweet for these duplications. The third filter was a location-term filter which filtered out the tweets that did not contain any location terms as used in the search queries. The existence of these tweets initially surprised us since we had explicitly required the tweets to contain location terms in our constructed search queries. Upon further examination, we found that this situation often happened when the external URLs of the tweets contained location terms. Nevertheless, we filtered out these tweets since their textual content did not contain location terms. After applying these three filters, we obtained a dataset with 53,404 tweets.

While the filtered dataset had a much smaller size, it still exceeded the capacity of a small group of human annotators to do the data annotation work. Thus, we designed a random sampling strategy to further obtain a smaller subset. To ensure that the final dataset does not over-represent or underrepresent a particular disaster, we designed our sampling strategy as follows. For a given disaster, if the total number of initially-collected tweets is fewer than 500, we retain all of them; otherwise, we randomly sample 10% of the tweets, with a minimum of 500 tweets and a maximum of 2,000 tweets. This sampling strategy allows each disaster to have a substantial number of tweets, while the disasters with a large number of initially-collected tweets do not dominate this dataset. Eventually, a total of 7,149 tweets were obtained after this sampling process.

We further split the 7,149 tweets into 15 data batches, with each batch containing between 100 and 700 tweets. These separate data batches facilitated the data annotation process by allowing human annotators to work in parallel. They also facilitated the quality checking process, i.e., we could check the quality of each data batch once it was done instead of having to wait until the entire dataset was completed to do one check. We also designed the batches in a way that the early batches (i.e., Batch 1 to 5) had smaller sizes and the later batches (i.e., Batch 6 to 15) had larger sizes. This small-to-large batch size design allowed human annotators and our research team to catch and correct annotation mistakes early on, and helped avoid mistakes in larger batches later that could be more costly to correct.

Step 3: Data annotation

With data prepared, we moved on to data annotation. Annotating detailed location descriptions from disaster-related messages was a challenging task. Previous studies have shown difficulties of using a crowdsourced approach to label location descriptions8, and some studies have instead convened a group of human annotators and conducted data annotation in house6,7. In this work, we also convened a group of human annotators by hiring six individuals with GIS backgrounds to annotate data. One of the human annotators also had a disaster management background.

For each of the 7,149 tweets, four elements were annotated: (1) location description text; (2) location category; (3) spatial footprint; and (4) topic of the message. Note that one tweet could have more than one location description, and in such cases, elements (1) to (3) were annotated for each of the location descriptions. Each tweet was annotated with only one main topic. Although one tweet could be related to multiple topics as well, we asked the human annotators to annotate only one main topic. This was because the focus of this dataset was on location descriptions, and asking human annotators to annotate one main topic reduced their workload, allowed them to focus on the main task, and also added some topic information into the created dataset.

For location description text, human annotators needed to label the textual content that described a location, such as “8219 Woodfield St, Houston 77424” and “Walden Ave & Benbro dr”. For location categories, we used a schema of ten categories. This schema was refined from our previous work9,10. We note that some other location category schemas have also been used in previous corpora. For example, the IDRISI-RE dataset used a schema with 12 location categories: “Continent”, “Country”, “State”, “County”, “City/town”, “District”, “Island”, “Neighborhood”, “Road/street”, “Human-made POI”, “Natural POI” and “Other locations”8. An earlier work by Gelernter & Balaji12 used a schema with four location categories, i.e., “street”, “building”, “toponym”, and “abbreviation”. Our location category schema differs from previous schemas by considering both the nature of location descriptions and the geospatial operations needed to locate them. In addition, our schema also includes a few fine-grained location categories that are not included in previous schemas, such as door number addresses, intersections, and highway exits. For spatial footprint, human annotators created the spatial representation for each location description. The spatial footprints could be points, lines, and polygons. We provided a general guideline for human annotators on the geometry type to use, which were consistent with typical GIS data representations14. For example, points were used for door number addresses and intersections; lines were used for roads, rivers, and highways; and polygons were used for parks and neighborhoods. Creating these spatial footprints requires GIS skills and knowledge, and our human annotators have the necessary GIS background to complete this task. In addition, a special geo-annotation tool, called GALLOC15, was used to support the creation of these spatial footprints.

The main topic of each message was also annotated, as done in other disaster-related datasets5,16. The rationale of doing so is that while it slightly increases the annotation workload, it enables much richer analyses later based on the annotated data (e.g., identifying the typical forms of location descriptions used in messages under the topic Requests or urgent needs). We used a specialized geo-annotation tool developed in our previous study, called GALLOC15, to support this data annotation project. GALLOC is an open-source and Web-based geo-annotation tool which provides a map-based user interface that facilitates data annotation work involving both textual messages and geospatial representations. We also slightly extended GALLOC in this work to support the annotation of message topics. Figure 2 shows a screenshot of GALLOC. Human annotators used GALLOC to annotate each message following four steps: (1) selecting and labeling the text of a location description; (2) choosing the category of this location description; (3) creating the spatial footprint which can be a point, a polyline, or a polygon; and (4) selecting the main topic of this message. These four steps are also shown in Fig. 2. For spatial footprints, GALLOC provides functions for supporting both automatic and manual creation of geometries. GALLOC integrates Google Maps API and Nominatim API (an OpenStreetMap-based geolocation Web service), and can automatically create the spatial footprint of a location description based on the labeled text. These two APIs complement each other in that location descriptions that cannot be found in one API may be found in the other. Google Maps API only returns point-based spatial footprints, while Nominatim can return points, polylines, and polygons. We asked human annotators to first use these functions to create spatial footprints automatically. In cases when the spatial footprint returned by Google Maps and Nominatim was unsatisfactory or no spatial footprint was returned, human annotators could use the drawing tools provided by GALLOC to manually revise the returned result or draw a spatial footprint from scratch. The source code of GALLOC is available on GitHub at: https://github.com/geoai-lab/GALLOC. The methodological details for developing the GALLOC tool have been published previously15, and we focus on the created dataset in this paper.

The entire data annotation process was done as follows. First, all human annotators attended a required orientation and training session about this data annotation project, the data to be annotated, and the data annotation tool GALLOC. Human annotators were also required to study the provided data annotation guidelines. Second, all human annotators were required to pass a data annotation test by annotating 25 example messages. Human annotators were considered as passing this test if their annotation has a 70% or higher consistency rate compared with the reference annotation of these 25 examples. If failing the test, a human annotator met with the first author to discuss mistakes and was given another chance to take the test. Third, human annotators started to work on the data batches in parallel, and each message was annotated by two annotators independently. We put two annotators on each message because previous research showed that location descriptions can be interpreted in different ways that are all reasonably correct9,10,17. For example, one may annotate a neighborhood name “River Oaks”, while another annotator may label “River Oaks neighborhood” by including the nearby word “neighborhood”. Thus, having two annotators for the same message can help add such interpretation diversity to the annotation results. Fourth, after a data batch was completed, it went through a quality check process. If it passed the quality check, the annotator would move on to the next batch; otherwise, this batch would need to be re-annotated. This process was repeated until all batches were completed. During the data annotation process, a Slack chat group was created to allow the human annotators and the research team to quickly discuss and resolve issues. A weekly meeting was also held in which human annotators reported data annotation progresses, shared experiences, and discussed issues and solutions.

Data Record

The dataset is available at Figshare18. In terms of file organization, this dataset contains 15 JavaScript Object Notation (JSON) files for the 15 batches of tweets, which are named with the batch number and disaster event name. We also share the source code for collecting and preparing this dataset and the data annotation guidelines in the GitHub repository: https://github.com/geoai-lab/DisasterLocDesc. The source code is provided in four Python files, and the data annotation guidelines are included in a PDF file.

In each JSON file, each line represents one annotation for a tweet. Figure 3 shows one example of the data record. Each data record is a JSON object that contains the original tweet id, annotated location descriptions, and annotated message topic. Annotated location descriptions are organized into a list via the “Annotation” attribute. In this list, each annotated location description contains its start and end positions in character index via the “startIdx” and “endIdx” attributes, the location description text (via the “locationDesc” attribute), the location category (via the “locationCate” attribute), and the spatial footprint (via the “spatialFootprint” attribute). Depending on a specific message, this list can contain zero (i.e., this message does not contain any location description), one, or multiple location descriptions. The “MessageTopic” attribute contains the annotated main topic of this message, such as “Requests or urgent needs” in the example below. Following the data sharing policy of X/Twitter, the dataset does not contain full tweets but contains tweet IDs.

Technical Validation

The quality of this dataset is ensured through both the data annotation process and quality check after each batch of data has been annotated. For the data annotation process, we have carefully designed and embedded multiple procedures in the data annotation workflow to ensure that the messages are annotated with high quality. These procedures include: (1) a required orientation and training session for all human annotators; (2) annotation guidelines that all human annotators are required to study; (3) an annotation test that each human annotator must pass before they can start the data annotation task; (4) a Slack chat group and weekly meetings that allow the authors and human annotators to discuss and resolve issues encountered during annotation; and (5) a requirement that a data batch must be re-done if it fails the quality check, and this requirement is communicated to all human annotators before they agree to join this project.

For quality check, a reference dataset is constructed by randomly sampling 5% of the messages from each data batch which are carefully annotated by the authors. We then write a Python script to compare the annotations from the human annotators with the reference annotations. An annotation is considered as consistent with the reference if it has the same location description text span, the same location category, and a spatial footprint within a tolerance threshold. Specifically, the text span of the annotated location description is compared with that of the reference annotation based on their start and end positions in the message, and the annotated location category is compared with the reference category using string matching. In terms of spatial footprints, we require the annotated footprint to have the same geometry type as the reference footprint. For point-based footprints, we further calculate the geodesic distance between the annotated and reference footprints, and we require their distance to be within 1000 meters to consider the annotated footprint consistent with the reference. For polyline- and polygon-based footprints, we calculate the intersection-over-union (IoU) of their bounding boxes, and consider the annotated footprint consistent with the reference if the IoU is higher than 0.7. IoU is a metric that assesses the overlap between two bounding boxes, and is calculated as a ratio between the area of intersection and the area of union. We do not compare message topics, since our primary focus in this work is location descriptions and one message could be related to more than one topic (e.g., “Damages and disruptions” and “Information and advice”). For the annotations that are identified as inconsistent from the reference data by the Python script, we further conduct manual inspections to understand the reasons. Some of these inconsistencies are due to slight differences in the annotated text spans (e.g., including or not including a nearby space character) or different but valid interpretations of location descriptions (e.g., labeling an ambiguous location as “Uncertain location”). Some other inconsistencies are indeed mistakes, e.g., missing a valid location description or choosing an incorrect location category. We have also considered using Inter-Annotator Agreement (IAA) metrics such as Cohen’s Kappa20–22 to assess this dataset, but have decided not to do so. This is for two reasons. First, IAA metrics are generally used when reference annotations are not available, while we have reference annotations in this work. Second, IAA measures the agreement between two annotators, and does not capture situations when two annotators have different but both valid interpretations of location descriptions. Given these two reasons, we think that annotation accuracy is more suitable for quantifying the quality of the annotated location descriptions.

Our dataset keeps two annotations for each disaster-related message. For training and evaluating AI models, we suggest taking into account both annotations, rather than using one annotation alone, to accommodate the possibly different but valid interpretations of locations. There has been increasing discussion in the broader AI and natural language processing literature on incorporating different human annotations for training AI models23–25. We believe that incorporating different perspectives is especially important for working with location descriptions, given the uncertain nature of location descriptions as discussed in the literature6,8,26–28 and based on our experiences working on this topic9,10,15. One simple approach is to consider the output of an AI model as correct if it matches either one of the two annotations. Advanced methods, such as soft-label learning24, could also be used to train AI models that can output a probability distribution over different human annotations.

Limitations

This dataset is not without limitations. First, it contains only English messages, while other languages are also used by people under disaster contexts. It can be helpful to create similar datasets in other languages beyond English, and our data annotation tool GALLOC15 supports the annotation of data in multiple languages. Second, while this dataset covers five types of major disasters that happened in the United States in recent years, it does not include earthquakes, which are another type of disasters. Future work could look into recent earthquake events in other countries to create such datasets. Despite these limitations, we hope that this dataset will serve as a useful resource for researchers and disaster managers to understand how people describe locations during natural disasters and to develop new AI models for extracting these important location descriptions.
